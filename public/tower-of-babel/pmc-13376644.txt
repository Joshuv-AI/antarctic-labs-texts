Spin-state regulation of high-entropy Ruddlesden-Popper perovskite oxides for efficient seawater electrolysis
Authors: Yifan Liu, Libin Hao, Caichao Ye, Yin Huang, Jing Jin, Mingming Gong, Long Chen, Jingwen Sun, Li Shi, Jingnan Wang, Wei Xu, Wenqing Zhang, Xin Wang, Pan Xiong, Junwu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-entropy oxides have emerged as a promising group of candidates for various catalytic fields, including seawater electrolysis. However, without rational understanding and guidance, designing multielement compositions for high-entropy systems and elucidating the synergistic effect of diverse elements in catalysis remain significant challenges. Herein, we report spin-state regulation of a high-entropy Ruddlesden–Popper perovskite oxide for efficient seawater electrolysis. The Cu2+ and Mn3+ with Jahn-Teller effect induced a transition of Fe3+/Co3+ spin state from low spin to high spin configuration. The high spin Fe3+/Co3+ favor OH− adsorption and deprotonation during seawater oxidation. Besides, the oxygen intermediates adsorbed on high spin active sites repel the Cl− ions. Consequently, (La0.76Sr0.24)3(Fe0.22Co0.21Ni0.18Cu0.17Mn0.22)2O7 achieved robust seawater oxidation for over 1200 h at 200 mA cm−2, showing competitive performance. The anion exchange membrane electrolyzer coupling with (La0.76Sr0.24)3(Fe0.22Co0.21Ni0.18Cu0.17Mn0.22)2O7 anode and Pt/C cathode could maintain the seawater splitting performance of 1 A cm−2 at 1.76 V and incessantly operate for 800 h. This spin-engineered strategy is universal to other high-entropy perovskite oxides and spinel oxides, offering promising way for designing efficient high-entropy catalysts.

Introduction

Green hydrogen, which is generated through water electrolysis using renewable electricity, has gained significant importance owing to its pollution-free nature and superior gravimetric energy density1. Compared to conventional water electrolysis using high-purity water as feedstock2, direct seawater electrolysis is a more appealing alternative. It is due to the scarcity of freshwater resources in many regions of the world, as well as the huge amount of seawater on our planet3. However, the complex composition of seawater poses substantial challenges to designing efficient electrocatalysts. The major challenges for electrocatalysts are Cl− adsorption and chlorine evolution reaction (CER) at the anode4. Diverse methods have been undertaken to engineer conventional metal oxide electrocatalysts, including building Cl− repulsion layer5,6, tailoring local environment7,8, and introducing oxygen evolution reaction (OER) protective layer9,10. However, conventional metal oxides with simple compositions and restricted active sites may struggle to achieve high-efficiency electrocatalysis within complex seawater environments, thus limiting their potential to meet the advanced requirements of future seawater oxidations.

High-entropy catalysts, such as high-entropy oxides (HEOs), have developed rapidly in recent years and are receiving notable attention for catalytic applications11. Due to the intrinsic traits of cocktail and sluggish-diffusion effect12, HEOs exhibit superior electrocatalytic activities and enhanced intrinsic stability than conventional metal oxides13,14. There are some compelling examples where HEOs with five components act as a threshold for entropy-induced property enhancement. For example, five-component rock salt HEOs showed a pronounced enhancement of electrochemical activity and cycling stability than any of the four-component analogs15. High-entropy (CoFeNiMnW)3O4 oxide leverages its high-entropy composition to optimally balance different oxygen evolution pathways, overcoming the traditional activity-stability trade-off and delivering a lower overpotential than ternary counterparts16. Besides, defect-rich high-entropy spinel oxide achieves higher water oxidation activity than quaternary counterparts in both freshwater and seawater environments17. High-entropy oxides containing noble metals can utilize their multi-metal coordination environment to supply electrons to the Ru/Ir active sites. This effectively suppresses the over-oxidation and dissolution of the noble metals, thereby significantly enhancing the stability of the catalyst18. While recent studies have impressively leveraged the “cocktail” effects and entropy stabilization to enhance performances, the development of HEOs catalysts is still impeded by unclear catalytic mechanisms due to complex elemental composition and the lack of rational principles for compositional design19. Then the question arises, whether the random incorporation of five components into HEOs can effectively enhance their electrocatalytic activity and stability? In pure water splitting systems, it is appropriate to screen elements in HEOs through extensive synthesis and performance tests20. However, relying on experimental screening to identify the optimal elemental compositions of HEOs in complex seawater splitting systems is inefficient, particularly for achieving both superior seawater oxidation activity and chlorine corrosion resistance. For instance, (FeCoNiMnAl)3O4 high-entropy electrocatalyst was reported to exhibit superior seawater oxidation activity than medium-entropy counterparts because of its strong adsorption ability for anions (OH− and Cl−)21. However, the strong adsorption of Cl− can corrode the anode electrocatalysts and inevitably cause the instability in crystal structure22. Therefore, establishing theoretical guidance for rational design and component screening of HEOs in complex catalytic systems is necessary.

The spin catalysis concept has been proposed to regulate the spin electron configuration in materials with relatively simple compositions23. For instance, Xue et al. introduced coordinatively unsaturated edge sites into the CoOOH structure to induce the spin transition of Co3+ from low to high spin configuration24, resulting in the electron transfer from face-to-face t2g orbitals to apex-to-apex eg orbitals and leading to fast OER catalytic kinetics. As a result, it is speculated that the spin configuration might be a potential decisive factor that links to high-entropy effects and catalytic performance in HEOs. In principle, the M3d orbitals (M = metal sites) with different spin states in HEOs exhibit distinct overlap and interaction with O2p and Cl3p orbitals of oxygen and chlorine species25,26, respectively. Therefore, adjusting the spin states could significantly refine the chemical adsorption of OH− and Cl−, thus enhancing the oxygen selectivity and chloride repulsion abilities during seawater oxidation. However, the intricate atomic arrangements of HEOs pose significant challenges in identifying active site distributions and controlling their spin states. Thus, the relationship between the spin states and seawater electrolysis in HEOs has not yet been reported.

In this work, we reported the spin state regulation of a high-entropy Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) phase perovskite oxide (RP-FeCoNiCuMn) for efficient seawater electrolysis. Fe, Co, and Ni elements are first selected for our high-entropy catalyst design because they are widely recognized as highly active sites for water oxidation reactions27. Considering that Cu2+ and Mn3+ with Jahn-Teller effects can optimize the local coordination geometry of active sites28, they are further incorporated to induce the spin transitions of active sites from low spin (LS) to high spin (HS) configuration. The HS states of electrolytically active Fe3+ and Co3+ in RP-FeCoNiCuMn favor both the adsorption of OH− ions and the deprotonation process during the seawater oxidation reaction. Meanwhile, the preferentially adsorbed oxygen intermediates on the anode would repel the Cl− ions through electrostatic repulsion, thus enhancing the catalytic stability in alkaline seawater. Consequently, the regulated RP-FeCoNiCuMn with HS active sites exhibited robust stability and efficient activity in alkaline seawater electrolysis, outperforming the high-entropy counterparts and other reported oxide-based catalysts. A seawater anion exchange membrane (AEM) electrolyzer with RP-FeCoNiCuMn anode and commercial Pt/C cathode achieves high activity (1 A cm−2 at 1.76 V) and good stability (1 A cm−2 over 800 h). Besides, the spin-state regulation strategy is universal and can be extended to other high-entropy perovskite and spinel phases for remarkably improved catalytic activities.

Results

Synthesis of high-entropy RP-FeCoNiCuMn for seawater oxidation

The RP-FeCoNiCuMn was prepared using a simple sol-gel approach, with specific procedures outlined in the “Methods” section. Figure 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1 schematically display the high-entropy RP crystal structure. The RP phase features a 2D layered structure with the chemical formula of A3B2O7. The structure is composed of two ABO3 perovskite layers interleaved with a single AO rock-salt layer, aligned along the c-axis. Five transition metal elements (Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Mn) are introduced into the B sites of RP perovskite lattices. The single-phase structure of the RP perovskite was confirmed with X-ray diffraction (XRD) data (Fig. 1b). Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns revealed that RP-FeCoNiCuMn has an I4/mmm structure with the lattice constants of a = b = 3.915(8) Å and c = 20.140(4) Å29. The inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) confirmed that the atomic ratios of La, Sr, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Mn were 45.81%, 14.67%, 8.53%, 8.38%, 7.16%, 6.87%, and 8.58% (Supplementary Fig. 2), respectively. The scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) image and associated elemental mapping analysis demonstrated a homogeneous distribution of all constituent elements within the RP-FeCoNiCuMn (Fig. 1c). Consequently, the RP-FeCoNiCuMn with a calculated B-site configuration entropy (ΔSconfig) of 1.59 R can be classified as “high-entropy” materials30, and can yield a formula of (La0.76Sr0.24)3(Fe0.22Co0.21Ni0.18 Cu0.17Mn0.22)2O7.Fig. 1The structural characterizations and electrocatalytic measurements.a Schematic presentation of the high-entropy Ruddlesden-Popper perovskite oxide structure. b Refined XRD profile of RP-FeCoNiCuMn. c The STEM image and corresponding elemental mapping of RP-FeCoNiCuMn. d HAADF-STEM and e ABF-STEM images of RP-FeCoNiCuMn along the [001] zone axis. f Enlarged ABF-STEM image of RP-FeCoNiCuMn and corresponding line profile exhibits apparent intensity variation and misalignment of atomic columns. g Polarization curves of RP-FeCoNiCuMn, RP-FeCoNi, commercial RuO2 and NiFe LDH in simulated alkaline seawater. h long-term stability tests in simulated alkaline seawater and alkaline seawater. i Polarization curves of RP-FeCoNiCuMn, RP-FeCoNiCuCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn, and RP-FeCoNiZnCr in simulated alkaline seawater.

To further investigate the structural details of RP-FeCoNiCuMn, selected-area electron-diffraction (SAED) patterns along the [001] zone axis (Supplementary Fig. 3) and the corresponding atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and annular bright-field (ABF) STEM images are analyzed. The spacing between adjacent layers is measured as 0.27 nm (Fig. 1d), which aligns with the (1 ± 10) crystallographic plane of RP-FeCoNiCuMn. The atomic positions observed in the ABF-STEM images (Fig. 1e) align well with the simulated structure of the Ruddlesden-Popper perovskite model (the insert in Fig. 1e). The B-site transition atoms are observed to overlap with A-site La/Sr atoms in the [001] direction, which are clearly revealed by the dark dots in the ABF-STEM images. The gray areas at the centers of dark dots should be the position of overlapped O atoms and B-site transition atoms. Furthermore, the intensity variation of atomic columns for the black areas is displayed in the line profile (Fig. 1f). Changes in peak intensity and shifts are evident across these layers, suggesting the occurrence of lattice distortion within RP-FeCoNiCuMn. In a control experiment, the medium-entropy RP-FeCoNi was prepared using the same synthetic method. The Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns and ICP-MS results showed RP-FeCoNi with I4/mmm perovskite structure can yield formulas of (La0.77Sr0.23)3(Fe0.36Co0.30Ni0.34)2O7 (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5).

The seawater oxidation activities of RP-FeCoNiCuMn were evaluated in simulated alkaline seawater electrolytes, with commercial RuO2 and RP-FeCoNi for comparison. All OER test curves in this work were 95% iR correction. Taking RP-FeCoNiCuMn as an example, LSV polarization curves with or without iR correction for seawater OER are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. The RP-FeCoNiCuMn showed a low overpotential of 320 mV at 100 mA cm−2 (Fig. 1g), surpassing the RP-FeCoNi (386 mV) and commercial RuO2 (439 mV). Given the excellent performance of NiFe-based LDH materials in alkaline seawater electrolysis, NiFe-LDH was synthesized via a simple hydrothermal method as a benchmark to validate the superior seawater electrolysis performance of RP-FeCoNiCuMn (Supplementary Fig. 7)31. At a current density of 100 mA cm−2, RP-FeCoNiCuMn exhibits a lower overpotential than NiFe-LDH (360 mV). Meanwhile, the operational stability of RP-FeCoNiCuMn was performed at 200 mA cm−2 (Fig. 1h). After an operation for over 1200 h, no significant decay (the decay ratio is less than 2.8%) was observed for RP-FeCoNiCuMn electrode, which performed better than RP-FeCoNi, commercial RuO2 and NiFe-LDH. Subsequently, the dissolved metal contents in the electrolyte were examined for the cycled RP-FeCoNiCuMn and control RP-FeCoNi samples. The ICP-MS analysis revealed that the elemental concentrations of all five metal elements were observed to be less than 1.5 wt% over the stoichiometric loadings of RP-FeCoNiCuMn catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 8), whereas a severe dissolution of Co and Fe ions was detected in RP-FeCoNi. The more positive corrosion potential (Ecorr) of RP-FeCoNiCuMn indicates a lower thermodynamic tendency for corrosion, while its significantly lower corrosion current density (Icorr) quantitatively confirms the suppressed corrosion kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 9)32. These parameters imply the material’s excellent structural and performance stability during long-term seawater electrolysis.

To simulate an application-relevant environment, the OER activities of RP-FeCoNiCuMn were carried out in alkaline seawater (Supplementary Fig. 10). RP-FeCoNiCuMn showed an overpotential of 355 mV at 200 mA cm−2 in alkaline seawater and showed an increase of only 11 mV compared to that in simulated alkaline seawater (344 mV), which may be attributed to impurities in seawater (e.g., microorganisms, bacteria, and undesirable ions)33. The prolonged chronopotentiometry measurement of RP-FeCoNiCuMn was also performed in alkaline seawater (Fig. 1h). Over an operational period of 1200 h, the RP-FeCoNiCuMn exhibited durable stability with only 3.9% performance degradation under alkaline seawater. The XRD analysis confirmed the I4/mmm structure of RP-FeCoNiCuMn remained well after cycling (Supplementary Fig. 11), and no distinct change in structure over cycled RP-FeCoNiCuMn was detected in the STEM image (Supplementary Fig. 12). The structural stability of the RP-FeCoNiCuMn catalyst under operating conditions is further confirmed by HRTEM (Supplementary Fig. 13). All these results confirmed the enhanced electrocatalytic activities and durable stability of the high-entropy RP-FeCoNiCuMn sample. Supplementary Table 1 further compared the seawater oxidation activity and durability of RP-FeCoNiCuMn with some reported oxide-based electrocatalysts21,34–43. The electrocatalytic performance of RP-FeCoNiCuMn is better than most reported conventional oxide and other high-entropy oxide catalysts so far.

Electrocatalytic seawater oxidation of high-entropy catalysts

To uncover the decisive factor of high-entropy effects that promotes the catalytic performances, another three high-entropy RP phase perovskite oxides were designed and synthesized by a similar synthetic method, denoted as RP-FeCoNiCuCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn, and RP-FeCoNiZnCr. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15, all the as-obtained RP-FeCoNiCuCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn, and RP-FeCoNiZnCr exhibited the tetragonal I4/mmm symmetry structure with high-entropy configuration. The RP-FeCoNiCuMn showed remarkably superior OER activity compared to RP-FeCoNiCuCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn and RP-FeCoNiZnCr (Fig. 1i). Besides, the Tafel slope of RP-FeCoNiCuMn (47 mV dec−1) is smaller than that of RP-FeCoNiZnMn (53 mV dec−1), RP-FeCoNiCuCr (55 mV dec−1) and RP-FeCoNiZnCr (63 mV dec−1) (Supplementary Fig. 16). The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results demonstrate that the RP-FeCoNiCuMn shows the smallest charge transfer resistance among these catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 17). Meanwhile, the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was estimated by determining the double layer capacitances (Cdl) of the samples from cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 18)44,45. The RP-FeCoNiCuMn demonstrates the largest Cdl of 16.61 mF cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 19) and ECSA of 277 cm2 (normalized per cm2 of electrode area) (Supplementary Fig. 20) compared to other catalysts. To clarify the inherent OER performance of these studied catalysts, the per-site turnover frequency (TOF) was calculated. Obviously, RP-FeCoNiCuMn achieves the highest TOF values of 0.08 s−1 at an overpotential of 300 mV, which is about 6.7, 2.7, and 2.4 times higher than RP-FeCoNiZnCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn, and RP-FeCoNiCuCr (Supplementary Fig. 21), respectively. It can be observed that the values of ΔSconfig for RP-FeCoNiZnCr (1.60 R), RP-FeCoNiZnMn (1.59 R) and RP-FeCoNiCuCr (1.59 R) and RP-FeCoNiCuMn (1.59 R) are similar. Therefore, the core high-entropy effects in all these samples are supposed to induce a similar degree of enhanced catalytic performances46. However, significant variations in seawater oxidation activities are observed among all these samples. Therefore, it is suspected that other decisive effects might exist in high-entropy systems.

Chemical and spin states of the active sites

The active sites in RP-FeCoNiCuMn were first analyzed by DFT calculations using the (001) facet of RP-FeCoNiCuMn theoretical model. Supplementary Fig. 22 compares the Gibbs free energy diagrams of OER on different transition metal sites in RP‑FeCoNiCuMn. According to the adsorbate evolution mechanism pathway47, the average Gibbs free energies for multiple elements is comprehensively considered in RP-FeCoNiCuMn model (Supplementary Fig. 23). Fe, Co, and Ni sites exhibited lower average energy barriers than Mn and Cu sites for the rate-determining step (*OH to O*), suggesting that Fe, Co, and Ni sites serve as primary active sites during seawater oxidation process. Supplementary Fig. 24 displays the structural evolution of OER intermediates at all active metal sites in RP-FeCoNiCuMn. Supplementary Table 2 summarizes the corresponding Gibbs free energy changes, which reflect the reaction energy barriers at each of these active sites.

While the edge position of X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) spectra offers a preliminary indication, it is often insufficient for accurately determining oxidation states48. Therefore, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) were employed in combination to elucidate the chemical states of the Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Mn active sites. The Fe, Co and Ni, Cu, and Mn 2p XPS displayed that Fe3+, Co3+, Ni2+, Cu2+, and Mn3+/4+ species are present in RP-FeCoNiCuMn (Supplementary Fig. 25). As Fe K-edge XANES displayed in Supplementary Fig. 26a, the absorption edge of Fe in RP-FeCoNiCuMn is situated away from FeO and closer to Fe2O3, demonstrating that the valence of Fe in RP-FeCoNiCuMn is ~ +3. The oxidation state of Fe was further determined to be 2.95 ± 0.06 through linear fitting of the half-edge energy against the oxidation state (Supplementary Fig. 26b). Co and Ni K-edges XANES curves (Supplementary Figs. 27a and 28a) revealed the pre-edge absorption features of Co and Ni species in RP-FeCoNiCuMn resemble those of Co2O3 and NiO references. The fitted Co and Ni oxidation states as a function of K-edge energy shift indicated that the average valence states of Co and Ni in RP-FeCoNiCuMn are 3.04 ± 0.09 and 2.02 ± 0.05 (Supplementary Figs. 27b and 28b), respectively. The Cu K-edge spectra of RP-FeCoNiCuMn are closer to CuO rather than Cu2O or Cu foil (Supplementary Fig. 29a). The fitted Cu oxidation state in Supplementary Fig. 29b indicated the valence state of Cu2+ in RP-FeCoNiCuMn. Besides, the Mn K-edge spectra of RP-FeCoNiCuMn is positioned between MnO2 and Mn2O3, indicating the existence of both Mn3+ and Mn4+ in RP-FeCoNiCuMn (Supplementary Fig. 30a). The fitted Mn oxidation state in Supplementary Fig. 30b indicated the average oxidation state of Mn element was determined to be 3.42 ± 0.10. The convergent evidence with the consistent trends observed in both XPS and XANES should be able to provide a reliable assessment of the elemental valences. Based on the above analysis, in RP-FeCoNiCuMn, Cu exists in the +2 oxidation state with an electronic configuration of t2g6eg3, while Mn is primarily in the +3 oxidation state with an electronic configuration of t2g3eg1. Within the high-symmetry octahedral coordination environment, both ions exhibit asymmetric occupation of the eg orbitals (with 3 electrons in the eg orbitals of Cu2+ and 1 electron in the eg orbitals of Mn3+). This electronic configuration induces spontaneous Jahn-Teller distortion, resulting in ligand elongation along the z-axis for both Cu and Mn centers49. Therefore, it is speculated that the significant enhancement of electrocatalytic performance in RP-FeCoNiCuMn may be attributed to the introduced Cu2+ and Mn3+ species with the J-T effect.

RP-FeCoNiCuCr and RP-FeCoNiZnMn, each incorporating one J-T dopant (Cu2+ or Mn3+), along with RP-FeCoNiZnCr without a J-T dopant, were designed for the purpose of validating the analysis. XPS results show Cu2+ and Mn3+ species were introduced into RP-FeCoNiCuCr and RP-FeCoNiZnMn, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 31a and 32a). Besides, the introduction of Cr3+/6+ (t2g3eg0/t2g0eg0) in RP-FeCoNiCuCr and Zn2+ (t2g6eg4) in RP-FeCoNiZnMn, with d orbits half-filled, unoccupied, or fully occupied, showed no J-T effect (Supplementary Figs. 31b and 32b). In Zn and Cr 2p XPS spectra of RP-FeCoNiZnCr (Supplementary Fig. 33), the Zn2+ and Cr3+/6+ could also be observed, indicating that no J-T dopant was introduced into RP-FeCoNiZnCr. The concentration of J-T elements was further qualitatively analyzed based on the ICP and XPS fitting results, with J-T element contents decreasing in the order of RP-FeCoNiCuMn (11.84 at%), RP-FeCoNiCuCr (7.52 at%), RP-FeCoNiZnMn (5.84 at%), and RP-FeCoNiZnCr (0 at%) (Supplementary Table 3). Based on previous reports50, introducing J-T dopants is generally accompanied by lattice spacing expansion. The Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns (Supplementary Table 4) shows that RP-FeCoNiCuMn, RP-FeCoNiCuCr, and RP-FeCoNiCuMn give rise to more severe lattice expansion than RP-FeCoNiZnCr, further demonstrating that the J-T dopants are incorporated into lattice. These results collectively confirm the successful design and synthesis of these samples. The local coordination environment of Fe sites was further examined using hard X-ray absorption spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 34). Fitting analysis of the Fe–O coordination shells revealed that RP-FeCoNiCuMn exhibits the longest average bond lengths, surpassing those in RP-FeCoNiCuCr and RP-FeCoNiZnMn (Supplementary Fig. 35 and Supplementary Table 5). Moreover, RP-FeCoNiCuMn shows the largest Debye–Waller factor (σ2) in the three high-entropy systems, indicating the strongest local structural disorder and distortion around the Fe sites51. These results provide direct structural evidence that RP-FeCoNiCuMn with two J–T dopants induces stronger local lattice distortion than the RP-FeCoNiCuCr and RP-FeCoNiZnMn with one J-T dopant. To validate this structural distortion at the atomic scale, we extracted the bond lengths and bond angles for the Fe, Co, and Ni sites in RP-FeCoNi and RP-FeCoNiCuMn based on DFT optimized models (Supplementary Figs. 36, 37 and Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). The local structural distortion was quantified by calculating the octahedral bond-length distortion parameter (Δ1) and bond-angle distortion parameter (Δ2). The results show that both Δ1 and Δ2 for the Fe, Co, and Ni sites in RP-FeCoNiCuMn are significantly higher than those in RP-FeCoNi (Supplementary Table 8), providing direct evidence at the atomic scale that the introduction of Cu2+/Mn3+ enhances the local octahedral distortion at the active sites51,52.

XPS and XANES were conducted to examine the impact of J-T dopants on the chemical and spin states of active sites. XPS reveals that the binding energy of Fe, Co, and Ni 2p for high-entropy controls and RP-FeCoNi are similar to RP-FeCoNiCuMn (Supplementary Fig. 38), demonstrating the unchanged valences of Fe, Co, and Ni ions. Furthermore, Fe, Co, and Ni L-edges of XANES were further conducted to investigate the spin configuration for all these samples. The multiplet spectral feature of near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (NEXAFS) spectra at the L2,3 edge of 3d transition metal is sensitive to occupancy rate of eg orbitals53. L-edge spectra are predominantly shaped by the Fe/Co 2p → 3 d transition and divided into two segments due to the spin-orbital coupling (Fig. 2a, b). The L3-edge significantly relies on the hybridization between Fe/Co 3d orbitals and ligand O 2p orbitals, and the normalized intensity of the L3-edge peak offers insights into spin states54. The Fe and Co L3-edge peak intensity of RP-FeCoNiCuMn are very close to that in the high-spin α-Fe2O3 and S-CoOOH references (Supplementary Fig. 39)24,55, demonstrating the high-spin state of Fe3+ and Co3+ in RP-FeCoNiCuMn. The lower Fe L3-edge peak intensity of RP-FeCoNiCuMn than RP-FeCoNiCuCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn, RP-FeCoNiZnCr, and RP-FeCoNi indicates that there are more unpaired electrons in eg orbitals (Fig. 2a), suggesting the higher spin state of RP-FeCoNiCuMn than other samples. Similarly, the peak intensity of Co L3-edge spectra gradually weakens in the order of RP-FeCoNi, RP-FeCoNiZnCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn, RP-FeCoNiCuCr, and RP-FeCoNiCuMn (Fig. 2b), indicating that the spin state of Co also rises successively. Theoretical modeling and calculations also identify the spin state of Co3+ and Fe3+ in RP-FeCoNiCuMn and RP-FeCoNi. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 40, the partial density of states (PDOS) for Co and Fe in RP-FeCoNiCuMn shows a more delocalized distribution compared to RP-FeCoNi. It is worth noting that the calculated LS state of RP-FeCoNi is 1.73 eV more stable than its HS state, whereas the HS state is 0.64 eV more stable than the LS state for RP-FeCoNiCuMn. It showed that the spin state of Fe and Co in RP-FeCoNiCuMn is higher than that in RP-FeCoNi56. Besides, following the analysis strategy by Kan et al.57, the energy level of all the d orbitals in one Fe and Co atom for RP-FeCoNiCuMn can be identified in Fig. 2c, d, respectively. Taking the Fe site as an example, the results show that five d orbitals prefer to be filled with one electron, which is consistent with experimental results. No significant changes in the intensity of the Ni L3 edge peak (Supplementary Fig. 41) were observed, indicating the unchanged spin states of Ni2+ ions in these samples. Distinct from Fe3+/Co3+, Ni2+ exhibits a unique 3d8 electronic configuration (t2g6eg2) with fully occupied t2g and half-filled eg orbitals. This symmetric electron occupation eliminates orbital degeneracy and inhibits Jahn-Teller-driven split energy minimization. Therefore, the spin state of Ni2+ is unaffected by incorporating J-T elements49. Furthermore, the simulated XANES spectra at RP-FeCoNiCuMn K-edge exhibit reasonable agreement with the corresponding experimental spectra (Supplementary Fig. 42a–c). The models used in the Finite Difference Method for Near Edge Structure (FDMNES) calculations are presented in Supplementary Fig. 42d. This agreement suggests that our computational model provides a plausible representation of the local coordination and electronic structure at the metal sites, thereby supporting the reliability of the theoretical analysis in determining the spin states. Notably, the pre-edge intensities in the theoretical spectra of RP-FeCoNi are slightly lower than those of RP-FeCoNiCuMn (Supplementary Fig. 43). The correlation between spin state and catalytic activity was explored by plotting the catalytic metrics against the spin population (Supplementary Fig. 45). Increased spin population leads to decreased overpotential and Tafel slope, while enhancing the turnover frequency. This consistent behavior across multiple catalytic parameters suggests a strong and positive correlation between spin population and seawater oxidation activity59. To obtain an accurate and quantitative determination of the unpaired electron count of the B-site transition metal elements, we rigorously evaluated all possible electronic configurations. The high-spin configuration exhibits excellent agreement with the M–T curve calculations, thereby providing strong evidence for the high-spin electronic state of the active sites. Detailed computational results are provided in the “Methods” part.

Based on the above phenomenon, a possible mechanism for modulating the spin state of active sites in high-entropy RP perovskite oxides could be proposed (Fig. 2g). Introducing J-T Cu2+ and Mn3+ may cause structural distortion around the active atoms and induce severe volumetric stretch49,60. According to crystal field theory, the interaction between the positively charged Fe3+ and Co3+ cations and the negatively charged non-bonding electrons of the oxygen ligand results in the splitting of the d-orbitals energy levels61. Large volumetric stretch would induce the expansion of [MO6] octahedra, thus the repulsion between the electrons in the d orbitals of Fe3+ and Co3+ and the non-bonding electrons in the oxygen ligands becomes weakened62. As a result, the energy gap (Δ0) between the higher-energy eg and lower-energy t2g orbitals narrows as the metal-ligand interaction weakens due to reduced oxygen ligand strength44. Because of the lower d-orbital split energy, the electrons in the d-orbital of Fe3+ and Co3+ atoms will rearrange to occupy the eg and t2g orbitals separately and then pair up, which further leads to the spin state transitions of Fe3+ and Co3+ from LS to HS62.

To explore the underlying reasons for the improved OER activity in seawater electrolysis, in situ Raman spectroscopy was performed during an OER test within the voltage range from 1.1 to 1.8 V versus RHE. The in situ Raman test setup is shown in Supplementary Fig. 46. The peak near 1086 cm−1 is linked to the M-OH bending mode, arising from the adsorption of *OH intermediates on the metallic active centers63. The Raman peaks at approximately 488 cm−1 and 686 cm−1 are assigned as two A1g modes corresponding to the stretching vibration of A-site and B-site atoms along the z-direction64, respectively. Besides, the Raman band at ~ 608 cm−1 corresponds to the Eg symmetry of octahedral sites [BO6] in RP perovskite oxides64. Since the transition metal sites are the primary electroactive sites in the perovskite structure65, the ratio of the M-OH peak area to the Eg peak area can serve as an indicator to track dynamic changes. DFT calculation was also employed to unveil the enigma of how HS sites in RP-FeCoNiCuMn facilitate both *OH adsorption and deprotonation. The four-electron transfer of RP-FeCoNiCuMn and RP-FeCoNi during OER was simulated. Taking Fe sites as an example, the RP-FeCoNiCuMn exhibited a lower energy barrier for *OH adsorption (−0.34 eV vs. −0.27 eV) and deprotonation (0.33 eV vs. 0.61 eV) than that in RP-FeCoNi, with the optimized pathways shown in Supplementary Fig. 47. Similar trends are also observed at the Co sites (Supplementary Fig. 48), indicating HS Fe3+/Co3+ in RP-FeCoNiCuMn exhibited better kinetics for *OH adsorption and deprotonation behavior.

To gain deeper insight into the role of HS sites in improving anode stability, the adsorption of OH− and Cl− anions on the electrode surface during the reaction process was investigated. The potentiometric titrator and ion chromatography were applied to test the amount of adhered Cl− and OH− ions at a constant 1.7 V vs. RHE, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 49)67. The amount of adsorbed and consumed OH− increased in the order of RP-FeCoNi, RP-FeCoNiCuCr, and RP-FeCoNiCuMn. Compared to RP-FeCoNi and RP-FeCoNiCuCr anode, the amount of the adsorbed Cl− decreased by 43% and 36% on the RP-FeCoNiCuMn anode, respectively. The substantial reduction in the concentration of adsorbed Cl− may suppress the corrosion processes. Additionally, ion chromatography was conducted at 1.91 V vs. RHE, a potential beyond the equilibrium for CER. Compared to RP-FeCoNi and RP-FeCoNiCuCr anode, the amount of the adsorbed Cl− decreased by 48% and 37% on the RP-FeCoNiCuMn anode, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 50). In addition, the Rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) technique was employed to investigate the relative contributions of OER and CER for the above catalyst under simulated seawater conditions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 51, RP-FeCoNiCuMn exhibited the lowest ring current density, indicating the minimal participation of CER during the reaction. Based on the RRDE measurements, the OER selectivity of RP-FeCoNiCuMn reached 93%, significantly higher than that of RP-FeCoNiCuCr (78%) and RP-FeCoNi (66%) (Fig. 3e), which further experimentally confirmed that Cl− adsorption is effectively suppressed. The classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were subsequently employed to simulate the distribution of OH− and Cl− anions near the electrode surface (Supplementary Data 1). The applied electric field drives the different anions towards the anode surface. For RP-FeCoNiCuMn slabs, the OH− anions tended to adsorb to the surface and led to more local concentrations of OH− in the region of inner Helmholtz plane (0−3 Å) (Fig. 3f)22. As a result, due to the electrostatic repulsion, the adsorbed OH− pushes Cl− anions moved toward the electrode surface. It is obvious that Cl− is difficult to approach within 15 Å of the RP-FeCoNiCuMn surface. In contrast, the amount of the adhered OH− is low in the RP-FeCoNi surface, as shown in Fig. 3g. As a result, there are more Cl− anions in the region of outer Helmholtz plane (3−10 Å) and the diffuse layer (10−30 Å) than on the surface of RP-FeCoNiCuMn surface22. It can be explained that the repulsive effect of minimal OH− outside the RP-FeCoNi surface is not as significant as that on the RP-FeCoNiCuMn surface.

Furthermore, ex-situ Fe, Co, and Ni L-edges NEXAFS spectra for RP-FeCoNiCuMn were measured after OER chronoamperometry tests at 200 mA cm−2 for 10 h and freeze-quenched at 1.21, 1.53, and 1.72 V versus RHE (Fig. 3h, i and Supplementary Fig. 52). The peak positions and intensities in Fe, Co, and Ni L-edge spectra of RP-FeCoNiCuMn showed negligible variations within the permissible error range of the NEXAFS technique. These observations demonstrated the spin structural integrity of HS Co3+ and Fe3+ sites at different voltages, demonstrating that HS active sites are almost not affected by Cl− in RP-FeCoNiCuMn.

Further analysis was conducted on the orbital interactions between active sites and oxygen intermediates, as well as their rejection capability towards Cl− ions68 (Fig. 3j). According to the principle of symmetry conservation69, the dxy and dx2-y2 orbitals of Co3+ and Fe3+ can be omitted due to their negligible interactions with the orbitals of the oxygen-containing intermediates. As the OER process proceeds, the O 2p orbitals of OH*/O* head-on-overlap with the eg orbitals of Co3+/Fe3+ to form σ bonds and σ* antibonds, while side-to-side overlap with the t2g orbitals of Co3+/Fe3+ to form π bonds and π* antibonds22. Less antibonding electrons in HS system enhance the orbital interaction between oxygen intermediates and HS Co3+/Fe3+ 3d orbitals. The bond order of intermediates bound to active sites can also be used to evaluate adsorption strength69, which is defined as half the difference between bonding and antibonding electrons. The bond order between HS Co3+/Fe3+ and adsorbed *OH is consistently 2.0, which is larger than the bond order of 1.5 between LS Co3+/Fe3+ and *OH. This finding suggests that HS Co3+/Fe3+ is beneficial to enrich the *OH50. Besides, *OH is bound to HS Co3+/Fe3+ to form more unpaired electrons, resulting in increased electron cloud density and enhanced repulsion against Cl− ions70. Meanwhile, it is worth noting that the bond orders between HS Co3+/Fe3+ and *O are consistently 0.5 larger than those of LS Co3+/Fe3+ and *O, and HS system also shows more unpaired electrons than LS system. It demonstrates that HS Co3+/Fe3+ also has stronger deprotonation and *O adsorption capacity than LS Co3+/Fe3+47, as well as improved rejection capability towards Cl− ions.

Based on experimental evidence and theoretical analysis presented above, a possible mechanism for the role of HS active sites in promoting efficient and stable seawater oxidation could be proposed (Fig. 3k). The HS Co3+/Fe3+ in RP-FeCoNiCuMn can promote seawater oxidation activity via facilitating both OH− adsorption and the deprotonation process. Simultaneously, the preferential adsorption of oxygen intermediates on HS Co3+/Fe3+ results in enhanced repulsion against Cl− ions, thereby suppressing the chlorine corrosion and enhancing the catalytic stability in alkaline seawater22.

Universality of the spin-state regulation in high-entropy catalysts

The spin-state regulation strategy can be extended to other representative high-entropy perovskite oxide and even high-entropy spinel oxide systems. Taking perovskite oxides with a single cubic phase symmetry (Pm-3m) as an example, high-entropy (denoted as P-FeCoNiCuMn) and ternary (denoted as P-FeCoNi) perovskite oxides with ideal cubic symmetry were synthesized (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 53 and 54), respectively. No significant changes in the shift of the Fe, Co, and Ni 2p XPS binding peak (Supplementary Fig. 55) demonstrated the negligible variations of valence states. As shown in Fig. 4b, c, the Fe and Co L-edge peak intensities in P-FeCoNiCuMn showed decreased trends compared to P-FeCoNi. The lower peak intensities account for the observed higher spin configurations of Fe and Co in P-FeCoNiCuMn. The intensity of Ni L-edge peak in Supplementary Fig. 56 remained unchanged. As expected, when applied as electrocatalysts for seawater oxidation, P-FeCoNiCuMn displayed obviously enhanced activities, outperforming the P-FeCoNi (Fig. 4d). The more positive corrosion potential, combined with the lower corrosion current density observed for P-FeCoNiCuMn compared to P-FeCoNi, indicates its improved corrosion resistance (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 9). Besides, high-entropy (denoted as S-FeCoNiCuMn) and ternary (denoted as S-FeCoNi) spinel oxides have been prepared (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Figs. 57 and 58). Performance of the AEM seawater electrolyzer

To evaluate the practical application potential of RP-FeCoNiCuMn catalyst for overall seawater splitting, an AEM seawater electrolyzer device was designed (Fig. 5a). The AEM seawater electrolyzer had an effective electrochemical area of 1 cm2, with RP-FeCoNiCuMn as the anode and commercial Pt/C as the cathode (Fig. 5b). Figure 5c shows the polarization curves (without iR compensation) of the AEM seawater electrolyzer setup operated at 60 °C, flowing alkaline seawater. The RP-FeCoNiCuMn| |Pt/C setup required 1.67 V and 1.76 V to deliver 0.5 A cm−2 and 1.0 A cm−2, respectively. The RP-FeCoNiCuMn| |Pt/C pair achieved a current density of up to 2.4 A cm−2 at 1.90 V, which is ~2.4 times higher than RuO2| |Pt/C couple (1.0 A cm−2) at the same voltage. At a current density of 200 mA cm−2 and 60 °C, the RuO2| |Pt/C seawater electrolyzer exhibited more significant performance degradation than RP-FeCoNiCuMn| |Pt/C with only 20 h of cycling (Fig. 5d). Besides, the RP-FeCoNiCuMn| |Pt/C seawater electrolyzer could incessantly operate for more than 800 h at 1 A cm−2 (Fig. 5e). A slight performance degradation in the electrolyzer can be observed after approximately 500 h. Considering the preserved structure of RP-FeCoNiCuMn anode after a test of 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 61), the slightly degraded performance of the seawater electrolyzer may be induced by the combined effects of dissolution of Pt/C catalysts and instability of the anion exchange membrane under seawater conditions (Supplementary Figs. 62 and 63). The high concentration of chloride ions promotes the electrochemical dissolution of the Pt/C cathode through the formation of soluble chloroplatinate complexes71. Oxidants (e.g., Cl2, ClO−) generated at the anode can migrate to the membrane interface, leading to oxidative degradation of the polymer backbone in the anion exchange membrane72. Besides, the degraded interface between the electrode and the current collector and the failure of sealing and isolation integrity might also cause performance degradation in seawater electrolyzer73. Besides, the RP-FeCoNiCuMn| |Pt/C couple seawater electrolyzer exhibited a Faradaic efficiency of ~95% for the production of both H2 and O2 at 1 A cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 64), demonstrating that almost no chlorine by-products are produced. When replacing commercial RuO2 with RP-FeCoNiCuMn as the anode, the electricity expense of H2 production decreased from 4.47 kWh Nm−3 H2 to 4.06 kWh Nm−3 H2 at 500 mA cm−2 and from 4.83 kWh Nm−3 H2 to 4.18 kWh Nm−3 H2 at 1000 mA cm−2 (Fig. 5f), which means using RP-FeCoNiCuMn anode decreases the electricity expense by 10.1% and 15.5% to the commercial RuO2, respectively. A radar chart was shown to comprehensively evaluate the performance parameters of RP-FeCoNiCuMn| |Pt/C and RuO2| |Pt/C electrolyzers (Fig. 5g), including peak current density, stability time, electricity expense at 1 A cm−2, current density at 1.8 V vs. RHE and current density of stability test. The RP-FeCoNiCuMn| |Pt/C electrolyzer outperformed RuO2| |Pt/C in these key performance parameters. Discussion

In summary, we reported the spin state regulation of high-entropy Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) phase perovskite oxides (RP-FeCoNiCuMn) for efficient and durable seawater oxidation. The Cu2+ and Mn3+ with J-T effects can induce the spin state transition of Co3+/Fe3+ sites from LS to HS, resulting in promoted OH− adsorption and deprotonation during the seawater oxidation reactions. The preferential adsorption of oxygen intermediates on HS Co3+/Fe3+ active sites could repel the Cl− ions in alkaline seawater to improve stability. Consequently, the as-synthesized RP-FeCoNiCuMn required an overpotential of 320 mV at 100 mA cm−2 and can operate for more than 1200 h at 200 mA cm−2 without obvious performance decay, outperforming the high-entropy counterparts, the representative alkaline seawater catalyst NiFe LDH, and a range of other reported oxide-based catalysts. When paired with Pt/C cathode in AEM electrolyzer, the RP-FeCoNiCuMn anode could maintain the seawater splitting activity of 1 A cm−2 at 1.76 V for 800 h. The spin-state regulation approach is applicable to other representative high-entropy perovskite oxide and even high-entropy spinel oxide systems. Our findings provide a spin engineering strategy to fabricate high-entropy electrocatalysts with high activity and stability.

Methods

Chemicals

Cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O) and Nafion solution (5 wt%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Manganese (II) nitrate tetrahydrate (Mn(NO3)2·4H2O) and RuO2 were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Zinc (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O), Iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O), Nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O), Copper (II) nitrate (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O), Lanthanum (III) nitrate (La(NO3)3·6H2O), Strontium (II) acetate (Sr(CH3COO)2·1/2H2O), and Chromic (III) nitrate (Cr(NO3)3·9H2O) were obtained from Xilong Scientific (Guangdong, China). Ethanol (C2H6O) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Commercial Pt/C (20 wt%) was purchased from Johnson Matthey Corporation (Shanghai, China). All the chemicals were used as received without further purification. The ultra-pure water (18 MΩ cm−1, Aqua Solutions) was used in all experiments.

Preparation of RP phase perovskite oxides

Taking RP-FeCoNiCuMn as an example, stoichiometric quantities of La(NO3)3·6H2O (2.25 mmol), Sr(CH3COO)2·1/2H2O (0.75 mmol), Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (0.40 mmol), Co(NO3)2·6H2O (0.40 mmol), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (0.40 mmol), Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (0.40 mmol), and Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (0.40 mmol) were mixed into 50 mL of water with continuous stirring. Then, a blend of C6H8O7·H2O (5 mmol) and EDTA (5 mmol) served as the chelating agent and was introduced into the solution. Subsequently, the pH was set to 8 using NH4OH. The mixture was heated while stirring to remove water, forming a thick gel. This gel was then calcined at 300 °C for 3 h to eliminate organic and nitrate residues. Finally, the obtained precursor powders were annealed in ambient atmosphere air at 1200 °C for 10 h to obtain the RP-FeCoNiCuMn bulks. Other RP phase perovskite oxides, including RP-FeCoNiCuCr, RP-FeCoNiZnMn, RP-FeCoNiZnCr, and RP-FeCoNi, were synthesized using a similar protocol for comparison.

Preparation of NiFe-LDH

Stoichiometric amounts of NiCl2·6H2O (5 mmol) and FeCl3·6H2O (1.9 mmol) were dissolved in 17 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring for 30 min. A homogeneous solution was prepared separately by dissolving Na2CO3 (13 mmol) in 13 mL of deionized water, which was then rapidly poured into the above metal precursor solution. The resulting mixture was stirred for an additional 30 min and subsequently transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave, maintained at 100 °C for 20 h, and allowed to cool naturally to room temperature (25 °C). The obtained precipitate was washed repeatedly with deionized water, collected by centrifugation, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h to yield NiFe-LDH.

Preparation of P-FeCoNiCuMn and P-FeCoNi

Stoichiometric quantities of La(NO3)3·6H2O (3.0 mmol), Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (0.60 mmol), Co(NO3)2·6H2O (0.60 mmol), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (0.60 mmol), Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (0.60 mmol), and Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (0.60 mmol) were added in amounts to 50 mL of water and stirred thoroughly. After dissolution, a blend of C6H8O7·H2O (6 mmol) and EDTA (6 mmol) was added as a chelating agent to the solution. Subsequently, the pH was then set to 8 using NH4OH. The mixture was heated with continuous stirring to evaporate water, forming a thick gel. This gel was then calcined at 300 °C for 3 h to eliminate organic and nitrate residues. Finally, the obtained precursor powders were annealed in ambient atmosphere air at 800 °C for 12 h to obtain the P-FeCoNiCuMn bulks. Ternary P-FeCoNi perovskite oxides were synthesized using the same protocol for comparison.

Preparation of S-FeCoNiCuMn and S-FeCoNi

Stoichiometric quantities of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (0.30 mmol), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (0.30 mmol), Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (0.40 mmol), Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (0.40 mmol), and Co(NO3)2·6H2O (0.40 mmol) were dissolved in 140 mL aqueous solution. A stable homogeneous emulsion was obtained after adjusting pH to 10 with NaOH solution. The mixture was kept at 180 °C for 24 h in a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. The resulting precursor was calcined at 700 °C for 5 h with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 in air to produce S-FeCoNiCuMn. Ternary S-FeCoNi spinel oxides were synthesized using the same protocol for comparison.

Polarization measurements

The corrosion behaviors of RP-FeCoNiCuMn and RP-FeCoNi in seawater were recorded by Tafel Plots. Before conducting the Tafel plot test, the stability of the corrosion potential was determined by measuring the open circuit potential (OCP) of the electrode after being exposed to the electrolyte for several hours. Tafel plots were measured between −0.6 V and −0.1 V vs. RHE at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1. The corrosion potential was derived from Tafel plots. The corrosion potential is the point at which the cathodic reaction rate matches the rate of anodic dissolution, causing zero current flow in the electrode. The corrosion potential was identified as the intersection of the cathode and anode tangent lines.

Ion chromatography and potentiometric titrator measurements

To test the amount of Cl− and OH− taken from the electrolytes, the solutions before and after the reaction were analyzed by ion chromatography (for Cl−) and a potentiometric titrator (for OH−). Electrolytes (10 mL, 5 mM KOH + 2.5 mM NaCl) were chosen in the experiments. To avoid ion exchange from the reference electrode, the system was used as a two-electrode system (RP-FeCoNiCuMn or RP-FeCoNi served as anode electrodes, and Pt foil served as cathode electrode). Considering the equilibrium CER potential is >1.71 V vs. RHE, we ran the system at a constant voltage (1.7 V) for 10 min and took it out from the solution carefully. The solutions before and after the test were quantified by ion chromatography and a potentiometric titrator, respectively.

Raman spectroscopy measurements

A 532 nm diode laser (250 mW) was used as the excitation source for all Raman measurements75. To avoid damaging the sample, the power of the incident laser was reduced by a factor of 10. In situ Raman experiments were conducted in a home-built open spectro-electrochemical cell, using an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference and a platinum foil as the counter electrode. The acquisition time for each Raman spectrum ranged from 30 to 60 s, with 1 to 2 accumulations per measurement. To ensure reproducibility and reliability, Raman spectra were collected from at least three different locations on the electrode surface. The working electrode was prepared by ultrasonically dispersing 2 mg of electrocatalyst and 20 µL of 5 wt% Nafion solution in 500 µL of a water/isopropyl alcohol mixture (4:1, v/v). Then, 10 µL of the resulting homogeneous ink was drop-cast onto a glassy carbon electrode.

Material characterization

High-resolution STEM imaging was performed on a Titan Cubed Themis G2 300 transmission electron microscope. XRD characterization was carried out using a RigakuD/MAX-2500 diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). XPS measurements were conducted with a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha+ spectrometer. EIS tests were recorded on a Bioanalytical electrochemical workstation, covering a frequency range from 100 kHz down to 0.01 Hz. XAS measurements were acquired in fluorescence mode with a Lytle detector. Post-processing and data analysis of XANES and EXAFS spectra were completed using Athena software. In situ Raman spectroscopic analysis was performed on an inVia Raman microscope, with spectral data processed via LabSpec software.

Flow-type AEM seawater electrolyzer

A custom-built AEM seawater electrolyzer was used to evaluate the catalytic performance. The anode was fabricated by spraying the catalyst ink solution onto stainless-steel fiber felt gas diffusion layer (SS GDL). The catalyst loading was 1.0 mg cm−2 for the anode, and 0.5 mg cm−2 for the cathode. For the AEM seawater electrolyzer, commercial anion-exchange membranes (Sustainion X37-50, thickness: 50 μm) were employed. Each membrane was pre-treated with 1.0 M KOH for 48 h, and then treated with fresh KOH for 24 h. Next, the membrane was washed with DI water until the pH of the water reached neutral. Prior to initiating cell tests, the fabricated electrodes and anion exchange membrane were subjected to a preliminary 1-h treatment in alkaline marine water. Subsequently, a membrane electrode assembly was assembled by placing the AEM between the cathode and anode, with both catalyst layers facing the membrane. The AEM seawater electrolyzer cell was operated at 60 °C using alkaline seawater solution as an electrolyte, which was fed separately to the anode and cathode sides by a peristaltic pump at a constant flow rate of 2 mL min−1. Prior to conducting seawater electrolysis measurements, the assembled membrane electrode was activated by applying a potentiostatic method at a constant voltage of 2.0 V, continuing until the polarization curves stabilized. Subsequently, the polarization data were captured within the voltage range of 1.3 to 2.0 V, with a scanning rate of 10 mV per second. Chronopotentiometry was applied with 1 A cm−2 to maintain constant gas generation. Hydrogen and oxygen gases generated in the cathode and anode chambers, respectively, were collected using a standard drainage method. It’s worth noting that all generated Cl2 will dissolve in solution and thus not contribute to the gas volume. The volume of water extruded by oxygen and hydrogen was recorded over the initial 10 h period.

Theoretical calculations

Based on the typical RP-phase perovskite oxides unit cell of Sr3Fe2O7, initial structures for RP-FeCoNiCuMn and RP-FeCoNi were constructed using the Special Quasi-random Structure (SQS) method via ATAT76,77. For RP-FeCoNiCuMn, a 2 × 5 × 1 supercell was built, on the Sr sublattice, Sr and La were disorderedly occupied (1: 3), while on the Fe sublattice, Fe, Co, Ni, Mn, and Cu were mixed equimolarly. Using Monte Carlo iterations, the correlation functions of 75 binary and ternary clusters (interatomic distance ≤4 Å) were optimized to approximate an ideal completely disordered system (satisfying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bar{\varPi }}_{k,m}(R)={\left\langle {\bar{\varPi }}_{k,m}\right\rangle }_{R}={(2x-1)}^{k}$$\end{document}Π¯k,m(R)=Π¯k,mR=(2x−1)k). Similarly, for RP-FeCoNi, a 2 × 3 × 1 supercell was constructed with Sr: La = 1: 3 on the Sr sublattice and Fe: Co: Ni = 1: 1: 1 on the Fe sublattice, where 30 selected clusters were optimized to satisfy the disordered criteria. The metal sites of both structures are distributed in two independent sublattices: one is the Sr/La sublattice, where Sr and La atoms fill the original Sr lattice points irregularly, with a fixed quantity ratio of 1: 3; the other is the Fe-based multi-metal sublattice, for RP-FeCoNiCuMn, Fe, Co, Ni, Mn, and Cu atoms (each accounting for 20%) occupy the original Fe lattice points disorderly in equal proportions, while for RP-FeCoNi, Fe, Co, and Ni atoms (each accounting for ~33.3%) are randomly distributed at the original Fe lattice points, with no obvious segregation or ordered arrangement in either sublattice.

All calculations were performed utilizing density functional theory (DFT) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) variant of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) functional78. The Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) was employed79. The threshold for plane wave expansion energy was fixed at 400 eV, and the energy (converged to 1e−5 eV/atom) and force (converged to −2e−2 eV/Å) were set as the convergence criteria for geometry optimization. The DFT + U approach was used, in which U is a Hubbard-like term describing the on-site Coulombic interactions80. For Co and Fe, a value of U = 4.0 eV was adopted. For the calculations of bulk models, the Brillouin zones were sampled by the original Monkhorst-Pack scheme with a grid of 2 × 4 × 281, and finer k-points grids of 4 × 8 × 4 were employed for the corresponding density of state (DOS) calculations to attain high accuracy. In addition, the DFT-D3 method was included to improve the description of the long-range weak van der Waals (vdW) interaction for all DFT calculations82.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The (001) sections of RP-FeCoNiCuMn and RP-FeCoNi were placed on the bottom of the box and fixed as the model of electrode surfaces, with a thickness of 8 Å. Fe, Co, Ni, Mn, Cu, and Fe Co Ni were exposed to the surfaces, respectively. Due to there is no potential for La and Sr within the transition metal oxide system, and considering that they do not play significant role in the simulation, La and Sr were substituted with Ba in the electrode model to ensure a more accurate fitting of the force field. To simulate the electrolyte in the experiment (1.0 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl), the solution model contains 600 H2O molecule, 12 Na+, 12 Cl−, 24 K+ and 24 OH−, which were placed on the electrode surface. A vacuum layer with a thickness of about 50 Å was added above the solution to exclude interactions between the neighboring images. The size of the model box containing RP-FeCoNiCuMn is about 15 Å × 19 Å × 128 Å, and the one containing RP-FeCoNi is about 22 Å × 15 Å × 128 Å. The interatomic potential parameters for the substrate were taken from the consistent valence force field (CVFF). The cross terms of the potentials were calculated by the Lorentz-Berthelot mixing rule. The interatomic interactions of substrate with H2O and various ions were described by Coulomb and Lennard-Jones potentials, and the corresponding parameters of Na+, Cl−, K+ and OH− were developed by C. Vega and Othonas A. Moultos83,84, as listed in Supplementary Table 13. The interaction between H2O molecules was described by TIP4P/2005 model85. The cutoffs of Coulomb and Lennard-Jones interactions were both 10.0 Å, and the time step was 1 fs. Dynamics simulations were performed for 500 ps for equilibration and 1 ns for statistical analysis under NVT ensemble at 300 K. All MD simulations were conducted using the large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator (LAMMPS)86, with the configurations subsequently rendered for visualization with the OVITO software87.

Theoretical XANES calculations

X-ray Absorption Near-edge Spectroscopy (XANES) spectra at the Fe, Co, and Ni K-edges were calculated using the FDMNES code based on the DFT-optimized structure of RP-FeCoNiCuMn. Based on the tetragonal crystal structure (space group I4/mmm, model size: a = 19.102 Å, b = 7.654 Å, c = 19.929 Å), atomic clusters centered on Fe, Co, Ni, and other constituent atoms were constructed. A cutoff radius of 6.5 Å was adopted for the calculations. The theoretical XANES spectra were calculated using the Green’s function method, incorporating quadrupole transitions. The energy range was set from –979 eV to 1021 eV relative to the absorption edge, with a step size of 2 eV. To enable direct comparison with the experimental spectra, the calculated spectra were broadened via convolution with the following optimized parameters: core-hole lifetime broadening (Gammahole) = 1.4 eV for Fe, Co, and Ni; energy-dependent broadening coefficient (Elarg) = 1.5 eV; maximum broadening (Gammamax) = 4.0 eV; cutoff energy (Ecut) = 1.0 eV; and center energy (Ecent) = 18.1 eV relative to the absorption edge. For each element, theoretical XANES spectra were calculated for multiple inequivalent sites within the supercell, and the reported spectra represent an average over these sites.
