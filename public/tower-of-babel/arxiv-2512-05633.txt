Hereditarily Structurally Complete Superintuitionistic Logics and Primitive Varieties of Heyting Algebras Journal: Annals of pure and Applied Logic
Alex Citkin

Abstract
We give an algebraic proof of the criterion for hereditary structural completeness of an intermediate logic, or, equivalently, of the primitiveness of a variety of Heyting algebras.

Introduction.

By a
logic
we understand a set of propositional formulas built in a regular way from propositional variables and the connectives
\land,\lor,\to
, and
\neg
, and which is closed under the rules of Modus Ponens and simultaneous substitution. Logic
\mathsf{L}
is called
superintuitionistic
(or
si-logic
, for short) if it contains all the theorems of Intuitionistic Propositional Logic
\mathsf{Int}
(see, e.g.,
[
19
]
).

Heyting (or pseudo-Boolean
[
26
]
) algebras are usually used as the algebraic semantics for si-logics. A Heyting algebra is an algebra
{\textbf{A}}=\langle A;\land,\lor,\to,\neg\rangle
, where
\langle A;\land,\lor\rangle
is a distributive lattice and
\to
and
\neg
are the respective relative pseudocomplement and pseudocomplement. Every Heyting algebra contains a top element, denoted by
\mathbf{1}
, and a formula
\alpha
is considered to be
valid
in
A
if
\nu(\alpha)=\mathbf{1}
for any valuation
\nu
in
A
. A Heyting algebra
A
is called a
model
of an si-logic
\mathsf{L}
if every formula from
\mathsf{L}
is valid in
A
.

A (finitary structural inference)
rule
is an ordered pair
(A;\alpha)
(also written as
A/\alpha
), where the first component
A
is a finite (possibly empty) set of formulas, and the second component
\alpha
is a formula. Given a logic
\mathsf{L}
, a rule
A/\alpha
is said to be
admissible in

\mathsf{L}
if for every substitution
\sigma
such that
\sigma(\alpha)\notin\mathsf{L}
, there exists a formula
\beta\in A
with
\sigma(\beta)\notin\mathsf{L}
. For example, the rule

\displaystyle\neg p\to(q\lor r)/((\neg p\to q)\lor(\neg p\to r))

is admissible in every si-logic (see
[
29
, Theorem 1]
). A rule
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n}/\alpha
is said to be
derivable in
a logic
\mathsf{L}
if the formula
((\alpha_{0}\land\dots\land\alpha_{n})\to\alpha)
belongs to
\mathsf{L}
. For instance, the aforementioned rule is derivable in classical logic but not derivable in intuitionistic logic.

In
[
27
]
, Pogorzelski introduced the notion of
structural completeness
: a logic is structurally complete if every rule admissible in it is derivable. For instance, classical propositional logic is structurally complete, while intuitionistic propositional logic is not.
Soon after structural completeness had been introduced, Dzik and Wroński observed in
[
12
]
that any si-logic extending Dummett’s logic (the smallest si-logic containing the formula
((p\to q)\lor(q\to p))
) is structurally complete. This observation led the author to introduce (see
[
7
]
) the notion of
hereditary structural completeness
: a logic is hereditarily structurally complete if it and all its extensions are structurally complete. For example, Dummett’s logic is hereditarily structurally complete. Moreover, the following simple criterion of hereditary structural completeness has been established in
[
7
]
:

)

\mathsf{L}
is hereditarily structurally complete if and only if none of the prohibited algebras
{\textbf{P}}_{i}
,

(whose Hasse diagrams are depicted in Fig.

We immediately obtain the following result from Theorem

Theorem 1.2

There exists an algorithm which, given a finite set of formulas

Furthermore:

The proof of Theorem

Theorem 1.4
.

Any si-logic for which prohibited algebra

In
[

, it was also observed that not every structurally complete si-logic is hereditarily structurally complete:
{\textbf{P}}_{4}

A proof of these theorems was published in 1987 in Russian in
[

We employ algebraic semantics to establish the theorems. There exists a one-to-one correspondence between si-logics and varieties of Heyting algebras, and a logic is hereditarily structurally complete if and only if the corresponding variety of Heyting algebras is
primitive
(or
deductive

[

). Recall (see, e.g.,
[
16

) that a variety is primitive if every subquasivariety of it is a subvariety. Consequently, we obtain the following results.

Theorem 1.5
.

A variety of Heyting algebras is primitive if and only if it omits all prohibited algebras.

Theorem 1.6
.

All primitive varieties of Heyting algebras are finitely based.

Theorem 1.7
.

Any variety of Heyting algebras omitting prohibited algebra
{\textbf{P}}_{2}
is locally finite.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. The paper is self-contained, and we provide all necessary definitions and background on Heyting algebras and their varieties in Sections

and Theorem

in Section

Heyting Algebras: Definitions and Properties.

Any Heyting algebra

\mathbf{0}_{\textbf{A}}

Ordinary elements.

We will often refer to the following simple observation.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let

.
If

Since

.

First,

\displaystyle c=\neg\neg c=\neg\neg(a\land b)=\neg(\neg a\lor\neg b)=\neg\neg b=b,

Let

2.2
Nodes.

Let
A
be an algebra. An element
a\in{\textbf{A}}
is said to be a
node
if
a
is comparable with every element of
A
. Thus,
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
are always

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
A
be a Heyting algebra with a nontrivial node
a\in{\textbf{A}}
. Then,
b\ll a
holds for any
b<a
.

Proof.

Assume that
b<a
. Hence to prove
b\ll a
we need only to show that
a\to b=b
. Moreover, since trivially
a\land b\leq b
, we only need to show that for any
c\in{\textbf{A}}
,
a\land c\leq b
, implies
c\leq b
.

Let
a\in{\textbf{A}}
and
a\land c\leq b

a
is a node, either
c\leq a
, or
a\leq c
.

If
c\leq a
, then
c=a\land c
and by assumption,
c=a\land c\leq b
, as desired.

If
a\leq c
, then we have
b<a\leq c
, as desired.
∎

Weak projectivity is relative to a class: an algebra may fail to be weakly projective in a class while being weakly projective in one of its subclasses. This observation justifies the following definition.

Definition 4.4
.

An algebra
A
is called
totally non-projective
if
A
is not weakly projective in the variety
\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})
that it generates.

Clearly, a totally non-projective algebra is not weakly projective in any variety containing it. As we shall see, all prohibited algebras are totally non-projective.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
A
be a totally non-projective finite s.i. algebra of finite type. Then
\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})
is not structurally complete.

Proof.

Since
A
is totally non-projective, there exists an algebra
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})
such that
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathbf{H}({\textbf{B}})
but
{\textbf{A}}\notin\mathbf{S}({\textbf{B}})
.
As
{\textbf{B}}\in\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})
, we have
\mathrm{V}({\textbf{B}})\subseteq\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})
. On the other hand, because
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathbf{H}({\textbf{B}})
, it follows that
{\textbf{A}}\in\mathrm{V}({\textbf{B}})
, and consequently
\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})\subseteq\mathrm{V}({\textbf{B}})
. Hence,
\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})=\mathrm{V}({\textbf{B}})
.

Since
A
is a finite s.i. algebra of finite type and
{\textbf{A}}\notin\mathbf{S}({\textbf{B}})
, by Proposition
4.1
we have
{\textbf{A}}\notin\mathcal{Q}({\textbf{B}})
. Therefore,
\mathcal{Q}({\textbf{A}})\neq\mathcal{Q}({\textbf{B}})
, while
\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})=\mathrm{V}({\textbf{B}})
, which shows that
\mathrm{V}({\textbf{A}})
is not structurally complete.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain a necessary condition for primitivity in varieties of finite type.

Corollary 4.6
.

A variety
\mathsf{V}
of finite type containing a totally non-projective finite s.i. algebra is not primitive.

Remark 4.7
.

Proposition
4.5
and Corollary
4.6
also hold for finitely presentable s.i. totally non-projective algebras.

In this section, we recall the notion of projective algebras and establish some of their properties used in the proof of the main theorem.

Definition 4.8

Well Quasi-Ordering.

In the proof of Theorem
1.6
, we shall use the notion of well quasi-ordered sets and some of their properties (in particular, we shall employ Corollary
5.11
).

Definition 5.1

(see, e.g.,
[
20
]
)
.

A quasi-ordered set is said to be
well-founded
if every strictly decreasing sequence is finite, and it is
well quasi-ordered
if, in addition, it contains no infinite antichains.

We next recall several auxiliary properties of well quasi-ordered sets.

Proposition 5.2

(see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

The class of well quasi-ordered sets is closed under taking subsets and finite Cartesian products.

A subset of a quasi-ordered set whose elements are pairwise incomparable is called an
antichain
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
(S_{1},\leq_{1})
and
(S_{2},\leq_{2})
be quasi-ordered sets.
If there exists a map
f:S_{1}\to S_{2}
such that, for any
a,b\in S_{1}
,

f(a)\leq_{2}f(b)\implies a\leq_{1}b,

and if
S_{2}
contains no infinite antichains, then
S_{1}
also contains no infinite antichains.

The proof is straightforward.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
(S_{1},\leq_{1})
and
(S_{2},\leq_{2})
be well-founded quasi-ordered sets.
If there exists a map
f:S_{1}\to S_{2}
such that, for any
a,b\in S_{1}
,

f(a)\leq_{2}f(b)\implies a\leq_{1}b,

then
S_{1}
is well quasi-ordered as well.

Example 5.5
.

If
S
is a set of finite s.i. algebras, it is quasi-ordered by
\leq
. In addition,
S
is well quasi-ordered, because, if
A
and
B
are finite and
{\textbf{A}}\leq{\textbf{B}}
with
{\textbf{B}}\nleq{\textbf{A}}
, the cardinality of
B
is less than cardinality of
A
. Hence,
(S;\leq)
is well quasi-ordered if and only if it does not contain infinite antichains.

We shall also use the following well-known fact (see, e.g.,
[
20

) concerning well quasi-ordered sets.

Proposition 5.6
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a well quasi-ordered alphabet, then the free monoid
\mathfrak{A}^{*}

under the operation of concatenation is well quasi-ordered by the
domination ordering

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{1}\dots\mathfrak{a}_{m}\leq\mathfrak{b}_{1}\dots\mathfrak{b}_{n}\iff(\exists\,1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{m}\leq n)\,(\forall\,i=1,\dots,m)\,(\mathfrak{a}_{i}\leq\mathfrak{b}_{j_{i}}).

5.1
Quasi-Orders on Classes of Finite s.i. Algebras.

Let

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
P
be the class of all finite projective Heyting algebras. Then

is well quasi-ordered.

It follows from

{\textbf{A}}\in P

{\textbf{B}}_{i}\in\{{\textbf{Z}}_{2},{\textbf{Z}}_{4}\}

does not contain an infinite antichain of algebras whose first summand is isomorphic to

Indeed, suppose that
\{{\textbf{A}}_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}

\displaystyle{\textbf{A}}^{(n)}:=\underbrace{{\textbf{Z}}_{4}+\dots+{\textbf{Z}}_{4}}_{{n}\text{ times}}+{\textbf{Z}}_{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle{\textbf{A}}^{(n_{0})}+{\textbf{A}}^{(n_{1})}+\dots+{\textbf{A}}^{(n_{m})}

Proposition 5.8

(about stretching)
.

Let
{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}

(a)

Let
\mathfrak{A}=\{\mathfrak{a}_{n},n\geq 0\}

, for all

Define on

\mathfrak{a}_{i}\leq\mathfrak{a}_{j}\iff i\leq j.

It should be clear that

Like in Proposition
5.6
, let
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
be a free monoid equipped with the domination quasi-order

.

Now we extend the map

to a map

\displaystyle f^{\prime}({\textbf{A}}^{(n_{0})}+{\textbf{A}}^{(n_{1})}+\dots+{\textbf{A}}^{(n_{m})})=(\mathfrak{a}_{n_{0}};\mathfrak{a}_{n_{0}}\mathfrak{a}_{n_{1}}\dots\mathfrak{a}_{n_{m}}).

(5.3)

To complete the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle\text{if }f^{\prime}({\textbf{A}})\leq f^{\prime}({\textbf{B}}),\text{ then }{\textbf{A}}\leq{\textbf{B}}.

(5.4)

Then, since the quasi-order on

.

Suppose that

\displaystyle{\textbf{A}}={\textbf{A}}^{(n_{0})}+{\textbf{A}}^{(n_{1})}+\dots+{\textbf{A}}^{(n_{m})},\text{ and}

(5.5)

\displaystyle{\textbf{B}}={\textbf{A}}^{(k_{0})}+{\textbf{A}}^{(k_{1})}+\dots+{\textbf{A}}^{(k_{s})}.

(5.6)

Then

\displaystyle f^{\prime}({\textbf{A}})=(\mathfrak{a}_{n_{0}};\mathfrak{a}_{n_{0}}\mathfrak{a}_{n_{1}}\mathfrak{a}_{n_{2}}\dots\mathfrak{a}_{n_{m}}),\text{ and}

(5.7)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}({\textbf{B}})=(\mathfrak{a}_{k_{0}};\mathfrak{a}_{k_{0}}\mathfrak{a}_{k_{1}}\mathfrak{a}_{k_{2}}\dots\mathfrak{a}_{k_{s}}).

minus

Basis.
Suppose that

(\mathfrak{a}_{n_{0}};\mathfrak{a}_{n_{0}})\leq(\mathfrak{a}_{k_{0}};\mathfrak{a}_{k_{0}}\mathfrak{a}_{k_{1}}\dots\mathfrak{a}_{k_{s}}).

that is,

(a)

(b)

;

(c)

the lattice

Remark 5.10
.

Theorem

holds in a much broader setting; see

Let us note that property (a) holds if and only if every subvariety of

is finitely based relative to
\mathsf{V}

\mathsf{V}

S
is well quasi-ordered.

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
\mathsf{V}
be a locally finite, finitely based variety of Heyting algebras, and let

be the class of all finite s.i. algebras from

(S,\leq)

Theorem 6.7
.

Let

{\textbf{P}}_{2}

To prove this statement, we will show that there exists a function

bounding the cardinalities of

, always belongs to
\mathsf{V}
.

If

, then, since the algebra

is a homomorphic image of

is a homomorphic image of
\mathbf{2}+{\textbf{Z}}_{\infty}+\mathbf{2}
, and therefore
{\textbf{P}}_{2}\cong\mathbf{2}+{\textbf{Z}}_{7}\in\mathsf{V}
.

If

S
be the class of all finite s.i. members of
\mathsf{W}
. If we can demonstrate that
S
is well quasi-ordered with respect to
\preceq
(defined in (
5.10
)), then, by Corollary
5.11
, we may conclude that all subvarieties of
\mathsf{W}
are finitely based.

By Lemma
6.5
, all members of
S
are weakly
\mathsf{W}
-projective. Therefore, for any
{\textbf{A}},{\textbf{B}}\in S
,

{\textbf{A}}\preceq{\textbf{B}}\iff{\textbf{A}}\in\mathbf{H}\mathbf{S}({\textbf{B}})\iff{\textbf{A}}\in\mathbf{S}({\textbf{B}})\iff{\textbf{A}}\leq{\textbf{B}}.

We aim to show that
(S;\leq)
is well quasi-ordered.

Lemma 7.1
.

If
(S,\leq)
contains an infinite antichain, then it contains an infinite antichain of algebras of the form

{\textbf{A}}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}{\textbf{B}}_{i}+\mathbf{2},

where
{\textbf{B}}_{i}\in\{{\textbf{Z}}_{2},{\textbf{Z}}_{4}\}
for all
i\in[0,m]
. That is,
(S,\leq)
contains an infinite antichain of finite projective Heyting algebras.

Proof.

Indeed, if
(S,\leq)
contains an infinite antichain, then it contains an infinite subantichain of algebras having the same first summand. That is,
(S,\leq)
contains an infinite antichain of algebras in each of which
{\textbf{B}}_{0}
is either
{\textbf{Z}}_{2}
,
{\textbf{Z}}_{4}
, or
{\textbf{Z}}_{8}
. In the first two cases there is nothing to prove. Let us consider the remaining case.

Suppose that the algebras
{\textbf{Z}}_{8}+{\textbf{C}}_{j}
,
j\geq 0
, form an antichain. Then, since
{\textbf{Z}}_{8}||{\textbf{Z}}_{4}
, by Corollary
2.18
(b), the algebras
{\textbf{Z}}_{4}+{\textbf{C}}_{j}
,
j\geq 0
, form an antichain as well.

Thus, by Lemma
7.1
, if
(S,\leq)
contained an infinite antichain, it would contain an infinite antichain of finite projective Heyting algebras, which is impossible by Theorem
5.7
. Hence,
(S,\leq)
does not contain infinite antichains and is well founded, which implies that it is well quasi-ordered. This observation completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

There is an algorithm that, given any finite set of equations, decides whether the variety of Heyting algebras defined by these equations is primitive.

Indeed, let
E
be a finite set of equations. To determine whether the variety
\mathsf{V}
defined by these equations is primitive, we need to check whether all equations from
E
hold in some algebra
{\textbf{P}}_{i}
,
i\in[1,5]
. If none of these algebras satisfy all equations from
E
, then the variety is primitive; otherwise, it is not primitive.

8
Conclusions

The Main Theorem and its corollaries provide a complete characterization of the primitive varieties of Heyting algebras: the variety
\mathsf{W}
is the largest primitive variety; it is locally finite, all its subvarieties are finitely based, and there exists an algorithm that, for any given finite set of equations, decides whether the variety of Heyting algebras defined by these equations is primitive. Much less is known about structurally complete varieties of Heyting algebras.

From the Main Theorem and Corollary
4.14
it follows that
\mathsf{W}\subseteq\mathsf{Md}
, and thus all primitive varieties are subvarieties of
\mathsf{Md}
. On the other hand, there are infinitely many subvarieties of
\mathsf{Md}
that are structurally complete but not primitive (see
[
32
]
), starting with
\mathsf{Md}
itself. Moreover,
\mathsf{Md}
is not locally finite, nor it is finitely based (cf.
[
25
]
).

The following problems remain open.

Problem 1
.

Is there a largest structurally complete variety of Heyting algebras?

Remark 8.1
.

Note, that from
[
29
, Theorem 1]
it follows that every structurally complete variety is a subvariety of the variety
\mathsf{KP}
defined by equation
\neg x\to(y\lor z)\approx(\neg x\to 5)\lor(\neg x\to z)
, which is not structurally complete, but contains
\mathsf{Md}
as a subvariety, but
\mathsf{KP}
is not structurally complete (cf.
[
34
]
).

Problem 2
.

Is the class of all structurally complete varieties of Heyting algebras countable?

Problem 3
.

Are there structurally complete but non-primitive varieties of Heyting algebras that are not subvarieties of the variety
\mathsf{Md}
?

We say that a variety has the
disjunction property
if its free algebra is finitely irreducible. For example, the variety
\mathsf{Md}
enjoys the disjunction property, and it was observed in
[
24
]
that
\mathsf{Md}
is a minimal (with respect to
\subseteq
) variety with the disjunction property. Hence, except for
\mathsf{Md}
, all known structurally complete varieties of Heyting algebras lack the disjunction property.

Problem 4
.

Is there a structurally complete variety of Heyting algebras that is not a subvariety of
\mathsf{Md}
?

Problem 5
.

Is there a structurally complete variety of Heyting algebras, distinct from
\mathsf{\mathsf{Md}}
, that enjoys the disjunction property?
