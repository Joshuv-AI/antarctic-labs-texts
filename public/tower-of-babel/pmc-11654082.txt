Competing risks multi-state model for time-to-event data analysis of HIV/AIDS: a retrospective cohort national datasets, Ethiopia
Authors: Tsegaye H Kumssa, Andargachew Mulu, Adane Mihret, Zeytu G Asfaw
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTuberculosis (TB) remains the most common opportunistic infection and leading cause of death among individuals living with HIV/AIDS in Ethiopia. Its significant impact on morbidity and mortality underscores the crucial link between these two diseases. While the advent of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has led to a dramatic decline in mortality rates among HIV/AIDS patients, TB continues to pose a substantial threat. This study aims to estimate the probability of death due to TB among HIV/AIDS patients on ART, considering the presence of various competing risks, including diarrhea, other infections, and unknown/unspecified causes. Also we have assessed the effects of prognostic factors on HIV/AIDS cause specific deaths, compared with the death from other competing risks, and exploring leading cause of death among HIV/AIDS patients on Antiretroviral Therapy.MethodsData from a retrospective research examining the effectiveness of antiretroviral therapy (ART) in Ethiopia were used in this investigation. The data came from medical records of patients who were part of the national ART program. A total of 39,590 records were gathered between October 2019 and March 2020 from all regions of Ethiopia as well as the administration cities of Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa. The study facilities were grouped using a multi-stage sample technique and simple random selection was used to select health facility and a person record from medical records. In the presence of the competing causes of death, Cause specific hazard, subdistribution hazard model and flexible parametric proportional hazard model have been used to assess the effect of covariates on the risk of death, with the cmprisk package in R4.3.2 software.ResultsOut of the total 1212 deaths, 542(44.7%) died competing with other opportunistic infection (TE-Esophageal Candidiasis, TO-oral, CT-CNS Toxoplasmosis, CM-Crypotococcal Meningitis…), 421 (34.7%) died due to tuberculosis and the remaining death were unknown/Not specified infection 222(18.3%) and diarrhea 27(2.2%). Rates of mortality caused by tuberculosis, competing with other opportunistic infection, diarrhea and unknown/Not specified were 3.5, 4.5, 0.2 and 1.8 per 1000 person-months, respectively. Having a higher CD4 count at diagnosis, responding to combination antiretroviral treatment (cART) six months after start, and having prophylactic treatment for pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP) decreased the risk of tuberculosis, other opportunistic infections, and unidentified and diarrheal causes of death. However, older age, late HIV.AIDS diagnosis, and the last HIV/AIDS WHO clinical stages increased the hazard of tuberculosis and other opportunistic disease mortality. Additionally, male gender, older age and last HIV clinical stages increased the mortality HIV/AIDS patients.ConclusionThe findings of this study demonstrated that TB, an opportunistic infection, was the primary cause of death in HIV/AIDS patients, despite the presence of several competing risks, such as diarrhea, other infections, and an undetermined or unclear cause. It's important to use effective techniques to quickly detect those who have HIV or AIDS and provide them with care and treatment to increase their chances of surviving.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10280-9.Keywords: AIDS, Competing risk, ·Cause-specific hazard, ·Sub-distribution hazard, Cumulative Incidence Function

Introduction

HIV is still a big problem around the world. It affected about 42.3 million people so far. Sadly, people are still getting infected with HIV in every country on Earth. By the end of 2023, the expected number of HIV positive individuals was 39.9 million, with 65% of those individuals residing in the WHO African Region [1]. An estimated 1.3 million individuals contracted HIV/AIDS in 2023, while an estimated 630 000 people died from HIV/AIDS-related causes. HIV/AIDS infection has no known treatment. But thanks to accessible and efficient HIV/AIDS care, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention including for opportunistic and healthy lives and there were about 9,984 fatalities each year in Ethiopia across all age groups [2].

The long-term survival rate of HIV-positive people has grown thanks to antiretroviral therapy (ART), which has also changed their prognosis from a fatal illness to a lasting and manageable disease. But this depends on a number of risk variables, such as if the HIV patient has TB, diarrhea, other opportunistic illnesses, or an unknown infection. As a result, rather than the virus itself, an HIV/AIDS patient died from TB, diarrhea, other opportunistic and unknown illness that were growing more common in the population. In order to develop effective intervention strategies, improve patient care, and minimize HIV patient mortality as much as possible, causes of death (CoD) among HIV-infected patients must be evaluated [3]. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) patients receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) were the subjects of cohort studies, where participants might go through a variety of circumstances. One must consider the possibility of opportunistic infectious disease while examining the time to death process in HIV positive individuals, for example. Understanding the prognostic variables affecting HIV patients' long-term survival is necessary [3, 4]. In addition to the risk of dying from tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS patients also run the risk of dying from other infections or diarrhea. When participants are able to observe one or more events, they are in a state of "competition" with one another, and the occurrence of one event may prevent the occurrence of the others or alter their probability of occurring, thereby creating competing hazards. Usually, to do this, independent research is conducted for each end point and the intermediate events; however, this is not enough because the relationships between these events are not taken into consideration [5–7]. The msm package for R allows a general multi-state model to be fitted such complex systems in this context [7].

The msm package for R framework is a very useful tool for providing answers to a range of survival analysis questions that traditional models are unable to handle [8, 9]. This study mainly looks at deaths caused by tuberculosis, but some patients may die from other causes as well. In patients on highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) who died from tuberculosis, diarrhea, other infections, or unknown causes, a competing risk model was used to determine the specific cause of death.. [10, 11]. In patients who died from opportunistic infections while on HAART, there are many international literatures that quantify death from other causes [9, 11].

However, relatively few researches were carried out in Ethiopia; their work was on HIV patients who were lost to follow-up who presented competing risks [12]. In this study, death-competing risks from tuberculosis, diarrhea, other opportunistic infections, and unidentified infections are examined using national dataset. Research of this kind could be helpful to the Ministry of Health in its efforts to enhance patient care and determine effective intervention strategies. For HIV-positive patients, decreased mortality from opportunistic diseases linked to AIDS is also very beneficial.The Kaplan–Meier estimator and Cox proportional hazard model are the traditional classical survival analysis techniques for time-to-event data [13]. These techniques work well in investigations where there is just one primary event type of interest but not fully reveal the relationship when there are several occurrences of interest.

To fill the gap this method, multistate models offer a versatile and expansive framework. The competing risks, which is a special case of a multi-state model and is a cause specific hazard, sub-distribution hazard, and flexible parametric hazard model, were used in this study. Because of this, the primary aim of this article was to estimate the risk of death among HIV patients receiving ART due to tuberculosis, diarrhea, other opportunistic infections, and unidentified infections, without considering covariates and in the presence of competing risks. Finally, the cumulative Incidence Function (CIF) [9, 11] was utilized to predict the likelihood of mortality and the cause specific hazard, sub-distribution hazard, and flexible parametric hazard model were employed to evaluate the covariate impacts on risk of death.

Method and materials

Study data

This study used data from a retrospective study that looked at how well HIV treatment (ART) was working in Ethiopia. The data came from medical records of patients who were part of the national ART program. A total of 39,590 records were gathered between October 2019 and March 2020 from all regions of Ethiopia as well as the administration cities of Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa.The study facilities were grouped using a multi-stage sample technique and simple random selection was used to select health facility and a person record from medical records. Individuals who had a viral load larger than 1000 copies/ml, a CD4 count < = 200, and WHO stages 3 and 4 were eligible to begin antiretroviral therapy (ART) [13].We collected the data on demographic information (Age, weight, hemoglobin, sex, marital status, educational attainment, location) and clinical information (type of ART regimen initiated, patient category, patterns of ARV drug regimen change, and level of adherence. Also checked for presence of any opportunistic infection at base line and during ART, liver enzyme level, Hepatitis B and C serology status, and counseling related to family planning).In addition, CD4 cells count and HIV viral load were measured at each visit. All the data entered to the central database using RedCap software. For detail reading refer the previous published articles methodology part [14].

Multi-state models

A multi-state model is a model for time-to-event data in which all individuals start in HIV/AIDS patient and eventually may die in one of the following cause(s) (e.g. TB or Diarrhea or other Opportunistic infection disease or unknown [8]. Some individuals are censored before they reach final state. Competing risks models are a sub-category of multi-state models: they have one starting state, at least two absorbing states and no intermediate states (see Fig. 1 (a) [6]. Their transitions are often indicated as causes of failure. An example of a multi-state model is illustrated in Fig. 1 (b) shown.

The competing risks multistate model

In the presence of competing events, a unique type of multistate model known as competing risk analysis aims to precisely forecast the marginal probability of an event. The probability of dying as a result of competing risk has been predicted using the Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF). The sub distribution hazard model and the cause specific hazard model were used in this competing risk study to analyse the covariate impacts on cause specific hazard.

In competing risks models, limitations include several assumptions and potential biases.

Assumptions: These models assume that different types of events are independent, which may not always hold true. Cause-specific hazard models assume that competing events do not influence the probability of the primary event once it's censored, while sub-distribution hazard models account for competing events in cumulative probability calculations.

Potential Biases: Unmeasured confounders, like adherence to HIV treatment or socioeconomic status, can bias results. Such factors might impact both the event of interest and competing risks, potentially skewing estimates [8, 9, 11].

Also here we assume that the event of interest is death of AIDS patient due to the opportunistic infection TB (indexed 1, 2, 3, 4), coded Tuberculosis (as the event of interest), diarrhea, other opportunistic infections and unknown causes respectively. Sub-distribution hazard (SHs) regression—the Fine-Gray model

Fine-Gray model is another name for subdistribution model. The Fine-Gray model [16], which is a Cox proportional regression model, was inspired by the possibility that a covariate's impact on the cause-specific hazard function may differ significantly from that on the CIF. Stated differently, a covariate may have little impact on CIF, but a significant impact on the cause-specific hazard function. The way the competing risk events are handled distinguishes between cause-specific hazard and subdistribution. While the later takes into consideration the informative censoring nature of the competing risk events, the former views competing risk events as non-informative censoring.

The reason WHO clinical stage increases the risk of death in all causes, the patient immunology decreases and they are exposed by opportunistic infections.For HIV/AIDS competing risk study, both cause-specific hazard and sub-distribution hazard models are key in analyzing competing risks, such as TB-related versus other opportunistic deaths. Cause-Specific Hazard Model: This model estimates the instantaneous risk of an HIV/AIDS-related event, considering only those at risk at each time point. It's useful for understanding factors affecting the occurrence of each event independently. Sub-Distribution Hazard Model: This model calculates the cumulative incidence of events while accounting for competing risks, offering insights into the long-term probability of events. This is particularly relevant in estimating AIDS versus non-AIDS mortality probabilities in clinical outcomes.Sub-Distribution Hazard Model: This model considers the probability of an event over time, accounting for competing events. It’s especially useful in clinical settings to estimate cumulative incidence while acknowledging competing risks.

Discussion

In order to support national health policy on the causes of death of HIV/AIDS patients falling under the ART category, we employed a national dataset in this study that comprised all regional states and two administrative cities between November 2019 and March 2020 patients on antiretroviral therapy (ART). It might be look at as a continuation of the earlier research that was completed in 2023 at the Ambo General Hospital health center. We have discussed the three common methods for handling competing risks and their applications to regression setting.

The analysis output displays the cause-specific hazard model, the sub-distribution hazard model, and the flexible parametric hazard model. The cause specific hazard model, sub-distribution hazard model and flexible parametric hazard model shows in the analysis output Table 2 and 3 produced nearly similar results with regard to the effect of covariates. Tuberculosis is the most common opportunistic infection cause of death among HIV/AIDS patients supported by previous global studies in India and Asia–Pacific region and Sub-Saharan Africa [17–20].During the period of observation, 34.7% were died due to tuberculosis, 44.7% were died due to other opportunistic infection but the others died due to diarrhea and unknown infection causes were 20.6%.This result agree with the study done in Germany [11], higher the study conducted in France [21] and a study done in South Africa [22] and in contrast with the study done in Kenya [23]. According to the Table 2 and 3, age group, CD4 count and WHO stage were significant indicators for HIV/AIDS patients.

Male patients had better survival than female HIV patients the start of ART treatment than female but female were better than male after 3 years of treatment periods. Patient had nearly high risk of probability of death due to other opportunistic infection and Diarrhea in the first 2 years but probability of death due to unknown were lower as compared to the others causes. After 3 years, diarrhea had lower risk as compared to the others opportunistic infection and unknown infections and in agreement with the study done in Asian-Region [24] but in contrast with the study done in Iran [25] but lower as compared to in Sub-Sahara country in Kenya [26].

According to all the sub-distribution hazard models, flexible parametric and cause specific hazard models approaches, CD4 cells count is a key indicator for HIV/AIDS patients. Cause specific, sub-distribution and flexible parametric hazard ratios for tuberculosis deaths were found that the higher CD4 cell count cells/mm3 patients were less risk of death due to tuberculosis compared to the patients with less than 200cells/mm3 CD4 cell count. This study was lower result than with the studies in Asia–Pacific region and Germany [11, 19] and similar result the study done in Ethiopia and Uganda [27, 28].

The patient with higher age group were higher risk of death as compared to patient with lower age group and in agreement with the study in Ethiopia [29] but death due to diarrhea were less as compared to lower age group this in agreement. This could be the cofounding effects of age.

The results of cause specific, sub-distribution and flexible parametric hazard model are found to be more than 2 times higher risk death in tuberculosis, diarrhea and opportunistic infection patient in ART treatment in WHO stage IV as compared to lower WHO stage I and lower with the studies done previously in Iran and South Africa [23, 25]. Because of this, it may be wise on the side of treatment and care providers to focus on early detection of opportunistic infection and fast action should be taken to reduce morbidity and improve quality of life for persons with HIV/AIDS. Early detection and treatment of tuberculosis in HIV-infected people need to be prioritized. The prediction of patient risk with fewer combinations of variables weight, age and sex, CD4 cell count had lower risk as the patient had increase CD4 count.

This study is the first to assess the mortality risks of tuberculosis and other opportunistic infections in Ethiopian individuals living with HIV/AIDS. The overestimation of cumulative incidence and bias in the covariate effects might both be avoided. Second, our study had a sizeable sample of 39,590 participants from across the country and a protracted follow-up period of (84 months). Last but not least, we looked into the connection between the time between starting ART and mortality in HIV/AIDS patients with high and low CD4 + cell counts.

According to the current study findings, late HIV/AIDS diagnosis at an older age, in stages 3 or 4, or with a lower CD4 count may significantly increase the chance of dying from the disease considerably. These findings were consistent with those of other investigations in Germany and Iran [11, 25].

This study has some limitations, the dynamic fluctuations in CD4 + cell counts over time and causes of death record not appropriate this could affect to see the survival of HIV/AIDS patients. Due to the study's record review methodology, some variables including height and hemoglobin were missing. Due to the incompleteness of the data, BMI and anemia status were excluded from the current analysis.

To enhance the clinical implications of the study on HIV/AIDS mortality, the findings could suggest specific actions: Prioritize CD4 Count Monitoring: Frequent CD4 count tracking, particularly in high-risk groups, can help healthcare providers intervene earlier in cases of immune system decline. Resource Allocation for Rural Patients: The study could recommend increased resources such as mobile clinics and telemedicine o support rural patients, who may face barriers to care and treatment adherence. Tailored Interventions for Socioeconomic Factors: Addressing socioeconomic disparities with targeted support services could help mitigate some unmeasured confounders impacting patient outcomes.

Conclusion

The results of this study clearly shown that tuberculosis, diarrhea, opportunistic infection and unknown cause are substantially accelerating death for HIV/AIDS patients in Ethiopia. Applying effective strategies is needed to achieve on time diagnosis of individuals with HIV and provide them with HIV/AIDS care and treatment services to enhance the survival of the patients. Moreover, female younger age living with HIV/AIDS requires more attention when receiving HIV/AIDS care and treatment as they experience a higher risk of death the first 3 years after ART initiation. The findings of the current research revealed that early HIV/AIDS care and treatment could substantially reduce opportunistic infection death among HIV/AIDS patients. Therefore, to reduce mortality rate HIV/AIDS patient, the current strategies should be revised to improve the timing of treatment initiation and also to optimize the adherence to the treatment.
