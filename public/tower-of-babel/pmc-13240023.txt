Numeracy, literacy, and formal education as mechanisms of inequities in late‐life cognitive performance between Indigenous‐speaking and non–Indigenous‐speaking older Mexican adults
Authors: Sirena Gutierrez, Zachary J. Kunicki, Iris Strangmann, Alexa S. Gonzalez, Gelan Ying, Sneha Sarah Mani, Jacqueline M. Torres, Jennifer Weuve, Antonio Terracciano, Jet M. J. Vonk, Emily M. Briceño, Miguel Arce Rentería
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIndigenous adults face educational inequities, but it is unclear how education, literacy, and numeracy contribute to disparities in late‐life cognition.METHODSThis study included 1958 adults ages 55+ (mean 68.1 [8.9 SD] years) from the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) Ancillary Study on Cognitive Aging (Mex‐Cog; 2016). Linear regression models estimated associations between speaking an Indigenous language (vs. not) and cognitive factor scores, adjusting for sociodemographic covariates. Mediation analyses decomposed associations into direct and indirect effects via education, literacy, and numeracy.RESULTSEleven percent of participants spoke an Indigenous language, which was associated with lower memory (β [95% confidence interval [CI] = −0.31 [−0.40, −0.21]), language (−0.65 [−0.75, −0.55]), executive function (−0.60 [−0.73, −0.47]), and orientation (−0.31 [−0.44, −0.18]) scores. Education explained 31%–61% of the association, literacy 11%–26%, and numeracy 2%–9%.DISUSSIONHigh‐quality education may help reduce cognitive disparities in Mexico. Future research should address data limitations by collecting measures of Indigeneity beyond language use (e.g., self‐identification, colorism).

BACKGROUND

Improvements in early‐ and mid‐life health have increased longevity, while sustained declines in fertility have reduced the proportion of younger cohorts in Mexico. 1 Together, these population‐level processes have contributed to a demographic shift in accelerated population aging, 2 with the share of adults aged 60 years or older projected to nearly triple by 2050. 3 As a result, the burden of dementia is expected to rise dramatically, with cases projected to increase to 3.5 million. 4 In low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including Mexico, the proportion of individuals living with dementia is anticipated to grow from 58% in 2010 to 71% by 2050. 1 , 4 Recent estimates of population‐attributable risk factors indicate that dementia prevention may be a more urgent public health priority in Latin America compared to high‐income countries, with evidence suggesting that increasing educational attainment could prevent up to 10.9% of dementia cases. 5 This growing burden, compounded by limited access to effective pharmacological interventions in LMICs, underscores the need to identify key population‐level factors that contribute to risk for dementia. 5 In particular, there is a critical need to identify within‐country drivers of dementia inequities as well as factors that might ameliorate these inequities.

In Mexico, stark socio‐economic and health inequities exist across ethnoracial subgroups. 6 , 7 , 8 While ethnoracial classification is highly complex, 9 influenced by national ideologies of mestizaje or miscegenation, 10 approximately 15%–20% of the population self‐identifies as Indigenous, 11 but only 6%–7% report speaking an Indigenous language. 12 , 13 Both self‐reported and interviewer‐assessed Indigenous identity have been associated with poorer self‐rated health and other health and mortality outcomes among Mexican adults. 6 While evidence relevant to dementia risk is limited, a recent study suggests that speaking an Indigenous language is associated with lower cognitive performance among older adults in Mexico. 14 Speaking an Indigenous language captures a subset of people with Indigenous ethnoracial identity, likely reflecting stronger retention of culture and community ties, but does not fully represent the broader spectrum of Indigenous identity, which includes self‐identification, ancestry, community belonging, and perceived identity. 15 , 16 Evidence from other countries indicates that Indigenous older adults often experience poorer cognitive performance, higher dementia risk, and greater mortality compared to non‐Indigenous peers. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

Drawing on cumulative disadvantage theory, dementia inequities for Indigenous groups have been attributed to early‐life structural factors such as limited access to formal education, lifelong exposure to socioeconomic disadvantage and discrimination, and barriers to culturally appropriate healthcare. 21 , 22 While sociological research has demonstrated socio‐economic inequities by Indigenous identity in Mexico, 8 scholars have yet to make a direct connection to cognitive outcomes. Most of the evidence on the mechanisms underlying Indigenous inequities in cognitive aging and dementia risk comes from high‐income countries like the United States (U.S.), Canada, and Australia, 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 limiting its relevance to Mexico and other LMICs contexts.

We aim to address this gap by quantifying education‐related factors underlying previously described cognitive inequities in a national sample of older adults who speak an Indigenous language in Mexico. We consider both formal years of educational attainment as well as measures of literacy and numeracy. Formal educational attainment may not fully capture the schooling experiences and skills most relevant to cognitive performance in populations with low socioeconomic status (SES). Literacy and numeracy, for example, may directly support cognitive functioning by enabling effective health management (e.g., medication adherence), facilitating social engagement, and promoting participation in mentally stimulating activities throughout life. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Indirectly, these skills may be essential for greater occupational opportunities, yielding more midlife socioeconomic resources that may support dementia prevention. 27 For Indigenous older adults, who have historically faced structural barriers to education and limited access to quality schooling in their native language, 28 , 29 literacy and numeracy, thus, may represent a critical mechanism linking early‐life educational inequality to late‐life cognitive disparities.

METHODS

Participants

We used data from the Mexican Cognitive Aging Ancillary Study (Mex‐Cog), a subsample from the fourth wave (2015) of the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS). The MHAS is a nationally representative longitudinal study that examines the health and aging trends among Mexican adults, which began in 2001. The Mex‐Cog subsample comprised adults aged 55 years and older, selected through stratified sampling from eight Mexican states chosen to capture national variation in socioeconomic conditions and health‐related exposures. Further details about the MHAS and Mex‐Cog sampling procedures are provided elsewhere. 30 , 31

Our analysis specifically focused on the Mex‐Cog participants who completed a comprehensive cognitive assessment designed as part of the Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (HCAP; n = 2042). 32 This protocol facilitates cross‐national comparisons of the prevalence and trends of dementia in aging populations worldwide. 33

For our analytical sample, we excluded respondents who were missing information on the Indigenous language spoken (n = 68) and respondents who did not have information on all four cognitive domain factor scores (n = 4). Finally, we excluded respondents missing measures across potential mechanistic variables (n = 12). The final analytical sample consisted of 1958 respondents (Figure S1).

Measures

Cognitive function

Participants completed a comprehensive cognitive battery assessing the domains of orientation, memory, language, and executive functioning in Spanish. A complete description of the Mex‐Cog cognitive battery can be found elsewhere. 31 The cognitive tests were carefully selected based on their ability to be adapted by considering cultural factors in Mexico across rural communities and populations with lower educational attainment. Cognitive domain factor scores were derived through confirmatory factor analysis. These domain‐specific factor scores were standardized in the pooled sample of all the HCAP studies such that the mean and variance of latent traits (i.e., memory, executive function, orientation, and language) were set to 0 and 1, respectively, with Health and Retirement Study‐HCAP as the reference. 32

Indigenous language status

Participants self‐reported whether they spoke an Indigenous language. Participants were asked: “Do you speak any Indigenous language?” Response options were (1 = yes; 0 = no) for which we generated a binary variable. No additional survey information was collected regarding self‐identification as Indigenous or other language‐related characteristics. Accordingly, this measure captures the presence of any self‐reported Indigenous language use but does not distinguish levels of proficiency, frequency or context of use, nor does it provide an inclusive measure of Indigenous identification. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and are discussed further below.

Educational factors

Numeracy. Numeracy was assessed through self‐report. Participants were asked, “Can you count from 1 to 10?” The response options were (1 = yes; 0 = no), and a binary variable was generated based on their responses.

Literacy. Literacy was assessed through self‐report. Participants were asked, “Do you know how to read and write a message?” The response options were (1 = yes; 0 = no), and a binary variable was generated based on their responses. The questionnaire did not explicitly specify a target language. Consequently, participants may have interpreted the questions as referring to Spanish.

Educational attainment. Participants self‐reported their years of schooling.

Covariates

The covariates were selected based on the criteria that they temporally preceded the exposure and outcome. This included a range of sociodemographic characteristics of respondents assessed in 2015, such as respondents’ age, sex, and the highest level of parental education (none or early childhood; primary education; lower secondary education; missing indicator for cases with both parents missing information).

To capture other dimensions of respondents’ early‐life socioeconomic conditions, we created a childhood disadvantage score. 34 Given that temporally, this may be downstream from the exposure and mediators, this was included in our secondary covariate adjustment. Respondents were asked if before the age of 10, they experienced any of the following: did not have access to sanitation facilities, generally went to bed hungry, did not regularly wear shoes, self/siblings had to drop out of school to support family, self/family slept in the kitchen, family received financial support, had a serious health problem that affected their normal activities. The disadvantage score (0–7) was generated from a summary count of their responses to the seven items, with higher values indicating a higher early‐life socioeconomic disadvantage.

Analyses

Descriptive analyses compared sociodemographic characteristics by Indigenous language status (Table 1). To assess the interrelationships between our exposure, proposed mediators, and outcome, we ran a series of models. First, we estimated the total association between speaking an Indigenous language and domain‐specific factor scores, adjusting for age, sex, and parental education through a series of linear regressions. We next evaluated whether Indigenous language status was individually associated with the mediators (numeracy, literacy, and education), utilizing Poisson and linear regressions with robust standard errors; this step is necessary for establishing the plausibility of numeracy, literacy, and education as potential mediators of the association between Indigenous language status and cognition.

To estimate the mediation parameters, we applied a counterfactual framework 35 to assess whether inequities in late‐life cognitive factor scores by Indigenous language status were explained through numeracy, literacy, and education, utilizing the natural direct effects (NDEs) and natural indirect effects (NIEs). Estimating these parameters required the specification of two models. The first model regressed the mediator on exposure and confounders of the exposure‐mediator relationship (age, sex, parental education). Predictions from this mechanism model were used to estimate the counterfactual mediator values for each respondent across exposure values. The second model regressed the cognitive factor score on Indigenous language status, the individual mediator, and mediator‐outcome confounders (age, sex, and parental education); we additionally included cross‐product terms between Indigenous language status and the mediator in the outcome models, given evidence of multiplicative interaction on late‐life cognition in our analysis. We used the “mediate” command in Stata, 36 which combined estimates from the mechanism and outcome models to estimate the NDE and NIE utilizing Imai et al.’s potential outcomes framework. 37 Percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were estimated by repeating the analysis on 1,000 resamples with replacement. Provided all mediation assumptions hold, the mediated proportion was computed as the ratio of the NIE divided by the total effect.

We conducted several supplemental analyses. First, given the correlated nature of education and literacy, we restricted analyses to participants without any formal schooling (n = 336) to better isolate the role of literacy independent of educational attainment. By limiting the sample to individuals who were uniformly educationally disadvantaged, we further reduced confounding related to schooling and early‐life social disadvantage, thereby enhancing comparability between Indigenous‐speaking and non‐Indigenous‐speaking participants. Within this subgroup, we examined the association between speaking an Indigenous language, literacy, and cognitive performance. This analysis was not conducted for numeracy due to positivity violations across the schooling strata. Second, to account for additional early‐life covariates, we presented results that were additionally adjusted for early childhood disadvantage. Lastly, to estimate the degree of residual confounding in our total associations, we calculated E‐values to quantify the strength of unmeasured confounding needed to explain away the observed estimates. 38

RESULTS

Descriptives

The sample had a mean age of 68.1 years (8.9 ± SD), and 60% were women (Table 1). Overall, 96% of participants reported being numerate, 86% reported being literate, and the average educational attainment was 5.4 years (4.5 ± SD). Eleven percent of participants reported speaking an Indigenous language. Compared to non‐Indigenous language speakers, those who spoke an Indigenous language were more likely to have parents with limited education (e.g., 87% vs. 69% had parents with fewer than 6 years of schooling) and to reside in rural areas (71% vs. 40%). Participants who spoke an Indigenous language were also less likely to report being numerate (93% vs. 97%) or literate (73% vs. 88%) and had, on average, significantly fewer years of schooling. Numeracy, literacy, and years of education were moderately correlated (r = 0.2–0.4, p < 0.05) in the analytic sample. While conceptually related, the magnitude of the correlation indicates that these measures capture overlapping but distinct dimensions of education‐related factors.

Total effect of speaking an Indigenous language on domain‐specific cognitive factor scores

Language use represents only one dimension of Indigenous identity, and its underlying components may differentially relate to cognitive outcomes. With this consideration in mind, speaking an Indigenous language was associated with lower memory (β = −0.31; 95% CI: −0.40, −0.21), language (β = −0.65; 95% CI: −0.75, −0.55), executive function (β = −0.60; 95% CI: −0.73, −0.47), and orientation factor scores (β = −0.31; 95% CI: −0.44, −0.18) on the Spanish‐administered Mex‐Cog battery, compared to those who did not speak an Indigenous language. These differences reflect performance on assessments conducted exclusively in Spanish and therefore may partially capture differences in Spanish language proficiency, in addition to potential differences in cognitive function (Figure 1; Table S1).

Total, direct, and indirect effects (through either numeracy, literacy, and education) of speaking an Indigenous language on cognitive function factors, Mexican Health and Aging Study Ancillary Study on Cognitive Aging (Mex‐Cog; 2016). Models were adjusted for age, sex, and parental education.

Exposure‐mediator relationships for speaking an Indigenous language with numeracy, literacy, and educational attainment

Speaking an Indigenous language was associated with a 4% lower likelihood of being numerate (relative risk [RR] = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.92, 0.99), a 17% lower likelihood of being literate (RR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.77, 0.90), and 2.03 fewer years of educational attainment (95% CI: −2.57, −1.49) (Table S2).

Mediation analyses

Numeracy

The estimated direct effect of speaking an Indigenous language, independent of numeracy, remained statistically significant across cognitive domains: −0.28 (95% CI: −0.39, −0.18) for memory, −0.64 (95% CI: −0.75, −0.52) for language, −0.58 (95% CI: −0.39, −0.18) for executive function, and −0.28 (95% CI: −0.41, −0.16) for orientation factor scores. There was no evidence that numeracy significantly mediated these associations. Although point estimates suggested that numeracy accounted for approximately 2% to 7% of the total association, these indirect effects were weak and were not statistically significant (NIEs: β = −0.02; 95% CI: −0.04, 0.00 for memory; β = −0.01; 95% CI: −0.03, 0.00 for language; β = −0.02; 95% CI: −0.04 0.00 for executive function; and β = −0.03; 95% CI: −0.05, −0.00 for orientation) (Table 2 Panel A; Figure 1).

Literacy

The estimated direct effect of speaking an Indigenous language, independent of literacy, was −0.22 (95% CI: −0.32, −0.12) for memory, −0.58 (95% CI: −0.70, −0.47) for language, −0.49 (95% CI: −0.62, −0.35) for executive function, and −0.23 (95% CI: −0.39, −0.10) for orientation factor scores. In contrast to numeracy, literacy significantly mediated the association between Indigenous language use and cognitive performance across all domains, as the confidence intervals for the indirect effects excluded zero. Specifically, the NIEs were: β = −0.08 (95% CI: −0.12, −0.04) for memory, β = −0.07 (95% CI: −0.11, −0.03) for language, β = −0.11 (95% CI: −0.16, −0.06) for executive function, and β = −0.08 (95% CI: −0.12, −0.04) for orientation factor scores. Overall, literacy accounted for approximately 11%–26% of the total association between speaking an Indigenous language and domain‐specific factor scores (Table 2 Panel B; Figure 1).

Education

The estimated direct effect of speaking an Indigenous language, independent of educational attainment, was −0.12 (95% CI: −0.22, −0.02) for memory, −0.45 (95% CI: −0.56, −0.33) for language, −0.27 (95% CI: −0.39, −0.14) for executive function, and −0.11 (95% CI: −0.24, 0.02) for orientation factor scores. Educational attainment significantly mediated the association between Indigenous language use and cognitive performance across all domains, as the confidence intervals for the indirect effects excluded zero. Specifically, the NIEs were: β = −0.18 (95% CI: −0.24, −0.12) for memory, β = −0.20 (95% CI: −0.27, −0.13) for language, β = −0.33 (95% CI: −0.42, −0.23) for executive function, and β = −0.19 (95% CI: −0.27, −0.11) for orientation factor scores. Overall, educational attainment accounted for approximately 31% to 61% of the total association between speaking an Indigenous language and domain‐specific factor scores (Table 2 Panel C; Figure 1).

Supplemental analyses

Separating literacy effects from formal education

Among participants without any formal schooling, the patterns of the NDEs and NIEs varied across cognitive domains. The total association between speaking an Indigenous language and domain‐specific cognitive performance was attenuated, and most of the estimates crossed the null. Correspondingly, the direct effects of speaking an Indigenous language, representing the portion of the association between Indigenous language use and cognitive performance not explained by literacy differences, were smaller in magnitude and imprecise across most domains. The exception was the language domain, where the estimate was attenuated but remained statistically significant, suggesting a potential language‐of‐testing effect. Conversely, when examining the indirect effects of speaking an Indigenous language through literacy, we found that the influence of literacy either increased in magnitude or remained statistically significant for the memory (β = −0.10; 95% CI: −0.19, −0.00) and executive function domains (β = −0.10; 95% CI: −0.19, −0.01). Given that the total association between Indigenous language status and cognitive performance was attenuated in this subgroup, the proportion of the effect mediated by literacy increased (e.g., NIE/TE). Specifically, among adults with normal schooling, differences in literacy accounted for most of the association between Indigenous language status and memory performance (proportion mediated: 85%), compared with only 26% in the overall sample with varying levels of schooling (Table 3).

Robustness of results to early‐life confounding

When additionally adjusting for early‐life covariates (e.g., early childhood disadvantage), the results were generally the same. While some indirect point estimates showed both increases and attenuations, 95% confidence intervals overlapped with the estimates of the main results (Table S3). Sensitivity analyses using E‐values indicated that, across the four cognitive domains, an unmeasured confounder would need associations of 1.97–3.02 times with both the exposure and outcome to explain away the observed effects – comparable in strength to the effect of carrying one apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele on Alzheimer's disease risk (Appendix S1).

DISCUSSION

In Mexico, older adults who self‐reported speaking an Indigenous language (vs. not) exhibited worse cognitive performance across multiple domains compared to their non‐Indigenous language speaking counterparts. Early‐life educational factors, including numeracy, literacy, and educational attainment, explained between 2% and 61% of these associations, with years of formal schooling emerging as the strongest determinant of differences in late‐life cognitive health by identifying as an Indigenous language speaker (accounting for 31%–61% of the association). Given that Indigenous language status is an imperfect proxy for Indigeneity and encompasses diverse linguistic experiences and forms of social marginalization, these associations likely reflect heterogeneous pathways linking language, education, and cognitive aging (e.g., marginalization and stress‐related processes that may be detrimental, as well as linguistic proficiency or bilingual language use that may be protective). Among older adults with no formal schooling, group differences in cognitive performance between Indigenous‐speaker and non‐Indigenous‐speaker groups were attenuated, while the relative indirect effect of literacy increased. These results suggest that inequities in late‐life cognitive health among aging Indigenous adults in Mexico are largely attributed to inequities in early‐life educational opportunities.

Emerging research on the cognitive health of Indigenous communities in later life remains limited, constrained by small sample sizes, exclusion from datasets, and broader patterns of systemic erasure, particularly in LMIC settings. 39 Recent meta‐analyses indicate that the prevalence of dementia among Indigenous adults 60 years and older residing primarily in high‐income countries varied between 0.5% and 26.8%, whereas the prevalence of cognitive impairment not dementia varied between 4.4% and 17.7%. 18 Prior studies across various countries consistently found that Indigenous older adults perform worse on cognitive function tests compared to non‐Indigenous adults. 14 , 18 , 19 , 20 In addition to worse late‐life cognitive outcomes, Indigenous groups in Latin America also have other worse health outcomes, such as greater cardiovascular disease, functional limitations, and other adverse psychosocial outcomes, all of which are associated with greater dementia risk. 40 The present study adds to this growing body of evidence by beginning to quantify the mechanisms underlying these.

Prior research supports the idea that determinants of late‐life cognitive health among Indigenous adults operate largely through socioeconomic and educational pathways. 18 , 41 , 42 Although Indigenous populations are largely heterogeneous in their cultural, linguistic, and social dimensions, they share elevated exposure to structural disadvantage across the lifecourse and intergenerationally. 43 , 44 In Mexico, Indigenous adults are more likely to encounter discrimination, have limited educational opportunities, exhibit higher rates of illiteracy (e.g., fivefold higher in 2005), reside in rural areas with fewer socioeconomic resources, and face greater barriers to accessing healthcare. 7 , 28 Consistent with this broader context, our results indicate that early‐life educational opportunities mediate up to 61% of the association between identifying as an Indigenous language speaker and late‐life cognition. Future research should examine additional lifecourse mechanisms, including mid‐life cardiometabolic and psychosocial risk factors, as well as downstream SES indicators (e.g., occupation, income, and wealth), to further elucidate the pathways contributing to poorer cognitive outcomes among aging Indigenous populations. Such evidence could help inform population‐level strategies to reduce inequities in cognitive aging.

When analyses were restricted to older adults without formal schooling, mean differences in cognitive performance by identifying as an Indigenous language speaker were generally attenuated or null, suggesting that disparities in late‐life cognition are largely shaped by differential access to formal schooling, including literacy acquisition. The observed indirect effects of literacy underscore its role as a key mechanism linking educational opportunity and cognitive performance in the absence of formal education. This aligns with prior research in the United States, indicating that higher literacy levels may serve as a more salient indicator of dementia risk than years of schooling among older adults with low levels of education, potentially capturing underlying cognitive capacity independent from years of formal schooling. 45 , 46 This is consistent with evidence that literacy enhances economic opportunities and facilitates health management, social participation, and information processing, thereby supporting cognitive maintenance into later life. 23 , 26 , 45 , 46 The direct effect of identifying as an Indigenous language speaker on executive function was small, positive, and not statistically significant, with imprecise estimates likely reflecting small subgroup sizes. While our data do not provide evidence of a cognitive advantage and such interpretations remain speculative given current data limitations, if replicated in larger, adequately powered samples, this trend may reflect sociocultural sources of resilience (e.g., spirituality, collective connectedness) 47 or cognitive benefits of bilingualism, 48 , 49 , 50 and warrants further investigation into factors contributing to cognitive heterogeneity among older Indigenous adults.

While this study has several strengths, such as leveraging a large population‐based cohort of adults in Mexico and a comprehensive cognitive battery, several limitations should be noted. First, beyond self‐reports of speaking an Indigenous language, the MHAS did not collect other measures that might reflect ethnoracial and cultural identity, including skin color, self‐reported identity, or observer‐based assessments of perceived identity. Moreover, the measure did not capture critical details such as which language was spoken. With more than 90 Indigenous languages represented across Mexico, each rooted in distinct sociocultural and sociopolitical contexts, 13 treating Indigenous populations as a single group likely obscures heterogeneous pathways that may uniquely shape cognitive health.

Second, the core MHAS questionnaire and the Mex‐Cog were administered exclusively in Spanish, and Spanish proficiency was not directly measured. While those with limited to no Spanish proficiency could rely on proxy informants to complete the core questionnaire, our analyses relied on direct (non‐proxy) responses to the Mex‐Cog assessment. This suggests that, in some cases, cognitive assessments may reflect Spanish language proficiency rather than cognitive aging or dementia risk. The magnitude and direction of this potential bias are difficult to determine. For example, if bilingualism among Indigenous language speakers confers cognitive benefits, our estimates may be attenuated. Conversely, linguistic mismatch may introduce systematic measurement error, potentially overstating disparities if Indigenous language speakers were not assessed in their strongest language. As indicated by the persistent differences in language performance associated with Indigenous language status among participants with no formal education. At the same time, limited access to formal Spanish‐language education may itself reflect structural disadvantage with long‐term implications for cognitive aging, complicating the distinction between measurement artifact and lived sociolinguistic inequality. 29 However, we were unable to conduct formal quantitative bias analyses to assess the extent of this potential misclassification due to unavailable bias parameters. This limitation also raises concerns about selection bias, as monolingual Indigenous language speakers or those with limited Spanish proficiency may have been excluded, potentially attenuating estimates. Nonetheless, in models restricted to participants with no formal schooling, we still observed evidence of mediating effects of literacy, suggesting that these associations may hold even in the absence of formal education. Future work is required to determine the measurement equivalence of the cognitive battery among culturally distinct groups, such as Indigenous populations.

Third, numeracy and literacy were assessed via self‐report rather than through validated objective measures such as the Wide Range Achievement Test. 51 Additionally, for Indigenous language speakers, it is also unclear whether reported skills reflect Spanish, an Indigenous language, or both, and proficiency level was not assessed. As a result, the observed indirect effects of these skills may be attenuated, particularly given the heterogeneity in abilities among individuals who reported being numerate or literate. Our ability to evaluate numeracy as a mediator was further limited by its high prevalence in this sample, with 96% of participants reporting being numerate.

Lastly, although the data are cross‐sectional, the established temporal ordering of language, education, and late‐life cognition supports the plausibility of interpreting the observed associations as reflecting mediation processes. Given the limitations described, these results should be considered in the context of potential biases that may influence key assumptions. 52 Despite these limitations, self‐reported Indigenous language use remains one of the few available indicators of Indigeneity in large‐scale aging cohorts of older Mexican adults. While imperfect, its inclusion allows for the examination of populations that are otherwise frequently rendered statistically invisible. Future research would benefit from nuanced measures of Indigeneity, language proficiency, numeracy, and literacy, as well as culturally sensitive cognitive assessments.

Our results suggest that early‐life educational opportunities are key drivers of inequities in late‐life cognitive health for older adults in Mexico defined by speaking an Indigenous language. Policies that expand both the quantity and quality of schooling for Indigenous populations have the potential to reduce inequities in cognitive performance and related health outcomes. More broadly, this work highlights the need for greater investment in validated assessments of literacy and numeracy skills in cohort studies, especially for populations with historically limited access to formal education, such as low‐SES and Indigenous older adults across Latin America. Advancing this research also requires richer and more nuanced data on Indigeneity (e.g., self‐identification) that capture the region's extensive cultural, linguistic, and historical diversity. 13 Approaches that rely solely on language as an identifier risk obscuring the heterogeneity of Indigenous communities and perpetuating their erasure in dementia research. Refining these measurement strategies, including evaluating potential measurement invariance in cognitive batteries across linguistically diverse Indigenous groups, will be essential for generating more accurate estimates and improving cross‐population comparability. A complementary collection of brain imaging and biomarker data can further strengthen the robustness of findings by reducing dependence on cognitive batteries that may not perform equivalently across cultural or linguistic contexts. Finally, future work should identify additional structural and systemic drivers of disparities in cognitive aging, including mid‐life health pathways, and determine whether patterns of inequity differ across specific Indigenous groups
