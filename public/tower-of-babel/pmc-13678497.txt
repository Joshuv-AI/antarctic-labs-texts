Simulated accelerated aging controlled by temperature and humidity for regulating the quality of Citri grandis fructus immaturus
Authors: Miao Su, Naiyu Xiao, Zheng Cheng, Lilan Huang, Xinye Ma, Qianbin Mo, Guojian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Citri grandis fructus immaturus (CGFI) is well-known for its dual usage in medicine and food. After aging, it wound bring about the transformation and enrichment of its medicinal components in CGFI. The content of active ingredients including flavonoids (such as naringin and rhoifolin) would increase, making its medicinal properties milder and its taste more mellow. However the transformation of active components in CGFI proceeds slowly under natural aging conditions, rendering conventional aging methods not only time-consuming but also inefficient. To address this issue, the present study employed an accelerated aging simulation under controlled temperature and humidity conditions to comprehensively evaluate changes in the quality attributes of CGFI. Three experimental groups were established for verification. CGFI were periodically assessed for visual appearance, moisture content, infusion color, as well as the contents of total flavonoids, naringin, and rhoifolin. Besides, non-targeted metabolomics analysis was conducted to investigate alterations in metabolite profiles and related pathway enrichment. The results demonstrated that the constant group (35 °C, 75% RH) treatment exhibited favorable aging effect, significantly increased the flavonoid compounds and enhanced antioxidant metabolites accumulation, due to the fact that the stability of flavonoids and tangerine glycosides was highly dependent on the storage environment. The research provides a theoretical foundation and methodological framework for optimizing the aging process of CGFI. This study is expected to provide new insight into the simulated accelerated aging-related evolution of active ingredient, as well as the material basis and sensory features of CGFI, thereby offering a candidate chemical basis and theoretical reference for CGFI quality evaluation and targeted regulation.

Introduction

Citri grandis fructus immaturus (CGFI) refers to the dried immature fruit of Citrus grandis ‘Tomentosa’, a plant belonging to the Rutaceae family.1 The outer peel of the unripe or nearly mature fruit of Citrus grandis ‘Tomentosa’ is normally processed into “Huajuhong”, while the immature fruit is processed into “Huajuhongtai” (also known as Huajuhong Pearl). Both forms possess a long history of medicinal application. CGFI as a genuine medicinal herb, which has the functions of relieving cough and phlegm, regulating qi and strengthening the spleen, aiding digestion and eliminating food accumulation, and fighting bacteria and inflammation. Its core advantage is that having a remarkable effect in relieving phlegm and cough, and it has been included in the “food and medicine sharing the same origin” directory. It can also be used for daily soaking in water or making soup with. CGFI is a valuable medicinal material, esteemed not only for its therapeutic applications but also as a collectible investment commodity-particularly in aged form.2,3 As documented in Compendium of Materia Medica, “fine-quality Juhong achieves greater efficacy with prolonged aging.” Since the Song dynasty, CGFI has been renowned as “Southern Ginseng” (Nanfang Renshen) and described as being “worth a piece of gold per slice”.4 In recent years, the industry of CGFI has presented a favorable development trend. According to statistics, the market scale reached 0.18 billion dollar in 2023, with a year-on-year growth of 8.6%, and the total demand approximated 18 000 tons. The usage and sales volume have also maintained steady growth. Since its inclusion in the Catalog of Medicinal and Food Homologous Substances in 2024, the development and application of CGFI have further expanded, leading to the derivation of over 200 related products spanning various sectors such as food and beverages. This expansion has significantly contributed to regional economic development.5 Standardization across all segments of industrial chain continues to improve, the coverage rate of high-quality cultivars has increased, and the number of market entities has grown markedly, indicating a solid industrial foundation and promising development potential.

The belief that Citri grandis fructus immaturus “improves with aging” is primarily rooted in classical textual records.6 Li Shizhen of the Ming dynasty noted in Compendium of Materia Medica: “Fine-quality Juhong gains efficacy with prolonged aging”.7 Similarly, Notes on the Classic of Materia Medica Bencao Jing Jizhu stated: “Fructus Aurantii Immaturus and Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium should be aged for optimal effect, while others are best used fresh”.8 To systematically investigate the rationale behind this tradition for CGFI, it is essential to first understand the variation in chemical composition and pharmacological effects across different aging periods. Modern scientific reviews on herbal aging have summarized that the primary purposes of aging traditional Chinese medicines include reducing harsh properties, diminishing toxicity, and side effects, and moderating thermal characteristics. As a key area within this field, citrus-based medicinal materials commonly exhibit enhanced efficacy after aging,9 providing an important reference for understanding the aging mechanism of CGFI. Along with Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium (Chenpi) and Fructus aurantii (Zhiqiao), CGFI belongs to the citrus herb category, characterized by acrid flavor and warm nature. While the fresh product tends to dissipate qi and potentially impair vital energy, aged specimens show moderated properties. Research on the relationship between chemical transformations and pharmacological effects during aging is still ongoing.10 For instance, Wei et al.11 reported dynamic changes in flavonoids such as naringin and kaempferol in CGFI over storage time, which were closely associated with the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. Studies on Chenpi by Bian et al.8 demonstrated that phenolic acid content increased with prolonged aging, correlating positively with enhanced antioxidant activity. A study by Zhang et al.12 on Sichuan Chenpi (Citrus reticulata ‘Chachi’) demonstrated that the contents of flavonoids and phenolic acids altered with prolonged storage time, particularly in terms of total flavonoids and total phenolics, which initially increased and subsequently declined. Citrus plants are rich in volatile compounds that confer their characteristic aroma and flavor. Through sensory evaluation combined with metabolomic analysis, Liu et al.9 revealed that the flavor profile of Guang Chenpi is primarily constituted by terpenes such as γ-terpinene and β-myrcene, along with components including nonanal and dimethyl anthranilate. The main active ingredients of CGFI are flavonoids, mainly including naringin, rhoifolin, etc. These flavonoids have powerful antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, and are also one of the key components for CGFI to exert various pharmacological activities. Naringin belongs to flavonoid compounds, and its chemical structure contains a flavonoid skeleton and a sugar group part. Rhoifolin belongs to the phenylpropyl compounds, and its chemical structure contains one benzene ring and multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups. The most core and distinctive component of CGFI is naringin, which is usually the standard component used to control its quality. In addition, rhoifolin is also an important substance that demonstrate the efficacy.

In the field of accelerated aging techniques, current research primarily employs methods such as controlled temperature and humidity,13 physical interventions (e.g. irradiation,14,15 ultrasonication,16 and microbial inoculation17) to simulate natural aging processes. Some researchers have previously proposed patented accelerated aging methods for Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium based on temperature and humidity regulation, demonstrating the feasibility of this approach.18 Liu et al.9 demonstrated that the flavor attributes of Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium tea liquid enhanced with the extension of aging time, and limonene-1,2-diol was identified as an important flavor enhancer. Tang et al.19 pointed out that flavonoids in Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium exhibited a “degradation to resynthesis” trajectory, with polymethoxyflavones steadily accumulating throughout the aging process. However, research on accelerated aging of CGFI remains at a preliminary stage. Few researches have correlated the aging methods with the chemical components and pharmacological effects under investigation. Standardized experimental protocols have not yet been established, and the synergistic mechanisms of different environmental stresses on its quality formation pathways are still unclear. Considering the presence of many active ingredients (mainly flavonoid, including naringin and rhoifolin) in CGFI, relatively little research on the changes of these substances during the aging process, and conventional aging methods not only time-consuming but also inefficient, we hypothesized that the facile simulated accelerated aging method could effectively promote the continuous enrichment and transformation of these core functional components during the aging process. To verify these hypotheses, a detailed investigation was conducted to examine the effects of three kinds of simulated accelerated aging on the quality of CGFI. Therefore, this study designed accelerated aging experiments under different temperature and humidity conditions to systematically evaluate the dynamic changes in appearance traits, main active components, and metabolomic profiles of CGFI during simulated aging. The aim was to elucidate its quality formation patterns, establish a reliable accelerated aging evaluation model, and provide a theoretical basis and methodological support for optimizing the aging process and quality control of CGFI.

Materials and methods

Materials

Citri grandis fructus immaturus (production area: Huazhou City, Guangdong Province; harvested in 2023, diameter: 35 ± 5 mm) were purchased from Qingping Herbal Medicine Market, Guangdong, China; methanol, naringin, rhoifolin, petroleum ether, aluminum nitrate, acetonitrile, acetic acid, and isopropanol were provided by Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All other chemicals were AR grade and can be used without further purification.

Simulated accelerated aging treatment of CGFI

Uniform CGFI fruits with similar coloration were selected and randomly divided into three groups (n ≥ 120 per group). From each group, a subset (n = 3) was randomly selected for photographic documentation at each sampling time point. The groups were subjected to the following conditions: 40 °C group (dynamic cycling group): dynamic cyclic acceleration (40 °C/40% RH for 4 days + 25 °C/95% RH for 2 days), with cycles repeated over 4 months; 35 °C group (constant group): constant acceleration (35 °C/75% RH), with the chamber opened and ventilated for 5 minutes at regular intervals (7 days) during the 4 month period; 25 °C group (control group): non-accelerated control (25 ± 3 °C/45 ± 10% RH, ventilated), maintained synchronously with the experimental timeline. Sampling was triggered when a noticeable color difference was first observed in the accelerated groups. Periodic sampling was subsequently conducted every 30 days, accompanied by photographic recording. For each sampling, specimens were randomly selected from homogeneous outer regions (0–10 mm beneath the epidermis), immediately frozen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. The aging process were maintained meticulous and strict ventilation, dryness, and coolness precisely to inhibit the growth of harmful microorganisms.

Appearance observation and moisture measurement

The appearance of the samples was photographed under consistent lighting and height conditions. The fruits were ground using a pulverizer, and the moisture content was determined with a rapid moisture analyzer (CS-001M, Shenzhen Guanya Technology Co., Ltd).

Color measurement of infusion

The samples were pulverized (sieved through a 60-mesh sieve) and stored for subsequent analysis. Exactly 0.3 g of the powder was weighed, mixed with 20 mL of boiling water, and allowed to stand for 30 min. The color values (L, a, b*) of the infusion were then measured using a colorimeter (CR8, Shenzhen ThreeNH Technology Co., Ltd).

Determination of total flavonoid content in CGFI

The total flavonoid content was determined according to the method described as follow: briefly, 0.5 g of powdered sample (sieved through a 60-mesh sieve) was accurately weighed and initially treated with petroleum ether via ultrasonication (300 W) for 60 min. After standing, the petroleum ether was removed using rotary evaporation. Then, 50 mL of methanol was precisely added, followed by ultrasonication for 45 min. The solution was cooled to room temperature, and 1 mL of the solution was accurately pipetted into a 10 mL volumetric flask, which was brought to volume with methanol. 1 mL aliquot of the diluted sample solution was transferred to a 10 mL volumetric flask, mixed with 1 mL of 10% aluminum nitrate solution, and diluted to volume with methanol. After standing for 20 min, the absorbance was measured at 385 nm. A reference solution was prepared by accurately weighing 5 mg of naringin standard, dissolving it in methanol, and diluting to obtain a final concentration of 0.1 mg mL−1. Aliquots of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 mL of the naringin reference solution were transferred separately into 10 mL volumetric flasks. Then, 1 mL of 10% aluminum nitrate solution was added to each flask, and the volume was adjusted with methanol. A solution containing only 10% aluminum nitrate was used as the blank. After thorough mixing and standing for 20 min, the absorbance was measured at 385 nm. A standard curve was plotted with absorbance as the (y-axis) and naringin concentration as the (x-axis). 1 mL aliquot of the test sample solution was mixed with 1 mL of 10% aluminum nitrate solution and diluted to 10 mL with methanol. After standing for 20 min, the absorbance was measured at 385 nm against a blank prepared with 10% aluminum nitrate solution. All measurements were performed in triplicate. The total flavonoid content was calculated based on the standard curve.

Determination of naringin and rhoifolin content in CGFI

The contents of naringin and rhoifolin were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) according to the method described by Chavda et al.20 Appropriate amounts of naringin (approximately 30 mg) and rhoifolin (approximately 4.8 mg) reference standards were accurately weighed and dissolved in methanol to prepare stock solutions. These stock solutions were subsequently diluted to obtain standard solutions at different concentrations. The naringin concentrations were 0.15, 0.30, 0.60, 0.90, 1.20, and 1.50 mg mL−1, while the rhoifolin concentrations were 0.024, 0.048, 0.096, 0.144, 0.192, and 0.24 mg mL−1. All solutions were filtered through a membrane, and the filtrates were collected. Exactly 0.5 g of powdered sample was accurately weighed, initially treated with petroleum ether under ultrasonication, and dried to remove the solvent. The residue was then extracted with methanol via ultrasonication. The resulting solution was filtered, and the filtrate was collected for analysis. For the chromatographic test, separation was performed on an Ecosil C18 AQ column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) maintained at 35 °C. The mobile phase consisted of methanol (A) and a mixture of glacial acetic acid and water (0.2 : 62, v/v) (B), using the following gradient program: 0–35 min, 25% A → 55% A; 35–38 min, 55% A → 25% A; 38–45 min, 25% A. The flow rate was 1.0 mL min−1, and the injection volume was 10 µL. Detection wavelengths were set at 283 nm and 330 nm. Both reference and test sample solutions were injected under the above chromatographic conditions, and chromatograms were recorded. Determination of non-targeted metabolites in CGFI

The samples were freeze-dried and precisely weighed (25 ± 1 mg). Homogenization beads and 1000 µL of extraction solvent (methanol : acetonitrile : water = 2 : 2 : 1, v/v), containing isotope-labeled internal standards, were added. The mixtures were homogenized (35 Hz, 4 min) using a homogenizer, followed by sonication in an ice-water bath for 5 min. This procedure was repeated three times. The samples were then stored at −40 °C for 1 h and subsequently centrifuged at 4 °C and 12 000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, transferred to injection vials, and subjected to LC-MS analysis. Additionally, equal volumes of supernatant from all samples were pooled to generate quality control (QC) samples, which were analyzed under identical conditions. For the liquid spectrometry, chromatographic separation was performed on a Kinetex C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 2.6 µm) maintained at 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.01% acetic acid in water (A) and a mixture of acetonitrile and isopropanol (1 : 1, v/v) (B). The following gradient elution program was applied: 0–0.5 min, 1% B; 0.5–4 min, 1% B to 99% B; 4–4.5 min, 99% B; 4.5–4.55 min, 99% B to 1% B; 4.55–6 min, 1% B. The flow rate was 0.3 mL min−1, and the injection volume was 2.0 µL. The sample tray temperature was maintained at 4 °C. For the mass spectrometry, analysis was conducted using a mass spectrometer with the following parameters: sheath gas flow rate, 50 arbitrary units (Arb); auxiliary gas flow rate, 15 Arb; capillary temperature, 320 °C; full scan resolution, 60 000; MS/MS resolution, 15 000; stepped normalized collision energy (SNCE), 20/30/40; spray voltage, 3.8 kV (positive ion mode) or −3.4 kV (negative ion mode).

Statistical analysis

Data on infusion color, moisture content, total flavonoids, naringin, and rhoifolin from CGFI treated under different accelerated aging conditions were statistically analyzed using SPSS 27.0 software. For data conforming to a normal distribution with homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by LSD test was applied. For data with a normal distribution but heterogeneity of variance, Dunnett's T3 test was used for pairwise comparisons. Graphical data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of three replicates. For metabolomics data processing, the raw dataset comprised 4 quality control (QC) samples and 21 experimental samples, from which 60 183 peaks were extracted. The data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard software, and metabolite identification was performed based on the BiotreeDB (V3.0) database developed by BenaGen. To minimize the impact of technical variability and enhance biological interpretability, the raw data underwent preprocessing including outlier filtering, missing value filtering, missing value imputation, and data normalization. Subsequent visualization and analysis were conducted using the ggplot2 package in R.

Results and discussion

Effects of different storage conditions on the appearance and infusion color of CGFI

Fig. 1a illustrates the impact of different storage conditions on the appearance of CGFI. Observations and analyses were conducted at four time points: 0, 60, 90, and 120 days. In the 35 °C group, noticeable color changes occurred as early as 90 days of storage. The initial light yellowish-brown outer surface and yellowish-white inner part gradually darkened to a deep yellowish-brown exterior and light yellow interior. This alteration may be attributed to the accelerated oxidative degradation of active components, particularly flavonoids, under high temperature and humidity conditions. In contrast, color changes progressed more slowly in both the 25 °C and 40 °C groups, with similar dark yellowish-brown outer and light yellow inner colors observed only after 120 days. These results suggested that the chemical reaction rates were lower under constant room temperature and humidity, while the fluctuating conditions in the dynamic cycling group may have partially inhibited certain degradation reactions. Although the peak temperature in the 40 °C group was higher than that in the 35 °C group, its cyclically varying humidity likely resulted in distinct degradation kinetics. This indicated that humidity may exert a greater influence than temperature on the stability of CGFI during storage. In conclusion, the appearance stability of CGFI was significantly affected by storage conditions, with the 35 °C group promoting earlier color changes, potentially due to enhanced enzymatic browning reactions. According to previous studies, the color of the infusion is closely associated with storage duration.21,22 As shown in Fig. 1b–d, under all three storage conditions, the brightness (L* value) of the infusion of CGFI did not change significantly, indicating that storage conditions had no notable effect on the lightness of the infusion. In the 25 °C group, the a* value increased significantly at 60 days, suggesting enhanced red tones. In the 35 °C group, the a* value also rose markedly by 60 days and then remained stable, exceeding the level observed in the 25 °C group. For the 40 °C group, the a* value increased significantly at 60 days, reaching its peak, before declining thereafter; however, the overall trend was similar to that in the 35 °C group. These color changes are consistent with those reported in aged tea infusions,22 indicating that the infusion of CGFI became darker and redder with extended aging. The reddening may result from the accumulation of carotenoids.23 Under the 25 °C condition, the b* value increased significantly at 60 days and continued to rise gradually thereafter, reflecting a progressive enhancement of yellow tones in the infusion. In both the 35 °C and 40 °C groups, the b* values increased rapidly by 60 days, reaching levels comparable to those observed in the 25 °C group at 120 days. These results suggested that both the 35 °C and 40 °C conditions accelerated the development of red and yellow tones in the infusion, with the 35 °C treatment exerting a more pronounced effect than the 40 °C condition.

Effects of different storage conditions on the moisture, total flavonoid, naringin, and rhoifolin content of CGFI

The mechanism diagram of simulated accelerated aging of CGFI is exhibited in Fig. 2a. During the aging process, through a series of physical and chemical changes, the volatile oils (such as limonene) and other irritating components were transformed into a mellow aged aroma. And the concentration of effective active ingredients (such as flavonoids) was increased, ultimately making the medicinal properties more mellow and the taste better. In order to quantitatively analyze the changes of relevant components, the moisture, total flavonoid, naringin, and rhoifolin content of CGFI were investigated. As shown in Fig. 2b, the moisture content of CGFI was considerably influenced by environmental conditions. In the 25 °C group, moisture content initially increased and subsequently decreased over time. No significant differences in moisture content were observed between the 35 °C and 25 °C groups across the three time points. In contrast, the 40 °C condition significantly affected moisture content, resulting in notably lower values compared to the 25 °C group at all time points. These findings demonstrated that both temperature and humidity significantly influenced the moisture content of CGFI. The temporal dynamics of the total flavonoid content in CGFI under different storage conditions is illustrated in Fig. 2c. The 25 °C group exhibited a slow yet steady increasing trend. In contrast, both the 35 °C and 40 °C groups showed a V-shaped fluctuation pattern, characterized by an initial decline followed by a subsequent rise. Notably, the 35 °C group reached its lowest value at 60 days, then rebounded significantly and achieved the highest levels during the 90–120 day period, surpassing both other groups. The marked increase in total flavonoid content in the 35 °C group during the later storage phase is consistent with the findings of Fu et al.,24 who reported that alternating high-temperature and high-humidity conditions accelerated the increase of five flavonoid components in Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium throughout storage. Since temperature and humidity influenced the transformation of plant secondary metabolites,25,26 this nonlinear variation pattern offered a new perspective for understanding the chemical stability and quality evolution of CGFI under different storage conditions. Regardless of the variation patterns, the total flavonoid content in the 35 °C group was significantly higher than that in the 25 °C group by the end of the storage period. This finding holds potential practical implications, suggesting that the 35 °C accelerated condition may serve as an effective method to enhance the content of flavonoid active compounds in CGFI. The more flavonoid active components accumulated during the aging process, the greater the nutritional and medicinal value of the CGFI would be. Despite these important findings, several limitations need to be acknowledged. There is a possibility of microbial intervention during the aging process of CGFI. Studies have revealed that microbial communities (such as fungal) played a pivotal role in the Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium aging process.27,28 Some findings provided direct evidence that Penicillium and Aspergillus not only dominated the early and mid-aging stages but also actively participated in flavonoid metabolism through their enzymatic activities during the Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium aging process.19 The microbial involvement of CGFI accelerated aging has not been investigated for the time being, while it would be systematically investigated in our future study. The temporal changes in naringin content in CGFI under different storage conditions is shown in Fig. 2d. Under all three conditions, the naringin content exhibited a trend of initial decrease followed by a subsequent increase, though the extent of change varied significantly among the groups. During the early storage period (0–60 days), the naringin content decreased to varying degrees in all groups, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the 35 °C group. The naringin content in the 40 °C group declined to approximately 110 mg g−1, while that in the 25 °C group showed a smaller decrease, reaching about 116 mg g−1. Statistical analysis indicated that at 60 days, the naringin content in both the 35 °C and 40 °C groups was significantly lower than that in the 25 °C group. By 90 days of storage, the naringin content in all three groups had begun to rebounded, with no significant differences observed among the groups. At 120 days, notable differences in naringin content emerged under the different storage conditions. The naringin content in the 35 °C group increased to approximately 133 mg g−1, which was significantly higher than that in the 25 °C group. In contrast, the content in the 40 °C group remained at about 116 mg g−1, which was significantly lower than that in the 25 °C group.

(a) Flavonoid transformation mechanism during simulated accelerated aging process, changes in (b) moisture content, (c) total flavonoids, (d) naringin content, and (e) rhoifolin content of CGFI under different storage conditions.

These results indicated that prolonged storage time significantly promoted an increase in naringin content in the 35 °C group, whereas the conditions in the 40 °C group may not be conducive to naringin transformation. As a flavonoid glycoside, changes in naringin content may be influenced by multiple factors.29,30 From a molecular mechanism perspective, during the initial storage period, both the 35 °C and 40 °C groups likely facilitated the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds and oxidation reactions by modulating enzymatic activity under specific temperature and humidity conditions,31,32 resulting in a significant decrease in naringin content compared to the 25 °C group. As storage time extended, the conditions in the 35 °C group may have activated specific enzymatic reactions,33 promoting the conversion of precursor compounds into naringin, whereas the conditions in the 40 °C group appeared less favorable for this transformation. Because in the aging process of CGFI, an appropriate temperature range is required to promote the transformation of active ingredients (such as naringin). Excessively high temperatures will accelerate the evaporation of oil, leading to the loss of effective components and even mold. If the temperature is too low, it will delay the transformation of components and prolong the aging period. As one of the primary active constituents in CGFI, naringin exhibited various pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiviral effects.34,35 In conclusion, the conditions in the 35 °C group significantly enhanced naringin content after 120 days of storage. Besides, the temporal dynamics of rhoifolin content in CGFI under different storage conditions is depicted in Fig. 2e. The content of rhoifolin varied distinctly across storage regimens. In the 25 °C group, the rhoifolin content exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease, peaking at 60 days before gradually declining. A similar trend was observed in the 40 °C group. In contrast, under 35 °C conditions, the rhoifolin content decreased around 60 days, after which it demonstrated a fluctuating upward trend. This variability may be attributed to combined temperature- and humidity-induced chemical alterations. Overall, these patterns suggested that the influence of temperature and humidity on rhoifolin was more complex under 35 °C conditions and warrants further in-depth investigation. After 120 days of storage, the rhoifolin content in the 35 °C group was significantly higher than that in the 25 °C group. As an important flavonoid compound in CGFI, rhoifolin was one of its key active constituents, known for its lipid-lowering and antiviral effects.36 Comprehensive analysis of the dynamic changes in appearance, moisture content, total flavonoids, naringin, and rhoifolin content in CGFI under different storage conditions revealed notable differences in the physical and chemical characteristics of the 35 °C group. Physically, samples stored at 35 °C exhibited earlier darkening in color, and the infusion also developed a reddish hue at an earlier stage. Chemically, although the contents of total flavonoids, naringin, and rhoifolin showed complex fluctuations, these components were generally higher in the 35 °C group than in the 25 °C group by the later storage period. This suggested that the 35 °C condition may accelerate the chemical reactions within CGFI, promoting the transformation and accumulation of active constituents, thereby influencing its overall quality. During the CGFI accelerated aging process, enzymatic browning may occur, but it is not the core stage of aging. The browning of CGFI is essentially the oxidation of phenolic substances into quinone polymers catalyzed by polyphenol oxidase, and the conditions require the simultaneous presence of phenolic substrates, active enzymes, and oxygen. The deepening of color that occurs during the aging stage is more of non-enzymatic browning (such as the Maillard reaction) and component oxidation transformation, rather than enzymatic browning. Research on Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium also showed that during the aging process, enzymatic oxidation and non-enzymatic browning coexist.37 The Maillard reaction is one of the important chemical changes that would occur during the aging process of CGFI. During the natural aging process, the Maillard reaction occurs slowly, but high-temperature accelerated aging significantly speeds up this reaction. The Maillard reaction is a real natural chemical pathway that exists in the aging process of CGFI. Together with enzymatic oxidation, microbial action, etc., it shapes the quality characteristic of “the older, the better”.

Based on these preliminary results, further metabolomic analysis comparing the 35 °C and 25 °C groups was warranted to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of elevated temperature and humidity on metabolite profiles in CGFI. Comparative assessment of metabolic dynamics under these two storage conditions would provide deeper insights into the mechanisms through which temperature and humidity regulate chemical transformations.

Analysis of metabolites in CGFI under different storage conditions

The stability of the data was evaluated by analyzing quality control (QC) samples, which were prepared by pooling aliquots from all sample extracts. As shown in Fig. 3a and b, all QC samples fell within the range of ±2 standard deviations (STD), and the correlations among QC samples exceeded 0.9. A correlation closer to 1 indicated better methodological stability and higher data quality. Therefore, the data obtained in this experiment were of high quality. The raw dataset included 4 QC samples and 21 experimental samples: an untreated sample at day 0 (denoted as 0 d), three time points (60, 90, and 120 days) under 25 °C storage (denoted as 25 °C-60 d, 25 °C-90 d, 25 °C-120 d; n = 3), and three time points under 35 °C storage (denoted as 35 °C-60 d, 35 °C-90 d, 35 °C-120 d; n = 3). The raw data were annotated and classified according to the latest database, as illustrated in Fig. 3c and d. After preprocessing, 2762 secondary identified compounds were retained. These compounds were categorized into 8 classes based on the HMDB database secondary classification: shikimic acids and phenylpropanoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, amino acids and short peptides, fatty acids, polyketides, carbohydrates, and others.

Multivariate statistical analysis of metabolites in CGFI

Multivariate statistical analysis was applied to comprehensively evaluate the metabolites of CGFI under different storage conditions, with consideration of two explanatory variables: treatment condition (25 °C group and 35 °C group) and processing time (0, 60, 90, and 120 days). An unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to characterize metabolic differences among sample groups, using the 2762 metabolites as dependent variables and the samples as independent variables. The analysis demonstrated clear separation among the sample groups based on both storage condition and time (Fig. 4a). Subsequently, the top 90 metabolites ranked by interquartile range were selected to generate a clustered heatmap. Samples were annotated by time (0, 60, 90, and 120 days) and treatment condition (25 °C and 35 °C groups). In the heatmap, red indicated higher relative abundance and blue indicated lower relative abundance. As shown in Fig. 4b, most metabolites exhibited earlier up- or down-regulation in the 35 °C group compared to the 25 °C group, suggesting that the 35 °C condition may accelerate changes in certain metabolic pathways. Furthermore, pattern correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the treatment conditions (Fig. 4c and d). Among the metabolites exhibiting the highest correlation with the variable “treatment condition”, ethyl ferulate and monobutyl phthalate demonstrated the strongest positive correlations. This indicated that under 35 °C storage conditions, the levels of these metabolites increased at 60, 90, and 120 days. Conversely, dodecanedioic acid, ethyl octanoate, and n-decanoic acid showed the most significant negative correlations, suggesting a reduction in their contents under the same conditions. The accumulation of positively correlated metabolites implies enhanced physiological activity. Ethyl ferulate, a common antioxidant in plants, possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective activities.38,39 Monobutyl phthalate, as a secondary metabolite, may participate in plant defense mechanisms.40 Their positive correlations suggested that 35 °C storage conditions may enhance the biosynthesis of biologically active molecules through upregulation of associated metabolic pathways. The reduction in negatively correlated metabolites reflects decreased metabolic stress. Dodecanedioic acid, an oxidation product of long-chain fatty acids, is often associated with oxidative damage such as lipid peroxidation.41 The negative correlation indicated that 35 °C storage may suppress stress-induced lipid degradation, thereby reducing harmful metabolic by-products. The decrease in decanoic acid and ethyl octanoate may reflect an optimization of energy metabolism pathways.

Comparative metabolomic analysis with specificity in CGFI

Data from different time points (60, 90, and 120 days) were analyzed separately to examine the further changes in metabolites of CGFI treated under the 35 °C condition. Comparisons were made between the two treatment groups (25 °C and 35 °C) at each time point (60, 90, and 120 days). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) confirmed clear separation between the sample groups (25 °C and 35 °C treated CGFI) at 60, 90, and 120 days of treatment (Fig. 5a–c). After 60 days, the OPLS-DA model constructed with the first two components explained 76.7% of the total variance (PC1-64.4%, PC2-12.3%; Fig. 5a); after 90 days, it explained 74.6% of the total variance (PC1-65.3%, PC2-9.3%; Fig. 5b); and after 120 days, it explained 76.2% of the total variance (PC1-67.7%, PC2-8.5%; Fig. 5c). Permutation tests (200 iterations) were performed for each OPLS-DA model, and as shown in Fig. 5d–f, no overfitting was observed in the predictive models (P < 0.05), confirming the validity of the models.

Using screening criteria of VIP > 1 and FC > 2 or FC < 0.5, 498 differential metabolites were identified between the 35 °C and 25 °C treatment groups after 60 days of storage; 412 differential metabolites were observed after 90 days; and 399 differential metabolites were detected after 120 days (Fig. 6a–c). Over time, the number of up-regulated metabolites increased while down-regulated metabolites decreased. At 60 days, a greater proportion of metabolites were down-regulated, indicating an initial predominantly negative regulatory response to the 35 °C condition. By 90 days, the number of up-regulated metabolites increased compared to the 60 day time point, suggesting that CGFI may have begun to adapt to the environmental conditions through metabolic adjustments. The shift toward more up-regulated than down-regulated metabolites reflected a transition toward active metabolic adaptation. At 120 days, significantly more metabolites were up-regulated with a notable reduction in down-regulated compounds, demonstrating progressive metabolic adaptation to the storage environment and suggesting the potential activation of adaptive or repair mechanisms. This temporal pattern indicated enhanced positive regulatory responsed that may help avoid accumulation of detrimental metabolic products. Finally, a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)-based pathway analysis was performed to further investigate the effects of 35 °C storage condition on various metabolic pathways. The results revealed significant alterations induced by the 35 °C treatment, affecting 14 pathways at 60 days, 8 pathways at 90 days, and 25 pathways at 120 days (Fig. 6d–f). The top three most significantly impacted pathways, ranked by impact value (a topological analysis-derived metric), were identified for each time point. At 60 days, the most prominently affected pathways included the biosynthesis of flavonoids and flavonols, amino acid metabolism such as alanine metabolism, and flavonoid biosynthesis. Flavonoids are among the major active constituents in CGFI, including compounds such as naringin and rhoifolin.42 The impact on this pathway suggested that the early stage of aging may promote the formation or transformation of these active components. Changes in amino acid metabolism, particularly alanine metabolism, may be associated with the development of aroma and taste.43 Flavonoids represent critical bioactive components in CGFI, and the significant alterations in this pathway further supported the positive influence of the aging process on flavonoid synthesis. The increase in flavonoids during the aging of CGFI is actually the result of the combined action of multiple physical, enzymatic, and chemical transformations. Firstly, the bound flavonoid, which originally exists in large quantities in CGFI and combines with sugar, organic acids, and proteins, cannot be directly detected by routine testing. During the aging process, endogenous enzymes such as glycosidase and esterase act slowly and continuously, breaking these binding bonds and converting them into detectable free flavonoids, directly increasing the flavonoid content. Secondly, under conditions of moderate oxygen, temperature and humidity, the small-molecule precursor substances such as phenolic acids and simple terpenoids in CGFI will undergo slow oxidation, cyclization, and rearrangement reactions. New flavonoids and flavonol derivatives are gradually generated, and these newly added substances will be directly included in the determination results of flavonoids. Finally, during the aging process, the free water and bound water in CGFI continuously and slowly dissipated, and the overall dry matter weight decreased significantly. Flavonoids are non-volatile components that hardly lose moisture. It is equivalent to being physically concentrated in a unit weight of the sample, and the measured flavonoid content value further increases. At 90 days, the most affected pathways were tyrosine metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and flavonoid biosynthesis. Tyrosine metabolism is linked to melanin production,44 which may relate to color changes observed during the aging of CGFI. Alterations in the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids such as cysteine and methionine may influence its characteristic odor. The continued significant changes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway indicated that flavonoid transformation was an ongoing process, further confirming the impact of aging on flavonoid constituents. By 120 days, the most significantly impacted pathways were pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, β-alanine metabolism, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism. The pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis pathway was involved in various metabolic reactions,45 thereby influencing the metabolite profile of CGFI. In summary, these results demonstrated that the alterations in metabolic pathways at different aging time points constituted a dynamic process rather than a simple linear progression.

Conclusion

In summary, the study employed a simulated accelerated aging controlled by temperature and humidity to investigate the effects on the quality of Citri grandis fructus immaturus. The results revealed that the constant group (35 °C, 75% RH) storage condition significantly influenced the physicochemical properties and dynamic changes in metabolites of CGFI. Compared to the dynamic cycling group and control group, the constant group exhibited an earlier darkening of appearance. The infusion color study demonstrated earlier increases in redness (a* value) and yellowness (b* value) compared to the control group, which may accelerated the process of changing the red and yellow tones of the infusion color for CFGI. No significant difference in moisture content was observed between the constant group and control group, indicating maintained fundamental product stability. Notably, the constant group (35 °C, 75% RH) condition significantly promoted the accumulation of total flavonoids and key active constituents such as naringin and rhoifolin during the later storage period, which was of great significance for enhancing the medicinal value of CGFI. Metabolomic analysis demonstrated that the constant group condition accelerated changes in most metabolites. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the pathways affected by the constant group treatment were closely associated with the formation of flavonoid-based active components. The constant group (35 °C, 75% RH) condition can thus be considered a viable accelerated storage protocol that correlates with natural storage conditions, offering the dual advantages of reducing experimental duration while maintaining quality evolution patterns similar to natural aging. Furthermore, it may enhance the accumulation of certain beneficial bioactive compounds. These findings demonstrated the effects of simulated accelerated aging conditions on the quality of CGFI, and provided a scientific basis for understanding the mechanism of CGFI aging process. It also clarified the material basis and sensory features of CGFI, and support the quality evaluation-regulation, and storage management. In consideration of the simulated accelerated aging process of CGFI is eco-friendly, easy operation, time-saving, and high-efficiency, this approach has a good application prospect in CGFI storage. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The present study was conducted using CGFI from one region, and the attributes mainly including visual appearance, moisture content, infusion color, total flavonoids, naringin, and rhoifolin. Future studies involving CGFI from more regions, bioactivity, microbial involvement, electronic tongue analysis, and specific reaction pathways are needed to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the simulated accelerated aging of CGFI.
