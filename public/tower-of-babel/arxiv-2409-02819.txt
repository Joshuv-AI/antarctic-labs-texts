Provably Efficient Simulation of 1D Long-Range Interacting Systems at Any Temperature
Rakesh Achutha 1,2, Donghoon Kim 1, Yusuke Kimura 1, Tomotaka Kuwahara 1,3,4

Abstract
We introduce a method that ensures efficient computation of one-dimensional quantum systems with long-range interactions across all temperatures. Our algorithm operates within a quasi-polynomial runtime for inverse temperatures up to β = poly ⁡ ( ln ⁡ ( n ) ) \beta={\rm poly}(\ln(n)) . At the core of our approach is the Density Matrix Renormalization Group algorithm, which typically does not guarantee efficiency. We have created a new truncation scheme for the matrix product operator of the quantum Gibbs states, which allows us to control the error analytically. Additionally, our method can be applied to simulate the time evolution of systems with long-range interactions, achieving significantly better precision than that offered by the Lieb-Robinson bound.

In this letter, we introduce an efficiency-guaranteed method for constructing the MPO based on the DMRG algorithm. Our method achieves a runtime of:

\displaystyle e^{\mathcal{O}(\beta\ln^{3}(n/\epsilon))}\quad\text{(general cases)},

(1)

Furthermore, while our primary focus has been on constructing MPOs for quantum Gibbs states, our approach is equally applicable to real-time evolutions.
To our knowledge, this is the first rigorous justification of a classical simulation method with guaranteed efficiency for managing the real and imaginary-time dynamics of long-range interacting Hamiltonians.

In terms of technical advancements, the traditional DMRG algorithm faced challenges in accurately estimating the truncation errors of bond dimensions during iterative steps
[

7

Hence, the primary challenge lies in the construction of the high-temperature MPO in the first step. We begin by constructing the quantum Gibbs state in independent small blocks and iteratively merge them into larger ones.
This approach retains the spirit of the original DMRG algorithm developed by White

A key innovation of our algorithm is the efficient control of the precision in the merging process, quantified by the general Schatten

Setup.—
We consider a one-dimensional quantum system composed of
n
qudits, each in a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space. The total set of sites in this system is denoted by
\Lambda
, i.e.,
\Lambda=\{1,2,3,\dots,n\}
. We define a
k
-local Hamiltonian
H
for this system by:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{|Z|\leq k}h_{Z},\quad\max_{i\in\Lambda}\sum_{Z:Z\ni i}\|h_{Z}\|\leq g,

(3)

where
Z\subset\Lambda
, and
|Z|
represents the cardinality of
Z
, meaning the number of sites involved in each interaction term
h_{Z}
. The term
\|\cdots\|
denotes the operator norm, which is the maximum singular value of the operator. For any arbitrary subset
L\subseteq\Lambda
, the subset Hamiltonian
H_{L}
is defined to include only those interaction terms involving sites in
L
, specifically:

\displaystyle H_{L}=\sum_{Z:Z\subset L}h_{Z}.

To elucidate the assumptions underpinning our analysis, let us consider a decomposition of the total system
\Lambda
into subsets
A
and
B
such that
\Lambda=A\sqcup B
. Here, subset
A
is defined as
[i,i^{\prime}]\cap\Lambda
with
1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n
. We assume that the norm of the boundary interaction between
A
and
B
, denoted by
\|\partial h_{A}\|
, is finite:

\displaystyle\|\partial h_{A}\|\leq\tilde{g},\quad\forall A,\quad\partial h_{A}:=H-(H_{A}+H_{B}),

(4)

where
\tilde{g}
represents an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant. This condition is more general than the assumption of power-law decay of interactions. If we specifically consider the power-law decay condition in the form:

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\ni\{i,i^{\prime}\}}\|h_{Z}\|\leq\frac{J}{|i-i^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\quad\textrm{for}\quad i\neq i^{\prime},

(5)

then the condition in (
4
) is satisfied as long as
\alpha>2

(see Supplementary materials
[
55
]
).

Throughout the paper, we analyze the quantum Gibbs state as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}:=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}e^{-\beta H},\quad Z_{\beta}:=\operatorname{tr}\left(e^{-\beta H}\right).

We aim to approximate the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
by a MPO, which is generally described as follows:

\displaystyle M=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}=1\\
s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime},\dots,s_{n}^{\prime}=1\end{subarray}}^{d}

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(M_{1}^{[s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime}]}M_{2}^{[s_{2},s_{2}^{\prime}]}\cdots M_{n}^{[s_{n},s_{n}^{\prime}]}\right)

\displaystyle\left|s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\right\rangle\left\langle s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime},\dots,s_{n}^{\prime}\right|,

(6)

where each
\{M_{j}^{[s_{j},s_{j}^{\prime}]}\}_{j,s_{j},s_{j}^{\prime}}
is a
D\times D
matrix, with
D
referred to as the bond dimension.
As measures of the approximation, we often use the Schatten
p
-norm as follows:

\left\|O\right\|_{p}:=[{\rm tr}(|O|^{p})]^{1/p}

with
O
an arbitrary operator and
|O|=\sqrt{O^{\dagger}O}
. For
p=1
, it is equivalent to the trace norm, and for
p=\infty
, it corresponds to the operator norm.

We denote the bond dimension of the MPO form of the Hamiltonian by
D_{H}
, which is at most of
n^{k}d^{k}
in general
[
56
]
.
In particular, if we restrict ourselves to a 2-local Hamiltonian in the form of:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<i^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|i-i^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}=1}^{d^{2}}J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}P_{i,\xi}\otimes P_{i^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}},

(7)

where
\{P_{i,\xi}\}_{\xi=1}^{d^{2}}
denotes the operator bases at site
i
and
J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}
are the corresponding coefficients, the Hamiltonian can be approximated by an MPO with bond dimension
D_{H}=c_{H}\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon)
, where
c_{H}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, up to an error of
\epsilon
(see Supplementary material
[
55
, Lemma 2 therein]
).
We note that any subset Hamiltonian
H_{L}
is also described by an MPO with the bond dimension
D_{H}
of the global Hamiltonian.

. After

\{\tilde{\Psi}_{s}^{(q)}\}_{s,q}

)], facilitating the construction of the MPO

By iteratively combining high-temperature quantum Gibbs states, the Gibbs state at any desired temperature
\beta
is achieved by exponentiating
e^{-\beta_{0}H}
to
\beta/\beta_{0}
, where
\beta_{0}
is chosen so that
\beta/\beta_{0}
is an integer. The approximation error between
e^{-\beta H}
and
(M_{\beta_{0}})^{\beta/\beta_{0}}
is managed by controlling the error between
e^{-\beta_{0}H}
and
M_{\beta_{0}}
in terms of an arbitrary Schatten
p
-norm, as achieved in (
13
).

Main Result.—

Our task is to develop a time-efficient method using an MPO to approximate the quantum Gibbs state for a given
\beta
within a desired error
\epsilon
.
The main result concerning the time-efficient construction of the MPO is as follows:

Theorem.

For an arbitrary
\beta
, we can efficiently compute the MPO
M_{\beta}
that approximates the unnormalized Gibbs state up to an error
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-\beta H}-M_{\beta}\right\|_{p}\leq\epsilon\left\|e^{-\beta H}\right\|_{p},\quad\forall p.

(8)

The bond dimension of
M_{\beta}
and the time complexity are given by

e^{C_{0}\beta\ln^{3}(n/\epsilon)}
,
where
C_{0}
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant.
Typically, we consider
\epsilon=1/{\rm poly}(n)
, which leads to a time complexity of
e^{\mathcal{O}(\beta)\ln^{3}(n)}
.
If we consider the 2-local Hamiltonian in Eq. (
7
),
the time complexity qualitatively improves to
e^{C_{0}\beta\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon)\ln\ln(n/\epsilon)}
.
From the theorem, we can efficiently simulate the quantum Gibbs state in long-range interacting systems
[
57
]
.
Finally, we emphasize that the preparation of the quantum Gibbs state on a quantum computer is also possible by a quasi-polynomial time complexity
[
55
]
.

In the following, we detail the explicit calculation steps for constructing the MPO
M_{\beta}
, which is also illustrated in Fig.
1
:

1.

Initial State Preparation:
We begin with a product state of the quantum Gibbs states of small blocks, each containing
2
sites, at an inverse temperature
\beta_{0}
. This temperature
\beta_{0}
is chosen to be smaller than
1/(24gk^{2})
, which will be utilized to ensure the convergence of the expansion (
10
). The exact MPO representation of the initial state has a bond dimension at most
d^{2}
, where
d
is the dimension of the local Hilbert space.

2.

Merging Operation:
Adjacent quantum Gibbs states on blocks are merged using a merging operator
\Psi
. This operator is approximated by
\tilde{\Psi}
using truncated expansions as described in Eq. (
10
), up to
m_{0}
-th order, where
m_{0}=\ln(n/\epsilon)
. Consequently, the bond dimension of the MPO form of
\tilde{\Psi}
becomes
D_{H}^{\mathcal{O}(m_{0})}
, with
D_{H}
representing the bond dimension of the Hamiltonian.

3.

Approximation of High-Temperature Gibbs State:
By repeating the merging processes
\log_{2}(n)
times, we achieve the global quantum Gibbs state
e^{-\beta_{0}H}
. This high-temperature quantum Gibbs state is approximated by the MPO
M_{\beta_{0}}
, which has a bond dimension
D_{H}^{\mathcal{O}(m_{0}\ln(n))}
.

4.

Approximation of Low-Temperature Gibbs State:
The high-temperature quantum Gibbs state is connected
(\beta/\beta_{0})
times to yield the MPO
(M_{\beta_{0}})^{\beta/\beta_{0}}
, serving as the approximation of the desired quantum Gibbs state
e^{-\beta H}
. In this process, we need to utilize the error bound in terms of Schatten
p
-norm (see Lemma 3 below).

In the next section, we will focus on analytically estimating the precision error and the time complexity resulting from these approximations.

Proof of main theorem.—

We here provide the outline of the proof, and we defer the details to Supplementary materials
[
55
]
.
We follow Steps 1 to 4 above and estimate the precision error depending on the bond dimension.
We denote the Gibbs state of the block
L_{s}^{(q)}
as
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{s}^{(q)}}
, where
H_{s}^{(q)}
serves as an abbreviation for the subset Hamiltonian
H_{L_{s}^{(q)}}=\sum_{Z:Z\subset L_{s}^{(q)}}h_{Z}
on block
L_{s}^{(q)}
. Under this notation,
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{1}^{(q_{0})}}=e^{-\beta_{0}H}
represents the Gibbs state of the entire system, encompassing all interactions within the system.

We then estimate the error arising from the merging operations.
In general, we define a merging operator
\Psi
that connects two subsets,
A
and
B
, as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi=e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}e^{\beta_{0}(H_{A}+H_{B})},

(9)

which implies the relation
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}=\Psi e^{-\beta_{0}H_{A}}e^{-\beta_{0}H_{B}}
. Here,
\Psi
facilitates the merging of the Gibbs states of
A
and
B
to form the combined Gibbs state on the larger block
AB
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a).
In this context,
H_{AB}=H_{A}+H_{B}+\partial h_{A}
, where
\partial h_{A}
represents the boundary interactions between systems
A
and
B
, as defined in Eq. (
4
).

To construct an MPO with a small bond dimension for
\Psi
, we first approximate
\Psi
using a polynomial expansion in terms of
\beta_{0}
.
However, the independent approximations of
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}
and
e^{\beta_{0}(H_{A}+H_{B})}
require polynomial degrees of at least
(\beta_{0}\left\|H_{AB}\right\|)^{1/2}
and
(\beta_{0}\left\|H_{A}\right\|+\beta_{0}\left\|H_{B}\right\|)^{1/2}
, respectively
[
58
]
. This results in a demand for sub-exponentially large bond dimensions for an accurate approximation.

The key idea here is that by combining
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}
and
e^{\beta_{0}(H_{A}+H_{B})}
the truncation order is significantly reduced for a good approximation of
\Psi
.
We adopt the following polynomial approximation of the merging operator (
9
):

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}=\sum^{m_{0}}_{m=0}\beta_{0}^{m}\sum_{s_{1}+s_{2}=m}(-1)^{s_{1}}\frac{H_{AB}^{s_{1}}(H_{A}+H_{B})^{s_{2}}}{s_{1}!s_{2}!},

(10)

where we apply the Taylor expansion for each of
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}
and
e^{\beta_{0}(H_{A}+H_{B})}
and truncate the terms with
\beta_{0}^{m}
for
m>m_{0}
.
Then, we prove the following precision error:

Proposition 1.
If
\beta_{0}
is smaller than
1/(24gk^{2})
, we achieve the approximation of

\displaystyle\|\Psi-\tilde{\Psi}\|\leq\delta_{0}\quad\textrm{for}\quad m_{0}\geq\log_{2}\left(c_{0}/\delta_{0}\right),

(11)

with
c_{0}=e^{\tilde{g}/(6gk^{2})}
which is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant.

To derive the MPO form of
\tilde{\Psi}
, we first create individual MPOs for each term in the expansion (
10
). These individual MPOs are then combined to obtain the MPO representation of
\tilde{\Psi}
.
Given the MPO form of the Hamiltonians
H_{AB}
and
H_{A}+H_{B}
, the bond dimension required for the term
H_{AB}^{s_{1}}(H_{A}+H_{B})^{s_{2}}
is at most
D_{H}^{s_{1}}D_{H}^{s_{2}}=D_{H}^{m}
, where
s_{1}+s_{2}=m
. The total bond dimension required to represent all terms up to
m_{0}
in the expansion is calculated as:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{m_{0}}(m+1)D_{H}^{m}\leq(m_{0}+1)^{2}D_{H}^{m_{0}}=:\tilde{D}_{\delta_{0}}.

(12)

Thus, we construct the MPO with the bond dimension
\tilde{D}_{\delta_{0}}
to approximate the merging operator
\Psi
for any arbitrary adjacent subsets
A
and
B
.

After constructing the MPO approximation of the merging operator, we proceed to find the MPO for the combined Gibbs state.
We in general consider the merging of
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}}
and
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s}^{(q-1)}}
, which is given by

e^{-\beta_{0}H_{s}^{(q)}}=\Psi_{s}^{(q-1)}e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}}e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s}^{(q-1)}}
.
Our purpose is to estimate the approximation by
\tilde{\Psi}_{s}^{(q-1)}M_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}M_{2s}^{(q-1)}
when approximate MPOs
M_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}
and
M_{2s}^{(q-1)}
are given for
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}}
and
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s}^{(q-1)}}
, respectively.
For simplicity, we denote
\Psi_{s}^{(q-1)}
as
\Psi
and
\tilde{\Psi}_{s}^{(q-1)}
as
\tilde{\Psi}
, by omitting the indices
s
and
q
.

Proposition 2.
Let
M_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}
and
M_{2s}^{(q-1)}
represent the MPOs approximating
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}}
and
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{2s}^{(q-1)}}
, respectively, such that

\left\|e^{-\beta_{0}H_{i}^{(q-1)}}-M_{i}^{(q-1)}\right\|_{p}\leq\epsilon_{q-1}\left\|e^{-\beta_{0}H_{i}^{(q-1)}}\right\|_{p}

\forall p
with
i=2s-1,2s
.
Using the approximate merging operator
\tilde{\Psi}
with
\|\Psi-\tilde{\Psi}\|\leq\delta_{0}
, the merged quantum Gibbs state
e^{-\beta_{0}H_{s}^{(q)}}
can be approximated as:

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-\beta_{0}H_{s}^{(q)}}-M_{s}^{(q)}\right\|_{p}\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon_{q}\left\|e^{-\beta_{0}H_{s}^{(q)}}\right\|_{p},

with
\epsilon_{q}=a_{2}\delta_{0}+a_{1}\epsilon_{q-1}
,
where the MPO
M_{s}^{(q)}
is constructed as
M_{s}^{(q)}=\tilde{\Psi}M_{2s-1}^{(q-1)}M_{2s}^{(q-1)}
, and
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are
\mathcal{O}(1)
constants with
a_{1}>1
.

This shows that
\epsilon_{q}
and
\epsilon_{q-1}
are related by a linear equation, leading to a recursive relation on

. By solving this recursive relation, we find:

\epsilon_{q}\leq a_{2}\delta_{0}qa_{1}^{q-2},

using the fact that
\epsilon_{1}=0
, because the MPO description of the initial state is exact.
We then obtain the MPO approximation
M_{\beta_{0}}
of the Gibbs state
e^{-\beta_{0}H}
, which results in the following error
\forall p

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-\beta_{0}H}-M_{\beta_{0}}\right\|_{p}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}_{0}\left\|e^{-\beta_{0}H}\right\|_{p}

(13)

\epsilon^{\prime}_{0}:=a_{2}\delta_{0}q_{0}a_{1}^{q_{0}-2}

The MPO
M_{\beta_{0}}=M_{1}^{(q_{0})}
is constructed through

\tilde{D}_{\delta_{0}}^{q_{0}}

\tilde{D}_{\delta_{0}}
was defined as the bond dimension of
\tilde{\Psi}
in Eq. (

If we consider the error
\|\Psi-\tilde{\Psi}\|\leq\delta_{0}=\epsilon/\mathrm{poly}(n)
, then the bond dimension becomes
\tilde{D}_{\delta_{0}}^{q_{0}}=D_{H}^{\mathcal{O}(\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon))}
, as

and
\tilde{D}_{\delta_{0}}=D_{H}^{\mathcal{O}(\ln(1/\delta_{0}))}
.

Finally, we use the MPO
M_{\beta_{0}}
for the high-temperature Gibbs state

to approximate the target low-temperature Gibbs state

, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(b). We combine the
(\beta/\beta_{0})
MPOs

to approximate the low-temperature Gibbs state, resulting in:

(M_{\beta_{0}})^{\beta/\beta_{0}}\approx e^{-\beta H}.

Consequently, the bond dimension is multiplied
(\beta/\beta_{0})
times, leading to a final required bond dimension of the order
D_{H}^{\mathcal{O}(\beta\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon))}
.

To derive the error bound, we use the statement from
[
7
, ineq. (C6) therein]
:

Lemma 3.

Let
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
be arbitrary positive integers.
Then, if

\|e^{-\beta_{0}H}-M_{\beta_{0}}\|_{p_{1}p_{2}}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\|e^{-\beta_{0}H}\|_{p_{1}p_{2}},

The inequality of

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-p_{1}\beta_{0}H}-M_{\beta_{0}}^{p_{1}}\right\|_{p_{2}}\leq(3e/2)p_{1}\epsilon^{\prime}\left\|e^{-p_{1}\beta_{0}H}\right\|_{p_{2}}

(14)

holds for arbitrary
\beta_{0}
and

.

We have already derived the MPO approximation for a general Schatten
p
-norm in (
13
).
Therefore, the above lemma yields

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-\beta H}-(M_{\beta_{0}})^{\beta/\beta_{0}}\right\|_{p}\leq\epsilon_{\beta}\left\|e^{-\beta H}\right\|_{p},

(15)

where

\epsilon_{\beta}=5(\beta/\beta_{0})\epsilon^{\prime}_{0}.

Now, let us choose
\beta_{0}=\beta/\lceil 24gk^{2}\beta\rceil
. Then, because of
a_{1}^{q_{0}}=\mathrm{poly}(n)
with
q_{0}=\log_{2}(n)
, the choice of
\delta_{0}=\epsilon/\mathrm{poly}(n)
yields the main precision bound in Eq. (
8
). Here, we let
M_{\beta}=(M_{\beta_{0}})^{\beta/\beta_{0}}
and assume
\beta<n
without loss of generality

59
]
.

By estimating each of the algorithm steps, we can see that the runtime is also upper-bounded by
D_{H}^{\mathcal{O}(\beta\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon))}
(see Supplementary materials
[
55
]
).
We thus prove the main theorem.
\square

Extension to Real-Time Evolution–

In our method, we did not rely on the fact that the inverse temperature
\beta
is a real number. Therefore, it is possible to generalize our approach to cases where
\beta
is a complex number. By considering the case where
\beta=it
, we can address the simulation of real-time evolution.

Given an MPO
M_{t}
that approximates
e^{-iHt}
, we can estimate the error as:

\left\|e^{-iHt}\ket{\Psi}-M_{t}\ket{\Psi}\right\|\leq\left\|e^{-iHt}-M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}
for an arbitrary quantum state
\ket{\Psi}
.
By following our algorithm, we can construct an MPO
M_{t}
for
e^{-iHt}
that satisfies the error bound
\left\|e^{-iHt}-M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon\left\|e^{-iHt}\right\|_{\infty}=\epsilon
. The bond dimension and time complexity required are given by a quasi-polynomial form of
e^{\mathcal{O}(t\ln^{3}(n/\epsilon))}
.
Compared to methods based on the Lieb-Robinson bound, our approach significantly reduces the time complexity. Assuming the Lieb-Robinson bound as
\left\|[O_{i}(t),O_{j}]\right\|\leq t^{\eta}/r^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>2

[
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
]
, the time cost to calculate the average value of a time-evolved local observable
O_{i}(t)
scales as
e^{(t^{\eta}/\epsilon)^{1/(\alpha-1)}}
.

Summary and outlook.—

In this letter, we have presented an algorithm that achieves quasi-polynomial time complexity in approximating the quantum Gibbs state of 1D long-range interacting systems using MPOs. This was accomplished by adopting a DMRG-type method, as depicted in Fig.
1
.
We note that achieving quasi-polynomial complexity is likely the best attainable result in general
[
64
]
.
However, there is hope to achieve polynomial time complexity for the
2
-local cases, as described in Eq. (
7
). Identifying the optimal time complexity for simulating long-range interacting systems remains an important open question.

Another intriguing avenue for exploration is whether our method can be extended to cases where the power-law decay is slower than
r^{-2}
(
\alpha<2
), where the assumption of finite boundary interactions in Eq. (
4
) may no longer hold. In specific cases, this condition can still be recovered. For instance, in fermion systems with long-range hopping and short-range fermion-fermion interactions, the condition in Eq. (
4
) holds for
\alpha>3/2

[
65
]
. Another interesting class is the non-critical quantum Gibbs state, where power-law clustering is satisfied. In such systems, the MPO approximation is expected to hold for
\alpha>1
, since the mutual information for any bipartition obeys the area law
[
66
]
. Our algorithm is designed to be generally applicable to all 1D long-range interacting systems with
\alpha>2
. However, when our algorithm is applied to specific Hamiltonians for practical applications, more stringent conditions are typically required, making the reduction of complexity necessary.

Finally, as in the case of short-range interactions
[
8
]
, extending the analysis to infinite system setups remains a critical challenge for future research.

S.I
Several basic statements

S.I.1
Operator norm of the boundary interaction for long-range interacting systems when
\alpha>2

We assume in the main text that the boundary interaction is bounded:

\displaystyle\left\|\partial h_{A}\right\|\leq\tilde{g},\qquad\forall A\subset\Lambda,

(S.1)

where the boundary interaction
\partial h_{A}
is defined as the interaction acting on both subsystems
A
and
B
, derived by subtracting the Hamiltonians
H_{A}
and
H_{B}
, which act exclusively on
A
and
B
, from the total Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\partial h_{A}:=\sum_{Z:Z\cap A\neq\emptyset,Z\cap B\neq\emptyset}h_{Z}=H-(H_{A}+H_{B}).

(S.2)

We demonstrate that this assumption is inherently satisfied for long-range interactions with a power law decay of
\alpha>2
:

Lemma 1
.

Considering the power-law decay of interactions in the form

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\ni{i,i^{\prime}}}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq\frac{J}{|i-i^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\quad\textrm{for}\quad i\neq i^{\prime},

(S.3)

we prove that
\tilde{g}
in Eq. (
S.1
) is
\mathcal{O}(1)
as long as
\alpha>2
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Let us consider two adjacent subsystems,
A
and
B
.
From the definition of
\partial h_{A}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\partial h_{A}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Z:Z\cap A\neq\emptyset,Z\cap B\neq\emptyset}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in B}\sum_{Z:Z\ni i,i^{\prime}}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in B}\frac{J}{|i-i^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{J}{(i+j-1)^{\alpha}}=J\zeta(\alpha-1),

(S.4)

where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function. For
\alpha>2
, we can choose
\tilde{g}=J\zeta(\alpha-1)=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
This completes the proof.
\square

S.I.2
MPO approximation of the 2-local Hamiltonians

We generally obtain the MPO forms of
k
-local Hamiltonians which have the bond dimensions at most of
n^{k}d^{k}
.
In this section, we show that the bond dimension is improved in the specific cases of the
2
-local Hamiltonians.

Lemma 2
.

For a 2-local Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n}\frac{1}{|i-i^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}=1}^{d^{2}}J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}P_{i,\xi}\otimes P_{i^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}},

(S.5)

where
\{P_{i,\xi}\}_{\xi=1}^{d^{2}}
are operator bases on site
i
and
J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}
are constants, there exists a Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
that approximates
H
with an error bounded by
\left\|H-\tilde{H}\right\|\leq\epsilon_{H}
, and admits an MPO representation with bond dimension

\displaystyle D_{\tilde{H}}=c_{H}\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon_{H}),

(S.6)

where
c_{H}
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant.

Remark.

Here, the MPO form for the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
is not exactly the same as that of the original Hamiltonian
H
.
We hence need to take into account the error between
H
and
\tilde{H}
for the simulation of the quantum Gibbs states (see Sec.
S.II.5
).

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We approximate
r^{-\alpha}
as an exponential series in accordance with Ref.
[
67
, Theorem 3]
, as follows
*0
*0
footnotetext:
We cannot directly employ Ref.
[
67
, Theorem 5]
since it treats only the regime
r<1
.
:

\displaystyle\left|r^{-\alpha}-\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}r}\right|\leq\epsilon\quad\textrm{for}\quad r\geq 1,

\displaystyle x=\frac{2\pi}{\ln(3)+\alpha\ln[1/\cos(1)]+\ln(1/\epsilon)}.

(S.7)

We then consider the finite truncation as
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}=[-m,m]
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}r}-\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}r}\right|\leq\sum_{s<-m}e^{\alpha sx}+\sum_{s>m}e^{\alpha sx-e^{sx}}.

(S.8)

We assume that
m
satisfies

\displaystyle\alpha mx-e^{mx}\leq-\alpha mx\quad\longrightarrow\quad e^{mx}\geq 2\alpha mx\quad

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad mx\geq-{\rm ProductLog}\left(-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\quad m\geq\frac{2}{x}\ln(2\alpha),

(S.9)

where
{\rm ProductLog}(x)
is equivalent to the Lambert W function
W(x)
, defined by
x=W(x)e^{W(x)}
.

Under the assumption, we reduce the inequality (
S.8
) to

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}r}-\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}r}\right|\leq 2\sum_{s>m}e^{-\alpha sx}=\frac{2e^{-\alpha xm}}{e^{\alpha x}-1}=\frac{2}{e^{\alpha x}-1}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2\alpha}\right)^{2\alpha}

(S.10)

with

\displaystyle m=\left\lceil\frac{2}{x}\ln(2\alpha/\epsilon)\right\rceil.

(S.11)

Under the above choice, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|r^{-\alpha}-\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}r}\right|\leq\epsilon+\frac{2}{e^{\alpha x}-1}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2\alpha}\right)^{2\alpha}\leq\zeta\epsilon,

(S.12)

where
\zeta
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant for
\alpha\geq 2
.

Based on this approximation, we define the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
as:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}|i-i^{\prime}|}\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}=1}^{d^{2}}J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}P_{i,\xi}\otimes P_{i^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}}.

(S.13)

We then obtain

\displaystyle\left\|H-\tilde{H}\right\|\leq\zeta\epsilon\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n}\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}=1}^{d^{2}}J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}\left\|P_{i,\xi}\otimes P_{i^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}}\right\|\leq\bar{J}n^{2}\zeta\epsilon,

(S.14)

with

\displaystyle\bar{J}:=\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}=1}^{d^{2}}|J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}|,

(S.15)

where we set the operator bases such that
\left\|P_{i,\xi}\right\|=1
for
\forall i
and
\forall\xi
.

To ensure an approximation error of
\epsilon_{H}
for
\left\|H-\tilde{H}\right\|
, we let
\bar{J}n^{2}\zeta\epsilon=\epsilon_{H}

(or
\epsilon=\epsilon_{H}/(\bar{J}\zeta n^{2})
), which implies

\displaystyle m=\left\lceil\frac{2}{x}\ln(2\alpha/\epsilon)\right\rceil\leq c^{\prime}_{H}\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon_{H}),

(S.16)

where
c^{\prime}_{H}
is a constant of order
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Moreover, it has been shown that each Hamiltonian of the form
\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq n}e^{\alpha sx}e^{-e^{sx}|i-i^{\prime}|}\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}=1}^{d^{2}}J_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}P_{i,\xi}\otimes P_{i^{\prime},\xi^{\prime}}

with exponential decay admits an MPO representation with a bond dimension of
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})

[
68
]
. Therefore, the MPO representation of
\tilde{H}
can be constructed by combining the MPOs of each term, resulting in a total bond dimension of:

\displaystyle D_{\tilde{H}}

\displaystyle=c_{H}\ln^{2}(n/\epsilon_{H})

(S.17)

for
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
, where
c_{H}
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant.
This completes the proof.
\square

Proof of main propositions and technical details

S.II.1
Proof of Proposition 1 in the main text

We now present the proof of Proposition 1 in the main text.
For the convenience of readers, we recall the setup.
We define a merging operator
\Psi
that connects two subsets
A
and
B
as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi=e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}e^{\beta_{0}(H_{A}+H_{B})},\qquad H_{AB}=H_{A}+H_{B}+\partial h_{A},

(S.18)

which leads to the relation

\displaystyle e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}=\Psi\,e^{-\beta_{0}H_{A}}e^{-\beta_{0}H_{B}}.

(S.19)

Proposition 1
.

Consider the polynomial expansion of the merging operator
\Psi
in powers of
\beta_{0}
, given by:

\displaystyle\Psi=e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}e^{\beta_{0}(H_{A}+H_{B})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}e^{\beta_{0}(H_{AB}-\partial h_{A})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\beta_{0}^{m}\sum_{s_{1}+s_{2}=m}\frac{1}{s_{1}!s_{2}!}(-H_{AB})^{s_{1}}(H_{AB}-\partial h_{A})^{s_{2}}.

(S.20)

We truncate the above series to
m_{0}
terms and denote the resulting truncated series as
\tilde{\Psi}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{m_{0}}\beta_{0}^{m}\sum_{s_{1}+s_{2}=m}\frac{1}{s_{1}!s_{2}!}(-H_{AB})^{s_{1}}(H_{AB}-\partial h_{A})^{s_{2}}.

(S.21)

Then, if
\beta_{0}
is smaller than
\frac{1}{24gk^{2}}
, we achieve the approximation

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi-\tilde{\Psi}\right\|\leq\delta_{0}\quad\textrm{for}\quad m_{0}\geq\log_{2}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{\delta_{0}}\right),

(S.22)

where
c_{0}=e^{\tilde{g}/(6gk^{2})}
is a constant of
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

We derive the approximation of the truncation of the expansion.
To this end, we first reformulate each term in the expansion (
S.20
) by employing an alternative expansion, specifically using the interaction picture:

\displaystyle e^{\beta_{0}(H_{AB}-\partial h_{A})}=e^{\beta_{0}H_{AB}}\mathcal{T}\left(e^{-\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\partial h_{x}\,dx}\right),

(S.23)

where we define

and

is the time-ordering operator. We then obtain

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta_{0}H_{AB}}e^{\beta_{0}(H_{AB}-\partial h_{A})}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\left(e^{-\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\partial h_{x}\,dx}\right)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{s}\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}dx_{1}\int_{0}^{x_{1}}dx_{2}\cdots\int_{0}^{x_{s-1}}dx_{s}\,\partial h_{x_{1}}\partial h_{x_{2}}\cdots\partial h_{x_{s}}.

(S.24)

By applying the decomposition

\displaystyle\partial h_{x}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-x)^{p}}{p!}\mathrm{ad}_{H_{AB}}^{p}(\partial h_{A})=:\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-x)^{p}}{p!}\partial h^{(p)},

(S.25)

the
m
-th order term in the expansion of
\Psi
in powers of
\beta_{0}
, which includes the contribution from
\beta_{0}^{m}
in Eq. (
S.24
), is expressed as

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}

\displaystyle:=1,

(S.26)

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+\cdots+p_{s}=m-s}(-1)^{s}\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}dx_{1}\int_{0}^{x_{1}}dx_{2}\cdots\int_{0}^{x_{s-1}}dx_{s}\,\frac{(-x_{1})^{p_{1}}}{p_{1}!}\cdots\frac{(-x_{s})^{p_{s}}}{p_{s}!}\,\partial h^{(p_{1})}\cdots\partial h^{(p_{s})}

(S.27)

for
\forall m\geq 1
.
As both Eq. (
S.20
) and Eq. (
S.24
) are expansions in powers of
\beta_{0}
, the term
\Psi_{m}
defined above corresponds to the
m
th order term in Eq. (
S.20
):

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}=\beta_{0}^{m}\sum_{s_{1}+s_{2}=m}\frac{1}{s_{1}!s_{2}!}(-H_{AB})^{s_{1}}(H_{AB}-\partial h_{A})^{s_{2}},

(S.28)

which we aim to upper-bound.

The norm of
\Psi_{m}
is upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{m}\beta_{0}^{m-s}\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+\cdots+p_{s}=m-s}\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}dx_{1}\,\int_{0}^{x_{1}}dx_{2}\cdots\int_{0}^{x_{s-1}}dx_{s}\,\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{1}{p_{j}!}\|\partial h^{(p_{j})}\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{0}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+\cdots+p_{s}=m-s}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{1}{p_{j}!}\|\partial h^{(p_{j})}\|.

(S.29)

From Theorem 2.1 in
[
69
]
, if
H
is a
k
-local and
g
-extensive Hamiltonian and
A
is a

-local operator, then

\displaystyle\left\|[H,A]\right\|\leq 6gkr\|A\|.

(S.30)

Since
H_{AB}
and
\partial h_{A}
are
k
-local Hamiltonians,
\text{ad}_{H_{AB}}^{p-1}(\partial h_{A})
is at most
kp
-local. Using the inequality (
S.30
), we get

\displaystyle\|\partial h^{(p)}\|=\|\text{ad}_{H_{AB}}^{p}(\partial h_{A})\|

\displaystyle\leq 6gk(kp)\|\text{ad}_{H_{AB}}^{p-1}(\partial h_{A})\|.

(S.31)

By iterating the above process for
\|\text{ad}_{H_{AB}}^{p^{\prime}}(\partial h_{A})\|
with

and using (
S.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\partial h^{(p)}\|\leq(6gk^{2})^{p}p!\tilde{g}=:C^{p}p!\tilde{g},

(S.32)

where we define

. Substituting Eq. (

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{0}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\frac{C^{m-s}\tilde{g}^{s}}{s!}\sum_{p_{1}+p_{2}+\cdots+p_{s}=m-s}1=\beta_{0}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\frac{C^{m-s}\tilde{g}^{s}}{s!}\left(\hskip-3.50006pt\binom{m-s}{s}\hskip-3.50006pt\right).

(S.33)

Using

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq(2C\beta_{0})^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\frac{1}{s!}\left(\frac{\tilde{g}}{C}\right)^{s}\leq(2C\beta_{0})^{m}e^{\tilde{g}/C}.

\displaystyle m_{0}\geq\log_{2}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{\delta_{0}}\right).

(S.37)

This completes the proof of Proposition
