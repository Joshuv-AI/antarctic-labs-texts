Movement behaviour policies in Canadian childcare facilities: Prevalence, content, and associations with facility characteristics and regional legislation
Authors: Brianne A Bruijns, Hannah J Coyle-Asbil, Sophie M Phillips, Leigh M Vanderloo, Scott Wong, Patricia Tucker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly childhood is a critical period for establishing healthy movement behaviours, including physical activity, sedentary behaviour, screen time, and sleep. Childcare represents a key setting for shaping these behaviours, as many young children spend a substantial proportion of their waking hours in these environments. This study provides a national overview of movement behaviour policies in Canadian childcare facilities and examines associations with facility characteristics and provincial/territorial legislation.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was completed by directors/owners of childcare facilities across Canada (n = 1,340). Respondents reported on the presence and characteristics of written policies across physical activity, sedentary behaviour/screen time, and sleep domains, along with facility characteristics. Descriptive statistics summarized policy prevalence, and thematic analysis was conducted on open-ended responses. Logistic regression models examined associations between facility characteristics (urbanicity, childcare type, enrolment size, daily fees) and the presence of written movement behaviour policies, as well as associations with explicit mention of these behaviours in provincial/territorial legislation.ResultsWritten policies were reported by 50.0% of facilities for overall physical activity, 61.1% for providing no screen time, and 68.7% for outdoor physical activity; fewer reported policies limiting prolonged seated time (17.0%) or ensuring age-appropriate nap durations (46.6%). Policies addressing educator professional development and family education were uncommon (< 21% and < 12%, respectively). Facilities in jurisdictions without explicit movement behaviour legislation were significantly less likely to report written policies for overall physical activity, prolonged seated time, and nap duration (p < .01). Urbanicity (p = .044) and enrolment size (p = .027) were associated with the presence of physical activity and nap duration policies, respectively, while family-/home-based childcare facilities had substantially lower odds of reporting a no screen time policy (OR = 0.22, p < .001) and higher odds of reporting a policy for prolonged seated time (OR = 1.94, p = .033) compared to centre-based facilities.ConclusionMovement behaviour policies in Canadian childcare settings are inconsistent and strongly shaped by governance and childcare context. While policies for physical activity and outdoor play are common, formal guidance for sedentary behaviour, screen time, sleep, and educator and family education remain limited. Greater coordination, implementation supports, and investment in professional learning may promote more equitable movement opportunities for young children in Canada.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44167-026-00103-4.Keywords: Physical activity, Sedentary behaviour, Sleep, Movement behaviours, Early childhood education, Childcare, Policy

Introduction

Early childhood is a critical time for young children (0 to 4 years) to develop healthy movement behaviours (i.e., physical activity, sedentary behaviour, sleep) that set them on a trajectory for lifelong healthy living habits [1, 2]. International [3] and national [4–7] guidelines consistently emphasize the importance of providing young children with regular opportunities for physical activity, minimizing prolonged (i.e., < 60 min) sedentary behaviour, limiting screen time, and supporting healthy sleep routines. Yet, while movement behaviours are shaped by many different environments, the childcare setting has been noted to be particularly impactful, especially given the large proportion of young children they reach [8]. Importantly, policies and regulations governing childcare facilities can influence the extent to which movement behaviours are supported, structured, or constrained within daily programming [9, 10], demonstrating the need for guidance in this domain.

To assist countries in developing and implementing effective movement behaviour policies, particularly those that can be applied within childcare environments, the World Health Organization (WHO) developed global Standards for Healthy Eating, Physical Activity, Sedentary Behaviour and Sleep in Early Childhood Education and Care Settings (henceforth, WHO Standards) [11]. These standards include: (1) incorporating opportunities to build children’s knowledge and skills around healthy eating and movement behaviours into the childcare programme; (2) establishing supportive environments for healthy eating and movement behaviours; (3) engaging families, caregivers, and communities in healthy eating and movement behaviours; and, (4) ensuring children’s safety through proper sleep, food hygiene, and safe play environments [11]. The toolkit also specifies the policies, staff training, and resources needed to achieve each standard, while also outlining concrete actions needed at national, subnational, and childcare provider levels to implement the standards [11]. However, despite the availability of these international standards, the extent to which they are reflected in national or subnational childcare policy frameworks varies considerably [12], creating gaps between recommended practice and regulatory guidance.

In Canada, nearly one million children 0 to 5 years attend licensed or regulated childcare (not including the Territories), positioning these settings as key environments for supporting healthy movement behaviours [13]. While recent national childcare initiatives, such as the Canada-Wide Early Learning and Child Care (CWELCC) program [14], have worked to regulate the affordability and accessibility of childcare across the country, national-level guidance for movement behaviours in childcare settings is non-existent. This is problematic, as policy is a powerful tool in ensuring equitable access to movement behaviours in early childhood; in fact, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies found that children attending childcare facilities with a formalized physical activity policy engaged in more physical activity than those attending facilities without a policy [15]. In Canada, provinces and territories (n = 13) are responsible for regulating childcare, with legislation varying substantially across jurisdictions. Only one province (British Columbia [BC]) references the Canadian 24 Hour Movement Guidelines in their regulations, four (BC, Northwest Territories [NT], Nunavut [NU], Nova Scotia [NS]) explicitly mention physical activity, three (BC, New Brunswick [NB], Québec [QC]) reference sedentary behaviour, and seven (Manitoba [MB], NB, Newfoundland and Labrador [NL], NT, Ontario [ON], Prince Edward Island [PE], QC) provide guidance on sleep [16]. With such fragmented guidance regarding movement behaviour opportunities in childcare settings, responsibility often falls on childcare facilities to independently establish and regulate these opportunities within their daily programming.

To gauge the landscape of childcare centre-specific policies pertaining to physical activity and screen-viewing in Canada, in 2016, Ott and colleagues [17] surveyed 514 directors of centre-based childcare facilities. They found that 44% of childcare centre directors reported they had a written physical activity policy, primarily regulated at the provincial/territorial level and focusing on the amount of time spent outdoors. Further, 29% of directors indicated they had a written screen-viewing policy, primarily regulated at the childcare centre-level and focusing on the amount of time children spent watching television. Since this study was conducted, views on physical activity and sedentary behaviour have shifted from a siloed approach to a more integrated approach that considers movement in the context of a 24 hour day (including sleep) [18]. Additionally, with the release of the Canadian 24 Hour Movement Guidelines for the Early Years in 2017 [4], as well as the WHO Standards in 2021 [11], a clearer picture of what daily proportions of movement behaviours are optimal for young children and how these can be achieved in the childcare setting has come to light. Building on this earlier work, the present study expands the scope to include all 24 hour movement behaviours, examines alignment with provincial/territorial legislation, and explicitly compares family-/home-based and centre-based childcare facilities. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to: (1) describe the prevalence, content, and governance of movement behaviour policies in Canada, including policy type (written, general practice) and governing body (province/territory, organization, childcare facility); (2) examine childcare facility characteristics associated with the presence of a written movement behaviour policy; and, (3) assess whether the presence of provincial/territorial legislation related to specific movement behaviours was associated with the existence of written childcare-level policies.

Methods

The protocol for this cross-sectional study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at the Non-Medical Research Ethics Board at Western University (REB # 127675).

Recruitment

Initial recruitment was completed using provincial and territorial childcare directories (available online, or via a government representative). Some provinces and territories (n = 8) were unable to provide directories or release childcare contact information due to privacy laws; in such instances, research assistants manually pulled childcare facility contact information, where available, via search engine. Recruitment materials were also shared with the research team’s academic and childcare organization contacts to encourage them to share with their childcare networks.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be eligible to participate in this study, participants had to be a director, owner, or administrator of a childcare facility in Canada that provided care for children under 5 years of age. For this study, “childcare facility” referred to regulated centre-based (i.e., daycare centres, preschools, nursery schools, church-based childcare, Indigenous early learning and childcare, Montessori schools) and family-/home-based childcare. Before and after school/extended day programs, and programs that only targeted older children that were captured in provincial and territorial directories (e.g., school-age programs) were excluded from this study.

Procedures

From September to November 2025, the director/owner of each childcare facility was contacted via email to invite participation in an anonymous online Qualtrics survey. When the director/owner’s contact information was missing, requests were sent to the childcare facility’s general inquiry address. Two reminder emails were circulated to encourage participation.

Childcare policy survey

The Qualtrics survey (n = 129 items) incorporated three sections: a movement behaviour policy audit (59 items); the modified Play Spaces and Environments for Children’s Physical Activity (PLAYCE) Centre Director and Educator Survey (51 items; 19); and, childcare facility (12 items) and participant (7 items) demographics. Data related to the PLAYCE Centre Director and Educator Survey are not reported here, as they are outside the scope of the present analysis.

Movement behaviour policy audit. The movement behaviour policy audit was modelled on Ott et al.’s [17] study and asked whether childcare facilities had written policies or general practices pertaining to 28 topics, including 13 related to physical activity, 9 related to sedentary and screen time, and 6 related to sleep. Most policy topics (n = 18) were derived from the PLAYCE Policy Environment Audit [19], while one topic was modified from the Environment and Policy Assessment and Observation Self-Report tool [19]. The remaining 9 topics were added to cover important sedentary behaviour and sleep content not covered in existing validated tools. If a written policy existed, participants were invited to comment on the level of enforcement for each policy (i.e., provincial/territorial-level, organization-level, centre- or family-/home-based childcare-level; n = 28 items). Participants were also invited to comment on whether policies for each movement behaviour grouping differed by child age (n = 3 items). To reduce participant burden, questions pertaining to screen time affordances for children in childcare were only displayed to participants who indicated that their childcare facility permitted screen time for children.

Childcare facility and participant demographics. Childcare facility demographics, such as which province/territory the facility was located in, the type of childcare facility, urbanicity, type of programming offered (i.e., full-day, partial day, or both), ages their facility provides care for, whether their facility was participating in the CWELCC program, daily rates (CAD) for parents/caregivers of children in their facility (by child age), the enrolment size, and the number of educators employed at their facility. Participant demographics, such as job position, years of experience working in childcare, age, gender, racial background, and highest level of education were also collected.

Data analysis

All quantitative analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 30), with statistical significance set at p < .05. Descriptive statistics were performed to report on childcare facility and participant demographics and frequencies were used to explore movement behaviour policy audit questions. The small sample in select provinces and territories limited inter-provincial/territorial analyses. Text responses to the open-ended survey questions regarding how policies differed by child age were combined in Microsoft Excel Workbook and analyzed using inductive thematic analysis [20].

To examine the associations between childcare facility characteristics and the presence of a written policy pertaining to the amount of overall physical activity, the amount of outdoor physical activity, limiting prolonged sedentary time, the provision of no screen time, and ensuring age-appropriate nap durations, five binary logistic regression analyses were conducted. Within each model, urbanicity (urban versus rural), childcare type (centre-based versus family-/home-based), facility enrolment size (continuous), and average daily rate for parents/caregivers (continuous) were examined as predictors. For the models predicting written policies related to overall physical activity, limiting prolonged sedentary time, no screen time, and age-appropriate nap duration, an additional predictor was included indicating whether the relevant behaviour was explicitly mentioned in provincial or territorial legislation, based on Vanderloo and colleagues’ classification [16]. This predictor was not included in the outdoor physical activity model, as outdoor physical activity is explicitly mentioned in the legislation of every province and territory. Childcare facilities classified by participants as “other” were excluded to directly compare between centre-based and family-/home-based settings. For logistic regression analyses, statistical significance was inferred when 95% confidence intervals for regression coefficients did not include 0.

Results

Of the 10,768 childcare facilities contacted, 1,340 childcare directors/owners participated (response rate of 12.4%; Table 1). A total of 1,117 (84%) were from centre-based childcare facilities (including preschools, nursery schools, church-based childcare, Indigenous early learning and childcare, forest/nature schools, and Montessori schools) and 179 (13.4%) were from family-/home-based childcare facilities. Most childcare facilities were in an urban centre (i.e., at least 1,000 residents [21]; 85.9%), and most were from BC (19.7%), QC (19.5%) and ON (16.1%). Participants were 47.5 (± 10.1) years old, had 20.9 (± 10.7) years of experience working in childcare, and most were women (95.8%), White (74.1%), and had completed college (50.8%) or university (38.8%) education. Prevalence and content of movement behaviour policies in childcare facilities

Nearly all (96.6%) childcare directors/owners reported they had at least one written movement behaviour policy, with 87.7%, 80.2%, and 71.0% reporting at least one written physical activity, sedentary behaviour/screen time, and sleep policy, respectively.

Physical activity policies

Half (50.0%; n = 593) of childcare directors/owners indicated they had a written policy for amount of time provided for overall physical activity, including 72.8% (n = 177) of BC childcare directors/owners and 66.7% (n = 16) of NL childcare directors/owners. Further, many childcare directors/owners (n = 263; 47.3%) noted their overall physical activity policies were regulated at the childcare facility level, followed by 30.0% (n = 167) at the provincial/territorial level and 15.1% (n = 84) at the organizational level. Over two-thirds (68.7%; n = 813) of childcare directors/owners indicated their facility had a written policy regarding the amount of daily outdoor physical activity, while less than one-third (29.6%; n = 351) reported having a policy pertaining to the amount of daily indoor physical activity; yet, despite not having a written policy for these items, many participants (62.2% for indoor physical activity; 30.3% for outdoor physical activity) indicated this was general practice. Other common written physical activity-related policies included having shoes and clothes that allow children and educators to participate in physical activity (61.3%; n = 725) and ensuring adequate physical activity on inclement weather days (50.6%; n = 600). Few childcare directors/owners indicated they had a written policy on providing education for families on children’s physical activity (11.7%; n = 137) or professional development for educators on children’s physical activity (19.7%; n = 232). Some (35.5%; n = 421) childcare directors/owners noted that physical activity-related policies differed by child age, with many indicating that older children receive more physical activity and outdoor time than younger children. For example, one participant noted that their “infant/toddler program spends less time participating in physical play as their nap time tends to be longer” while another indicated that “infants cannot be out as long in the winter months.” Physical activity types were also noted to be developmentally tailored; for example, one provider noted that “for children under 3, [they] focus on safer activities such as dancing, simple movement games, and playing with balls…” while for older children they “…also include more active play such as running and jumping.” See Supplementary File 1 for a full list of themes and example quotes.

The logistic regression model examining the associations between childcare facility characteristics, explicit provincial/territorial legislation, and the presence of a written policy pertaining to overall physical activity was significant (χ2 = 34.17, df = 5, p < .001). Urbanicity was found to be a significant, with urban childcare facilities reporting higher odds of having a written policy for the amount of time provided for overall physical activity compared to rural centres (OR = 1.57, 95% CI = [1.01, 2.44], p = .044). The logistic regression analyses also showed that, compared with directors/owners of childcare facilities located in provinces or territories where physical activity was explicitly referenced in childcare legislation, those located in provinces or territories without such legislative reference had significantly lower odds of reporting a written overall physical activity policy (OR = 0.43, 95% CI [0.31, 0.61], p < .001). The logistic regression model examining associations among childcare facility characteristics and existence of a written policy for outdoor physical activity was not statistically significant (χ2 = 8.48, df = 4, p = .075).

Sedentary behaviour and screen time policies

Few (17.0%; n = 184) childcare directors/owners indicated they had a written policy for the limiting periods of seated time (Table 4), with the highest prevalence rates in BC (28.0%; n = 63) and NB (25.8%; n = 16; Table 1). Most childcare directors/owners (n = 104; 58.4%) noted their policy on limiting long periods of seated time was regulated at the childcare facility level, followed by 21.3% (n = 38) at the provincial/territorial level and 16.3% (n = 29) at the organizational level (Table 5). While less than one-quarter (23.8%; n = 257) of childcare directors/owners indicated their facility had a written policy regarding providing children with opportunities for meaningful sedentary time (e.g., reading, colouring), many participants (n = 782; 72.4%) indicated this was general practice (Table 4). Few childcare directors/owners reported they had a written policy on providing education for families on children’s sedentary and/or screen time (6.3%; n = 67) or professional development for educators on sedentary and/or screen time (10.2%; n = 110; Table 4).

Nearly two-thirds (61.1%; n = 688) of childcare directors/owners indicated they had a written policy for providing no screen time for children, with the highest prevalence in NS (81.1%; n = 30) and NL (69.6%; n = 16; Table 1). Under one-third (31.4%; n = 337) of childcare directors/owners noted they had a written policy regarding the use of screen-based technology by educators in the children’s presence (Table 4), mostly regulated at the childcare facility-level (52.0%; n = 171; Table 5). Of those permitting screen time in their facility (33.7%; n = 380), 46.2% (n = 96) indicated they had a written policy for the amount of screen time allowed to watch shows or videos (largely regulated at the childcare facility level [n = 52; 54.7%]) and 28.2% (n = 59) reported they had a written policy for the amount of interactive screen time allowed (e.g., videogames, tablets; largely regulated at the childcare facility level [n = 33; 55.9%]; Table 4). Few childcare directors/owners indicated they had a written policy on not using screen time as a reward or to manage challenging behaviours (20.3%; n = 42).

Less than one-quarter (22.5%; n = 243) of childcare directors/owners noted that sedentary behaviour and screen-related policies differed by child age; common themes included longer sitting times and different sedentary behaviours for older children. For example, one participant mentioned that “time sitting and listening is determined by age-appropriateness of the activity and expectations for attention span.” Screen time was also noted to differ by child age, with one participant noting that they use “very minimal TV for all children but [that] toddlers and infants don’t get screen time.” The unique context of the home-based childcare setting was also apparent, with one provider noting that “[they] work 10-hour days with rarely a break. If [they are] tending to an infant or putting younger children down for a nap, preschool-age children are permitted to play educational games quietly in a tablet…”. See Supplementary File 1 for a full list of themes and example quotes.

The logistic regression model examining associations among childcare facility characteristics, explicit provincial/territorial legislation, and existence of a written policy for limiting long periods of seated time was statistically significant (χ2 = 17.09, df = 5, p = .004). Compared with directors/owners of childcare facilities in provinces or territories where sedentary behaviour was explicitly referenced in childcare legislation, those in provinces or territories without such legislation had significantly lower odds of reporting a written policy for limiting long periods of sedentary time (OR = 0.503, 95% CI [0.33, 0.76], p = .001). Childcare type was also a significant predictor, with directors/owners of family-/home-based childcare facilities having a significantly higher odds of reporting a policy for limiting long bouts of sedentary time compared to centre-based childcare facilities (OR = 1.94, 95% CI = [1.05, 3.57], p = .033).

The logistic regression model examining these associations with the presence of a written policy for providing no screen time for children was significant (χ2 = 49.65, df = 5, p < .001). Notably, directors/owners of family-/home-based childcare facilities had significantly lower odds of reporting a policy for no screen time compared to those from centre-based childcare facilities (OR = 0.22, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.37], p < .001), and directors/owners from urban childcare facilities had significantly higher odds of reporting a no screen time policy than those from rural facilities (OR = 1.63, 95% CI = [1.04, 2.58], p = .035).

Sleep policies

Just under half (46.6%; n = 486) of childcare directors/owners indicated they had a written policy regarding effort to ensure children achieve age-appropriate nap durations (Table 4), including 85.5% (n = 142) of ON childcare directors/owners and 80.6% (n = 29) of NS childcare directors/owners (Table 1). Further, many childcare directors/owners (n = 226; 47.3%) noted their nap duration policies were regulated at the childcare facility level, followed by 34.5% (n = 165) at the provincial/territorial level and 16.5% (n = 79) at the organizational level (Table 5). Common written sleep-related policies included ensuring stimulation-free and comfortable nap environments for children (58.7%; n = 617) and providing children with quiet play/rest time when they choose not to nap (58.3%; n = 609). Few childcare directors/owners indicated they had a written policy on providing education for families on sleep (10.0%; n = 104) or professional development for educators on sleep (12.6%; n = 131).

Most (62.4%; n = 655) childcare directors/owners noted that sleep-related policies differed by child age; common themes included varied nap frequency/duration and environments. For example, one participant mentioned “[their] babies require different bedding (cribs vs. mats) and longer nap periods than [their] 5-year-olds.” Further, it was common for educators to be in conversation with families regarding children’s sleep; one provider noted that “[they] work with families to ensure sleep routines are consistent with routines at home” while another shared that “[Educators in] infants/toddler [rooms] have more communication with parents via [their] app.” See Supplementary File 1 for a full list of themes and example quotes.

The logistic regression model examining the associations between childcare facility characteristics, explicit provincial/territorial legislation, and the presence of a written policy for ensuring children achieve age-appropriate nap durations was significant (χ2 = 18.69, df = 5, p = .002). Centre enrolment size was a significant predictor of having a written sleep policy. For each additional child enrolled, the odds of having a written sleep policy increased by 0.2% (OR = 1.002, 95% CI [1.00, 1.01], p = .027). The logistic regression analyses also showed that, compared with directors/owners of childcare facilities located in provinces or territories where sleep was explicitly referenced in childcare legislation, those located in provinces or territories without such legislation had significantly lower odds of reporting a written policy for ensuring children achieve age-appropriate nap durations (OR = 0.64, 95% CI [0.47, 0.87], p = .004).

Discussion

This study provided a national overview of movement behaviour policies and practices in childcare settings in Canada and a critical look at the factors that influence the existence of such policies. We found that movement behaviour policies were inconsistent across behaviours and were often referred to as informal practice rather than written policy statements. Physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep policies were also most prevalent in jurisdictions with behaviour-specific legislation, reflecting the impact of governance level on ensuring quality movement opportunities in childcare settings. Factors such as urbanicity, childcare type, and enrolment size also impacted existence of movement behaviour policies, warranting innovative solutions to address imbalances in policy and practice.

Half of directors/owners reported having a written policy for daily overall physical activity, and more than two-thirds reported policies for daily outdoor physical activity. These proportions represent modest increases compared with earlier national estimates reported by Ott and colleagues [22], suggesting gradual progress in this domain. Notably, BC introduced mandatory Active Play Standards in licensed childcare settings in 2017 [23], and subsequent work has demonstrated higher prevalence of written movement behaviour policies following their implementation [24]. Although this study did not include direct verification of facility-level policy documents or assessment of compliance with provincial standards, results from both the BC natural policy experiment and the present analyses suggest that regional legislation may contribute to greater consistency in childcare policy environments - an important finding given that on a global scale, most existing physical activity policies are regionally rather than nationally governed [12]. Expanding and continuing to embed movement behaviour guidance within legislation may help reduce inequities across childcare settings by establishing minimum expectations that providers must meet to maintain their license. Yet, despite these advances, progress remains unbalanced, particularly for behaviours beyond physical activity and outdoor play.

Formal policies addressing sedentary behaviour were notably limited. Less than 20% of childcare directors/owners in the present study reported that they had a written policy for limiting prolonged bouts of sedentary behaviour; however, nearly three-quarters of participants noted this was already general practice. This aligns with previous research that young children attending both centre- and home-based childcare primarily accumulate their sedentary behaviour in short bouts that are often under 4 minutes in length [25, 26]. While it is encouraging to see that most childcare facilities do not require children to remain seated for extended periods of time, it remains important to have explicit guidance on this behaviour within formal childcare policies. Current provincial/territorial legislation related to sedentary behaviour (unrelated to screen time) is lacking; only BC and QC mention the recommendation of limiting prolonged sitting in their regulations and statements are vague and without time limits for sedentary bouts [16]. On a global scale, Wenden et al.’s international scoping review [12] of 43 childcare-specific movement behaviour policies found that approximately half of childcare policy documents specified a duration for bouts of sedentary behaviour, which ranged from 15 to 60 min at any one time. Evidently, mixed messaging remains an issue with respect to recommended duration of bouts of sedentary behaviour, and future research is needed to explore if the 60-minute cap on sedentary bouts per the 24-hour guidelines [3] is appropriate for childcare settings given the seldom nature of longer bouts. Further, meaningful sedentary behaviours, like reading, storytelling, and fine motor activities are often overlooked within the context of movement behaviour policies, despite their impact on cognitive and social-emotional development [27]. A compositional analysis by Bourke and colleagues [28] on 405 preschoolers in Canada found that more time spent in sedentary behaviour relative to total physical activity was associated with better psychosocial functioning. While the evidence base is still developing with respect to meaningful sedentary behaviour as a proportion of total sedentary behaviour and its impact on early childhood development, it is important to inform educators that not all activities performed while sedentary have negative implications for children.

Contrary to limiting long bouts of sedentary behaviour, approximately two-thirds of childcare directors/owners reported they had a written policy for no screen time. The most prominent factor predicting whether childcare facilities had a written policy for having no screen time for children was childcare type, with family-/home-based programs having significantly lower odds of having such a policy in place. This finding is consistent with Vanderloo and colleagues’ systematic review [29], which found that home-based childcare programs provided more screen time for preschoolers (1.8 to 2.4 h per day) than centre-based programs (0.1 to 1.3 h per day). This is worrisome, given the large body of evidence surrounding the negative impacts of screen time on healthy child development [30]. In fact, a study with 2,441 mothers and children from AB, Canada [31] found that higher levels of screen time at 2 and 3 years of age were associated with lower scores on developmental screening tests at 3 years of age. Further, young children have also been found to engage in substantial amounts of additional screen time once they return home from childcare (upwards of 3.6 additional hours) [32], underscoring the importance of both a screen-free childcare day and increasing communication with families about the health impacts of excessive screen time in early childhood. However, while it may be best practice to ensure sedentary screen time is not routine in childcare [33], the unique demands of the family-/home-based childcare setting often make screen-free childcare more difficult. The present study highlighted how family-/home-based educators often use screens to help occupy older children while younger ones are being settled for a nap, or to give the educator a break during their long workday. With many families in Canada turning to home-based childcare arrangements due to long waitlists for securing centre-based care [34], as well as the greater reliance on home-based childcare in select countries across the OECD [35], it is more important than ever to ensure the proper supports are in place for family-/home-based educators to better manage screen use, including a toolbox of screen-free alternatives.

Findings related to sleep suggest that while many childcare facilities recognize the importance of supporting children’s nap needs, formal sleep policies remain inconsistent across settings. Less than half of directors/owners reported having a written policy to ensure age-appropriate nap durations, and where policies existed, most were at the childcare facility level rather than guided by provincial/territorial or organizational standards, which can result in considerable local variability in practice. Encouragingly, most facilities reported written policies promoting stimulation-free, comfortable sleep environments and offering quiet rest alternatives for non-napping children, which aligns with best practices that respect individual sleep needs [11, 36]. However, consistent with Thorpe et al. [37] these findings suggest that formal policies alone may be insufficient to ensure responsive, child-centred sleep-rest practices. They found that only a small proportion of services adopted genuinely responsive sleep-rest practices, emphasizing the pivotal role of leadership within childcare facilities in conceptualizing sleep and rest and creating supportive sleep environments and educator practices. Further, while it was encouraging that sleep policies were often informed by ongoing communication with families, this also suggests reliance on informal processes rather than systematic guidance. As such, the influential role of government in standardizing healthy sleep practices in childcare is essential, particularly given the presence of sleep-related legislation was associated with higher odds of facilities having written nap duration policies. Conversely, despite a small effect, there was evidence that larger enrolment size was associated with higher odds of having such policies, suggesting the potential for more robust organizational systems to support formal policy development and implementation, warranting targeted supports for smaller-capacity centres.

Across all movement behaviours, written policies related to both providing movement behaviour education to families and providing educators with movement behaviour professional development were consistently low. These findings echo those of Ott and colleagues [22], and elucidate the low prioritization of movement behaviour education both as essential professional development for educators and as a shared responsibility of educators and families. In fact, Hewitt and colleagues [38] reported that compared to childcares in Australia and the United States, childcare facilities in Canada were least compliant with the guideline of providing professional learning to staff and education to families about children’s physical activity. Early childhood educators in Canada [39], and elsewhere [40], have communicated their lack of professional training in movement behaviours, as well as their desire for additional education [41, 42]. It is not surprising, then, that policies regarding providing movement behaviour education for families are lacking when the educators feel they do not have necessary background knowledge nor relevant resources to share with families. Early childhood educators’ access to professional development during working hours is also compounded by a highly strained workforce, as they have reported to spend much of their time covering staffing shortages to maintain basic child-to-staff ratios [43]. Evidently, greater support for the childcare workforce is crucial to improve working conditions and safeguard time for paid professional learning, particularly if the optimal goal is to follow the WHO Standards; [11] as trusted professionals who engage regularly with families, educators are key influencers on families’ decision-making regarding children’s movement behaviours through the provision of evidence-based information and reinforcing these behaviours across both childcare and home environments [11]. Movement behaviour capacity building interventions for educators have proven effective at increasing information shared with families about movement behaviours; [44] as such, standardizing paid professional learning in this domain, including communication strategies with families, can help improve relationships and contribute to healthy child development.

Future directions

International evidence indicates that most childcare movement behaviour policies are enacted at subnational levels and are often broadly worded, resulting in considerable variability in interpretation and practice [12]. Consistent with the WHO Standards [11], countries across the globe should prioritize the development of national movement behaviour standards for childcare settings – designed to be legislated, monitored, and adapted by subnational governments (e.g., provinces and territories) – as these may support greater coherence across jurisdictions. However, given the lengthy timelines associated with national coordination and policy implementation, interim, culturally tailored supports are needed to strengthen current practice. Leveraging existing professional learning platforms and resource libraries, alongside immediate investment in paid professional learning time and workforce retention, may help bridge policy gaps and improve movement behaviour support in childcare settings today.

Limitations

While this study has many strengths, including strong participation across provinces and territories and childcare types, there are some limitations worth noting. First, recruitment of childcare facility directors/owners was conducted via provincial/territorial childcare directories, not all of which were publicly available nor provided upon request. In these instances, search engine results for facility contact information were used, which often led to general inquiry addresses that were not closely monitored, or contact information was out of date; this likely impacted response rates and representativeness of data, particularly in ON. Additionally, given this study was based on self-reported existence of written policies rather than a formal audit of childcare policy documents (as intended by the PLAYCE Policy Environment Audit [19]), social desirability bias likely inflated frequencies of movement behaviour policies. This study design also impacted the ability to gather evidence on the detail within movement behaviour policies or how well (or not) policies were being implemented and followed by staff in respective childcare facilities.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that movement behaviour policies in childcare settings in Canada remain inconsistent, often informal, and strongly shaped by governance and childcare context. While written policies for physical activity and outdoor play appear to be slowly improving, particularly in jurisdictions introducing new legislation, formal guidance for limiting prolonged sedentary behaviour, managing screen time, and supporting educator and family education is markedly lacking, especially in family-/home-based childcare. Given the slow pace of national coordination and subsequent lag to implementation, interim supports are needed, including leveraging existing professional learning and resources while urgently investing in paid time for professional development and addressing staffing shortages. Collectively, these findings underscore the need for evidence-informed federal policy direction, stronger implementation supports, and greater investment in the childcare workforce to ensure equitable, high-quality movement behaviour opportunities for all young children in Canada.
