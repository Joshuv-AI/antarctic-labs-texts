Chromosome-level genome assembly of a cosmopolitan marine harmful algal bloom diatom species Chaetoceros socialis (Chaetocerotaceae)
Authors: Zongmei Cui, Nansheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chaetoceros socialis is a cosmopolitan diatom species that is crucial for maintaining marine ecosystem structure and driving elemental cycles. C. socialis can form harmful algal blooms (HABs) that may cause a negative impact on the marine ecosystems. Whole-genome information for C. socialis is still unavailable, which may hinder more targeted studies on its ecological adaptive responses and evolutionary drivers. To address this gap, we employed cutting-edge genomic technologies including PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing and high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) to achieve the first chromosome-level genome assembly of C. socialis. The assembled genome is 60.22 Mb in size with a scaffold N50 of 7.81 Mb and has been anchored to eight pseudochromosomes. A total of 13,378 protein-coding genes were predicted, of which 12,069 (90.22%) were functionally annotated. This high-quality genomic resource provides a fundamental data platform for systematically elucidating the ecological adaptation mechanisms of C. socialis.

Background & Summary

The diatom genus Chaetoceros (Ehrenberg, 1844) is a dominant and widely distributed group in coastal and oceanic waters worldwide, critical for both primary production and biogeochemical cycling1–4. The genus is highly diverse with 283 species currently reported5. Metabarcoding analysis confirmed that Chaetoceros species are not only diverse, but also globally distributed6–8. Despite their ecological importance and high biodiversity, genomic sequences are available for only two species, including Chaetoceros tenuissimus (strain NIES-3715)9 and Chaetoceros muelleri (strain NMCA1316)10, with both genome assemblies reaching only the contig and scaffold levels, without chromosome-level assemblies. In addition, there is genomic data for an undetermined species (Chaetoceros sp. strain Eukbin1)11, which is a metagenome-assembled genome (MAG). Mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of only a few Chaetoceros species have been assembled12,13. The limited availability of genomic information for Chaetoceros species severely limits in-depth investigation into the genetic evolution and adaptive mechanisms of Chaetoceros species.

Chaetoceros socialis is a cosmopolitan species, found from polar to tropical waters, and often dominates phytoplankton assemblages in both open oceanic and coastal environments14–16. This species is characterized by its colonial structure, which consists of intertwined curved chains that form a secondary spherical shape17. Chaetoceros socialis plays a significant role in marine organic matter burial due to its capacity for rapid growth, bloom formation, and spore production18,19. Recent studies indicate that this species alone exerts a dominant influence on the amount of organic carbon reaching the seafloor20. Blooms formed by intense and prolonged proliferation of C. socialis have been found to diminish the appetite of farmed fish and hinder the growth of cultivated laver21,22. Beyond its ecological roles, C. socialis has drawn interest for its reported bioactive properties23. Preliminary in vitro studies have indicated that this species exhibits selective cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects on LNCaP (prostate cancer) and U-87 MG (glioblastoma) cell lines via apoptotic and survival pathways24. Therefore, constructing a high-quality chromosome-level genome of C. socialis is not only conducive to fundamentally understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying its environmental adaptation and metabolite synthesis, but also holds significant importance for in-depth functional gene exploration and genetic resource development.

In this study, we report the first chromosome-level genome assembly of C. socialis. The initial assembly based on PacBio single-molecule sequencing yielded a genome size of 62.45 Mb with a contig N50 of 4.794 Mb. Subsequent Hi-C scaffolding anchored 97.63% of the contigs into eight pseudochromosomes. The final assembly size is 60.22 Mb with a super-scaffold N50 of 7.81 Mb, and a total of 13,378 protein-coding genes were predicted, of which 90.22% were functionally annotated. This high-quality genome provides a valuable resource for elucidating the ecological adaptation and bioactivity-related metabolic mechanisms of Chaetoceros species.

Methods

Sampling collection and genome sequencing

The C. socialis strain (CNS00121) analyzed in this study was isolated under a microscope using a single-cell capillary from water samples collected in the Bohai Sea, China (geographic coordinates: 38° N, 120.117° E), in October 2019. The isolated single cell was cultured in cell culture plates with L1 medium and 1-2% penicillin-streptomycin mixture (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., P1400, 100X) was added to remove bacteria. The cultures were maintained at 18–20°C, and the irradiance was 60 μM photons m−2 s−1 at the photoperiod of 12 h light-12 h dark. This strain showed typical morphological features of C. socialis (Fig. 1B). Exponentially growing C. socialis cultures (20 L) were collected by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 5 min, and the collected samples were used for genome survey sequencing, PacBio sequencing, transcriptome sequencing and the Hi-C sequencing. Magnetic-bead based protocol was used to extract long fragment DNA from harvested cells to provide high quality long fragment DNA (≥40Kb)26. For genome survey analysis, short reads were obtained using MGI short-reads sequencing. The MGI sequencing library was constructed using MGISEQ-2000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads.

The PacBio Continuous Long Read sequencing library was constructed using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 and then was sequenced using the PacBio Sequel SMRT Cell 1 M platform. For transcriptome sequencing, total RNA with high quality was extracted through cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method26. The mRNA library (DNBSEQ) was sequenced by the MGISEQ-2000-PE150 platform. The full-length transcriptome libraries were sequenced by PacBio Sequel SMRT Cell 1 M. The Hi-C library was constructed according to the Hi-C library protocol by Liu et al.26, and sequencing was carried out on the MGISEQ-2000-PE150 + 150 + 10 platform.

Genome survey and genome assembly

Raw sequencing data were filtered using SOAPnuke (v1.6.5) (https://github.com/BGI-flexlab/SOAPnuke) to obtain clean reads for subsequent analysis. To estimate the features of the genome, the 21-mer frequencies were calculated using jellyfish (v2.1.4)27, then GenomeScope (v1.0)28 is used to fit the 21-mer spectrum.

The PacBio continuous long reads were assembled de novo. The assembly strategy consisted of an initial Mecat229 (Parameter settings: GENOME_SIZE = 73 Mb, MIN_READ_LENGTH = 10 kb, CNS_OVLP_OPTIONS = “-kmer_size 13”, CNS_OUTPUT_COVERAGE = 40, TRIM_OVLP_OPTIONS = “-skip_overhang”, FSA_OL_FILTER_OPTIONS = “--max_overhang = −1, --min_Identity = −1”), followed by third-generation polishing with Arrow (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/.

GenomicConsensus), second-generation polishing with Pilon30, and finally, redundancy removal using Purge Haplotigs31 (parameter settings: -l 15 -m 110 -h 180). Genome completeness and quality were evaluated by BUSCO (v6.0.0)32 against the stramenopiles_odb12 dataset.

The Hi-C-assisted genome assembly pipeline was as follows: sequencing reads were aligned to the genome via bwa invoked by Juicer (v1.6)33 with quality assessment, and alignment results were filtered to remove unaligned, invalid ligation, and duplicate reads. Subsequently, four-step assembly was performed using 3D-DNA34 software: filter the assembly draft to discard low-confidence scaffolds; correct errors by identifying and splitting misassembled sequences via locus interaction frequency consistency analysis, retaining fragments with consistent interaction patterns; anchor, order and orient scaffolds based on inter-sequence interaction intensity to obtain preliminary chromosome-level sequences; merge scaffolds and contigs by sequence homology and long-range interaction patterns, finally generating high-quality chromosome-length assembled sequences with comprehensive evaluation.

Genome annotation

Genome annotation primarily encompasses three aspects: repetitive sequence annotation, non-coding RNA annotation, and gene structure prediction along with functional annotation.

Repetitive sequences were annotated through homology-based alignment and de novo prediction. The homology-based approach used RepBase (v21.12)35 along with RepeatMasker (v4.0.7) and RepeatProteinMask (v4.0.7) (http://www.repeatmasker.org/cgi-bin/RepeatProteinMaskRequest) to identify regions similar to known repetitive sequences. De novo prediction was performed using software such as RepeatScout36 (http://bix.ucsd.edu/repeatscout), Piler37, and LTR_FINDER (v1.07)38 (http://tlife.fudan.edu.cn/ltr_finder/) to construct a species-specific repetitive sequence library, followed by prediction using RepeatMasker. Additionally, Tandem Repeats Finder (v4.09)39 (http://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html) was used to identify tandem repeat sequences in the genome.

Non-coding RNAs were annotated using a combination of tools. Specifically, tRNAs were predicted by tRNAscan-SE 1.3.140 based on their characteristic structures, whereas rRNAs were identified by aligning them against known rRNA sequences from related species using BLASTN. Additionally, miRNAs and snRNAs were detected by applying covariance models from the Rfam database41 with the INFERNAL tool.

Gene structure prediction employed a multi-step integrated strategy. First, initial homology-based annotation was performed using MAKER (v2.31.8)42 with protein sequences from six related species (Arabidopsis thaliana, Fragilariopsis cylindrus, Phaeodactylum tricornutum, Seminavis robusta, Thalassiosira pseudonana, Chaetoceros tenuissimus). Subsequently, 2,000 homologous genes from this set were selected as a training set to train the de novo prediction software Augustus43 and SNAP44. Meanwhile, RNA-seq and ISO-Seq transcriptomic data were integrated for auxiliary annotation: sequencing reads were aligned to the genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0)45, transcripts were assembled using StringTie (v1.3.4 d)46, and corrections were applied via PASA_lite (https://github.com/PASApipeline/PASA_Lite). Combining the protein sequences from the six related species, the transcript information obtained from transcriptome prediction, and the trained Augustus and SNAP models, a second round of gene structure prediction and integration was conducted using MAKER. The completeness of the gene set was assessed using BUSCO (v6.0.0)32 against the stramenopiles_odb12 database.

Functional annotation of genes was based on the gene set derived from structural annotation. BLAST (v2.2.31)47 was used to compare against known protein databases, including SwissProt48, TrEMBL, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)49, InterPro50, GenBank Nr, eukaryotic orthologous groups (KOG)51, and InterPro and gene ontology (GO)52, with e-value less than 10−5 to obtain functional information. Additionally, InterProScan53 was used to search domain databases for functional inference.

Data Overview

In this study, 30.12 Gb of short reads (~500× sequencing depth) were generated via genome survey, with the species’ estimated genome size of 73.50 Mb and repetitive sequence content of 52.7% (Table 1). Concurrently, 18.69 Gb of PacBio long reads (254× sequencing depth) were obtained, assembling a 62.45 Mb genome with a contig N50 of 4.794 Mb; BUSCO (v6.0.0)32 assessment against the stramenopiles_odb12 database yielded a gene coverage rate of 99.0% (Table 1). The smaller size of the third-generation assembly compared with the estimate might result from the high proportion of repetitive sequences (52.7%) interfering with k-mer analysis, generating false repeat peaks and thus causing overestimation. Hi-C sequencing produced 25.58 Gb of data. Following Hi-C scaffolding, redundancy removal via purge haplotigs, and curation, the final genome assembly was 60.22 Mb with a super-scaffold N50 of 7.81 Mb (Table 1). Of this assembly, 58.79 Mb of sequences (97.63%) were anchored onto eight pseudochromosomes (Fig. 1A), with individual lengths ranging from 5,491 to 9,254 kb (Fig. 1B); the size reduction was directly attributable to redundant sequence elimination during these two quality control steps. In addition, the genome was divided into 100 kb bins, and a heatmap based on Hi-C interaction intensity confirmed the assembly reliability (Fig. 1A). Integrating multiple annotation methods, 26.11 Mb of transposable element sequences were identified, accounting for 43.35% of the total genome length (Table 1), and 13,378 protein-coding genes were predicted. BUSCO assessment showed 94.8% coverage of 661 conserved proteins, including 642 single-copy (92.1%) and 19 multi-copy (2.7%) proteins. Evaluate the completeness of genome assembly and annotation

To evaluate the consistency of the genome assembly (i.e., the matching degree between the assembled sequence and the original sequencing data), this study performed a single-base coverage depth analysis. The results showed that the base coverage depth of the assembled sequence was concentrated around 100×, and the cumulative coverage rate approached 100% when the depth reached 100×, with no obvious zero-coverage or extremely low-coverage regions. This indicates that the genome assembly in this study has good consistency, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent analyses (Fig. 3). Telomere sequence analysis is a reliable tool for verifying the quality of genome assembly. In this study, we found that the telomere sequence of C. socialis is (TTAGGG/CCCTAA)n, which is consistent with that of pseudochromosomes of the model brown alga Ectocarpus60, but differs from the (AACCCT)n telomere sequence of the model diatom Phaeodactylum tricornutum61. The telomeric repeat sequences (TTAGGG/CCCTAA) of the eight pseudochromosomes were detected and visualized using ggplot262.
