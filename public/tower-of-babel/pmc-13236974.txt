ABSTRACT
Condylar hyperplasia (CH), characterized by progressive facial deviation, occlusion disorders, and temporomandibular joint dysfunction, often requires combined temporomandibular joint (TMJ)-orthognathic interventions to reconstruct joint and occlusal functions as well as rebuilding harmonious facial expressions. Managing the dentomaxillofacial deformities secondary to CH involves hyperplastic condylar resection, orthognathic surgery, facial contour surgery and orthodontic treatment. Based on the activity of condylar hypertrophy, the severity of dentomaxillofacial deformity and malocclusion, an individualized treatment plan should be formulated to reconstruct joint functions, correct deformities and recover occlusal relationships. This expert consensus, informed by the latest clinical research and practical experience, addresses clinical considerations for surgical treatment strategies for patients with different CH types, delineating indications, objectives, procedures, and principles with the aim of providing clear and practical guidance for clinical practitioners.

Introduction

Condylar hyperplasia (CH), also known as condylar hyperactivity, condylar hypertrophy, hypermetabolic condyle, is the nontumor overgrowth of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) condyle in three-dimensional (3D) space. The manifestation of CH often affects the unilateral side of TMJ. In addition to the abnormal size and shape of the condylar head and neck, excessive hyperplasia often extends to the ascending ramus or even the mandibular body, resulting in progressive facial deviation, occlusion disorders, and temporomandibular joint dysfunction,1–3 and ultimately impairs the quality of life for patients with CH.

The dentomaxillofacial deformity secondary to CH is the main demand of patients for medical treatment. The suggested surgical intervention timing for CH is in the adult period. There is controversy about the sequence and approach of surgical treatment for the dentomaxillofacial deformity secondary to CH owing to the complexity and diversity of the development of CH. Therefore, uniform treatment protocol is still unestablished.

To this end, this expert consensus summarizes existing clinical experience, and research results and aims to provide oral and maxillofacial surgeons with a systematic cognitive framework and clear idea of diagnosis and treatment. We hope this consensus offers a clear, practical reference framework for clinicians, promoting standardization and consistency in accurate diagnosis and treatment of CH and secondary dentomaxillofacial deformities.

Etiology Of CH

CH was initially considered to be related to rheumatoid arthritis.4 But the etiology and pathogenesis mechanisms are relatively complicated and remain unclear. Early trauma and infection, local circulatory impairment and the functional load of the TMJ area, and genetic and endocrine disorders may all cause CH.5,6 Moreover, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) and its receptor (IGF-1R), B-cell CLL/lymphoma 2 (BCL2) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF-A) are key regulators of chondrocyte proliferation and apoptosis, which molecular expressions were found upregulated in CH patients and may play an important role in the development of CH.7–11 The present diagnosis and treatment of CH have no relation to the etiology. Further clarification of the etiology and pathogenesis of CH is important for the development of treatment strategies.

Clinical Manifestations Of CH And Secondary Dentomaxillofacial Deformities

CH usually occurs in people at 11–30 years old, and the incidence rate in females is higher than that in males.12–16 CH often involves a unilateral condyle, leading to facial asymmetry, occlusion disorders, and temporomandibular joint dysfunction. According to the clinical and radiological features of dentomaxillofacial deformities secondary to CH, the classification published by Obwegeser and Makek is recommended in clinical practice:16

Type I: Hemimandibular elongation (HE): HE refers to the overdevelopment and elongation of the mandible on the affected side in the horizontal direction. The main clinical manifestations include the following: (1) The chin and the midline of the lower jaw significantly deviate toward the unaffected side, and the lateral view shows a skeletal Class III pattern. (2) The mandibular angles on both sides are located on the same horizontal plane. (3) The occlusal plane does not significantly cant. The affected side of the mandible mostly exhibits Angle Class III malocclusion, and the molars of the unaffected side are often inclined and crossbite. (4) The volume and shape of the condylar head are close to those on the unaffected side, but the neck is slender and curved. (5) The ascending ramus and mandibular body on the affected side are longer than those on the unaffected side (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Clinical feature and radiological manifestations of CH-Type I. a–c CT volume rendering images. d CT coronal slice of bilateral condyles. e Intraoral occlusion. f Panoramic radiograph. CT with 3D reconstruction is recommended to elucidate the 3D morphology changes of the condyle and the characteristics of the dentomaxillofacial deformity secondary to CH, to determine the classification based on the clinical manifestations, to develop surgical plans, and to predict the treatment effect.17

Differential diagnosis

Diagnosis and classification of CH is based on the clinical manifestations and imaging examinations. Attention should be given to the following diseases for differential diagnosis: condylar tumors, mainly condylar osteochondroma; unilateral micro-mandibular deformities, especially dentomaxillofacial malformations secondary to unilateral condylar resorption; and jaw overdevelopment caused by pituitary or endocrine factors.

Pathological examination

Pathological examination of the resected hyperplastic condyle is suggested for definitive diagnosis. Histological examinations of CH often show that the thickness of the hypertrophic layer of the condylar fibrocartilage near subchondral area is significantly increased in the resected CH samples.10,18–29 Therefore, the combination of medical history and imaging or pathological examination could be used for diagnosis.

Determination of active hypertrophy condyle

The activity of condylar growth is the key to the timing of treatment and the selection of different surgical approaches for CH and secondary dentomaxillofacial deformity. The criteria for determining that the condyle is in an active proliferative phase include: (1) SPECT with scintigraphy is performed to compare the uptake rates of 99 m technetium-methylene diphosphonate (99mTc-MDP) in the bilateral condyles quantitatively.30 The threshold of 99mTc-MDP uptake degree for CH diagnosis varies according to ethnic disparity underscores the necessity of developing region-specific.31 The general consensus is that when the ratio of condylar uptake to total bilateral condylar uptake reaches 55% or more, or when the difference in condylar uptake between the two sides is 10% or more, the condyle on side with the high intake rate is in the active hyperplasia stage.12,18,32–37 (2) Spiral CT or CBCT could also be performed at an interval of 6 months to compare condylar and occlusal change. If the condylar height or diameter was found increased by 5% at two consecutive follow-up CT examinations or by 10% at one follow-up CT examination, or if significant occlusal/facial appearance change was reported by the patient, the condyle is deemed to be at an active phase.17,27

Treatment Strategies of CH And Secondary Dentomaxillofacial Deformities

Treatment objectives

The main treatment for CH and dentomaxillofacial deformity is surgery combined with necessary preoperative and postoperative orthodontics in the adult period of the patient. The objectives should include correction of the jaw deformity, improvement of the occlusal relationship, and resection of the diseased condyle to block the progression of the asymmetric deformity and reconstruct good function of the temporomandibular joint, thus achieving stable long-term effects of good function and morphology of the stomatognathic system. The treatment mainly involves two parts: condylar surgery and orthognathic surgery. In addition, pre-operative and post-operative orthodontic treatment are beneficial for occlusion management and improvement. Early diagnosis and treatment of overactive condyles can effectively reduce the incidence of severe dysfunction and the degree of secondary dentomaxillofacial deformity and help relieve or prevent the psychosocial distress associated with the deformity.3,38,39 It is recommended that children and adolescent CH patients with secondary dentomaxillofacial deformities wait until adulthood before undergoing surgical intervention. Orthodontic treatment is applicable to patients who only have malocclusion but no dentomaxillofacial deformities.

Recommended treatment principles of hypertrophic condyle

Condylar resection or retention is the key factor in the surgical treatment of CH and secondary dentomaxillofacial deformities. The appropriate timing of resection can not only improve the function and appearance of maxillofacial tissues but also reduce postoperative complications and possibility of surgery in patients.40 Whether hypertrophic condyle is in the active stage affects the necessity of condyle resection,3 and should be determined based on the imaging examination results and clinical manifestations, with special attention given to the degree of deformity in patients in adulthood.When the hypertrophic condyle is in the active stage, partial resection of the condyle is recommended to prevent overgrowth of the condyle and progressive aggravation of the subsequent dentomaxillofacial deformity. Combined orthognathic surgery is optional based on the degree of dentomaxillofacial deformity. For some patients with the milder dentomaxillofacial deformity, the ideal function and facial shape improvement can be achieved by resection of the active hyperplastic condyle combined with orthodontic treatment.36,41–44 It is worth noting that the active hypertrophy condyle has a certain probability of transforming into an inactive one. This situation usually occurs in patients under the age of 20 to whom a longer observation period is valuable.

However, if a patient has obvious joint dysfunction, such as pain, limited mouth opening, and severe dentomaxillofacial deformity or obvious condylar deformity, it is difficult to restore the best mandibular movement function, occlusal relationship, and facial shape through orthognathic surgery alone, therefore it is suggested that these patients take surgery combined with condyle resection.

According to the assessment results of the CH activity, combined with the severity of dentomaxillofacial deformity, functional impairment and treatment needs of the patient, a personalized treatment plan can be developed to provide accurate treatment according to the actual condition of the patient.

Recommended treatment principles of dentomaxillofacial deformity treatment

Dentomaxillofacial deformity is the most intuitive clinical manifestation of CH, and treatment includes improvements in jaw deformity, occlusal reconstruction, and the stability of temporomandibular joint function. Therefore, the correction of dentomaxillofacial deformity should consider both function and aesthetics. According to the deformity of the patient, the most suitable personalized orthognathic or jaw bone contouring surgery should be selected for the patient. The main surgical methods used are Le Fort I osteotomy of the maxilla, sagittal split ramus osteotomy (SSRO), intraoral vertical ramus osteotomy (IVRO), genioplasty, mandibular contouring surgery (angle or border resection of the mandible). Digital surgical technologies

Digital surgical technologies are highly recommended in surgery planning of dentomaxillofacial deformities secondary to CH.55 Surgical treatment involving hyperplastic condylar resection, orthognathic surgery, facial contour surgery, the traditional occlusal plate technology is unable to provide precise positioning in three-dimensional space. Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM) and have fundamentally transformed the management paradigm for CH.

For condylar section, the precise cutting line could be designed by CAD, and be implemented by CAM, therefore the condyle could be functionally preserved to the most extent.52,56–58 For orthognathic surgical procedures, CAD significantly improves the three-dimensional symmetry of the jaw, and helps the orientation of the condyle after ramus osteotomy. At the same time, CAM by cutting and drilling guides, pre-bent titanium plate and 3D printed surgical plates could remarkably enhance the accuracy of bone cutting, bone movement, and fixation, which further improve the surgical efficiency.58–61 For facial contouring surgery, the CAD/CAM technique also provides both precision and aesthetic results to the patients.62

Conclusions

CH is a dynamically developing disease, and secondary dentomaxillofacial deformities involves complex changes in jaw morphology, temporomandibular joint function and occlusion.5 The unpredictability of the disease course, the significance of individual differences, and the diversity of surgical strategies make the diagnosis and treatment of CH challenging. Therefore, accurate determination of the hypertrophic condyle activity, reasonable selection of surgical timing, development of a personalized surgical plan, and combination of orthodontic treatment and joint function rehabilitation training are the keys to ensuring successful treatment. At the same time, the tendency of maxillary re-canting and mandibular re-deviation in CH patients should be fully considered in the surgical planning. And post-surgical occlusion management by the orthodontists is also necessary for avoiding re-lapse of the jaw deformity and malocclusion in these patients.

This expert consensus provides theoretical guidance and a practical reference for the standardized diagnosis and surgical treatment of CH and secondary dentomaxillofacial deformities, and to improve the clinical treatment effect (Fig. 7).
