Tagln1-Cre–driven Ercc1 deficiency reveals sex-specific effects on adipose tissue in mice
Authors: Annika A Jüttner, Patric J D Delhanty, Jadey D Raben, Yoëlle Goos, Anke McLuskey-Dankbar, Youri Boon, Rene de Vries, Jan von der Thüsen, A H Jan Danser, Anton J M Roks, Jenny A Visser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic aging is marked by changes in insulin sensitivity and adipose tissue remodeling, including the vasculature. Transgelin 1 (Tagln1), a marker for vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC), is used as a promoter for Cre-driven gene knockout (KO) in mice. However, Tagln1 is also expressed in white adipocytes. We previously showed that Tagln1-Cre–driven KO of DNA repair endonuclease Ercc1 in mice (Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice) leads to accelerated aging of VSMC and perivascular adipocytes, resulting in vascular dysfunction and lipodystrophic-like perivascular adipose tissue. The aim of this study was to investigate the consequences of Tagln1-driven Ercc1 deficiency on whole-body metabolism and adipose tissue morphology and function. Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice did not show the usual age-related gain in fat mass. Adipose tissue depots from male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice shared similarities with adipose tissue depots from advanced-aged mice, including smaller white adipocyte size, decreased vascularization, increased oxidative stress, macrophage infiltration, and fibrosis compared with male wild-type (WT) mice. Female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice showed an upregulation of antioxidant genes, which might be a protective response resulting in minor changes in their adipose tissue depots compared with female WT mice. Male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice showed partially preserved adipose tissue function and improved insulin sensitivity. Interestingly, analyses showed that Tagln1 expression varied by sex and adipose tissue depot in WT mice. To conclude, we showed that accelerated aging induced by Ercc1 deficiency in Tagln1-dependent cell populations leads to white adipose tissue and brown adipose tissue remodeling and inflammation, as also observed in normal aging

Aging is associated with chronic metabolic disorders, including obesity and type 2 diabetes (1). During aging, adipose tissue undergoes significant changes, including redistribution and structural and functional remodeling, leading to adipose tissue dysfunction, which promotes chronic inflammation, altered adipokine secretion, and insulin resistance (1, 2). These age-related changes can be referred to as metabolic aging.

Healthy adipose tissue is responsible for maintaining systemic energy, glucose and lipid homeostasis (1). It consists of adipocytes, smooth muscle cells (SMC), endothelial cells (EC), neuronal and immune cells (3). Adipose tissue can be classified into brown adipose tissue (BAT), which is critical for energy expenditure (EE) and nonshivering thermogenesis (3-5), white adipose tissue (WAT), which is responsible for energy homeostasis and lipid storage, and an intermediate beige adipose tissue subtype (5). WAT is further subdivided by anatomical location into gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT), inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), axillary white adipose tissue (aWAT), and visceral white WAT (6). The function of WAT and BAT is tightly regulated by insulin, lipids, hormones and adipokines, and the transport and distribution thereof rely on correct vascular perfusion of the adipose tissue depots (1, 7). Vascular aging could be a precipitating factor. Female WAT from young mice is more vascularized than male WAT (8), but it is unclear whether this sex difference persists with aging (8).

Age-related vascular dysfunction could contribute to impaired adipose tissue function and alterations in insulin sensitivity. The decline in insulin sensitivity and WAT function develops sex-specifically in middle-aged mice (9, 10). It is unclear what drives metabolic aging associated with adipose tissue changes, particularly the sequence of events, the involved adipocyte development factors, and the causal mechanism of aging require further research. Most studies on advanced aged mice exclude female mice (9-12), leaving it unclear whether sex differences persist until very old age. Mouse models in which aging is driven by a specific causal mechanism and controlled by an adipocyte development factor could be instrumental for untangling age-related mechanistic changes in the adipose tissue depots.

One driver of metabolic aging is genomic instability caused by accumulated DNA damage (13), triggering a DNA damage response, which features increased cellular stress, senescence, and inflammation [reviewed extensively elsewhere (14)]. KO of DNA repair endonuclease Ercc1 induces accelerated DNA damage accumulation and thereby premature aging in mice (15). Moreover, Ercc1 KO induces the accumulation of senescent cells, which are marked by increased expression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (p21), plasminogen activator inhibitor (Pai-1), and pro-inflammatory markers monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (Mcp-1), Il-6, and Tnf-α (16-18). The upregulation of these inflammatory markers is also characteristic of chronic low-grade inflammation during aging (19, 20). Furthermore, it has also been shown that Ercc1 KO in mice activates antioxidant genes that are involved, among others, in glutathione and quinone metabolism (21, 22). In cell-specific Ercc1 KO mice driven by transgelin 1 (Tagln1)–Cre (Tagln1-Cre Ercc1), we previously showed vascular dysfunction and low perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) mass at the age of 22 weeks (16, 23). Tagln1, also known as smooth muscle 22 alpha, is an established SMC marker and is frequently used as a promoter to drive SMC-specific Cre (24, 25). Tagln1 supports the structural integrity and stability of the cytoskeleton by crosslinking actin (25). However, Tagln1 was recently reported to be a novel marker for white adipocyte precursors (26), where it plays a role during adipocyte differentiation (26). Moreover, lower PVAT mass has been reported for two independent Tagln1-Cre–driven KO mouse models (23, 27). Thus, Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice could reveal important aging-related pathophysiological mechanisms in adipose tissues in which Tagln1-controlled cell differentiation plays a role, and their impact on whole-body metabolic changes.

Here, we investigate the impact of Ercc1 deficiency driven by Tagln1-Cre on EE, specifically adipose tissue function and morphology other than PVAT. Given the lack of data on female metabolic aging, we also assessed potential sex differences in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice.

Methods

Mouse model

Mice were bred as described previously (16). In brief, the Cre-loxP system was used to generate Tagln1-Cre–driven Ercc1 KO mice (Tagln1-Cre Ercc1) by cross-breeding of Tagln1-promoter (Taglntm2(Cre)Yec, 118 c57Bl/6 background, The Jackson Laboratory) with Ercc1flox/− (Ercc1tm2Dwm, FVB 119 background, Erasmus MC colony) mice. The corresponding Cre+/−::Ercc1flox/+ healthy wild-type littermates (WT) were used as control WT mice. Only Cre-positive mice were used for the WT control group. All mice had access to water and food (complete universal vegetal diet, DS801727G10R, SDS diets) ad libitum. At the age of 8 weeks, 7 male WT, 6 male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1, 6 female WT, and 7 female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice were analyzed. At the age of 22 weeks, 23 male WT, 19 male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1, 8 female WT, and 10 female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice were divided over several experiments (see in vivo measurements discussed later). Mice were euthanized by exsanguination while deeply anesthetized under 3% isoflurane at the age of 8 or 22 weeks. BAT and WAT depots (gWAT, aWAT, and iWAT) were isolated, weighed, with one part stored in paraformaldehyde and the other part snap-frozen for mRNA analysis as described previously (28, 29). The iWAT depot refers to the posterior subcutaneous WAT, and the aWAT depot refers to the anterior subcutaneous WAT.

Animal experiments were performed at the Erasmus Laboratory Animal Science Center Rotterdam following the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU. All studies were approved by the National Animal Care Committee and the Erasmus University Medical Center Rotterdam.

Body composition and intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test

At the age of 8, 12, 16, and 20 weeks, 11 male WT, 9 male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1, 9 female WT, and 10 female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice were weighed, and their body composition was assessed by EchoMRI™ (100 V Whole Body Composition Analyzer, EchoMRI LLC) in the morning. Afterwards, mice were fasted (no food, with water ad libitum) for 5 hours, followed by an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) as described previously (28). In brief, fasting glucose was measured with a glucometer (FreeStyle Freedom Lite glucometer, Abbott) followed by intraperitoneal injection of a glucose bolus (2 g/kg body weight). Blood glucose was measured 15, 30, 60, and 120 minutes after injection. The average plasma glucose levels were then plotted per group over time. To assess the area under the curve, glucose levels were normalized to fasting glucose level per mouse, plotted over time, and the area under the curve was calculated with GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0.1).

Hormone measurements

Fasting insulin was measured in blood plasma collected at baseline of the IPGTT with the Ultra-Sensitive Rat insulin ELISA kit (Crystal Chem cat no. 62300, RRID:AB_3662575) following the manufacturer's instructions. From fasting glucose and insulin levels, the quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) was calculated using the formula QUICKI = 1/[(log(fasting insulin) + log(fasting glucose)] as described previously (30).

Moreover, leptin, IL-6 and TNF-α plasma levels were measured in blood collected in EDTA-coated tubes during the sacrifice of the mice. After centrifugation for 5 minutes at 2500 rpm and 4 °C, the EDTA plasma was stored at −20 °C for measurement. Leptin levels were quantified with the Leptin Mouse ProcartaPlex™ Simplex Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat no. EPX01A-26036-901, RRID:AB_2575954) and IL-6 and TNF-α with the High Sensitivity 5-Plex Mouse ProcartaPlex™ Panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat no. EPXS050-22199-901, RRID:AB_2576189) on a Bio-Plex MAGPIX multiplex reader (Bio-Rad, Laboratory Medical Immunology, Erasmus MC) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Indirect calorimetry assessment

One week before sacrifice (21 weeks of age), the mice (11 male WT, 7 male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1, 9 female WT, and 9 female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1) were housed individually in a metabolic cage system (Promethion, Sable Systems International). The cages (Greenline, Tecniplast) contained woodchip bedding (Lignocel BK 8/15, J. Rettenmaier & Söhne GmbH) and were fitted with a water bottle, food hopper, and a nest. These were designed to measure water, food intake, and body weight, respectively, via load cells located in the lids of the cages. The horizontal and vertical locations of the mice in each cage were detected by an infrared beam grid, and data were converted to locomotor activity. All mass and positional measurements were detected at 1 Hz. Cage air influx was set at 2 L/min, and volumes of consumed oxygen (V̇O2), produced carbon dioxide (V̇CO2), and water vapor (V̇H2O) were measured at 5-minute intervals, from which EE and respiratory exchange ratio (RER) were derived. Data from the system were collected using MetaScreen (Version 2.3; Sable Systems International) software and processed with MacroInterpreter (version 23.9; Sable Systems International). Indirect calorimetry data (1-hour bins) from a 48-hour period following approximately 24 hours of habituation were analyzed with CalR (version 1.3) (31). Two-way analysis of covariance (IBM SPSS Version 28) with lean mass as a covariate was used to analyze effects of sex and genotype on EE. Two-way ANOVA (GraphPad Prism 10.0.1) was used to evaluate differences in RER and locomotor activity between the 4 sex/genotype experimental groups. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Acute insulin test

For the acute insulin test, only male mice were analyzed, because no differences were observed between female WT and Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice in the IPGTT. Mice were fasted for 5 hours and weighed, and the appropriate amount of insulin/body weight was calculated. Male mice were injected with 1% (w/v) BSA in saline (vehicle) or 1 IU/kg insulin (WT mice, n = 6 per group; Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, n = 5 per group). Mice were sacrificed 15 minutes after injection, and tissues were dissected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for later analysis.

Protein expression

Total and phosphorylated AKT protein expression was evaluated by Western blot. Proteins were extracted from insulin-stimulated or vehicle gWAT, aWAT, iWAT, and BAT in homogenization buffer (0.3 mol/L sucrose, 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 1 mmol/L EGTA, 1 mmol/L sodium orthovanadate, 50 mmol/L sodium fluoride, 1 mmol/L DTT, 1 mmol/L phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 1% [v/v] Triton X-100, phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 3 [Sigma-Aldrich] and cOmpleteTM Protease Inhibitor Cocktail [Roche]). Samples were normalized to the lowest protein concentration with 1 × Laemmli buffer and incubated for 10 minutes at 65 °C. A total of 20 µg (BAT and gWAT) or 25 µg (aWAT and iWAT) protein per sample was loaded on a 4% to 20% Criterion™ TGX™ Precast Midi Protein Gel (Bio-Rad) together with a protein ladder (PageRuler™ Plus Prestained Protein Ladder, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Trans-Blot Turbo 0.2 µm PVDF Transfer Pack, Bio-Rad) with the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) BSA or nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 1% [v/v] Tween 20 (TBS-T) buffer for 2 hours at room temperature (RT) according to the primary antibody, followed by overnight incubation in the primary antibodies rabbit anti-total AKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology cat no. 4691, RRID:AB_915783) and rabbit anti-phosphorylated AKT (Ser473) (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology cat no. 4060, RRID:AB_2315049) or rabbit anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; 1:10 000; Proteintech cat no. 10494-1-AP, RRID:AB_2263076) as loading control (diluted in 5% [w/v] BSA or nonfat milk [GAPDH] in TBS-T buffer) at 4 °C. The membranes were then incubated for 1 hour at RT in the secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit-horseradish peroxidase [HRP] conjugate [Bio-Rad, 1:3000; Bio-Rad cat no. 170-6515, RRID:AB_11125142] diluted in 5% [w/v] nonfat milk in TBS-T buffer) and visualized with the Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad) following the manufacturer's instructions using an Amersham Al600. Membranes were exposed for a timeframe so that all bands were clearly visible but not overexposed. Moreover, colorimetric pictures of the protein ladder per membrane were taken. Pictures were saved in TIFF format, and the signals of the bands for the proteins were quantified in Image Studio Lite (version 5.2.5). Data are presented as the ratio of protein of interest/GAPDH for individual proteins or as the ratio of phosphorylated/total AKT as average per group ± SEM.

mRNA expression

Samples from gWAT, aWAT, iWAT, BAT, liver, and skeletal muscle were homogenized in TRIzol (Thermo Fisher), and RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was synthesized according to the manufacturer's protocol (Maxima H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, Thermo Scientific). Quantitative PCR was performed with the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher) measured as duplicates per sample (1 × PCR Master Mix, 200 nmol/L forward primer, 200 nmol/L reverse primer, 10 ng cDNA). β-Actin, Gapdh, and Rpl13a were used as reference genes measured in the CFX Opus Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad). Data were analyzed with the 2ΔΔCt method, expressed as relative quantity. Used primers (self-designed and ordered from IDT) are listed in Table 1.

Histology

Paraffin-embedded adipose tissue and liver samples, fixed in paraformaldehyde, were sectioned into 5-µm-thick sections using a microtome. These sections were mounted 1:20 on glass slides and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The adipose tissue sections from the 22-week-old mice were also stained with Picrosirius Red to assess the collagen content. Representative pictures per group were included in the manuscript. Whole slide scans were performed with a digital imaging system (Hamamatsu NanoZoomer 2.0HT Digital Slide Scanner).

Adipocyte size measurement and lipid area quantification

Representative images from 3 H&E-stained sections per depot and animal were randomly selected and captured using viewing software (NDP.view2). For BAT and aWAT, lipid area quantification was performed in an automated manner in ImageJ (Fiji, Version 2.16.0) using a macro that contained image conversion to black and white (8-bit) and a threshold of 210 to 255 for BAT and 220 to 255 for aWAT. Area (µm) and area fraction (%) were the outcome measurements, which were exported to Excel. Lipid area was calculated as the percentage of total area and presented as average ± SEM per adipose tissue depot, sex, and genotype.

For gWAT and iWAT, adipocyte size was determined in the H&E-stained sections in ImageJ (Fiji, Version 2.16.0) as described previously (32). In brief, the images were first converted to black and white, and a threshold was set manually per adipose tissue depot according to the best differentiation between adipocytes. The following features were used for the automated size determination: Circularity was set at 0.2 to 1.00, and size was set at 100 to 50,000 µm. Data are presented as average ± SEM per adipose tissue depot, sex, and genotype.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining for F4/80 and cleaved Caspase-3 was performed in gWAT sections from 2 male and 2 female mice per group. For liver tissue, the staining for TAGLN1 and CD31 was performed on sections from 2 male and 2 female 22-week-old mice. Sections (5 µm thick) were mounted, deparaffinized, blocked for endogenous peroxidase and alkaline phosphatase activity, and washed with demi water. Following antigen retrieval in 1 × Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0), sections were cooled down, rinsed in 1 × PBS and blocked for nonspecific binding with 5% (v/v) normal goat serum (X090710-8, Agilent Technologies) in 1 × PBS for 30 minutes. Then, sections were incubated with primary antibodies rabbit anti-F4/80 (1:400; Cell Signaling, 70076; RRID:AB_2799771), rabbit anti-cleaved Caspase-3 (1:100; Cell Signaling, #9661; RRID:AB_2341188), rabbit anti-Tagln1 (1:400; Abcam, cat no. ab14106, RRID:AB_443021) or rabbit anti-CD31 (1:2000; Abcam, cat no. ab182981). Sections were incubated at 4 °C overnight and washed with 1 × PBS. Next, sections were incubated for 30 minutes at RT with BrightVision antirabbit HRP-conjugated (ready-to-use, PVR55HRP, VWR) as the secondary antibody, washed with 1 × PBS, and the antibody binding sites were revealed with 3,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride. Finally, all sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

TAGLN1 staining on gWAT, aWAT, iWAT, and BAT sections from 2 male and 2 female WT mice was performed according to the protocol as previously described, but with the following adjustments: antigen retrieval was performed in 1 × citrate buffer (pH 6.0), sections were blocked for nonspecific binding with 10% (v/v) normal goat serum (S-1000-20, VectorLabs) in 5% (w/v) BSA, and blocked for endogenous biotin. Sections were incubated with primary antibody rabbit anti-Tagln1 (1:200, Abcam cat no. ab14106, RRID:AB_443021) and then incubated with biotinylated antirabbit (1:200, Vector Laboratories cat no. BA-1000, RRID:AB_2313606) as secondary antibody. Afterward, the sections were incubated with streptavidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (avidin-biotin complex-HRP, 1:200 dilution, Vector Laboratories cat no. PK-6100, RRID:AB_2336819), the antibody binding sites were revealed with 3,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride, and all sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Liver lipid analysis

The liver H&E-stained sections were blindly scored regarding compactness of the tissue and the relative presence of lipids with the following criteria per category: 1, compact, few lipids; 2, compact, some lipids; 3, fairly compact, some lipids (mix); 4, less compact, some lipids; 5, less compact, more lipids; and 6, not compact, many lipids.

Triglyceride assay

The liver triglyceride content was analyzed with the Triglyceride Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience; #E-BC-K238-96) in homogenized tissue. Twenty milligrams of liver was washed in cold PBS buffer and then homogenized on ice in 180 µL PBS buffer with added protease inhibitor (cOmplete, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor cocktail tablets, REF 11873580001, Roche). After centrifugation (10 minutes, 4 °C, 12 000 × g), the supernatant was collected and immediately used for the measurements at 510 nm with the CLARIOstar plate reader (BMG LABTECH; RRID:SCR_026330) following the kit manual. The resulting absorption values were then normalized to protein content in the individual samples, measured in a Bradford assay (CLARIOstar plate reader, BMG LABTECH).

Statistics

Results are presented as average ± SEM for all groups except for mRNA expression, where results are presented as geometric mean ± SD of geometric mean (33). Results presented in column graphs were either evaluated by an unpaired Student t test (when 2 groups were compared) or 2-way ANOVA with a post hoc Bonferroni multiple comparisons test when assessing how sex and genotype (2 independent categorical variables) affect a dependent continuous variable (eg, leptin levels; GraphPad Prism 8.0.1). For each gene panel, the data were analyzed by 2-way ANOVA with a post hoc Bonferroni multiple comparisons test to correct for the number of genes analyzed. IPGTT data were analyzed with 2-way ANOVA with a post hoc Bonferroni multiple comparisons test and a general linear model with repeated measures (GraphPad Prism 8.0.1).

Results

Effects of Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 KO on general metabolic parameters

Body weight and composition were recorded starting at the age of 8 weeks until the age of 20 weeks (Fig. 1A), since at these ages we previously observed vascular dysfunction in this mouse model (16, 23). We hypothesized that potential metabolic changes might occur around the same age. In contrast to WT mice, body weight of Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice did not show the age-related increase as observed in the WT mice but remained unchanged over time in both sexes (Fig. 1A, left panel). Furthermore, Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice were not able to gain fat mass over time, in contrast to their corresponding WT littermates (Fig. 1A, middle panel). The difference in fat mass was particularly pronounced in male mice starting already after 8 weeks, whereas in female mice the genotype effect became statistically significant after 12 weeks. However, there was no statistical difference between male and female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice regarding total fat mass. To more clearly analyze these changes, we compared the endpoints at the age of 8 and 20 weeks per sex. Male 20-week-old Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice showed a trend of losing fat mass compared with male 8-week-old Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice without reaching statistical significance (P = .0588), while male WT mice strongly gained fat mass over time (P < .0001). Female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice neither gained nor lost fat mass over time (P > .9999), which is in contrast to female WT mice, which showed an increase in fat mass over time (P < .0001). Male WT mice displayed a high body weight and fat mass (34), reminiscent of an obese-like phenotype (35-37), which has been reported previously for male mice from this strain (34). However, female WT mice did not show a similar high gain in body weight at 22 weeks of age, which is in line with a previous report (38). This sex difference in body weight likely contributed to the more pronounced genotype effect in male mice. Absolute lean mass increased over time in male and female WT mice. In contrast, in male and female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, lean mass remained relatively constant over time (Fig. 1A, right panel), resulting in significantly lower absolute lean mass in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with WT mice for both sexes at the age of 16 and 20 weeks.

Indirect calorimetry was used to assess changes in EE, RER, mean locomotor activity, and food intake (Fig. 1B and 1C). EE (full day and dark phase) was significantly decreased in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with WT mice in both sexes (Fig. 1B, left panel). Furthermore, we observed a significantly higher RER in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice (full day and light phase) (Fig. 1B, right panel). There was neither a genotype nor a sex effect on locomotor activity (Fig. 1C, left panel). Food intake was significantly increased in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice in the light phase, but there was no genotype effect during the dark phase or the whole assessment period (Fig. 1C, right panel).

To evaluate potential changes in glucose sensitivity, we performed an IPGTT. Male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice were significantly more glucose tolerant than male WT mice, while there were no differences between female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 and WT mice (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, compared with male WT mice, fasting glucose and plasma insulin levels were lower in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, suggesting they have greater insulin sensitivity (Fig. 1E, left and middle panels). This was supported by the QUICKI, which was higher in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice than in male WT mice (Fig. 1E, right panel).

Changes in body composition and glucose metabolism also affected circulating levels of the adipokine leptin and the proinflammatory markers (Fig. 1F). Plasma leptin levels were significantly lower in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice, but there was no difference between female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 and WT mice (Fig. 1F, left panel). We previously reported no genotype or sex effect in circulating adiponectin levels (23). Plasma IL-6 levels were significantly increased in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice (Fig. 1F, middle panel), while no differences were observed between female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 and WT mice. Plasma levels of proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α were not different between the groups (Fig. 1F, right panel).

The only difference was the denser iWAT from female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice (Fig. 2B, right panel), which was confirmed by a significantly decreased average adipocyte size (Fig. 2B and 2C, right panels).

In all adipose tissue depots of Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, insulin-induced pAKT levels were increased, but particularly for gWAT and BAT, Ercc1 deficiency led to increased insulin sensitivity compared with male WT mice (Fig. 12A, top-to-bottom panels, respectively). In the latter, insulin-induced pAKT levels only increased significantly in iWAT and aWAT. In gWAT and BAT, the higher insulin-induced pAKT levels did not reach statistical significance. In female mice, there was no genotype effect visible in the H&E staining. The triglyceride levels in the livers of male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice were reduced compared with male WT mice (Fig. 13B, right panel), but there was no genotype effect on triglyceride levels in female mice, which correlated with the histological assessment.

Ercc1 mRNA expression was decreased in the livers from 8-week-old Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with WT mice, independent of sex. At the age of 22 weeks, there was a similar pattern, but in males, the reduction did not reach statistical significance (39) (Fig. S2C). To analyze whether in the skeletal muscle, another metabolically active tissue, Ercc1 mRNA expression might also be affected, we performed similar measurements in skeletal muscle tissue at both ages. Ercc1 mRNA expression was decreased in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with WT mice, independent of sex and time point (39) (Fig. S2C).

As a positive control, we measured Ercc1 mRNA expression in the lung, since this is an SMC-rich tissue. Ercc1 mRNA expression was similarly decreased in lungs of both male and female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male and female WT mice, respectively, at the age of 22 weeks (39) (Fig. S1C).

Potential role for transgelin in sex-dependent ERCC1 loss in the adipose tissue depots of Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice

Ercc1 KO in our mice was achieved using the Tagln1-Cre construct. Since, to our knowledge, sex-biased Cre activity is unknown, we analyzed whether sex differential expression of Tagln1 could have impacted our results. Therefore, Tagln1 mRNA expression was measured in all adipose tissue depots from 22-week-old male and female WT mice. Tagln1 mRNA expression differed sex- and depot-specifically (Fig. 14A). In gWAT and BAT, Tagln1 mRNA expression was increased in female WT mice compared with male WT mice (Fig. 14A). However, in aWAT and iWAT, an opposite pattern was observed with decreased Tagln1 mRNA expression in females compared with males. In the liver from female WT mice, Tagln1 mRNA expression was increased 1.5-fold compared with male WT mice, and in skeletal muscle from female WT Tagln1 mRNA expression increased 1.8-fold compared with male WT mice (P = .04 and P = .0002, respectively).

Tagln1 expression in adipose tissue depots and liver from 22-week-old wild-type (WT) mice. (A) Tagln1 mRNA expression in adipose tissue depots from 22-week-old WT mice, analyzed with an unpaired t test. #Significant effect of sex with P < .05. (B) Representative immunohistochemical staining for TAGLN1 in adipose tissue depots and in the liver from 22-week-old WT mice. Arrows in blue indicate positive staining for TAGLN1 around adipocytes, and black arrows indicate positive staining around arteries.

To investigate the localization of TAGLN1 within the different adipose tissue depots, we performed immunohistochemical staining on adipose tissue sections from 22-week-old WT mice (Fig. 14B). We observed a subpopulation of TAGLN1-positive adipocytes (marked by a blue arrow) in male and female gWAT, as well as in male and female aWAT and iWAT (Fig. 14B, left panels). Furthermore, in BAT we observed clusters of TAGLN1-positive adipocytes in male and female WT mice, which were generally more abundant in female BAT (Fig. 14B, left panels), which is in line with the mRNA data (Fig. 14A). Additionally, there was TAGLN1-positive staining in SMC in small arteries in all four adipose tissue depots (indicated with a black arrow).

We also analyzed the localization of Tagln1 in the liver given the reduced hepatic Ercc1 mRNA expression. Only SMC in the (micro)vasculature of the liver expressed TAGLN1, and we confirmed the identity of small blood vessels with CD31 staining. We did not detect TAGLN1-positive staining in hepatocytes of male and female WT mice (Fig. 14B, bottom pictures).

To sum up, we observed sex- and adipose depot-specific differences in Tagln1 mRNA expression and protein localization, which possibly contributed to the observed sex differences in adipose depots of Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Tagln1 expression appeared to be specific for SMCs and adipocytes in the analyzed tissues.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effect of Tagln1-Cre–driven Ercc1 KO, a strategy used to induce DNA damage-based accelerated aging, on adipose tissue depot function and morphology and consequences for glucose homeostasis in mice. Male and female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice failed to gain adipose depot mass after 8 weeks of age. Additionally, male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice showed pronounced morphological and molecular changes in the adipose tissue depots, including smaller adipocytes, giving them a denser appearance compared with male WT mice. mRNA expression of vascularization markers was strongly decreased in all adipose tissue depots from male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice, which was accompanied by increased fibrosis and macrophage infiltration in WAT, suggesting low-grade inflammation typically occurring during aging. Moreover, male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice were more insulin sensitive compared with male WT mice, suggesting improved metabolic health. However, it is important to consider the obese-like phenotype of male WT mice, which has been reported previously (34) and partially contributed to the pronounced differences in the evaluated parameters compared with male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. In contrast, in female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, there were only minor morphological, molecular, and functional changes in the adipose tissue compared with female WT mice, despite the impairment of gaining fat mass over time. Thus, the consequences of ERCC1 deficiency in adipocytes and SMCs driven by Tagln1-Cre varied sex-specifically, with adipose tissue depots of male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice being more affected and female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice being partially protected. Moreover, we observed sex- and adipose tissue depot-specific Tagln1 expression in WT mice, which is a novel finding for Tagln1.

The Tagln1-Cre promoter, which was used to drive Ercc1 KO, is highly expressed in SMC-rich tissues like aorta, heart, and lung, whereas lower expression has also been reported in BAT (24, 42). Recently, Tagln1 has been reported to be a novel marker for a specific subpopulation of male white pre- and mature adipocytes (26). However, our study is the first to report that Tagln1 is expressed in a sex-specific manner in WAT and BAT of adult WT mice, which might contribute to differences in adipose tissue depot composition and function between male and female WT mice (43, 44). In male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, ERCC1 deficiency occurred in all adipose tissue depots, leading to pronounced changes in morphology and function compared with male WT mice, while in female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice only partial ERCC1 deficiency was observed in the adipose tissue depots, correlating with mild morphological and functional changes compared with female WT mice. Vascular dysfunction has been observed in both male and female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice (16, 23), which correlated with a similar degree of ERCC1 deficiency in vascular (SMC-rich) tissue. Tagln1 is located on chromosome 11 (45), which is not a sex chromosome, and we currently know of no data that demonstrate sex-specific Ercc1 expression or Cre activity that could explain the sex dimorphic effects of Ercc1 KO in the adipose tissue of Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Transgelin 2, a member of the same gene family as Tagln1, has a known sex-dimorphic function in adipose tissue (46), and in combination with our results, this suggests a sex-dependent role for both Tagln variants.

Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice failed to gain adipose depot mass after 8 weeks of age, which was accompanied by pronounced morphological and molecular changes in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, including a decreased average adipocyte size, leading to a denser BAT and WAT morphology at the age of 22 weeks. Mean adipocyte size in aWAT and vWAT from male mice first increases with age (middle-aged mice) and then decreases with advanced age, accompanied by a reduction of total fat mass at the age of 24 to 30 months (12, 47, 48). Moreover, in mouse models of premature aging, which exhibit a severe phenotype in the adipose tissues, including loss of adipose tissue depot mass, average adipocyte size was significantly decreased compared with WT mice (19, 34, 48, 49). Our findings in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice strongly resemble the adipose morphology of these advanced aged mice, suggesting an advanced aging phenotype in all adipose tissue depots in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Impaired vascularization of the adipose tissue depots in advanced-aged male mice contributes to increased oxidative stress in WAT (48), which aligns with decreased vascularization and antioxidant markers in WAT from 22-week-old male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice. Moreover, the excessive accumulation of collagens during aging leads to adipose tissue fibrosis and thickening of the vascular wall of the microvasculature, contributing to impaired perfusion of the adipose tissue (48, 50, 51), which also aligns with our findings in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Unfortunately, the studies discussed herein about advanced and premature aging mice did not include female mice. However, it is known that middle-aged female mice are partially protected from metabolic aging (10). Female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice showed smaller differences in adipose tissue morphology and gene expression compared with female WT mice and normal glucose tolerance despite the impairment in gaining fat mass. These minor changes could indicate a partial protection of female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice from metabolic aging persisting until advanced age. Indeed, the upregulation of antioxidant and mitochondrial markers in BAT from female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with female WT mice suggests a protective response, which might have mitigated the metabolic phenotype in female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice.

The impairment in gaining fat mass was not linked to increased lipolysis in the adipose tissue depots from Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Furthermore, decreased mRNA expression of Dlk1, an inhibitor of adipocyte differentiation (40), in the adipose tissue depots from Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, suggests enhanced adipogenesis rather than compromised preadipocyte differentiation. However, further research is required to investigate potential alterations in preadipocyte differentiation in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Instead, the impairment to gain fat mass could potentially be linked to apoptosis in Tagln1-expressing preadipocytes. We indeed observed increased cleaved caspase 3 protein expression in gWAT from Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with WT mice starting at the age of 8 weeks and progressing until the age of 22 weeks. Senescent Ercc1-deficient fibroblasts secrete Tnf-α, which induces apoptosis in neighboring Ercc1-deficient fibroblasts (52). Similarly, Ercc1-deficient preadipocytes (white and brown) could undergo apoptosis, which could contribute to the hampered age-related fat mass gain in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice.

The increased IL-6 plasma levels in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice suggest the presence of elevated systemic inflammation compared with male WT mice, which is also indicative of aging (13). Aging aWAT and epididymal adipose tissue have been identified as major sources of circulating proinflammatory cytokine IL-6 (41). At the age of 8 weeks, we observed increased mRNA expression of Mcp-1 across all adipose tissue depots from Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice, independent of sex, which was accompanied by increased Il-6 mRNA expression compared with WT mice. Over time, this leads to chronic inflammation, which affects the composition and gene expression pattern of adipose tissue depots (1, 3) and might partially explain the downregulation of most of the evaluated markers in aWAT, gWAT, iWAT, and BAT from 22-week-old male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice. Moreover, MCP-1 is involved in the process of macrophage infiltration in WAT (53), which contributes to the progression of chronic low-grade inflammation during natural aging (19, 20) but has also been reported for premature aging (19), as observed in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome, which is marked by accelerated aging features in the whole body, loss of aWAT, and insulin resistance (54-57). In contrast to the insulin resistance observed in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome, insulin sensitivity improved in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice and was unchanged in female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with their WT counterparts. Systemic insulin sensitivity was also improved in naturally advanced-aged male mice and whole-body ERCC1-deficient male mice compared with young and corresponding male WT mice, respectively (12, 34), suggesting that male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice exhibit typical age-related metabolic changes. We excluded a contribution of ectopic lipid accumulation, which typically occurs with age (48) and is linked to impaired lipolysis driven by adipose tissue macrophages (58), to the metabolic phenotype of Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Despite decreased lipolysis markers in WAT, male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice showed less lipids and triglycerides in the livers compared with male WT mice at the age of 22 weeks, suggesting enhanced metabolic health, which correlates with improved systemic insulin sensitivity in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice. Furthermore, tissue-specific (BAT and WAT) insulin sensitivity was increased in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice, suggesting that BAT and WAT functions are at least partially maintained. Preservation of function despite the low amount of fat mass was also observed in PVAT from Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice (23), which mainly consists of beige adipose tissue depot, a mixture of BAT and WAT. Despite this partial preservation of function and denser appearance, the expression of most of the analyzed markers, including all BAT markers and the mitochondrial marker Pgc1α was decreased in BAT from male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice compared with male WT mice, which indicates impaired mitochondrial function and has been linked to disrupted BAT activity during aging (13, 59, 60). The lower BAT mass in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice could potentially be linked to apoptosis in a subpopulation of preadipocytes, similar to what has been discussed for white preadipocytes above, which might also contribute to the reduced expression of BAT markers. Furthermore, it should also be taken into consideration that the large difference in fat mass between male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 and male WT mice could have contributed to the pronounced differences in the mRNA expression data, despite correction for cDNA (ng) and normalization to the housekeeping genes.

Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice additionally displayed adaptations in energy metabolism, including decreased EE and increased RER compared with the corresponding WT mice. The reduced lean mass in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice could explain the decrease in EE. However, because EE is suppressed independently of lean mass (as assessed by analysis of covariance), there may be an additional effect of the genotype. The lower RER of male WT mice compared with male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice is possibly related to their increased fat mass and might be explained by shifts in energy metabolism toward utilizing fat as the primary fuel source instead of carbohydrates. Excess fat storage alters their metabolic pathways, encouraging fat oxidation and reducing reliance on carbohydrate metabolism (61). Additionally, the relative insulin resistance observed in the male WT mice compared with female mice suppresses carbohydrate metabolism, leaving the body to rely more on fat as a fuel source (62). The lower RER of male WT mice compared with male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice also correlates with decreased food intake in the light phase, which could be linked to their obese-like phenotype and has been reported for obese mice fed with a high-fat diet compared with lean mice (63). However, it cannot be ruled out that the altered feeding behavior in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice is partly driven by changes in the hypothalamus, the main regulator of feeding and energy balance signals (64). However, Tagln1 expression was not detected in single-cell expression data of the murine hypothalamus (65), suggesting that Tagln1-Cre–driven Ercc1 deficiency probably does not play a major role in this brain region. Low levels of Tagln1 have been reported in the pericytes in the blood–brain barrier (66). Therefore, Tagln1-Cre–driven Ercc1 deficiency in pericytes could potentially impair blood-brain barrier function, similar to the observations in EC-specific Ercc1 KO mice (67). Changes in blood–brain barrier function can alter the transport of metabolic signals, such as leptin, into the brain. Whether Tagln1 shows sex-differential expression in pericytes is unknown. Therefore, further research is warranted to unravel the mechanisms underlying the differences in food intake observed only between male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice and male WT mice.

One limitation of this study is that the obese-like phenotype of male WT mice, including relative insulin resistance compared with female WT mice, should be taken into consideration. However, similar observations regarding high body and fat mass and relative insulin resistance have previously been reported for male WT mice with an FVB::C57b6/J hybrid background (34), which suggests that the hybrid strain background also contributes to these metabolic sex differences. Across strains, female mice are generally more insulin sensitive compared with male mice (68), and females also show a milder phenotype to high-fat diet and genetically induced forms of insulin resistance compared with male mice (69, 70). Therefore, the FVB::C57b6/J hybrid strain background could also induce a milder metabolic effect in female WT mice compared with male WT mice, which could partially explain the observed sex differences between the WT mice. As a consequence, the observed sex differences between the WT mice are more pronounced.

Furthermore, decreased Ercc1 mRNA expression in the liver and skeletal muscle (bulk tissue) might also affect glucose homeostasis in male Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Since TAGLN1 was only present in VSMCs and not in hepatocytes, we concluded that using Tagln1 as the promoter to drive Ercc1 KO, VSMCs are most likely the only cells in the liver with ERCC1 deficiency. Whether this loss of Ercc1 expression hampers hepatocyte function remains to be studied. Given that despite decreased Ercc1 mRNA expression in the liver and skeletal muscle, female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice did not show changes in glucose homeostasis compared with female WT mice, the contribution of possible altered liver and muscle function to the metabolic phenotype in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice is likely minor.

To conclude, we showed that genomic instability induced in VSMC and Tagln1-dependent adipocyte populations leads to an acceleration of WAT and BAT aging and remodeling, decreased vascularization, and increased inflammation, which is also observed in normal aging. Male and female Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice failed to gain fat mass over time, with males being more prominently affected compared with females. This phenotype included partial preservation of adipose tissue function and insulin sensitivity in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Moreover, we discovered sex- and depot-specific differences in Tagln1 expression, which could indicate a sex-dimorphic role of Tagln1 that might have contributed to the sex differences in Tagln1-Cre Ercc1 mice. Our results contribute to improving the understanding of adipose tissue aging related to genomic instability in the context of specific tissue development drivers such as Tagln1 and consequences thereof for glucose homeostasis.
