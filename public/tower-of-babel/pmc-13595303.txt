Metabolomics-Driven Discovery of Targets for Hepatocytotoxicity Induced by Triphenyltin Chloride through Mitochondrial Function Involving HSD17B10 and HADH
Authors: Yanwei Wang, Jiahui Zhao, Shanshan Pan, Xuesong Liu, Yong Chen, Tengfei Xu, Mingliang Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triphenyltin chloride (TPTC), a member of the organotin family, has been widely restricted due to its endocrine-disrupting effects. Nevertheless, TPTC continues to be detected in aquatic environments and poses potential health risks owing to its persistence and bioaccumulation. Although previous studies have shown that triphenyltin compounds can disrupt lipid homeostasis through nuclear receptor pathways involving retinoid X receptor (RXR) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), whether TPTC exerts hepatocytotoxicity through direct metabolic targets remains unclear. This study employed a metabolomics-driven target discovery strategy to analyze intracellular metabolite extracts from HepG2 cells exposed to TPTC for 24 h, aiming to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms of TPTC-induced metabolic toxicity. Untargeted global metabolomics analysis revealed that TPTC exposure caused significant disturbances in hepatic lipid metabolism, characterized by the accumulation of medium- and long-chain fatty acids and depletion of key metabolites associated with fatty acid β-oxidation. Pathway enrichment analysis and protein–protein interaction network analysis further indicated that two mitochondrial dehydrogenases involved in fatty acid catabolismhydroxysteroid 17β-dehydrogenase 10 (HSD17B10) and hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADH)are potential metabolic targets responsible for the observed alterations. Molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance, and cellular thermal shift assays confirmed direct interactions between TPTC and both HSD17B10 and HADH, with binding affinities at the micromolar level. Functional validation demonstrated that TPTC disrupts mitochondrial respiratory function and redox balance by interfering with HADH- and HSD17B10-dependent generation of acetyl-CoA and NADH, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. In summary, this study reveals a previously underappreciated mechanism of TPTC toxicity involving mitochondrial metabolism, extending the current understanding of its toxicological pathways beyond nuclear receptor-mediated signaling to include direct disruption of mitochondrial energy and redox metabolism.

Introduction

Triphenyltin chloride (TPTC), a member of the organotin family, was once widely used as a fungicide, pesticide, wood preservative, and antifouling agent in marine coatings. , However, due to its discovered toxicity to the endocrine system, reproductive system, and developmental system, it has been banned or restricted in many countries and regions. Nevertheless, TPTC continues to be detected in marine environments, freshwater bodies, and sediments at concentrations ranging from nanograms per liter to micrograms per liter. Combined with its slow elimination and metabolic processes, TPTC bioaccumulates in humans through the food chain. Therefore, elucidating the potential toxicity of TPTC to human health is of considerable importance.

The liver, as a crucial metabolic organ in the body, plays a significant role in the detoxification of environmental pollutants. Studies have demonstrated that organotins significantly accumulate in the livers of marine animals , and mice, , leading to metabolic disorders. The mechanisms of action for triphenyltin (TPT) and tributyltin (TBT) have been shown to potentially disrupt lipid homeostasis in mammals by interacting with nuclear receptor heterodimer modules formed by the retinoid X receptor (RXR) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs). − In addition, molecular mechanisms such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and immunosuppression have also been reported as potential toxic mechanisms of organotin compounds. More importantly, the disruption of lipid metabolism mediated by RXR/PPARs can hardly explain the rapid energy and redox imbalance induced by organotin compounds, particularly TPTC. Therefore, it is critical to further investigate the mechanism underlying the impact of TPTC on energy imbalance at the hepatic metabolic level.

To comprehensively investigate the potential hepatotoxicity of TPTC and its underlying mechanisms, this study employed untargeted metabolomics to assess TPTC-induced metabolic dysregulation in hepatocytes. By correlating key metabolic enzymes within the metabolic network, potential therapeutic targets were identified. In the present study, pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the differential metabolites screened by metabolomics to identify key metabolic enzymes regulating the affected pathways. Subsequently, a protein–protein interaction network of these key enzymes was constructed and subjected to cluster analysis. Further validation was conducted using molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance, and cellular thermal shift assays. Finally, functional rescue experiments were performed to confirm the critical role of the screened targets in TPTC-induced functional outcomes. This study addresses a critical knowledge gap regarding the potential mechanisms of TPTC-induced hepatocytotoxicity and enhances the understanding of its toxic effects.

Cell Viability Analysis

To determine the overall cytotoxicity of TPTC on HepG2 cells, elucidate its dose–response relationship, and select an appropriate concentration range for subsequent mechanistic experiments, this study first assessed cell viability after 24 h of treatment with varying concentrations of TPTC using the CCK-8 assay. HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL in a 96-well plate. Referring to mechanistic exposure dose, , TPTC was diluted with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mmol/L l-glutamine and 1% pyruvate to prepare concentration gradient working solutions from 0 to 10 μmol/L. The cells were incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 reagent, measured at 450 nm according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To simultaneously compare the cytotoxic effects of TPTC on hepatocytes and other cell types, the impact of TPTC exposure on the viability of human renal proximal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) was assessed under the same conditions following a 24-h treatment period.

Metabolomics Analysis

To systematically characterize the metabolic reprogramming features of HepG2 cells following TPTC exposure and to identify potentially affected key metabolic pathways as well as potential targets, this study conducted nontargeted/broadly targeted metabolomics analysis. Based on the preliminary IC50 and cell viability results, concentrations of 0.5 and 1 μmol/L were selected for treatment. This selection ensures a sufficient number of viable cells after 24 h for stable and reproducible metabolite extraction and detection, while also aiming to capture metabolic perturbations associated with the toxic phenotype as comprehensively as possible, thereby providing a basis for subsequent mechanistic validation and target screening. HepG2 cells were plated at 5 × 104 cells/mL in 96-well plates. Following adhesion, the cells were exposed to TPTC at concentrations of 0.5 or 1 μmol/L or 0.5% DMSO as the solvent control for 24 h. Following TPTC treatment, cellular metabolites were extracted according to established protocols. Metabolic profiling was performed using an HPLC system coupled to a 6550 iFunnel quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF) mass spectrometer. Polar metabolites were separated using a BEH Amide column under ESI-negative mode, while hydrophobic metabolites were analyzed using an Atlantis T3 column under both ESI-positive and ESI-negative modes. Specific chromatographic gradients, mobile phase compositions, and mass spectrometry parameters (including dry gas temperature of 225 °C, gas flow rate of 14 L/min, and capillary voltage of ±3500 V) were strictly configured as per the established method. To ensure data quality, pooled quality control samples and blank samples were interspersed throughout the entire analytical sequence, and a randomized injection order was implemented. Data acquisition and preanalysis were conducted using Xcalibur software version 4.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), facilitating peak comparison, selection, and quantification through mzCloud, mzVault and MassList databases. Metabolites were identified by matching mass spectrometry data with the METLIN database (http://metlin.scripps.edu/) and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (http://www.hmdb.ca/). Statistical analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA), were utilized to differentiate sample distributions between treatment and control groups. Metabolic pathways were analyzed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.kegg.jp/).

Seahorse Assay

Given that metabolic disturbances are often closely associated with alterations in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation function, to assess whether TPTC causes impairment of mitochondrial energy metabolism, this study further conducted measurements of parameters related to real-time oxygen consumption in cells using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test. Mitochondrial function was assessed using the Agilent Seahorse XF24 analyzer, following previously published methods. HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in XFe24 microplates and exposed to either 0.5% DMSO or TPTC (0.5 and 1 μmol/L) for 24 h. Seahorse XF24 sensor cartridges (Agilent, USA) were hydrated and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Real-time mitochondrial stress analysis was performed in Seahorse XF RPMI base medium (Agilent, USA) containing sodium pyruvate (1 mmol/L), l-glutamine (2 mmol/L), and glucose (10 mmol/L). Oligomycin (1.5 μmol/L), carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP, 1.5 μmol/L), and Rotenone/Antimycin A (Rot/AA, 0.5 μmol/L) inhibitors were added. The experiment was conducted on the Seahorse XF24 system (Agilent, USA). Basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production were calculated using Wave software (version 2.6.1).

Relative Quantification of NAD+ and NADH

To investigate whether TPTC exposure disrupts cellular redox homeostasis and induces abnormalities in energy metabolism coupling, this study measured the relative levels of NAD+/NADH. NAD+ and NADH are core cofactors in glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and the electron transport chain. Their ratio sensitively reflects the accumulation of reducing equivalents, oxidative stress, and changes in mitochondrial electron transfer efficiency. During the oxidation of ethanol to acetaldehyde by alcohol dehydrogenase, NAD+ is reduced to NADH. Subsequently, NADH reduces WST-8 in the presence of 1-Methoxy-5-methylphenazinium Methyl Sulfate, resulting in the formation of an orange-yellow formazan compound. This reaction principle is used to determine the levels of NAD+ and NADH in the samples. Following the protocol outlined in the assay kit, the samples were processed, and the maximum absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Additionally, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 to quantify the relative levels of NAD+ and NADH.

Relative Quantification of NADP+ and NADPH

To further evaluate whether the supply of antioxidant reducing equivalents is affected by TPTC, this study measured the relative levels of NADP+/NADPH. NADPH is a critical source of reducing power for the glutathione/thioredoxin systems to maintain redox balance and eliminate ROS; its depletion or an altered ratio typically indicates enhanced oxidative stress or impairment of supplying pathways such as the pentose phosphate pathway. The glucose-6-phosphate is oxidized to 6-phosphogluconate under the action of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, while reducing NADP+ to NADPH. The resulting NADPH subsequently reduces WST-8 to produce an orange-yellow formazan in the presence of 1-Methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methyl sulfate. This reaction principle is used to determine the levels of NADP+ and NADPH in the samples. Following the protocol outlined in the assay kit, the samples were processed, and the maximum absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Additionally, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 to quantify the relative levels of NADP+ and NADPH.

Relative Quantification of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

To directly verify whether TPTC induces oxidative stress in cells and to quantify its extent, this study employed the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe to detect the relative levels of intracellular ROS. HepG2 cells were exposed to complete culture medium containing 0.5% DMSO or TPTC (0.5 and 1 μmol/L) and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was removed, and the cells were incubated with 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA diluted in serum-free medium for 20 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The cells were then washed three times with serum-free medium to remove excess DCFH-DA. The fluorescence intensity of dichlorofluorescein (DCF) was measured using a microplate reader. Additionally, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 to determine the relative quantification of ROS.

Relative Quantification of Malondialdehyde (MDA)

To assess whether the oxidative stress induced by TPTC further leads to oxidative damage of membrane lipids, this study measured the levels of MDA, a terminal product of lipid peroxidation. As a classic biomarker reflecting the extent of lipid peroxidation, MDA levels can be used to characterize the progression from ROS accumulation to irreversible oxidative damage. According to the protocol outlined in the assay kit, the concentration of MDA in the cells was determined by measuring the absorbance of the MDA/thiobarbituric acid complex. A standard curve was constructed using the MDA standard solution provided in the kit to quantify the MDA content. Additionally, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 to determine the relative quantification of MDA.

Protein–Protein Interaction Network Analysis

To systematically integrate the differential metabolites obtained from metabolomics analysis with potential regulatory proteins/pathways and to identify possible key hub proteins and functional modules, this study constructed a protein–protein interaction network based on the inference of relevant regulatory proteins derived from KEGG pathway annotations. Based on previous literature, differential metabolites were used to identify regulatory proteins via the KEGG database. Protein–protein interaction networks were then retrieved from the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). The resulting STRING network was visualized using Cytoscape version 3.8.2 (https://www.cytoscape.org/). In the visualization, node size and edge transparency were utilized to represent the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) scores, reflecting the significance and interactions of the proteins within the network.

Molecular Docking

To assess the potential binding feasibility and relative binding affinity trends between TPTC and candidate target proteins at the structural level, this study performed molecular docking analysis. Molecular docking is a research method based on the “key and lock” pairing principle. By screening the structures of ligands and receptors from databases, software algorithms can analyze the binding conformations and binding energies between ligands and receptors. The three-dimensional structures of the following proteins were obtained from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/): tubulin (PDB ID: 2BTO), 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 10 (HSD17B10, PDB ID: 7ONU), 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADH, PDB ID: 3RQS), medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADM, PDB ID: 4P13), acetyl-CoA acyltransferase 2 (ACAA2, PDB ID: 1WDK), short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADS, PDB ID: 7Y0A), acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1, PDB ID: 6P2J), long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADL, PDB ID: 8W0T), very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADVL, PDB ID: 8CA1), enoyl-CoA hydratase short chain 1 (ECHS1, PDB ID: 2HW5). The DS Docking software (BIOVIA) was used to evaluate the binding affinity of TPTC with the aforementioned target proteins. The spatial conformations and electrostatic surfaces of the ligands and proteins were visualized using PyMOL (https://pymol.org/), aiding in the understanding of the binding mechanisms and interaction characteristics.

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) Interaction Assay

To obtain direct evidence of “real-time, label-free” binding between TPTC and candidate target proteins, and to assess their binding kinetics, this study employed SPR to validate the interaction with HADH and HSD17B10. Based on previous literature, SPR assay was using to confirm interactions between TPTC and the proteins (HADH and HSD17B10) by the Biacore T200 system (GE Healthcare). The real-time, label-free SPR interaction analysis was performed as follows: recombinant human proteins were immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip through amine coupling, with a reference channel (protein-free) serving as a negative control. TPTC was applied at five concentrations (31, 63, 125, 250, and 500 μmol/L) to measure response units (RU) for affinity screening, and each concentration was tested in triplicate to ensure data reliability. Data fitting of the association and dissociation phases was conducted using Scrubber 2 software (BioLogic Software Pte Ltd.), which enabled accurate modeling of the binding kinetics. The running buffer used for the assays contained 10 mmol/L HEPES, 3 mmol/L EDTA, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, and 0.5% DMSO, adjusted to a final pH of 7.4. This buffer composition was optimized to maintain protein stability and provide reproducible binding conditions for TPTC interaction analysis.

Western Blotting for Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) Verification

To further validate the stabilizing effect of TPTC binding to candidate target proteins, this study employed the CETSA coupled with Western blot. The binding of a small molecule to its target protein typically alters the protein’s thermal stability. By comparing the amount of soluble protein remaining at different temperatures, this method provides evidence of target engagement/binding under near-physiological conditions, thus complementing the SPR results and helping to minimize biases inherent to any single in vitro system. Based on previous literature, proteins were extracted from cell lysates that did not contain PMSF. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. A total of 100 μL of protein solution at a concentration of 60 μg/μL was incubated with 0.5% DMSO and 1 μmol/L of flutamide for 10 min. Subsequently, the protein samples were equally divided into six tubes and subjected to heat treatment for 3 min at varying temperatures (37, 42, 47, 52, 57, and 62 °C) in a metal bath. Following this, the samples were transferred to ice for further protein sample preparation. The levels of specific proteins were detected using immunoblotting with antibodies against HADH and HSD17B10, along with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody.

Transfection of HADH or HSD17B10 Expression Plasmid

To elucidate the causal role and specific contribution of HADH and HSD17B10 in TPTC-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, this study constructed and transfected plasmids overexpressing HADH or HSD17B10 for functional intervention. By increasing the expression level of the target protein and observing subsequent changes in relevant phenotypic indicators, this approach allows for the verification of whether these proteins serve as key mediators in the process. To investigate the roles of HADH and HSD17B10 in TPTC-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, the open reading frame sequences of human HADH (GenBank Accession No: NC_000004.12) and human HSD17B10 (GenBank Accession No: NC_000023.11) were cloned into the pCMV-3 × HA-Neo vector to construct HADH and HSD17B10 overexpression plasmids. Cells transfected with these plasmids were designated as HADH overexpression (HADH-OE) cells and HSD17B10 overexpression (HSD17B10-OE) cells. The constructed plasmids were transfected into the cells using UltraFection 3.0 reagent. Transfection efficiency was verified using Western blotting (Figure S2A,B).

Real-Time Fluorescence Quantitative PCR (qPCR) Detection

HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL in a 96-well plate. TPTC was diluted in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, and 1% pyruvate to prepare working solutions. The cells were treated with 0.1, 0.5, and 1 μmol/L TPTC for 24 h, in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzol reagent, and RNA quality and concentration were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mRNA levels of HADH, HSD17B10, and CPT1A were analyzed using QPCR. The primers used were: human HADH (Forward: 5′-ACGAAGTTCATCGTGGATGG-3′, Reverse: 5′-AACCTGGTGAGGCAGGATTT-3′), human HSD17B10 (Forward: 5′-AAGGGACATGGTCAGGGTCA-3′, Reverse: 5′-GCACACCTAGTTTACCCGCA-3′), human PPARG (Forward: 5′-TCTCAAACGAGAGTCAGCCTT-3′, Reverse: 5′-CTGCATAGTCCAACTGACTGGA-3′, and human β-actin (Forward: 5′-CCATGGTGAGCTGCGAGAAT-3′, Reverse: 5′-CGGCTTCCTTTGTCCCCAAT-3′). qPCR reactions were carried out using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on an QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal control. Statistical analysis was performed to compare the gene expression levels between the TPTC treatment groups and the control group.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± SD of at least three independent biological replicates. Unless otherwise stated, differences between multiple groups were assessed by ANOVA. If a significant overall difference was found by ANOVA, posthoc Dunnett’s test or Tukey’s test was applied to determine specific group differences. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software.

Results

TPTC Induced Metabolic Reprogramming in HepG2 Cells

TPTC exhibited significant hepatocytotoxicity, as evidenced by a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability following TPTC treatment (IC50 = 2.1 μmol/L) (Figure A). To evaluate whether TPTC cytotoxicity varies across cell types, we further assessed cell viability in HK-2 and HUVEC cells, yielding IC50 values of 7.7 and 3.22 μmol/L, respectively, both higher than that in HepG2 cells (2.1 μmol/L) (Figure S1). These results indicate that HepG2 cells are more sensitive to TPTC exposure, providing a rationale for our subsequent metabolomics investigation into the mechanisms underlying TPTC-induced hepatocytotoxicity. To investigate the underlying mechanisms of TPTC-induced hepatocytotoxicity, we conducted a comprehensive metabolomics analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct separations in metabolite profiles between the control group and groups treated with different doses (Figure B). A total of 44 differentially expressed metabolites were found to be common in both the 0.5 and 1 μmol/L TPTC treatment groups (FDR < 1%, |fold change| > 1.2). Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed metabolites indicated that TPTC exposure significantly disrupted energy metabolism pathways in hepatocytes, including nucleotide metabolism, lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism pathways (Figure C). Further heatmap analysis of the relevant metabolites within the energy metabolism pathways revealed that, although the nucleotide metabolism pathway exhibited the highest enrichment score, the changes in its differentially expressed metabolites lacked a clear dose-dependent trend. Notably, the significant elevation of UMP and dCMP, which are metabolites that promote cellular function, alongside the significant reduction in the level of Dihydrouracil, a metabolite indicative of cytotoxicity, presents a counterintuitive pattern given the substantial decrease in cell viability observed following TPTC exposure. In contrast, all metabolites associated with the lipid metabolism pathway exhibited changes in a clear dose-dependent manner. Specifically, there was a significant accumulation of medium- and long-chain fatty acids, alongside a marked depletion of very long-chain fatty acids (Figure D). It is well established that alterations in fatty acid levels are primarily influenced by uptake, synthesis, and oxidation. Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that TPTC significantly affected cellular fatty acid degradation (Figure E). Notably, the accumulation of medium- and long-chain fatty acids suggests that mitochondrial fatty acid import remained unaffected. Therefore, these results suggest that TPTC exposure is associated with a functional impairment of fatty acid β-oxidation, as evidenced by convergent metabolic alterations.

TPTC Inhibited Cellular Mitochondrial Function and Induced Oxidative Damage

Metabolic disorders of β-oxidation, the core pathway for fatty acid catabolism, not only lead to the accumulation of medium- and long-chain fatty acids but also trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. − To validate the impact of TPTC on fatty acid β-oxidation, this study further assessed intracellular levels of β-oxidation metabolites, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress markers. As illustrated in Figure A, fatty acid β-oxidation supplies acetyl-CoA and NADH to mitochondria through four enzymatic steps, supporting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS). Following exposure to 0.5 and 1 μmol/L TPTC, although intracellular pyruvate levels were significantly increased (Figure D), the levels of acetyl-CoA (Figure B) and NADH (Figure C) were markedly reduced. This further suggests that TPTC may affect cellular energy metabolism by impairing fatty acid β-oxidation. To test this hypothesis, mitochondrial function in HepG2 cells exposed to 0.5 and 1 μmol/L TPTC was further evaluated using a Seahorse XF24 Analyzer (Figure E). As shown in Figure F, TPTC exposure significantly reduced basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production in HepG2 mitochondria. Furthermore, the ability of NADH to regenerate NADPH is considered to play a role in the antioxidant regulation within the body. Both 0.5 and 1 μmol/L TPTC exposure significantly decreased NADPH levels while increasing NADP+ levels (Figure G). This subsequently led to a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels (Figure H) and the level of the oxidative stress marker malondialdehyde (MDA) (Figure I). Thus, TPTC exposure likely reduces acetyl-CoA and NADH levels in HepG2 cells by impairing fatty acid β-oxidation, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial function and inducing cellular oxidative stress.

Binding Affinity Evaluation between TPTC and the Potential Target Proteins

The variations in metabolites are closely associated with the activity of metabolic enzymes. To further investigate the potential mechanisms by which TPTC affects energy metabolism, this study utilized the KEGG database to screen for metabolic enzymes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, particularly those participating in the catabolism of medium- and long-chain fatty acids. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was subsequently constructed based on the identified enzymes. Cluster analysis revealed that these enzymes could be grouped into two major clusters, each represented by a distinct color (Figure A). To further explore the interactions between TPTC and these metabolic enzymes, molecular docking simulations were performed to evaluate the binding affinity of TPTC with the identified enzymes. The binding free energies of TPTC with 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 10 (HSD17B10) (PDB ID: 7ONU) and hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADH) (PDB ID: 3RQS) were −10.2 and −9.8 kcal/mol, respectively, which were significantly lower than the previously reported binding energy of TPTC with its known strong interaction partner tubulin (Figure B). Molecular docking images further indicated that TPTC was surrounded by amino acid residues of the target proteins (Figure C,D). These results suggest that TPTC may influence energy metabolism by interacting with HADH and HSD17B10.

PPI screening of target proteins affected by TPTC. (A) PPI network of enzymes regulating differential metabolites influenced by TPTC. (B) Heatmap showing molecular docking energies between TPTC and target proteins. Docking sites of TPTC with HSD17B10 (C) and HADH (D) proteins, respectively. HSD17B10 with TPTC were at positions 34.83, 56.83, 54.08, 148.979, 140.421, and 108.04. HADH with TPTC were at positions 17.25, 25.5, 36.75, 5.336, 94.214, and 100.748.

To further validate the binding efficacies of TPTC to the target proteins HSD17B10 or HADH, this study employed SPR technology, which is based on the principle of surface plasmon resonance and measures changes in reflected light intensity to elucidate intermolecular interactions. The results presented in Figure A,B indicated robust interactions between TPTC with HSD17B10 or HADH, with calculated dissociation equilibrium constants (K d) of 49.2 and 12.2 μmol/L, respectively. To enhance the reliability of the binding results, this study further examined the interaction between TPTC with HADH or HSD17B10 using CETSA. CETSA is based on the principle of small molecules binding to target proteins alter their thermal stability; thus, quantitative assessment of such binding effects can be achieved by detecting protein level changes after temperature-gradient treatment through Western blot analysis. As shown in Figure C,D, TPTC significantly improved the thermal stability of HADH and HSD17B10.

TPTC Impaired Mitochondrial Function and Induced Cellular Oxidative Stress through the Inhibition of HADH and HSD17B10

To further verify whether the toxic effects of TPTC on mitochondrial function and oxidative stress were mediated through HADH and HSD17B10, HepG2 cells were transiently transfected with plasmids overexpressing HADH or HSD17B10 prior to TPTC exposure. Transfection with HADH-OE#2 and HSD17B10-OE#2 plasmids partially alleviated TPTC-induced cytotoxicity. As shown in Figure A,B, overexpression of either HADH or HSD17B10 significantly restored mitochondrial respiratory function, including basal and maximal respiration, indicating that both enzymes contribute to maintaining mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity under TPTC exposure. Notably, despite comparable improvements in mitochondrial respiration, the effects of HADH and HSD17B10 overexpression on oxidative stress differed markedly. While overexpression of HSD17B10 significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels and MDA accumulation, overexpression of HADH exerted only marginal effects on these oxidative stress markers (Figure C–E). This functional divergence is consistent with the distinct metabolic roles of the two enzymes. HADH primarily catalyzes the dehydrogenation of medium- and long-chain fatty acyl-CoAs, contributing mainly to acetyl-CoA and NADH production for mitochondrial ATP synthesis. In contrast, HSD17B10 participates not only in fatty acid β-oxidation but also in branched-chain amino acid metabolism, processes that are more closely coupled to mitochondrial redox homeostasis. , NADH generated through HSD17B10-associated pathways can indirectly support NADPH regeneration via mitochondrial redox shuttles, thereby enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity. Collectively, these results indicate that TPTC disrupts mitochondrial function and redox balance by targeting HADH- and HSD17B10-associated metabolic pathways. Notably, the observation that overexpression of HADH or HSD17B10 only partially rescued the TPTC-induced phenotypes may also suggest that additional mechanisms beyond these two enzymessuch as transcriptional reprogramming through nuclear receptor pathways and/or other mitochondrial targetscould contribute to the overall mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Therefore, this study further examined the levels of HADH, HSD17B10, and PPARγ in cells following TPTC exposure. The results showed that exposure to 0.1, 0.5, and 1 μmol/L TPTC did not lead to significant changes in the mRNA levels of HADH and HSD17B10, supporting the idea that TPTC primarily affects their functions through direct interactions. However, at the same exposure doses, PPARγ mRNA levels were significantly increased (Figure S3), consistent with previous studies on nuclear receptor pathways. , These findings suggest that TPTC toxicity operates through a multitarget/multilevel parallel mechanism.

Discussion

This study employed a metabolomics-driven strategy to investigate the mechanisms underlying TPTC-induced metabolic dysregulation in hepatocytes and to identify previously unrecognized molecular targets. Global metabolomic profiling revealed pronounced disturbances in lipid metabolism following TPTC exposure, particularly involving metabolites associated with fatty acid β-oxidation. Integrative pathway enrichment and protein–protein interaction analyses further highlighted HADH and HSD17B10 as key metabolic nodes potentially underlying these alterations. Subsequent molecular interaction assays, including SPR and CETSA, confirmed direct interactions between TPTC and both enzymes. Functional analyses demonstrated that interference with HADH- and HSD17B10-associated metabolism impaired acetyl-CoA and NADH production, leading to mitochondrial bioenergetic dysfunction and redox imbalance.

The present study suggests that, beyond RXR/PPAR-mediated transcriptional regulation, TPTC may also provoke rapid metabolic disequilibrium through direct interference with mitochondrial metabolic enzymes; these mechanisms may coexist or dominate sequentially across different exposure stages. Previous studies on TPTC toxicity have primarily focused on endocrine and reproductive effects, , with particular emphasis on the RXR/PPAR nuclear receptor axis in transcriptional control of lipid homeostasis. TPT/TBT acts as an RXR ligand and to form heterodimers with PPAR, thereby altering lipid metabolism-related gene expression and promoting lipid droplet accumulation and lipid homeostatic disruption. − While this “upstream signaling–transcriptional regulation–metabolic phenotype” paradigm is mechanistically informative, it does not fully account for the rapid β-oxidation-associated metabolic perturbations observed in our short exposure window, including accumulation of medium- and long-chain fatty acids, depletion of acetyl-CoA and NADH, and the concurrent impairment of mitochondrial respiration and redox homeostasis. Our data instead support a more proximal toxicological cascade in which TPTC directly targets mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, leading to “target inhibition–flux restriction–energy/reducing equivalent deficiency–mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress.” HADH and HSD17B10 represent critical dehydrogenase nodes within the β-oxidation network, , and their inhibition would be expected to directly compromise acetyl-CoA and NADH production, constrain oxidative phosphorylation, and impair antioxidant defense capacity via disruption of NADH/NADPH coupling and mitochondrial redox shuttling, thereby exacerbating ROS generation and lipid peroxidation. This framework provides a more direct explanation for the coupled metabolic, bioenergetic, and redox phenotypes observed here, and indicates that TPTC hepatocytotoxicity involves not only transcriptional dysregulation but also direct disruption of mitochondrial metabolic processes. Notably, this mode of toxicitydirect pollutant–enzyme interaction causing β-oxidation impairmentis consistent with evidence reported for other hydrophobic organic pollutants in environmental toxicology. Multiple hydrophobic contaminants have been shown to directly interact with proteins involved in fatty acid oxidation or mitochondrial energy metabolism, thereby reducing β-oxidation flux and inducing fatty acid accumulation and mitochondrial oxidative stress via nongenomic mechanisms. , In parallel, prior animal studies have shown that TPT/TPTC exposure induces liver injury, lipid metabolic disorders, and steatosis, , which is directionally consistent with the β-oxidation node impairment identified at the cellular level in the present study. Certainly. The partial rescue effect observed upon overexpression of HADH and HSD17B10 also suggests the existence of additional parallel mechanisms converging on the same phenotypic end point. Triphenyltin compounds, including TPTC, bind to and activate nuclear receptors, which in turn regulate master transcriptional regulators of lipid metabolism, potentially directly modulating the expression of genes encoding fatty acid oxidation enzymes, including HADH and HSD17B10. Therefore, TPTC may exert a dual effect: acute, post-translational inhibition through direct binding to HADH and HSD17B10 proteins (as indicated by our SPR and CETSA data), and a more sustained, transcriptional downregulation potentially mediated by RXR/PPAR signaling, thereby reducing the cellular abundance of these enzymes over time. This dual mechanism model, encompassing both direct metabolic enzyme inhibition and indirect transcriptional regulation, offers a more comprehensive perspective for understanding TPTC-induced hepatocytotoxicity. Future research could employ chronic exposure models and transcriptomic analyses to dissect the temporal dynamics and relative contributions of these two pathways.

Although the binding affinities between TPTC and HADH or HSD17B10 fall within the micromolar range, similar affinity levels have been reported for environmentally relevant toxicant–protein interactions, , particularly when sustained exposure and intracellular accumulation are considered. Moreover, the exposure concentrations applied in this in vitro study exceed those typically reported in environmental matrices but should be interpreted in the context of the strong lipophilicity and bioaccumulation potential of organotin compounds. The liver serves as a central hub for fatty acid oxidation and detoxification metabolism. Impairment of β-oxidation is more likely to result in lipid accumulation and an imbalance in energy/reducing equivalents within hepatocytes. Therefore, even though mitochondrial toxicity can be a general phenomenon, the liver may exhibit a more pronounced metabolic phenotype. Furthermore, many lipophilic pollutants accumulate in liver tissue, and hepatocytes possess abundant mitochondria and metabolic enzyme systems, rendering mitochondrial metabolic toxicity more consequential in terms of functional outcomes within these cells. This phenomenon is commonly observed in studies of other pollutants; for instance, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) accumulate in the liver, reaching concentrations far exceeding environmental exposure levels. Similarly, in large yellow croaker cultured in offshore waters, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) exhibit significant bioaccumulation, with tissue concentrations approximately 3.6 times higher than those in the open ocean environment. The μmol/L concentrations employed in this study are intended to elucidate molecular targets and metabolic mechanisms, thereby providing a foundation for subsequent research on chronic low-dose exposure and in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although multiple lines of evidence support the involvement of disrupted fatty acid β-oxidation, direct measurements of β-oxidation flux, such as isotope-labeled fatty acid tracing or acyl-carnitine profiling, were not performed. Consequently, the observed impairment should be interpreted as a functional consequence inferred from integrated metabolomic, bioenergetic, and enzymatic data rather than as a direct kinetic measurement. Future studies incorporating stable isotope tracing, chronic low-dose exposure models, and in vitro–in vivo extrapolation approaches will further refine causal inference and environmental risk assessment.

In summary, this study identifies HADH and HSD17B10 as metabolic targets of TPTC and demonstrates that direct interference with mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism represents an important component of TPTC-induced hepatocytotoxicity. By revealing a metabolic mechanism that complements classical nuclear receptor–mediated pathways, these findings provide new insights into the health risks posed by persistent environmental organotin pollutants and underscore the importance of considering mitochondrial metabolism in environmental toxicology.

Conclusions

This study provides novel insights into the hepatocytotoxicity of TPTC, a persistent environmental pollutant, by employing a metabolomics-driven approach to investigate its underlying molecular mechanisms. The metabolomics analysis revealed significant disturbances in lipid metabolism, particularly affecting fatty acid β-oxidation. This disruption was further validated through molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance, and cellular thermal shift assays, which confirmed direct binding of TPTC to both HSD17B10 and HADH. Functional validation through overexpression of these enzymes partially restored mitochondrial bioenergetic function, underscoring their crucial role in maintaining cellular metabolic integrity under TPTC exposure. In conclusion, this study highlights a previously unrecognized mechanism of TPTC toxicity involving direct interference with mitochondrial metabolism, which complements the known nuclear receptor-mediated pathways. The findings not only enhance our understanding of TPTC-induced hepatocytotoxicity but also emphasize the need to consider mitochondrial dysfunction as a critical aspect of the toxicological profiles of environmental pollutants.
