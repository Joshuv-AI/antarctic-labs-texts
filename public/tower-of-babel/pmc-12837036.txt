Epigenetics of glaucoma in the trabecular meshwork
Authors: Zhihao Liu, Yajuan Zheng, Jing Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glaucoma represents a predominant cause of irreversible blindness globally, characterized by the association of elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and retinal ganglion cell loss with dysfunction of the trabecular meshwork (TM), the principal tissue regulating conventional aqueous humor outflow. Emerging evidence suggests that this dysfunction is not exclusively driven by genetic variation or mechanical stress; rather, it is significantly influenced by epigenetic mechanisms that integrate factors such as aging, hypoxia/oxidative stress, glucocorticoid exposure, and other environmental challenges into enduring alterations in TM phenotype. This review synthesizes current understanding of the primary epigenetic mechanisms involved in glaucomatous TM remodeling, encompassing DNA methylation, histone modifications, non-coding RNAs (including microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs), and RNA N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A) methylation. In this study, we elucidate the role of aberrant DNA methylation in the regulation of profibrotic genes, such as TGF-β1 and GDF7, elasticity-modifying genes like LOXL1, and repetitive elements, which collectively contribute to extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation, tissue stiffening, and increased outflow resistance. Furthermore, we explore how dysregulated miRNA–lncRNA networks and histone acetylation/methylation influence central signaling pathways, including TGF-β/BMP–Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, RhoA/ROCK, PI3K–Akt, and NF-κB. These pathways are crucial in orchestrating trabecular meshwork (TM) fibrosis, cytoskeletal remodeling, cellular senescence, and impaired stress responses. Additionally, we investigate the emerging roles of m⁶A regulators, such as METTL3, YTHDF2, and YTHDC2, at the intersection of outflow pathway fibrosis and retinal ganglion cell vulnerability. We propose that epigenetic modifiers, ncRNA-based therapies, and partial epigenetic reprogramming could offer innovative, TM-targeted, and neuroprotective strategies beyond conventional IOP-lowering treatments. Collectively, our findings support an integrated model wherein diverse epigenetic modifications converge to produce a stereotypical glaucomatous TM phenotype, thereby presenting novel opportunities for mechanism-based diagnosis and therapeutic intervention in glaucoma.

Introduction

Glaucoma encompasses a spectrum of neurodegenerative disorders that are characterized by irreversible vision impairment, marked by the progressive degeneration of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) and subsequent damage to the optic nerve [1]. The global prevalence of glaucoma among individuals aged 40–80 years is estimated to be approximately 3.5%, with projections indicating that the number of affected individuals will surpass 110 million by the year 2040 [2]. Glaucoma is categorized into primary, congenital, and secondary types. Primary glaucoma is further subdivided into primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), primarily differentiated by the status of the anterior chamber angle, whether it is closed or open [1]. Glaucoma is linked to a multitude of risk factors, encompassing both ophthalmic and systemic elements. Ophthalmic risk factors include elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), advancing age, a familial predisposition to glaucoma, and racial background. Systemic or metabolic risk factors comprise the use of steroids, diabetes, hypertension or hypotension, high myopia, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, hypothyroidism, and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome [3–5]. Among these, IOP is recognized as the most significant modifiable risk factor in the majority of glaucoma cases [6]. Elevated IOP exerts mechanical stress that can compromise axonal function at the optic nerve head, is associated with local ECM remodeling and RGCs death, and is recognized as one of the major contributors to vision loss in glaucoma [7]. In addition to mechanical stress, factors such as ischemia, metabolic stress, and neuroinflammation are believed to collectively contribute to glial activation at the optic nerve head and the axonal degeneration of RGCs. Consequently, glaucoma is widely recognized as a chronic neurodegenerative disease [8–10]. Currently, most therapeutic strategies focus on reducing IOP through pharmacological treatments, laser procedures, or surgical interventions [1]. However, in a subtype of POAG known as normal-tension glaucoma (NTG), untreated IOP values remain within the statistically normal range. This observation suggests that IOP-independent mechanisms, such as impaired vascular perfusion, autonomic dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, and immune/inflammatory responses, may also contribute to the pathogenesis of glaucoma [11].

In addition to IOP, both genetic and environmental factors contribute to the pathophysiology of glaucoma [12]. As a multifactorial hereditary ocular condition, the genetic framework of glaucoma remains incompletely understood, and dysregulated epigenetic mechanisms resulting from environmental exposures may offer significant insights into its complex phenotypic manifestations [13]. Epigenetics encompasses a range of heritable biological processes that modulate gene expression through chemical modifications without altering the DNA sequence itself. These processes include DNA methylation, histone modifications (such as acetylation and methylation), non-coding RNA-mediated regulation (e.g., microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs), and RNA epitranscriptomic modifications, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation [14]. Epigenetic modifications serve as a "memory mechanism" for organisms, enabling them to respond to environmental stimuli without alterations to the DNA sequence. These modifications translate signals such as aging, oxidative stress, hypoxia, and inflammation into enduring changes in chromatin structure and gene expression. Consequently, they are often considered crucial mediators that connect environmental exposures to stable phenotypic remodeling, particularly in the context of chronic diseases. Epigenetic abnormalities have been implicated in a broad spectrum of diseases, including cancer, hematologic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and respiratory diseases [15]. Furthermore, epigenetic regulation has been demonstrated to influence the development and progression of ocular diseases, such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and diabetic retinopathy (DR) [16]. Collectively, these findings suggest that epigenetic mechanisms may also play a significant role in the functional remodeling of tissues associated with glaucoma.

DNA methylation represents a pervasive epigenetic modification that is integral to cell fate determination and lineage maintenance [17]. It has been implicated in critical pathophysiological processes, including embryonic development, stem cell differentiation, tumorigenesis, and aging [18]. Furthermore, a substantial body of research suggests that aberrant DNA methylation is intricately linked to fibrosis and tissue remodeling across various organs [19, 20]. In mammalian systems, several derivatives of DNA methylation have been identified, most notably 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), 5-formylcytosine (5fC), and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC) [21]. In humans, DNA methylation predominantly involves 5mC, which is catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs). These enzymes utilize S-adenosylmethionine as a methyl group donor to selectively attach a methyl group to the 5′ position of the cytosine ring via a covalent bond, thereby forming 5mC [22]. DNA methylation predominantly takes place at Cytosine–phosphate–guanine (CpG) dinucleotide sites, which are pairs of nucleotides where cytosine and guanine are contiguous within the DNA sequence. In this context, "C" and "G" denote cytosine and guanine, respectively, while "p" signifies the phosphodiester bond that connects adjacent cytosine and guanine nucleotides. Within the genome, CpG sites are frequently clustered in regions referred to as CpG islands [23]. This region is located adjacent to the gene promoter and may extend into the coding regions and introns [24]. Typically, these regions exhibit hypomethylation to sustain active gene expression. Consequently, methylation of the promoter region can result in the silencing of gene transcription, whereas demethylation can activate gene expression [25].

DNA methylation is primarily facilitated by two categories of DNA methyltransferases: maintenance methyltransferases and de novo methyltransferases [26]. Maintenance methyltransferases, such as DNMT1, are responsible for recognizing and replicating existing DNA methylation patterns. They achieve this by methylating the corresponding positions on the newly synthesized DNA strand, based on the methylation pattern present on the parental strand, thereby ensuring the cellular inheritance of epigenetic modifications [27, 28]. In contrast, de novo methyltransferases, which include DNMT3A, DNMT3B, and DNMT3L, initiate methylation patterns. This process is crucial for embryonic development and cellular differentiation, as it involves the addition of methyl groups to CpG sites [29]. DNA demethylation occurs through two principal mechanisms. Passive demethylation transpires during DNA replication, leading to a gradual reduction in methylation levels with successive cell divisions [30]. Active demethylation, on the other hand, entails the oxidation of 5-methylcytosine to 5-carboxylcytosine by ten-eleven translocation (TET) proteins, followed by the excision of the oxidized base by thymine DNA glycosylase (TDG), thus completing the demethylation process [31].

The TM serves as the primary conduit for the drainage of aqueous humor from the eye, and its dysfunction is a significant contributor to elevated IOP [32]. In the context of glaucoma, the TM is characterized by decreased cellularity, impaired ECM turnover, and increased biomechanical stiffness. These alterations are associated with the stiffening of the outflow region and reduced porosity, which may consequently enhance aqueous outflow resistance and elevate IOP [33]. Structurally, the TM is a complex, porous, three-dimensional network comprising TMCs embedded within the ECM. It can be broadly categorized into an anterior (non-filtering) segment, which does not interface with Schlemm’s canal, and a posterior (filtering) segment, which does [34, 35]. The posterior TM is composed of several anatomically distinct regions, including the uveal, corneoscleral, and juxtacanalicular tissues. Among these, the juxtacanalicular tissue is characterized by a loosely organized structure, with porosity decreasing from the inner to the outer layers, thereby serving as a critical site for regulating aqueous outflow resistance [32, 36]. TMCs constitute a highly plastic cell population, exhibiting characteristics of endothelial cells, myofibroblasts, and macrophage-like cells. TMCs located in the uveal and corneoscleral regions predominantly display an endothelial-like phenotype, characterized by relatively large, round to oval cell bodies. These cells play a crucial role in maintaining the permeability of the filtration barrier and neutralizing reactive oxygen species, while also possessing phagocytic and immune-mediated functions [32]. In contrast, TMCs within the juxtacanalicular tissue exhibit a fibroblast-like phenotype, characterized by a spindle-shaped morphology and the ability to secrete a variety of ECM components, including collagens, fibronectin, elastin, fibrillins, and proteoglycans, as well as their degrading enzymes, namely matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, -2, -3, -9, -12, -14, etc.). This secretion supports ongoing ECM remodeling [37]. Furthermore, TMCs demonstrate contractile properties through the expression of smooth muscle actin and myosin, contributing to mechanotransduction and the regulation of aqueous humor outflow [34]. This phenotypic plasticity implies that TMCs may undergo relatively stable reprogramming of gene expression in response to chronic mechanical stress and inflammatory or metabolic stimuli, with epigenetic regulation being a likely mechanism facilitating such phenotypic transitions.

While numerous reviews have explored glaucoma in the context of epigenetics or general disease pathogenesis, there is a paucity of comprehensive analyses specifically addressing the "epigenetic remodeling of the TM" and systematically integrating evidence across various regulatory layers, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, non-coding RNAs, and RNA methylation. Consequently, this review aims to concentrate on the TM, a critical tissue in the conventional aqueous outflow pathway, by summarizing current evidence on the role of epigenetic modifications in TM cellular phenotypic alterations and ECM remodeling, and discussing their potential implications for the diagnosis and targeted therapy of glaucoma.

DNA methylation in the TM

DNA methylation in glaucoma

The pathological accumulation of ECM in the TM and optic nerve head is proposed as a critical mechanism contributing to fibrotic processes, thereby linking it to the damage of RGCs and optic nerve fibers [38]. Methylation can modulate fibroblast activation and fibrogenesis [39], indicating that DNA methylation may play a role in the pathogenesis of glaucoma. To clarify this mechanism, numerous studies have been conducted from various perspectives, uncovering specific associations between DNA methylation and glaucoma. A Mendelian randomization analysis utilizing a large-scale Genome-Wide Association Study for POAG identified 27 novel DNA methylation sites across 15 genes. This study advanced the integration of glaucoma-associated methylation levels with gene expression data, identifying two pivotal sites within the BICC1 gene, thereby underscoring the functional significance of DNA methylation in the pathogenesis of glaucoma [40]. The case–control study conducted by Burdon et al. [41] revealed a significant correlation between the methylation level of a specific CpG site in the CDKN2B promoter region and normal tension glaucoma, with this association being particularly pronounced in female patients. Recent proposals suggest that altered DNA methylation may contribute to increased aqueous outflow resistance and disrupt IOP regulation by modulating the expression of relevant genes. In a study conducted by Cai et al., 298 significantly differentially methylated sites and notable differentially methylated regions were identified in key genes such as TBX3 and TNXB1 within the Schlemm's canal endothelial cells of glaucoma patients [42].These findings provide further evidence supporting the involvement of DNA methylation in the pathogenesis of glaucoma. Collectively, these discoveries reinforce the notion that DNA methylation is not merely a hypothetical but a progressively well-substantiated pathological mechanism in glaucoma.

DNA methylation of key TM genes

TGF-β1

Transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), a multifunctional cytokine essential for tissue repair, inflammation, and fibrosis [43], facilitates fibrosis through the SMAD3 signaling pathway by modulating the synthesis of type I collagen and its deposition into the ECM [44]. Importantly, this profibrotic pathway is also active in the TM of the eye [45]. When aberrantly activated, it has been documented to obstruct aqueous humor outflow, thus contributing to the pathogenesis of glaucoma [46]. Moreover, the interplay between TGF-β1 and connective tissue growth factor can influence cellular architecture in the juxtacanalicular region of the TM by inducing a transition of TMCs toward a contractile myofibroblast phenotype, thereby promoting tissue stiffening [47]. These pathological transformations have been linked to the degeneration and fibrosis of elastic microfibrils within the TM. Such alterations are believed to enhance TM rigidity and increase resistance to aqueous humor outflow, potentially contributing to elevated IOP in POAG [48]. TGF-β1 has been demonstrated to contribute to the fibrosis observed in POAG by upregulating protease inhibitors. These inhibitors impede the normal breakdown of the ECM, leading to abnormal tissue deposition [49]. Concurrently, TGF-β1 also enhances the expression of transglutaminase 2, a critical cross-linking enzyme that stabilizes the ECM and may directly increase its resistance to degradation [50]. As a result, therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways are currently under investigation. For instance, trans-resveratrol has been shown to potentially reduce ECM deposition in the TM of POAG by suppressing TGF-β1/SMAD4 signaling and promoting SMAD7 signaling [51]. In addition to pharmacological interventions, the development of a supportive ECM can counteract the effects of TGF-β1. Research has demonstrated that three-dimensional Matrigel sustains the phenotype of human TCMs and inhibits fibrosis by activating focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and suppressing TGF-β1-induced Smad signaling pathways [52]. The involvement of TGF-β1 signaling is observed not only in POAG but also in pseudoexfoliation glaucoma (PEXG), where its activation within the TM has been associated with the pathogenesis of the disease [53].

Beyond its role in pathway activity, TGF-β1 is intricately regulated by epigenetic mechanisms, offering novel insights into its pathogenic roles in glaucoma. Notably, a specific hypomethylation of the TGF-β1 promoter has been observed in glaucomatous lamina cribrosa (LC) cells, which occurs within a context of overall increased DNA methylation and is correlated with elevated expression levels [54]. This methylation pattern, characterized by both global hypermethylation and localized hypomethylation, is similarly present in TMCs. McDonnell et al. demonstrated that in TMCs affected by glaucoma and hypoxia, there was an increase in global DNA methylation, accompanied by upregulated expression of TGF-β1 and downregulated expression of the anti-fibrotic factor RAS protein activator-like 1 (RASAL1), in comparison to normal controls [55]. Their study further revealed that exposing normal TMCs to TGF-β1 resulted in elevated levels of DNMT1 and collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1), while concurrently reducing RASAL1 expression [55]. It has been reported that promoter hypermethylation of RASAL1, an anti-fibrotic factor, leads to its epigenetic silencing, which is associated with constitutive activation of the Ras pathway. This activation is believed to promote fibroblast proliferation, alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression, and collagen production, thereby contributing to fibrosis [39]. RASAL1 has the capacity to inhibit Ras protein activity [56], while overexpression of Ras can enhance the transcriptional activity of DNMT1 [57]. Concurrently, TGF-β1 has been demonstrated to downregulate RASAL1 expression through a bifurcated mechanism: short-term exposure to TGF-β1 transiently decreases RASAL1 transcription, whereas prolonged exposure results in irreversible gene silencing of RASAL1 via DNMT1-mediated promoter methylation [39] (Fig. 1). This indicates that DNA methylation, the pro-fibrotic cytokine TGF-β1, and the anti-fibrotic factor RASAL1 interact in a manner that may collectively facilitate fibrotic progression in TMCs associated with glaucoma. Research has demonstrated the presence of a hypoxic microenvironment within the TM of glaucomatous tissue [58]. Moreover, hypoxia is hypothesized to play a critical role in the fibrogenesis associated with glaucoma, potentially through the epigenetic modulation of TGF-β1. Notably, the promoter region of TGF-β1 contains hypoxia response elements (HREs) that are subject to regulation by hypoxic conditions [59]. Hypoxia has been shown to promote DNA methylation of these HREs, enhance their interaction with HIF-1α, and ultimately facilitate the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition of cells within the TM [60]. Consequently, the epigenetic regulation of TGF-β1 by hypoxia is posited to represent a fundamental pathway in fibrogenesis, offering novel mechanistic and therapeutic insights into the pathophysiology of glaucoma.

GDF7

Growth Differentiation Factor 7 (GDF7), a constituent of the TGF-β superfamily, is implicated in the pathogenesis of cellular fibrosis. It engages with various TGF-β receptors, facilitating the recruitment and activation of SMAD transcription factors. This signaling cascade ultimately influences the expression of genes associated with the ECM and cellular adhesion [61]. Recent studies have provided substantial evidence supporting the specific involvement of GDF7 in the fibrotic processes of the glaucomatous TM. Wan et al. conducted studies on patients with POAG and discovered that hypomethylation of the GDF7 promoter region in TM tissue is associated with increased expression of GDF7 protein. This hypomethylation state is contingent upon the activation of ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes [62]. Concurrently, elevated levels of GDF7 protein have been reported to activate the bone morphogenetic protein receptor 2 (BMPR2)/Smad signaling pathway and are accompanied by increased expression of pro-fibrotic markers such as α-SMA and fibronectin (FN), which are associated with TM fibrosis [62]. Moreover, in glaucomatous TM, GDF7 expression is hypothesized to establish a self-reinforcing feedback loop that may significantly impede aqueous humor outflow in individuals with POAG. Subsequent in vivo studies using the rhesus monkey model have directly investigated these molecular mechanisms. In the eyes of rhesus monkeys, intravitreal injection of recombinant GDF7 (rhGDF7) has been reported to induce TM fibrosis, obstruct aqueous outflow, elevate IOP, and cause optic nerve damage. Conversely, the administration of a GDF7 neutralizing antibody (nGDF7) significantly reduced IOP, improved aqueous outflow function, and inhibited TM fibrosis and Smad signaling activation [62]. This study suggests that GDF7 hypomethylation is a critical epigenetic mechanism contributing to TM fibrosis and aqueous outflow obstruction in POAG. LOXL1

Lysyl oxidase-like 1 (LOXL1) is a principal causative gene associated with PEXG, encoding a critical enzyme involved in the cross-linking of elastic fibers during their formation and remodeling [63]. In cultured human TMCs, LOXL1 expression is significantly upregulated in response to TGF-β stimulation, mediated through both canonical Smad and non-Smad signaling pathways [63]. A reciprocal regulatory relationship exists between TGF-β signaling and LOXL1 activity, whereby increased LOX activity appears to partially mediate the TGF-β-induced elevation in aqueous outflow resistance and IOP [64]. Furthermore, in addition to TGF-β1, various external stressors—including oxidative stress, ultraviolet radiation, hypoxia, and mechanical strain—have been demonstrated to enhance LOXL1 expression in both Tenon’s capsule fibroblasts and TMCs in vitro [65, 66]. Nutritional factors also play a role in modulating LOXL1 expression; specifically, folate and vitamin D3 have been shown to increase its levels in these cell types, while caffeine and retinoic acid exert a suppressive effect [67].

The regulation of LOXL1 expression through epigenetic mechanisms involves several layers, including DNA methylation and non-coding RNA-mediated control by long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) [68]. In PEXG, hypermethylation of LOXL1 has been linked to the suppression of its expression, which may disrupt ECM homeostasis, reduce elasticity, and facilitate the formation of exfoliative material. The accumulation of this material in the TM is believed to impede aqueous humor outflow, potentially leading to increased IOP and subsequent retinal ganglion cell death [69–72].

HSPA1A

HSPA1A is an intronless gene that primarily encodes heat shock protein 70 (HSP70). As a molecular chaperone, HSP70 plays a crucial role in facilitating the proper folding of newly synthesized proteins and maintaining protein homeostasis within cells [73]. Unlike pseudoexfoliation syndrome (PEXS), where HSP70 expression is reportedly silenced due to DNA hypermethylation in its exon region, such epigenetic silencing is not observed in PEXG [74]. However, an independent study on PEXG identified hypermethylation of the HSP70 promoter, which impaired protein folding and weakened the cellular stress response. This impairment was linked to the accumulation of misfolded protein aggregates within the TM, potentially leading to increased IOP and contributing to RGC death [72].

Interspersed repetitive sequences

Interspersed repetitive sequences, which are CpG-rich elements constituting up to 45% of the human genome, can be classified into two primary categories: DNA transposons and retrotransposons [75]. The Alu repeat sequence, a 282-base pair non-long terminal repeat (non-LTR) DNA sequence, is the most prevalent short interspersed nuclear element (SINE) in human DNA [76]. Chansangpetch et al. reported a reduction in Alu sequence methylation in the TM of individuals with POAG,PACG, and secondary glaucoma, compared to normal subjects. Furthermore, patients with POAG exhibited significantly increased methylation levels of the human endogenous retrovirus K (HERV-K) sequence. These aberrant methylation patterns are hypothesized to facilitate fibrotic transformation within the TM [73]. Alu sequences demonstrate heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress and hypoxia, whereas HERV-K sequences exhibit more specific epigenetic regulation in genetically predisposed individuals [73]. Investigating these abnormal methylation changes enhances our understanding of the molecular pathological mechanisms underlying glaucoma and may offer novel biomarkers for early diagnosis.

External stimuli and TM methylation

Within the epigenetic context of glaucoma, in addition to the established pathways of hypoxia and oxidative stress, glucocorticoid exposure and the aging process have been identified as significant factors that may disrupt DNA methylation homeostasis in the TM. These factors, through distinct yet convergent mechanisms, collectively contribute to abnormal ECM metabolism and impaired aqueous humor outflow, thereby representing critical elements in the pathogenesis of the disease.

Glucocorticoids

Glucocorticoids represent a class of anti-inflammatory agents frequently employed in the management of various ocular and systemic conditions; however, prolonged administration of these steroids can lead to the development of steroid-induced glaucoma [77]. Corticosteroids, such as dexamethasone (DEX), are believed to elevate IOP primarily by impairing TM function. This impairment includes the specific upregulation of the myocilin (MYOC) gene [78], increased accumulation of ECM [79], and reduced phagocytic activity [80]. Furthermore, DEX exposure modifies DNA methylation patterns through interactions with glucocorticoid receptor binding sites, thereby altering the transcriptional profile of TMCs [81]. Matsuda et al. demonstrated that DEX is associated with demethylation of promoter CpG sites and the upregulation of genes such as FKBP5, ZBTB16, and Sodium Channel Epithelial 1 Subunit Alpha (SCNN1A) in TMCs. Conversely, DEX increases promoter methylation and downregulates the expression of ARSI, GREM1, GREM2, and MATN2 [82]. The administration of 5-aza-dC notably augmented the DEX-induced mRNA expression of FKBP5 and SCNN1A. As reported by Zhang et al. [83], FKBP5 is involved in the nuclear translocation of the glucocorticoid dominant-negative receptor, suggesting a potential protective role against glucocorticoid-induced glaucoma. In another study, the α subunit of the epithelial sodium channel, encoded by SCNN1A, was detected in human TMCs [84] and is recognized for its significant contribution to both the secretion and reabsorption of aqueous humor [85]. Consequently, glucocorticoids are hypothesized to contribute to ocular hypertension, at least partially, through the epigenetic reprogramming of TM gene expression. Understanding this network is essential for elucidating the pathogenesis of steroid-induced glaucoma and lays the groundwork for the development of targeted therapies aimed at reversing these specific methylation abnormalities.

Aging

Aging is associated with epigenetic changes, including heterochromatin formation, nucleosome remodeling, histone modifications, and alterations in DNA methylation status [86]. DNAm Age, a promising biomarker for aging, is a computational model that estimates biological age based on the methylation state of specific CpG sites [87]. Research has demonstrated a strong correlation between the epigenetic clock and accelerated glaucoma progression [88], as well as an interactive relationship with IOP [89]. In glaucoma, damage to RGCs, often accompanied by DNA strand breaks, disrupts epigenetic regulation. Advancing age is a significant risk factor for glaucoma, with its prevalence increasing markedly in older populations [90]. This has been suggested to result in the establishment of an aging-like DNA methylation pattern, potentially driving disease progression [90]. Additionally, patients with POAG exhibit an age-related acceleration in TM cell loss, exacerbating the functional impairment of the aqueous outflow pathway [91, 92]. Aging may contribute to glaucoma through changes in the DNA methylation of TM cells; however, this mechanism remains poorly understood and requires comprehensive investigation.

Glaucoma treatment and TM methylation

DNA methylation presents significant potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases [85]. In the field of glaucoma research, gene interventions targeting DNA methylation have demonstrated promising outcomes in preclinical models. Notably, the introduction of the OSK genes (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4) has been shown to enhance optic nerve signaling and improve the optomotor response in both glaucomatous and aged mice [86]. Importantly, subsequent studies have indicated that this therapeutic approach operates through OSK-mediated activation of TET1/TET2, which reconfigures the DNA methylome. This reconfiguration is associated with the restoration of a more youthful methylation pattern, correlating with axonal regeneration and vision restoration [90]. Therefore, modulating the methylation of key genes in the TM emerges as a highly promising target for glaucoma treatment. For instance, in glaucomatous TMCs, the DNMT inhibitor 5-aza has been shown to reverse fibrosis by downregulating DNMT1, TGFβ1, and COL1A1, while upregulating RASAL1 [55]. The validation of this mechanism in renal fibrosis further underscores its therapeutic potential [38]. Considering this promise and the existing paucity of studies specific to the TM, elucidating the precise methylation landscape of the TM constitutes a crucial step toward the development of targeted therapies for glaucoma.

Genetic modifications by non-coding RNAs in the TM

MicroRNA (miRNA)

MicroRNA and TM

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short RNA molecules that can bind to messenger RNA, thereby inhibiting its translation into protein [93, 94]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that miRNAs are not only implicated in the development of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease, but also play a significant role in the pathogenesis of glaucoma [95–98]. Utilizing TargetScan prediction software to analyze 962 non-housekeeping genes in TM tissue, it has been discovered that miRNAs can influence changes in the TM by regulating gene expression within this tissue [99]. Therapeutically targeting these specific miRNAs offers substantial potential for developing innovative treatment strategies that more precisely address the pathological progression of glaucoma. Research indicates that miR-204 is critically involved in TM function [100]. In human TMCs, miR-204 downregulates the transcription factor Forkhead Box C1 (FOXC1), a critical regulator of ocular development and glaucoma pathogenesis. This downregulation leads to decreased expression of FOXC1 and its downstream targets, including CLOCK, PLEKHG5, ITGb1, and MEIS2, within TM cultures [101]. miR-204 has been suggested to act as a pivotal regulator in TMCs, affecting processes such as apoptosis, protein aggregation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and inflammatory responses [102]. Furthermore, a study indicated that miR-21-5p can reduce IOP by 17% through the suppression of SMAD7/FGF18 and the activation of the MMP-9-mediated extracellular matrix degradation pathway [103]. The regulation of TM contraction by miRNAs constitutes a significant in vivo mechanism for controlling IOP [104]. Specifically, miRNA-450 can influence TMC contraction by modulating MyoD family proteins [105, 106]. Moreover, the miR-143/145 cluster has been demonstrated to enhance TMCs contraction by inducing myosin phosphorylation, a process linked to increased IOP. The reduction in IOP observed in knockout models of this cluster underscores the pivotal role of this pathway in the regulation of aqueous humor dynamics [107–110]. This mechanistic understanding has facilitated the development of therapeutic strategies targeting the inhibition of cell contraction. The effectiveness of this approach is corroborated by a new Rho inhibitor drug, which significantly decreases IOP via this pathway [110].

Upon activation, the TGF-β signaling pathway facilitates the formation and nuclear translocation of the Smad2/3-Smad4 complex, which subsequently regulates the transcription of target genes, thereby allowing for precise modulation of diverse effector molecules, including miRNAs [111, 112]. It is important to note that while the TGF-β pathway governs miRNA expression, several components of the pathway are themselves targets of miRNAs. This reciprocal regulatory interaction establishes a critical feedback loop that significantly impacts the initiation and progression of fibrotic diseases [113]. Notably, the miR-29 family is intricately associated with the TGF-β family [114]. Transforming growth factor-beta 2 (TGF-β2) markedly decreases the expression levels of miR-29b and miR-29c, whereas miR-29b suppresses TGF-β1 expression. This interaction suggests that the miR-29 family may play a crucial role in modulating the TGF-β1 signaling pathway [115]. Additionally, mechanical signals influence TGF-β1 through mechanisms mediated by miRNAs. In TM tissue, cyclic mechanical stress increases miR-24 expression, resulting in the suppression of FURIN and subsequent downregulation of TGF-β1 [116]. Furthermore, mechanical signals have been reported to induce the upregulation of miR-16-5p in TMCs [116]. The effects of these factors on the same substrate are contrasting: miR-144-3p enhances TMC proliferation and invasion by inhibiting FN-1 [117], whereas TGF-β2, in contrast, promotes FN-1 expression in TMCs [118], thereby illustrating their divergent regulatory roles.

MicroRNA and oxidative stress

In the pathogenesis of glaucoma, oxidative stress serves as a crucial mediator of TM dysfunction by modulating the expression of specific miRNAs. Under conditions of oxidative stress, TMCs release miRNAs, including miR-21 and miR-450, into the aqueous humor. These miRNAs act as distal signaling molecules that can reach the posterior retina, potentially facilitating the spread of injury across different ocular segments [105]. miR-29b functions as an essential negative regulator of ECM expression, with its expression being upregulated by Wnt/β-catenin signaling [119] but downregulated under chronic oxidative stress [120]. This dynamic regulation allows for precise control of ECM homeostasis. Secreted Frizzled-Related Protein 1 (sFRP1), which acts as an inhibitor of Wnt signaling, is regulated by miRNAs rather than DNA methylation within the TM. Elevated sFRP1 expression has been shown to increase TM stiffness, decrease aqueous humor outflow, and is associated with heightened cell apoptosis [121].

Stress-induced senescence of the TM, exacerbated by aging and oxidative stress, has been proposed as an important factor associated with increasing aqueous humor outflow resistance [122]. Oxidative stress appears to undermine the anti-apoptotic capacity of TMCs by downregulating key protective miRNAs, such as miR-15 and miR-106b, thereby enhancing the p21-mediated senescence pathway and accelerating cellular senescence[123]. Furthermore, miR-182 contributes to TM senescence by targeting retinoic acid receptor gamma (RARG) [123]. The upregulation of miR-182, along with its genetic variants, has been linked to increased IOP, which aligns with its observed elevation in the aqueous humor of POAG patients and in aged cells [124]. In TMCs undergoing senescence induced by H₂O₂, miR-183 is upregulated and exerts inhibitory effects by targeting ITGB1 and KIF2A. This targeting relationship was confirmed through the use of an ITGB1 construct lacking the 3'-UTR, which rescued the inhibitory effects [125]. Additionally, oxidative stress leads to the downregulation of miR-200c-3p in TMCs, resulting in the derepression of PTEN and subsequent inhibition of the PTEN/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. This process is ultimately associated with enhanced apoptosis and reduced proliferation [126, 127]. In stress-induced senescent human TMCs, miR-204 expression is markedly downregulated; however, its overexpression promotes apoptosis. This suggests that miR-204 plays a complex and context-dependent role in the maintenance of cellular homeostasis [123]. Salidroside has been shown to confer protection to TMCs against oxidative damage induced by hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) through the upregulation of miR-27a, which subsequently activates the PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways [128]. Concurrently, another study reported an upregulation of miR-210-3p expression in the TM of POAG patients. Salidroside was found to downregulate miR-210-3p, thereby mitigating the miR-210-3p-mediated enhancement of transforming growth factor-beta 2 (TGF-β2) pro- ECM expression and contributing to a reduction in IOP [129].

In response to detrimental stimuli such as oxidative stress, intrinsic protective mechanisms are extensively activated in TMCs. Within this framework, various miRNAs and their associated pathways assume a pivotal role, thereby uncovering novel targets for the development of cytoprotective strategies. Research has demonstrated that miR-483-3p exerts anti-fibrotic effects by directly targeting Smad4, leading to the subsequent inhibition of ECM production in stressed TMCs, which suggests its potential as a therapeutic target for glaucoma [130]. Additionally, miR-1298 has been shown to mediate multifaceted cytoprotective effects under chronic oxidative stress by concurrently suppressing the TGF-β2/Smad4 axis and activating Wnt signaling through the targeting of EIF4E3 [131]. In the regulation of cellular senescence, miR-181a mitigates H₂O₂-induced apoptosis in TMCs by inhibiting both the Nuclear Factor Kappa B and JNK signaling pathways [132]. Concurrently, miR-93 confers protective effects by inhibiting the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 [133]. The upregulation of miR-146a may contribute to the suppression of inflammatory mediator production in senescent cells, thereby mitigating their harmful impact on adjacent tissues [134]. Additionally, miR-137 and miR-29b-3p have been documented to provide dual protective effects by inhibiting apoptosis and preventing abnormal ECM accumulation. This is achieved through targeting Src to inhibit the YAP/TAZ pathway [135] and downregulating the E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase RNF138 [136], respectively. Future therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating these essential miRNA networks may represent an innovative approach for precise glaucoma intervention by arresting or reversing TM pathogenesis. Long non-coding RNA (lncRNA)

Long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) functions as a competitive endogenous RNA, possessing miRNA response elements (MREs) that enable it to competitively bind to miRNAs. This interaction effectively "adsorbs" or "sponges" miRNAs, thereby inhibiting their binding to target messenger RNA (mRNA) [137]. A study examining hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)-induced oxidative stress in human TMCs identified 70 differentially expressed lncRNAs, of which 24 were integral to a lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA interaction network [138]. The lncRNA GAS5 has been documented to induce apoptosis in TMCs and facilitate ECM deposition under oxidative stress conditions by sponging miR-29b-3p, leading to the upregulation of STAT3 [139]. Additionally, lncRNA-RP11-820 has been shown to directly bind miR-3178, modulate MYOD1 expression, transcriptionally activate ECM genes in TMCs via MYOD1, and subsequently interact with STAT3 to enhance ECM production [140].

LncRNAs are increasingly recognized as significant targets for the diagnosis and assessment of glaucoma severity. A microarray study conducted by Xie et al. identified lncRNAs T267384, ENST00000607393, and T342877 as potential diagnostic biomarkers for POAG. Notably, the knockdown of ENST00000607393 was found to mitigate pathological calcification in primary human TMCs in vitro [141]. In the context of steroid-induced glaucoma, corticosteroids have been shown to induce senescence in TMCs, with the lncRNA Antisense non-coding RNA in the INK4 locus (ANRIL) emerging as a potentially critical mediator in this process. The ANRIL/p15 axis is notably enriched in human TMCs and is linked to cellular dysfunction; targeting this pathway has been shown to alleviate senescence, thereby presenting a novel approach for clinical management [142]. LncRNA ANRIL appears to confer protection to human TMCs against oxidative damage by inhibiting miR-7 and simultaneously activating both the mTOR and MEK/ERK signaling pathways. Furthermore, polymorphisms in ANRIL and Metastasis-Associated Lung Adenocarcinoma Transcript 1 (MALAT1), including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and haplotypes, exhibit a significant association with susceptibility to POAG [143]. The combined analysis of these two lncRNAs offers considerable diagnostic potential for the detection of glaucoma [144]. Histone epigenetic modifications

Histones serve as the fundamental structural proteins of chromatin in eukaryotic cells, playing a crucial role in DNA packaging and functional regulation. Modifications of histones predominantly occur at their N-terminal tails and encompass processes such as methylation, phosphorylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination [145]. Contemporary research concerning histone modifications in the context of glaucoma predominantly emphasizes acetylation and methylation [146]. Histone acetylation and deacetylation involve the addition or removal of acetyl groups on lysine residues located within the histone N-terminal tails and on the nucleosome core surface. These processes are catalyzed by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs), respectively. Such modifications lead to the relaxation (euchromatin) or tightening (heterochromatin) of chromatin structure, thereby modulating gene transcription levels [147]. Furthermore, histone methyltransferases mediate the transfer of methyl groups to histone lysine or arginine residues in a site-specific manner, thereby regulating chromatin configuration and influencing transcriptional activity and DNA methylation patterns [146].

Histone modifications in RGCs

In the pathogenesis of glaucoma, the apoptosis of RGCs is increasingly understood to be modulated by specific epigenetic mechanisms. Notably, histone modifications serve as dynamic regulators of chromatin architecture, directly affecting the survival or apoptosis of RGCs under stress by modulating the expression of particular gene sets. Recent investigations have progressively elucidated that various histone modifications—including deacetylation, methylation, and lactylation—are significantly altered in glaucoma models and are implicated in the signaling pathways that lead to RGC apoptosis. In healthy RGCs, HDACs play crucial roles; specifically, HDAC1 and HDAC2 function within the nucleus to maintain chromatin structure and regulate gene expression, whereas HDAC3 predominantly resides in the cytoplasm [38]. Research indicates that acute optic nerve injury leads to a marked increase in the mRNA expression of Class I HDACs, specifically HDAC2 and HDAC3. Concurrently, HDAC3 undergoes a distinct translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, an occurrence associated with a global decrease in the acetylation level of histone H4 [148]. In parallel, Pelzel et al. reported that in the aging DBA/2 J glaucoma model, dying cells exhibited nuclear localization of HDAC3 along with diminished H4 acetylation. Their study further revealed that the suppression of Fem1cR3 gene expression occurs prior to RGC death. Importantly, the HDAC inhibitor Trichostatin A (TSA) was found to mitigate progressive somatic degeneration of RGCs in this model, although it did not significantly affect axonal degeneration [149]. In the initial stages of glaucomatous optic neuropathy, it has been suggested that HDAC3-mediated deacetylation of histone H4 contributes to the suppression of normal gene expression, a process occurring prior to the programmed apoptosis of RGCs [150]. Additionally, histone methylation has been implicated in the apoptosis of RGCs in the context of glaucoma. Under typical physiological conditions, histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27) undergoes demethylation during somatic cell development [16]. Conversely, in a rat model of chronic glaucoma, the silencing of the lncRNA growth arrest-specific transcript 5 (GAS5) leads to the upregulation of the histone methyltransferase enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (Ezh2). This elevation results in an increase in H3K27me3 levels, which subsequently suppresses the expression of ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) and has been linked to the promotion of RGC apoptosis [151]. In contrast, Pan et al. identified a splice-site mutation in the gene encoding the histone arginine methyltransferase METTL23 within a cohort of normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) patients. This mutation impairs the enzyme's capacity to catalyze histone H3 arginine 17 (H3R17) dimethylation in the retina. The absence of this modification downregulates the transcription of the estrogen receptor-responsive gene pS2, which subsequently enhances NF-κB signaling, ultimately leading to RGC apoptosis [152, 153]. Under conditions of hypertensive metabolic stress, elevated retinal lactate levels induce H4K8 lactylation, which selectively accumulates at the MFN2 promoter, thereby repressing its transcription. The suppression of mitochondrial fusion, coupled with a negative feedback interaction with Wnt/β-catenin signaling, establishes a self-reinforcing cycle. This axis, in conjunction with metabolic and mitochondrial dysregulation in Müller cells, is hypothesized to facilitate RGC apoptosis. The partial amelioration observed upon inhibition of lactylation or Wnt signaling highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting this metabolic-epigenetic-mitochondrial cascade [154].

HDAC modulation for RGC neuroprotection

Modulating retinal HDAC activity constitutes a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of glaucoma. Siwak et al. have associated the expression of HDAC6 and Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) with the pathogenesis of POAG [155]. Concurrently, Luo et al. demonstrated that the downregulation of SIRT1 in ischemic retinal injury supports the therapeutic approach of enhancing SIRT1 activity [156, 157]. Notably, HDAC inhibitors have exhibited neuroprotective effects on RGCs. Yuan et al. reported that tubacin, an HDAC6-selective inhibitor, mitigated RGC apoptosis in a rat model of retinal ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury by suppressing the upregulation of HDAC6 and modulating the expression of apoptosis-related factors, specifically by reducing Bax and increasing Bcl-2 levels [158]. Moreover, the HDAC8 inhibitor H7E has demonstrated efficacy in suppressing abnormal activation of Müller glia and oxidative stress, thereby alleviating retinal damage, diminishing inflammation, and preventing RGC death [159]. In a distinct study, Zaidi et al. reported that Class I and IIb HDACs are activated and upregulated during the early stages of glaucoma. Their findings indicated that early intervention with the δ-opioid receptor agonist SNC-121 could inhibit the activity and expression of these HDACs, suggesting its potential role in RGC neuroprotection [160]. The effects of HDAC3 inhibition are complex; one study indicated that genetic ablation of HDAC3 did not confer protection to RGCs, while pharmacological inhibition using RGFP966 resulted in a modest reduction of somatic loss in aged glaucomatous mice. These findings suggest that the therapeutic potential of targeting Class I HDACs necessitates further elucidation, thereby justifying the exploration of other family members [161]. The HDAC inhibitor valproic acid (VPA) demonstrates neuroprotective properties in glaucomatous models, as evidenced by its mitigation of retinal structural damage in GLAST knockout mice [162]. This effect is further supported by its ability to preserve retinal function and RGCs in hypertensive rat models [163]. These preclinical results are complemented by clinical observations indicating enhanced visual function in patients with advanced-stage glaucoma undergoing VPA treatment [164]. However, the clinical application of broad-spectrum HDAC inhibitors is limited by their potential toxic effects on non-target cells during prolonged, high-dose administration [165]. A more refined strategy entails localized intravitreal administration or the creation of isoform-selective inhibitors to optimize the therapeutic window. Illustrating this method, entinostat (MS-275)—a selective inhibitor of HDAC1 and HDAC3—has demonstrated a superior safety profile compared to VPA, while simultaneously offering substantial neuroprotection to RGCs in experimental models of optic nerve injury [166].

Histone modifications in the TM

Recent studies have started to elucidate the pivotal role of histone modifications within the TM in the pathogenesis of glaucoma, marking a novel frontier in comprehending the disease's progression. As glaucoma progresses from moderate to severe stages, persistent oxidative stress in the TM may contribute to significant epigenetic changes. A notable event in this process is the marked upregulation of histone H3 lysine 14 acetylation (H3K14ac). This particular histone modification functions as a molecular "switch," potentially driving a fundamental transition in the mechanisms of TMCs death—from predominantly apoptotic pathways to those characterized by autophagy. Importantly, this epigenetic reprogramming is not restricted to ocular tissues; similar alterations in histone modification patterns are observable in peripheral blood. This systemic aspect provides an innovative perspective for understanding the global pathophysiology of glaucoma and facilitates the development of novel epigenetic biomarkers [167]. Bermudez et al. demonstrated that in a perfused bovine anterior segment culture, histone H3 and global protein hyperacetylation—triggered either by a glaucomatous condition or the HDAC inhibitor TDP-A—significantly increased TGF-β2 expression. This increase was associated with the deposition of extracellular matrix components such as collagen and fibronectin, as well as elevated IOP [168]. Consequently, histone hyperacetylation is implicated as a potential initiating factor in TM injury during the pathogenesis of glaucoma.

Consequently, the modulation of HDAC activity represents a promising novel approach for TM-based glaucoma therapy. Research by Su C–C et al. demonstrated that cyclic mechanical stretch (CMS), a critical biomechanical stressor in glaucoma, leads to the downregulation of HDAC6 in TMCs. This downregulation markedly increases the acetylation level of α-tubulin at lysine 40 (Ac-TUBA4). Importantly, Ac-TUBA4 levels were significantly elevated in glaucomatous TMCs and tissues compared to non-glaucomatous controls, suggesting a potential compensatory stabilization mechanism in response to increased IOP or tissue stiffening. This acetylation process enhances microtubule stability, an effect that can be mimicked by the use of the HDAC6 inhibitor tubacin. Moreover, intravitreal administration of tubacin in mice resulted in a significant reduction in IOP for a duration of at least three days. These findings collectively indicate that promoting microtubule acetylation through the inhibition of HDAC6 represents a promising novel approach for the treatment of glaucoma [169]. In a rabbit model of glaucoma induced by TGF-β2, the pan-HDAC inhibitor Suberoylanilide Hydroxamic Acid (SAHA) was observed to mitigate IOP elevation and counteract the associated increases in transepithelial electrical resistance, ECM protein expression, and cytoskeletal protein expression in cells of the aqueous humor outflow pathway. This effect was demonstrated to be mediated via the regulation of non-Smad TGF-β signaling pathways [170]. In addition to its IOP-lowering effects, SAHA exhibits further therapeutic potential in the context of glaucoma filtration surgery (GFS). The inhibition of both angiogenesis and the activation of conjunctival fibroblasts is achieved through the suppression of Smad and non-Smad TGF-β signaling pathways, thereby mitigating postoperative wound fibrosis and scar formation [171]. Sharma et al. demonstrated that the application of SAHA led to an increase in bleb length and area, a reduction in IOP, and decreased vascularity compared to control groups. These effects were correlated with enhanced acetylation of histones H3 and H4 in corneal fibroblasts and conjunctiva, as well as reduced levels of αSMA, F-actin, and collagen deposition at the sclerotomy site [172]. Conversely, Rodgers et al. reported that a single intraoperative injection of SAHA did not significantly extend bleb survival in a rabbit model [173]. These contrasting results underscore the need for further research to explore various dosing regimens and administration strategies to definitively ascertain its efficacy. m6A

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) represents the most prevalent and reversible internal modification within eukaryotic mRNA, characterized by the methylation of the nitrogen-6 position of adenosine [174]. The regulation of the m6A modification is intricately controlled through the coordinated actions of methyltransferases (writers), demethylases (erasers), and binding proteins (readers) [175]. Writers facilitate the deposition of m6A, a modification that can be reversed by erasers, ensuring its dynamic nature [176]. Subsequently, readers interpret the m6A mark by binding to these modified sites, thereby influencing the fate of the target mRNA through the modulation of processes such as splicing, export, degradation, and translation [177]. As a dynamic regulatory mechanism, m6A methylation plays a crucial role in fine-tuning gene expression by regulating essential steps in mRNA metabolism, including splicing, export, translation, and decay, thus impacting a wide range of physiological processes [178].

Research findings demonstrate that aqueous humor from patients with PEXG exhibits elevated levels of m6A, alongside a marked upregulation of five key regulatory enzymes: METTL3, YTHDC2, HNRNPA2B1, HNRNPC, and LRPPRC [179]. The authors suggest that m6A methylation may play a role in the pathogenesis of PEXG by influencing the composition of the ECM, with METTL3 identified as a potential central regulator in this process [179]. Moreover, m6A methylation may affect PEXG pathogenesis by modulating lncRNA expression and participating in competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks. Specifically, the ENST00000485383/hsa-miR-592/ROCK1 axis is proposed as a potential regulatory pathway that merits further investigation within the context of m6A-related mechanisms in PEXG [180]. In a rabbit model, research identified a pathway through which TGF-β1 upregulates METTL3, leading to m6A remodeling that enhances Smad3 signaling. This process is associated with the proliferation of human Tenon's capsule fibroblasts (HTFs) and ECM deposition following GFS [181]. Additionally, in a rat model of glaucoma, inhibiting METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of HMGCS1 was found to play a protective role, significantly reducing glutamate excitotoxicity-induced ferroptosis in RGCs and thereby contributing to the restoration of visual function [182]. Beyond its involvement in ferroptosis, METTL3 also influences autophagy in retinal ischemia–reperfusion (RIR) injury. Overexpression of METTL3 was demonstrated to mitigate RGC loss and electrophysiological deficits by enhancing m6A methylation on FoxO1 mRNA, thus suppressing FoxO1-driven autophagic activity [183]. Furthermore, Niu et al. identified YTHDF2, an m6A reader protein highly expressed in mouse RGCs, as a crucial negative regulator that inhibits dendritic development and maintenance [184]. Mechanistically, YTHDF2 functions as a downstream effector by binding to m6A-modified HMGCS1 transcripts and promoting their degradation, thereby enhancing the pro-ferroptotic signaling in RGCs [182]. Utilizing a combined RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP-Seq) and transcriptomic analysis, Kalrn (isoforms 7, 9, 12), Striatin, and Ubr4 were identified as direct targets through which YTHDF2 mediates the inhibition of dendritic branching [185]. Consequently, YTHDF2 and its associated target network emerge as promising therapeutic targets for the treatment of glaucoma.

While the majority of existing evidence regarding m⁶A in glaucoma is derived from models involving RGCs and Tenon’s fibroblasts, it is plausible that analogous m⁶A-dependent pathways are also active in TMCs, particularly through TGF-β1-mediated profibrotic remodeling and ferroptosis. In the context of the TM, differentially expressed m⁶A regulators show potential as diagnostic biomarkers for POAG. For example, the expression of the m⁶A reader YTHDC2 in TMCs is significantly altered in response to oxidative stress or TGF-β2 stimulation. By recognizing m⁶A modifications, YTHDC2 plays a crucial role in modulating cell migration and ECM synthesis, thereby positioning itself as a significant factor in the pathogenesis of POAG [186]. These findings significantly enhance our mechanistic comprehension of glaucoma pathogenesis and highlight the translational potential of targeting the m6A regulatory axis, which includes critical components such as METTL3, YTHDF2, and YTHDC2, for the development of innovative diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in disease management. An integrative model of epigenetic remodeling in the glaucomatous TM

In summary, the current body of evidence supports a comprehensive model in which various epigenetic modifications converge to produce a common glaucomatous TM phenotype, marked by fibrosis, tissue stiffening, and stress-induced cellular senescence. Alterations in DNA methylation of key profibrotic genes, such as TGF-β1 and GDF7, along with changes in the methylation of ECM-modifying genes like LOXL1 and repetitive elements, are believed to drive TMCs towards excessive matrix synthesis, cross-linking, and impaired ECM turnover. Concurrently, the dynamics of histone acetylation and methylation, as well as deregulated miRNAs, lncRNAs, and m⁶A regulators, collectively influence central signaling pathways, including TGF-β/BMP–Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, RhoA/ROCK, PI3K–Akt, and NF-κB. These pathways coordinate cytoskeletal remodeling, increased contractility, and mechanotransduction, while also promoting oxidative stress-induced apoptosis, senescence, and dysregulated autophagy in TMCs. External factors highly pertinent to glaucoma—such as aging, chronic hypoxia, oxidative stress, and prolonged glucocorticoid exposure—serve as upstream triggers that induce epigenetic modifications and perpetuate self-sustaining feed-forward loops involving fibrosis, increased tissue stiffness, and cellular damage. Within this framework, seemingly distinct epigenetic pathways ultimately converge on a characteristic glaucomatous TM state, which is associated with increased resistance to aqueous humor outflow and elevated IOP. This provides a conceptual foundation for integrating molecular findings and designing multi-target epigenetic interventions. An integrative model summarizing these interactions is shown in Fig. 7. Integrative model of epigenetic remodeling in the glaucomatous TM. External stimuli pertinent to glaucoma, including aging, hypoxia/oxidative stress, mechanical stretch/elevated IOP, and glucocorticoids, converge on multiple layers of epigenetic remodeling within TMCs. This remodeling encompasses DNA methylation, histone modifications, m⁶A RNA methylation, and non-coding RNAs. These epigenetic mechanisms modulate key signaling pathways such as TGF-β/BMP–Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, RhoA/ROCK, NF-κB, and PI3K–Akt. Consequently, these pathways drive ECM deposition and fibrosis, cytoskeletal remodeling and tissue stiffening, as well as oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and senescence. Conclusion

This review has systematically elucidated the critical role of epigenetic dysregulation in the pathogenesis of glaucoma, with a particular emphasis on TM dysfunction. We have synthesized evidence demonstrating the involvement of key epigenetic mechanisms—such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, non-coding RNAs (including miRNAs and lncRNAs), and the emerging field of RNA m⁶A methylation—in fundamental pathological processes. These processes, including aberrant extracellular matrix deposition, fibrotic transformation, cellular senescence, and impaired oxidative stress response within TMCs, collectively contribute to increased aqueous humor outflow resistance and elevated IOP, which is the primary modifiable risk factor in glaucoma.

The synthesis of research underscores specific epigenetic modifications as significant contributors to TM pathology. These modifications encompass the aberrant methylation of genes such as TGF-β1, GDF7, and LOXL1; the dysregulated expression of miRNAs, including miR-29b, miR-204, and miR-210-3p; and the involvement of lncRNAs like ANRIL and MALAT1. Additionally, alterations in the retinal epigenome, such as histone deacetylation and H3K27 trimethylation, directly affect the vulnerability and apoptosis of RGCs. The dynamic and reversible characteristics of these epigenetic marks render them not only as biomarkers of disease but also as promising therapeutic targets for intervention.

The identification of distinct epigenetic markers in readily obtainable biological fluids, such as aqueous humor or peripheral blood, presents a promising method for non-invasive or minimally invasive diagnostic procedures. The methylation status of genes such as GDF7, alongside the levels of miRNAs (e.g., miR-210-3p) and the expression of lncRNAs (e.g., ANRIL, MALAT1), may serve as highly sensitive biomarkers for the early detection of diseases, the stratification of patients into specific pathogenic subtypes, and the monitoring of disease progression and therapeutic response. This approach has the potential to facilitate a transition towards more personalized and proactive management of glaucoma.

Targeting the epigenome represents a potent strategy for directly reversing aberrant gene expression patterns associated with disease. The application of DNMT inhibitors, such as 5-azacytidine, has shown efficacy in reversing hypermethylation and silencing pro-fibrotic genes. Similarly, HDAC inhibitors, including SAHA and entinostat, facilitate a more relaxed chromatin state, thereby restoring protective gene expression profiles. These approaches have demonstrated promising results in preclinical models of TM fibrosis and RGC neuroprotection. Furthermore, the use of miRNA mimics to replenish downregulated protective miRNAs, such as miR-29b, or antagomirs to inhibit overexpressed pathogenic miRNAs, such as the miR-143/145 cluster, enables precise modulation of critical molecular pathways. Additionally, targeting lncRNAs or components of the N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) machinery, including METTL3 and YTHDF2, offers novel avenues for therapeutic intervention. Techniques such as OSK-mediated epigenetic reprogramming, which rejuvenates the DNA methylome and promotes axonal regeneration in experimental models, underscore the potential of epigenetic therapies not only to arrest but also to reverse glaucomatous damage.

To fully harness the potential of epigenetic therapies in the treatment of glaucoma, future research must address several pivotal areas. Firstly, the advancement of sophisticated, TM-specific drug delivery systems, such as those utilizing viral vectors or nanoparticles, is essential to ensure high on-target efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. Secondly, there is a need for large-scale, longitudinal multi-omics studies to thoroughly map the dynamic epigenetic landscape of the TM and retina across various glaucoma subtypes and stages. This will facilitate the identification of the most critical and druggable nodes within the epigenetic network. Lastly, the successful translation of these findings into clinical practice will depend on well-designed clinical trials aimed at validating the safety and efficacy of epigenetic modulators, both as standalone treatments and as adjuncts to conventional IOP-lowering therapies.

In summary, the rapidly advancing field of epigenetics is significantly transforming our comprehension of glaucoma, shifting the focus from merely controlling IOP to addressing the underlying molecular pathophysiology. By leveraging the reversible characteristics of epigenetic modifications, there is potential for the development of novel disease-modifying therapies that could preserve vision and enhance the quality of life for millions of individuals affected by glaucoma globally. The future of glaucoma treatment extends beyond pressure reduction to include the modification of the epigenetic mechanisms that contribute to the disease.
