Health literacy and syphilis knowledge: findings from a national survey of U.S. women of reproductive age
Authors: Elena Ghezzi, Boyun Kim, Kyeorda Kemp, Deidre Hurse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAfter decades of relative stability, syphilis rates among reproductive-age women in the United States have sharply increased, with maternal cases tripling between 2016 and 2022. This surge has also contributed to a concerning rise in congenital syphilis. Gaps in healthcare access and stigma surrounding sexually transmitted infections have compounded the problem. Focused public health initiatives are needed to strengthen prevention, screening, and treatment efforts.MethodsWe conducted a national survey to assess health literacy and syphilis knowledge among 643 women aged 18 to 49. Participants completed validated measures, including the Brief Health Literacy Screener and a syphilis knowledge assessment. Recruitment was conducted online to achieve geographic diversity across 48 states, with intentional oversampling of Michigan participants to support future region-specific analyses.Overall, 120 (18.7%) screened positive for inadequate health literacy and 523 (81.3%) did not. Among those who screened positive, 72.5% (87/120) were syphilis-knowledgeable versus 89.1% (466/523) among those who did not screen positive. In logistic regression, syphilis knowledge was associated with nearly three times the odds of not screening positive for inadequate health literacy (AOR = 2.99, 95% CI: 1.71–5.22; p < .001). Education was not associated with health literacy (p = .802). Age and race were significant predictors of health literacy, but neither age nor race predicted syphilis knowledge in adjusted models.ConclusionThis study highlights the connection between health literacy and syphilis knowledge. It emphasizes the need for integrated health education that targets knowledge and literacy, especially among younger and marginalized groups. Incorporating health literacy and syphilis education into sexual health strategies could improve prevention and reduce disparities in syphilis rates among women of reproductive age and racially marginalized populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25506-x.

Introduction

Syphilis, which remains a significant public health concern, is caused by the spirochete bacterium Treponema pallidum and is primarily spread through sexual contact [1]. However, it can also be transmitted from mother to fetus during pregnancy. Syphilis can manifest with a wide range of nonspecific symptoms that often resemble those of other diseases, which can complicate timely diagnosis. The infection typically progresses through primary, secondary, and tertiary stages, beginning with a painless sore and, if untreated, potentially leading to systemic symptoms and severe long-term complications [2]. Because these symptoms are often nonspecific and may go unnoticed, timely diagnosis is frequently delayed.

Since the early 2010 s, syphilis rates among women have increased at a faster pace than among men, contributing to a narrowing of the historical gender gap in infection rates [3]. Between 2020 and 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported a 50.1% increase in primary and secondary syphilis cases among women in the United States [4]. The rise in congenital syphilis cases from 2020 to 2024 has been quite significant, with an 81.8% increase [4]. Transplacental transmission of Treponema pallidum can result in severe adverse pregnancy outcomes, including stillbirth, preterm birth, low birth weight, and neonatal death. Although congenital syphilis can have devastating consequences, it is largely preventable with timely screening and appropriate treatment during pregnancy. When cases do occur, they often reflect broader systemic gaps in access to quality prenatal care and follow-up services [5]. It is important to note that this issue disproportionately affects those facing systemic barriers such as poverty, limited prenatal care access, and poorly funded public health systems [6]. This concerning trend has prompted health organizations, including the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, to call for improved education, enhanced diagnostics, and more robust prevention and screening measures for patients by healthcare providers [7, 8].

Health literacy, the capacity to obtain, comprehend, and use health-related information effectively, is essential for reducing the transmission of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and understanding treatment and prevention [9–11]. Rather than an individual deficit, health literacy is shaped by broader systemic factors such as the complexity of health communication, the availability of culturally and linguistically appropriate materials, and the accessibility of healthcare services [12]. In the context of syphilis, structural barriers-including inadequate access to prenatal care, culturally relevant health information, transportation, and insurance coverage- hinder prevention efforts and contribute to the rising infection rates [13]. These barriers delay timely healthcare engagement and contribute to missed opportunities for screening and treatment, illustrating how structural determinants intersect with low health literacy to reinforce stigma and sustain inequities [13].

Systemic inequities such as structural racism, underfunded healthcare systems, and economic disadvantage contribute to disproportionately high rates of syphilis among Black women and non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native women [3, 14, 15]. These disparities are reinforced by discriminatory healthcare practices, limited investment in reproductive health services and community engagement, racialized stigma surrounding STIs, and persistent gaps in trust between public health systems and marginalized communities [13–19].

Despite the well-documented resurgence in syphilis rates, limited research has examined how health literacy influences syphilis knowledge and prevention, particularly among women of reproductive age, even though extensive literature exists regarding health literacy and HIV outcomes, including lower knowledge, higher viral loads, and reduced adherence to antiretroviral therapy among those with low literacy [20–24]. Studies on health literacy and STIs more broadly also associate higher literacy with safer sexual behaviors, infection prevention, and medication adherence [25]. Community-based interventions that strengthen health literacy have proven effective in improving STI prevention and reducing disparities, underscoring the need for equitable, culturally tailored, and community-engaged approaches to syphilis prevention and education [25, 26]. In light of these studies and a gap in syphilis literature, we aimed to analyze the relationship of health literacy to syphilis-specific knowledge, as this has implications and helps inform community-level interventions aimed at syphilis prevention.

To address this issue, this study examines the relationship between health literacy and knowledge of syphilis screening and prevention behaviors among U.S. women of reproductive age, 18–49 years. By identifying specific knowledge gaps and literacy-related barriers, the findings aim to inform the design of equitable, community-engaged interventions that support syphilis prevention and improve maternal and child health outcomes in populations most at risk. The survey was designed to assess participants’ knowledge of syphilis transmission, risk factors, prevention behaviors, and how these relate to health literacy. Building on existing methodologies used in HIV and STI research, this study applies those frameworks specifically to syphilis. In doing so, it aims to generate actionable insights to inform public health strategies addressing the growing rates of syphilis and congenital syphilis among U.S. women.

Methods

Survey distribution

The online survey was conducted using Oakland University’s Qualtrics platform and distributed through Centiment, a platform specifically designed for research and data collection. Participants voluntarily opted into Centiment through various channels, such as social media ads, search engines, and partnerships with different websites. The research team did not directly recruit participants. Centiment managed all recruitment efforts and the distribution of incentives. Respondents were informed that the incentives varied dynamically.

Prior to distributing the survey, Centiment verified that participants were assigned female at birth, aged 18–49 years, and residing in the United States based on zip code verification. Participants from Michigan were intentionally oversampled as the team was interested in identifying information that may help improve health outcomes for the region in which they reside. Survey responses reported to the research team did not contain direct identifiers. Details about the Process for Verifying Participants and Ensuring Anonymity can be found in Fig. 1.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined pragmatically, ensuring it was sufficiently large to support logistic regression analyses with multiple predictors, rather than through a formal pre-study power calculation. The achieved sample of 643 participants exceeded standard methodological benchmarks for such analyses.

Survey instruments

The survey consisted of 28 items, with 18 questions based on two well-established instruments used to assess health literacy and syphilis-specific knowledge [27, 28]. These instruments were selected based on their relevance, reliability, and ease of application to women of reproductive age.

Health literacy measurement description

Health literacy was assessed using the Brief Health Literacy Screener (BHLS) developed by Chew et al. [27]. This instrument was chosen because it aligns with the study’s aim of assessing health literacy in relation to knowledge about syphilis. Previous research has shown that this brief instrument is as effective as other standards, validated tools for identifying inadequate health literacy [27]. Responses were reverse-scored and summed (range 3–15), with scores ≤ 9 classified as inadequate health literacy and scores ≥ 10 as not-inadequate.

Syphilis knowledge assessment description

Syphilis knowledge was measured using a validated instrument developed to assess awareness among the South China populations [28, 29]. The tool includes eight true/false statements covering prevention, transmission, screening, and disease presentation. Participants were categorized as having adequate syphilis knowledge if they answered at least six questions correctly. This instrument was chosen because it is the only knowledge assessment tool specific to syphilis identified in the literature at the time of survey development.

Data analysis

Demographic data collected

Demographic characteristics included age, race/ethnicity, education, and zip code. ZIP codes were collected only to derive regional indicators and were stripped from the analytic and shareable datasets prior to analysis. The race/ethnicity categories are as follows: Caucasian/White, African American/Black, Asian, Indigenous/Native American, Latina/Hispanic, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, Middle Eastern and North African (MENA), multiple race/ethnicity, and other. Participants could select more than one option for the race/ethnicity category. Thus, we categorized “multiple race/ethnicity” when participants checked more than one category. For example, if a participant chose Hispanic and White, they were categorized as more than one race/ethnicity. While respondents identifying only as Hispanic or Latina were classified as Hispanic. These categories were chosen to reflect the new guidance announced by the U.S. Office of Management and Budget (OMB) in March of 2024 [30]. Of note, only one individual self-identified as MENA. Therefore, they were folded into the category of “white” for analysis purposes, as it would be inappropriate to run statistical analysis on one individual, and historically, individuals who identify as MENA have been characterized as white (Dow vs. United States, 1915) [31]. Individuals chose the following for education: “some high school,” “high school,” “trade school,” “some college,” “associate’s degree,” “bachelor’s degree,” “master’s degree,” “PhD or higher”. Individuals were assigned to one of four age ranges: 18–24, 25–34, 35–44, and 45–54. The four age ranges used were adapted based on the OECD Frascati Manual guidelines [32]. The 45–54 category was included because the upper limit of “child-bearing age” as defined by the WHO is 49 years.

Statistical test performed

Analyses were designed to explore the relationship between syphilis knowledge (dependent variable) and inadequate health literacy (independent variable). Health literacy was explicitly assessed to identify inadequate health literacy, rather than to evaluate health literacy as a continuous construct.

We tested variance equality with an F test and used Welch’s t-test when variances were unequal. Associations between adequate health literacy and syphilis knowledge were assessed with Pearson’s chi-square test, and syphilis knowledge (binary) was modeled with logistic regression using health literacy as the primary predictor. Pearson chi-square test and Logistic regression were also performed between health literacy (independent variable) and the demographic factors (dependent variables). Multivariate logistic regressions were performed between the dependent variable, health literacy, and the independent variables, syphilis knowledge, age group, and race/ethnicity (also referred to as race). These variables were also examined in relation to both health literacy and syphilis knowledge using chi-square tests. A formal sample size calculation was not performed; however, the final sample size exceeded recommended minimums for logistic regression analyses based on participant-to-variable ratios. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 29.0.2.0 and STATA Version 18.5, with a significance level (alpha) set at 0.05.

Results

Participant distribution

The survey was launched on November 17, 2023, and data collection ended on November 21, 2023. Incomplete surveys and those that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded using listwise deletion, resulting in only 643 complete and valid datasets for analysis. 88% of the distributed surveys were completed and met the inclusion criteria.

Participant representation was observed throughout the continental United States, with the exceptions of Vermont and South Dakota. Participants from Michigan (16.8%) were intentionally oversampled to support regional research (Fig. 2). Most participants (50.7%) held a high school diploma, with a subset attaining higher education (18.8% with a bachelor’s degree, 5.1% with a master’s degree, and 1.6% with a PhD or higher). The racial and ethnic distribution highlights that the majority of participants identify as only Caucasian or White (68.7%), followed by only African American or Black (12.3%), and only Latina or Hispanic (7.5%) (Table 1). Discussion

In light of the concerning increase in syphilis cases among women of reproductive age and the corresponding rise in congenital syphilis, this study explored the relationship between health literacy and syphilis knowledge, finding a strong and statistically significant association (p < 0.001). These findings align with recent national epidemiological analyses that documented sharp increases in syphilis incidence between 2018 and 2022, particularly among Black and Indigenous women, and identified widening regional disparities across the United States [3]. Strengthening health literacy capacities may therefore represent an essential component of broader efforts to reduce syphilis transmission and improve maternal and infant outcomes, particularly in populations disproportionately affected by these trends. Previous studies have shown that higher health literacy is associated with greater engagement in protective sexual health behaviors and lower prevalence of sexually transmitted infections [33, 34], supporting the premise that literacy-sensitive public health strategies could enhance prevention and care engagement [25].

Our analysis highlights the crucial role of health literacy in understanding medical information about syphilis. The observed association between health literacy and syphilis knowledge (p < 0.001) provides evidence to bolster the contention that programs supporting women of childbearing age with limited health literacy could also improve their understanding of medical information about syphilis transmission and prevention. A recent scoping review examining sexual and reproductive health literacy among college students found that sexual and reproductive health literacy influenced knowledge related to general sexual and reproductive health [10]. Analysis of three studies explored in the scoping review focused on sexual and reproductive health literacy and knowledge found that age, religion, education, and country of origin impacted the relationship. In our study, among the variables we assessed, race and age impacted the relationship between health literacy and syphilis knowledge, as the relationship between age, race/ethnicity, syphilis knowledge, and health literacy was statistically significant in the multivariable model (p < 0.001). This contradiction may be because our study included individuals from various educational and occupational backgrounds, as opposed to college students. Indeed, the scoping review highlighted that the year in college and their degree program impacted the relationship. In our study, the proportion of individuals demonstrating adequate health literacy increased with age, suggesting that younger populations may particularly benefit from initiatives designed to strengthen health literacy capacities and syphilis knowledge (Table 2). This result is similar to that of a recently published national study, which found that more than 60% of U.S. adults demonstrated inadequate health literacy. Moreover, they found that health literacy improved with age, with individuals over 65 having the highest health literacy [35]. This trend was also seen in the scoping review of college students, with those over 20 years of age having greater adequate health literacy compared to younger college students [10]. These findings underscore the need for health promotion strategies for women that prioritize younger populations and address health literacy and syphilis prevention through community-centered, strengths-based approaches across the lifespan.

In examining other contributing factors, race/ethnicity was associated with health literacy in this study, with higher proportions of white women reporting adequate health literacy. These findings should be interpreted with caution, given the broad, self-reported categories used and the potential influence of unmeasured structural and social factors. While we asked about race/ethnicity, we did not account for birthplace and country of origin; this may mask within-group heterogeneity and fail to capture the complex social and structural factors that shape health literacy. A recent paper published since the onset of this study shows syphilis rates vary in the US by geographical region, with rates highest in the southern states and South Dakota [3]. This difference may be due to a host of factors, including access to education about STIs. Indeed, sexual and STI education and access to preventative services varied greatly in the United States [36, 37]. Additionally, some racial/ethnic groups were represented by small numbers, which limited the precision of estimates and the ability to make meaningful comparisons across groups. In this study, the relationship between race, health literacy, and syphilis knowledge is driven by those who identify as white. Our findings correlate with previous studies showing a relationship between health literacy and race, with ethnic and racial minorities having less health literacy [38].

The study shows that, among women of reproductive age, limited health literacy is strongly linked to lower syphilis knowledge, underscoring the value of asset-based, community-centered education that builds on women’s existing strengths and knowledge. A recent study that focused on engaging individuals with low health literacy in a community-based STI prevention program reported that participants found it both useful and engaging [25]. Previous research has highlighted the necessity for enhanced public health education. Moreover, low or inadequate health literacy has been linked to poor prevention and treatment outcomes in STIs like HIV [39]. While various factors can contribute to STI prevention and control, public health strategies that focus on reducing inadequate health literacy could support women’s ability to read and understand health information about STIs. Our study emphasizes the need for further research to identify effective educational measures, such as community outreach and engagement, that enhance literacy and understanding of medical terminology related to sexual health. Due to the relationship found between health literacy and knowledge of syphilis, and the risk of congenital syphilis transmission, we suggest targeting health literacy education specifically for women. Additionally, more research is necessary to explore the relationship between syphilis prevention and community members with limited health literacy.

Limitations

This national sample provides important insights into the relationship between health literacy and syphilis knowledge among U.S. women of reproductive age. However, some limitations should be taken into consideration. The sample overrepresented Caucasian or White participants and Michigan residents, which may limit the generalizability of findings to the broader U.S. population. While participants were drawn from 48 states, the distribution did not accurately reflect the current U.S. population density, which may have influenced regional health literacy patterns and healthcare access (Fig. 2).

Additionally, the cross-sectional design prevents us from drawing conclusions about causality between health literacy and knowledge of syphilis. Although validated instruments were used, the health literacy tool focused primarily on functional literacy, and the syphilis knowledge instrument, developed for an international population, may not fully capture U.S.-specific cultural understanding. Future research should prioritize the development and validation of syphilis knowledge instruments specifically tailored to the cultural and health communication contexts of diverse U.S. populations. Online recruitment through Centiment may also have introduced self-selection bias.

Future research should prioritize greater geographic and cultural diversity. Additionally, it is important to examine factors like the differences between rural and urban living, socioeconomic status, stigma, and the intersections of race, ethnicity, and age. Future analysis of this survey data set could include a comparison of the relationship between HL and SK in rural versus urban areas, categorizing participants’ self-reported zip codes using Rural-Urban Commuting Area Codes (RUCA) provided by the USDA. By broadening our focus in these areas, we can create more effective public health interventions that directly address the disparities in syphilis prevention.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of addressing inadequate health literacy to enhance syphilis knowledge among women of reproductive age. Although education level was not a significant mediator of the relationship between health literacy and syphilis knowledge, and age was not significant for syphilis knowledge, race showed some significant associations. However, the strong association between health literacy and syphilis knowledge persisted after adjusting for race, age, and education. The strong association observed between limited health literacy and lower syphilis knowledge underscores the need for targeted, literacy-sensitive educational interventions. Future research should explore social, cultural, and systemic determinants to inform the development of more effective, tailored public health strategies. By integrating health literacy into syphilis prevention efforts, public health initiatives can more effectively address persistent disparities and improve maternal and child health outcomes.
