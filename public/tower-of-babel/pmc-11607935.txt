Perspectives of people with schizophrenia on clinical outcome scales and patient-reported outcome measures: a qualitative study
Authors: Utako Sawada, Asami Matsunaga, Ayano Taneda, Natsu Sasaki, Sosei Yamaguchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOver the past half-century, numerous scales have been designed to quantify outcomes in people with severe mental health disorders. However, little is known about the views of people diagnosed with schizophrenia on individual outcome scales, particularly outside of European countries.AimWe conducted a qualitative study to examine the perspectives of people with schizophrenia on multiple scales in Japan.MethodsEleven participants took part in focus group interviews. Five had extensive experience as mental health peer supporters; the others had no such experience. To address potential power imbalances and facilitate dynamic discourse, participants were intentionally divided into two groups on the basis of peer support experience, and two simultaneous two-hour focus group interviews were conducted in separate rooms. The participants reviewed 12 clinical and patient-reported outcome measures and discussed their views on each measure. Interview data for each group were combined prior to analysis and qualitatively analyzed by four researchers using a thematic analysis approach.ResultsThe average age of the participants was 42.7 years (SD = 8.3), and six were male. On average, the participants had been living with schizophrenia for 22.2 years (SD = 11.1). After analyzing the interview data, the following five themes were identified, each containing two to seven subthemes: 1) validity and format of the scale construct, 2) factors affecting the accuracy of responses, 3) plain language and familiar words in Japanese culture, 4) psychological impact on the respondents, and 5) containing important items in everyday life.ConclusionThe participants provided both favorable and unfavorable feedback on each scale. When implementing research utilizing clinical outcome scales and patient-reported outcome measures, researchers should exercise caution considering the potential emotional impact on respondents. Furthermore, scale development should take into account the cultural background and psychological burden experienced by the respondents.

Introduction

Outcome evaluation in severe mental illness, including schizophrenia, is complex. Consequently, it has traditionally relied on symptoms, functioning, and subjective evaluation. In psychiatry, diverse scales have been developed to assess recovery in people with schizophrenia. Over the past two decades, a global movement emphasizing personal recovery has gained traction among people with schizophrenia and clinicians. The recovery movement has also influenced clinical research and its outcomes [1]. Consequently, patient-reported outcome measures gaining increasing importance in clinical research [2, 3], adding complexity for researchers in selecting appropriate outcome measures.

While the term “recovery” has multiple meanings, there are two main aspects: clinical recovery and personal recovery [4]. Clinical recovery primarily focuses on enhancements in symptomatology and functioning, for example, evaluated through scales like the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) [5, 6] and Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) [7]. Notably, a recent core outcome study emphasized that people diagnosed with schizophrenia often prioritize symptom reduction as a pivotal treatment objective [8]. Personal recovery denotes a unique and individualized process focused on achieving a meaningful life [9, 10]. The CHIME framework delineates five indispensable elements for personal recovery: Connectedness, Hope and Optimism, Identity, Meaning, and Empowerment [9]. While several scales have been developed to assess personal recovery, some scales also capture subjective perspectives related to personal recovery [11, 12] such as quality of life, empowerment, self-stigma, and direct quantification of the degree of personal recovery. Reviews emphasize the incorporation of patient-reported outcome measures in mental health research, enabling the evaluation of personal recovery alongside clinical outcome measures [1, 13]. To summarize, previous studies in the mental health field have generated many scales that enable the measurement of diverse dimensions and provide valuable insights into the concept of "recovery."

The proliferation of numerous scales in mental health research has posed a novel challenge in selecting appropriate outcome measures. One potential approach to address this challenge involves people with schizophrenia in the process of selecting appropriate assessment scales. The involvement of people with schizophrenia in mental health research has become increasingly important [14–16]. Their active participation significantly contributes to generating valuable study findings by aiding in selecting relevant themes, outcome measures, and target populations based on their unique perspectives [17–19]. Notably, patients’ viewpoints hold particular significance when considering the choice of outcome measures [20]. Consequently, it becomes imperative for researchers to engage in collaborative endeavors with people diagnosed with schizophrenia and incorporate both clinical outcome measures and patient-reported outcome measures.

Despite acknowledging the importance of involving people with schizophrenia in selecting outcome measures, existing knowledge remains limited. Even when researchers collaborate with people with schizophrenia to determine the most appropriate scale, a comprehensive understanding of their nuanced ideas regarding each scale may remain elusive to researchers. Indeed, patient-reported outcome measures need to carefully reflect patients’ perspectives in the selection process due to insufficient involvement in developing these scales [21]. For instance, a study conducted in the United Kingdom highlighted the preference of people with mental health conditions for patient-reported outcome measures over scales rated by staff members [22]. The participants in that study voiced concerns regarding the quality of life scale, which is widely used but appeared to fall short in capturing the viewpoints and experiences of people with severe mental illness [22]. The implications derived from that study hold particular significance in the context of scale development and selection within the United Kingdom. Given the varying perceptions of recovery across different countries [23], it becomes essential to investigate how people outside of the United Kingdom who have severe mental illnesses, such as schizophrenia, perceive existing outcome scales. However, few studies have assessed how people with schizophrenia perceive existing outcome scales elsewhere. Furthermore, previous studies have highlighted culture-dependent findings, such as the significance of communication with the familiar as a facilitator of recovery, which appears unique to Japan [23]. Hence, it becomes crucial to consider the cultural background of people with schizophrenia when exploring their perspectives on scales, indicating the need for further research in this domain.

To address the evidence gap, we conducted a focus group interview and qualitative analysis that aimed to identify the views of people with schizophrenia on clinical outcome scales and patient-reported outcome scales in Japan. Of note, the primary objective of this investigation was not to solicit or assess preferences for existing measures, but rather to delve into the thoughts and emotions of people with schizophrenia when they come across outcome scales.

Methods

Subjects and procedures

The research protocol and interview methods were developed through discussion with two peer supporters. A focus group interview was conducted with people diagnosed with schizophrenia to explore their perspectives on the scales. A focus group interview was deemed appropriate for this study due to the potential for group dynamics to provide diverse perspectives and insights on the scales through discussions among participants. Eligibility criteria included a diagnosis of schizophrenia, age 20 years or above, proficiency in the Japanese language, and availability to attend the interview session. Participants were recruited via the e-mail newsletter of the Community Mental Health & Welfare Bonding Organization, a non-profit organization that advocates for benefits for people with mental health conditions and disseminates mental health information and useful services. In addition, the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry's website was utilized for recruitment purposes. Participants were provided with comprehensive information regarding the study objectives, methods, voluntary nature of their involvement, right to withdraw from this study, and other ethical considerations. Signed consent forms were obtained from each participant after they had fully understood and agreed to the terms. The interview took place in November 2016 at National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry in Tokyo, Japan. We reported the methodology based on the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist [24]. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (No. A2016-066).

List of outcome measures

We utilized the 12 outcome scales in this study (Table 1). The scales were selected based on one or more of the following criteria: 1) frequent use by members of the authors’ departments in their research, 2) involvement of the authors’ departments in the scale development process, or 3) preference expressed by people with schizophrenia working as peer supporters in our center. In addition, since this study incorporated a broader perspective on recovery, we included scales related to several meanings of recovery from mental illness, including two scales related to personal meaning of recovery. Among the 12 scales, 3 scales were observer -rated clinical outcome measures, including 1 symptom measure (PANSS) [5, 6] and 2 function measures (GAF) [7] and the Life Assessment Scale for the Mentally (LASMI) [25]). The other nine scales were patient-reported outcome measures. Two were recovery-related scales (Japanese version of the Questionnaire about the Process of Recovery (QPR-J) [26–28] and INSPIRE [29, 30]). The remaining seven scales included the domains for empowerment (Japanese version of Boston University Empowerment Scale (BUES)) [31–33], quality of life (QOL)/well-being (WHO-5) [34, 35], social support (Japanese version of the Social Support Questionnaire — Short Form (SSQ-6)) [36, 37], subjective value (Japanese version of the Personal Values Questionnaire II (PVQ-II)) [38, 39], relationships between patients and clinicians (Japanese version of the Scale to Assess Therapeutic Relationships (STAR-J)) [40, 41], self-stigma (Japanese version of the Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness (ISMI) scale) [42, 43], and adherence (Medication Adherence Scale) [44]. Only the LASMI and Medication Adherence Scale were developed in Japan. The other scales originated from Western countries. Interviews and data collection

Before the focus group interviews, participants were provided with printed materials containing 12 scales and interview guide via e-mail one week before the focus group interviews. This preparation was intended to reduce the psychological and physical burden on the day of the interview and to encourage the participants to express many opinions in the limited interview time. Preliminary research showed that five participants had extensive experience as peer supporters within the mental health system, while others had no such experience. It was expected that participants who had experience with peer support would be more likely to talk about their experiences and opinions than those who had not. Therefore, to mitigate potential power imbalances among participants and to facilitate more active discussions, participants were divided into two groups of five participants with experience in peer support and six participants without experience, and focus group interviews were conducted simultaneously in separate rooms. The interviews were facilitated by four researchers (US, AM, SY, and a research collaborator), including one author who had received training in qualitative methods. The professional backgrounds of the interviewers included psychiatric nursing (AM, US, and the research collaborator) and social work (SY). AM and US hold Master of Health Sciences degrees, the research collaborator has a Bachelor of Nursing degree, and SY has a Doctor of Social Work degree. The facilitator pairs consisted of SY (male) and US (female), as well as AM (female) paired with the (male) research collaborator. The facilitators were assigned to separate groups based on their work experience in mental health welfare, research background, interview experience, and gender to foster meaningful discussions.

Each focus group interview took place in a designated room at the interview site. While some facilitators and participants had been previously acquainted, there were no professional or financial relationships among them. The interviews followed a semi-structured format with an interview guide to ensure consistency and reliability in the content of the questions posed to each group. The interview guide was the following: "These scales are commonly used in Japanese mental health service research. Considering your personal recovery, life experiences, perspectives, and symptoms, what are your thoughts on these scales?" During the interviews, the participants read the 12 outcome scales, discussed how they felt about the scales, and gave feedback on the scales based on their views of clinical and personal recovery. The facilitators ensured a psychologically safe atmosphere where participants could freely express their opinions about each scale to collect diverse opinions on each scale from participants with varying perspectives. In this study, the participants were simply asked for their perspectives on scales. This study did not specifically ask the participants about interview skills that can reduce the psychological impact or distress experienced by individuals with schizophrenia when undergoing assessments or interviews by professionals using clinical measures such as the PANSS [5, 6] .

The focus group interviews, lasting 2 hours with a 10-min break in the middle, were recorded using a voice recorder to ensure accurate documentation. Furthermore, socio-demographic data, including age at onset of schizophrenia and number of hospitalizations in the previous 12 months, were collected through a brief questionnaire.

Analysis

The four authors (US, AM, AT, and SY) conducted the analysis; two had received training in qualitative research. In particular, the professional backgrounds of SY and AM, coupled with their profound insights into stigma reduction and recovery from mental illness, increased the likelihood that they would be able to deeply understand and contribute to a better understanding and more appropriate analysis of the statements of people with schizophrenia. In this study, we analyzed the data with reference to qualitative analysis based on thematic analysis [45] within the following steps: 1. Familiarization with the data, 2. Generating initial codes, 3. Combining coding into themes, 4. Reviewing themes, 5. Defining and naming themes, and 6. Producing the report. First, all data were transcribed verbatim. The aim of this study was not to compare disagreements between two groups. Thus, data from the two focus group interviews were merged by scale. The first author (US) became familiarized with the data by reading through it repeatedly. Next, the first author generated initial ideas about themes. All four authors (US, AM, AT, and SY) read the transcripts and discussed the initial coding. For consistency, the first author (US) coded all interviews. Next, the other authors collectively carefully checked the coding data, discussed the codes, and made amendments as necessary. The four authors generated themes from the initially extracted codes and reviewed the codes, themes, subthemes, and definitions together. Repeating this process continuously, some codes were incorporated into other themes or new themes were generated. When we found any inconsistencies, we carefully re-read the transcript, re-corded if necessary, and grouped codes into the appropriate theme. Finally, clear definitions and names were generated for each theme. The final themes and their definitions were shared with the participants for their review and agreement. Data were managed using MAXQDA (Verbi GmbH).

Results

Participant characteristics

Fourteen eligible participants reached out to the research team. However, three could not participate in the interview due to work schedule conflicts or physical health issues. As a result, 11 participants took part in the focus group interviews after receiving an explanation of the ethics issues. During the interview process, none of the participants complained of feeling unwell or withdrew from the interview. In addition, all participants responded to questions and participated in the discussion. The average age of the participants was 42.7 years (SD = 8.3), and six were male. On average, the participants had been living with schizophrenia for 22.2 years (SD = 11.1). Two participants had experienced psychiatric hospitalizations within the past year. Five participants had previous experience working as peer support workers (Table 2). Theme A: validity and format of the scale construct

This theme concerned scale construct validity, defined as whether the content and structure of the scale are valid for measuring the recovery of people with schizophrenia in Japan. This theme consists of five subthemes, such as exploring the underlying reasons and context that have led to the present condition, range of response options, and duration of the measurement period. Some participants expected to be able to write about the background and reasons for their choices, as well as their opinions and values, rather than simply answering the scale. In addition, participants considered the QOL measure to inadequately cover all the aspects of QOL/well-being due to a small number of questions. They were also concerned about their hesitation to choose with confidence the extreme option in the patient-reported measures (e.g., 1 or 5 on a Likert scale).

Theme B: factors affecting the accuracy of responses

The second theme concerns the accuracy of responses. The theme was defined as factors affecting the accuracy of responses, such as the content of the question and the physical and psychological conditions at the time of the response. This theme consisted of seven subthemes. Three subthemes were related to vague question wording, criteria, and response methods. The participants perceived the question item "Whom can you really count on to be dependable when you need help? How many are there, and to what extent are you satisfied?" within the social support scale as abstract. They felt that the definition of trust in individuals varies significantly among people and it is difficult to quantify the number of individuals involved. Some expressed concern about the questions that rated "degree of feeling," "standard," and "normal," recognizing that as these perceptions varied from person to person. The other three subthemes were associated with respondent factors. Participants expressed concerns that being asked about past experiences could depend on the time period within which memories can be retrieved and the respondent’s condition at the time of response. Moreover, participants feared that their responses might be biased in a manner that aligns with the researcher’s or physician’s expectations. The remaining subtheme was fatigue associated with the number of questions and free-text items.

Discussion

This study explored the perspectives of people with schizophrenia on commonly used scales in Japanese research through interviews and qualitative analysis. While the goal of this study was not to identify the best scale, we carefully analyzed the participants’ perspectives on the scales and discovered a mixture of positive and negative feelings toward each scale. Specifically, the analysis revealed three themes related to scale structure, language use, and factors influencing response accuracy. In addition, two themes emerged regarding the psychological impact of scale content on respondents and the divergence in perspectives regarding the scales between people with schizophrenia and service providers.

The present study’s findings align with previous studies regarding the fundamental structure and language used in scales. For instance, participants in this study expressed concerns that scales with a small number of items might not adequately cover crucial content for assessing the desired outcomes. Similarly, the previous study highlighted that scales that are overly short and simplistic might not effectively capture the desired outcomes [22, 46]. The participants in our study also recognized that rating criteria, event definitions, and individual conditions influence responses to the scales. These limitations of staff-rated and patient-reported measures have often been criticized in psychiatry, particularly when no objective biological markers are available. Therefore, it is important for researchers to use scales while acknowledging their imperfections.

Both our study and a previous study indicated that including a space for additional comments in the scales would be beneficial [22]. This is particularly significant when assessing personal recovery, as it is a highly individualized process [10, 47]. However, too many spaces for free-text descriptions might burden respondents and affect the accuracy of their responses. In other words, the results suggest the need for a well-balanced scale structure. The analysis also found that imported scales require caution, as such scales may use unfamiliar concepts and Japanese words. As scale development guidelines point out [48–50], a careful translation process for imported scales and an explanation of key concepts should be addressed in future scale development research.

Regarding the themes of impact on respondents and subjective recovery, participants experienced a combination of positive and negative emotions. They recognized the practicality of specific scales as tools for self-monitoring through their responses. This might be a unique finding in the current study and a benefit of using these scales. However, while acknowledging the usefulness of certain scales for self-reflection, participants expressed concerns about the presence of discriminatory terms in these scales, such as the self-stigma scale. Although each scale has been developed to measure an outcome of interest, stigmatizing expressions might unintentionally trigger feelings of internalized stigma and worse mental health [22]. Moreover, participants expressed that they felt some scale items conveyed expectations about what a person should be, which are closely linked to notions of normality and social norms. Within the context of personal recovery, the concept of normality and its criteria have been extensively discussed, recognizing that it primarily derives from social norms rather than individual values [10, 51]. Furthermore, a previous study conducted with families of children experiencing severe mental illness showed that "normal" within professional social services is a forced value and professionals in social services need to consider how to build communities that embrace diversity [52]. Specifically, people with severe mental illness in Japan value rebuilding their own identities and minimizing the influence of social norms during their personal recovery process [23]. This emphasizes the alignment between the concerns raised by participants in the current study regarding the measurement of "normal" on the scale and previous research findings.

In addition, participants emphasized that not all scales included in the study are universally regarded as essential for evaluating the lived experiences of people with schizophrenia. Specifically, scales like the GAF [7] and PANSS [5, 6], which assess functioning and symptomatology, were critiqued for their traditional structure, which predominantly reflects a professional perspective and tendency to overlook the viewpoints of people with schizophrenia. This dichotomy is consistent with a previous study that pointed out the discordance between people with schizophrenia and professionals in using the GAF scale, criticizing its singular score approach [20]. This disparity might stem from differing perspectives on the concept of recovery. While professionals might prioritize clinical parameters in the recovery process, people with schizophrenia often base their definition of recovery on their daily experiences and subjective emotions [47]. On the other hand, one participant recognized that psychiatrists frequently use functional and symptom assessment scales for medical monitoring. These findings demonstrate the participants’ understanding of the divergent perspectives on these scales between professionals and people with schizophrenia and their comprehension of the rationale behind the use of functional and symptom assessment scales.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study lies in its sampling methodology. The research incorporated participants both with and without experiences of working as peer supporters. Moreover, participant recruitment was conducted through a network of a non-profit community-based mental health organization rather than mere convenience sampling. This approach ensured a diverse range of insights into the perceptions of individuals with schizophrenia regarding relevant scales. However, the current study has some limitations. First, our sample consisted solely of people diagnosed with schizophrenia. people with other psychiatric diagnoses might hold different values regarding each scale. In addition, we did not assess the participants' secondary diagnoses or symptom severity when they were participating in the study. While their condition might have influenced the results, this remains a future challenge. Second, the scales chosen for this study were based on our frequent use of them. If a broader range of scales were considered in future research, the results might vary. Third, we used only two scales related to personal recovery. Therefore, the results of this study might not fully reflect the participants' view of their personal recovery. Fourth, this study did not directly inquire about interview skills that can reduce the psychological impact on participants when being assessed or interviewed by professionals using clinical measures such as the PANSS. Nevertheless, the results suggest that the use of certain clinical scales might cause psychological distress among participants. The clinical scales used in this study are internationally standardized tools that have been widely adapted in clinical research to assess an individual’s condition. Future studies should explore participants' perspectives on less invasive assessments by professionals using such scales.

It is crucial to note that the scales examined in this study represent only a small portion of those used in the field of mental health care. Therefore, the current study’s findings might not be applicable to all scales. Nevertheless, when researchers select scales for mental health research, careful consideration should be given to how respondents perceive their content. This aspect of consideration might be even more crucial for indicating the quality of the scales.

Conclusion

This study aimed to explore the perspectives of people diagnosed with schizophrenia concerning scales commonly utilized in community mental health research. The participants provided both favorable and unfavorable feedback regarding each scale. Researchers should exercise caution when selecting scales, considering the potential emotional impact on respondents. Future studies focusing on scale development should consider the validity of the scale construct, familiar language in each culture, factors affecting the accuracy of responses, psychological burden on respondents, and presents of items that are important for people with schizophrenia.
