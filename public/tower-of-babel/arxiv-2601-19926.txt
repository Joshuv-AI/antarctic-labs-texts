The Grammar of Transformers: A Systematic Review of Interpretability Research on Syntactic Knowledge in Language Models
Nora Graichen, Iria de-Dios-Flores, Gemma Boleda, Universitat Pompeu Fabra, ICREA*Equal senior authorship. nora.graichen@upf.edu

Abstract
We present a systematic review of 337 articles evaluating the syntactic abilities of Transformer-based language models, reporting on 1,015 model results from a range of syntactic phenomena and interpretability methods. Our analysis shows that the state of the art presents a healthy variety of methods and data, but an over-focus on a single language (English), a single model (BERT), and phenomena that are easy to get at (like part of speech and agreement). Results also suggest that TLMs capture these form-oriented phenomena well, but show more variable and weaker performance on phenomena at the syntax–semantics interface, like binding or filler-gap dependencies. We provide recommendations for future work, in particular reporting complete data, better aligning theoretical constructs and methods across studies, increasing the use of mechanistic methods, and broadening the empirical scope regarding languages and linguistic phenomena.

Introduction

Transformer-based language models (
TLMs
) have become the engine behind a global shift in AI, producing fluent text across diverse languages. A growing body of research is examining how modern LMs acquire, represent, and deploy linguistic knowledge
249
;
358
;
34
;
137
; however, their grasp of natural language syntax remains only partially understood
4
.
The present article aims at charting the state of the art regarding syntactic knowledge in TLMs. Given the sizable literature on the topic, and the fact that previous reviews are narrative (see below), we decided to instead produce a
systematic review
of the literature.

A systematic review
(
222
)
gathers and synthesizes relevant studies, providing a transparent content-to-conclusion mapping. Unlike narrative reviews, to reduce bias, it follows explicit procedures for article search, inclusion, and processing; unlike meta-analyses or meta-reviews, it does not build a statistical model with the different variables, but still synthesizes results quantitatively (and may do so qualitatively when outcome measures are not directly comparable).

In our review, we have identified 337 relevant articles, spanning different languages, evaluation methods, datasets, and syntactic phenomena, totalling 1,015 individual results.
We created a database of the reviewed studies, annotated with relevant methodological and empirical characteristics, which will be made publicly available alongside the analysis code upon acceptance. In the remainder of the paper, we analyse the database to provide evidence-based conclusions and identify research gaps.

Our work builds on six previous reviews on similar topics:
165
;
169
;
138
;
40
;
199
;
173
.
These reviews found that modern LMs, mainly Transformer-based architectures, acquire rich syntactic representations early during pretraining
(
40
)
,
localize much of this information in their middle layers, and encode at least some syntactic relations via specialized attention heads and groups of neurons
(
173

.
The reviews also point out limitations, including reliance on surface-level heuristics, lexical sensitivity, and challenges with rare constructions and subtle distinctions
(

;
169

.
While valuable, these prior reviews are largely narrative and interpretive. We aim at providing a more descriptive, quantitative, and transparent mapping between the available evidence and the conclusions.

The overarching research question guiding this review is:
What do TLMs learn about syntax from the language modeling task?

This RQ is motivated, on the one hand, by the fact that Transformers are the main architecture as of 2025; and, on the other hand, by the open question, common to interpretability research on the area, to what extent do computational models learn syntax just from a prediction task, without explicit instruction about syntax.
We pursue this question through the following sub-questions.

•

RQ1
: What is the scope and nature of the current empirical evidence on syntactic knowledge in TLMs, and which linguistic phenomena are most consistently investigated? We include in our review traditional syntactic phenomena such as agreement and syntactic function, morphosyntactic phenomena (e.g. part of speech), and phenomena at the syntax-semantics interface such as binding or negative polarity. This is because comprehensive understanding of the syntactic capabilities of LMs requires covering these three broad areas.

•

RQ2
: Which interpretability methods are most widely used, and what are their strengths and limitations for making claims about syntactic knowledge?

•

RQ3
: What can we confidently state regarding syntactic knowledge in TLMs?

3.2
RQ2: Methods

We coded for the three main experimental paradigms that are currently used to study syntactic knowledge in TLMs: probing, behavioral, and mechanistic interpretability methods
199
. Behavioral methods assess models based on their input–output behavior at the final layer, without inspecting internal representations. This includes both analysing generated outputs and comparing model-assigned probabilities for competing alternatives in controlled settings, such as minimal pairs
170
;
153
. The latter paradigm, known as “targeted syntactic evaluation” and highly influenced by the psycholinguistic tradition, treats the higher probability of a correct form as evidence that the TLM has mastered a given syntactic construct.
For instance, example
3.2
shows a filler-gap dependency stimulus from the BLiMP dataset
309
.
A model that masters filler-gap constructions is expected to assign higher probability to
what
than to
that
for this stimulus.

many waiters find

Probing methods, instead, aim to test what information is encoded in internal representations, most often by training auxiliary classifiers on activations from specific layers
101
;
300
. On the one hand, probing tasks offer scalability and clear feature detection, but typically lack causal insight
29
;
318
. On the other hand, behavioral methods provide controlled, human-like evaluation of model generalization, without however any insight into internal mechanisms.

This is where mechanistic interpretability methods come in: these methods, very varied in nature, inspect and intervene on internal components (e.g., layers, neurons, circuits, or training dynamics) to causally link representations to behavior
318
.
While mechanistic methods may (and often do) incorporate behavioral or probing elements, they go beyond correlation by including causal interventions, too. Note that, consequently, in the database we categorized studies using exclusively behavioral or probing methods as such, while those investigating internal dynamics, even if including behavioral or probing tests as well, were coded as mechanistic.

3.3
RQ3: Knowledge status - BLiMP

We originally set out to map what is known about how TLMs process syntax on the basis of the whole database; however, this goal proved unrealistic, given the daunting conceptual and methodological variation across studies (see Section
4
for discussion).
Responding to these difficulties, and in order to keep our review objective and quantitative, we chose to focus on the results obtained with the BLiMP benchmark, which allows for systematic comparisons, and for which we have enough datapoints.
BLiMP is designed for behavioral evaluation of models through minimal pair comparison

Perhaps the most salient feature of these results is the large within-phenomenon variance across TLMs. We find that this variance is mostly driven by how good each model is at syntax generally (as measured by its overall BLiMP score): TLM rankings are highly consistent across phenomena, shown quantitatively in App.

Notably, the best TLM is at or above human performance for 7 out of the 11 categories.

Despite the large variance, the figure also clearly suggests that TLMs perform better on formal syntactic relations, such as agreement (anaphor, determiner-noun, and subject-verb agreement all have medians over 85%), than on phenomena at the syntax-semantics interface (binding, argument structure, NPI licensing, control/raising, quantifiers and island effects all have medians below 75%).
In particular, quantifiers and island effects exhibit the poorest overall model performance.

However, there is one exception to the observed pattern: ellipsis is also a phenomenon at the syntax-semantics interface, and models excel at it, with several models matching or outperforming humans.
As noted by
309
, however, this BLiMP category covers only special cases of noun phrase ellipsis, whose grammatical violation we suspect might be easier to detect than others.
Thus, the exception could be due to the particular characteristics of BLiMP rather than ellipsis as a phenomenon being easy for models.

More generally, note that BLiMP’s coverage of syntactic phenomena is not comprehensive, even for English; despite its breadth, it contains only some selected categories and, for each category, it includes only cases that can be straightforwardly transformed into minimal pairs.
Furthermore, as discussed by
138
, BLiMP aggregates diverse phenomena into composite scores with equal weighting, thereby masking important differences in difficulty and relevance.
Qualitative analysis of model behavior is difficult, and no single dataset can be expected to be definitive. It is thus important to interpret results in the light of dataset characteristics.

Based on this analysis, thus, we tentatively conclude that formal syntax including morphosyntax is easier for models than the syntax-semantics interface, and look forward to future analyses of our database expanding on ours.

4
Discussion & Conclusion

Our analyses reveal a rich but heterogeneous landscape in the state of the art on syntactic knowledge in TLMs.
The field boasts a wealth of studies (over 300) encompassing a varied and balanced set of methods (behavioral, mechanistic, probing-based) and data (naturalistic and controlled), yielding over 1,000 individual results regarding syntactic knowledge in TLMs.
We also find that relevant formal syntactic phenomena such as agreement, dependencies, constituency, hierarchical structure, word order, and morphosyntactic properties such as part of speech have been well studied, with over 50 articles on each of these topics in our database.
The literature also reveals a substantial and successful transfer of theoretical and methodological insights from psycholinguistics into LLM interpretability research. This is a particularly encouraging development, as it enables more principled comparisons between human and model behavior and strengthens the connection to linguistic theory.

However, on the negative side, we find an over-study of a single language (69% of the articles are on English only, and 91% include English) and a few models (30% of the results concern BERT; including BERT and its variants raises the total to 58%),
which severely limits the robustness and generality of the conclusions.
We also find that the syntax-semantics interface has not received enough attention to date: phenomena such as binding, coreference, negation, ellipsis, or filler-gap dependencies have been much less studied than formal syntactic phenomena.
This is especially relevant given that, based on the partial data reviewed above, TLMs seem to perform worse on phenomena at the syntax-semantics interface than on formal syntactic phenomena connected to syntactic function and structure.

Based on these results and our experience when building and analysing the database, we propose the following
recommendations
for future research on the topic.
Starting with the basics, studies should
report complete data
.
This is good practice in general, but especially in interpretability.
AI models are a hard nut to crack, and we cannot expect single studies or even labs to produce all the relevant data; thus, as is done in fields like medicine, we’ll need to start relying more and more on systematic reviews like our own, meta-analyses, and meta-reviews. For that, it is necessary to access the relevant variables, which need to be reported in the individual articles.
Instead, when populating the database, we found that a lot of the relevant parameters were missing or only partially reported.

A case in point are the analysed models; often, articles report only incomplete model names (!), training details, and method descriptions.
Since results can vary as a function of model architecture, size, training data, tokenization method, training objective, and a host of other factors, it is crucial to report at least the specific model version tested. It is also important to include technical information when available, because models and the associated documentation often disappear with time (and because this way the information is easier to find; e.g. for us it was not generally feasible to look for information sources outside the articles).
Another illustrative case are benchmark results: for instance, as mentioned above, only 11 out of 25 articles in our database reported per-category results for BLiMP (not all of which complete). Reporting per-category results, if only in an appendix, is only a small effort for authors, and enables large-scale analyses like ours that can paint a more comprehensive picture.
As a last example, performance is heavily influenced by task framing (e.g., apparent syntactic failures in zero-shot settings may disappear in few-shot conditions) and by how the input is presented, such as through different prompting strategies.
Systematically documenting these factors helps better understand results and enables their replication and reproduction.

Second, we should collectively
standardize
investigations to a greater extent.
Most importantly, differences in how syntactic phenomena are defined create a fragmented evaluation map with limited comparability across findings—and even the very notion of “syntactic knowledge” can vary from article to article.
For instance,
300
place several of the phenomena we have considered here outside of syntax (e.g. negation in reasoning, and negative polarity item licensing in semantics).

At a methodological level, regarding experimental materials we think that the field should keep the current healthy variety of data; regarding analysis methods, that it should decisively
move towards causal approaches connecting behavior with internal mechanics
.
As discussed above, probing methods when used on their own suffer from a lack of causal insight. It is one thing to decode linguistic features, another to show that this linguistic knowledge is effectively used in predictions—and how
4
. As for behavioral methods on their own, strong performance does not necessarily imply underlying linguistic competence.
These methods thus need to be combined with experimental interventions that can get at the causal mechanisms.

Combining methods can also offer insight into many outstanding open questions: how syntax is represented and processed in TLMs
(
32
, e.g. whether TLMs implement near-symbolic mechanisms, as hypothesized in)
, when and how TLMs fall back on superficial statistical patterns
(
150
, e.g. performance on agreement, binding, or scope often degrades for rare or nonce lexical items)
, whether improvements with model scale reflect genuine generalization or massive memorization of specific instances
147
.

And, last but not least, the community should
broaden the empirical scope
of the studies to include a much more varied set of languages, a typological perspective, and a wider variety of phenomena at syntax-semantics interface.
To facilitate this and keep with the standardization effort, we recommend that new multi-lingual benchmarks be created; for instance,
120
recently released a multi-lingual BLiMP covering 101 languages (albeit only focusing on agreement).
We also recommend moving beyond the simple adaptation of English benchmarks, which is often limited to translation, as seen in efforts like IberoBench
(
28
)
.
This practice, related to the over-Englishness of the field, implies that non-English phenomena can remain untested even when languages exhibiting these phenomena are included in the analysis.
As an example of a “best of both worlds”, standardization plus room-for-variation approach, the Spanish variant of SyntaxGym
230
incorporates language-specific features like subjunctive mood and flexible word order besides English-compatible phenomena.

To sum up, while the current literature suggests that TLMs possess remarkable syntactic capabilities, achieving greater mechanistic insights as well as more robust and holistic conclusions will require greater methodological harmonization, more explicit theoretical commitments, more fine-grained evaluation practices, and a broader empirical landscape. We hope that our review and the database and annotation standard we release with it contributes to moving the field forward along this path.

Limitations

For feasibility, this study focuses on Transformer-based language mdoels, and our findings may not fully generalize to recurrent or other neural architectures.
Also for feasibility, our answer to RQ3 is based only on one benchmark (BLiMP) and one language (English). Future analyses can use the
findings
variable of our database to get at a broader understanding of the patterns.
Finally, our database is necessarily incomplete and evolving; the resource is intended to remain interactive and expandable as the field progresses. We see this work as a foundation that invites continued contributions and updates from the research community.
