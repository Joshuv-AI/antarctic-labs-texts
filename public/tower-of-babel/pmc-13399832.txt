Lower Extremity Sprains and Knee/Ankle Injury Predominate Among Pediatric Dancers
Authors: Katherine Koch, Philip M. Lee, Cameron Nishida, Sonya B. Levine, Kyle K. Obana, Jennifer M. Weiss, Jennifer King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine the most common body regions, diagnoses, and mechanisms of injury among pediatric dance‐related injuries in the United States.MethodsA descriptive epidemiologic analysis of the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023 identified emergency department presentations for primary dance‐associated injuries using the recreational activity code 3728. Data on demographics, body region, diagnosis, and mechanism of injury were collected. Patients were stratified by sex (male vs female) and age (children (3‐9) vs adolescents (10‐17)). Descriptive statistics and chi‐square tests were used, with significance set a P < .05.ResultsA total of 3600 (National Estimate = 93,062) cases were analyzed. The most commonly injured regions were knees (20.8%) and ankles (15.8%). The most common diagnoses were sprains/strains (31.4%) and fractures (12.3%). Common mechanisms included impacts with the floor (37.0%) and ankle rolling (13.5%). Compared with adolescent dancers, children sustained knee (26.2% vs 5.5%, P < .001) and ankle (17.5% vs 10.4%, P < .001) injuries, experience sprains/strains (35.5% vs 18.3%, P < .001), and were injured due to ankle rolling (14.4% vs 8.1%, P < .001) and noncontact movements (11.8% vs 2.3%, P < .001). Adolescent dancers more often sustained fractures (14.4% vs 11.3%, P = .010). Females were more likely to sustain knee (21.8% vs 15.3%, P < .001) and ankle (16.6% vs 11.7%, P = .012) injuries and experience sprains/strains (33.1% vs 22.2%, P < .001), whereas males frequently sustained head injuries (23.7% vs 11.5%, P < .001) and fractures (15.7% vs 11.7%, P = .019).ConclusionsKnees and ankles were the most commonly injured body regions, with sprains/strains and fractures representing the most frequent diagnoses. The most common mechanisms of injury included impact with the floor, ankle rolling, and noncontact movements. Females and younger children were significantly more likely to sustain knee and ankle injuries and experience sprains/strains, whereas males and adolescents more often sustained fractures.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, retrospective epidemiological study.

Dance has been used as a form of physical exercise, personal expression, education, and motor development, often having an underappreciated impact on society. 1 Bronner and Worthen report that as many as 10,400,000 athletes engage in some form of dance each year. 2 Moreover, the U.S. Census Bureau reported that in 2020, up to 37% of all girls and 27% of all boys (ages 6‐17) were enrolled in dance lessons. 3 There have been significant injury associations with pediatric patients who engage in dance, with Dadoo et al. finding a total of 489,119 children presenting to emergency departments (EDs) for dance‐associated injuries from 2000 to 2020. 4 Despite this, dance‐associated injuries have been often associated with underscreening and suboptimal health care delivery, attributed to dance being not as widely acknowledged as an athletic endeavor when compared with other sports. 5

Several studies have attempted to describe the injury trends of dance‐associated injuries. Vassallo et al. found that among children older than 10, lower extremity injuries, particularly to the ankles and knees, were the most common. 6 Similarly, Dadoo et al. found higher rates of lower extremity injuries among females and younger dancers, and noted that ballet and pointe work were particularly associated with lower extremity injury. 4 However, these studies are limited in scope. The study by Vassallo et al. relied on small, clinic‐based samples that are not representative of the broader pediatric population. Further, Dadoo et al. provided only age and sex stratification, without a deeper analysis of the mechanism of injury (MOI) that pediatric dancers in these subgroups may face.

As a result, the current literature lacks a comprehensive, population‐level analysis of dance‐related injuries further stratified by age, gender, and MOI, factors that may contribute to injury frequency as well as specific injury patterns. The purpose of this study was to determine the most common body regions, final diagnoses, and MOIs among all pediatric dance‐related injuries in the United States. We hypothesized that children would sustain more sprains/strains and adolescents would sustain more fractures.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Collection

This study was a retrospective descriptive epidemiologic analysis using data obtained from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) database to evaluate all dance‐associated injuries in pediatric populations that presented to EDs from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023. The years 2019 to 2023 were selected to ensure consistent reporting practices within the NEISS dataset and to provide an updated analysis reflecting the most current trends in pediatric dance‐related injuries. Inclusion criteria for our study included patients between the ages of 3 and 17 years old, who had an ED visit from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023 due to an injury that occurred while actively dancing. Patient narratives were excluded if they did not fit the above criteria, described nondance‐related injuries (e.g., injured while walking to dance practice) or if the injury was associated with another primary mechanism (e.g., initial injury while in gym class, exacerbated with dancing).

The NEISS dataset is publicly available and provided by the Consumer Product Safety Commission, incorporating patient data collected from over 5000 EDs and 100 admitting hospitals, that has been previously used to evaluate national injury trends in various sports. 7 , 8 Variables provided by the NEISS dataset include patient demographic information such as patient age, sex, race, as well as date of presentation, body region injured, and final diagnosis. Additionally, each patient case is provided with a 400‐character narrative, providing detailed history regarding the sequence of events leading up to injury onset. For our study the following variables were extracted: patient age, sex, race, ethnicity, date of ED visit, body region injured, final diagnosis, patient disposition, and the 400‐character narrative.

All dance‐associated injuries from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023 were selected from the NEISS database using the NEISS product code 3278, a code that is assigned when the ED coder determines that an injury occurred during dance participation. All identified patient records were exported into a spreadsheet containing all available variables, including age, sex, body region, diagnosis, disposition, and the 400‐character narrative. Patient narratives were independently reviewed by the first 3 authors to ensure that the injury occurred while actively dancing. Once eligibility was confirmed, the first 3 authors assigned only one MOIs to each narrative, which were categorized as fall/impact with the floor, ankle rolling, noncontact movements, pulling movements from another dancer, impact with terrain, impact with another dancer, overexertional, and dynamic movements (jumping, leaps, kicking). If narratives did not provide sufficient information to assign an MOI (i.e., patient was injured while dancing), they were assigned as “unspecified.”

MOIs were derived from the most common patterns observed in the NEISS patient narratives during the preliminary review. “Fall/Impact with the floor” referred to any contact with a level dance surface, including falls, except when the narrative described ankle rolling. “Impact with terrain” referred to contact with any nonfloor surface or object, including walls, equipment, props, furniture, or irregular surfaces. “Ankle rolling” was categorized if the narrative described the athlete “rolling,” “twisting,” or “turning” the ankle during a dance activity. “Noncontact” injuries were assigned by the narrative specifically identified a noncontact movement that did not meet criteria for any other mechanism category. “Overexertional” injuries were defined as those resulting from excess physical demand or fatigue as described in the narrative. “Dynamic movements” included skills such as jumps, leaps, and kicks, and were only assigned when the narrative explicitly identified that movement as the cause of injury. If there were disagreements on the interpretation of narratives, the senior author would be consulted.

Statistical Analysis

The IBM SPSS software (version 29.0; IBM, Armonk, NY) was used for all statistical analyses. The NEISS dataset assigns each hospital a statistical sample weight based on that institution's resource capacity, and National Estimates (NEs) were calculated by multiplying the associated statistical weight of each respective hospital by their raw data point. Linear regression models were used to analyze NE trends for body regions injured, final diagnoses, and MOIs. Additionally, Chi‐squared analyses and post hoc residual analyses were used to analyze categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

The primary outcome of this study was to characterize overall injury patterns among pediatric dancers, including body region injured, final diagnosis, and MOI. Overall trends were first evaluated as the primary analysis, including body region injured, final diagnosis, and MOI. Secondary analyses then stratified these outcomes by sex and age. Age was categorized as child (ages 3‐9) and adolescent (10‐17), consistent with the World Health Organization definition of adolescence beginning at age 10. 9 COVID‐19, which has been previously defined as from 2019 to 2021, analyses were descriptive only and were included to contextualize injury volume during pandemic‐related activity restrictions. 10

RESULTS

Patient Demographic Characteristics

There were a total of 3713 (NE = 96,087) patients with a dance‐related injury included in the NEISS dataset from 2019 to 2023. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 3600 (NE = 93,062, 97.0%) were analyzed (Figure 1), with 1536 (NE = 38,520, 41.2%) in the COVID group and 2195 (NE = 57,567, 58.8%) in the post‐COVID group. The mean age was 11.63 ± 4.093, with 2718 (NE = 71,081, 61.0%) in the child group and 1405 (NE = 35,495, 39.0%) in the adolescent age group. There were 3045 (NE = 78,925, 84.6%) females and 554 (NE = 14,081, 15.4%) male patients. The most common races were Caucasian (1316, NE = 34,386, 36.6%) and African American (976, NE = 18,603, 27.1%), and a majority of patients were not Hispanic (1908, NE = 46,592, 53.0%). Patient characteristics can be seen in Table 1. A small portion of all injuries were associated with alcohol/drug intoxication (9, NE = 266, 0.3%). Patients were discharged home (3498, NE = 91,103, 97.2%), admitted for further treatment (41, NE = 604, 1.1%), left against medical advice (AMA) (52, NE = 1131, 1.4%), transferred (6, NE = 194, 0.20%), or held for observation (3, NE = 29, 0.10%).

General Trends in Final Diagnoses, Body Locations, and MOIs

Overall, the most commonly injured body parts were knees (n = 749, NE = 19,302, 20.8%), ankles (n = 569, NE = 15,429, 15.8%), and the head (n = 481, NE = 12,539, 13.3%). The most common final diagnoses were sprains/strains (n = 1130, NE = 31,371, 31.4%), fractures (n = 442, NE = 9718, 12.3%), and contusions/abrasions (n = 288, NE = 7821, 8.0%). There were 892 (NE = 21,885, 24.8%) final diagnoses as other/not stated. The most common MOIs were impacts with the floor (n = 1311, NE = 34,466, 37.0%), ankle rolling (n = 474, NE = 12,583, 13.5%), and noncontact movements (n = 345, NE = 9720, 10.4%). There were 898 (NE = 22,200, 23.9%) unspecific dance injury mechanisms. Differences in Overall Body Region, Diagnosis, and MOI Trends Between Genders

Among female athletes, the most commonly injured body regions were the knees (n = 663, NE = 16,538, 21.8%), ankles (n = 504, NE = 13,773, 16.6%), and the head (n = 350, NE = 9318, 11.5%). Their most frequent final diagnoses were sprains/strains (n = 1007, NE = 28,190, 33.1%), fractures (n = 355, NE = 8066, 11.7%), and contusions/abrasions (n = 251, NE = 6636, 8.2%). The most common MOIs were impacts with the floor (n = 1007, NE = 28,078, 35.4%), ankle rolling injuries (n = 424, NE = 11,402, 13.9%), and noncontact movements (n = 310, NE = 8720, 10.2%).

Among all male athletes, the most commonly injured body regions were the head (n = 131, NE = 3220, 23.7%), knees (n = 85, NE = 2708, 15.3%%), and ankles (n = 65, NE = 1656, 11.7%). The most common final diagnoses were sprains/strains (n = 123, NE = 3181, 22.2%), lacerations (n = 97, NE = 2429, 17.5%), and fractures (n = 87, NE = 1653, 15.7%). The most common MOIs were impacts with the floor (n = 234, NE = 6388, 42.0%), impact with the terrain (n = 65, NE = 1451, 11.7%), and ankle rolling (n = 50, NE = 1181, 9.0%).

Post hoc standardized residuals from the chi‐square analysis showed that when comparing sex, females were significantly more likely to sustain injuries to the knee (21.8% vs 15.3%, P < .001) and ankle (16.6% vs 11.7%, P = .012) injuries, whereas males were significantly more likely to sustain head (23.7% vs 11.5%, P < .001) injuries. Additionally, males were more likely to sustain fractures (15.7% vs 11.7%, P = .019) and females were more likely to sustain sprains/strains (33.1% vs 22.2%, P < .001). When evaluating MOIs, females had higher proportions of ankle rolling (13.9% vs 9.0%, P = .007) and noncontact movements (10.2% vs 6.1%, P < .001), whereas males experience more impacts with the floor (42.0% vs 35.4%, P = .007). P values for body region, final diagnosis, and MOI are presented in Tables 2 to 4.

Differences in Overall Body Region, Diagnosis, and MOI Trends Between Children and Adolescents

When stratifying by age groups, the most commonly injured body region in children were knees (n = 712, NE = 18,569, 26.2%), ankles (n = 476, NE = 13,131, 17.5%), and feet (n = 262, NE = 7534, 9.6%). The most common final diagnoses were sprains/strains (n = 965, NE = 26,403, 35.5%), fractures (n = 306, NE = 7152, 11.3%), and dislocations (n = 243, NE = 6462, 8.9%). The most common MOIs were impacts with the floor (n = 808, NE = 21,495, 29.7%), ankle rolling injuries (n = 392, NE = 10,553, 14.4%), and noncontact movements (n = 322, NE = 9034, 11.8%).

When evaluating adolescents, the most commonly injured body regions were the head (n = 116, NE = 2968, 11.5%), ankles (n = 105, NE = 2652, 10.4%), and feet (n = 93, NE = 2569, 9.2%). The most common final diagnoses were lacerations (n = 200, NE = 5349, 19.7%), strains/sprains (n = 185, NE = 5509, 18.3%), and fractures (n = 146, NE = 2675, 14.4%). The most common MOIs were impacts with the floor (n = 503, NE = 12,971, 49.7%), impact with the terrain (n = 107, NE = 2742, 10.6%), and ankle rolling (n = 82, NE = 2031, 8.1%).

Post hoc standardized residuals from the chi‐square analysis showed that when comparing age, children were significantly more likely to sustain injuries to the knee (26.2% vs 5.5%, P < .001) and ankle (17.5% vs 10.4%, P < .001) injuries. Additionally, adolescents were more likely to sustain fractures (14.4% vs 11.3%, P < .010) whereas children were more likely to sustain sprains/strains (35.5% vs 18.3%, P < .001). When evaluating MOIs, children were more likely to be injured secondary to ankle rolling (14.4% vs 8.1%, P < .001) and noncontact movements (11.8% vs 2.3%, P < .001), whereas adolescents were more likely to sustain injuries secondary to impact with the floor (49.7% vs 29.7%, P < .001). P values for body region, final diagnosis, and MOI are presented in Tables 2 to 4.

COVID Comparison

Dance‐related pediatric injuries were also stratified by the COVID period. However, this comparison was descriptive only. The number of dance‐related injuries during the COVID period (n = 1536, NE = 38,520) was lower than the post‐COVID period (n = 2195, NE = 57,567), consistent with activity restrictions. No additional significant differences in injury patterns were evaluated between these time periods.

DISCUSSION

Our findings show that the most commonly injured body regions among pediatric dancers include the knees, ankles, and head. The most common final diagnoses were sprains/strains, fractures, and contusions/abrasions, which were primarily due to impact with the floor and ankle rolling mechanisms. When stratifying by age and sex, females and younger children were significantly more likely to sustain knee and ankle injuries, experience sprains/strains, and have primary MOIs of ankle rolling and noncontact movements, in comparison with male and adolescent dancers, who have a significantly greater proportion of fracture injuries.

Our study identified a substantially lower number of dance‐related injuries during the COVID‐19 period, likely reflecting reduced participation in and availability of dance‐related activities due to pandemic‐related restrictions. These restrictions included reduced number of organized dance classes, performances, and an overall decline in physical activity among children. 11

This analysis found that the lower extremity is the most commonly injured body region for dance‐related injuries, consistent with the previous body of literature. 12 Dance incorporates unique biokinetics, as participants perform high‐impact, dynamic movements such as kicks, leaps, pirouettes, and inversions, all of which require closed‐chain kinetics through the lower extremities to push off the ground, followed by a dynamic open‐chain movement in the air, then finally absorbing impact back onto the ground. 13 The repetitive nature of jumping and landing significantly increases the likelihood of inappropriate weight distribution, such as landing with the ankle joint in slight varus or valgus, leading to an ankle roll and traumatic injury to surrounding ligaments and bony structures. 14 Similarly, when dancers do not evenly distribute their weight on the foot upon landing, force can inappropriately propagate up the lower extremity and potentially lead to traumatic fractures of the long bone, or patients may lose center of gravity, leading to subsequent falls with compensatory mechanisms such as falling out onto an outstretched hand. 13 , 15

In dance styles such as ballet and contemporary, a large proportion of movements require the dancers to externally rotate their hips for prolonged periods, placing additional stress and uneven weight distribution on their hips, knees, and feet. In combination with repetitive hip flexion and leg extension, children are significantly predisposed to acute injuries from muscle imbalance and uneven weight distribution. 5 Although other dance styles may require dancers to move and balance on their relevé (their heel is raised off the ground), weak ankles and muscles in the calf can put additional stress on the ankle of dancers, specifically young dancers who may have not fully developed strength and stability. 16 A potential solution to mitigate the detrimental biokinetic forces in dancers is increasing awareness of intrafloor force reduction (FR) variability among dance floors. Intrafloor FR refers to the ability of a dance floor to absorb and disperse impact forces when a dancer lands, jumps, or changes direction. Hopper et al. found that dance floors with inconsistent FR containing both “soft” and “hard” sports were associated with greater lower extremity loading and increased injury risk more than compared with floors with uniform FR. This finding encourages studios and performance spaces to regularly assess and maintain their flooring to ensure uniform FR, which may reduce the transmitted force through dancers’ bodies when landing and prevent injuries. 17

When stratifying by sex, females were significantly more likely to sustain injuries to the knee (21.8% vs 15.3%) and ankle (16.6% vs 11.7%), whereas males were significantly more likely to sustain head (23.7% vs 11.5%) injuries. Although previous studies have suggested that males are more often engaging in lifts/partner work, predisposing them to sustaining direct head trauma by their partner, this mechanism was not commonly observed in the current dataset, indicating that other factors such as movement patterns or participation characteristics may contribute to the higher proportion of head injuries among males seen in our dataset. 18 , 19 However, there is convincing evidence that shows females more likely experience anterior knee pain such as patellofemoral pain syndrome due to a combination of biomechanical, anatomic, and hormonal differences when compared with male counterparts. 20 , 21 For example, females evolutionarily adapted wider pelvises for childbearing, which subsequently creates wider quadriceps angles (Q‐angles); larger Q‐angles lead to significantly greater lateral force placed on the patella. 22 Females also have greater joint and ligamentous laxity due to higher estrogen levels, predisposing them to a pattern of biokinetic strain as discussed above. There is strong evidence showing that braces significantly decrease rates of ankle sprains/strains, which athletes should consider using when participating in dance. 23 Similarly, a randomized controlled trial by Vera et al. showed that implementation of an injury prevention program oriented around strengthening pelvic and core stabilization led to an 82% decrease in injury rate for the intervention group, as well as extended period from previous injury to subsequent injury. 24

Children were significantly more likely to injure their knee (26.2% vs 5.5%) and ankle (17.5% vs 10.4%), and sustained sprains/strains more frequently, compared with adolescents with a higher incidence of fractures. The available literature has supported that adolescents are more predisposed to sustaining fractures due to partial skeletal maturity; the rapid bone growth, decreased bone mineral density, and loss of bone pliability seen during puberty while long bone physes are beginning to close all contribute to an increased likelihood of sustaining serious injuries like fractures. 25 Moreover, it is generally recognized that adolescent patients are more likely to engage in higher risk behaviors and more dynamic movements, which may be associated with the observed increased proportion of injuries secondary to impact with the floor (49.7% vs 29.7%). 26 , 27

Emphasizing early physical training, including resistance and weight‐bearing exercises, has been shown to promote osteogenesis and increase bone mineral density. 5 Additionally, discouraging early specialization, especially in certain dance styles, may decrease the overall incidence of dance‐associated injuries by decreasing the risk of imbalanced musculoskeletal development and overuse injuries. 28

When comparing our findings with the existing literature, several similarities and essential differences emerged. Prior studies completed by Vassallo et al., Dadoo et al., and Yin et al. identified the lower extremity, particularly the knee and ankle as the most frequently injured body region of pediatric dancers, which is consistent with the patterns observed in our dataset. 4 , 6 , 12 Furthermore, sprains were the most common final diagnosis seen within the studies of Vassallo et al. and Dadoo et al., also aligning with our results. Although the Dadoo study reported general MOIs such as falls, contact, and noncontact, they did not stratify mechanisms by age or sex, nor did they categorize MOIs deeper than the 3 groups listed above. Furthermore, the studies above provide little to no sex‐stratified analysis, especially regarding the injury patterns among male dancers. This study expands on prior studies by providing a more granular MOI classification and showing age‐ and sex‐based differences.

Limitations

There are several notable limitations to this study. Although the NEISS database offers valuable insight into general epidemiologic trends, the patient narratives provide limited detail, inhibiting our ability to comprehensively identify corresponding MOIs to all patient cases. This lack of detail limited this study's ability to further stratify injuries by the specific type of dance and setting of dance (structured vs unstructured) and accounts for the substantial number of final diagnoses, body regions injured, and MOIs that are categorized as “other.” Additionally, NEISS diagnostic codes do not provide subdiagnoses (e.g., anterior cruciate ligament sprains, types of ankle sprains) and do not reliably link a specific diagnosis to a specific MOI, limiting our ability to describe more detailed injury patterns. The inability to differentiate injury patterns across dance disciplines such as ballet, jazz, hip‐hop, or contemporary limits the studies’ ability to determine whether certain styles carry higher or unique injury risks and prevents more granular interpretation of mechanisms and diagnoses. Moreover, the NEISS dataset reports patient presentations to the ED, which may potentially under‐represent the true incidence of dance‐associated injuries, such as those seen in outpatient clinics or urgent care. Further, the NEISS does not include any information about the treatment course or long‐term outcomes, limiting the ability to assess the clinical impact of injuries. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

CONCLUSIONS

Knees and ankles were the most commonly injured body regions, with sprains/strains and fractures representing the most frequent diagnoses. The most common MOIs included impact with the floor, ankle rolling, and noncontact movements. Females and younger children were significantly more likely to sustain knee and ankle injuries and experience sprains/strains, whereas males and adolescents more often sustained fractures.
