Engineered dCas12f1-SAM enables robust transcriptional activation and gain-of-function screening in primary human cells
Authors: Jiaxuan Cao, Zhirui Liu, Xinwen Chen, Zilin Lan, Li Liu, Yixin Zhai, Wentian Wang, Chaoyou Xue, Hui Cheng, Yao Yao, Tao Cheng, Shuquan Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite considerable powers, the application of CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) screens in primary human cells remains a formidable challenge. Here, we develop dCas12f1-SAM, a compact SAM-based transcriptional activation platform, that outperforms existing systems in both immortalized cell lines and primary human T cells and hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs). Using dCas12f1-SAM, we perform a pooled CRISPRa screen targeting 1559 human transcription factors (TFs) in primary human T cells and identify multiple positive regulators of IL-2 expression. We further implement a single-cell CRISPRa screen via our miCROP-seq construct, resolving how these genetic perturbations reshape T cell activation dynamics and drive functionally distinct cellular states. Among the top-ranking genes, we spotlight KLF12 and LHX5, whose overexpression significantly improves antigen-specific responses of chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells. Collectively, these findings establish dCas12f1-SAM as a robust transcriptional activation tool, highlighting its potential to advance applications in cellular engineering and immunotherapy.

Introduction

CRISPR screens, which enable unbiased interrogation of gene function in a scalable and customizable manner have greatly facilitated basic discovery research and translational applications1,2. While widely employed across various biological disciplines, CRISPR screens have predominantly been conducted in cell lines or animal models engineered to stably express Cas93–5. While cell lines and animal models allow for interrogation of cellular and biological processes, they do not fully recapitulate human biology. In contrast, ex vivo cultured primary human cells that retain normal markers, functions, heterogeneity, and finite lifespan can mimic the physiological state more closely6. However, challenges with efficient Cas9 delivery (albeit not impossible) have limited their applications in primary human cells7.

The comprehensive exploration of regulators for cell physiology necessitates the implementation of both gain-of-function and loss-of-function CRISPR screens8–10. Different from loss-of-function CRISPR screens, CRISPRa gain-of-function screens can identify genes that may not be normally active in the tested conditions, but when upregulated, can modify cell physiology and/or disease progression11,12. Recently, loss-of-function CRISPR screens have recently been achieved in primary human T cells and HSPCs through transient electroporation of Cas9 protein or mRNA and lentiviral delivery of sgRNA library13–15. Harnessing the remarkable payload capacity of recombinant Sendai virus (rSeV)16, our group has successfully conducted loss-of-function CRISPR screens in human HSPCs by employing rSeV for transient Cas9 delivery, while utilizing lentivirus for sgRNA library delivery17. However, these approaches are not amenable to gain-of-function CRISPRa screens in primary human cells as they necessitate sustained expression of the transcriptional activators that drive target gene expression.

An alternative strategy involves developing compact CRISPRa tools that enable lentiviral delivery of the CRISPRa system to primary human cells. Compared with Cas9 or Cas12a (usually 1000–1500 amino acids), the discovery of smaller naturally occurring Cas effectors offers a natural reservoir of compact Cas effectors, including CasX (986 amino acids)18, CasΦ (700–800 amino acids)19, Cas12n (400–700 amino acids)20, Cas12f1 (Cas14, 400–700 amino acids)21–29, TnpB (~400 amino acids)30,31 and IscB (~400 amino acids)32. The Type-V Cas12f is a family of exceptionally compact RNA-guided nucleases, and tremendous efforts have been dedicated to harnessing the Cas12f system for efficient genome editing in mammalian cells21–29. The Un1Cas12f1 variant, known as CasMINI, was further engineered to enable transcriptional activation by fusing nuclease-deactivated CasMINI with a tripartite-VP64-P65AD-Rta (VPR) transcriptional activator (dCasMINI-VPR)25,33. Nevertheless, the total size of dCasMINI-VPR, along with the selection marker, is ~4.0 kb, posing challenges in both lentivirus packaging and efficient transduction into primary human cells.

Here, we undertake structure-guided engineering of the CasMINI nuclease and develop a highly efficient miniature CRISPRa system (hereafter referred to as dCas12f1-SAM) based on the SAM framework34. Through lentiviral delivery of dCas12f1-SAM, we demonstrate robust activation of endogenous genes in both primary human T cells and CD34+ HSPCs. We then conduct a proof-of-concept CRISPRa screen targeting 1559 human transcription factors (TFs) to identify regulators, upon activation, capable of enhancing IL-2 production in primary human T cells. To enable pooled CRISPRa screens with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) readout, we further engineer the miCROP-seq construct based on our dCas12f1-SAM platform. By leveraging the miCROP-seq construct, we reveal how key hits modulate T cell activation and drive distinct cellular states marked by unique cytokine expression signatures. Among the top-ranking genes, we spotlight KLF12 and LHX5, whose overexpression improves antigen-specific responses of CAR-T cells, reinforcing its potential for future cancer-agnostic therapies. Collectively, these findings establish dCas12f1-SAM as a powerful and versatile transcriptional activation tool and underscore its transformative potential in the fields of cellular therapy.

Results

Structure-guided design of dCas12f1-SAM for transcriptional activation

Several distinct CRISPRa concepts have been described in ref. 35. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated efficient transcriptional activation of target genes with the SAM system, which utilizes MS2 hairpin aptamer-incorporated sgRNA to recruit MCP (MS2 bacteriophage coat protein)-p65-HSF1 co-activator fusion protein (MPH)34. To investigate the feasibility of engineering the Cas12f1 nucleases for a SAM-compatible CRISPRa system, we examined the previously determined crystal structure of deactivated Un1Cas12f1 variant (known as dCasMINI, D326A, and D510A) in complex with one sgRNA36,37. We observed that stem loop 1, stem loop 2, stem loop 4, and anti-repeat: repeat 2 (AR:R 2) protrude outside of the Cas12f1-sgRNA ribonucleoprotein complex (Fig. 1A). Based on these observations, we reasoned that stem loop 1, stem loop 2, stem loop 4, and AR: R 2 could withstand the addition of one MS2 aptamer to create a SAM-compatible sgRNA scaffold. We removed the four or five bases comprising the loops of the four stems (Fig. 1B) and then appended the MS2 aptamer to generate scaffold variants (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1A–D, and Supplementary Data 1). To minimize potential disruption of the original sgRNA structure caused by incorporation of the MS2 aptamer, we initially appended one MS2 aptamer into stem loop 1, stem loop 2, AR:R 2, or stem loop 4, respectively, resulting in four distinct scaffold variants referred to as scaffold 1.1- scaffold 1.4 (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1A–D, and Supplementary Data 1). This resulting SAM system comprises a dCasMINI-VP64 fusion protein, an MPH co-activator fusion protein, and a hU6-driven sgRNA, which is referred to as dCas12f1-SAM in the following experiments (Fig. 1D).

Using a TRE3G-eGFP HEK293T reporter cell line, we evaluated the ability of four sgRNA scaffold variants with a spacer sequence targeting the tet operator array with a TTTA PAM upstream the TRE3G promoter to activate eGFP expression (Fig. 1E). The dCas12f1-SAM system was transfected into the HEK293T reporter cells and flow cytometry was employed to quantify eGFP expression. Our results revealed significant activation of eGFP expression mediated by dCas12f1-SAM, as evidenced by an increase in mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1E, F). Amongst all four scaffold variants tested, scaffold 1.3 exhibited the highest efficiency in activating eGFP expression (Fig. 1E, F). Notably, when compared to dCasMINI-VPR, dCas12f1-SAM (scaffold 1.3) demonstrated a 1.6-fold enhancement in eGFP expression (Fig. 1E, F).

dCas12f1-SAM efficiently activates endogenous genes in mammalian cells

Encouraged by these findings, we subsequently evaluated the activation efficiency of endogenous genes (CD2, HBG, and IFNγ) using dCas12f1-SAM in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1G–I). For each gene, we designed one sgRNA with either a TTTA or TTTG PAM targeting within 500 bp upstream of the transcriptional start site (TSS). Across all tested sgRNAs, dCas12f1-SAM robustly activated transcription of all three target genes. Notably, scaffold 1.3 conferred the highest activation efficiency, exhibiting an 8.0- to 30.4-fold increase in mRNA levels relative to dCasMINI-VPR (Fig. 1G–I). This transcriptional upregulation was further validated at the protein level for CD2 and IFNγ, with scaffold 1.3 displaying the most superior activation efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). HBG protein expression could not be assessed in HEK293T cells due to the lack of commercially available primary antibodies.

Subtle modifications in the scaffold sequence and structure have been demonstrated to impact the functionality of sgRNA34,38. Therefore, we generated another four scaffold variants by eliminating the entire disordered region of stem loop 1, stem loop 2, AR:R 2, or stem loop 4, followed by incorporation of the MS2 aptamer (MS2 substitutions in Supplementary Fig. 1E–H and Supplementary Data 1). These four scaffold variants were denoted as scaffold 1.5, scaffold 1.6, scaffold 1.7, and scaffold 1.8, respectively. Using the TRE3G-eGFP HEK293T reporter cell line, we observed that all four scaffold variants effectively activated eGFP expression with scaffold 1.7 exhibiting the most pronounced activation effect (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). Consistent with the reporter assay results, scaffold 1.7 induced the strongest transcriptional and protein-level activation among all tested scaffolds across the three endogenous target genes, including CD2, HBG, and IFNγ (Supplementary Fig. 2E–G). These findings suggest that incorporation of the MS2 aptamer into AR:R 2 would stabilize the sgRNA structure and enhance its association with MPH complexes, thereby maximizing CRISPRa efficiency.

Notably, despite minimal sequence divergence, scaffold 1.3 consistently outperformed scaffold 1.7 in both the TRE3G-eGFP reporter assay and endogenous gene activation (Supplementary Fig. 2D–G). To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the functional superiority of scaffold 1.3 over scaffold 1.7, we performed integrated structural and biophysical analyses. mFold predictions indicated that scaffold 1.3 adopts a more thermodynamically stable conformation than scaffold 1.7 (ΔG = −75.1 versus −69.7 kcal/mol; Supplementary Fig. 3A, B)39, and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays quantitatively confirmed that scaffold 1.3 exhibits significantly higher binding affinity for Cas12f1 relative to scaffold 1.7 (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D). Together, these data establish a direct mechanistic link between enhanced sgRNA structural stability, improved Cas12f1 engagement, and superior transcriptional activation output.

Collectively, these results strongly indicate that the dCas12f1-SAM system can efficiently activate endogenous gene expression in mammalian cells, with the most effective scaffold 1.3 exhibiting 8.0- to 30.4-fold improvement in activation over dCasMINI-VPR.

Optimization of dCas12f1-SAM for enhanced endogenous gene activation in mammalian cells

Having demonstrated the efficient transcriptional activation of endogenous genes by dCas12f1-SAM, we reasoned that incorporating additional MS2 aptamers into the sgRNA scaffold could lead to an additive effect on transcriptional activation over scaffold 1.3. To test this hypothesis, we introduced a second MS2 aptamer into stem loop 1, stem loop 2, or stem loop 4 based on scaffold 1.3, resulting in three additional scaffold variants referred to as scaffold 2.1, scaffold 2.2, and scaffold 2.3, respectively (Fig. 1J, Supplementary Fig. 4A–C, and Supplementary Data 1). Using the TRE3G-eGFP HEK293T reporter cell line, we found that scaffold 2.1 enhanced eGFP expression 1.8-fold relative to dCasMINI-VPR, whereas scaffolds 2.2 and 2.3 exhibited significantly lower activation (Fig. 1K). Moreover, across the three endogenous target genes (CD2, HBG, and IFNγ), scaffold 2.1 elicited 58.2- to 160.6-fold greater transcriptional activation relative to dCasMINI-VPR (Fig. 1L–N), and correspondingly enhanced protein expression by 6.2- to 23.8-fold (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B).

Subsequently, we explored the feasibility of incorporating a third or fourth MS2 aptamer into the sgRNA scaffold for further augmentation of transcriptional activation effect. Building upon scaffold 2.1, we incorporated a third MS2 aptamer to stem loop 2 or stem loop 4 to create scaffold 3.1 and scaffold 3.2, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 5C and 4D, E and Supplementary Data 1). Additionally, we introduced two more MS2 aptamers simultaneously into stem loops 2 and 4 to generate scaffold 4.1 (Supplementary Figs. 5C and 4F and Supplementary Data 1). Unexpectedly, scaffolds 3.1, 3.2, and 4.1 exhibited progressively diminished transcriptional activation relative to scaffold 2.1 for CD2, HBG, and IFNγ (Supplementary Fig. 5D–F). AlphaFold 3-predicted models indicate that scaffold 2.1 adopts a conformation highly similar to the experimentally determined wild-type sgRNA-Cas12f1 complex (PDB ID: 7L48), whereas scaffolds incorporating three or four MS2 aptamers display markedly reduced structural similarity to this reference architecture (Supplementary Fig. 6)40. These results indicate that excessive incorporation of MS2 aptamers may disrupt the native sgRNA secondary structure and impair functionally competent binding to CasMINI, thereby accounting for the observed decline in transcriptional activation efficiency. Collectively, these data establish scaffold 2.1 as the optimal configuration for the dCas12f1-SAM system. For clarity and consistency, all subsequent experiments utilize dCas12f1-SAM, incorporating scaffold 2.1, hereafter designated dCas12f1-SAM.

dCas12f1-SAM outperforms available CRISPRa tools for endogenous gene activation

Next, we thoroughly evaluate the efficiency of dCas12f1-SAM in activating endogenous genes by testing additional sgRNAs. For each gene (CD2, HBG, or IFNγ), we designed a panel of 7–10 sgRNAs with either TTTA or TTTG PAM, targeting within the proximal promoter region spanning from −500 to +1 bp TSS (Fig. 1O–Q). All tested sgRNAs exhibited significant activation of their respective target gene by magnitude ranging from tens to thousands-fold (Fig. 1O–Q). Notably, testing across these sgRNAs revealed that the effectiveness of gene activation was highly dependent on the sgRNA targeting site, which is consistent with previously reported findings25,34.

We then conducted a side-by-side comparison of the performance between dCas12f1-SAM and dCasMINI-VPR for endogenous gene activation. Across all 26 tested sgRNAs, we consistently observed enhanced activation of their target gene (CD2, HBG, or IFNγ) with dCas12f1-SAM over dCasMINI-VPR (Fig. 1O–Q). For example, across ten distinct CD2-targeting sgRNAs, dCas12f1-SAM achieved 2.6- to 108.1-fold higher mRNA expression and 1.4- to 4.9-fold greater protein abundance relative to dCasMINI-VPR (Fig. 1P and Supplementary Fig. 7A). Across nine distinct IFNγ-targeting sgRNAs, dCas12f1-SAM induced 5.0- to 128.6-fold higher mRNA expression and 1.5- to 9.3-fold greater protein abundance relative to dCasMINI-VPR (Fig. 1Q and Supplementary Fig. 7B).

To evaluate the genomic specificity of dCas12f1-SAM-mediated transcriptional activation in a mammalian cellular context, we conducted whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). A comparative analysis was performed between dCas12f1-SAM and dCasMINI-VPR under identical experimental conditions. Specifically, HEK293T cells were transfected with either dCas12f1-SAM or dCasMINI-VPR, each co-expressed with an HBG-targeting (sgHBG) or non-targeting sgRNA (sgNT). High concordance in global gene expression profiles was observed between sgHBG and sgNT for both effectors (Supplementary Fig. 8), indicating minimal off-target transcriptional perturbation. Furthermore, direct comparison of normalized RNA-seq data revealed that dCas12f1-SAM induced significantly higher expression of the HBG locus relative to dCasMINI-VPR (Supplementary Fig. 8). Collectively, these results demonstrate that dCas12f1-SAM achieves robust and highly specific transcriptional activation in mammalian cells.

We further benchmarked dCas12f1-SAM against dCas9-VPR and dCas9-SAM for endogenous gene activation (Supplementary Fig. 9A). To enable direct functional comparison, we selected genomic target sites at the CD2, HBG, and IFNγ loci harboring either a 5′-TTTR-spacing-NGG-3′ motif or a 5′-CCN-TTTR-spacing-3′ motif, which support same-direction gRNA pairs or oppositely oriented gRNA pairs design for Cas12f1 and Cas9 effectors (Supplementary Fig. 9B–D). We demonstrated that dCas12f1-SAM consistently achieved superior transcriptional activation relative to both dCas9-VPR and dCas9-SAM across all three loci (Supplementary Fig. 9B–D). Specifically, dCas12f1-SAM achieved 12.8- to 20.0-fold greater transcriptional activation than dCas9-VPR and 1.7- to 25.0-fold relative to dCas9-SAM (Supplementary Fig. 9B–D).

We next performed a comparative assessment of lentiviral transduction efficiency between dCas12f1-SAM and dCas9-SAM in primary human T cells. Notably, the compact dCasMINI-VP64 construct (~3.8 kb) yielded approximately fivefold higher functional viral titers than dCas9-VP64 (~ 6.2 kb) (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Both dCasMINI-VP64-mCherry and MPH-eGFP/sgRNA displayed highly efficient transduction of primary human T cells, reaching up to 80% at moderate multiplicities of infection (MOI = 3–10) (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the dCas9-VP64-mCherry construct exhibited substantially lower transduction efficiency, plateauing at approximately 30% even at MOI of 100 (Fig. 2B). Similarly, dCasMINI-VPR (~ 5.3 kb) also showed limited transduction efficiency under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10B). Consistent with observations in primary T cells, the compact dCasMINI-VP64 construct achieved higher lentiviral transduction efficiency than dCas9-VP64 in primary human HSPCs (Fig. 2C).

Promoters of varying lengths and activity can potentially influence lentiviral packaging efficiency and drive variable transgene expression41. Therefore, we assessed the transduction efficiency of dCasMINI-VP64-mCherry and MPH-eGFP/sgRNA in primary human T cells driven by four different promoters: CMV, PGK, SFFV, and EFS (Supplementary Fig. 10C). Our findings revealed that SFFV facilitated the most superior transduction efficiency of both constructs in primary human T cells, which was employed for subsequent experiments (Supplementary Fig. 10D–E).

Using sgRNA pairs described above (Supplementary Fig. 9B–D), we next evaluated the activation effect of endogenous genes by dCas12f1-SAM and dCas9-SAM in primary human T cells. Lentiviral delivery resulted in 42.7% and 18.8% double-positive cells for dCas12f1-SAM and dCas9-SAM, respectively (Fig. 2D). We enriched double-positive cells and quantitatively assessed transcriptional and protein levels. Relative to dCas9-SAM, dCas12f1-SAM exhibited a 1.7- to 13.4-fold enhancement in transcriptional activation, and a 1.3- to 2.2-fold increase in protein expression (Fig. 2E–G and Supplementary Fig. 11A, B).

We further evaluated the performance of dCas12f1-SAM in primary human HSPCs. Lentiviral delivery resulted in 11.1% and 2.6% double positive cells for dCas12f1-SAM and dCas9-SAM, respectively (Fig. 2H). After enrichment of double positive cells, we demonstrated that dCas12f1-SAM induced 2.6- to 99.9-fold greater mRNA expression relative to dCas9-SAM across the three targeted loci, accompanied by a 2.9- to 4.5-fold increase in corresponding protein levels (Fig. 2I–K and Supplementary Fig. 11C, D). To assess functional HBG induction, HSPCs were differentiated toward the erythroid lineage in vitro, and HbF+ cell frequency was quantified at the terminal differentiation stage. Relative to dCas9-SAM, dCas12f1-SAM increased the proportion of HbF+ cells by approximately 1.6-fold (Supplementary Fig. 11E). These findings underscore the promising potential application of dCas12f1-SAM in primary human cells.

CRISPRa gain-of-function screens identified regulatory factors of IL-2 production in primary human T cells

CRISPRa screens demonstrated efficacy in identifying regulators with low or no expression under the tested conditions, but upon activation, showing the ability to modulate cell physiology or disease progression. Subsequently, we assessed the feasibility of conducting CRISPRa gain-of-function screens for cytokine production using dCas12f1-SAM in primary human T cells. We focused on IL-2, which is predominantly secreted by CD4+ T cells and is therapeutically applied at varying doses in autoimmunity and cancer treatment42.

To systematically identify transcription factors that can be manipulated to modulate the production of IL-2, we designed a library consisting of 9869 sgRNAs targeting 1559 transcription factors with a TTTR PAM (Supplementary Fig. 12A and Supplementary Data 2). For the majority of genes (1183/1559), six to ten sgRNAs were specifically designed to target the proximal promoter region spanning from −1000 to +1 bp TSS (Supplementary Fig. 12B, C). For the remaining genes, one to five sgRNAs were designed due to limited availability of PAM sequences (Supplementary Fig. 12B, C). Additionally, we included 30 sgRNAs targeting IL-2 and four positive control genes (LCP2, IL12B, TRAF3IP2, and TNFRSF1A) previously identified in CRISPRa screens of T cells9, along with 1000 non-targeting sgRNAs as negative controls. Single-cell CRISPRa screens in primary human T cells characterize the molecular phenotypes of cytokine regulators

To characterize the global molecular signatures elicited by each CRISPRa gene activation, we devised a platform (designated miCROP-seq) that couples pooled CRISPRa perturbations with scRNA-seq readouts (Fig. 4A). The miCROP-seq construct was adapted from the original CROP-seq architecture46, wherein the hU6-sgRNA (scaffold 2.1) cassette from dCasMINI was inserted into the 3′ long terminal repeat of the MPH-eGFP/sgRNA vector (Fig. 4A). This design allows the sgRNA to be transcribed together with the eGFP polyadenylated (poly-A) mRNA, thereby ensuring full compatibility with existing scRNA-seq methodologies for precise sgRNA detection. We produced lentiviruses using both the MPH-eGFP/sgRNA and miCROP-seq constructs, each carrying one of two validated gRNAs (sgKLF12 or sgIL-2). Following transduction, sgRNAs from the miCROP-seq construct were reliably detected through real-time qPCR analysis, whereas those from the MPH-eGFP/sgRNA construct remained undetectable (Fig. 4B). Remarkably, the miCROP-seq construct demonstrated exceptionally robust transcriptional activation of endogenous genes, achieving levels of efficiency on par with those observed using the MPH-eGFP/sgRNA construct (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, we performed CRISPRa Perturb-seq to characterize key regulators of stimulation responses in 21,147 primary human T cells (Fig. 4D). This analysis targeted 26 top-performing screen hits from our prior TF-targeting CRISPRa screen alongside 3 non-targeting controls (Supplementary Fig. 14A–D). For each selected hit, the most significantly enriched sgRNA was chosen and individually validated for its robust activation of target gene expression through real-time qPCR analysis (Supplementary Data 5). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction revealed a discrete separation between resting and restimulated cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 14E). By leveraging gene expression signatures, we were able to classify the majority of T cells as either CD4+ or CD8+ subtypes (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 16A). Thus, we successfully generated a high-quality, high-dimensional CRISPRa Perturb-seq dataset.

To identify CRISPRa gene perturbations capable of modulating the overall intensity of stimulation-responsive gene programs, we devised an activation score for each cell. This score was derived from a gene signature established by comparing resting and restimulated cells within the non-targeting control sgRNA cohort (Supplementary Fig. 14F). When projected onto the UMAP embedding of restimulated cells, these activation scores unveiled distinct spatial clusters exhibiting either high or low activation states (Fig. 4F), indicating that different T cell subsets responded heterogeneously to stimulation. We next examined activation scores across CRISPRa perturbations (Fig. 4G). Many of the positive regulators were found to elevate activation scores to varying degrees, suggesting their roles in broadly amplifying stimulation responses. Among these regulators, LHX5, PRDM8, ZNF614, KLF12, and TGIF2LX emerged as the most potent enhancers of T cell activation (Fig. 4G).

We next explored the impact of various CRISPRa perturbations on the expression of cytokines and other effector genes within primary human T cells. Through pseudobulk differential gene expression analysis between each sgRNA-targeted cell group and control cells under restimulated conditions, we uncovered widespread upregulation of multiple key effector genes, including IL-2, TNFα, CCL5, CSF2, CD40LG and TNFSF14 across most sgRNA-targeted conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15A). Remarkably, a broad enhancement in effector gene expression was evident for several key regulators, including LHX5, PRDM8, ZNF614, KLF12 and TGIF2LX, which algins with the corresponding activation scores rankings (Supplementary Fig. 15A). When contrasting gene activation patterns between restimulated and resting cellular states, profound disparities emerged (Supplementary Fig. 15). Under resting conditions, IL13, IL4, CCL5, and CXCL10 displayed the most pronounced and widespread upregulation within sgRNA-targeted cells, suggesting that the capacity to amplify effector gene expression following CRISPRa perturbations is linked to the stimulation status of T cells (Supplementary Fig. 15B).

Finally, we conducted clustering analysis to characterize the spectrum of CRISPRa-induced cellular states in both restimulated and resting T cells (Fig. 4H, Supplementary Fig. 16B, and Supplementary Data 6). For each distinct cluster, we identified the top upregulated gene signatures and cytokine expression profiles, thereby defining the unique transcriptional identify of each cluster (Fig. 4I and Supplementary Fig. 16C). The spatial enrichment of specific sgRNAs within these clusters was visualized through contour density plots, offering a striking depiction of the diverse transcriptional landscapes sculpted by CRISPRa perturbations (Fig. 4J and Supplementary Fig. 16D). For instance, cluster 3 was marked by elevated expressions of IL-2, IFNγ and TNFα, alongside signature genes including NFKBID1 and PIM3. Notably, sgLHX5 was highly enriched in this cluster, strongly suggesting its pivotal role in enhancing cytokine production upon stimulation (Fig. 4J). SgKLF12 was significantly enriched in clusters 2, 4, and 5, implying its involvement in promoting memory T cell differentiation and enhancing cytotoxicity (Fig. 4J). Collectively, these data revealed how individual CRISPRa perturbations fine-tune the intensity of T cell activation.

Discussion

In summary, we have employed a structure-guided approach to designing the dCas12f1-SAM CRISPRa system. This compact dual-construct CRISPRa system demonstrates exceptional performance in activating endogenous genes, superior to the dCasMINI-VPR design and other CRISPRa tools. More importantly, dCas12f1-SAM facilitates CRISPRa gain-of-function screens using lentiviral delivery module in primary human cells. We further engineered the miCROP-seq construct based on our dCas12f1-SAM platform, which enabled pooled CRISPRa screens with scRNA-seq readout. Leveraging the miCROP-seq construct, we revealed how key hits modulate T cell activation and drive distinct cellular states at single-cell levels. These results highlight dCas12f1-SAM as a powerful technology for transcriptional activation in cell engineering and gene therapy.

A diverse array of CRISPRa toolkits has been devised, which can generally be categorized into three groups: directly fusing activation domains with inactivated Cas nucleases, utilizing engineered protein or sgRNA scaffold to recruit activator fusion protein35. Although dCas9- and dCas12a-based CRISPRa systems achieve high transcriptional activation efficiency for endogenous genes, their large size imposes stringent limitations on lentiviral packaging capacity48. Critically, beyond physical packaging limits, the large size of the viral genomic RNA is a well-established determinant of transduction efficiency, with longer transcripts exhibiting markedly reduced integration efficacy in hard-to-transduce primary cells49. The length of inserted DNA also highly correlated with human silencing hub complex-mediated transgene repression50. Collectively, larger genetic cargos may be more susceptible to reduced genomic integration efficiency, epigenetic silencing, or cell-type–specific transcriptional constraints. Consistent with this principle, the compact dCasMINI-VP64 construct achieved significantly higher transduction efficiency than the dCas9-VP64 counterpart under identical MOI conditions in both primary human T cells and HSPCs (Fig. 2B, C). In this context, compact Cas effector systems such as dCas12f1-based CRISPRa may offer practical advantages for applications where viral packaging capacity, delivery efficiency, or stable expression of large transgenes are limiting, expanding the range of CRISPRa tools available for primary cell engineering and therapeutic development.

Extensive efforts have been made to develop compact CRISPRa systems for transcriptional activation in primary human cells. The Type-V Cas12f is a family of exceptionally compact RNA-guided nucleases, and several groups have tried to harness the Cas12f system for gene regulation (dCasMINI-VPR and SminiCRa/SminiCRi) in mammalian cells25,33. Despite the engineered dCasMINI-VPR exhibited robust efficiency in endogenous gene activation, only 20–40% of sgRNAs exhibited significant activation for tested genes as reported in the original study25. Furthermore, the combined size of dCasMINI-VPR and the selection marker is approximately 4.0 kb, rendering challenges in both lentivirus packaging and transduction into primary human cells. Compared to dCasMINI-VPR, we demonstrated superior endogenous gene activation achieved by dCas12f1-SAM across 26 tested sgRNAs (Fig. 1O–Q). Despite a limited number of tested sgRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 9), dCas12f1-SAM also exhibited better endogenous gene activation than both dCas9-VPR and dCas9-SAM.

Given the rapid expansion of functionally characterized Cas12f-family nucleases in recent years, analogous engineering strategies may be broadly applicable to other compact Cas12f variants, including OsCas12f1 and CnCas12f122–25,27,29,36,51,52. The multi-stem loop structure of Cas12f1-sgRNA provides an optimal platform for engineering of the SAM-compatible sgRNAs. Previous studies have demonstrated that truncation of stem loop 1 and AR:R 2 minimally impacts or even enhances the activity of Un1Cas12f1 in both in vitro and in vivo settings22,37,53, while truncation of stem loop 4 leads to a significant decrease of activity22,53. Consistent with these biochemical insights, we demonstrated that dCas12f1-SAM exhibited the highest activation efficiency when appending the MS2 aptamer to the AR:R 2 site. Furthermore, incorporation of MS2 aptamers into both AR:R 2 and stem loop 1 could synergistically enhance the activation efficiency of dCas12f1-SAM. In contrast, incorporation of three or more MS2 aptamers progressively impaired transcriptional output. This attenuation likely reflects structural perturbation of the Cas12f1-sgRNA ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, compromising its functional integrity. Beyond canonical MS2 hairpin integration into sgRNA stem loops, a recent study demonstrated that appending an MS2 hairpin to the 5′ terminus of the sgRNA can also drive robust transcriptional activation, thereby diversifying scaffold-based recruitment paradigms54. In addition to MS2, alternative adapters such as PP7 or Com sequences can also be utilized to recruit activator fusion domains55. Moreover, structure-guided engineering or saturation mutagenesis of Cas12f1 effectors can enhance Cas12f1-sgRNA complex stability and binding affinity to target sequences, thereby potentiating transcriptional output25,28. Critically, the efficacy of CRISPRa systems is inherently constrained by the native cellular and genomic context, including chromatin architecture, epigenetic landscape, and transcriptional competence56. Accordingly, fusion of dCas12f1 with epigenetic writer modules, such as histone acetyltransferases, can enhance local chromatin accessibility and synergistically potentiate transcriptional activation at target loci57–60. These findings underscore the importance of aptamer valency and positioning in optimizing scaffold-based CRISPRa systems, and highlight the broader potential of customizable sgRNA architectures for orthogonal recruitment of diverse regulatory effectors.

Current efforts are increasingly focused on identifying genetic perturbations that enhance the functional potency of adoptive T cell therapies15,61–63. Leveraging a CRISPRa-based functional genomics screen restricted to transcription factors, we identified 114 high-confidence regulators of IL-2 production, including both canonical factors and previously uncharacterized candidate, thereby validating the sensitivity and reproducibility of our screening platform. A subset of these hits was orthogonally validated via arrayed activation assays, further strengthening the reliability of our screening methodology. Compared with a prior genome-wide CRISPRa screen for IL-2-producing regulators9, our transcription factor-focused library identified a substantially greater number of IL-2-activating transcription factors (114 versus 24 hits). This suggests that strategic restriction to focused sublibrary would confer enhanced detection sensitivity for modest-effect regulators, facilitating the identification of candidates with subtler yet functionally consequential phenotypes. Moreover, differences in sgRNA composition between our transcription factor-focused library and the prior genome-wide CRISPRa screen likely contribute to the observed discrepancies in positive hit identification.

Among the individually validated regulators, PRDM8 has been implicated in the exacerbation of inflammatory responses in individuals suffering from multiple sclerosis and rheumatoid arthritis, pointing to its plausible role in modulating T cell activation64,65. Earlier investigations have also linked KLF12 to the regulation of NK cell proliferation and T cell activation in the context of tuberculosis66,67. While we acknowledge that not all CRISPRa-induced enhancements in T cell activation may directly translate into improved in vivo antitumor activity, we remain optimistic about the discovery of genes capable of broadly amplifying cellular stimulation responses. Looking ahead, future screening studies conducted under diverse experimental conditions hold the promise of uncovering additional regulators that influence T cell states and functional dynamics.

Methods

Ethics statements

Human umbilical cord blood from anonymous healthy donors was obtained from the Tianjin Cord Blood Bank. Mobilized peripheral blood of anonymous healthy donors was collected at the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institute of Hematology & Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. All the animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Hematology & Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. As donor sex information was not documented in the anonymized cord blood inventory, sex was not included as a variable in downstream analyses.

Plasmid construction

Plasmids were constructed using standard molecular cloning methods. All genetic elements for gene expression were integrated into a common lentiGuide-Puro plasmid backbone (Addgene, #196709) employing the pEASY®-Basic Seamless Cloning and Assembly Kit (TransGen Biotech, China). The dCasMINI-VP64, dCasMINI-VPR, dCas9-VP64, and MPH sequences were PCR-amplified from plasmids Addgene #176269, #176269, #61422, and #61423, respectively. The sequence of the MS2 aptamer68 and the engineered sgRNA scaffolds are shown in the Supplementary Data 1. The sgRNA sequences utilized in the present study were summarized in Supplementary Data 7.

Cell lines maintenance, transduction, and transfection

Human cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection: HEK293T (CRL-3216), Nalm-6 (CRL-3273), and Raji (CCL-86). HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific), whereas Nalm-6 and Raji cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All media were supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

To generate TRE3G-eGFP HEK293T reporter cell line, wild-type HEK293T cells were transduced with lentivirus expressing TRE3G-eGFP (Addgene #111177) in the presence of polybrene (10 μg/ml). Forty-eight hours after transduction, 2 µg/mL puromycin were added to the culture medium to remove non-transduced cells. Raji-Luc-eGFP and Nalm6-Luc-eGFP cell lines were generated by lentiviral transduction of PCDH-luciferase-eGFP followed by FACS to isolate single positive cells.

All transfection experiments were performed using Transporter™ 5 Transfection Reagent (Polysciences, USA). Cells were plated in a 24-well plate with 150,000 cells in 500 µL culture medium per well one day before transfection, followed by being transfected with 1:1:1 mass ratio of sgRNA plasmid with gene-specific targeting sequence or non-targeting control sequence: MPH plasmid or pUC19: dCas constructs (dCasMINI-VP64/dCasMINI-VPR/dCas9-VP64) with a total mass to 1.5 μg. Transfected cells were analyzed 48 h post-transfection by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) for endogenous gene activation or flow cytometry for eGFP activation.

Lentivirus production and functional titer determination

Lentivirus production was performed according to previously described protocols9. Briefly, HEK293T cells (8.0 × 106) were seeded onto 100-mm cell culture dishes. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with Opti-MEM (Opti-MEM I Reduced Serum Medium with GlutaMAX Supplement, Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 5% FBS (Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Fisher Scientific, USA), 1× MEM nonessential amino acids (Fisher Scientific), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), or fresh complete DMEM. A mixture containing the transfer plasmid (9 μg), psPAX2 (Addgene #12260; 6 μg), and pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene #8454; 3 μg) was then transfected into the HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The lentivirus-containing medium was collected at 24 and 48 h after transfection. Medium was centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter (Pall Corporation, USA), concentrated, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

Functional lentiviral titers were determined by transducing HEK293T cells and quantifying transduction efficiency via flow cytometry. Cells were transduced with serial dilutions of concentrated lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL). Seventy-two hours post-transduction, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the percentage of fluorescent-positive cells. Viral titer (transducing units per milliliter; TU/mL) was calculated from wells exhibiting ~30% transduction efficiency.

Isolation, culture, and transduction of human T cells

CD3+ T cells were isolated from human umbilical cord blood (obtained from the Tianjin Cord Blood Bank) using anti-CD3 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). The isolated T cells were cultured in X-VIVO 15 (Lonza Bioscience, Switzerland), supplemented with 5% FBS, 50 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10 ng/mL of recombinant human IL-2 (Peprotech, USA). Primary T cells were activated using Dynabeads™ Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 for T Cell (Gibco) at a concentration of 25 μL/mL in the culture medium, with a cell density of 1.0 × 106 cells/mL.

One day following T cell activation, cells were transduced with lentivirus encoding either dCasMINI-VP64 or dCas9-VP64. Two days later, a second transduction was performed with MPH-eGFP/sgRNA lentivirus. For lentiviral transduction, 6 μg/mL polybrene was added. Four days after initial activation, the mCherry⁺eGFP⁺ population was FACS-sorted for downstream assays.

Isolation, culture, and transduction of primary human CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs)

Primary human HSPCs were isolated from mobilized peripheral blood of healthy donors at the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, using the CliniMACS microbeads (Miltenyi Biotech). HSPCs were cultured in StemSpan™ SFEM II medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Canada) supplemented with 10% StemSpan™ CD34⁺ Expansion Supplement (10×; STEMCELL Technologies). Lentiviral transduction and subsequent enrichment of transduced cells were carried out using the same optimized protocol developed for human T cells, including polybrene (6 μg/mL) enhancement and FACS-based isolation of double-positive (mCherry⁺eGFP⁺) cells.

Erythroid differentiation from CD34 + HSPC in vitro

In vitro erythroid differentiation was carried out following previously established protocols69. Briefly, CD34+ HSPCs were cultured in erythroid differentiation medium (EDM), which consists of IMDM (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 330 μg/ml human holo-transferrin, 10 μg/ml recombinant human insulin, 2 IU/ml heparin, 5% human AB serum (Sigma, USA), 3 IU/ml of erythropoietin, 1% of L-glutamine (Thermo Fisher), and 1% of penicillin/streptomycin. During the first 7 days of culture, EDM was further supplemented with 10−6 M hydrocortisone (Sigma), 100 ng/ml human SCF, and 5 ng/ml human IL-3, referred to as EDM-1. Between days 7 and 11, only 100 ng/ml human SCF was added, forming EDM-2. From days 11 to 18, no additional supplements were included, known as EDM-3. For HbF detection, cells were harvested, fixed, and stained with PE anti-human HbF antibody (BD Biosciences, clone #2D12, USA).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted employing the SPARKeasy Cell RNA Kit (SparkJade, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using the Hifair®Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen, China) and stored at −80 °C for subsequent use. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) reactions were meticulously prepared in 384-well plates utilizing the Hieff UNICON® Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen) and executed on QuantStudio 6 Pro Real-Time PCR Systems (Thermo Fisher). Relative expression fold-changes were discerned utilizing the ΔΔCq method, with fold repression normalized to the expression of GAPDH. Primers sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Westen blotting

Cells were lysed using Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, USA), and the total cellular lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SATB1, MYB, IL12B (all from Abclonal, China; 1:1000 dilution), and PRDM8 (from Santa Cruz, USA; 1:500 dilution), or β-actin (Proteintech; 1:1000 dilution) as a loading control. The goat anti-mouse and anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Proteintech, USA, 1:5000 dilution) was applied, and immunoreactive bands were visualized using Pierce™ ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher).

Flow cytometry analysis

For intracellular staining, 0.1 million cells were fixed using 100 μL of eBioscienceTM Fixation/Permeabilization Concentrate (Thermo Fisher) for 20 min in the dark at room temperature, and then washed with 1 × Permeabilization buffer. Subsequently, the cells were stained with antibodies for 30 min in the dark. After being washed twice, the cells were prepared for flow cytometry analysis.

For cell surface marker analysis, the cells were stained with specific antibodies for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Following this, the cells were washed twice before being prepared for flow cytometry analysis.

The antibodies used in this study for flow cytometry included: APC anti-CD2 antibody (Biolegend, clone TS1/8, USA), anti-CD19-PE antibody (Biolegend, clone HIB19), anti-CD3-PerCP/Cy5.5 (Biolegend, clone OKT3), anti-CD4-APC (Biolegend, clone SK3), anti-CD8-APC/Cy7 (Biolegend, clone SK1), anti-IL-2-PE (Biolegend, clone MQ1-17H12), and anti-IFNγ-FITC (Biolegend, clone B27).

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected 72 h post-transfection. Human IFN-γ concentration in supernatants was quantified using the human IL-2 ELISA Kit or human IFN-γ ELISA Kit (Boster Biological Technology, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with absorbance measured on a Synergy H4 Hybrid Reader (BioTek Instruments).

Design and cloning of TF library

The sgRNAs of the TF library were designed utilizing the transcription start sites (TSS) cataloged within the FANTOM database as a primary reference. For genes exhibiting more than 10 TTTR PAM sequences within a vicinity of −500 bp from the TSS, the 10 TTTR PAMs closest to the TSS were selected. In instances where genes harbored between 6 and 10 TTTR PAM sequences within the region spanning from the TSS to TSS-500 bp, all available sgRNAs were inclusively incorporated into the library. For genes presenting fewer than 6 TTTR PAM sequences, the target region was extended to TSS-1000 bp to facilitate the selection of 6 sgRNAs nearest to the TSS, or all available sgRNAs if the count was less than 6.

To serve as positive controls, a set of five genes, including IL2, LCP2, IL12B, TNRSF1A, and TRAF3IP2 was designated, with each gene meticulously designed to encompass 6 sgRNAs for inclusion within the library. Conversely, for negative controls, 1000 non-targeting sgRNAs were curated from the work of Morgens et al.70, and an additional 3 randomly generated sequences were appended for each sgRNA. The comprehensive library composition is detailed in Supplementary Data 2.

Protospacer sequences flanked by PCR adaptors were custom-synthesized by GenScript. Subsequently, pooled sgRNA libraries were cloned into the modified lentiGuide-Puro vector (Addgene, #52963) expressing the MPH protein (as detailed in the “Plasmid Construction” section) using the Gibson Assembly strategy. The assembled products were column-purified and electroporated into E. cloni 10 G Elite Electrocompetent Cells (Lucigen, USA). A comprehensive effort was made to isolate enough colonies to guarantee approximately 1000 × library coverage for all libraries. Representation of the sgRNAs within the library was confirmed through next-generation sequencing (NGS).

IL-2 CRISPRa screen

Twenty-four hours post-activation, T cells were transduced with 20% vol/vol dCasMINI-VP64 lentivirus, achieving a transduction efficiency exceeding 85%. Another 24 h later, 3% vol/vol MPH-puroR/sgRNA lentiviral library was introduced into T cells, resulting in transduction efficiencies ranging from 25 to 40%, to achieve >1000 × coverage relative to the initial number of sgRNAs. Four days after activation, puromycin (2 μg/mL) was added to the culture medium to select cells stably expressing the MPH protein and sgRNA. Seven days post-activation, cells were centrifuged, resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/ml in complete X-VIVO 15 culture media without IL-2. On the eighth day post-activation, cells were harvested and resuspended at 2 × 106 cells/mL in X-VIVO 15 without supplements. The following day, cells were restimulated and stained for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) following the procedures described in the “Intracellular Cytokine Staining” section and the “Flow Cytometry” section.

Genomics DNA extraction, library preparation, and NGS

For the extraction of genomic DNA, sorted cells were centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min and resuspended in a homemade lysis buffer comprising 100 mM Tris pH 8.5, 5 mM EDTA, 0.2% SDS, 200 mM NaCl, and 100 μg/mL proteinase K. The suspension underwent an overnight incubation at 56 °C, followed by a 95 °C step for 20 min to deactivate proteinase K. Genomic DNA in the solution was purified using DNA Extraction Reagent (Enol: Chloroform: Isoamylol = 125:24:1, pH ≥ 7.8) (Solarbio, China), following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

To amplify the integrated sgRNAs, multiple reactions were initiated, each containing 500 ng of genomic DNA, using PrimeSTAR® Max DNA Polymerase (Takara, Japan) under the following cycling conditions: 98 °C for 30 s; 23 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; 72 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, a second PCR was performed using handle-specific primers to introduce indexes to each sample under the following conditions: 98 °C for 30 s; 18 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 20 s; 72 °C for 10 min. The primers employed for the first and second PCRs are listed in the Supplementary Data 5.

PCR samples were purified using DNA Clean & ConcentratorTM-5 (Zymo Research, USA). Each sample was quantified after purification with the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Fisher). The resulting purified PCR products were then submitted to Novogene Co. (Tianjin, China) for library preparation and sequencing using NovaS4 (Illumina). Clean reads were obtained by filtering out reads containing adapters, poly-N reads, and low-quality reads from the raw data. Subsequent analyses were performed using the clean data.

Screen hits analysis

Pooled CRISPR screens were analyzed using a modified MAGeCK-iNC pipeline43. Specifically, reads were aligned to the appropriate reference library employing MAGeCK count (MAGeCK version 0.5.9.5-3)71. The –control-sgrna./negative_control.txt –norm-method control parameters were utilized to count and normalize the sgRNA read counts across both library sets. Normalized read counts in the IL-2hi versus IL-2low bins were compared using the “mageck test -k” command with the –norm-method none option. This facilitated the calculation of log2 fold change. P values were calculated based on the two-tailed negative binomial distribution and adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Subsequently, only sgRNAs with P values < 0.05 were considered for the calculation of the gene-level overexpression phenotype. For genes with fewer than three sgRNAs with P values < 0.05, the top three sgRNAs were included in the calculation. The overexpression phenotype was determined as the average log2 fold change of the selected sgRNAs. Statistical significance for each gene was ascertained by comparing the set of P values for sgRNAs targeting it with the set of P values for non-targeting control sgRNAs using the Mann–Whitney U test. For mock negative control genes, we performed random sampling of six with replacement from non-targeting control sgRNAs to generate “negative-control-quasi-genes”. The phenotype and statistical significance were calculated similarly to the target genes. The correction for multiple hypothesis testing followed the approach previously described43. The hit strength, defined as the product of the overexpression phenotype score and -log (P value), was calculated for all genes in the library and for the “negative-control-quasi-genes”. Based on the distribution of all the products, a cutoff value was chosen to ensure a false-discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05.

Single-cell CRISPRa screening in primary human T cells utilizing the miCROP-seq platform

TF-targeting and non-targeting sgRNAs were subcloned into the miCROP-seq vector. All sgRNA-harboring plasmids were combined in equimolar ratios, with the exception that non-targeting sgRNA-encoding plasmids were introduced at a threefold higher concentration relative to each of the other plasmids. Primary human T cells were transduced, maintained, and restimulated under conditions as detailed in the “IL-2 CRISPRa screen” section. Twenty-four hours post-restimulation, T cells were collected and sorted for mCherry+eGFP+ subpopulations, which were then subjected to scRNA-seq analysis using the Singleron platform.

miCROP-seq data analysis

Gene expression profiling and sgRNA read alignment and quantification were carried out using the Celescope pipeline (v2.6.0) under default parameters. Gene expression reads were mapped to the Ensembl GRCh38 (release 99) human reference genome and its associated annotation. Simultaneously, sgRNA reads were aligned to the custom sgRNA library and quantified via a tailored bioinformatic workflow. This workflow identifies sgRNA sequences by detecting the conserved GGAATGCAAC upstream anchor and TTTTTTAAGC downstream motif, permitting up to two nucleotide mismatches. Droplets containing multiple sgRNAs were excluded from further analysis. To enhance specificity, only droplets harboring at least five sgRNA unique molecular identifiers were retained for downstream processing.

Gene expression data were imported and analyzed in R (version 4.5.0) using the Read10× function from the Seurat package (version 5.3.0). Initial quality control filtering was applied to exclude cells with mitochondrial read content exceeding 25% or with fewer than 1000 or more than 7500 detected RNA features.

For both restimulated and resting conditions, dimensionality reduction was executed using UMAP with the RunUMAP function, incorporating principal components 1 through 30. Clustering was performed using the FindClusters function at a resolution of 0.5 for both experimental conditions. To construct the T cell activation score, a pseudobulk differential expression analysis was conducted on non-targeting control cells, contrasting the restimulated group with the resting group. Genes exhibiting an absolute log2(fold change) greater than 0.25 and expressed in at least 10% of cells in either condition were selected as gene weights (GWs). For each restimulated cell, the activation score was computed as the weighted sum of normalized gene expression, calculated by multiplying each gene’s normalized expression by its GW, and then dividing by the gene’s mean expression in non-targeting control cells.

A pseudobulk differential expression analysis was further conducted on sgRNA-assigned cell populations to evaluate the impact of specific genetic perturbations on cytokine and chemokine expression profiles. For each sgRNA group, the FindMarkers function was employed to compare its transcriptomic signature against the non-targeting control, with a focus on a curated list of cytokine and chemokine genes.

Cytotoxicity assay

To assess the cytotoxic efficacy of CD19 CAR-T cells, tumor target cells were co-cultured with effector cells for 12 h in 96-well plates under varying effector-to-target (E:T) ratios. Following incubation, residual tumor cells were identified based on the DAPI⁻eGFP⁺ phenotype and quantified using precision count beads (BioLegend). The extent of specific cytolysis (%) was determined using the following formula:

Specific cytolysis (%) = (Tumor cell count in control group–Residual tumor cell count in co-culture group)/(Tumor cell count in control group) × 100%.

Bulk RNA-seq

RNA sequencing was performed by Novogene (China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing reads were mapped to the GRCh38 human reference genome using the STAR aligner (v2.7.11b). Gene-level expression counts were subsequently generated using RSEM (v1.3.3) in paired-end mode, leveraging STAR-aligned transcriptome BAM files and the GENCODE v48 basic annotation database. Genes of low expression were systematically filtered based on a dual threshold: a minimum of 10 total reads and 5 transcripts per million across all samples. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.48.2). Functional enrichment analysis of upregulated genes was further performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.16.0).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis of Cas12f1-sgRNA interaction

Protein and RNA preparation

The His-MBP-CasMINI fusion protein was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified via Ni2+-affinity and size-exclusion chromatography. Analytical SEC confirmed the protein exists predominantly as a dimer. The sgRNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription from a linearized DNA template using T7 RNA polymerase and purified with the HiPure Oligo Purification Kit (TIANYOU BIOTECHNOLOGY, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

SPR instrumentation and binding cycle design

All experiments were performed on a Biacore 8 K instrument (Cytiva) at 25 °C using a Series S Sensor Chip NTA. The running buffer was 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2. To ensure a consistent and regenerable ligand surface for each measurement, a dedicated capture-regeneration cycle was implemented prior to every analyte injection. This cycle comprised three sequential steps: (1) Stripping: injection of 350 mM EDTA for 90 s at 30 µL/min to chelate and remove Ni2+ along with any bound protein; (2) Re-charging: injection of 0.5 mM NiCl2 for 60 s at 10 µL/min to re-activate the NTA surface; (3) Ligand capture: injection of fresh His-MBP-CasMINI at 10 µg/mL to achieve a standardized capture level of ~1000 RU. A reference flow cell underwent identical treatment but without protein capture.

Kinetic binding measurements

Initial binding experiments were conducted across a broad range of sgRNA concentrations (3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800 nM in running buffer) to determine the optimal range for kinetic analysis. For each concentration, a complete capture-regeneration cycle was executed to establish a fresh protein surface. The sgRNA sample was then injected over both flow cells at 30 µL/min (association: 120 s), followed by dissociation in running buffer for 120 s. Reference-subtracted sensorgrams were globally fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model using Biacore 8 K Evaluation Software. The reported association (Ka) and dissociation (Kd) rate constants, and the derived equilibrium dissociation constant (KD = Kd/Ka).

Mouse experiment

Female NCG (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt) mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech (China) and maintained in a specific pathogen-free barrier facility with controlled environmental parameters: a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, temperature 20–26 °C, and relative humidity 30–60%. On day 0, mice received an intravenous injection of 3 × 106 luciferase-expressing Nalm6 cells (Nalm6-Luc). Five days later, they were administered either 2 × 106 CD19-directed CAR-T cells or control T cells via intravenous injection. Tumor burden was monitored weekly by bioluminescence imaging. Mice were humanely euthanized upon reaching predefined humane endpoints: (i) total flux exceeding 1 × 1010 photons/second (p/s), as quantified by Living Image Software v4.7; or (ii) onset of clinical signs including hind-limb paralysis, sustained hunched posture, or severe locomotor impairment. All procedures complied with institutional animal care and use guidelines.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism and R package. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test. A one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests and a two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc tests were used for comparisons between multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Sample size was not predetermined. No data was excluded from the analysis. Mouse experiments were randomized. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or mean ± standard error of the mean, with each data point representing an independent biological replicate. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
