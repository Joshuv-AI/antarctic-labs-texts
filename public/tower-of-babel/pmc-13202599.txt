Impact of mitochondrial reductive stress due to respiratory chain limitation on cancer via posttranslational modifications
Authors: Christos Chinopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory chain limitation is a recurrent but spatially heterogeneous state in solid tumors, driven by hypoxic subregions, electron transport chain defects, and signaling- or therapy-imposed respiratory inhibition. Restricted electron flow supports an ETC-linked MRS state characterized by accumulation of reduced electron carriers and configuration-dependent reactive oxygen species formation. This review describes how electron transport chain limitation remodels posttranslational modifications through (i) oxygen partitioning and substrate control of oxygen-dependent dioxygenases -considering that respiratory complex IV is the dominant intracellular molecular oxygen sink, thus shaping its availability for hydroxylation and demethylation reactions- and (ii) redox backpressure that shifts NAD(P)+/NAD(P)H balance, perturbs acyl-CoA and citric acid cycle metabolite pools, and rewires protonmotive force-linked matrix chemistry and thiol buffering. These constraints are predicted to remodel lysine acylations, HIF hydroxylation, histone and DNA methylation, cysteine-centered redox PTMs, phosphorylation networks, ubiquitin-dependent proteostasis, ADP-ribosylation, and lactylation. Together, these relationships support a site- and context-dependent PTM-routing state in which the position of respiratory chain limitation and the local tumor environment shape which PTM chemistries become rate-limiting, adaptive, or growth-restrictive.Keywords: Hypoxia, Sirtuins, Dioxygenases, Ubiquinone, NAD+/NADH ratio, Reverse electron transfer

Reductive stress: general considerations

Reductive stress denotes a redox-imbalance state in which reducing equivalents (i.e., NAD(P)H and GSH) accumulate relative to their oxidized counterparts, creating “electron pressure” that can be cytotoxic rather than protective. Although this concept predates the term [1], it was later explicitly presented as a stress paradigm based on experimental observations that blocking ATP synthesis causes marked NADH accumulation and loss of viability; this injury was attributed to reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by incomplete oxygen reduction under excessive reducing pressure [2]. Earlier metabolic physiology had already provided a quantitative model for disease-associated reductive shifts. Using the lactate dehydrogenase-coupled pyruvate-to-lactate ratio to infer cytosolic NAD redox state, diabetic liver cytosol was reported to exhibit increased NADH, whereas mitochondrial pyridine nucleotide redox was comparatively buffered [3], [4]. Under baseline conditions, NAD+ was described as the predominant species in both compartments. Finally, reductive stress should not be interpreted as “low ROS”: when the NAD and ubiquinone (UQ) pools become highly reduced, ROS production may increase while concurrently reducing effective NAD+ availability, as it may occur during reverse electron transfer (RET) at mitochondrial respiratory complex I [5].

Mitochondrial reductive stress due to limitations of the electron transport chain

Mitochondrial reductive stress (MRS) is a compartmental form of reductive stress that emerges when electron flux through the ETC is constrained by limited molecular oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor or by dysfunction of its components (Fig. 1). Electron backlog upstream of the bottleneck increases the reduction state of mitochondrial carrier pools, prominently increasing NADH and reduced coenzyme Q (UQH2). These carrier shifts encode electron “backpressure” into changes in redox cofactor availability and metabolite handling, and they provide the biochemical context for the RET-linked ROS signaling and NAD+ redistribution [5].

Because MRS is not yet a standardized cancer biology term, this review uses it in a restricted, ETC-linked sense. Recent reductive stress reviews define the parent state as accumulation of reducing equivalents, especially NADH, NADPH, and GSH, beyond countervailing oxidative capacity [6], [7]. In cancer models, NRF2 activation has been shown to induce NADH-reductive stress through ALDH3A1-linked NAD+ consumption, and this state increases vulnerability to complex I inhibition, supporting NADH/NAD+ imbalance as an experimentally tractable reductive-stress variable [8]. Here, MRS denotes a mitochondrial state in which limited electron transfer to terminal or alternative electron acceptors is accompanied by increased reduction pressure in respiratory carrier systems. Sufficient experimental support consists of a defined respiratory bottleneck -severe O2 limitation at CIV, genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of complexes I–IV, high Δψm-imposed slowing of NADH oxidation, or sulfide-dependent CIV/UQ-junction stress-together with at least one mitochondrial consequence: increased NADH/NAD+ or compartment-assigned NAD(P)H signal; increased UQH2/UQ or RET-dependent complex I ROS; impaired respiratory NAD+ regeneration rescued by electron acceptors or compartment-targeted NADH oxidase; succinate/fumarate/L-2HG accumulation attributable to altered ETC/TCA directionality; or altered Δψm/ΔpH, ATP, or NADPH/thiol buffering accompanying carrier over-reduction [5], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. The strongest MRS → PTM attribution comes from measuring one or more of these variables in the same model as the PTM change and from separating redox backpressure from O2-substrate limitation by oxygen-independent ETC perturbation or redox correction.

By contrast, MRS remains inferential when evidence is limited to tumor hypoxia, pO2 histography, HIF stabilization, dioxygenase inhibition, glycolysis/lactate accumulation, ROS changes, or PTM remodeling without direct measurement of respiratory carrier reduction or ETC-linked metabolite consequences. VHL loss and other pseudohypoxic lesions are not themselves MRS, because they activate HIF or dioxygenase outputs without demonstrating electron backpressure. In such settings, the appropriate assignment is that the data are consistent with MRS or support an MRS-related hypothesis pending quantification of NADH/NAD+, UQH2/UQ, RET, ETC flux, succinate/fumarate/L-2HG, ATP/Δp, and thiol-redox variables.

Hypoxia as a primary reason for MRS in tumors

Hypoxia is one of the most common routes through which cells within hypoxic compartments of solid tumors, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), cervical cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), breast, prostate, and lung cancers, melanoma, and glioblastoma, are predicted to develop ETC limitation and MRS when local O2 availability falls below respiratory demand. In essence, when molecular oxygen becomes sufficiently limiting, respiratory complex IV cannot accept electrons at the required rate; electrons then accumulate upstream, shifting carrier pools toward a more reduced state, i.e., higher NADH/NAD+ and UQH2/UQ ratios (Fig. 1). Solid tumors develop regions of low O2 availability when oxygen delivery fails to match oxygen consumption, a supply-demand imbalance compounded by structurally and functionally abnormal tumor vasculature, leading to pronounced spatial and temporal heterogeneity, including chronic diffusion limitation and intermittent perfusion limitation [15], [16], [17], [18]. Importantly, hypoxia does not impose a single limiting O2 concentration across mitochondrial respiration and PTM-relevant dioxygenase reactions. The apparent p50 for mitochondrial respiration in isolated mitochondria and small cells is approximately 0.01–0.10 kPa (∼0.075–0.75 mmHg; ∼0.1–1 μM O2), indicating that substantial O2 -as substrate-limitation of CIV-supported respiration generally requires severe local oxygen depletion, although intact-cell and tissue thresholds vary with respiratory demand, substrate state, diffusion geometry, pH, nitric oxide or sulfide exposure, and ETC reserve [19]. These values should also not be mapped directly onto electrode-measured tumor pO2, because tissue pO2, intracellular pO2, and mitochondrial pO2 are separated by diffusion and consumption gradients. By contrast, many Fe(II)/α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases that regulate PTMs are constrained at higher, tissue-relevant oxygen ranges. Representative kinetic values illustrate this separation: HIF prolyl hydroxylases have reported Km(O2) values of approximately 230–250 μM, FIH is lower at approximately 90–110 μM depending on substrate and assay, KDM4A/KDM6A occupy a high-μM oxygen-sensitive range, and TET1/2 show lower but still oxygen-dependent kinetics with assay-dependent variation [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. Thus, dioxygenase-dependent PTM changes may occur in tumor regions that are not yet severely oxygen-limited for mitochondrial respiration, whereas oxygen-limited MRS should be inferred more conservatively in compartments where O2 delivery, O2 consumption, and ETC capacity together generate respiratory redox backpressure.

The fraction of a tumor satisfying these conditions is therefore expected to be variable rather than universal. Direct pO2 histography provides useful clinical anchors, but these measurements define hypoxic subvolumes rather than MRS itself. Across clinically accessible tumors, including cancers of the uterine cervix, head and neck, and breast, the overall median pO2 has been reported near 10 mmHg, with an overall severe hypoxic fraction (pO2 ≤ 2.5 mmHg) of approximately 25%. In pooled cervical carcinoma series, hypoxic fractions were approximately 28%, 44%, and 59% using pO2 cutoffs of ≤2.5, ≤5, and ≤10 mmHg, respectively; in head-and-neck cancers, corresponding pooled values were approximately 21% and 32% for ≤2.5 and ≤ 5 mmHg [26]. These values emphasize that oxygen-limited MRS is likely to be regional and dynamic, not tumor-wide, while also indicating that clinically meaningful hypoxic subvolumes are common in several solid tumors. At the cellular-redox level, early work demonstrated that prolonged hypoxia increases reduced NAD(P)H signals in cells, consistent with a more reduced redox state under oxygen limitation [27]. In tumor contexts, hypoxia-linked increases in free NADH were functionally coupled to invasive behavior through a CtBP-dependent redox-sensing transcriptional corepressor mechanism, supporting the interpretation that regulatory proteins sense hypoxia-driven reductive shifts and alter malignant phenotypes [28]. Because mitochondrial respiration is the dominant intracellular O2 sink, variation in respiratory complex IV flux alters the effective oxygen available to oxygen-dependent dioxygenases that install or erase PTMs. This respiration-dioxygenase “oxygen redistribution” interface is addressed in further detail below [29], [30], [31].

Electron transport chain limitation independent of hypoxia as a driver of MRS in solid tumors

In addition to oxygen scarcity, solid tumors experience ETC limitation through tumor-intrinsic mitochondrial defects and therapy- or environment-imposed respiratory inhibition; these settings support MRS assignments when they produce electron accumulation upstream of the bottleneck and shift carrier pools toward a more reduced state (Fig. 1). A particularly well-supported route is genetic impairment of respiratory complex I: disruptive mtDNA mutations in complex I subunits are enriched in oncocytic lineages and are associated with severe complex I loss or dysfunction, implying chronic impairment of mitochondrial NADH oxidation and persistent reductive pressure [32], [33], [34]. In breast cancer, modulation of complex I activity was shown to control the NAD+/NADH balance and to regulate aggressive phenotypes, with restoration of NADH oxidation suppressing metastasis [9]. More generally, loss of respiration from non-hypoxic ETC dysfunction leads to electron acceptor limitation in proliferating cells, in which insufficient regeneration of NAD+ constrains biosynthesis (notably aspartate), and growth can be rescued by exogenous electron acceptors or alternative NAD recycling routes [35], [36], [10]. Pharmacologic ETC inhibition provides another clinically relevant non-hypoxic route to MRS: metformin inhibits respiratory complex I and respiration in human cancer cells, and the antiproliferative consequences depend strongly on extracellular context through NAD redox homeostasis and aspartate availability [37], [38], [39]. Finally, ETC “backpressure” could arise even under normoxia when coupled respiration is constrained by high mitochondrial membrane potential; increasing pyruvate oxidation elevates membrane potential, impede NAD+ regeneration by the ETC, and shift cells toward a more reduced redox state, which can be relieved by uncoupling or increasing ATP turnover [40].

Reduced sulfur metabolism as a nonhypoxic CIV/UQ-junction route to MRS

A further nonhypoxic route to ETC-limited MRS is provided by reduced sulfur metabolism. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is generated endogenously by sulfur-metabolic enzymes, principally cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), cystathionine γ-lyase/cystathionase (CSE/CTH), and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (MPST/3-MST). CBS and CSE are PLP-dependent transsulfuration enzymes whose canonical role is cysteine production but whose substrate promiscuity supports H2S generation from cysteine and/or homocysteine [41], [42], [43], [44]. MPST uses 3-mercaptopyruvate, generated by cysteine aminotransferase/glutamate-oxaloacetate transaminase activity, to form an enzyme-bound persulfide from which H2S can be released by physiological acceptor/reductant systems such as thioredoxin or dihydrolipoic acid [45], [46], [47]. In addition, tumors at the host-microbiome interface, especially colorectal tumors, may be exposed to microbiome-derived sulfide, making sulfide handling particularly relevant in this anatomical context [48], [49].

At low concentrations, sulfide acts as an inorganic respiratory substrate, whereas higher sulfide inhibits CIV, limiting electron transfer to O2 and thereby imposing a distal respiratory bottleneck [50], [51]. Mammalian cells that express sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase (SQOR) commit H2S to oxidation by transferring electrons to coenzyme Q, reducing UQ to UQH2 while converting sulfide into a persulfide product, predominantly glutathione persulfide when GSH is the sulfur acceptor [52], [53], [54]. Thus, H2S creates a distinctive ETC state: it can both feed electrons into the UQ pool through SQOR and restrict their canonical exit through CIV.

When CIV is inhibited by sulfide, CoQH2 generated by SQOR can be reoxidized by complex II operating in reverse. In this noncanonical redox cycle, reverse complex II transfers electrons from CoQH2 to fumarate, regenerating CoQ for continued SQOR activity while reducing fumarate to succinate [55], [56]. In this configuration, fumarate functions as a terminal electron acceptor for sulfide-linked electron flow. This mechanism is conceptually aligned with the broader finding that mammalian ETCs may use fumarate as an alternative electron acceptor when O2 reduction is constrained [11]. Therefore, sulfide metabolism identifies a biochemical setting in which distal ETC limitation at the CIV/UQ junction is predicted to increase UQH2 pressure, disturb NADH/NAD+ balance, and reroute electron disposal through fumarate reduction.

The resulting metabolite pattern links this sulfur-dependent ETC state directly to PTM-relevant MRS chemistry. In sulfide-treated HT29 colon adenocarcinoma cells, metabolomics showed decreased malate, aspartate, glutamate, and α-Kg, together with increased succinate [49]. This pattern is consistent with malate being drawn toward fumarate production, fumarate being consumed by reverse complex II, and succinate accumulating as the reduction product of fumarate. The same state perturbs the malate-aspartate shuttle: limited mitochondrial NAD+ availability, impaired MDH2-dependent malate oxidation, altered aspartate/glutamate exchange, and diversion of cytosolic oxaloacetate toward fumarate together explain the observed depletion of aspartate and glutamate and the partial aspartate insufficiency reported under sulfide stress [49], [57]. In parallel, α-Kg can be diverted toward reductive carboxylation of glutamine-derived carbon, supporting citrate and lipid biosynthesis under sulfide-induced reductive pressure [49], [58].

This mechanism supports a direct biochemical link from reduced sulfur metabolism to PTM remodeling. As it will be examined in more detail below, succinate is a competitive inhibitor of α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases, including HIF prolyl hydroxylases, JmjC histone demethylases, and TET DNA dioxygenases [59], [60], [61]. Therefore, H2S-induced succinate accumulation is predicted to suppress α-Kg-dependent PTM-erasing reactions, decreasing HIF hydroxylation and reducing histone and DNA demethylation capacity. This connects sulfide metabolism and cancer relevance strongest in sulfide-rich or sulfide-adapted contexts, such as colorectal cancer, where CBS-derived H2S has been linked to tumor bioenergetics and angiogenesis, sulfide oxidation enzymes are upregulated, and recent SQOR loss-of-function work reduced HCT116 xenograft growth [48], [49], [62].

Malignant contexts in which ETC-limited MRS is most likely to be operative

The relevance of ETC-limited MRS is highest in malignant settings where O2 delivery, ETC capacity, electron-acceptor availability, or tumor metabolism creates a sustained tendency toward reduced respiratory carrier pools. Four contexts are especially pertinent: hypoxic solid tumors, pseudohypoxic tumors driven by VHL/HIF or SDH/FH-linked metabolite lesions, tumors with intrinsic ETC defects, and sulfide-rich or sulfide-adapted colorectal tumors. These categories overlap mechanistically but are not equivalent. Hypoxia directly limits O2 as the terminal electron acceptor and as a dioxygenase substrate; pseudohypoxia denotes activation of HIF or other α-Kg-dependent dioxygenase outputs despite less severe O2 limitation; and ETC-limited MRS requires evidence or strong expectation of respiratory redox backpressure, such as increased NADH/NAD+, reduced UQ, reverse complex I/RET activity, succinate/fumarate/L-2HG pressure, or sulfide-dependent UQ loading.

Among hypoxic solid tumors, the strongest clinical relevance resides in tumors with substantial hypoxic fractions or hypoxia-linked therapy resistance, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), cervical carcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), breast carcinoma, prostate carcinoma, lung carcinoma, melanoma, and glioblastoma [16], [17], [18]. In HNSCC, the DAHANCA-5 nimorazole trial established clinical benefit from hypoxic radiosensitization, supporting hypoxia as a therapeutically relevant variable [63]. In cervical carcinoma, direct oxygen measurements linked low tumor pO2 to malignant progression and poor outcome [64]. In PDAC, polarographic pO2 measurements showed marked tumor hypoxia relative to normal pancreatic tissue [65]. In this group, MRS is expected where O2 restriction decreases CIV flux sufficiently to increase upstream NADH/UQH2 pressure. The immediate PTM implications are reduced HIF prolyl/asparaginyl hydroxylation, altered JmjC/TET demethylase output, and secondary NAD+-dependent deacylation stress. The clinical implications therefore center on prognosis, radiotherapy/chemotherapy resistance, immune exclusion, and selection of hypoxia/MRS biomarkers rather than on tumor type alone.

Pseudohypoxic tumors form the second group, but they require separation into HIF-degradation lesions and metabolite-driven dioxygenase inhibition. Clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the clearest VHL-dependent pseudohypoxic malignancy; VHL is inactivated by mutation or methylation in over 80% of ccRCC [66], and TCGA confirmed recurrent VHL-centered HIF-pathway lesions [67]. In this setting, HIF activation and HIF2α-directed therapy have direct clinical relevance, highlighted by belzutifan in VHL disease–associated tumors and advanced RCC [68], [69]. However, VHL loss is not equivalent to ETC-limited MRS, because the primary lesion is impaired HIF-α degradation rather than electron backpressure. Thus, ccRCC is best presented as a clinically important pseudohypoxic comparator in which MRS variables require direct measurement.

By contrast, SDHx-associated pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma (PPGL) and SDH-deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) are more directly connected to the MRS → PTM concept because SDH is both a TCA enzyme and complex II of the ETC. SDH loss produces succinate accumulation, inhibiting HIF prolyl hydroxylases and JmjC/TET dioxygenases [59], [60]). SDH-mutant PPGLs show a hypermethylator phenotype linked to succinate-dependent inhibition of α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases [61], and TCGA placed many PPGLs with SDHx/VHL/EPAS1/EGLN alterations within a pseudohypoxic molecular class [70]. SDH-deficient GISTs constitute a defined GIST subset with SDH loss as a central metabolic defect and global epigenomic divergence [71], [72]. FH-deficient RCC belongs to a parallel fumarate-driven group: fumarate accumulation inhibits α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases and promotes epigenetic remodeling and EMT-like traits [73]. These SDH/FH tumors are not merely hypoxia mimics; they provide fixed genetic examples of metabolite pressure on PTM erasers, especially HIF hydroxylases, JmjC histone demethylases, and TET DNA dioxygenases.

Third, tumors with intrinsic respiratory-chain defects comprise nonhypoxic examples of ETC-limited MRS. Oncocytic thyroid tumors/Hürthle cell carcinoma and renal oncocytoma are particularly relevant because complex I mtDNA lesions and complex I loss are recurrent biological features [32], [34], [33], [74]). These tumors place impaired NADH oxidation and adaptive glutathione/redox rewiring at the center of the phenotype, rather than treating mitochondrial stress as a secondary consequence. Breast carcinoma models provide functional evidence that complex I activity and the NAD+/NADH balance regulate progression and metastasis [9]. Brain tumor stem-like models add a UQ-centered configuration in which RET through complex I is active and coupled to signaling outputs [12]. In these contexts, the MRS evidence is stronger than in VHL-only pseudohypoxia because the studies interrogate ETC function, NAD redox balance, or RET-linked UQ/complex I behavior. The predicted PTM outputs are especially relevant to sirtuin-dependent lysine deacylation, cysteine-centered redox PTMs, phosphorylation signaling downstream of RET-linked ROS, and ADP-ribosylation through altered NAD+ partitioning.

Fourth, colorectal tumors represent a distinct ETC-limited MRS context because they are exposed to epithelial, tumor-derived, and microbiome-derived reduced sulfur metabolism. CBS-derived H2S supports colon carcinoma bioenergetics, proliferation, and angiogenesis [48], while sulfide exposure in colon cells perturbs mitochondrial bioenergetics, increases succinate, depletes α-Kg/aspartate/glutamate-related pools, and promotes glycolytic and lipid-biogenic rerouting [49]. Recent SQOR loss-of-function work reducing HCT116 xenograft growth strengthens the clinical relevance of sulfide oxidation as a colorectal carcinoma liability [62]. In this setting, the MRS route is not generic hypoxia but sulfide-dependent UQ loading combined with CIV inhibition and fumarate-supported electron disposal. Its PTM implications converge on succinate/α-Kg-dependent suppression of HIF, JmjC, and TET dioxygenase reactions, together with altered NADH/NAD+ and thiol-redox handling.

Finally, hypoxic PDAC deserves explicit mention because it links tumor hypoxia to a specific MRS-adjacent oncometabolite branch. Hypoxia-driven L-2HG in pancreatic tumor and stromal cells was linked to histone hypermethylation, stemness-differentiation balance, and immune evasion; lowering L-2HG improved antitumor response in an orthotopic model [75].

Overall, the malignant contexts most relevant here are those in which ETC limitation or its metabolite consequences are sufficiently prominent to alter PTM chemistry: hypoxic regions of solid tumors; VHL/HIF pseudohypoxic tumors as clinically important comparators; SDH/FH-deficient tumors as genetic metabolite-pressure exemplars; complex I-defective oncocytic and selected breast/brain tumor models as direct redox-backpressure examples; and sulfide-adapted colorectal carcinoma as an anatomical and biochemical niche for CIV/UQ-junction MRS. The operational distinction introduced above applies across these groups. Directly supported MRS assignments combine a respiratory bottleneck with measured NAD+/NADH, UQ/UQH2, RET, ETC flux, sulfide oxidation, or succinate/fumarate/L-2HG variables. Hypoxic or pseudohypoxic tumors without these measurements remain MRS-inferential. This distinction links MRS to measurable clinical and biochemical variables such as hypoxia-associated outcome and radioresistance, HIF2α dependence, SDH/FH genetic stratification, sulfur-metabolism vulnerabilities, and site-resolved PTM biomarkers, without implying a uniform MRS state across all solid tumors.

MRS as a PTM-routing state in cancer

MRS is best treated here as a biochemical routing state: respiratory chain limitation changes the reaction conditions under which PTM writers and erasers operate, whereas tumor context shapes which of these constraints becomes phenotype-limiting. This view is consistent with contemporary cancer metabolism models in which metabolic liabilities are context-specific rather than universal, being shaped by tissue environment, nutrient availability, oncogenic genotype, and functional demand [76], [77].

In this routing model, the position of ETC limitation provides the first layer of interpretation. Limitation of NADH oxidation, as occurs with complex I dysfunction or high membrane-potential-imposed electron backpressure, is expected to increase NADH pressure and restrict NAD+ regeneration. This preferentially affects NAD+-dependent deacylation, ADP-ribosylation economics, lactate production as a cytosolic redox outlet, and redox-linked aspartate production. By contrast, limitation at or beyond the ubiquinone/CIII/CIV region emphasizes UQH2 pressure, RET-permissive ROS microdomains, fumarate-dependent electron disposal through reverse SDH, and succinate-mediated inhibition of α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases. Hypoxia engages both arms, but additionally imposes direct O2-substrate limitation on PHD/FIH, JmjC, and TET enzymes. Thus, the central variable is not simply whether a tumor is hypoxic or reductive, but which MRS configuration is present and which PTM chemistry is thereby constrained. Accordingly, the PTM sections are best viewed as an ordered set of reaction constraints rather than as an inventory of unrelated modifications; the relevant constraints include O2 availability, α-Kg/oncometabolite balance, NAD+ availability, acyl-CoA donor pressure, matrix acid–base chemistry, thiol buffering, RET-linked oxidant microdomains, ATP supply, low-ROS reductive-state sensing, and lactate accumulation.

Routing respiratory chain limitation toward PTMs

Building on this routing model, respiratory chain limitation remodels PTMs through two partially separable mechanisms. The first is O2-substrate limitation, in which oxygen scarcity directly reduces the activity of PTM enzymes whose chemistry requires O2, most notably iron/α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases that regulate HIF hydroxylation and chromatin demethylation. The second is redox backpressure, in which restricted ETC electron flow increases the reduction state of mitochondrial NADH and/or coenzyme Q pools and thereby changes NAD+ availability, UQ redox poise, ATP production, protonmotive-force-dependent matrix chemistry, metabolite pools, and thiol buffering. In tumors, hypoxia engages both mechanisms simultaneously, whereas genetic or pharmacologic ETC defects isolates redox backpressure even under normoxia. The distinction is important here, because it explains why MRS-linked PTM remodeling is coherent at the level of proximal biochemical constraints even when individual PTM outcomes are context-dependent.

ETC limitation is linked to PTM remodeling through convergent routes involving redox-coupled cofactor availability, altered metabolite substrates or inhibitors, ROS-dependent signaling chemistry, and protonmotive force-dependent matrix chemistry (Fig. 1). First, when respiratory complex I cannot efficiently oxidize NADH, the NADH/NAD+ ratio shifts toward the reduced form, lowering effective NAD+ availability for NAD+-dependent PTM enzymes including sirtuins; complex I-linked control of NAD redox has been tied to cancer phenotypes and to altered protein acylation consistent with impaired NAD+-dependent deacylation [9], [78]. Second, ETC limitation commonly increases the reduction state of the coenzyme Q pool; a highly reduced coenzyme Q pool favors reverse electron transfer through respiratory complex I, and RET elevates ROS even when oxidative phosphorylation is constrained, thereby influencing ROS-sensitive PTMs and signaling networks [12]. Third, respiratory limitation influences tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolite levels; for example, succinate accumulates during SDH disruption and also when distal ETC limitation drives reverse complex II/fumarate reduction, including during sulfide-induced CIV inhibition coupled to SQOR-dependent CoQ reduction [55], [11]. Elevated succinate inhibits oxygen- and α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases that install or erase PTMs, linking mitochondrial metabolic constraints to HIF hydroxylation, histone demethylation, and DNA demethylation [59], [60]. Beyond redox couples, ETC limitation also modifies the protonmotive force consisting of membrane potential and pH gradient, and matrix pH is a kinetic determinant of nonenzymatic lysine acylation; physiologic matrix pH and acyl-CoA concentrations are sufficient to drive enzyme-independent lysine acetylation and succinylation, supporting a model in which mitochondrial deacylases counteract a persistent chemical acylation pressure, inherent to the mitochondrial matrix [79], [80]. Finally, the protonmotive force couples ETC state to thiol redox buffering through nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase, transferring reducing equivalents from NADH to NADP+; because NADPH availability governs glutathione and thioredoxin reducing capacity, this coupling links ETC limitation to the steady-state occupancy and reversibility of cysteine-centered PTMs [81].

PTMs as redox-sensitive integrators

PTMs regulate protein activity, stability, localization, and interactions. They also link mitochondrial state to cancer phenotypes because many PTM enzymes are constrained by redox-dependent cofactors, by O2 availability, or by metabolites whose levels change during ETC limitation. At the level of phosphorylation, many phosphatases -especially protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs)- depend on an active-site cysteine that is vulnerable to oxidation; ROS may oxidize this cysteine and reversibly inactivate the enzyme, biasing signaling toward higher phosphorylation [82], [83]. In reductive stress, ROS depletion below physiological signaling levels has been reported to impair signaling; the same low-ROS state is consistent with activation of defined ubiquitin-mediated remodeling responses [84].

In a similar manner, NAD+-dependent sirtuin deacetylases provide a direct cofactor link: when mitochondrial redox shifts toward higher NADH and lower NAD+ availability, sirtuin activity decreases, contributing to mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation and a shifted “acetylome” [85], [86] More generally, hypoxia and respiratory chain limitation restrict sirtuin-mediated deacylation because sirtuins require NAD+ for catalysis [87], [88] In some contexts, NAD+ decline under hypoxia reduces SIRT1 activity, permitting acetylation of HIF-1α and enhancing HIF-1 signaling with consequences for tumor growth and angiogenesis in vivo [89].

Mitochondria-derived metabolites created under reductive pressure also reshape PTMs by inhibiting α-ketoglutarate (α-Kg) -dependent dioxygenases. Under hypoxia, reverse SDH flux (fumarate reduction to succinate) acts as an electron sink, generating succinate; succinate accumulation inhibits prolyl hydroxylases that hydroxylate HIF-α to promote degradation, thereby favoring HIF stabilization leading to “pseudohypoxia” [90], [91]. A parallel route involves 2-hydroxyglutarate (L-2HG). Under O2 limitation, cells produce L-2HG by enzymatic reduction of α-Kg, including promiscuous activity of lactate dehydrogenase A and malate dehydrogenases; perturbation experiments support L-2HG as a contributor to increased repressive histone methylation marks such as H3K9me3. L-2HG has also been described as part of a metabolic response to MRS, accumulating with increased mitochondrial reducing potential and feeding back on ETC and glycolysis [92], [93]. In pancreatic cancer, hypoxic tumors and patient samples showed L-2HG accumulation linked to histone hypermethylation, stemness programs, and immune modulation; in line with this, reducing L-2HG promoted tumor regression and improved response to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 therapy in an orthotopic model [75].

Cysteine PTMs intersect ETC function directly: S-glutathionylation of respiratory complex I subunits leads to a decrease in complex I activity, and the balance of glutathionylation and deglutathionylation is sensitive to glutathione redox status and glutaredoxin control [94]. Cysteine redox PTMs, including glutathionylation and nitrosylation, are increasingly treated as modulators of redox-dependent cancer processes [95].

Finally, histone acetylation is a core chromatin PTM that increases chromatin accessibility and gene expression by neutralizing lysine positive charge within histone tails; it is written by histone acetyltransferases and erased by histone deacetylases, and is constrained by acetyl-CoA availability [96], [97], [98].

The following sections group PTM classes according to the cofactor, substrate, metabolite, or redox variable through which respiratory chain limitation alters PTM installation, removal, or steady-state occupancy. HIF hydroxylation and chromatin demethylation are governed primarily by O2 availability and α-Kg/oncometabolite balance. Lysine acylations are influenced by the balance between NAD+-dependent deacylation, acyl-CoA donor availability, and mitochondrial matrix acid–base chemistry, whereas ADP-ribosylation is constrained by compartmental NAD+ partitioning and competition with other NAD+-consuming reactions. Cysteine-centered redox PTMs and phosphorylation are shaped by thiol-buffering capacity, RET-linked oxidant microdomains, and ATP availability; ubiquitin-mediated remodeling is treated as a low-ROS reductive-state response at the mitochondrial import gate; and lactylation reflects diversion of excessive NADH into lactate. These considerations separate two non-identical mechanisms: i) O2-substrate limitation, in which low O2 directly constrains dioxygenase reactions, and ii) respiratory redox backpressure, in which restricted electron flow changes NADH/NAD+, UQH2/UQ, metabolite pools, matrix acid-base chemistry, and redox signaling. Because hypoxia can engage either or both mechanisms, each PTM class is discussed below with attention to whether oxygen limitation or ETC redox backpressure is the more proximate driver. It should be emphasized, that these biochemical constraints are not unique to malignant cells; in cancer, their significance arises when chronic or cycling exposure, spatial heterogeneity, oncogenic context, and therapy pressure stabilize PTM outputs and select phenotypes.

Shared oxygen/redox constraints and cancer-specific modifiers

Hypoxia and ETC inhibition impose core biochemical constraints that are largely shared between non-transformed and cancer cells. In both settings, limiting electron flux to molecular oxygen (or inhibiting ETC components) shifts respiratory redox carrier pools toward a more reduced state and restricts NAD+ regeneration, while simultaneously limiting O2 availability for oxygen-dependent dioxygenases that write/erase PTMs (e.g., HIF hydroxylation and chromatin demethylation, see below). Moreover, hypoxia responses are not limited to “switching on glycolysis”: canonical oxygen-sensing programs actively remodel mitochondrial oxygen consumption, linking respiration to the intracellular O2 available for non-respiratory PTM enzymes [99].

Furthermore, cancer differs primarily in how frequently and persistently these shared constraints are engaged and in the additional oncogenic inputs that modulate PTM outcomes. Tumors commonly experience chronic and cycling hypoxia due to abnormal vasculature and supply-demand imbalance, creating spatially and temporally heterogeneous oxygen landscapes that repeatedly impose reductive/energetic stress. In addition, many cancers display “pseudohypoxia,” in which HIF and other dioxygenase-regulated PTM outputs are activated under less severe oxygen limitation because of genetic lesions (e.g., VHL loss) or metabolite-driven inhibition (e.g., succinate/fumarate accumulation inhibiting α-KG-dependent dioxygenases). These cancer-associated modifiers support conversion of reversible hypoxia-linked PTM shifts into more durable and selectable transcriptional/epigenetic states, thereby coupling mitochondrial redox/metabolite status to malignant phenotypes such as invasion, stemness-like programs, and therapy resistance [16].

Importantly, many hypoxia-associated metabolic features attributed to cancer reflect general solutions to oxygen and redox constraints that are also used by ordinary proliferating or activated cells. Aerobic glycolysis, for example, has been argued to support the anabolic and redox requirements of proliferation rather than being uniquely malignant, and cells engage fermentation when NAD+ demand outstrips ATP demand even in the presence of oxygen. Similarly, hypoxia-induced accumulation of metabolites with “oncometabolite-like” signaling properties (e.g., L-2-hydroxyglutarate) has been described as a broad adaptive feature of hypoxia responses across cell types. In this view, cancer frequently co-opts physiologic hypoxia modules but differs in the chronicity, microenvironmental heterogeneity, and genetic/epigenetic lesion landscape that stabilize and repurpose these modules toward tumor progression [100].

Metabolic pathway crosstalk under ETC limitation through PTM relays

Before considering individual PTM classes, this section outlines recurring metabolic relays through which ETC limitation is predicted to propagate across pathways; the specific PTM mechanisms introduced here are developed below. Within the MRS model, the site of ETC limitation is predicted to shape how metabolic crosstalk is routed. Upstream limitation, exemplified by complex I dysfunction, primarily imposes NADH pressure and restricts NAD+ regeneration [9], [10], whereas limitation at or beyond the ubiquinone junction favors UQH2 accumulation, reverse SDH flux, and succinate formation [11]. These shifts propagate into TCA directionality, pyruvate oxidation and aspartate synthesis, glutamine use, fatty acid oxidation, lipogenesis, and NADPH-thiol buffering. The propagation is relayed through PTMs that read the altered biochemical state: hydroxylation and demethylation read O2 availability and the α-Kg/succinate/L-2HG balance [59], [60], [92], [93], lysine acylations read NAD+ availability and acyl-CoA pressure [78], [101], [102], [103], [104], phosphorylation reads energy stress at the level of acetyl-CoA carboxylase 2 (ACC2) [105], [106], and cysteine-centered PTMs read RET-permissive redox configurations together with nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase (NNT)-linked thiol buffering [81], [13], [107], [108].

One branch of this crosstalk links the ETC to TCA directionality, pyruvate oxidation, and aspartate supply. Loss of respiratory electron-acceptor capacity limits aspartate synthesis and can be bypassed by alternative electron acceptors or engineered NAD+ regeneration [35], [36], [10]. When electron flow is restricted at or beyond the UQ pool, fumarate can serve as an electron acceptor through reverse SDH, generating succinate [11]. Sulfide metabolism provides a specific example of this topology: SQOR reduces the UQ pool during H2S oxidation, while H2S inhibits CIV at higher concentrations; reverse complex II then regenerates CoQ by reducing fumarate to succinate, allowing sulfide oxidation to continue through a fumarate-supported electron sink [55], [56]. Succinate then inhibits prolyl hydroxylases and other α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases [59], [60], while hypoxia-induced L-2HG provides a parallel input into the same dioxygenase network [92], [93]. In this way, altered TCA metabolite handling is relayed into reduced HIF hydroxylation and reduced histone/DNA demethylation. In parallel, the same ETC state is relayed through lysine acylation: complex I deficiency increases mitochondrial protein acetylation [78], SDHA activity is controlled by SIRT3-dependent deacetylation [101], GOT2 acetylation increases malate-aspartate shuttle activity and pancreatic tumor growth [102], and SHMT2 acetylation/succinylation extend the same relay logic to the one-carbon arm [103], [104].

A second branch links ETC limitation to glutamine-to-lipid rerouting and to the balance between fatty acid oxidation and lipogenesis. When pyruvate oxidation is restricted, reductive glutamine metabolism sustains citrate production and lipid synthesis under hypoxia or mitochondrial dysfunction [109]. HIF hydroxylation is a relay step at this node, because HIF output suppresses pyruvate entry into mitochondrial oxidation through PDK1 and suppresses fatty acid β-oxidation through acyl-CoA dehydrogenases [110], [111]; in some contexts, reduced NAD+ also permits SIRT1-sensitive HIF-1α acetylation and further increases HIF output [89]. At the FAO/lipogenesis gate, ACC2-derived malonyl-CoA restrains CPT1-dependent fatty-acid entry, whereas AMPK phosphorylation of ACC lowers malonyl-CoA under energy stress [105], [106]. In parallel, SIRT3-dependent deacetylation activates long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase [112], whereas SIRT5-dependent desuccinylation or deglutarylation supports glutaminolysis through glutaminase and glutamate dehydrogenase 1 [113], [114]. Acetylcarnitine shuttling then provides a route by which mitochondrial acetyl-group handling is transmitted to histone acetylation and lipogenic programs [115].

The NADPH-thiol arm follows the same MRS model. ETC state and protonmotive force influence NNT directionality and thus mitochondrial NADPH availability [81]. This branch is also PTM-mediated: SIRT3-dependent deacetylation of IDH2 supports NADPH-linked redox buffering [116], and loss of this control has been linked to tumor growth [117]. When the ubiquinone pool is highly reduced in a RET-permissive configuration, localized ROS signals are generated at complex I [13] and are detected as cysteine-centered PTMs on respiratory proteins; targeted cysteine modification of complex I in turn alters ETC reactivation and downstream ROS output [107], [108]. Accordingly, ETC limitation is predicted to reshape pathway interactions not only by changing metabolite pools, but by shifting PTM reactions that regulate enzyme activity, pathway directionality, and pathway selection during tumor adaptation.

MRS and lysine acylations

Lysine acetylation and related lysine acylations (including succinylation, malonylation, and glutarylation) are abundant on mitochondrial matrix enzymes and can, in parallel, influence extra-mitochondrial programs (including chromatin) through acyl-group transfer and metabolite exchange [115]. A central mechanistic reason these PTMs are MRS-sensitive is that the dominant mitochondrial “erasers” are NAD+-dependent deacylases: SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5 [118]. SIRT5 is particularly relevant beyond acetylation because it exhibits robust desuccinylase and demalonylase activity [119] and also functions as a deglutarylase [120]. An integrative model for how malonylation, succinylation, and glutarylation reprogram mitochondrial enzyme function is discussed in Ref. [121]. Importantly, deacylation capacity can also be modulated independently of NAD+ economics, for example through regulation of SIRT3 SUMOylation state by sentrin-specific protease 1 [122].

ETC-limited MRS would remodel lysine acylation through two determinants: sirtuin cofactor supply and matrix chemical pressure for acyl transfer (Fig. 2). ETC backpressure shifts NAD+/NADH toward NADH and decreases effective NAD+ availability for SIRT3-, SIRT4-, and SIRT5-dependent deacylation; this favors persistence of acyl marks when deacylation becomes NAD+-limited. In parallel, the mitochondrial matrix favors enzyme-independent lysine acylation: physiologic matrix pH and acyl-CoA concentrations drive lysine acetylation and succinylation in vitro, supporting the view that mitochondrial deacylases oppose a persistent chemical acylation pressure [79], [80]. Changes in protonmotive force and matrix acid-base chemistry are therefore predicted to alter acyl-transfer kinetics as well as eraser activity.

ETC limitation couples NAD + economics, acyl-CoA donor pools, and matrix bioenergetics to remodeling of lysine acylations. Respiratory backpressure (e.g., limited O2 or inhibition of electron flow through the ETC) drives accumulation of NADH and reduction of the ubiquinone pool (↑UQH2, ↓UQ), lowering effective NAD+ availability and decreasing proton pumping with consequent changes in protonmotive force. Reduced NAD+ constrains the major mitochondrial matrix “erasers” SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5, limiting NAD+-dependent deacylation (with production of NAM (nicotinamide) and OAADPr (O-acetyl-ADP-ribose)) and thereby favoring persistence/accumulation of acetyl-, succinyl-, malonyl-, and glutaryl-lysine marks on matrix enzymes. In parallel, ETC-linked rerouting of metabolism reshapes acyl-donor availability and thus the “forward pressure” for acylation: acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA decrease with suppressed TCA flux, whereas glutaryl-CoA increases when electrons from glutaryl-CoA oxidation (via GCDH → ETF/ETFDH) cannot be efficiently transferred into an oxidized UQ pool. Malonyl-CoA is poised for context-dependent changes (energy-stress signaling versus sustained lipogenic demand), influencing fatty-acid synthesis and related pathways. Beyond enzymatic control, ΔpH and Δψm changes are expected to modulate the kinetics of non-enzymatic acyl transfer in the chemically reactive matrix, further tuning steady-state acyl-lysine occupancy. Respiratory limitation also remodels acyl-donor pools rather than uniformly increasing all lysine acyl marks. Acetylcarnitine shuttling transmits mitochondrial acetyl-group dynamics to histone acetylation and lipogenesis [115]. Studies using rotenone showed that complex I inhibition reduces glucose contribution to acetyl-CoA and decreases succinyl-CoA in CoA profiling, whereas hypoxia suppresses fatty acid β-oxidation through HIF-1α and thereby reduces one acetyl-CoA input from fatty acids [123], [124], [111]. Other donors are predicted to move differently. Glutaryl-CoA oxidation transfers electrons through ETF/ETFDH to the UQ pool; a highly reduced UQ pool is therefore predicted to slow glutaryl-CoA clearance and increase glutarylation pressure, in parallel with NAD+-dependent deglutarylation by SIRT5 [125], [120], [126]. Malonyl-CoA remains bidirectional: AMPK phosphorylation lowers acetyl-CoA carboxylase activity under energy stress, whereas reductive glutamine metabolism sustains lipid anabolism in hypoxic or mitochondrially impaired cells [105], [106], [109]. Thus, ETC-limited MRS couples weaker NAD+-dependent deacylation to selective donor-pool redistribution, producing mark- and site-specific acylation outputs.

Site-resolved studies provide especially strong tests linking mitochondrial acylomes to malignant phenotypes, because residue substitutions test whether a single modified lysine alters enzyme function and cancer behavior.

A well-validated node is SOD2, which couples acetylation state to mitochondrial ROS handling and hypoxia signaling outputs. In breast cancer models, SOD2 lysine 68 acetylation supported increased mitochondrial ROS, HIF-2α stabilization, and stemness programs; K68 acetyl-mimetic and deacetylation-mimetic substitutions supported a direct role for this site in tumorigenicity and invasion-associated phenotypes [127]. Complementarily, genetic deletion of SIRT3 increased mitochondrial superoxide and was associated with SOD2 hyperacetylation and reduced activity, establishing a SIRT3-SOD2 deacetylation axis that limits a tumor-permissive oxidative environment in vivo [128], consistent with broader evidence positioning SIRT3 as a SOD2-activating deacetylase [129]. These data support a mechanistic interpretation directly relevant to MRS: if ETC backpressure lowers effective mitochondrial NAD+ and constrains SIRT3 activity, discrete cancer-relevant acetyl-lysine states on matrix antioxidant enzymes accumulate as functional indicators of impaired deacetylation capacity.

MRS-sensitive deacylation also intersects mitochondrial NADPH supply and downstream thiol buffering via acylation control of NADPH-producing enzymes. In a breast-cancer-focused mechanistic study, acetylation of IDH2 at lysine 413 was shown to promote a tumor-permissive phenotype: an acetyl-mimetic substitution reduced enzymatic function, increased ROS and glycolytic bias, and enhanced transformation and xenograft tumor growth [117]. This cancer phenotype study is mechanistically aligned with biochemical evidence that lysine 413 acetylation markedly impairs IDH2 activity and that SIRT3-mediated deacetylation restores function and supports reduced glutathione maintenance under oxidative stress [116]. Thus, these data support a role for IDH2 K413 acetylation in shifting tumor growth capacity and redox buffering in the tested models.

One-carbon metabolism provides a clear example of site- and mark-dependent directionality. In colorectal cancer models, acetylation of serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2 (SHMT2) at lysine 95 suppressed enzyme function and tumor phenotypes; acetylation at this residue disrupted oligomerization and promoted degradation, and site-resolved designs linked acetylation state to xenograft growth and in vivo tumorigenesis readouts [103]. In contrast, serine SHMT2 is controlled by succinylation in a direction that supports proliferation: SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of this enzyme at lysine 280 increased its activity and supported cancer cell growth, particularly under metabolic stress [104]. These two residues illustrate a central theme: MRS-driven constraints on NAD+-dependent deacylation do not predict a uniform “pro-tumor” direction; rather, the net phenotype depends on which site-dominant acylation events are most sensitive in a given tumor context.

The SOD2, IDH2, and SHMT2 cases separate association from mechanism. Each links a defined lysine site to enzyme activity and a tumor-relevant phenotype; the SHMT2 pair also shows that acetylation and succinylation of the same enzyme point in opposite phenotypic directions. These examples argue against treating MRS-linked acylation as a global pro-growth or anti-growth signal.

Additional respiratory and metabolic targets extend this site-specific pattern. SIRT3-deficient tissues showed hyperacetylation of several complex I components and selective inhibition of complex I activity, supporting a feedback route in which impaired deacylation further depresses respiration [130]. SDHA acetylation supports SIRT3-dependent regulation of complex II activity, and GOT2 acetylation at lysine 159 promotes malate-aspartate shuttle activity and pancreatic tumor growth [101], [102].

Non-acetyl acylations controlled by SIRT5 show the same residue- and lineage-dependence. Citrate synthase succinylation suppresses proliferation and migration in colon cancer at lysines 393/395 but supports mitochondrial activity and proliferation in hepatocellular carcinoma at lysine 375 [131], [132]. SIRT5-linked desuccinylation or deglutarylation also regulates glutaminase, glutamate dehydrogenase 1, and MTHFD2 in breast or colorectal cancer models [113], [114], [133]. IDH/SDH-linked hypersuccinylation and emerging malonylation work further support tumor-associated acylome remodeling, but the available evidence argues against a single tumor-wide direction for lysine acylation [134], [135].

Related lysine-linked mitochondrial PTMs fall outside the strict lysine-acylation category but intersect the same NAD and matrix-metabolism variables. Reduced DLAT lipoylation impaired hepatocellular carcinoma xenograft growth, and lipoylated DLAT/DLST act as central substrates in copper-ionophore-induced cuproptosis [136], [137]. SIRT4-linked ADP-ribosylation of glutamate dehydrogenase 1 suppressed prostate cancer proliferation, invasion/migration, and xenograft growth [138]. These examples support a broader point for this section: respiratory limitation connects mitochondrial NAD+ use and matrix chemistry to several lysine-linked PTM channels, not only to canonical acylation.

Many mitochondrial proteins have mapped lysine modifications, including ACADL, ACSS1, HMGCS2, OTC, PPIF, DBT, GCSH, MCCC1, MCCC2, MLYCD, PDHX, CPS1, and GCDH. However, most entries lack direct evidence connecting a defined residue, a defined MRS variable, enzyme activity, and a cancer phenotype. Existing biochemical work supports acylation-dependent regulation for selected metabolic enzymes, including ACADL, HMGCS2, OTC, and GCDH [112], [139], [140], [126]. This gap argues for experiments that combine ETC backpressure measurements (NAD+/NADH, UQ/UQH2, and protonmotive force), site-resolved acyl-occupancy quantification, residue-level perturbation, and tumor-relevant phenotype testing.

Taken together, ETC-limited MRS is predicted to favor higher steady-state lysine acylation in many settings by weakening NAD+-dependent deacylation and altering matrix acid-base chemistry, but it does not predict a uniform acylation phenotype.

Oxygen- and metabolite-sensitive HIF control by proline and asparagine hydroxylation

Hydroxylation is a posttranslational modification installed by iron(II)/α-Kg dioxygenases that require O2. In cancer biology, the most central hydroxylation targets are the HIF-α subunits, which are regulated by the prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD/EGLN) enzymes and by factor inhibiting HIF (FIH). Prolyl hydroxylation at the two canonical von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) recognition sites (at Pro402 and Pro564) promotes binding to the VHL E3 ligase and proteasomal degradation of HIF-1α, whereas asparaginyl hydroxylation (Asn803) restricts transcriptional potency by blocking recruitment of transcriptional coactivators [141]. Quantitative analyses of endogenous HIF-1α hydroxylation under graded hypoxia established that suppression of hydroxylation is site-dependent, with hypoxic inhibition occurring in the order Pro402 > Pro564 > Asn803 [141]. Consistent with this hierarchy, biochemical/kinetic comparisons indicate that FIH reacts with O2 at lower apparent Km than PHD2, helping explain why asparaginyl hydroxylation persists into oxygen ranges where prolyl hydroxylation is already substantially curtailed [22].

Importantly, these hydroxylation switches do not operate in a binary “on/off” mode. Proline-hydroxylated HIF-1α (at both Pro402 and Pro564) is detectable in hypoxic regions across multiple human tumors and xenografts, implying that residual O2 is sufficient to sustain measurable PHD output and that downstream steps (e.g., VHL capacity and/or proteasomal throughput) become rate-limiting even when hydroxylation persists [142].

Within the MRS model, the effective O2 sensitivity of these hydroxylation PTMs is set not solely by ambient pO2, but by the balance between O2 delivery and intracellular O2 consumption (Fig. 3). Mitochondrial respiratory complex IV accounts for most intracellular molecular oxygen reduction, retaining substantial flux at low oxygen tensions because of high apparent oxygen affinity and excess complex IV capacity relative to upstream chain flux [143], [144]. By contrast, many regulatory iron(II)- and α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases that install or erase PTMs exhibit O2 dependencies that place their catalytic output within the physiologic range of intracellular oxygen. For HIF control, the oxygen Michaelis constants of the prolyl hydroxylases have been reported to be in a range consistent with their role as oxygen sensors [20], and cellular measurements demonstrate graded loss of HIF prolyl and asparaginyl hydroxylation under decreasing oxygen [141].

Functionally, multiple experimental systems show that changing mitochondrial respiration modulates these O2-dependent PTM readouts by altering intracellular oxygen distribution, even when extracellular oxygen is held constant. In hypoxia, inhibition of mitochondrial respiration by nitric oxide increased intracellular oxygen signals and promoted PHD-dependent degradation of HIF-1α, consistent with oxygen being redistributed from respiration toward non-respiratory O2-dependent targets [29]. In tumor cell culture settings, inhibition or genetic inactivation of the ETC reduced oxygen consumption, raised effective cellular oxygenation, and reduced HIF-α stabilization at intermediate oxygen, highlighting that dioxygenase outputs are shaped strongly by respiratory oxygen demand [30]. Conversely, increasing mitochondrial oxygen consumption by inducing mitochondrial biogenesis was sufficient to generate intracellular hypoxia at ambient oxygen, stabilizing HIF-1α and activating HIF target genes via reduced PHD activity [31]. These observations support a competition model in which oxygen-dependent PTMs reflect both oxygen supply and mitochondrial oxygen consumption. Consistent with this, HIF-1 signaling actively suppresses mitochondrial oxygen consumption through pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1, increasing intracellular oxygen tension and thereby altering oxygen-dependent enzyme activity [110].

MRS also introduces inhibitory metabolite pressure, and the net effect depends strongly on where electron flow is constrained in the ETC. When respiration is limited at or distal to the UQ junction (e.g., complex III/IV limitation by hypoxia or pharmacologic blockade), the ubiquinone pool becomes highly reduced, and mitochondria route electrons to fumarate as an alternative electron acceptor. In this setting, ubiquinol accumulation supports reverse SDH activity, reducing fumarate to succinate [11], [145]. A chemically distinct but mechanistically convergent case is H2S: sulfide-induced CIV inhibition combined with SQOR-driven CoQ reduction favors the same UQH2-to-fumarate electron disposal route, increasing succinate and thereby predicted to suppress PHD-dependent HIF-α hydroxylation [55], [59]. Succinate accumulation competitively inhibits HIF-α prolyl hydroxylases and supports HIF-α stabilization, linking ETC backpressure/MRS to reduced prolyl hydroxylation-dependent restraint of HIF [59] (Fig. 3). In parallel, fumarate accumulation -as it may occur in fumarate hydratase (FH)-deficiency- also antagonizes PHD output, and metabolite inhibition often impacts the prolyl arm more than the asparaginyl arm: in FH-deficient settings, fumarate causes severe impairment of HIF prolyl but not asparaginyl hydroxylation, underscoring that PHD output is generally more vulnerable to oncometabolite pressure than FIH output [146]. Conceptually, SDH loss phenocopies the succinate-accumulating arm of distal ETC limitation, enabling pseudohypoxic HIF activation even without profound hypoxia [59].

These two MRS-associated inputs -secondary O2 redistribution, which favors more hydroxylation, and succinate/fumarate-mediated dioxygenase inhibition, which favors less hydroxylation-predict ETC-site-specific outcomes relevant to cancer: i) upstream limitation (e.g., complex I) primarily increases upstream reductive pressure, yielding high NADH and decreased O2 consumption; this is predicted to bias toward the O2-sparing arm, preserving or increasing prolyl/asparaginyl hydroxylation and thereby restraining HIF, especially in moderate hypoxia where O2 redistribution is impactful; ii) downstream limitation (i.e., complexes III or IV) maximizes UQ-pool reduction, favors SDH reversal and succinate formation, and is predicted to make metabolite inhibition of PHDs dominant, reducing prolyl hydroxylation with relative sparing of FIH-dependent Asn hydroxylation in some settings; iii) SDH dysfunction favors succinate accumulation directly, supporting PHD inhibition and pseudohypoxic HIF activation even without profound hypoxia [59].

In tumors, where O2 gradients and heterogeneous ETC constraints exist together, MRS is predicted to tune HIF hydroxylation PTMs in either direction depending on the dominant local consequence: (i) O2 redistribution that sustains hydroxylase chemistry versus (ii) inhibitory metabolite pressure that antagonizes it [29].

Because PHDs and FIH are members of the broader α-Kg-dependent dioxygenase family, the same two MRS variables introduced here -oxygen partitioning between complex IV and non-respiratory dioxygenases, and α-Kg/succinate/fumarate-controlled metabolite competition-also apply to chromatin erasers (JmjC histone demethylases and TET DNA dioxygenases). The next section extends this oxygen- and metabolite-based interpretation from HIF hydroxylation PTMs to histone and DNA methylation, emphasizing how respiratory chain limitation is consistent with altered chromatin state over longer timescales than acute signaling PTMs [147], [148].

Lastly, although there are α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases that operate in/around mitochondria (e.g., trimethyllysine dioxygenase in carnitine biosynthesis), during MRS it is expected that β-oxidation flux is redox-limited, so altered carnitine biosynthesis is unlikely to be the primary determinant of acute fatty acid oxidation suppression. However, the carnitine/acylcarnitine system still modulates mitochondrial acyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA buffering, with potential downstream impact on mitochondrial protein acetylation and metabolic flexibility [149], [150], (see section above: MRS and lysine acylations).

Histone and DNA methylation shaped by oxygen and redox through α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases

Histone and DNA methylation are dynamically regulated by methyltransferase “writers” and dioxygenase “erasers,” and this balance is sensitive to mitochondrial redox state because many of the erasers are dioxygenases that require O2 and α-Kg. This includes Jumonji C domain histone lysine demethylases (JmjC-KDMs) and the ten-eleven translocation (TET) family of DNA dioxygenases. As a consequence, conditions that limit O2 availability and/or distort the α-Kg/succinate/fumarate/2-hydroxyglutarate metabolite landscape would rapidly shift chromatin toward a more methylated, transcriptionally reprogrammed state. Within the MRS model, two coupled variables set effective demethylation capacity: (i) oxygen partitioning (delivery vs consumption, dominated by complex IV) and (ii) competitive inhibition or substrate limitation within the α-Kg-dependent dioxygenase reaction network (Fig. 4).

Regarding oxygen limitation and partitioning in the tumor microenvironment, hypoxia rapidly increases repressive histone trimethylation marks in cells and in tumor regions, consistent with inhibition of oxygen-dependent demethylation (Fig. 4); inactivation of lysine demethylase 6B (KDM6B; Jumonji domain containing 3) by low O2 was directly implicated [147]. Tumour hypoxia also reduces TET activity and thereby promotes DNA hypermethylation in cells and tumors [148]. Enzyme kinetic measurements support graded oxygen sensitivity across chromatin dioxygenases by quantifying oxygen Michaelis constants for TET enzymes and for some JmjC histone demethylases (e.g., KDM4A) [24], [23]. Hypoxia also increases trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 27 through inhibition of the oxygen-dependent demethylase lysine demethylase 6A (KDM6A), linking O2 dependence to lineage and differentiation programs that are relevant to tumor cell state [25].

It should be emphasized that “O2 sensitivity” is enzyme-specific across the α-Kg-dependent dioxygenase family. Several JmjC histone demethylases display relatively high Km(O2) and are therefore strongly responsive to physiological oxygen variation, whereas TET1/2 exhibit a lower Km(O2) and retain substantial catalytic capacity until oxygen becomes more limiting [24] [23]. This kinetic diversity helps rationalize why hypoxia rapidly elevates specific repressive histone marks (e.g., H3K27me3 via limitation of KDM6-family demethylation capacity) while DNA demethylation changes are more locus- and context-dependent, and why respiration-driven O2 partitioning would exert distinct effects on different chromatin PTM erasers.

A second route by which MRS influences methylation is metabolite competition, which becomes particularly relevant when mitochondrial electron flow is constrained and tricarboxylic acid metabolite handling is perturbed. As detailed above for HIF hydroxylation, distal ETC limitation favors SDH reversal and succinate accumulation [11], [145]. Succinate and fumarate competitively inhibit α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases, including both JmjC histone demethylases and TET enzymes [60], supporting a route from respiratory chain limitation to suppressed demethylation capacity. Critically, metabolite-driven inhibition and O2 limitation are not independent: succinate/fumarate inhibition of α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases is weak at supraphysiologic oxygenation but becomes much more apparent as O2 is lowered into tissue-relevant ranges, implying that hypoxic tumor regions amplify the epigenetic consequences of succinate pressure [151]. Thus, in tumors, the combination of oxygen limitation and respiratory-chain-associated succinate/fumarate burden is predicted to support hypermethylation-like chromatin shifts. This rationale also applies to sulfide-induced MRS. In sulfide-treated colon cells, succinate accumulation occurs together with depletion of malate, aspartate, glutamate, and α-Kg [49], a pattern consistent with fumarate consumption by reverse complex II and α-Kg diversion toward reductive carboxylation. Because succinate and fumarate competitively inhibit JmjC histone demethylases and TET DNA dioxygenases [60], sulfide-triggered succinate pressure provides a plausible route from reduced sulfur metabolism to chromatin PTM remodeling.

Metabolite-driven dioxygenase inhibition supports durable epigenetic remodeling in cancers with mitochondrial enzyme lesions, and these genetic contexts serve as examples of sustained metabolite pressure resembling transient metabolite excursions during MRS. SDH-mutant paraganglioma established an SDH-linked hypermethylator phenotype tied to succinate accumulation and inhibition of α-Kg-dependent histone and DNA demethylases, demonstrated by the finding that α -Kg supplementation reversed elevated nuclear 5-methylcytosine signals in SDH-deficient cells [61]. Work in SDHB-deficient tumors and models further supported DNA hypermethylation via TET inhibition, separating epigenetic effects from pseudohypoxic signaling [152]. In parallel, fumarate hydratase (FH) loss in renal cancer models linked fumarate accumulation to inhibition of TET-mediated demethylation at regulatory regions controlling anti-metastatic microRNA programs, inducing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition-like transcription and increased migratory traits [73]. These SDH/FH syndromes support the principle that mitochondrial metabolite imbalance suppresses α-Kg-dependent demethylation chemistry; in the MRS-centered interpretation, non-genetic succinate/fumarate excursions produced by respiratory chain limitation are predicted to push chromatin in the same direction, especially in hypoxic tumor zones.

Regarding the scenario of an MRS-native “2-HG route” without IDH mutation, 2-hydroxyglutarate accumulates in IDH-wildtype contexts relevant to respiratory chain limitation and hypoxia. Under low O2 and/or ETC restriction, increased NADH and altered redox poise favor enzymatic reduction of α-Kg to L-2HG through promiscuous substrate use by enzymes such as malate dehydrogenase, generating an MRS-linked dioxygenase-inhibitory metabolite pressure that is mechanistically parallel to succinate/fumarate competition [92], [93]. L-2HG inhibits α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases, including chromatin demethylases, and in tumor-relevant IDH-wildtype contexts where L-2HG accumulates, restoring L-2HG clearance capacity (e.g., via L-2-hydroxyglutarate dehydrogenase reconstitution) increases 5-hydroxymethylcytosine and reduces repressive histone methylation marks, consistent with relief of TET/JmjC inhibition [153]. Beyond metabolite excess, α-Kg depletion itself reduces demethylation and produces hypermethylation phenotypes even without SDH/FH mutation, consistent with substrate limitation of TET and JmjC activity [154].

In aggregate, MRS due to respiratory chain limitation is poised to remodel histone and DNA methylation by (i) redistributing O2 away from oxygen-dependent demethylation chemistry in hypoxic tumor regions, or in some settings of upstream ETC limitation redistributing O2 toward these enzymes by sparing respiratory oxygen demand, and (ii) biasing mitochondrial metabolite pools toward competitive inhibition of α-Kg-dependent dioxygenases by succinate, fumarate, and L-2HG [147], [148], [154]. The likely cancer impact in many settings is a shift toward more persistent methylation states that stabilize cell-state programs (lineage, differentiation, EMT-like traits, immune interactions) and thereby alter tumor behavior over longer timescales than acute signaling PTMs, with the direction and advantage of that shift depending on which gene-regulatory programs are selected in a given tumor context [73], [152].

Cysteine-centered redox modifications as fast relays from ETC limitation to signaling

Cysteine residues undergo a set of reversible PTMs that directly change thiol oxidation state, bonding patterns, or mixed-thiol adduct formation, allowing protein function to respond rapidly to local redox chemistry. Examples include disulfide bond formation and reshuffling, S-glutathionylation, and sulfenic acid formation as an intermediate oxidation state [155], [156], [157]. H2S and its oxidation products can also participate in protein persulfidation/S-sulfhydration, but in the present MRS framework this sulfur chemistry is treated mainly as an upstream ETC-modifying input, because the strongest mechanistic connection to respiratory-chain-limited PTM remodeling is H2S-induced CIV inhibition, SQOR-dependent CoQ reduction, reverse complex II activity, and succinate-mediated dioxygenase inhibition rather than a generalized increase in cysteine persulfidation [158], [159], [56]. At the ETC itself, complex I contains reactive cysteine “redox hubs” that are directly modified by local ROS chemistry: complex I superoxide-generating turnover drives thiyl-radical chemistry and S-glutathionylation at defined cysteines [160], and site-restricted complex I ROS may reversibly oxidize a single thiol residue to gate downstream physiological outputs [108]. Conversely, targeted cysteine PTMs on complex I suppress pathological ROS bursts, as exemplified by selective S-nitrosation of ND3 Cys39 slowing complex I reactivation during high ROS [107]. Mitochondria-derived H2O2 can also oxidatively inactivate canonical cancer signaling nodes such as PTEN, linking ROS to PI3K/AKT activation and angiogenic outputs via a cysteine-centered redox switch [161]. Because these modifications form and resolve on rapid time scales and are directly driven by thiol and small-molecule redox couples, they are well positioned to translate mitochondrial redox state into changes in signaling, transcription, and metabolism.

MRS due to respiratory chain limitation reshapes cysteine PTM occupancy through two convergent influences. First, ETC state couples to glutathione redox buffering, because mitochondrial NADPH supply (and thus glutathione/thioredoxin reducing power) is linked to electron flux and to protonmotive force-dependent systems that support reductive capacity, such as the NNT (Fig. 5); shifts in reducing power change depending how readily mixed disulfides such as S-glutathionylation accumulate or are cleared. In cancer settings with chronic complex I limitation, adaptive remodeling of glutathione metabolism and mitochondrial thiol availability has been observed, consistent with thiol buffering capacity being selectable during respiratory constraint [74], [162]. Second, in RET-permissive configurations, highly reduced ETC states generate compartmental ROS, creating localized oxidant signals (Fig. 5) that selectively install cysteine oxidation intermediates and redox adducts rather than uniformly oxidizing the proteome. RET-dependent superoxide production requires a highly reduced ubiquinone pool together with a high protonmotive force (Δp), particularly high Δψm; these conditions are typically achieved when succinate oxidation drives electron entry into the UQ pool. Conversely, RET-driven superoxide is suppressed by complex I blockade or by lowering Δψm, both of which disrupt the electrochemical and redox configuration required for reverse electron transfer [13], [163], [164]. RET-derived ROS has also been shown to function as a regulated signaling output in mammalian cells using genetic RET deficiency, reinforcing that an over-reduced electron supply is converted into discrete oxidant microdomains rather than global oxidative collapse [165]. Consistent with the broader “reductive-to-oxidative” paradox, thiol antioxidant loading provokes rapid matrix oxidation through the ETC, emphasizing that increases in reducing equivalents do not preclude rapid mitochondrial oxidation signaling [166].

Overall, MRS from respiratory chain limitation is expected to repattern cysteine-centered PTMs rather than drive a uniform increase or decrease, because it simultaneously perturbs reducing capacity and localized ROS production. The cancer impact of that repatterning is that cysteine PTMs are positioned as rapid molecular switches tuning redox-sensitive signaling and metabolic control nodes during respiratory constraint. In support of this, RET has been demonstrated as an active metabolic state in tumor stem-like contexts, where modulating RET alters ROS readouts and downstream signaling outputs alongside NAD+/NADH balance [12]. Separately, oxidation of a discrete NDUFS1 cysteine “redox hub” in lung cancer models promotes respirasome incorporation and rewires Δψm, respiration, and sensitivity to ROS-inducing chemotherapeutics, with proteomic detection of Cys92 overoxidation (sulfonation) under ROS-elevating conditions [167]. This perspective also suggests interpretive cautions for cancer biology: agents often used to perturb mitochondrial dynamics/metabolism (e.g., mdivi-1, metformin) directly modulate complex I/RET-ROS and thereby shift cysteine PTM signaling states [168], [163].

Phosphorylation rewired by MRS through phosphatase redox control, and ATP supply

Phosphorylation states emerge from the balance between kinase activity, phosphatase counter-regulation, and pathway compartmentalization. MRS caused by respiratory chain limitation would shift that balance through two interacting routes: (i) redox regulation of cysteine-based phosphatases, which are reversibly inhibited by oxidant signals generated downstream of ETC redox backpressure; and (ii) energetic limitation, because ETC inhibition restricts ATP supply and thereby cap phosphorylation capacity even when upstream signaling inputs are present.

In cancer-relevant settings, respiratory chain impairment has been linked to concrete phosphorylation outputs. In respiration-deficient ρ− leukemia and lymphoma clones, elevated NADH correlated with increased phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473 and Thr308, and acute ETC inhibition recapitulated its activation [169]. Consistent with respiratory impairment biasing growth and survival signaling, driving a reduced UQ pool to favor complex I reverse electron transfer (RET) activated non-canonical Notch–mTORC2 signaling with increased AKT Ser473 phosphorylation in glioblastoma stem-like cells and other cancer models [12]. In the same work, genetic disruption of Notch-dependent RET and RET-targeting pharmacologic probes altered this AKT phosphorylation readout alongside RET-linked redox remodeling, supporting mechanistic coupling between reduced UQ/RET states and phosphorylation network rewiring [12].

At the same time, ETC limitation also suppresses phosphorylation outputs via energy limitation (Fig. 5). In melanoma, broad ETC inhibition decreased ATP and produced an energy crisis, associated with reduced cyclin-dependent kinase 1 phosphorylation of dynamin-related protein 1 in Ser616, promoting mitochondrial fusion, and decreased cyclin-dependent kinase 2 activity with G1 arrest [170]. This illustrates that MRS simultaneously activates certain signaling phosphorylation pathways (through redox relays downstream of ETC backpressure) while constraining other phosphorylation-dependent processes when ATP becomes limiting.

Overall, MRS due to respiratory chain limitation would reprogram phosphorylation networks rather than uniformly increasing or decreasing phosphorylation. By coupling ETC redox state (including reduced NADH/UQ pools and RET-linked ROS propensity) to phosphatase sensitivity and imposing an ATP-driven ceiling on kinase outputs, MRS tilts cells toward pro-survival circuits such as AKT in some contexts while blunting cell-cycle phosphorylation programs in others [169], [170].

Ubiquitin-mediated remodeling at mitochondria under reductive stress

Within a mitochondria-centered ubiquitin response, MRS is linked to a defined reductive state sensing pathway that is triggered not by high ROS, but by low ROS accompanying low ETC activity. The clearest example is the CUL2-FEM1B reductive stress response, in which low ETC activity and low ROS promote ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of folliculin interacting protein 1 (FNIP1) at the mitochondrial translocase of the outer membrane (TOM) import gate [171]. Structural and biochemical work provided a direct molecular mechanism for reductive state culminating in ubiquitination: zinc acts as a molecular glue that enables preferential binding of reduced FNIP1 by FEM1B, creating redox-dependent substrate recognition that feeds into ubiquitin-mediated turnover [84]. The cancer relevance of this pathway has been presented as plausible through broader links between antioxidant/reductive states and tumor progression, and through the possibility that excessive pathway engagement imposes proliferative costs, although direct in vivo demonstrations that manipulating this specific circuit is sufficient to control tumor progression are limited [172], [171].

ADP-ribosylation under respiratory chain limitation through NAD partitioning and competition with sirtuins

ADP-ribosylation attaches adenosine diphosphate ribose units to proteins as mono-ADP-ribosylation (MARylation) or poly-ADP-ribosylation (PARylation) and consumes NAD+ as the obligate donor. This places ADP-ribosylation in direct competition with other NAD+-dependent PTM systems, most notably sirtuin deacylation, and makes NAD supply, regeneration, and compartmentalization a mechanistic hinge for how MRS is routed into PTM remodeling.

An obvious constraint is that NAD is compartmentalized, and mitochondrial NAD+ is not simply a passive reflection of the cytosolic pool. Identification of solute carrier family 25 member 51 (SLC25A51) as a mammalian mitochondrial NAD+ transporter established mitochondrial import of intact NAD+ [173]. This matters for MRS because ETC limitation changes NADH oxidation and thus alter NAD redistribution between compartments. In human cells, rotenone-induced respiratory chain inhibition increased mitochondrial protein ADP-ribosylation and increased mitochondrial NAD+ levels, interpreted as compensatory NAD+ uptake during impaired respiratory NAD+ regeneration [14]. Earlier biochemical work also suggested that respiratory inhibition modulates mitochondrial ADP-ribosylation chemistry, with cyanide stimulating ADP-ribosylation of multiple mitochondrial proteins in rat liver mitochondria preparations [174].

Within mitochondria, ADP-ribosylation has been linked to an identifiable writer node and crosstalk with lysine acylation control. Neuralized-like protein 4 (NEURL4) was implicated as a mitochondrial ADP-ribosyltransferase candidate: NEURL4 loss reduced mitochondrial ADP-ribosylation signals and decreased mitochondrial protein acetylation, consistent with mitochondrial ADP-ribosylation acting as an NAD+ sink that competes with NAD+-dependent deacylation [175].

MRS-linked lactate accumulation and lactylation-dependent PTM remodeling in cancer

The preceding sections considered mitochondrial NAD+ as a limiting substrate. However, respiratory chain limitation also diverts NAD redox pressure toward lactate, creating an additional NAD-linked route to PTM remodeling. During ETC-limited MRS, inefficient respiratory oxidation of NADH reduces effective NAD+ regeneration, whereas glycolysis requires NAD+ at the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) step. Lactate dehydrogenase-dependent reduction of pyruvate to lactate regenerates cytosolic NAD+ and sustains glycolytic flux during respiratory redox backpressure. Luengo et al. [40] showed that aerobic fermentation arises when NAD+ demand exceeds ATP demand and mitochondrial electron transport fails to regenerate NAD+ at the required rate. This interpretation aligns with cancer settings in which oxygen limitation, complex I dysfunction, or pharmacologic respiratory inhibition shifts pyruvate away from mitochondrial oxidation and toward lactate dehydrogenase-dependent lactate production.

Several cancer-relevant observations support this MRS-to-lactate link. In HT29 colon adenocarcinoma cells, Vitvitsky et al. [57] showed that sulfide stimulated aerobic glycolysis and lactate production, and that this response depended on the mitochondrial rather than cytosolic NADH pool. In breast cancer cells, Yu et al. [176] reported that mtDNA-depleted T47D ρ0 cells showed reduced oxygen consumption and ATP production together with significantly increased lactate production, providing a tumor cell example of respiratory impairment coupled to lactate accumulation. Together, these studies identify lactate as a redox outlet for ETC-limited NADH pressure rather than a passive glycolytic byproduct.

Lactate becomes PTM-relevant most directly through lysine lactylation. Zhang et al. [177] identified histone lysine lactylation as a lactate-derived epigenetic modification: exogenous l-lactate increased histone lactylation, isotope-labeled 13C3-l-lactate was incorporated into histone lactyl marks, and hypoxia or the complex I inhibitor rotenone increased intracellular lactate and histone lactylation, whereas LDH blockade or LDHA/LDHB loss reduced the mark. Isomer-resolved work further refined this conclusion. Zhang et al. [178] established lysine L-lactylation as the dominant histone lactylation isomer induced by glycolysis, and Gao et al. [179] showed that hypoxia preferentially increases lysine L-lactylation rather than D-lactylation or Nε-carboxyethyl-lysine. Although Gaffney et al. [180] described non-enzymatic lysine lactoylation of glycolytic enzymes through methylglyoxal/glyoxalase chemistry, the tumor-hypoxia/glycolysis-linked histone signal is best treated as L-lactylation.

The donor and writer machinery for lactate-derived lactylation has also become more tangible. Zhu et al. [181] identified ACSS2 as a lactyl-CoA synthetase and coupled ACSS2-derived lactyl-CoA to KAT2A-mediated histone lactylation, gene expression, and tumor immune evasion. Liu et al. [182] identified nuclear GTPSCS as another lactyl-CoA synthetase that cooperates with p300 to promote H3K18la, GDF15 expression, glioma proliferation, and radioresistance.

Several recent cancer studies connect lactate-dependent lactylation to malignant phenotypes. Breast cancer provides clear tumor-cell examples: Hou et al. [183] demonstrated that KCNK1 activates LDHA, increases lactate and H3K18la, and promotes proliferation, migration, invasion, xenograft growth, and lung metastasis; Pandkar et al. [184] linked high glycolytic lactate to H3K18la-dependent c-MYC upregulation and downstream SRSF10-dependent splicing changes. In colorectal cancer under hypoxia, Zhang et al. [185] identified HIF-1α/LDHA-dependent lactate accumulation and histone lactylation as a metabolic-epigenetic switch promoting stemness gene expression; Xiong et al. [186] showed that tumor lactate induces H3K18la-dependent METTL3 expression in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, enhancing m6A-dependent JAK1/STAT3 signaling and immunosuppression.

Other tumor models extend this lactate-PTM axis beyond breast and colorectal cancer. In hepatocellular carcinoma, Du et al. [187] linked histone lactylation-driven YTHDC1 expression to lipid metabolic remodeling and tumor progression, while Pan et al. [188] reported suppression of liver cancer stem-cell tumorigenicity by targeting lactate/histone lactylation. In lung cancer, Zhang et al. [189] reported H3K18la-dependent activation of the POM121/MYC/PD-L1 pathway and immune escape in non-small cell lung cancer, and Yan et al. [190] showed that hypoxia-associated SOX9 lactylation promotes stemness, migration, and invasion. The non-histone arm is exemplified by Chen et al. [191], who showed that NBS1 K388 lactylation supports homologous recombination DNA repair and chemotherapy resistance; lowering lactate production reduced NBS1 lactylation and restored sensitivity.

Nevertheless, not all lactate-driven PTM effects are lactylation. In prostate cancer, Ippolito et al. [192] showed that CAF-derived lactate supports lipid metabolic rewiring and provides acetyl moieties for histone acetylation, with inhibition of the loop reducing tumor growth and metastasis. In intestinal tumor organoids, Nguyen et al. [193] showed that lactate increases histone acetylation, activates MYC through BRD4-dependent chromatin accessibility, suppresses differentiation, and drives dedifferentiation into a proliferative cancer stem-cell state. These studies support a secondary lactate-histone acetylation route.

Indirect routes from MRS to PTM remodeling: protein arginine methylation

The PTM systems discussed above are mechanistically close to MRS because their chemistry directly uses O2, NAD+, acyl-CoA donors, ATP, or thiol redox buffering. Protein arginine methylation substantiated by protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) are not primary sensors of the redox variables that define MRS. PRMTs transfer methyl groups from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to the guanidino nitrogens of arginine residues, generating monomethylarginine, asymmetric dimethylarginine, or symmetric dimethylarginine marks depending on the PRMT class [194], [195]. Thus, PRMTs are SAM-dependent methyltransferases, not NAD+-, NADH-, NADPH-, ubiquinone-, Δψm-, or ROS-dependent enzymes. In the MRS setting, in which respiratory chain limitation shifts NAD+/NADH, NADPH/NADP+, ubiquinone redox poise, ROS microdomains, Δψm, and metabolite pools [196], these variables are therefore expected to affect arginine methylation only through longer and indirect routes.

One indirect route is the SAM/SAH methylation-potential axis. PRMT reactions consume SAM and generate S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), and SAH accumulation inhibits methyltransferase activity. In HepG2 cells, it has been shown that increasing SAH decreased the SAM/SAH ratio and reduced protein-arginine methylation, supporting the general causal logic that one-carbon stress or altered methylation potential may change PRMT-dependent methylarginine marks [197]. Protein arginine methylation may be particularly sensitive to SAH accumulation, because experiments on endothelial cells showed that intracellular adenosylhomocysteine accumulation inhibited protein arginine methylation more readily than DNA methylation, without requiring reduced PRMT1 or PRMT5 expression [198]. In an MRS setting, this does not create a direct NADH affecting PRMT mechanism; rather, it creates a second-order route in which altered NAD/nicotinamide and one-carbon metabolism could reshape SAM/SAH and thereby PRMT output.

A specific NAD-linked version of this route is nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT). NNMT methylates nicotinamide using SAM, producing 1-methylnicotinamide and SAH; in cancer cells, NNMT was shown to create a “methylation sink” by consuming SAM-derived methyl units and lowering cellular methylation potential [199]. In ovarian cancer stroma, NNMT expression in cancer-associated fibroblasts depleted SAM, reduced histone methylation, and supported cancer-associated fibroblast differentiation and cancer progression [200]. This NNMT route is relevant because NAD+ turnover produces nicotinamide, while respiratory chain limitation changes NAD+/NADH partitioning and NAD+ consumption [196]. However, the causal claim remains limited: the existing evidence supports NAD/nicotinamide–SAM/SAH coupling as a plausible route to altered methylation potential, not direct proof that MRS itself alters PRMT-dependent arginine methylation in solid cancer.

A second indirect route is redox control of PRMT catalytic state, most clearly shown for PRMT1. Oxidized PRMT1 exhibits a decreased methyltransferase activity, reduction can restore activity, and H2O2 causes concentration-dependent PRMT1 inhibition through oxidation of one or more cysteine residues to sulfenic acid [201]. This route becomes relevant to MRS only when the reductive state generates an oxidant component. Such a reductive-to-oxidant output is plausible under respiratory chain configurations that favor RET, where a highly reduced ubiquinone pool together with high Δψm drives complex I superoxide/H2O2 production [13], and during succinate-driven reperfusion-like RET states [164]. It is also relevant to cancer because cysteine/N-acetylcysteine treatment of glioblastoma cells was reported to induce mitochondrial H2O2 production and reductive stress while reducing growth and mitochondrial oxygen consumption [202]. Nevertheless, this remains an inferred PRMT link: PRMT1 redox inhibition is established biochemically, and MRS-linked H2O2 generation is established in selected contexts, but a complete solid-cancer sequence of MRS leading to PRMT1 oxidation that alters arginine methylation and as an extension of this, tumor phenotype, has not yet been demonstrated.

A third route is NAD+-dependent regulation of PRMTs or PRMT-associated proteins by sirtuins. PRMTs themselves do not use NAD+, but sirtuins -and thus NAD+ availability-tune proteins that regulate PRMT stability, complex assembly, or catalytic competence [203]. For PRMT1, p300/SIRT1-dependent acetylation–deacetylation was reported to regulate an acetyldegron controlling PRMT1 stability through SCFFBXL17 recognition [204]. For PRMT5, SIRT7 deacetylates WDR77/MEP50, weakens the WDR77-PRMT5 interaction, reduces PRMT5 transmethylase complex activity, decreases H4R3me2, and suppresses HCT116 colon cancer cell growth and migration [205]. A second PRMT5-centered example is even closer to cancer metabolism: SIRT7-mediated desuccinylation of PRMT5 at K387 increases PRMT5 methyltransferase activity, promotes SREBP1a arginine methylation, and supports lipid metabolism reprogramming, tumor growth, and metastasis [206]. In an MRS setting, reduced effective NAD+ availability could therefore influence PRMT-dependent arginine methylation through sirtuin-regulated PRMT complexes.

A fourth route is mitochondrial localization, especially for PRMT5. It was recently reported that a fraction of PRMT5 localizes to mitochondria and symmetrically dimethylates TFAM, with PRMT5 loss reducing mitochondrial symmetric dimethylarginine signal, mtDNA copy number, mitochondrial transcripts, oxidative phosphorylation, and respiratory capacity [207]. Recombinant PRMT5 import into mitochondria was strictly dependent on mitochondrial membrane potential [207]. The supported causal sequence is therefore: altered Δψm leads to altered mitochondrial PRMT5 import/localization that influences mitochondrial SDMA-marked substrates and thus altered TFAM methylation, mtDNA maintenance and respiration. Because ETC dysfunction, hypoxia/anoxia, and reductive stress alter Δψm, this observation is consistent with an MRS-to-mitochondrial arginine methylation route, although this has not been demonstrated in a solid tumor model.

The demethylation side appears closer to MRS chemistry than the PRMT writer side, but it is also less settled. Canonical arginine “erasers” are less clearly established than PRMT writers, and JMJD6 in particular has been discussed as a case of conflicting catalytic assignments [208]. However, biochemical work supports the ability of a subset of JmjC-domain lysine demethylases to demethylate Nω-methylarginine residues [209], and newer work identifies KDM3A and KDM4A as mitochondrial arginine demethylases that remove CARM1-dependent IDH2 R188me2a, restoring IDH2 activity and increasing mitochondrial membrane potential and oxygen consumption [210]. This matters for MRS because JmjC enzymes are Fe(II)-, O2-, and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenases, with activity sensitive to oxygen concentration and the α-ketoglutarate/succinate/fumarate/2-hydroxyglutarate metabolite landscape [211], [23], [212]. MRS, ETC dysfunction, and hypoxia alter these variables, including through hypoxia/MRS-linked L-2HG generation [93], [92].

Overall, protein arginine methylation is an example of a PTM class that MRS likely reaches only through long and indirect biochemical wiring. The most plausible routes are: altered SAM/SAH methylation potential, NNMT-driven nicotinamide methylation, ROS-sensitive PRMT1 cysteine oxidation, NAD+/sirtuin-dependent regulation of PRMT complexes, Δψm-dependent mitochondrial PRMT5 localization, and metabolite/O2-sensitive JmjC-type arginine demethylation.

Integrative view: from MRS configuration to cancer phenotype

Across these PTM classes, the common denominator is not the identity of the modification but the biochemical variable that limits its turnover. MRS supplies these variables in a constrained but configuration-dependent order. Acute changes in NADH/UQ redox state and RET-permissive ROS microdomains are predicted to rapidly alter cysteine PTMs, phosphatase activity, and phosphorylation outputs. More sustained NAD+ limitation, acyl-CoA remodeling, and matrix pH changes remodel lysine acylomes and NAD+-consuming PTM systems. Persistent oxygen limitation and oncometabolite pressure alter HIF hydroxylation and chromatin demethylation, thereby stabilizing transcriptional and epigenetic states over longer timescales. Finally, diversion of NADH pressure into lactate supports a route from respiratory limitation to lactate-derived PTMs such as histone and non-histone lactylation. This time- and chemistry-resolved view explains why MRS is associated with apparently opposite cancer phenotypes. In one context, MRS-linked PTM remodeling supports HIF activity, stemness-like programs, invasion, immune evasion, or therapy resistance. In another, the same class of redox pressure impairs ETC function, limits ATP or aspartate supply, destabilizes central metabolic enzymes, or activates growth-restrictive stress responses. The coherent argument is therefore not that MRS is intrinsically tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive. Rather, MRS specifies which PTM reactions become constrained, and tumor context selects which constrained node becomes phenotypically dominant.

This interpretation also defines a practical standard for the field. A PTM change observed during hypoxia should be called MRS-mediated only when accompanied by evidence of respiratory-chain redox backpressure or its defining consequences, such as altered NAD+/NADH, UQ/UQH2, Δψm/ΔpH, ATP, NADPH/thiol buffering, succinate/fumarate/L-2HG, or RET-linked ROS. Conversely, perturbations that alter these variables independently of oxygen availability provide stronger tests of MRS as an upstream determinant of PTM remodeling.

Conclusions

Respiratory chain limitation in tumors arising from hypoxic microenvironments, intrinsic ETC defects, and signal- or therapy-imposed respiratory inhibition could remodel posttranslational modifications through ETC-linked MRS, defined by restricted electron flux and respiratory redox backpressure. This should be interpreted as a spatially and temporally resolved tumor state rather than a constitutive property of every malignant cell. The fraction of tumor experiencing oxygen-limited MRS varies with oxygen delivery, oxygen consumption, vascular dynamics, respiratory-chain genotype, and therapy exposure; measured hypoxic fractions provide useful clinical anchors but do not substitute for direct MRS measurements, including NADH/NAD+, ubiquinone redox poise, Δψm/ΔpH, ATP, NADPH/thiol buffering, succinate/fumarate/L-2HG, and spatially resolved ROS. Although the oxygen- and redox-imposed biochemical constraints discussed here are not unique to cancer cells, tumors differ in the chronicity, spatial heterogeneity, and genetic fixation of these constraints, which support stabilization of PTM outputs and coupling to malignant phenotypes. In such conditions, upstream carrier pools of the ETC become more reduced (increased NADH and/or UQH2 depending on the site of the bottleneck), with secondary consequences that are mechanistically positioned to alter PTM occupancy: diminished effective NAD+ availability, altered compartmental NAD handling, changes in protonmotive force and matrix acid–base chemistry, ATP limitation, and -when the redox configuration permits RET-localized ROS generation. A central inference of the present review is that the position of ETC constraint matters: proximal limitations bias toward NADH pressure and NAD+ restriction, whereas distal limitations bias toward a highly reduced ubiquinone pool with greater propensity for RET-linked oxidant microdomains and redox-dependent metabolite rerouting. Accordingly, MRS is best considered a proximal biochemical state that predicts which PTM-writing and PTM-erasing reactions are favored under respiratory constraint.

Within this MRS-centered backbone, multiple PTM systems are rationalized as redox- and energy-sensitive outputs of respiratory backpressure. First, NAD+ economics provide a unifying constraint across NAD+-coupled PTMs: limitation of NAD+ availability suppresses mitochondrial sirtuin-dependent deacylation while simultaneously influencing NAD+-consuming ADP-ribosylation through shifts in mitochondrial NAD+ import and intramitochondrial NAD+ spending. Second, MRS-linked changes in protonmotive force and matrix pH are predicted to modulate the steady-state “forward pressure” for chemically favored lysine acylation in the matrix, thereby altering the balance between nonenzymatic acylation and enzymatic deacylation. Third, thiol-buffering capacity is coupled to ETC state via NADPH supply and protonmotive-force-dependent transhydrogenation, supporting the prediction that MRS repatterns cysteine-centered redox PTMs through altered glutathione/thioredoxin reducing power, while RET-permissive configurations superimpose spatially restricted oxidant signals that selectively install thiol oxidation intermediates and mixed-thiol adducts. Fourth, phosphorylation networks are rewired by MRS through two nonredundant constraints: redox control of cysteine-based phosphatases by localized oxidant chemistry downstream of ETC backpressure, and an energetic ceiling imposed by ATP limitation that caps kinase outputs even when upstream signaling inputs persist. Finally, MRS-associated low-ROS/low-ETC-activity states are consistent with mitochondria-centered ubiquitin remodeling through reductive-state sensing programs, exemplified by the CUL2–FEM1B pathway at the mitochondrial import gate. Collectively, these mechanisms support the conclusion that MRS does not impose a uniform directional shift in PTMs; rather, it redefines the permissible reaction space for PTM writing and erasing in a compartment- and site-dependent manner, such that the phenotypic outcome is shaped by the redox- and cofactor-sensitive nodes that dominate the limiting behavior of the system.

A stringent implication for the field is that causal attribution must be formulated at the level of proximal drivers. In tumors, hypoxia frequently coexists with MRS, but PTM changes observed under low oxygen cannot be assumed to be MRS-mediated unless electron backpressure and its defining biochemical consequences are explicitly demonstrated. The most informative experimental strategies will therefore be those that (i) quantify MRS state variables (NAD+/NADH, ubiquinone redox poise, Δψm/ΔpH, ATP, NADPH/thiol buffering, and spatially resolved ROS where relevant) alongside site-resolved PTM occupancy, and (ii) perturb ETC backpressure independently of oxygen availability to separate redox backpressure effects from oxygen-substrate effects when both are plausible. Such approaches provide the strongest basis for establishing a rigorous MRS → PTM → phenotype chain and for identifying which PTM nodes constitute actionable liabilities, adaptive mechanisms, or growth-restrictive consequences under respiratory chain limitation in cancer.
