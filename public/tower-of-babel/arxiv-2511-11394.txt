Relaxation to an Ideal Chern Band through Coupling to a Markovian Bath
Bruno Mera, Tomoki Ozawa

Abstract
We propose a microscopic, weak-coupling mechanism by which generic Chern bands relax toward ideal bands. We consider coupling interacting electrons to a Caldeira-Leggett like Ohmic bosonic bath. Using the Born-Markov approximation, Slater determinant states of a Chern band under Hartree-Fock approximation evolve toward Slater determinant states corresponding to an ideal Chern band. We validate our proposal by performing numerical simulation of a massive Dirac model, showing that the Berry curvature and quantum metric indeed co-evolve to saturate the trace condition. Our proposal provides a concrete dissipative route to realize ideal Chern bands, a fundamental building block for the stabilization of fractional Chern insulators.

Although the importance of the ideal Chern bands and generalized Landau levels have been increasingly appreciated, how one can obtain such bands, either theoretically or experimentally, remains to be nontrivial. Theoretically, one needs special models which fulfill the ideal conditions. Experimentally, there is no known simple method to obtain ideal Chern bands or generalized Landau levels.
In this paper, we propose a generic mechanism under which ideal Chern bands or generalized Landau levels can be obtained. We show that, by coupling weakly interacting electrons to Caldeira-Leggett type Markovian bath, mean-field (Slater determinant) states evolve into states which minimize the Dirichlet energy functional determined from the quantum geometry. When the Dirichlet energy is dominant over the kinetic energy, the resulting state one obtains in a long-time limit is nothing but the ideal Chern band. Numerical simulations for the massive Dirac model confirm this evolution, with the Dirichlet energy approaching the topological lower bound
\pi|\mathcal{C}|
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the Chern number of the occupied states, as the Berry curvature and quantum metric co-evolve toward the ideal-band condition. In this picture, ideal Chern bands correspond to the global minima of the Dirichlet energy, while generalized Landau levels are stationary points.
We note that recently, in Refs.
Yu et al. 2025
;
Fukui 2025
, gradient flow with respect to the Dirichlet energy of the Bloch projector was proposed to achieve the ideal-band conditions. Compared to these recent works, our work provides a concrete dissipative mechanism through which ideal bands can be achieved.

Model and projection to Slater dynamics. —

We first consider, for demonstration, a single-site model and later extend it to a lattice model. The model consists of a system of interacting electrons coupled to a Caldeira-Legget like bosonic bath,

\widehat{H}=\widehat{H}_{S}+\widehat{H}_{B}+\widehat{H}_{I}
,
where

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\,c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,l}v_{ijkl}\,c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k},

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\Omega_{\alpha}\,b_{\alpha}^{\dagger}b_{\alpha},\quad\widehat{H}_{I}=\lambda\sum_{i,j,\alpha}\left(b_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\;c^{\dagger}_{i}\left(V_{\alpha}\right)_{ij}c_{j}+\text{H.c.}\right).

(1)

Above,
c_{i}^{\dagger}
and
c_{i}
create and annihilate an electron in orbital
i
,
while
b_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
and
b_{\alpha}
create and annihilate a bosonic bath mode
\alpha
of frequency
\Omega_{\alpha}
.
The
t_{ij}
encode single-particle hoppings, and

v_{ijkl}
denote the two-body interaction matrix elements.
The system–bath coupling is parameterized by a real constant
\lambda
and by the matrices
V_{\alpha}
,
whose elements
(V_{\alpha})_{ij}
specify the interaction vertex between orbital
i
and
j

mediated by bath mode
\alpha
.

Assuming (i) an initial Slater-determinant state and Gaussian fermionic density matrices at all times—so that Wick’s theorem holds—(ii) a weak-coupling Born factorization

\widehat{\rho}(t)\simeq\widehat{\rho}_{S}(t)\otimes\widehat{\rho}_{B}(t)
,
and (iii) a Markov approximation,
the system’s dynamics remains confined to the manifold of Slater determinants,
which is parametrized by the Grassmannian manifold of rank equal to the particle number.
We will denote this manifold simply as
\mathrm{Gr}
. Each point of
\mathrm{Gr}
corresponds to a Slater determinant state uniquely described by the single-particle density matrix

P=[P_{ij}]=[\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\rangle]
,
an orthogonal projector of rank equal to the particle number.

In the absence of system–bath coupling (
\widehat{H}_{I}=0
),
the dynamics reduces to the time-dependent Hartree-Fock equation,

\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=[T+V(P),P],\qquad V_{ik}(P)=\sum_{jl}\!\big(v_{ijkl}-v_{ijlk}\big)P_{lj},

(2)

where
T=[t_{ij}]
is the hopping matrix and
V(P)=[V_{ij}(P)]
,
with
V_{ij}(P)=\sum_{kl}\!\big(v_{ikjl}-v_{iklj}\big)P_{lk}
the mean-field interaction potential.
This evolution can be written geometrically as a Hamiltonian flow on the Grassmannian,

\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}\,[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}],

(3)

where the Hamiltonian function
H_{S}=H_{S}(P)=\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(T+\frac{1}{2}V(P)\right)P\right]
is the expectation value of the system Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}_{S}

evaluated on a Slater determinant, and
\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}=[\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P_{ij}}]
denotes the derivative with respect to
P
,
which coincides with the single-particle Hartree-Fock Hamiltonian
\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}=T+V(P)
.
Here, “Hamiltonian flow” means Hamilton’s equations generated by
H_{S}

with respect to the Fubini-Study symplectic form on the Grassmannian,

\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}=-\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(P\,\mathrm{d}P\wedge\mathrm{d}P\right)
.

Ohmic bath leads to metriplectic flow. —

Solving the bath equations formally using retarded Green’s functions, inserting the result into the system’s equation of motion, and performing a standard Markov limit with an Ohmic spectral weight

J(\omega)=\eta\,\omega e^{-\omega/\omega_{c}}

yields (see the appendix for a detailed derivation)

\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}\,\Bigl[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}\Bigr]-\gamma\,\Bigl[P,\Bigl[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}\Bigr]\Bigr],\qquad\gamma=\lambda^{2}\eta>0.

(4)

Here
\eta
sets the overall dissipation rate and
\omega_{c}
is a high-frequency cutoff,
assumed to be large so that memory effects are negligible and the dynamics becomes local in time.
Equation (
4
) defines a
Kähler metriplectic
dynamics on the Grassmannian:
the first term generates Hamiltonian motion with respect to the Fubini-Study symplectic form,
while the second term corresponds to gradient flow in the compatible Fubini-Study metric
g_{\mathrm{FS}}=\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\mathrm{d}P\odot\mathrm{d}P\right)
, where
\odot
denotes the symmetrized tensor product.

Eq. (
4
) defines a metriplectic flow in the sense introduced by Morrison in Ref.
Morrison 1986
, combining Hamiltonian with gradient flow (see the appendix for more details). Here the metriplectic structure is very natural because the Grassmannian is Kähler, and hence we have both symplectic and Riemannian structures, compatible through the complex structure.

Monotonically decreasing energy along the flow. —

Consider the system’s Hamiltonian function
H_{S}(P)
.
Using the identity
\Pi_{P}(X)=[P,[P,X]]=(1-P)XP+PX(1-P)
,
which gives the orthogonal projection of an operator
X
onto the tangent space

T_{P}\mathrm{Gr}
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product,
one finds

\frac{\mathrm{d}H_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}\right]=-\gamma\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\Pi_{P}\Bigl(\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}\Bigr)^{2}\right]\leq 0,

(5)

so
H_{S}
is a Lyapunov function in the broader sense of LaSalle’s invariance principle (Theorem 3.15 of Ref.
Hokayem and Gallestey 2020
)—nonincreasing along trajectories—and, as
t\to+\infty
, the flow relaxes to the Hartree-Fock critical set
defined by
\Pi_{P}(\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P})=0
(
\iff[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]=0
),
that is, the critical points of
H_{S}(P)
on
\mathrm{Gr}
,
with Hamiltonian motion quenched at late times.

Generalization to lattice models and emergence of the Dirichlet energy. —

We can generalize the microscopic model of Eq. (
1
)
to a translation-invariant two-dimensional lattice with
N
internal degrees of freedom per site
(e.g., orbitals, spin, or sublattice).
Now the annihilation and creation operators in Eq. (
1
) should carry positional indices
\mathbf{r}
as well as indices for internal degrees of freedom. After performing Fourier transformation to move to momentum space, we denote the Bloch Hamiltonian describing the hopping to be

T(\mathbf{k})=[t_{ij}(\mathbf{k})]

and short-range density-density interactions to be
controlled by a potential
v(\mathbf{q})
. The system Hamiltonian in momentum space then reads

\widehat{H}_{S}=\lambda_{T}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}c_{\mathbf{k},i}^{\dagger}t_{ij}(\mathbf{k})c_{\mathbf{k},j}+\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})\,{:n_{-\mathbf{q}}n_{\mathbf{q}}:}.

(6)

Here
n_{\mathbf{q}}
denotes the Fourier transform of the density operator,
and
\lambda_{T}
sets the overall hopping strength. Assuming the system occupies a Slater determinant obtained by filling a Chern band,
described by an
N\times N
orthogonal rank-
r
projector

P(\mathbf{k})=[P_{ij}(\mathbf{k})]
, and following the same Born-Markov approximation used for the model in Eq. (
1
) together with an Ohmic-bath assumption homogeneous in momentum (see the appendix), the evolution of the band is governed by a generalization of Eq. (
4
),

\frac{\partial P(\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}=\mathrm{i}\!\left[P(\mathbf{k}),\frac{\delta H_{S}}{\delta P(\mathbf{k})}\right]-\gamma\!\left[P(\mathbf{k}),\left[P(\mathbf{k}),\frac{\delta H_{S}}{\delta P(\mathbf{k})}\right]\right],

(7)

where
\frac{\delta H_{S}}{\delta P(\mathbf{k})}=[\frac{\delta H_{S}}{\delta P_{ij}(\mathbf{k})}]
denotes the functional derivative of the Hartree-Fock energy density functional

\displaystyle H_{S}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{T}\!\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\!\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\big[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})\big]

\displaystyle\quad-\!\!\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\!\!\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\!\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,v(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{tr}\!\big[P(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\big].

(8)

(The appearance of functional derivatives is because we have a continuum of components
P_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
parametrized by
\mathbf{k}
.)
For a finite lattice with (twisted or not) periodic boundary conditions,
this functional derivative naturally reduces to an ordinary derivative,
since only a finite set of momenta appears in the Fourier decomposition of
P
.

In the limit of small transferred momentum (small
\mathbf{q}
) for the interaction term,
together with isotropy under a flat—i.e., constant in
\mathbf{k}
—metric
h=h_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}k^{i}\mathrm{d}k^{j}

(interpretable as an inverse mass tensor, and not to be confused with the quantum metric
g
),
we obtain the geometric contribution

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{D}}(P)=\frac{1}{2}\!\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\sqrt{\det h}\,h^{ij}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\!\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial k^{i}}\frac{\partial P}{\partial k^{j}}\right),

(9)

that is, the Dirichlet energy of the map

P:\mathrm{BZ}^{2}\!\to\!\mathrm{Gr}

between the Riemannian manifolds
(\mathrm{BZ}^{2},h)
and
(\mathrm{Gr},g_{\mathrm{FS}})
.
The functional above depends only on the conformal class of
h
; since
h
is flat, this means it is defined only up to an overall scale.
Up to scale,
h
can be naturally identified with the Brillouin-zone–integrated quantum metric,

\displaystyle h_{ij}\propto\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,g_{ij}(\mathbf{k}),

(10)

because
g_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
inherits the lattice symmetries—present in both the hopping matrix
T(\mathbf{k})
and the interaction potential
v(\mathbf{q})
—
(for example,
C_{4}
symmetry in a square lattice implies
h_{ij}\propto\delta_{ij}
).
See the appendix for a more detailed discussion of this point.

In this limit,

H_{S}(P)\simeq\lambda_{T}\!\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\!\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{tr}[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})]+\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\,E_{\mathrm{D}}[P],

(11)

and

\frac{\delta H_{S}}{\delta P(\mathbf{k})}=\lambda_{T}T(\mathbf{k})-\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\Delta_{h}P(\mathbf{k}),

where
\Delta_{h}P=h^{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial k^{i}\partial k^{j}}

is the anisotropic Laplacian associated with
h
. The constant
\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}
(derived in the appendix) is given by
\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}=\int_{0}^{Q}\!\mathrm{d}q\,v(q)\,q^{3}
, where
Q
is the momentum cutoff.

Relaxation toward ideal bands. —

The Dirichlet energy functional has extrema given by
generalized Landau levels
(nonzero Chern number harmonic maps),
which satisfy
Q(\Delta_{h}P)P=0
, equivalently
[P,\Delta_{h}P]=0
.

Ideal bands
are precisely the minima of
E_{\mathrm{D}}
;
they saturate the topological bound
E_{\mathrm{D}}\geq\pi|\mathcal{C}|
,
the global version of the quantum-geometric trace inequality
relating the Berry curvature and quantum metric.
In the limit
\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\gg\lambda_{T}
,
the Hartree-Fock energy
H_{S}
is dominated by the Dirichlet term.
In this regime,
E_{\mathrm{D}}
decreases monotonically
under the flow of Eq. (
7
),
while the Chern number
\mathcal{C}
is conserved.
The Laplacian term
\gamma[P,[P,\Delta_{h}P]]
ensures the smoothness of
P(\mathbf{k})
,
as it effectively yields a convolution with a smoothing kernel—
it generates the harmonic-map heat flow.
By LaSalle’s invariance principle, with
H_{S}\simeq\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}E_{\mathrm{D}}
,
the long-time solutions are therefore
ideal
bands
within the fixed-topology sector labeled by
\mathcal{C}
.

Numerical demonstration: massive Dirac model. —

We integrate Eq. (
7
) for a two-band Dirac model (see, for instance, Ref.
Mera et al. 2022
for more details on the quantum geometry of this model),

T(\mathbf{k})=\vec{d}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\vec{\sigma}
with

\vec{d}(\mathbf{k})=(\sin k_{x},\sin k_{y},M-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})
,
M=-0.5
so the lower band has
\mathcal{C}=1
. Writing
P(t,\mathbf{k})=(I+\vec{n}(t,\mathbf{k})\cdot\vec{\sigma})/2
, the flow becomes a Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation

\frac{\partial\vec{n}}{\partial t}=-\,\vec{n}\times\!\vec{h}+\gamma\,\vec{n}\times\!\left(\vec{n}\times\!\vec{h}\right),\quad\vec{h}=\lambda_{T}\vec{d}-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}}{4}\nabla^{2}\vec{n},

(12)

with initial condition
\vec{n}(t=0,\mathbf{k})=-\vec{d}(\mathbf{k})/|\vec{d}(\mathbf{k})|
.

Using
(\gamma,\lambda_{\mathrm{D}},\lambda_{T})=(1.5,5.0,0.05)
, we show in Fig.
1
the energy as a function of time. In Fig.

(a) we confirm that the Dirichlet energy
E_{\mathrm{D}}(t)
decreases monotonically to
\pi\mathcal{C}=\pi
, which is the value one should achieve for the ideal Chern bands.
In Fig.

(b), we compare the Dirichlet energy
E_{\mathrm{D}}(t)
and the hopping energy
E_{\mathrm{T}}(t)
; we are in a regime where the Dirichlet energy is dominant over the hopping energy, which is the condition we required in order to have the flow evolve toward the ideal Chern bands.

Discussion and outlook. —

The microscopic theory developed in this work, where electrons with dominant short-range repulsive density-density interactions weakly couple to a bosonic Ohmic bath, leads to the Kähler metriplectic flow of Eq. (
7
).
Dominated by the harmonic-map heat flow, this provides a microscopic mechanism under which how generic Chern bands relax toward ideal bands in the long-time limit.

We have numerically shown how the ideal Chern band, which is the minimum of the Dirichlet energy functional, can be obtained with this method. Generalized Landau levels, on the other hand, correspond to extrema of the energy functional. We expect that, once the flow reaches an extremum, the system remains to be in the generalized Landau level for a certain period of time, before further relaxing toward the ideal Chern band. Further exploration of precise engineering of generalized Landau levels using our method will be discussed in future works. We have assumed that, in the Hartree-Fock energy, the lowest order in the small-
\mathbf{q}
expansion is the dominant term; it would be of practical interest to consider the full density-density interaction and/or alternative forms of system-bath coupling and to explore how the results can be affected.

Once the ideal Chern bands and generalized Landau levels are obtained, with further addition of appropriate short-range quantum inter-particle interactions, the ground state of the system should exhibit fractional quantum Hall effects. Our proposal thus opens an avenue toward obtaining fractional Chern insulator phases starting from generic Chern bands.

Appendix A
Mean-field theory and harmonic bands

A.1
Hartree-Fock mean-field theory

We consider the following fermionic Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\widehat{H}=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,l}v_{ij,kl}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k},

(13)

and we wish to find a Slater determinant state
\ket{\psi}
minimizing the energy

\displaystyle H=\bra{\psi}\widehat{H}\ket{\psi}.

(14)

Above
c_{i}^{\dagger},c_{i}
are, respectively, the creation and annihilation operator for an orbital
i=1,\dots,N
. The constants
t_{ij}
are hoppings describing the kinetic term and
v_{ij,kl}
the two-body interaction couplings which satisfy the obvious symmetries
v_{ij,kl}=-v_{ji,kl}=-v_{ij,lk}=v_{ji,lk}
.

The Fock space for fermions we work with is nothing but the vector space of the exterior algebra
\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
equipped with the induced inner product coming from that on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, exterior product by a vector
v=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}e_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, or in Dirac notation
\ket{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bra{i}v\rangle\ket{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, is identified with
c_{v}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bra{i}v\rangle c_{i}^{\dagger}
, and
1\in\mathbb{C}=\Lambda^{0}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
is identified with the vacuum state
\ket{0}
. The grading
\Lambda^{*}(\mathbb{C}^{N})=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{N}\Lambda^{i}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
is nothing but a decomposition into particle number sectors, i.e., eigenvalues of
\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}
. A slater determinant state is determined by choosing a collection of linearly independent single-particle orbitals,
\ket{v_1},\dots,\ket{v_r}
—which without loss of generality we may assume are orthogonal—and creating them on top of the vacuum state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=c_{v_{1}}^{\dagger}\cdots c^{\dagger}_{v_{r}}\ket{0}.

(15)

In exterior algebra language this a homogeneous vector
v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{r}\in\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
. The associated quantum state—the one dimensional line through
v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{r}
—is exactly the image of the dimension
r
subspace
E=\mathrm{span}\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\right\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
under the Plücker embedding
\iota:\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
, where
\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
denotes the Grassmanian of
r
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
\mathbb{P}\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
denotes the projectivization of the vector space
\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
. The energy
H=\bra{\psi}\widehat{H}\ket{\psi}
restricted to those states that are Slater determinants of
r
orbital then becomes a function
h\in C^{\infty}(\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N}))
. Points in the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
are
r
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
and can be described in terms of slater determinants of
r
-orbitals up to a scale. Alternatively, can also be equivalently be described by rank
r
orthogonal projection operators in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, namely for each subspace
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
we can uniquely associated an orthogonal projection
P_{E}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to E\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
. This projection matrix
P_{E}=[(P_{E})_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
can be read off from
\ket{\psi}
as

\displaystyle(P_{E})_{ij}=\langle\psi_{E}|c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{i}|\psi_{E}\rangle,

(16)

where we added the label
E
on
\ket{\psi_E}
to denote that the state comes from the image of
E
under the Plücker embedding,
i.e.,
P_{E}
is nothing but the single-particle density matrix of the associated Slater determinant state.
We can use Wick’s theorem to evaluate
h
, in particular,

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_E}c_{i}^{\dagger}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{l}c_{k}\ket{\psi_E}=\bra{\psi_E}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{k}\ket{\psi_E}\bra{\psi_E}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{l}\ket{\psi_E}-\bra{\psi_E}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{l}\ket{\psi_E}\bra{\psi_E}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{k}\ket{\psi_E},

(17)

to obtain

\displaystyle H(E)=\bra{\psi_E}\widehat{H}\ket{\psi_E}=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}(P_{E})_{ji}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,l}v_{ij,kl}\left[(P_{E})_{ki}(P_{E})_{lj}-(P_{E})_{li}(P_{E})_{kj}\right]

(18)

With the identification
E\leftrightarrow P_{E}
, we will right
H(E)=:H(P_{E})
, and we will drop the
E
in
P_{E}
, so

\displaystyle H(P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}P_{ji}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,l}v_{ij,kl}\left[P_{ki}P_{lj}-P_{li}P_{kj}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}P_{ji}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{jl}\left(\sum_{k,i}(v_{ij,kl}-v_{ij,lk})P_{ki}\right)P_{lj}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(T+\frac{1}{2}V(P)\right)P\right],

(19)

where
T=[t_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
and
V(P)=[\sum_{k,i}(v_{ij,kl}-v_{ij,lk})P_{ki}]_{1\leq j,l\leq N}
. Observe that
V
contains two terms which differ by a permutation of the last indices—these correspond to the
direct, or Hartree,
term and the
exchange, or Fock,
term. So now we can take the differential of
h
. To do so, we must take into account that
P^{2}=P
, which implies

\displaystyle P\mathrm{d}PP=0\quad\text{and}\quad Q\mathrm{d}PQ=0,

(20)

with
Q=I-P
the orthogonal complement projector, so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}P=Q\mathrm{d}PP+P\mathrm{d}PQ.

(21)

One important point is the differential of the second term which simplifies as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{jl}\left(\sum_{k,i}(v_{ij,kl}-v_{ij,lk})\mathrm{d}P_{ki}\right)P_{lj}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{jl}\left(\sum_{k,i}(v_{ij,kl}-v_{ij,lk})P_{ki}\right)\mathrm{d}P_{lj}

\displaystyle=\sum_{jl}\left(\sum_{k,i}(v_{ij,kl}-v_{ij,lk})P_{ki}\right)\mathrm{d}P_{lj},

(22)

due to the symmetry
v_{ij,kl}=v_{ji,lk}
. Thus, we get, using
P^{2}=P
,
Q^{2}=Q
, Eq. (
21
) and the cyclic property of the trace

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}H=\operatorname{tr}\left[(T+V(P))\mathrm{d}P\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(Q(t+V(P))P\right)P\mathrm{d}PQ\right]+\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(P(t+V(P))Q\right)Q\mathrm{d}PP\right].

(23)

Now for
\mathrm{d}h=0
we have the two equations

\displaystyle Q(T+V(P))P

\displaystyle=(T+V(P))P-P(T+V(P))P=0,

\displaystyle P(T+V(P))Q

\displaystyle=P(T+V(P))-P(T+V(P))P=0

(24)

These equations are not independent (they are Hermitian conjugate of each other), and we can simplify to get the simpler form

\displaystyle[T+V(P),P]=0,

(25)

which is the self-consistent mean-field equation. It is self-consistent because
V
itself is dependent on
P
.

A.2
Time-dependent Hartree-Fock mean-field theory

Now we consider the dynamical problem, to do that, we consider the action functional

\displaystyle S=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\left(\langle\psi(t)|\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ket{\psi(t)}-\bra{\psi(t)}\widehat{H}\ket{\psi(t)}\right).

(26)

If we did not restrict the allowed states to be Slater determinants, variation of this action with respect to the curve of states would simply yield the Schrödinger equation, thus recovering the standard quantum dynamics. However, if we only allow Slater-determinant states, we get Hamilton’s equations on the Grassmannian manifold seen as a symplectic manifold equipped with the Fubini-Study symplectic form. To see this, first observe that

\displaystyle\langle\psi(t)|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ket{\psi(t)}\mathrm{d}t,

(27)

is the local connection coefficient of the standard connection on the tautological bundle on
\mathbb{P}\Lambda^{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
along the curve of states
\ket{\psi(t)}
. As such, when we vary we should obtain the curvature two-form, which is essentially the Fubini-Study symplectic form. Let us see that this is indeed the case. We want to vary the curve along the image of the Plücker embedding, i.e., we have a two-parameter Slater determinant state
\ket{\psi(s,t)}
corresponding two a family of orthogonal projectors
P(s,t)
with
P(s=0,t)=P(t)
. The variation of the action is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}s}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\left(\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\psi(s,t)|\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ket{\psi(s,t)}-\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(s,t)|\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\ket{\psi(s,t)}-\mathrm{d}H(\frac{\partial P}{\partial s})\right),

(28)

where we assumed a parallel transport “gauge”, i.e.,
\bra{\psi(s,t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ket{\psi(s,t)}=0
which allowed us to eliminate the boundary term (which can be done, and the curve in the Grassmannian does not care about that). Observe that the first two terms are just

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(\frac{\partial P}{\partial t},\frac{\partial P}{\partial s}),

(29)

where we take
\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}
to have area
2\pi
on
\mathbb{C}P^{1}
(otherwise there would be a factor of
2
in the formula). So the action is extremal if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}s}\bigg|_{s=0}=0

(30)

for all variations, which yields

\displaystyle\iota_{\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}}\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}=\mathrm{d}h,

(31)

which is to say that we flow along the Hamiltonian vector field associated with
h
. Additionally, because the Grassmannian manifold is Kählerian, we have

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(X,JY)=g_{\mathrm{FS}}(X,Y),

(32)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(X,Y)=g_{\mathrm{FS}}(JX,Y),

(33)

so we get

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{FS}}(J\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t},\cdot)=\mathrm{d}H,

(34)

which is to say that
J\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}
is the gradient vector field of
h
with respect to the Fubini-Study metric. But,

\displaystyle J\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}Q\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}P-\mathrm{i}P\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}Q=-\mathrm{i}[P,\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}]

(35)

Using

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}H=\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(T+V(P)\right)\mathrm{d}P\right)=\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(T+V(P)\right)\mathrm{d}P\right),

(36)

and

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{FS}}(J\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t},\cdot)=\mathrm{Re}\operatorname{tr}\left(J\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}P\right),

(37)

we immediately get, from nondegeneracy of
g_{\mathrm{FS}}

\displaystyle J\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=Q(T+V(P))P+P(T+V(P))Q.

(38)

Acting with
J
on both sides yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=[Q(T+V(P))P+P(T+V(P))Q,P]=[T+V(P),P],

(39)

since
[P,PXP]=0
and
[P,QXQ]=0
, for any
X
. The equation above is simply the von Neumann equation for the density matrix (in this case the single-particle density matrix), except that the effective Hamiltonian is itself dependent on the density matrix (nonlinear equation).

A.3
Mean-field theory and harmonic bands

We now consider the problem of density-density interactions on a translation-invariant system on a lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, or, in the finite size case, a discrete torus. We are going to consider Slater determinant states which are associated to fully filled Bloch bands. So in this more general setting a state is parametrized by a map
P:\mathrm{BZ}^{d}\to\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
(
N
here is the total number of bands)—a smooth family of rank
r
orthogonal projection operators—or, equivalently, by a vector bundle
E\to\mathrm{BZ}^{d}
over the Brillouin zone, subbundle of the trivial rank
N
bundle
E\subset\mathrm{BZ}^{d}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}
, and whose fiber at
\mathbf{k}\in\mathrm{BZ}^{d}
is
\mathrm{Im}\;P(\mathbf{k})
. In other words, when we do mean-field theory, we will be looking at a functional in the space of maps
P:\mathrm{BZ}^{d}\to\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
and when we look at time-dependent mean-field theory we will be looking at a flow in this space.

We start with the following Hamiltonian with hopping and general density-density interactions for fermions on a lattice

\displaystyle\widehat{H}=\lambda_{T}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}c_{\mathbf{k},i}^{\dagger}t_{ij}(\mathbf{k})c_{\mathbf{k},j}+\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q}):n_{-\mathbf{q}}n_{\mathbf{q}}:,

(40)

where

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},i}c_{\mathbf{k},i}

(41)

is the Fourier transform of the particle density and
V
is the volume, and
:-:
is normal ordering. Our Slater determinant states parameterized by maps
P:\mathrm{BZ}^{d}\to\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
satisfy

\displaystyle\langle c_{i,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}c_{j,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}P_{ji}(\mathbf{k}),

(42)

where we denoted
\langle-\rangle
the average on the state. We then compute
h(P):=\langle H\rangle
. The first term is simply

\displaystyle\langle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}c_{\mathbf{k},i}^{\dagger}t_{ij}(\mathbf{k})c_{\mathbf{k},j}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}t_{ij}(\mathbf{k})P_{ji}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\operatorname{tr}\left[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})\right],

(43)

with
T(\mathbf{k})=[t_{ij}(\mathbf{k})]_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
.
In the thermodynamic limit this goes to

\displaystyle\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{d}}\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}\;\operatorname{tr}\left[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})\right].

(44)

We evaluate the second term by writing

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}v(\mathbf{q}):c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{q},i}c_{\mathbf{k}_{1},i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q},j}c_{\mathbf{k}_{2},j}:=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}v(\mathbf{q})c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{q},i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q},j}c_{\mathbf{k}_{2},j}c_{\mathbf{k}_{1},i}.

(45)

Then, Wick’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\langle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{q},i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q},j}c_{\mathbf{k}_{2},j}c_{\mathbf{k}_{1},i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{q},i}c_{\mathbf{k}_{1},i}\rangle\langle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q},j}c_{\mathbf{k}_{2},j}\rangle-\langle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{q},i}c_{\mathbf{k}_{2},j}\rangle\langle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q},j}c_{\mathbf{k}_{1},i}\rangle

\displaystyle=P_{ii}(\mathbf{k}_{1})P_{jj}(\mathbf{k}_{2})\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}\delta_{\mathbf{q},0}-P_{ji}(\mathbf{k}_{2})P_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{1})\delta_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q}},

(46)

so

\displaystyle\langle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q}):n_{-\mathbf{q}}n_{\mathbf{q}}:\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})r^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}(\delta_{\mathbf{q},0})^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left[P(\mathbf{k}_{2})P(\mathbf{k}_{1})\right]\delta_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{q}},

(47)

in the discrete sum
\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2}=\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
(since it is just a Kroenecker delta), so we can simplify this to

\displaystyle\langle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q}):n_{-\mathbf{q}}n_{\mathbf{q}}:\rangle=Vr^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})-\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{tr}\left[P(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\right],

(48)

We then conclude that

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{H}\rangle=\lambda_{T}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\operatorname{tr}\left[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})\right]+Vr^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})-\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{tr}\left[P(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\right].

(49)

The second term is independent of
P
so we may ignore it, and we will simply redefine

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{H}\rangle=\lambda_{T}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\operatorname{tr}\left[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})\right]-\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}v(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{tr}\left[P(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\right].

(50)

In the thermodynamic limit, we actually need to consider
h(P):=\langle\widehat{H}\rangle/V
so the factors of
V
drop out,

\displaystyle H(P)=\lambda_{T}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;\operatorname{tr}\left[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})\right]-\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;v(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{tr}\left[P(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\right].

(51)

Now the second term is manifestly quantum geometric in nature. In fact, we have a small-
\mathbf{q}
expansion

\displaystyle-\operatorname{tr}\left[P(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\right]

\displaystyle=-r-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{tr}\left[P(\mathbf{k})\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}(\mathbf{k})\right]q_{i}q_{j}+\dots

\displaystyle=-r+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{tr}\left[\frac{\partial P}{\partial k_{i}}(\mathbf{k})\frac{\partial P}{\partial k_{j}}(\mathbf{k})\right]q_{i}q_{j}+\dots

\displaystyle=-r+\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(\mathbf{k})q_{i}q_{j}+\dots,

(52)

where
g=P^{*}g_{\mathrm{FS}}
denotes the pullback under
P
of the Fubini-Study metric, also know in condensed matter as the quantum metric (here we use the normalization convention for the Fubini-Study metric
\int_{\mathbb{C}P^{1}}\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}=\pi
—compare with different sections). Here
\dots
denote terms of higher order in the transferred momentum
\mathbf{q}
. For the lowest Landau level band, truncating this expansion is justified because the above quantities falls off as a Gaussian in the
\mathbf{q}
-variable. Either way, we may consider the first non-trivial contribution

\displaystyle v(\mathbf{q})\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(\mathbf{k})q_{i}q_{j}.

(53)

To take care of
\mathbf{q}
-integration we now use similar reasoning to that used in Ref.
Onishi et al. 2025
. Under an isotropy assumption of
v(\mathbf{q})=v(|\mathbf{q}|)
, it is reasonable to average over a sphere of radius
q=|\mathbf{q}|
and then integrate
q
until a cutoff high energy momentum
Q
. The result is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{Q}\mathrm{d}q\;q^{d-1}v(q)\left(q^{2}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j}\delta^{ij}g_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\right),

(54)

In other words, we get an effective energy functional which, discarding constant terms and higher order contributions, looks like

\displaystyle h(P)=\lambda_{T}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;\operatorname{tr}\left[T(\mathbf{k})P(\mathbf{k})\right]+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}}{2}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sqrt{\det(h)}\sum_{i,j}h^{ij}g_{ij},

(55)

where in the second term we introduced a flat metric
h=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}\;\mathrm{d}k_{i}\mathrm{d}k_{j}
which accounts for different anisotropy conditions for
v
, so the average on the sphere of before becomes on ellipsoids defined by this metric and
\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}
is the constant resulting from the integral of
v(q)
. The functional that
\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}
multiplies is the so-called Dirichlet energy functional—
E_{\mathrm{D}}(P)=\frac{1}{2}{}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{d}}\mathrm{d}^{d}\mathbf{k}\sqrt{\det(h)}\sum_{i,j}h^{ij}\left(P^{*}g_{\mathrm{FS}}\right)_{ij}
— for maps
P:(\mathrm{BZ}^{d},h)\to(\mathrm{Gr}_{r}(\mathbb{C}^{N}),g_{\mathrm{FS}})
whose critical points are harmonic maps—referred to in this context as Harmonic bands. If we assume that the band is such that the associated tight-binding energy dispersion, the integrand of the first term, is approximately flat, then we only care about the second terms and the mean-field solution is exactly a harmonic band. In general, extremizing this functional gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{T}QTP-\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}Q(\Delta_{h}P)P=0,

(56)

with
Q(\Delta_{h}P)P=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(h)}}Q\sum_{i,j}h^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{i}}(\sqrt{\det(h)}\frac{\partial P}{\partial k_{j}})P=\sum_{i,j}h^{ij}Q\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}P
(since
h
is flat).

Appendix B
Microscopic derivation of the metriplectic flow

Our starting point will be the equation of motion for Heisenberg picture operators

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{O}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},\widehat{O}],

(57)

and then take the average in the density matrix of the composite system.

To be able to properly recover our results in the previous notes, in principle we have to add an additional assumption of having a Slater determinant state for the system at all times, which is to say that we are considering the motion of the system’s state only along the manifold of Slater determinants. This is translated, in this language, into
\widehat{\rho}_{S}(t)
—in the Schrödinger picture—being a free-fermion Gaussian density matrix—hence satisfying Wick’s theorem—with

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{S}(\widehat{\rho}_{S}(t)c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}):=\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\rangle=P_{ji}(t),

(58)

here
P=[P_{ji}]
is an orthogonal projector of rank equal to the total number of particles in the system—i.e., determines a curve in a Grassmannian manifold.

Our results should then be obtainable through the equation of motion

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{ji}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{tr}_{S\cup B}(\widehat{\rho}(t_{0})c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{j}(t))=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{tr}_{S}(\widehat{\rho}_{S}(t)c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}),

(59)

where in the first equality we use the Heisenberg picture and in last the Schrödinger picture. Actually, as we will see later, if we assume that the system’s initial state is a Slater determinant, it turns out that provided the system remains Gaussian—satisfying Wick’s theorem— it will remain a Slater determinant. It is then enough to consider it to be Gaussian.

We will first consider the case of no interaction with the bath, i.e.,
H_{I}=0
, that means that it is enough to focus on the first term. We will recover the time-dependent Hartree-Fock mean-field equation. Then we will consider the interaction term.

We will deal with
\widehat{H}=\widehat{H}_{S}+H_{B}+\widehat{H}_{I}
, with the system’s piece being

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{S}=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k,l}v_{ijkl}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k},

(60)

and the bath piece

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{B}=\sum_{\alpha}\Omega_{\alpha}b_{\alpha}^{\dagger}b_{\alpha},

(61)

and the interaction between the system and bath being

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{I}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{\alpha,i,j}\left(b_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c^{\dagger}_{i}\left(V_{\alpha}\right)_{ij}c_{j}+b_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{i}\overline{\left(V_{\alpha}\right)}_{ji}c_{j}\right)=\lambda\sum_{\alpha}\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}V_{\alpha}c+b_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}V_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)c,

(62)

where
(V_{\alpha})_{ij}
’s are the interaction vertices and
\lambda
is a real parameter. In the last two lines we used a compact notation where the fermionic indices
ij
are omitted.

The relevant Heisenberg equations of motion are for the operators
S=S_{ji}(t):=[c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{j}(t)]
and
b_{\alpha}(t)
. They read

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{ji}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},S_{ji}]=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},S_{ji}]+\mathrm{i}\lambda\sum_{\alpha,k}\left(S_{jk}\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{ki}-\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{jk}S_{ki}\right)

(63)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}b_{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},b_{\alpha}]=-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\alpha}b_{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}\lambda\sum_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}V_{\alpha}c.

(64)

Observe that
P_{ji}(t)=\operatorname{tr}_{S\cup B}(\widehat{\rho}(t_{0})S_{ji}(t))
.

We will assume, as it is common in the derivation of quantum master equations in open quantum systems (see Chapter. 3.3. in the book by Breuer-Petruccione
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
), that at each time
t
, the system and the bath decouple

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(t)\cong\widehat{\rho}_{S}(t)\otimes\widehat{\rho}_{B}(t),

(65)

where this equation is in the Schrödinger picture. This is the so-called
Born approximation
, in which we assume that the influence of the system on the bath is small because their coupling is weak. We will also assume that, in addition to the system being Gaussian at all times, the bath is Gaussian at initial time
t=t_{0}
, so

\displaystyle\langle b_{\alpha}\rangle=\langle b_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\rangle=0\text{ and }\langle b_{\alpha}^{\dagger}b_{\alpha}\rangle=n_{B}(\Omega_{\alpha}),

(66)

with Wick’s theorem being satisfied. Here
n_{B}(\Omega_{\alpha})
is the Bose-Einstein distribution.

B.1
No interaction, i.e.,
H_{I}=0
: time-dependent Hartree-Fock mean-field equation

In this case, the dynamics of the system decouples from that of the bath, and we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}P_{nm}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{tr}_{S}(\widehat{\rho}_{S}(t)c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n})=\mathrm{i}\operatorname{tr}_{S}(\widehat{\rho}_{S}(t)[\widehat{H}_{S},c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}]).

(67)

Because of the Gaussian density matrix assumption, this expectation value can be expressed in terms of single-particle density matrix, i.e., in terms of the projector
P(t)
. First we evaluate
[H_{S},c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}]
. The first term is the hopping

\displaystyle[\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j},c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}]=\sum_{i}t_{im}c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}-\sum_{j}t_{nj}c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{j}.

(68)

After taking the average on
\rho_{S}
, this yields

\displaystyle\Big\langle[\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j},c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}]\Big\rangle=\sum_{i}t_{im}P_{nm}-\sum_{j}t_{nj}P_{ji}=-[T,P]_{nm},

(69)

where
T=[T_{ij}]
is the hopping matrix. Then we evaluate the quartic term:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Biggl[\sum_{i,j,k,l,}v_{ijkl}\,c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k},\;c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}\Biggr]=\sum_{i,j,l}v_{ijml}\,c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{n}\;-\;\sum_{i,l,k}v_{inkl}\,c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k}.

(70)

Finally, we take the average on the density matrix
\rho_{S}(t)
, simplifying quartic averages according to Wick’s theorem,

\displaystyle\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k}\rangle=P_{ki}P_{lj}-P_{li}P_{kj}.

(71)

The result is

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\sum_{ijkl}v_{ijkl}\,c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{l}c_{k},\;c_{m}^{\dagger}c_{n}\Bigr]\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,l}v_{ijml}\,\bigl(P_{ni}P_{lj}-P_{li}P_{nj}\bigr)-\sum_{i,l,k}v_{inkl}\,\bigl(P_{ki}P_{lm}-P_{li}P_{km}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}P_{ni}\left(\sum_{j,l}(v_{ijml}-v_{jiml})P_{lj}\right)-\sum_{l}\left(\sum_{i,k}(v_{inkl}-v_{inlk})P_{ki}\right)P_{lm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}P_{ni}\left(\sum_{j,l}(v_{ijml}-v_{ijlm})P_{lj}\right)-\sum_{l}\left(\sum_{i,k}(v_{nilk}-v_{nikl})P_{ki}\right)P_{lm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}P_{ni}V(P)_{im}-\sum_{l}V(P)_{nl}P_{lm}

\displaystyle=-\bigl[V(P),\,P\bigr]_{mn},\qquad V(P)_{ik}=\sum_{j,l}\bigl(v_{ijkl}-v_{ijlk}\bigr)\,P_{lj}.

(72)

Thus, we find that the equation of motion for
P
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=[T+V(P),P],

(73)

which is consistent with the obtained in the other notes, coming from Hamilton’s equations of motion in the Grassmannian

\displaystyle\iota_{X_{H}}\omega=\mathrm{d}H_{S}\iff\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=[\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P},P]

(74)

with
H_{S}=\langle\widehat{H}_{S}\rangle=\operatorname{tr}\left[P\left(T+\frac{1}{2}V(P)\right)\right]
.

B.2
Interaction with the bath

First we formally solve Eq. (
64
) and plug it in Eq. (
63
). Eq. (
64
), which we reproduce here,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}b_{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},b_{\alpha}]=-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\alpha}b_{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}\lambda\sum_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}V_{\alpha}c,

(75)

is a standard linear ODE with a nonhomogeneous term. We can solve it by resorting to the
b_{\alpha}
’s retarded Green’s function

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}(t)=\theta(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\alpha}(t)},

(76)

namely

\displaystyle b_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=b_{\alpha}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\alpha}(t-t_{0})}-\mathrm{i}\lambda\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\left(c^{\dagger}V_{\alpha}c\right)(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=b_{\alpha}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\alpha}(t-t_{0})}-\mathrm{i}\lambda\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\sum_{ij}S_{ji}(\tau)(V_{\alpha})_{ij}\;\mathrm{d}\tau

(77)

For readability we reproduce the formula for the equation of motion of
S_{ji}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S_{ji}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},S_{ji}]=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},S_{ji}]+\mathrm{i}\lambda\sum_{\alpha,k}\left(S_{jk}\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{ki}-\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{jk}S_{ki}\right).

(78)

We can now take the average of this equation on
\widehat{\rho}(t_{0})
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{ji}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\langle[\widehat{H},S_{ji}]\rangle=\mathrm{i}[\widehat{H},S_{ji}]+\mathrm{i}\lambda\langle\sum_{\alpha,k}\left(S_{jk}\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{ki}-\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{jk}S_{ki}\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{i}[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]\right)_{ji}+\mathrm{i}\lambda\sum_{\alpha,k}\left(P_{jk}\langle\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{ki}\rangle-\langle\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+bV_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{jk}\rangle P_{ki}\right),

(79)

where the second step follows from the result with
\widehat{H}_{I}=0
and from the factorization of the density matrix (Born approximation). Thus, what we need to plug in this equation is the average in
\widehat{\rho}(t_{0})
of
b_{\alpha}(t)
, which is given by

\displaystyle b_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\lambda\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\sum_{ij}P_{ji}(\tau)(V_{\alpha})_{ij}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=-\mathrm{i}\lambda\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\operatorname{tr}(P(\tau)V_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau

(80)

Observe that the
b_{\alpha}(t_{0})=b_{\alpha}
’s enter linearly and they will hence average to zero. Thus, only the convolution part from the formal solution remains. We then find

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\langle\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}+b_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)_{ki}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\lambda\sum_{\alpha}\left(\left(V_{\alpha}\right)_{ki}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\overline{G_{\alpha}}(t-\tau)\operatorname{tr}(P(\tau)V^{\dagger}_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau-\left(V^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\right)_{ki}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\operatorname{tr}(P(\tau)V_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle=:\lambda F_{ki},

(81)

so we can write the resulting equations of motion compactly in matrix notation as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]+\mathrm{i}\lambda^{2}[P,F].

(82)

From this, we can already understand that if the initial state of the system was a Slater determinant, it will remain a Slater determinant. The reason is that

\displaystyle P\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}P=0\text{ and }(1-P)\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}(1-P)=0,

(83)

provided
P^{2}=P
. That means that the vector field on the right-hand side restricted to the Grassmannian is tangent to the Grassmannian, and hence it defines a flow inside the Grassmannian. General fermionic Gaussian states do not satisfy
P^{2}=P
.

B.3
Markov approximation

We now wish to perform a Markov approximation, which assumes that the system has little memory with regards to what happened in the past. We only need to focus on studying the memory-dependent term

\displaystyle F=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\alpha}\left(V_{\alpha}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\overline{G_{\alpha}}(t-\tau)\operatorname{tr}(P(\tau)V^{\dagger}_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau-V^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G_{\alpha}(t-\tau)\operatorname{tr}(P(\tau)V_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

(84)

We will do the Markov approximation in three steps. The first is to consider that
t_{0}\to-\infty
and change the integration variable,

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\alpha}\left(V_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{G_{\alpha}}(\tau)\operatorname{tr}(P(t-\tau)V^{\dagger}_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau-V^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G_{\alpha}(\tau)\operatorname{tr}(P(t-\tau)V_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\alpha}\left(V_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\Omega_{\alpha}\tau}\operatorname{tr}(P(t-\tau)V^{\dagger}_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau-V^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-i\Omega_{\alpha}\tau}\operatorname{tr}(P(t-\tau)V_{\alpha})\;\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha}e^{i\Omega_{\alpha}\tau}\operatorname{tr}(P(t-\tau)V^{\dagger}_{\alpha})V_{\alpha}\;\mathrm{d}\tau-\mathrm{i}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-i\Omega_{\alpha}\tau}\operatorname{tr}(P(t-\tau)V_{\alpha})V^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

(85)

The third step will require further assumptions about the bath. It is useful to note that
\operatorname{tr}(A^{\dagger}B)=\langle A|B\rangle
determines an inner product in the complex linear space of matrices. We can then write
F
in a perhaps more appealing form

\displaystyle F=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha}e^{i\Omega_{\alpha}\tau}\bra{V_{\alpha}}P(t-\tau)\rangle V_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\tau+\text{H.c.}

(86)

We introduce the bath’s spectral density (super-)operator as

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\sum_{\alpha}\delta(\omega-\Omega_{\alpha})\ket{V_{\alpha}}\bra{V_{\alpha}},

(87)

so that we can write

\displaystyle F=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega\tau}J(\omega)\left[P(t-\tau)\right]\mathrm{d}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}\tau+\text{H.c.}

(88)

It is common in physics to assume that the bath is Ohmic with exponential cutoff. Here this will translate to

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\eta\;\omega e^{-\tfrac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}},

(89)

where the identity (super-)operator is omitted to simplify the notation and
\eta
is a friction constant. Using

\displaystyle J(\tau):=\int_{0}^{\infty}J(\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}\tau\omega}\mathrm{d}\omega=\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta\;\omega e^{-\left(\tfrac{1}{\omega_{c}}-\mathrm{i}\tau\right)\omega}\mathrm{d}\omega=\frac{\eta}{\left(\tfrac{1}{\omega_{c}}-\mathrm{i}\tau\right)^{2}}

(90)

this yields

\displaystyle F=\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta}{\left(\tfrac{1}{\omega_{c}}-\mathrm{i}\tau\right)^{2}}P(t-\tau)d\tau-\mathrm{H.c.}

(91)

The Markovian limit is to assume that
\omega_{c}\to\infty
. Recall that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\pm\mathrm{i}0^{+}}=\mathrm{P.V.}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\mp\mathrm{i}\pi\delta(\tau),

(92)

so

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left(\frac{1}{\tau+\mathrm{i}0^{+}}\right)=-\frac{1}{\left(\tau+\mathrm{i}0^{+}\right)^{2}}=-\mathrm{P.V.}\left(\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)\mp\mathrm{i}\pi\delta^{\prime}(\tau)

(93)

so that—as a distribution—we have

\displaystyle J(\tau)=\frac{\eta}{\left(\tfrac{1}{\omega_{c}}-\mathrm{i}\tau\right)^{2}}=-\frac{\eta}{\left(\tau+\mathrm{i}\tfrac{1}{\omega_{c}}\right)^{2}}\to-\frac{\eta}{\left(\tau+\mathrm{i}0^{+}\right)^{2}}=-\eta\mathrm{P.V.}\left(\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)-\mathrm{i}\pi\eta\delta^{\prime}(\tau),

(94)

observing that for any test function
\varphi(\tau)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t-\tau)\delta^{\prime}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau=\varphi^{\prime}(t),

(95)

we have

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=2\pi\eta\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\left(-\mathrm{i}\eta\;\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta}{\tau^{2}}P(t-\tau)d\tau-\mathrm{H.c.}\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi\mathrm{i}\eta[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]+\left(-\mathrm{i}\eta\;\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta}{\tau^{2}}P(t-\tau)d\tau-\mathrm{H.c.}\right)+\mathrm{O}(\lambda^{2}).

(96)

The last term is still non-local in
P(t)
, so we may do one last Markovian approximation
P(t-\tau)\cong P(t)
, which produces a force term which is proportional to
P(t)
and hence after the commutator, it yields zero so we may neglect it. So effectively, we may consider just

\displaystyle F\approx 2\pi\mathrm{i}\eta[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}].

(97)

Hence, we obtain the equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]-\gamma[P,[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]],

(98)

which describes relaxation toward the minima of
H_{S}
, due to the second term, with relaxation constant
\gamma=2\pi\eta\lambda^{2}
which is second order in the coupling.

B.4
Relaxation toward the minima of
H_{S}

The equation we obtained

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{i}[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]-\gamma[P,[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]],

(99)

describes a Kähler metriplectic flow on the Grassmannian. What this means is that the Grassmannian manifold
\mathrm{Gr}
(omitting rank and ambient dimension for brevity) is a Kähler manifold equipped with the Fubini-Study symplectic form
\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}=-\mathrm{i}\operatorname{tr}(P\mathrm{d}P\wedge\mathrm{d}P)
and the Fubini-Study metric
g_{\mathrm{FS}}=2\operatorname{tr}(P\mathrm{d}P\odot\mathrm{d}P)
. These two nondegenerate bilinear forms define tensor fields (bivectors)
\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{2}T\mathrm{Gr})
and
g_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}\in\Gamma(S^{2}T\mathrm{Gr})
by considering the inverse matrices of their local representatives. In particular, if in local coordinates we have
\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}
and
g_{\mathrm{FS}}=\sum_{ij}g_{ij}dx^{i}\odot dx^{j}
, we have
\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\omega^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}
and
g_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}=\sum_{ij}g^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\odot\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}
, where
[\omega^{ij}]
and
[g^{ij}]
are the inverse matrices of
[\omega_{ij}]
and
[g_{ij}]
, respectively. The flow equation can therefore be expressed intrinsically in terms of the symplectic and metric bivectors as

\displaystyle X=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}H_{S},\cdot)-\gamma g_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}H_{S},\cdot).

(100)

The vector field
g_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}H_{S},\cdot)
is referred to as the Riemannian gradient of
H_{S}
and denoted
\nabla H_{S}
, because it is the unique vector field satisfying

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{FS}}(\nabla H_{S},\cdot)=\mathrm{d}H_{S}.

(101)

In local coordinates
\nabla H_{S}=\sum_{j}g^{ji}\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}
.
Also,
X_{H_{S}}=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}H_{S},\cdot)
is the Hamiltonian vector field for
H_{S}
, and it is the unique vector field satisfying

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(X_{H_{S}},\cdot)=\mathrm{d}H_{S}.

(102)

In local coordinates
X_{H_{S}}=\sum_{j}\omega^{ji}\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}
.
To use the Kähler structure more explicitly, observe that due to compatibility with respect to the complex structure
J
(which at the matrix level is just
J(X)=-\mathrm{i}[P,X]
)

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{FS}}=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(\cdot,J\cdot)\implies g_{\mathrm{FS}}(\nabla H_{S},\cdot)=-\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(J\nabla H_{S},\cdot)=\mathrm{d}H_{S}=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(X_{H_{S}},\cdot)

(103)

from which it follows that
X_{H_{S}}=-J\nabla H_{S}
, which is consistent with

\displaystyle-J([P,[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]])=i[P,([P,[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]])]=i[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}].

(104)

The vector field
X
can then be compactly written in two ways

\displaystyle X=J\nabla H_{S}-\gamma\nabla H_{S}=X_{H_{S}}-\gamma JX_{H_{S}},

(105)

the first is in terms of the Riemannian gradient, hence a Riemannian picture; and the second in terms of the Hamiltonian vector field, hence a symplectic picture. Both pictures are naturally related by the complex structure
J
, with
J^{2}=-1
. This also shows once and for all that

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{FS}}(\nabla H_{S},X_{H_{S}})=-g_{\mathrm{FS}}(\nabla H_{S},J\nabla H_{S})=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(\nabla H_{S},JX_{H_{S}})=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(\nabla H_{S},\nabla H_{S})=0,

(106)

so the two vector fields are orthogonal to each other. Alternatively,
g_{\mathrm{FS}}(\nabla H_{S},X_{H_{S}})=\mathrm{d}H_{S}(X_{H_{S}})=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}(X_{H_{S}},X_{H_{S}})=0
. This last method shows that this identity holds for arbitrary nondegenerate metriplectic systems.

Notably,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}H_{S}(X)

\displaystyle=X\cdot H_{S}=\mathcal{L}_{X}H_{S}=\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}H_{S},\mathrm{d}H_{S})-\gamma g_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}H_{S},\mathrm{d}H_{S})

\displaystyle=-\gamma g_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}H_{S},\mathrm{d}H_{S})=-\gamma||\mathrm{d}H_{S}||^{2}\leq 0,

(107)

which follows from skew-symmetry of
\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}
and, consequently, of
\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}
and positive-definiteness of
g_{\mathrm{FS}}
and, hence, of
g_{\mathrm{FS}}^{-1}
.
It follows that if
t\mapsto P(t)
is an integral curve of
X
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(H_{S}(P(t)))=-\gamma||\mathrm{d}H_{S}||^{2}(P(t))\leq 0\implies

\displaystyle H_{S}(P(t))-H_{S}(P(t_{0}))

\displaystyle=-\gamma\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\;||\mathrm{d}H_{S}||^{2}(P(s))\leq 0,

(108)

so that the energy decreases with time along the integral curves of
X
.

Remark B.1

(Local coordinates version)
.

In local coordinates the integral curve of
P(t)
reads, using the Einstein summation convention,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x^{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\omega^{ji}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{j}}-\gamma g^{ji}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{j}}.

(109)

Multiplying by
\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{i}}
and summing over
i
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}H_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x^{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\omega^{ji}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial x^{i}}-\gamma g^{ji}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial x^{i}}=-\gamma g^{ji}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial x^{i}}\leq 0,

(110)

where the first term vanishes due to
\omega^{ij}=-\omega^{ji}
. The previous result then follows.

Remark B.2

(Orthogonal projector version)
.

Take the trace of the equation of motion with
\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}H_{S}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)=\mathrm{i}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}[P,\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}]\right)-\gamma\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}[P,[P,\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}]]\right)

\displaystyle=-\gamma\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi(\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P})^{2}\right)=-\gamma||\mathrm{d}H_{S}||^{2}(P(t)),

(111)

where we used the cyclic property of the trace and where

\displaystyle\Pi(\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P})=[P,[P,\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}]]=(1-P)\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}P+P\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}(1-P),

(112)

denotes the projection onto the tangent space of the Grassmannian at
P
(with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product), from which the previous result follows. This projection appears naturally because
\frac{\partial H_{S}}{\partial P}
is just a derivative in the conventional sense—it is a matrix whose components are the derivatives of the function
H_{S}
with respect to the matrix components of
P
—and it does not capture the constraint
P^{2}=P
and
P^{\dagger}=P
which determines that
P
lives in
\mathrm{Gr}
.

Thus, the weak coupling to an Ohmic environment induces a metriplectic vector field on the Grassmannian, realizing dissipative relaxation as a gradient descent in the Fubini–Study metric while preserving the underlying Kähler geometry.

B.5
Harmonic band heat flow

If we do the same reasoning for a Bloch band with short-range density-density interactions and a translation-invariant interaction with a bath of bosonic oscillators—modes
b_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}
with frequencies
\Omega_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
—of the form

\displaystyle\lambda\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\left(b^{\dagger}_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}V_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})+b_{\alpha,\mathbf{k}}V_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k})\right)c_{\mathbf{k}},

(113)

the same reasoning will apply, provided we have now

\displaystyle J(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\alpha}\delta(\omega-\Omega_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k}))\ket{V_{\alpha}(\bf{k})}\bra{V_{\alpha}(\bf{k})}\sim\eta\omega e^{-\tfrac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}}.

(114)

The short-range density-density interactions will yield
H_{S}
, to lowest order in the transferred momentum, proportional to the Dirichlet energy, as discussed earlier. If this is the main source of system energy
H_{S}
, then the resulting equation of motion for the band projector
P(\mathbf{k})
is of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P(\mathbf{k})}{\partial t}=-\mathrm{i}[P,\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\Delta_{h}P(\mathbf{k})]+\gamma[P,[P,\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\Delta_{h}P(\mathbf{k})]],

(115)

with
\Delta_{h}
a Laplacian with respect to a flat metric
h
which describes the anisotropy of the system (essentially the inverse mass tensor). In the absence of the first term this is the Harmonic map heat flow. It’s presence forces the band to relax to a harmonic one.

Appendix C
Symmetries of the hopping matrix, the quantum metric and
h

In the main text, we claimed that
h
, interpreted as the inverse mass tensor, can be identified up to scale with the integrated quantum metric.
Here we show that the integrated quantum metric indeed inherits the same lattice symmetries—present in both the hopping matrix
T(\mathbf{k})
and the interaction potential
v(\mathbf{q})
—and hence can be identified with
h
up to an overall scale.

Suppose a discrete group of symmetries
G
preserves the free part of the Hamiltonian. For each
R\in G
, we have

\displaystyle T(R\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k})=U(R)^{-1}T(\mathbf{k})U(R),

(116)

where
U(R)
is a unitary or antiunitary representation of the symmetry.
The
U(R)
’s are required to satisfy the group law projectively, i.e.,

U(R_{1}R_{2})=U(R_{1})U(R_{2})e^{i\alpha(R_{1},R_{2})}
for some
U(1)
phase
\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha(R_{1},R_{2})}
.
The inverse-mass tensor
h
must therefore transform compatibly with these symmetries:

\displaystyle h_{kl}=h_{ij}R^{i}_{\ k}R^{j}_{\ l},

(117)

where
R=[R^{i}_{\ j}]
is the matrix representation of the symmetry acting on quasimomenta
\mathbf{k}
.

The Bloch projector satisfies an equivalent covariance property,

\displaystyle P(R\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k})=U(R)^{-1}P(\mathbf{k})U(R),

(118)

which implies, using the invariance of the Fubini–Study metric under projective unitary and antiunitary transformations, that

\displaystyle g_{ij}(R\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k})R^{i}_{\ k}R^{j}_{\ l}=g_{kl}(\mathbf{k}).

(119)

It then follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,g_{kl}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,g_{ij}(R\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k})R^{i}_{\ k}R^{j}_{\ l}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,g_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\Bigr)R^{i}_{\ k}R^{j}_{\ l},

(120)

where we used invariance of the Brillouin-zone integral under diffeomorphisms,
together with the fact that
R
acts linearly on quasimomenta and is
\mathbf{k}
-independent
(note that, to define a diffeomorphism of the Brillouin zone,
R
must belong to
\mathrm{GL}(2;\mathbb{Z})

and hence satisfies
\det R=\pm 1
).
The claim then follows.
In particular, for
C_{4}
symmetry we have

R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
,
and it follows that the integrated quantum metric—and hence
h
—is proportional to
\delta_{ij}
, as stated in the main text.

In the main text, we claimed that
h
, interpreted as the inverse mass tensor, can be, up to scale, interpreted as the integrated quantum metric. Here we show that the integrated quantum matric has the correct symmetries, i.e., the symmetries of the hopping matrix
T(\mathbf{k})
and so we can identify it, up to an overall scale with
h
. Suppose a discrete group of symmetries
G
preserves the free part of the Hamiltonian. That means for
R\in G
, we have

\displaystyle T(R\cdot\mathbf{k})=U(R)^{-1}T(\mathbf{k})U(R),

(121)

where
U(R)
is a unitary or anti-unitary matrix. The
U(R)
’s are only required to satisfy group homomorphism property projectively, i.e.,
U(R_{1}R_{2})=U(R_{1})U(R_{2})e^{i\alpha(R_{1},R_{2})}
for some
U(1)
cocycle
e^{i\alpha(R_{1},R_{2})}
. The inverse mass tensor, is then required to be a tensor which is compatible with these symmetries, namely,

\displaystyle h_{kl}=h_{ij}R^{i}_{\,k}R^{j}_{\,l},

(122)

where
R=[R^{i}_{\ j}]
is the matrix representation of the symmetry acting on the quasimomenta
\mathbf{k}
.

The Bloch projector will satisfy equivalent symmetry properties

\displaystyle P(R\cdot\mathbf{k})=U(R)^{-1}P(\mathbf{k})U(R),

(123)

which implies that, due to invariance of the Fubini-Study metric under projective unitary and anti-unitary transformations,

\displaystyle g_{ij}(R\cdot\mathbf{k})R^{i}_{\,k}R^{j}_{\,l}=g_{kl}(\mathbf{k}).

(124)

It then follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;g_{kl}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;g_{ij}(R\cdot\mathbf{k})R^{i}_{\,k}R^{j}_{\,l}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;g_{ij}(R\cdot\mathbf{k})\right)R^{i}_{\,k}R^{j}_{\,l}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathrm{BZ}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\;g_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\right)R^{i}_{\,k}R^{j}_{\,l},

(125)

where we used invariance of the integral under diffeomorphisms. The claim then follows. In particular for
C_{4}

1&0\end{bmatrix}
and it follows that the integrated quantum metric, and hence
h
, is proportional to
\delta_{ij}
, as written in the main text.
