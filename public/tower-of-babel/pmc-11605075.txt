Susceptibility-induced internal gradients reveal axon morphology and cause anisotropic effects in the diffusion-weighted MRI signal
Authors: S. Winther, H. Lundell, J. Rafael-Patiño, M. Andersson, J.-P. Thiran, T. B. Dyrby
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Diffusion-weighted MRI is our most promising method for estimating microscopic tissue morphology in vivo. The signal acquisition is based on scanner-generated external magnetic gradients. However, it will also be affected by susceptibility-induced internal magnetic gradients caused by interactions between the tissue and the static magnetic field of the scanner. With 3D in silico experiments, we show how internal gradients cause morphology-, compartment-, and orientation-dependence of spin-echo and pulsed-gradient spin-echo experiments in myelinated axons. These effects surpass those observed with previous 2D modelling corresponding to straight cylinders. For an ex vivo monkey brain, we observe the orientation-dependence generated only when including non-circular cross-sections in the in silico morphological configurations, and find orientation-dependent deviation of up to 17% for diffusion tensor metrics. Interestingly, we find that the orientation-dependence not only biases the signal across different brain regions, but also carries a sensitivity to the morphology of axonal cross-sections which is not attainable by the idealised theoretical diffusion-weighted MRI signal.

Introduction

Diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI) can reveal microscopic tissue morphology of the brain by measuring water diffusion properties within the tissue. The image contrast is induced by manipulating the phase coherence of nuclear spins with external magnetic gradients applied with the MR scanner. However, because different tissue components possess different magnetic susceptibilities, also internal magnetic gradients are induced in the tissue and will contribute to the image contrast. The internal gradients are morphology- and orientation-dependent, and correlate with the strength of the MR scanner’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0-field; thereby having a larger impact in the emerging ultra-high field scanners. The morphology-dependence gives rise to internal gradients across different scales; from the microscopic scale (e.g. between myelin and neighbouring tissue compartments) to the macroscopic scale (e.g. between different tissue regions). As diffusion lengths in a typical dMRI experiment is on the order of (10-100) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu \hbox {m}$$\end{document}μm, the internal gradients at the microscopic scale are particularly important. Meanwhile, gradients at the macroscopic scale will appear constant or linear within this diffusion range and are hence easily cancelled out. Some MRI sequences have been shown to successfully leverage functional and structural information contained by internal gradients to measure e.g. blood oxygenation1, tissue iron content2, myelination3, axon density4, axonal fiber directions5–8, and axonal orientation dispersion9.

dMRI sequences, on the contrary, are per design less sensitive to internal gradients, and effects of these are commonly disregarded. Early ex vivo studies found that orientation-dependent internal gradients have no measurable effect on the estimated diffusion anisotropy for optic garfish nerves and sciatic frog nerves at 2.35 T10, nor pig spinal cord at 4.7 T11. An in vivo study found no difference between ADC of human corpus callosum obtained from pulse-gradient spin-echo (PGSE) and the cross-term-reducing bipolar gradient pair (BGP) sequence at 1.5 T12. Recently, an in vivo study at 3 T13 found that fibre orientation w.r.t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0was responsible for up to 7% variance in the fractional anisotropy, when analysing white matter tracts across the whole brain and multiple subjects. Despite these findings, still no effect was found when analysing individual white matter fibres. Furthermore, in silico studies have investigated the effect of susceptibility on diffusion tensor (DT) metrics under variable diffusion times for 2D discs, randomly folded solid tubes, and random medium14and sample orientation for straight cylinders15. Nevertheless, given the dependence on morphology, orientation and field strength, further investigations for realistic axon morphologies and higher field strength hold the potential to provide novel insights into the underlying microstructure.

The most commonly applied sequence in dMRI is the pulsed-gradient spin-echo (PGSE)16. In the presence of internal gradients and diffusion beyond a lengthscale transcending the non-linearities of these gradients, the isolated spin-echo (SE) will be attenuated due to dephasing17. This is measured as a T2 effect. Moreover, because cross-terms arise between the external pulsed gradients and the internal gradients16,18, the PGSE signal becomes sensitive not only to diffusion in specific directions (as desired), but also to internal gradients in specific directions. These aspects may cause both the SE and PGSE signals from identical microstructure to deviate if oriented differently w.r.t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0.

Diffusion-weighting of the PGSE sequence can be signified by a b-vector. However, in the presence of internal gradients the effective b-vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{eff}$$\end{document}beff is a sum of contributions also from the internal gradients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{int}$$\end{document}bint, and from cross-terms between the internal and external gradients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{cross}$$\end{document}bcross16,18. This means that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{eff}$$\end{document}beffwill depend on the internal gradients of the given tissue, and thereby may reflect the morphology of the tissue. In addition, spatial variation in the internal gradient field would cause variance in the local dephasing and consequently contribute to non-mono-exponential attenuation of the signal. This would be reflected in higher-order diffusion terms14. Furthermore, because the impact of the internal gradients may be different across tissue compartments, the common normalization w.r.t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(b_{des}=0)$$\end{document}S(bdes=0) may shift the weighting of different compartments.

Different strategies have been developed to correct for cross-terms in PGSE sequences. Cross-terms with internal gradients that appear linear over a characteristic diffusion length can be corrected by e.g. taking the geometric average of the signals from two b-vectors of opposite polarity19,20, using bipolar pulsed gradients on each side of the inversion pulse21, or numerical optimization scheme for general waveforms18. However, to cancel out cross-terms with internal gradients that appear nonlinear, more advanced sequences are required. A 13-interval STEAM sequence22 has proven capable - but is only practically applicable at long diffusion times. Effects of internal gradients in PGSE must therefore be addressed as part of the signal modelling. To do so, we need a better understanding of the mechanisms behind these internal gradients and their effects on dMRI signals.

In brain white matter, the main contribution to orientation-dependent internal gradients is believed to come from myelin due to its microscopic anisotropy23,24. Myelinated axons have commonly been approximated as infinitely long non-touching straight cylinders for microstructure modelling with dMRI25. For the special case of an infinitely long isolated straight coaxial cylinder, the susceptibility-induced gradients have been solved analytically; both when considering the susceptibility of myelin as isotropic26, and as anisotropic (i.e. modelling the polarity of the lipids within the myelin)27,28. In either case, gradients are induced only in the extra-axonal and myelin compartments, while the intra-axonal compartment is affected homogeneously; thereby leaving any intra-axonal SE signal unaffected. Furthermore, gradients are induced only when the orientation of the cylinder is non-parallel with the applied magnetic field.

From recent 3D synchrotron X-ray nano-holotomography (XNH) in monkey brain29, and 3D electron microscopy in rodent brains30,31, the morphology of myelinated axons has been found to demonstrate non-circular cross-sections, longitudinal diameter variations, and tortuous trajectories. Tortuous trajectories have previously been quantified by micro-dispersion29. These deviations from straight coaxial cylinders may induce internal gradients also in the intra-axonal compartment which have not been accounted for previously. More realistic axon morphology has been simulated in 2D based on ellipses and segmentations of electron microscopy images from mouse white matter32. These experiments do express internal gradients in the intra-axonal compartment. However, the 2D representation corresponds to infinitely long straight axons and thereby neglects all effects from the histology-documented longitudinal variations (diameter variation and tortuosity). Hence, any gradients that would be induced in the third dimension along the axons are not expressed.

In this work, we explore susceptibility-induced orientation-dependence of the SE and PGSE signals of myelinated axons at 7 T. We perform in silico experiments by integrating susceptibility-induced internal gradients in Monte Carlo diffusion simulations. With in silico experiments in individual axons, we study how different 3D morphological characteristics affect the orientation-dependence of the intra-axonal compartment. The characteristics have been quantified from XNH imaging of the corpus callosum (CC) of a vervet monkey brain29, and include non-circular cross-sections, longitudinal diameter variations, and trajectory variations. With in silico experiments in hexagonally packed helical axons, we study differences in orientation-dependence across the intra-axonal and extra-axonal compartments at different orientations. To compare our simulations with real MRI scans, we scan a cube of an ex vivo vervet monkey brain, and analyse the PGSE signals of the CC and the two cingula (CING-L and CING-R).

Susceptibility-induced orientation-dependence varies across tissue compartments and with the degree of axonal undulations

We now explore the effect of increasing the degree of undulation, and how the internal gradients can give rise to compartment-specific orientation-dependence, which in turn can cause orientation-dependent filtering of these tissue compartments. Undulation describes bending in a regular and repeating pattern, whereas tortuosity describes irregular bending. For this purpose, we construct numerical substrates of densely packed myelinated axons, where myelinated axons are constructed as coaxial helices on a hexagonal grid. The substrates resemble the C3 axons from the XNH-informed axon configurations (Fig. 1a), i.e. constant diameter and tortuous axon trajectories.

Discussion

With in silico experiments, we show that the susceptibility-induced orientation-dependence trends for the intra-axonal SE and PGSE signals are specific to the morphological characteristics expressed in an axon population. Both the cross-sectional and longitudinal morphological variations prove to impact the orientation-dependence. Compared to previous 2D modelling32, our 3D modelling thereby provides additional insight into susceptibility-induced orientation-dependence of myelinated axons. Interestingly, our findings show potential for a sensitivity to cross-sectional morphology of myelinated axons. This sensitivity is not attainable with the theoretical dMRI signal (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{des}$$\end{document}Sdes), due to the limited spatial resolution averaging over an ensemble of axons, and thereby smearing out the signal fromthe individual axons. However, we demonstrated that the sensitivity to cross-sectional morphology can be obtained by leveraging the susceptibility-induced orientation-dependence. Furthermore, we show that the intra- and extra-axonal compartments can be affected differently by these internal gradients, and thereby cause orientation-dependent filtering of the signals from those compartments.

With ex vivo experiments in monkey (CC, CING-L, and CING-R), we reproduced the orientation-dependence trend observed only for the in silico axon population expressing the full range of morphological characteristics (longitudinal diameter variations, tortuosity, and cross-sectional variation - i.e. real axons segmented from XNH volumes). Hence, our results contradict the more simple representations of myelinated axons without longitudinal morphological variation.

We show that AD demonstrates orientation-dependence while RD remains stable. We attribute this anisotropic effect of the PGSE signal to arise from the cross-terms between the external and internal gradients. Previously, orientation-dependence of dMRI has only been documented across different white matter regions where differences in microstructure across regions cannot be ruled out as a contributing factor8,15,34.

The orientation-dependence observed in this study is further reason to take caution when comparing signals from different regions of myelinated axons. This is relevant both for axon populations oriented differently w.r.t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0, and for axon populations expressing different morphological characteristics. If effects of internal gradients are not incorporated in the applied modelling, the certainty of the model estimates may be limited due to the orientation-dependence. This becomes only more relevant with higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0 strengths.

Ex vivo orientation-dependence trends deviate from in vivo observations

We show that the orientation-dependence trend of MD and AD observed in the corpus callosum and cingula of an ex vivo monkey brain corresponds to that observed for the numerical C5 axons (XNH-segmentation from an ex vivo monkey corpus callosum expressing longitudinal diameter variation, tortuosity, and non-circular cross-sections).

A recent study by Kleban et al13. examined the impact of head orientation w.r.t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0 on DT metrics pooled from white matter fibres across five in vivo human brains at 3 T. A tiltable head coil (originally designed to increase patient comfort in the scanner) was re-purposed for acquiring scans at different orientations w.r.t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B035. While correlations were found between DT metrics and the orientation for specific white matter regions (Fig. 4 in13), it was not possible to interpret any orientation-dependence trends - likely due to the limited 18 deg difference in orientations w.r.t. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0. Instead, trends were observed for voxels pooled from across different white matter regions (Fig. 3 in13). Although these are not specific to the morphology and compartmental volume fraction of specific white matter regions, a comparison to our results is relevant. Interestingly, these trends deviate from the trends observed in our study. Instead of resembling the numerical C5 axons, the trends observed by Kleban et al. resemble that of the numerical C4 axons (i.e. axons expressing longitudinal diameter variation and tortuosity but circular cross-sections rather than non-circular cross-sections).

The studies have several differences. Most prominent is that one study was performed in vivo and the other ex vivo. Tissue fixation is necessary in order to conserve the tissue ex vivo. Although this process intends to minimise changes in microstructural properties, some change is inevitable (see Sect. 3.7.3). Hence, the deviation between ex vivo and in vivo orientation-dependence trends could indicate a change in the morphological features of myelinated axons caused by ex vivo tissue processing.

Undoubtedly, this motivates further research due to the potential impact on the transfer of morphological observations between ex vivo and in vivo studies. Not only for MRI but also for other ex vivo imaging modalities.

With great b-value comes great filtering

We show that AD of the intra-axonal signal decreases through the morphological configurations C1-5; in correspondence with increasing obstructions in the axial direction caused by the morphological characteristics (Fig. 1c). The dependence on obstructions in the axial direction increases significantly at higher b-values. Especially the configurations expressing tortuosity (i.e. C3-5) are affected. For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{des}(b)$$\end{document}Sdes(b) this is solely due to the higher b-values filtering out the faster diffusing components (i.e. sections of the axons parallel with the external gradient). In continuation, this leads to filtering of which morphological aspects are represented in the signals. Morphological aspects leading to lower diffusivity will thereby dominate the signal. Because such aspects are likely to be correlated with characteristics of internal gradients, we expect \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{eff}(b)$$\end{document}Seff(b) to likewise reflect a subset of internal gradients and thereby influence the filtering. Furthermore, the stronger external gradients at the higher b-values will lead to a stronger influence by the cross-terms between the internal and external gradients. Since the internal gradients are anisotropic, so are the cross-terms. This may lead to additional directional filtering of the signal (discussed further in Sec. 3.5).

Our ex vivo experiments reproduced the orientation-dependence trend observed only for the in silico axon population expressing the full range of morphological characteristics (longitudinal diameter variations, tortuosity, and non-circular cross-sections). We observe an increase in orientation-dependence at higher b-values where the signal is dominated by contributions of lower diffusivity, and therefore assumed to be dominated by the intra-axonal compartment. This may indicate that the subset of internal gradients associated with the contributions of lower diffusivity causes more pronounced susceptibility-induced effects. This is likely a consequence of a higher degree of non-linearity of these internal gradients. However, disentangling the combined effects of internal and external gradients is a complex matter.

Anisotropy of cross-term contributions

With both in silico and ex vivo experiments, we show that the PGSE signal in white matter is affected anisotropically by susceptibility-induced internal gradients. How the internal gradients affect the PGSE signals depends on the anisotropic cross-terms between internal and external gradients, and the anisotropic apparent diffusivity16,18. The DT assumes the signal to follow \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (-b_{des} D)$$\end{document}exp(-bdesD)36,37, when in fact the signal follows \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\exp (-b_{eff} D)$$\end{document}exp(-beffD). This means that any diffusion weighting deviating from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{des}$$\end{document}bdes will be reflected in the fitted variable D. Hence, any additional diffusion weighting from internal contributions will lead to an underestimation of Din the given direction - and vice versa. A spatially variant internal gradient field may provide a spatially dependent augmented dephasing of spins. Variance in local dephasing effects may further provide a non-mono-exponential attenuation14. If we consider the hexagonally packed axons with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{helix} = 0\,\mu \hbox {m}$$\end{document}rhelix=0μm (Fig. 3), we see that whenever the axons are not parallel with the applied field, internal gradients will be induced in the extra-axonal space. This is in correspondence with analytic solutions for the straight coaxial cylinder26–28. The gradients will be induced perpendicular to the axis of the axons, meaning that any line parallel with the axon will be an isoline. As expected, the AD (in this case modelling diffusion along the isolines of the internal gradients) shows no impact from internal gradients. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{helix}> 0\,\mu \hbox {m}$$\end{document}rhelix>0μm the isolines of the internal gradients are no longer straight and parallel with the AD. In this case, the degree of orientation-dependence increases with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{helix}$$\end{document}rhelix. The RD, however, is modelling diffusion perpendicular to the isolines of the internal gradients for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{helix} = 0\,\mu \hbox {m}$$\end{document}rhelix=0μm, but still shows no impact by internal gradients. We believe this is due to the much lower diffusivity in the plane perpendicular to the axons, as this allows much less probing of the internal gradients.

Expected impact of susceptibility-induced biases in dMRI modelling

We studied the orientation-dependence of signals from myelinated axons. Naturally, demyelination38–40would affect the results. While demyelination is best detected with other MRI modalities such as T2 and FLAIR41,42, and the primary effect is assumed to be on the extra-axonal diffusion processes and increased exchange across compartments, our findings suggest that also intra-axonal DT metrics can be affected directly through the change in internal gradients.

We show that axon diameter variations can cause significant intra-axonal signal loss when the axons are oriented parallel with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0 (C2 in Fig. 1). Axonal beading (and thereby amplified diameter variations) has been observed in various diseases such as multiple sclerosis38, ischemic stroke43, and traumatic brain injury44. We expect such pathomorphology to influence the orientation-dependence trends of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{eff}(b=0)$$\end{document}Seff(b=0) and the AD.

We document morphology- and orientation-dependence of AD. Several models for PGSE signals are based on a fixed value for the axial diffusivity. This may lead to a bias across different brain regions because the different regions express different axon morphologies and orientations. By assuming a fixed value for the axial diffusivity, the susceptibility-induced variation of axial diffusivity may therefore be reflected in the additional model parameters instead. Examples are axon diameter estimations with ActiveAx45and with SMT46, and orientation dispersion and density estimates with NODDI47. However, some tolerance to imprecise fixation of axial diffusivity has been documented for the SMT model48.

We study predominantly parallel axons. For brain regions with a high degree of global dispersion, we expect the orientation-dependence of the SE and PGSE signals to cancel out. This is in agreement with findings for the gradient-echo signal9.

Limitations

Field strength and sequence-parameters

The degree of susceptibility-induced internal gradients increases with increasing field strength. Here, we studied the effects at 7 T. We expect more severe orientation-dependence at higher field strengths. With a tendency of moving towards higher field strength due to the increased SNR, understanding the effects of internal gradients become only more relevant.

We show the results for one set of PGSE sequence-parameters. However, the results for both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{des}(b)$$\end{document}Sdes(b)29,49,50 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{eff}(b)$$\end{document}Seff(b)14 will depend on the choice of sequence-parameters.

In silico experiments

Due to the high memory usage when computing the susceptibility-induced internal gradients at adequate spatial resolution, our in silico experiments were limited to only very small unit voxels. This did not enable representation of a realistic packing of the axons. Chen et al51. showed that internal gradients from hexagonal packing cause faster signal loss for the gradient-echo compared to random packing in 2D simulations. We expect a similar effect for the hexagonal packing investigated here in 3D. A more realistic packing could be obtained by assuming the interaction between the fields of individual axons to be negligible compared to the interaction between the individual axon and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B0. This could be implemented by computing the internal gradients of individual axons, and then adding these contributions to an arbitrary grid while allowing super-position only in the extra-axonal compartment.

The XNH-informed axon configurations were based on 29 axons from the same tissue sample. Due to the low signal-to-noise ratio of the XNH volumes, a robust manual segmentation proved challenging for axons with mean diameters smaller than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \mu$$\end{document}2μ. Hence, the segmented axons are not representative of the underlying distribution w.r.t. diameter29, and the diameter variation between the axons considered here, may therefore be more limited than that of the true underlying distribution. Higher morphological variation among the axons may lead to higher variation in the signals shown in Fig. 1.

Myelin is believed to be the main contributor to internal gradients in white matter23. We studied the effect of different axon and myelin morphology but did not consider effects from nodes of Ranvier. Such variations in myelin are expected to induce further inhomogeneity. We neglected the anisotropic organisation of the myelin lipids. The anisotropic organisation can potentially give rise to additional dependence on the orientation between axons and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_{\textbf{0}}$$\end{document}B032, along with dipolar effects28, which could contribute to a magic angle effect35. We approximated the susceptibility values for myelin, intra-axonal and extra-axonal compartments based on those of biological fat (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-7.79\cdot 10^-{-6}$$\end{document}-7.79·10--6) and water (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-9.04\cdot 10^-{-6}$$\end{document}-9.04·10--6)52,53. Deviation in the differences of susceptibility across compartments would influence the results. Larger differences would cause stronger field inhomogeneity and vice versa54. measured the susceptibility of pure phospholipids DPPC to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-9.68\cdot 10^-{-6}$$\end{document}-9.68·10--6. Using this approximation would result in weaker field inhomogeneity. A change from positive to negative difference is expected to inverse the field. However, the resulting effect on the phase accumulation would be the same.

Due to the differences in the internal gradients induced in the individual tissue compartments, we expect that exchange across the myelin has an impact on the effective signal. We did not model such effects here.

Ex vivo experiments and relation to in vivo systems

Several aspects make it infeasible to directly transfer our ex vivo findings to in vivo predictions. The complete impact of tissue fixation is unknown, but both microstructural and diffusion properties undergo changes compared to when the tissue is in its in vivo state. The tissue scanned here, was perfusion fixated with formaldehyde which is the most well-established chemical fixation agent for MRI55. However, chemical fixation with aldehydes may in some cases compromise the myelin structure by loosening and splitting of adjacent membrane layers56. This would change the bulk susceptibility properties of the myelin, and hence, the susceptibility-induced internal gradients. Chan et al57. found contributions of MR phase contrast to differ substantially between in vivo and ex vivo scans. Although fractional anisotropy is reported as stable under these conditions, D’Arceuil et al58. report up to 80% decrease in the apparent diffusion coefficient in white matter due to formalin fixation. Birkl et al59. report a 4% decrease in water content, and a >25% decrease in T2 values in white matter due to formalin fixation. By rinsing the tissue with saline prior to scanning (as done here), T2 values can be partly restored60. Based on these deviations between in vivo and ex vivo tissue, we expect stronger susceptibility effects in vivo due to 1) higher diffusivity, 2) longer TEs, and 3) more structured myelin.

Methods

Monkey brain tissue

One brain was prepared as part of the synchrotron experiment described in Andersson et al29. to image axons in nanometer image resolution. The second brain was used for the dMRI experiments. The tissue comes from two perfusion fixed female and age matched (32 month) vervet (Chlorocebus aethiops) monkey brains, obtained from the Montreal Monkey Brain Bank. The monkeys, cared for on the island of St. Kitts, had been treated in line with a protocol approved by The Caribbean Primate Center of St. Kitts. The brains had previously been stored and prepared according to Dyrby et al61.

In silico experiments

In silico DWI experiments were carried out for two branches of axon-mimicking numerical substrates: 1) isolated axons expressing different combinations of morphological characteristics quantified from axons previously segmented from synchrotron X-ray nano-holotomography (XNH) volumes29, and 2) hexagonally packed helical axons (coaxial cylinders). Monte Carlo diffusion simulations were carried out with surface mesh representations of the numerical substrates. For each mesh representation we generated a three-compartment volume segmentation (intra-axonal, extra-axonal and myelin) as a voxelized representation from where we computed the susceptibility-induced internal gradients. The internal gradients were integrated into the Monte Carlo simulations by including the phase accumulation caused by the internal gradients when computing the DWI signal.

XNH-informed axon configurations (intra-axonal compartment)

To study how different morphological characteristics of axons affect the susceptibility-induced internal gradients and the related SE and PGSE signals, we conducted simulations for 29 segmentations of real axons, and four different configurations of morphological characteristics quantified from each of these (Fig. 1a). The quantified characteristics are mean axon diameter, longitudinal diameter variation, and axonal trajectory. Expressed in the five configurations (C1-5) we have C5: segmentations of real axons, C1: mean diameter with straight axon trajectories (cylinders), C2: longitudinal diameter variations with straight axon trajectories, C3: mean diameter and tortuous axon trajectories, and C4: longitudinal diameter variations and tortuous axon trajectories. All axons are aligned with the z-axis.

The axons were segmented from a 3D XNH volume of the splenium of a 32-month old vervet monkey brain. The volume was acquired with 75 nm isotropic voxel size and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$153.6\,\upmu \text {m}$$\end{document}153.6μm cylindrical field-of-view at beamline ID16A of the European Synchrotron. The meshes were decimated in Blender to reduce triangle density, while preserving the mesh volume, to reduce the computational burden of the simulations.

For these simulations, the main direction of each axon was calculated using a principal-component analysis of its trajectory, after which it could be rotated into the z-axis. 29 axons were then selected based on an upper limit of the volume of their bounding boxes; in the interest of feasibility of computing the corresponding internal gradients (described in 4.2.3). These make up the C5 substrates. Axonal configurations C1-C4 were first modelled as consisting of z-aligned cylindrical segments, after which one or more of the following properties from the C5 axons was inherited by deforming the cylindrical segments in the appropriate position along the axonal trajectory: mean axon diameter, longitudinal diameter variation, tortuous trajectories. Importantly, although the axonal cross-sections in the plane perpendicular to the local trajectory could be non-circular and non-symmetrical for the C5 configuration, this property was not inherited by C1-C4 for which the axonal cross-sections were circular.

Computation of susceptibility-induced internal gradients

To compute the susceptibility-induced internal gradients of the numerical substrates, we computed how the 3D distribution of magnetic susceptibilities (based on the three tissue compartments) perturbs an externally applied magnetic field (B-field). This is infeasible to do analytically for all but simple and analytically described geometries. To solve B-fields for the complex geometries presented here, we therefore implemented a finite-difference (FD) method.

The FD method requires a voxel-representation of the substrate, i.e. the volume of the unit-voxel partitioned into voxels. This was obtained with a ray-casting-based65 in-house GPU-compatible method. From the surface mesh-representation, the method segments the volume within the unit-voxel into three compartments (intra-axonal, extra-axonal, and myelin). All voxels in the myelin compartment of the voxel-representation are strictly contained within the myelin compartment of the surface mesh-representation; i.e. no myelin-voxels are transcending the meshes. This is crucial for preventing the B-field in the myelin compartment from contaminating the particles diffusing in the intra- and extra-axonal compartments. The resolution of the segmentations is a trade-off between descriptive accuracy of the morphology, and memory usage when computing the B-fields. We applied a desired resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$res_{desired} = 0.0655\,\mu \hbox {m}$$\end{document}resdesired=0.0655μm corresponding to 0.0252 times the mean diameter of the XNH-informed axon configurations and 0.0492 times the diameter of the helical axons. This resolution had to be slightly modified (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 0.0001\,\mu \hbox {m}$$\end{document}±0.0001μm) to match the unit-voxel of the individual substrate.

Image post-processing

First, Gibbs ringing effects and noise were suppressed for all dMRI scans using gibbs_removal() and mppca()from the DIPY library74. Then, all scans were co-registered to a slightly rotated version of one of the scans using aling()from the DIPY library. To minimize cross-term effects between imaging gradients, macroscopic background field inhomogeneities and the diffusion gradients cross-term correction20 was applied (Fig. S7).
