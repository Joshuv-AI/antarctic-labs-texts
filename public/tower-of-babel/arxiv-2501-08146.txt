Proximal Flow Inspired Multi-Step Methods
Yushen Huang, Yifan Sun

Abstract
We investigate a family of approximate multi-step proximal point methods, framed as implicit linear discretizations of gradient flow. The resulting methods are multi-step proximal point methods, with similar computational cost in each update as the proximal point method. We explore several optimization methods where applying an approximate multistep proximal points method results in improved convergence behavior. We also include convergence analysis for the proposed method in several problem settings: quadratic problems, general problems that are strongly or weakly (non)convex, and accelerated results for alternating projections.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the following optimization problem:

\min_{{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;F({\mathbf{x}})=f({\mathbf{x}})+h({\mathbf{x}})

(1)

where
f({\mathbf{x}})
is an
L
-smooth function,
h({\mathbf{x}})
is a closed, convex but not necessary smooth function, and
F({\mathbf{x}})
is bounded below.
The problem with the following settings has been raised in many applications in data science, machine learning, and image processing
[
29
,
32
,
4
,
11
]
.
In this paper, we consider a family of multi-step proximal point updates.
The algorithm is a generalization of the proximal point method (PPM)
[
18
]

where we use a linear combination of the previous
\tau
steps in each iteration, as follows:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+i)},\qquad{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{F}(\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}).

(2)

Here,
\mathcal{F}
is an approximate proximal point step. When
\tau=1
and
\xi_{1}=1
, (
2
) reduces to the “vanilla” approximate proximal point method, of which there are many works
[
18
,
2
,
20
,
3
]
.
In this paper, we investigate improvements garnered by higher order
\tau>1
. Note that
there is very little overhead in increasing
\tau
, as the averaging is done in an online manner.
Moreover, while tuning
\tau
and
\xi_{i}
to a wider range of values would often result in a more general class of methods, this paper focuses on choices of
\tau
and
\xi_{i}
are generalizable across variations of problems, to avoid cumbersome parameter tuning.
This is often possible, for example, when
\xi_{i}
and
\tau
are inspired by
implicit discretization methods of gradient flow
, which have been heavily studied
[
27
,
24
,
33
,
22
]
.
Specifically, we study (
2
) where
\xi_{i}
are inspired by the
backwards differential formula

[
1
]
.

We give convergence result of such extensions, under the presence of a
\gamma
- contractive approximate proximal point method.
We also demonstrate numerically that these methods give improvements in several important applications, such as proximal gradient in compressed sensing with both convex and nonconvex penalties, alternating projections over linear subspaces, and alternating minimization for matrix factorization.

1.1
Related Work

Proximal point method.

The proximal point method, originally introduced in
[
18
]
, involves minimizing the following subproblem at each iteration:

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}:=\underset{{\mathbf{u}}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;F({\mathbf{u}})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\|^{2}.

In cases where the objective function
F
is convex, this subproblem exhibits a larger strong convexity parameter, thereby enabling faster numerical methods. In practical applications, an approximate solution to the subproblem is obtained at each iteration. This is achieved using methods such as stochastic projected subgradient
[
6
,
2
]
, prox-linear algorithm
[
7
]
, and the catalyst generic acceleration schema
[
16
]
.
Furthermore, the proximal point method can be generalized
[
20
]
by altering the penalty norm to

\|\cdot\|_{2}^{p+1}
, where
p\geq 1
. Such generalizations often result in faster convergence rates compared to the standard proximal point methods.

Methods inspired by dynamical systems.

Several works have leveraged the insight that minimizing a function is equivalent to finding the stationary point of a dynamical system. For instance,
[
27
]
analyzed the Nesterov accelerated gradient descent method as the discretization of a second-order ordinary differential equation. Similarly,
[
24
]
examined a method formed by employing a higher-order symplectic discretization of a related differential equation, as detailed in
[
25
]
, to achieve acceleration. Others have pursued a similar approach using Runge-Kutta explicit discretizations, as demonstrated in
[
33
]
. Some researchers have explored optimization methods by discretizing alternative flows, such as rescaled gradient flow
[
31
]
.
A noteworthy example of optimization improvements inspired by discretization is the extragradient method
[
14
]
, widely applied in min-max optimization problems
[
8
]
and variational inequality problems
[
9
]
. The connection between dynamical systems and optimization methods also appears in other works
[
23
,
31
,
15
,
21
,
28
]
.

Contributions

In this paper, we do the following.

•

We propose a dynamical system that, when discretized either explicitly or implicitly, leads to gradient descent, the proximal gradient method, or the proximal point method.

•

We propose a higher order implicit discretization scheme, based on the backward differentiation formulas, which are computationally efficient extensions of existing methods, and offer stabelizing improvements to certain methods that come from unstable discretizations of gradient flow.

•

We give convergence analyses on smooth quadratic problems, as well as convex, strongly convex, and nonconvex smooth problems.

•

We apply our methods to several problems, such as proximal gradient over nonconvex sparse regularization, alternating minimization and alternating projections. Numerically, we see that in these scenarios, multi-step methods improve noticeably over their single-step versions.

•

Finally, we show that in the case of alternating projections, the coefficients can be tuned to accelerate convergence.

2.1
Function classes

Definition 2.1
.

A function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is

if for all
{\mathbf{x}}
,
\nabla F({\mathbf{x}})
exists.
It is additionally
L
-smooth
if its gradient is
L
-Lipschitz:

\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}})-\nabla F({\mathbf{y}})\|\leq L\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|,\quad\forall{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}.

We now give a (relaxed) definition of convexity.

Definition 2.2
.

F
is

\mu
-convex at
{\mathbf{x}}
, if

F({\mathbf{y}})\geq F({\mathbf{x}})+\langle\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}),{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}.

for any
{\mathbf{y}}
.
If
F
is
\mu
-convex for all points
{\mathbf{x}}
, we say that
F
is
\mu
-convex.

Specifically,
\mu\geq 0
implies
F
is convex, and if
\mu<0
then the function may be nonconvex.
Note that if
F
is
L
-smooth, it is also
-L
-convex.
However, the condition of
\mu
-convex with a negative
\mu
is more general; for example, the function
F({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{x}}^{4}-{\mathbf{x}}^{2}
is not
L
-smooth nor convex, but is weakly convex (
\mu=-2
).

2.2
The proximal flow

Consider (
1
) where
f({\mathbf{x}})
is
L
-smooth and
h({\mathbf{x}})
is a convex function.
The local minimum of
F({\mathbf{x}})=f({\mathbf{x}})+h({\mathbf{x}})
is a stationary point of the following differential equation (DE), which we term the
proximal flow

\dot{{\mathbf{x}}}(t)=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}}(t))-{\mathbf{x}}(t)}{\beta}

(3)

where

\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})=\underset{{\mathbf{z}}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,F({\mathbf{z}})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}^{2}

is the
proximal operator
of the function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
at point
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The parameter
\beta>0
is often used as a step length control.

Proposition 2.1
.

(
3
) is well-defined; that is, the limit of its right-hand-side always exists and is attained.

Proof.

We first show that for the 1-D parametrized function

g(\beta;{\mathbf{v}})={\mathbf{v}}^{T}(\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{x}}),

the directional derivatives

\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{g(\beta;{\mathbf{v}})-g(0;{\mathbf{v}})}{\beta}=\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{{\mathbf{v}}^{T}(\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{x}})}{\beta}

(4)

exist, for all
{\mathbf{v}}
. To see this is true, note that since
F
is a composition of an
L
-smooth and convex function, it is locally Lipschitz; therefore,
g
is locally Lipschitz w.r.t.
\beta
. Therefore, either
g(\beta;{\mathbf{v}})
is differentiable at
\beta=0
(and thus
g(\beta;{\mathbf{v}})
is continous at
\beta=0
), or there exists a small neighborhood where, for all
|\beta|\leq\epsilon
,
\beta\neq 0
,
g(\beta;{\mathbf{v}})
is differentiable. Therefore, the limit (
4
) exists, for all
{\mathbf{v}}
.
Therefore, the partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})
exists, and we may apply l’Hopital’s rule and conclude that the limit in (
3
) exists.

∎

This DE has a close connection with the Moreau envelope of
F

F_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}})=\inf_{{\mathbf{u}}}\;F({\mathbf{u}})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2},\qquad\nabla F_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{\beta}\left({\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})\right).

Note that this gradient exists and is unique for all
0<\beta<\frac{L}{2}
.
Also, when
F
is smooth, (
3
) reduces to vanilla gradient flow

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=-\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}(t)).

(5)

2.3
Existence and uniqueness

To show the existence and uniqueness of (
3
), we will first show that its right-hand-side always exists, and that it is a special instance of subgradient flow.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider
F=f+h
where
f
is
L
-smooth and
h
is convex. Then the right-hand-side of (
3
) always exists, and satisfies

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\in-\partial F({\mathbf{x}}(t))

(6)

where
\partial F({\mathbf{x}}(t))
is the Clarke subdifferential of
F
at
{\mathbf{x}}(t)

[
5
]

\partial F({\mathbf{x}}):=\{{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:F^{D}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{v}})\geq\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{g}}\rangle,\;\forall{\mathbf{v}}\}

(7)

with

F^{D}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{v}}):=\limsup_{{\mathbf{y}}\to{\mathbf{x}},\rho\to 0}\frac{F({\mathbf{y}}+\rho{\mathbf{v}})-F({\mathbf{y}})}{\rho}.

A common alternative representation of the Clarke subdifferential set is the convex hull of all limiting gradients, when
F({\mathbf{x}})
is locally convex at
{\mathbf{x}}
.

\partial F({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{conv}\left(\{{\mathbf{g}}:\exists{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\to{\mathbf{x}},\nabla F({\mathbf{u}}_{i})\to{\mathbf{g}}\}\right).

(8)

However, note that (
7
) and (
8
) are not equivalent when
F
is locally concave at
{\mathbf{x}}
. (For example, if
F({\mathbf{x}})=-|{\mathbf{x}}|
, then (
8
) gives
\partial F({\mathbf{x}})=[-1,1]
, although the support function doesn’t exist at
{\mathbf{x}}=0
.)

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

If
\nabla F({\mathbf{x}})
exists, then it is trivially true that

\lim_{\beta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{x}}}{\beta}=-\nabla F({\mathbf{x}})\in-\partial F({\mathbf{x}}).

Let us consider only when
\nabla F({\mathbf{x}})
does not exist.
Taking
{\mathbf{x}}_{\beta}=\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})
, then
the right-hand-side of (
3
) is simply

g:=\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{\beta}-{\mathbf{x}}}{\beta}\in-\partial F({\mathbf{x}}_{\beta})

where the inclusion is by optimality of the prox operator. Therefore,

{\mathbf{v}}^{T}g\in-{\mathbf{v}}^{T}\partial F({\mathbf{x}}_{\beta}).

Next, for a fixed
{\mathbf{y}}
and
{\mathbf{v}}
, the function
\phi(\rho)=F({\mathbf{y}}+\rho{\mathbf{v}})
is 1-D (for
\rho\geq 0
), locally Lipschitz (because
F
is locally Lipschitz) and thus all its nonsmooth points are isolated. Therefore, the limit

\lim_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\frac{\phi(\rho)-\phi(0)}{\rho}=:\phi^{\prime}(0^{+})

exists, and moreover,
\phi^{\prime}(0^{+})\in{\mathbf{v}}^{T}\partial F({\mathbf{y}})
.
Therefore,

F^{D}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{v}})=\limsup_{{\mathbf{y}}\to{\mathbf{x}}}\{\alpha:\alpha\in{\mathbf{v}}^{T}\partial F({\mathbf{y}})\}=\sup\{\alpha:\alpha\in{\mathbf{v}}^{T}\partial F({\mathbf{x}})\}\geq-{\mathbf{v}}^{T}g.

Therefore,
-g\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
.

∎

We now consider whether (
3
) always exists and is unique. Specifically, system (
3
) is a differential inclusion. Solutions of such systems exist when
\partial F
is an upper hemicontinuous map, and is unique when it satisfies a one-sided Lipschitz condition.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
F=f+g
where
f
is
L
-smooth and
g
is convex, then the negative of the Clarke subdifferential
[
5
]
(
7
)
is always a closed and convex set, and is upper hemicontinuous

\forall{\mathbf{x}},\;\exists\epsilon>0,\;\forall{\mathbf{u}}:\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}}\|\leq\epsilon,\quad\partial F({\mathbf{u}})\subseteq\partial F({\mathbf{x}}),

and satisfies the one-sided Lipschitz condition, e.g.

\langle g_{x}-g_{y},{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle\leq L\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2},

(9)

for all
g_{x}\in-\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
,
g_{y}\in-\partial F({\mathbf{y}})
.

Proof.

By construction,
\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
is always closed and convex.
Note that
-\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
is upper hemicontinuous if
\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
is upper hemicontinuous.
Then, since

\lim_{{\mathbf{u}}\to{\mathbf{x}}}\limsup_{{\mathbf{y}}\to{\mathbf{u}},\rho\to 0}\frac{F({\mathbf{y}}+\rho{\mathbf{v}})-F({\mathbf{y}})}{\rho}\leq\limsup_{{\mathbf{y}}\to{\mathbf{x}},\rho\to 0}\frac{F({\mathbf{y}}+\rho{\mathbf{v}})-F({\mathbf{y}})}{\rho}

upper hemicontinuity of the Clarke subdifferential immediately follows.
Now we consider if (
3
) is one-sided Lipschitz. Recall that since
f
is
L
-smooth, it simply follows that

|\langle\nabla f({\mathbf{x}})-\nabla f({\mathbf{y}}),{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle|=|\langle g_{x}-g_{y},{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle|\leq L\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}.

Next, since
h
is convex and
h_{x},h_{y}
is the subgradient for
h({\mathbf{x}}),h({\mathbf{y}})
respectively,

\langle h_{x}-h_{y},{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle\geq 0\iff\langle g_{x}-g_{y},{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rangle\leq 0.

Therefore,
F=f+h
satisfies (
9
).
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

For
F=f+h
where
f
is
L
-smooth and
h
is convex, the proximal flow defined by (
3
) always admits an existing and unique solution
{\mathbf{x}}(t)
.

Proof.

Since (
3
) is an instance of subgradient flow (
6
), and since the Clarke subdifferential is upper hemicontinuous, then (
3
) always admits an existing solution.
Additionally,
-\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
satisfies the one-sided Lipschitz condition, and therefore the solution is unique.
∎

2.4
Optimality

Next, we show that the stationary point of the proximal flow (e.g.
\lim_{t\to\infty}{\mathbf{x}}(t)
) is equivalent to finding the stationary point of
F({\mathbf{x}})
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Consider the proximal flow in (
3
) with
{\mathbf{x}}(0)={\mathbf{x}}_{0}
.
Then a stationary point of the ODE (
3
) is a stationary point of
F({\mathbf{x}})
.

Proof.

Recall that if
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=0
, then equivalently

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{x}}}{\beta}=0

which is equivalent to

0=\lim_{\beta\to 0}\nabla F_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}})\in\lim_{\beta\to 0}\partial F(\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}}))=\partial F({\mathbf{x}}).

Hence
0
is a subgradient of
\partial F({\mathbf{x}})
, if and only if
{\mathbf{x}}
is a stationary point of
F
.
∎

2.5
Examples

To give a more intuitive understanding of the proximal flow, we give the several examples of
F({\mathbf{x}})
and demostrate how to calculate its corresponding proximal flow.

Example: Smooth minimization.

Suppose
F(x)=f(x)
is differentiable everywhere. Then,

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{prox}_{\beta f}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{x}}}{\beta}=-\nabla f({\mathbf{x}})

and the proximal flow reduces to gradient flow (
5
).

Example: Smooth + nonsmooth.

Now suppose that
f
is not only smooth, but is second-order differentiable and
where
h
is nonsmooth but convex. Then, in the limit of
\beta\to 0
, we may use the Taylor approximation for
{\mathbf{u}}\approx{\mathbf{x}}

f({\mathbf{u}})\approx\nabla f({\mathbf{x}})^{T}({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}})

and therefore, in this limit,

\mathbf{prox}_{\beta f}({\mathbf{x}})=\underset{{\mathbf{u}}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;\beta\nabla f({\mathbf{x}})^{T}({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{x}})+\beta h({\mathbf{u}})+\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{u}}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{prox}_{\beta h}({\mathbf{x}}-\beta\nabla f({\mathbf{x}})).

In other words, in the limit of
\beta\to 0
, the proximal gradient method also reduces to the proximal point flow.

Example: Shrinkage.

Let us consider
h({\mathbf{x}})=\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{1}
. Then

\mathbf{prox}_{h}({\mathbf{x}})_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}-\mathbf{sign}(x_{i})\beta,&|x_{i}|>\beta,\\
0&\text{ else,}\end{cases}

and therefore, in the limit
\beta\to 0
, elementwise,

\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}-\mathbf{sign}(x_{i}),&|x_{i}|\neq 0,\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Note that in this limit,
\dot{x}_{i}
is not continuous (although
x_{i}
is still continuous).
And, for all convex nonsmooth
h
, by using the proximal operator and not the subgradient, we ensure that
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)
is always attaining a unique value.

Example: LSP.

Let us now consider a nonconvex loss
F({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{i}\log(1+\theta^{-1}|x_{i}|)
.
Note that if
n=1
, then

F({\mathbf{x}})=\underbrace{\log\left(1+\frac{|{\mathbf{x}}|}{\theta}\right)-\frac{|{\mathbf{x}}|}{\theta}}_{g({\mathbf{x}})}+\underbrace{\frac{|{\mathbf{x}}|}{\theta}}_{h({\mathbf{x}})}

where
g
is
L=\theta^{-2}
smooth and
h
is convex. Therefore, (
3
) is well defined for this choice of function.
Additionally, this proximal flow may also be computed element-wise:

\displaystyle\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{u}{\mathrm{argmin}}~\log\left(1+\frac{|u|}{\theta}\right)+\frac{(u-x_{i})^{2}}{2\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{(x_{i}+\theta)^{2}-4\theta\beta}-\theta+x_{i}}{2}\cdot\mathbf{sign}(x_{i}),&x_{i}-\theta\geq\sqrt{(x_{i}+\theta)^{2}-4\theta\beta},\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

For each

, if
x_{i}\neq 0
, then

\dot{x}_{i}=-\frac{\partial h({\mathbf{x}})}{\partial x_{i}}=-\frac{\mathbf{sign}(x_{i})}{b+|x_{i}|}.

Now consider
x_{i}=0
. Then

\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})_{i}=\underset{u}{\mathrm{argmin}}~\beta\log\left(1+\frac{|u|}{b}\right)+u^{2}

which is monotonically increasing in

for any value of

\beta>0
, and is minimized at
u=0
. Therefore,

\dot{x}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}{\beta}=\begin{cases}-\frac{\mathbf{sign}(x_{i})}{b+|x_{i}|},&x_{i}\neq 0,\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

(10)

Again, this is not a typical DE because
\dot{x}_{i}
is not continuous in
t
; however,
x_{i}(t)

is
continuous in

(and exists and is unique everywhere).

3.1

At this point, we have now observed three different possible discretizations of the right hand side of (

\frac{{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}}{\beta}=\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})

\displaystyle-\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}),

3.2
Truncation error and stability

Truncation error.

For a multi-step method, the
local truncation error
of an iterative process that discretizes

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=-\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}(t))
,
is defined as

\epsilon_{k}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+i)}-\alpha\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})\right).

(13)

If
\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\epsilon(\alpha)=0
, the iterative update is considered consistent. Moreover, if the truncation error
\epsilon(\alpha)
behaves as
\Theta(\alpha^{\tau})
, then the iterative update
\mathcal{A}
is said to exhibit a truncation error of order
\tau

It is well-known
[
1
]
that the truncation error of a
\tau

In other words, the higher order the method, the more likely the iterates
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
hug the continuous trajectory
{\mathbf{x}}(t)

4
Quadratic convergence analyses

We now present the convergence analyses of the BDF method under quadratic problems.

4.1
Approximate proximal methods

First, we establish our metric of approximation in computing the proximal point step, which is necessary for all remaining sections.
In general, a full proximal point step
\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}
is not much computationally cheaper than fully minimizing
F
; therefore, in practice, only approximation methods are reasonable to consider.

Definition 4.1
.

Denote the exact solution to the proximal operation as

{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}:=\underset{{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;\underbrace{F({\mathbf{x}})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\mathbf{x}}-\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}}_{=:\tilde{F}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}})}.

We say that an approximate solution
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}
is
\gamma
-contractive
if it satisfies

\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}\|_{2}\leq\gamma\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}\|_{2}.

(14)

There are many ways of achieving a
\gamma
-contractive solution, such as using
m
iterations of a gradient or proximal gradient method, for small
m
.

Example.

Suppose
F=f+h
and
f
is
L
-smooth and
\mathbf{prox}_{h}
is easy to compute. Then computing
m
steps of proximal gradient descent with step size
\alpha=\beta/(\beta L+1)
over
\tilde{F}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}})

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k,i+1)}=\mathbf{prox}_{\beta h}\left({\mathbf{x}}^{(k,i)}-\alpha\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k,i)})-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k,i)}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})\right),

with

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k,0)}={\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k)},\quad{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}={\mathbf{x}}^{(k,m)}

satisfies (
14
) with
\gamma=(1-\frac{1}{(L\beta+1)})^{m}
.

4.2
Convergence over quadratic functions

We now consider the error analysis of BDF over quadratic functions

f({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{x}}^{T}Q{\mathbf{x}},\qquad h({\mathbf{x}})=0.

Here
Q
is a positive definite matrix with eigenvalues bounded
\mu\leq\lambda_{i}\leq L
.
We define the radius of convergence of a sequence
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
as
\rho
where
0\leq\rho\leq 1
and

\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\leq\rho^{k}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|.

(15)

Then, since
\tilde{F}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}})
is
\mu=\beta^{-1}
strongly convex and
L+\beta^{-1}
smooth, then using an optimal step size of
\frac{\beta}{2+\beta L}
yields

\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}\|\leq\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta L+1}\right)^{m}}_{\gamma}\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}\|.

Then, the algorithm dictated by (
11
) and (
12
) can be written precisely as

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau+1)}=A^{m}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau)}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A^{j-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i)},\qquad A=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)I-\alpha Q.

Defining also

B:=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A^{j-1}

we may construct a first-order system

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau+1)}\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau)}\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau-1)}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+2)}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{{\mathbf{z}}^{(k+1)}}\hskip-4.30554pt=\hskip-4.30554pt\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A^{m}+\xi_{\tau}B&\xi_{\tau-1}B&\cdots&\xi_{1}B\\
I&0&\cdots&0\\
0&I&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{=:M}\hskip-4.30554pt\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau)}\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau-1)}\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau-2)}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{{\mathbf{z}}^{(k)}}

and defining
{\mathbf{z}}^{*}=\textbf{vec}({\mathbf{x}}^{*},{\mathbf{x}}^{*},...,{\mathbf{x}}^{*})

we see that it is the eigenvalues of
M
that define the method radius of convergence

({\mathbf{z}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{z}}^{*})=M^{k}({\mathbf{z}}^{(0)}-{\mathbf{z}}^{*}).

Unfortunately,
\|M\|_{2}
is bounded below by 1, so we invoke Gelfand’s rule
[
12

to produce an asymptotic bound

\|{\mathbf{z}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{z}}^{*}\|\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{\max}(M)))^{k}\|{\mathbf{z}}^{(0)}-{\mathbf{z}}^{*}\|.

(16)

That is, although
M
is not symmetric, (
16
) holds asymptotically.

We begin with the following Lemma which allows us to extend many of the proof techniques from the analyses of

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\{a_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
be a
\tau
-step sublinear sequence:

a_{k}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}a_{k-i},

(17)

where
k\geq\tau
. Then there exists
k>i_{1}>i_{2}>\cdots i_{[\frac{k}{\tau}]}
such that

a_{k}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\xi_{i}|\right)a_{i_{1}}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\xi_{i}|\right)^{2}a_{i_{2}}\leq\cdots\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\xi_{i}|\right)^{\lceil\frac{k}{\tau}\rceil}a_{i_{\lceil\frac{k}{\tau}\rceil}}

(18)

for all
k\in\{1,2,\cdots,\lceil\frac{k}{\tau}\rceil\}
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

We prove this result by mathematical induction. When
k=\tau
, by using Holder’s inequality for
p=1,q=\infty
, we will have:

\displaystyle a_{\tau}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}a_{k-i}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|\right)\left(\max_{i\in\{1,2,\cdots,\tau\}}a_{\tau-i}\right).

In this first step, we pick
i_{1}=\underset{i\in\{1,2,\cdots,\tau\}}{\mathrm{argmax}}a_{\tau-i}
. This gives

a_{k}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|\right)a_{i_{1}},\qquad i_{1}=\underset{i\in\{1,2,\cdots,\tau\}}{\mathrm{argmax}}a_{\tau-i}.

Now applying (
17
)
recursively, we get

a_{i_{n}}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|\right)a_{i_{n+1}},\qquad i_{n+1}=i_{k}-j,\qquad j=\underset{i\in 1,2,...,\tau}{\mathrm{argmax}}\;a_{i_{n}-i}.

This inequality can be chained for as long as there exists
i_{n}\geq 0
. Note that at each step, the smallest value
i_{n}
can take is
k-\tau n
. So, the largest value of
k
such that
i_{n}\geq 0
is guaranteed is
n=\lceil\frac{k}{\tau}\rceil
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Lemma
5.1
holds for some initial
i_{[\frac{n}{\tau}]}\in\{0,1,2,\cdots,\tau-1\}
.

5.1
Exact proximal point steps

We first consider the case of exact proximal point iterations. That is, we minimize
F({\mathbf{x}})
via the sequence

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}(\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}),\qquad\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+i)}.

(19)

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
F({\mathbf{x}})
be a
\mu
-strongly convex function. We minimize
F({\mathbf{x}})
via (
19
) where
\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}=1
and
\xi_{i}\geq 0
.
Then
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
converges to
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
linearly:

\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\beta\mu}\right)^{\lfloor\frac{k}{\tau}\rfloor}\;\cdot\;\max_{j\in\{0,1,\cdots\tau-1\}}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|.

Proof.

First, note that if
F
is
\mu
-strongly convex, then for
\zeta\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})
,

\mu\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\leq\|\zeta\|

(20)

and

0\geq F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})\geq\langle\zeta,{\mathbf{x}}^{*}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|^{2}.

(21)

Now, the BDF step (
19
) can be written as

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}(\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)})=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}-\beta\zeta_{k+1}

where
\zeta_{k+1}\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})
. Rearranging, we see that

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\|\zeta_{k+1}\|^{2}+2\beta\langle\zeta_{k+1},{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:mustrongconvex-1}}{\geq}

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mu^{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\beta\langle\zeta_{k+1},{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:mustrongconvex-2}}{\geq}

\displaystyle(1+2\beta\mu+\beta^{2}\mu^{2})\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|^{2}.

As a result, by convexity of the
\ell_{2}
norm,

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\beta\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|.

(22)

Now by Lemma
5.5
, we will have
i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{\lceil\frac{k+1}{\tau}\rceil}
such that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\beta\mu}\right)\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(i_{1})}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\beta\mu}\right)^{\lceil\frac{k+1}{\tau}\rceil}\hskip-4.30554pt\max_{j\in\{0,1,\cdots\tau-1\}}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

for all
k\in\{1,2,\cdots,\lceil\frac{n}{\tau}\rceil\}
.
∎

Although Thm.
5.3
only shows the case that
\xi_{i}\geq 0

we can still have
linear convergence
when
\xi_{i}<0

by setting
\beta
large enough.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
F({\mathbf{x}})
be a
\mu
-strongly convex function. We minimize
F({\mathbf{x}})
via a
\tau
-multistep proximal point method (
19
) where
\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}=1
.
Then it will converge linearly

\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\leq\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|}{1+\beta\mu}\right)^{[\frac{k+1}{\tau}]}\max_{j\in\{0,1,\cdots\tau-1\}}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

for

\beta\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|-1}{\mu}
.

Proof.

Similar to (
22
), we will have:

\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\leq\frac{1}{1+\beta\mu}\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*})\|\\
\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|}{1+\beta\mu}\|({\mathbf{x}}^{(i_{1})}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*})\|\leq\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|}{1+\beta\mu}\right)^{\lceil\frac{k+1}{\tau}\rceil}\max_{j\in\{0,1,\cdots\tau-1\}}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

where the index
i_{1}
is as defined in Lemma
5.1
.

∎

When
\tau=1
, Theorem
5.3
reduces to the well-known convergence rate of the proximal point method for
\mu
-strongly convex functions.
For larger
\tau
, the overhead is a factor of
\tau
in the number of iterates needed; however, for good choices of
\xi_{i}
this is never observed.

The proof of convergence for strongly convex functions is consistent with our expectation, and matches our quadratic analysis as well. However, in general, we cannot expect
F
to be strongly convex, or even at times convex. Thus, we now consider
nonconvex
F
through the condition of weak convexity.
First, we confirm that even if
F
is not convex, the mapping
\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}
is still contractive, as long as
\beta
is suitably small. This is intuitive, since as
\beta\to 0
, the proximal regularization term will overwhelm the nonconvexity in
F
.

Lemma 5.5

(
[
13
]
)
.

Suppose that
F({\mathbf{x}})
is
\mu
-weakly convex function, and suppose
\beta
is such that
0<\beta\mu<1
. Then

\|\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{y}})\|<\frac{1}{1-\beta\mu}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|.

Now we give the convergence result.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
F({\mathbf{x}})
be a
\mu
-weakly convex function; e.g.
\mu\leq 0
.
We minimize
F({\mathbf{x}})
via (
19
) where

\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}=1
and

\delta=(\tau-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\tau-i)\xi_{i}^{2}\right)<1.

Then, given the step size requirement

0\leq\beta\leq\frac{1-\delta}{-\mu}

we will have:

\min_{0\leq s\leq k}\epsilon_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})\leq\frac{1}{1-\mu\beta}\sqrt{\frac{2(F({\mathbf{x}}^{(\tau)})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*}))}{k\beta}+\frac{\delta\sum_{s=0}^{\tau-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}}{k\beta^{2}}}

where
{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}
is an
\epsilon
-stationary and

\epsilon_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}})=\|\frac{\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{x}}}{\beta}\|
.

Proof.

By
\mu
-convexity, we will have:

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})+\langle\zeta_{k+1},\,{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:bdf-proxpt}}{=}

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})+\frac{1}{\beta}\langle\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)},\,{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{=}

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\underbrace{\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}\|^{2}}_{=\beta^{2}\|\zeta_{k+1}\|^{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{2\beta}+\frac{\mu}{2}\right)\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2\beta}\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})\|^{2}

where
\zeta_{k}\in\partial F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})
, and
(*)
is
2a^{T}b=\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}-\|a-b\|^{2}
.
Telescoping,

F({\mathbf{x}}^{(\tau)})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\geq\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|\zeta_{j+1}\|^{2}+\\
\left(\frac{1}{2\beta}+\frac{\mu}{2}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(j+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(j+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(j+\tau)})\|^{2}.

For any
n\geq 1
,

\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\|^{2}\leq n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|^{2}
.
Indicate this fact as
(*)
.
Then

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau)})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{\leq}(\tau-1)\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}^{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(**)}{\leq}(\tau-1)\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}^{2}\|\sum_{j=i}^{\tau-1}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j-1)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{\leq}(\tau-1)\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}(\tau-i)\sum_{j=i}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}^{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j+1)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\tau-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\tau-i)\xi_{i}^{2}\right)\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j+1)}\|^{2}

where
(**)
is from reverse telescoping

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+\tau)}=\sum_{j=i}^{\tau-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+j-1)}).

Hence for
c_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\tau-i)\xi_{i}^{2}
we have:

\sum_{s=0}^{k}\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(s+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+\tau)})\|^{2}\leq(\tau-1)\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1}c_{j}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(r+j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(r+j+1)}\|^{2}\\
\overset{s=r+j}{=}(\tau-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1}c_{j}\sum_{s=j}^{j+k}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}\|^{2}\leq\underbrace{(\tau-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1}c_{j}}_{=\delta}\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}\\

and thus

\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(j+i)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(j+\tau)})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\delta}{2\beta}\sum_{s=0}^{k+\tau-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}.

As a result, we will have:

F({\mathbf{x}}^{(\tau)})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\geq\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i=\tau}^{k}\|\zeta_{i+1}\|^{2}\\
+\left(\frac{1}{2\beta}+\frac{\mu}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2\beta}\right)\sum_{i=\tau}^{k}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(i+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\|^{2}-\frac{\delta}{2\beta}\sum_{s=0}^{\tau-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}.

Now, we pick
\beta,\delta
such that
\frac{1}{2\beta}+\frac{\mu}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2\beta}\geq 0
(e.g.
\beta<\frac{\delta-1}{\mu}
). Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|\zeta_{i+\tau+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{k\beta}(F({\mathbf{x}}^{(\tau)})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*}))+\frac{\delta}{k\beta^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{\tau-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}.

By the nonexpansiveness of the proximal operator from Lemma
5.5

\displaystyle\beta\epsilon_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})\|

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})-\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\mu\beta}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}\|=\frac{\beta}{1-\mu\beta}\|\zeta_{k+1}\|.

Since the minimum is smaller than the average, combining the previous two inequalities gives the result.
∎

The biggest disadvantage in the weakly convex case is the restriction on the step size, which becomes more stringent with more negative
\mu
(indicating weaker convexity). Overall,
\delta
also acts as an added noise term, although the overall rate is still
O(\sqrt{(1+\delta)/k})
, provided a step size is feasible (
1>\delta
). It is not surprising that
some
restriction on the amount of nonconvexity should be present, as adding too large of a regularization in the proximal step will warp the optimization landscape.

5.2
Inexact proximal steps

Although Theorem

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}
satisfies (
14
) and

{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{prox}_{\beta F}(\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}),\qquad\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+i)}.

(23)

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
F({\mathbf{x}})
be a
\mu
-strongly convex function.
We minimize
F({\mathbf{x}})
via (

\sum_{i}\xi_{i}=1
, and
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
satisfies
\gamma
-contractiveness (
14
) for
\gamma<1
. Then
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
converges to
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
linearly

\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\leq\left(\gamma+\frac{(1+\gamma)\eta}{1+\beta\mu}\right)^{\lceil\frac{k+1}{\tau}\rceil}\,\cdot\,\max_{j\in\{0,1,\cdots\tau-1\}}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

where
\eta=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}|\xi_{i}|

Convergence is guaranteed when
\gamma<\frac{\beta\mu-\eta+1}{\beta\mu+\eta+1}

Proof.

We compose a sequence of contractions:

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}\|+\|{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:approx_contract_assp}}{\leq}

\displaystyle\gamma\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}\|+\|{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|+\|{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\right)+\|{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Th. \ref{th:mu_strong_conv}}}{\leq}

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(i_{1})}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|+\frac{\eta}{1+\beta\mu}\|{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(i_{1})}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|\right)+\frac{\eta}{1+\beta\mu}\|{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(i_{1})}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\gamma+\frac{(\gamma+1)\eta}{1+\beta\mu})\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(i_{1})}-{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\|

and

is as constructed in Lemma

. Applying Lemma

∎

Theorem

We now consider the approximate multistep proximal point method applied to nonconvex functions. Here, a more stringent step size requirement is needed.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
F({\mathbf{x}})
be a
\mu
-weakly convex function; i.e.
\mu\leq 0
.
We minimize
F({\mathbf{x}})
via (
23
), where
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
satisfies
\gamma
-contractiveness (
14
) for
\gamma<1
. Assume
\xi_{i}
is such that

\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}=1
and

\delta=(1+\gamma^{2}(1+\beta\mu)^{2})\delta_{*}<\frac{1}{2},\quad\delta_{*}=(\tau-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\tau-i)\xi_{i}^{2}\right).

Then, given the step size requirement

0\leq\beta\leq\frac{2-4\delta}{-\mu}

we will have:

\min_{0\leq s\leq k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(F({\mathbf{x}}^{(\tau)})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*}))}{k\beta}+\frac{\delta}{k\beta^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{\tau-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}}.

Proof.

Recall that the proximal operation’s optimality condition ensures

{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+\tau+1)}=\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}^{(k+\tau)}-\beta\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+\tau+1)}).

(24)

By the
\mu
-convex property, we have

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})+\langle\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}),{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})\|^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2\beta}+\frac{\mu}{2}\right)\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-\beta\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})\|^{2}.

Take

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-\beta\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}),

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}-\sum_{i}\xi_{i}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}+\beta\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}),

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\tau-1}\xi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+i-\tau)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}),

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}+\beta\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}-\beta\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}).

Then
A_{k}+B_{k}=C_{k}+D_{k}
.
By (
24

B_{k}=0
. So,

\|A_{k}\|^{2}=\|A_{k}+B_{k}\|^{2}=\|C_{k}+D_{k}\|^{2}\leq 2\|C_{k}\|^{2}+2\|D_{k}\|^{2}.

In addtion, by
\gamma
-contraction and
\mu-
smoothness, we have

\displaystyle\|D_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}_{*}^{(k+1)}\|+\beta\|\zeta_{k+1}-\zeta^{*}_{k}\|\leq\gamma(1+\beta L)\|C_{k}\|.

And, similar to Theorem
5.6

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{k}\|C_{s+\tau}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{(\tau-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1}\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\tau-i)\xi_{i}^{2}}_{=\delta_{*}}\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}

and thus

\sum_{s=0}^{k}\|C_{s+\tau}\|^{2}+\|D_{s+\tau}\|^{2}=\underbrace{(1+\gamma^{2}(1+\beta L)^{2})\delta^{*}}_{\delta}\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}.

Therefore, via telescoping from
k=\tau
to termination, we will have:

F({\mathbf{x}}^{(\tau)})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*})\geq\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(i+1)})\|^{2}\\
+\left(\frac{1-2\delta}{\beta}+\frac{\mu}{2}\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(i+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\|^{2}-\frac{\delta}{2\beta}\sum_{s=0}^{\tau-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}.

As a result, provided
\beta\leq\frac{2-4\delta}{-\mu}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{k=0}^{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(F({\mathbf{x}}^{(\tau)})-F({\mathbf{x}}^{*}))}{k\beta}+\frac{\delta}{k\beta^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{\tau-1}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(s+1)}-{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}\|^{2}.

∎

Taking
\gamma=0

\delta

1/2

O(\sqrt{(1+\delta)/k})
is still similar.
Overall, the rates in this section is consistent with those for single-step approximate proximal methods, and indicates that
under very general construction of
\xi_{i}

\tau

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbf{x}_{2}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,f({\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\mathbf{x}_{2}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}_{2}\|^{2}.

7
Acceleration of alternating projections

Finally, we demonstrate that the weights of BDF can be tuned for acceleration, in the case of the alternating projection method. Heere, we consider the projection on
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{2}
where
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
are the idempotent projection matrices such that

\Pi_{1}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{proj}_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\Pi_{2}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{proj}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}(\mathbf{x}).

Then, a vanilla alternating projections method performs

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}=\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}=(\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2})^{k+1}{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}.

We perform the Jordan canonical decomposition for
M:=\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}=VDV^{-1}
where
D=J+\Lambda
,
and
J
is 0 everywhere except possibly at its first offdiagonal (
J_{i,i+1}\in\{0,1\}
), and
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of
M
.
Since
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
are projection matrices, their eigenvalues are all
\in\{-1,1\}
, so the eigenvalues of
M
must also exist in this bound.
Then

{\mathbf{r}}^{(k)}=(I-\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}){\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}=(I-\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2})(\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2})^{k}{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}=V(I-D)D^{k}V^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}

and

D^{k}=(\Lambda+J)^{k}=\Lambda^{k}+O(\Lambda^{k-1}).

Now, define
\rho
as the smallest nonzero generalized eigenvalue of
I-\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}
, e.g.

\rho:=\min_{i:\Lambda_{ii}\neq 0}1-\Lambda_{ii}.

Then, it is clear that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{r}}^{(k)}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho((1-\rho)^{k}+O((1-\rho)^{k-1}))\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}\|_{2}\exp(-\rho k)+O(\exp(-\rho(k-1))).

That is, the constant
1/\rho
is a measure of
problem conditioning
; when
\rho
is very small, it means that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
have a subspace that is very close together. Note that in this regime, the alternating projections operations tend to zig-zag.

Lemma 7.1
.

Alternating projections using
\tau
multi-step updates converges at rate
O(\exp(-\eta k))
where
\eta=\max_{i,j}\eta_{i,j}
is the largest root of

\left(\sum_{s=2}^{\tau}\xi_{s}\eta_{i,j}^{2-s}+\eta_{i,j}^{-(\tau-1)}\xi_{1}\right)\lambda_{i}=\eta_{i,j},\quad j=1,...,\tau,\quad i=1,...,n.

Proof.

Define
\tilde{\xi}=(\xi_{2},...,\xi_{\tau})\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau-1}
, and recall that the variable
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For multicase updates, define

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{x}^{(k-\tau+1)})^{T}&({\mathbf{u}}^{(k)})^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau n},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}({\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+2)})^{T}&\cdots&({\mathbf{x}}^{(k)})^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tau-1)n}.

Here, we initialize
{\mathbf{z}}^{(0)}=({\mathbf{x}}^{(0)},..,{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)})
.
Then
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
and

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\xi_{i}\mathbf{x}^{(k-\tau+i)}=\xi_{1}\mathbf{x}^{(k+\tau+1)}+(\tilde{\xi}^{T}\otimes I)\mathbf{u}^{(k)},

can be written succinctly as

{\mathbf{z}}^{(k+1)}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}\\
{\mathbf{u}}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}(\tilde{\xi}^{T}\otimes I)&\xi_{1}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}\\
I_{n(\tau-1)}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{u}}^{(k)}\\
{\mathbf{x}}^{(k-\tau+1)}\end{bmatrix}=:A\mathbf{z}^{(k)}.

Now by Gelfand’s rule, since

\mathbf{r}^{(k)}=(I-\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2})\mathbf{x}^{(k)}=(I-\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2})(I\otimes\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T})\mathbf{z}^{(k)}=(I-\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2})(I\otimes\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T})A^{k}\mathbf{z}^{(0)}

then

\|\mathbf{r}^{(k)}\|\leq\lambda_{\max}(A)^{k}\rho\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\|.

It is left to derive
\lambda_{\max}(A)
. By using Schur complements, it can be shown that if
\eta
is an eigenvalue of
A
, then the characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle\det(A-\eta I)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-\eta)^{(\tau-1)n}\det(\underbrace{(\xi_{2}+\eta^{-1}\xi_{3}+\cdots+\eta^{-(\tau-1)}\xi_{1})}_{\bar{\xi}(\eta)}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}-\eta I)

and

\det(A-\eta I)=0\iff\eta=0\text{ or }\det\left(\bar{\xi}(\eta)\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}-\eta I\right)=0.

Define
\eta_{i,j}
as the
j
th root of the following polynomial, for the
i
th eigenvalue of
\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}
. Then it must be that

\bar{\xi}(\eta_{i,j})\lambda_{i}=\eta_{i,j}.

∎

In general, it is not clear how to identify these roots.
We offer a tighter analysis for
\tau=2
.

Theorem 7.2
.

The 2nd order multi-step method with coefficients

\xi_{1}=\frac{-\rho+\sqrt{\rho}}{1-\rho},\qquad\xi_{2}=\frac{1-\sqrt{\rho}}{1-\rho}

achieves an accelerated rate

\|{\mathbf{r}}^{(k)}\|\leq 2\rho\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}\|\exp(-\sqrt{\rho}k).

Proof.

In the case of
\tau=2
, the characteristic polynomial becomes a quadratic equation

\xi_{2}\lambda_{k}\eta+\xi_{1}\lambda_{k}=\eta^{2}

may be written explicitly as

\eta_{k,1},\eta_{k,2}=\frac{\lambda_{k}\xi_{2}\pm\sqrt{(\lambda_{k}\xi_{2})^{2}+4\lambda_{k}\xi_{1}}}{2}.

Specifically, picking

\xi_{1}=\frac{\lambda_{k}-2\mp 2\sqrt{1-\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}},\qquad\xi_{2}=\frac{2\pm 2\sqrt{1-\lambda_{k}}}{\lambda_{k}}

then

\eta_{k,1},\eta_{k,2}=1\pm\sqrt{1-\lambda_{k}}
,
which means the modulus of the eigenvalues of
I-A
are
\sqrt{1-\lambda_{k}}
, with smallest nonzero value being
\sqrt{\rho}
.
So, since

{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}=[0,...,0,I]{\mathbf{z}}^{(k)},\quad{\mathbf{r}}^{(k)}=[0,...,0,I](I-A)A^{k}{\mathbf{z}}^{(0)},

then

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{r}}^{(k)}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\rho(1-\sqrt{\rho})^{k}\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}\|+O((1-\sqrt{\rho})^{k-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\rho\exp(-k\sqrt{\rho})\|{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}\|+O(\exp(-(k-1)\sqrt{\rho})).

This is an acceleration of the problem’s dependence on its condition value
1/\rho
.
∎

Note that in the above analysis, the specific BDF constants of
\xi_{1}=-1/3
,
\xi_{2}=4/3
apply specifically when
\rho=3/4
, and provide an acceleration from
O((3/4)^{k})
to
O((1/2)^{k})
. For a wider range of
\rho\in(0,2/3)
, acceleration of a lesser amount of acceleration is also possible is also possible.
It is also to find higher order accelerating schemes, by solving higher order polynomials.

Discussion.

In general, tuning multi-step weights indeed has the potention to offer improved convergence behavior; however, tuning
\xi_{i}
is a tedious process, and may require access to problem parameters that are difficult to compute. For example, knowing
\rho
for two random subspaces requires knowing the smallest eigenvalue of
\Pi_{1}\Pi_{2}
, which in computing (for example via power iteration) would require as much complexity as solving the original problem. Therefore, this section is largely presented to satisfy a theoretical curiosity, but we believe the original BDF weights are better choices in practice.

8
Conclusion

The goal of this work is to investigate the use of approximate implicit discretizations of (
3
) in badly conditioned non-smooth problem settings. In this work, we found that the higher-order approximate implicit discretization helps in many optimization problems. However, it is worth pointing out that it is also important to find an efficient manner of choosing the approximate methods. In our work, we focus on the BDF method, which works well in practice. It is worth also investigating other types of multi-step methods and a wider range of problems for which acceleration may be possible.
