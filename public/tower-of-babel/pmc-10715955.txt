Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch in Pregnancy
Authors: Allison Akers, Viviana De Assis, Christopher Scoma, Mary Ashley Cain, Daniela R. Crousillat
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
A 24-year-old gravida 3 para 1 woman with history of bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement complicated by patient-prosthesis mismatch presented for prenatal care. Her pregnancy was managed by a multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics team, resulting in an uncomplicated repeat cesarean section at term.

Case Presentation

A 24-year-old gravida 3 para 1 woman with bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement (AVR) presented for prenatal care at 6 weeks gestation. Her pregnancy 12 months prior was complicated by chronic hypertension requiring a cesarean delivery (CD) after a failed induction of labor at term. She was readmitted 8 weeks postoperatively for abdominal pain and multiple embolic splenic infarcts secondary to Enterococcus faecalis bacteremia complicated by aortic valve endocarditis with severe aortic regurgitation. Emergent surgical AVR with a 21-mm Magna Ease bioprosthesis (Carpentier-Edwards) with accompanying aortic root enlargement because of a small (19-mm) aortic root was performed. Her intraoperative transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) demonstrated normal valve leaflet motion but elevated transprosthetic gradient (peak velocity: 3.5 m/s) immediately after implantation (Figures 1A to 1C, Videos 1 and 2). The patient was referred to multidisciplinary high-risk cardio-obstetric care at first notification of her pregnancy status. She reported mild dyspnea on exertion with stair climbing or moderate exertion (NYHA functional class II). She was counseled on her options, including pregnancy termination, repeat aortic valve intervention during pregnancy for refractory heart failure (HF), or continuation of pregnancy with close observation and medical optimization. She opted to continue with the pregnancy without surgical intervention unless in an emergent, life-threatening condition. The patient was managed by a multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics team with surveillance brain natriuretic peptide testing and TTE every trimester, the results of which remained stable. She developed mild HF, as evidenced by new elevation in jugular venous distention and progressive exertional shortness of breath and orthopnea by 25 weeks gestation requiring initiation of diuretics. She was counseled on trial of labor after cesarean vs repeat cesarean section and ultimately underwent an uncomplicated repeat cesarean section in the cardiovascular operating room with postplacental intrauterine device placement. Her postpartum TTE had normal left ventricular function and similar valve hemodynamics (peak velocity: 4.7 m/s; mean gradient: 47 mm Hg). Her postpartum course was uncomplicated, with discharge on postoperative day 3 without readmission for HF. The patient was counseled on the need for repeat AVR based on reassessment of symptoms and serial postpartum echocardiograms.

Question 1: What is the differential diagnosis of this patient’s prosthetic aortic valve pathology?

Given the severely elevated transprosthetic gradients by echocardiography, the differential includes prosthetic aortic valve stenosis caused by structural and nonstructural dysfunction. Structural dysfunction involves damage to the prosthesis or its leaflets, including leaflet wear and tear, leaflet fibrosis, and calcific degeneration. Nonstructural dysfunction, by comparison, includes any abnormality resulting in valvular dysfunction that is not intrinsic to the prosthesis itself. Nonstructural dysfunction includes pannus formation, paraprosthetic regurgitation, malpositioning, and PPM.1 Echocardiography remains the imaging method of choice in establishing the cause of prosthetic valve pathology. Echocardiographic evaluation of elevated prosthetic aortic gradients requires evaluation of the DI (a ratio of the left ventricular outflow tract velocity to the transprosthetic velocity) and AT (the time to peak velocity) to determine the suspected level of stenosis and to discern true prosthetic stenosis from other causes of elevated transprosthetic gradients. Our patient’s low DI (<0.3) suggests stenosis at or above the level of the prosthesis, and the normal AT suggests a normally functioning prosthesis; at this juncture, a calculated estimation of the EOA is warranted to discern PPM from a normally functioning prosthesis under high-flow conditions. Our patient’s calculated EOA was severely reduced, confirming PPM. It is important to note that she had an elevated transvalvular gradient immediately after implantation of the valve on intraoperative TEE, and both her intraoperative and follow-up echocardiograms demonstrated good prosthesis positioning and normal leaflet mobility and morphology, corroborating the diagnosis. PPM is not a dysfunction inherent to the structure of the prosthesis itself but, rather, is caused by an imbalance between the patient’s hemodynamic needs and the size of the valve itself—in effect, the prosthetic valve is too small for the patient’s cardiac output (CO), resulting in elevated transprosthetic gradients.1,2 The primary measurement of PPM is the indexed EOA to BSA, with severe PPM defined as an indexed EOA of ≤0.65 cm2/m2. Our patient’s indexed EOA was 0.33 cm2/m2, confirming the diagnosis.

Question 2: What are the normal hemodynamic changes during pregnancy, and how is PPM physiology affected by these changes?

Pregnancy is associated with numerous physiologic adaptations of the cardiovascular system—most importantly, a marked increase in CO. CO increases between 20% and 50% during pregnancy, peaking in the third trimester and increasing further during active labor and delivery. This rise in CO is caused by an increase in stroke volume early in pregnancy and maintained by an increase in heart rate of approximately 10 to 20 beats/min in the third trimester. Vasodilation of the systemic vasculature results in a decrease in peripheral vasculature resistance throughout pregnancy, with a mirrored decline of arterial blood pressure.3

PPM, a relative state of functional aortic stenosis (AS), may be further exacerbated by the physiologic increases in CO associated with normal pregnancy, leading to augmentation of “mismatch” between the patient’s hemodynamic demands and CO allowance by the small prosthesis. Traditionally, BSA has been used as surrogate marker for CO and to define the presence of PPM. However, despite insignificant changes in BSA during pregnancy, the changes in CO can result in increased transprosthetic gradients, elevation in left ventricular diastolic pressures, and increased risk of pulmonary venous congestion and HF. Like severe AS, there is an increased risk of maternal arrhythmias, left ventricular dysfunction, and intractable HF that may require premature delivery or valvular intervention during pregnancy to mitigate risks of maternal and fetal complications.3

Question 3: What preconception counseling should women with severe functional or structural AS receive before pregnancy?

The modified World Health Organization (mWHO) risk classification is an assessment used to estimate maternal cardiac risk in pregnancy. mWHO class IV lesions, such as severe, symptomatic AS, are conditions in which pregnancy is generally contraindicated because of extremely elevated risk of maternal morbidity.4 Although PPM specifically is not included in the mWHO risk classification, its physiology is most closely related to severe AS. Patients should be counseled on maternal, fetal, and obstetric complications associated with severe AS before pregnancy, including maternal HF, intrauterine fetal growth restriction, preterm delivery, and maternal or fetal death.3,4 In the context of severe, symptomatic structural or functional AS, pregnancy is generally recommended against, and effective contraceptive options should be offered in addition to counseling surrounding options such as surrogacy or adoption.3 For patients who choose to pursue pregnancy, correctable cardiac risk factors and consideration of preconception surgical interventions may be considered to minimize maternal and fetal risks during pregnancy.5,6

Question 4: What are the management options for PPM during pregnancy?

Patients with severe, symptomatic PPM should have multidisciplinary discussions led by patient autonomy and shared decision making. Pregnancy termination because of the exceptionally high maternal and fetal risks should be discussed and offered.3,4 Patients choosing to continue pregnancy should be managed by a multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics team.3 Optimal management for PPM in pregnancy has not been defined, but close fetal and maternal monitoring is recommended. Patients with PPM should be offered individualized fetal monitoring because of the increased risk of adverse outcomes.3,4,6 Maternal evaluation includes serial brain natriuretic peptide measurements and echocardiograms at least every trimester as well as monitoring for clinical signs of HF.3 Diuretics can be initiated if signs of volume overload develop, although they should be used cautiously to avoid a decrease in preload.5,6 In patients with severe PPM and worsening clinical status with refractory HF, valve intervention consisting of redo valve surgery can be considered. Because of normal valve function as well as limited prosthetic EOA, patients with PPM are not candidates for balloon valvuloplasty or valve-in-valve transcatheter procedures used as salvage therapies in pregnancies complicated by structural valve dysfunction.5 Although cardiac surgery in pregnancy is associated with substantial fetal risk, delaying surgical intervention until postpartum can increase maternal mortality.5,6 Therefore, shared decision making regarding maternal and fetal risks associated with surgical intervention is essential with the patient and a multidisciplinary team, including cardiothoracic surgery and interventional cardiology.

Question 5: What considerations need to be made for severe PPM during labor and delivery?

Delivery planning for patients with PPM must consider obstetrical indications as well as maternal cardiac status. Given elevated maternal and fetal risks, patients with severe PPM should give birth in a tertiary care facility with management by an experienced cardio-obstetrics team, including cardiac anesthesiology and cardio thoracic surgery.3,4,6 Vaginal delivery is preferred in the absence of refractory HF given the decreased risk of postpartum blood loss, infection, and thromboembolic events as compared with CD. An assisted second stage of labor may be considered if there is concern about maternal intolerance of repeated Valsalva maneuvers or prolonged labor.3,4 Neuraxial anesthesia is favored with close hemodynamic monitoring to avoid sudden drops in preload and systemic vascular resistance, which may be poorly tolerated. CD is generally recommended in the context of severe PPM or in symptomatic patients with conditions refractory to medical therapy.2,3,6 For symptomatic patients with severe PPM, delivery should be considered in an intensive care unit or in a cardiac operating room for CD with cardiac anesthesia and cardiac surgery on call in case of a maternal cardiac emergency.

Conclusions

Although PPM is a well-documented surgical complication of AVR, the disease process in pregnancy is poorly understood. The physiologic hemodynamic changes of pregnancy may exacerbate PPM and contribute to increased maternal and fetal risks. As such, preconception counseling, risk stratification, and shared decision making regarding pregnancy considerations are recommended. Management during pregnancy involves a multidisciplinary team approach with close maternal and fetal monitoring. Despite increased risks associated with PPM, outcomes can be favorable, as in this case.
