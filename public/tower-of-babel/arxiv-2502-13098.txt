Abstract
. We find necessary and sufficient conditions for the Law of Large Numbers for random discrete N N -particle systems with the deformation (inverse temperature) parameter θ \theta , as their size N N tends to infinity simultaneously with the inverse temperature going to zero. Our conditions are expressed in terms of the Jack generating functions, and our analysis is based on the asymptotics of the action of Cherednik operators obtained via Hecke relations. We apply the general framework to obtain the LLN for a large class of Markov chains of N N nonintersecting particles with interaction of log-gas type, and the LLN for the multiplication of Jack polynomials, as the inverse temperature tends to zero. We express the answer in terms of novel one-parameter deformations of cumulants and our description recovers previous work by Bufetov–Gorin [ 12 ] on quantized free cumulants when θ = 1 \theta=1 , and by Benaych-Georges–Cuenca–Gorin [ 5 ] after a deformation to continuous space of random matrix eigenvalues. Our methods are robust enough to be applied to the fixed temperature regime, where we recover the LLN of Huang [ 31 ] .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Motivated by classical problems in statistical physics and their deep connections with random matrix theory, understanding the large
N
global asymptotics – that is, the limiting behavior of empirical measures and linear statistics – of various random
N
-particle systems
x_{1}<\dots<x_{N}
on the real line, at inverse temperature
\beta>0
, has been a central question in probability theory for the last few decades. When the parameter
\beta
is one of the values
1,2,4
, the random
N
-particle systems usually correspond to the spectra of self-adjoint random matrices. The first insight into their global asymptotics traces back to Wigner’s discovery
[
63
]
of the semicircle distribution as the limiting empirical measure for the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble, which corresponds to a specific model at
\beta=2
. In many cases, it has been shown that the global asymptotics of these random
N
-particle systems exhibit the following universality: they display the same behavior regardless of the actual value of
\beta
, as long as it remains fixed. Another important discovery, relevant to us, is due to Voiculescu
[
61
,
62
]
, who showed that the behavior of large independent random matrices can be captured by freely independent random variables. In particular, the spectrum of the sum of large independent uniformly-rotated matrices with fixed eigenvalues is described by the free convolution of probability measures.

In the limiting case
\beta=0
, the interaction between random particles disappears and they become independent; then the classical Law of Large Numbers describes the system when the number of particles
N
tends to infinity.
Moreover, in the regime where
\beta
tends to zero at the same time as
N
grows, one can observe phenomena interpolating between classical and free probability.
This middle ground is known as the
high temperature regime
and occurs when
\beta N
tends to a positive constant
\gamma
.
The high temperature LLN for the Hermite and Laguerre
\beta
-ensembles, as well as the
\gamma
-deformation of the semicircle and Marchenko-Pastur distributions, were studied in
[
1
,
2
,
23
,
58
]
. More general ensembles and
\gamma
-dependent limits, including
\beta
-sums of random matrices, were studied in
[
5
]
(see also
[
43
]
), where the authors provided necessary and sufficient conditions for the high temperature LLN. High temperature limits of
\beta
-ensembles have also appeared in a different but closely related context, initiated by Spohn’s work on generalized Gibbs ensembles of the classical Toda chain
[
55
]
, and have since motivated further asymptotic results for
\beta
-ensembles and integrable systems
[
29
,
28
,
42
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, and in sharp contrast with the continuous case, the high temperature regime of the global asymptotics of discrete random
N
-particle systems at inverse temperature
\beta>0
has not been studied in the literature. Other discrete models of
\beta
-ensembles, however, have only recently been studied in the high and low-temperature regimes, see
[
20
,
44
,
18
]
, but the case of discrete random
N
-particle systems was the remaining case to be understood. In this paper, we fill this gap by providing necessary and sufficient conditions for the LLN for a very general class of discrete random
N
-particle systems in the high temperature regime.

1.3.
Applications

1.3.1.
Quantized
\gamma
-convolution and the Jack Littlewood–Richardson coefficients

Recall that classical cumulants linearize the classical convolution of two probability measures. Similarly, free cumulants linearize the free convolution of probability measures, which, as shown by Voiculescu in his seminal papers
[
61
,
62
]
, describes the spectrum of the sum of large independent uniformly-rotated matrices. An analogous result was shown by Bufetov and Gorin
[
12
]
, who demonstrated that their quantized
R
-transform linearizes the operation of decomposing the tensor product of two random irreducible representations of the unitary group
U(N)
into irreducible components, and they established the LLN for this operation as
N\to\infty
. We will show that the quantized
\gamma
-deformed cumulants linearize the Jack-deformed version of the aforementioned operation of tensoring representations, and we will prove the associated LLN in the high temperature regime. Interestingly, we will show that the existence of quantized
\gamma
-convolution for probability measures is closely related to the long-standing open problem posed by Stanley
[
57
]
regarding the structure constants associated to the Jack symmetric functions.

Let
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\}_{N\geq 1}
and
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}\}_{N\geq 1}
be sequences of probability measures on
N
-signatures that satisfy the growth-rate condition from
Definition

2.2
, and such that (see
Section

5.1
for more details):

•

Their empirical measures
\big\{\mu_{N}^{(1)}\big\}_{N\geq 1}
and
\big\{\mu_{N}^{(2)}\big\}_{N\geq 1}
are supported on the same compact set in the high temperature regime.

•

\mu_{N}^{(1)}\to\mu^{(1)}
and
\mu_{N}^{(2)}\to\mu^{(2)}
as
N\to\infty
, weakly, in probability.

Let
\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}[\mu^{(1)}]
and
\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}[\mu^{(2)}]
be the quantized
\gamma
-cumulants of
\mu^{(1)}
and
\mu^{(2)}
, respectively. We are interested in whether the sequence of sums

\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{\gamma}:=\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}[\mu^{(1)}]+\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}[\mu^{(2)}],\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1},

and the associated sequence
\tilde{m}_{1},\tilde{m}_{2},\dots
of “moments” obtained from
\big(\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{\gamma}\big)_{n\geq 1}
by the combinatorial formula (
2
) correspond to some measure that arises as a limit of certain natural “convolution” in the high temperature regime.

Define the finite signed measures
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}
on
N
-signatures
\mathbb{S}(N)
by

\big(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}\big)(\lambda):=\sum_{\mu,\,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}(N)}c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\,\nu}(\theta)\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}(\mu)\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}(\nu),\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N),

where
c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\,\nu}(\theta)
are the Jack Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, defined by
Equation

82
.
Equivalently,
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}
is the unique measure on
\mathbb{S}(N)
whose Jack generating function is the product of Jack generating functions of
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}
and
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}
.
We prove in
Section

5.1
that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}}\mu_{N}
converges in the sense of moments, in the high temperature regime, and the limiting moment sequence is
\tilde{m}_{1},\tilde{m}_{2},\cdots
. Moreover, if we additionally knew that
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}
is a sequence of probability measures, then we would have a natural operation of

\mu^{(1)}\boxplus^{(\gamma)}\mu^{(2)}

\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}\big[\mu^{(1)}\boxplus^{(\gamma)}\mu^{(2)}\big]=\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}\big[\mu^{(1)}\big]+\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}\big[\mu^{(2)}\big],\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}.

In that case, the empirical measures
\mu_{N}

\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}

One of the celebrated conjectures of Stanley
[

states that the Jack Littlewood–Richardson coefficients are (up to a proper normalization) polynomials in

\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}
are probability measures. Even though Stanley’s conjecture is still wide open, we will actually show the existence of the quantized

In fact, next we demonstrate that it naturally appears in the study of global asymptotics of the law of certain Markov chains on
N
nonintersecting particles with log-gas type interaction in the high temperature regime.

-axis represents the time; the marginals of this process at positive times
t
are given by the pure-Plancherel Jack measures with the parameter
\delta=t
.

Let us also mention that the pure-Plancherel Jack measure is a discrete
\beta
-ensemble, in the sense of
[
9
]
.
That paper proves the LLN for fixed
\theta
, but does not consider the high temperature regime; moreover, it expresses the limit measures as unique solutions to variational problems, while our characterizations are as unique solutions to certain moment problems.
Lastly, we mention that the proof of
Theorem

5.17
shows that the limiting measure in the appropriate scaling for the law of the Gorin–Shkolnikov process starting from the empty partition has all vanishing quantized
\gamma
-cumulants, except for the first one. This should be compared with the usual cumulant characterizations of the Gaussian and semicircle laws: the Gaussian has only one nonzero classical cumulant, while the semicircle law has only one nonzero free cumulant. A similar high-temperature statement was obtained for Hermite
\beta
-ensembles, where the limiting measure has only one nonzero
\gamma
-cumulant
[
5
,
43
]
. In this sense,
Theorem

5.17
gives a discrete counterpart of these classical, free, and high-temperature random matrix limits.

This analogy can also be viewed dynamically. In free probability, convolution with the family of semicircle laws defines the free Brownian-motion semigroup, also known as the Wigner semicircle flow; analytically, this is the free counterpart of the classical heat flow by Gaussian convolution
[
7
,
8
]
. Moreover, this flow is related to Dyson Brownian motion through the large
N
limit of its empirical eigenvalue process at fixed temperature
[
7
]
. The quantized
\gamma
-convolution developed here suggests the corresponding semigroup framework in the high-temperature discrete regime, with the pure-Plancherel limiting measure playing a role analogous to the semicircle law.

1.4.
Previous related works

Motivated by asymptotic representation theory, Vershik–Kerov
[
59
]
and Logan–Shepp
[
40
]
initiated the study of random partitions by proving the limit shape phenomenon for Plancherel-distributed Young diagrams. The connection to random matrices was later hinted at by Kerov
[
35
,
36
]
, who linked this shape to Wigner’s semicircle distribution. This parallelism between the Plancherel measure and Gaussian Unitary Ensembles has been shown to be much deeper in a series of groundbreaking works
[
3
,
48
,
10
,
32
]
, which catalyzed further investigations and insights into the parallelism between random matrices and partitions that continue to this day. In particular, motivated by the one-parameter
\beta
-extrapolations of random (continuous) eigenvalue ensembles, it is natural to ask: what are the parallel one-parameter random partition ensembles?

Many articles have addressed this question by studying several alternatives; see, e.g.,
[
37
,
11
,
19
,
9
,
20
,
27
,
31
,
44
,
18
]
. The common theme is that all these partition ensembles are based on the theory of Jack symmetric functions and depend on the Jack parameter
\theta>0
, which is related to the inverse temperature
\beta
from the continuous world by
\theta=\beta/2
. It is worth noting that the random
\theta
-deformed partitions arise not only as natural discrete versions of
\beta
-eigenvalues, but they also have intrinsic interest in various areas, including algebraic geometry
[
50
,
39
,
52
]
and Seiberg–Witten theory
[
46
]
.

Unlike the continuous case, there are many different models of random partitions, and they differ quite drastically, even regarding the state space: some ensembles are defined on the set of all partitions
\mathbb{Y}
, e.g., the Jack measures
[
24
,
44
]
; some on the set
\mathbb{Y}_{d}
of partitions of size
d
, e.g., the Jack–Plancherel measure
[
37
,
19
]
, and more general measures from Jack characters with the Approximate Factorization Property
[
20
]
; finally, some ensembles are defined on the set
\mathbb{Y}(N)
of partitions with at most
N
parts, e.g., discrete
\beta
-ensembles
[
9
]
. In the case of fixed
\theta
, the LLN and CLT for the global asymptotics of the various models of random
\theta
-partitions have been shown to be independent of the value of
\theta
, as in the continuous case
[
19
,
9
,
20
,
31
,
44
,
18
]
.

In the high and low temperature regimes, Dołęga–Śniady
[
20
]
proved LLN and CLT for the global asymptotics of a model of measures on partitions of a fixed size, while Moll
[
44
]
proved LLN and CLT for a subclass of Jack measures on the set of all partitions. Further, Cuenca–Dołęga–Moll
[
18
]
studied both of these models simultaneously and exposed a deep connection between them, which in particular led to a universal moment-cumulant-type formula deforming the celebrated free version of Speicher
[
54
]
. This formula is naturally expressed as a weighted generating function of Łukasiewicz paths (see
Definition

3.3
), and it resembles the generalized framework of the combinatorial approach of Viennot
[
60
]
to orthogonal polynomials.

The universal character of the combinatorial moment-cumulant-type formula from
[
18
]
motivates the question of whether a similar universal moment-cumulant-type formula exists for the aforementioned models of random partitions with at most
N
parts. In this paper, we provide such a formula. The connection between the global asymptotics of random partitions with at most
N
parts and free probability was observed by Biane
[
6
]
and Collins–Śniady
[
17
]
in the context of the tensor product of large unitary representations. However, the asymptotic behavior of their model was studied under a different scaling than the one studied in this paper. In the
\theta=1
case of the model studied here, Bufetov and Gorin noticed that the free version of cumulants from the previous works of Biane and Collins–Śniady should be replaced by the so-called
quantized
free cumulants
[
12
]
. Further, the global asymptotics for a fixed, but generic
\theta
, studied in Huang
[
31
]
, turned out to be the same as for
\theta=1
after the appropriate normalization. The aforementioned papers did not provide a combinatorial moment-cumulant formula and described the LLN in analytic terms. In
Section

6
, we show that the analytic description of LLN from
[
31
]
, in the regime when
\theta
is fixed, can be recovered from our combinatorial moment-cumulant formula.
It is worth mentioning that a different limiting case of our combinatorial formula recovers another recent moment-cumulant formula from
[
5
]
.

1.5.
Methodology

The main technique employed in this article is the method of moments.
More explicitly, we compute the moments of the prelimit empirical measures
\mu_{N}
of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
by extracting them from the Jack generating functions using certain differential-reflection operators of representation-theoretic origin and then study the asymptotics of the resulting expression.
This general approach was pioneered for random partition ensembles by Bufetov and Gorin
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
, who considered the Schur generating functions and differential operators with the Schur polynomials as eigenfunctions. Some applications and extensions followed swiftly by Bufetov–Knizel
[
15
]
and Gorin–Sun
[
25
]
.

In the continuous framework, Benaych-Georges, Cuenca, and Gorin
[
5
]
utilized the multivariate Bessel functions and Dunkl operators to study the large-scale asymptotics of one-parameter extrapolations of random matrix eigenvalues in the high temperature regime. These ideas have been extended to the BC-type by
[
64
]
, and they have already found several applications
[
65
,
33
,
26
]
. In the discrete case, Huang
[
31
]
explored similar ideas for ensembles of partitions of a given length, for a generic but fixed finite temperature. In the main part of his paper, he used the Nazarov–Sklyanin operators
[
45
]
together with a formal symmetric-function version of the Jack generating function, whose expansion in the power-sum basis was crucial, as the Nazarov–Sklyanin operators behave well with respect to this basis. An
N
-finite version of the Jack generating function, closer to the one used here, also appears in Appendix B of
[
31
]
.

The present research was mainly motivated by
[
5
,
31
,
12
]
and, simultaneously, it generalizes them all.
On the one hand, the random partition ensembles that we study degenerate in a certain regime to the random eigenvalues considered in
[
5
]
; at the technical level, our Jack generating functions and Cherednik operators also degenerate to the Bessel generating functions and Dunkl operators used in that reference (see (
6
), (
50
) for the definitions of the Cherednik and Dunkl operators
\xi_{i},\tilde{\xi}_{i}
). Moreover, we recover their main result from our proof of the LLN by picking up the highest degree terms in all polynomial equations.
On the other hand, though we focus on the high temperature regime, we can use the very same analysis to prove analogues of our results at fixed temperature, allowing us to recover the LLN from
[
31
]
. As we have mentioned before, although a version of the Jack generating function (JGF) also appears in that paper, there are at least two advantages of our version that employs polynomials and not symmetric functions:
(a) our JGF admits explicit product forms for a wide class of Jack measures, as shown in
Section

5.2
; (b) the power-sum expansion of the JGF used in
[
31
]
was an artifact of the used tools, rather than a natural choice of the monomial basis directly related to the model of partitions with a fixed number of parts; the latter choice allows for a direct analysis, which was not possible in
[
31
]
and required many technicalities.

A naive approach of mimicking the analysis of the action of Dunkl operators on the Bessel function performed in
[
5
]
and applied to the action of Cherednik operators on the JGF fails. One of the key ideas in
[
5
]
was a decomposition of Dunkl operators as a sum of operators, whose action on the Bessel functions always produces the same result after the specialization of all variables to zero. In fact, this kind of symmetry was always present explicitly or implicitly in all previous analyses of similar problems. Such symmetry does not hold in the case of Cherednik operators
3
3

3

Added after revision: at a similar time when we finished this paper, Dunkl and Gorin wrote a paper
[
22
]
about yet another family of operators defined in terms of Dunkl operators, the so-called Heckman–Polychronakos operators, and they wrote:
“Many questions posed
for matrix
\beta
-ensembles have discrete counterparts, and similar techniques may be applied
to analyze them. This, however, requires lifting the power sums of Dunkl operators to the
discrete setting - leading naturally to either Cherednik or Heckman–Polychronakos operators. It is not yet clear which of the two classes is better suited for asymptotic analysis”
. The lack of symmetry that we observe when working with the Cherednik operators does not occur in the “building blocks” of Heckman–Polychronakos operators, but the cost to pay is to perform an analysis that allows one to control all the mixed moments; in the case of the Cherednik operators these mixed moments are obtained directly by acting on Jack polynomials, which is not the case for the Heckman–Polychronakos operators.
and requires new ideas. Our new key insight is that the representation-theoretic Hecke relations allow us to reduce an analysis of generic terms to the analysis of some special terms, and this reduction is also the source of the appearance of some new ingredients in the moment-cumulant formula (see
Section

4.4
, and
Section

6
for the comparison with the formula from
[
5
]
). We hope that this idea will be exploited for further applications.

2.
Jack polynomial theory and Jack generating functions

2.1.
Partitions and signatures

In this paper,
N
denotes a positive integer that will later tend to infinity.

A partition is an infinite sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots)\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{\infty}
of weakly decreasing nonnegative integers such that
\lambda_{i}=0
, for all large
i
. The size of
\lambda
is defined to be
|\lambda|:=\sum_{i\geq 1}{\lambda_{i}}
, while its length is
\ell(\lambda):=\max\{i\geq 1\colon\lambda_{i}\neq 0\}
. For the empty partition
\emptyset=(0,0,0,\cdots)
, the size and length are both zero, by convention. For a partition
\lambda
with
\ell(\lambda)\leq N
, we can write
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})
, with the understanding that infinitely many zeroes are being omitted.
We denote the set of all partitions by
\mathbb{Y}
.
For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we also denote

\mathbb{Y}(N):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}\colon\ell(\lambda)\leq N\}.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we say that an
N
-tuple
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
of weakly decreasing integers is an
N
-signature
.
By analogy to partitions, we say that an
N
-signature
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{N})
has length
N
.
The set of all
N
-signatures is denoted by

\mathbb{S}(N):=\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}\}.

Observe that
\mathbb{Y}(N)\subseteq\mathbb{S}(N)
.

2.2.
The Jack parameter

In this paper, there is a positive real
\theta>0
that will later tend to zero simultaneously with
N
tending to infinity.
The parameter
\theta
is called the
Jack parameter
, because of its relationship with Jack symmetric polynomials.
In the literature, e.g.
[
57
,
41
]
, sometimes a different Jack parameter
\alpha>0
is used; the relation between them is
\theta=\frac{1}{\alpha}
.

To any
N
-signature
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{S}(N)
, we associate the
N
-tuple of reals
(\mathscr{L}_{1}>\cdots>\mathscr{L}_{N})
defined by

(3)

\mathscr{L}_{i}:=\lambda_{i}-(i-1)\theta,\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N.

This gives the following bijection that will be used throughout the paper:

\mathbb{S}(N)\longleftrightarrow\{(\mathscr{L}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{L}_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon\mathscr{L}_{i}-\mathscr{L}_{i+1}\geq\theta\text{ for }i=1,\dots,N-1,\\
\text{ and }\mathscr{L}_{i}+(i-1)\theta\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ for }i=1,\dots,N\}.

2.3.
Jack Laurent polynomials

Given any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, the
Jack (symmetric) polynomials

P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)
, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N)
, are defined as the unique homogeneous symmetric polynomials of degree
|\lambda|
of the form

(4)

P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)=x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\lambda_{N}}+\text{ monomials of the form }c_{\mu}x_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\mu_{N}}\\
\text{ with }\mu\leq\lambda\text{ in the lexicographic order},

that are orthogonal with respect to a certain explicit weight function; we do not discuss the details further since they will not be needed, but see
[
41
, Ch. VI.10]
.
If
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}
has
\ell(\lambda)>N
, we use the convention
P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta):=0
.
We have the index-stability property:

P_{(\lambda_{1}+1,\dots,\lambda_{N}+1)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)=(x_{1}\cdots x_{N})\cdot P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N).

This property allows us to define the
Jack Laurent polynomials
(see
[
53
]
), for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)
, by taking any
M>0
with
\lambda_{N}+M>0
, so that
(\lambda_{1}+M,\dots,\lambda_{N}+M)\in\mathbb{Y}(N)
, and setting

(5)

P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta):=(x_{1}\cdots x_{N})^{-M}\cdot P_{(\lambda_{1}+M,\dots,\lambda_{N}+M)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta).

The definition (
5
) does not depend on the choice of
M>0
, because of the index-stability.

From (
4
), all monomials
x_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\mu_{N}}
in the expansion of the Jack Laurent polynomial
P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)
, are such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\max_{i}{\mu_{i}}\geq\min_{i}{\mu_{i}}\geq\lambda_{N}
.
We will also need the fact that for any
\theta>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)
, the coefficients of
P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)
are nonnegative, see
[
41
, Ch. VI, (10.12)-(10.13)]
.

2.4.
Cherednik operators

The Cherednik operators are the differential-reflection operators
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}
acting on functions with
N
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
, given by

(6)

\xi_{i}:=x_{i}\partial_{i}-\theta(i-1)+\theta\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})+\theta\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\frac{x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j}),

where
s_{i,j}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{j},\dots,x_{N}):=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{j},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{N})
acts by transposing the variables
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, and we denote throughout
\partial_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
, for simplicity. The operators
\xi_{i}
were introduced by Cherednik in
[
16
]
.

Evidently, each
\xi_{i}
preserves the space of
N
-variate polynomials of degree
d
, for any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
They also preserve the space of functions that are analytic in some neighborhood of any point of the form
(x_{0}^{N}):=(x_{0},\dots,x_{0})
, for example
(1^{N})
.

The following theorem is due to Opdam
[
51
, (2.7)]
; see also
[
38
, Corollary 3.2]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

The Cherednik operators
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}
satisfy the following Hecke relations:

(7)

\displaystyle\xi_{i}s_{i}-s_{i}\xi_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\theta,

\displaystyle\xi_{i+1}s_{i}-s_{i}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=-\theta,

\displaystyle\xi_{i}s_{j}-s_{j}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle=0,\text{ for all }j\neq i-1,i,

where
s_{i}
denotes the transposition
s_{i}:=s_{i,i+1}
of variables
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
.
Moreover, the operators
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}
pairwise commute and for any symmetric polynomial
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
, and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)
, we have

(8)

f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})\,P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)=f(\mathscr{L}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{L}_{N})\cdot P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta),

where
\mathscr{L}_{i}:=\lambda_{i}-(i-1)\theta
.

We point out that (
8
) is stated for
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N)
in the references
[
51
,
38
]
, but this identity can be extended to all
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)
.

2.5.
Measures on
N
-signatures and Jack generating functions

In this paper, we will generally consider sequences of finite signed measures
\mathbb{P}_{N}
on the sets
\mathbb{S}(N)
of
N
-signatures; moreover, we will always assume that
\mathbb{P}_{N}
have total mass equal to
1
.
Sometimes, to be concise, we may omit the qualifiers finite or signed or of total mass one, and simply say
measures
.
In applications, however, we will usually restrict ourselves to the case when
\mathbb{P}_{N}
are probability measures, except in
Section

5.1
.

Given a finite signed measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}
on
N
-signatures
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{S}(N)
, with total mass equal to
1
, define its
Jack generating function
as the following formal Laurent series on
N
variables:

(9)

G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)}\mathbb{P}_{N}(\lambda)\frac{P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)}{P_{\lambda}(1^{N};\theta)}.

We implicitly assume that the measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}
might additionally depend on the Jack parameter
\theta
, so that the Jack generating function
G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}
is defined with respect to the measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}
evaluated at
\theta>0
, see
Section

5
for examples.
Next, we identify a large family of measures
\mathbb{P}_{N}
for which
G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}
is an analytic function in a neighborhood of
(1^{N})
.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that the finite signed measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}
on
\mathbb{S}(N)
has
small tails
if the Laurent series

(10)

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)}{\mathbb{P}_{N}(\lambda)\frac{P_{\lambda}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N};\theta)}{P_{\lambda}(1^{N};\theta)}}

converges absolutely on an
N
-dimensional annulus of the form

(11)

A_{N;R}:=\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\colon R^{-1}<|z_{i}|<R,\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,N\},

for some
R>1
.

Example 1
.

If
\mathbb{P}_{N}
is supported on a finite subset of
\mathbb{S}(N)
, then
\mathbb{P}_{N}
has small tails.

If
\mathbb{P}_{N}
has small tails, then evidently its Jack generating function
G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}
is an analytic function in the annulus (
11
), and moreover

G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}(1^{N})=1,

since
\mathbb{P}_{N}
has total mass equal to
1
.

Remark 1
.

If
\mathbb{P}_{N}
is a probability measure with small tails and is supported on the set
\mathbb{Y}(N)\subseteq\mathbb{S}(N)
of partitions of length
\leq N
, then the series (
10
) is a power series (not a Laurent series) and it converges absolutely on an
N
-dimensional disk

D_{N;R}:=\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\colon|z_{i}|<R,\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,N\},

for some
R>1
.
This follows from the fact that the coefficients of any Jack symmetric polynomial
P_{\lambda}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N};\theta)
are nonnegative.

For our examples in
Section

5
, all the probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{N}
are supported on the set
\mathbb{Y}(N)\subseteq\mathbb{S}(N)
as in the previous remark, and
Definition

2.2
is easy to check.

2.6.
Jack symmetric functions

We recall some material from
[
41
, Ch. VI.10]
,
[
57
]
.
Let
\Sym
be the real algebra of symmetric functions, that is, the projective limit of the chain

\cdots\xrightarrow{\ x_{N+2}=0\ }\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N},x_{N+1}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{N+1}}\xrightarrow{\ x_{N+1}=0\ }\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\xrightarrow{\ x_{N}=0\ }\cdots

of symmetric polynomials in finitely many variables.
Let
\mathbb{Y}:=\bigcup_{N\geq 1}{\mathbb{Y}(N)}
be the set of all partitions.
For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}
, we have the stability property of Jack polynomials

P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},x_{N+1};\theta)\big|_{x_{N+1}=0}=P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta),

which shows the existence of the projective limits
P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\theta)=\varprojlim{P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)}
, called the
Jack symmetric functions
.
Sometimes we denote the Jack symmetric functions by
P_{\lambda}(;\theta)
, without specifying the alphabet
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
.
It is known that
\{P_{\lambda}(;\theta)\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}
is a basis of
\Sym
.
We will also need the following
Cauchy identity
for Jack symmetric functions:

(12)

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\theta)Q_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y};\theta)=\prod_{i,j\geq 1}\left(1-x_{i}y_{j}\right)^{-\theta}=\exp\Bigg\{\theta\sum_{k\geq 1}{\frac{p_{k}(\mathbf{x})p_{k}(\mathbf{y})}{k}}\Bigg\}=:H_{\theta}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y}),

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
, and

(13)

Q_{\lambda}(;\theta):=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\frac{(\lambda_{i}-j)+\theta(\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-i)+\theta}{(\lambda_{i}-j)+\theta(\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-i)+1}\cdot P_{\lambda}(;\theta)

is the
Q
-normalization of Jack polynomials/symmetric functions.
In
Equation

13
, we denoted
\lambda^{\prime}
the conjugate partition to
\lambda
, so that
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}
is the length of the
j
-th largest column of the Young diagram of
\lambda
.
We will also need the following dual Cauchy identity:

(14)

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}P_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\theta)P_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y};\theta^{-1})=\prod_{i,j\geq 1}\left(1+x_{i}y_{j}\right)=\exp\Bigg\{\sum_{k\geq 1}{\frac{(-1)^{k-1}p_{k}(\mathbf{x})p_{k}(\mathbf{y})}{k}}\Bigg\}.

For any partitions
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{Y}
, we also define the
skew-Jack symmetric function

Q_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\theta)
(and analogously, the
P
-normalized version
P_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\theta)
) by the following identity:

(15)

Q_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\theta):=\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Y}}Q_{\mu}(\mathbf{y};\theta)Q_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\theta).

We remark that the sum in the right hand side of (
15
) is actually finite, since it is known that the skew-Jack symmetric functions
Q_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\theta)
vanish unless
\mu\subseteq\lambda
.
Moreover,
Q_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\theta)
coincides with
Q_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\theta)
when

We will also need the following

:

(16)

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}P_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\theta)Q_{\lambda/\rho}(\mathbf{y};\theta)=H_{\theta}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}Q_{\mu/\lambda}(\mathbf{y};\theta)P_{\rho/\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\theta).

2.7.
Jack-positive specializations

Definition 2.3
.

A
specialization
of
\Sym
is a unital algebra homomorphism
\rho\colon\Sym\to\mathbb{R}
.
For any
f\in\Sym
, we denote the image of
f
under
\rho
by
f(\rho)
.
The specialization
\rho\colon\Sym\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
Jack-positive
if

P_{\lambda}(\rho;\theta)\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}.

Note that this definition depends on the value of
\theta
, which is usually understood from the context.

Remark 2
.

Technically speaking, it would be more appropriate to call this specialization
Jack-non-negative
, as the definition allows
P_{\lambda}(\rho;\theta)=0
. However, the above terminology is much more common in the literature and became standard in the field, so we will stick to it, and ask the reader to keep in mind that the inequality is weak.

The Cauchy identity (
12
) turns into a numerical equality if
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
are replaced by two specializations
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
, as long as the resulting LHS in (
12
) converges absolutely.
Furthermore, if
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are Jack-positive, then each term
P_{\lambda}(\rho_{1};\theta)Q_{\lambda}(\rho_{2};\theta)
is nonnegative, hence (
12
) is a series of nonnegative real numbers that converges to
H_{\theta}(\rho_{1};\rho_{2})
and this value is strictly positive, as
H_{\theta}(\rho_{1};\rho_{2})\geq P_{\emptyset}(\rho_{1};\theta)Q_{\emptyset}(\rho_{2};\theta)=1>0
.

Theorem-Definition 2.4

(
[
34
]
)
.

Given
\theta>0
fixed, the set of Jack-positive specializations is in bijection with points of the following
Thoma cone
:

\Omega:=\Big\{(\alpha,\beta,\delta)\in(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{\infty}\times(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{\infty}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\ \mid\ \alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots),\ \beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots),\\
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0,\quad\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})\leq\delta\Big\}.

For any
\omega=(\alpha,\beta,\delta)\in\Omega
, the corresponding Jack-positive specialization
\rho_{\omega}\colon\Sym\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

p_{1}(\rho_{\omega})=\delta,\qquad p_{k}(\rho_{\omega})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big(\alpha_{i}^{k}+(-\theta)^{k-1}\beta_{i}^{k}\Big),\quad k=2,3,\cdots.

If
\omega=(\alpha,\beta,\delta)\in\Omega
, we say that

are the
Thoma parameters
of
\rho_{\omega}
.

Example 2
.

If all Thoma parameters of
\omega=(\alpha,\beta,\delta)\in\Omega
vanish, except possibly
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
and

and we denote it by

.
For any

i.e.
\Alpha(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})

n

and the rest of

for all partitions
\lambda

\lambda_{n+1}>0

Example 3
.

If the only possibly nonzero Thoma parameters of

\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}

For any

where

\Sym

\omega_{\theta^{-1}}(p_{r}):=(-1)^{r-1}\theta^{-1}p_{r}

This means that

Example 4
.

If all Thoma parameters of
\omega=(\alpha,\beta,\delta)\in\Omega

\delta

is a

Equivalently, it is uniquely defined by

For any specializations

of

If

Furthermore, for any specialization

3.
Statement of the main theorem: Law of Large Numbers at high temperature

We are going to study sequences of finite signed measures
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
on
N
-signatures in the
high temperature regime

(17)

N\to\infty,\quad\theta\to 0,\quad N\theta\to\gamma,

where
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
is a fixed constant.

Let us assume for the rest of this paper that all measures
\mathbb{P}_{N}
have small tails in the sense of
Definition

2.2
.
Then the Jack generating functions, denoted by

(18)

G_{N,\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)}\mathbb{P}_{N}(\lambda)\frac{P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)}{P_{\lambda}(1^{N};\theta)},

are analytic in a neighborhood of
(1^{N})
and
G_{N,\theta}(1^{N})=1
.
Consequently, the logarithm

(19)

F_{N,\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\ln(G_{N,\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}))

also defines an analytic function in a neighborhood of
(1^{N})
and
F_{N,\theta}(1^{N})=0
.

Definition 3.1

(HT-appropriateness)
.

Let
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite signed measures on
N
-signatures with small tails and let
F_{N,\theta}=F_{N,\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
be the logarithms of the Jack generating functions of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
, i.e. the functions
F_{N,\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
defined by (
18
)–(
19
).

We say that the sequence
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is
HT-appropriate
4
4

4

HT stands for high temperature.
if there exists a sequence of real constants
\kappa_{1}^{\gamma},\kappa_{2}^{\gamma},\dots
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)!}F_{N,\theta}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=\kappa_{n}^{\gamma}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{r}}F_{N,\theta}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=0
, for all
r\geq 2
and
i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
with at least two distinct indices among
i_{1},\dots,i_{r}
.

We also say that the sequence
\{F_{N,\theta}\}_{N\geq 1}
is HT-appropriate if these conditions hold.

The measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}
on
N
-signatures
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{S}(N)
can also be regarded as a measure on real
N
-tuples
(\mathscr{L}_{1}>\dots>\mathscr{L}_{N})
, where
\mathscr{L}_{i}+(i-1)\theta\in\mathbb{Z}
, for all
i=1,\dots,N
, by using the shifted coordinates
\mathscr{L}_{i}=\lambda_{i}-(i-1)\theta
, as explained in
Section

2.2
.

Definition 3.2

(LLN-satisfaction)
.

Let
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite signed measures on
N
-signatures with small tails.
We say that
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}

satisfies the LLN
(Law of Large Numbers) if there exist
m_{1},m_{2},\dots\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}\geq 1
, we have

(20)

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty,\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{N^{s}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathscr{L}_{i}^{k_{j}}}}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{s}{m_{k_{j}}},

where, in the LHS, the
N
-tuples
(\mathscr{L}_{1}>\dots>\mathscr{L}_{N})
are
\mathbb{P}_{N}
-distributed.
5
5

5

We use here the probabilistic terminology and notation, as if
\mathbb{P}_{N}
were a probability measure, but in general, for a function
f\colon\mathbb{S}(N)\to\mathbb{R}
, the expression
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}[f(\lambda)]
means
\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{S}(N)}{f(\lambda)\mathbb{P}_{N}(\lambda)}
.

If all
\mathbb{P}_{N}
are probability measures, then the LHS of (
20
) can be interpreted in terms of the
empirical measures

\mu_{N}
of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
, which by definition are the (random) probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, given by

(21)

\mu_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\mathscr{L}_{i}},\text{ where }(\mathscr{L}_{1}>\dots>\mathscr{L}_{N})\text{ is }\mathbb{P}_{N}\text{--distributed}.

Indeed, the moments of the empirical measures are

m_{k}(\mu_{N}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{k}\mu_{N}(\text{d}x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathscr{L}_{i}^{k}},

then (
20
) is equivalent to:

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{s}{m_{k_{j}}(\mu_{N})}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{s}{m_{k_{j}}}.

As a result, LLN-satisfaction is equivalent to the convergence
\mu_{N}\to\mu
in the sense of moments, in probability, to a probability measure
\mu
with moments
m_{1},m_{2},\cdots
.
In the case that
m_{1},m_{2},\dots
are the moments of a unique probability measure, then LLN-satisfaction implies
\mu_{N}\to\mu
weakly, in probability.

Our main result below shows that a sequence
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is HT-appropriate if and only if it satisfies the LLN. However, this result would not be complete if we did not show how the sequences
(\kappa^{\gamma}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(m_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in these definitions are related to each other.
This relation depends on the combinatorial gadgets called Łukasiewicz paths.

Definition 3.3
.

A
Łukasiewicz path
\Gamma

\ell

\mathbb{Z}^{2}
that starts at the origin
(0,0)
, ends at
(\ell,0)

(1,0)
(horizontal steps),
(1,-1)
(down steps) and steps of the form
(1,j)

\ell

It is known that the cardinality of

C_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\ell+1}{2\ell\choose\ell}

For example, the five Łukasiewicz paths of length

It turns out that the transformation

(\kappa^{\gamma}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that comes from an HT-appropriate sequence of measures and explicitly depends on
\gamma

\gamma
from the notation of
\kappa^{\gamma}_{n}

Definition 3.4
.

Given a constant
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
and

\cdot\prod_{j\geq 1}(\kappa_{j}+\kappa_{j+1})^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}}(j+\gamma)^{\text{\#\,down steps from height $j$ in $\Gamma$}},\quad\text{for all $\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}$},

where the term
\Delta_{\gamma}
is the divided difference operator, defined by

(23)

\Delta_{\gamma}(f)(x):=\frac{f(x)-f(x-\gamma)}{\gamma},

and then evaluated at

.
We denote the map
\vec{\kappa}\mapsto\vec{m}
by
\mathcal{J}_{\gamma}^{\kappa\mapsto m}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
and the compositional inverse
\vec{m}\mapsto\vec{\kappa}
by
\mathcal{J}_{\gamma}^{m\mapsto\kappa}:=(\mathcal{J}_{\gamma}^{\kappa\mapsto m})^{(-1)}
.

Note that
\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(\ell)
cannot have steps
(1,j)
with

\Gamma
should begin at
(0,0)
, end at
(\ell,0)
, and the only down steps are
(1,-1)
.
Also, there is exactly one Łukasiewicz path in

\ell-1

.
Both observations imply that only

appear in the right hand side of (

\kappa_{\ell}
appears only from the unique Łukasiewicz path with a step
(1,\ell-1)
at the beginning, followed by
\ell-1

As a result, we deduce

(24)

m_{\ell}=(\gamma+1)^{\uparrow(\ell-1)}\cdot\kappa_{\ell}\,+\text{ polynomial over }\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]\text{ in }\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{\ell-1},

where

(25)

g^{\uparrow 0}:=1,\qquad g^{\uparrow n}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{(g+i-1)},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1},

is the
rising factorial
. Therefore the system of equations (

(26)

\kappa_{\ell}=\frac{1}{(\gamma+1)^{\uparrow(\ell-1)}}\cdot m_{\ell}\,+\text{ polynomial over }\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)\text{ in }m_{1},\cdots,m_{\ell-1},

which indeed guarantees the existence of the compositional inverse map

Certain limit of formula (

\gamma=\infty

Remark 3
.

Note that our combinatorial formula (
22
) for the moments is expressed as a sum over weighted lattice paths, which is reminiscent of Viennot’s combinatorial construction
[
60
]
of moments as sums over lattice paths weighted by the Jacobi parameters of the associated orthogonal polynomials. However, our formula is very different, and it is not clear at all how to “read” the associated orthogonal polynomials from our combinatorial expression, which we leave as an open problem here. In Viennot’s construction the sum runs only over Motzkin paths, and the Jacobi weights are associated to the horizontal and down steps, while in our case we sum over a larger set of Łukasiewicz paths, the weights are coming from the quantized
\gamma
-cumulants, and additionally we have a non-trivial weight coming from the appearance of the divided difference operator.

4.1.
The action of Cherednik operators at high temperature

Recall that
\xi_{i}
denote the Cherednik operators from equation (
6

Moreover, it will be convenient to define

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\xi_{i}^{\ell}},\quad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}.

Let
\{F_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\}_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of smooth functions in some neighborhoods of
(1^{N})=(1,\dots,1)
such that
F_{N}(1^{N})=0
, for all
N\geq 1
.
Let

be arbitrary and let the
k
-th order Taylor expansion of

at

be

(28)

F_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N)\colon|\lambda|\leq k}{c_{F_{N}}^{\lambda}\,\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}(x_{1}-1,\dots,x_{N}-1)}+O(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{1}\|^{k+1}),

Note that the coefficients corresponding to the empty partition vanish:

The proof of

3.6

The first

4.1

In all these three theorems, we regard

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, let
\{F_{N}=F_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\}_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of smooth functions near
(1^{N})
such that

given by (

\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{s}>0)

and
\ell(\lambda)=s
. For

, we can write

(29)

N^{-s}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{j}}}\right]e^{F_{N}}\bigg|_{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=b^{\lambda}_{\lambda}\,c^{\lambda}_{F_{N}}+\sum_{\mu\colon|\mu|=k,\,\ell(\mu)>s}{b^{\lambda}_{\mu}\,c^{\mu}_{F_{N}}}\\
+\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\frac{1}{N}\cdot\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{j}}e^{F_{N}}\bigg|_{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{s=1\}}\cdot k(\gamma+1)^{\uparrow(k-1)}\cdot c^{(k)}_{F_{N}}\\
+R_{1}\left(c^{\nu}_{F_{N}}\colon|\nu|<k\right)+R_{2}\left(c^{\nu}_{F_{N}}\colon|\nu|\leq k\right),

where

are uniformly bounded coefficients in the high temperature regime (

). In particular,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\

,

.

,
|\nu|\leq k
, of degree at most
k
with coefficients of order
o(1)
in the regime (

).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let

Assuming the validity of Theorems

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{N}
be any finite signed measure on
N
-signatures with small tails and with Jack generating function
G_{N,\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
.
For any
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}\geq 1
, we have

(34)

\left[\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}}\right]G_{N,\theta}\,\bigg|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathscr{L}_{i}^{k_{j}}}}\right].

Proof.

The desired equation (
34
) follows from writing
G_{N,\theta}
as the Laurent series (
9
), then exchanging the order between the operator
\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}}
and the infinite sum defining
G_{N,\theta}
, and finally applying
Theorem

2.1
, which shows that
\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}}
acts on each

as the eigenvalue
\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathscr{L}_{i}^{k_{j}}}}
.
The condition of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
having small tails is needed to argue that the series (
9
) converges and that we can exchange the order between the operators
\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}
and the sum.
Note also that the operators
\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}
are not differential operators because of the transpositions
s_{i,j}
from formula (
6
), so it is not immediately obvious that we can exchange the order between them and the summation.
However, a result due to Heckman
[
30
, Theorem 1.7]
shows that each
\mathcal{P}_{k_{j}}
is equivalent to a differential operator when restricted to the ring of symmetric functions in the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
; this justifies our argument.
∎

HT-appropriateness implies LLN-satisfaction.

We prove that if
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is HT-appropriate with sequence
\vec{\kappa}=(\kappa_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, then it satisfies the LLN with
\vec{m}=(m_{n})_{n\geq 1}:=\mathcal{J}_{\gamma}^{\kappa\mapsto m}(\vec{\kappa})
, i.e. we verify the condition from
Definition

3.2
, for any
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}\geq 1
.

Assume w.l.o.g. that
k_{1}\geq\dots\geq k_{s}
.
By
Lemma

4.5
and
Theorem

4.1
,
N^{-s}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathscr{L}_{i}^{k_{j}}}}\right]
is equal to the RHS of (
29
) with
\lambda=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})
and
F_{N}
replaced by
F_{N,\theta}=\ln(G_{N,\theta})
(we will drop the dependence of
\theta
on
N
in the notation for brevity, but we should keep in mind that we choose any sequence
\theta_{N}
such that
N\theta_{N}\to\gamma
, as
N\to\infty
, as in
Remark

4
).
It remains to prove that this converges to
\prod_{j=1}^{s}{m_{k_{j}}}
in the regime (
17
).
Indeed,
R_{2}\to 0
because of the hypothesis on its coefficients, whereas

because each monomial contains a factor

with

The terms

, the second line in the RHS of (

LLN-satisfaction implies HT-appropriateness.

Assume that
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
satisfies the LLN with
\vec{m}=(m_{n})_{n\geq 1}
; let us prove HT-appropriateness with
\vec{\kappa}=(\kappa_{n})_{n\geq 1}:=\mathcal{J}_{\gamma}^{m\mapsto\kappa}(\vec{m})
.
By
Remark

4
, we need to prove the following limits:

(a’)

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}{c^{(n)}_{F_{N,\theta}}}=\frac{\kappa_{n}}{n}
,

(b’)

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}{c^{\nu}_{F_{N,\theta}}}=0
, for all
\nu\in\mathbb{Y}(N)
with
\ell(\nu)>1
and
|\nu|=n
.

The proof is by induction on
n
. Let us start with the base case
n=1
.
We only need to verify (a’) for
n=1
, as condition (b’) is non-existent for
n=1
.
By the assumption of LLN-satisfaction and
Lemma

4.5
, we have that

(35)

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}{\frac{1}{N}\,\mathcal{P}_{1}e^{F_{N,\theta}}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}}=m_{1}.

By definition,
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{N}
, where
\xi_{i}
’s are the Cherednik operators (
6
).
Since
F_{N,\theta}
is symmetric in its arguments, it is killed by operators
1-s_{i,j}
, therefore
\mathcal{P}_{1}e^{F_{N,\theta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}\partial_{i}-\theta(i-1))e^{F_{N,\theta}}
.
The symmetry also shows that
x_{1}\partial_{1}F_{N,\theta}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=x_{i}\partial_{i}F_{N,\theta}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}
, for all
i=1,\dots,N
.
Together with the fact that
F_{N,\theta}(1^{N})=0
, we deduce

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\,\mathcal{P}_{1}e^{F_{N,\theta}}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Big(Nx_{1}\partial_{1}-\theta\,\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\Big)e^{F_{N,\theta}}\Big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}F_{N,\theta}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}-\frac{\theta(N-1)}{2}.

From (
35
) and (
36
), we obtain

(37)

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\partial_{1}F_{N,\theta}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=m_{1}+\frac{\gamma}{2}.

Since
\kappa_{1}=m_{1}+\gamma/2
(see
Example

5
), and the Taylor coefficient
c^{(1)}_{F_{N,\theta}}
can be expressed as
c^{(1)}_{F_{N,\theta}}=\partial_{1}F_{N,\theta}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}
(see Equation (
28
)), it follows that (
37
) is precisely (a’) for
n=1
.

For the inductive step, take any
k\geq 2
and assume that (a’)-(b’) above are true for all
n\leq k-1
; to complete the argument, it remains to prove these conditions for
n=k
.

We first prove (b’) for all partitions of size
k
with more than one part.
Take any
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{s})
with
\ell(\lambda)=s>1
,
|\lambda|=k
, and write Equation (
29
) for this partition.
The LHS of this equation converges to
\prod_{j=1}^{s}m_{\lambda_{j}}
, by
Lemma

4.5
and LLN-satisfaction.
Since
s>1
, we have
\lambda_{j}<k
for every
j=1,\dots,s
.
Therefore, by the induction hypothesis and
Equation

32
, applied with
\ell=\lambda_{j}
, we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{j}}e^{F_{N,\theta}}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=m_{\lambda_{j}},\qquad j=1,\dots,s.

Indeed, every monomial of the corresponding
S_{1}
contains a factor
c^{\nu}_{F_{N,\theta}}
with
\ell(\nu)>1
and
|\nu|\leq\lambda_{j}<k
, so it tends to zero by the induction hypothesis; the
S_{2}
term tends to zero by definition.

Multiplying these equations and comparing with (
29
), we obtain, for every partition
\lambda\vdash k
with
\ell(\lambda)>1

b^{\lambda}_{\lambda}c^{\lambda}_{F_{N,\theta}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash k\\
\ell(\mu)>\ell(\lambda)\end{subarray}}b^{\lambda}_{\mu}c^{\mu}_{F_{N,\theta}}=o(1).

Let
p(k)
be the number of partitions of size
k
.
By letting
\lambda
range over all partitions of size
k
with more than one part, we obtain a system of
p(k)-1
equations in the
p(k)-1
variables

c^{\mu}_{F_{N,\theta}},\qquad|\mu|=k,\quad\ell(\mu)>1.

Equation (
30
) implies that its coefficient matrix is asymptotically triangular with nonzero diagonal entries, therefore

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}c^{\mu}_{F_{N,\theta}}=0,\qquad|\mu|=k,\quad\ell(\mu)>1.

It remains to prove (a’) for

Take

with

.
Its LHS converges to

. Every monomial of

on the RHS contains at least one coefficient with

Such coefficients tend to zero by the induction hypothesis when

.
Therefore

, while

by definition.

Finally, since

appears in

This proves (a’)-(b’) for

4.3.
Method of moments: Proof of Theorem
4.1

Consider the
k
-th order truncations

(38)

\tilde{F}_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{\lambda\colon|\lambda|\leq k,\,\ell(\lambda)\leq N}c_{F_{N}}^{\lambda}\,\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}(x_{1}-1,\dots,x_{N}-1).

The operators
x_{i}\partial_{i}
and
\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})
for some

i,j,\ell
, are constituents of the Cherednik operators (
6
). Their actions on
shifted monomials
, that is, monomials in the variables
x_{1}-1,\dots,x_{N}-1
, are the following:

(39)

x_{i}\partial_{i}\left[(x_{1}-1)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(x_{N}-1)^{\alpha_{N}}\right]=\alpha_{i}\bigg((x_{1}-1)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(x_{N}-1)^{\alpha_{N}}+\\
\hfill+(x_{1}-1)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(x_{i}-1)^{\alpha_{i}-1}\cdots(x_{N}-1)^{\alpha_{N}}\bigg),\\
\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})\left[(x_{1}-1)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(x_{N}-1)^{\alpha_{N}}\right]=\pm x_{\ell}(x_{1}-1)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(x_{i}-1)^{a}\cdots(x_{j}-1)^{a}\cdots(x_{N}-1)^{\alpha_{N}}\\
\times\sum_{c=0}^{b-a-1}{(x_{i}-1)^{c}(x_{j}-1)^{b-a-1-c}},

where
a:=\min(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
,
b:=\max(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
, and the sign is positive (negative, respectively) if
a=\alpha_{j}
(
a=\alpha_{i}
, respectively). This leads to the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\leq N
be arbitrary integers, not necessarily distinct. Then

(40)

\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\xi_{i_{j}}\right)\exp(F_{N})\Big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\xi_{i_{j}}\right)\exp(\tilde{F}_{N})\Big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}.

Moreover, this is a polynomial in the variables
c_{F_{N}}^{\lambda}
,
|\lambda|\leq k
, of degree at most
k
, and with coefficients that are uniformly bounded over all
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
in the regime (
17
).

Proof.

Note that

\exp(F_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}))=\exp(\tilde{F}_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}))+R(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}),

where
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=O(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{1}\|^{k+1})
.
This means that
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
vanishes up to degree
(k+1)
at
(1^{N})
.
After applying any sequence of
k
operators
\theta(i-1)
,
x_{i}\partial_{i}
or
\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})
, we obtain a continuously differentiable function that still vanishes at
(1^{N})
. Since
\prod_{j=1}^{k}\xi_{i_{j}}
is a linear combination of sequences of
k
operators as just described, we obtain the desired (
40
).

We now prove the polynomiality part. Consider any product of
k
operators, each of the form

\theta(i-1)
,
x_{i}\partial_{i}
or
\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})
, for some
i,j,\ell
. We want to apply them inductively to

\exp(\tilde{F}_{N})
using the following rules:

(41)

\begin{gathered}x_{i}\partial_{i}\left(H(\mathbf{x})\cdot\exp(\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x}))\right)=\left(x_{i}\big(\partial_{i}H(\mathbf{x})+H(\mathbf{x})\cdot\partial_{i}\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\big)\right)\cdot\exp(\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x})),\\
\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})\left(H(\mathbf{x})\cdot\exp(\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x}))\right)=\left(\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})H(\mathbf{x})\right)\cdot\exp(\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x})).\end{gathered}

Taking into account that
\tilde{F}_{N}(1^{N})=0
, the result of having
k
such operators act on
\exp(\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x}))

and then setting all variables equal to
1
is a finite linear combination of products of actions of
\theta(i-1)
,
x_{i}\partial_{i}
and
\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})
on
\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x})
, followed by evaluation at
\mathbf{x}=(1^{N})
.
Since
\tilde{F}_{N}(\mathbf{x})
is a polynomial of the shifted variables
x_{1}-1,\dots,x_{N}-1
with coefficients
c^{\lambda}_{F_{N}}
, and the actions of
x_{i}\partial_{i}
,
\frac{x_{\ell}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}(1-s_{i,j})
on shifted monomials are given by (
39
), we conclude the polynomiality. It remains to verify the statements about the degree and uniform boundedness.

The previous argument shows in fact that the rules (
39
) and (
41
) imply that the action of
\xi_{i_{q}}\cdots\xi_{i_{1}}
on
\exp(\tilde{F}_{N})
gives an expression of the form

\xi_{i_{q}}\cdots\xi_{i_{1}}\exp(\tilde{F}_{N})=\left(\sum_{r_{1},\dots,r_{N}\geq 0}{h^{(q)}_{r_{1},\dots,r_{N}}(x_{1}-1)^{r_{1}}\cdots(x_{N}-1)^{r_{N}}}\right)\cdot\exp(\tilde{F}_{N}),

for all
q=1,\dots,k
, and some coefficients
h^{(q)}_{r_{1},\dots,r_{N}}
. At the end, since
\tilde{F}_{N}(1^{N})=0
, we have

\xi_{i_{k}}\cdots\xi_{i_{1}}\exp(\tilde{F}_{N})\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=h^{(k)}_{0,\dots,0}.

We claim that each
h^{(q)}_{r_{1},\dots,r_{N}}
, with
1\leq q\leq k
and
r_{1},\dots,r_{N}\geq 0
, is a polynomial in the
c^{\lambda}_{F_{N}}
,
|\lambda|\leq k
, of degree at most
r_{1}+\dots+r_{N}+q
, and with coefficients that are uniformly bounded in the regime (
17
). In particular, this will be true of
h^{(k)}_{0,\dots,0}
and will end the proof.

The previous claim follows from the explicit expression for the Cherednik operators (
6
).
Indeed, the action of Cherednik operators on a homogeneous polynomial of degree
r
in the shifted variables
x_{1}-1,\dots,x_{N}-1
can be expressed as an inhomogeneous polynomial in these shifted variables, with nonzero components only in degrees
r-1
and
r
; this is a consequence of the calculations (

, therefore

\bigg|\prod_{i\geq 1}(\kappa_{1}+i)^{\text{\# horizontal steps at height $i$\,}}\bigg|=\prod_{i=1}^{2\ell}|\kappa_{1}+i|^{\text{\,\# horizontal steps at height $i$}}\\
\leq\prod_{i=1}^{2\ell}(|\kappa_{1}|+2\ell)^{\text{\# horizontal steps at height $i$}}\leq(2\ell C_{1})^{\text{\# horizontal steps at heights $\geq 1$}},

for some constant
C_{1}>0
. Similarly,

\bigg|\prod_{j\geq 1}(j+\gamma)^{\text{\# down steps from height $j$}}\bigg|=\prod_{j=1}^{2\ell}|j+\gamma|^{\text{\,\# down steps from height $j$}}\leq(2\ell C_{2})^{\text{\# down steps}},

for some constant
C_{2}>0
. Finally, by using the condition of the theorem, we obtain

\bigg|\prod_{j\geq 1}(\kappa_{j}+\kappa_{j+1})^{\text{\# steps $(1,j)$}}\bigg|\leq\prod_{j\geq 1}(|\kappa_{j}|+|\kappa_{j+1}|)^{\text{\# steps $(1,j)$}}\leq\prod_{j\geq 1}(C^{j}+C^{j+1})^{\text{\# steps $(1,j)$}}\\
\leq C_{3}^{\,\sum_{j\geq 1}j\,\cdot\,\text{\# steps $(1,j)$}}=C_{3}^{\,\text{\# down steps}},

for some constant
C_{3}>0
. To explain the last equality, observe that
(1,j)
is an up step of size
j
, so the sum
\sum_{j\geq 1}j\cdot\text{\# steps$(1,j)$}
is the total (upward) vertical displacement caused by the up steps in the Łukasiewicz path.
Since the path starts and ends at the same horizontal line
y=0
, then the total (downward) vertical displacement caused by down steps should equal this sum and, simultaneously, equal the number of down steps, since each down step goes down exactly by
1
; this justifies the last equality.

From the previous inequalities, the absolute value of the weight of
\Gamma
in (
22
) is upper bounded by
(2\ell A)^{\text{\# horizontal steps}\,+\,\text{\# down steps}}\leq(2\ell A)^{2\ell}
,
where
A:=\max\{C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}C_{3}\}>0
, for all large
\ell
.
It is known that the number of Łukasiewicz paths of length
\ell
is the
\ell
-th Catalan number:

|\mathbf{L}(\ell)|=\frac{1}{\ell+1}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\sim\frac{4^{\ell}}{\ell^{3/2}\sqrt{\pi}},\quad\text{ as }\ell\to\infty,

therefore

m_{2\ell}\leq|\mathbf{L}(2\ell)|\cdot(2\ell A)^{2\ell}\leq(8\ell A)^{2\ell},

for all large
\ell
.
Evidently,
m_{2\ell}\geq 0
because
m_{2\ell}
is an even moment, consequently,
m_{2\ell}^{-\frac{1}{2\ell}}\geq\frac{1}{8\ell A}
, for all large
\ell
.
Since
\sum_{\ell\geq 1}{\frac{1}{8\ell A}}
diverges, so does
\sum_{\ell\geq 1}m_{2\ell}^{-\frac{1}{2\ell}}
.
Thus, Carleman’s condition is satisfied and the proof is finished.
∎

5.1.1.
Jack-Littlewood–Richardson convolution

Given partitions
\lambda,\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}(N)
, we will consider the normalization of Jack Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, to be denoted by
c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}(\theta)
, uniquely determined by the equality

(82)

\frac{P_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)}{P_{\mu}(1^{N};\theta)}\frac{P_{\nu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)}{P_{\nu}(1^{N};\theta)}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N)}{c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}(\theta)\,\frac{P_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)}{P_{\lambda}(1^{N};\theta)}}.

From the definition, it is clear that
c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}(\theta)\neq 0
implies
|\lambda|=|\mu|+|\nu|
.
Also, for any fixed
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}(N)
, we have

(83)

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N)}{c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}(\theta)}=1,

as deduced by setting
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{N}=1
in (
82
).

Let
\mathbb{Y}(N)^{\leq L}
denote the finite subset of partitions in
\mathbb{Y}(N)
with longest part at most
L
. If
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}(N)^{\leq L}
, then
c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}(\theta)\neq 0
implies that
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N)^{\leq 2L}
.
This follows because the largest monomials (in the lexicographic order) in
P_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)
and
P_{\nu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N};\theta)
are multiples of
x_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\mu_{N}}
and
x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\nu_{N}}
, respectively.
Then the largest monomial in the LHS of (
82
) is a multiple of
x_{1}^{\mu_{1}+\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\mu_{N}+\nu_{N}}
.
Since
\mu_{1}+\nu_{1}\leq L+L=2L
, by assumption, it follows that any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N)
with nonzero
c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\,\nu}(\theta)
must have
\lambda_{1}\leq 2L
.

Definition 5.1
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}

\mathbb{Y}(N)
, define the signed measure

\big(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}\big)(\lambda)=\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Y}(N)}c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\,\nu}(\theta)\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}(\mu)\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}(\nu),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}(N).

The measure
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(1)}\boxplus_{\theta}\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(2)}
can be called the

of probability measures.

Due to (

has total mass equal to

.
Moreover, due to the previous discussion, if

,

are supported on

, then the support of

is contained in

is also finitely supported.
For

, the Jack-LR convolution was introduced and studied in
[
31
]
.

Remark 6
.

It is a conjecture of Stanley

]
that

, for all partitions

and any

.

7

6.
Comparison with previous related results

Here, we discuss the relations between our results and related ones from the literature.

Even though the main topic of this paper is the asymptotic behavior of measures in the high temperature regime
N\to\infty,N\theta\to\gamma
, one can prove the analogous results in the fixed temperature regime, i.e. when
N\to\infty
and
\theta
is fixed, by the same arguments.
As the proofs are the same, we will only sketch the arguments and leave the details to the interested reader.
In the case of a fixed
\theta>0
, the correct scaling of the empirical measure
\mu_{N}
of

\mathbb{P}_{N}
is as follows:

(115)

\tilde{\mu}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\frac{\mathscr{L}_{i}}{N\theta}},

where
\mathscr{L}_{i}:=\lambda_{i}-(i-1)\theta
, for
i=1,2,\dots,N
.

Definition 6.1
.

Given a sequence
\vec{\kappa}=(\kappa_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, define
\vec{m}=(m_{n})_{n\geq 1}
by

(116)

m_{\ell}:=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(\ell)}\frac{\kappa_{1}^{1+\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $0$ in $\Gamma$}}-(\kappa_{1}-1)^{1+\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $0$ in $\Gamma$}}}{1+\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $0$ in $\Gamma$}}\\
\cdot\kappa_{1}^{\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height larger than $0$ in $\Gamma$}}\cdot\prod_{j\geq 1}(\kappa_{j}+\kappa_{j+1})^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}},\quad\text{for all $\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}$}.

We denote the map
\vec{\kappa}\mapsto\vec{m}
by
\mathcal{J}_{\infty}^{\kappa\mapsto m}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
and the compositional inverse
\vec{m}\mapsto\vec{\kappa}
by
\mathcal{J}_{\infty}^{m\mapsto\kappa}:=(\mathcal{J}_{\infty}^{\kappa\mapsto m})^{(-1)}
.

Then we have the following result.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\theta>0
be fixed.
Let
\{\mathbb{P}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of probability measures with total mass
1
on

N
-signatures and with small tails, and let
\{\tilde{\mu}_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be their
empirical measures, given by (
115
).
Also, recall that
G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
denotes the Jack generating function of
\mathbb{P}_{N}
.
Suppose that the following two conditions are satisfied, for some constants
\kappa_{1}^{\infty},\kappa_{2}^{\infty},\cdots
:

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N\theta}\frac{\partial_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)!}\ln\big(G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}\big)\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=\kappa_{n}^{\infty}
exists and is finite, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N\theta}\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{r}}\ln\big(G_{\mathbb{P}_{N},\theta}\big)\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(1^{N})}=0
, for all

and
i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
with at least two distinct indices among
i_{1},\dots,i_{r}

Then
\tilde{\mu}_{N}
converges, as
N\to\infty
, in the sense of moments, in probability, to a probability measure
\mu_{\infty}
with finite moments
m_{n}^{\infty}
.
Moreover, the sequences
\vec{\kappa}=(\kappa^{\infty}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
\vec{m}=(m^{\infty}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are related to each other by
\vec{m}=\mathcal{J}_{\infty}^{\kappa\mapsto m}(\vec{\kappa})
.
Finally, if
\sup_{n\geq 1}\big|\kappa_{n}^{\infty}\big|^{1/n}<\infty

\mu_{\infty}
is uniquely determined by its moments.

This theorem can also be generalized to the case where
\mathbb{P}_{N}
are finite signed measures, as in
Theorem

3.6
.

The first few moments

\displaystyle m_{1}^{\infty}

The fixed temperature regime was studied previously by Bufetov–Gorin
[

]
for
\theta=1

for arbitrary

Bufetov–Gorin employed certain differential operators on the space of symmetric polynomials that were designed from the explicit determinantal formula for Schur polynomials.
As a result, there is no obvious extension of their ideas to our setting, since Jack polynomials do not have determinantal formulas; moreover, the Cherednik operators that we use are differential-reflection operators.
On the other hand, the Jack generating function used in the main part of Huang’s work is a formal power series of symmetric functions, rather than symmetric polynomials.
In
[

The assumptions in the main formulation of
[

are more complicated and technical than ours and seem more difficult to verify in practice; this is a consequence of the fact that the techniques in that paper are different and rely on the analysis of the Nazarov–Sklyanin operators
[
45
]
that do not directly reflect the behavior of the model.
11
11

11

Huang’s technical assumptions involve infinite sums of differential operators with respect to the power-sum symmetric functions. They were derived from the explicit form of Nazarov–Sklyanin operators, which are differential operators in these power-sum variables. As a sanity check, let us next deduce the formula obtained in

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\vec{\kappa}=(\kappa_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be arbitrary, let
R(z):=\sum_{n\geq 1}{\kappa_{n}z^{n-1}}
and
(m_{n})_{n\geq 1}:=\mathcal{J}_{\infty}^{\kappa\mapsto m}(\vec{\kappa})
.
Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we have

(117)

m_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}[z^{-1}]\frac{1}{1+z}\left(z^{-1}+(1+z)R(z)\right)^{n+1}.

Proof.

Let
F(z)=1+\sum_{i\geq 1}f_{i}z^{i}
be a formal power series. It is a classical result in enumerative combinatorics (see e.g.
[
56
, Sections 5.3–5.4]
) that the generating function for Łukasiewicz paths

L(t):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(n)}t^{n}\prod_{j\geq 0}(f_{j+1})^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}}

satisfies the following functional equation:

t\cdot L(t)=t\cdot F(t\cdot L(t)).

In particular, it follows from the Lagrange inversion formula (see
[
56
, Theorem 5.4.2]
) that

(118)

\frac{1}{n+1}[z^{-1}]\frac{1}{1+z}\left(z^{-1}\cdot F(z)\right)^{n+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i+1}\cdot\frac{i+1}{n+1}[z^{n+1-(i+1)}]\left(F(z)\right)^{n+1}\\
=[t^{n+1}]\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i+1}\left(t\cdot L(t)\right)^{i+1}.

Note that
\left(t\cdot L(t)\right)^{i+1}
can be interpreted as the generating series for the sequences of
i+1
Łukasiewicz paths, where each of them starts with a horizontal step at height
0
. By concatenating these paths and then removing the first horizontal step at height
0
, we obtain a Łukasiewicz path of length
n
(if looking at the coefficient of
t^{n+1}
) with
i
marked horizontal steps at height
0
. Therefore, we can rewrite the RHS of (
118
) as a sum over all Łukasiewicz paths of length
n
with some number of marked horizontal steps at height
0
(we can mark
i
steps in
\binom{h_{0}(\Gamma)}{i}
ways, where
h_{0}(\Gamma)
is the number of horizontal steps at height
0
in
\Gamma
), and then assigning the corresponding weight:

\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(n)}\sum_{i=0}^{h_{0}(\Gamma)}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i+1}\binom{h_{0}(\Gamma)}{i}f_{1}^{(\text{\#\,steps $(1,0)$ in $\Gamma$})\,-\,i}\prod_{j\geq 1}(f_{j+1})^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}}.

By choosing
F(z)=1+z(1+z)R(z)=1+\kappa_{1}z+\sum_{j\geq 1}{(\kappa_{j}+\kappa_{j+1})z^{j+1}}
, and applying the identity

\frac{1}{i+1}\binom{h_{0}(\Gamma)}{i}=\frac{1}{1+h_{0}(\Gamma)}\binom{1+h_{0}(\Gamma)}{i+1}
in the above expression, we can express the RHS of (
117
) as

\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(n)}\frac{1}{1+h_{0}(\Gamma)}\sum_{i=0}^{h_{0}(\Gamma)}(-1)^{i}\binom{1+h_{0}(\Gamma)}{i+1}\kappa_{1}^{\text{(\#\,steps $(1,0)$ in $\Gamma$})\,-\,i}\prod_{j\geq 1}(\kappa_{j}+\kappa_{j+1})^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}}.

This latter formula is clearly equal to the expression from
Definition

6.1
, due to the binomial formula, thus the proof is completed.

\widetilde{R}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{quant}(z)
adapted to our convention linearizes the
\gamma=\infty
quantized convolution. It turns out that the coefficients of that transform are not exactly
\kappa_{n}^{\infty}

Example

6

\widetilde{R}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{quant}(z)=\kappa_{1}^{\infty}+(\kappa_{2}^{\infty}+\kappa_{1}^{\infty})z+\left(\kappa_{3}^{\infty}+\frac{3}{2}\kappa_{2}^{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{1}^{\infty}\right)z^{2}+O(z^{3}),

so even the first few coefficients of the quantized

-transform adapted to our convention are already nontrivial linear combinations of
\kappa_{n}^{\infty}

We claim that the map
\mathcal{J}_{\infty}^{\kappa\mapsto m}
from
Definition

6.1
can be regarded as the limit of
\mathcal{J}_{\gamma}^{\kappa\mapsto m}
, as
\gamma\to\infty
.
Indeed, in the high temperature regime
N\to\infty
,
N\theta\to\gamma
, the moments
m^{N}_{\ell}
of the empirical measure
\mu_{N}
have limits
m_{\ell}
if and only if the moments
\tilde{m}^{N}_{\ell}
of the empirical measure
\tilde{\mu}_{N}
have limits
\tilde{m}_{\ell}
.
In that case, these limits are related to each other by
m_{\ell}=\gamma^{\ell}\cdot\tilde{m}_{\ell}
.
Similarly, the relation between the
\kappa_{\ell}
’s that appear in
Definition

3.1
and the
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}
’s from
Theorem

6.2
is given by
\kappa_{\ell}=\gamma\cdot\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}
.
Therefore, the formula (
22
), rewritten in terms of
\tilde{\kappa}_{\ell}
and
\tilde{m}_{\ell}
, reads

(120)

\tilde{m}_{\ell}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(\ell)}\frac{\tilde{\kappa}_{1}^{1+h_{0}(\Gamma)}-(\tilde{\kappa}_{1}-1)^{1+h_{0}(\Gamma)}}{1+h_{0}(\Gamma)}\cdot\prod_{i\geq 1}(\tilde{\kappa}_{1}+i/\gamma)^{\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $i$ in $\Gamma$}}\\
\cdot\prod_{j\geq 1}(\tilde{\kappa}_{j}+\tilde{\kappa}_{j+1})^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}}(j/\gamma+1)^{\text{\#\,down steps from height $j$ in $\Gamma$}},\quad\text{for all $\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}$},

where
h_{0}(\Gamma):=\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $0$ in $\Gamma$}
.
Evidently, the formula (
120
) in the limit
\gamma\to\infty
is exactly the formula (
116
) for the moments
m^{\infty}_{\ell}
, as claimed.
This limit can also be understood by introducing a certain gradation on the polynomial algebra
\mathbb{C}[\gamma,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots]
.
If we define

\deg_{1}(\gamma)=\deg_{1}(\kappa_{1})=\deg_{1}(\kappa_{2})=\dotsm=1,

then the combinatorial formula (
22
) for the moments
m_{\ell}
implies that
m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}[\gamma,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots]
has
\deg_{1}(m_{\ell})=\ell
, and
m_{\ell}^{\infty}
is equal to the top degree term of
m_{\ell}
followed by the substitutions
\gamma\mapsto 1
and
\kappa_{n}\mapsto\kappa^{\infty}_{n}
.

It turns out that there is another gradation
\deg_{2}
on the polynomial algebra
\mathbb{C}[\gamma,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots]
that allows us to deduce a recent result of
[
5
]
from our formula (
22
), namely:

\deg_{2}(\gamma)=0,\quad\deg_{2}(\kappa_{n})=n,\quad\forall\,n\geq 1.

In order to motivate this gradation, recall that in the proof of
Theorem

3.6
, the moments
m_{\ell}
were calculated as the limits

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\xi_{i}+\tilde{\xi}_{i}\big)^{\ell}\exp\left(\hat{F}_{N}\right)\bigg|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(0^{N})},

where
\xi_{i}
are the Cherednik operators,
\tilde{\xi}_{i}
are the Dunkl operators, and
\hat{F}_{N}=\hat{F}_{N}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
is a formal power series such that

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)!}\hat{F}_{N}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(0^{N})}=\kappa_{n}
exists and is finite, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{r}}\hat{F}_{N}\big|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(0^{N})}=0
, for all
r\geq 2
and
i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
with at least two distinct indices among
i_{1},\dots,i_{r}
;

see
Equation

51
.
The Cherednik operators preserve the degree of power series, while the Dunkl operators decrease the degree by
1
, therefore by introducing a formal variable
t
, the limit

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\xi_{i}+t\cdot\tilde{\xi}_{i}\big)^{\ell}\exp\left(\hat{F}_{N}\right)\bigg|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(0^{N})},

is equal to the following
t
-deformation of the formula for
m_{\ell}
:

\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(\ell)}\frac{\Delta_{\gamma}\left(x^{1+\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $0$ in $\Gamma$}}\right)(t\cdot\kappa_{1})}{1+\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $0$ in $\Gamma$}}\cdot\prod_{i\geq 1}(t\cdot\kappa_{1}+i)^{\text{\#\,horizontal steps at height $i$ in $\Gamma$}}\\
\cdot\prod_{j\geq 1}(t^{j}\kappa_{j}+t^{j+1}\kappa_{j+1})^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}}(j+\gamma)^{\text{\#\,down steps from height $j$ in $\Gamma$}}.

In this formula, the coefficient of
t^{d}
has degree
\deg_{2}
equal to the exponent
d
.
Define
m_{\ell}^{\topp}
as the top homogeneous degree part of the RHS of
Equation

22
with respect to the gradation given by
\deg_{2}
.
From the formula above, it follows that

(121)

m^{\topp}_{\ell}=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{L}(\ell)}\prod_{j\geq 0}\kappa_{j+1}^{\text{\#\,steps $(1,j)$ in $\Gamma$}}(j+\gamma)^{\text{\#\,down steps from height $j$ in $\Gamma$}}.

This formula corresponds exactly to the limit

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty\\
N\theta\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\tilde{\xi}_{i}\big)^{\ell}\exp\left(\hat{F}_{N}\right)\bigg|_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(0^{N})},

with the above assumptions on
\hat{F}_{N}
.
The calculation of such a limit was one of the main calculations in
[
5
]
, where the authors showed that it is the
\ell
-th moment of the limiting measure of a sequence of empirical measures of certain
continuous
\beta
-ensembles defined by the Bessel generating functions.
Their combinatorial formula was formulated in terms of set partitions, but is, in fact, equivalent to
Equation

121
; indeed,
[
64
, Proposition 5.11]
formulates it in terms of noncrossing set partitions and it can be translated to Łukasiewicz paths by using the well-known bijection in
[
47
, Proposition 9.8]
.
Our discrete model is a quantization of the continuous model from
[
5
]
(see the introduction of
[
12
]
), yet it is still quite interesting that we can recover their exact results as corollaries of our proofs.
