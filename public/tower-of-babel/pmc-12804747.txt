Periplaneta americana glycoprotein alleviates fatigue through modulation of oxidative stress and gut microbiota
Authors: Zhongze Chen, Xiao Yang, Jingyu Zhang, Kailing Li, Rongmao He, Yongshou Yang, Jingxuan Wan, Zhengchun He, Peiyun Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFatigue is an increasingly prominent global health issue. Periplaneta americana glycoprotein (PAG) possesses multiple pharmacological activities including antioxidation, regulation of gut microbiota (GM) and immunity, which are highly consistent with the targets of anti-fatigue treatment. This study aimed to investigate the efficacy and mechanisms of PAG in anti-fatigue. Initially, the antioxidant activity of PAG was evaluated in vitro. Subsequently, a fatigue mouse model was established to evaluate its anti-fatigue effects in vivo. Finally, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore correlations between gut microbiota shifts, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, and fatigue indicators. The results showed that PAG possesses significant antioxidant activity. Furthermore, PAG alleviated fatigue through multiple pathways: it prolonged swimming time, ameliorated liver injury, increased the activities of liver glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), reduced liver malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, decreased serum blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and muscle lactic acid (LA) accumulation, inhibited serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase (CK) activities, promoted glycogen storage in the liver and muscles of fatigued mice, altered the composition and structure of GM, and increased SCFA levels. In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that PAG alleviates fatigue by improving oxidative stress, regulating energy metabolism, and modulating the GM–SCFA axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31459-3.Keywords: Gut microbiota, Anti-fatigue, Periplaneta americana glycoprotein, AntioxidantSubject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Microbiology, Physiology

Fatigue refers to the inability of an organism to maintain its physiological functions at a certain level or to sustain appropriate exercise intensity, and is characterized by sustained lack of energy, decreased physical strength, and a sense of exhaustion throughout the body. It not only hinders daily activities but also significantly reduces overall health and quality of life. Prolonged fatigue without effective relief can lead to the development of a variety of serious diseases, including neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s disease, and metabolic disorders like obesity. In addition, fatigue is strongly associated with mental health problems, such as anxiety and depression1,2. Moreover, conditions such as diabetes, hypothyroidism, anemia, elevated body mass index, and liver disease can also contribute to fatigue2,3. However, the types of drugs currently used to relieve fatigue symptoms are limited and have obvious side effects4–6. With the development of society and fast-paced life, various enormous pressures have made fatigue-related syndromes a serious global health problem. Therefore, the exploration of novel bioactive substances with fatigue relieving properties has received extensive attention.

The causes of fatigue are diverse and complex, and its pathophysiology and etiology have not been fully elucidated. At present, a large number of researchers have confirmed that the mechanisms of relieving fatigue mainly include reducing oxidative stress damage7, regulating gut microbiota (GM)8, enhancing energy reserve9, and reducing metabolite levels10, etc. In particular, oxidative stress and imbalance of GM are regarded as factors closely related to fatigue. Sufficient evidence indicates that excessive exercise disrupts the balance of the body’s oxidation/antioxidant system. Therefore, scavenging free radicals such as 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), hydroxyl, and 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) is an effective approach to alleviate fatigue7. Studies have demonstrated that short-chain fatty acid (SCFA), the metabolites of GM, are energy sources for skeletal muscle and play a regulatory role in maintaining redox homeostasis in vivo. Modulating the balance of GM has been shown to extend exercise duration, enhance antioxidant enzyme activity, decrease metabolic byproduct accumulation, and promote glycogen storage in mice experiencing exercise-induced fatigue (EIF)11. Strains of genera such as Lactobacillus, Akkermansia, and Bifidobacterium not only maintain intestinal balance but also promote SCFA production, further exerting anti-fatigue effects12–14. The above results clearly indicate that improving oxidative stress and regulating GM balance are effective means to alleviate fatigue. Significantly, due to the good biological activity of macromolecular substances like glycoproteins, they have attracted the attention of many researchers. Glycoproteins are a class of bioactive macromolecules formed by connecting sugar chains with proteins or peptides, widely existing in nature, which possess various pharmacological activities including anti-fatigue, antioxidation, regulating GM, anti-aging, immune regulation, and antitumor15. According to reports, glycoprotein can play an anti-fatigue role by scavenging excessive free radicals, improving antioxidant enzyme activity, increasing glycogen reserves, and reducing metabolites accumulation16,17. Trichiurus lepturus glycoprotein has been confirmed to have obvious antioxidant activity in vitro and relieve fatigue in exercise mice17,18. The glycoprotein of breast milk and edible bird’s nest have various biological activities like antioxidant and regulating GM19,20. Therefore, glycoproteins have broad application prospects in the development of anti-fatigue functional products.

Periplaneta americana (PA), commonly known as “cockroach”, was first recorded in the ancient book “Divine Farmer’s Materia Medica"21. It is a famous traditional medicinal insect with a medicinal history of more than 2,000 years and has been widely raised and used commercially in China22. In ethnic minority areas of China, there has been a custom of eating insects since ancient times. PA is an insect with drug and food homology. In recent years, its medicinal and nutritional functions have attracted extensive attention and research. The adult powder of PA has been certified as a health supplement23. Modern studies have shown PA, which rich in bioactive substances such as amino acids, peptides, polysaccharides and nucleosides, exhibits diverse pharmacological activities, including antioxidation, GM regulation, wound healing, and immunomodulation24–26. Lu et al.. confirmed PA played a preventive role in streptozotocin-induced diabetes in mice by reducing inflammation and oxidative stress, improving pancreatic function, enhancing immunity, and regulating GM27. In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated the excellent biological activity of macromolecular substances. Researchers firmly believe macromolecular substances are key bioactive components of PA21. Based on this, we previously isolated and purified a glycoprotein (PAG) from PA, and characterized its structural composition, revealing that the total sugar and protein content of PAG are about 20.60% and 66.00%, respectively. PAG is mainly composed of 7 kinds of monosaccharides and 10 kinds of amino acids. The molecular weight of PAG was divided into four segments, with 66–14.5 kDa accounting for approximately 79.05%28. In previous research, we found that PAG can effectively eliminate DPPH, hydroxyl and ABTS free radicals to exert antioxidant activity29, and also promote the proliferation of Lactobacillus plantarum and Bifidobacterium adolescentis and the generation of SCFA30. Therefore, we hypothesize that PAG may alleviate fatigue by enhancing antioxidant capacity, modulating gut microbiota composition, and increasing SCFA production.

For this reason, we first investigated the antioxidant activity of PAG under simulated gastrointestinal digestion conditions. On this basis, we established a fatigue mouse model based on the swimming test and assessed fatigue-related indicators such as swimming time, antioxidant parameters, and glycogen content. Subsequently, the diversity of GM in intestinal contents and the levels of SCFAs in feces were analyzed. Finally, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the correlations among GM composition, SCFA levels, and fatigue-related indicators, thereby clarifying the role and potential mechanisms of PAG in preventing and alleviating fatigue. In summary, this study provides scientific evidence supporting PAG as a promising candidate for the development of anti-fatigue drugs or nutritional supplements.

Results

In vitro antioxidant activity of PAG

Physical fatigue often results from the excessive accumulation of free radicals31. Therefore, we evaluated the antioxidant activity of PAG in a simulated gastrointestinal environment using several markers, such as total reducing capacity and the scavenging activities of ABTS, DPPH, and hydroxyl radicals, providing foundational data supporting subsequent in vivo investigations on its antioxidant and anti-fatigue effects. As shown in Fig. 1, when assessed in the simulated gastric environment, no significant differences were observed in the total reducing capacity, DPPH, or hydroxyl free radical scavenging capacity between the pepsin and gastric acid groups and the gastric blank group (P > 0.05). With increasing PAG concentration, the ABTS free radical scavenging rate increased gradually. At the same PAG concentration, the ABTS free radical scavenging rate of the pepsin group was significantly greater than those of the gastric acid and gastric blank groups (P < 0.01).

After trypsin digestion, the total reducing capacity, ABTS and hydroxyl free radical clearance rates of PAG increased with increasing concentration, as shown in Fig. 2. The scavenging rate of the trypsin group was higher than that of the intestinal blank group, but there was no statistically significant difference in the rate of DPPH free radical clearance between the two groups (P > 0.05).

EIF effect of PAG

Effects of PAG on the body weight change and organ index

Body weight change and organ index reflect the effects of PAG and American ginseng capsules (AGC) administration on the health of mice. During the experimental period, the weight of mice in the 5 experimental groups continued to increase (Fig. 3A). However, there was no significant difference in the weight change of the Positive (5.58 ± 0.21 g), PAG-L (3.87 ± 0.29 g), PAG-M (3.72 ± 0.17 g), and PAG-H (3.67 ± 0.22 g) groups compared with the Control group (4.32 ± 0.31 g, P > 0.05), indicating that PAG and AGC did not affect growth and development of mice. Moreover, the observed weight gain corresponded to food intake. We noted that intervention with PAG and AGC significantly reduced the liver weight of mice with EIF (P < 0.01), suggesting that continuous high-intensity exercise may induce mild liver edema, resulting in an increased liver weight (Fig. 3B). PAG appeared to alleviate liver oxidative stress, exerting a protective effect. The indices of the other organs were not significantly different from those of the Control group (Fig. 3C-E, P > 0.05).

Effect of PAG on exhaustive swimming time

The exhaustive swimming time of mice is one of the primary indicators for evaluating anti-fatigue effects. The duration of weight-loaded swimming directly reflects the exercise tolerance of EIF mice. Generally, a longer swimming time indicates a greater efficacy of the sample in mitigating fatigue. The weight-loaded swimming model provides a reliable method to objectively assess both the fatigue tolerance of mice and the anti-fatigue effects of samples12. As the PAG dosage increased, the exhaustive swimming time of the mice was prolonged compared to the Control group (Fig. 3F). The PAG-M (43.20 ± 6.33 min) and PAG-H (42.94 ± 6.28 min) groups swam for approximately 27 min longer than the Control group (15.89 ± 4.98 min, P < 0.05), and their durations also exceeded that of the Positive group (31.50 ± 9.14 min). This study confirms that PAG prolongs exercise time, enhances fatigue tolerance, and exerts a significant anti-fatigue effect in mice.

Morphological analysis of liver tissue

According to the available literature32, EIF in mice is closely related to liver metabolism, as oxidative stress can lead to liver tissue damage during exercise. This is reflected in the microscopic observations of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained paraffin sections of liver tissue, presented in Fig. 3G. After 30 min of exhaustive swimming, hepatocytes in the Control group exhibited hepatocyte disarray, a cytoplasmic vacuolization, and irregularly arranged nuclei of varying sizes, as indicated by arrows. Conversely, in the PAG-treated groups, the intercellular spaces decreased with increasing PAG dosage, and the cellular structure tended to normalize. H&E staining confirmed that the hepatocytes of EIF mice were irregularly arranged, indicating oxidative stress-induced damage in the liver tissue. Following PAG administration, the arrangement of hepatocytes returned to normal, and oxidative stress injury was alleviated to a certain extent. These findings suggest that PAG mitigated oxidative stress damage to hepatocytes in mice with EIF and played a protective role in liver function.

Effect of PAG on liver superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and malondialdehyde (MDA)

Critical antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD and GSH-Px, play a pivotal role in the body’s defense against oxidative damage, while MDA is an important end product of lipid peroxidation triggered by excessive free radicals. Therefore, the anti-fatigue effect of PAG was evaluated by measuring the levels of SOD, GSH-Px, and MDA in the livers of mice.

As shown in Fig. 4A-C, compared with the Control group (SOD: 141.42 ± 31.33 U/mg prot; GSH-Px: 592.32 ± 115.29 U/mg prot; MDA: 2.43 ± 0.36 nmol/mg prot), the PAG-M (SOD: 252.26 ± 31.74; GSH-Px: 895.66 ± 117.78; MDA: 1.50 ± 0.23), PAG-H (SOD: 256.47 ± 50.45; GSH-Px: 817.31 ± 83.85; MDA: 1.45 ± 0.28), and Positive (SOD: 252.56 ± 59.75; GSH-Px: 918.15 ± 157.88; MDA: 1.57 ± 0.38) groups all showed significant increases in hepatic SOD and GSH-Px activities and a substantial decrease in MDA levels (P < 0.01). The PAG-L group (SOD: 161.24 ± 25.35; GSH-Px: 818.04 ± 82.26; MDA: 1.79 ± 0.33) also demonstrated a significant enhancement in GSH-Px activity and a reduction in MDA level compared to the Control; however, the increase in SOD activity was not statistically significant.

These results suggest that PAG reversed the decline in antioxidant enzyme activity and the elevation of MDA levels in the livers of exercise-fatigued mice, indicating favorable antioxidant activity in vivo. Moreover, the changes in hepatic SOD and GSH-Px activities and MDA content exhibited a dose-dependent trend with increasing PAG concentration. Specifically, among the three PAG dose groups, the PAG-H group demonstrated the most pronounced anti-fatigue effect.

Effect of PAG on serum blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and muscle lactic acid (LA)

During intense exercise, the body’s metabolism produces harmful metabolites, including BUN and LA. whose accumulation is negatively correlated with exercise endurance and are recognized contributors to fatigue. In the Control group, elevated levels of serum BUN (6.57 ± 0.47 mmol/L) and muscle LA (3.98 ± 0.97 mmol/L) were observed. In contrast, administration of PAG significantly reduced serum BUN (PAG-L: 5.79 ± 1.01 mmol/L; PAG-M: 4.11 ± 0.48 mmol/L; PAG-H: 3.95 ± 0.23 mmol/L) and muscle LA (PAG-L: 0.30 ± 0.06 mmol/L; PAG-M: 0.27 ± 0.03 mmol/L; PAG-H: 0.25 ± 0.05 mmol/L) (P < 0.05) levels. Moreover, the levels of both metabolites decreased with increasing PAG dosage (Fig. 4D, E). These results suggests that PAG effectively mitigated the accumulation of exercise-induced metabolites—serum BUN and muscle LA—reduced exercise-induced injury, and improved exercise tolerance in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, among the three PAG dose groups, the PAG-H group exhibited a the most potent anti-fatigue effect.

Effect of PAG on serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase (CK)

Serum levels of LDH and CK are established biomarkers of muscle fatigue; therefore, we measured their activities. As illustrated in Fig. 4F, G, the serum LDH and CK activities in the PAG and Positive (LDH: 2800.93 ± 345.14 U/L, CK: 0.67 ± 0.12 U/mL) groups were significantly lower than those in the Control group (LDH: 4097.22 ± 271 U/L.96, CK: 1.51 ± 0.30 U/mL) (P < 0.05). In the PAG-L (LDH: 3557.10 ± 563.45 U/L, CK: 1.32 ± 0.25 U/mL), PAG-M (LDH: 3067.90 ± 315.72 U/L, CK: 0.88 ± 0.20 U/mL), and PAG-H (LDH: 2708.33 ± 559.79 U/L, CK: 0.67 ± 0.17 U/mL) groups, the serum LDH activity were decreased by 13.18%, 25.10%, and 33.90%, and serum CK activity was reduced by 12.34%, 41.97%, and 55.61%, respectively. Our results indicate that PAG effectively inhibited the activities of LDH and CK in the serum of fatigued mice, thereby alleviating exercise-induced muscle injury.

Effect of PAG on muscle glycogen (MG) and liver glycogen (LG)

Glycogen storage, reflects the body’s fatigue tolerance, is a key biomarker for fatigue assessment. Accordingly, we measured glycogen content in muscle and liver tissues of mice. As shown in Fig. 4H, the MG content in the PAG-M (1.15 ± 0.09 mg/g), PAG-H (1.22 ± 0.10 mg/g), and Positive (1.16 ± 0.05 mg/g) groups was significantly higher than that in the Control group (0.92 ± 0.12 mg/g). However, no significant difference was observed in MG content between the PAG-L (0.96 ± 0.06 mg/g) and Control groups. Similarly, the LG content in all PAG groups (PAG-L: 7.74 ± 0.59 mg/g; PAG-M: 9.24 ± 0.35 mg/g; PAG-H: 9.65 ± 0.30 mg/g) and Positive (9.51 ± 0.61 mg/g) group was significantly higher than that in the Control group (6.18 ± 0.28 mg/g) (Fig. 4I, P < 0.01). Both MG and LG levels increased in a dose-dependent manner with PAG administration. Notably, the PAG-H group exhibited the most pronounced anti-fatigue effect among the three doses. These results demonstrate that PAG increased glycogen reserves in mice, providing an energy source to offset exercise-induced consumption, and thereby improving exercise tolerance.

Effects of PAG on GM

Alpha diversity analysis of GM

To investigate the effect of PAG on the GM of fatigued mice, we performed 16 S rRNA sequencing of colonic contents. Alpha diversity was assessed to evaluate within-sample microbial community diversity (Fig. 5A-D). The Ace and Chao1 indices reflect species richness, with higher values indicating greater richness. The Shannon and Simpson indices reflect species diversity; the Shannon index is positively correlated with diversity, whereas the Simpson index is negatively correlated14. Compared to the Control group (Ace: 833.40 ± 167.07; Chao1: 797.40 ± 154.58; Shannon: 3.73 ± 0.31; Simpson: 0.08 ± 0.03), the Positive (Ace: 903.80 ± 184.27; Chao1: 867.60 ± 173.23; Shannon: 3.87 ± 0.49; Simpson: 0.06 ± 0.03) and PAG-L (Ace: 865.00 ± 119.25; Chao1: 827.00 ± 108.55; Shannon: 3.84 ± 0.21; Simpson: 0.06 ± 0.01) groups exhibited increases in the Ace, Chao1, and Shannon indices and a decrease in the Simpson index. Conversely, the PAG-M (Ace: 802.80 ± 158.42; Chao1: 766.80 ± 152.75; Shannon: 3.61 ± 0.52; Simpson: 0.08 ± 0.02) and PAG-H (Ace: 745.00 ± 63.30; Chao1: 714.20 ± 60.77; Shannon: 3.60 ± 0.31; Simpson: 0.09 ± 0.02) groups showed opposite trends. However, no significant differences (P > 0.05) existed between the groups. Although PAG did not significantly affect overall microbial richness (Ace, Chao1) or diversity (Shannon, Simpson). More importantly, distinct compositional alterations in the gut microbiota were induced by PAG, as revealed by beta diversity analysis.

Beta diversity analysis of GM

Beta diversity analysis evaluates the compositional differences in microbial communities between samples. To visualize these inter-group differences, we performed principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). In PCoA plot, the distance between samples reflects the similarity of microbial community composition and abundance. Specifically, the smaller the distance between samples, the more similar the microbial community composition and species abundance14. As shown in Fig. 5E, the microbial communities of the Positive, PAG-L, and PAG-M groups showed varying degrees of separation from the Control group, suggesting that these treatments induced shifts in GM composition. Notably, the PAG-H group formed a distinct cluster that was clearly separated from the Control group, indicating that a high dose of PAG effectively modulated the gut microbiota structure in fatigued mice, resulting in a unique microbial community.

Impact of PAG on the phylum-level structure and composition of the GM

To investigate the impact of PAG on the GM of exercise-fatigued mice, we compared the microbial community structures at the phylum level across different groups (Fig. 5F). In the Control group, the GM was predominantly composed of Firmicutes (82.79%). In addition, there were six phyla with relative abundances above 0.02%, namely Desulfobacterota (6.74%), Patescibacteria (4.95%), Actinobacteria (4.21%), Bacteroidetes (0.79%), Verrucomicrobiota (0.02%), and Bacteria_unclassified (0.24%). The effect of PAG on the GM at the phylum level was mainly concentrated in the Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, Desulfobacterota, and Patescibacteria. Firmicutes dominated the GM of the PAG-treated mice, but there were differences in the relative abundance between the groups. Compared with the Control group, the abundance of Firmicutes was upregulated by 6.49%, 4.31%, and 4.15% in the PAG-L, PAG-M, and PAG-H groups, respectively. Concurrently, the abundance of Desulfobacterota and Patescibacteria in the PAG group decreased to varying degrees compared to that in the Control group, whereas Verrucomicrobiota and Actinobacteria increased to varying degrees.

Impact of PAG on the genus-level structure and composition of the GM

Moving from the phylum level to the genus level, we examined the community structure of the gut bacteria in mice. The distribution of genera is shown in Fig. 5G. The dominant bacteria in the mice intestines were Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Lactobacillaceae_unclassified, Limosilactobacillus, Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, and Desulfovibrio. Compared to the Control group, the bacterial genera that showed increases in relative abundance in the intestinal contents after the different PAG concentrations were administered included Ligilactobacillus, Lactobacillaceae_unclassified and Akkermansia. Meanwhile, the abundance of Limosilactobacillus, and Desulfovibrio, decreased. In addition, the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium and Faecalibaculum increased in the PAG-H group.

Impact of PAG on GM structure

To further identify GM biomarkers across the different mouse groups, we performed linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis (LDA score > 2)33. Figure 6A shows an evolutionary branching diagram of LEfSe analysis, illustrating the taxonomic relationships from phylum to genus among the major sample communities. Firmicutes and Desulfobacterota were the predominant GM in the Control group. The dominant microbial communities in PAG-L and PAG-H consisted mainly of Dubosiella and Bifidobacterium, respectively. Figure 6B displays the LDA score distribution histogram derived from the LEfSe analysis. This histogram shows the LDA threshold of each marker species, with higher values indicating more obvious differences in the species. In the Control group, four phyla and 21 genera were identified. Taxa with LDA scores greater than 4 included Limosilactobacillus, Desulfobacterota, Desulfovibrionales, Desulfovibrionaceae, Desulfovibrionia, Desulfovibrio, Lachnospiraceae_UCG-006, and Turicibacter, which were mainly distributed within the phyla Firmicutes and Desulfobacterota. Among these taxa, Desulfovibrio showed a significantly higher abundance compared with the other groups. In the Positive group, 12 genera across three phyla (Firmicutes, Desulfobacterota, and Bacteroidetes) were identified, which differed significantly from those in the other groups. The abundance of bacteria in the PAG-H group was significantly higher than those in the other groups, with 13 genera across three phyla (Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteria_unclassified), including five unclassified genera. In contrast, the PAG-M and PAG-L groups had only one identified for genera, all of which belonged to the Firmicutes, a prominent phylum of GM that plays a crucial role in maintaining GM balance. Collectively, the LEfSe results indicate that PAG markedly modulated the gut microbiota composition in mice with exercise-induced fatigue.

Effect of PAG on fecal SCFA content

Dysbiosis of GM profoundly affects the production of its metabolites, especially SCFAs, which play a crucial role in alleviating exercise-induced fatigue. The effects of PAG on the SCFAs content in feces of mice are shown in Fig. 6C-F. When EIF mice were administered PAG, their feces had significantly higher levels of acetic acid (Control: 5.06 ± 1.10 mmol/g, Positive: 7.96 ± 1.31 mmol/g, PAG-L: 8.52 ± 1.35 mmol/g, PAG-M: 9.56 ± 1.34 mmol/g, PAG-H: 10.90 ± 0.84 mmol/g), propionic acid (Control: 2.77 ± 0.50 mmol/g, Positive: 2.60 ± 0.54 mmol/g, PAG-L: 4.08 ± 0.77 mmol/g, PAG-M: 4.12 ± 0.38 mmol/g, PAG-H: 5.63 ± 0.59 mmol/g), butyric acid (Control: 2.44 ± 0.43 mmol/g, Positive: 2.86 ± 0.37 mmol/g, PAG-L: 3.87 ± 0.37 mmol/g, PAG-M: 3.94 ± 0.39 mmol/g, PAG-H: 5.43 ± 1.08 mmol/g), and valeric acid (Control: 0.43 ± 0.08 mmol/g, Positive: 0.39 ± 0.06 mmol/g, PAG-L: 0.54 ± 0.13 mmol/g, PAG-M: 0.57 ± 0.13 mmol/g, PAG-H: 0.66 ± 0.10 mmol/g) than the Control group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the amount of fecal SCFA increased as the PAG concentration rose. This indicated that PAG enhanced the production of SCFA by the GM, thereby promoting intestinal health in mice and thereby contributing to the alleviation of exercise-induced fatigue.

Spearman correlation analysis of GM, SCFAs, and EIF evaluation indices

To elucidate the “GM–SCFA–anti-fatigue indices” relationship, and clarify the potential pathways through which PAG alleviates fatigue. We conducted Spearman correlation analysis on GM, fecal SCFAs, and fatigue-resistance-related indices, including serum LDH, serum CK, serum BUN, muscle LA, muscle MG, liver LG, liver MDA, liver GSH-Px, liver SOD, and swimming duration, As shown in Fig. 7A, the increase in fecal SCFA levels after PAG administration was positively correlated with muscle MG, liver LG, liver SOD content, and swimming duration to varying degrees, while exhibiting a negative correlation with serum LDH, serum BUN, and liver MDA content. Furthermore, butyric acid showed a negative correlation with serum CK and muscle LA levels and a positive correlation with liver GSH-Px content. These correlations collectively highlight the close association between SCFA production and fatigue-related physiological responses.

A Correlation analysis between fecal SCFAs and fatigue-related parameters; B, C Spearman correlation analysis between fatigue-related indices, SCFAs, and changes in GM at the phylum level; D, E Spearman correlation analysis between fatigue-related indices, SCFAs, and changes in GM at the genus level.

Figure 7B, C depict the phylum-level analyses. Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota displayed positive correlations with muscle MG, liver LG, liver GSH-Px, liver SOD, fecal SCFAs, and swimming duration to varying degrees. Actinobacteria were positively correlated to varying degrees with muscle MG, liver LG, liver GSH-Px, and liver SOD content, but negatively correlated with serum LDH, serum CK, serum BUN, muscle LA, liver MDA, and fecal SCFAs. Desulfobacterota and Patescibacteria were positively associated with serum LDH, serum CK, serum BUN, muscle LA, and liver MDA content and negatively associated with muscle MG, liver LG, liver GSH-Px, and liver SOD levels and swimming duration. Based on these findings, we speculated that PAG enhanced the production of fecal SCFAs by increasing the abundance of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota in the intestinal microbiota. PAG also increased Actinobacteriota while reducing Desulfobacterota and Patescibacteria, thereby reducing serum LDH leakage, limiting serum BUN, muscle LA, and liver MDA accumulation, and promoting glycogen storage and antioxidant enzyme activity. Collectively, these effects contributed to the increased fatigue tolerance observed in mice.

Analyses at the genus level are shown in Fig. 7D, E. The genera that were positively correlated with muscle MG and liver LG levels, liver GSH-Px and SOD activity, and swimming duration to varying degrees included Ligilactobacillus, Lactobacillaceae_unclassified, Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, Faecalibaculum, and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, which were also negatively correlated with serum LDH, serum CK, serum BUN, muscle LA, and liver MDA levels. Conversely, Lactobacillus, Limosilactobacillus, Desulfovibrio, Candidatus_Saccharimonas, Lachnospiraceae_UCG-006, Lachnospiraceae_unclassified, Turicibacter, and Oscillospiraceae_unclassified were positively correlated with serum LDH, serum CK, serum BUN, muscle LA, and liver MDA to varying degrees but negatively correlated with other indices. Moreover, Lactobacillus, Lactobacillaceae_unclassified, Akkermansia, and Dubosiella were positively correlated with fecal SCFA levels to varying degrees. In summary, we inferred that PAG plays an anti-fatigue role by regulating the richness of GM to promote the production of fecal SCFAs, alleviating oxidative stress damage, reducing metabolite accumulation, and increasing energy storage.

Discussion

Fatigue is a complex physiological phenomenon involving a variety of mechanisms, including oxidative stress damage, energy metabolism disorders, and GM disorders. Although numerous studies have demonstrated the diverse pharmacological activities of PA, no research has reported its anti-fatigue effects or potential underlying mechanisms. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the anti-fatigue effects and potential mechanisms of PAG. Particular attention was given to oxidative stress and GM regulation in EIF.

The molecular structure and amino acid residues are key determinants of the antioxidant activity of glycoproteins32. O-glycopeptide bonds tend to be stable under acidic conditions but are prone to dissociation in alkaline environments21, and the exposure of antioxidant amino acid residues can enhance ABTS free radical neutralization34. Our simulated gastrointestinal digestion experiments corroborate these principles. Specifically, in simulated gastric juice (pH 2), PAG is likely to maintain its native structure, resulting in no significant changes in its total reducing capacity, DPPH and hydroxyl free radical scavenging capacity, while exhibiting an increased ABTS free radical scavenging capacity. In simulated intestinal fluid, PAG is hydrolyzed by trypsin into hydrophilic small peptides and amino acids, which significantly enhances its scavenging activity toward hydrophilic free radicals such as Fe²⁺, ABTS, and hydroxyl free radicals35. In contrast, due to the lipophilic nature of DPPH, its interaction with these hydrophilic hydrolysis products is limited, and the DPPH scavenging capacity remains largely unchanged. Overall, the in vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion experiments demonstrate that PAG possesses strong and stable antioxidant activity, providing experimental support for its potential in vivo antioxidant and anti-fatigue effects.

Intense exercise significantly increases reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby disturbing the balance between oxidative stress and antioxidant defense. Antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and GSH-Px play a crucial role in mitigating ROS-induced damage by converting ROS into less harmful molecules and preventing lipid peroxidation31. In this study, PAG significantly increased the activities of SOD and GSH-Px while reducing the level of MDA in the liver. These results suggest that PAG enhances the endogenous antioxidant capacity, which may be one of the mechanisms underlying its anti-fatigue effect. These findings are consistent with our previous results29.

Under anaerobic exercise, BUN and LA accumulate as by-products of protein and glucose metabolism and are widely used as fatigue-related indicators2,12. Elevated serum BUN and LA are associated with reduced exercise tolerance36–38, while LDH promotes the conversion of LA to pyruvate39, and CK reflects muscle damage during intense exercise40. PAG dose-dependently reduced the activities of LDH and CK, as well as the levels of BUN and LA, indicating that PAG alleviated the metabolic burden and muscle damage caused by exercise38. Moreover, our results revealed that PAG significantly increased the contents of LG and MG, suggesting that enhancing energy storage might be another mechanism for its fatigue relief effect.

Emerging evidence highlights the crucial role of the GM in maintaining physiological homeostasis and regulating fatigue2,11,14. This study confirmed through β-diversity analysis that PAG altered the diversity and relative abundance of key microbial groups in fatigue mice and significantly changed the structure of the GM. These results support that the anti-fatigue effect of PAG is related to the regulation of intestinal flora.

At the phylum level, the supplementation of PAG mainly regulated the Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, Verrucomicrobiota, Desulfobacterota, and Patescibacteria. The Firmicutes and Actinobacteriota phyla include many bacteria that produce SCFAs and are metabolically active, which contribute to intestinal homeostasis and energy regulation41–46. The changes in the relative abundance of these GM were observed at the phylum level, indicating that PAG plays a role in improving EIF by reshaping the relative abundance of GM in EIF mice.

At the genus level, PAG has influenced dominant groups such as Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, Faecalibaculum and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group. These genera are widely recognized for their roles in strengthening the intestinal barrier44,46,47, maintaining intestinal homeostasis48, regulating inflammation46,49, reducing oxidative stress50, promoting the production of SCFAs51, and regulating metabolism52–54, among other functions. The enrichment of these beneficial bacteria, accompanied by a reduction in the harmful sulfide-producing Desulfovibrio55, indicates that PAG regulates the GM profile that is conducive to improving metabolic balance and reducing the burden of inflammation, both of which are associated with fatigue recovery.

LEfSe analysis further supported these findings, showing increased abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Dubosiella, concomitantly decreasing the abundance of Desulfovibrio. Previous studies have reported that Dubosiella is associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity and reduced oxidative stress56. Overall, these results indicate that PAG regulates the GM composition and abundance by enriching beneficial bacteria while inhibiting pro-inflammatory or harmful taxa, thereby contributing to anti-fatigue effects.

SCFAs can promote intestinal epithelial proliferation, maintain barrier function and reduce the accumulation of LA in the blood and muscle, thereby alleviating EIF57,58. Acetic acid is an important energy source for the GM59. Propionic acid has the functions of immune regulation and reversing muscle damage caused by fatigue60. Butyric acid is the main energy substrate for colonic epithelial cells, which can reverse intestinal damage and mucosal dysfunction caused by EIF and promote apoptosis of damaged cells61–63. The Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) results showed that PAG could significantly increase the level of SCFAs in mouse feces in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting that PAG might exert an anti-fatigue effect by promoting the generation of SCFAs.

Spearman analysis revealed that, within a certain range of PAG concentrations, the increase in SCFAs was positively correlated with the elevation of MG, LG, SOD, and the extension of swimming time, while negatively correlated with fatigue indicators such as LDH, BUN, and MDA. Furthermore, butyric acid enhanced GSH-Px activity and reduced CK and LA levels. Considering this, we speculated that PAG may enhance SCFA production in the intestine, thereby providing energy substrates for colonocytes, and the increased SCFAs may subsequently contribute to systemic resistance against oxidative stress, which may be one of the pathways for PAG’s anti-fatigue effects. Spearman analysis also found that Limosilactobacillus, Turicibacter and other bacterial genera were positively correlated with the expression of key metabolite enzymes (LDH, CK) and metabolic wastes (BUN, LA, and MDA). The abundance of these bacteria was higher in the Control group than in the PAG group, suggesting that they may be associated with accelerated fatigue. PAG may reduce the accumulation of metabolic by-products by decreasing their abundance. Conversely, beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Faecalibaculum significantly increased in the PAG group and were positively correlated with MG, LG, GSH-Px, SOD, and swimming time, indicating that PAG can enhance the metabolic activity of beneficial bacteria and improve fatigue tolerance. Spearman correlation analysis at the genus level further suggested that PAG could regulate the gut microbiota, and the effects of different bacterial taxa on SCFAs and fatigue-related indices varied.

Based on the comprehensive simulation of gastrointestinal digestion and the results of animal experiments, PAG maintains a stable antioxidant capacity during digestion, can significantly prolong the exhaustion swimming time, improve liver injury, increase the activities of SOD and GSH-Px in the liver, reduce the levels of MDA in the liver, LA in muscle, BUN, LDH, and CK in serum, and increase the storage of LG in the liver and MG in muscle. It also optimizes the microbiota composition and promotes the production of SCFAs. Correlation analysis also supports the interrelationship between GM, SCFAs, and fatigue indicators. In future studies, we will employ approaches such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to further verify the causal relationship between GM and the anti-fatigue effects of PAG.

In summary, this study elucidates the potential of PAG to exert anti-fatigue effects by reducing oxidative stress damage, decreasing metabolite accumulation, increasing glycogen storage, and regulating GM. This study provides a scientific basis for PAG as an anti-fatigue drug/nutritional supplement and for its development and utilization. Future studies are needed to further clarify its exact target of action and validate its clinical impact on EIF and the effective dose range for the human body, thereby facilitating translational application.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

PA was purchased from the GAP breeding base of Yunnan Jingxin Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Yunnan, China). PAG was isolated from PA via water extraction and alcohol precipitation. AGC were obtained from Xiamen Yongfuxing Health Products Co., Ltd. (Xiamen, China). Pepsin (porcine, activity: 1:10000), trypsin (from the porcine pancreas, activity: 1:250), and ABTS were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). DPPH was obtained from Tokyo Kasei Kogyo Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Appropriate kits for the determination of LG, MG, BUN, LA, LDH, CK, SOD, GSH-Px, MDA, and total protein were purchased from the Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Institute (Nanjing, China). Acetic, propionic, butyric, and valeric acid standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

PAG extraction from PA

The method for the extraction and purification of PAG was based on previously reported methods, with slight modifications28,64. Specifically, the dried PA was ground (24-mesh sieve) and subjected to three reflux extractions with ultrapure water (1:5, g/v). The resulting filtrate was concentrated to one-quarter of the original volume, defatted three times with petroleum ether (1:3, w/v) at 30 °C, and precipitated with 95% ethanol (1:5, v/v) at 4 °C for 12 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 min) and dissolved in an appropriate volume of water. Then, free proteins were removed using three volumes of Sevag reagent (chloroform/n-butanol = 4:1, v/v), and the supernatant was concentrated and subsequently freeze-dried to yield PAG.

Formulation of PAG solution for simulating gastrointestinal digestion

Simulated gastrointestinal fluid was prepared according to a previously described method65, with slight modifications. The simulated gastric fluid was obtained by adding 4.2 mL of hydrochloric acid and 2.5 g of pepsin to deionized water, and the final volume was adjusted to 250 mL with deionized water. The simulated intestinal fluid was prepared by mixing potassium dihydrogen phosphate (1.7 g), sodium hydroxide (adjusted to pH 6.8), deionized water (500 mL), and trypsin (10 g) and diluting it to 1000 mL with water.

Simulated gastrointestinal digestion was performed as described by Sun et al.66, with slight modifications. Briefly, PAG was dissolved in 0.9% saline to prepare solutions of various concentrations for antioxidant assays. The PAG concentrations used to determine the total reducing capacities were 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, and 1.6 mg/mL. The PAG concentrations used for the DPPH and hydroxyl free radicals scavenging experiments were 0.025, 0.050, 0.100, 0.200, and 0.400 mg/mL. The concentration of PAG for the ABTS free radical scavenging experiment were as follows: 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.08, and 0.16 mg/mL. To obtain the pepsin, gastric acid, and gastric blank groups, 15 mL of simulated gastric juice, 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid, and 0.9% saline, respectively, was added to different concentrations (injection nitrogen) of the sample solutions. After digestion for 0 and 2 h, the samples were removed and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was removed to obtain the simulated gastric digestion sample solution and stored at −80 °C. The 2 h pepsin group, which had varying concentrations of sample solutions and injected nitrogen, was mixed with simulated intestinal solution and phosphate buffer solution (pH ≈ 6.8) to generate sample solutions for the intestinal digestion and blank intestinal groups. After digestion for 0, 3, or 6 h, the sample solutions were removed, and the remaining steps were the same as those used for the simulated gastric digestion sample solution.

Assessment of PAG antioxidant capacity in vitro

Different concentration ranges were applied in the antioxidant assays to match the sensitivity and linear detection range of each method. The ABTS assay exhibits high analytical sensitivity, requiring only low sample concentrations (0.01–0.16 mg/mL) to produce measurable absorbance changes. In contrast, the total reducing capacity assay has lower sensitivity, requiring higher sample concentrations (0.1–1.6 mg/mL) to achieve detectable and linear color development. For the DPPH and hydroxyl free radical scavenging assays, intermediate concentration ranges were selected to ensure that the measured values fell within the corresponding linear response intervals. These ranges were selected based on preliminary experiments and previously published methods67–69.

Total reducing capacity assay

The total reducing capacity of PAG was measured according to the method described by Vasylie et al.67, with slight modifications. A mixture was prepared in a centrifuge tube by combining phosphate buffer (0.025 mol/L, 1 mL), simulated gastric digestion samples (at different concentrations, 1 mL) taken at 0 and 2 h, and potassium ferricyanide (K₃Fe(CN)₆, 1% w/v, 1 mL). Animals

In this experiment, eighty healthy male Kunming species mouse (SPF grade, 6–8 weeks old, weighing 36 ± 3 g) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Hunan, China; No. SCXK (Xiang) 2019–0004). Mice were kept at the Laboratory Animal Center of Dali University (Use License NO.: SYXK(Dian)2024-0001) at a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C, relative humidity of 55 ± 10%, alternating light and dark for 12 h, and free access to feed and water. The mice were kept in this environment to adapt for one week. All experimental procedure strictly according to ARRIVE guide (https://arriveguidelines.org) and Dali university experimental animal ethics committee guidelines and related regulations, and by Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Dali University (No. 2021-PZ-071; Approval date: 28 June, 2021).

Experimental design

AGC was recognized as an anti-fatigue compound70; therefore, it was used as a positive control drug in this study, and its dosage was determined by preliminary experiments and dose conversion between mice and humans71. To determine the optimal dose of PAG, we referred to the effective dose of the positive control and the results of acute toxicity testing (showing no observable toxicity at 40 g/kg). Accordingly, three experimental doses (200, 400, and 600 mg/kg/d) were designed to systematically evaluate the anti-fatigue efficacy and dose–response relationship of PAG. Furthermore, based on body surface area normalization (Km method), the mouse doses of 200, 400, and 600 mg/kg/d correspond to approximate human equivalent doses (HEDs) of 16.76, 33.52, and 50.28 mg/kg/d71. These levels are within the range commonly used for functional food ingredients and active extracts, suggesting pharmacological relevance and potential feasibility for future human applications. Nevertheless, further studies are warranted to assess the safety, bioavailability, and effective human dosage of PAG in clinical settings.

After one week of adaptation, based on a review of an extensive body of anti-fatigue-related literature60,61,72 and rigorous calculations using PASS software and simple methods, eighty mice were randomly divided into five groups according to their body weight. With sixteen mice in each group, the sample size was sufficient to meet the statistical requirements for both the weight-loaded swimming experiment (n = 8) and the unloaded swimming experiment (n = 8). This design, which minimizes the sample size of experimental animals, is in line with the ethical standards for animal experimentation. The dose settings and assignments were as follows: the control group (saline), the Positive group (AGC, 400 mg/kg/d), the PAG-L group (200 mg/kg/d), the PAG-M group (400 mg/kg/d), and the PAG-H group (600 mg/kg/d). The drug was dissolved in physiological saline and administered at a volume of 10 mL/kg body weight via oral gavage. In the control group, an equal volume of normal saline was administered by gavage according to the body weight to match that of the experimental group. All treatments were administered once daily for 4 consecutive weeks, and body weights were recorded every 2 days for intergroup comparisons. During the study period, the mice were acclimated to adaptive free swimming for 15–30 min using an experimental water tank (25 ± 2 °C, water depth 30 cm), twice a week.

Determination of exhaustive swimming time

At the end of the fourth week, a weight-loaded swimming test was performed on 8 mice from each group to measure exhaustive swimming time. To standardize the metabolic state of the mice prior to behavioral and biochemical assessments, food was removed for 12 h after the final gavage, while water was provided ad libitum. This fasting period helped minimize the influence of recent food intake on fatigue-related indicators, including glycogen levels, antioxidant enzyme activity, and SCFAs content. After cleansing the tail to ensure proper adhesion, a lead block weighing 5% of the mouse’s body weight was attached to the tail base. Subsequently, they were placed individually in a water tank, and timing began. The timing concluded when the mice sank to the bottom of the water due to exhaustion and remained submerged for 7 s, which was defined as the point of exhaustion. After the experiment, the mice were euthanized via inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane anesthesia.

Determination of fatigue-related biochemical indices

By the conclusion of the fourth week, the remaining eight mice in each group were subjected to a 12 h fasting period but allowed to drink water. Following a 30 min gap post-gavage, the mice engaged in free swimming without any additional load for 30 min. After a 30-min rest period, the mice were anesthetized, and blood samples were collected via retro-orbital bleeding. The blood samples were clotted for 2 h at room temperature and then centrifuged for 15 min (4000 rpm, 4 °C) to obtain serum. After blood collection, the mice were euthanized with an anesthetic overdose and immediately dissected. The liver, kidney, spleen, thymus, and leg muscle were excised and weighed. The organ index was calculated according to the following formula: Organ index (%) = organ mass (g)/mouse body weight (g) × 100%.

The liver levels of SOD, GSH-Px, and MDA were examined to gauge the PAG antioxidant potential. The levels of BUN, LDH, and CK in the serum, as well as LA in the muscle were determined to evaluate the protective effects of PAG on muscle and exercise metabolites in mice. The expression of LG in the liver and MG in the muscle was detected to evaluate the effect of PAG on body energy in mice. All biochemical assays were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for the respective commercial kits.

Morphological observation of liver tissue

Liver tissue specimens were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 18–24 h, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, sectioning, and H&E staining. The stained sections were examined under a light microscope (BX-53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera to observe pathological alterations in hepatocytes.

16 S rRNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from intestinal content samples using a genomic DNA extraction kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Its concentration and purity were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The hypervariable V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was targeted for amplification. Barcoded primers were synthesized for this region to facilitate multiplexed sequencing. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed to amplify the target region from all samples. Using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Recovery Kit (AXYGEN, San Francisco, CA, USA), the PCR products were recovered, after being detected using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The recovered products were quantified using a QuantiFluor™-ST Blue Fluorescence Quantification System (Promega, Madison, USA). To construct sequencing libraries and obtain sequence products, we utilized the Illumina PE250 platform. The high-throughput sequencing of samples was conducted at the Shanghai Yuanxin Biomedical Technology Co. (Shanghai, China).

Paired-end reads generated on the Illumina PE250 platform were merged based on their overlap, followed by quality control and filtering to produce high-quality clean sequences. These sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold using Usearch (v7.0.1090). The Ribosomal Database Project Naive Bayes classifier (v2.2, http://sourceforge.net/projects/rdp-classifier/) was used to perform taxonomic analysis on representative OTU sequences, and the community composition of the samples was separately calculated at different taxonomic levels (phylum and genus). Alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted based on the OTU clustering results to assess within-sample and between-sample microbial diversity, respectively. LEfSe was typically utilized to analyze the variations in microbiota between groups and identify the major microbiota among groups. The relative microbiota abundance of corresponding groups (LDA > 2) was found to be higher than that of other groups, as indicated by an LDA score greater than 2. We employed Spearman’s correlation analysis to examine the relationships between SCFAs, GM, and EIF indices. This analysis helped us understand how these factors related to one another and their connection to the anti-fatigue properties of PAG.

Determination of fecal SCFAs content

Determination of the SCFAs content was based on the method described by Yang et al.73, with some modifications. The mouse fecal samples (0.15 g) were precisely weighed, added to methanol (1 mL), vortexed for 3 min, ultrasonicated for 2 min, and thoroughly mixed to prepare a fecal suspension. Following a pH adjustment (pH = 2.0–3.0) with concentrated sulfuric acid, the suspension underwent a 2-min vortex, was centrifuged for 20 min at 5000 rpm, and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm microporous membrane. Acetic, propionic, butyric, and valeric acid standards were prepared appropriately, and methanol was added to prepare mixed standard solutions at concentrations of 1.00, 0.75, 0.75, and 0.15 mol/L, respectively. The filtrate was analyzed using a GC-MS (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with an Agilent DB-WAX chromatography column (30 m × 250 μm, 0.25 μm). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The injection volume was 1 µL with a split ratio of 10:1. The temperature of the column was initially set to 110 °C and kept at that level for 2 min. After that, it was raised to 135 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and kept at that level for 0 min. Finally, it was raised to 150 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min and kept at that level for 0 min. It was further increased to 160 °C at 5 °C/min, maintained for 0 min, and finally raised to 200 °C at 10 °C/min, with a maintenance period of 0 min. The mass spectrometer was operated with the following temperatures: ion source, 230 °C; inlet, 230 °C; and detector, 230 °C.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD and analyzed by one-way ANOVA using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. * P < 0.05 and ** P < 0.01 versus the Control group; # P < 0.05 and ## P < 0.01 versus the Positive group.
