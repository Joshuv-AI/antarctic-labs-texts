Behavioural activation for the prevention of poststroke depression in low-income older stroke survivors (LIVE WEL): protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Jennifer E S Beauchamp, Sean I Savitz, Anjail Sharrief, Belinda M Reininger, Ronald Acierno, Robert Suchting, Jason Burnett, Lauren Rosales, Jenan Ahmed Albaqali, Rinitha Rajan, Namkee G Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPoststroke depression (PSD) affects approximately 33% of stroke survivors and is associated with worse outcomes, poor quality of life (QOL) and mortality. Despite its prevalence and consequences, there is no consensus on the most effective strategy for PSD prevention. Behavioural activation (BA) is an effective intervention for depression across diverse populations and is considered safer, better tolerated and a longer-lasting alternative to antidepressant medications. This study aims to test the effectiveness of a remotely delivered BA intervention to prevent PSD (Tele-BA-S).Methods and analysisWe will conduct a randomised effectiveness trial of 350 low-income adults (≥ 55 years) within 3 months of ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke and with subthreshold depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 score <9 and 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) score <15). Eligible and consented adults will be randomly assigned to Tele-BA-S or treatment-as-usual. Tele-BA-S will be comprised of an orientation session, 5 weekly BA sessions and 2 follow-up monthly booster calls delivered by trained community health workers. We will conduct assessments at 1 month, 2 months, 4 months, 6 months and 9 months after baseline. The primary outcome (PSD) will be measured by the 24-item HDRS. Secondary outcomes will include anxiety (Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 Scale), psychological well-being (Ryff Scale of Psychological Well-being), QOL (Stroke Specific QOL Scale), medication adherence (Medication Adherence Report Scale-5), rehabilitation adherence (Rehabilitation Adherence Inventory), number of emergency department visits and hospitalisations, functional outcome (Barthel Index) and degree of disability (Modified Rankin Scale). Mediating variables will include self-efficacy (Stroke Self-Efficacy Questionnaire), motivation (Motivation in Stroke Patients for Rehabilitation Scale) and activity engagement (Neuro-QOL Participation in Roles and Activities). Exploratory implementation measures will also be collected. Primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle and evaluate intervention effects over time using mixed-effects models.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston’s (UTHealth Houston) Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects Institutional Review Board. The trial protocol, statistical analysis plan and code, and deidentified participant data will be made available via the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive. The results will be presented at academic conferences and submitted for publication. Introduction

Poststroke depression (PSD) occurs in about 40% of older patients (≥55 years)1 2 and typically peaks 3 to 6 months after the stroke event.3,6 Subthreshold depression, clinically relevant depressive symptoms that do not meet full criteria for a depressive disorder, can affect up to 60% of survivors, usually within 3 months of the stroke event,7 and if untreated, is likely to progress to PSD.8 The consequences of PSD in older adults may be devastating and exacerbated by age-related life stressors (eg, bereavement,9,11 limited retirement income,12 physical disabilities5 and cognitive13 and communication impairments).5 14 PSD is associated with a twofold increase in disability,15 16 lower rehabilitation adherence,3 greater care dependence,3 17 poor mental health and quality of life (QOL),3 diminished social functioning,18 deteriorated cognitive function,13 higher risk for recurrent stroke,19 rehospitalisation1 and suicide and non-suicide mortality,3 20 as well as increased healthcare costs.3 20 Despite its high occurrence and negative consequences, there is no consensus on the most effective PSD prevention strategy in the older adult population.3 21 22

Pharmacological and psychosocial interventions show some efficacy in preventing PSD,21 23though pharmacotherapy is limited by inconsistent or delayed response, low adherence and potential side effects,3,59 24 25 particularly in older adults and those on dual antiplatelet therapy.25 Psychosocial interventions, such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), are well-tolerated, but are often inaccessible due to licensed mental health workforce shortages, financial constraints, situational isolation and geographic barriers especially among low-income populations.26,28 There is a critical need for safe, accessible and effective interventions to prevent PSD among low-income, older adults.

Behavioural activation for depression

Behavioural activation (BA) is an effective treatment for depression across diverse populations and is generally considered to be a safer, better tolerated and longer-lasting alternative to antidepressant medications.29,31 BA can be delivered by laypeople (eg, community health workers (CHWs)), is cost-effective and can be easily adapted for individuals with a range of disabilities and impairments.831,36 BA uses problem-solving techniques to reduce avoidance and withdrawal behaviours and overcome barriers to engaging in value-based activities, alongside self-regulation techniques, such as self-monitoring and self-reward, to maintain health-promoting behaviours.30 Health events such as stroke can lead to avoidance behaviours, limiting engagement in activities perceived as risky (eg, fear of falling).37 Through structured, achievable behavioural goals, BA enhances mastery experiences, elevates self-efficacy, strengthens motivation and progressively restores engagement in meaningful activities and social connections,30 mechanisms particularly relevant for older adults’ mental health after stroke.38

Preliminary data show that paraprofessional-delivered, video-conferenced BA (eg, Tele-BA) effectively reduced depressive symptoms in 40% of low-income, homebound older adults, including those with a history of stroke.33 39 Tele-BA is particularly suitable for low-income, older adults who have experienced a stroke, as they may face limited opportunities for social connection, pleasurable activities or activities outside of the home environment due to financial constraints, stroke- and/or age-related physical disabilities and/or cognitive impairments.40 41 Moreover, a CHW-delivered Tele-BA approach represents a potentially sustainable, scalable, cost-effective and responsive model of preventive poststroke mental healthcare, independent of licensed mental health provider workforce shortages, geographic location or socioeconomic status.

Study objectives and hypotheses

The primary objective is to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of Tele-BA-S compared with treatment as usual (TAU) in preventing PSD in low-income, older stroke survivors with subthreshold depression. For the purpose of this study, subthreshold depression is defined as none to mild depressive symptoms. We hypothesise that Tele-BA-S, compared with TAU, will lead to a greater reduction in symptoms of subthreshold depression and a lower rate of progression to PSD at 2 months after baseline by preventing survivors from developing new or worsening symptoms of depression that could lead to PSD. We will examine self-efficacy, motivation and activity engagement as potential mediators associated with BA-induced therapeutic changes and PSD. We hypothesise that improvements in self-efficacy, motivation and activity engagement will mediate effectiveness of Tele-BA-S in preventing PSD. Secondary objectives are to examine effectiveness of Tele-BA-S for reducing anxiety, healthcare visits and disability and improving psychological well-being and QOL, medication and rehabilitation adherence and functional outcomes. We hypothesise that compared with TAU, Tele-BA-S will lead to (1) greater decreases in anxiety, healthcare visits and degrees of disability; (2) greater adherence to medication and rehabilitation; and (3) improvements in function, psychological well-being and QOL.

Methods and analysis

The study is a randomised effectiveness trial with a parallel arm design. Patients will be recruited from the University of Texas Physicians Outpatient Stroke Clinic in Houston, Texas. This study will span 5 years from June 2025 to April 2030, including the preparation phase (eg, hiring and training CHWs), June 2025 through February 2026, followed by the implementation phase from March 2026 to August 2029. Specifically, the planned study start date will be April 2026 and the anticipated study completion date will be November 2029. The study protocol is guided by the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines.42

Participant inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria are as follows: age ≥55 years, study enrolment ≤3 months + 2 weeks after a first-time ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, community-residing (eg, not discharged to a rehabilitation facility), and English or Spanish proficiency. Eligibility will also be limited to patients with an annual single person income ≤US$45,000, representing approximately three times the federal poverty level in 2024.43 To target the prevention of PSD, eligibility will be limited to patients with subthreshold depression. Patients must demonstrate none to mild depressive symptoms, defined as a Patient Health Questionnaire-944 (PHQ-9) pre-screening score of ≤9 and a confirmatory 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale 2445 (HDRS) screening score of <15. Patients will be excluded if they meet any of the following criteria: stroke unrelated to vascular risk factors (eg, drug use) or transient ischaemic attack, high-risk alcohol or drug misuse (CAGE-AID46 score=4), aphasia diagnosis, or severe language deficit (Language Screening Test score <15).47 Additional exclusion criteria include: a dementia diagnosis, receiving cognitive enhancers, amyloid-targeting medications, or orexin receptor antagonists, or severe cognitive impairment (Mini-Montreal Cognitive Assessment (Mini-MoCA) score ≤10).48 Lastly, patients participating in psychotherapy, receiving antipsychotic medications, or with an elevated risk for suicide (active suicidal ideation as indicated with a positive response to Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) questions 2, 3, 4, 5 and or 6)49 will be excluded. Patients receiving pharmacologic treatment for mood disorders must be on a stable dose for ≥4 weeks prior to enrolment.

Consent and randomisation

The study timeline and procedures for prescreening, screening, enrolment and follow-up are outlined in figure 1. Briefly, patients will be approached sequentially for eligibility prescreening and participation during an outpatient stroke clinic visit after hospital discharge. Sex-balanced enrolment will be supported by routine monitoring of recruitment demographics to guide targeted efforts. Written informed consent (see online supplemental materials) will be obtained by trained research coordinators during a clinic visit and captured electronically using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). Given the eligibility criteria, we do not anticipate that anyone willing to participate will be unable to provide informed consent. The final eligibility screening criterion of subthreshold depression (24-item HDRS) will be assessed by trained research coordinators who will also obtain the post-baseline HDRS assessments. These coordinators will be blinded to the intervention assignment. Enrolment will be defined as an eligible patient’s agreement to participate in the study following the completion of the informed consent process and baseline forms, and random assignment to the Tele-BA-S intervention group or the TAU control group using a REDCap randomisation module. Enrolment must occur ≤3 months + 2 weeks of the stroke incident. Participants will be randomised 1:1 using permuted blocks of size 4, with stratification by time since stroke (≤1.5 months, >1.5 months) and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score (0–5, 6–15, >15). The allocation sequence was generated by the study biostatistician (RS), who is not directly involved in patient allocation, and implemented in REDCap by an unblinded data manager. The allocation sequence is not accessible to any other study investigator. Due to the distinct activities in the intervention group, participants cannot be blinded. However, as previously mentioned, to reduce potential bias, the postbaseline outcome assessments will be evaluated by research coordinators who are blinded to the treatment allocation. Moreover, to ensure equity of information and to avoid differential exposure to supportive resources, all participants, regardless of group assignment, will receive the same standardised community resource pamphlet on enrolment, outlining available services to address potential social determinants of health (eg, transportation and food assistance).

Study arms

Behavioural activation

The goal of BA is to encourage participants to engage in rewarding values-based activities, limit activities that contribute to depression, and identify and overcome obstacles that hinder participation in meaningful activities or that contribute to social withdrawal.30 The video-conferenced, Tele-BA intervention will be adapted for adults who have had a stroke (Tele-BA-S) based on the Telehealth Behavioural Activation Treatment Manual for Home-bound Older Adults.50 Prior to implementing Tele-BA-S, each participant will be assigned a trained CHW who will conduct an orientation session to review the structure of the Tele-BA-S intervention and provide technical guidance for joining the teleconferencing sessions. Although a teleconference orientation visit is preferred to test a fully remote intervention model, telephone or, on participant request and need, in-home orientations may be conducted to reduce barriers to participation, when possible. As is standard in BA interventions, Tele-BA-S sessions will be delivered once per week.30 Although sessions are intended to be delivered via tele-conference, telephone sessions will be allowed, as needed, to support participant ongoing access and to accommodate participant circumstances (eg, technology issues with video-conferencing). BA has been successfully delivered to low-income, older adults via telephone with evidence supporting comparable effectiveness across remote delivery modalities.51,53 iPADS and internet hotspots will be made available to participants without access to support engagement. Five 60-minute BA sessions will be delivered by the assigned CHW over 5 weeks (1 session per week for 5 weeks). Given that poststroke fatigue is prevalent, affecting 42% to 53% of stroke survivors,54 CHWs may divide the 60-minute weekly sessions into two 30-minute weekly sessions, delivered across 5 weeks. If a session is missed, a rescheduling attempt for the same week will be made. If no makeup session is possible for a given week, the participant will be given the option of either having two sessions the following week or continuing up to two additional weeks, for up to two missing sessions. Completion will be defined as a participants’ completing 4 out of 5 of the sessions. During sessions, participants will be asked to identify and schedule activities (pleasant events scheduling), set goals for their activities, identify some barriers to completing these activities, implement their activities while working on ways to overcome any barriers, and review their activity-related accomplishments. Participants will be encouraged to complete a weekly activity monitoring form, which includes documentation of their activities, sense of accomplishment and enjoyment associated with their activities to help them see the link between behaviour and mood. CHW training and intervention fidelity monitoring

CHWs will maintain their Department of State Health Services (DSHS) CHW certification. In order to be certified as a CHW in Texas, individuals must complete a DSHS-certified 180-hour competency-based CHW training programme or have at least 1000 hours of verified CHW service within the past 3 years.55 CHWs will receive additional study-specific training and supervision from a licensed master social worker (LMSW). The LMSW has received Tele-BA-S training from coinvestigator (NGC), who has extensive experience conducting Tele-BA trials and will provide ongoing oversight during the Tele-BA-S implementation phase. Based on a previously developed and implemented layperson Tele-BA training manual (NGC and others),50 CHWs will receive didactic training and materials covering eight core BA knowledge and skill competencies, along with Tele-BA-S role playing. CHWs will receive training in depressive symptom screening (PHQ-9),56 suicide risk assessment (C-SSRS),49 ethical responsibilities, and strategies to build a trusting professional alliance. Figure 2 outlines the CHW-training plan. All Tele-BA-S sessions will be recorded, and at least 20% of the sessions will be monitored for fidelity by the LMSW using a standardised fidelity checklist developed by NC and used in other funded clinical trials and adapted for this study. Weekly participant homework completion will also be tracked as a central component and potential moderator of the intervention.

Treatment-as-usual

Participants randomly assigned to the TAU group will receive the institution’s standard poststroke hospital discharge care, which includes routine stroke education and a scheduled outpatient follow-up appointment with a stroke practitioner.

Outcome measures and assessment points

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is PSD and will be assessed at baseline and 2, 4, 6 and 9 months after baseline using the 24-item HDRS. The HDRS is a well-established, observer-rated instrument with strong diagnostic validity for PSD. Specifically, the 24-item HDRS GRID version integrates the structured 21-item49,57 HDRS interview with three additional items relevant to late-life depression (worthlessness, helplessness and hopelessness) and uses a scoring system that separates symptom intensity and frequency to enhance rating precision.58 Higher total scores indicate greater symptom severity. Consistent with established thresholds in geriatric depression and Tele-BA trials, PSD will be defined as a HDRS score of ≥15, indicating moderate to severe depressive symptoms.59,62

The PHQ-9 will also be used to assess depressive symptom severity, given its feasibility and reliability when administered by non-clinicians, widespread use in outpatient clinical settings, validation in stroke populations,63 and strong correlation with established depression measures, including the 24-item HDRS. The PHQ-9 yields total scores ranging from 0 to 27, with a cut-off score of ≥10 demonstrating 91% sensitivity and 89% specificity for detecting major depressive disorder. The PHQ-9 is collected in the outpatient stroke clinic (baseline) and will also be administered at the beginning of each Tele-BA-S session, during the booster calls, at 1 month, and at all postbaseline assessments to monitor participant safety and to identify clinically meaningful changes in depressive symptom severity or potential suicidal ideation. Important to note, the emergence of active suicidal ideation on the PHQ-9 or HDRS, confirmed by the C-SSRS, will trigger an escalation plan which includes additional evaluation of the participant by the study’s psychologist (RA) to determine level of risk. Emergency services will be arranged for participants at imminent suicide risk, their stroke neurologist will be notified, and they will be removed from the study. Participants who develop passive suicidal ideation or worsening depressive symptoms since the last assessment point (a>4-point increase in PHQ-9 score or a >30% increase in HDRS score) will be permitted to remain in the study. The study PI or psychologist will refer the participant to their stroke neurologist and, with the participants’ permission, the study psychologist will contact the stroke neurologist or other healthcare professional to discuss the clinical concerns and assist the provider in management recommendations. Secondarily, the PHQ-9 will be administered to assess its concurrent validity with the 24-item HDRS to further inform its suitability for use in future pragmatic trials and, if the intervention proves effective, provide evidence for its adoption as a primary measure of PSD in clinical and community-based settings implementing Tele-BA-S.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will include anxiety, psychological well-being, QOL, healthcare visits, functional outcomes, medication and rehabilitation adherence, and disability and activity engagement measurements that will be collected at baseline and 2, 4, 6 and 9 months after baseline. Anxiety will be measured using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 Item (GAD-7),64 which has demonstrated good psychometric properties for diagnosing GAD.49 The Ryff Psychological Well-being Scale, specifically the purpose in life subscale, will be used to measure psychological well-being.65 The Stroke Specific Quality of Life Scale is a self-administered assessment of health-related QOL specific to stroke survivors.66 Healthcare visits will be documented as the number of self-reported emergency department visits and the number of hospitalisations over the course of the study. The Modified Rankin Score (mRS) is a standardised measure of stroke disability.67 The Medication Adherence Scale-5 and Rehabilitation Adherence Index is a standardised measure used for medication and rehabilitation adherence, respectively. The Barthel Index is a functional outcome measure used to assess performance in basic activities of daily living. Mediating variables will include the Stroke Self-Efficacy Questionnaire,68 Motivation for Rehabilitation scale,69 and Neuro-QOL70 which reflects the ability to participate in roles and activities, and will be completed at baseline and 2, 4, 6 and 9 months after baseline. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics will be abstracted from outpatient electronic health records, supplemented by a sociodemographic survey, including the following: age, gender identity, race and ethnicity, marital status, insurance status, contact information, prestroke living situation, education, income, occupation, clinical data related to the stroke event, history of depression and medications. Lastly, exploratory implementation data will be collected based on the Reach Effectiveness Adoption Implementation Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework and will include acceptability, feasibility and appropriateness intervention measures.71

Participant retention

Participants will be compensated for completing the surveys at each of the six assessment timepoints (baseline and 1, 2, 4, 6 and 9 months after baseline). If participants cannot be located for follow-up, we will attempt to reach provided alternate contacts before mailing a letter to their homes. We will also send swag bags, holiday, birthday and thank you cards to promote retention.

Statistical analysis and sample size calculations

Analyses will follow intention-to-treat principles and be conducted in R.72 Primary analyses will use generalised linear mixed models to assess intervention effects over time via time-by-group interactions across repeated measures. Mediation analyses will evaluate the extent to which treatment effects on PSD prevention are explained by self-efficacy, motivation and activity engagement, estimating both direct and indirect effects. Models will be tested with and without adjustment for prespecified covariates, including sociodemographic (sex, race, age, socioeconomic status), clinical (NIHSS, mRS, Charlson Comorbidity Index, antidepressant use, time since stroke) and fidelity factors (Tele-BA-S participant homework completion). If resulting inferences differ based on adjustment, we will report both models; otherwise, we will retain the simpler model. Follow-up analyses will evaluate potential moderation of treatment effects by covariates through interaction terms.

Power was estimated via Monte Carlo simulation, defined as the proportion of simulations in which the group-by-time interaction was statistically significant at a two-sided α=0.05. Assuming an analyzable sample size of N=280 (140 per group) after attrition and a 14.6% absolute difference in outcome rates between groups at 2 months, power was estimated at 80.5%. Our planned 20% attrition-adjusted sample size is N=350 (175 per group).

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not formally involved in the trial design and will not be formally involved in the conduct of the trial. However, the PI consulted a community advisory board of stroke survivors during the planning phase to inform the study. While the primary outcome is a measure of PSD, secondary and exploratory outcomes include measures that relate to QOL and patient satisfaction with the intervention relative to TAU.

Ethics and dissemination

The study design, risks and benefits, and participant confidentiality were judged rigorously. Specifically, ethical approval for this study has been obtained by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston’s (UTHealth Houston) Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects’ Institutional Review Board (HSC-SN-25-0265) and a Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) has been established. The DSMB has met prior to the initiation of participant enrolment and will meet biannually to independently monitor trial conduct. Use of protected health information (PHI) will be minimised and any electronic records containing PHI will be stored on password-protected systems on secure servers and accessible only to authorised personnel. Each participant will be assigned a unique study identification number, no PHI will appear in reports, and all research personnel will undergo training on confidentiality and disclosure policies. A manuscript with the results of the study will be published in a peer-reviewed journal. Additionally, our dissemination plan will include organisations (eg, West Houston Stroke Warriors) and avenues (eg, social media) to effectively transfer the research knowledge we gain to adults who have had a stroke.

Data monitoring and management

Baseline forms and outcome assessments will be entered into a secure REDCap database. The database structure will include range checks for data values and missingness, and each data field will contain a description of the data element. Deidentified data will be made available through the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive. See online supplemental materials for the data management sharing plan.

Discussion

Despite the prevalence of PSD, there is no consensus on the most effective strategy to prevent PSD. Few studies have examined the implementation of evidence-based preventive interventions within secondary stroke care, despite PSD being independently associated with a twofold increase in disability, lower rehabilitation adherence, greater care dependence, poor mental health and QOL, diminished social functioning, deteriorated cognitive function, higher risk for recurrent stroke, rehospitalisation, mortality, including suicide, and higher healthcare costs.3 This gap in care represents a missed opportunity to prevent PSD and the associated negative effects. Findings from this study will advance understanding of subthreshold depression, its progression to PSD, and inform prevention strategies, addressing a critical gap in secondary stroke prevention.

BA directly targets core behavioural mechanisms underlying PSD, including reduced activity engagement, behavioural avoidance and withdrawal, and diminished exposure to positive reinforcing experiences,30 which are common following stroke.73 By increasing engagement in meaningful, value-based activities, BA enhances environmental reinforcement and self-efficacy, leading to improvements in mood.30 This mechanistic focus distinguishes BA from more resource-intensive psychotherapies, such as CBT and supports its scalability for treating subthreshold depression and preventing PSD. However, most BA interventions targeting PSD have been delivered in person by trained mental health professionals (eg, advanced practice nurses and psychotherapy specialists), limiting its scalability. Increasing evidence supports the effectiveness of BA when delivered by non-specialist lay counsellors (eg, CHWs).74 Although CHW models are widely implemented in the USA, they have rarely been leveraged to deliver mental healthcare.75 This approach allows trained clinicians to focus on adults who have had a stroke with more severe psychiatric needs while expanding access to preventive mental health interventions after stroke. If the effectiveness of Tele-BA-S is demonstrated, Tele-BA-S may be incorporated into poststroke care to prevent PSD and enhance recovery, particularly among low income older adult stroke survivors.
