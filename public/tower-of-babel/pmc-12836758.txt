Ethnic and geographic disparities in proteinuria and high blood pressure: a cross-sectional study from community kidney screenings in the Washington DC area
Authors: Abdelrhman Refaey, Alexander Bunce, Ahmed Ebeid, Ahmed Attia, Omar Saadi, Ishan Abdullah, Jacob Zarkower, Mohamed Abdou, Clive O. Callender, Joseph K. Melancon, Pablo Serrano Rodriguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Backgroundchronic kidney disease (CKD) affects 14% of U.S adults, with 90% unaware of their condition. This study assessed ethnic and geographic disparities in proteinuria and hypertension through community outreach screening in underserved areas in the Washington, DC Metropolitan Statistical Area.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 1,597 adults through community screenings in underserved areas of the Washington DC Metropolitan Area. Screening assessments included semi-quantitative urine dipstick testing for proteinuria and automated blood pressure (BP) measurements. Participant forms collected demographic information, comorbidities, and healthcare engagement history. Two BP readings were obtained per participant, with the average recorded; measurements were repeated when initial readings were inconsistent. High-risk participants were defined as individuals with ≥ 1 of the following: proteinuria ≥ 30 mg/dL ( ≥ + 1), systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic BP ≥ 80 mmHg, or self-reported kidney disease. All participants received basic health counseling; high-risk participants received additional individualized guidance. High-risk participants (n = 245) were contacted once for follow-up, 2–4 weeks post-screening, to assess care-seeking behavior and CKD awareness.ResultsAfrican Americans were the predominant group and exhibited the highest prevalence of proteinuria and high blood pressure, followed by Hispanic/Latino individuals, with Caucasians having the lowest rates. Adjusted analysis showed African Americans and Hispanic/Latino participants had significantly higher odds of markedly elevated blood pressure (> 160/95 mmHg) compared to Caucasians. Crude analysis revealed higher proteinuria rates among residents of DC Wards 1–4 and 7–8, and Maryland, compared to Virginia. Among 245 high-risk participants contacted, 92 (37.6%) responded; 48 (52.2%) had visited a physician, and 31 (33.7%) planned to seek care. Post-screening surveys indicated that 60% of respondents reported increased awareness of CKD.ConclusionThis community-based screening program identified significant racial/ethnic and geographic disparities in CKD risk factors, particularly among African American participants and residents of specific DC wards. Screening events can improve early identification of CKD risk markers, increase awareness, and encourage healthcare engagement in high-risk populations.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant but often overlooked public health issue. It affects 14% of U.S adults, with an estimated 90% of them unaware of their condition [1]. Recent estimates of patients over the age of 66 suggest that the adjusted mortality rate for those with CKD is more than double that of patients without the disease [2]. CKD often presents with non-specific symptoms and progresses slowly until advanced stages. When left untreated, it greatly increases the risk for adverse health outcomes such as kidney failure, heart disease, and stroke [1]. Furthermore, end-stage renal disease (ESRD) management is limited to three options: dialysis, kidney transplantation, or conservative care. National data revealed that approximately 44,187 adult candidates were added to the kidney waiting list in 2022. The list of patients waiting for a kidney transplant has grown steadily over the years, now at approximately 92,000 people [3, 4]. Beyond the public health implications, CKD is also a financial burden for our healthcare system. Treatment cost among Medicare beneficiaries alone reached $87.2 billion in 2019 [1].

The need for early detection and management is apparent, particularly in populations that are at high risk for developing CKD, including individuals with hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, a family history of CKD, or belonging to racial and ethnic minority groups disproportionately affected by kidney disease. One way to address this need is by providing free community-based health screenings, targeted at areas where risk factors are prevalent [5, 6]. Differences in awareness rate among adults with CKD Stages 3–5 exist across ethnic groups, with higher awareness seen in non-Hispanic Blacks compared to non-Hispanic Whites, likely reflecting a higher prevalence of disease within ethnic communities and families, higher rates of CKD comorbidities, and more advanced disease stage at time of diagnosis among ethnic groups [7–9]. By improving awareness of CKD incidence and management, patients are empowered to seek necessary, timely follow-up care, begin appropriate treatment, manage risk factors, lower long-term healthcare costs, and avoid progression and complications [10].

Community health screenings have demonstrated some success in raising awareness and facilitating early detection of CKD, but the overall effectiveness remains uncertain without an objective measure of its direct impact [5]. Many existing screening programs, such as the World Kidney Day (WKD) campaign and the Kidney Early Evaluation Program (KEEP), have reported modest increases in CKD awareness [11, 12]. Despite these mixed findings, the apparent benefit of kidney screening programs to detect early disease and improve awareness can outweigh the risk when utilizing non-complex screening tools such as dipstick proteinuria tests and automated blood pressure measurements. This may be especially effective in areas where the incidence of kidney disease is highest [13, 14].

The Washington, DC Metropolitan Area is characterized by substantial racial and socioeconomic diversity and one of the highest CKD burdens in the United States. More than 8,300 individuals in this region are on the kidney transplant waitlist, approximately 80% of whom identify as ethnic minorities [15]. To address these disparities, we conducted around 270 kidney screening events from January 1, 2019, to July 1, 2024, in strategically selected underserved neighborhoods across the DC metropolitan area (Fig. 1). These events were held at community centers, health fairs, and other accessible public venues and included blood pressure measurement, semi-quantitative urine dipstick testing, demographic and medical history collection, and optional health awareness surveys.

The goals of this cross-sectional study were to: (1) identify demographic and clinical variables associated with proteinuria and elevated blood pressure; and (2) evaluate the utility of community-based screening events in promoting CKD awareness and healthcare engagement. By examining both clinical and geographic patterns of CKD risk markers, this study aims to inform targeted public health strategies and contribute to reducing the burden of kidney disease in high-risk communities.

Methods

The George Washington Ron and Joy Paul Kidney Center, in partnership with the National Minority Organ Tissue Transplant Education Program (MOTTEP), conducts community kidney screenings and educational events across the Washington D.C. metropolitan area. These community-based screenings took place in accessible locations such as churches, community centers, and health fairs to reach underserved populations. The center participates in approximately 45 events annually, with a total of around 270 events from 2019 to 2025. These were held both independently and in partnership with local organizations. Events were strategically located in underserved neighborhoods to target populations at higher risk for CKD, including African Americans, Hispanic/Latino individuals, older adults, and those with hypertension, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease.

Statistical methods

Chi-squared and Analysis of Variance tests were used to evaluate the relationships between potential covariates, including sex, age, history of type II diabetes, heart disease, previous stroke, and history of hypertension, home zip code, and patient ethnicity. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression were used to elucidate the relationship between ethnicity and the likelihood of screened proteinuria or elevated blood pressure. Logistic regression analysis was repeated for markedly elevated blood pressure (≥ 160/95). Subjects with missing values were excluded. Insurance status was excluded from regression analyses due to 65.5% missingness.

Geographic analysis

Participants’ home locations were categorized by D.C. wards and neighboring Maryland and Virginia regions. For analysis, DC Wards 1–4 and 7–8 were combined due to smaller sample sizes in individual wards, allowing for more robust comparisons. Participants from Wards 5–6 were included in descriptive analyses but were excluded from combined-ward regression models due to low representation.

Results

A total of 1,597 participants were included in this cross-sectional study. The cohort predominantly consisted of African Americans (73%), with additional representation from Caucasians (10.1%), Hispanic/Latino (5%), Asian (4.6%), Alaskan (1.8%), and other ethnicities (5.6%). The mean age was 54.2 years (SD 16.1), with 52.3% female and 47.7% male participants. Significant comorbidities were prevalent, with 37.4% reporting Type II diabetes mellitus and 54.9% having a history of hypertension. Approximately 21% of participants reported not having a primary care provider (PCP) or not engaging with a PCP in the prior year (Table 1).

Proteinuria prevalence

Proteinuria prevalence varied significantly across ethnic groups, with the highest observed among individuals identifying as “Other” (16.9%) and African Americans (16.1%), followed by Hispanic/Latino (12.7%), Alaskans (10.3%), Asians (9.6%), and Caucasians (8%). In the crude analysis, African Americans demonstrated higher odds of proteinuria compared to Caucasians (OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.19–4.08). However, after adjustment for potential confounders such as age, history of hypertension, sex, home address, and history of diabetes, this difference was not statistically significant (adjusted OR 1.35, 95% CI 0.65–2.82). Additionally, residence location was associated with differences in proteinuria prevalence. Participants living in DC Wards 1–4 OR 2.63 (95% CI 1.19–5.82), DC Wards 7–8 OR 2.10 (95% CI 0.99–4.47), and Maryland OR 1.94 (95% CI 0.94-4.00), had higher odds of proteinuria compared to those living in Virginia.

High blood pressure prevalence

High blood pressure (≥ 130/80 mmHg) prevalence also varied by ethnicity, with the highest rates among African Americans (69.3%), followed by Hispanic/Latino (56.9%), Other ethnicities (57.0%), Caucasians (53.1%), Asians (47.9%), and Alaskans (34.5%). Crude analysis showed African Americans (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.43–2.79) and Alaskans (OR 2.65, 95% CI 1.07–6.58) were more likely to have measured high blood pressure compared to Caucasians. After adjustment, African American participants remained significantly more likely to have hypertension (adjusted OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.05–2.70) (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Markedly elevated blood pressure (≥ 160/95 mmHg) was most prevalent among Hispanic/Latino participants (24.1%), followed by those who identified as Other ethnicities (22.5%), African Americans (20.5%), Asians (10.9%), Caucasians (8.6%), and Alaskans (6.9%). In adjusted analysis, African Americans were three times (OR 3.04, 95% CI 1.39–6.67), and Hispanic/Latinos were nearly four times (OR 3.91, 95% CI 1.35–11.30) as likely as Caucasians to have measured markedly elevated blood pressure (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Insurance status and follow-up outcomes

Insurance status was available for 35% of participants: only 27% (n = 321) were insured, 7.5% (n = 89) were uninsured, with missing insurance status in 65.5% of the cohort. Given the high proportion of missing data, insurance was reported descriptively only.

Among 245 participants classified as high-risk (blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mmHg or proteinuria ≥ 30 mg/dL) ( ≥ + 1), follow-up response rate was 37.6% (n = 92). Most respondents were African American (91%, n = 84). Of these, 52.2% (48/92) reported having already visited a healthcare provider, and 33.7% (31/92) planned to seek care.

Among respondents, 19 of those who had followed up and 16 of those planning to follow up were insured. Regarding CKD awareness, 36 of 60 respondents (60%) reported increased awareness post-screening, while six were already aware prior to screening.

Primary care access and screening outcomes

Participants without a regular PCP were more likely to present with elevated blood pressure (58.2% vs. 52.1%) and proteinuria (15.2% vs. 12.8%) compared to those with a PCP, underscoring the role of community screening events in bridging existing care gaps.

Discussion

According to the National Kidney Foundation, there is a 5-step plan for CKD evaluation and referral, including knowing the criteria for CKD, recognizing risk factors, screening for CKD, classifying CKD to guide testing and treatment, and implementing a clinical action plan based on the patient’s CKD classification [16].

In our study, we focused on the screening and risk factor recognition phases. CKD screening typically involves two simple tests: a spot urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) to detect albuminuria and/or a serum creatinine test to estimate glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Identifying risk factors requires obtaining a thorough patient’s medical history of chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, social history, and family history of kidney disease.

Our study aligns with prior programs such as the World Kidney Day (WKD) campaign and the Kidney Early Evaluation Program (KEEP) by [11, 12].

Nationally, approximately 20% of African American adults and 14% of Hispanic adults are affected by CKD, compared to 12% of non-Hispanic White adults [1]. African Americans have a fourfold higher incidence of progression to ESRD, and Hispanics and Native Americans have a twofold higher incidence [2]. Our findings mirror these disparities: African Americans were more than twice as likely as Caucasians to have proteinuria in crude analysis. However, after adjustment for age, comorbidities, sex, and zip code, the association was no longer significant. These findings suggest that disparities in proteinuria are partly explained by comorbid conditions and socioeconomic factors, but systemic barriers—including healthcare access, knowledge gaps, and financial constraints—likely contribute to persistent differences [11, 12, 14]. Our analysis also revealed disparities in blood pressure: African Americans were twice as likely to have high blood pressure (> 130/80 mmHg) compared to Caucasians, and this association remained significant after adjustment. Markedly elevated blood pressure (≥ 160/95 mmHg) was also most prevalent among African American and Hispanic/Latino participants. These findings emphasize the disproportionate burden of CKD risk factors in these populations [17–20].

Community-based screenings, like those in our study, help address barriers including low CKD awareness, limited trust in healthcare systems, and financial constraints. Recruiting community members and facilitators such as church health leaders or peer educators can improve participation, engagement, and follow-up adherence [6, 19].

In our study, follow-up among 245 high-risk participants was 37.6%, with 91% African American respondents. Of these, 85.9% reported either already visiting a healthcare provider or planning to do so. CKD awareness increased in 60% of respondents, demonstrating the potential impact of screenings on health knowledge. However, the low response rate also underscores ongoing systemic challenges, including technological access, medical mistrust, and competing health priorities, which must be addressed to maximize program effectiveness [21]. Awareness assessment was based on structured follow-up calls; while not a formally validated questionnaire, it provided practical insight into participants’ knowledge and engagement.

Innovative strategies are needed to improve CKD follow-up care in populations who have these barriers. This could include community partnership initiatives and utilizing patient navigation services that can ensure that patients are not lost during the follow-up period. For providers, there is a need for greater supportive technology embedded in the EMR and clinical guideline suggestions in ensuring that kidney disease is properly addressed during visits and followed up appropriately with the patient [21]. By improving follow-up rates, the largely asymptomatic progression of CKD can be mitigated, thereby reducing morbidity and mortality.

Limitations

This study focuses on diverse populations. Its strength is found in the sample size and in integrating both crude and adjusted analyses for disparities. However, the study has several notable limitations that should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about causality. While the overall sample size was large, representation from certain ethnic groups, such as Alaskan and Hispanic/Latino populations, was limited, reducing the statistical power to detect differences. Many variables, including hypertension and diabetes status, were self-reported and may be subject to recall or reporting bias. Proteinuria was assessed using urine dipstick tests, which provide a semi-quantitative estimate and do not directly measure the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), limiting precision. Additionally, possible selection bias may exist, as individuals attending health screenings could be more health-conscious or have better access to healthcare than the general population. The low proportion of responses to the follow-up calls and the absence of objective outcome data to evaluate the long-term impact of increased awareness also highlight areas for future improvement [5].

Future implications

Despite these limitations, the findings underscore the value of community-based screening programs in identifying high-risk populations and informing public health strategies. Future programs may benefit from strategies to increase attendance and improve response to follow-up calls, including reminder systems, local advertisement through community leaders, and designated patient navigators. Geographic and demographic insights can guide resource allocation, such as targeting mobile clinics, health education campaigns, and interventions to underserved zip codes.

Conclusion

Our study highlights persistent disparities in CKD risk factors among African American and Hispanic/Latino populations in the Washington, DC metropolitan area. Community-based screenings serve as an effective tool for early detection, awareness, and engagement, particularly in underserved communities. Expanding these initiatives and improving follow-up strategies may help reduce CKD burden and advance health equity.
