Optimal Multi-Objective Wave-Momentum Shaping in Scattering Media
Tristan Nerson, Jakob Hüpfl, Clément Ferise, David Globosits, Marlene Hudler, Matthieu Malléjac, Stefan Rotter, Romain Fleury

Abstract
Radiation forces and torques are key to manipulating objects with acoustic or electromagnetic waves. An important concept in this context is the Generalized Wigner–Smith (GWS) matrix, which has previously been primarily studied for optimizing radiation forces and torques on single objects embedded inside complex scattering environments. Here, we develop a unified scattering framework that rigorously establishes this connection for arbitrary inhomogeneous, lossless electromagnetic and acoustic media, as well as for controlling multiple objects individually. Variational identities relate parametric changes of the medium to perturbations of the scattering fields, from which the GWS matrix emerges as a natural generator of radiation forces and torques. For a single object, its extremal eigenstates yield maximal force or torque along a chosen direction; for multiple objects, the same framework defines Pareto-optimal compromises among competing objectives and reveals uncertainty relations for their simultaneous optimization. This establishes a comprehensive foundation towards collective and selective manipulation of objects in complex media.

When propagating through complex media, waves – acoustic or electromagnetic, among others – undergo repeated scattering events due to inhomogeneities, which challenges our ability to control and predict their propagation. Foundational works in acoustics by Fink and colleagues showed that wavefronts can be time-reversed to refocus through complex media, revealing that multiple scattering does not necessarily lead to information loss
Fink et al. 2000
;
Horodynski et al. 2021
. Building on these ideas, Vellekoop et al. later demonstrated that tailored wavefronts can focus light through opaque, strongly scattering media, marking the experimental inception of the field of wavefront shaping in optics
Vellekoop and Mosk 2007
;
Mosk et al. 2012
, and further accelerated by the experimental measurement of the optical transmission matrix (a subset of the full scattering matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
capturing the far-field information of the wave propagation)
Popoff et al. 2010
. A key concept in modern wavefront shaping is the use of the Generalized Wigner-Smith (GWS) matrix, defined as

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}=\mathrm{j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{\dagger}\partial_{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}},

(1)

where
\alpha
denotes a given global or local parameter of the scattering region, and
\dagger
denotes the conjugate transpose. Here, we assume the
+\mathrm{j}\omega t
time-harmonic convention, with the angular frequency
\omega
. First described in nuclear and mesoscopic quantum physics
Smith 1960
;
Froissart et al. 1963
;
Brouwer et al. 1997
, this matrix was repurposed as a practical tool to optimize incoming waves for given operations
Ambichl et al. 2017
;
Horodynski et al. 2020
. This has notably found applications in wave focusing, particle cooling and trapping, information retrieval, inverse design and analysis of scattering resonances
Bouchet et al. 2021
;
Matthès et al. 2021
;
del Hougne et al. 2021
;
Horodynski et al. 2022
;
Hüpfl et al. 2023a
;
Sol et al. 2024
;
Būtaitė et al. 2024
;
Globosits et al. 2024
;
Goïcoechea et al. 2025
;
Byrnes and Foreman 2025
;
Bliokh et al. 2025
.

Beyond controlling the spatial distribution of wave amplitude or phase, many applications rely on the mechanical action that waves exert on objects through radiation forces and torques. A connection between these mechanical effects and the GWS matrix has previously been identified, showing that its extremal states can maximize the response of a single object undergoing a small translation or rotation in a scattering environment
Ambichl et al. 2017
;
Horodynski et al. 2020
;
Horodynski et al. 2023
;
Orazbayev et al. 2024
. What has been lacking, however, is a general theoretical framework that unifies these ideas for arbitrary acoustic or electromagnetic media and extends them to the individual control of multiple objects.

Here, we develop such a framework based on variational relations that link parametric changes of the medium to modifications of the scattering fields, leading naturally to GWS matrices that encode individual forces and torques on multiple particles at once. This multi-objective formulation establishes that compromises between competing mechanical targets are inevitable, with GWS eigenstates defining Pareto-optimal solutions that outperform naive Monte Carlo approaches. In particular, we demonstrate how tailored input states can realize coordinated motion of several objects (

Finally, we identify constrained variants that enable selective manipulation, such as maximizing the force on a target object along a prescribed direction while suppressing its motion in an orthogonal direction and simultaneously constraining forces experienced by some other objects (

Radiation forces in inhomogeneous media—

In the following, we assume lossless propagation, under which the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
-matrix is unitary. In acoustics, we are interested in the radiation force density in a fluid containing some inclusions. It is assumed that the inclusions, even though potentially of solid nature, can be modeled as equivalent inviscid fluids in which the Euler equations hold, so that the whole medium is described as an inhomogeneous fluid, while any transverse wave is neglected. A rule of thumb is to consider solid inclusions of characteristic size
d
in which
\omega\ll\pi c_{s}/d
, where
c_{s}
is the shear speed of sound. It has been shown that the time-averaged acoustic body force density can then be written as
Karlsen et al. 2016

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\text{ac}}=-\frac{1}{4}(|p_{1}|^{2}\nabla\kappa_{0}+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}|^{2}\nabla\rho_{0}),

(2)

where
\kappa_{0}
,
\rho_{0}
,
p_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
are the compressibility, density, first order complex pressure and acoustic velocity fields, respectively, with

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}=-\mathrm{j}\omega\kappa_{0}p_{1}\text{ and }\nabla p_{1}=-\mathrm{j}\omega\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}.

(3)

In electromagnetism, for a linear, isotropic, non-dispersive and lossless inhomogeneous medium with no free charges or currents, the time-averaged force density for harmonic fields is analogous to the one in acoustics
Anghinoni et al. 2022
:

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{\text{em}}=-\frac{1}{4}(|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}|^{2}\nabla\varepsilon+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}|^{2}\nabla\mu),

(4)

where
\varepsilon
and
\mu
are the permittivity and permeability of the inhomogeneous medium, respectively, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}
the complex electric and magnetic vector fields. By exploiting the analogy between
Eqs.

2
and
4

Immittance formalism of scattering—

Following circuit theory nomenclature

;
Dicke 1987
, we introduce effective scattering quantities – hereafter called immittance voltage
u_{m}

u_{m}=\sqrt{2}(s_{m}^{+}+s_{m}^{-}),\quad i_{m}=\sqrt{2}(s_{m}^{+}-s_{m}^{-}),

(5)

where

is the incident (
+
) and outgoing (
-
) complex amplitude of the wave, normalized such that
|s_{m}^{\pm}|^{2}
is equal to the incident/outgoing power at the corresponding port. The scattering matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{-}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}
, hence the immittance vectors read

\mathbf{u}=\sqrt{2}(\mathds{1}+\mathbf{S})\mathbf{s}_{+},\quad\mathbf{i}=\sqrt{2}(\mathds{1}-\mathbf{S})\mathbf{s}_{+},

where

1

1

inside the dashed boundary

is positive when flowing into the scattering region. Defining the complex Poynting vector as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}p_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{*}\quad\text{(in acoustics)},

(7a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}^{*}\quad\text{(in electromagnetism)},

(7b)

it can be shown that in both acoustics and electromagnetism

-2\int_{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{*})\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{u^{\dagger}}\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{i}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}.

(8)

Equation (
8

V

Variational integrals—

We extend here an important variational integral relation
Hüpfl et al. 2023b
connecting parametric changes occurring in the scattering region to changes of the immittance voltages and currents in the asymptotic regions. We are interested in the variations of the fields
\delta p_{1}
and
\delta\mathbf{v}_{1}
(resp.
\delta\mathbf{E}
and
\delta\mathbf{H}
) when a parameter
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}
in the system is slightly varied. Consider a system built from
N_{p}
spherical particles of diameter
d_{i}
and density
\rho_{0,i}
in a
\rho_{0}^{(b)}(\mathbf{r})
background, such that the density distribution can be written as

\rho_{0}(\mathbf{r})=\rho_{0}^{(b)}(\mathbf{r})+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\text{rect}_{d_{i}}(|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{i}|)(\rho_{0,i}-\rho_{0}^{(b)}(\mathbf{r})),

(9)

with
\text{rect}_{d_{i}}(r)
being the rectangular function of width
d_{i}
, and
\mathbf{r}_{i}
the positions of the particles. We decide to move the
k
th
particle by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}=\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, inducing a local change in the system, and modify the density distribution by changing
\mathbf{r}_{k}
into
\mathbf{r}_{k}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
in the formula. In a volume
V_{p}
surrounding the particle, we assume that the background density is constant and that only the
k
th
term of the sum contributes to the density distribution. We then observe that

\nabla\rho_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}})=-\nabla_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}\rho_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}})\quad\text{in $V_{p}$},

(10)

with
\nabla_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=(\partial_{\Delta x},\partial_{\Delta y})
in 2D. This also applies to the compressibility, the permittivity and the permeability.

Now, we use
\delta
to denote small variations of fields or parameters due to perturbations such that
\delta f=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}f\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}
. In acoustics,
Eq.

3
leads at first variational order to

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1})=-\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}(\kappa_{0}p_{1}),

(11a)

\displaystyle\nabla(\delta p_{1})=-\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}(\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}).

(11b)

To relate this to the immittance vectors,
Eqs.

11a
and
11b
may be combined as follows:

-\nabla\cdot(p_{1}^{*}\mathbf{\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}}+\delta p_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{*})=\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot(|p_{1}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\kappa_{0}+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\rho_{0}).

(12)

It is then possible to compute the volume integral of
Eq.

12
and use
Eq.

8
to obtain

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot\int_{V}(|p_{1}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\kappa_{0}+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\rho_{0})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V.

(13)

Derivation details, as well as the electromagnetic equivalent of
Eq.

13
are shown in the End Matter. With this, we evidence that the variations of the immittance vectors (at the ports) can be linked to the local changes in the scattering medium.

We now prove the link between these variations, the local radiation force, the GWS matrix and the optimal motion of an object.

Optimal motion of one object —

If
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}
is the spatial shift
\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
,
Eq.

10
applies. The volume of integration in
Eq.

13
can be reduced from
V
to
V_{p}
since all the
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}
terms are zero outside of
V_{p}
. Using
Eqs.

2
and
4
for the time-averaged force densities, the total radiation force
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}
on the volume
V_{p}
in the direction of the shift is

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}\cdot\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}=-\frac{\mathrm{j}}{4\omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}).

(14)

Yet, from
Eq.

6
,

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{u}=\sqrt{2}[(\mathds{1}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}})\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}+\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}]

(15a)

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{i}=\sqrt{2}[(\mathds{1}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}})\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}-\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}]

(15b)

For a fixed chosen input state,
\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
, and the change in immittance vectors is only due to the parametric change of the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
-matrix:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=-4\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{\dagger}\delta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}.

(16)

We extend the GWS matrix defined in
Eq.

1
to multidimensional parameters
\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
to arrive at this normalized GWS matrix:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}&=\mathrm{j}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{\dagger}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}\cdot\nabla_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}|}(\delta(\Delta x)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta x}+\delta(\Delta y)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta y}),\end{split}

(17)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}=\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}/|\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}|
. The expectation value over the input states is

\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}=-\frac{\mathrm{j}}{4|\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}|}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}).

(18)

Using
Eq.

14
, we finally obtain

\frac{1}{\omega}\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}.

(19)

Eq.

19
is the central equation of our Letter. Since
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}
is Hermitian for a unitary scattering matrix, the projection of the radiation force can be written as a Rayleigh quotient. The Courant–Fischer min–max theorem then ensures that

\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}|^{2}\leq\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{N}}{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}|^{2},

(20)

where the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}
are ordered as
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}
. Of particular interest are the extremal values of
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}
, which are reached when the incident state coincides with the associated eigenvector, enabling the maximization or minimization of the applied force projection. More generally, if only
L
out of
N
ports are accessible, the largest force projection lies between
\lambda_{L}/\omega
and
\lambda_{N}/\omega
, with typical values in between. In the following, all the expectation values
\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\cdot}
are taken with respect to the normalized input state such that
|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}|^{2}=1$\mathrm{W}$

Optimal motion of multiple objects—

When
N_{d}
objects are displaced, the variational integrals split into non-overlapping contributions over the disjoint volumes
V_{p,i}
. The total quantity

\frac{1}{\omega}\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\text{tot}}}}=\frac{1}{|\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r_{\text{tot}}}}|}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}|\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{i}|\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{i}},

(21)

therefore measures the infinitesimal (virtual) work of the radiation force on the system. Here,
\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\text{tot}}=(\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{1},\cdots,\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{N_{d}})
is the stacked displacement vector and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{i}}
is the time-averaged force on the
i
th
object in the direction of its push. Without loss of generality, we can focus on the generic case of two objects (
N_{d}=2
). Because
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{2}}
typically do not commute, they cannot, in general, be diagonalized simultaneously; hence, no single incident state maximizes both projections
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}_{1}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{1}}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}_{2}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{2}}

1
1

1

An exception occurs if the two matrices happen to share the same eigenvector associated with their maximal eigenvalues. This non-generic case is understood in
Eq.

23
by observing that what matters is not ultimately the commutation of the two matrices but the vanishing of the expectation value of the commutator.
. Yet, the largest eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\text{tot}}}
maximizes the sum of the projections. Consequently, the multi-object formulation can be directly interpreted through the lens of multi-objective optimization
Miettinen 1999
. Each
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{e}}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{i}}
defines an objective to be maximized and
\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{\text{tot}}}}
corresponds to a so-called linear scalarization of this problem (i.e., forming a weighted sum to be maximized), with the coefficients
|\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{i}|
playing the role of optimization weights. An extension of the theory to optimal torque on objects is proposed in the End Matter. For two objectives – such as displacing/rotating two objects along/about one direction each – the feasible set of force components corresponds to the joint numerical range of two Hermitian matrices, which is always convex
Hausdorff 1919
. This is also true for three objectives when
N>2

Fan and L Tits 1987
. In these cases, it is known that linear scalarization generates an entire Pareto front
Miettinen 1999
– that is, the set of solutions where no objective can be improved without degrading another. We then discover that the GWS explores the full set of such compromises. For four or more objectives, convexity can fail, and the GWS recovers only the exposed subset of the Pareto front, leaving non-convex regions inaccessible.

Uncertainty relation—

In two-objective optimization with GWS matrices, one seeks to simultaneously maximize the expectation values of the matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta}
, corresponding for instance to distinct forces or torques on one or multiple objects. We define the deficits from utopia values (highest eigenvalues) for a given input state
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}
as

\epsilon_{\alpha}=\lambda_{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha})-\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}},\quad\epsilon_{\beta}=\lambda_{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta})-\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta}}.

(22)

A direct application of the Schrödinger-Robertson uncertainty relation
Schrödinger 1999
,
combined with the Bhatia-Davis inequality
Bhatia and Davis 2000
,
yields the following bound:

\epsilon_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\beta}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{4}|\langle[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta}]\rangle|^{2}+\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta})^{2}}{(\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}}-\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}))(\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta}}-\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta}))}.

(23)

Here, the symmetrized covariance is

\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}-\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta}-\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta}}\}\rangle,

(24)

and
[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}]
and
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}\}
denote the commutator and anti-commutator, respectively. The right-hand side of
Eq.

23
cannot vanish unless the expectation value of the commutator vanishes and the two GWS matrices are uncorrelated in the chosen input state. In particular, the case
\epsilon_{\alpha}=\epsilon_{\beta}=0
corresponds to the situation where the two matrices share the same eigenstate associated with their respective maximal eigenvalues, which is very non-generic. This inequality highlights that the two objectives cannot be simultaneously optimized: reducing the deficit for one inevitably increases the other, which is precisely the trade-off structure captured by the Pareto front.

Constrained optimization—

Another interesting problem is the one of maximizing the expectation value of a given
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
while enforcing

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}

\displaystyle\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}}

(25)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\beta_{i}}}\triangleright_{i}b_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N_{c}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{s}}_{+}=1\,,

where “
\triangleright
” can signify “=”, “
\leq
” or “
\geq
”. This is known as a complex-valued homogeneous quadratically constrained quadratic program, and is generally an NP-hard problem for
N_{c}>2

Huang and Palomar 2010
;
Luo et al. 2010

Discussion—

We have shown that radiation forces in complex media can be optimized through the Generalized Wigner–Smith matrix: its extremal eigenstates maximize force or torque projections on a single object, while in the multi-object case they realize Pareto-optimal trade-offs among competing objectives. Together with a realistic demonstration of constrained optimization of objects’ motion along targeted directions, our Letter establishes a unified framework across acoustics and electromagnetism, and provides a principled route to coordinated manipulation in strongly scattering environments. In addition, the uncertainty relation of
Eq.

23
quantifies the fundamental trade-offs that emerge when simultaneously optimizing forces or torque components. From the present study, the state-of-the-art strategies of Horodynski et al.
Horodynski et al. 2020
(superposition of two torque eigenstates to suppress unwanted linear forces) and Orazbayev et al.
Orazbayev et al. 2024
(separate optimization of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta x}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta y}
instead of diagonalizing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}
) can be seen as important first steps, but they may fail or become suboptimal in a general situation; the present framework unifies and extends these ideas, providing a systematic route to the truly optimal radiation force control.

Beyond fundamental interest, our results may contribute to the development of in-vivo precision therapy and drug delivery, where navigation through deep tissues requires controlling radiation forces in multiply scattering media
Del Campo Fonseca and Ahmed 2024
;
Ghanem et al. 2020
;
Lo et al. 2021
;
Del Campo Fonseca et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Medany et al. 2025
;
Burstow and Pouliopoulos 2025
. We also note interesting connections between our findings and a recent preprint
Li et al. 2025
, which investigates the simultaneous control of transmission, reflection and absorption. Our Letter strongly suggests that the notion of Pareto-optimality for multi-objective wavefront shaping introduced here could offer a general framework that naturally extends from optimal radiation forces and torques to other joint-control scenarios such as those considered in Ref.
Li et al. 2025
.

Derivation details and electromagnetic equivalent of
Eq.

13
—

For a given physical state
a
,
Eq.

8
reads in acoustics

P(a)\equiv-\int_{A}(p_{1a}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1a}^{*}+p_{1a}^{*}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1a})\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{u}_{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{i}_{a}+\mathbf{i}_{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{a}.

(26)

By extension, it is possible to compose any other physical state
a\pm b
or
a\pm\mathrm{j}b
. Using the linearity of
p_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}
, one can verify the so-called polarization identity
Blanchard and Brüning 2015
:

\frac{P(a+b)+\mathrm{j}P(a+\mathrm{j}b)-[P(a-b)+\mathrm{j}P(a-\mathrm{j}b)]}{4}=-\int_{A}(p_{1a}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1b}^{*}+p_{1b}^{*}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1a})\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{u}_{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{i}_{a}+\mathbf{i}_{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}_{a}.

(27)

Taking the complex conjugate of
Eq.

27
for the states
a\rightarrow(p_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}})
and
b\rightarrow(\delta p_{1},\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}})
, we obtain

-\int_{A}(p_{1}^{*}\mathbf{\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}}+\delta p_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{*})\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}.

(28)

Applying the divergence theorem to the volume integral of
Eq.

12
and combining with
Eq.

28
directly leads to
Eq.

13
.

In electromagnetism, Maxwell-Faraday and -Ampère laws in frequency domain give

\displaystyle\nabla\times\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}=-\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}(\mu\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}})

(29a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}=\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}(\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}})

(29b)

and

\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot&(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}^{*}\times\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}+\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}^{*})\\
&=\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot(|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\varepsilon+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\mu),\end{split}

(30)

from which
Eq.

30
gives

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=\mathrm{j}\omega\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\cdot\int_{V}(|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\varepsilon+|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}|^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\mu)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V.

(31)

This is completely analogous to the acoustic case and yields the same end result.

Optimal torque—

Rotations enter the variational identities
Eqs.

13
and
31
through the generator of infinitesimal rigid rotations: a small angle
\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
about a pivot point
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0}
moves each material point by
\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}=\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}\times(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0})
. This gives the parametric derivative
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}=-(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0})\times\nabla
. Defining the time-averaged torque
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0})
as

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0})=\int_{V}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0})\times\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{V},

(32)

one obtains

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0})\cdot\delta\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=-\frac{\mathrm{j}}{4\omega}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{i}}^{\dagger}\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}).

(33)

Then, in analogy with the linear force case, the GWS reads

\frac{1}{\omega}\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}},

(34)

and

\frac{1}{\omega}\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{\text{tot}}}^{(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0,i})}}=\frac{1}{|\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta_{\text{tot}}}}|}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d}}|\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{i}|\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{0,i})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}}_{i},

(35)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{n}}}=\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}/|\delta\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}|
. Interestingly, for axisymmetric objects around
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
(such as the discs in
Fig.

2
),
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
, and no input state can transfer angular momentum to the objects. This counter-intuitive result, holding in general for any lossless scattering, was already discovered in both acoustics and electromagnetism via other methods, see for example Refs.
Toftul et al. 2025
;
Marston and Crichton 1984
;
Zhang and Marston 2011
;
Silva et al. 2012
;
Smagin et al. 2024

Chaumet and Rahmani 2009

I.1
Statistics of expectation values

The probability density of expectation values of any Hermitian matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
over some random input states forms a unimodal non-negative B-spline (also called an M-spline) of degree
N-2
, with knots corresponding to the eigenvalues of the matrix:

f_{\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}}}(\xi)=(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\max(0,\lambda_{i}-\xi)^{N-2}}{\prod\limits_{j\neq i}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})}.

(36)

Examples of this distribution for some random Hermitian matrices are given in
Fig.

3
.

Figure 3:
Probability density function of expectation values of Hermitian matrices of size
N
. Comparison between
10^{5}
random input states (in grey) and the theoretical M-spline (black curves).

The expected value of
\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}}
is

\mathbb{E}(\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}})=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha})}{N},

(37)

and the variance is

\operatorname{Var}(\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}})=\frac{\dfrac{\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{2})}{N}-(\dfrac{\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha})}{N})^{2}}{N+1}\leq\frac{\max(|\lambda_{1}|,|\lambda_{N}|)^{2}}{N+1},

(38)

which goes to zero for large
N
, since
|\lambda_{1}|
and
|\lambda_{N}|
are physically bounded. The probability that the expectation value on a random input state approaches the largest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
up to a small fixed parameter
\epsilon
is

\mathbb{P}(\innerproduct*{[}{[}1]{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}}>\lambda_{N}-\epsilon)=\frac{\epsilon^{N-1}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N-1}(\lambda_{N}-\lambda_{i})}\leq(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda_{N}-\lambda_{N-1}})^{N-1},\quad\epsilon<\lambda_{N}-\lambda_{N-1}.

(39)

The upper bound goes to zero for increasing
N
, if

\lim_{N\to+\infty}N(1-\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda_{N}-\lambda_{N-1}})=+\infty,

(40)

which is likely to hold, except if the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
shrinks to the top (i.e.,
\lambda_{N-1}\to\lambda_{N}
) sufficiently fast. We conjecture that this is unexpected without any symmetry in the system. It is worth noting that the exact statistics of the distributions of eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
have been studied by Brouwer, Frahm and Beenakker
Brouwer et al. 1997
, which may provide a good basis for a more in-depth treatment of this likelihood. Analogue discussions were recently performed in the case of the transmissivity distribution by Wang and Guo
Wang and Guo 2025
.
