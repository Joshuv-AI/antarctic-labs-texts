The paper is devoted to the classification problem of associative algebras over any base field. We provide a list of three-dimensional associative algebras with canonical representatives of the isomorphism classes over a base field of the characteristic is not two and three. Comparisons of our lists with the most recent lists over the field of complex numbers in dimensions three, with some comments, are given.



1.
Introduction

The problem of classification of finite-dimensional algebras is one the challenging problems in algebra up to date. We focus on the class of associative algebras. First attempts to classify these algebras were made at the end of 19-th Century by B. Pierce.
The theory of finite-dimensional associative algebras is one of the ancient area of the modern algebra. It originates primarily from works of W.R. Hamilton, who discovered the famous quaternions, and A. Cayley, who developed the theory of matrices. Later the structural theory of finite dimensional associative algebras have been treated by a number of mathematicians, notably B. Pierce, C.S. Pierce, W.K. Clifford, K. Weierstrass, R. Dedekind, C. Jordan, F.G. Frobenius.

A new era in the development of the theory of finite-dimensional associative algebras begun due to works of J. Wedderburn, who obtained the fundamental results of this theory: description of the structure of semisimple algebras over a field, a theorem on the lifting of the quotient by the radical, the theorem on the commutativity of finite division rings, and others. Then the baton was taken over by German school of algebraists, headed by E. Noether, E. Artin, R. Brouwer, the theory of semisimple algebras has found its modern form, and most of the results have been extended to some types of rings.

Further development of the theory of associative algebras was in the 80-s of the last century, when many open problems, remaining unsolved since 30-s, have been solved.
Whatever, time by time the researchers turn to the classification problem of associative algebras again and over again due to a wide applications and relations of this class with other classes of algebras. Up to a complete classification or an universal method of the classification in any fixed dimension even over the field of complex numbers is still far away.

The classification problem of associative algebras can be reduced to the classification problem of semisimple and nilpotent associative algebras.

Recall that an associative algebra (without unit)
\mathbb{A}
is said to be nilpotent if
\mathbb{A}^{m}=0
for some natural
m
(i.e.,
a_{1}\cdot a_{2}\cdot...\cdot a_{m}=0
for any
a_{i}\in\mathbb{A}).
The radical
Rad\ \mathbb{A}
of an algebra
\mathbb{A}
is the largest nilpotent ideal of
\mathbb{A}
(containing all nilpotent ideals of the algebra). The quotient
\mathbb{A}/Rad\ \mathbb{A}
is semisimple, i.e., it has a zero radical.

The next two theorems are basis of the structural theory of semisimple associative algebras.

Theorem 1
.

(Wedderburn-Artin)
Any finite-dimensional semisimple complex associative algebra
\mathbb{A}
is uniquely decomposed into a direct sum of a number of simple algebras:

\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{B}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{2}\oplus...\oplus\mathbb{B}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}.

Recall that an algebra is simple if it has no nontrivial two-sided ideal.

Theorem 2
.

Any finite-dimensional simple complex associative algebra
\mathbb{A}
is isomorphic to the algebra of matrices
M_{n}(D)
over a division ring
D,
the number
n
and the division ring
D
are uniquely determined by the algebra
\mathbb{A}.

These theorems give a complete description of semisimple algebras. At the same time on the structure of nonsemisimple algebras (nilpotent part), not much is known, even over an algebraically closed field.

Many attempts have been made on complete classification of low-dimensional unital associative algebras and a number of lists have been published.
To mention just a few: the classification in dimensions up to four was obtained in 1935 by G. Scorza
[
9
]

and reproved by P. Gabriel by means of quivers in 1974
[
4
]
. In
[
2
,
3
]
A. Fialowski, M. Penkava and M. Phillipson provided a list of three-dimensional complex associative algebras studying their deformations. G. Mazzola
[
7
]
gave a list in dimension five in studying the geometric classification and a list in dimension six belongs M. Goze and
A. Makhlouf
[
5
]
. These all were happened over the field of complex numbers.

To start this project was invoked due to
[
6
]
, where the authors claim to obtain a complete classification of three-dimensional associative algebras over
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
. Having a result of
[
1
]
on a complete classification of two-dimensional algebras over any base field in our hand we decided to apply it to the classification of three-dimensional associative algebras over any base field implementing a method calling “extension method”. The core of the method is as follows: we fix a two-dimensional subalgebra of the three-dimensional associative algebra is supposed to get and write its matrix of structure constants. Taking its structure constants as unknowns and subjecting the associativity condition we get a system of equations. It seemed the systems of equations appeared in each fixed subalgebra case can be successfully solved and as a result we obtain a redundant, in general, list of algebras with the fixed earlier subalgebra. The list of such two-dimensional subalgebras over any base field is available due to a classification result obtained earlier in
[
8
]
. To make the list of three-dimensional algebras irredundant we use the automorphism groups of two-dimensional associative algebras over any base field from
[
8
]
. Note that the both to produce three-dimensional algebras and make the lists obtained irredundent seemed to be a quite computational therefore we had to use Maple software for such computations.

2.
Preliminaries

Definition 1
.

A vector space
\mathbb{V}
over a field
\mathbb{F}
equipped with a function

\cdot:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}

((\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y})\mapsto\mathrm{x}\cdot\mathrm{y})
such that

(\alpha\mathrm{x}+\beta\mathrm{y})\cdot\mathrm{z}=\alpha(\mathrm{x}\cdot\mathrm{z})+\beta(\mathrm{y}\cdot\mathrm{z}),\ \ \mathrm{z}\cdot(\alpha\mathrm{x}+\beta\mathrm{y})=\alpha(\mathrm{z}\cdot\mathrm{x})+\beta(\mathrm{z}\cdot\mathrm{y}),

whenever
\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y},\mathrm{z}\in\mathbb{V}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}
, is said to be an
algebra
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{V},\cdot)
.

Definition 2
.

Two algebras
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{V},\cdot_{\mathbb{A}})
and
\mathbb{B}=(\mathbb{V},\cdot_{\mathbb{B}})
are called isomorphic
if there is an invertible linear map
f:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}

such that

f(\mathrm{x}\cdot_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{y})=f(\mathrm{x})\cdot_{\mathbb{B}}f(\mathrm{y}),

where
\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}\in\mathbb{A}.

Definition 3
.

An invertible linear map
f:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
is said to be an automorphism if

f(\mathrm{x}\cdot\mathrm{y})=f(\mathrm{x})\cdot f(\mathrm{y}),

whenever

\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}\in\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{V},\cdot).

The set of all automorphisms of an algebra
\mathbb{A}
forms a group with respect to the composition operation and it is denoted by
Aut(\mathbb{A}).

Let
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{V},\cdot)
be an
n
-dimensional algebra over
\mathbb{F}
and

\mathbf{e}=(\mathrm{e}_{1},\mathrm{e}_{2},...,\mathrm{e}_{n})
be its basis. Then the bilinear map
\cdot:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\longrightarrow\mathbb{V}
is represented by a
n\times n^{2}
matrix (called the matrix of structure constant, shortly MSC)

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccccc}a_{11}^{1}&a_{12}^{1}&...&a_{1n}^{1}&a_{21}^{1}&a_{22}^{1}&...&a_{2n}^{1}&...&a_{n1}^{1}&a_{n2}^{1}&...&a_{nn}^{1}\\
a_{11}^{2}&a_{12}^{2}&...&a_{1n}^{2}&a_{21}^{2}&a_{22}^{2}&...&a_{2n}^{2}&...&a_{n1}^{2}&a_{n2}^{2}&...&a_{nn}^{2}\\
...&...&...&...&...&...&...&...&...&...&...&...&...\\
a_{11}^{n}&a_{12}^{n}&...&a_{1n}^{n}&a_{21}^{n}&a_{22}^{n}&...&a_{2n}^{n}&...&a_{n1}^{n}&a_{n2}^{n}&...&a_{nn}^{n}\end{array}\right)

as follows:

\mathrm{e}_{i}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{j}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}a_{ij}^{k}\mathrm{e}_{k},\ \mbox{where}\ i,j=1,2,...,n.

This means that if a basis
\mathbf{e}
is fixed then the algebra can be represented as a
(n\times n^{2})
matrix
A\in M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F})
. Further once a basis is fixed we
do not make a difference between the algebra

\mathbb{A}
and its MSC
A
, and work with matrices
A
.

The product on
\mathbb{A}
with respect to the basis
\mathbf{e}
is written as below

(2.1)

\mathrm{x}\cdot\mathrm{y}=\mathbf{e}A(x\otimes y)

for any
\mathrm{x}=\mathbf{e}x,\mathrm{y}=\mathbf{e}y,

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})^{T},
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{n})^{T}
are column coordinate vectors of
\mathrm{x}
and
\mathrm{y},
respectively,
x\otimes y
is the tensor (Kronecker)
product of
x
and
y
. Now and onward for the product “
\mathrm{x}\cdot\mathrm{y}
” on
\mathbb{A}
we use the juxtaposition “
\mathrm{x}\mathrm{y}
”.

Definition 4
.

An algebra
(\mathbb{A},\cdot)
is said to be associative if
for all
\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y},\mathrm{z}\in\mathbb{A}
the following axiom is satisfied

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{rll}(\mathrm{x}\mathrm{y})\mathrm{z}&=&\mathrm{x}(\mathrm{y}\mathrm{z}).\end{array}

Note the the condition (
2.2
) in terms of MSC
A
can be wriitten as follows

\mathbf{e}A(A(x\otimes y)\otimes z))=\mathbf{e}A(x\otimes A(y\otimes z)),

i.e., an algebra
\mathbb{A}
with MSC
A
is associative if and only if

(2.3)

\begin{array}[]{ccc}A(A\otimes I)&=&A(I\otimes A),\end{array}

where
I
is
n\times n
identity matrix.

Note that in terms of MSC Definition
2
can be rewritten as

(2.4)

gB=Ag^{\otimes 2}\Longleftrightarrow B=g^{-1}Ag^{\otimes 2}

and it produces a linear representation of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
on
M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F})
as follows

(2.5)

\rho:(GL_{n}(\mathbb{F}),M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F}))\longrightarrow M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F}):\ \ \rho(g,A)=g^{-1}Ag^{\otimes 2}.

For
A\in M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F})
we introduce the vectors

Tr_{1}(A)=\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j1}^{j},\ \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j2}^{j},...,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{jn}^{j}\right),\ \ Tr_{2}(A)=\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{1j}^{j},\ \sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{2j}^{j},...,\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{nj}^{j}\right).

It is not hard to see that

(2.6)

Tr_{1}(gA(g^{-1})^{\otimes 2})=Tr_{1}(A)g^{-1},\ \ Tr_{2}(gA(g^{-1})^{\otimes 2})=Tr_{2}(A)g^{-1}.

In particular, if
\lambda Tr_{1}(A)=Tr_{2}(A)
is true then
\lambda Tr_{1}(gA(g^{-1})^{\otimes 2})=Tr_{2}(gA(g^{-1})^{\otimes 2})
is valid as well.
Therefore, we split
M(n)=M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F})
into the following disjoint invariant subsets:

(1)

M_{2}(n)=\left\{A\in M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F}):\ \ \{Tr_{1}(A),\ Tr_{2}(A)\}\ \mbox{is linearly independent}\right\};

(2)

M_{1,\lambda}(n)=\left\{A\in M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F}):\ \ \lambda Tr_{1}(A)=Tr_{2}(A)\ \mbox{and non of}\ Tr_{1}(A),Tr_{2}(A)\ \mbox{is zero}\right\};

(3)

M_{1,0}(n)=\left\{A\in M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F}):\ \ Tr_{1}(A)\ \mbox{is nonzero and}\ Tr_{2}(A)\ \mbox{is zero }\right\};

(4)

M_{1,\infty}(n)=\left\{A\in M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F}):\ Tr_{1}(A)\ \mbox{is zero and}\ Tr_{2}(A)\ \mbox{is nonzero}\right\};

(5)

M_{0}(n)=\left\{A\in M_{n\times n^{2}}(\mathbb{F}):\ \mbox{the both}\ Tr_{1}(A),Tr_{2}(A)\ \mbox{are zero}\right\}.

Evidently, each of these subsets is invariant under the above action of
\rho
and some of
M_{1,\lambda}(n)
may be empty as well.

Let now turn back to three-dimensional case and

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}&a_{7}&a_{8}&a_{9}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}&b_{4}&b_{5}&b_{6}&b_{7}&b_{8}&b_{9}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}&c_{4}&c_{5}&c_{6}&c_{7}&c_{8}&c_{9}\end{pmatrix}

be MSC. Then the trace vectors
Tr_{1}
and
Tr_{2}
are read as follows:

Tr_{1}=(a_{1}+b_{4}+c_{7},a_{2}+b_{5}+c_{8},a_{3}+b_{7}+c_{9}),\ \ Tr_{2}=(a_{1}+b_{2}+c_{3},a_{4}+b_{5}+c_{6},a_{7}+b_{8}+c_{9}).

5.
Sketch of the extension method

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a two-dimensional associative algebra and

As(2)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}\\
\beta_{1}&\beta_{2}&\beta_{3}&\beta_{4}\end{array}\right)

be its MSC on a basis
\mathbf{e}=\left(\mathrm{e}_{1},\mathrm{e}_{2}\right)
.
Then three-dimensional associative algebra with a subalgebra
\mathbb{A}
on the extended basis
\mathbf{e}=\left(\mathrm{e}_{1},\mathrm{e}_{2},\mathrm{e}_{3}\right)
can be written as follows:

As(3)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&x_{3}&\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}&x_{6}&x_{7}&x_{8}&x_{9}\\
\beta_{1}&\beta_{2}&y_{3}&\beta_{3}&\beta_{4}&y_{6}&y_{7}&y_{8}&y_{9}\\
0&0&z_{3}&0&0&z_{6}&z_{7}&z_{8}&z_{9}\end{pmatrix}.

Now we impose the condition (
2.3
) to
As(3)
and get a system of equations with respect to
x_{i},y_{j},z_{k}
, where
i,j,k=3,6,...,9
and
\alpha_{s},\beta_{t},
being known, where
s,t=1,2,3,4
. The two-dimensional subalgebra
As_{2}
we take a trivial algebra and the algebras from the lists of Theorem
3
.
Now the steps we have to go over are as follows:

•

Solve the system of equations with respect to
x_{i},y_{j},z_{k}
, where
i,j,k=3,6,...,9
and get a set of three-dimensional algebras with the two-dimensional subalgebra chosen;

•

Split the set obtained according to the trace vectors
Tr_{1}
and
Tr_{2}
;

•

Check the isomorphisms within each of the groups above and remove redundant, if any;

•

Form the list of three-dimensional algebras.

The detailed calculations will be given in a separate paper.
