Topological semimetals for advanced node interconnects
Authors: Seo Hyun Kim, Jieun Seo, Jaewoon Koo, Jiwon Chang, Gangtae Jin, Hyeuk Jin Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural sciences; Physics; Quantum physicsSubject areas: Natural sciences, Physics, Quantum physics

Main text

The continuous miniaturization of transistors has significantly advanced semiconductor technology, enabling faster and more efficient integrated circuits (ICs). However, this downscaling introduces significant challenges for the metal interconnects that connect billions of transistors. As interconnect dimensions shrink, their resistance increases due to a reduction in cross-sectional area, causing Resistive-Capacitive (RC) delay and power dissipation. Particularly in traditional materials like copper, electron scattering at surfaces and grain boundaries exacerbates this issue, thereby posing a major limitation to the further downscaling of ICs and setting a physical limit to their miniaturization. In this article, we present the need to explore ultra-low resistivity materials capable of maintaining high conductivity even at extremely narrow dimensions and describe topological semimetals (TSMs) as promising candidates that could outperform traditional copper interconnects. Topological materials confined to one dimension hold great promise for low-dissipation interconnects and have the potential to transform current computing technologies. To do so, continued research to advance their synthesis and enhance the properties of topological nanowires is essential.

In ICs, interconnects are the nanometers-wide metal wires that electrically connect billions of transistors, enabling the transfer of signals and distribution of electric power.1 As the feature size of transistors continues to shrink following Moore’s law, the dimensions of the interconnects must also decrease. As the back-end-of-line (BEOL) interconnect of ICs becomes narrower, the resulting increase in electrical resistance significantly heightens signal delay and energy consumption. This creates critical bottlenecks in IC performance and power efficiency, contributing to a substantially increasing portion of global energy consumption.2 Therefore, to meet the rapidly growing demand for high-performance semiconductors, especially for artificial intelligence servers, it is crucial to focus on improving interconnect materials. This will permit the continuous downscaling of ICs and result in electronic devices with higher performance and lower power consumption.

In the early days of semiconductor technology, the 1960s, aluminum (Al) was introduced for interconnects because of its good conductivity, ease of deposition, and the ability to form ohmic contacts with silicon.3 However, as technology nodes advanced, the high resistivity and electromigration reliability of Al became problematic.4 Electron migration refers to the transport of metal atoms within a conductor due to momentum transfer. Electromigration increases as the dimensions of the IC decrease, potentially leading to electric shorts in the device.5 This led to the adoption of copper (Cu), which not only can withstand higher current densities than aluminum while maintaining similar electromigration reliability but also has lower resistivity and better scalability.6

Copper has been used as the primary metal material since IBM developed and introduced the dual damascene process in the 1990s. Current copper-based BEOL technology has a complex interconnect network with multiple stacked horizontal layers of copper lines, dielectrics, and a Ta–TaN liner-barrier bilayer inserted between them. Each layer is connected vertically through “vias” that are filled with metal. The liner-barrier layers are necessary to suppress electromigration because of copper’s inherent tendency to diffuse into the dielectric. However, in interconnects with extremely small dimensions, the area occupied by these layers has a significant impact on overall BEOL efficiency.7,8,9 The TaN/Ta stack needs to be at least 3–4 nm thick to function effectively as a barrier and liner.10 For example, in a 10 nm wide interconnect, the copper occupies only about 30% of the total interconnect cross-sectional area due to the thickness of these layers.11 In addition, the liner-barrier layer’s own high resistance contributes to the increase in overall resistance. The high resistivity of current Cu interconnects can account for up to 35% of total signal delays and 50% of power dissipation in computer chips12 (Figure 1A).

The other challenge for interconnects is the increased resistivity of metal wires as their width decreases below the bulk electron mean free path (λ). In narrow wires, electrons also scatter at surfaces and grain boundaries, further increasing resistivity beyond that of the bulk metal. This additional scattering leads to a significant rise in resistivity compared to the bulk metal.1 Figure 1B indicates that the resistivity increases sharply when the linewidth of the Cu interconnect line is below the 10 nm scale and becomes much larger than the bulk resistance. The blue arrows show how electron scattering at the surface and grain boundaries and the 3-nm-thick liner contribute additional resistivity.15

To address the inherent challenges of advancing to sub-10 nm technical nodes, various studies are exploring ways of identifying materials that can minimize the barrier-liner layer and are less affected by grain boundary scattering and surface scattering. Recently, Ru and Co have been considered as attractive alternatives for next-generation interconnect materials. This is because the electron mean free path in Ru (6.6 nm) and Co (11.8 nm) is shorter than that of Cu (39.9 nm), which is predicted to result in less electron scattering from surfaces and grain boundaries at narrow interconnect dimensions.9 These materials are also promising because they are highly conductive and have good compatibility with complementary metal-oxide semiconductor. They have actually been reported to exhibit lower effective resistances than Cu at and below 10 nm because their size effect on resistivity is smaller, resulting in improved electromigration reliability.16 For example, Intel has introduced Co as an interconnect metal at the 10 nm technology node and demonstrated lower line resistance than Cu lines (with TaN barrier layers).17,18 However, conventional metals such as Co and Ru still experience surface scattering at the nanoscale, which makes it necessary to continue to explore alternative interconnect metals19 (Figure 1C).

What is the next candidate for nanoscale interconnect metals? A topological material could be a potential option. The topologically protected surface states lead to the reduced scattering and hence could potentially reduce resistivity at the nanoscale (Figure 2A). A topological insulator (TI) was suggested as a candidate interconnect metal because of its unique electronic properties. However, the carrier density in TIs is too low for interconnect application. Bi2Se3 nanoribbons, for example, suffer significantly decreased mobility at room temperature due to electron-phonon interactions, which affect not only the surface states but also the mobility of the bulk states.20 Furthermore, interconnect applications require low resistivity and a reasonably high carrier density, comparable to Cu. However, most TIs and topological crystalline insulators (TCIs) do not meet these requirements. Unlike TIs or TCIs, TSMs have a significant amount of bulk carriers as well as topological surface states at the Fermi level. This feature has attracted significant interest for interconnect applications. Several TSMs, like molybdenum phosphide (MoP), molybdenum ditelluride (MoTe2), tungsten carbide (WC), and tungsten diphosphide (WP2), exhibit both high carrier densities and low resistivity values. This has restored the initial potential of topological materials as interconnects with minimal dissipation at compact dimensions.

MoP topological metal nanowires have been demonstrated as a breakthrough material for interconnect applications and exhibited better dimensional scaling of resistivity than Cu (with a TaN liner) and Ru.21 Recent studies have calculated the width-dependent resistivities of MoP, tungsten phosphide (WP), and WP₂ square wires to evaluate electron scattering at surfaces.22 To consider surface scattering using the Fuchs-Sondheimer model, the a and c axes were chosen as the transport directions for WP and WP₂, and along the c axis for MoP, due to their anisotropic variations in Fermi surfaces. Based on the assumed calculations with the specularity parameter set to p = 0,30 MoP was expected to have the most promising performance in the nanoscale regime, when compared to the resistivities of Cu wires, both with a 2 nm liner. Further, the calculated mean free paths of WP (3.15 nm) and WP₂ (4.33 nm) were significantly smaller than the observed grain sizes, presumably minimizing the impact of grain boundary scattering on the dimensional scaling of resistivity for WP and α-WP₂. However, the bulk resistivity of WP and WP₂ is higher than that of Cu, so these materials do not appear promising as low-resistance interconnects without additional contributions from topological surface states.

Indeed, several recent studies on nanostructured TSMs have shown promising results. The Weyl semimetal niobium arsenide (NbAs) showed a decrease in resistivity, from 35 μΩ cm in bulk crystals to 3 μΩ cm in nanoribbons, suggesting that a large fraction of transport takes place through the topological surface states.31 Similarly, recent theoretical results have predicted that the conduction of TSM cobalt monosilicide (CoSi) would be dominated by its topological surface states, which is expected to make the line resistance of CoSi significantly lower than that of Cu in a 5-nm technology node.27,32,33 For a clear comparison, recent studies have documented the resistivity values of selected TSM nanowire and thin film structures, which have been compared to their bulk counterparts and benchmarked against traditional materials such as copper, cobalt, and ruthenium, as shown in Figure 2B (experimental value) and 2C (simulation results), respectively. The results indicate the potential of TSM in maintaining or even reducing resistivity at very small dimensions.

What types of research efforts should be prioritized to realize low-dissipation topological nanomaterial interconnects? First, we need to improve current synthesis methods. High-throughput, precision synthesis of target nanomaterials with controlled morphology, dimensions, crystallinity, and doping is essential. To study various promising topological nanomaterials, the synthesis technique needs to be simple and adaptable for diverse materials. One such method is chemical vapor deposition (CVD), which produces highly crystalline nanostructures. However, it lacks precise control over nanostructure dimensions and morphology, making it difficult to achieve desired properties. An effective engineering approach for large-scale precision synthesis involves top-down fabrication using standard lithography techniques, and reactive ion etching to pattern thin films of topological materials into nanowires, which would ensure controlled and accurate production. However, the top-down approach can result in potential surface roughness during etching as shown in previous research,34,35 which may degrade the desirable transport properties of topological nanostructures.36

To address this issue, a recent approach involves chemical conversion using the nanoconfinement effect, which enables precise size manipulation at the nanoscale. This technique not only enhances control over the size and dimensions of nanomaterials but also significantly affects the quantitative analysis of the intrinsic contributions of the nanostructure size effect, posing a significant challenge.21,22,37 Materials are transformed within the confined dimensions of templates, converting transition metal oxide or chalcogenide compounds (like sulfides, selenides, and tellurides) into different chalcogenides, phosphides, or oxides. The confined environment during the chemical conversion can affect reaction kinetics and thermodynamics because of surface energy considerations and dimensional confinement.22 Consequently, the chemical conversion process can lead to distinct nanocrystalline structures with unique properties compared to their bulk counterparts. Recent studies have explored controlling size and crystallinity using the nanoconfinement effect with chemical conversion.21,22,23,38 These methods for controlling and manipulating the nanostructures could create new opportunities for application in electronic devices.

In another recent nanofabrication advance, the thermomechanical nanomolding (TMNM) of ordered phases has shown promise, providing precise control over shape and enabling efficient screening like a top-down approach, but without the defects from lithography.39,40 A prime example of TMNM’s efficacy was its application in the fabrication of Mo4P3 nanowires using a bulk MoP feedstock.40 This technique was used to successfully fabricate single crystal nanowires of Mo4P3, which is the first example with these materials. Additionally, combining data-driven and calculation-based screening strategies might accelerate the synthesis process. These techniques allow for the prediction and selection of topological materials appropriate for specific applications. Moreover, based on synthesis results focused on material properties, developing large-area processes will be essential. Unlike conventional metals, topological metals consist of intermetallic compounds or chemical compounds, requiring efforts that can advance existing semiconductor manufacturing methods such as single-atom-based sputtering, atomic layer deposition, physical vapor deposition, and CVD to deposit high-quality compounds.

Second, the synthesis efforts need to be followed by a thorough characterization of transport properties, not only of the topological surface states but also of the bulk states, as a function of various material parameters. For transport studies of the topological surface states, merely demonstrating their presence in transport signals is not sufficient. We must understand what fraction of transport signals is due to the topological surface states and what fraction is due to bulk electrons or scattering between the two electronic states. For TSMs as interconnects, transport studies should include room temperature resistivity measurements, line resistance, and carrier density as a function of the dimensions of the nanostructured TSMs and fabrication processes. A detailed understanding of the contribution of the topological surface states, or perhaps dominant bulk transport, to the overall resistance of nanostructured TSMs is required to help establish design rules for interconnects based on TSMs.

This article highlights the challenges of reducing interconnect dimensions and explores the transition from conventional metal to alternative materials like TSMs, emphasizing the need for research and possible future directions, including the synthesis and characterization needed to ensure high-performance, low-power electronic devices. Given the complexity of this field, these research efforts are multi-disciplinary, requiring closely integrated collaborations between materials engineers, condensed matter experimental and theoretical physicists, and electrical engineers. The potential for utilizing the topological surface states of TSMs at the nanoscale depends on researchers’ collaborative efforts in material exploration and associated property investigations. This research aims to push the boundaries of nanoscale electronics and aid in developing design rules for next-generation technological advancements.
