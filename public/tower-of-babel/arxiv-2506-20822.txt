Uncovering Hidden Violent Tendencies in LLMs: A Demographic Analysis via Behavioral Vignettes
Quintin Myers, Yanjun Gao

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed for detecting and responding to violent content online, yet their ability to reason about morally ambiguous, real-world scenarios remains underexamined. We present the first study to evaluate LLMs using a validated social science instrument designed to measure human response to everyday conflict, namely the Violent Behavior Vignette Questionnaire (VBVQ). To assess potential bias, we introduce persona-based prompting that varies race, age, and geographic identity within the United States. Six LLMs developed across different geopolitical and organizational contexts are evaluated under a unified zero-shot setting. Our study reveals two key findings: (1) LLMs’ surface-level text generation often diverges from their internal preference for violent responses; (2) their violent tendencies vary across demographics, frequently contradicting established findings in criminology, social science, and psychology.

Introduction

Community violence, defined as violence between unrelated individuals
CDC 2025
, remains a major public health concern in the United States. Traditional face-to-face violence allowed culturally embedded Violence Prevention Professionals (VPPs) to intervene directly through Community Violence Intervention (CVI) programs. Yet, the shift to online platforms has made violent threats harder to detect and address, especially given the frequent disagreement over what constitutes as violent content
Lane 2018
;
Patton et al. 2013
;
Patton et al. 2016
;
Kleiner et al. 2024
;
Frey et al. 2020
. This challenge highlights an opportunity for NLP methods to support automated detection of threats, conflict, and violence in text
Patton et al. 2018
.
Large language models (LLMs) have been applied to detect violence in diverse settings, including intimate partner violence
Basile et al. 2024
, political violence
Hu et al. 2022
, and narratives from survivors
Shokri et al. 2025
.

We focus our analysis on the United States, where our team includes a sociologist actively involved in local CVI programs. The six selected counties reflect diverse population sizes, racial and ethnic compositions, political affiliations, and crime patterns. While our findings offer insight into US-based dynamics, extending this analysis to other countries would require careful attention to cultural and institutional differences.

However, prior work often overlooks the limitations of LLMs in interpreting offline context and socially nuanced language around conflict and violence, treating their outputs as reliable without sufficient scrutiny
Kleiner et al. 2024
;
Frey et al. 2020
. Most LLMs are pre-trained on open web data and preference data from human annotators, suffering from the risk of perpetuated bias
Hu et al. 2025
;
Gallegos et al. 2024
. As a result, their interpretive behavior, especially in high-stakes domains like violence prevention, is far from guaranteed to be reliable or safe.

Our work systematically examines how LLMs respond to real-world conflict scenarios, focusing on risks and embedded biases. We adapt the Violent Behavior Vignette Questionnaire (VBVQ)
Nunes et al. 2021
, a validated social science instrument consisting of 10 everyday conflict scenarios designed to assess how individuals might respond to provocation. These morally ambiguous vignettes are used to probe both surface responses and internal tendencies of LLMs, analyzed via next-token sequence likelihood. A second motivation for our study is to examine whether LLMs, by extension their developers, apply safety and moral reasoning consistently across social groups. We introduce persona settings that vary race, age, and US region to simulate different hypothetical subjects of the same conflict vignette (Figure 1). This design allows us to test whether LLM responses shift based on demographic cues, revealing potential inconsistencies in moral alignment or embedded bias.

We evaluate six LLMs from diverse organizational and geopolitical backgrounds: Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1 (Mistral-7B) and Mixtral-8x22B-Instruct-v0.1 (Mixtral-8x22B)
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2024
(Mistral AI (
France
)); DeepSeek-Distill Qwen and its 32B variants
Guo et al. 2025
(DeepSeek,
China
); and GPT-4o-mini
Hurst et al. 2024
(OpenAI,
U.S.
) and Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Meta,
U.S.
). While all models are trained on large-scale open web data, differences in national regulations and institutional norms shape how training data is curated and alignment strategies are applied. Rather than optimizing for task performance, our goal is to
probe and compare
how these LLMs reacts towards morally ambiguous conflict scenarios, under a zero-shot setting.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate LLM responses to real-world conflict scenarios using a validated social science instrument. Our findings reveal significant variation across personas and models, with some patterns contradicting to criminology findings and expectation, and raising concerns about fairness in high-stakes applications.

3
Methods

All selected LLMs are instruction-tuned, reflecting industry practices that prioritize alignment and usability by improving the models’ ability to follow prompts in a controlled and interpretable manner. In a follow-up analysis that assessed the internal confidence of the LLM, we replaced GPT-4o-mini with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct due to API limitations with GPT-4o-mini, which prevented the extraction of logarithmic probabilities at the token level.

Vignette dataset.

Vignettes in VBVQ were developed through semi-structured interviews about conflicts experienced by criminal offenders and community members in Canada
Nunes et al. 2021
. Following
Nunes et al. 2021
;
Nunes et al. 2023
, we evaluated LLMs using three response formats: two categorical and one open-ended. For the
open-ended
format, the vignette was presented directly and the model generated a free-form response. For the categorical formats, we used: (1) the
original 10 options
ranging from passive (e.g., “Ignore it”) to violent actions (e.g., “Hit, punch, kick, or tackle him”); and (2) a binned version with
three categories
:
Pacifist (pa)
,
Non-Violent (nv)
, and
Violent (vi)
. A criminologist on our team created brief (2–3 sentence) descriptions for each category, drawing on follow-up question patterns from the original study
Nunes et al. 2021
. To reduce spurious lexical correlations in LLM evaluation, we use GPT-4o to generate four paraphrased variants per human-labeled response, creating a more semantically robust and diverse reference set for each intent category.

Prompting with varying demographics.

Each input prompt appended a system instruction of the form:

“Put yourself in this situation and answer as if you are a
{
Race
}
,
{

}
from
{

We varied six racial and ethnic groups, six U.S. counties (Figure

To capture variation in model behavior, we use open-text generation with self-consistency decoding
Wang et al.

Evaluating open-text generation by semantic similarity.

He et al. 2020

Probing LLM response tendencies via sequence likelihood.

To examine internal tendencies in the LLMs’ responses, we compute the sequence likelihood of human-labeled intent category responses (
pa, nv, vi
), conditioned on each vignette input. For robustness, we use both human written response and their paraphrased variants of each category label and average their likelihoods. These likelihoods are then normalized using a softmax function to yield a probability distribution over the three intent categories. We define the Top-Rank Rate as the proportion of generations in which each class receives the highest softmax-normalized likelihood, providing a categorical summary of model preference. This allows us to assess which response type an LLM is most likely to favor in probabilistic terms, even when generation outputs appear neutral or similar on the surface. To isolate demographic effects, we calculate pairwise differences in
Prob VI
(likelihood assigned to
vi
responses) across a given demographic variable of the same input while controlling for others.

4
Results and Discussion

Overall results.
When prompting with the 10 categorical options, all LLMs consistently choose the most passive options as responses to all vignettes input, regardless of the persona. In the free-form generation setting, differences emerge: as shown in Table
1
, Mixtral-8x22B achieves the highest overall similarity to human-labeled references, followed closely by Mistral-7B and GPT-4o-mini, while both DeepSeek models perform worse across all categories. Table
2
shows that several LLMs exhibit significant variation in BERTScore across race, location, and age, with DS-Qwen models showing the strongest and most consistent demographic sensitivity, particularly for
vi
responses. In contrast, GPT-4o-mini and Mistral-7B show limited sensitivity, with only a few significant effects. On the overall ranking through sequence likelihood, DS-Qwen-32B has shows the highest tendancy towards
vi
category, while Llama3.1-8B shows the lowest on
vi
and highest tendency towards
PA
.

LLMs camouflage their internal violent tendencies.
While models appear aligned through their categorical and free-form outputs, which are often polite and nonviolent (see Fig
5
), sequence likelihood probing reveals hidden leanings toward
VI
, especially under varying demographic cues. Pairwise differences in
Prob VI
(Figure
2
) uncover demographic patterns that diverge from criminological expectations.

Notable finding#1: the age categories are opposite of expectations.
In all of the models and at all sizes, the models demonstrated that they believed the youngest group (15 years old) would choose less violent responses than the older cohorts. This contrasts with findings from criminology and developmental psychology, which suggest that adolescents are more prone to violence due to heightened risk-taking, underdeveloped self-regulation and an immature frontal cortex
Stolzenberg and D’Alessio 2008
;
Steffensmeier et al. 2025
;
Shulman et al. 2013
;
Sapolsky 2018
. This finding then suggests that the LLMs we prompted were actively trying to provide non-violent responses in case it was an actual teenager prompting them. Ostensibly, this would be to prevent violence among teenage groups. Interestingly, within the human written labels, the Qwen models show the expected pattern among age while all the other models demonstrate the same unexpected directionality. This is important to understand because of the ways models can react differently when LLMs experience a human input versus a paraphrased machine input.

Notable finding#2: the location data do not have any particular pattern.
For example,
denver,co
appeared more violent in Qwen models but less so in Llama and Mixtral. This inconsistency suggests LLMs may struggle to interpret location-based cues, likely due to limited exposure to structured crime data during training, such as the FBI’s crime data explorer
noa
.

Notable finding#3: the racial categories demonstrate a bias toward political correctness, though the larger models seem to be less prone to this bias.
In the U.S. context, criminological and sociological literature suggests a racialized bias linking
Black
and
Hispanic
individuals to violence, and
Asian
individuals to passivity due to gendered stereotypes
Alexander 2012
;
Feagin 1991
;
Chou 2015
. When LLMs depict
White
personas as most or least violent, they appear to overcorrect toward color-blind, politically correct outputs. While well-intentioned, such overcorrections risk reinforcing “positive” stereotypes that can perpetuate systemic bias
Chou and Feagin 2014
.

Outward responses of the LLMs often contradict their internal thinking. This suggests the models may be overcorrecting, even if they ultimately reflect what humans might say in a similar scenario. These guardrails are important but potentially problematic for real-world deployment. Future work should examine the gap between hidden tendencies and surface-level alignment to better ensure reliability and safety in real-world deployment.

5
Conclusion

Our analysis of LLMs in morally ambiguous scenarios reveals a gap between surface-level neutrality and latent violent preferences influenced by demographic cues. While instruction tuning curbs overt bias, sequence likelihood probing uncovers deeper disparities, suggesting LLMs may subtly shape sociocultural narratives through overcorrection or hidden bias.

Limitations

This study is limited in a few important ways. First, the use of vignettes is useful and an ethical way to address violence. However, it limits the ability for us to assess how well LLMs can detect and react to violence in real world scenarios. To improve this, the next step will be to take real world social media posts and create similar vignettes to assess the response to a real world situation. Similarly, using vignettes created by Canadian researchers only allows conflicts in a specific racial/ethnic use case. It is important to understand that the language used among youth, and particularly youth of color in the US, will be different and specific and may cause even more misunderstandings within the LLMs. Next steps will be to help create vignettes with the help of teenagers and young adults to include vignettes that utilize important slang terms to assess how this affects the LLMs’ ability to utilize specific terminology. Finally, this study is potentially limited by the training models of the LLMs. While each LLM is trained on the entire internet, what is privileged in the training may affect how the LLM understands and reacts to the situations they are presented with and how they understand the US context, specifically as it refers to race and place.

Ethical Considerations

Probing LLMs with vignettes that make up scenarios, even if they are based on real-world scenarios, is an ethical way to discover how LLMs understand violence. In this way, we are not using data from real people until it is discovered that LLMs can accurately detect violence without putting real people in potentially difficult scenarios. The outcomes of this study can help us to improve ethical use of LLMs in attempting to detect violent situations online in real time. Understanding how LLMs interpret violent situations, and improving this, will allow for LLMs to help CVI programs to utilize the "digital street" to intervene on violent situations before they become issues on the physical street.

Our findings are a systematic evaluation. At this point, we do not recommend that CVI programs use LLMs to attempt to detect violence. We do believe this is possible in the future but for the moment our findings suggest that LLMs are not yet prepared to ethically detect violence in a real world setting.

A.3
More related work on social science study for NLP methods in violence detection

Scholars in the US have only just begun to explore the centrality of the internet in violent situations
Lane 2018
;
Patton et al. 2013
;
Patton et al. 2019
;
Patton et al. 2018
;
Pyrooz et al. 2015
. This line of research began with
Patton et al. 2013
who identified "internet banging." Internet banging is a set of online behaviors by those perceived to be gang affiliated
Patton et al. 2013
. Others have found that gang members use the internet, and social media in particular, more than their non-gang affiliated counterparts
Pyrooz et al. 2015
. The activity on social media by these real and perceived gang members was extended in an ethnography by
Lane 2018
who demonstrated that violence and gang activity were increasingly started through online ’beefs’ instead of having conflicts start in person.
Lane 2018
terms this the "digital street."

Internet banging and the digital street are just the tip of understanding how social media and the internet play a role in violent interactions
Patton et al. 2013
;
Lane 2018
. Some scholars have also begun calling for more utilization of LLMs in understanding the context of violence and the digital street
Patton et al. 2018
;
Patton et al. 2020
;
Kleiner et al. 2024
;
Frey et al. 2020
. However, one concern among scholars is how well LLMs can understand the human element of violent interactions
Patton et al. 2020
;
Kleiner et al. 2024
;
Frey et al. 2020
. Specifically, scholars have called into question the ability of LLMs to understand the language, culture, and offline context of a post potentially marking something as violent when it is not
Patton et al. 2020
;
Kleiner et al. 2024
;
Frey et al. 2020
. The concern of accurate identification of violent situations is extremely important if LLMs are going to be used to improve violence intervention in the future.

With that in mind, it’s also important to note that violence is hard to measure. Unlike many things in the social world, violence can often go undetected or be as misunderstood by humans as it can be by machines. Violence is often measured by proxy, such as using crime and homicide rates to assess violence in an area
Martinez et al. 2010
;
Lee and Martinez 2009
. Another way to assess violence, and how someone might react to a violent act, in particular, is to use hypothetical vignettes
Nunes et al. 2021
. In the social sciences, vignettes are hypothetical situations that are posed to a person who is then asked to put themselves in that situation and asked how they would respond to that.
Nunes et al. 2021
and their team have developed the Violent Behavior Vignette Questionnaire (VBVQ) to assess how certain individuals would react in provocative situations. The VBVQ does not present violent scenarios but rather, scenarios that present the reader with conflicts and asks them how they would respond
Nunes et al. 2021
.

Vignettes provide an ethical way to understand whether someone would react to a conflict violently. When presented to a panel of Canadian and US adults
Nunes et al. 2023
find that adults are likely to respond to conflict violently 25 percent of the time. These vignettes and studies are especially relevant to understanding how LLMs can understand violence and conflict. By utilizing these vignettes and proposing different answers, we can compare how frequently an LLM chooses a violent reaction to how frequently a human sample does
Nunes et al. 2023
. Moreover, using vignettes in this way allows our research team to better understand how well LLMs can understand the nuances of human language and interactions
Kleiner et al. 2024
;
Frey et al. 2020
;
Patton et al. 2020
.
