Patterns of early exposure to screens by children with ASD
Authors: Takoua Brahim, Naoufel Gaddour, Hela Ben Abid, Sarra Bouslah, Asma Guedria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimTo further explore and discuss the characteristics of exposure to screens in Autism Spectrum Disorder in the Tunisian context.MethodsThis case control study explored the exposure to TV in 150 children with ASD, aged 1 to 4 years old in comparison to a comparable group of 150 typically developing children.ResultsResults showed Significant differences were observed between groups regarding the age of television viewing onset (4 vs. 14 months) and daily viewing duration (7 h vs. <1 h). Children with ASD predominantly viewed television alone, showing preference for repetitive content such as music videos, with viewing occurring throughout the day. Television exposure served as both a child-occupying strategy and a parental soothing technique, and was associated with increased autistic symptomatology.ConclusionWhile acknowledging methodological limitations, this study suggests that the impact of early intensive screen exposure warrants further investigation in ASD populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-025-00318-y.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by abnormalities in communication and stereotypic pattern of behaviors and rituals [1]. Many reports arising from clinical practice describe an intensive exposure to screens, mainly television, during the first 2 years of life, and some authors proposed a possible impact of this exposure and ASD developmental trajectories and symptom severity [2]. In our daily clinical practice, parents frequently report using television as a strategy to calm distressed children (subsequently diagnosed with ASD) or to occupy them during periods when parental attention is directed elsewhere. Despite clinicians’ explanations of established neurobiological mechanisms underlying ASD, many parents express guilt regarding early screen exposure. This underscores the need for evidence-based research to inform clinical guidance on this phenomenon.

Few studies have examined screen use among children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). In a study conducted in Thailand, involving 54 children with ASD and 84 typically developing controls, Chonchaiya and Pruksananonda (2011) found that children with ASD watched television three times longer than the control group, with exposure beginning approximately six months earlier [3]. Similarly, an earlier investigation of 84 children with ASD revealed that these children spent two to three times more time watching television or videos compared to engaging in any other recreational activity [4].

A controversial study by Waldman et al. (2006) examined potential correlations between autism prevalence and television exposure, suggesting a possible causal relationship based on increased autism incidence following television introduction in US households and in regions with higher precipitation rates [5]. More recently, Kushima et al. (2022) demonstrated a statistically significant association between prolonged screen time exposure at 12 months of age and increased likelihood of ASD diagnosis at 36 months, with this relationship being particularly pronounced among boys [6].

More broadly, and even if screens cannot be equated with inanimate objects, several authors have highlighted the documented preference of children with ASD for objects over social interactions as a key component of the Social Attention Hypothesis in autism [7, 8].

We conducted this study to explore early exposure to TV in children with ASD and its characteristics.

Methodology

A case-control study was conducted in the outpatient clinic of the department of Psychiatry of University Hospital Fattouma Bourguiba, Monastir, Tunisia. Over two consecutive years, we included the first 150 patients with ASD aged 1 to 4 years old, whose parents consented to participate. Parents were approached during the second follow-up appointment. We chose this moment because: (1) this second appointment is usually when the psychiatrist will confirm the diagnosis of ASD, (2) from this session, the therapeutic interventions will be planned with the child and the parents, and stopping the exposition of the child to TV screen is one of the first recommendations that will be given to parents.

After an informed consent, parents filled in the study questionnaires in the department or via phone call assisted by researchers from the team.

Control group was randomly recruited from four kindergartens in the region of Monastir. The first 150 children aged 1 to 4 years old whose parents consented to participate were included.

The diagnosis of ASD was established through a comprehensive multi-source evaluation process. This included: (1) a clinical semi-structured interview involving direct child interactions to assess social communication competencies (joint attention, imitation, pointing, and declarative symbolic play skills), language development, cognitive abilities, and behavioral disturbances; (2) a thorough developmental and clinical interview with parents; (3) review of family videos when available; and (4) collection of observational data from kindergarten settings when applicable.

All assessments were conducted by board-certified child and adolescent psychiatrists and finalized according to DSM-5 criteria. The Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS-T) was completed by the evaluating psychiatrist during sessions with the child and family to confirm the diagnosis and determine ASD severity [9].

It should be noted that most children in the ASD group attended kindergarten irregularly, as a national inclusion program for children with ASD in kindergarten settings was under development during the study period [10].

To exclude the possibility of ASD in the control group, children were screened using the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M-CHAT) [11], a high-sensitivity instrument previously translated and validated in Arabic [12].

To assess children’s television viewing habits and behaviors, we developed a structured questionnaire for the purpose of this study. The questionnaire collected: (1) general information including sociodemographic data, medical history of the child and family, and developmental milestones of the child and siblings; (2) detailed assessment of television exposure including age of initiation, duration of current and pre-12-month exposure, and characteristics of viewing patterns; evaluation of the child’s behavior during screen time; assessment of primary caregiver interactions with the child during viewing; and (3) broader family lifestyle factors. Parents were asked to provide a comprehensive chronological account of their child’s television exposure from initiation to the current age. The complete questionnaire is available as supplementary material in Tunisian Arabic (TV Exposure Questionnaire).

Questionnaires was administered to the parents of patients with ASD at the child psychiatry clinic either immediately after the end of the consultation with the attending physician or during the same week, depending on the parents’ availability and wishes. For the control group, questionnaires were administered at the kindergarten during child pickup time. Administration duration ranged from 35 to 60 min depending on parental elaboration.

Data analysis was done by the SPSS 21 for Windows software. In the descriptive part, the qualitative variables were expressed as absolute (effective) and relative (percentages) frequencies; while the quantitative variables were expressed as means and standard deviations. In the analytical part, the study of the associations between qualitative variables was carried out using the Pearson chi2 test, and if not applicable by the Fischer test. For the association between dichotomous variables and discrete variables, we used the chi2 trend test. The materiality threshold was set at 0.05.

Ethical aspects

This study was approved by Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir, University of Monastir, Tunisia. Its general aim was to improve prognosis and management of children with developmental problems. Participation in the study was conditioned by parental informed consent through a signed acceptance form including a synthetic explanation of the aim and methodology.

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Mean age of children with ASD was 31.92 months ± 6.55 [12, 45.60 months], and it was 33 ± 5.16 [15.6, 46.8] for the control group. The Male/Female ratio was 3.2 for the ASD group and 3.8 for the control group.

The type of television content consumed differed markedly between groups. Children with ASD predominantly watched repetitive video clips designed for children, particularly those broadcast on specialized Middle Eastern networks such as Toyor Al Jana and Toyor Baby. These networks feature highly repetitive programming, with the same video clip potentially airing more than five times daily (sample content available at: https://www.youtube.com/hashtag/toyorbaby, accessed April 22, 2025). To a lesser extent, children with ASD also watched news broadcasts and advertisements.

Children with ASD demonstrated a clear preference for repetitive content, including recurring melodies, theme music, Quranic recitations, and commercial jingles. This contrasted with the control group, who showed greater interest in animated cartoons and varied programming intended for general family viewing (Table 3).

The majority of households from both groups had two TV sets at home. TV set in the child’s bedroom was reported more often in the control group (89% versus 76%) but this was a non-significant difference.

Children with ASD typically viewed television while standing directly in front of the screen at distances of less than one meter. They remained alone throughout viewing sessions, displaying a quiet, absorbed demeanor often accompanied by elementary stereotyped behaviors (e.g., hand flapping, self-turning, jumping) that appeared to be stimulated by the television content. They rarely sought adult attention or interaction during viewing and frequently exhibited temper tantrums when parents attempted to turn off the television or change channels.

Visual behavior during screen time differed markedly between groups. In the ASD group, peripheral gaze toward the screen was observed in 36.7% of children (versus none in controls), repetitive eye blinking occurred in 20.4% (versus 4.7% in controls, p < 0.001), and head tilting was noted in 6.6% (versus 1.3% in controls).

A major difference was found regarding adult presence during television viewing. Significantly, children with ASD watched television alone in 92.5% of cases, while 97.3% of control group children were accompanied by someone else (p < 0.001).

External factors influencing television viewing patterns also differed between groups. Bad weather conditions were associated with increased television viewing in 19% of control group cases, whereas weather had minimal interference on viewing patterns in the ASD group (only 4.8% of cases).

Discussion

The present study investigated the frequently reported phenomenon of early and intensive television exposure among children with ASD, as consistently described by their parents in clinical settings. This case-control study, comprising 150 children with ASD and 150 typically developing controls, documented several key findings:

When compared to international recommendations regarding screen exposure in children [13, 14, 15], both parents of groups with and without ASD were far from respecting the safety rules. However, the exposure as early as less than 4 months of age in the ASD group is strikingly significant. This age of onset of exposure is not only lower than the minimum age of television exposure for all international recommendations but also earlier than what has been reported in similar studies. The study by Chonchaiya et al. (2011), targeting children with ASD with comparable age range to ours, found an age of onset of exposure of 6.44 ± 6.35 months. More recently, a Turkish study led in 2017 concluded that children aged 10 to 60 months with ASD have an earlier onset of TV viewing and watch more adult programs [16]. In general, studies of ASD children’s screen viewing habits are not very common and are mainly of interest to school-aged populations or adolescents, with a focus on internet and video gaming. As an example, among the 47 studies listed in the systematic review on media use among children and adolescents with ASD by Stiller and Mössle (2018), only 7 included preschool age children [17].

The number of hours spent watching television by infants under 1 year of age in the ASD group was 7.36 h per day ± 3.21, while this number was less than one hour per day on average for the control group. More children in the ASD group watched television continuously. Non-attendance at kindergarten may be a contributing factor to the continuous and increased exposure to TV in the ASD group. The number of hours reported in the Thai study was 4.60 ± 1.91 h per day, which is lower than our findings. Indeed, our findings are the highest even compared to findings reported in populations of children with ASD at school age or in adolescence. Shane and Albert (2008) conducted a study including 90 school-age children with ASD and found that only 23% of the population watched television for more than 3 h a day during weekends and 16% during weekdays. The study by Mazurek et al. (2012), conducted on 920 adolescents aged 13 to 18 with ASD in the USA, found that 28% of their population was described as “high users,” meaning they had more than 3 h of exposure per day [18]. Orsmond and Kuo (2011) conducted a study on 103 teenagers with ASD in the USA and found an average of 2.27 h per day [19]. Comparisons with these findings should be made carefully, as age groups are not comparable and the authors also explored other types of screen devices that we did not assess (video games, computers, tablets, or smartphones).

We did not document a salient relationship between climate and TV exposure in children with ASD, versus a slight interference in the control group, with a moderate increase in TV time during hot or rainy days. This may suggest that TD children engage in other activities outside the home when weather is suitable for these activities. Children with ASD probably have fewer outdoor activities, hence the lack of climate impact. In fact, television, and screens in general, are the main leisure activities of children with ASD. Nally et al. reported that television was the only way to soothe their children [20]. In the Shane and Albert study, only 5% of ASD children spent nearly 3 h a day in outdoor activities. According to Orsmond and Kuo, only 15% of adolescents engage in leisure activities other than screens. Mazurek and Wenstrup reported that children with ASD did not spend more than 2.8 h a day at home on all “non-screen” activities (including homework, reading, sports, and games with peers).

Surprisingly, we did not note any particular association between TV viewing and the presence of more than one TV set at home or the presence of a TV set in the child’s bedroom. This result must be interpreted with great caution because we did not collect data on the socio-economic level of the families in the two populations. Several studies on children with typical development show a positive correlation between the presence of television in the child’s room and television consumption [21, 22]. However, we can find an explanation for this result in the very nature of this exposure. Indeed, as we mentioned previously, for children with ASD, television seems to be a necessity and not just a leisure activity. The parents in this study often reported that shutting down the television led to insurmountable anxiety attacks. This excessive attachment of children with ASD to television might be seen as a need for sameness/immutability.

Children with ASD were described by parents as mostly quiet and absorbed by TV. The TV stimulus is an intense sensory stimulus, with a very fast pace, which needs significant sustained attention [23]. Moreover, children with ASD prefer visual stimuli, hence the great attention they pay to television [24]. Children with ASD mimed more often what was displayed on the screen than the control group. It was reported that in contrary to TD children, ASD children learn more easily from screens than a living model [25]. Concerning the exacerbation of stereotypies, some studies reported the same results but the underling process is unclear [23]. The peripheral gaze can be considered as a means of sensory regulation [26]. Children with ASD perceive the world as hyper stimulating, with too much sensory influence [27, 28, 29]. The peripheral gaze reduces the visual field and thus limits the amount of visual stimuli besieging the child. The same explanation is also valid for the regulation of the very high or mute sound, as well as the short distance between the screen and the child.

Children with ASD in our study mainly viewed video-clip networks for children. These networks have the particularity of looping the same videos. A similar phenomenon is often reported: ASD children prefer to watch pre-recorded videos rather than watching original TV programs [19]. Children with ASD prefer to watch the same videos and even the same particular sequences. We can consider this behavior as a restricted interest or immutability. It seems that TV brings ASD children the repeated and reassuring stimulus they need to adhere to, echoing the old concept of adhesive identification by the psychoanalyst Meltzer (1975) [30]. This finding is, in our view, a very interesting avenue to better understand autistic mechanisms and deduce prevention strategies. These findings support the view that, while autism has clear genetic and neurobiological origins, its clinical manifestation may be modulated by the interaction of inherited biological vulnerabilities with cumulative environmental influences.

Children with ASD watched television mainly alone. We can explain this result by the type of programs viewed by ASD children. These repetitive programs demotivate siblings and parents from accompanying the child in such a monotonous activity. However, we can also explain the child’s interest in TV by the absence of adults accompanying them. This corresponds to a vicious circle or a ‘trap’ that will perpetuate withdrawal and social communication deficits. The presence of adults who accompany the child, ask questions, make comments, and broadly create meaning from what the child is ‘sensorially’ exposed to, will encourage the brain to do advanced information processing and connect in a more adaptive way.

Even if our results are to be taken with caution, we can clearly emphasize the importance of parent education about the risks of exposure to screens, preferably immediately after diagnosis of ASD.

Strengths and limits, way forward

This study is the first of its kind in the Middle East and Africa. It documents a clear disturbance in exposure to TV is children with ASD, compared with comparable TD children regarding age and social and cultural context, and arises interesting debate on possible better understanding of autistic mechanisms and hence potential secondary of tertiary preventive strategies. This last point is very relevant in the context of low resourced settings since it can at least lead to better parents’ engagement in social communication with their child. However, we remind that our methodology could indicate correlations between ASD and exposure to screens, but provides no evidence regarding any causality or impact on the prognosis.

These results are to be considered with caution due to some methodological limitations. The main limitation is related to informant bias, as the data are collected retrospectively, especially those regarding the beginning and the first patterns of TV exposure, and as parents of atypical children are more likely to remember and exaggerate information that is collected with our questionnaire, with possible attribution of a higher role to screens in an attempt to better understand the child’s condition, and possible generalization of current exposure to screens to the exposure at earlier ages. We tried to reduce this bias by choosing an early age for our groups, but this remains a possible source of confusion, and prospective cohort studies are needed to confirm these findings. Another limitation is related to screening and diagnostic procedures: the diagnostic process for ASD relied on the clinical expertise of child psychiatrists, using DSM criteria and CARS-T, without using a gold standard tool such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule ADOS-2 [31] or Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised [32], and m-CHAT was used beyond its recommended age range of 16–30 months (other screening instruments could cover ages higher than 30 months in the control group). Besides, a difference exists in kindergarten attendance, with less attendance in the ASD group. This reduces comparability as it may lead to higher risk that children in the ASD group are left at home with little to do and thus indulge in screens.

More research, mainly prospective, is needed to further explore mutual relationships between ASD and exposure to screens. The association described in this study can be understood from different points of view: high exposure to screens can be a consequence of the social communication challenges of ASD, of decreased stimulation from the family with a poorly interactive child or can reflect a worsening environmental factor. Even if some positive aspects of exposure to screens can be observed in ASD (learning, augmentative and alternative communication), the precautionary principle suggests referring to international guidelines regarding screen exposure for all children. For example, the guidelines of the American Academy of Pediatrics released in 2016 and reaffirmed in 2022 [13] recommend no screen time for children under 18–24 months, except for video-chatting [33]. We also think that investigation and rationalization of screen exposure should be part of the intervention with children with a suspicion or diagnosis of ASD.
