Biomechanical stability evaluation of isolated straddle fracture using finite element analysis
Authors: Jung-Soo Lee, Kwang Gi Kim, Yong-Cheol Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Straddle fractures, bilateral fractures of the superior and inferior pubic rami, are considered mechanically stable when the posterior pelvic ring remains intact. However, progressive displacement or delayed union, even in isolated straddle fractures, raise concerns about their true biomechanical stability. This study aimed to quantitatively evaluate the biomechanical stability of isolated straddle fractures with an intact posterior pelvic ring using finite element analysis based on computed tomography–derived three-dimensional pelvic models. Two models were developed: model A, an isolated straddle fracture model with an intact posterior pelvic ring; model B, a posterior fracture model with sacroiliac joint fracture. Under a 600 N axial load, model A exhibited a maximum displacement of 2.03 mm within the physiological safety limit and a maximum von-Mises stress of 100.6 MPa, lower than the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of the cortical bone (110 MPa). Conversely, model B exhibited severe instability, with a displacement > 50 mm and a stress surpassing the UTS. Model A maintained biomechanical stability under a 600 N bending load and 10 Nm torsional moment, with displacements < 2 mm and stresses remaining below the UTS. These findings indicate that an intact posterior pelvic ring provides sufficient biomechanical stability for isolated straddle fractures, supporting conservative management when posterior ring integrity is maintained. In contrast, injury to the posterior pelvic ring results in severe mechanical instability, emphasizing its crucial role in pelvic load transfer. Collectively, this quantitative biomechanical evidence refines traditional treatment principles for isolated straddle fractures and supports clinical decision-making in pelvic ring injuries.

Introduction

Pelvic ring injuries involve mechanically complex structures and are one of the most clinically significant types of fractures because of their close relationship with pelvic stability and load transfer mechanisms1,2. Straddle fractures, defined as bilateral fractures of the superior and inferior pubic rami, are generally regarded as stable injuries when the posterior pelvic ring remains intact3. Based on this concept, traditional treatment principles emphasize conservative management for isolated straddle fractures with an intact posterior pelvic ring, assuming sufficient biomechanical stability can be achieved without surgical fixation3,4.

According to the conventional Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO) and Tile classification systems, the integrity of the posterior pelvic ring is the major determinant of overall pelvic stability, whereas injuries limited to the anterior pelvic ring are not considered to cause mechanical instability on their own2,5. Although these classification systems are based on long-term clinical experience and retrospective studies, quantitative evaluation of their actual mechanical stability remains limited6,7. To date, no study has directly investigated whether isolated straddle fractures resulting from high-energy trauma are biomechanically stable when the posterior pelvic ring remains intact3,8.

Finite element analysis (FEA), a computer-aided engineering tool, enables the quantitative evaluation of the mechanical behavior of fracture mechanics, including stress distribution, displacement, and potential instability under various load conditions9,10. Despite the increasing use of FEA in orthopedic biomechanics10, only few studies have quantitatively assessed the mechanical stability of isolated straddle fractures with an intact posterior pelvic ring8. Several clinical studies have reported cases of progressive displacement or complications, even after conservative treatment; however, whether these phenomena are attributable to true mechanical instability or biological factors remains unclear11–13.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the biomechanical stability of isolated straddle fractures with an intact posterior pelvic ring using FEA. We sought to verify whether the current conservative treatment principles are mechanically justified and, if instability is identified under specific load conditions, to discuss whether treatment strategies should be reconsidered. The findings of this study may contribute to refining traditional classification systems and treatment principles, thereby providing quantitative evidence to support future clinical decision-making in the management of pelvic ring injuries8–10.

Material and methods

Data preparation

An isolated straddle fracture model for the FEA was created using preprocessed computed tomography (CT) data (Fig. 1a). The CT data of 22 adults (1 person in their 20 s and 21 persons in their 30 s) at the Gachon University Gil Medical Center were acquired from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) to reconstruct the pelvis as a three-dimensional (3D) model. The images were saved in the Digital Imaging and Communications (DICOM) format, the standard for medical images in PACS. The DICOM file was analyzed using an image review platform (AVIEW Research Build 1.1.44.17; Coreline Soft Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea, https://www.corelinesoft.com) to segment the region of interest (ROI) corresponding to the shape of the pelvis and sacrum. In each CT image slice, lines were manually drawn along the cortical bone to perform ROI segmentation, which was repeated for all slices. The ROI of the cortical bone was converted to the NIfTI format and exported as an STL file using the open-source medical imaging platform 3D Slicer software. Based on the pelvic shape and cortical bone thickness, a 3D model of the pelvic cortical bone was reconstructed using a single-subject dataset (n = 1). The high-resolution CT data was obtained from a 39-year-old female with normal pelvic anatomy and no prior history of trauma, and was subsequently used to develop the finite element (FE) model. The Hounsfield units used to identify the bones ranged from 300 to 1300. The CT model was SOMATOM Definition Flash, with a continuous slice thickness of 0.6 mm and a total of 97 slices. The CT images were postprocessed using the freehand slice technique.

All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Committee of Human Subjects Protection and Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Gachon University Gil Medical Center, Incheon, South Korea (IRB no. GBIRB2024-251).

Finite element model

Two fracture models were developed (models A and B; Fig. 1b). The isolated straddle fracture model (model A) was created by generating fractures in the pelvis 3D model as follows. First, a fracture was produced by cutting the superior pubic ramus with a plane shifted 40 mm horizontally to the right from the vertical plane passing through the center of the pubic symphysis and tilted by 30°. Second, a corresponding fracture was created on the contralateral side by cutting the superior pubic ramus with a plane shifted 42 mm horizontally to the left and tilted by 30°. Finally, the left and right inferior pubic rami were cut symmetrically relative to these fracture planes to complete the model. This fracture configuration corresponds to zone II of the Nakatani classification system, which includes fractures within the obturator foramen involving the middle third of the superior pubic ramus14.

The posterior fracture model (model B) was defined as model A with an additional fracture involving the contact surface of the sacroiliac joint and the right wing of the ilium, representing a straddle fracture with a fracture in the posterior pelvic ring.

Ansys Workbench 2020 R2 (ANSYS, Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA, https://www.ansys.com) was employed for the three-dimensional modeling, preprocessing, and FEA. Ten nodes of quadratic tetrahedral elements with three degrees of freedom were used to mesh and discretize models A and B15,16. A mesh convergence study was performed, and the element sizes of the sacrum, pelvis, sacroiliac joint, and pubic symphysis, including the straddle fracture fragments, were 2, 3, 1, and 2 mm, respectively. The total nodes and elements were 130,907 and 26,571 (sacrum); 171,039 and 99,698 (right pelvis); 174,006 and 101,558 (left pelvis); 147,733 and 96,897 (right sacroiliac joint); 146,825 and 96,005 (left sacroiliac joint); 11,472 and 7264 (pubic symphysis); 41,966 and 27,529 (right straddle fracture fragment); and 41,045 and 27,026 (left straddle fracture fragment).

Boundary condition

With regard to the contact conditions, four fracture contact surfaces in model A (indicated by the red diagonal lines in Fig. 1b) were defined: two between the right straddle fracture fragment and the right pelvis and two between the left straddle fracture fragment and the left pelvis. In model B, one fracture contact surface (indicated by a red arc in Fig. 1b) was defined between the sacroiliac joint and the right iliac wing. All fracture contact surfaces were assigned a friction coefficient of 0.3 to allow finite sliding15,17 under load conditions. All other components, including the sacrum, pelvis, sacroiliac joint, and pubic symphysis, were assumed to be perfectly bonded.

Under the boundary condition in which the posterior center of the sacrum was fixed, a standing posture was simulated by applying a 500 N load to each of the right and left acetabula along a direction aligned with the femoral head axis, which corresponds to a 125° inclination from the femoral shaft axis, as shown in Fig. 1c. In addition, three supplemental load conditions were applied to the superior surface of the sacrum: an axial compressive force of 600 N18–20,23 representing upper body weight, a bending force of 600 N simulating forward trunk flexion, and a torsional moment of 10 Nm23 representing leftward trunk rotation.

These loads were intended to replicate upper-body forces during standing and the external loads typically encountered during daily activities, with their magnitudes deliberately increased to represent a physiological worst-case scenario and induce mechanical instability in models A and B.

In model B, the right iliac wing was subjected to a 500 N load on the acetabulum but had no constraints other than the friction coefficient defined at the fracture surface. Consequently, extreme mechanical instability occurred, resulting in an unbounded displacement. To enable FEA, anatomically relevant ligaments were implemented as spring connections on the basis of pelvic anatomy and including the sacrum, right iliac wing, and pubic symphysis with the straddle fracture fragment (Fig. 1b)24,25.

Specifically, 10 anterior sacroiliac ligaments were applied between the sacrum and the right iliac wing on the anterior side of the pelvis. On the posterior side, six long posterior sacroiliac ligaments, four short posterior sacroiliac ligaments, three sacrospinous ligaments, and eight sacrotuberous ligaments were applied, with four of the sacrotuberous ligaments attached symmetrically to the right and left sides of the straddle fracture fragment.

A total of 31 spring connections were implemented, and a spring constant of 5 N/mm was assigned to provide appropriate constraint and ensure convergence of the simulation. To enable a comparative evaluation of the biomechanical stability between models A and B, the same spring configuration was applied to model A.

Results

FEA simulations were performed to evaluate the biomechanical stability of the isolated straddle fracture model (model A) under the three load conditions. Simulations were conducted using Ansys Workbench 2020 R2. Nonlinear behaviors, including contact interactions at the fracture surfaces where a friction coefficient was applied, were solved using the Newton–Raphson method9,10.

This study assessed the biomechanical stability of isolated straddle fractures with an intact posterior pelvic ring. We focused on the displacement and stress distribution of model A to characterize the mechanical behavior of the pelvis with respect to structural stability. Discussion

This FEA study was designed to quantitatively assess the biomechanical stability of an isolated straddle fracture with an intact posterior pelvic ring. Traditional treatment principles have long regarded such injuries as mechanically stable, assuming that adequate stability can be maintained with conservative management alone, without requiring surgical treatment2,6,7. Existing classification systems, such as the AO/OTA and Tile frameworks, identify the posterior pelvic ring as the primary determinant of pelvic stability2,5, but these systems have been built more on long-term clinical experience and retrospective studies rather than direct mechanical verification. Clinical reports have also described cases in which patients with seemingly stable isolated straddle fractures with an intact posterior pelvic ring developed progressive displacement or delayed union3,4,11,12, suggesting that unrecognized biomechanical instability may exist under physiological load conditions.

Therefore, this study sought to provide a clearer and more quantitative biomechanical basis for this commonly encountered yet insufficiently investigated clinical scenario by analyzing displacement patterns and stress distributions in an isolated straddle fracture model with an intact posterior pelvic ring under physiological load conditions.

To address this, we performed FEA biomechanical simulations designed to replicate the physiological load conditions encountered during daily activities. Using anatomically detailed CT-derived geometry, nonlinear frictional contact at the fracture interfaces (friction coefficient, 0.3), and ligamentous spring connections that provide anatomically appropriate constraints24,25, we evaluated the mechanical behavior of the isolated straddle fracture model (model A) under axial compression, bending load, and torsional moment. A comparison construct with the posterior fracture model (model B), incorporating the same ligamentous spring constraints, enabled direct biomechanical contrast and provided an essential context for interpreting the biomechanical stability of isolated straddle fractures. To account for its critical role as a primary pathway for load transition between the upper body and lower extremities—as emphasized in previous FEA studies on bipedal walking25—the sacroiliac joint was reconstructed as a three-dimensional model and integrated into the pelvic structure. These FE model configurations allowed the quantitative assessment of displacement and stress distribution patterns and facilitated the evaluation of whether the traditional assumption of stability in isolated straddle fractures is biomechanically valid.

Across all load conditions, model A consistently exhibited adequate biomechanical stability. Consistent with previous FEA studies8,10, under a 600 N axial compressive load—representing upper-body weight—the straddle fracture fragment showed a maximum displacement of 2.03 mm, with the global pelvis remaining within 0.4–0.6 mm. The maximum von-Mises stress (100.6 MPa) was lower than the cortical bone UTS of 110 MPa15,16, providing a safety factor against mechanical failure. Similar stability was observed under a 600 N bending load (maximum displacement, 2 mm; maximum von-Mises stress, 95 MPa) and 10 Nm torsional moment (maximum displacement, 1.89 mm; maximum von-Mises stress, 97.4 MPa). These results quantitatively support the idea that an intact posterior pelvic ring prevents excessive displacement and appropriately redistributes stress in straddle fractures.

In marked contrast, model B exhibited severe mechanical instability following the posterior pelvic ring injury. Consistent with previous biomechanical findings that the posterior ring serves as the primary load-bearing pathway8,18,23, under axial compressive load alone, the displacement of the right tubercle of the iliac crest exceeded 50 mm, far beyond the clinically acceptable displacement, whereas the maximum von-Mises stress (131.5 MPa) exceeded the UTS of the cortical bone. This dramatic displacement highlights that the presence of a fracture at the sacroiliac joint contact surface—which defines the posterior pelvic ring injury—renders the right iliac wing highly unconstrained, inducing extreme mechanical instability under load conditions. This increase in deformation and stress clearly demonstrates that the posterior pelvic ring serves as the principal load-bearing pathway of the pelvis. Despite the focused application of ligamentous spring connections at the fracture site between the sacroiliac joint and right iliac wing to account for posterior pelvic ring injury, the posterior fracture nonlinearly amplified global pelvic instability and generated stress concentrations. These findings are consistent with the clinical and biomechanical evidence that even subtle or radiographically occult posterior pelvic ring injuries can compromise pelvic stability3,4,13,26.

From a biomechanical standpoint, stress distribution patterns provide key insights into pelvic load transfer mechanisms. In model A, the stress concentrations were consistently localized around the posterior sacral foramen rather than along the anterior fracture lines, highlighting the dominant role of the posterior pelvic ring in transmitting axial compressive load, bending load, and torsional moments18,23,25. Conversely, the compromised posterior load-bearing pathway in model B forced load redistribution through the mechanically inefficient anterior pelvic ring, resulting in markedly increased bending moments and pronounced stress concentrations across both the bony interfaces and the simulated ligament connections. These load-transfer characteristics offer a biomechanical explanation for why patients with an intact posterior pelvic ring often tolerate partial weight-bearing13,27, whereas those with posterior pelvic ring injury are unable to perform basic activities of daily living, such as standing or walking, and, in severe cases, may develop physiological instability that can be life-threatening.

The FEA results have several important clinical implications. First, the consistently small displacements (~ 2 mm) and sub-UTS stress contributions observed in model A support conservative treatment or limited fixation strategies in patients with isolated straddle fractures in whom posterior pelvic ring integrity is confirmed on CT imaging or other high-resolution modalities11–13. Importantly, these mechanical findings are highly consistent with published clinical outcome data, demonstrating that the model maintains minimal displacement within the critical 2 mm clinical threshold associated with fixation failure for strictly isolated anterior injuries3,11. Second, the severe instability demonstrated in model B, where displacements exceeded 50 mm, underscores the need for meticulous evaluation of the posterior pelvic ring using CT or comparable imaging techniques, particularly in high-energy trauma such as occult sacroiliac joint injuries, ligamentous insufficiency, or subtle sacral disruptions, which may convert an apparently stable anterior pelvic ring injury into a mechanically unstable configuration1,4,6,23. Seemingly stable anterior pelvic ring injuries may represent mechanically unstable posterior pelvic ring injuries; therefore, surgical fixation should be considered more aggressively. Third, the robust biomechanical safety margin observed in model A indicates that controlled early mobilization and guided partial weight bearing may be permissible; however, these decisions must be individualized based on bone quality (e.g., normal bone vs. osteoporotic bone), comorbidities, pain response, and overall patient frailty15–17. For patients with osteoporosis or cortical thinning, diminished pelvic strength dictates that both conservative and surgical treatments should be carefully considered. Therefore, radiographic follow-up during the early healing phase remains essential.

This study has some limitations. First, although CT-based geometry and nonlinear contact modeling enabled realistic simulation of fracture surface separation, sliding, and recontact, the pelvic morphology was derived from a single patient-specific CT dataset22. Consequently, the results may not fully capture interindividual variations in pelvic size or sacroiliac joint geometry across different ages and sexes. Second, ligamentous structures were represented using anatomically distributed spring elements rather than fully 3D soft-tissue models24,25, and the surrounding muscles and soft tissues were excluded. Although this approach effectively prevented abnormal rigid-body motion and reproduced the physiological constraint directions, it could not fully replicate the complex biomechanical behavior of actual ligaments, muscles, and soft tissues, which may influence dynamic pelvic stability. Third, although the two-model comparative framework allowed targeted assessment of how an intact posterior pelvic ring influences anterior injury biomechanics, it does not encompass partial sacroiliac joint injuries, heterogeneous ligament failures, or combined injury patterns commonly encountered in clinical settings20,23. Fourth, although the three load conditions applied in this simulation were based on established biomechanical values9,10, they do not represent the full spectrum of physiological loads generated during activities of daily living. Fifth, the pelvic bone was modeled using linear elastic and isotropic material assumptions17,18 representing cortical bone without separately incorporating cancellous bone, specifically to evaluate the macro-level biomechanical stability along the primary load-bearing pathways. In this configuration, breakage was defined as exceeding the UTS of 110 MPa based on established literature18–23. Thus, in situations where the maximum von-Mises stress surpasses the yield strength but remains lower than the UTS, the assumption of linear material behavior may be invalid. Finally, although the modeling strategy followed validated FE methodologies8–10,22, direct validation through cadaveric or experimental biomechanical testing remains necessary to establish quantitative accuracy14,19.

Despite these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first FEA to directly quantify the influence of an intact posterior pelvic ring on the biomechanical behavior of isolated straddle fracture. By incorporating patient-specific pelvic geometry, nonlinear contact interactions, and a comparative two-model framework8–10,22, the analysis delineates the mechanical pathways governing pelvic load transfer and demonstrates that an intact posterior pelvic ring maintains physiologic load sharing with minimal displacement of the anterior fracture fragments. In contrast, the findings clearly show that when a posterior ring injury is present, this stabilizing effect is lost, revealing a latent instability that may arise even in injuries that only appear on the anterior on imaging. Collectively, these findings establish a more robust biomechanical basis for clinical decision-making regarding diagnosis, injury classification, weight-bearing strategies, and the threshold for surgical stabilization in patients with isolated straddle fractures and other anterior pelvic ring injuries.

Crucially, the numerical framework established in this study provides a foundational baseline for advanced biomechanical simulations. Accordingly, to expand the scope of this research, conducting further studies that incorporate literature-based peak muscle forces acting on the pelvis during gait serves as an interesting and challenging future task. Furthermore, expanding this framework to patient-specific modeling that accounts for individual anatomical variations and bone quality could improve the clinical prediction of long-term fracture progression and potential delayed union, and could be readily extended to evaluate the biomechanics of surgical implants such as plates and screws. Additionally, integrating this FEA framework with machine learning could substantially improve prediction accuracy and facilitate image-based predictive models, thereby bridging the gap to digital healthcare applications. Investigating the biomechanical stability of partial posterior ring injuries, particularly whether they exhibit a gradual or threshold-based instability response, represents a highly interesting topic for future research.

Conclusion

The FEA results demonstrated that isolated straddle fractures with an intact posterior pelvic ring maintained sufficient biomechanical stability under axial, bending, and torsional load conditions. Both the maximum von-Mises stress and displacement remained within the physiological safety limits, confirming that an intact posterior pelvic ring effectively supports load transfer and prevents anterior fracture displacement. In contrast, the model with the posterior pelvic ring injury exhibited stress exceeding the UTS of the cortical bone and deformation corresponding to fixation failure in fracture fixation, indicating severe mechanical instability. These findings underscore that the posterior pelvic ring is the principal determinant of overall pelvic stability and provide quantitative evidence supporting conservative treatment when the posterior pelvic ring remains intact. Therefore, this study provides a quantitative biomechanical foundation for refining the traditional treatment principles for isolated straddle fractures and underscores the importance of careful evaluation of the posterior pelvic ring in clinical decision-making for pelvic ring injuries.
