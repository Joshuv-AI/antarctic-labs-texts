Reflecting on the dynamics of surgical training: a call for a deeper understanding and structured frameworks
Authors: Hongshuo Shi, Hutang Wen, Guobin Liu, Weijing Fan, Lei Wang
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

I write in response to the article ‘Does an aptitude for surgery exist and can we predict it? An experimental study’1 recently published in your esteemed journal, International Journal of Surgery. The study offers a commendable exploration of the factors influencing surgical training outcomes, highlighting the roles of personality, motivation, and psychomotor skills. However, there are several aspects within the study’s findings and conclusions that warrant further discussion and clarification to enhance our understanding and application in surgical curricula.

Firstly, the study intriguingly distinguishes between the significant driving forces of students and physicians—personality and motivation versus psychomotor skills, respectively. This observation suggests an evolution of skills throughout the surgical training continuum. It is critical to understand how these elements interact rather than function independently to influence training outcomes. The study’s use of Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) could have been leveraged further to analyze these interactions. The absence of a detailed discussion on interaction effects seems a missed opportunity to deepen our understanding of how these complex factors coalesce to shape surgical proficiency.

Moreover, while the study commendably emphasizes the importance of nontechnical skills like personality traits and motivation, the practical integration of these findings within surgical curricula is not thoroughly articulated. It is mentioned that these aspects are crucial, yet the article lacks specificity in how these insights could be practically applied. For instance, the suggestion to incorporate personality assessments and motivation enhancement strategies is forward-thinking but remains vague. Without concrete examples or detailed methodologies, these recommendations do not fully assist curriculum developers in implementing these insights effectively.

Additionally, the article briefly discusses the shifting importance of psychomotor skills with advancing experience but stops short of exploring the implications of this evolution in depth. This transition from personality-driven aptitude to a predominance of psychomotor skills in experienced surgeons suggests a developmental trajectory that could be crucial in tailoring training at different career stages. Understanding this shift is vital, yet the study does not provide a structured framework or specific hypotheses for future research, which are essential for developing targeted, effective training programs.

The conclusion rightly points out the need for longitudinal investigations and the exploration of interrelationships between conventional and robotic techniques. However, the lack of a proposed structured framework or specific hypotheses for these future studies is a notable limitation. Given the rapid evolution of surgical technologies and training methods, establishing a clear research agenda that addresses these gaps is crucial.

In light of previous research, such as the findings by Kramp et al.2 and El Boghdady and Ewalds-Kvist3, which explored the relationship between innate aptitude and surgical performance, this study adds valuable insights but also highlights the necessity for a more nuanced approach. Training programs must account for the variable importance of skills and traits across different stages of a surgeon’s career, incorporating continuous adjustments and evaluations.

To advance our understanding and application of these findings, future research should focus on developing a comprehensive framework that integrates both technical and nontechnical skills. Longitudinal studies that track the progression of surgical trainees over time, examining the interplay between personal traits, motivation, and skill acquisition, would provide invaluable data to refine training programs further.

In conclusion, while the study under discussion has laid a solid foundation for understanding the complex dynamics of surgical training, there is a significant need for more detailed analysis and structured approaches to fully harness these insights in educational practice. I appreciate the opportunity to contribute to this ongoing discussion and look forward to seeing how it evolves in future research.
