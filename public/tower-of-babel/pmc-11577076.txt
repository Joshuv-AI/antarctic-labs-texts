Therapeutic and antioxidant properties of Acridocarpus monodii Arène & Jaeger, an endemic plant of Dogon Country in Mali
Authors: Mamadou A. Konaré, Joudy Dougnon, Issiaka Togola, Nah Traoré, Nouhoum Diarra, Moussa Karembé
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Acridocarpus monodii Arène & Jaeger is an endemic plant species to the ‘’Dogon Country’’ in Mali. This species is highly popular in Dogon ethnic groups, but no scientific data on its medicinal and biochemical properties are available in Mali. This work aimed to list its medicinal uses and assess its phytochemical composition and antioxidant power. An ethnobotanical investigation based on semi structured questionnaire was used to identify the traditional uses of A. monodii among the local population in three villages (Ireli, Youdiou and Yon-biré) inside ‘’Dogon country’’ in Mali. The phytochemical composition was determined using qualitative tests, while the antioxidant potency was performed using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picryhydazyl (DPPH) and phosphomolybdate (TAC) tests. A total of 45 people were interviewed, the majority of whom were male (82.20%) and over 52 years of age. A. monodii is used to treat a dozen pathologies or diseases, dominated by malaria (71.10%), yellow fever (24.40%) and dysuria (20%). Although, all parts of this species are used to treat these pathologies, leaves (88.90%) were the most coveted followed by roots (37.80%). The use of these parts was significantly (p = 0.016 < 0.05) associated with the educational levels of the respondents (77.70%), who coveted primarily roots and barks, and thus constituted a threat to the species. These medicinal recipes derived from these organs were mostly prepared as decoctions, before being administered by oral (100%) or bath (82.20%) routes. Extracts of A. monodii were found to contain various biocompounds, especially a high quantity of flavonoids and polyphenols and demonstrated increased in vitro antioxidant inhibitory effects. The decoction extracts showed the highest content of phenolic compounds (109.82 ± 2.36 mg GAE/g). The strongest activity for the DPPH free radical scavenging were recorded by the decoction with IC50 = 107.41 ± 4.25 µg/mL and the hydroethanolic macerate with 107.31 ± 9.28 µg/mL. But, based on the results from of TAC test, the antioxidant capacity was higher for the hydroethanolic macerate 42.85 ± 2.59 mg EQ/g than those of the decoction 19.27 ± 0.93 mg EQ/g. This is the first scientific report on the therapeutic use of A. monodii within ‘’Dogon country’’ in Mali. This work highlights its medicinal, phytochemical and biochemical properties and therefore contributes to its improved valorization. These results demonstrated that A. monodii is a promising species for the discovery of novel medicines.

Introduction

The ‘’Dogon country’’ site in Mali, covering an area of 4000 km2, has been a World Heritage Site since 1989. It is characterized by cultural and ethnic diversity. The inhabitants are renowned for their expertise in traditional medicine, which led to the establishment of a Regional Center of Traditional Medicine (CRMT) in Bandiagara, the capital of ‘’Dogon country’’. As a result of health cooperation between Mali and Italy, the CRMT has been operational since 1990 1. As a rural branch of the Department of Traditional Medicine (DMT), the CRMT’s mission is to promote relationships between traditional and Western medicines to address priority public health concerns, and to support the associative dynamics of traditional practitioners from Dogon country.

Due to their hydric and biological characteristics, the cliffs of “Dogon country’’ are considered to be islands or oases inside the Sahel, which hosts a large variety of medicinal plant species 2. Traditional healers have explored these medicinal plants for primary health reasons. While most of these species are widely distributed, some are considered endemic to the Dogon country, such as Acridocarpus monodii Arène & Jaeger 2,3.

This tropical and subtropical genus is composed of flowering plants belonging to the Malpighiaceae family. Primarily found in West Africa, the genus Acridocarpus has many therapeutic aims for the management of blood disorders, infertility, and pains 4,5. According to the literature, the worldwide geographic distribution of A. monodii is restricted to a few square kilometers in the southern cliffs of the Dogon country 2,3. Some rare studies have also mentioned its presence in Sangha, South Africa 6. Works conducted by Birnbaum and Florence 3 reported that very few specific uses were known by traditional Dogon practitioners, who have been questioned in some villages near certain explored sites. In response to this challenge, well-documented local works are needed to bridge this gap between scientific reports and popular guidebooks. Thus, the current study was undertaken with the goal of contributing to the valorization of A. monodii through ethnobotanical and biochemical investigations.

Materials & methods

Materials

Study area

This study was conducted in Bandiagara, Center of Mali, a West African country (Fig. 1). Bandiagara is the centre of ‘’Dogon country’’ which covers an area of 4000 km2 and has been considered as world heritage Site since 1989 2. It has a population of 868,916 inhabitants in 2023. With a Sahelian climate, the ‘’Dogon country’’ is inhabited by three ethnic groups: Peulhs, Dogons, and Bozos. In addition, there are other ethnic groups, such as Songhoi, Bamanan, Touareg and Maures. Three villages inside this region were chosen for people interviews, due to their richness in A. monodii: Ireli (Bandiagara circle), Youdiou and Yon-biré (Koro circle).Fig. 1Map of Mali showing study areas.

Map of Mali showing study areas.

Plant material

Plant material consisting of wild leaves of A. monodii was used to extract and quantify the levels of phytomolecules and in vitro antioxidant power. At the end of the survey, the leaves of A. monodii were collected at Ireli village in compliance with the local ecological and cultural standards under the supervision of Prof. Savio Samaké, botanist at the ‘’Laboratoire de Botanique et d’Ecotoxicologie, LaboTec” of the University of Sciences, Techniques and Technologies of Bamako (USTTB). This collection took place after obtaining the permission of the village Chief. The collected leaves were then cleaned, air-dried, away from the sun’s rays (in the shade) and then ground into powder using an electric grinder (Hausberg, HB 7567, 150 W). The freshly obtained powders were further used.

Reagents

All reagents used in this work were of analytical grade and were purchased from different companies: Carlroth Karlsruhe, Germany (quercetin); Sigma Aldrich Steinheim, Germany (DPPH, gallic acid, ascorbic acid, ferric chloride, sodium hydroxide, ethanol, methanol); Merck Darmstat, Germany (Folin Ciocalteu); and Fischer Scientific, Hemel Hempstead, UK (anhydrous sodium carbonate, sodium nitrite, aluminum chloride).

Methods

Ethnobotanical survey

Information gathering from the survey

This survey was carried out between December 2020 and February 2021. The data were collected via a semi-structured questionnaire. The targeted groups for this survey were composed of salespersons and consumers of A. monodii, herbalists and traditional medical practitioners who were proposed by the local traditional authorities of each village. The respondents were clearly informed of the aim of the study and oral consent was obtained. The questionnaire was administered in the local language ‘’dogon’’. Each targeted person was interviewed alone to maintain confidentiality. First, information about the sociodemographic parameters of the respondents (age, sex, profession, address, etc.) was recorded. Second, ethnobotanical indices, including species’ therapeutic virtues, parts used for the plant, and methods of preparation and administration, were noted.

Identification of specimens

With the help of traditional healers, specimens of A. monodii, were collected for the first identification. The confirmation was then given by of Prof. Savio Samaké, botanist at the ‘’Laboratoire de Botanique et d’Ecotoxicologie, LaboTec” of the University of Sciences, Techniques and Technologies of Bamako (USTTB) and Dr. Sidi SANOGO, Botanist at Unité des Semences Forestières et Herbier (USFH), Institut d’Economie Rurale (IER), where a voucher specimens exist under Reference ML 533 in Flore du Mali.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted with the help of traditional and customary authorities of each village. This accompaniment allowed us to select respondents and to obtain their verbal free consent.

Experimental study

Extract preparation

The extracts were prepared by macerating 100 g of powder in 1000 mL of hydroethanolic 70% or water. The mixture was stirred for 48 h at 30 °C and then filtered under vacuum. The obtained filtrates were evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 45 °C (Bucchi R-100).

As for the aqueous infusion, 100 g of powder were introduced into 1000 mL of boiled water. The mixture was kept at room temperature until cooled (about 30 min), then filtered and evaporated under the same conditions as the macerate.

The aqueous decoction was prepared by introducing 100 g of powder into 1000 mL of water. The mixture was boiled and stirred for 15 min, before being filtered and evaporated under the same conditions above.

The contents were incubated in the dark for 30 min, and the absorbances were read at 515 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Biomate 3S, Madison, WI53711, USA). Statistical analysis of data

The entry of data and statistical tests were performed using of IBM SPSS Statistics version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Minitab statistical software version 18.1 (Minitab Inc., PA., USA). Ethnobotanical data on A. monodii

Treated diseases

The treated illnesses by A. monodii, which were cited by the people of interest, are summarized in Fig. 2. The analysis of this figure shows that a number of pathologies are managed by the local population of ‘’Dogon country’’ by using the species A. monodii. Data obtained from the survey allowed us to record ten (10) pathologies or illnesses treated by different parts of A. monodii. These pathologies included malaria, mentioned by 71.10% of respondents, followed by yellow fever (24.40%) and dysuria (20%). This species is also used to treat the pains and anemia by a minority of informants (2.20%) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Frequencies of therapeutic uses of A. monodii by local population.

Used plant parts/organs

Figure 3 shows the different parts of A. monodii used by the respondents for therapeutic purposes. Globally, the leaves were the most coveted parts (88.90%) of A. monodii (Fig. 3A). The most important unsafe use of roots and barks was recorded among the illiterate informants (37.80% and 4.44%, respectively). We also tried to understand the use of the plant parts according to the sex of the respondents and the investigated villages (Fig. 3C). Analysis of this Table reveals that the safeguarding of the species is threatened through the excessive use of roots mainly by men (35.56%). A large majority of the men were residents of the village of Ireli (31.11%) followed by those of Youdiou (6.67%). Phytochemical composition

Phytochemical screening of different extracts revealed the presence of many major groups of secondary metabolites, as depicted in Table 3. The contents of some identified compounds (total polyphenols and flavonoids) in addition to extraction yields have been estimated (Table 4). These data reveal a variation between the levels of these biocompounds and extraction yield. Decoction method was found to be the best process to extract the bioactive compounds with an extraction yield of 25.16 ± 0.80% followed by hydroethanolic maceration 18.52 ± 0.47% of extraction yield. The highest content of polyphenols (109.82 ± 2.36 mg GAE/g), was detected in the decocted extracts, and the infusion ones presented the lowest level (8.66 ± 1.96 mg GAE/g). Discussion

This work is the first to be carried out on A. monodii inside the ‘’Dogon country’’ to highlight its therapeutic uses for better valorization and preservation.

Our investigations confirmed that this endemic plant is highly valued in the ‘’Dogon country’’ of Mali, as previously reported in the literature 2,3.

Our survey outreached 45 individuals from the three mentioned villages with a strong participation of men (82.20%). This gender imbalance is reinforced by many studies reporting a notable gender bias favoring males related to traditional knowledge and plant use in therapeutics 7,12. In broad terms, inheritance is the main source of traditional knowledge acquisition in West Africa 13, so this acquisition is passed down from father to son, i.e., primarily male. Furthermore, this transmission only occurs when the son reaches a certain age in traditional society. This could explain why an outright majority of respondents (75.60%) were over 52 years old in the investigated villages in Mali. Consequently, these traditional postures again demonstrate the pressing need to document the traditional healers’ knowledge in West Africa voiced by many authors 5,13. The social importance of this herbal medicine is revealed through our recorded data. Indeed, three professional categories were interviewed, with 46.70% of respondents being traditional healers, 35.60% being traditional medicine users, and 17.80% being sellers of these medicines. This practice is a good source of income for those involved.

The socioeconomic importance of herbal medicine was revealed through our recorded data. Indeed, 46.70% of respondents in the three professional categories were traditional healers, followed by 35.60% and 17.80% who were traditional medicine users and salespersons, respectively. Whether this activity constitutes a good source of income for those involved, the monthly incomes remain lower than the guaranteed interprofessional wage in Mali (40 000 West African CFA Francs or 65.25 USD). Mostly 86.67% of the people involved in this activity earned less than 50,000 CFA francs per month i.e., 76 US dollars. Barely, 13.33% had monthly revenues between CFA francs 50,000 and 100,000 (≈ 65.25–162 USD).

According to Birnbaum & Florence 3, until 2005, no precise use of A. monodii was known to the traditional Dogon practitioners interviewed in certain villages in Mali. The only information reported by villagers was that of goats mad after ingesting leaves. Today, knowledge about the medicinal uses of this cultural species has progressed considerably among the public in the investigated zones. A dozen diseases or troubles treated by different parts of A. monodii have been listed, led by malaria cited by 71.10% of respondents. Accordingly, the aphrodisiac effect of the roots of Acridocarpus was reported in the literature 5. The mystical side is also evoked by some informants (6.70%), i.e., its involvement in the management of nightmares. This confirms the sacred character of this endemic and cultural species. These findings are in accordance with those of the literature which strongly highlights the valuable therapeutic benefits of Acridocarpus species for the treatment of blood disorders, infertility, pain and aphrodisiac issues 4,5,14.

Like other medicinal plants, all parts of A. monodii are used by the local population in ‘’Dogon’s country’’ with the highest frequency of leaves (88.90%). Importantly, the predominant use of leaves is known as a classic method for traditional medicine in general, which is highly beneficial for the species, since it contributes valuably to preserving in its natural habits. However, some respondents used risky and unsafely destructive practices to harvest this species for medicinal purposes. The use of roots (33.33%) and barks (4.44%) by a sizeable population from the village of Ireli (31.11%) constituted a real hazard for this valuable Dogon patrimony. These practices raise conservation concerns for this treasured species. These practices strongly support the alert given by Albuquerque & Nóbrega Alves 14 according to which the biodiversity crisis is caused primarily by human behavior and activities and highlight the necessity of safeguarding local biological and cultural diversity. Statistical tests strongly indicated that the education level of respondents with these hazardous behaviors (p = 0.016 < 0.05) was predominantly men (35.56%). Our study also aimed to propose solutions to better conserve this cultural species, which is very precious for “Dogon country’’ in Mali. This is why these behaviors must be fight by increasing the rate of literacy which is very low in the investigated zones (11.10%). Therefore, proper education and awareness-raising and reforestation programs are recommended for the sustainability of this species. Previous studies in the same zone already highlighted the threat of this endemic species due to coercion on land from different forms of agriculture in addition to the fuelwood-cutting 2.

The major mode of preparation of A. monodii organs was decoction followed by maceration. This desire for decoction and maceration techniques is in line with experimental work showing high extraction yields for biocompounds: 25.16 ± 0.80% for decoction and 14.61 ± 0.56–18.52 ± 0.47% for aqueous and hydroethanolic maceration. These high extraction yields obtained with decoction and hydroethanolic maceration could be due to the high temperature and the polarity of ethanol, respectively. It is known that these factors facilitate the solubility of biocompounds and improved their mass transfer due to more penetration of solvent into the plant matrix 15. As in previous work, decoction remains the preferred method for preparing plant extracts for the treatment of ailments in traditional medicine 7,16. This method is a major advantage and helpful, especially in Malian’s context, where a hot climate favors the deterioration of organic products.

Phytochemical screening of A. monodii leaves revealed the presence of many major groups of phytocompounds, such as alkaloids, tannins, phenolic compounds, coumarins, sterols and terpenoids, in all the extracts. Interestingly, these biomolecules are endowed with many properties and consequently provide a strong scientific support for the use of this plant for medicinal goal in ‘’Dogon country’’, in Mali. For instance, Railson et al. 17 have demonstrated the ability of alkaloids to lower the permeability of blood capillaries and to increase their resistance to hemolysis. In addition, the analgesic power of alkaloids has been solidly documented 18,19. These results strongly support the use of A. monodii in the management of anemia, pain and aches, as revealed by our ethnobotanical investigation results. Moreover, the spectrophotometric assessment showed the abundance of phenolic compounds, especially flavonoids in our extracts. The presence of these phytocompounds in plant extracts is associated with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antimicrobial and antimalarial potential 8,20–22.

Nowadays, Artemisinin based combination therapies (ACTs) is known as the most effective anti-malarial drugs. The involvement of antioxidant agents is well documented in the management of malaria through their ability to decrease the free radicals generation, which are among side effects of malaria pathogenesis 22. All our extracts could be considered to have a high antioxidant potential due to their low IC50 values (from 107.31 ± 9.28 to 156.70 ± 19.10 µg/mL) which are below the recommended threshold (< 200 µg/mL) 22. In addition, statistical analysis revealed a strong relationship between polyphenols and extracts’ antioxidant potential with R2 = 0.76–0.81. Flavonoids have been also found to play a moderate role in the antioxidant power (R2 = 0.48–0.49). These registered data were in good agreement with previous findings reported by other authors reporting the same trends 15,21. This species could be helpful to research the new ACTs which have good antimalarial power with very low side effects. Various studies have highlighted the role of polyphenols and flavonoids as effective scavengers of free radicals in vitro and antioxidant activities through their effects on transcription factors and enzyme activities 23. These data from literature are in accordance with our findings; the decoction extract which contained the greatest quantity of total polyphenols (109.82 ± 2.36 mg GAE/g), was the most potent at reducing the free radical DPPH (with an IC50 = 107.41 ± 4.25 µg/mL). Our results are good news, given that decoction represents the main method of preparing A. monodii organs by populations in ‘’Dogon country’’ for their healthcare. Numerous studies have demonstrated the involvement of free radicals in the pathogenesis of diseases or metabolic disorders, such as malaria, cancer, diabetes, hypertension, and inflammation 20,24,25. Many scientific works suggest the involvement of phenolic compounds in biological systems by affecting peroxide decomposition, quenching oxygen, inhibiting free radicals, and inactivating metals and consequently can significantly contribute to preventing diseases caused by oxidative stress 19,25,26. The extracts of A. monodii, with their antioxidant potential, could play a crucial role in preventing the undesirable actions of free radicals generated inside the body. The antioxidant power of plant extracts is also highly linked to a reduced risk of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer 24,26,27. Due to these processes, the use of this valuable species could be helpful for enhancing local population health.

Conclusion

This work offers insights into the therapeutic use of A. monodii in ‘’Dogon country’’. This highlights the number of medicinal virtues of the species and marks a further step toward its promotion and preservation. All parts or organs are operated upon to manage many pathologies, such as malaria, painful and aches issues, and infections. This work contributes to understanding the therapeutic use of A. monodii within the ‘’Dogon country’’ in Mali and highlights the pressing need for future research on this species. Phytochemical investigations underlined the presence of natural compounds and demonstrated that the antioxidant agent had medicinal effects. These recorded data could profoundly help drug developers from A. monodii, as a result boosting local population health by fighting and safeguarding some infections.
