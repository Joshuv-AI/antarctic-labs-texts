Chronic cranial window implantation for high-resolution intravital imaging of the endothelial glycocalyx in mouse cortex
Authors: Anna Lindsay Gray, Ingo Schiessl, Douglas Philip Dyer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe endothelial glycocalyx is an integral component of the brain vascular barrier. Visualizing its structure in vivo is essential to understand its physiological and pathophysiological mechanisms. Here, we present a surgical protocol for chronic cranial window implantation in mice, alongside the use of multiphoton microscopy tools to image the cortical vasculature. We describe steps for cranial window implantation, intravenous injection of fluorescent markers, and intravital imaging. We then detail a technique to quantify glycocalyx thickness using Imaris image analysis software.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Gray et al. (2023).1Subject areas: Immunology, Microscopy, Neuroscience

Before you begin

The endothelial glycocalyx is a membrane-bound, negatively charged layer of proteoglycans and glycoproteins, which line the luminal surface of blood vessels.2,3 Its significance has been somewhat overlooked in the context of disease for many years; however, it is now becoming increasingly recognized as an important mediator of immune cell recruitment.4,5,6 Intravital imaging is a powerful technique which has the capacity to enhance our understanding of complex biological processes and the deleterious mechanisms associated with disease.7

The protocol below describes a detailed surgical technique for the implantation of cranial windows,8,9,10,11 alongside intravital imaging tools for anesthetized mice. This is performed following intravenous injection of fluorescent markers.7 The wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) conjugate used in this study labels N-acetyl-D-glucosamine and N-acetylneuraminic acid (sialic acid) residues.12 The broad spectrum proteoglycan marker binds to the endothelial glycocalyx lining the luminal surface of blood vessels. Changes induced by inflammation can be assessed. Additionally, it also labels the leukocyte glycocalyx and their activity can be monitored.

We also detail how Imaris image analysis software (any package containing the XT module) can be used to comprehensively measure the thickness of the endothelial glycocalyx. This software is not free, however a free trial is available to download and use for ten non-consecutive days at the Oxford Instruments Imaris website: https://imaris.oxinst.com/microscopy-imaging-software-free-trial. This technique can also be adapted to image the activity of different cells, including neurons, glia or immune cells, at sub cellular resolution.13,14

Cranial window preparation

Note: Cranial windows are always prepared prior to the day of surgery. These can be made in batches of up to ten at a time and sterilized prior to use. A cranial window is made by gluing a 3 mm coverslip and a 4 mm coverslip together. Two windows are fixed together so that the 3 mm window will slot into the craniotomy and the 4 mm window will sit on top to enable secure fixation, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Cross-sectional illustration of cranial window implantation

A head plate with a wide circular opening in the center is secured in place using dental cement. A craniotomy is performed on the bone in the center of this opening, to remove a 3 mm circular portion of skull and expose the brain. The dura mater is left intact and a cranial window is positioned with the 3 mm window at the bottom to fill the craniotomy and the 4 mm window to rest on top. It is carefully secured in place using 3M Vetbond Tissue Adhesive and clear dental cement.

Expected outcomes

The animals recover well after surgery and can be co-housed with their littermates.

Successful completion of this protocol will allow high-resolution intravital imaging of meningeal and cortical vasculature in mice alongside comprehensive analysis of endothelial glycocalyx thickness, as described later in the manuscript. The implanted head plate enables secure fixation within the imaging setup so that movement artefact is not an issue within the data. This method allows for repeat imaging within the same animal over several months.

The data presented in this paper is from one ROI in one animal as an example of a procedure we routinely use in the lab and suggests that endothelial glycocalyx thickness may depend on vessel diameter. A larger scale investigation is required to further verify this across multiple datasets. The system can also be challenged to examine the changes that happen following inflammatory stimulus, both immediately and over time.15

Limitations

Although the described protocol allows high-resolution imaging of brain vasculature in living animals, the imaging depth is limited to around 200 μm because of the type of microscope and objective used in this protocol. Focal distance will always depend upon the microscope equipment, but the protocols described in this paper will still be relevant. The imaging area is limited to the circular 3 mm region defined by the size of the glass window. This method describes intravital imaging of anesthetized animals and therefore will have effects of anesthesia, e.g., isoflurane-dependent dilation of blood vessels. Awake imaging is more complex and would require additional facilities, alongside conditioning of the animals. Furthermore, this protocol is designed for adult mice and not suitable for animals less than 5 weeks old.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Bleeding during surgery (Cranial Window Implantation – Steps 12, 14‒15).

Potential solution

The amount of bleeding will depend on the expertise of the surgeon. A small amount of bleeding may occur from drilling as there are blood vessels in the bone. This will be minimal if the suture bone removal technique described in this protocol is applied effectively. Rinse any areas with saline. Gel foam can be used if required.

Problem 2

Change in fluorescence during imaging due to depth (Intravital Imaging – Steps 36‒43).

Potential solution

Z compensation can be used to increase laser power and/or gain with increasing depth.

Problem 3

Channel bleed-through using the multiphoton microscope (Intravital Imaging – Steps 36‒43).

Potential solution

Researchers should be aware of the emission spectrum of the individual dyes used and check if there is a significant overlap. If in doubt carry out separate experiments using only single fluorophores and check for differences compared to multi fluorophore experiments.

Problem 4

Movement artefact in imaging data due to heart beat artifacts or slow drift over time even in the presence of anesthesia. (Intravital Imaging – Steps 39‒43).

Potential solution

Ensure that the animal is fixed into the setup as firmly as possible using the implanted head plate. Ensure anesthetic levels are optimal and the animal stays well hydrated. We have not found this to be a limitation in our setup, but technical solutions can be used to correct for movement artefact, if required.16

Problem 5

Brain sinking during long imaging sessions. The imaging parameters are set up at the beginning of the experiment but the brain may sink slightly during a lengthy experiment and therefore the bottom of the stack will be lower than at the start (Intravital Imaging – Step 43).

Potential solution

The animal can be given a subcutaneous injection of saline during imaging to limit dehydration. Also, when setting up the acquisition of the regions a deeper Z depth can be used to accommodate any shift.

Problem 6

Change in fluorescence during imaging due to reduction of fluorescent markers e.g., in the bloodstream (Intravital Imaging – Step 43).

Potential solution

The saturation LUT function can be used throughout the experiment to ensure the laser power and individual gains for each marker are at the correct level, avoiding saturation of the pixels.
