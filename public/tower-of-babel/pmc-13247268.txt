Morphological characterization, divergence and disease reaction studies for improvement of mid-late and late maturity genotypes of cauliflower under North-Western Himalayas
Authors: Arshia Prashar, Devinder Kumar Mehta, Ramesh Kumar, Neha, Srishti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough numerous varieties have been recommended across different agro-climatic zones of Himachal Pradesh, however the information on high yielding genotypes with higher quality and disease resistance is lacking in this state. Diseases like black rot and stalk rot are the main obstacle preventing cauliflower production in this area. Therefore, in this investigation, thirty genotypes of cauliflower were evaluated for morphological characters based on DUS, horticultural traits, character associations, genetic divergence and disease reaction studies to identify superior genotypes for commercial cultivation. The experiment was designed in Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three replications. The genotypes were morphologically classified based on twenty-one DUS characters, further identifying genotypes for inclusion in breeding programmes or direct use as a cultivar. Mean performance of genotypes for various traits disclosed that Pusa Himjyoti recorded the highest marketable curd yield/hectare and genotypes Snowball Super, White Heart, UHF-CAU-1, Early Snowball, UHF-CAU-4, UHF-CAU-8 and KT-18 were determined to be statistically at par with Pusa Himjyoti. All these genotypes have white to snow white curd with self- blanching habit. High heritability along with moderate genetic advance as per cent of mean was observed for net curd weight confirming the occurrence of additive gene action. Correlation coefficient discovered that the number of leaves/plant, plant spread, curd polar diameter, curd equatorial diameter, curd size index, curd solidity, gross plant weight and net curd weight were found to be significantly and positively correlated with marketable curd weight. The maximum positive direct effect on marketable curd weight was contributed by curd equatorial diameter followed by curd polar diameter, net curd weight, curd solidity and number of leaves/plant. Thus, perfection in these traits will lead to higher yield in cauliflower. The D2 analysis grouped the genotypes into three clusters consists maximum genotypes in cluster II. Gross plant weight (32.00%), net curd weight (18.16%), plant height (8.33%) and marketable curd yield (8.21%) were identified as significant traits contributing to divergence. In case of disease reaction studies, no genotype was found resistant to stalk rot while, in black rot, only one genotype White Heart was found resistant whereas Snowball Super and PSBK-1 showed moderately resistant reaction.Keywords: Association, Black rot, Diversity, Morphological traits, Screening, Stalk rotSubject terms: Biotechnology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Cauliflower (Brassica oleracea L. var. botrytis L.) 2n = 2x = 18 is a commercially significant vegetable crop belonging to the Brassicaceae family. It is grown all year round in different agro-climatic zones of India1. The Latin terms caulis, which means stem or stalk, and floris, which means flower, are the origins of the English word cauliflower2. Boswell3 traced the origin of cauliflower to the island of Cyprus and its virtual spread to countries such as Syria, Turkey, Egypt, Italy, Spain, and North Western Europe. Now, it is cultivated in all continents of the world, with Asia leading the way, followed by Europe and small acreages in North America, South America, Africa, Australia and New Zealand2. Cauliflower is a low-calorie food, abundant in vitamins (C, B, A and K), minerals, carotenoids, flavonoids, isothiocynates, indoles and tocopherols with good dietary fiber. It has wide therapeutic applications such as anti-cancer, anti-diabetic and reduced risk of cardiovascular diseases4. Pusa Beta Kesari-1 is the first biofortified variety of cauliflower developed through pure line selection by IARI, New Delhi, containing high beta carotene (8.0 to 10.0 ppm) as compared to other popular varieties of the crop5.

India is the second largest producer of cauliflower in the world after China having an area of 473 thousand hectares with a production of 9,225 thousand metric tonnes and productivity of 19.50 metric tonnes per hectares6. India fares better in productivity of cauliflower than world’s figure, however the country has a potential productivity of 35–40 metric tonnes per hectare as achieved by top cauliflower producing countries of the world7. The leading cauliflower growing states in India are West Bengal, Bihar, Haryana, Gujarat, Assam, Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Himachal Pradesh. In mid and high hills of the Himachal Pradesh, it is grown as an off-season crop during summer months, which sells at a premium price in plains hence providing lucrative returns to the farmers8. Besides growing Indian cauliflower groups, the hilly terrains of Himachal Pradesh are unique and compatible for growing and seed production of the temperate type commonly known as snowball group9.

The registration and protection of new and existing plant varieties in India are envisioned by the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmer’s Right Act, 200110 and are determined by the criteria of Distinctness, Uniformity, and Stability (DUS) of morphological traits. The database of cauliflower genotypes produced by morphological characterization may be valuable for the choice of suitable varieties to be compared against the varieties developed in India as and when required11. The enhancement of genetic diversity has been greatly aided by spontaneous mutations, recombination, and anthropogenic seed dispersion, since evolution of agriculture12,13. Even today, traditional plant breeding techniques are at the forefront of vegetable breeding, which has generated numerous superior varieties with improved traits. To begin any crop improvement programme, a full understanding of the amount of genetic diversity present for specific traits is required. The more genetic diversity present in the available germplasm, the better the possibilities of finding elite genotypes. To meet the growing need of the changing agricultural ecology, phenotypic and genotypic dissection of present germplasm is required to identify genetically diverse lines with desired traits14. Cauliflower curds come in a variety of shapes, sizes, colours and compactness. Since many of these features are quantitative and greatly affected by the environment, it is vital to categorize total variability into heritable and non-heritable components. Because yield is a complex and polygenic trait and is influenced by various plant characteristics. As a result, if the correlation and path analysis of a yield contributing characteristic with yield is available, one should consider them while selecting for yield. Furthermore, studies of genetic divergence provide an effective method for evaluating the divergence prevailing in the population and measuring the dynamics of differentiation at intra-cluster and inter-cluster cluster levels for the purpose to choose more diverse parents for hybridization programs15.

Diseases like black rot (Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris) and stalk rot (Sclerotinia sclerotiorum) are the principal factors restricting profitable cauliflower production in Western Himalayan region particularly Himachal Pradesh16. Black rot is one of the most serious diseases in vegetable Brassicas globally17. It has adverse impact on quality with yield losses of up to 10% to 50% in cauliflower have been documented under favourable environmental conditions18. Although nine pathogenic races of this gram-negative bacterium have been discovered19, but race 1 and 4 are predominant worldwide. Recently, race 1, 4, and 6 of the pathogen were discovered in India; of these, race 1 predominated in the majority of the states, followed by race 420. The stalk rot has also seriously threatened the cauliflower seed industry and the losses of 80–100 per cent in Saproon valley and 5–61 per cent in Katrain valley were reported21 whereas Khan22 reported losses up to 90 per cent in certain areas of Himachal Pradesh under favourable conditions. The stalk rot disease is favoured by high humidity and warm temperature. Thus, the crop is badly damaged at the time of curding and flowering. Therefore, availability of resistant genotypes is an eco-friendly and sustainable option for management of these two major diseases of cauliflower23. Hence, in view of these factors, the current research was, therefore, undertaken with the following objectives.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The investigation was conducted at the Research Farm of Department of Vegetable Science, Dr Yashwant Singh Parmar University of Horticulture and Forestry, Nauni, Solan (HP) during rabi, 2020–21. The Research Farm is situated at an altitude of 1276 m above mean sea level, lying between 30º 52’ North latitude and 77 º 11’ East longitude. The area of the farm falls under sub-humid, sub-temperate and mid-hill agro-climatic zone II of Himachal Pradesh. The important meteorological observations made during the period of investigation have been presented in the Fig. 1. The soil structure of the Farm is sandy loam to clay loam comprising of sand (46.09%), silt (32.12%) and clay (25.01%). The pH of the soil varied from 6.79 to 7.02.

Observations recorded

Observations with respect to morphological and horticultural traits were recorded on five randomly taken plants from each plot and their means were calculated for statistical analysis. All the genotypes were assessed with respect to morphological traits viz., seedling anthocyanin colouration of hypocotyl, stalk length, leaf attitude, leaf length, leaf width, leaf shape, leaf lobe, leaf colour, leaf waxiness, leaf puckering, curd covered by inner leaves, curd polar diameter, curd equatorial diameter, curd shape, curd doming, curd colour, curd knobbing, curd texture, curd compactness, curd anthocyanin colouration at maturity and curd maturity group. Further, all the genotypes were assessed with respect to horticultural traits: Days to 50 per cent curd harvesting, plant height (cm), number of leaves/plant, leaf length (cm), leaf breadth (cm), stalk length (cm), plant spread (cm2), curd equatorial diameter (cm), curd polar diameter (cm), curd size index (cm2), curd solidity (g/cm), gross plant weight (g), marketable curd weight (g), marketable curd yield (t/ha), net curd weight (g) and harvest index (%). Group constellation

Considering D2 as the square of the generalized statistical distance between a pair of populations (genotypes), all populations were grouped into number of clusters according to the method described by Rao33. The criterion used in clustering by this method is that any two genotypes belonging to the same cluster should, at least on an average, show a smaller D2 value than those belonging to two different clusters. In other words, if variety V1 and V2 are close together and variety V3 is distinct from both shown by this generalized distance then V1 and V2 will be grouped in the same cluster.

The average D2 values of all possible genotypes combinations in one cluster with those in the other were computed and its square root was used to represent the ‘statistical distance’ between two clusters.

Disease reaction studies

Collection of disease samples

Plants showing typical symptoms of black rot (Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris) and stalk rot (Sclerotinia sclerotiorum) were identified and the samples were taken to the laboratory for isolation of pure culture for further studies. Autoclave and hot air oven were used for the sterilization of glassware and media. All activities of pouring, isolation and inoculation were carried out in aseptic conditions using laminar airflow.

Preparation of culture media

For culture of Xanthomonas campestris pv. Campestris, the common basal medium for the isolation, purification, multiplication and maintenance is nutrient agar (NA) with 5.0 g peptone, 3.0 g beef extract, 15.0 g agar, 5.0 g sodium chloride and 1000 ml distilled water. For culture of Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, the common basal medium is potato dextrose agar (PDA) whose composition includes 200 g peeled potato, 20 g dextrose, 20 g agar–agar and 1000 ml distilled water. NA media and PDA media was sterilized in an autoclave at 1.05 kg/cm2 pressure for 15 min and later was poured in sterilized glass Petri plates (20 ml/plate)35.

Isolation of pathogens

The black rot and stalk rot pathogens i.e. Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris and S. sclerotiorum were isolated into pure culture under in vitro conditions.

Isolation of black rot pathogen

Infected leaves exhibiting characteristic symptoms were brought to the laboratory for isolation of the pathogen. Rectified spirit was used to swab the affected regions of the leaves, and then regularly cleaned in sterile distilled water. Small tissues of diseased portion were cut from the damaged leaves along the margin of the lesion using a sterile blade. The fragments were aseptically positioned in sterile water droplets on a flamed glass slide. Every piece was cut with a sterile tool to release the bacterial ooze. An additional set of similar slides was also checked for the presence of bacterial cells under the microscope. A loopful of bacterial suspension was streaked on sterilized nutrient agar plates under aseptic conditions. A loopful of bacterial suspension was streaked on nutritional agar plates that had been sterilized under aseptic circumstances. These Petriplates were incubated at 30 °C for 72 h and observed for colony formation of the pathogen. Circular, yellow and mucoid colonies of the bacterium were picked and further transferred to plate containing NA. These cultures were kept on NA slants at 5 °C for future research after being further purified using the streak plate procedure.

The culture was periodically sub-cultured at an interval of two weeks frequently. The pathogen was identified on the basis of morphological, cultural and biochemical characters as suggested by Schaad and Stall36.

Isolation of stalk rot pathogen

The diseased plants collected showing stalk rot symptoms were first washed in running water and then small bits consisting of both diseased and healthy portion of size 1–3 mm was cut using sterilized razor. They were surface sterilized using 1% of sodium hypochlorite for 10 to 20 s and simultaneously washed using distilled water for 3 times, then placed on a sterilized filter paper to remove excessive moisture and subsequently transferred to Petri plates containing PDA under aseptic conditions and incubated at 25 ± 1 °C for 7 days. These plates were observed daily for the fungal growth protruding out of the plant tissues. Growing colonies were marked and hyphal tips were transferred to petri plates containing PDA medium under aseptic conditions. Pure culture was obtained by repeated transfers of hyphal tips and purity was maintained by storing at 4 ± 1 °C in a refrigerator and were sub-cultured regularly at four weeks interval.

Screening against black rot

For in vitro and in vivo inoculation, the mass multiplication of the bacterium was carried out on nutrient broth. Sterilized nutrient broth in conical flasks was inoculated with bacteria and incubated for 48 h. Further inoculation of leaves and plants in pots was done by using 48 h old culture having 1 × 108 cfu/ml with injection infiltration method.

In vitro evaluation

The matured disease-free leaves from each of the thirty genotypes were collected from the field. These were surface sterilized using distilled water and then using ethanol with a cotton wool to make them free from microbes. Humidity of 95 ± 5 per cent inside the plastic trays was maintained by spraying water on plastic sheet used for covering the tray and the leaf surface and were incubated in BOD at 25 ± 1 °C. Mass multiplication of pathogen

The isolated pathogen was mass cultured on sand-maize medium. The crushed maize grains were boiled for 30–45 min till they get softened. Grain surplus water was taken off after boiling, and grains were spread out on blotting paper to air dry. After that, two percent sucrose was added to a mixture of three parts maize grains to one part sand. This mixture of sand, maize and sucrose was filled in conical flasks (250 ml) to 75 per cent of their capacity. These conical flasks were then plugged with non-absorbent cotton and these were then sterilized by the process of tyndallisation i.e. autoclaved at 1.05 kg/cm2 for one hour for 2–3 consecutive days. A seven-day-old pathogen culture was then aseptically set into the sterilized medium. Five to six bits of 4 mm size of fungi were placed in different sides of each flasks and incubated at 25 ± 1 °C for fifteen days in the BOD incubator under in vitro conditions. After three days, the inoculated flasks were shaken often to ensure that the culture expanded consistently for a full fifteen days. Following this, mass cultures of fungi were utilized for an assortment of pot experiments.

Seed sowing of different genotypes were done in the plastic pots (10 cm diameter). Five seedlings were maintained per pot in three replications per genotype. They were inoculated after one month of sowing to check the disease incidence on the plants. For inoculation, soil around the stalk was removed and culture was spread around it and covered with the removed soil. The inoculated plants were irrigated immediately and subsequent irrigation was given at 3 days interval through micro sprayer. The inoculated plants were then placed under polyethylene sheet at 25 °C and watered periodically through sprayer to maintain high humidity required for the occurrence of the disease. Results and discussion

Morphological characterization based on DUS

Morphological characters are beneficial for characterizing genotypes against highly stable and heritable traits. Further, association of any morphological character with desirable traits or yield components serves as a phenotypic marker in the selection process39. Characterization consists of recording those characters which are highly heritable, can be easily distinguished by naked eye and are expressed in all environments. Before starting any improvement programme, it is required to collect available germplasm and their characterization is very important for varietal improvement and selection40. Therefore, present investigation was undertaken to characterize the genotypes of cauliflower on the basis of qualitative and quantitative morphological characters and to establish distinctness among the newly developed genotypes along with old ones and the results obtained are discussed in detail here.

Performance of genotypes for leaf traits in cauliflower

The varieties were classified on the basis of seedling anthocyanin pigment of hypocotyls into two groups i.e. absent and present. All of the varieties expressed seedling anthocyanin colouration in them. For the attribute stalk length, the genotypes were classified into three groups based on the outer stem length i.e. short (< 0.5 cm), medium (0.5–1.0 cm) and long (> 1 cm) as per the DUS characterization. In the current investigation short stalk length was observed in Pusa Himjyoti, while all the other cultivars posseses medium stalk length. For the attribute leaf attitude, the genotypes were classified into three groups namely, erect, semi-erect and semi-spreading as per the DUS characterization. Erect and semi erect leaf attitude in cauliflower is desirable as it leads to self-blanching of curds thus improving overall appearance of the curds. Leaf length and breadth (cm)

Heritability and genetic gain (%)

The heritability estimates varied from 35.02% in curd polar diameter to 88.24% in plant height (Table 9 and Fig. 4). Path coefficient analysis

Although correlation studies are helpful in determining important yield contributing traits, but when we include more number of variables in the correlation studies the association becomes more intricate. In such cases, two characters may show correlation because they may correlate with a common third one. Under such cases, path coefficient analysis method was devised by Dewey and Lu31 which helps in partitioning the correlation coefficient under direct and indirect effects which permit a critical examination of the relative importance of each trait. Divergence study

Genetic diversity is important in all crossbreeding programmes because crossing genetically distinct parents results in a large number of required segregants. The clustering pattern of 30 genotypes of cauliflower has been presented in Table 12 and Fig. 6. Thirty genotypes of cauliflower were assembled into seven clusters by estimating D2 values in all the groupings of genotypes. Maximum eleven genotypes were received by cluster II, therefore making it the largest cluster. The second biggest cluster was cluster I having eight genotypes. Cluster III and V comprises four genotypes each. Minimum one genotype were received by cluster IV, VI and VII. Purpose is to group genotypes based on the characteristics they possess, so that individuals with similar descriptions are mathematically gathered into the same cluster.

Disease reaction studies

The present study pertains to evaluation of disease reaction in the cauliflower genotypes and explores the possibility of identifying a resistant genotype for use in breeding programme. Hausbeck and Lamour61 also showed that the development of resistant cultivars is less expensive and a sustainable alternative to other management practices.

Isolation of diseases

The pathogens of black rot (Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris) and stalk rot (Sclerotinia sclerotiorum) was successfully isolated from the naturally infected plants on a PDA and NA media and a pure culture was obtained by repeated transfers of hyphal tips and purity was maintained by storing cultures at 4 ± 1 °C in a refrigerator. The pathogen was reproduced on nutrient agar medium at 25 °C for three days. The culture was carefully scrapped from the medium with the help of sterilized slides. The scrapped bacterial culture was mixed in 100 ml sterilized distilled water and shaken vigorously to make a uniform suspension of bacterial cells in the water. The bacterial concentration was counted approximately 1 × 108 cfu/ml. The concentration was visually looking like a turbid, transluscent and light milky suspension.

Identification of diseases

Stalk rot

The culture of the Sclerotinia rot pathogen S. sclerotiorum was examined for cultural and morphological characteristics as well as sclerotial formation on PDA medium after 24 h interval. The pathogenic fungus was identified on the basis of morphological characters documented in taxonomic keys62 and the identity of the fungus was confirmed as Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (Lib.) de Bary. The fungus produced white cottony aerial mycelium and took 4–5 days to fully occupy the surface of Petri plate. The sclerotia were formed after 6–7 days of inoculation at the periphery and also in the middle of the colony forming rings. Initially sclerotia were white in colour then turned black and are round to irregular in shape.

Black rot

The pathogen was identified on the basis of cultural, morphological and biochemical characters as suggested by Schaad and Stall36. In cultural characteristic bacterial growth appeared as minute and transparent colonies after 48 h of incubation at 28 ± 1 °C. The colonies became light yellow after 72 h of incubation. The colour of bacterial colonies after 5 days of incubation at 28 ± 1 °C became pale yellow. The morphology of bacterial cell was also studied with staining methods i.e. Gram staining and Flagella staining. The study indicated that pathogen is gram negative, short rod with single polar flagellum, mostly single and rarely in chains.

Screening against stalk rot

Thirty genotypes of cauliflower were screened for disease reaction against stalk rot under in vitro and in vivo conditions by artificial inoculation method. The screening was conducted on a 1 to 4 scale as described by Dohroo and Korla38 (Table 4). It is evident from the data presented in Table 17 that among all genotypes screened against stalk rot pathogen, S. sclerotiorum, minimum incubation period was recorded in UHF-CAU-10 (104.00 h) followed by UHF-CAU-5 (112.00 h), UHF-CAU-5 (112.00 h), Pusa Sharad (128.00 h), KT-22 (128.00 h), UHF-CAU-7 (128.00 h), UHF-CAU-9 (128.00 h), Pusa Parijat (136.00 h), UHF-CAU-8 (136.00 h) KT-18 (136.00 h) and UHF-CAU-4 (152.00 h) though statistically at par with each other. However, maximum incubation period was found in Early London genotype of cauliflower (328.00 h) followed by White Heart (312.00 h), Early Champion (312.00 h) and White Rock (312.00 h). Similar trend was observed for lesion size, wherein minimum lesion size was observed in UHF-CAU-2 (18.63 mm) genotype followed by Albado (20.33 mm), Autumn King ( 20.33 mm), Improved Majestic (21.00 mm), Snowball Super (20.50 mm), UHF-CAU-9 (23.60 mm), UHF-CAU-8 (24.80 mm), Pusa Sharad (24.87 mm), UHF-CAU-3 (24.90 mm) and Early Snowball (25.00 mm) were statistically at par with each other. However, maximum lesion was recorded in Pusa Himjyoti (36.67 mm). Data further revealed that the minimum disease incidence was found in genotype Albado (6.33%), Improved Majestic (6.87%), Early Champion (7.67%), UHF-CAU-1 (8.47%), White Heart (8.33%), Pusa Himjyoti (8.60%), All the Year Round (8.63%), Autumn King (8.93%), PSBK-1 (9.20%), Early London ( 9.43%), UHF-CAU-3 (9.43%), UHF-CAU-2 (9.60%), White Rock (9.67%), PSBK-25 (9.70%) and UHF-CAU-6 (9.87%) though statistically at par with each other. None of the screened genotypes was found resistant to stalk rot, however, fifteen genotypes showed moderately resistant reaction and thirteen genotypes were categorized as susceptible. Two genotypes namely, Pusa Sharad and Pusa Parijat showed highly susceptible reaction.

Screening against black rot

Thirty genotypes of cauliflower were also screened for disease reaction against black rot under in vitro and in vivo conditions by artificial inoculation method and the data obtained after statistical analysis has been presented in Table 17 according to the scale given by William and co-workers37 (Table 3).

The minimum incubation period of 108.00 h was recorded in the genotype Pusa Himjyoti. This was followed by the genotypes White Rock (130.33 h), UHF-CAU-2 (133.33 h), Pusa Parijat (134.00 h) and Early London (134.00 h) though statistically at par with each other. The maximum incubation period was shown by the genotype White Heart (246.67 h) followed by UHF-CAU-5 (197.67 h) and UHF-CAU-10 (190.00 h). Data pertaining to the evaluation of genotypes under pot conditions revealed that thirty genotypes comprising commercial cultivar PSBK-1 were evaluated for black rot under pot conditions. The genotype Early Snowball (40.17%) showed highest black rot severity followed by the genotype White Heart (1.70%) was found to have lowest black rot severity and was found resistant. The genotypes Snowball Super and PSBK-1 were moderately resistant to disease, whereas the remaining twenty-seven genotypes were found susceptible.

Conclusion

The mean performance of genotypes for various traits revealed that genotype Pusa Himjyoti recorded the highest marketable curd yield per hectare and genotypes Snowball Super, White Heart, UHF-CAU-1, Early Snowball, UHF-CAU-4, UHF-CAU-8 and KT-18 were found to be statistically at par with Pusa Himjyoti. All these genotypes have white to snow white curd with self- blanching habit. The three best genotypes for different characters have been presented in Table 18. High heritability along with moderate genetic advance as per cent of mean was recorded in net curd weight indicated the occurrence of additive gene action. High heritability along with low genetic advance as per cent of mean was observed in plant height, leaf length, plant spread, stalk length and gross plant weight indicating the prevalence of non-additive gene interaction for these traits. Correlation coefficient revealed that the number of leaves per plant, plant spread, curd polar diameter, curd equatorial diameter, curd size index, curd solidity, gross plant weight and net curd weight were highly significantly and positively correlated to marketable curd weight. Thus, selection of these traits may be effective for selecting preferred genotypes with high marketable curd weight. The maximum positive direct effect on marketable curd weight was contributed by curd equatorial diameter, followed by curd polar diameter, curd solidity, net curd weight and number of leaves per plant. The D2 analysis grouped the genotypes into seven clusters consists maximum genotypes in cluster II. An analysis of percentage contribution of each trait towards diversity concluded that gross plant weight, plant height, net curd weight and marketable curd yield were the chief characters contributing to genetic diversity in cauliflower. The graphical architecture of the experimental results has been represented in Fig. 10. It was concluded that Pusa Himjyoti, Snowball Super and White Heart were the best genotypes as they gave higher marketable and net curd weight, comparatively fewer days to reach marketable maturity and also moderate resistance to black rot and stalk rot diseases (Fig. 11).
