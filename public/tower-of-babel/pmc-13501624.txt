Acupuncture for vascular mild cognitive impairment: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: An-Qi Cai, Le-Yan Hu, Yi-Ying Wang, Shuai Dong, Bo Li, Guo-Yan Yang, Jian-Ping Liu, Hui-Juan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVascular mild cognitive impairment (VMCI) is a potentially transitional state between normal aging and vascular dementia. The presence of macroscopic white matter lesions (WML) of moderate or severe extension on brain MRI is the hallmark of the vascular mild cognitive impairment.ObjectiveTo assess the effectiveness and safety of acupuncture for vascular mild cognitive impairment.MethodsSix electronic databases and two clinical trial websites were searched for randomized controlled trials which investigated the effects of acupuncture compared with sham acupuncture, drugs or usual care (such as cognitive rehabilitation). Studies comparing acupuncture combined with other therapies with this therapy alone for VMCI were also included. The quality of the trials selected was evaluated according to the Cochrane ‘risk of bias’ tool. RevMan V.5.4 software was employed for data analysis. Risk ratio (RR) or mean difference (MD) and their 95% confidence interval (CI) were used for a dichotomous or continuous variable, respectively.ResultsForty-nine trials with 3555 participants were included, with the exception of one trial judged to have a low risk of bias, 43 trials were all judged to have some concerns or a high risk of bias. Meta-analysis showed superior effect of acupuncture as add-on treatment to usual care on increasing the MMSE scores (MD 2.15, 95% CI 1.61 to 2.69, 7 trials, 536 participants, I2 = 22%, P < 0.00001). Five trials also found superior effect of body acupuncture as add-on treatment to usual care on increasing the MoCA scores (MD 2.50, 95% CI 1.80 to 3.20, 5 trials, 340 participants, I2 = 0%, P < 0.00001). Among the included studies, two studies reported adverse reactions.ConclusionsAcupuncture seems to be a promising treatment for vascular cognitive impairment to slow the progression of the disease and improve cognitive function. However, evidence is limited due to heterogeneity and the small number of included trials.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD42022382245.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03207-9.

Introduction

Vascular mild cognitive impairment (VMCI) refers to an abnormal condition caused by vascular diseases, in which patients show cognitive deficits that are not severe enough to meet the criteria for dementia [1]. The increasing incidence of stroke in low- to middle-income countries over the past four decades is likely explained by health and demographic transitions in these countries [2–5]. However, the global burden of stroke is likely underestimated by not accounting for silent strokes, transient ischaemic attacks in many cases, vascular dementia (VaD) and long-term stroke-related disability in case definition. Mild cognitive impairment is common in non-demented Parkinson’s disease patients (PD-MCI) and cerebrovascular disease, which is considered as a risk factor for dementia [6, 7]. A review suggests that 3 to 4% of the dementia in the population would be due to Parkinson’s disease. The estimated prevalence of Parkinson’s disease in the general population aged 65 years and over is 0.2 to 0.5% [8]. Brain imaging can distinguish whether cognitive impairment is neurodegenerative, vascular-related, or mixed. Neurodegenerative mild cognitive impairment (NMCI), prodromal for dementia of Alzheimer’s type (DAT), showed more medial temporal lobe atrophy with enlarged temporal horns, and fewer vascular lesions [9]. The presence of macroscopic white matter lesions (WML) of moderate or severe extension on brain MRI is the hallmark of the VMCI [6–10]. The global burden of ischaemic strokes is almost fourfold greater than haemorrhagic strokes. Current evidence suggests that 25–30% of ischaemic stroke survivors develop immediate or delayed vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) or vascular dementia (VaD) [11]. It is particularly important to identify vascular cognitive impairment at an early stage because its progression can be slowed or stopped by aggressive treatment of vascular risk factors and disease [12]. A 2010 meta-analysis showed that an estimated 35.6 million people worldwide are living with dementia, more than half of whom live in low- and middle-income countries, and this is expected to double by 2030 and reach 135 million by the end of 2050 [13]. Vascular mild cognitive impairment, the second most common cause of dementia, is considered to be associated with cerebrovascular dysfunction, and clinical intervention has the potential to stop the progression of the disease if detected at an early stage [14].

Vascular risk factors for vascular mild cognitive impairment are treatable, and appropriate treatment may prevent or delay progression to dementia. Therefore, this population is an excellent candidate for secondary prevention [15]. The most common underlying mechanism of vascular cognitive impairment is age-related chronic cerebral blood flow dysregulation of cerebral blood flow, but other factors such as inflammation and cardiovascular dysfunction also play a role [16], and multimodal strategies to control these risk factors, including lifestyle modification, are the most promising strategies to treat and prevent vascular cognitive impairment. Acupuncture intervention on vascular mild cognitive impairment can not only improve clinical symptoms, but also delay the progress of the disease. Modern medical research has proved that acupuncture can improve cognitive function by affecting central neurotransmitters, vasoactive substances, oxygen free radicals, cell apoptosis and other links [17, 18].

Seventeen systematic reviews (SRs) were published from 2013 to 2022 (Table 1, Supplementary Material 1). Results of quality evaluation of the 17 SRs by PRISMA and AMSTAR-II tools showed generally good reporting quality and poor methodological quality. Overall low or very low quality evidence showed better effect of acupuncture single used or combined with other treatments on improving cognitive function than controls through assessing Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Barthel Index (BI), Activities of Daily Living (ADL) or other scales. However, limited evidence was concluded regarding the safety of acupuncture for VCI. The latest SR covering the largest number of included trials was published in 2021, which included 48 trials involving 3778 patients with VCI. Due to poor methodological quality and considerable heterogeneity among trials, the certainty of the evidence was low or very low. Though there were several SRs that reported the clinical evidence of acupuncture for VCI, only three of them focused on the effect of acupuncture for VMCI, one of these reviews focused on acupuncture for specific subtypes of cognitive impairment such as post-stroke cognitive impairment. Another study, in which subgroup analyses were attempted to reduce the heterogeneity for different acupuncture styles; but as a complicated intervention, the success of acupuncture in treatment depends on multiple elements, which always differed among studies. The remaining SRs did not clearly distinguish the effect of acupuncture on different degrees of cognitive impairment. In 2013, we published the first systematic review synthesizing the evidence on the efficacy and safety of acupuncture for this disease, and the meta-analysis at that time showed that acupuncture combined with other therapies could significantly improve Mini-Mental State Examination scores (mean difference 1.99, 95% CI 1.09 to 2.88, random model, P < 0.0001, 6 trials). The other two reviews involved few trials and were also published 7 years ago. Considering the update and improvement of clinical research in recent years, this study aims to update the evidence-based medical evidence of the efficacy and safety of acupuncture in the treatment of vascular mild cognitive impairment, and establish a valuable supplement to the previous review.

Methods

The review was undertaken and reported in accordance with the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [19]. A detailed prospective protocol of this study was registered at PROSPERO (Center for Reviews and Dissemination, University of York, No. CRD42022382245) before formal commencement. There was no deviation from the approved protocol.

Inclusion criteria

Randomized controlled trials for the treatment of VMCI using acupuncture as the primary intervention were published without restrictions on language and type of presentation, and participants were not limited by age, sex, and severity of VMCI. Participants were diagnosed with VMCI according to any accepted criteria, such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-III, IIIR, IV), International Classification of Diseases Editions 10 and 11 (ICD-10, ICD-11). In the control group, treatment included no treatment, sham acupuncture, and basal drug therapy, as well as studies comparing acupuncture combined with other therapies versus acupuncture alone for VMCI. The primary outcome measure was an internationally recognized cognitive performance scale. For example, the Mini-mental state examination (MMSE), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA), and Assessment) were used. MOCA), activities of daily living Scale (ADL). The secondary outcomes included Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS), Clock Drawing Test (CDT), and the incidence of dementia. Safety outcome measures included the occurrence of adverse events and serious adverse events.

Plagiarized literature was excluded.

Identification and selection of studies

The databases including China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), China Biology Medicine Disc (CBM), Wanfang Data, PubMed and Cochrane Library, We also searched two Clinical Trial websites, clinicaltrials.gov and Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR). Our 2013 study retrieved China’s National Knowledge Infrastructure (1979–2012), China Scientific Journal Database (1989–2012), Wanfang (Papers and Conference Papers) Database (1985–2012), PubMed (1966–2012), and the Cochrane Library (2012 No. Issue 2). The retrieval period of the update was from January 1, 2012, to October 31, 2022. Two researchers (An-Qi Cai and Le-Yan Hu) independently screened the literature according to the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two researchers (An-Qi Cai and Le-Yan Hu) independently extracted data from the included studies. The extracted contents included: name and title of study authors, year of publication, diagnostic criteria, study size, age and sex of participants, methodological information, type of acupuncture, treatment process, control interventions, outcomes, and adverse events. Two reviewers independently evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies. According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions 6.3 [20], the methodological quality of studies was divided into three levels: low risk of bias, high risk of bias, and uncertain risk, which can be judged according to the proportion of results judged by the corresponding items. If all the low risks are low risks, high risks are high risks and no high risks but uncertain risks are uncertain risks.

Data analysis

Data analysis and quantitative synthesis were performed by Review Manager software (RevMan 5.4). After outcome measurement, the data obtained for ordinal variables were summarized as relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and the data for continuous variables were summarized as mean difference (mean difference, MD) and 95% CI. Clinical heterogeneity was determined from the aspects of study design, study subjects, intervention measures, control methods and outcome measures, and statistical heterogeneity was measured by I2 heterogeneity test. The clinical homogeneity was good and the statistical heterogeneity was low (I2 < 75%) were analyzed by meta-analysis. When I2 ≤ 25%, the fixed effect model was used for data synthesis,Random effects model was used for data synthesis when 25% < I2 ≤ 75%. Subgroup analyses were performed to account for statistical heterogeneity across trials. If there was significant statistical heterogeneity (I2 > 75%) between trials that could not be explained or handled by subgroup analysis, data were not pooled. Subgroup analyses was used to explore the source of heterogeneity among the trials. Subgoups were planned to be classified by gender, treatment duration (more than 3 months/less than 3 months), type of vascular mild cognitive impairment (single cognitive domain cognitive impairment type/single cognitive domain impairment was non-amnestic/multiple cognitive domains impairment was non-amnestic/amnestic type with multiple cognitive domains impairment), type of acupuncture (manual acupuncture or electroacupuncture), methods of acupoint selection (syndrome differentiation or not) or intensity of the needling insertion (deep insertion or shallow insertion). Removing low-quality trials or using different analytical models (fixed effect model or random effect model) were used to conduct sensitivity analysis to examine the stability of the pooling analysis. Publication bias was detected by funnel plot if there are more than 10 trials include in one meta-analysis.

Assessment of the certainty of the evidence

The quality of evidence for the primary outcome measures was assessed using the online version of Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) Guideline Development Tool (GDT) (https://gradepro.org/). The level of the evidence is assessed as high, moderate, low or very low according to the limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias of the evidence. We reported the evidence quality of the key indicators in the summary of findings tables for each comparison.

Results

Research process inclusion

Microsoft Excel was utilized to compile an electronic data extraction form and manage the information extracted from the eligible articles, including the general characteristics of the study, eligibility criteria, participant demographics, interventions, weight-related outcomes, and AEs. All data were extracted and cross-checked by the investigators to ensure accuracy (An-Qi Cai and Le-Yan Hu). As to trials with multiple therapy groups, irrelevant data from other arms were not extracted for analysis. Finally, a total of 49 trials were included in the review after screening titles, abstracts, and full texts of citations retrieved from literature searches (refer to Fig. 1 for study flow). The needle retention time of most included trials is 20–45 min, of which 30 min is the most (in 39 trials). Three trials with scalp acupuncture kept the needles for more than 6 h. The average treatment duration was 49 days, which ranged from 10 to 180 days in the 49 included trials.

Outcome measurements reported in the included trials were MMSE (in 35 trials), MoCA (in 28 trials), ADL (in 17 trials). BI (in 8 trials), functional comprehensive assessment (FCA, in 1 trial) and WMS (Wechsler Memory Scale) (in 1 trial).

Risk of bias of the included trials

Thirty-one trials described randomization procedures, including central randomization and the use of random number tables and computer software, however, only 8 of them reported using opaque envelopes for allocation concealment. Two trials which used sham acupuncture reported the procedure for blinding the participants. Though the other trials did not mention blinding method, it is hard to blind the participants without sham control. Four trials indicated there were no dropouts, five reported dropouts, and none used intention-to-treat analysis. Sample size calculation was not reported in all included trials, and no trial registered its protocol in advance. Therefore, with the exception of one trial judged to have a low risk of bias, 19 trials were judged to have some concerns on the risk of bias, the remaining 29 trials were judged to have a high risk of bias (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Risk of bias graph: review authors' judgments about each risk of bias item presented aspercentages across all included studies

Estimate effects of the acupuncture treatment

Subgroup analysis based on different genders, type of VCMI, treatment courses, methods of acupoint selection or intensity of the needling insertion cannot be conducted as there is no available data. Thus, we conduct subgroup analysis according to type of acupuncture or type of medication in the control group (NMDA receptor antagonist, cholinesterase inhibitor, etc.). The reason for adding subgroup comparisons of different control drugs is that we did find differences in the effectiveness of acupuncture among subgroups. This not only explains the heterogeneity of some meta-analyses, but may also provide suggestions for clinical practice.

Acupuncture compared to sham acupuncture

One trial found better effect of acupuncture on increasing MMSE scores (MD 1.67, 95% CI 0.70 to 2.64, 1 trial, 60 participants) than sham control, but no difference on increasing MoCA scores (MD 1.50, 95% CI − 0.19 to 3.19, 1 trial, 60 participants) between groups.

Acupuncture plus usual care compared to usual care

Twelve trials compared acupuncture plus usual care to usual care alone. Ten of them were included in 2 meta-analysis. No subgroup analysis was conducted since it could not interpret the source of the heterogeneity among trials.

MMSE

Seven trials reported the MMSE scores which showed superior effect of acupuncture (including body acupuncture and scalp acupuncture) as add on treatment to usual care on increasing the MMSE scores (MD 2.15, 95% CI 1.61 to 2.69, 7 trials, 536 participants, I2 = 22%, P < 0.00001, Fig. 3).Fig. 3Forest plot of acupuncture plus usual care versus usual care on improving cognitive impairment according to MMSE scores

Acupuncture plus rehabilitation training versus rehabilitation training

Fifteen trials employed rehabilitation training as basic treatment and evaluated the adjunctive effect of acupuncture. One trial selected acupoints around eye area and achieved different results from other trials chose body acupoints. Thus, we conducted subgroup meta-analysis to explore the potential difference of effect between different area of needling insertion.

MMSE

Nine trials reported the improvement of cognitive function according to MMSE scores. Meta-analysis (Fig. 5) showed better effect of body acupuncture (MD 1.54, 95% CI 0.76 to 2.33, 8 trials, 497 participants, I2 = 56%, P = 0.0001) and even larger effect of eye acupuncture (MD 3.52, 95% CI 1.47 to 5.57, 1 trial, 86 participants) combined rehabilitation training on this outcome compared to rehabilitation training alone. Acupuncture plus drugs versus drugs

Among the 16 trials compared combination of acupuncture and drugs to drugs alone, 14 trials were included in 3 meta-analysis to assess the adjunctive effect of acupuncture for VMCI patients. MMSE

Adverse events

Among the included studies, only 2 studies reported adverse reactions. In one study [48], there was 1 case of nausea and vomiting and 1 case of dizzy during the treatment in the observation group, and the total incidence of adverse reactions was 4.4%(2/45). There were 2 cases of nausea and vomiting in the control group during the treatment. The overall reaction rate was 4.4% (2/45). There was no significant difference in the total incidence of adverse reactions between the two groups (P > 0.05). In another study [55], a total of 6 patients in the observation group had adverse reactions, including 4 patients with dizziness, 2 patients with subcutaneous hematoma and local slight bruising after needle initiation, and the above adverse reactions did not affect the subsequent clinical treatment.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Based o ofn the above research resuan lts, ac ofupuncture as an adjunctive therapy may improve the cognitive function of VMCI patients, and increase the MMSE score by average 2 points, MoCA score by average 2 points. Among the included clinical trials, the three most selected acupuncture points were Bai-hui(GV20), Shen-ting(GV24) and Si-shen-cong(EX-HN1). Subgroup meta-analysis did not find the influence of factors such as syndrome differentiation, electric stimulation, duration of treatment on the therapeutic effect, but suggested that the effect of eye acupuncture might be better. Expert consensus is more recommended to select acupoints based on syndrome differentiation, longer needle retention time, and more frequent treatment sessions.

Advantages and limitations

At present, many systematic reviews have been published on the effects of acupuncture on vascular cognitive impairment, but most of them did not evaluate mild cognitive impairment independently, nor did they explain the source of heterogeneity in the pooled analysis. This study focused solely on mild cognitive impairment, providing evidence-based medical evidence for the efficacy and safety of clinical acupuncture in the treatment of vascular mild cognitive impairment, and establishing a valuable complement to the update of previous reviews.

The main limitations of this report are that we finally included 49 randomized controlled trials by searching six databases, but we did not search the relevant clinical trial websites, and the general methodology and reporting quality of the included studies were unsatisfactory. Many authors do not detail their randomization procedures, assignment hiding, and evaluation blindness, which leads to a degree of potential selection bias and detection bias. In addition, only two trials established sham acupuncture in the control group. The lack of high-quality randomized controlled trials inevitably hinders reliable evaluation of acupuncture’s efficacy. Besides, in the selection of outcome measurements, in addition to the generally used cognitive function scores (e.g., MMSE, MoCA), the time of disease progression can also be considered to evaluate whether acupuncture treatment can delay the progression of mild cognitive impairment to dementia; the health economic evaluation should also be considered to comprehensively evaluate the advantages of acupuncture as an adjunctive therapy.

Implications for clinical practice

Meta-analysis showed that acupuncture and moxibustion for vascular cognitive impairment seemed to be a promising treatment, as a supplement to routine care, significantly improved MMSE score and even better improved MoCA score. It is suggested that timely intervention of clinical acupuncture on the basis of syndrome differentiation in patients with vascular mild cognitive impairment, or combined with related cerebrovascular drugs or rehabilitation exercises and other therapeutic measures, can slow down the progress of the disease and improve cognitive function, which has important clinical value and social significance. Meanwhile, the existing results suggest that in acupuncture treatment, in addition to the acupoint selection based on syndrome differentiation, appropriate extension of needle retention time and periodic regular treatment according to traditional Chinese medicine theory, some special parts of acupuncture treatment such as eye acupuncture and scalp acupuncture are worthy of further clinical research and exploration.

Implications for future studies

Most clinical trials of acupuncture in the treatment of vascular mild cognitive impairment have been retrieved from Chinese databases. However, most randomized-controlled studies are not satisfactory in the quality of design, implementation process, and reporting of outcome indicators. Some key elements, such as the generation, grouping, and allocation of random sequences, are scientifically rigorous and high-quality. Both concealment and blindness have parts that are not specified or properly described. Therefore, it is suggested that future studies should be designed adequately and scrupulously in terms of prospective registration, randomization, blind method and outcome evaluation to objectively evaluate the actual efficacy of acupuncture and moxibustion, so as to provide high-quality evidence for clinical practice. The relevant scales for vascular cognitive impairment assessment are also worthy of comprehensive and rigorous assessment to avoid similar indicators and different score norms.

The existing health economic evaluation of acupuncture and moxibustion treatment points out that the relevant research on health economics of acupuncture and moxibustion mainly focuses on cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), cost-utility analysis (CUA) and cost–benefit analysis (CBA). CEA is a relatively mature health economic evaluation method at present. In the face of the same treatment effect, the smaller the cost-effectiveness ratio (CER) indicates the smaller the acquisition cost, indicating that this method has good economic value. The report on the economics of acupuncture and moxibustion also pointed out that the combined application of acupuncture and rehabilitation should be popularized in the top three hospitals and large and medium-sized cities with solid economic foundation, perfect basic equipment, and more emphasis on maximizing curative effect. It is more suitable for the promotion and popularization of acupuncture therapy in basic hospitals and poor mountainous areas with relatively insufficient economic strength and limited medical resources. Studies have analyzed the health economic evaluation of acupuncture in the treatment of dysphagia after stroke, using the functional oral feeding scale FOIS score as the index and the related costs of the treatment of dysphagia as the cost. For the recovery of neurological impairment, the cost required by acupuncture group is lower than that of the control group to achieve the same therapeutic effect. Domestic and foreign studies have found that acupuncture and moxibustion has good clinical efficacy, low cost and excellent overall evaluation, but the lack of cooperation between domestic and foreign institutions and teams also suggests that future clinical and scientific research is very important to further explore the health economic evaluation of acupuncture and moxibustion as a complementary treatment of VMCI.

In addition, for the outcome indicators of future research, it is basic and important to evaluate the related scales of cognitive ability level, but more attention should be paid to the prognosis of vascular mild cognitive impairment treatment and later follow-up studies, such as increasing the rate of vascular dementia obtained by follow-up in the outcome indicator report. It is believed to be of great benefit to further explore the treatment of vascular cognitive impairment and the prevention of dementia.

Conclusions

Although the trails included in the updated systematic review still have limitations, such as small sample size and poor methodology quality, resulting in low-quality evidence, based on the results of quantitative analysis and qualitative interviews, along with reference to basic research findings, we maintain that acupuncture therapy can be used as an adjunct therapy to drug therapy or rehabilitation training, and can be reasonably applied in the early stage of diagnosis and treatment to improve the cognitive function of patients with mild vascular cognitive impairment, delay disease progression, and improve the quality of life of patients in the later stage.
