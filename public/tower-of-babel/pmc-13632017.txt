Agatston-2.0: A next-generation AI-based coronary calcium quantification approach to improve risk stratification among individuals with zero Agatston scores – Part I
Authors: Morteza Naghavi, Amir Azimi, Kyle Atlas, Anthony P Reeves, Chenyu Zhang, Seyed Reza Mirjalili, Mohammadhossein Mozafarybazargany, Amir Ghaffari Jolfayi, Ali Hashemi, Thomas Atlas, Jakob Wasserthal, Rozemarijn Vliegenthart, Claudia I Henschke, David F Yankelevitz, Andrea D Branch, Sion K Roy, Jamal S Rana, Zahi A Fayad, Oren Mechanic, Koen Nieman, Jagat Narula, Roxana Mehran, Prediman K Shah, John A Rumberger, Kim A Williams Sr, Paolo Raggi, David J Maron, Michael V McConnell, Robert A Kloner, Matthew J Budoff, Nathan D Wong, Arthur S Agatston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA coronary artery calcium (CAC) score of zero using the conventional Agatston scoring method (Agatston-1.0) is associated with very low cardiovascular risk (the “Power of Zero”); however, a small proportion of individuals with CAC=0 still develop coronary heart disease (CHD). Agatston-1.0 relies on thick slices (2.5–3 mm) and a fixed attenuation threshold (≥130 HU), which may miss early, small, low-density, or partially calcified coronary plaques. Agatston-2.0 is an artificial intelligence (AI) framework for automated coronary segmentation and continuous voxel-wise calcium quantification without fixed thresholds, applicable to CT scans with slice thickness ≥0.2 mm and generating an AI-derived CAC score (AI-CAC).ObjectivesTo evaluate the prognostic value of Agatston-2.0 for risk stratification among individuals with a baseline CAC=0.MethodsWe pooled 3,965 participants with CAC=0 from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA, n=2,816) and the Framingham Heart Study (FHS, n=1,149). Associations with incident CHD were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models with up to 20 years of follow-up.ResultsAn AI-CAC score>0 was detected in 862 participants (21.7%) in population with CAC=0. Participants with AI-CAC>0 had higher 20-year CHD incidence than those with AI-CAC=0 (7.7% vs. 3.8%, p<0.0001). After adjustment for traditional risk factors, AI-CAC>0 remained independently associated with incident CHD (HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.18–2.47). AI-CAC also predicted progression to positive CAC score (adjusted HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.70–2.24).ConclusionsThe Agatston-2.0 framework identifies clinically meaningful coronary calcification in individuals classified as CAC=0. If validated in additional cohorts, Agatston-2.0 could become the new standard for coronary calcium scoring.Keywords: Coronary artery calcium, Coronary heart disease, Artificial intelligence, Agatston score

Introduction

In 1990, Agatston and colleagues introduced quantitative assessment of coronary artery calcium (CAC) using ultrafast computed tomography (CT), establishing the conventional Agatston scoring method (Agatston-1.0) as the foundation for modern noninvasive evaluation of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis[1]. Over the subsequent three decades, large prospective studies confirmed CAC as a powerful predictor of coronary events across diverse racial and ethnic populations, firmly establishing CAC quantification as one of the most robust, reproducible, and cost-effective tools for coronary artery disease risk assessment in asymptomatic individuals[[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Reflecting this evidence base, the recently published 2026 ACC/AHA/Multisociety Guideline on the Management of Dyslipidemia has further cemented CAC as a cornerstone of primary prevention, formally incorporating CAC-specific LDL-C treatment goals and CAC-guided risk reclassification into the standard of care[8]. Importantly, a recent publication by an expert panel stated that CAC scanning should be considered as a diagnostic test and called on healthcare payers and policymakers to reimburse CAC when used accordingly[9]. This conceptual reframing reflects the maturation of CAC from a population-level risk stratification tool into a precise diagnostic modality capable of detecting subclinical atherosclerotic coronary artery disease.

A defining strength of Agatston-1.0 lies in its exceptionally high negative predictive value. Individuals with a CAC score of zero experience remarkably low short- and intermediate-term rates of cardiovascular events, a phenomenon widely referred to as the "power of zero”. This strong negative predictive value supports clinical decisions to defer pharmacologic therapy, reduce unnecessary downstream testing, and improve healthcare efficiency[[10], [11], [12], [13]].

Yet despite this strong prognostic reassurance, accumulating evidence demonstrates that a small subset of individuals with CAC scores of zero experience incident coronary heart disease (CHD) events over long-term follow-up[14,15]. Coronary CT angiography studies reveal that up to 4% of individuals with zero CAC scores harbor coronary plaques associated with adverse outcomes[[16], [17], [18]], and individuals with CAC scores of 1–10 experience significantly higher event rates than those with CAC=0[16,17], underscoring the clinical relevance of detecting even small amounts of coronary calcification. Several technical limitations inherent to the Agatston-1.0 likely contribute to this residual risk. The scoring system relies on rigid thresholds established in the late 1980s: a minimum lesion area of 1 mm² and an attenuation threshold of 130 Hounsfield units (HU) — parameters defined during the era of electron-beam CT and unchanged despite dramatic advances in spatial resolution, detector sensitivity, and noise reduction in contemporary scanners[1]. These fixed criteria, combined with the required 2.5–3 mm slice thickness, inherently exclude small, fragmented, or semi-calcified lesions, many of which fall below detection limits due to partial-volume averaging and image noise[19,20]. Furthermore, Agatston-1.0 depends on peak voxel intensity, rendering it vulnerable to stochastic noise and interscan variability, thereby limiting reproducibility[19,21,22].

To address these limitations, we introduce Agatston-2.0, a next-generation framework that replaces rigid attenuation and area thresholds with a continuous, voxel-wise probabilistic model incorporating relative density calibration, spatial neighborhood analysis, and fully automated deep learning–based coronary segmentation. Unlike the Agatston-1.0, Agatston-2.0 can be applied to CT scans with any slice thickness greater than 0.2 mm, generating an AI-derived CAC score (AI-CAC). While prior studies have explored alternative CAC scoring techniques — including lower HU thresholds, thinner slice reconstructions, individualized attenuation thresholds, and spatial weighting strategies[15,20,[23], [24], [25], [26]] — a fully automated, scalable, and outcome-validated AI-based framework applicable to large populations has remained lacking. To fill this gap, we pooled data from two large prospective cohorts — the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) and the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) — and tested the hypothesis that AI-CAC based on the Agatston-2.0 framework detects occult coronary calcium in a substantial proportion of individuals with CAC=0, and that the magnitude of AI-CAC is independently associated with incident CHD events over extended follow-up. We additionally assessed whether AI-CAC predicts conversion from CAC=0 to a positive CAC score over time.

Methods

Study population

Individual participant–level data were pooled from two prospective cohort studies: MESA and FHS. Detailed study designs, recruitment procedures, and event adjudication methods have been previously published[[27], [28], [29], [30]].

Briefly, MESA is a multi-ethnic, community-based cohort study that enrolled 6,814 participants aged 45–84 years without known cardiovascular disease between 2000 and 2002 across six U.S. field centers (Baltimore, MD; Los Angeles, CA; Chicago, IL; Forsyth County, NC; New York, NY; and St. Paul, MN). Participants underwent comprehensive baseline assessments and electrocardiographically gated CT for CAC quantification.

The FHS Offspring cohort enrolled 5,124 participants beginning in 1971, and the FHS Third Generation cohort enrolled 4,095 participants between 2002 and 2005. CAC assessment was performed using multidetector CT (MDCT) as part of dedicated FHS CT ancillary studies. Participants from both the Offspring and Third Generation cohorts who underwent MDCT imaging during the second MDCT examination cycle (MDCT II, 2008–2011) were included. A total of 2,354 participants from the combined FHS Offspring and Third Generation cohorts underwent CAC scanning and were eligible for the present analysis.

For the present analysis, participants were excluded if they declined authorization for non-commercial data use, had incomplete or unavailable CAC CT scans, had a CAC >0 at baseline, or had missing cardiovascular outcome data or unavailable AI-CAC metrics (Fig. 1).

All participants in both cohorts provided written informed consent at recruitment. Ethical approval was obtained from Institutional Review Boards at all participating centers, and the study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This analysis follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

CT imaging protocols

In MESA, baseline non-contrast cardiac CT scans were performed using two different modalities across the six field centers. Electron-beam CT (EBCT) scanners were employed at three sites (New York, Illinois, and California), while MDCT systems were used at the remaining three sites (Maryland, Minnesota, and North Carolina). EBCT scans were prospectively gated at 50% of the R–R interval, whereas MDCT scans used 80% gating. All scans were acquired during a mid-inspiratory breath-hold and extended from the carina through the cardiac apex, capturing adjacent thoracic structures including the liver and spleen. Reconstruction parameters were standardized across all sites: 2.5 mm slice thickness, 512 × 512 matrix, 350 mm field of view, and 0.68 mm in-plane pixel spacing. In MESA Exam 1, participants underwent two sequential non-contrast cardiac CT scans during the same visit, with repositioning between acquisitions to assess interscan variability of CAC measurements[31].

In FHS, CAC imaging was performed during the MDCT II examination cycle (2008–2011). Participants underwent non-contrast CT scans in the supine position at full inspiration using a 64-detector-row scanner (Discovery, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI). Imaging parameters included 120 kVp, body weight–adjusted tube current (300–350 mA), and a gantry rotation time of 350 ms, acquired at 0.68 mm in-plane resolution and 2.5 mm slice thickness[32].

In both cohorts, a calibration phantom containing hydroxyapatite reference rods of known calcium density (e.g., 0, 50, 100, and 200 mg/cm³) was positioned beneath the participant during scanning to enable scanner-specific attenuation calibration.

Agatston-2.0 framework

CAC was quantified using Agatston-2.0, a fully automated AI-based framework applied to non-contrast cardiac CT scans, generating a continuous AI-CAC score. Unlike Agatston-1.0, which relies on fixed attenuation thresholds and two-dimensional lesion identification, Agatston-2.0 introduces a fully automated, three-dimensional, voxel-wise probabilistic framework designed to enhance sensitivity, robustness, and biological fidelity, particularly for early-stage, low-burden coronary calcification. A schematic overview of the complete Agatston-2.0 framework, including coronary artery segmentation, intensity calibration, relative density weighting, and spatial neighborhood filtering, is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Schematic overview of the AI-assisted coronary artery calcium (Agatston-2.0) quantification framework. Step 1: Automated deep learning–based segmentation of the coronary artery tree, including the left main, left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries. Step 2: AI-assisted intensity calibration and noise normalization using physical phantom measurements when available, or patient-derived reference tissues when phantoms are absent. Step 3: Voxel-wise relative density weighting using a continuous probabilistic transformation to enhance sensitivity to low-burden and small calcifications, particularly in thin-slice CT acquisitions. Spatial near-neighbor filtering is applied to suppress noise and aggregate biologically coherent calcification, with the final AI-CAC score computed as the sum of all weighted voxels within the coronary artery mask. Right panel: Comparison of conventional Agatston-1.0 and Agatston-2.0 approaches. Traditional Agatston-1.0 relies on thick-slice CT (∼3.0 mm), which may miss small or low-density calcifications due to partial volume effects. In contrast, Agatston-2.0 leverages thin-slice imaging (∼0.6 mm) and probabilistic voxel-wise weighting to improve detection of small and early calcifications, thereby enhancing sensitivity to subclinical coronary artery disease.

Coronary artery segmentation was performed using an ensemble of deep learning models based on the nnU-Net architecture. The models incorporated a skeleton-recall loss function specifically designed to enhance detection of thin, tubular vascular structures while maintaining computational efficiency[32].

Across the overall AI-CAC coronary segmentation development pipeline, a total of 840 imaging series were used. An initial binary coronary segmentation model was developed using 400 paired imaging series consisting of manually annotated high-resolution contrast-enhanced coronary CT angiograms (CCTA; 0.33-mm resolution) and corresponding non-contrast CT scans (1-mm slice thickness), with all labels curated by human experts. This preliminary model was used to generate candidate coronary labels for a subsequent set of 440 additional scans, including 350 paired, registered CCTA and upsampled non-contrast CT scans (0.62-mm resolution) and 90 high-resolution non-contrast CAC scans acquired at 0.62-mm resolution. All candidate labels in this independent set were reviewed in detail, corrected as needed, and relabeled by human experts into four coronary branches, including the left main, left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries. The resulting 440 expert-refined annotated series were then used to train the final multiclass coronary segmentation model used for this analysis. To optimize performance on non-contrast CT, post-processing steps, including connected-component analysis and vessel skeletonization, were applied to refine coronary artery masks and suppress non-vascular structures. Detailed information on model training, validation, and testing is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Within the segmented coronary artery tree, calcification was quantified using a voxel-wise probabilistic scoring approach adapted from previously described spatially weighted CAC scoring methods. Each voxel was assigned a continuous weight reflecting the probability of true CAC rather than applying a fixed HU threshold. Intensity normalization utilized phantom-based calibration when available. When physical phantoms were not present, cohort-consistent calibration was performed based on scanner protocol parameters and internal reference distributions derived from quality-controlled scans. Voxel-wise probability maps were further refined using two-dimensional and three-dimensional spatial neighborhood analysis to account for clustering of calcium, a key feature distinguishing true calcified plaque from image noise or isolated artifacts. Voxels with high attenuation surrounded by similarly high-attenuation neighboring voxels were up-weighted, whereas isolated voxels were down-weighted using a convolution-based approach. The final AI-CAC score was calculated as the sum of all non-zero weighted voxels within the coronary artery mask, yielding a continuous, Agatston-like measure of coronary calcium burden without reliance on fixed HU cutoffs.

Outcome definitions

Incident CHD outcomes were ascertained and adjudicated in both MESA and FHS using standardized surveillance procedures and physician review. In MESA, hard CHD was defined as incident myocardial infarction (MI), resuscitated cardiac arrest, or CHD death, while all CHD was defined as incident MI, resuscitated cardiac arrest, definite angina, probable angina followed by coronary revascularization, or CHD death, consistent with MESA Steering Committee–approved combination endpoint definitions. Participants were contacted every 9–12 months to identify interim hospitalizations, outpatient cardiovascular diagnoses and procedures, and deaths. Medical records and death certificates were obtained, and events were classified by paired independent physician reviewers with adjudication by committee when disagreements persisted. MI was classified as definite or probable based primarily on combinations of symptoms, electrocardiographic findings, and cardiac biomarker levels. Angina required symptoms with supporting clinical evidence, and probable angina required subsequent revascularization for inclusion in the all-CHD definition. Two additional ASCVD composite outcomes were defined in MESA using the same adjudicated surveillance framework. PREVENT ASCVD was defined as a composite of incident MI (recognized and unrecognized), ischemic stroke (brain infarction only, excluding hemorrhagic subtypes), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular death attributable to coronary heart disease, stroke, or other atherosclerotic disease. All ASCVD was defined as a broader composite additionally including angina pectoris, coronary revascularization (percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting), transient ischemic attack, and peripheral arterial disease including claudication and abdominal aortic aneurysm, with each component ascertained and adjudicated using established MESA endpoint criteria. Median follow-up in MESA was 17.95 years (interquartile range 15.24–18.58), with a maximum of 19.43 years.

Agatston score progression was assessed as a secondary outcome in MESA only, restricted to participants with at least one follow-up coronary CT scan at Exam 2, 3, 4, or 5. Progression was defined as development of any detectable coronary calcium (Agatston score >0) at any follow-up examination.

In FHS, cardiovascular outcomes were identified through serial in-person examinations, continuous surveillance of hospital admissions, outpatient medical record review, and systematic follow-up of deaths. MI was diagnosed using standardized criteria incorporating symptoms, electrocardiographic changes, cardiac enzyme elevations, and physician documentation, encompassing both recognized and silent events. Hard CHD included MI, resuscitated cardiac arrest, and CHD death; all CHD additionally included angina pectoris and coronary insufficiency, identified through physician assessment and clinical documentation review. All suspected events were adjudicated by FHS physicians using comprehensive review of data from hospitals, outpatient clinics, and the research center, in accordance with established FHS criteria. Two additional ASCVD composite outcomes were defined in FHS using the same surveillance framework, restricted to Generation 2 and Generation 3 cohort participants, with only events occurring after the index cardiac CT scan date included to ensure incident-only ascertainment. PREVENT ASCVD was defined as a composite of incident MI, ischemic stroke (atherothrombotic brain infarction and cerebral embolism, excluding hemorrhagic subtypes), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular death including coronary heart disease death and cerebrovascular death. All ASCVD was defined as a broader composite additionally encompassing angina pectoris, coronary insufficiency, transient ischemic attack, and peripheral arterial disease including first episode of intermittent claudication, consistent with established FHS Sequence of Events surveillance criteria. Pre-baseline events were excluded from all analyses. Median follow-up was 9.39 years (IQR 8.49–10.27), with a maximum of 11.29 years.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0) and Python (version 3.10). Statistical tests were two-sided with a significance level of α = 0.05. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [IQR], as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

Missing data were handled by excluding participants with incomplete outcome or AI-CAC data, resulting in a complete-case analysis. Correlations between AI-CAC and baseline clinical characteristics were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis and visualized with a symmetric correlation matrix (Supplementary Fig. 1). Associations between AI-CAC and incident MI, hard CHD, all CHD, Agatston score progression, PREVENT ASCVD, and All ASCVD were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. AI-CAC was modeled as a binary variable (AI-CAC >0 vs =0), as sex-specific tertiles among participants with AI-CAC >0, and as a continuous variable per 1-standard deviation increment. Restricted cubic spline analyses with three knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles were used to assess potential non-linear associations between AI-CAC and CHD risk (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To enhance model stability and address potential collinearity, penalized Cox regression with L2 regularization was applied. Model assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals for proportional hazards and martingale residuals for linearity. Fully adjusted models included age, sex, race and ethnicity, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, antihypertensive medication use, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, lipid-lowering medication use, diabetes mellitus, and current smoking status. In pooled analyses, models were additionally adjusted for cohort (MESA vs FHS).

Cumulative incidence curves were generated using 1 minus Kaplan–Meier estimates to compare event rates across AI-CAC categories, with analyses performed separately for binary AI-CAC status (0 vs >0) and for sex-specific tertiles of AI-CAC >0. Between-group differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted in the pooled cohort by stratifying participants according to age, sex, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and scanner type to evaluate the consistency of associations across clinically relevant subgroups.

A sensitivity analysis was performed in MESA to evaluate the robustness of AI-CAC measurements when calibrated using virtual phantom methods instead of physical phantom calibration. Virtual phantoms leverage machine learning models (Random Forest and XGBoost regressors) trained to predict phantom-equivalent calibration parameters (mean attenuation and noise characteristics) from patient-derived anatomical features, including adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and aortic attenuation values. AI-CAC scores derived from virtual phantom calibration were compared with standard phantom-based AI-CAC scores across the entire MESA cohort using Pearson correlation, Deming regression, and Bland–Altman analyses to ensure robust sample size and comprehensive comparison. Cumulative incidence curves for cardiovascular outcomes stratified by AI-CAC categories (0 vs >0) were generated for both calibration methods and compared using the log-rank test specifically in participants with baseline Agatston score of zero to assess reclassification performance in this clinically relevant subgroup. Detailed methodology, model performance metrics, and complete results are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 1. Furthermore, to account for potential inter-reader variability as well as CT-related noise or motion artifacts, the CAC score and AI-CAC were recalculated as the mean of duplicate baseline scan–rescan measurements, and all primary analyses were repeated using these averaged values.

To illustrate the heterogeneity of cardiovascular risk within the CAC = 0 population, cumulative incidence curves were generated for All CHD, All ASCVD, and PREVENT ASCVD outcomes across composite risk profiles defined by AI-CAC status and AI-CVD components (LV/RV ratio, LA/RA ratio, visceral fat, and pericardial fat; detailed definitions in Supplementary Methods).

Results

Study population

This study included 2,816 MESA participants and 1,149 FHS participants, all with baseline CAC score of zero. The MESA cohort (mean age 57.9±9.2 years, 38.1% male) demonstrated substantial racial and ethnic diversity: 33.7% White, 29.6% Black/African American, 24.3% Hispanic, and 12.4% Chinese American. The FHS cohort was younger (mean age 53.4±9.1 years, 38.8% male) and exclusively White. The MESA cohort had higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors compared with FHS, including current smoking (13.5% vs 4.8%), diabetes mellitus (9.1% vs 1.4%), antihypertensive medication use (28.2% vs 8.4%), and lipid-lowering medication use (10.7% vs 5.0%). Incident event rates

During follow-up, an incident MI occurred in 76 participants (1.9%) across the pooled MESA and FHS cohorts over 55,339 person-years (incidence rate: 1.37 per 1,000 person-years). Hard CHD events occurred in 107 participants (2.7%) over 55,314 person-years (incidence rate: 1.93 per 1,000 person-years). All CHD events occurred in 131 participants (3.3%) over 55,067 person-years (incidence rate: 2.38 per 1,000 person-years). PREVENT ASCVD events occurred in 198 participants (5.0%) over 54,886 person-years (incidence rate: 3.61 per 1,000 person-years), while all ASCVD events occurred in 298 participants (7.5%) over 54,065 person-years (incidence rate: 5.51 per 1,000 person-years).

Prognostic value of AI-CAC among participants with CAC = 0

Table 2 and Fig. 3 show that among participants with CAC = 0 at baseline, the presence of AI-CAC > 0 identified a higher-risk subset with significantly increased incidence of CHD events across all time horizons in the pooled cohort. In the pooled cohort (n=3,965), participants with AI-CAC > 0 (n=862) demonstrated substantially higher event rates compared to those with AI-CAC = 0 (n=3,103). For all CHD, the cumulative incidence at 5-year follow-up was 1.8% versus 0.5% (p=0.0003), at 10-year follow-up was 3.1% versus 1.4% (p=0.0008), and at 20-year follow-up was 7.7% versus 3.8% (p<0.0001) for AI-CAC > 0 versus AI-CAC = 0, respectively. In fully adjusted models, the presence of AI-CAC > 0 was independently associated with increased risk of all CHD across all time horizons (5-year HR 2.31, 95% CI 1.10–4.86; 10-year HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.06–3.01; 20-year HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.18–2.47). Similar associations were observed for hard CHD (20-year HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.23–2.78) and MI (20-year HR 1.73, 95% CI 1.06–2.82), as well as for PREVENT ASCVD (20-year HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.04–1.93) and all ASCVD (20-year HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.08–1.78).

Graded association between AI-CAC score and incident CHD

Table 3 and Fig. 4 demonstrate an overall positive association between AI-CAC score and 20-year incident CHD risk among participants with CAC = 0 in the pooled cohort. Higher AI-CAC scores were associated with higher event rates, with the third tertile consistently showing the highest risk across all outcomes. For all CHD, the incidence rate increased from 1.9 per 1,000 person-years in participants with AI-CAC = 0 to 4.0, 3.2, and 5.2 per 1,000 person-years in the first, second, and third tertiles of AI-CAC > 0, respectively. The third tertile demonstrated the highest risk in unadjusted models for all CHD (HR 2.61, 95% CI 1.61–4.23), hard CHD (HR 2.58, 95% CI 1.50–4.42), and MI (HR 2.08, 95% CI 1.03–4.21), as well as for PREVENT ASCVD (HR 1.83, 95% CI 1.17–2.86) and all ASCVD (HR 2.21, 95% CI 1.58–3.10). After full multivariable adjustment, the third tertile remained independently associated with increased risk of all CHD (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.12–3.07) and hard CHD (HR 1.88, 95% CI 1.06–3.32), with consistent associations observed for PREVENT ASCVD (HR 1.28, 95% CI 0.80–2.04) and all ASCVD (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.08–2.18) compared to AI-CAC = 0. Each 1-SD increment in AI-CAC score was associated with an 8–13% increased risk across outcomes in fully adjusted models, including all CHD (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.02–1.23), hard CHD (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.23), MI (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.24), PREVENT ASCVD (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04–1.12), and all ASCVD (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.04–1.13).

Sensitivity analysis: virtual phantom calibration

Machine learning models trained to predict phantom-equivalent calibration parameters from patient-derived anatomical features demonstrated excellent performance (R²=0.912 and 0.900 for phantom mean and noise, respectively; Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3). Virtual phantom-based AI-CAC scores exhibited near-perfect agreement with standard phantom-based measurements across the entire MESA-1 cohort (n=5,308; Pearson r=0.999; mean bias=-3.3, 95% limits of agreement: -40.2 to 46.8). Among participants with baseline CAC=0, both calibration methods yielded equivalent prognostic discrimination for cardiovascular outcomes (all log-rank p<0.01), confirming that virtual phantom calibration preserves the clinical utility of AI-CAC for risk stratification (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis: scan–rescan averaged measurements

To account for potential inter-reader variability and CT-related noise, a sensitivity analysis was performed in MESA using AI-CAC values averaged across duplicate baseline scan–rescan acquisitions. Results were mostly consistent with primary analyses and are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Risk stratification by AI-CAC and AI-CVD components

Among 2,816 MESA participants with Agatston CAC = 0, 10-year PREVENT ASCVD cumulative incidence varied markedly across composite risk profiles defined by AI-CAC status and AI-CVD components (Supplementary Fig. 5). Risk was lowest in participants with AI-CAC = 0 and all AI-CVD components below their medians (0.3%; 95% CI 0.0%–2.2%), compared with 2.5% (1.9%–3.3%) in the broader AI-CAC = 0 group. Among AI-CAC > 0 participants, risk increased progressively with AI-CVD component burden: 4.8% (3.4%–6.8%) overall, 9.0% (5.7%–14.0%) with additionally elevated LV/RV ratio, and 15.7% (7.3%–31.6%) with LV/RV ratio, visceral fat, and pericardial fat all above their 75th percentiles.

Discussion

Agatston-2.0 identified subclinical coronary calcification in approximately one in five individuals with a CAC score of zero and was associated with significantly increased risk for incident CHD events across 5-, 10-, and 20-year follow-up. Those with detectable AI-CAC experienced markedly higher cumulative incidence of all CHD, hard CHD, MI, all ASCVD and ASCVD PREVENT compared with those with AI-CAC=0, with the highest AI-CAC tertile conferring nearly double the risk of CHD events after multivariable adjustment. Additionally, baseline AI-CAC predicted future CAC score progression, and these associations remained consistent across clinically relevant subgroups, demonstrating the robustness and generalizability of AI-CAC for risk stratification.

Importantly, this study refines the "Power of Zero" by demonstrating that individuals with AI-CAC=0 — as defined by the Agatston-2.0 framework — have lower CHD risk than those classified as zero by Agatston-1.0, thereby identifying a truly low-risk subgroup within the broader zero-CAC population.

Our findings extend prior investigations that sought to overcome Agatston-1.0 farmwork’s limitations and improve risk stratification in individuals with zero CAC score. The spatially weighted CAC score, previously validated in MESA demonstrated that incorporating calcium density information from neighboring voxels could assign quantitative scores to individuals with a zero Agatston score and predict long-term CHD events[15,23]. Agatston-2.0 shares similar foundational principles—continuous probability weighting and spatial neighborhood analysis rather than fixed HU cutoffs—but extends this framework through fully automated deep learning-based coronary artery segmentation, eliminating manual region-of-interest definition and reducing inter-reader variability, and opens the door to virtual phantom calibration, thereby enhancing reproducibility and scalability for clinical implementation. Other investigators have explored reduced slice thickness reconstruction and lowered HU thresholds reclassifying 13–21% of individuals from zero to positive CAC scores, but these approaches require protocol modifications, may increase image noise necessitating higher radiation exposure, and lack standardization across institutions[20,24,25]. In contrast, Agatston-2.0 applies sophisticated voxel-wise probability weighting and spatial clustering analysis to standard-acquisition CT scans, extracting additional prognostic information without requiring protocol modifications, increased radiation dose, or specialized reconstruction algorithms. Notably, Agatston-2.0 is also applicable to thin-slice reconstructions, where its voxel-wise probabilistic framework can detect small, focal calcifications that are susceptible to partial-volume averaging and may be entirely missed on conventional thick-slice acquisitions—further extending its sensitivity for early subclinical disease.

While current guidelines recognize that a zero CAC score confers a favorable prognosis and often supports deferral of statin therapy, our data reveal substantial heterogeneity within this population. The approximately one in five zero-CAC individuals who harbor AI-CAC represent a higher-risk subset who may benefit from earlier risk factor modification, closer surveillance, and shorter rescanning intervals. Current consensus suggests that zero-CAC individuals can safely defer repeat scanning for 3–7 years depending on baseline risk, yet approximately 25% of middle-aged adults with zero scores convert to CAC positivity within this window[33,34]. AI-CAC could help personalize these intervals, with AI-CAC=0 supporting longer deferral and elevated AI-CAC warranting closer monitoring. Furthermore, current guidelines recognize that individuals with a CAC score of 1–10 are at intermediate risk and warrant consideration for statin therapy[16,17]. Our findings suggest that individuals with a positive AI-CAC score but a zero CAC score should equally be offered the opportunity for preventive pharmacotherapy through a shared decision-making approach.

Among individuals with AI-CAC=0 who nonetheless experienced incident CHD events, the underlying mechanisms are likely heterogeneous, including non-calcified plaques and, perhaps more importantly, factors beyond the epicardial coronary wall. Myocardial ischemia and infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA and MINOCA) play a significant role in clinical manifestations of CHD, accounting for 5–15% of all CHD events[[35], [36], [37], [38]]. In these cases, microvascular dysfunction through vasospasm is believed to be the primary culprit, followed by thromboembolism due to plaque erosion and increased blood thrombogenicity[[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. More broadly, the "vulnerable patient" framework emphasizes that acute coronary syndromes and fatal CHD arise from the convergence of vulnerable plaque, vulnerable blood (prone to thrombosis), and vulnerable myocardium (prone to fatal arrhythmia), rather than from atherosclerotic plaques alone[[40], [41], [42]].

Additionally, among individuals with CAC=0 but AI-CAC>0 who experienced events, a meaningful portion of previously attributed residual risk may represent misclassified calcified disease. Multiple studies have shown that up to 4% of patients with zero CAC scores harbor calcified plaques on high-resolution CCTA images with slice thickness <1.5 mm, and even this minimal calcification is associated with adverse outcomes[[16], [17], [18]]. Agatston-2.0 likely captures this subtle, subthreshold calcification that falls below Agatston-1.0 detection limits due to partial volume averaging in thicker slices (2.5–3 mm), small lesion size, or borderline calcium density near the 130 HU threshold. Consistent with this, recent data show that coronary CT angiography misses over one-third (37.6%) of calcified plaques detectable on co-registered thin-slice non-contrast CT, particularly smaller and lower-density lesions that are more prone to under-detection[43].

The technical characteristics of Agatston-2.0, particularly its continuous probability weighting and spatial neighborhood analysis, reflect the biology of coronary calcification more closely than discrete, threshold-based methods. Atherosclerotic calcification exists along a continuum, and early deposits often exhibit intermediate attenuation values that fluctuate around the 130 HU threshold due to partial-volume effects and image noise. By integrating voxel-wise probability modeling with contextual neighborhood information, Agatston 2.0 provides a more biologically faithful representation of coronary calcific burden. This refinement likely explains the observed association between AI-CAC and CHD risk, as well as its independent association after multivariable adjustment, indicating that AI-CAC captures clinically meaningful atherosclerotic signal beyond conventional risk factors and traditional CAC scoring.

A further technical advance is virtual phantom calibration. Physical phantoms are standard in research but rarely used in clinical practice due to logistical constraints and workflow complexity. Our machine learning–based virtual phantom demonstrated near-perfect agreement with standard phantom measurements and preserved prognostic discrimination, enabling standardized AI-CAC quantification from routine clinical scans without physical phantoms and facilitating broader clinical deployment.

Another key technical consideration is CT slice thickness. Thinner reconstructions (≤1.5 mm) detect small coronary calcifications missed by standard 2.5–3 mm acquisitions due to partial-volume averaging, yet this increased sensitivity has not consistently translated into improved risk discrimination with Agatston-1.0[44,45]. Both MESA and FHS used 2.5–3 mm protocols; therefore, the potential advantage of thinner slices is not captured in the present analysis. Notably, Agatston-2.0 is not constrained by slice thickness and can be applied to reconstructions as fine as 0.2 mm — combining high-resolution imaging with the Agatston-2.0 framework may further improve detection of early and semi-calcified plaques, potentially enhancing risk stratification beyond what was observed here.

Among Agatston CAC = 0 participants, integration of AI-CAC and AI-CVD parameters enabled refined risk stratification across the full PREVENT-ASCVD risk spectrum. AI-CAC > 0 status identified a borderline-risk subgroup at 4.8%, exceeding the ≥3% threshold at which statin therapy warrants consideration per the 2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia Guideline[8]. Further incorporation of elevated LV/RV ratio among AI-CAC > 0 participants increased risk to 9.0%, corresponding to the intermediate-risk category (5% to <10%) for which at least moderate-intensity statin therapy is recommended. When LV/RV ratio, visceral fat, and pericardial fat were all above their 75th percentiles, risk reached 15.7%, meeting the high-risk threshold (≥10%) for which high-intensity statin therapy is recommended. Conversely, AI-CAC = 0 identified a low-risk group at 2.5%, below the ≥3% statin consideration threshold. Participants with AI-CAC = 0 and all AI-CVD components below their medians represented a very low-risk subset with zero observed CHD events and a near-zero ASCVD rate over 10 years. These findings demonstrate that integrating AI-CAC with AI-CVD parameters — including LV/RV ratio, visceral fat, and pericardial fat — enables meaningful reclassification within the traditionally low-risk CAC = 0 population, with direct implications for statin initiation decisions.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the observational design precludes causal inference; although robust associations were observed across two large prospective cohorts with long-term follow-up, residual confounding by unmeasured risk factors cannot be excluded. Second, AI-CAC was assessed at a single baseline time point, whereas coronary calcification is dynamic; longitudinal AI-CAC measurements may provide incremental prognostic value by capturing early progression among individuals initially classified with zero Agatston scores. Third, Agatston-2.0 performance depends on image quality and acquisition parameters; although analyses incorporated scans acquired across different eras, scanner technologies (EBCT and MDCT), and gating protocols—with consistent results and excellent reproducibility—subtle differences in spatial resolution, noise characteristics, and reconstruction parameters may influence quantification. Fourth, because Agatston-2.0 relies on accurate coronary segmentations, CT images with significant noise and other artifacts can hinder the model's ability to detect and segment the coronary tree, thereby imposing limitations on generalizability to all CT scans. Finally, although over 19 million non-gated, non-contrast chest CT scans are performed annually and represent a promising opportunity for opportunistic cardiac risk assessment, such scans pose technical challenges related to unknown cardiac phase and motion-induced variability; because our findings derive from dedicated cardiac CT acquisitions, further technical validation is required before extrapolating Agatston-2.0 performance to non-gated chest CT protocols[46].

Future priorities include prospective validation across diverse populations and CT platforms, randomized trials to assess whether AI-CAC–guided interventions improve clinical outcomes, and development of standardized AI-CAC thresholds and risk-equivalent categories analogous to existing benchmarks to support guideline integration and reimbursement pathways. Longitudinal studies with serial AI-CAC measurements, integration with complementary AI-derived imaging biomarkers, and cost-effectiveness analyses will be essential for scalable, equitable implementation of AI-enabled preventive cardiovascular care. In addition, the value of Agatston 2.0 across other CAC categories beyond CAC = 0 warrants further investigation in future studies.

Conclusion

This pooled analysis from the MESA and the FHS shows that Agatston-2.0 detects clinically meaningful coronary calcification in individuals who are classified as CAC=0 by Agatston-1.0. This subset of CAC=0 cases exhibited a higher CHD risk and greater progression to positive CAC scores. In contrast, individuals without detectable calcification by Agatston-2.0 experienced the lowest event rates, defining a truly low-risk subset within the conventional CAC-zero population. These findings refine the clinical interpretation of the “Power of Zero” by distinguishing truly absent coronary calcification from subthreshold disease undetected by Agatston-1.0.
