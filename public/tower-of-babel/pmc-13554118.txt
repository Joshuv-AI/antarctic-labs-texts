Digital twin of biomacromolecular thermodynamics in cryo-EM data
Authors: Kimihiro Yamazaki, Mutsuyo Wada, Yuichiro Wada, Atsushi Tokuhisa, Kento Ohga, Akira Nakagawa, Yoshinobu Akinaga, Yoko Sasakura, Takashi Katoh, Shigeyuki Matsumoto, Yasushi Okuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The biological functions of biomacromolecules are linked to their thermodynamics. Thus, a quantitative understanding of the full scope of these dynamics, ranging from local fluctuations to large-scale conformational change on the free-energy landscape, is essential for elucidating the biomacromolecular mechanisms. Recently, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has garnered increasing attention for determining novel static structures with large molecular sizes and complex structural assemblies. However, identifying their comprehensive thermodynamic behaviors remains challenging. In this study, we present a deep learning framework that reconstructs a provable “digital twin” of the thermodynamics recorded in cryo-EM data with a theoretically guaranteed isometric autoencoder. This framework enables a thermodynamic analysis of the heterogeneous conformational states and their transition pathways in a semi-automatic manner. We demonstrated the application of the framework to the cryo-EM data obtained from a 50S-Ribosome and two SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins, which yielded novel biological insights without supplementary assumptions. This work establishes a provable approach to achieve the quantitative analysis of biomacromolecular thermodynamics using only cryo-EM data, whereas current similar methods do not guarantee the exact construction of the free-energy landscape. Overall, our approach has the potential to advance biological foundations by uncovering the thermodynamics of complex biological systems.

Introduction

Biological phenomena are governed by the functions of biomacromolecules, which are intrinsically linked to their conformational dynamics on the free-energy landscape, i.e., their thermodynamic characteristics1–3. The dynamic behaviors of biomacromolecules are represented as conformational transitions among heterogeneous structural states, and they span a wide range of timescale and magnitudes, from rapid local fluctuations to large-scale structural changes. Solving all their relevant conformations, including transient states and capturing the transition pathways among them in a quantitative manner is paramount in the field of life sciences.

Analyses of static three-dimensional (3D) structures provide a basis for understanding their associated dynamic behaviors. In recent years, single particle analysis of cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM)4 has gained popularity because of its capability to solve the high-resolution 3D structures of large and complex biomacromolecules, of which structural determination has been difficult when using other techniques, such as X-ray crystallography. A particularly notable advantage of cryo-EM is its capability to record all structural states present in solution, allowing for solving heterogeneous conformations, such as heterogeneous states during ribosome assembly5, from data acquired under a single experimental condition. Consequently, cryo-EM has substantially advanced our understanding of the static structures of biomacromolecules.

Despite these advances, understanding the thermodynamics of biomacromolecules targeted using cryo-EM remains challenging. Molecular dynamics at an atomic resolution has been investigated via experimental and computational approaches, such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations6,7. Nonetheless, applying these methods to large and complex macromolecules is often limited because of several obstacles, such as signal overlapping, signal broadening, and requirements of high computational costs. Therefore, while cryo-EM has dramatically advanced structural biology by elucidating static snapshots, a critical gap remains in the comprehensive understanding of their complex systems.

Considering these limitations, several machine learning (ML) methods have been reported to capture the molecular dynamics from a collection of biomacromolecular cryo-EM particle images, which record all the dynamic behaviors8–16. For example, deep learning (DL) methods, such as cryoDRGN9 and 3DFlex12, compute the continuous conformational changes of biomacromolecules, employing the flexible regression capabilities of deep neural networks. However, currently existing ML methods are not guaranteed to generate an exact duplication of the entire free-energy landscape that is recorded in cryo-EM data, hindering identification of the reliable behaviors through quantitative analyses. Furthermore, the characterization of complex structural transition pathways, such as parallel assembly pathways in 50S-Ribosome, often necessitates reliance on laborious manual estimation that is guided by supplementary experimental information and prior knowledge. Davis et al. reconstructed heterogeneous static structures of multiple assembly intermediates of the 50S-Ribosome large subunit with an ML software cryoSPARC17 and inferred their assembly pathways by manually ordering them in a consistent manner based on experimental observations, such as “increasing molecular weight”, and the functional context from prior knowledge5. Consequently, an automated methodology for the comprehensive and unbiased extraction of the full thermodynamic ensemble of biomacromolecules directly from cryo-EM data is currently lacking.

Herein, we propose PaStEL with cryoTWIN (Fig. 1), a DL method that enables an unbiased extraction of the full thermodynamic behavior solely from a collection of biomacromolecular cryo-EM images, which is supported by rigorous theoretical guarantees. In the method, an isometric autoencoder, cryoTWIN, is first trained with the images. Then, the trained cryoTWIN exactly duplicates the whole free-energy landscape recorded in the images into a latent space via a theoretically guaranteed isometric embedding, while allowing for the accurate reconstruction of 3D structures through its decoder. Subsequently, PaStEL generates energetically favorable conformational pathways as minimum free-energy paths18 along with their occurrence probabilities using the latent space and the trained decoder. PaStEL with cryoTWIN: a framework for the quantitative analysis of biomacromolecular thermodynamics.

The proposed method, PaStEL with cryoTWIN, provides a digital twin of the thermodynamics recorded in biomacromolecular cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) data, thereby enabling quantitative analysis of the thermodynamics. The method is performed through the following three steps. (Step 1) Train an isometric autoencoder, cryoTWIN, with cryo-EM particle images that record the free-energy landscape. (Step 2) These particle images are input into the trained encoder, and an exact duplication of the free-energy landscape is constructed in a low-dimensional latent space. This duplication is expressed as a simple mathematical formula. Using this formula and a pathway searching algorithm PaStEL, optimal paths between various latent variables representing conformational states are calculated. (Step 3) The calculated optimal paths are passed through the decoder to construct the minimum free-energy paths as sequences of 3D density maps. Each pathway is assigned its probability of occurrence. These sequences, occurrence probabilities, and the obtained simple mathematical formula are then used to perform a quantitative analysis.

Our main results are threefold. First, we mathematically proved that our method creates a digital twin of the thermodynamics recorded in the cryo-EM data. Second, using simulated cryo-EM images for Chignolin and Trp-cage, we verified accurate reconstructions of their diverse 3D structures and extensive free-energy landscapes. Finally, we demonstrated the efficiency and potential of our method in the field of structural biology research using real-world cryo-EM images of the 50S-ribosome, which undergoes complicated structural assembly of the subunits, and SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins, which undergo conformational changes to interact with a host cell receptor. A series of their analyses was performed using modest GPU resources, indicating that researchers in the cryo-EM community can readily apply this method to their targets. The derived thermodynamic behaviors of complex biological systems will provide unprecedented insights into their molecular mechanisms, advancing the field of life science research.

Results

Theory behind PaStEL with cryoTWIN

PaStEL with cryoTWIN generates optimal free-energy paths only from the particle images. This approach is guaranteed by two original theoretical findings: (i) optimally trained cryoTWIN using the particle images builds an isometric latent space for a conformational space, i.e., the free-energy landscape is expressed by the trained latent distributional model of cryoTWIN, and (ii) a generated path by PaStEL based on the trained cryoTWIN is equivalent to the MaxFlux path: optimal solution of the MaxFlux objective [ref. 18, Eq. (17)]. Intuitively, isometricity in (i) means that we can create a copy of the conformational distribution, which is equivalent info to the free-energy landscape for generating MaxFlux paths, in the low-dimensional latent space. Isometricity facilitates accessing the free-energy info via the latent space of cryoTWIN. As for (ii), it is a consequence of the isometricity and mathematical formula of the MaxFlux objective. Because the MaxFlux path is known to be an optimal conformational path on the free-energy landscape18, finding (ii) implies the generation of optimal free-energy paths. In the following, we present the findings (i) and (ii) in Theorems 1 and 2, respectively.

Remark 1

The isometry guaranteed by Theorem 1 is local, not global. A local isometry preserves only the local distance structure of the original space, whereas a global isometry preserves the distance information between any two points. Consequently, even when a highly accurate local isometry is achieved, distances between distant points on the energy landscape estimated by cryoTWIN may be distorted and may not accurately reflect the distances between the corresponding conformations in conformational space. In contrast, Theorem 2 ensures that the thermodynamics computed by PaStEL can be distortion-free, even between distant start and end conformations. This is because the MaxFlux path is defined by integrating local thermodynamic segments. Each local segment is distortion-free, as it benefits from the underlying local isometry in Theorem 1. By integrating these accurate local segments, the resulting global path avoids accumulated distortion. In practice, as the local scale approaches zero, i.e., as the number of partitions m in Theorem 2 approaches infinity, the distortion in the thermodynamics computed by PaStEL converges to zero.

CryoTWIN builds an isometric latent space for a synthetic protein conformation space from the particle images

PaStEL is theoretically guaranteed to be able to generate optimal free-energy paths once cryoTWIN is optimally trained by the expected training objective. According to our theoretical analysis related to Theorem 2, PaStEL and the subsequent structural analysis require three properties of the trained cryoTWIN: (i) the isometric property of dz1 ⋅ dz2 ∝ dV1 ⋅ dV2 (Eq. (1)), (ii) free-energy equality by the trained latent distributional model pψ (Eq. (2)), and (iii) accurate reconstruction of the 3D density maps by the trained decoder. In practice, as we do not obtain the ideal training objective (Eq. (4)) in using a real-world dataset, we must employ the empirical version of the expected training objective (Eq. (5)). Hence, for practical use, it is important to hold the properties of (i)–(iii) for trained cryoTWIN based on the empirical objective.

To evaluate the aforementioned points, we analyzed three essential properties using the synthetic particle images for two proteins: Chignolin and Trp-cage. The diverse conformations on almost the entire free-energy landscape were prepared using multi-canonical MD (mcMD) simulations19,20, and the 2D projection images were generated from these simulations according to their free-energy values (see Methods). The cryoTWIN trained with the synthetic images and their pose orientations demonstrated that the Pearson correlation coefficient between dz1 ⋅ dz2 and dV1 ⋅ dV2 for Chignolin and Trp-cage were 0.80 and 0.87, respectively (Fig. 3a, b). In addition, the pseudo free-energy landscapes depicted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\log {p}_{{\psi }^{* }}$$\end{document}−logpψ*, were in agreement well with the reference free-energy landscapes obtained from the generalized ensemble MD simulations (Fig. 3c, d; the correlation coefficients for Chignolin and Trp-cage were 0.89 and 0.93, respectively). These results demonstrated that (i) the isometric property and (ii) the free-energy equality were held in cryoTWIN trained via the empirical objective, allowing for the generation of ridgelines on the built latent space corresponding to the free-energy landscapes. Furthermore, the reconstructed 3D density maps from the latent variables were fitted well by the reference all-atom models at the corresponding position on the reference free-energy landscapes (Fig. 3e, f), demonstrating (iii) the accurate reconstruction of the 3D density maps by the trained decoder. Collectively, it was confirmed that PaStEL, combined with a trained cryoTWIN based on the empirical objective, can generate optimal free-energy paths for practical use. PaStEL with cryoTWIN depicts the whole assembly pathways of 50S-Ribosome on the free-energy landscape from the experimental particle images

PaStEL with cryoTWIN uncovers a thermodynamic basis for increased infectivity in the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein D614G mutant

Pathogenic amino acid substitutions in macromolecules often perturb the dynamic behaviors, leading to their functional alterations or dysfunctions. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanism from a view of free-energy governing molecular dynamics will be of great importance for drug development targeting a pathogenic mutant. In our approach, when the wildtype and mutant cryo-EM data are acquired under the same temperature, the individually obtained free-energy differences can be quantitatively analyzed on the same scale. This property prompted us to investigate the thermodynamic mechanism of functional alteration induced by pathogenic amino acid substitution using cryoTWIN with PaStEL.

SARS-CoV-2 infection is triggered by a structural change in the spike protein receptor-binding domain (RBD) from closed to open conformations required for angiotensin-converting enzyme 2-binding of host cells. Although the wildtype predominantly forms the Closed conformation, the D614G mutant with enhanced infectivity primarily forms the Open conformation (particularly the Open 1up state)21. However, the thermodynamic basis for these conformational differences under the equilibrium condition remains unclear, hampering the comprehension of the molecular mechanism of the enhanced infectivity of the mutant.

We prepared particle images of both the wildtype and D614G mutant spike proteins from the micrographs acquired under the same experimental conditions (EMPIAR-10469 and EMPIAR-10725, respectively) and individually trained cryoTWIN with latent GMM pψ. The reconstruction of the intermediate maps illustrated gradual structural changes in the RBD between the Closed and Open states. The free-energy profile showed that the Closed state was more stable than the Open 1up state, and there existed a high energy barrier between the Closed and Open 1up states. In contrast, the D614G mutant was shown to favor the Open 1up state compared with the other states (right panel of Fig. 5c, e). The Open 2up state, uniquely found in the mutant, was more unstable than the Closed state, as estimated in the previous experiment21. Notably, the free-energy barrier between the Closed and Open 1up states in the D614G mutant was lower than that in the wildtype. This observation indicated that the conformations between the inactive Closed and active Open 1up states were readily exchangeable in the mutant compared with the wildtype. This mutation-induced perturbation in the dynamic property could offer enhancement of infectivity. The previous study reported that, in wildtype, the outward movement of the N-terminal domain (NTD) was accompanied by the structural change in the RBD to the Up state, while the NTD of the D614G mutant adopted a nearly identical outward conformation in the Closed and Up states (Fig. 5f)21. The reconstructed intermediate states in wildtype showed that the RBD and NTD domains were located at the intermediate position between the Up and Closed states, and their movements were concerted during the conformational exchange (Fig. 5f, left panel). On the other hand, in the D614G mutant, the NTD conformation was not changed during the exchange (Fig. 5f, middle and right panels). These results indicated that ignoring the concerted NTD movement in the D614G mutant could be attributed to the lowering of the energy barrier between the two states. These energy-based insights cannot be obtained from static structures and existing methods, highlighting the advantages of our method in understanding molecular mechanisms only from cryo-EM data.

Discussion

Herein, based on the theoretical findings for PaStEL with cryoTWIN, we developed a method to generate the free-energy landscape and compute optimal free-energy transition pathways of multiple conformations using only the cryo-EM particle images. Several ML methods have been reported for reconstructing heterogeneous conformations from the particle images, but they do not generate the free-energy landscape and free-energy-based pathways, hindering the analysis of the dynamic behavior. In this regard, MD simulation could be an approach to complement existing ML methods. However, considering that large structural changes occur on millisecond or longer timescales in general, the application to macromolecules targeted by cryo-EM is often unrealistic. Meanwhile, our approach can automatically reconstruct the energetically stable conformations and uncover the dynamic properties from cryo-EM particle images only, thereby reducing experimental costs (see e.g., Fig. SI-3), highlighting its potential to accelerate the analysis of complex biological systems.

Our framework, like other cryo-EM analyses that interpret state occupancies in thermodynamic terms15,22,23, assumes that the analyzed vitrified particles approximate an equilibrium ensemble, such that relative occupancies can be related to free-energy differences through Boltzmann statistics. Accordingly, the recovered free-energy landscape should be interpreted as an estimate for the analyzed particle set under these assumptions, rather than as an exact description of the full solution-phase conformational ensemble. Rapid cooling can alter the structural ensemble during vitrification24, and discarding low-quality particles during processing can further bias state populations. To accurately reconstruct the 3D density maps and estimate their free-energy values with our method, a sufficient number of high-quality particle images and reliable pose estimates is required. Practical limitations in real cryo-EM pipelines–namely, finite data, high noise levels, and pose estimation errors–can directly bias the learned latent density, which forms the basis of our free-energy estimation. Finite data particularly hampers the analysis of low-populated conformations, because the probability density for these rare states is poorly estimated, whereas image noise and pose errors can bias the learned landscape more broadly across the full conformational distribution. Because the use of these reconstructed maps currently relies on visual inspection, a method for estimating map quality should be developed and integrated with our method in future work. These limitations are not unique to cryoTWIN but are shared by current cryo-EM-based conformational heterogeneity analyses9,15, and addressing them will require future advances in vitrification, particle-processing pipelines, and statistical methods that account for sampling bias and uncertainty. Advancements in cryo-EM equipment, such as cold field emission gun25, and image processing methods, will expand the application scope of the ML-based methods, including our method.

In conclusion, this article is the first to propose a DL method for generating plausible conformational paths as a 3D density map on the free-energy landscape from only the particle images. Our method was designed based on novel theoretical guarantees—cryoTWIN and PaStEL theories. Through intensive experiments, PaStEL based on cryoTWIN successfully analyzed the dynamics of several biomacromolecules. Thus, our method will advance the biological foundation by elucidating the dynamics of biomacromolecules with large sizes and complex structural assemblies.

Hyper-parameter selection

Through this study, the MLP architecture was 1024 × 3 (1024: number of nodes per layer; 3: number of layers) for both the encoder and decoder, and the dimension of the latent space was fixed to 8. For Eq. (5), λ1 = λ2 = 8 and C = 100 were used. Regarding the variance σ in the random noise ε, it was fixed at 0.9 for the simulation data. For the real-world data, it was scheduled to increase gradually from 0.9 at the start of training to 1.0 at the end. The trainable parameters in the autoencoder were initialized by the He initialization method and were optimized by the RAdam27 with the learning rate 0.0001, where the number of epochs was 100, and the mini-batch size was 128 (only the 50S-ribosomal experiment employed a size of 64). Details of experiments using cryo-EM data of spike proteins

The particle images for the wildtype and the mutant D614G were derived from the micrographs, EMPIAR-10469 and EMPIAR-10725, respectively, using RELION software31. After conducting motion correction and CTF estimations, particle images were picked up by a template-based particle picking algorithm. All particle images were classified into five classes by a 3D classification algorithm, and the images associated with clear structural density maps were selected. Finally, 84,945 (256 × 256, 1.2229 Å/pixel) and 40,954 (256 × 256, 0.8566 Å/pixel) images together with the corresponding pose orientations were obtained for the wildtype and the mutant D614G, respectively. We trained cryoTWIN by the empirical objective of Eq. (5). The training datasets are publicly available, see Data availability. In Fig. 5a, b, the 3D density maps were obtained via the following procedure. First, we reconstructed 30 density maps from the GMM mean vectors with the 30 lowest pseudo free-energy values. Then, based on the existing structural labels, we classified the 3D maps via visual inspections using UCSF ChimeraX29. Finally, among the 3D density maps with the same label, we picked one with the lowest energy. The all- atom models shown in a and b were derived from the protein data bank: 6zge (wildtype Closed), 6zgg (wildtype Open 1up), 7bnm (D614G Closed), 7bnn (D614G Open 1up), and 7bno (D614G Open 2up). Regarding Fig. 5c, we first applied PCA to a set of latent variables on the conformational pathway. Let A denote the PCA projector from the latent space to the PCA plane.
