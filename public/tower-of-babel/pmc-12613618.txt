Plant identity and environmental filtering are the key drivers of bacterial community structure in four desert plant species from the Sahara Desert in Morocco
Authors: Hafsa Debbagh-Nour, Said Khourchi, Ayoub El Mouttaqi, Rachid Elfermi, Anass Bourazza, Oscar Pascal Malou, Marc Ducousso, Hassan Boukcim, Mohamed Hijri, Abdelaziz Hirich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlant microbiome is a very wide research area playing a major role in agriculture and plant health. This study investigates the interactions between root-associated bacterial communities and soil physico-chemical characteristics of four Moroccan desert plant species: Lavandula coronopifolia Poir. (n = 20), Lycium intricatum Boiss. (n = 20), Nitraria retusa Asch. (n = 20) and Searsia tripartita (Ucria) Moffett. (n = 20). Soil samples were collected in the rhizosphere of each plant species, and root samples from four different sites, with five random samples per site. Elemental chemical composition of soil sample was analyzed using the X-Ray fluorescence spectrometry and soil carbon, i.e., organic carbon (SOC) and inorganic carbon (SIC) were measured using the Rock–Eval® Oxypure method. Roots were assessed for their microbial community, using the 16S rDNA metabarcoding analysis.ResultsSignificant variation in soil parameters was observed across sites and among the four desert plant species. SOC and SIC contents were highest in soils associated with S. tripartita, N. retusa, and Ly. intricatum, while La. coronopifolia was associated with elevated levels of Si, Al, K, and Mn. Soil EC was highest in N. retusa and S. tripartita. Microbial analysis revealed that Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota dominated the root-associated bacterial communities, comprising around 60% of total abundance. Alpha diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) varied significantly across species and sites, especially in La. coronopifolia. Beta diversity analyzes confirmed that geographic location significantly influenced microbial community structure. Rhizosphere network complexity and composition varied by species, with N. retusa showing broad ecological tolerance, while S. tripartita communities were strongly shaped by SOC, SIC, K, Ca, Mn, and Fe. Mantel test results further highlighted that plant identity and environmental factors differentially influence root microbial composition across sites.ConclusionEach plant species showed unique responses in terms of soil characteristics and microbial communities, highlighting the importance of adopting a species-specific approach.Keywords: Root-associated bacteria, Microbial diversity, Soil properties, X-ray fluorescence, Desert plant

Background

Desert ecosystems span around 35% of lands worldwide and play an essential role in the global ecological processes on earth [1]. In Morocco, the Sahara Desert covers a very large area of the Country land surface. They are characterized by harsh environmental conditions: low water and nutrients availability, rare and irregular rainfall, high temperature and evapotranspiration rate [2, 3]. The combination of these conditions with environmentally destructive activities such as overgrazing present a serious threat to plant species wildly grown in the desert making their survival a lot more complicated.

Despite these challenges, numerous plant species have adapted through diverse mechanisms. Some plants for instance, develop expanding vigorous deep root system to access deep groundwater. Others have a Crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), this photosynthetic adaptation allows a nocturnal CO2 fixation to avoid high evaporation during daytime [4, 5]. Plant microbiome in some cases is considered as major factor by which plants adjust their behaviour in the face of environmental changes [6]. These multiple adaptations enable the plants to interfere in the preservation and the structuration of desert soils, through contribution to organic matter formation, soil fixation and creation of micro-habitats for the establishment of other life forms.

Plant microbiome gathers all microorganisms living together in interaction with plant, forming the so-called holobiont: bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses. Their presence in the roots enhances plant growth and stress tolerance through the production of phytohormones and bioactive compounds [7–9]. Bacteria are crucial elements to plants when it comes to the role they play in nutrient cycling especially in the arid lands [10, 11]. Some beneficial bacteria, known as plant growth promoting bacteria (PGPB), can be used as biofertilizers. They can be present through different forms: rhizospheric (reside in a thin layer encircling plant roots), epiphytic (live on the surface of leaves or roots) or endophytic (thrive inside plant tissues) [12]. These bacteria are involved in plant growth whether directly or indirectly [13]. The direct mechanisms consist of the production of antimicrobial compounds and inducers of resistance against soilborne diseases, in addition to the acquisition of nutrients through zinc and phosphate solubilization, nitrogen fixation and iron sequestration [14–16]. Whereas the indirect mechanism involves the modulation of phytohormone levels, for instance gibberellins and cytokinins. Nevertheless, detailed mechanisms of bacteria composition and diversity, and specific interactions with desert plants are far from completely elucidated.

Therefore, our study aims to assess rhizospheric soils of four pioneer desert plant species: Lavandula coronopifolia Poir., Lycium intricatum Boiss., Nitraria retusa Asch. and Searsia tripartita (Ucria) Moffett. across different locations in the Laâyoune Sakia El Hamra region of Morocco, and to investigate the variation of microbial communities associated with their roots. We further seek to elucidate the ecological mechanisms underlying the interactions between soil properties and the root-associated microbiome. This study will provide deeper insight into soil-plant-microbiome synergies in desert ecosystems and offer new perspectives for plant conservation. We hypothesize that interactions between soil physicochemical properties and rhizosphere microbial communities play a pivotal role in shaping plant adaptation and resilience in these ecosystems.

Material and methods

Study site and sample collection

Field trips were conducted in different sites located in the provinces of Boujdour, Es-Semara, Laayoune, and Tarfaya (Fig. 1). Sample collection was held during December 2022 and January 2023 to obtain rhizospheric soil and root samples from four plant species: Lavandula coronopifolia, Lycium intricatum, Nitraria retusa and Searsia tripartita (Table 1). Sampling generally corresponded to the upper root zone (approximately 10–30 cm depth, depending on root distribution). For each species, four sites were selected to reflect landscape and biotope variability. At each site, five plants were randomly selected, yielding 20 rhizospheric soil samples and 20 corresponding root samples per species (4 sites × 5 replicates/site). Soil was sieved on-site using a 2 mm mesh sieve, and samples were stored at -80°C at the African Sustainable Agriculture Research Institute (ASARI) until analysis.

Soil physico-chemical analyses

Soil samples were prepared according to the international standard ISO 11464. They were spread in trays without exceeding 5 cm thick and put in the oven at 40 °C for 48 hours. Once samples were totally dry, they were sieved at 2 mm.

Soil salinity and pH

Salinity and pH were determined according to the international standard ISO 11265. This method consists of using a soil:water ratio of 1:5 with water or KCl solution (1 mol/L). The sample is mixed to allow the dissolution of electrolytes then sieved. The electrical conductivity (EC), pHwater, and pHKCl were measured using a multiparameter (Mettler Toledo, USA). The EC results were displayed in microsiemens per cm (μS/cm).

Available phosphorus

Available phosphorus content of soils was measured following the international standard NF 11263 based on the sodium hydrogen carbonate solution. Results were displayed in mg/kg of P2O5 of dry soil.

Soil elemental analysis

The analytical technique X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF) was used to determine the relative abundance of the elemental composition of soil samples (81 elements ranging from magnesium to uranium in the periodic table of elements). The S1 Titan Analyzer (Bruker, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) was utilized for the analysis of our samples, opting for the ‘geoexploration’ calibration with a total exposure time of 105 seconds on samples including three main phases (35 s each): (1) Sample excitation: X-ray beam is pointed at the sample which atoms absorb the energy. The excited state of the atoms results in the emission of secondary X-rays specific to the elements present in the soil sample (2) Detection and signal acquisition: emitted fluorescent X-rays are captured via a detector, their intensity and energy are recorder with a spectrometer (3) Data processing and quantification: the spectrum is interpreted with the instrument’s software, and each peak is designated to the corresponding element. Concentrations are calculated using the intensities. The analysis was performed in triplicate, where soil samples were pressed at 20 Tons for 2 min and each pellet was then analyzed in triplicate. The nine measurements were then averaged and calibrated according to the limit of optical detection (LOD) of each analyzed element (provided by Brucker). Only main elements were considered for statistical analysis: Mg, Al, Si, P, K, Ca, Ti, Mn, and Fe. Obtained results required a centred log ratio transformation due to the compositional nature of data [17].

Soil carbon analysis

Soil organic C (SOC) and soil inorganic C (SIC) contents were simultaneously determined in 100 mg of finely ground soil (<200 µm) using the Rock-Eval® Oxypure method [18]. This method consists in oxidation or combustion phase, under artificial atmosphere (N2/O2: 80/20), starting at 200 °C and ending at 850 °C with a heating rate of 25 °C min−1. The thermograms COoxi and CO2oxi are measured, corresponding respectively to CO, CO2 of organic origin (S4CO and S4CO2) and CO2 of mineral origin (S5). The integration of these thermograms enables measurement of the standard parameters: Total Organic Carbon (TOC in %) and Mineral Carbon (MINC in %) which correspond to total SOC and total SIC, respectively (Fig. 2).

Thermograms associated with the CO and CO2 effluents released during the combustion phase of the Rock–Eval® Oxypure method, enabling the simultaneously quantification of soil organic carbon (SOC) and soil inorganic carbon (SIC)

DNA extraction

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from plant root using a quantity of 5 mg of lyophilized tissue. The extraction was performed using the standard protocol of the DNeasy Plant Pro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the standard manufacturer's instructions. Before extraction, samples were suspended in a lysis buffer (provided) then homogenized through bead beating for 15 minutes at 30 Hz using a tissue lyser device (TissueLyser II, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The DNA extraction was automated on the QIAcube Connect system, where each run consisted of 12 samples. Samples were stored at −20°C after being diluted in a final volume of 50 μl of TE elution buffer.

PCR amplification, library preparation, and sequencing

The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing was targeting the bacterial V3-V4 variable regions using the Fluidigm Access Array™ Barcode Library for Illumina® Sequencers - 384, Single Direction (Cat # 100–4876), compatible with the Illumina MiSeq platform. Primers used are the 515 F (5′-CS1-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and the 806R (5′-CS2-GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT-3′), linked to custom sequence adapters CS1 (ACACTGACGACATGGTTCTACA) and CS2 (TACGGTAGCAGAGACTTGGTCT). Primers were HPLC-purified by Alpha DNA (Montreal, QC).

To each PCR reaction, a Fluidigm tag was added, and a library was generated for bacterial samples. Purification was carried out using a 0.85 ratio of AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter), to which equal quantities of amplified DNA were added. Library quantification was performed with a Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity and BR Assay Kits (Cat # Q32851), and fragment size was assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer and Agilent DNA 1000 assay kit (Cat # 5067-1504).

PCR reaction consisted of 1 ng of DNA and the KAPA HiFi HotStart Ready Mix which is containing DNA Polymerase (0.5 U/25 µL reaction), dNTPs (0.3 mM/1X) and MgCl2 (2.5 mM/1X) along with stabilizers (Roche). Amplification conditions in the thermocycler were an initial cycle of denaturation for 2 minutes at 94 °C, then 30 cycles of 30 seconds at 94 °C, 30 seconds at 55 °C, 30 seconds at 72 °C and a final cycle of elongation of 7 minutes at 72°C.

Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq instrument using the MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (600-cycles) PE at the African Genome Center (AGC-UM6P; Cat # MS-103–1003).

Data processing and statistical analysis

The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess significant differences between groups of data according to the parameters studied. This non-parametric test was chosen because of the lack of normality in the data and the heterogeneity of the variances. When the Kruskal-Wallis test revealed a significant difference (p < 0.05), a post hoc analysis was carried out using Dunn's test to identify the groups with significant differences. To limit the risk of type I error in multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. The XRF data were transformed using the centered log-ratio (clr) method due to their compositional nature, as using raw values without transformation can introduce statistical bias. The clr transformation overcomes this constraint by projecting the data into a Euclidean space, making multivariate analyses more robust and interpretable. It is based on the calculation of the logarithm of the ratio between each component and the geometric mean of all the components in a sample.

Fastq files containing raw reads were analyzed using R (R Project for Statistical Computing) [19]. Read quality was evaluated through the DADA2 pipeline also in R, and low-quality reads were eliminated. Filtration and trimming were also performed in DADA2 to exclude primer and adapter sequences. Error rates were estimated for each consensus quality score. Additionally, the EasyMicrobiome pipeline was used for downstream graphical analyses and visualizations [20].

Results

Soil physico-chemical properties

Soil carbon contents

Soil organic (SOC) and inorganic (SIC) contents varied markedly among rhizospheric soils of the studied species (Fig. 3A and B). Soil organic content ranged from 0.8 to 24.4 g C kg⁻1 and was significantly lower in La. coronopifolia soils. Soil inorganic content was consistently higher than SOC in all sites, often by a factor of 2 to 6. Both SOC and SIC followed the same increasing trend: S. tripartita = N. retusa = Ly. intricatum >La. coronopifolia.

Values represent mean ± standard deviation. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, **p < 0.001, and 'ns' denotes non-significant differences

Soil pH and soil salinity

For La. coronopifolia, pH differences are mostly negative across sites, indicating predominant anion exchange capacity, with site 3 showing the strongest anion exchange (Fig. 3C and D). For Ly. intricatum, most values are negative but some positive values at sites 5, 7, and 8 reveal localized cation exchange zones and soil heterogeneity. For N. retusa, sites 9, 10 and 11 show mainly positive differences indicating moderate to high cation exchange, while site 12 is mostly negative but heterogeneous. For S. tripartita, variability within sites shows mixed cation and anion exchange, with site 15 mostly negative and site 16 moderately heterogeneous.

Electrical conductivity and its variations are shown in Fig. 3E. It varied widely among species, with values up to 8800 μS/cm in N. retusa. Electrical conductivity was significantly higher in N. retusa and S. tripartita rhizospheric soils.

Soil phosphorus

Soil available phosphorus (P₂O₅) contents varied widely among species (Fig. 3F). They were significantly higher in N. retusa and S. tripartita, with particularly high variability under N. retusa. The lowest values were recorded in La. coronopifolia soils.

Elemental composition of rhizospheric soils

The principal component analysis (PCA) of La. coronopifolia displayed three clusters (Fig. 4A). ‘Khyal Sbaa’ and ‘Oued El Mahjoub’ clustered together and were explained by an increased proportion of P and Si, and higher SOC and pHwater. Conversely, ‘Mguiraate’ was positioned on the opposite direction, reflecting poorer soils in terms of SOC, P, and Si. However, this site was richer in Ti. ‘Oued Jehch’ was also separated from the other sites, with a chemical composition characterized by elevated proportion of Fe and Mn.

Four sites are represented in distinct colors for each species. Dimensions 1 and 2 explain 63.3%, 63%, 76.1%, and 64.9% of the total variance, respectively

For Ly. intricatum, the PCA revealed two distinct groups (Fig. 4B). ‘Khribichate’ soils displayed high proportions of K and Al. ‘Oudai Lemgymim’ and ‘Sebkha Boulssane’ clustered together. ‘Oued Lakraa’ was distinguished by high SIC and Ca proportion.

Nitraria retusa soil samples from Oued Jehch were significantly correlated with high Al and Si proportions, in contrast to the rest of the sites (Fig. 4C). Site on Tarfaya/Akhfennir road was marked by high SIC and electrical conductivity, along with increasing proportions of Ca and P. Oued Labouir and Sebkha Boulssane clustered together.

For S. tripartita, the closeness of three clusters in the PCA projection displays a moderate variation between the soils of the corresponding sites, suggesting that they share, to some extent, underlying soil properties (Fig. 4D). The fourth cluster was slightly separated from the others, revealing partial differentiation. ‘Grara Oued El Khet’ was characterized by increasing proportions of Al, K, Mn, Fe, and high SOC.

Composition of bacterial communities associated with the roots of four native plant species from desert ecosystems

The relative abundance and composition of root bacterial communities in each plant species across different sites based on phylum level are presented in Fig. 6. Across the different roots of sampled plants in different sites, a total of 1026 bacterial ASV sequences including 16 different phyla were detected. Regardless of the site, the four plant species exhibited some similarities in root microbiota composition, with Pseudomonadota phylum representing more than 50% of the total root associated bacterial community. Similarly, an average of 20% of the bacterial communities in the four plant species belonged to the Actinomycetota phylum. Although these similarities, significant variations in terms of abundance and composition of bacterial communities were observed both at plant species and site levels.

Relative abundance of root-associated bacteria in four desert plants species across different sites. Stacked bar plots show the top 10 most abundant bacterial taxa at phylum level in the roots of La. coronopifolia (A), Ly. intricatum (B), N. retusa (C), and S. tripartita (D) collected from different geographical sites

For La. coronopifolia, significant variations in terms of bacterial communities abundance and composition were noted between the four sites, where Actinomycetota and Bacillota phyla were the most abundant in the site 1, while Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota were the most abundant phyla in the three other sites (Fig. 6A). When it comes to Ly. intricatum, Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota represent more than 60 % of the total root microbiota regardless of the site (Fig. 6B). Besides the two previously cited phyla, Bacteroidota phylum was also abundant in the roots of N. retusa regardless of the site, with a notable presence of Planctomycetota phylum in site 11 (Fig. 6C). Finally, in the root of S. tripartita, Pseudomonadota was the dominant phylum, while Bacteroidota was more prevalent in site 15, and Bacillota was detected exclusively in site 13 (Fig. 6D).

Alpha and beta diversities of bacterial communities associated with the roots of four plants species from different geographical locations

In order to assess the composition of the root-associated bacterial communities associated with native plant species, 16S amplicon community analysis was performed on four desert plant species across different sites. The alpha diversity indices (evaluated using Shannon and Simpson) of the bacterial communities showed significant differences between the sites and the plant species across different sites (Fig. 7). Shannon’s diversity index revealed significant differences in La. coronopifolia across different sampled sites, diversity was significantly higher in roots collected from sites 2, 3, and 4 compared to site 1. In contrast, no significant variation in Shannon diversity was observed for Ly. intricatum, N. retusa, and S. tripartita. Similarly, Simpson index followed the same pattern as the Shannon index for L. coronopifolia, indicating greater microbial evenness in the same sites, while no significant differences were detected for the three other species.

Alpha diversity of root-associated bacterial communities in La. coronopifolia (A), Ly. intricatum (B), N. retusa (C), and S. tripartita (D) sampled across different geographical sites. Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon and Simpson indices

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and permuted multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) results indicated that geographical site significantly influenced the structure and the composition of bacterial community associated with the roots of the four desert plant species (Fig. 8A-D). The PCoA revealed significant variations in bacterial communities both within the same site among different plant species and between the sites (p < 0.05 except for Ly. intricatum), indicating that both plant species and geographical site shape the structure of root-associated bacterial in desert ecosystems. The Adonis PERMANOVA results indicate that 28.1 %, 17.6 %, 21.0 %, and 21.9 % of the variation in beta-diversity is explained by geographical site for La. coronopifolia, Ly. intricatum, N. retusa, and S. tripartita, respectively (Fig. 8A-D).

Interaction between soil properties and microbial communities

The rhizosphere network structures associated with the four desert plant species demonstrated notable differences in both the complexity and composition of their bacterial communities (Fig. 9 A-D). Despite this variability, all species shared key features, particularly the predominance of the bacterial phyla Actinomycetota and Pseudomonadota, which consistently dominated the networks in terms of connectivity and prevalence. The bacterial communities linked to the roots of N. retusa and S. tripartita displayed more complex network structures compared to those of La. coronopifolia and Ly. intricatum. Moreover, the presence of low abundance but ecologically relevant phyla such as Patescibacteria and Myxococcota was also detected .

Co-occurrence networks of root-associated bacterial communities in four desert plant species. Network visualizations indicate significant co-occurrence relationships among amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) detected in the root bacteriome of La. coronopifolia (A), Ly. intricatum (B), N. retusa (C), and S. tripartita (D)

The Mantel test indicated that root-associated bacterial community composition is variably shaped by environmental conditions and plant identity across the different study sites (Fig. 10). In the case of La. coronopifolia and N. retusa, no significant correlations emerged between microbial assemblages and the assessed soil physicochemical parameters (Fig. 10A and C). In contrast, for Ly. intricatum, Mg content appeared as a key environmental driver explaining variations in root-associated bacterial communities (Fig. 10B). Notably, the bacterial assemblages associated with S. tripartita were significantly influenced by multiple environmental factors, showing strong correlations with SOC, SIC, K, Ca, Mn, and Fe (Fig. 10D).

Mantel and Spearman correlation analysis between soil physicochemical properties and root microbial communities of La. coronopifolia (A), Ly. intricatum (B), N. retusa (C), and S. tripartita (D)

The environmental drivers of soil microbiome variation were evaluated using a multivariate statistical method: Redundancy Analysis (RDA) (Fig. 11). This approach enabled us to identify the soil parameters that most strongly influenced the composition of microbial communities associated with each plant species. For La. coronopifolia, K, pHKCl, Mg, and Ca were the primary factors shaping the microbial structure (Fig. 11A). In contrast, for Ly. intricatum, only P and Mg contributed (Fig. 11, B). Meanwhile, a broader range of soil parameters influenced the microbial composition associated with N. retusa and S. tripartita, suggesting more complex environmental interactions (Fig. 11C and D). These findings highlight the species-specific nature of soil-microbiome relationships, where each plant responds to distinct sets of environmental characteristics. They also underscore the intricate dynamics of plant-microbe interactions, which vary not only by species but also by the specific soil context in which each species grows.

Discussion

Descriptions of soil habitats of La. coronopifolia, Ly. Intricatum, N. retusa and S. tripartita

Our study aimed to investigate the soil properties associated with La. coronopifolia, Ly. intricatum, N. retusa, and S. tripartita, as well as the composition of their root-associated bacterial communities across four locations. Soils play a fundamental role in providing ecosystem services and are central to global challenges such as climate change mitigation, sustainable development, and food security [21–23]. Their characteristics are shaped by both natural and anthropogenic factors, including climate, biological activity, and land use [24]. Assessing these parameters is essential for understanding soil fertility and identifying potential limitations on plant growth.

Soil carbon (C), comprising organic carbon (SOC) and inorganic carbon (SIC), represents the largest carbon reservoir in the terrestrial biosphere and is a key component of the global carbon cycle [25]. Soil C stocks are highly heterogeneous and influenced by multiple factors. Soils in arid regions generally have some of the lowest SOC stocks worldwide, when expressed per unit area, due to constraints imposed by temperature, moisture, and coarse-textured sandy soils on annual primary productivity. In these ecosystems, SOC rarely exceeds 20 metric tons of carbon per hectare [26].

Electrical conductivity (EC) and soil pH are among main chemical properties determining soil health. Electrical conductivity reflects the concentration of soluble ions and overall ionic strength, influencing nutrient availability, microbial activity, and soil structure. While commonly used as a salinity indicator, EC combined with pH provides insight into soil cation exchange capacity. In arid soils, high iron (Fe) oxide content can block exchange sites, further limiting nutrient availability. Soil pH regulates mineral solubility and nutrient availability, with neutral to slightly alkaline values (6.0–7.5.0.5) being optimal for microbial activity, including organic matter decomposition, nitrogen fixation, and soil structure formation [27].

Lycium intricatum is a shrub wildly distributed in arid and semi-arid regions across many continents [28]. Populations from south Morocco are found on alkaline soils with pH ranging from 7.5 to 10, and EC values up to 1611 μS cm−1. While no previous study has examined the soil properties associated with Ly. intricatum, a related species, Ly. athium Bernardello, from semi-arid Argentina grows in soils with similar pH but much higher salinity, reaching up to 18 000 μS cm−1 [29].

N. retusa is a drought and salt-tolerant shrub that thrives in salt marshes, sand dunes and wadis channels near the littoral [30]. In our study, this plant species was found across a large variety of soils, from slightly acidic to strongly alkaline, with low to high salinity and low to relatively high SOC. Previous work by Hammad et al on N. retusa communities along the Red Sea coast in Egypt reported that the species grows in highly saline soils (around 26000 μS cm−1) with alkaline sandy substrates (pH = 8.99) [31]. Another study in Egypt reported variations in soil characteristics according to habitat type: populations in sand dunes and salt marshes occurred in soils with higher pH and EC, whereas those in wadi channels were found in soils that were considerably less saline and closer to neutral pH [32].

Significantly, S. tripartita demonstrates remarkable ecological plasticity, thriving primarily in sandy soils under xeric conditions [33]. In North Africa, this species has been reported to grow across arid, semi-arid, and hyper-arid regions [34]. Beyond its ecological presence, S. tripartita plays a key role in stabilizing sand movement, which underscores its potential use in the rehabilitation of degraded and marginal soils [35]. Our soil analysis showed that S. tripartita populations from southern Morocco establish in alkaline soils, with pH values varying from 7.2 to 9, relatively low EC ranging from 95 to 1611 μS cm−1, and SOC values between 2.8 and 24.4 g kg−1. Similar results were reported for populations from north-central Saudi Arabia, where soils exhibited pH values ranging between 7.05 and 7.95, EC values between 3.25 and 5.75 μS cm−1, and SOC between 12.5 and 31.9 g kg−1 [36]. Interestingly, another plant species belonging to same genus, Searisa coriaria L., has been observed to grow in soils rich in organic matter, Ca, and K in western Iran, further highlighting the ecological versatility within the genus [37].

Bacterial communities and diversity indices

Understanding biodiversity indices is essential in the present study, as they provide a quantitative measure of how plant species and their environments shape root-associated microbial communities. The diversity of these bacterial communities tends to be both plant-specific and site-dependent. Regardless of plant species and sites, Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota were consistently dominant, confirming their functional importance and adaptability to arid environments. These phyla are well known for their roles in nutrient cycling, drought tolerance, and plant growth promotion [38, 39]. In particular, Actinomycetota have been identified as ‘aridity-winners’ because of their remarkable adaptability to dry conditions and their role in shaping microbial communities by promoting the prevalence of aridity-tolerant taxa [40, 41]. Additionally, the relative abundance of rare phyla such as Myxococcota, Patescibacteria, and Verrucomicrobiota varied significantly between plant species and sites, further confirming the key role of plant host identity and environmental context in shaping specific microbiomes. For instance, Myxococcota was more prominent in Ly. intricatum and S. tripartita, whereas Patescibacteria were enriched in N. retusa. Notably, the roots of N. retusa displayed a distinct abundance of Halobacteriota (a phylum uniquely observed in this plant species), known for its high tolerance to salinity. Since N. retusa is a halophytic species adapted to saline environments and was collected from sites with EC ranging from 146 to over 8000 µS/cm, the enrichment of Halobacteriota provides strong evidence that plant species actively shape their root-associated bacterial communities in response to specific environmental conditions. Members of the Halobacteriota phylum have previously been reported to associate with saline environments and contribute to salt stress tolerance [42, 43]. Furthermore, the significant increase in the relative abundance of Bacillota in the root of S. tripartita at site 13 likely reflects a selective enrichment of spore-forming and drought-resilient taxa, consistent with this site being a Grara ecosystem characterized by high aridity and prolonged drought. These findings align with previous studies showing that extended drought conditions increase the relative abundance of Bacillota and Actinomycetota, while reducing Bacteroidota populations [38, 44].

The alpha diversity analysis indicated that both plant species and sites significantly influenced the structure of root-associated bacterial communities, as reflected by the Shannon and Simpson indices. In La. coronopifolia, bacterial diversity was significantly lower at Site 1 compared to the other sites (p<0.05), suggesting that site 1 is characterized by harsh environmental conditions that limit bacterial evenness and richness [45, 46, 60]. A similar trend was observed in Ly. intricatum and S. tripartita, where sites 5 and 13 exhibited the lowest diversity, further highlighting the strong impact of site-specific environmental factors on bacterial communities [46, 47]. In contrast, for N. retusa, although site 9 showed the lowest diversity values, bacterial diversity remained relatively stable across all sites. Overall, these patterns demonstrate that the alpha diversity of root-associated bacterial communities in desert soils is jointly shaped by host plant identity and local environmental conditions.

Interaction between surface rhizospheric soil properties and microbial communities

The rhizosphere network structures observed across the four desert plant species revealed substantial variation in bacterial community complexity and composition, underscoring the crucial role of each host species in shaping its own bacteriome. Despite these differences, all four species, being adapted to arid and hyper arid environments, shared common features in their root-associated bacterial communities, particularly the dominance of phyla well known for their resilience to drought and salinity. Actinomycetota and Pseudomonadota dominated the networks in terms of connectivity and prevalence across all plant species, consistent with previous studies that reported their persistence in extreme soils due to their metabolic versatility in nutrient cycling, and in the case of Actinomycetota, spore-forming ability [48, 49, 61]. Complex bacterial networks were more pronounced in roots of N. retusa and S. tripartita compared to La. coronopifolia and Ly. intricatum, which may be driven by differences in root exudate profiles and soil physico-chemical properties [50]. The detection of low-abundance phyla such as Patescibacteria and Myxococcota further highlights the ecological significance of rare microbial taxa and the potential of each plant species to shape distinct root-associated bacteriomes depending on environmental conditions [51, 52]. For instance, a study by Wang et al. demonstrated that rare microbial taxa including Patescibacteria, Nitrospirae, and Armatimonadetes regulate microbial necromass contributions to soil organic carbon under drought conditions, emphasizing their functional significance [53]. Similarly, Dong et al. reported that Myxococcota were enriched in the rhizosphere of Caragana intermedia Kuang & H.C.Fu in regions with an average annual precipitation of 450 mm, compared to sites with variable rainfall, suggesting that rare microbial taxa may act as bioindicators of drought adaptation [54].

The Mantel test revealed that the composition of root-associated bacterial communities was differentially influenced by environmental factors and plant species across the studied sites. In La. coronopifolia and N. retusa, no significant correlations were observed between microbial communities and the measured soil physicochemical properties, suggesting that the assembly of bacterial communities is primarily driven by plant-specific traits rather than by soil conditions. In contrast, in Ly. intricatum, Mg content emerged as a major correlating factor explaining the observed variation in root-associated bacterial communities. Moreover, microbial communities associated with S. tripartita were strongly shaped by several environmental parameters, showing significant correlations with SOC (p < 0.05), SIC (p < 0.01), K, Ca (p < 0.01), Mn (p < 0.01), and Fe (p < 0.01). These findings emphasize that the influence of environmental factors on microbial community assembly is highly dependent on both plant physiological traits and site-specific edaphic conditions. Altogether, our findings corroborate previous studies demonstrating that the interplay between plant species and environmental filtering strongly governs the structure and diversity of root-associated bacterial communities in desert soils [46, 55–59].

Correlation analyses at the phylum-level with soil physiochemical properties revealed distinct microbial responses to abiotic stress and nutrient availability, in a manner dependent on plant species. In Ly. intricatum, Bacillota and Pseudomonadota were positively correlated with pH, K content, and EC, suggesting that these bacterial phyla may be selectively enriched to support plant tolerance to alkaline and saline conditions and to enhance nutrient availability. In N. retusa and S. tripartita, Verrucomicrobiota and Planctomycetota were positively correlated with Ca, SIC, and EC, indicating a potential role of these phyla in adaptation to calcareous and saline soils, as well as in carbon cycling. These associations between plant species, microbial communities, and environmental conditions highlight that each plant species selectively recruits a distinct set of bacterial taxa to perform specific ecological functions under given pedoclimatic conditions. This targeted microbiome shaping reflects adaptive plant-microbe-soil interactions that sustain microbial resilience in extreme environments.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into the interactions between desert plants, soil properties, and their root-associated microbial communities. The distribution of plant species across sites reflected their specific preferences for soil quality, underscoring the importance of edaphic factors in defining ecological niches. At the microbial level, although community composition remained relatively stable at the phylum level, with Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota consistently dominant, geographic variation influenced the relative abundance of root-associated taxa, pointing to environmental filtering as a key driver of community structure. These shifts, while subtle at higher taxonomic levels, may become more pronounced when examined at the genus or species scale. Importantly, each plant species exhibited unique responses in both soil characteristics and microbial assemblages, highlighting the necessity of a species-specific approach to fully capture the complexity of plant-soil-microbe interactions in desert ecosystems. Taken together, our findings emphasize the ecological significance of these interactions and provide a framework for future research aimed at conserving desert biodiversity and enhancing ecosystem resilience under increasing environmental stress.
