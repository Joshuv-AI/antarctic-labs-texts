Human herpesvirus-6B infection leading to hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in an adult: a case report
Authors: Ji Yuan, Jinjin Li, Lei Jin, Jun Ye, Zhenhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) secondary to human herpesvirus-6B (HHV-6B) infection is rare in immunocompetent adults.Case presentationWe report the case of a 43-year-old East Asian (Chinese) woman who developed HLH, presenting with persistent fever, jaundice, and cytopenias. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) identified HHV-6B in both peripheral blood and liver tissue, thereby establishing the etiologic diagnosis. Prompt initiation of antiviral therapy with ganciclovir, combined with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin, resulted in rapid clinical remission.ConclusionsThis case highlights the critical importance of early pathogen identification using mNGS and timely immunomodulatory treatment for improving outcomes in adult patients with virus-associated HLH.

Background

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome driven by uncontrolled and sustained activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells. This dysregulated immune response leads to excessive proinflammatory cytokine secretion and macrophage activation, culminating in systemic inflammation and multiorgan dysfunction. HLH is classified into primary and secondary forms. Primary HLH results from genetic defects in immune regulation, whereas the secondary HLH usually develops due to intense immune activation triggered by infectious factors (such as viruses, bacteria, fungi, or parasites) or non-infectious agents (such as connective tissue diseases or malignant tumors) [1, 2]. Viral infections represent the most common trigger among secondary HLH cases, with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection being particularly prevalent and associated with an especially aggressive disease course and unfavorable outcomes. SARS-CoV-2, human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), human herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8), herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), parvovirus B19, adenoviruses, dengue, hepatitis C, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), have also emerged as recognized viral triggers for secondary HLH [3–9].

Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) was first isolated in 1986 from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with lymphoproliferative disorders and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) [10]. The species was formally subdivided into two subtypes, 6A and 6B, in 2011 [11]. HHV-6B, a member of the β-herpesvirinae subfamily, most commonly causes primary infection during infancy and early childhood, typically manifesting as exanthema subitum. The virus is transmitted predominantly via the respiratory tract, including infection through tonsils and olfactory nerve ensheathing cells in the nasal cavity. HHV-6B exhibits broad tropism, productively infecting immune cells, neurons, hepatocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cell, epithelial cells, and reproductive tract epithelial cells, and has been implicated in post-transplant infection, lymphoproliferative disorder, and multiple sclerosis [2]. Cases of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) caused by HHV-6B are extremely rare [12].

We report a case of HHV-6B infection in a healthy adult who initially presented with fever and jaundice, and subsequently progressed to HLH. The aim is to explore the pathogenesis and treatment strategies of HHV-6B-related HLH by analyzing the clinical manifestations and treatment course of this case and referring to relevant literature.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old female (East Asian, Chinese) was admitted to our department on October 28, 2024, with a 1-month history of intermittent fever and a 3-week history of scleral icterus and dark urine. The patient was previously healthy and took no regular medications, denying histories of smoking, alcohol consumption, and commercial sexual exposure. Immunization history could not be verified. Laboratory tests performed at a local hospital had shown total bilirubin (TBil) 270 μmol/L(normal: 0–17.1 μmol/L), direct bilirubin (DBil) 202 μmol/L, and hemoglobin (Hb) 152 g/L (normal: 115–150 g/L). During the course of the disease, she had a transient maculopapular rash. On admission, our investigations revealed TBil 201 μmol/L (normal: 0–17.1 μmol/L), CRP 39.6 mg/L (normal: 0–6 mg/L), procalcitonin (PCT) 1.89 ng/mL (normal: 0–0.1 ng/mL), ferritin (Fer) 28,600 ng/mL (normal: 13–150 ng/mL), LDH 2676 U/L (normal: 120–250U/L), and AFP 79.98 ng/mL (normal: 0–7 ng/mL). Urinalysis was positive for urobilinogen (1 +) but negative for bilirubin. Antinuclear antibody titer was 1:320, whereas viral hepatitis serology, coagulation studies, autoimmune hepatitis panel, immunoglobulins, IgG4, Coombs’ test and ceruloplasmin were all negative. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed no vascular abnormalities in the liver. The chest and abdominal CT scan demonstrated splenomegaly.

After admission, the patient experienced continuous high fever. On the second day, she abruptly developed progressive leukopenia and anemia. The re-examination showed WBC 3.63 × 109/L(normal: 3.5–9.5 × 109/L), Hb 51 g/L, TBil 138 μmol/L. At this juncture, our differential diagnosis included sepsis, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), lymphoma, and aplastic anemia. Subsequent workup demonstrated Fer 64635 ng/ml, Soluble CD25 antigen (sCD25) 2391.3 pg/mL (normal: 0–600 pg/mL), triglycerides (TG) 3.57 mmol/L (normal: 0.56–1.7 mmol/L). Based on the HLH-2004 diagnostic guidelines, the diagnosis of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis was thus unequivocally confirmed [2]. We immediately conducted peripheral blood metagenomic sequencing (mNGS) and bone marrow puncture on the patient. At the same time, empiric treatment was given with cefoperazone sulbactam, ganciclovir, methylprednisolone at 60 mg/d and intravenous immunogloblin (IVIG) at 20 g/d, together with washed-red-cell transfusions, hematopoietic stimulation, hepatoprotective and anti-transaminase therapy. Five days later, laboratory values showed WBC 61.42*10^9/L, Hb 47 g/L, TBil 66.8 μmol/L, Fer 8130 ng/mL, and TG 6.70 mmol/L.

On hospital day 3, the peripheral blood mNGS returned positive for HHV-6B (235RPTM) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (102RPTM). Bone marrow aspirate showed an infectious marrow pattern with pure red cell aplasia; However, bone marrow biopsy did not demonstrate hemophagocytosis. Flow cytometry revealed an aberrant γδ T-cell population, yet lymphoma gene rearrangement studies were negative. We further conducted the PET–CT technique, which showed no abnormal FDG uptake throughout the body. At this point, we excluded SFTS, lymphoma, and aplastic anemia. Subsequently, we calculated the H-score for this patient: fever > 39.4℃ (+ 49), hepatosplenomegaly (+ 38), pancytopenia involving two lineages (+ 24), ferritin > 6000 ng/mL (+ 50), hypertriglyceridemia (+ 64), hypofibrinogenemia (+ 0), hemophagocytosis in bone marrow(+ 0), and AST ≥ 30 IU/L (+ 19), without known underlying immunosuppression (+ 0), resulting in a total H-score of 244. This score substantially exceeds the diagnostic threshold of 169, indicating > 99% probability of HLH [13]. Based on the results of all the above examinations, antimicrobial and antiviral therapy were continued, while the dosage of methylprednisolone was gradually reduced (60 mg/d for 5 days, then 40 mg/d for 5 days, then 20 mg/d for 5 days), and IVIG was reduced to 2.5 g daily to modulate immune response. Following these interventions, the patient’s temperature remained normal. Two weeks later, the WBC was 6.01*109/L, Hb was 53 g/L, TBil was 52 μmol/L, Fer was 4967 ng/ml. Subsequently, we reduced methylprednisolone to 16 mg/d and discontinued IVIG.

After the patient’s condition stabilized, informed consent was obtained and a percutaneous liver biopsy was performed to clarify the underlying aetiology. Histology revealed acute hepatocellular injury (grade 3 inflammation, stage 1 fibrosis, G3S1), and mNGS of liver tissue detected HHV-6B (85RPTM), confirming HHV-6B-driven HLH as the root cause of the febrile illness. After 22 days of inpatient management, the patient was discharged and gradually reduced the dosage of methylprednisolone in the outpatient department. The fever did not recur, liver and kidney function tests remained within normal limits, Hb gradually increased. Methylprednisolone was discontinued 6 weeks after discharge. Follow-up laboratories showed WBC 6.92 × 10⁹/L, Hb 140 g/L, TBil 7.8 µmol/L, and Fer 592 ng/mL.

Conclusions and discussion

HLH is a multisystem inflammatory syndrome characterized by dysregulated immune activation. Despite similar fundamental pathophysiology across triggers, HHV-6B-associated HLH exhibits distinctive features that differentiate it from other viral etiologies, particularly EBV-related HLH.

Unlike EBV, which primarily infects B cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and impairs their cytotoxic function through latent membrane proteins, HHV-6B exhibits preferential tropism for CD4 + T cells and evades NK cell attack by down regulating activating ligands [14, 15]. This fundamental difference in target cell specificity and immune evasion strategy results in distinct immunopathological consequences and clinical outcomes. HHV-6B infection drives sustained activation of CD8 + T cells through indirect mechanisms, releasing large amounts of IFN-γ, which in turn activates macrophages and induces high levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18 and TNF-ɑ. TNF-ɑ further amplifies macrophage activation in an autocrine and paracrine manner, causing tissue damage via IL-33 and IL-1β, and reduces lipoprotein lipase activity, leading to hypertriglyceridaemia that worsens HLH. IFN-γ can further enhance this cycle by up-regulating MHC-I expression on infected cells. This reciprocal T-cell/macrophage activation leads to synchronous increases in soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R, a T-cell activation marker) and soluble CD163 (a macrophage activation marker) [16, 17].

Clinically, viral-associated HLH typically presents with a fulminant course. In HHV-6B-associated HLH, the characteristic roseola-like rash often heralds disease onset, reflecting the viral CD4 + T-cell tropism and subsequent immune activation [18]. However, the prognosis differs markedly by etiology: mortality exceeds 90% in untreated HLH [19], HHV-6B-associated cases demonstrate relatively better treatment response compared to EBV-related HLH [20, 21].

The discordant mNGS findings between peripheral blood and liver tissue provided critical insights into pathogen causality. Pseudomonas aeruginosa was detected exclusively in peripheral blood with negative liver tissue results and absence of neutrophilic abscess formation, strongly suggesting a non-initiating factor. Conversely, HHV-6B was identified in both specimens with high viral loads, indicating active replication in the primarily affected organ.

Regarding the hemophagocytic phenomenon, studies have also shown that the hemophagocytic phenomenon observed in virus-associated HLH may result from phagocytic cells themselves or the original changes of the substances being phagocytosed. The virus can directly act on the membrane of red blood cells, representing a significant contributory mechanism to the severe anemia encountered in this setting [22]. In addition, CD1 + can macrophages phagocytize free hemoglobin and produce IL-10 and heme oxygenase-1(HO-1). HO-1 decomposes heme into carbon monoxide (CO) and ferrous iron (Fe2⁺). The latter is oxidized to ferric iron and further generates Fer [23]. The combination of direct viral effects on erythrocytes and macrophage activation contributes to the cytopenias characteristic of HLH.

The treatment response represents another critical distinction. HHV-6B-associated HLH demonstrates favorable response to antiviral therapy with ganciclovir or foscarnet, whereas EBV–HLH requires antiviral drugs, immunosuppressive agents and systemic chemotherapy with or without allogeneic stem cell transplantation(SCT) [24]. Severe bacterial HLH often requires combined antimicrobial and immunosuppressive therapy. In contrast, tick-borne HLH is driven by direct pathogen-induced inflammation rather than primary immune dysregulation, and usually resolves with targeted antimicrobial therapy alone. A scoping review of 98 cases of HLH secondary to tick-borne infections showed that 43.9% of patients received antimicrobial therapy without immunosuppression, with 88.4% achieving complete recovery [25]. These findings are helpful in guiding the appropriate classification of treatments and avoiding unnecessary immunosuppression.

HHV-6B is a common pathogen causing roseola in infants. However, it can also cause secondary HLH in adult individuals. Although HHV-6B-associated HLH shares the common final pathophysiological pathway of T-cell/macrophage activation with other forms, its characteristic exanthema, preferential CD4 + T-cell tropism and favorable response to antiviral therapy distinguish it from EBV and other viral triggers. This case highlights the importance of rapid pathogen diagnosis, such as mNGS, and early identification and prompt immunomodulatory intervention in adult HLH. Clinicians confronted with fever, exanthema, jaundice, and decreased blood cells should keep virus-associated HLH in the differential diagnosis. Prompt etiologic identification and immediate intervention are pivotal for improving the prognosis.
