The Hasse principle for diagonal forms restricted to a hypersurface of adjacent degree
Anna Theorin Johansson

Abstract
. We investigate the Hasse principle for Diophantine systems consisting of one diagonal form of degree k k and one general form of degree k − 1 k-1 . By refining the method of Brandes and Parsell [ 1 ] in this specific setting, we improve the bound n > 2 k ​ k n>2^{k}k to n > 2 k − 1 ​ ( 2 ​ k − 1 ) n>2^{k-1}(2k-1) ; in particular, the requirement n > 24 n>24 in the case of degrees three and two is relaxed to n > 20 n>20 .

1.
Introduction

Suppose that we are given a pair of forms
F,G\in\Z[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of degree
k
and
d
respectively. The goal of this paper is to give an asymptotic formula for the number
N_{F,G}(X)
of integer points
\x\in[-X,X]^{n}
satisfying
F(\x)=G(\x)=0
. For
n
sufficiently large and for suitably non-singular forms, we expect this to be of the form

(1.1)

N_{F,G}(X)=X^{n-k-d}\left(\Constant+O\left(X^{-\nu}\right)\right),

where
\Constant\geq 0
is a constant and
\nu>0
is a small number, both dependent at most on the forms
F
and
G
. In particular, the objective is to find a
n_{0}=n_{0}(k,d)
such that
Eq.

1.1
is satisfied for all
n>n_{0}
.

If both
F
and
G
are diagonal, Wooley’s efficient congruencing method gives

n_{0}(k,d)\leq k(k+1)

for any two distinct degrees
k>d
, with better bounds if
d
is much smaller than
k
(see
[
7
, §14]
). In the case
k=3
and
d=2
, one can achieve

n_{0}(3,2)\leq\frac{32}{3},

such that
n=11
would suffice
[
6
]
, which reaches the square root barrier.

On the other hand, without any assumption of diagonality, one must resort to the work of Browning and Heath-Brown in
[
3
]
, which studies systems of forms of differing degrees in full generality. These methods give an upper bound for
n_{0}
which grows exponentially in both
k
and
d
; to be precise, in
[
3
, Corollary 1.8]
it is shown that

(1.2)

n_{0}(k,d)\leq(2+d)(k-1)2^{k-1}+d2^{d-1}.

With
k=3
and
d=2
, this corresponds to

n_{0}(3,2)\leq 36,

but this particular result can actually be sharpened. Indeed, Browning, Dietmann and Heath-Brown, in
[
2
, Theorem 1.3]
, show that for one cubic and one quadratic, one can actually take

(1.3)

n_{0}(3,2)\leq 28.

In the case where
F
is diagonal but no particular shape restriction is put on
G
, Brandes and Parsell showed in
[
1
]
that one can use the diagonal structure of
F
to replace the exponential growth in
k
in
Eq.

1.2
by a quadratic one. With
F
diagonal of degree
k
and
G
of degree
d<k
,
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
gives

(1.4)

n_{0}(k,d)\leq\begin{cases}2^{k}(d+1)&\quad\text{if }d+1\leq k\leq d+4,\\
2^{d}(26+32d)&\quad\text{if }k=d+5,\\
2^{d}\left[(2d+1)k^{2}-L_{d}(k)\right]&\quad\text{if }k\geq d+6,\end{cases}

with
L_{d}(k)=(4d^{2}+8d+1)k-2d^{3}-7d^{2}-5d-4d\left\lfloor\sqrt{2k-2d}\right\rfloor-2\left\lfloor\sqrt{2k-2d+2}\right\rfloor
. This is an improvement of
Eq.

1.2
in all cases; in particular, if
d=k-1
, this is

(1.5)

n_{0}(k,k-1)\leq 2^{k}k.

For instance,

(1.6)

n_{0}(3,2)\leq 24

is a clear improvement to
Eq.

1.3
.

The goal for this paper is to streamline the use of the Weyl inequality in the proof of the above in the case
d=k-1
to improve
Eq.

1.5
. The following is our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
F,G\in\Z[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a pair of non-singular forms, where
F
is diagonal of degree
k\geq 3
of the shape

F(\x)=F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=c_{1}x_{1}^{k}+\dots+c_{n}x_{n}^{k},

and
G
has degree

d=k-1.

Suppose that

(1.7)

n>2^{k-1}(2k-1).

Then for some
\nu>0
, we have

N_{F,G}(X)=X^{n-k-d}\left(\Constant_{F,G}+O\left(X^{-\nu}\right)\right),

where
\Constant_{F,G}\geq 0
is a product of local solution densities associated with the system
F(\x)=G(\x)=0
.

Here, as will be shown below, the constant is
\Constant_{F,G}=\chi_{\infty}\prod_{p}\chi_{p}
, where
\chi_{\infty}
and
\chi_{p}
can be interpreted as the volume of the solution set of the system
F(\x)=G(\x)=0
in the real and
p
-adic unit cubes, respectively.

In particular, for the case of one diagonal cubic and one quadratic form, this beats
Eq.

1.6
by four variables:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
C,Q\in\Z[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a pair of non-singular forms, where
C
is cubic and diagonal of the shape

C(\x)=C(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=c_{1}x_{1}^{3}+\dots+c_{n}x_{n}^{3},

and
Q
is quadratic. Suppose that

n>20.

Then, for some
\nu>0
, we have

N_{C,Q}(X)=X^{n-5}\left(\Constant_{C,Q}+O\left(X^{-\nu}\right)\right),

where
\Constant_{C,Q}\geq 0
.

While our methods, combined with those of
[
1
, §2]
, would work also for
d<k-1
, this would give

n>2^{k-1}(k+d),

which is weaker than
Eq.

1.4
for all
d\leq k-3
(and equal for
d=k-2
).

2.
Overview of paper

Theorem

1.1
will be proved using the Hardy-Littlewood circle method, following the arguments of
[
1
, Theorem 1.3]
. As mentioned in the introduction, the goal is to obtain an asymptotic formula for the quantity

N_{F,G}(X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\x\in[-X,X]^{n}\cap\Z^{n}\\
F(\x)=G(\x)=0\end{subarray}}1.

Using the identity

\oint e(\alpha n)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if }n=0,\\
0&\quad\text{if }n\in\Z\setminus\left\{0\right\},\end{cases}

we can reformulate the sum as

N_{F,G}(X)=\oint S(\bm{\alpha})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}.

Here, for each
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\in\T^{2}
, we define

S(\bm{\alpha})=S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d};X):=\sum_{\x\in[-X,X]^{n}\cap\Z^{n}}e\left(\alpha_{k}F(\x)+\alpha_{d}G(\x)\right).

The strategy is now to split the region of integration
\T^{2}
into two sets

\T^{2}=\Ka\sqcup\ki,

and then show an asymptotic formula

\int_{\Ka}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\sim C_{X}X^{n-k-d}

as
X\to\infty
, with
C_{X}
a product of local densities, together with a bound

\int_{\ki}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}=O\left(X^{n-k-d-\nu}\right)

for some
\nu>0
. In fact, this is done in two steps: first we define a one-dimensional set of major and minor arcs for
\alpha_{k}
of the form

\T=\Ma\sqcup\mi,

and then we proceed to, inside
\Ma\times\T
, define a two-dimensional set of major and minor arcs so that

\T^{2}=\left(\Na\sqcup\nn\right)\sqcup\left(\mi\times\T\right).

Using classic Weyl differencing as presented in
Section

3
, we show in
Section

4
that the contributions from
\mi
and
\nn
are suitably small, and are left to deal with
\Na
in
Section

5
.

The major arcs for
\alpha_{k}
are defined as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

For a parameter
\theta
, let
\Ma(\theta)
be the set of
\alpha_{k}\in\T
with the property

\left\|\alpha_{k}q\right\|\leq X^{-k+\theta}

for some natural number
q\leq X^{\theta}
. Set
\mi(\theta)=\T\setminus\Ma(\theta)
.

The major arcs
\Ma(\theta)
are disjoint for all
\theta\leq 1
, and

(2.1)

\text{vol}\Ma(\theta)\ll\sum_{q\leq X^{\theta}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}^{q}\frac{X^{-k+\theta}}{q}\ll X^{-k+2\theta}.

2.1.
Minor arc contribution

The minor arcs
\mi(\theta)
are defined as having either
q>X^{\theta}
or
\left\|\alpha_{k}q\right\|X^{k}>X^{\theta}
. To treat these, we use
d
successive Weyl differencing steps, to remove the degree
d
term.

For
d
differencing variables
\w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})\in\Z^{d}
, define

(2.2)

p_{\w}(x):=\partial_{w_{1}}\cdots\partial_{w_{d}}x^{k}=w_{1}\cdots w_{d}\ell_{\w}(x).

where
\ell_{\w}(x)
is a linear polynomial whose leading coefficient is independent of
\w
. This means that

\partial_{w_{1}}\cdots\partial_{w_{d}}\left(\alpha_{k}F(\x)+\alpha_{d}G(\x)\right)=\alpha_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}p_{\w}(x_{i})+\alpha_{d}c,

with
c
a constant independent of
\x
.

For
\h_{1},\dots\h_{d}\in\Z^{n}
, set
\h^{(i)}:=(h_{1,i},\dots,h_{d,i})
for each
i=1,\dots,n
. Vaughan’s proof of
[
5
, Lemma 2.3]
shows that for suitable sets
I(\h^{(i)})\subseteq[-X,X]\cap\Z
, after
d
differencing steps,

\left|S(\bm{\alpha})\right|^{2^{d}}\ll X^{\left(2^{d}-d-1\right)n}\sum_{\left|\h_{1}\right|\leq X}\dots\sum_{\left|\h_{d}\right|\leq X}\left|\sum_{x_{1}\in I(\h^{(1)})}\cdots\sum_{x_{n}\in I(\h^{(n)})}e\left(\alpha_{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}p_{\h^{(i)}}(x_{i})\right)\right)\right|.

This means that the question of estimating
\left|S(\bm{\alpha})\right|
is turned into the task of studying the inner sum above. Unless
\alpha_{k}
is close to a rational number with small denominator, classic exponential sum estimates from
Section

3
can be used to get a satisfactory bound for the contribution to the integral, as will be done in
Lemma

4.3
.

2.2.
Major arc contribution

To estimate the contribution from
\Ma(\theta)
, we use the approach of
[
1
]
, defining a two-dimensional set of major and minor arcs
\Na(\eta)
and
\nn(\eta)
with the help of a parameter
\eta
. As in the previous step, we are able to say in
Lemma

4.4
that unless
\alpha_{d}
is close to a rational number with small denominator, the contribution to the integral is suitably small; a precise estimate is shown in
Lemma

4.7
. What is left is finally the set
\Na(\eta)
where both
\alpha_{k}
and
\alpha_{d}
are well approximated, which is treated in
Section

5
.

We wish to prove an asymptotic formula of the form

\int_{\Na(\eta)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}=X^{n-k-d}\chi_{\infty}\prod_{p}\chi_{p}+O\left(X^{n-k-d-\nu}\right)

for some
\nu>0
. Here,

\chi_{\infty}:=\int_{\R^{2}}J(\bm{\gamma})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma},

where for each
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{k},\gamma_{d})\in\R^{2}
, we define

J(\bm{\gamma}):=\int_{[-1,1]^{n}}e\left(\gamma_{k}F(\bm{\xi})+\gamma_{d}G(\bm{\xi})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

Moreover,

\chi_{p}:=\lim_{h\to\infty}\left(p^{h}\right)^{2-n}\Gamma(p^{h})

with

\Gamma(q):=\#\left\{\x\in(\Z/q\Z)^{n}:F(\x)\equiv G(\x)\equiv 0\mod q\right\}.

3.
Weyl estimates

Let
\ell\in\Z[x]
be a linear polynomial and let
\Interval\subseteq[-X,X]
be an interval. Set

\varphi(\alpha)=\varphi(\alpha;\Interval):=\sum_{x\in\Interval\cap\Z}e\left(\alpha\ell(x)\right).

As in
Definition

2.1
, define the major arcs
\Ma(\theta)
and
\mi(\theta)=\T\setminus\Ma(\theta)
. We want to show that the sum
\varphi(\alpha)
is small when
\alpha
lies on the minor arcs.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
\alpha\in\mi(\theta)
for some
\theta\leq 1
. Then

\sum_{h=1}^{X^{k-1}}\left|\varphi(h\alpha)\right|\ll X^{k-\theta+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

This is shown using Dirichlet’s theorem and
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
.
∎

Together with a standard divisor bound,
Lemma

3.1
proves the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
\alpha\in\mi(\theta)
for some
\theta\leq X
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|h_{1}\right|,\dots,\left|h_{k-1}\right|\leq X\\
h_{1}\cdots h_{k-1}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\left|\varphi(h_{1}\cdots h_{k-1}\alpha)\right|\ll X^{k-\theta+\varepsilon}.

4.
Minor arc contribution

Lemma

3.2
can be used to bound the size of
S(\bm{\alpha})
on the minor arcs.

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
\theta\in(0,1]
and every
\alpha_{d}\in\T
, we have

\sup_{\alpha_{k}\in\mi(\theta)}\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|\ll X^{n-\frac{1}{2^{d}}n\theta+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

This goes along the lines of
[
1
, §5]
. Recall from the discussion in
Section

2.1
that one can estimate the size of
S(\bm{\alpha})
by

\left|S(\bm{\alpha})\right|^{2^{d}}\ll X^{\left(2^{d}-d-1\right)n}\sum_{\left|\h_{1}\right|\leq X}\dots\sum_{\left|\h_{d}\right|\leq X}\left|\sum_{x_{1}\in I(\h^{(1)})}\dots\sum_{x_{n}\in I(\h^{(n)})}e\left(\alpha_{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}p_{\h^{(i)}}(x_{i})\right)\right)\right|

for
\h_{1},\dots\h_{d}\in\Z^{n}
. Here,
p
is as in
Eq.

2.2
, there are
d
-dimensional differencing variables
\h^{(i)}:=(h_{1,i},\dots,h_{d,i})
for each
i=1,\dots,n
, and the
I(\h^{(i)})\subseteq[-X,X]\cap\Z
are the sets appearing in
[
5
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Define

\displaystyle f(\alpha;\h)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x\in I(\h)}e\left(\alpha p_{\h}(x)\right),\quad\alpha\in\R,\ \h\in\Z^{d},

\displaystyle g(\alpha;\BBox)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\h\in\BBox}\left|f(\alpha;\h)\right|,\quad\BBox\subseteq\Z^{d},

\displaystyle g(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=g\left(\alpha;[-X,X]^{d}\cap\Z^{d}\right).

Now the sum above can be reformulated. With the general inequality

\left|a_{1}\cdots a_{m}\right|\leq\left|a_{1}\right|^{m}+\dots+\left|a_{m}\right|^{m}\quad\forall a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\C,

applied to the product of the
g(\alpha_{k}c_{i})
, one obtains

(4.1)

\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|^{2^{d}}\ll X^{(2^{d}-d-1)n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i}\right)\ll X^{(2^{d}-d-1)n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i}\right)^{n}.

The shape of the terms in
Eq.

2.2
means that the sum defining
f
is trivial if
h_{1}\cdots h_{d}=0
, which suggests that we should treat any terms with
h_{i}=0
for some
i
separately in the exponential sums above in order to get a decent bound. To this end, the box is split up into a sum over the terms with
h_{1}\cdots h_{d}=0
and the complement of this. Set

\Zero:=\left\{\h\in[-X,X]^{d}\cap\Z^{d}:h_{1}\cdots h_{d}=0\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Zero^{c}:=[-X,X]^{d}\cap\Z^{d}\setminus\Zero.

Clearly

\left|\Zero\right|\ll X^{d-1},

since at least one of the components must be zero, and for
\h\in\Zero
, we have

p_{\h}(x)=h_{1}\cdots h_{d}\ell_{\h}(x)=0,

which means that, for such
\h
, the sum defining
f
is

f(\alpha,\h)=\sum_{\x\in I(\h)}1\ll X.

Hence

g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i}\right)\ll X^{d-1}\max_{\h\in\Zero}\left|f\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\h\right)\right|+g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\Zero^{c}\right)\ll X^{d}+g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\Zero^{c}\right).

The bound in
Eq.

4.1
then becomes

\displaystyle\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|^{2^{d}}

\displaystyle\ll X^{(2^{d}-d-1)n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X^{d}+g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\Zero^{c}\right)\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\ll X^{(2^{d}-1)n}+X^{(2^{d}-d-1)n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\Zero^{c}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\ll X^{(2^{d}-1)n}+X^{(2^{d}-d-1)n}\max_{i}g\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\Zero^{c}\right)^{n}.

There must now be an
i\in\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
such that

\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|^{2^{d}}\ll X^{(2^{d}-1)n}+X^{(2^{d}-d-1)n}\left(\sum_{\h\in\Zero^{c}}\left|f\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\h\right)\right|\right)^{n}.

Lemma

3.2
gives

\sup_{\alpha_{k}\in\mi(\theta)}\sum_{\h\in\Zero^{c}}\left|f\left(\alpha_{k}c_{i};\h\right)\right|\ll X^{k-\theta+\varepsilon}=X^{d+1-\theta+\varepsilon},

which in particular means that

\sup_{\alpha_{k}\in\mi(\theta)}\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|^{2^{d}}\ll X^{(2^{d}-1)n}+X^{(2^{d}-d-1)n}\left(X^{d+1-\theta+\varepsilon}\right)^{n}\ll X^{(2^{d}-\theta)n+\varepsilon}.

Now one can take the
2^{d}
th root of the above to arrive at the desired result.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In particular, with
\theta=1
, this means that for any
\alpha_{k}\in\mi(1)
and any
\alpha_{d}\in\T
, we have

\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|\leq X^{\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{d}}\right)n+\varepsilon},

which implies

\oint\int_{\mi(1)}\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{d}\ll X^{\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{d}}\right)n+\varepsilon}.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
Eq.

1.7
holds. For
\theta_{*}\in(0,1]
, if

(4.2)

\theta_{*}>\frac{2^{d}d}{n-2^{d+1}},

then there is a

such that for all
\theta\in[\theta_{*},1]
, we have

\oint\int_{\mi(\theta)}\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{d}\ll X^{n-k-d-\nu}.

Proof.

To prove this, as in the proof of

1.7

.

Construct a decreasing sequence

of the form

. With the requirement for

in

, it is clear that this can be chosen with

and

2^{d+1}\left(\theta_{i-1}-\theta_{i}\right)<\left(n-2^{d+1}\right)\theta_{*}-2^{d}d.

Note that

so it remains to show that

and then each set
\mi(\theta_{i})\setminus\mi(\theta_{i-1})
give an acceptable contribution to the integral.

For
\theta_{0}=1
, combining

4.2
with the restriction for

in

yields

\oint\int_{\mi(\theta_{0})}\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{d}\ll X^{n-k-d-\nu}

for some suitable

.

Next, for each

, the bound for

on the minor arcs in

4.1
together with the volume estimate

2.1
on the major arcs gives

\displaystyle\oint\int_{\mi(\theta_{i})\setminus\mi(\theta_{i-1})}\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha_{d}

\displaystyle\ll\text{vol}\Ma(\theta_{i-1})\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{k}\in\mi(\theta_{i})\\
\alpha_{d}\in\T\end{subarray}}\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})\right|

\displaystyle\ll X^{-k+2\theta_{i-1}}X^{n-\frac{1}{2^{d}}n\theta_{i}+\varepsilon}.

Now

2\theta_{i-1}-\frac{n}{2^{d}}\theta_{i}=2\left(\theta_{i-1}-\theta_{i}\right)-\left(\frac{n}{2^{d}}-2\right)\theta_{i}<-d,

4.1.
Two-dimensional split

We have now shown that unless
\alpha_{k}
is well approximated by rationals, the contribution to the minor arc integral is small. What can then be said in the other case? As in
[
3
]
, a similar Weyl argument shows that whenever
\alpha_{k}
has a good rational approximation
a/q
, unless
\alpha_{d}
is well approximated with the same denominator
q
, the corresponding contribution will also be small. The following lemma makes this precise.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
\theta\in(0,1]
. If
\alpha_{k}\in\Ma(\theta)
, with corresponding rational approximation
a/q
, then for any
\eta
in the range

(4.4)

0<\eta\leq 1-\theta,

one of the following three statements are true:

(A)

Either

\left|S(\bm{\alpha})\right|\ll X^{n-\kappa\eta+\varepsilon},

(B)

or there is a natural number
r\leq X^{(d-1)\eta}
such that

\left\|qr\alpha_{d}\right\|\ll X^{-d+(d-1)\eta+\theta},

(C)

or

n\leq 2^{d-1}\kappa.

Proof.

This is
[
3
, Lemma 6.1]
.
∎

In order to exclude
(C)
, we assume from now on that

Remark 4.5
.

Without loss of generality, one can set

(4.5)

\kappa\eta=\frac{1}{2^{d}}n\theta.

To see this, combine the bound on
S(\bm{\alpha})
in
(A)
with that of
Lemma

4.1
.

Next, the set
\Ma(\theta)
is partitioned into two sets, depending on properties of
\alpha_{d}
as per the subdivision in

4.4
. With a careful choice of constant, this yields a two-dimensional set of major arcs
\Na(\eta)
consisting of those
\bm{\alpha}
for which
(B)
holds, with complement
\nn(\eta)
consisting of the remaining
\bm{\alpha}
where
(B)
fails but
(A)
holds.

Definition 4.6
.

For a constant
c>0
, let
\Na(\eta)=\Na(\eta,\theta)
be the set of
\bm{\alpha}\in\T^{2}
with

\displaystyle\left\|\alpha_{k}q\right\|\leq cX^{-k+\theta},

\displaystyle\left\|\alpha_{d}qr\right\|\leq cX^{-d+(d-1)\eta+\theta}

for some
1\leq q\leq X^{\theta}
and
1\leq r\leq X^{(d-1)\eta}
. Define
\nn(\eta)=\T^{2}\setminus\Na(\eta)
.

Two remarks are in order:

(1)

Combining the size restrictions on
S(\bm{\alpha})
in
Lemma

4.1
and in
(A)
using the equality
Eq.

4.5
gives

(4.6)

\left|S(\bm{\alpha})\right|\ll X^{n-\kappa\eta+\varepsilon}

for all
\bm{\alpha}\in\nn(\eta)
.

(2)

Using the equality
Eq.

4.5
, we can estimate:

(4.7)

\begin{split}\text{vol}\Na(\eta)&\ll\sum_{1\leq q\leq cX^{\theta}}\sum_{1\leq a\leq q}\frac{X^{-k+\theta}}{q}\sum_{1\leq r\leq cX^{(d-1)\eta}}\sum_{1\leq b\leq qr}\frac{X^{-d+(d-1)\eta+\theta}}{qr}\\
&\ll X^{-k-d+2(d-1)\eta+3\theta}\\
&\ll X^{-k-d+\left(2(d-1)+\frac{3\cdot 2^{d}\cdot\kappa}{n}\right)\eta}.\end{split}

4.2.
Two-dimensional minor arcs

We can apply

4.3
and

4.4
using the two estimates in

and

to bound the integral over

.

.

Suppose that

holds, together with

(4.8)

and

(4.9)

in the range

(4.10)

the integral over the two-dimensional minor arcs can be bounded by

\int_{\nn(\eta)}\left|S(\bm{\alpha})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\ll X^{n-k-d-\nu}

for some

Proof.

This is shown using
[
1
, Lemma 3.4]
, with

4.3
in place of
[

, Lemma 3.2]
. We have
\rho=1
,

, and

, so that

\eta_{*}=\frac{n}{2^{d}\kappa}\theta_{*}.

To complete the proof, we need to make sure that a
\theta_{*}
satisfying
Eq.

4.2
can indeed be found (and hence a suitable
\eta_{*}
). Since

4.4
is equivalent to
Eq.

4.10
under the assumption

on
\kappa
and
\eta
, taking

\displaystyle\eta_{\max}:=\left(1+\frac{2^{d}\kappa}{n}\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle\theta_{\max}:=\frac{2^{d}\kappa}{n+2^{d}\kappa},

gives a permissible
\theta_{\max}
, and now

\theta_{\max}>\frac{2^{d}d}{n-2^{d+1}}

is equivalent to
Eq.

4.8
.
∎

Suppose now that
Eq.

1.7
holds. It is clear that one can find a
\kappa
that satisfies

as well as the hypotheses of
Lemma

4.7
. First,

4.3
ensures that the contribution from

is suitably small, and then

4.4
guarantees that for any

). Next, by

, for sufficiently small

, the counting function is

(4.11)

It remains only to bound the contribution from the major arcs

5.
Major arc contribution

Next, we turn to the analysis of the major arcs. This is performed exactly as in

We can begin by enlarging the set of major arcs slightly.

Definition 5.1
.

Define

(5.1)

\omega=(d-1)\eta+\theta.

With this, let
\Pa(\omega)
be the set of
\bm{\alpha}\in\T^{2}
with the property that there is a natural number
s\leq c^{\prime}X^{\omega}
, an
\a=(a_{k},a_{d})\in\Z^{2}
and a
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{k},\gamma_{d})
such that

(5.2)

\alpha_{k}=\frac{a_{k}}{s}+\gamma_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{d}=\frac{a_{d}}{s}+\gamma_{d},

with

\left|\gamma_{k}\right|=\left|\alpha_{k}-\frac{a_{k}}{s}\right|\leq c^{\prime}X^{-k+\omega}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\gamma_{d}\right|=\left|\alpha_{d}-\frac{a_{d}}{s}\right|\leq c^{\prime}X^{-d+\omega}

for some suitable constant
c^{\prime}
.

Two remarks are again in order:

(1)

Looking at

4.6
, it is clear that if we set
s=qr
, we can take
c^{\prime}
such that
\Na(\eta)\subseteq\Pa(\omega)
. Then assuming

1.7

4.7
tells us that there is some

\int_{\Pa(\omega)\setminus\Na(\eta)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\ll\int_{\nn(\eta)}\left|S(\bm{\alpha})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}\ll X^{n-k-d-\nu}.

(2)

We can compute directly:

\text{vol}\Pa(\omega)\ll\sum_{1\leq s\leq c^{\prime}X^{\omega}}\sum_{1\leq a_{k}\leq s}c^{\prime}X^{-k+\omega}\sum_{1\leq a_{d}\leq s}c^{\prime}X^{-d+\omega}\ll X^{-k-d+5\omega}.

Definition 5.2
.

Define

S(s;\a)=S(s;a_{k},a_{d}):=\sum_{\x\,(\mathrm{mod}\,s)}e_{s}\left(a_{k}F(\x)+a_{d}G(\x)\right)

and

J(\bm{\gamma})=J(\gamma_{k},\gamma_{d}):=\int_{[-1,1]^{n}}e\left(\gamma_{k}F(\bm{\xi})+\gamma_{d}G(\bm{\xi})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

To make the notation simpler, set

(5.3)

J_{X}(\bm{\gamma}):=X^{n}J(X^{k}\gamma_{k},X^{d}\gamma_{d})=\int_{[-X,X]^{n}}e\left(\gamma_{k}F(\bm{\xi})+\gamma_{d}G(\bm{\xi})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

Lemma 5.3
.

The integral over the major arcs can be written as

\int_{\Pa(\omega)}S(\bm{\alpha})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}=\sum_{s\leq c^{\prime}X^{\omega}}s^{-n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a_{k},a_{d}\leq s\\
(s,a_{k},a_{d})=1\end{subarray}}S(s;\a)\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|\gamma_{k}\right|\leq c^{\prime}X^{-k+\omega}\\
\left|\gamma_{d}\right|\leq c^{\prime}X^{-d+\omega}\end{subarray}}J_{X}(\bm{\gamma})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}+O\left(X^{n-k-d-1+7\omega}\right).

Proof.

For each
\bm{\alpha}\in\Pa(\omega)
, the separation in
Eq.

5.2
yields the estimate

(5.4)

\left|S(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{d})-s^{-n}S(s;\a)J_{X}(\bm{\gamma})\right|\ll X^{n-1}s\left(1+X^{k}\left|\gamma_{k}\right|+X^{d}\left|\gamma_{d}\right|\right),

and the right hand side is
\ll X^{n-1+2\omega}
for
\bm{\alpha}\in\Pa(\omega)
by construction.
∎

With a large number

A variable change

Assume that

1.7
