Abstract
. For a subfamily ℱ ⊆ 2 [ n ] \mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]} of the Boolean lattice, consider the graph G ℱ G_{\mathcal{F}} on ℱ \mathcal{F} based on the pairwise inclusion relations among its members. Given a positive integer t t , how large can ℱ \mathcal{F} be before G ℱ G_{\mathcal{F}} must contain some component of order greater than t t ? For t = 1 t=1 , this question was answered exactly almost a century ago by Sperner: the size of a middle layer of the Boolean lattice. For t = 2 n t=2^{n} , this question is trivial. We are interested in what happens between these two extremes. For t = 2 g t=2^{g} with g = g ⁡ ( n ) g=g(n) being any integer function that satisfies g ⁡ ( n ) = o ⁡ ( n / log ⁡ n ) g(n)=o(n/\log n) as n → ∞ n\to\infty , we give an asymptotically sharp answer to the above question: not much larger than the size of a middle layer. This constitutes a nontrivial generalisation of Sperner’s theorem. We do so by a reduction to a Turán-type problem for rainbow cycles in properly edge-coloured graphs. Among other results, we also give a sharp answer to the question, how large can ℱ \mathcal{F} be before G ℱ G_{\mathcal{F}} must be connected?



1.
Introduction

Sperner’s theorem
[
23
]
is a revered result in combinatorics that
gives a precise answer to the following problem.
Find the largest number
\La(n)
such that there exists some family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
of subsets of the
n
-element ground set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
no two of which are comparable, i.e. there are no
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
for which either
A\subseteq B
or
B\subseteq A
, satisfying that
|\mathcal{F}|=\La(n)
.
Equivalently, find the smallest number
\La(n)
such that in any family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
of subsets of
[n]
with at least
\La(n)+1
members there must be a pair
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
of comparable members, so
A\subseteq B
or
B\subseteq A
, otherwise known as a
2
-chain
.
In this context,
2^{[n]}
is often referred to as the
Boolean lattice
.

For our purposes, we prefer to cast
\La(n)
as a
threshold
. There is some threshold value
\La(n)
such that in any family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
of subsets of
[n]
with at least
\La(n)+1
members there must be a
2
-chain, while, on the other hand, there exists some family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
of subsets of
[n]
with only
\La(n)
members that contains no
2
-chain.
Sperner’s theorem
[
23
]
asserts that this threshold satisfies
\La(n)=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=\binom{n}{\lceil n/2\rceil}
. The extremal family is
\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
, all subsets of
[n]
of size
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, or, if
n
is odd, the symmetric one
\binom{[n]}{\lceil n/2\rceil}
, the so-called
middle layer(s)
.

It has long been known that something remarkable happens at this threshold called
supersaturation
. Confirming a conjecture of Erdős and Katona, Kleitman
[
18
]
showed that in any family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
of subsets of
[n]
with at least
\La(n)+q
members there must be at least
q(\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1)

2
-chains. The extremal family here takes subsets of
[n]
of sizes as close as possible to
n/2
.
(See
[
11
,
8
,
5
,
22
]
for the recent generalisation of this phenomenon to
\ell
-chains,
\ell\geq 3
.)

In other words, as we cross the threshold, not only must there be one
2
-chain, but also there must be
many
.
A natural question to ask is, what does the space of
2
-chains look like? Since by supersaturation the volume of this space must be large, must it exhibit some clustering? When we demand the family to be of size at least
2^{n}
, the space of
2
-chains is trivially connected, so more fully we ask, what happens between the threshold
\La(n)\sim 2^{n+1/2}/\sqrt{\pi n}
and the extreme
2^{n}
?
Does a large cluster in the space appear suddenly? Or more gradually?

These musings preoccupy us here.
Since these questions are subject to interpretation, we need to ground our definitions.
Given a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, we define an auxiliary graph
G_{\mathcal{F}}
with vertex set
\mathcal{F}
and two vertices
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
are adjacent if
A,B
comprise a
2
-chain. Of course, if
\mathcal{F}=2^{[n]}
, then
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is a complete graph on
2^{n}
vertices.
We focus on the following novel prospect.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
k,n
be integers with
0\leq k\leq n
such that
k=0
,
k=1
or
k
has the same parity as
n
. If for some
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
have order at most
2^{k}
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq 2^{k}\binom{n-k}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}.

Note that when
k=0
, then the statement is Sperner’s theorem.
The
k=1
case is corollary of a result of Katona and Tarján
[
15
]
.
The
k=n
case corresponds to the complete case
\mathcal{F}=2^{[n]}
, so trivially holds.
A fuller confirmation of the cases
1<k<n
—but especially those
k=k(n)
for which
k=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
— would constitute a natural generalisation/extension of Sperner’s theorem.

(By Stirling’s approximation, note that when
n-k\to\infty
, then for all even
n-k
we have

2^{k}\binom{n-k}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}\sim 2^{k}\frac{2^{n-k}}{\sqrt{(n-k)\pi/2}}=\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{(n-k)\pi/2}}

and that this is asymptotically equal to
\La(n)
when
k=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
.)

This conjecture seems difficult, but, if it were true, it would be best possible, essentially by a disjoint union of complete subfamilies.

Proposition 1.2
.

For all integers
k,n
with
0\leq k\leq n
, there exists a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
of size
2^{k}\binom{n-k}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}
such that the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
have order
2^{k}
.

Proof.

Consider the family described as follows. Let
\mathcal{F}_{s}=\binom{[n-k]}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}
, i.e. the set of all
\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor
-element subsets of
[n-k]
. Then let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of all sets
F
such that
F_{s}\subseteq F\subseteq F_{s}\cup([n]\backslash[n-k])
for some
F_{s}\in\mathcal{F}_{s}
.
Then
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is made up of exactly
\binom{n-k}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}
components, each of which is identified by a unique collection of
\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor
elements from the set
[n-k]
. Also note that each component has order
2^{k}
, since each element of a component is determined by a unique subset of
[n]\backslash[n-k]
, of which there are
2^{k}
. It only remains to show that any two components are disconnected. For this, assume we have
X=F_{X}\cup X^{\prime}
and
Y=F_{Y}\cup Y^{\prime}
, with
F_{X},F_{Y}\in\mathcal{F}_{s}
, and suppose
X\subseteq Y
. Then

F_{X}\subseteq X\subseteq Y\subseteq F_{Y}\cup([n]\backslash[n-k]).

But this is a contradiction if
F_{X}\neq F_{Y}
, since then
F_{X}
contains some element not contained in
F_{Y}
, and since
F_{X}\subseteq[n-k]
, this element cannot be contained in
F_{Y}\cup([n]\backslash[n-k])
either.
∎

Note these constructions are quite distinct from the extremal families for supersaturation.

For the discussion that follows, let us denote by
\La(n,t)
the size of the largest family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
such that all components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
have order at most
t
. So
\La(n,1)=\La(n)
, and
Conjecture

1.1
and
Proposition

1.2
posit that
\La(n,2^{k})=2^{k}\binom{n-k}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}
if
k=0
,
k=1
, or
0\leq k\leq n
has the same parity as
n
.
By definition, if a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
has at least
\La(n,t)+1
elements, then
G_{\mathcal{F}}
must have a component of order at least
t+1
.
Therefore, understanding the behaviour of
\La(n,t)
does indeed give us some idea, at least in one interpretation, of how the space of
2
-chains looks as we tune the prescribed number of elements between
\La(n)+1
and
2^{n}
, just as we asked above.

In our main result, we establish for a large range of choices of
t
the asymptotic behaviour of
\La(n,t)
, which aligns with
Conjecture

1.1
and
Proposition

1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
t=t(n)=O(2^{g(n)})
for some
g(n)
that satisfies
g(n)=o(n/\log n)
as
n\to\infty
. Then
\La(n,t)=(1+o(1))\La(n)
.

What
Theorem

1.3
says is that even if we allow for the components in
G_{\mathcal{F}}
to be quite large as a function of
n
, the family
\mathcal{F}
may not grow much larger than a middle layer family.
So our theorem is a qualitative strengthening of Sperner’s theorem.

The main idea in the proof is to relate the size of the family to the average number of times a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
meets elements of the family. In our proof, we draw an unexpected connection to a Turán-type problem, namely, an upper bound on the edge-density of a graph on two consecutive layers of the Boolean lattice. To obtain this upper bound, we invoke a recent result of Alon, Bucić, Sauermann, Zakharov, Zamir
[
2
]
concerning rainbow cycles in proper edge-colourings.

We also pursue further partial, but exact results in support of
Conjecture

1.1
. In particular, we are interested in problems near the boundary cases
k=0
and
k=n
.
We have verified that the conjecture holds if
k=1
or
k=2
(and
n
is even).
In doing so, we have developed a slight generalisation of the BLYM inequality (
Theorem

3.1
below) which implies
Conjecture

1.1
conditional upon the hypothetical shape of the components in
G_{\mathcal{F}}
of an optimal family
\mathcal{F}
.
(We see this same shape in
Proposition

1.2
.)
This generalisation of the BLYM inequality may be of independent interest.
Since the
k=n
case corresponds to
G_{\mathcal{F}}
being trivially connected, we found it interesting to pursue the largest size of
\mathcal{F}
before
G_{\mathcal{F}}
must be connected.

Theorem 1.4
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is such that
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is disconnected, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\begin{cases}2^{n}-2^{n/2+1}+2&\text{ if $n$ is even, and }\\
2^{n}-3\cdot 2^{(n-1)/2}+2&\text{ if $n$ is odd}\end{cases}

Moreover, these bounds on
|\mathcal{F}|
are sharp.

1.1.
Notation and preliminaries

The
Boolean lattice
, often denoted merely by
2^{[n]}
, is the partially ordered set
(2^{[n]},\subseteq)
made up of all subsets of
[n]=\{1\dots,n\}
, together with the inclusion relation. We are considering subsets
\mathcal{F}
of the Boolean lattice. When talking about the Boolean lattice, we often use the term
layer
, which is all subsets of
[n]
of a given size, say,
k
, denoted by
\binom{[n]}{k}
. We imagine the layers to be arranged bottom to top, with the empty set
\emptyset
at the bottom and the full set
[n]
at the top.
We refer to the
height
of a given family
\mathcal{F}
, which is the span of the highest and lowest layers that contain sets in
\mathcal{F}
.

Two distinct sets
X,Y\in\mathcal{F}
are
comparable
if either
X\subseteq Y
or
Y\subseteq X
, otherwise they are
incomparable
. We call a tuple
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
of distinct sets such that
X_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq X_{k}
a
k
-chain
, and if
k=n
we call it a
maximal chain
. We denote by
G_{\mathcal{F}}
the undirected graph which has as vertices the elements of
\mathcal{F}
and an edge
XY
for every comparable pair
X\subseteq Y
. We also work with a subgraph
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{F}}
of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
where we only take those edges
XY
with
|Y|=|X|+1
. This paper concerns the connectivity and component structure of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
. We are sometimes ambiguous by the use of phrases like, “the components of
\mathcal{F}
” or “
\mathcal{F}
does not contain some substructure”, which should always be read as statements about
G_{\mathcal{F}}
.

We denote by
S_{n}
the set of permutations of
[n]
. As it turns out, studying how the permutations in
S_{n}
interact with the sets in the family
\mathcal{F}
is useful for results about
G_{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that we can interchangeably use either permutations or maximal chains, since a permutation
\sigma
corresponds to the maximal chain
(\emptyset,\{\sigma(1)\},\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2)\},\dots,[n])
.
We will say that a permutation
\sigma

meets
a family
\mathcal{F}
if the intersection of
\mathcal{F}
and the maximal chain corresponding to
\sigma
is nonempty.

Throughout,
\log n
denotes the base-2 logarithm, while
\ln n
denotes the natural logarithm.

In
Sections

2.2
and
2.3
, we discuss and make use of a classic result of Kruskal
[
19
]
and Katona
[
14
]
.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
19
,
14
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
for some
k
. Suppose we write

|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{n_{k}}{k}+\binom{n_{k-1}}{k-1}+\dots+\binom{n_{j}}{j}

for some
n_{k}\geq n_{k-1}\geq\dots\geq n_{j}\geq j
. There is a unique way to do this. Then call
{\mathcal{S}}_{r}
the family of sets of size
k-r
that are subsets of some element of
\mathcal{F}
. Then

|{\mathcal{S}}_{r}|\geq\binom{n_{k}}{k-r}+\binom{n_{k-1}}{k-1-r}+\dots+\binom{n_{j}}{j-r}.

We also refer occasionally to another classic result, which generalises Sperner’s theorem, called the BLYM inequality and due, independently, to Yamamoto
[
25
]
, Mešalkin
[
21
]
, Bollobás
[
6
]
, Lubell
[
20
]
.

Theorem 1.6

(
[
20
,
6
,
25
,
21
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is a family with no
2
-chain, then, writing
a_{k}=|\mathcal{F}\cap\binom{[n]}{k}|
,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\leq 1.

\Lambda^{\star}(n,t)
, which is the average number of times a permutation

. Our goal is then to upper bound
\Lambda^{\star}(n,t)
.

Section

Theorem

1.3
, first in the case where
t

, showing how

\xi^{\star}_{n}

2.1.
The Lubell function

In the proof of
Theorem

1.3
, we relate the problem to another quantity.
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
. For
\sigma\in S_{n}
, call
T(\sigma)
the number of times that
\sigma
meets
\mathcal{F}
, that is
T(\sigma)=|\{F\in\mathcal{F}:\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\dots,\sigma(|F|)\}=F\}|
. Then the
Lubell function
of
\mathcal{F}
is

\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})=\frac{\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}T(\sigma)}{n!}.

That is,
\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})
measures the average number of times a permutation meets
\mathcal{F}
, over all permutations
\sigma\in S_{n}
.
We also define
\Lambda^{\star}(n,t)
to be the maximum of
\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})
over all families
\mathcal{F}
such that
G_{\mathcal{F}}
has components of order at most
t
.
The following further justifies the utility of the Lubell function.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\mathcal{F}\in 2^{[n]}
,

\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{|F|}}.

Proof.

For a set
F\in\mathcal{F}
, call
T(F)
the number of permutations
\sigma\in S_{n}
that have
F
as an initial segment. The key observation is that

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}T(\sigma)=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}T(F).

This holds because both sums are counting exactly the number of pairs
(\sigma,F)
for which
F
is an initial segment of
\sigma
. But, for a given
F
, we can compute
T(F)
exactly, namely
T(F)=|F|!(n-|F|)!
. Thus

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}T(\sigma)=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}|F|!(n-|F|)!.

Dividing both sides by
n!
, we get

\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})=\frac{\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}T(\sigma)}{n!}=\frac{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}|F|!(n-|F|)!}{n!}=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{|F|}}.\qed

As we will see in
Section

3
, this proof is very closely related to Lubell’s proof of the BLYM inequality, which relies on the observation that if
\mathcal{F}
contains no comparable pairs, then
\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})\leq 1
.
Like in Lubell’s proof,
Lemma

2.1
can be used to upper bound the size of
\mathcal{F}
as follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
n\geq 1
and all
1\leq t\leq 2^{n}
,

\La(n,t)\leq\Lambda^{\star}(n,t)\La(n).

Proof.

Notice that the righthand-side in
Lemma

2.1
is minimised if
|F|=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
, giving

\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})\geq\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}}\quad\text{ or, equivalently, }\quad|\mathcal{F}|\leq\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})\La(n)

for all
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
. In particular, if
G_{\mathcal{F}}
has components of order at most
t
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\Lambda^{\star}(n,t)\La(n).

∎

So for
Theorem

1.3
it is sufficient to show that
\Lambda^{\star}(n,t)\leq 1+o(1)
.

2.2.
Towards
Theorem

1.3

Before proving
Theorem

1.3
, we first handle the simpler case where
t
is fixed, as the proof strategy is the same, but the details are easier to follow. The full proof of
Theorem

1.3
requires a more careful analysis and fine control on the edge density of a subfamily on two consecutive layers.

For a given family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, it will be helpful to have a certain basic property.
Let us call a set
Y\in 2^{[n]}
a
skip
of
\mathcal{F}
if
Y\notin\mathcal{F}
and there exist
X,Z\in\mathcal{F}
such that
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
skipless
if it admits no skip.
In the context of computing
\La(n,t)
, we may assume skiplessness without loss of generality,
by the following claim.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
t>0
, there exists a skipless family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
of size
\La(n,t)
such that the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
all have order at most
t
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of size
\La(n,t)
such that the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
all have order at most
t
, and moreover assume that
\mathcal{F}
is such that it has the fewest skips. For a contradiction, let us assume that
\mathcal{F}
is not skipless. That is, there is some set
Y\in 2^{[n]}
such that
Y\notin\mathcal{F}
and there exist
X,Z\in\mathcal{F}
such that
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
.

Let us first consider the family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
formed by adding
Y
to
\mathcal{F}
.
In
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
clearly
Y
is not a skip.
Moreover,
Y
does not certify a skip in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
that was not a skip in
\mathcal{F}
, since
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
.
Thus
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has fewer skips than
\mathcal{F}
has.

Let
C
be the component of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
that contains
X
and
Z
. Let
C^{\prime}
be the component of
G_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}
that contains
X
,
Z
and, necessarily,
Y
. Since
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
, we know that
C^{\prime}
is equal to
C
with
Y
added.
Let
X_{\max}
be the maximal element of
C
, which is also the maximal element of
C^{\prime}
.

Next consider the family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\setminus\{X_{\max}\}
of cardinality
|\mathcal{F}\cup\{Y\}\setminus\{X_{\max}\}|=|\mathcal{F}|=\La(n,t)
. The component of
G_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}}
containing
Y
must be
C\cup\{Y\}\setminus\{X_{\max}\}
and has cardinality
|C|
. We thus conclude that the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}}
all have order at most
t
.
The removal of
X_{\max}
from
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
cannot produce a skip, and so
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
has fewer skips than
\mathcal{F}
, a contradiction to the choice of
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Note that this only says there is at least one skipless family that is optimal, but does not imply that all optimal families are skipless (although that might be true).

In our argument, we find it convenient to isolate our consideration of sets in the family that are not too large or too small, so we introduce a third parameter in the definitions of
\La(n,t)
and
\Lambda^{\star}(n,t)
to indicate this constraint. Let
\La(n,t,k)
denote the size of a largest family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
such that all the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
have order at most
t
and such that
k\leq|F|\leq n-k
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Similarly
\Lambda^{\star}(n,t,k)
is the maximum of
\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})
over all such families
\mathcal{F}
. The analogue of
Lemma

2.2
holds:

(1)

\La(n,t,k)\leq\Lambda^{\star}(n,t,k)\La(n).

Moreover, the analogue of
Lemma

2.3
for
\La(n,t,k)
holds by the same proof: there is a skipless family
\mathcal{F}
of size
\La(n,t,k)
, no member of which has more than
k
or fewer than
n-k
elements, such that the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
all have order at most
t
.

Before proceeding, we require some more notation.
For
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, we denote by
S(\mathcal{F})
the set of permutations that meet
\mathcal{F}
at least once and let
s(\mathcal{F})=|S(\mathcal{F})|
. Furthermore, for
i=0,\dots,n
, denote by
S_{i}(\mathcal{F})
the set of permutations that meet
\mathcal{F}
exactly
i
times and let
s_{i}(\mathcal{F})=|S_{i}(\mathcal{F})|
. So
s(\mathcal{F})=s_{1}(\mathcal{F})+\dots+s_{n}(\mathcal{F})
.
Finally, denote by
\overline{\Lambda}(n,\mathcal{F})
the average number of times a permutation
\sigma\in S(\mathcal{F})
meets
\mathcal{F}
, that is,

\overline{\Lambda}(n,\mathcal{F})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}is_{i}(\mathcal{F})}{s(\mathcal{F})}.

Note that
\overline{\Lambda}(n,\mathcal{F})\geq\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})
.

The reason for this finer notation is that it will be helpful to partition
\mathcal{F}
and consider the average over the permutations meeting the specific parts separately, by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Given a partition
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{F}_{k}
of
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
,

\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})\leq\overline{\Lambda}(n,\mathcal{F})\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\overline{\Lambda}(n,\mathcal{F}_{i}).

Proof.

This is a general fact: if we partition a set of positive numbers, then the average of all the numbers cannot be larger than the maximum of the averages of the parts.
∎

Generally speaking, the more parts, the easier the bound on
\overline{\Lambda}(n,\mathcal{F}_{i})
is to compute, but the weaker the resultant bound in
Lemma

2.4
. For the next proof, it suffices to partition
\mathcal{F}
into singletons
\{\{F\}\}_{F\in\mathcal{F}}
, but later for
Theorem

1.3
in full we will need to consider larger parts.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
if

is fixed.

We first show that
\La(n,t,\log n)\leq(1+o(1))\La(n)
, and then at the end derive the result by simply adding in all the sets of size less than
\log n
or more than
n-\log n
. The choice of
\log n
here is not canonical, as any slowly increasing function in

will do.

By
Lemma

2.2
, or rather
Equation

1
, it is sufficient to show that
\Lambda^{\star}(n,t,\log n)\leq 1+o(1)
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of size
\La(n,t,\log n)
, no member of which has more than
n-\log n
or fewer than
\log n
elements, such that the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
all have order at most
t
.
We want then to show that
\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})\leq 1+o(1)
(where the
o(1)
term depends only on
n
and not on the family
\mathcal{F}
).
By
Lemma

2.3
, or rather its
\La(n,t,k)
analogue, we may assume that
\mathcal{F}
is skipless.

We actually show something slightly stronger than we need, that for each set
F\in\mathcal{F}
we have
\overline{\Lambda}(n,\{F\})\leq 1+o(1)
as
n\to\infty
. By
Lemma

2.4
, we will then have
\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})\leq\max_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\overline{\Lambda}(n,\{F\})\leq 1+o(1)
.
To do this, our goal is to show that for any given set
F
in component
C
, most permutations meeting
F
only meet
C
once. So consider the set
S(\{F\})
of permutations meeting
F
. We partition
S(\{F\})
into parts according to the valuation of the pair
(\sigma(|F|),\sigma(|F|+1))
. There are
|F|(n-|F|)
such parts, since
F
has
|F|
elements that can determine the first coordinate and
n-|F|
non-elements that can determine the second coordinate. These parts are all disjoint and of equal size, namely, all having
(|F|-1)!(n-|F|-1)!
permutations.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is skipless, any permutation
\sigma\in S(\{F\})
meets
C
(and thus
F
) only once if and only if both sets
\sigma([|F|-1])
and
\sigma([|F|+1])
are not in
C
.

Writing
M_{1}
for the number of subsets of
F
in
C
, at most
(n-|F|)M_{1}
of the parts of the partition of
S(\{F\})
contain permutations
\sigma
with
\sigma([|F|-1])\in C
. Similarly, writing
M_{2}
for the number of supersets of
F
in
C
, at most
|F|M_{2}
of the parts contain permutations
\sigma
with
\sigma([|F|+1])\in C
. But since
|C|\leq t
, we have
M_{1}+M_{2}\leq t
. So at most
M_{1}(n-|F|)+M_{2}|F|\leq t\max(|F|,n-|F|)
of the parts contain permutations that meet
C
more than once, that is,

(2)

\sum_{i=2}^{t}s_{i}(\{F\})\leq t\max(|F|,n-|F|)(|F|-1)!(n-|F|-1)!.

We also know that
s(\{F\})=|F|!(n-|F|)!
. Hence

\displaystyle\overline{\Lambda}(n,\{F\})

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{1}(\{F\})+\sum_{i=2}^{t}is_{i}(\{F\})}{|F|!(n-|F|)!}\leq 1+\frac{t^{2}\max(|F|,n-|F|)(|F|-1)!(n-|F|-1)!}{|F|!(n-|F|)!}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{t^{2}\max(|F|,n-|F|)}{|F|(n-|F|)}=1+\frac{t^{2}}{\min(|F|,n-|F|)}\leq 1+\frac{t^{2}}{\log n}\leq 1+o(1).

In the first inequality, we used
Equation

2
and the fact that
s_{1}(\{F\})\leq s(\{F\})
. In the penultimate inequality, we used the fact that
\log n\leq|F|\leq n-\log n
, and at the end we used that
t
is fixed.

By
Lemma

2.4
and
Equation

1
, this suffices to show

\La(n,t,\log n)\leq(1+o(1))\La(n)

as
n\to\infty
.
It only remains to account for the constraint on the size of
F
. But for this, we can just observe that the number of sets we excluded is negligible, specifically, that

\displaystyle\La(n,t)

\displaystyle\leq\La(n,t,\log n)+|\{X\subseteq 2^{[n]}:|X|<\log n\text{ or }|X|>n-\log n\}|

\displaystyle\leq(1+o(1))\La(n)+2\log n\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor\log n\rfloor}\leq(1+o(1))\La(n)

as

, where the last inequality follows for example by Stirling’s approximation.
∎

That there is room for improvement in the estimates we made is evident. For example, we bounded the entire average by separately bounding the average for each singleton set in
\mathcal{F}
, which clearly is not optimal.
Instead of singletons, we can take the parts as components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
. If we believe
Conjecture

1.1
and the correctness of our proposed extremal construction, it is intuitive that all components are (near) equal in size in the optimal case, and so we should not lose much by doing this. In other words, we believe there is an optimal family
\mathcal{F}
such that
\Lambda(n,\mathcal{F})
is equal to
\overline{\Lambda}(n,C)
for all components
C
.

Another important step is where we upper bounded, for a given set
F\in C
, the number of subsets and supersets of
F
that are also in
C
in the layers immediately above and below that of
F
. Thanks to the assumption that
\mathcal{F}
is skipless, this shows that only a small fraction of the permutations meeting
F
also meet other sets in
\mathcal{F}
. We have crudely bounded this number of subsets and supersets by
t
. When
t
is a fixed constant this is good enough, but if
t(n)
grows quickly it is far too loose.
Indeed, if this bound were near to optimal on average, it would mean that the component
C
has approximately
t^{2}/2
edges, which we will see is impossible.

Being more careful, we are in fact interested in a bound on the average degree of a vertex of
G^{\prime}_{\mathcal{F}}
. It turns out that a good general bound on the edge density between two consecutive layers on the Boolean lattice will suffice for our purposes.
Let us define some notation for this task.
For
0\leq k<n
, let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k+1}
. Then we denote by
\xi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
the number of comparable pairs in
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
.
We also denote by
\xi^{\star}_{n}(m)
the function that, given

, returns the maximum of
2\xi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})/m
over all pairs
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k+1}
and
|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|=m
over all
0\leq k<n
.
That is,
\xi^{\star}_{n}(m)
is the largest average degree of a subgraph of order

on any two consecutive layers of
G_{2^{[n]}}
.
Using a refinement of the argument above, we next show how control on

is enough to deduce

.

Let

As

We shall always assume that

By

Let

be a family of size

, no member of which has more than

).
By

, or rather its

We actually show

For a given component

2.3.
Bounding
\xi^{\star}_{n}

Upper bounds on
\xi^{\star}_{n}
can be seen in interesting counterpoint to the result of Kleitman
[
18
]
that minimises the number of
2
-chains.
Indeed, bounding
\xi^{\star}_{n}
from above is a question of maximising
2
-chains, and similar questions have a long tradition; see for example
[
10
,
4
,
3
]
.
Curiously, the following question was only posed relatively recently.

Question 2.6

(Question 5.1
[
3
]
)
.

Given
0\leq k_{1}\leq k_{2}\leq n
,
0\leq a\leq\binom{n}{k_{1}}
and
0\leq b\leq\binom{n}{k_{2}}
, which families
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k_{1}}
of size
a
and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k_{2}}
of size
b
maximise
\xi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
?

Maximising
\xi^{\star}_{n}(m)
corresponds to optimising this question over all
k_{1},k_{2},a,b
satisfying
k_{2}=k_{1}+1
and
a+b=m
.
As the authors of
[
3
]
point out, there is an interesting connection between
Question

2.6
with
k_{2}=k_{1}+1
and the Kruskal–Katona theorem (
Theorem

1.5
) in the case
r=1
. Indeed we can infer that the minimum possible size of
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
is such that
|\mathcal{F}|k\leq\frac{1}{2}(|\mathcal{F}|+|{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|)\xi^{\star}_{n}(|\mathcal{F}|+|{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|)
, since
|\mathcal{F}|k
is the number of edges between
\mathcal{F}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
, which by definition of
\xi^{\star}_{n}
has to be less than
\frac{1}{2}(|\mathcal{F}|+|{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|)\xi^{\star}_{n}(|\mathcal{F}|+|{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|)
. So an upper bound on
\xi^{\star}_{n}
translates into a lower bound on
|{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|
, potentially giving a weaker version of Kruskal–Katona.

As we showed in
Section

2.2
,

Lemma

2.5
allows us to use results about
\xi^{\star}_{n}
to obtain results about
\La(n,t)
. We now relate
\xi^{\star}_{n}
to a well-studied Turán-type problem.
Recall that a proper edge-colouring of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a mapping
c:E\to\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that any two incident edges
e_{1},e_{2}
must have
c(e_{1})\neq c(e_{2})
.
A cycle in
G
of length
\ell\geq 3
is called
rainbow
(with respect to
c
) if no two edges of the cycle receive the same colour.
For
\ell\geq 3
, we denote by
\mad^{\star}(t,\mathcal{O}_{\ell})
, the largest average degree in a graph
G
of order
t
such that there exists a proper edge-colouring of
G
with no copies of a rainbow cycle of length
\ell
.
We denote by
\mad^{\star}(t,\mathcal{O}^{\star})
the largest average degree in a graph
G
of order
t
such that there exists a proper edge-colouring of
G
with no rainbow cycles.

Lemma 2.7
.

For all
n,t\geq 1
we have

\xi^{\star}_{n}(t)\leq\mad^{\star}(t,\mathcal{O}^{\star}).

Proof.

Assume for a contradiction that the inequality does not hold. This means there are two sets
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k+1}
for some
0\leq k<n
with
|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|=t
and
2\xi(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})/t>\mad^{\star}(t,\mathcal{O}^{\star})
. By definition of
\mad^{\star}(t,\mathcal{O}^{\star})
, this means that every proper edge-colouring of the graph
G_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}
contains a rainbow cycle. However, let us consider the following edge-colouring: given any edge
e
of
G_{\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}}
, and supposing it corresponds to the pair
(A,A\cup\{i\})
for some
i\in[n]
, then colour
e
with colour
i
.

The edge-colouring is clearly proper, since if
X
had two incident edges with colour
i
, these would both have as other endpoint
X\cup\{i\}
(or
X\backslash\{i\}
depending on whether
X\in\mathcal{A}
or
X\in\mathcal{B}
). Also, the colouring contains no rainbow cycles: consider any cycle and any starting point
A\in\mathcal{A}
for a traversal of the cycle (in either direction). The first edge will have some colour
i\in[n]
, corresponding to its other endpoint
A\cup\{i\}
. Now as we continue the transversal, the element
i
will eventually have to be removed at some point, at which time the corresponding edge will have colour
i
(and it is necessarily distinct from the edge corresponding to
(A,A\cup\{i\})
). So the cycle has colour
i
at least twice, and so is not a rainbow cycle. We have arrived at a contradiction, as desired.
∎

Notice that the above argument in fact gives some handle on

2.6
in the specific case

, but it moreover breaks down for this purpose when

.

2.8
,
\xi^{\star}_{n}(t)=O(\log t\log\log t)
. Since
t=O(2^{g(n)})
with
g(n)=o(n/\log n)
as
n\to\infty
, which is the premise of
Theorem

1.3
, it follows that
\log t\log\log t=O(g(n)\log g(n))=o(n)
. And so we conclude from
Lemma

2.5
that

\La(n,t)\leq(1+o(1))\La(n).

∎

3.
Exact approaches

We now switch to a discussion about exact results relating to
Conjecture

1.1
. These include in
Section

3.1
a generalisation of the BLYM inequality and a proof that
Conjecture

1.1
holds if the components have a specific shape, and in
Section

3.2
the cases
k=1
and
k=2
of
Conjecture

1.1
.

3.1.
Generalising the BLYM inequality

In order to tackle some special cases of
Conjecture

1.1
, we employ a modified version of the BLYM inequality (
Theorem

1.6
) which allows for the presence of
2
-chains. The problem then is that it becomes more difficult to precisely count each permutation when we add up over the sets of
\mathcal{F}
. In
Section

2
, we showed that on average we count each permutation
1+o(1)
times, but the error term, while negligible, makes this inexact. On the other hand, if we assume a particular form for each the components, then we can count the permutations precisely.

Let us call a family
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
a
diamond
if it consists of all sets
X
such that
A\subseteq X\subseteq B
for some sets
A\subseteq B\subseteq[n]
, which we refer to as the bottom and top corners, respectively, of the diamond.

The following result may be of independent interest.

Theorem 3.1

(Diamond BLYM)
.

For
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, suppose that the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
are induced by diamond subfamilies
\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
.
For
i,j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, let
a_{i,j}
denote the number of components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
whose bottom corner is on layer
i
and whose top corner is on layer
i+j
. Then

(5)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,j}}{\binom{n-j}{i}}\leq 1.

Proof.

We count the permutations of
[n]
in two different ways. On the one hand there are
n!
permutations. On the other hand, we can count the number
s(\mathcal{F})
of permutations
\sigma
that meet
\mathcal{F}
. Notice that the permutations counted for each component are distinct, since if
X\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
Y\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
are both initial segments of the same permutation, then
X\subseteq Y
or
Y\subseteq X
, thus
i=j
. So

\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}s(\mathcal{F}_{k})\leq n!.

Now say
\mathcal{F}_{k}
has bottom corner
A_{k}
and top corner
B_{k}
. Then notice that a permutation
\sigma
contains as initial segment some set
X\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
if and only if all the elements of
A_{k}
appear before all the elements of
[n]\backslash B_{k}
in
\sigma
. Indeed, if this is the case, then all the initial segments of
\sigma
whose last element comes after or at the last element of
A_{k}
and before the first element of
[n]\backslash B_{k}
are sets in
\mathcal{F}_{k}
since they are supersets of
A_{k}
and subsets of
B_{k}
. On the other hand, if not all elements of
A_{k}
come before all elements of
[n]\backslash B_{k}
, then no initial segment of
\sigma
can be a set in
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, since the last element of the segment would have to be after or at the last element of
A_{k}
, but then it would also contain some element of
[n]\backslash B_{k}
, which would imply that it cannot be a subset of
B_{k}
.

With this, we can simply count
s(\mathcal{F}_{k})
as

\frac{n!}{(n-(|B_{k}|-|A_{k}|))!}|A_{k}|!(n-|B_{k}|)!

(i.e. the elements of
B_{k}\backslash A_{k}
can be at any position, but then the positions for the elements of
A_{k}
are fixed, apart from their order, and the same holds for
[n]\backslash B_{k}
). Hence we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}s(\mathcal{F}_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{n!|A_{k}|!(n-|B_{k}|)!}{(n-(|B_{k}|-|A_{k}|))!}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}\frac{n!i!(n-i-j)!}{(n-j)!}\leq n!.

By dividing both sides of this inequality by
n!
, we get

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}\frac{i!(n-i-j)!}{(n-j)!}\leq 1,

as required.
∎

From this, it follows that
Conjecture

1.1
holds under the additional assumption that each component is a diamond.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
k,n
be integers with
0\leq k\leq n
. If for some
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
the components of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
are diamonds of height at most
k
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq 2^{k}\binom{n-k}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}.

Proof.

We can rewrite
Equation

5
as follows:

\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{2^{j}a_{i,j}}{2^{j}\binom{n-j}{i}}\leq 1.

Note that we have

|\mathcal{F}|=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{n}2^{j}a_{i,j}.

Thus, to make the family as large as possible, we want all the elements of
\mathcal{F}
in terms with the largest denominator. The expression in the denominator is maximised for
j=k
and
i=\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor
, giving

\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{2^{k}\binom{n-k}{\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor}}\leq 1.\qed

3.2.
The cases
k=1
and
k=2

It is intuitive that the diamond shape is optimal for maximising the size of
\mathcal{F}
subject to a bound on component sizes in
G_{\mathcal{F}}
, at least when
k
and
n
have the same parity (so that the diamond shape is exactly centred). This is because the diamond shape is very compact: it minimises the number of sets in the middle layer that are unavailable for other components.
In general, it is tricky to translate this intuition into a proof that the optimal families have to be diamond-shaped (as then we would immediately have
Conjecture

1.1
in full via
Theorem

3.2
).

This is at least the case if
k=1
, as the only possible component of order
2
is the diamond shape. The case
k=1
of
Conjecture

1.1
is not a new result, since having components of order at most
2
is equivalent to
G_{\mathcal{F}}
not containing any copies of the
\vee
-shaped graph. This was resolved by Katona and Tarján in 1983
[
15
]
. We note how this result also follows from the generalised BLYM inequality.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
15
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is such that
G_{\mathcal{F}}
contains no copy of the
\vee
-shaped graph, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq 2\binom{n-1}{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}.

Proof.

Since the components of
\mathcal{F}
have order 1 or 2 and by
Lemma

2.3
we may assume that
\mathcal{F}
is skipless, the conditions of
Theorem

3.2
are satisfied for
k=1
.
Theorem

3.2
yields the result.
∎

In the case
k=2
, if
n
is even, we can show that the diamond shape is at least as good as any other shape, when it is in the middle layers. This is enough to prove the case
k=2
of
Conjecture

1.1
. The proof is a bit more involved and requires some case checking.

Theorem 3.4
.

If
n
is even and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is such that
G_{\mathcal{F}}
has components of order at most 4, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq 4\binom{n-2}{n/2-1}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.3
we may assume that
\mathcal{F}
is skipless. Let
\mathcal{F}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
be the components of
\mathcal{F}
. We start again by noting that

(6)

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}s(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq n!.

We now try to minimise
s(\mathcal{F}_{i})
, so that we can have as many components as possible. There are a few cases we have to consider to account for different component orders and different shapes. Note that we only consider shapes that are actually different, so we avoid computing the same bound for symmetric shapes (for example the
\vee
-shape and the
\wedge
-shape). In general, for each shape we will use the inclusion–exclusion principle to calculate
s(\mathcal{F}_{i})
, which says

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{F}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}s_{1}(\{F\})-\sum_{F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}s_{2}(\{F_{1},F_{2}\})+\sum_{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}s_{3}(\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\})-\dots

\displaystyle=\sum_{F}s_{1}(\{F\})-\sum_{F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}}s_{2}(\{F_{1},F_{2}\})+\sum_{F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq F_{3}}s_{3}(\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\})-\dots

Then, once we obtain the expression in terms of
n
and
k
(the layer on which the middle of the component is located), we deduce that the expression is minimised for
k=n/2
. To do this, we use facts about binomials and simple computations of derivatives. We split the different shapes by component order:

(i)

If
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{F\}
, then
s(\mathcal{F}_{i})=|F|!(n-|F|)!
, which is minimised when
|F|=n/2
, hence

s(\mathcal{F}_{i})\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{F_{1},F_{2}\}
, then, since
\mathcal{F}
is skipless, we may assume that
F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}
with
|F_{2}|=|F_{1}|+1
. Then

s(\mathcal{F}_{i})=n|F_{1}|!(n-|F_{2}|)!.

This is the case
k=1
of the computation we did for diamond shapes in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
. It is minimised for
|F_{1}|=n/2
or
|F_{1}|=n/2-1
, giving

s(\mathcal{F}_{i})\geq n\cdot\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!

(iii)

If
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\}
we have two options:

•

F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq F_{3}
with
\big(|F_{1}|,|F_{2}|,|F_{3}|\big)=(k-1,k,k+1)
. Then

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{F}_{i})

\displaystyle=(k-1)!(n-k+1)!+k!(n-k)!+(k+1)!(n-k-1)!-(k-1)!(n-k)!-

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-k!(n-k-1)!-2(k-1)!(n-k-1)!+(k-1)!(n-k-1)!

\displaystyle=(k-1)!(n-k-1)!\Big(n^{2}-nk+k^{2}-1\Big)\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{3n^{2}}{4}-1\right),

since in the second to last line
k=n/2
minimises both the product of the factorials (since
\binom{n-2}{k-1}
is maximum for
k=n/2
) and the expression in the parenthesis (this can be checked by computing the derivative with respect to
k
).

•

F_{1},F_{2}\subseteq F_{3}
with
\big(|F_{1}|,|F_{2}|,|F_{3}|\big)=(k,k,k+1)
. Then

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{F}_{i})

\displaystyle=2k!(n-k)!+(k+1)!(n-k-1)!-2k!(n-k-1)!

\displaystyle=k!(n-k)!+k!(n-k-1)!(n-1)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{3}{4}n^{2}-\frac{3}{2}n\right),

since both summands in the second line are minimised when
k=n/2
.

The latter case is slightly smaller.

(iv)

If
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},F_{4}\}
we have 5 cases:

•

F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq F_{3}\subseteq F_{4}
with
\big(|F_{1}|,|F_{2}|,|F_{3}|,|F_{4}|\big)=(k-1,k,k+1,k+2)
. Then

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{F}_{i})

\displaystyle=(k-1)!(n-k+1)!+k!(n-k)!+(k+1)!(n-k-1)!+(k+2)!(n-k-2)!-

\displaystyle-(k-1)!(n-k)!-k!(n-k-1)!-(k+1)!(n-k-2)!-2(k-1)!(n-k-1)!-

\displaystyle-2k!(n-k-2)!-6(k-1)!(n-k-2)!+(k-1)!(n-k-1)!+k!(n-k-2)!+

\displaystyle+4(k-1)!(n-k-2)!-(k-1)!(n-k-2)!

\displaystyle=(k-1)!(n-k-2)!\left(n^{3}-2n^{2}k-n^{2}+2nk^{2}+nk-n+k^{2}+k-2\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(n^{2}+\frac{3}{2}n+2\right).

This follows by noticing that in the second to last line both the product of factorials and the sum in the parenthesis are minimised for
k=n/2
(or for
k=n/2-1
giving the same result).

•

F_{1},F_{2}\subseteq F_{3}\subseteq F_{4}
, with
\big(|F_{1}|,|F_{2}|,|F_{3}|,|F_{4}|\big)=(k-1,k-1,k,k+1)
. Then

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{F}_{i})

\displaystyle=2(k-1)!(n-k+1)!+k!(n-k)!+(k+1)!(n-k-1)!-2(k-1)!(n-k)!-

\displaystyle-k!(n-k-1)!-4(k-1)!(n-k-1)!+2(k-1)!(n-k-1)!

\displaystyle=(k-1)!(n-k-1)!\left(2n^{2}-3nk+2k^{2}-2\right)

\displaystyle=(k-1)!(n-k)!n+(k-1)!(n-k-1)!(2k^{2}-2nk+n^{2}-2)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!(n^{2}-2),

since both summands in the second to last line are minimised for
k=n/2
.

•

F_{1}\subseteq F_{2},F_{3}\subseteq F_{4}
, with
\big(|F_{1}|,|F_{2}|,|F_{3}|,|F_{4}|\big)=(k-1,k,k,k+1)
. This is the diamond shape, so we know from the proof of
Theorem

3.1
that

s(\mathcal{F}_{i})=n(n-1)(k-1)!(k+1)!\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!(n^{2}-n).

•

F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\subseteq F_{4}
, with
\big(|F_{1}|,|F_{2}|,|F_{3}|,|F_{4}|\big)=(k,k,k,k+1)
. Then

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{F}_{i})

\displaystyle=3k!(n-k)!+(k+1)!(n-k-1)!-3k!(n-k-1)!

\displaystyle=k!(n-k-1)!(3n-2k-2)=2k!(n-k)!+k!(n-k-1)!(n-2)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!(n^{2}-n),

since in the second to last line both summands are minimised for
k=n/2
.

•

F_{1},F_{2}\subseteq F_{3}
and
F_{2}\subseteq F_{4}
, with
\big(|F_{1}|,|F_{2}|,|F_{3}|,|F_{4}|\big)=(k,k,k+1,k+1)
. Then

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{F}_{i})

\displaystyle=2k!(n-k)!+2(k+1)!(n-k-1)!-3k!(n-k-1)!=k!(n-k-1)!(2n-1)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(n^{2}-\frac{n}{2}\right).

The third and fourth case both give the lowest bound. This seems to suggest that the optimal family could also be made up of fork-shaped components, that is,
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\subseteq F_{4}
, and not just diamond shapes. However, it seems likely that space constraints in the middle layer would make it impossible, and that in reality the diamond shape is the only optimal shape. However we cannot infer it from this proof.

For
i=1,2,3,4
, write
c_{i}
for the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
of order
i
. We are maximising

(7)

|\mathcal{F}|=c_{1}+2c_{2}+3c_{3}+4c_{4}.

By our previous analysis, we can now replace in
Equation

6
each
c_{i}
with the lower bound we found for each component order:

\displaystyle c_{1}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!+c_{2}n\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!+

\displaystyle+c_{3}\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{3}{2}n-1\right)+c_{4}\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!(n^{2}-n)\leq n!.

We can in turn rewrite this as

\displaystyle c_{1}\left(\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\right)+2c_{2}\left(\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\right)+

\displaystyle+3c_{3}\left(\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\right)+4c_{4}\left(\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)\leq n!

The coefficient of
4c_{4}
is the smallest, and so to maximise
Equation

7
we should set
c_{1}=c_{2}=c_{3}=0
and
c_{4}
as large as possible, which gives

c_{4}\leq\frac{n!}{\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)!(n^{2}-n)}=\binom{n-2}{n/2-1}.

This then implies

|\mathcal{F}|=4c_{4}\leq 4\binom{n-2}{n/2-1}.\qed

We note that this approach breaks down for
k=2
when
n
is odd, because then a diamond shape centred on one of the middle layers is no longer the component for which
s(C)
is minimised. If we could find a general lower bound on
s(C)
this would at least give a weaker version of
Conjecture

1.1
.

4.
Largest disconnected family

Rather than bounding component order, in this section we consider instead the largest possible family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
such that
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is disconnected.
If you like, this can be seen as an extreme case of
Conjecture

1.1
, where
k
is just shy of
n
.
In essence we are asking what is the largest
k
(now not necessarily an integer) such that the bound on the size of the family is nontrivial, meaning that we can fit more than one component.
As
Theorem

1.4
shows, this
k
gets arbitrarily close to
n
as
n\to\infty
.

This proof of
Theorem

1.4
will be quite different from the ones in the previous sections, as it will be more direct. The main idea is to show that, given a family made up of two components, there is a minimum number of sets that cannot be in the family. To make it easier to read, we split the proof into a series of lemmas, which are structured as follows: first we define some necessary notation.
Fact

4.1
and
Fact

4.2
will give necessary preliminaries for
Lemma

4.3
, which encapsulates the main idea of the proof.
Lemma

4.5
is the engine of the proof, as this is where we do most of the work for computing a lower bound on the number of sets that have to be excluded. This, together with the technical
Lemma

4.7
, will prove
Lemma

4.8
, which combined with
Lemma

4.3
completes the proof.

We will from now on assume that we are working with some fixed disconnected family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
that is optimal, i.e. no sets can be added to
\mathcal{F}
without making it connected.
Given a family
\mathcal{A}
, we denote by
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})
, the set of all supersets of some element of
\mathcal{A}
, that is,

\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})=\{X\in 2^{[n]}:A\subseteq X\text{ for some }A\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Similarly we define
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
to be the set of all subsets of some element of
\mathcal{A}
, that is,

\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})=\{X\in 2^{[n]}:X\subseteq A\text{ for some }A\in\mathcal{A}\}.

For a set
A
, we also denote by
\partial(A)
the set of all subsets of
A
of size
|A|-1
.

Suppose now
\mathcal{F}
is split in two components
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, so that there are no comparable pairs
(A,B)
with
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Then we define the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{+}

\displaystyle=\{F\in 2^{[n]}:F\notin(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))\text{ and }\nexists F^{\prime}\subsetneq F\text{ with }F^{\prime}\notin(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}

\displaystyle=\{F\subseteq[n]:F\notin(\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B}))\text{ and }\nexists F^{\prime}\supsetneq F\text{ with }F^{\prime}\notin(\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B}))\}.

It will be useful to notice the following fact.

Fact 4.1
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is split into
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
as above, then
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}
and
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})=\mathcal{B}
. Also
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})=\emptyset
and
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

The first property is immediate from the definition. For the second property, if, for example, there were some set
X\in\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
, then we would get the chain
A\subseteq X\subseteq B
for some
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

The definition of
\mathcal{F}^{+}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}
may seem quite arbitrary at face value. The idea is that they represent the point where the two components come very close to intersecting. This is illustrated in
Figure

2
. Defining
\mathcal{F}^{+}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}
in this way is useful due to the following fact.

\dots

and

.

Fact 4.2
.

If
F\subseteq X
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
, then
X\notin\mathcal{F}
. If
X\subseteq F
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}
, then
X\notin\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Suppose
F\subseteq X
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
. Then
X\notin\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
and
X\notin\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
. If this was not the case then also
F
would be in either
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
or
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
, which is a contradiction. But if
X
were in
\mathcal{F}
, then it would be in either
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{B}
, and hence it would be in either
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
or
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
. We conclude that
X\notin\mathcal{F}
. The proof of the second statement is analogous.
∎

This means that we can use
\mathcal{F}^{+}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}
to lower bound the number of sets that cannot be in
\mathcal{F}
. More specifically we get the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\mathcal{F}
consists of two components
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq 2^{n}-|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|-|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|.

Proof.

By
Fact

4.2
, we know that none of the sets in
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})
or in
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})
can be in
\mathcal{F}
. The only thing left to observe is that
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})=\emptyset
. This is by optimality of
\mathcal{F}
. Indeed, if there is some
F\in\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})
, then in particular
F\notin(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
and
F\notin(\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B}))
. But then
F
isn’t comparable with any set in
\mathcal{F}
, hence we could add
F
to
\mathcal{F}
, which is a contradiction since we assumed
\mathcal{F}
to be optimal. Thus
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})=\emptyset
, proving the lemma.
∎

We now lower bound
|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|
. First we observe
\mathcal{F}^{+}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}
are nonempty.

Claim 4.4
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is disconnected, then
\mathcal{F}^{+}\neq\emptyset
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We have
\emptyset\notin\mathcal{F}
and
[n]\notin\mathcal{F}
, or else
\mathcal{F}
would be connected. But now notice the following facts:

(i)

[n]\notin(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
, otherwise we would have
[n]\in\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)

\emptyset\in(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
, since both components contain at least one set.

Now if all proper subsets of
[n]
are in either
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
or
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
, then
[n]\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
and we are done. Otherwise, we construct a chain of subsets
[n]\supseteq X_{n-1}\supseteq X_{n-2}\supseteq\dots\supseteq X_{k}
such that each next subset has one fewer element than the previous and for each
k\leq i\leq n
we have
X_{i}\notin(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
. Since
\emptyset\in(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
, the chain must stop at some point, i.e. eventually we find a set
X_{k}
such that
X_{k}\notin(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
but all proper subsets of
X_{k}
are in either
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
or
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
. Then
X_{k}\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
.

We can use the same reasoning for
\mathcal{F}^{-}
starting at the empty set, and we will eventually have to find some set in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
.
∎

Now we can use the following lemma to show both that
\mathcal{F}^{+}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}
have a certain minimum size, and that they contain sets that are very close to each other in size.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
, then
\mathcal{F}^{-}
must contain at least
|F|-1
sets, each of size at least
|F|-2
. Similarly, if
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}
, then
\mathcal{F}^{+}
must contain at least
n-|F|-1
sets, each of size at most
|F|+2
.

In order to prove the lemma, we first prove another claim.

Claim 4.6
.

If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
, then
\partial(F)
contains at least one set in
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
and at least one set in
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Assume
\partial(F)\subseteq\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
. We know that
F\notin\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
and
F\notin\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
. Then we must have
F\in\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, otherwise we could safely add
F
to
\mathcal{A}
, which breaks optimality of
\mathcal{F}
. But this is a contradiction: if
F\in\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, then in particular
F^{\prime}\in\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})
for some
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
with
|F^{\prime}|=|F|-1
. But we assumed that
F^{\prime}\in\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
, so we get a contradiction by
Fact

4.1
. So we cannot have
\partial(F)\subseteq\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
, and similarly we cannot have
\partial(F)\subseteq\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
. But since each set in
\partial(F)
is in either
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
or
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
, we deduce that
\partial(F)
must contain at least one set in
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
and at least one set in
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.5
.

We only show the first statement, as the second is analogous. So let
F_{1}\in\partial F\cap(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
and let
F_{2}\in\partial F\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
. We can do this by
Claim

4.6
. Now consider the set
F_{1}\cap F_{2}
. First, notice that
|F_{1}\cap F_{2}|=|F|-2
. We claim that
F_{1}\cap F_{2}\notin\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})
and
F_{1}\cap F_{2}\notin\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})
. If it were in
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})
, then
F_{2}
would also be in
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})
, which is a contradiction by
Fact

4.1
since
F_{2}\in\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
. Similarly if
F_{1}\cap F_{2}
were in
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, then
F_{1}
would also be in
\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})
, which is a contradiction since
F_{1}\in\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
. So we deduce that
F_{1}\cap F_{2}\notin(\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B}))
, which implies that either it is in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
, or it has a superset in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
.

So we have found a set in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
of size at least
|F|-2
. To show that there must actually be at least
|F|-1
such sets, we observe that we could actually have picked multiple pairs
(F_{1},F_{2})
in the beginning of the proof and by the same reasoning obtain more sets in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
. However, it could be that the same set in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
corresponds to multiple pairs
(F_{1},F_{2})
. It turns out that we can still find at least
|F|-1
pairs that each lead to distinct sets in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
. To do this, we pick a set of
\ell
distinct pairs
\{(F_{1},F_{2})^{(1)},\dots,(F_{1},F_{2})^{(\ell)}\}
, with
F_{1}^{(i)}\in\partial F\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
and
F_{2}^{(i)}\in\partial F\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
for all
i
, such that for any two pairs
(F_{1},F_{2})^{(i)}
and
(F_{1},F_{2})^{(j)}
one of the following holds:

(1)

F_{1}^{(i)},F_{1}^{(j)},F_{2}^{(i)},F_{2}^{(j)}
are all distinct,

(2)

F_{1}^{(i)}=F_{1}^{(j)}
and
F_{1}\in\partial F\cap(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
, or

(3)

F_{2}^{(i)}=F_{2}^{(j)}
and
F_{2}\in\partial F\cap(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A}))
.

If we can find
\ell
pairs satisfying these conditions, then each pair will lead to a distinct set
F^{(i)}\in\mathcal{F}^{-}
. We show this for each of the three cases:

(
1
)

From
F_{1}^{(i)}\cap F_{2}^{(i)}
and
F_{1}^{(j)}\cap F_{2}^{(j)}
we derive sets
F^{(i)},F^{(j)}\in\mathcal{F}^{-}
as described previously. Now assume
F^{(i)}=F^{(j)}
. Since the four sets
F_{1}^{(i)},F_{2}^{(i)},F_{1}^{(j)},F_{2}^{(j)}
are all distinct and they are all subsets of
F
of size
|F|-1
, we deduce that each is missing a distinct element of
F
, thus
F_{1}^{(i)}\cap F_{2}^{(i)}
is missing two elements of
F
, which must be contained in
F_{1}^{(j)}\cap F_{2}^{(j)}
. But then
(F_{1}^{(i)}\cap F_{2}^{(i)})\cup(F_{1}^{(j)}\cap F_{2}^{(j)})=F
. So since
F^{(i)}\supseteq F_{1}^{(i)}\cap F_{2}^{(i)}
and
F^{(j)}\supseteq F_{1}^{(j)}\cap F_{2}^{(j)}
, we must have
F^{(i)}=F^{(j)}\supseteq F
. But this is a contradiction, as then (by definition of
\mathcal{F}^{-}
) we would have
F\notin(\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B}))
, but we also know that
F\notin(\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cup\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B}))
(since
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
). This contradicts the optimality of
\mathcal{F}
, as we could safely add
F
to
\mathcal{F}
. It follows that
F^{(i)}\neq F^{(j)}
.

(
2
)

Like before, assume
F^{(i)}=F^{(j)}
. This time we have
(F_{1}^{(i)}\cap F_{2}^{(i)})\cup(F_{1}^{(j)}\cap F_{2}^{(j)})=F_{1}^{(i)}=F_{1}^{(j)}
. Therefore
F^{(i)}=F^{(j)}\supseteq F_{1}^{(i)}=F_{1}^{(j)}
. But this is also a contradiction, since then we would have
F_{1}^{(i)}\notin\partial^{+}(\mathcal{B})\cup\partial^{+}(\mathcal{A})
(by definition of
\mathcal{F}^{-}
), but we also have
F_{1}^{(i)}\notin\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
by assumption. This contradicts the optimality of
\mathcal{F}
, as we could safely add
F_{1}^{(i)}
to
\mathcal{A}
.

(
3
)

This is similar to (
2
), with the roles of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
inverted.

Therefore it only remains to show that there exists a set of pairs of size
\ell\geq|F|-1
satisfying conditions (
1
)–(
3
). We can achieve this by choosing

•

all pairs
(F_{1},F_{2})
with
F_{1}\in\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
and
F_{2}\in\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
, and

•

a pair
(F_{1},F_{2})
for each
F_{2}\in\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
, with
F_{1}
being any set in
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
.

In total we get

\displaystyle\ell

\displaystyle=|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|\cdot|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|

\displaystyle\geq|F|-|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|-1+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|=|F|-1.

The inequality holds because each of
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})
and
\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})
has at least one element, and
|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})|=|F|-|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|
, the worst case being therefore when
|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|=1
and
|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})\backslash\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})|=|F|-|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{A})\cap\partial^{-}(\mathcal{B})|-1
(or vice versa).

Since each of the
\ell
pairs leads to a distinct set in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
with size at least
|F|-2
, this concludes the proof of the first part of the lemma. The argument for the second statement is exactly the same where we invert the roles of subset and superset.
∎

This lemma is sufficient to lower bound the size of
|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|
. For this, we use a technical lemma which is essentially a corollary of the Kruskal–Katona theorem (
Theorem

1.5
).

Lemma 4.7
.

If
S
is a set of
k+1
sets of size at least
k
, then
\partial^{-}(S)\geq 2^{k+1}-1
.

If
S^{\prime}
is a set of
k
sets of size at least
k
, then
\partial^{-}(S^{\prime})\geq 2^{k+1}-2
.

We also have the counterparts for supersets, that is, if
S
is a set of
n-k+1
sets of size at most
k
, then
\partial^{+}(S)\geq 2^{n-k+1}-1
, and, if
S^{\prime}
is a set of
n-k
sets of size at most
k
, then
\partial^{+}(S^{\prime})\geq 2^{n-k+1}-2
.

Proof.

The proof works by adding up the bound given by Kruskal–Katona layer by layer. Clearly if the sets in
S
and
S^{\prime}
are larger than
k
, then the number of subsets is even larger, so we may assume they all have size exactly
k
. Conveniently,
|S|=\binom{k+1}{k}
, therefore by Kruskal–Katona there are at least
\binom{k+1}{i}
subsets of elements of
S
on layer
i
, for
0\leq i\leq k
. If we add these up, we get in total

\partial^{-}(S)\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k+1}{i}=2^{k+1}-1.

For
S^{\prime}
, we have
|S^{\prime}|=\binom{k}{k}+\binom{k-1}{k-1}+\dots+\binom{1}{1}
. Hence there are at least

\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\binom{k-j}{k-i-j}

subsets of elements of
S^{\prime}
on layer
i
. If we add these up, we get in total

\partial^{-}(S^{\prime})\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\binom{k-j}{k-i-j}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{k-j}\binom{k-j}{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}2^{k-j}\geq 2^{k+1}-2.

The proof for the converse is analogous, and it uses an analogous version of Kruskal–Katona for lower bounding the number of supersets instead of subsets.
∎

The technical lemma will be useful in the proof of this final lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|\geq\begin{cases}2^{n/2+1}-2&\text{ if $n$ is even, and}\\
3\cdot 2^{(n-1)/2}-2&\text{ if $n$ is odd.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
F
be the smallest set in
\mathcal{F}^{+}
, with
|F|=k
. By
Lemma

4.5
, there are at least
k-1
sets in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
, each of size at least
k-2
. Suppose any of these sets had size at least
k
. Then we would have

|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|\geq 2^{n-k}+2^{k}.

This is minimised by
2^{n/2+1}>2^{n/2+1}-2
when
n
is even and by
3\cdot 2^{(n-1)/2}>3\cdot 2^{(n-1)/2}-2
when
n
is odd.

So from now on we may assume that there are at least
k-1
sets in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
of sizes
k-2
and
k-1
. Suppose there is one of size
k-2
. Then by
Lemma

4.5
, there are at least
n-k+1
sets in
\mathcal{F}^{+}
of size at most
k
. Then by
Lemma

4.7
, we must have

|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|\geq 2^{n-k+1}-1+2^{k-1}-1.

This is minimised with
k=n/2
if
n
is even and
k=(n+1)/2
if
n
is odd, giving exactly the bound of this lemma.

The only case we are left with is when all the at least
k-1
sets in
\mathcal{F}^{-}
we found have size
k-1
. Then, again by
Lemma

4.7
, we have

|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|+|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|\geq 2^{n-k}+2^{k}-2,

which is minimised with
k=n/2
if
n
is even and
k=(n-1)/2
if
n
is odd, giving once more the exact bound of this lemma. This concludes the proof.
∎

Now we finally have all the ingredients to prove
Theorem

1.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

By
Lemmas

4.3
and
4.8
, we deduce that

|\mathcal{F}|\leq 2^{n}-|\partial^{+}(\mathcal{F}^{+})|-|\partial^{-}(\mathcal{F}^{-})|\leq\begin{cases}2^{n}-2^{n/2+1}+2&\text{ if $n$ is even, and}\\
2^{n}-3\cdot 2^{(n-1)/2}+2&\text{ if $n$ is odd.}\end{cases}

Sharpness is witnessed by the family
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
with
\mathcal{A}=\{[n/2]\}
if
n
is even and
\mathcal{A}=\{[(n-1)/2]\}
if
n
is odd, and
\mathcal{B}=\{B\in 2^{[n]}:B\nsubseteq A\text{ and }A\nsubseteq B\text{ for any }A\in\mathcal{A}\}
.
∎

5.
Concluding remarks

Recall the narrative we began with in the introduction.
As we cross the cardinality threshold
\La(n)=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
, how does the space of
2
-chains in a subfamily of the Boolean lattice evolve?
In this work, we interpreted this rough question in terms of largest component in the comparability graphs
G_{\mathcal{F}}
of subfamilies
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
,
as we tune the prescribed lower bound on
|\mathcal{F}|
beyond
\La(n)
.
Because of the middle layer family
\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
,
G_{\mathcal{F}}
may consist of a disjoint union of isolated vertices unless we prescribe that
|\mathcal{F}|>\La(n)
.
Our main result (
Theorem

1.3
) implies the following statement.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, if
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{[n]}
is a family satisfying
|\mathcal{F}|\geq(1+\varepsilon)\La(n)
, then
G_{\mathcal{F}}
must have a component of order
\exp(\Omega(n/\log n))
.
Qualitatively, this already shows how
\La(n)
constitutes a sharp threshold with respect to the component structure of the comparability graph, in that up to cardinality
\La(n)
it can be all isolated vertices, while a modest
1+\varepsilon
factor above
\La(n)
cardinality guarantees a strikingly large component.

Our main conjecture (
Conjecture

1.1
) proposes that the size guarantee
\exp(\Omega(n/\log n))
above could be improved to
\exp(\Omega(n))
.
(Consider the Stirling’s approximation computations displayed parenthetically after the conjecture.)
This improvement would in fact follow if we could establish that
\mad^{\star}(t,\mathcal{O}^{\star})=O(\log t)
, which is an open problem mentioned in
[
2
]
, or, more specifically, if we could show
\xi^{\star}_{n}(t)=O(\log t)
, which is related to
Question

2.6
and is an interesting problem in its own right.
We contend that the special case
k=n-2
of
Conjecture

1.1
alone is enticing, even only asymptotically.
Moreover,
Conjecture

1.1
suggests that the size of a largest component will be of order
2^{n-o(n)}
once we prescribe that
|\mathcal{F}|=\omega(\La(n))
.
Thus, the broader picture sketched (if correct) compares interestingly against the intuition we might have borrowed from, say, the theory of random graphs or percolation.

As a side remark, we note how the study of such ‘condensation’ phenomena has been pursued quite intensively in stochastic, algorithmic contexts, see e.g.
[
1
]
, but to our knowledge —and to our surprise— not systematically in the context of (extremal) combinatorics. In a parallel work
[
7
]
, Buys, van den Heuvel and the second author establish a similar (but qualitatively distinct) phenomenon with respect to Turán’s theorem.

The study undertaken here not only links nicely to many classic studies, but also raises numerous interesting research questions. The first author’s thesis contains an account of several of these. To conclude the paper, we highlight just one, which takes an alternative, and perhaps more authentic, interpretation of our original motivating question.

Given a positive integer
m
, how large can
\mathcal{F}
be before
G_{\mathcal{F}}
must contain some component of
size
greater than
m
?
That is, instead of measuring the number of elements of
\mathcal{F}
it spans, we measure the number of edges (i.e. comparable pairs) a component of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
has.
While this alternative interpretation is related to the problems we tackled in this paper, it does not seem that we can straightforwardly apply the same techniques.
Nevertheless, we ask, could the disjoint union of complete subfamilies be extremal for this question?
