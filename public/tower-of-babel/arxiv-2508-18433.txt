The Painlevé I hierarchy: Correspondence between the isomonodromic approach and the minimal models of the KP hierarchy
1 Mohamad Alameddine , 2 Nathan Hayford , 3 Olivier Marchal

Abstract




Abstract
: Two approaches to the Painlevé I hierarchy are discussed: the isomonodromic construction based on meromorphic connections, and the minimal models construction based on a reduction of the KP hierarchy. An explicit correspondence between both formalisms is built which gives the identification of these setups. In particular, this provides new expressions for the Lax matrices and Hamiltonians.

Introduction

The primary subject of investigation of the present work is the Painlevé I (PI) hierarchy, a sequence of non-linear ordinary differential equations whose first element is the famous Painlevé I equation. The PI hierarchy appears in many contexts ranging from physical applications in the study of field theories of closed strings
[
7
,
15
,
20
,
21
]
, to universal critical ‘edge’ transitions in random matrix theory
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
19
]
, to integrable regularizations of dispersionless wave equations
[
14
,
16
,
17
]
, and to geometric and topological structures linking the study of moduli spaces of connections and character varieties of surfaces
[
5
]
through the irregular Riemann-Hilbert correspondence. In this work, we focus on two different approaches that generate the PI hierarchy: the geometric isomonodromic deformations approach of
[
29
]
and the string equation/minimal models approach detailed in
[
41
]
arising from a specific reduction of the KP hierarchy.

The
geometric isomonodromy approach
developed in
[
29
]
for the PI hierarchy relies on the study of the space of twisted rank
-2
meromorphic connections over the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}
having one ramified pole at infinity of arbitrary order
r_{\infty}\geq 3
. The starting point (inspired from the non-ramified case of
[
26
,
3
,
31
]
) is the Fabry-Hukuhara-Turritin-Levelt theorem offering a local diagonalization for the connection in a neighborhood of the pole. This local diagonalization offers an extension of the space of deformations beyond the space of complex structures of the Riemann surface by including the irregular type of the monodromy considered, generalizing the symplectic structure of Atiyah and Bott
[
2
]
beyond the tame case
[
3
]
. Considering monodromy preserving deformations of the base, one obtains an explicit Hamiltonian formulation of the chosen Darboux coordinates. It is well known that the Painlevé equations admit a Hamiltonian formulation
[
28
,
35
]
and in
[
29
]
the Hamiltonians for the PI hierarchy were given explicitly. This was achieved after the reduction of the tangent space of deformations over the base, providing a Liouville-integrable Hamiltonian system.

The
\mathbf{(2,2g+1)}
minimal model approach
to the PI hierarchy, also known as the
\mathbf{(2,2g+1)}
string equation approach
, is based on the fact that this hierarchy can be characterized as a reduction of KP (more specifically here, the KdV) hierarchy. This is also the case for string equations of all types
[
18
,
27
]
. This observation was made first for the simplest equation in the hierarchy, the Painlevé I equation, which characterizes the
(2,3)
minimal model. Higher order analogues given by an odd integer
2g+1
constitute the higher elements of the hierarchy. The string equations admit a maximally twisted Lax representation (the coalescing of all the eigenvalues)
[
40
,
39
,
11
]
, and in rank
-2
(the case considered in this work) this Lax representation gives the PI hierarchy through the Lenard recursion operators. This Lax representation admits many similarities with the Mumford system
[
34
]
, which motivated researchers to apply its Poisson bracket and “spectral Darboux coordinates” to the PI hierarchy, building all the ingredients needed for considering isomonodromic deformations and thus obtaining the underlying Hamiltonian structure. In this way, one obtains a Hamiltonian for the PI hierarchy, modulo some correction terms which were made explicit in
[
41
]
.

The two approaches to the PI hierarchy are manifestations of two different perspectives. The isomonodromic approach is built on the symplectic nature of isomonodromy systems, and the Darboux coordinates in this setup are motivated by the historical idea of apparent singularities arising when considering the oper gauge. On the other hand, the
(2,2g+1)
string equation approach relies on an extension of the Mumford system (an isospectral system), and its well known structure. Both approaches provide a Hamiltonian structure for the PI hierarchy and thus a natural question arises: are both approaches giving the same hierarchy? If the answer is in the positive, how could we identify both formalisms and the different perspectives considered? The primary goal of the present work is to address these questions.

The main result of this paper is a positive answer to the above questions: one can identify both formalisms and build an explicit correspondence between these perspectives. Let us state that for this to hold, one needs to identify the symplectic structures of both formalisms. This is accomplished through the identification of the
times
, Lax matrices and the
Hamiltonians
(through the oper Darboux coordinates). This is discussed in
section 5
. In particular, the correspondence between the base of times (and the tangent space) giving the Hamiltonian flows is done in
Theorem 5.1
. The time-correspondence is manifested by a dilatation of the base and consequently a dilatation of the Hamiltonian flows of the tangent space of deformations. This result is complemented by the identification of the Hamiltonian structure given in
Theorem 5.5
, whose proof is based on the identification of the oper and spectral invariants done in
Theorem 5.3
. It turns out, that the correspondence of both setups allows one to identify the Lax formulation of both sides obtaining a full correspondence between the two rank-
2
Lax representations (
Theorem 5.2
). In other words, the Lax representation of the geometric isomonodromic approach is equivalent to the one used on the minimal model side which yields the string equation. This result also extends to an identification of the apparent singularities with the coordinates used on the minimal model side and allows us to generalize the symmetric coordinates of
[
33
]
(also used in
[
29
]
) to the minimal model, an issue left open in
[
41
]
.

The article is organized as follows. In
section 3
the twisted isomonodromic approach leading to the PI hierarchy is presented based on results of
[
29
]
. In
section 4
, the minimal model approach is reviewed with notations mostly based on
[
41
]
. The identification of both formalisms is the main topic of
section 5
where several theorems associated to different parts of the correspondence are discussed and proved. Furthermore, the extension of the results of
[
33
]
left open in
[
41
]
are presented using the symmetric Darbooux coordinates. We left the concluding remarks and possible generalizations for
section 6
. The article is meant to be self-contained, providing a short but complete review on the subject. With this in mind, we present an additional
Appendix A
explaining the reduction of the KP hierarchy giving the PI hierarchy and
Appendix B
explaining the origin of the Poisson bracket that identifies the coordinates on both sides as Darboux coordinates.

3
The Painlevé I hierarchy as a twisted isomonodromy system

3.1
General geometric construction

The goal of this section is to review the construction of
[
29
]
leading to the PI hierarchy obtained from the isomonodromic deformations of twisted meromorphic connections of rank
2
with a single pole at infinity.

Definition 3.1

(Space of meromorphic connections with a pole at infinity)
.

Let
r_{\infty}\geq 3
be a given integer. We consider

F_{\infty,r_{\infty}}:=\left\{\hat{L}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{r_{\infty}-1}\hat{L}^{[\infty,k]}\lambda^{k-1}\,\,/\,\,\left\{\hat{L}^{[\infty,k]}\right\}_{k=1}^{r_{\infty}-1}\in\left(\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\right)^{r_{\infty}-1}\right\}/{GL}_{2}

(3-1)

where
{GL}_{2}
acts simultaneously by conjugation on all the coefficients
\{\hat{L}^{[\infty,k]}\}_{1\leq k\leq r_{\infty}-1}
. The corresponding meromorphic connection is defined by

d\hat{\Psi}=\hat{L}(\lambda)d\lambda\hat{\Psi}\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\Psi}=\hat{L}(\lambda)\hat{\Psi}

(3-2)

where
\hat{\Psi}
is referred to as the wave matrix and
\hat{L}(\lambda)
is referred to as the Lax matrix.

There are several notions of twists in the literature. In this work, the term “twisted” is manifested by a ramification of the formal normal solution. The PI hierarchy is obtained from a twisted system and for this, it is necessary to consider the following subset of
F_{\infty,r_{\infty}}
.

Definition 3.2

(Set of twisted meromorphic connections at infinity)
.

Let
r_{\infty}\geq 3
be a given integer. We consider the subset of
\hat{F}_{\infty,r_{\infty}}
defined by

\hat{F}_{\infty,r_{\infty}}=\left\{\hat{L}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{r_{\infty}-1}\hat{L}^{[\infty,k]}\lambda^{k-1}\,\,/\,\,\left\{\hat{L}^{[\infty,k]}\right\}_{k=1}^{r_{\infty}-1}\in\left(\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\right)^{r_{\infty}-1}\text{ , }\hat{L}^{[\infty,r_{\infty}-1]}\text{ not diagonalizable }\right\}/{GL}_{2}

(3-3)

The fact that the leading order is not diagonalizable implies that infinity is ramified. In other words, infinity is a ramification point of the spectral curve, which is the algebraic affine curve defined as the characteristic polynomial of
\hat{L}(\lambda)
:

\mathcal{S}:=\{(\lambda,y)\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\times\overline{\mathbb{C}}\,\,/\,\,\det(yI_{2}-\hat{L}(\lambda))=0\}

(3-4)

The starting point when considering this case is the formal normal solution (also referred to as the Birkhoff factorization), this will define the irregular type and the exponent of formal monodromy associated to our case.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
z\overset{\text{def}}{:=}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}
. For any given
\hat{L}(\lambda)
in an orbit of
\hat{F}_{\infty,r_{\infty}}
, there exists a local gauge matrix
G_{\infty}(z)
around
\infty
such that

G_{\infty}(z)=G_{\infty,-1}z+G_{\infty,0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}G_{\infty,k}z^{-k}\,\text{ with }\,G_{\infty,-1}\,\text{ of rank 1}

(3-5)

and

•

\Psi_{\infty}(z)\overset{\text{def}}{=}G_{\infty}(z)\hat{\Psi}
is a formal fundamental solution, also known as a Turritin-Levelt fundamental form (or Birkhoff factorization):

\displaystyle\Psi_{\infty}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi_{\infty}^{(\text{reg})}(z)\,{\operatorname{diag}}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}\frac{t_{\infty,k}}{k}z^{k}-\frac{1}{2}\ln z\right),\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}(-1)^{k}\frac{t_{\infty,k}}{k}z^{k}-\frac{1}{2}\ln z\right)\right)

(3-6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi_{\infty}^{(\text{reg})}(z)\,{\operatorname{diag}}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}\frac{t_{\infty,k}}{k}\lambda^{\frac{k}{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\ln\lambda\right),\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}(-1)^{k}\frac{t_{\infty,k}}{k}\lambda^{\frac{k}{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\ln\lambda\right)\right)

(3-7)

where
\Psi_{\infty}^{(\text{reg})}(z)\in\text{GL}_{2}[[z^{-1}]]
is holomorphic at
z=\infty
.

•

The associated Lax matrix
L_{\infty}=G_{\infty}\hat{L}G_{\infty}^{-1}+(\partial_{\lambda}G_{\infty})G_{\infty}^{-1}
has a diagonal singular part at
\infty
:

\displaystyle L_{\infty}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{diag}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}t_{\infty,k}z^{k-2}-\frac{1}{4z^{2}},\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}(-1)^{k}t_{\infty,k}z^{k-2}-\frac{1}{4z^{2}}\right)+O(1)

(3-9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\operatorname{diag}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}t_{\infty,k}\lambda^{\frac{k}{2}-1}-\frac{1}{4\lambda},\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}(-1)^{k}t_{\infty,k}\lambda^{\frac{k}{2}-1}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}\right)+O(1)

(3-10)

The complex numbers
\left(t_{\infty,k}\right)_{1\leq k\leq 2r_{\infty}-2}
define the base of “irregular times” at infinity that we denote
\mathbf{t}=\{(t_{\infty,k})_{1\leq k\leq 2r_{\infty}-2}\}
parameterizing the irregular type of
\hat{L}\in\hat{F}_{\infty,r_{\infty}}
.

Note that (
3-9
) implies that
\det\hat{L}=\det(L_{\infty}+(\partial_{\lambda}G_{\infty})G_{\infty}^{-1})
. Thus locally at infinity we have an expansion of the eigenvalues
(y_{1}(\lambda),y_{2}(\lambda))
of
\hat{L}(\lambda)
(See eq.
3.4
of
[
29
]

\displaystyle y_{1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{\lambda\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}t_{\infty,k}\lambda^{\frac{k}{2}-1}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}+O\left(\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)

(3-12)

\displaystyle y_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{\lambda\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}(-1)^{k}t_{\infty,k}\lambda^{\frac{k}{2}-1}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}+O\left(\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)

(3-13)

Fixing the irregular type of
\hat{L}(\lambda)
does not fix it uniquely. In fact, the space

\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty,r_{\infty},\mathbf{t}}:=\left\{\hat{L}(\lambda)\in\hat{F}_{\infty,r_{\infty}}\,\,/\,\,\hat{L}(\lambda)\,\text{ has irregular type }\mathbf{t}\right\}

(3-14)

is a symplectic manifold, seen as the symplectic quotient of a product of coadjoint orbits
[
4
]
, of dimension

\dim\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty,r_{\infty},\mathbf{t}}=2r_{\infty}-6=2g

(3-15)

where

g:=r_{\infty}-3

(3-16)

is the genus of the spectral curve. Consequently, in order to obtain explicit formulas for the Lax matrix and for the Hamiltonians, one needs to choose an appropriate set of Darboux coordinates
(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})
. Several sets of coordinates are introduced in this work, the first of which is the set of apparent singularities of the oper gauge used in
[
29
]
. This idea dates back to Garnier, and is widely used for meromorphic differential systems.

The irregular type of the local diagonalization is parameterized by the set of irregular times
\mathbf{t}
, which constitute the base of deformations
\mathbb{B}
whose fiber is the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{t}}\mathbb{B}
. Note that in our case, the monodromy of the system is constant. Monodromy preserving deformations amount to deforming the fundamental solutions of our system (
3-2
), for this, one defines a general deformation vector given by

Definition 3.3
.

The general isomonodromic deformation operator is defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}\alpha_{\infty,k}\partial_{t_{\infty,k}}

(3-17)

where the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{2r_{\infty}-2}=\mathbb{C}^{2g+4}
is defined by

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{2r_{\infty}-2}\alpha_{\infty,k}\mathbf{e}_{k}.

(3-18)

which is a vector of the tangent space.

The fact that the system is integrable is equivalent to say that the auxiliary matrix
\hat{A}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\lambda)
defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)]=\hat{A}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\lambda)\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)

(3-19)

is also meromorphic with a polar structure dominated by the one of
\hat{L}(\lambda)
. In our present case, it corresponds to say that
\hat{A}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\lambda)
is a polynomial of degree at most
r_{\infty}-2
. The compatibility of the system

\partial_{\lambda}\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)=\hat{L}(\lambda)\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)\,\,,\,\,\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)]=\hat{A}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\lambda)\hat{\Psi}(\lambda)

(3-20)

is non trivial and is equivalent to the so-called compatibility equation (or zero curvature equation)

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[\hat{L}]-\partial_{\lambda}\hat{A}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+[\hat{L},\hat{A}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]=0.

(3-21)

Following the steps of
[
29
]
, the compatibility equation is equivalent to the evolution of a chosen set of Darboux coordinates; these evolutions turn out to be Hamiltonian. We review this construction here and refer to
[
29
]
for the full statements and proofs.

Note that at this stage, one could observe that the dimension of the symplectic space (
g=r_{\infty}-3
) is lower than the dimension of the space of irregular times
2g+4=2r_{\infty}-2
. This suggests that there are directions in which the Darboux coordinates evolve trivially. This statement was proved in
[
29
]
where the trivial and non-trivial directions were made explicit. In order to eliminate the trivial directions that play no role in the symplectic structure, it is convenient to select the so-called “canonical choice of trivial times” of
[
29
]
. In the present setup, it corresponds to the following assumption:

Assumption 1

(Canonical choice of trivial times: reduction of the deformation space)
.

Following
[
29
]
we set

•

all even irregular times to
0
: for all
k\in\llbracket 1,r_{\infty}-1\rrbracket
:
t_{\infty,2k}=0
.

•

t_{\infty,2r_{\infty}-3}=2
and
t_{\infty,2r_{\infty}-5}=0
.

This assumption is based on two observations: the first one is the manifestation of the fact that the passage from
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}\rightarrow\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
does not change the symplectic structure
[
23
]
. The second is the action of the linear projective group of the projective line (the Möbius transformations) which preserves the symplectic structure. Moreover, using the conjugation action of
GL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, one could consider a representative
\hat{L}(\lambda)\in\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty,r_{\infty},\mathbf{t}}
normalized such that

\hat{L}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\lambda^{r_{\infty}-2}+\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
X&0\end{pmatrix}\lambda^{r_{\infty}-3}+O\left(\lambda^{r_{\infty}-4}\right).

(3-22)

In the rest of the paper
we always assume that
1
is verified and that
\hat{L}(\lambda)
is normalized according to (
3-22
).
This implies in particular that
{\operatorname{Tr}}\,\hat{L}(\lambda)=0
.
This assumption allows for a reduction of (
3-12
):

Proposition 3.2

(Local expansion of the eigenvalues at infinity)
.

Let
(y_{1}(\lambda),y_{2}(\lambda))
be the two eigenvalues of
\hat{L}(\lambda)
, then under
1
, we have

\displaystyle y_{1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{\lambda\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle\lambda^{r_{\infty}-\frac{5}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{r_{\infty}-4}t_{\infty,2k+1}\lambda^{k-\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}+O\left(\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)

(3-23)

\displaystyle y_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{\lambda\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle-\lambda^{r_{\infty}-\frac{5}{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{r_{\infty}-4}t_{\infty,2k+1}\lambda^{k-\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{4\lambda}+O\left(\lambda^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)

(3-24)

3.2
Explicit formulas in the oper gauge

Let us now briefly recall the results of
[
29
]
in the oper gauge. In this gauge, the appropriate Darboux coordinates are given using the apparent singularities. In particular one defines:

Definition 3.4

(Oper Darboux coordinates and apparent singularities)
.

We define
\left(q_{i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq g}
as the
g

\left[\tilde{L}(\lambda)\right]_{1,2}

We denote

The choice of the dual partners is such that for any

They appear when performing the gauge transformation seen as a change of trivialization taking the connection to the oper gauge, i.e. the gauge in which the Lax matrix is companion-like and thus reduces to a scalar equation. The terminology “oper Darboux coordinates” stands for the fact that these coordinates are linked with the oper gauge which corresponds to the scalar reformulation of the connection. For rank two systems, the gauge transformation towards the oper gauge is explicit and given by:

4.1

Let us define the PI hierarchy via the so-called

Remark 4.1

The fact that (

u

Definition 4.3

4.3
Eigenvalues and spectral invariants

4.1
provides a natural framework for the PI hierarchy to compare with the isomonodromic approach. Let us first stress that the eigenvalues
\left(\xi_{g,1}(\lambda),\xi_{g,2}(\lambda)\right)
of

(that we shall identify later to

and a contribution from

appears starting from the order

4.4
Spectral Darboux coordinates

In the previous subsection, we defined
g
times
\left(s_{2k+1}\right)_{0\leq k\leq g-1}
that give the flows of the PI hierarchy. Since one has an underlying symplectic structure of dimension
2g
, one defines a set of Darboux coordinates whose evolutions with respect to these times characterize this symplectic structure. There are several choices for these coordinates. The most standard one is to take the so-called “spectral Darboux coordinates” (See Section
8
of
[
41
]
).

Definition 4.7

(Spectral Darboux coordinates (See, Section
8
of
[
41
]
))
.

We define the spectral Darboux coordinates
\left(\lambda_{j},\mu_{j}\right)_{1\leq j\leq g}
by

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda_{j})\right]_{1,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,\,,\,\forall\,j\in\llbracket 1,g\rrbracket,

(4-76)

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda_{j})\right]_{1,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\,\,,\,\forall\,j\in\llbracket 1,g\rrbracket.

(4-77)

In other words,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}:=\left(\lambda_{j}\right)_{1\leq j\leq g}
are the zeros of
\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\right]_{1,2}
(which is a monic polynomial of degree
g
) and
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=\left(\mu_{j}\right)_{1\leq j\leq g}
are the evaluation of
\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\right]_{1,2}
at these values.

It is well-known that these coordinates
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
are canonical (See for example Section 8 of
[
41
]
and references therein) with respect to the Poisson bracket associated to the so-called “Mumford system”. We give a brief recounting of the properties of this Poisson bracket and a sketch of the proof that these coordinates are indeed canonical with respect to it in Appendix
B
. In particular, we have that

\forall\,(j,k)\in\llbracket 1,g\rrbracket^{2}\,:\,\left\{\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k}\right\}=0\,\,,\,\,\left\{\mu_{j},\mu_{k}\right\}=0\,\,,\,\,\left\{\lambda_{j},\mu_{k}\right\}=\delta_{j,k},

(4-78)

which follows from
B.7
,
and also

\forall\,j\in\llbracket 1,g\rrbracket\,:\,\left\{\lambda_{j},\tilde{I}_{0}(\lambda)\right\}=0=\left\{\mu_{j},\tilde{I}_{0}(\lambda)\right\},

(4-79)

with
\tilde{I}_{0}(\lambda)
defined in
4.6
(this is an immediate corollary of Proposition
B.5
). Here the Poisson bracket
\{.,.\}
is the Poisson bracket associated to the
3g+1
-dimensional moduli space of the matrix
\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)
whose definition is detailed in
Appendix B
. In the end, we have the following results

Proposition 4.4

(Darboux coordinates property and Casimirs)
.

The coordinates
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})
are Darboux coordinates for the Poisson bracket. Moreover, coefficients of
\tilde{I}_{0}(\lambda)
are Casimirs.

Proof.

The proof is immediate from (
4-78
) and (
4-79
).

.

5.5
Symmetric Darboux coordinates

Symmetric Darboux coordinates
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}):=\left(Q_{j},P_{j}\right)_{1\leq j\leq g}
are introduced in
3.5
and correspond to the symmetric coordinates used by Mazzocco and Mo
[
33
]
in the derivation of the Painlevé II hierarchy. An important feature of these coordinates is that they are related to the oper Darboux coordinates
\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}\right)
by a time-independent and symplectic transformation as proved in
[
29
]
. In other words, we have:

\sum_{j=1}^{g}dq_{j}\wedge dp_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}dQ_{j}\wedge dP_{j}

(5-33)

A consequence of this property is that the Hamiltonians
\tilde{K}_{2k-1}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P};\mathbf{s})
(resp.
\tilde{\text{Ham}}^{(\mathbf{e}_{2k-1})}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P};\mathbf{t})
) governing the Hamiltonian evolutions of
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P})
relative to
s_{2k-1}
(resp.
t_{\infty,2k-1}
) can be obtained from
K_{2k-1}
(resp.
\text{Ham}^{(\mathbf{e}_{2k-1})}
) by simply replacing the Darboux coordinates according to
3.5
. This observation was made by Takasaki in Section
11
of
[
41
]
but left as an open question there. The results of the present article combined with the proof of the symplectic property of the change of coordinates in
[
29
]
provides a proof of this observation.

In the direct isomonodromic approach developed in
[
29
]
, expressions for all relevant quantities are given in terms of these symmetric Darboux coordinates and some of them have been pasted (upon notation adaptation) in this article. For example, expressions of the Lax matrix
\hat{L}(\lambda)
are given in
3.8
and expressions of the Hamiltonians
\tilde{\text{Ham}}^{(\mathbf{e}_{2k-1})}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P};\mathbf{t})
are given in
3.9
. The main advantage of these symmetric Darboux coordinates is that the corresponding Hamiltonians are polynomials in the Darboux coordinates (and quadratic in
\mathbf{P}
). Expressions of the auxiliary matrices
\hat{A}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\lambda)
can also be found in
[
29
]
. To our knowledge, no general formula for the Hamiltonians
\tilde{K}_{2k-1}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P};\mathbf{s})
or for the Lax matrices in terms of
(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P})
exist on the
(2,2g+1)
minimal side in the literature. Consequently, the identifications proposed in this article and results of
[
29
]
fill this gap.

Another point of interest is to clarify the role of
u
in the isomonodromic formalism. Indeed, from
4.3
and
4.1
, we have under
1
that

\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\right]_{2,1}=\lambda^{g+1}-\frac{u}{2}\lambda^{g}+O\left(\lambda^{g-1}\right)

(5-34)

At the same time,
3.8
provides

\displaystyle\left[\hat{L}(\lambda)\right]_{2,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-P^{(2)}_{\infty,2g+1}h_{0}(\{\mathbf{q}\})\lambda^{g+1}-\left(P^{(2)}_{\infty,2g+1}h_{1}(\{\mathbf{q}\})+P^{(2)}_{\infty,2g}h_{0}(\{\mathbf{q}\})\right)\lambda^{g}

(5-36)

\displaystyle+O\left(\lambda^{g-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{g+1}+Q_{1}\lambda^{g}++O\left(\lambda^{g-1}\right)

6
Discussion

The main objective of this article was to provide a clear identification regarding the two approaches (isomonodromic or minimal model) of the PI hierarchy. This is achieved through
Theorem 5.1
,
Theorem 5.2
and
Theorem 5.5
. As a by-product, it gives a non-trivial check of the validity of the formulas proposed by the two approaches. A natural consequence of this identification of the two formalisms is to be able to build a bridges between quantities that can be easy to understand on one side, but difficult on the other. For example, as discussed in
subsection 5.5
, the identification provides explicit formulas for the Lax matrices and Hamiltonians in terms of symmetric Darboux coordinates, which were missing in the minimal model side.

The identification of both formalisms also provides interesting consequences.

•

The isomonodromic side provides that the Hamiltonians
\left(K_{2k-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu};\mathbf{s})\right)_{1\leq k\leq g}
can be expressed (up to irrelevant purely time-dependent terms) as time-dependent but Darboux coordinates independent linear combinations of coefficients
\left(H_{\infty,j}\right)_{0\leq j\leq g-1}
that do not depend on the isomonodromic deformation (i.e. independent of
k
) and appears as coefficients in the Lax matrix in the oper gauge. More precisely:
K_{2k-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu};\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{2k-1}\underset{j=0}{\overset{g-1}{\sum}}\nu^{(\mathbf{e}_{2k-1})}_{\infty,j+1}(\mathbf{t}=D^{-1}\mathbf{s})H_{\infty,j}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu};\mathbf{t}:=D^{-1}\mathbf{s})
. This fact is definitely not obvious on the
(2,2g+1)
minimal side where the quantity is defined from
4.6
.

•

Theorem 5.2
provides an alternative formula for the Lax matrices in terms of the
\left(\mathcal{U}_{2\ell+1}(\lambda)\right)_{\ell\geq 0}
matrices. This is particularly interesting for the auxiliary matrices and gives:

\hat{A}_{\mathbf{e}_{2k-1}}(\lambda)=(2k-1)\mathcal{U}_{2k-1}(\lambda)=\left[\lambda^{k-1}U(\lambda)\right]_{\infty,+}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
R_{2k-1}[u]&0\end{pmatrix}.

(6-1)

for all
k\in\llbracket 1,g\rrbracket
with
u=-2Q_{1}
.

There are several open questions that arise from the present work. Indeed, in this paper, we could identify the direct isomonodromic approach describing the PI hierarchy with the corresponding reduction of the KP hierarchy. However, there exist generalizations on both sides, and it would be interesting to obtain similar results in these cases.
For example, the case of rank
2
connections with arbitrary non-twisted singularities is done in
[
31
]
from the direct isomonodromic approach, while generalization of the present work to the reduction of the mKdV exists and provides the so-called Painlevé II hierarchy
[
33
]
. In particular, this case should correspond to the case of one regular singularity at
\lambda=0
and one irregular but non-twisted singularity at infinity and it would be interesting to test if formulas of
[
31
]
and
[
33
]
are consistent and can be identified similarly to the work done in this paper.

Another direction is the generalization to any
(p,q)
minimal models with positive coprime integers
p
and
q
. In particular, the rank of the underlying connection is given by the integer
p
. On the minimal model side, there are existing results
[
12
,
6
,
42
,
32
]
and recent results covering specific rank
3
cases
[
22
]
. Performing a similar analysis may allow for the understanding of how to generalize the rank two results of
[
31
,
29
]
to higher rank cases which is currently an open question.

A.1
Pseudodifferential operators

We first review the basic facts about pseudodifferential operators that we will need.
Let
u=u(x)
be a smooth function. We denote by
\mathbb{A}
the ring of formal polynomials in
u
and its derivatives.
3
3

3

An obvious generalization would be to take
\mathbb{A}
to be the ring of formal differential polynomials in a finite collection of variables
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
, but we will not need this generalization.

Definition A.1
.

A
pseudodifferential operator
over
\mathbb{A}
is a formal expression of the form

X=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}X_{i}\partial^{i},

(A-1)

where
X_{i}\in\mathbb{A}
and where it is assumed that there is an index
i_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
X_{i}\equiv 0
for all indices
i\geq i_{0}
. The order of a pseudodifferential operator is then defined as the index
i_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
X_{i_{1}}\neq 0
and
X_{i}=0
for all
i>i_{1}
.

The symbol
\partial
represents the derivative,
in a sense we shall make clear shortly. We can add two pseudodifferential operators in the obvious way (add the coefficients), and similarly multiplication by scalars (i.e. elements of
\mathbb{A}
) is also a well-defined operation. Thus, the set of all pseudodifferential operators forms a vector space, which we denote by
\PsiDO
4
4

4

Another common notation for this space is
\mathbb{A}((\partial^{-1}))
. We prefer here to keep the notation of
[
13
]
.
.
\mathbb{A}
can be seen as a subset of pseudodifferential operators corresponding to pseudodifferential operators with only
i=0
as non-zero term. We define the action of

\partial^{k}
on element of

by

\forall\,a\in\mathbb{A},\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,\partial^{k}\circ a:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{k}{j}a^{(j)}(x)\partial^{k-j}.

(A-2)

Note that this definition makes sense also for
negative integers
as pseudodifferential operators, and thus extends the usual Leibniz property. For example,

\partial^{-1}\circ f=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}f^{(k)}\partial^{-k-1}=f\partial^{-1}-f^{\prime}\partial^{-2}+f^{\prime\prime}\partial^{-3}+...

Note also the relation
\partial^{-1}\circ\partial=\partial\circ\partial^{-1}=1
.
Finally, we can define multiplication, denoted

\PsiDO
by requiring that
\partial^{k}\circ\partial^{i}=\partial^{k+i}
for all
(k,i)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and that multiplication is distributive:
(X+Y)\circ Z=X\circ Z+Y\circ Z

(X\circ Y)\circ Z=X\circ(Y\circ Z)

.

We now define the purely differential (respectively, purely pseudodifferential) parts of a pseudodifferential operator.

Definition A.2
.

Any element

(A-3)

We also define the

\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{\partial}X:=X_{-1}.

(A-4)

Finally, as it is relevant for the definition of the KdV hierarchy, we consider the operator

(A-5)

which is a purely differential operator. We have the following result.

There exists a unique pseudodifferential operator

(A-7)

Proof.

The proof here is standard, and can be found in

Appendix B
The Poisson bracket

Here, we briefly describe the Poisson bracket for the “Mumford system” (it appears in the earlier works
[
24
]
, which is used in
[
41
]
to construct a suitable Hamiltonian structure for the PI hierarchy. This is a Poisson structure on the
3g+1
-dimensional moduli space of the matrix
\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)
, with coordinates given by the coefficients of the entries of the matrix. Its construction was first introduced in
[
38
]
.
Let us first recall that in the Saint Petersburg (tensorial) notation, the Poisson bracket in
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is expressed as:

\left\{\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\otimes}}{{,}}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu)\right\}:=\sum_{a,b,c,d\in\{1,2\}}\{\mathcal{A}^{(g)}_{ab}(\lambda),\mathcal{A}^{(g)}_{cd}(\mu)\}E_{ab}\otimes E_{cd}.

(B-1)

Thus, if we denote

\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha(\lambda)&\beta(\lambda)\\
\gamma(\lambda)&-\alpha(\lambda)\end{pmatrix},

(B-2)

then we have the following proposition.

Proposition B.1

(Expression of the Poisson bracket with entries of the matrix)
.

Denoting
\sigma_{+}=E_{12},\,\sigma_{-}=E_{21},\,\sigma_{3}=\text{diag }(1,-1)
, then from (
B-1
) and (
B-2
), we have that

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\otimes}}{{,}}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\alpha(\lambda),\alpha(\mu)\}\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{3}+\{\alpha(\lambda),\beta(\mu)\}\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{+}+\{\alpha(\lambda),\gamma(\mu)\}\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}

(B-5)

\displaystyle+\{\beta(\lambda),\alpha(\mu)\}\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{3}+\{\beta(\lambda),\beta(\mu)\}\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{+}+\{\beta(\lambda),\gamma(\mu)\}\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{-}

\displaystyle+\{\gamma(\lambda),\alpha(\mu)\}\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}+\{\gamma(\lambda),\beta(\mu)\}\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{+}+\{\gamma(\lambda),\gamma(\mu)\}\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{-}

The Poisson bracket is then specified using the following definition.

Definition B.1

(Definition of the Poisson bracket)
.

The Poisson bracket is defined to be

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\otimes}}{{,}}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu)\right\}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\otimes\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu),r(\lambda-\mu)\right]

(B-8)

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\otimes\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu),\Delta\right],

where
\Delta:=E_{21}\otimes E_{21}
, and
r(\lambda)
is the standard “
r
-matrix” on the loop algebra
\hat{\mathfrak{gl}}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
:

r(\lambda)=\frac{\mathcal{P}}{\lambda},

(B-9)

and
\mathcal{P}\in\text{End}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
is the linear operator defined on basis elements of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
as

\mathcal{P}(v\otimes w):=w\otimes v,

(B-10)

The commutator is defined for any
(A,B,C,D)\in\left(\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\right)^{4}
by

[A\otimes B,C\otimes D]:=(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)-(C\otimes D)(A\otimes B)=AC\otimes BD-CA\otimes DB,

following from the multiplication in the
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
algebra defined by

(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D):=AC\otimes BD,\qquad\forall\,(A,B,C,D)\in\left(\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\right)^{4}.

Remark B.1
.

This Poisson bracket is not invariant under general gauge transformations, but does behave nicely with the “smaller” gauge group consisting of matrices of the form
G:=\mathbb{I}+g(\mathbf{s})E_{21}
, where
g
is any function on the phase space. In particular, it is possible to show that, under the mapping
\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\to G^{-1}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)G
,

\{G^{-1}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\otimes}}{{,}}G^{-1}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu)G\}=(G\otimes G)^{-1}\{\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\otimes}}{{,}}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu)\}(G\otimes G).

(B-11)

This transformation property is immediate from the definition of the Poisson bracket, and the fact that
[G\otimes G,r(\lambda-\mu)]=[G\otimes G,\Delta]=0
, for any
G
of the form

G=\mathbb{I}+g(\mathbf{s})E_{21}
. The reason that this property does not hold for any larger class of matrices is because of the second term in the definition of the bracket involving
\Delta
.

One can compute the Poisson bracket to get the following proposition.

Proposition B.2

(Computation of the Poisson bracket)
.

The Poisson bracket is given by

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\otimes}}{{,}}\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu)\right\}

\displaystyle=-2\left(\alpha(\lambda)-\alpha(\mu)\right)\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{-}+2\frac{\alpha(\lambda)-\alpha(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{+}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta(\lambda)-\beta(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{+}-\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma(\lambda)-\gamma(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right)+\beta(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\beta(\mu)\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}.

Proof.

The proof follows from a series of computations that can be split into different lemmas. The proof is certainly not new but we could not find a reference where computations where made explicit and we decided to produce them for completeness.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be as defined in (
B-10
), then:

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{3},\mathcal{P}]

\displaystyle=-[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbb{I},\mathcal{P}]=2\left(\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{+}-\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{-}\right),

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{+},\mathcal{P}]

\displaystyle=-[\sigma_{+}\otimes\mathbb{I},\mathcal{P}]=\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{+},

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{-},\mathcal{P}]

\displaystyle=-[\sigma_{-}\otimes\mathbb{I},\mathcal{P}]=\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}.

Let
\Delta=\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{-}
. Then:

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{3},\Delta]

\displaystyle=[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbb{I},\Delta]=-2\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{-},

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{+},\Delta]

\displaystyle=\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3},\qquad[\sigma_{+}\otimes\mathbb{I},\Delta]=\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-},

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{-},\Delta]

\displaystyle=[\sigma_{-}\otimes\mathbb{I},\Delta]=0.

The proof of the above follows from elementary (albeit tedious) calculations. The fundamental identity is

[E_{ij}\otimes E_{k\ell},E_{ab}\otimes E_{cd}]=\delta_{aj}\delta_{\ell c}E_{ib}\otimes E_{kd}-\delta_{ib}\delta_{kd}E_{aj}\otimes E_{c\ell}.

(B-12)

The next lemma that we shall need is the following:

Lemma B.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\otimes\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu),r(\lambda-\mu)\right]

\displaystyle=2\frac{\alpha(\lambda)-\alpha(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{+}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta(\lambda)-\beta(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{+}-\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right)

(B-13)

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma(\lambda)-\gamma(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right).

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)=\alpha(\lambda)\sigma_{3}+\beta(\lambda)\sigma_{+}+\gamma(\lambda)\sigma_{-}
. Then, by the previous lemma,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\otimes\mathbb{I},r(\lambda-\mu)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(\lambda)}{\lambda-\mu}[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbb{I},\mathcal{P}]+\frac{\beta(\lambda)}{\lambda-\mu}[\sigma_{+}\otimes\mathbb{I},\mathcal{P}]+\frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{\lambda-\mu}[\sigma_{-}\otimes\mathbb{I},\mathcal{P}]

(B-14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha(\lambda)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{+}\right)+\frac{\beta(\lambda)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{+}-\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right)

(B-16)

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right)

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu),r(\lambda-\mu)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{3},\mathcal{P}]+\frac{\beta(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{+},\mathcal{P}]+\frac{\gamma(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{-},\mathcal{P}]

(B-17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\alpha(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{+}\right)-\frac{\beta(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{+}-\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right)

(B-19)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}\left(\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}\right)

Combining these formulae yields the result of
B.2
.
∎

Finally the last lemma that we need is the following:

Lemma B.3
.

We have

\left[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\otimes\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu),\Delta\right]=-2\left(\alpha(\lambda)-\alpha(\mu)\right)\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{-}+\beta(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-}-\beta(\mu)\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}.

(B-20)

Proof.

Again writing
\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)=\alpha(\lambda)\sigma_{3}+\beta(\lambda)\sigma_{+}+\gamma(\lambda)\sigma_{-}
, we have that

\displaystyle[\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\lambda)\otimes\mathbb{I},\Delta]

\displaystyle=\alpha(\lambda)[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbb{I},\Delta]+\beta(\lambda)[\sigma_{+}\otimes\mathbb{I},\Delta]+\gamma(\lambda)[\sigma_{-}\otimes\mathbb{I},\Delta]

\displaystyle=-2\alpha(\lambda)\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{-}+\beta(\lambda)\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{-},

and

\displaystyle[\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{(g)}(\mu),\Delta]

\displaystyle=\alpha(\mu)[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{3},\Delta]+\beta(\mu)[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{+},\Delta]+\gamma(\mu)[\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma_{-},\Delta]

\displaystyle=-2\alpha(\mu)\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{-}+\beta(\mu)\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{3}.

Combining these two results yields the result of
B.3
.
∎

Eventually, combining
B.2
and
B.3
provides the proof of
B.2
.
∎

B.2
combined with the tensorial expression of

Proposition B.3

We have the following scalar Poisson brackets:

\displaystyle\{\alpha(\lambda),\alpha(\mu)\}

Proposition B.7

With respect to the Poisson bracket (

This is essentially proven in

\{\beta(\lambda),\beta(\mu)\}=0.

Now, using the identity
\{\alpha(\lambda),\beta(\mu)\}=\frac{\beta(\lambda)-\beta(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}

\{\mu_{i},\beta(\mu)\}=\frac{\beta(\mu)}{\mu-\lambda_{j}}.

On the other hand, writing

\displaystyle\frac{\beta(\mu)}{\mu-\lambda_{j}}=\{\mu_{i},\beta(\mu)\}

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}=-\{\mu_{i},\lambda_{j}\}.
