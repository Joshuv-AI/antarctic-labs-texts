The root cause of atypical facial cutaneous lesions: a case series
Authors: Fawad Aslam, Kevin McKenna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental sinus tracts can present as persistent cutaneous lesions on the lower face, often misdiagnosed as dermatological conditions such as squamous cell carcinoma. This case series describes four patients with extraoral lesions initially suspected to be of dermatological origin. Detailed assessment, imaging and histopathology confirmed a dental source in each case. Treatment included root canal treatment, tooth extraction and cyst enucleation, resulting in lesion resolution.

Lower-face cutaneous lesions referred to dermatology on a red-flag skin-cancer pathway may arise from odontogenic infection rather than the skin. Most extraoral sinus tracts originate from mandibular teeth and commonly present as erythematous, crusted or draining lesions that mimic inflammatory dermatoses or squamous cell ­carcinoma (SCC).1–4 Misdiagnosis can lead to unnecessary ­biopsies, repeated antibiotics, scarring and diagnostic delays.1–4 In most cases, resolution follows timely dental intervention, ­typically via endodontic therapy, extraction or cyst surgery.1–4

We present four patients with persistent lower-face lesions initially managed in dermatology with concern for malignancy, in whom appropriate dental assessment identified an odontogenic source and treatment led to complete healing. This case series aims to highlight practical diagnostic cues and re­inforce the need to consider dental aetiology early in assessment of nonhealing lower face lesions. It is intended to illustrate ­diagnostic pitfalls rather than to estimate incidence or prevalence.

Patient 1

An 80-year-old man presented with a 1-month history of a persistent, erythematous, crusted lesion on the left chin (Figure 1a) that failed to respond to topical and oral antibiotics and was triaged as a suspected SCC. Dermatology examination revealed a depressed, oozing plaque with poor dentition in the corresponding mandibular segment, raising suspicion of an odontogenic source (Figure 1b).

Extraoral dental sinus presentations in four patients. (a, b) Patient 1: persistent erythematous, crusted lesion on the left chin associated with chronic dental infection in the left mandibular region. (c, d) Patient 2: erythematous, ulcerated lesion with crusting mimicking malignancy at presentation. (f) Patient 3: persistent sinus with central dimpling on the chin. (e) Intraoral view shows discolouration and restorations of anterior teeth, both previously root-treated, indicating periapical pathology. (g) Patient 4: erythematous, crusted draining sinus on the left chin. (h) Lateral view shows proximity to the mandibular area, with intraoral examination revealing a necrotic mandibular molar as the infection source.

Oral medicine review confirmed multiple nonvital teeth, and extraction was performed under sedation. The lesion resolved completely within weeks following dental treatment.

Patient 2

An 82-year-old woman with advanced dementia was referred on the skin-cancer pathway with a rapidly enlarging crusted lesion on the chin that persisted despite multiple antibiotic courses. Dermatology evaluation showed a dark, friable, tethered nodule on the right submandibular region (Figure 1c, d) with a solitary loose lower right tooth adjacent to the lesion.

An orthopantomogram demonstrated a periapical cyst consistent with chronic dental abscess (Figure 2a). A punch biopsy was performed due to concern for malignancy, which showed a sinus tract without dysplasia. She was referred to oral medicine; the affected tooth was extracted under local anesthaesia and the lesion was fully resolved.

Orthopantomograms (OPGs). (a) Patient 2: radiographic evaluation of the tooth confirms a nonvital grade 3 mobile LR3 tooth. (b, c) Patient 3: OPGs confirm an area of radiolucency consistent with a small dental cyst associated with the apices of both right lateral incisor teeth. (d) Patient 4: OPG shows periapical periodontitis associated with the LL3.

Patient 3

A 24-year-old woman presented with a small, persistent dimple-like lesion on the chin that began after a presumed insect bite and failed to resolve. Dermatology assessment found a depressed lesion with a central punctum raising suspicion of a sinus tract (Figure 1e). Intraoral examination showed discolouration of anterior teeth, both previously root-treated, indicating periapical pathology (Figure 1f).

She was referred to oral medicine and radiographs demonstrated periapical infection affecting previously treated anterior teeth corresponding to the lesion (Figure 2b, c). Conservative endodontic management produced partial improvement but incomplete closure. Definitive apicectomy and cyst enucleation achieved complete healing with minimal scarring.

Patient 4

A 77-year-old woman with a history of a cutaneous SCC and Bowen disease was referred on the skin-cancer pathway with a 2-month history of an intermittently draining chin lesion unresponsive to antibiotics. Dermatology examination showed a puckered erythematous area with granulation tissue mimicking recurrent malignancy (Figure 1g). Oral examination showed a decayed adjacent mandibular tooth with gingival inflammation nearby, raising suspicion for a dental sinus (Figure 1h). A punch biopsy excluded dysplasia and malignancy. She was referred to oral medicine and imaging identified a periapical infection in the adjacent mandibular tooth (Figure 2d), which was subsequently extracted. The lesion resolved completely after dental extraction.

Discussion

All patients in this series were assessed on the skin-cancer pathway because their lower-face lesions were persistent, crusted or friable, and raised significant concern for cutaneous SCC. Odontogenic sinus tracts are a recognized but easily overlooked mimic of cutaneous malignancy and may manifest as a lesion that fails to improve despite antibiotics.1–4 When dental sources are not recognized early, patients often undergo investigations, including biopsies, before the correct aetiology is identified.

In this case series, biopsy was undertaken when malignancy could not be safely excluded clinically, which is appropriate within a 2-week wait pathway and consistent with previous observations on the role of histology when the presentation is ambiguous.5 Baseline imaging and clinical photography were used to document the lesions while cancer was being excluded.6 Once odontogenic infection was identified radiographically, dental intervention led to rapid and complete resolution in all cases.1–4 Recognition of a dental source earlier may prevent escalation along the cancer-diagnostic pathway and shorten time to cure.

Persistent lower-face lesions seen in dermatology, particularly those referred to on the cancer pathway or following failed antibiotic treatment, should prompt consideration of a dental source. A brief intraoral examination and correlation with focal tenderness can point to an odontogenic source. Early imaging can then ­confirm infection and redirect management to oral medicine, avoiding unnecessary escalation within dermatology and the ­cancer-diagnostic pathway. Incorporating routine dental assess­ment earlier in the evaluation of persistent lower-face lesions may enable more timely referral to oral medicine and more efficient management within dermatology and skin-cancer pathways.

Early recognition of odontogenic sinus tracts in dermatology can prevent unavoidable biopsy, reduce inappropriate use of the cancer pathway and enable rapid cure through dental treatment. Lower-face lesions should not advance to invasive investigations until a dental source has been actively excluded when the location or clinical behaviour is suggestive. Incorporating a routine step to consider dental origin, together with a defined referral route to oral medicine, can improve diagnostic accuracy, shorten time to treatment and preserve urgent dermatology capacity for true skin malignancy.
