Ion pairing enhances hydroquinone stability toward oxygen in aqueous electrochemical carbon dioxide capture
Authors: Abdulrahman M Alfaraidi, Nina Ni, Jordan Sosa, Sara Lia, Mayar Alhelaili, Nathan Faialaga, Nawal M Alghoraibi, Husain H Al Naji, Ammar H Alahmed, Aqil Jamal, Michael J Aziz, Richard Y Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of redox-active organic molecules for aqueous electrochemical carbon dioxide capture is limited by their tendency to undergo reversible oxidation by oxygen. Here we show that a naphthoquinone derivative, when reduced in the presence of tetraalkylammonium countercations, displays enhanced stability toward oxygen while maintaining carbon dioxide binding ability. By combining structural modification with control of non-covalent interactions, we mitigate a previously observed trade-off between carbon dioxide capture performance and resistance to aerobic oxidation. In situ spectrophotometry and comparative voltammetry indicate that ion pairing stabilizes the reduced quinone both by shifting its redox potential and by promoting carbon dioxide adduct formation. Among the cations tested, tetraethylammonium provides the most favorable balance, supporting efficient capture and release cycle with 87 % Coulombic efficiency and an energy cost of 157 kilojoules per mole of carbon dioxide from a gas mixture containing carbon dioxide, oxygen, and nitrogen. These findings illustrate how molecular design combined with electrolyte engineering can improve the durability of aqueous quinone-based electrochemical carbon capture systems and may inform the development of more robust and energy-efficient approaches for sustainable carbon management.Subject terms: Carbon capture and storage, Materials for energy and catalysis, Organic chemistry

Introduction

The development of efficient, low-cost, and scalable technologies for capturing CO2 is a critical challenge for mitigating climate change while meeting global energy demand1–4. In response, extensive chemical research has focused on the design of systems for carbon capture and storage from emission sources5,6, as well as for direct air capture7,8 from the atmosphere. Compared to conventional CO2 capture processes, which are driven by thermal solvent regeneration9,10, electrochemical alternatives may be considerably less energy-intensive and interface naturally with emissions-free electrical energy sources whose costs continue to drop. Such approaches can operate at ambient temperature using small voltage swings, among a variety of other reported advantages11–13.

A promising class of electrochemical approaches employs redox-active organic molecules (RAOMs) to facilitate CO2 capture and concentration through either a pH-swing or an adduct-formation (nucleophilicity-swing) mechanism14–18. These methods feature low energy requirements per unit of CO2 captured19,20, and are expected to exhibit robust process stability, particularly in implementations similar to aqueous organic redox flow battery systems that are currently being commercialized. Despite these advantages, the instability of the reduced states of known RAOMs due to reversible chemical oxidation by atmospheric O2 is a significant remaining barrier to the deployment of this technology.

In organic solvents, important efforts to address the oxygen sensitivity of RAOM systems have been described21–24. Although these studies provide valuable insights into strategies for inhibiting oxidation, the use of organic solvents in electrochemical flow systems presents serious practical challenges. These include toxicity, high costs, flammability, poor ionic conductivity, and high cell resistance, as well as issues with membrane compatibility that can lead to system instability. These same drawbacks have hindered the advancement of non-aqueous redox flow batteries toward practical application. In contrast, RAOMs in aqueous environments can exhibit excellent electrochemical performance and stability at the molecular and system level, with temporal fade rates reaching below 0.01% per day at high solubilities exceeding 1 M25–27. Additionally, water as a solvent is non-flammable, environmentally friendly, inexpensive, and high in ionic conductivity, making it an ideal choice for scalable redox flow systems.

The development of RAOMs in aqueous flow systems for CO2 capture remains difficult, mainly due to the challenge of simultaneously meeting the minimum performance criteria for the process, which include solubility, electrochemical cycling stability, high electron utilization for CO2 capture, and oxygen stability. Further, the oxygen reactivity of RAOMs in aqueous environment remains a general limitation. In contrast to aprotic organic solvents, where oxygen reduction occurs at a relatively low redox potential of ∼ −1.3 V vs. Fc/Fc+, the reduction of oxygen in water can proceed through various pathways at significantly higher potentials than the reduction potentials of most reported RAOMs suitable for CO2 capture.

Quinones are a particularly promising class of aqueous RAOMs due to their structural tunability and demonstrated stability and solubility in aqueous energy storage systems. Recently, a water-soluble anthraquinone, 1,5-BTMAPAQ, has been utilized for efficient CO2 capture and concentration through a combination of nucleophilicity- and pH-swing mechanisms28. In its reduced form, this electron-rich quinone is prone to rapid aerobic oxidation, however, resulting in a Coulombic efficiency of only 50% in the presence of 3% O2 during a complete CO2 capture cycle (Fig. 1a). Although raising the redox potential of the dianion cuts the driving force for aerobic oxidation, this modification also decreases the binding constant for CO229,30, so tuning by substituent effects alone has not been sufficient to solve the problem.

Here, we report that cationic additives can stabilize the reduced form of a designed quinone and encourage its binding to CO2. Specifically, we describe a naphthoquinone (NQ) derivative with a single cationic chain and higher redox potential than that of 1,5-BTMAPAQ. Our findings indicate that the reduced form of this NQ, when bound to CO2, can form tight ion pairs with cationic ammonium salts, resulting in an increased CO2 binding constant while maintaining high redox potential. Notably, these combined effects significantly enhance stability against O2 during electrochemical capture-release cycles (Fig. 1b). We evaluate several performance indicators of this synthesized quinone for aqueous CO2 capture and utilize in situ spectrophotometry with ancillary characterization techniques to explore this ion-pairing strategy.

Results

For this study, we designed a redox-active organic molecule with the goal of effective pairing with an external cation, keeping in mind known principles for performance in aqueous electrochemical CO2 capture. We hypothesized that the NQ core, which has one less aromatic ring than well-explored anthraquinones, might be more resistant to aerobic oxidation. The properties of NQs as they relate to electrochemical carbon capture are essentially undescribed in the literature, providing an opportunity for valuable fundamental insight. It is known that the 2- and 3-positions of NQs are vulnerable to Michael addition by nucleophiles, including hydroxide, during electrochemical cycling31,32. We selected a 2,3-diamino functionalized NQ core, aiming to circumvent these issues. Further, the selective monoalkylation of these ortho-diamines might be possible due to the preorganization of one amine to act as an internal base during the first alkylation—this would provide a convenient way to attach a single cationic water-solubilizing chain. In the fully reduced form, the molecule would therefore still retain a net –1 charge (the R side chain includes a cationic ammonium group), setting the stage for specific ion-pairing interactions with an external cation.

Figure 2a illustrates the synthesis of TMAP-NH2-NQ, beginning with nucleophilic substitution of 2,3-dichloronaphthoquinone with potassium phthalimide. Following deprotection with hydrazine hydrate, the dark purple 2,3-diaminonaphthoquinone was selectively monoalkylated with 3-Bromo-N,N,N-trimethylpropan-1-aminium bromide (TMAP-Br) in acetonitrile at 90 °C for 14 h, yielding the yellow salt TMAP-NH2-NQ. The solubility of the oxidized form of TMAP-NH2-NQ was greater than 0.5 M in deionized water at pH 7 (Supplementary Fig. 4) and remained stable as the pH increased to 12. Similarly, the solubility of the reduced form was maintained across the anticipated working pH range (pH 6–12) and under a CO2 atmosphere. In later experiments conducted under CO2, we confirmed that the solubility is maintained after reduction and formation of CO2 adducts at the relevant pH conditions.

TMAP-NH2-NQ demonstrates highly reversible redox as assessed by cyclic voltammetry (CV) in different pH environments (Fig. 2b). TMAP-NH2-NQ has a potential of −0.317 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) in 1 M KCl (Fig. 2d). In general, higher potentials are expected to lower the thermodynamic driving force for oxidation, and so, TMAP-NH2-NQ should have advantages over previously reported 1,5-BTMAPAQ (−0.465 V vs. SHE). The Pourbaix diagram (Fig. 2c) reveals that the molecule predominantly undergoes a two-proton/two-electron redox process below pH 9.6, a one-proton/two-electron process between pH 9.6 and 11.2, and a zero-proton redox process beyond pH 11.2. Above pH 12 we observed a loss of reversibility, potentially due to decomposition involving the amine group (Supplementary Fig. 7). The pKa values are important for the capture capability of proton-coupled organic redox systems, as they define the maximum pH achievable during reduction. Electrochemical cycling of 0.1 M TMAP-NH2-NQ in 1 M KCl under an N2 atmosphere for 10 days reveals that this molecule has a capacity fade rate of 1.6%/day (Supplementary Fig. 5); furthermore, the pH profile during cycling is consistent with the measured pKa (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Aqueous hydroquinones capture CO2 not only through a pH swing mechanism, in which CO2 is converted to bicarbonate ions, but also through direct binding to CO2 molecules via the two phenoxide oxygens. Theoretically, the maximum capture capacity is 2 molecules of CO2 per quinone. When quinone is reduced to 100% state of charge (SOC) under CO2, major species include hydroquinone (H2Q), a CO2 adduct (Q(CO2)22−), and the dianion (Q2-), which can exist in an equilibrium that depends on pH, total quinone concentration, and CO2 partial pressure. A key quantity is the binding constant (KCO2) for the interaction between the quinone and CO2. As shown in Fig. 2d, CV data under nitrogen and CO2 conditions reveal that KCO2 for TMAP-NH2-NQ is 1.1×109M−2 (see Supplementary section 5 for binding constant evaluation). However, increasing the redox potential to diminish reactivity with O2 may also diminish CO2 affinity, counteracting some of the beneficial effect.

Encouraged by the attractive electrochemical properties of TMAP-NH2-NQ, its performance for CO2 capture in a continuous-flow cell was evaluated using a CO2/N2 mixture, with CO2 released into the same atmosphere as that from which it was captured. The inlet CO2 pressure ranged from 0.5 bar to 0.03 bar. The active electrolyte consisted of 0.1 M TMAP-NH2-NQ, cycled against (ferrocenylmethyl)trimethylammonium chloride (FcNCl) in 1 M KCl, separated by an anion-exchange membrane. The system was operated using a constant current followed by a constant voltage (CCCV) protocol to maximize the utilization of TMAP-NH2-NQ capacity during both the capture and release stages. A wait time of ~1.5 h was implemented between the charge/capture and discharge/release phases to effectively equilibrate the gas and liquid phases during CO2 absorption and outgassing. The performance profile under 10% CO2/90% N2 conditions over 5 cycles is presented in Fig. 3. We confirmed that the electrochemical reduction and oxidation of TMAP-NH2-NQ were associated with CO2 capture and release, as evidenced by the oscillations in downstream CO2 partial pressure and pH variations within the TMAP-NH2-NQ electrolyte (Fig. 2c, d).

The electron utilization, defined as the moles of CO2 captured per mole of electrons accessed during reduction, across five cycles is depicted in Fig. 3e, showing near-unity efficiency. This indicates that 2 moles of CO2 are captured per mole of TMAP-NH2-NQ, consistent with theoretical expectations based on the combined mechanisms of pH swing and adduct formation. Figure 3e also shows the release-to-capture ratio, averaging ~93% over the five cycles. This value matches well with the measured Coulombic efficiency shown in Fig. 3f, further supporting the idea that electrochemical triggering governs the capture and release processes. The slight decrease of both metrics from unity is most likely due to trace oxygen that lowers the CE. The energetic cost associated with the capture and release process over these five cycles was around 60 kJ/mol CO2. Good performance for TMAP-NH2-NQ was also observed under CO2 pressures of 0.5, 0.2, and 0.03 bar, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 13–15.

To characterize the performance of TMAP-NH2-NQ in the presence of O2, we integrated an ultra-short-pathlength in situ UV-Vis spectrophotometry apparatus into the redox flow capture system, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. This module enables direct observation and tracking of the organic species throughout the process, facilitating comparisons of different operating environments and the associated oxidation behavior. First, we identified for reference the absorption profiles of the fully oxidized TMAP-NH2-NQ (0% SOC) and its fully reduced form (100% SOC) by recording their UV-Vis spectra during electrochemical reduction and oxidation under an N2 atmosphere (Fig. 4b). Next, we aimed to characterize the oxidation behavior of TMAP-NH2-NQ under a 3% O2/97% N2 mixture and under a 3% O2/10% CO2/87% N2 mixture. To do so, the oxidized species in 1 M KCl was tracked using its absorption at 530 nm during electrochemical reduction followed by 1 h of rest and subsequent electrochemical oxidation. The results shown in Fig. 4c, d indicate that in the presence of CO2, TMAP-NH2-NQ exhibits higher oxidation resistance compared to conditions where only O2 is present. This increased tolerance is likely due to the binding of CO2, which drives the equilibrium toward an oxidation-resistant adduct and away from the oxidation-susceptible reduced dianion form. The data also indicates that reaction with O2 does not decompose the organic molecule in some irreversible way, but simply oxidizes it back to 0% SOC (Fig. 4c). Though such a process is still undesirable, as it wastes energy and shifts the cell out of balance, it is preferable to permanent electrolyte destruction. Electrochemically stable RAOMs can enable electrochemical rebalancing to restore electrolyte composition19. In terms of energy cost, the technique can be rationalized only if it can be used infrequently between multiple cycles of capture and concentration of CO2.

We conducted further investigations into how CO2 partial pressure and pH influence oxidation in 1 M KCl electrolyte solutions. Having confirmed that O2 oxidizes TMAP-NH2-NQ to its oxidized form without causing decomposition within the cycle, we used coulombic efficiency as a key metric to compare different experimental conditions. As shown in Fig. 5a, increasing CO2 partial pressure enhances coulombic efficiency in the presence of a fixed 3% O2, supporting our hypothesis that CO2-adduct formation slows oxidation. Similarly, the effect of pH on oxidation (Fig. 5b) reveals that lower pH enables higher coulombic efficiency. This is likely due to the domination of the protonated form, which is more stable in the presence of oxygen, at near neutral pH. This agrees with previously reported studies that revealed that rates of aerobic oxidation of hydroquinones are highly pH-dependent, increasing significantly with rising pH33,34. These findings suggest that the electron-rich dianion is especially prone to rapid reactions with O2, as its concentration increases with higher pH and decreases with higher CO2 partial pressure. Therefore, by shifting the equilibrium toward protonated forms and CO2 adducts, the oxidation rate can be effectively suppressed.

We hypothesized that tuning of the secondary coordination sphere through ion pairing may be a promising and underexplored strategy to further stabilize reduced TMAP-NH2-NQ and its CO2 adduct against oxidation. Given the diffuse negative charge density of reduced TMAP-NH2-NQ, we focused our study on quaternary ammonium countercations as additives in the form of their chloride salts. These compounds are inexpensive, commercially available, and highly soluble in water, making them ideal candidates as both supporting salts and ion-pairing agents.

The results of our investigation with four commonly available chloride salts containing ammoniums of differing sizes are summarized in Fig. 6. In situ UV-Vis studies were conducted following a procedure identical to that used in Fig. 4c, under conditions of 3% O2, 10% CO2, and 87% N2. Intriguingly, as shown in Fig. 6b, the use of each of the four ammonium salts enabled improved oxygen tolerance of TMAP-NH2-NQ compared to experiments using KCl (Fig. 4c). Among them, tetraethylammonium chloride (TEAC) resulted in the highest coulombic efficiency of ~86% with 1 h of gas exposure. CV analysis of TMAP-NH2-NQ in 1 M TEAC under N2 and CO2 shows positive shift of the oxidation peak of ~40 mV (Supplementary Fig. 16) and ~100 mV (Fig. 6d), respectively. The ~40 mV shift is consistent with a significant attractive interaction that develops between the reduced form of TMAP-NH2-NQ and the ammonium cation, thus increasing the energy required for oxidation and hence oxygen tolerance. Critically, this stabilization was more pronounced in the presence of CO2 (~100 mV), which implies that ammonium pairing results in a net increase of CO2 binding energy. This behavior is illustrated in thermodynamic terms in Fig. 7a, b, which highlights the enhancement of K’CO2 through ion pairing, yielding a new value of 1.5×1010M−2, which is an order of magnitude larger than that measured with potassium countercations. This stabilization is also evident in the voltage–capacity profile of a full cell (Supplementary Fig. 26), where the discharge plateau corresponding to the CO2 adduct shifts to a higher oxidative potential when using 1 M TEAC compared to 1 M KCl. While this enhanced stabilization improves oxygen resistance, it comes with a modest increase in electrical energy input.

In addition to the ion-pair effect, we noted a retardation of the interphase mass transport of gaseous molecules into the aqueous electrolyte phase caused by the presence of ammonium salts. This may have beneficially slowed the oxidation reaction by impeding the kinetics of O2 dissolution, but it also led to a decrease in electron utilization compared to in 1 M KCl (Fig. 3e). This decline in electron utilization was directly correlated with the size of the ammonium salt, as shown in Fig. 6c. To characterize the effect of ammonium salts on mass transport, we experimentally estimated the mass transfer coefficient (kLa) of both O2 and CO2. The mass transfer coefficient is a parameter that quantifies the rate at which a gaseous compound reaches its equilibrium distribution between a gas phase and solution phase. O2 has low solubility in water, resulting in mass transport being primarily limited by the liquid phase. We measured the interphase mass-transfer coefficient (kLa) for O2 using a dissolved oxygen probe following the static gassing-out method35. Our findings indicate that the presence of ammonium salts significantly reduces kLa values, with the magnitude of the decrease correlating with the size of the ammonium salts (see Supplementary Fig. 18). For example, in the presence of TEAC, the mass transport coefficient was approximately 30% lower than that observed in KCl.

Similarly, we assessed kLa for CO2 by fitting a model proposed by Hill, Kordač, and Linek, which utilizes the temporal pH profile during CO2 absorption to estimate the transient concentration of dissolved CO236,37. Our results revealed that kLa in the presence of TEAC (8.16×10−4s−1) was notably lower than that observed with KCl (1.97×10−3s−1) as the supporting salt. This explains the reduced electron utilization observed in the presence of TEAC and opens avenues for optimizing the trade-off between Coulombic efficiency and electron utilization. The use of cationic agents or surfactants that can act as liquid membrane to selectively hinder O2 diffusion into the aqueous phase while facilitating CO2 passage may be one promising avenue for future development.

Our results highlight the critical role of mass transport and reaction kinetics in governing the behavior of this gas–liquid electrochemical CO2 capture system. Following the electrochemical reduction of TMAP-NH₂-NQ, the solution is predominantly (>90%) composed of the highly oxygen-sensitive dianion species. Both CO2 and O2 must diffuse from the gas phase into the aqueous solution, with rates dictated by their respective partial pressures, Henry’s constants, and mass transport coefficients. To achieve high Coulombic efficiency, it is essential that either CO2 transport outpaces O2 transport, or that the CO2 capture reaction occurs faster than the oxidation reaction. This ensures that the system rapidly shifts toward formation of the protonated species (NQH₂) and the CO2 adduct, both of which exhibit significantly enhanced oxygen stability, as demonstrated by our analysis.

To quantify the capture kinetics, we employed a kinetic model fitted to CV data to extract the chemical rate constant (kc) for the reaction between reduced TMAP-NH₂-NQ and CO2 (Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Fig. 11). TMAP-NH₂-NQ exhibited a kc of 19.3 s⁻¹, over three times that of the more electron-rich 1,5-BTMAP-AQ38. This higher reaction rate, together with stabilization of the reduced species and the mass transport effects of ammonium salts, provides a pathway to improve oxygen tolerance in aqueous electrochemical CO2 capture systems.

Finally, we tested a 0.1 M solution of TMAP-NH₂-NQ in 1 M TEAC electrolyte in a full flow cell for an entire capture and release cycle, with capture from a gas mixture containing 10% CO2 and 3% O2 and release into pure CO2 (Fig. 7c). This cycle, conducted at 40 mA cm⁻² with 3 h of exposure to oxygen during capture, achieved 87% Coulombic efficiency and an 82% release-to-capture ratio at an energy cost of 157 kJ/mol CO2. The electron utilization for this cycle was about 64.5%. Overall, these results indicate that molecular-level modification combined with electrolyte engineering can help address the oxygen sensitivity of RAOMs in aqueous environments and improve the practicality of electrochemical carbon dioxide capture.

Discussion

The continued development of aqueous electrochemical carbon dioxide capture systems employing RAOMs must address their susceptibility to aerobic oxidation, which currently reduces efficiency and long-term stability. Our study shows that interactions between the designed NQ TMAP-NH₂-NQ and quaternary ammonium cations can help mitigate oxygen sensitivity in aqueous electrochemical carbon dioxide capture. TEAC simultaneously (i) increases the redox potential of the reduced quinone, (ii) increases the equilibrium carbon dioxide binding constant, and (iii) slows oxygen mass transfer into the electrolyte. These combined effects shift solution speciation toward the protonated hydroquinone and the carbon dioxide adduct—both of which are more resistant to oxygen. This combination enabled about 87% Coulombic efficiency during cycling in gas mixtures containing 3% oxygen. By integrating molecular design of the redox-active quinone with electrolyte modification, we provide an approach to addressing oxygen sensitivity in aqueous systems and suggest design principles that may be applicable to future redox-active organic molecule–based capture technologies.

Methods

Materials

Unless noted, all chemicals were obtained from commercial sources (MilliporeSigma, Combi-Blocks, Oakwood, Alfa Aesar, TCI America, or Ambeed) at reagent grade, stored at rt under nitrogen, and used without further purification. pH measurements were performed on an Oakton pH 700 Benchtop Meter with a Mettler Toledo Micro pH Electrode LE422. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectral data were obtained on an Agilent Cary 60 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer. (Ferrocenylmethyl)trimethylammonium Chloride (FcNCl) was purchased from TCI America chemical company. Tetramethyl ammonium chloride, tetraethyl ammonium chloride (TEAC), tetrabutyl ammonium chloride (TBAC), and benzyltributylammonium chloride (BTBAC) were purchased from MilliporeSigma. All those chemicals were directly used without further purification.

Synthesis of ((3-Amino-1,4-dioxo-1,4-dihydronaphthalen−2-yl)amino)-N,N,N-trimethylpropan-1-aminium bromide (TMAP-NH2-NQ)

2,3-Diamino-1,4-naphthoquinone was synthesized according to a literature procedure39. A 350 mL pressure flask was charged with 2,3-diamino-1,4-naphthoquinone (4.70 g, 25.0 mmol, 1.0 equiv), Cl– Me3N+(CH2)3Br (26.10 g, 100.0 mmol, 4.0 equiv), and CH3CN (100 mL). The flask was sealed with a PTFE threaded cap, heated at 90 °C for 14 h, and then allowed to cool to rt. The reaction mixture was diluted with 150 mL of EtOAc, filtered under vacuum, and the black solids were washed with 2 × 50 mL portions of EtOAc and 2 × 100 mL portions of EtOH. The resulting dark brown solids were dissolved in water (ca. 150 mL) and the solution passed through PTFE syringe filters (0.45 μm) to remove undissolved solid impurities. The aqueous solution was concentrated under vacuum and dried by azeotropic distillation with benzene (3 × 10 mL portions). The brown residue was washed with EtOAc (3 × 10 mL portions) and EtOH (3 × 10 mL portions) to afford 6.23 g (55%) of TMAP-NH2-NQ·HBr as a light brown solid. TMAP-NH2-NQ·HBr was dissolved in water and transferred to an anion-exchange resin column prepared in advance to replace bromide with chloride. After passing through the column, the solution was concentrated in vacuo to remove water and the solid collected. Before conducting any electrochemical experiment, TMAP-NH2-NQ·HCl was dissolved and neutralized to near pH 7 using 1 M KOH.

Electrochemical characterization

For all three-electrode CV measurements, a 3 mm diameter glassy carbon working electrode (BASi MF-2012), an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (BASi MF-2052, pre-soaked in 3 M KCl solution), and a graphite counter electrode were used. All CV, linear sweep voltammetry, and chronoamperometry measurements were performed using Gamry Instruments and CHI Instrument electrochemical analyzers.

Flow cell setup

The flow cell apparatus was constructed using cell hardware from Fuel Cell Tech (Albuquerque, NM). The flow cell was assembled in a zero-gap configuration, utilizing pyrosealed POCO graphite flow plates with identical interdigitated flow fields. Each electrode consisted of a single layer of AvCarb HCBA carbon cloth as purchased, with a geometric surface area of 5 cm². The outer portion of the space between the electrodes was gasketed by Viton sheets with the area over the electrodes cut out. Selemion DSV-N was employed as the anion exchange membrane. Electrochemical measurements were conducted using a Biologic SP-150e and Gamry Reference 3000 potentiostat. KNF diaphragm pumps were used to circulate electrolytes through the flow fields and electrodes within the cell stack. A Cole-Parmer Digital gear pump was used for some tests.

Electrochemical CO2 capture

The flow cells were charged at a constant current, followed by voltage holds until the current returned to background levels. The cells were then placed in rest mode for a specific duration to allow for carbon capture. For CO2 release, the cells were discharged at constant current, followed by a voltage hold, after which the battery was set in rest mode to complete the release process. FS4001 MEMS Mass Flow Sensor, LuminOX O2 sensor (CM-42990), and SprintIR CO2 sensor (GC-0018) were used during the tests. A drierite drying tube (Cole Parmer) was placed in between the sensors and the negolyte chamber to reduce the humidity level of the gas. A feed gas mixture of N2, CO2, and O2 was continuously supplied to the electrolyte flask through a tube placed 3 cm above the electrolyte surface, maintaining pre-set partial pressures. The total pressure was 1 bar, and the total gas flow rate was set between 12−25 mL/min. The active electrolyte for capture was held in 20 mL scintillation vial, that has gas-liquid interfacial surface area of 5 cm2. All tests were conducted at room temperature at 23 °C.

For experiment in Fig. 3: A flow cell comprises 7 mL of 0.1 M TMAP-NH2-NQ in 1 M KCl and 40 mL of 0.2 M FcNCl in 1 M KCl. Selemion DSV-N was used as the anion exchange membrane. Electrolyte flow rate was set at 30 mL/min. The total gas flow rate was set at 15 mL/min. The cell was cycled with a constant current (40 mA cm−2) followed by constant voltage of 1 V (charging) and 0.4 V (discharging) at rt. The rest times after charging and discharging are ~1.5 h and ~1 h, respectively. A similar procedure was used for experiments in Supplementary Fig. 9.

For experiment in Fig. 7c: A flow cell comprises 6 mL of 0.1 M TMAP-NH2-NQ in 1 M TEAC and 40 mL of 0.2 M FcNCl in 1 M TEAC. Selemion DSV-N was used as the anion exchange membrane. Electrolyte flow rate was set at 20 mL/min. The total gas flow rate was set at 12 mL/min. The cell was cycled with a constant current (40 mA cm−2) followed by constant voltage of 1.3 V (charging) and 0.05 V (discharging) at rt. The rest times after charging and discharging are ~3 h and ~1.5 h, respectively.
