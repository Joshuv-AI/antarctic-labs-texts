Reply to: mRNA COVID-19 vaccinations are not associated with RVO development 21 days and 12 weeks after vaccination
Authors: Jing-Xing Li, Yu-Hsun Wang, Henry Bair, Shu-Bai Hsu, Connie Chen, James Cheng-Chung Wei, Chun-Ju Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We appreciate your interest in our study. This study contrasts with Dorney et al.‘s focus on the first 21 days. We conducted a re-analysis regarding selection bias and found consistent results. Our research highlights the elevated risk of retinal vascular occlusion after vaccination, which can last up to two years. We also discussed the differences in study design and the effects of different vaccine brands.

We are grateful for your interest in our research and your efforts to highlight the similarities and differences between our findings. Dorney et al.1 primarily focused on 21 days after the initial COVID-19 vaccination. In contrast, our study examined retinal vascular occlusion development at 12 weeks and two years after vaccination.

The concern regarding selection bias and uncontrolled confounders in our study has been duly acknowledged. Before enrollment, we excluded individuals on medications that could impact blood osmolality and coagulation profile. In response to your concern, we conducted a re-analysis without restrictions on these drugs. The results of the subgroup analysis are presented in Table 1. Compared to the original results, we observed a similar trend of retinal vascular occlusion at 12 weeks and two years following COVID-19 vaccination. Shaia et al. express uncertainty regarding the timeframe of retinal vascular development after vaccination. However, our research, as depicted in Figure 5 from ref. 2, unequivocally illustrates a significant escalation in the risk of all subtypes of retinal vascular occlusion, particularly branch retinal vascular occlusion. Additionally, Supplementary Table 3 from ref. 2 provides evidence of relatively higher hazards within 3 to 45 days following vaccination. We found that the risk of retinal vascular occlusion was prominent after vaccination and lasted 12 weeks and two years after vaccination.

The lack of a significant association observed in the subgroup analysis based on the vaccine brand can be attributed to the fact that most vaccines used were generic (66.2%). Patients who received generic brand vaccines were not included in the subgroup analysis. The substantial reduction in case numbers and the mitigation of the risk effect led to the absence of the association in the subgroup analysis.

Ophthalmic manifestations of COVID-19 vaccination may be acute, delayed, or prolonged, lasting weeks to months3. A Danish national vaccine trial, ENFORCE3, prospectively investigated the effects of COVID-19 vaccines on inflammatory cytokines with a 2-year follow-up. The levels of CRP and IL-6 were still elevated in the group that received the mRNA vaccine4. The second exposure to the S protein during the second dose of the COVID-19 vaccine may stimulate a stronger immune response, potentially damaging the endothelial cells of the microvessels in the retina and facilitating the formation of blood clots. Examining the ocular adverse effects of COVID-19 vaccination up to 2 years post-vaccination is reasonable.

We commend Dorney et al.1 for their commendable efforts in utilizing influenza and Tdap vaccinations in 2018 and 2019 as a control group, effectively eliminating SARS-CoV-2 infection as a potential confounding factor. Their study design was distinct from ours, but both have advantages and disadvantages.

In conclusion, we would like to express our gratitude to Shaia et al. for giving us the opportunity to explain our study’s objectives and methodology in more detail. Furthermore, we would like to correct the error in Table 3 from ref. 2 regarding the misplacement of incidence rates for the 2-year and 12-week periods.
