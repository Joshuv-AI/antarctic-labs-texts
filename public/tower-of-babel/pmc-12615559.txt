Heatwave exposure and sarcopenia progression among middle-aged and older adults: the longitudinal evidence from CHARLS
Authors: Jie Wang, Chang Liu, Xiangyun Li, Xiuzhu Cao, Leilei Qiu, Duanbin Li, Linfang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith global climate change, heatwaves are intensifying in frequency and duration, adversely affecting human health. How heatwaves (HW) exposure contribute to the decline in muscle strength and sarcopenia progression among middle-aged and older adults remains unclear.MethodsUtilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) from 2011 to 2015, low muscle strength and sarcopenia were defined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 consensus. HW exposure were defined based on temperature thresholds and durations, quantified by event and day counts. HW exposure was measured over 12 months prior to sarcopenia assessments at baseline (2011) and follow-up (2015). Introduction

Sarcopenia, a progressive disease affecting skeletal muscles, is marked by a loss of muscle strength and mass, as well as physical performance, which heightens the risk of negative outcomes such as falls, fractures, and mortality, thereby imposing considerable social and economic burdens [1, 2]. The Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 [3] has highlighted sarcopenia as a significant global public health challenge. In an effort to enhance early detection and intervention, this group introduced the concept of ‘possible sarcopenia,’ characterized by reduced muscle strength and/or physical performance decline [3]. Studies have demonstrated that low muscle strength and the condition of possible sarcopenia are associated with an increased risk of various adverse health outcomes, such as cognitive impairment [4], elevated cancer incidence [5], and 1-year mortality risk [6]. Identifying and addressing preventable risk factors could potentially delay or reverse the progression of sarcopenia [7]. While previous research has investigated the role of environmental factors like air pollution in the deterioration of sarcopenia status [8, 9], the impact of other environmental stressors, such as heatwaves, remains underexplored.

Under the climate crisis, the effects of heat exposure on sarcopenia have garnered increasing attention. Evidence suggests that heat-induced daily temperature fluctuations impair mitochondrial function and lead to muscle atrophy by altering the gut microbiota [10]. Another study demonstrated that sustained 37 °C exposure over 14 days in a murine model induced skeletal muscle volume loss, leading to reduced muscle strength and function [11]. In addition, mechanisms linking heat exposure to sarcopenia include oxidative stress [12]. Relevant study involving small cohorts of human volunteers further indicate that heat exposure diminishes maximal arm and leg strength in young adults [13]. Overall, existing research predominantly focuses on single temperature metrics as proxies for heat exposure, with limited attention to its impact on vulnerable populations, such as middle-aged and older adults. As climate change intensifies, the frequency and severity of heatwaves (HW) have increased and this trend is expected to persist [14]. HW exposure was defined based on combinations of temperature thresholds and durations, and was quantified by event and day counts, as described in previous studies [14, 15]. The heightened risks are particularly pronounced among middle-aged and older adults, who exhibit greater sensitivity to environmental stressors [16, 17]. With global warming, numerous aging-related degenerative diseases have been linked to HW exposure, including cognitive impairment in the elderly [18], cardiovascular dysregulation (e.g., increased blood pressure, accelerated heart rate, elevated blood viscosity, and myocardial damage) [19], and elevated stroke mortality [20]. The potential association between HW exposure and sarcopenia progression becomes increasingly relevant, direct studies in this area remains largely unexplored.

As heatwaves become increasingly frequent and severe, it is crucial to understand the potential impact of HW on muscle strength decline, sarcopenia deterioration to inform effective prevention strategies and public health policies that protect vulnerable populations.

Materials and methods

Study population

This population-based study leveraged data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey that compiles comprehensive data on health, economic, and social well-being. CHARLS focuses on individuals aged 45 and older, encompassing 150 counties and 450 villages across China. Initiated in June 2011, the CHARLS baseline survey was followed by subsequent waves in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Comprehensive details regarding the CHARLS project have been detailed elsewhere [21]. For our analysis, we selected the 2011 CHARLS data as the baseline and the 2015 data for follow-up to gather comprehensive information necessary for a full assessment of sarcopenia. Eligibility criteria included: (1) participants interviewed in both 2011 (Wave 1) and 2015 (Wave 3), (2) individuals classified as middle-aged and older (45 years and above), (3) availability of complete sarcopenia-related data, including height, weight, grip strength, and physical performance metrics, (4) presence of essential independent variable data. Participants were excluded if meteorological data for HW assessment were unavailable. In total, 3,554 participants fulfilled the criteria, as depicted in Fig. 1A. The CHARLS study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University, with the National Development Research Institute at Peking University obtaining written informed consent from all participants involved.

Assessment of heatwave exposure

Meteorological data from the National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) were utilized to ascertain HW exposure at the city level. Assuming that participants’ residences were relatively stable, we conducted an assessment of HW exposure across 50 Chinese cities, with sample sizes from each city outlined in Table S1. The daily heat index (C), which represents the 24-hour average of the apparent temperature, was computed for each city using the ‘weathermetrics’ package in R [14]. In the absence of a standardized definition for HW, we defined HW exposure based on various temperature thresholds and durations, adhering to the guidelines from previous research [14]. For each city, HW thresholds were set at the 97.5th, 95th, 92.5th, and 90th percentiles of daily apparent temperature. HW events were classified as periods when the daily apparent temperature reached or surpassed these thresholds for at least 2, 3, or 4 consecutive days (e.g., P97.5 for at least 4 days, P95.0 for at least 3 days, P92.5 for at least 2 days). The notation P95_4d, for example, signifies a period when the daily apparent temperature was at or above the 95th percentile for at least four consecutive days. The day count refers to the cumulative duration of these HW events. Building on the framework established [22, 23], we defined 12 distinct HW scenarios (Table 1), employing a one-year exposure window to reflect HW exposure [15, 24]. HW exposure was quantified by tallying the number of HW days and events in the 12 months preceding both baseline and follow-up assessments for each participant. Employing the difference-in-differences (DiD) method, we constructed ΔHW to measure the variation in HW exposure between the baseline and follow-up periods. A schematic overview of the DiD method is presented in Fig. 1B.

Diagnosis of sarcopenia and measurement of muscle strength

Sarcopenia diagnosis requires a comprehensive assessment of physical performance, muscle strength, and muscle mass. Prior research has established that low muscle strength, as measured by grip strength using the YuejianTM WL-1000 dynamometer in kilograms by trained examiners, classifies male grip strength below 28.0 kg and female grip strength below 18.0 kg as low muscle strength [25–27]. Muscle mass is assessed through the anthropometric equation for height-adjusted muscle mass (ASM/Ht2), defined as follows: ASM/Ht2 = (0.193*weight + 0.107*height − 4.157*gender − 0.037*age − 2.631)/ height2. This equation aligns with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) predictions and accurately estimates muscle mass in the Chinese population [28]. Weight, height, and age are measured in kilograms, centimeters, and years, respectively. Gender is coded numerically, with males as 1 and females as 2. Low muscle mass was defined as an ASM/Ht2 value below the sex-specific lowest quintile of the study cohort [29], with thresholds set at < 7.13 kg/m² for men and < 5.46 kg/m² for women. For physical performance, gait speed and chair stand tests were conducted following the methods described by Wu et al. [26]. So low physical performance was defined as 5-CST ≥ 12.0 s or a gait speed of < 1.0 m/s [26]. Following previous studies [27, 30], low muscle strength and/or reduced physical performance are indicative of possible sarcopenia. Sarcopenia is diagnosed when low muscle mass is accompanied by low muscle strength and/or a decline in physical performance.

Employing the DiD method, we calculated the change in muscle strength, denoted as ΔMuscle strength, between baseline and follow-up assessments. ΔMuscle strength is considered both a continuous variable and a binary outcome (ΔMuscle strength ≥ 0 or < 0), where a positive value indicates an increase in muscle strength and a negative value indicates a decrease. Additionally, in line with the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 [3], sarcopenia status are categorized into three: non-sarcopenia, possible sarcopenia, and sarcopenia. Recognizing that sarcopenia can transition in both directions, we compared sarcopenia status between the first and second follow-ups and identified two types of transitions: (1) sarcopenia state recovery transitions; (2) Sarcopenia state deteriorate transitions. Examining these transitions provides a more comprehensive understanding of health outcomes and patterns of change among individuals across different sarcopenia status, facilitating the early identification of modifiable risk factors.

Covariates

Baseline characteristics, collected during Wave 1 in 2011, encompassed age, gender (male or female), Body Mass Index (BMI), and marital status (categorized as married, separated, divorced, widowed, never married, or cohabiting). Economic status was assessed based on whether participants received salary or bonus income in the past year, classified as ‘no income’ or ‘income received’. Education levels were classified as illiterate, literate but below primary school, or primary school and above. Geographical data included residence type (rural or urban) and region (northern or southern China), with the Qinling Line serving as the demarcation. Lifestyle factors evaluated included drinking frequency (never, less than once a month, or more than once a month), smoking status (never, former smoker, or current smoker), and difficulty in performing activities, which was measured by a 7-item scores assessing any difficulty across seven tasks: walking 100 m, climbing stairs, rising from a chair, bending/kneeling/squatting, reaching out, lifting 5 kg, and picking up a small coin. Health-related variables comprised self-reported chronic diseases, including diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cancer, and heart disease. A meta-analysis has demonstrated a strong link between depression and sarcopenia [31]. Depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CESD-10) scale, where participants rated experienced symptoms on a scale from 0 to 3, with a maximum score of 30, indicating more severe depression.

To account for changes in lifestyle and psychological health during the study period, we considered time-varying covariates between the 2011 baseline and the 2015 follow-up. These included changes in drinking habits (transitions from no to yes, yes to no, or remaining unchanged), smoking status (from no to yes, yes to no, or unchanged), and shifts in depressive symptoms, measured as a continuous variable.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize participant demographics, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges. The DiD method was utilized to evaluate the association between changes in heatwave exposure (ΔHW), changes in muscle strength (Δmuscle strength), and changes in sarcopenia status from baseline to follow-up, offering a more robust causality estimate than cross-sectional designs [32, 33]. Generalized linear models with a Gaussian family distribution were applied to estimate associations between HW days and events with muscle strength differences as continuous outcomes. For binary outcomes, including muscle strength differences and two types of sarcopenia status transitions—1) sarcopenia status recovery transitions (reference: Deterioration, Stable); and 2) sarcopenia status deterioration transitions (reference: Recovery, Stable)—we used generalized linear models with a binomial family distribution. To explore the dose-response relationship between HW exposure and sarcopenia status deterioration transitions, restricted cubic spline curves were applied across various HW definitions. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, geographical region, education level, marital status, salary, and place of residence. Model 2 included behavioral factors such as drinking, smoking, and difficulty in activities, along with self-reported hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and Body Mass Index, assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CESD-10) scale. Model 3 further adjusted for changes in smoking, drinking, and depressive symptoms over the study period. All models were also adjusted for baseline grip strength and sarcopenia status to account for their potential influence on changes. Model 3 served as the primary model for subsequent analyses.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between exposure to different categories of ΔHW and changes in sarcopenia status, considering factors such as age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years), gender (female or male), smoking status (current smokers vs. never smokers), drinking status (current drinkers vs. never drinkers), BMI (< 24 or ≥ 24 kg/m²), residence (rural or urban), geographical region (northern or southern China), and education level (primary school or higher). The significance of subgroup effects was assessed using interaction p-values. To ensure the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) categorizing BMI as < 24 kg/m2 or ≥ 24 kg/m2 based on Model 3; (2) excluding participants with low muscle strength and sarcopenia at baseline to more distinctly investigate the association between HW exposure and changes in muscle strength and sarcopenia status; (3) excluding and re-analyzing participants with muscle strength outliers (over 60 kg or less than 3 kg) to address potential anomalies; (4) employing random intercept linear mixed-effects models and generalized linear mixed models to capture regional variations in extreme temperatures, specifically among northern rural, southern rural, northern urban, and southern urban areas. All statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.4.1.

Associations between HW exposure and muscle strength

We utilized the DiD approach to evaluate the association between the cumulative event and daily counts of ΔHW and ΔMuscle strength, analyzing these as both continuous and categorical outcomes (Fig. 3and Tables S4, S5). These analyses were fully adjusted for baseline characteristics and time-varying covariates. In the continuous analysis of ΔMuscle strength, each additional HW events (defined as HW12: with a maximum temperature at or above the 97.5th percentile for at least four consecutive days) was associated with a decrease in muscle strength of 0.66 kg (95%CI: -0.933, -0.386, p < 0.001) (Table S4), and each additional HW12 day contributed to a decrease in muscle strength of 0.097 kg (95% CI: -0.124, -0.069, p < 0.001) (Table S4). In categorical analyses, each additional HW12 event increased the odds of experiencing a decrease in muscle strength (ΔMuscle strength < 0) by 13.0% (odds ratio: 1.130, 95% CI: 1.031, 1.237, p = 0.009) (Table S5), while each additional HW12 day increased the odds by 2.5% (odds ratio: 1.025, 95% CI: 1.015, 1.035, p < 0.001) (Table S5). The regression coefficients for heatwave exposure increased with stricter definitions, suggesting that the impact of more intense and prolonged HW on muscle strength is more substantial (Fig. 3).

Associations between HW exposure and sarcopenia progression

DiD approach was used to evaluate the association between the cumulative event and daily counts of ΔHW and changes in sarcopenia status, as depicted in Fig. 4and Tables S6 and S7. Our analysis, which incorporated a comprehensive adjustment for all covariates in model 3, revealed that each additional HW11 day (P97.5_3d) was associated with a 2.2% increased risk of sarcopenia status deterioration transitions (OR: 1.022, 95% CI: 1.012 to 1.033, p < 0.001) (Table S6), and a reduced likelihood of sarcopenia status recovery 1.7% (OR: 0.983, 95% CI: 0.968 to 0.999, p = 0.032) (Table S6). Each additional HW12 (P97.5_4d) event was associated with an 18.7% increased risk of sarcopenia status deterioration transitions (OR: 1.187, 95% CI: 1.07 to 1.316, p = 0.001) (Table S7), and a 14.1% decreased risk for sarcopenia status recovery (OR: 0.859, 95% CI: 0.739 to 0.999, p = 0.048) (Table S7). With increasing temperature thresholds and durations, the odds ratios for sarcopenia status progression incrementally increased, while those for sarcopenia status recovery progressively decreased, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Furthermore, the restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a positive dose-response relationship between the cumulative days (Fig. 5) and events (Figure S1) of HW exposure and the progression of sarcopenia status, underscoring the observed associations between HW exposure and the sarcopenia deterioration transition across varying definitions.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses revealed that the association between ΔHW and sarcopenia status deterioration transition was generally consistent across most subgroups, albeit with varying effect sizes (Table 4). Individuals with an educational attainment exceeding primary school demonstrated a more pronounced association with HW exposure, with the OR of 1.035 (95%CI: 1.019, 1.051), and this association was statistically significant with a P-value of 0.027. Urban dwellers displayed heightened sensitivity to HW exposure, as evidenced by an increased OR of 1.035 (95% CI: 1.018, 1.054, P = 0.026) for the number of HW days and an OR of 1.464 (95% CI: 1.206, 1.778, P = 0.006) for HW events. No significant interactions were observed with respect to age or gender; however, a slightly stronger association was noted among younger participants and males.

Sensitivity analyses

The robustness of our study findings was substantiated by several sensitivity analyses. These included adjustments for model categorization using Body Mass Index (BMI) (Figures S2 and S3), the exclusion of participants with low muscle strength and sarcopenia at baseline (Figures S8 to S11), and the elimination of outliers in muscle strength assessments (Figure S4). Accounting for regional variability, linear mixed-effects models affirmed the consistency of the association between ΔHW and Δmuscle strength, treated as a continuous variable, with our primary outcomes (Table S12). Additionally, generalized linear mixed models examining the categorical outcomes of muscle strength corroborated the consistency of our principal analysis (Table S13).

Discussion

This study enrolled 3,554 participants aged 45 and above from the CHARLS cohort to elucidate the significant link between HW exposure and the decline in muscle strength and sarcopenia progression risk. Our findings suggest that, amidst escalating global climate change, the heightened temperature thresholds and prolonged durations of HW are not only correlated with reduced muscle strength but also intimately tied to the exacerbation of sarcopenia status. Subgroup analyses revealed that urban-dwelling individuals and those with higher education levels are particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of HW exposure on sarcopenia status deterioration transitions.

To our best knowledge, the clinical and epidemiological research on the interplay between muscle strength, sarcopenia, and HW is scant. The nexus between sarcopenia, a pivotal geriatric condition, and heatwaves merits thorough investigation. With the progress of global warming, the prevalence and intensity of HW are expected to increase, and their harmful effects on health will expand. Our observations indicate that HW are associated with a decline in muscle strength and an increased risk of sarcopenia progression, which is consistent with previous studies highlighting the negative impact of heat waves on age-related degenerative diseases. For instance, Zhou et al. have shown that HW exposure significantly raises the incidence of hypertension among the elderly [34], as well as conditions such as stroke [35], cognitive impairment [18], and sleep disorders [15]. In our study, we employed a DiD design, providing stronger causal inference than cross-sectional studies. Employing grid-specific relative temperature thresholds and durations, our study comprehensively characterized HW exposure. The RCS analysis demonstrated a significant positive dose-response association between HW events and days and the sarcopenia status deterioration transitions, correlating with the intensity and duration of experienced HW. Muscle strength and sarcopenia status are subject to temporal fluctuations. A solitary evaluation of sarcopenia at baseline fails to capture the full extent of sarcopenia progression. Our research further expands upon previous studies, demonstrating how heatwaves may affect the potential transition patterns of sarcopenia. Our investigation spanned the transition of sarcopenia status from baseline to follow-up, encompassing the evolution from non-sarcopenia to possible sarcopenia, the escalation from possible sarcopenia to sarcopenia, and the direct transition from non-sarcopenia to sarcopenia. In 2019, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) introduced the concept of ‘possible sarcopenia,’ which has been increasingly recognized for its association with the risk of adverse health outcomes. Consequently, our study has heightened its focus on the potential transition patterns of possible sarcopenia, acknowledging the significance of these shifts in the context of geriatric health and the broader implications for public health interventions.

The potential mechanisms underlying the relationship between heatwaves and muscle and sarcopenia remain unclear. Studies have shown that exposure to extreme heat accelerates thermoregulatory processes, leading to fluid loss, dehydration, and potentially core body temperature increases when thermoregulation fails, triggering systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and direct cytotoxic effects [36], overlapping with sarcopenia’s biological mechanisms. Enhanced temperature fluctuations may exacerbate muscle atrophy by inhibiting mitochondrial function through gut microbiota [10], and with global warming, heatwaves and cold spells induce more intense temperature fluctuations [17], potentially worsening muscle atrophy. Our results indicate that as HW temperature thresholds and durations escalate, so does the risk of HW contributing to muscle strength decline, which is biologically plausible given that sarcopenia diagnosis includes the assessment of muscle strength. Additionally, multiple comorbidities, including depression [37], neurological diseases [18, 38], and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [20], which are also heatwave-induced, increase the risk of sarcopenia. Overall, heatwaves can affect muscle strength and sarcopenia through various pathways and comorbidities.

Subgroup analyses revealed that urban-dwelling individuals and those with higher education levels are particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of HW exposure on sarcopenia status deterioration transitions. Our analysis reveals that a higher proportion of urban residents (approximately 15.2%) experienced changes from non-sarcopenia to possible sarcopenia or sarcopenia compared to rural residents(Table S14). Urban residents typically face a higher risk of heat exposure during intense heatwave periods [15, 39]. Firstly, in urban settings, despite abundant infrastructure and medical resources, characteristics like the urban heat island effect, air pollution, and crowded living conditions heighten urban residents’ health risks during extreme climates. The urban heat island effect raises urban temperatures significantly above rural areas, diminishing natural heat alleviation. This aligns with Tao et al.‘s report of significantly higher diabetes mortality related to heatwaves in urban areas compared to rural areas [40]. Secondly, individuals with higher education levels often face sustained strain from greater occupational pressures and societal expectations [41, 42]. This aligns with the report by Shen et al. that individuals with higher education levels have a significantly higher risk of depression related to heatwave exposure [43]. This demographic often faces sustained strain and they may rely more on air conditioning and lose interest in physical activity closely related to health.

Our study has several limitations: (1) The CHARLS cohort study is observational, limiting our ability to establish a causal link between heatwave exposure and muscle strength and sarcopenia. (2) Although we adjusted for various confounding factors, residual confounding may persist, and the presence of household air conditioning and work style could affect the association between heatwaves and sarcopenia. (3) Information on participants’ chronic diseases is based on self-reported data, potentially underestimating prevalence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the association between HW exposure and decreased muscle strength and increased sarcopenia progression risk in middle-aged and older adults. Such investigations will be crucial for developing comprehensive public health strategies that protect vulnerable aging demographics from the adverse effects of climate change.
