Early warning signals in rumor models
Eva Rifà, Julian Vicens, Emanuele Cozzo

Abstract
We study the emission and control of a rumor using the modified Maki-Thomson model. A key challenge in social networks is distinguishing between natural increases in transmissibility and artificial injections of rumor spreaders, such as through broadcast events or astroturfing. Using stochastic simulations, we compare two scenarios: one with organic growth in transmissibility, and another with externally injected spreaders. Although both lead to high autocorrelation, only the organic growth produces oscillatory patterns in autocorrelation at multiple lags, an effect we can analytically explain using the N-intertwined mean-field (NIMFA) approximation. This distinction offers a practical tool to identify the origin of rumor virality and also infer its transmissibility. Our approach is validated analytically and tested on real-world data from Twitter during the announcement of the Higgs boson discovery. In addition to detection, we also explore control strategies. We show that the average lifetime of a rumor can be manipulated through targeted interventions: placing spreaders at specific locations in the network. Depending on their placement, these interventions can either extend or shorten the lifespan of the rumor.

early-warning signals, rumor spreading, complex networks, critical phenomena

1
Introduction

The way we interact and communicate is shaped by how information spreads online, a process that in recent years has been largely driven by the growing role of social media and online platforms in framing public discourse. Nevertheless, social networks are arguably the most useful, or at least the most accessible, source we have for studying social movements. For this reason, it is essential to examine the phenomena that occur within them, as they significantly influence our lives. It has long been recognized that as long as dominant social media platforms are owned by monopolistic companies whose primary goal is profit maximization, they cannot serve as spaces where free expression truly prevails or where everyone can have an equal voice
(
Proyecto UNA 2024
)
. Furthermore, these platforms have enabled mechanisms of persuasion in the battle over public opinion, including disinformation campaigns and the emergence of covert manipulation tactics such as astroturfing.

Astroturfing refers to the practice of simulation the appearance of grassroots movement through the mass mobilization of fake or automated accounts
(
Proyecto UNA 2024
)
. Although this practice predates the internet, its current scale, amplified by platforms like X (formerly Twitter), makes it cheaper, more global, and more difficult to detect than ever before. The Internet Research Agency’s campaigns during the 2016 U.S. presidential elections
(
Bail et al 2020
)
are a well-known example, but recent studies have documented similar operations in
South Korea
(
Keller et al 2020
)
and Thailand
(
Sirikupt 2024
)
, among others
(
Wang et al 2023
)
. These operations have implications ranging from aggressive marketing strategies to electoral manipulation. However, the availability of online social networks data enabled the development of empirical and robust detection techniques
(
Schoch et al 2022
)
. Although astroturfing remains difficult to detect
(
Arce-García et al 2023
)
, it is useful to focus on identifying coordinated behavior rather than specific content
(
Schoch et al 2022
)
, given that ground truth is rarely available
(
Keller et al 2020
)
.

As mentioned, astroturfing must be driven by the interests of economic or political power. When this is not the case, we can more generally speak of an external impulse to the system: a set of agents that introduce information in a coordinated manner steering the flow of communication. For example, the diffusion through traditional media (television, radio, streaming) can trigger similar dynamics without manipulative intent: simultaneous audience exposure generates spikes in participation that are subsequently re-amplified on social networks
(
Gelper et al 2024
;
Brannon and Roy 2024
)
. Analyzing both phenomena from a unified perspective allows us to explore common strategies to maximize (or minimize) the reach of a message. Nevertheless, only by understanding the surrounding circumstances we can distinguish between a legitimate broadcast and a manufactured campaign. In this context, it is important to identify the phenomenon of an external impulse and to develop tools for controlling rumor spreading within social systems.

One of the most extensively studied models for rumor spreading is the Maki-Thompson model
(
Maki and Thompson 1973
)
, which, similar to epidemiological models, describes individuals transitioning between states of ignorance, spreading, and stifling depending on their interactions. For many years it was believed that this model would always have a probability to reach out a non-vanishing fraction of the population
(
Barrat et al 2008
)
, and therefore that it did not exhibit a phase transition. However, recent study by
Ferraz de Arruda et al 2022
demonstrated the existence of a critical phase transition. This finding, which arises from accounting for node-to-node correlations neglected in mean-field approaches, challenges long-standing assumptions and opens new directions for research in rumor dynamics.

The existence of a critical transition in the rumor model has important implications. In particular, it enables the study of early warning signals (EWS), statistical indicators that detect when systems are approaching a tipping point and experiencing critical slowing down (CSD). EWS have been successfully applied in fields such as epidemiology
(
O’Regan and Drake 2013
)
, ecology and climate science
(
George et al 2023
)
, while, as far as we know, they have not yet been explored in the context of rumor dynamics. This opens a new direction for studying how EWS could help anticipate transitions and characterize the mechanisms driving rumour spreading in complex systems. While in recent years intervention strategies and forecasting have become standard tools in epidemic modelling
(
Ullon and Forgoston 2023
)
, only a few studies have taken a similar approach to investigate the spread of rumors
(
Belen et al 2005
;
Pearce et al 2005
)
, that do not account for the system’s critical dynamics.

The current context, together with theoretical advances in rumor propagation modeling, prompts us to pose the following fundamental questions: Given knowledge of the system’s dynamics, what are the optimal strategies to control the spread of a rumor? And can we detect whether the growth of a rumor is driven by an external impulse (such as a broadcast or astroturfing campaign), or by an internal increase in virality? Answering these questions is not only theoretically relevant but also carries significant practical implications. Distinguishing between the organic growth of rumors and coordinated external interventions is crucial for identifying disinformation campaigns and preserving the integrity of online discourse. Furthermore, by identifying the optimal way for controlling the rumor spread, taking into account its underlying dynamics, can serve as a basis for designing robust communication strategies to amplify beneficial information and mitigate harmful or manipulative content on digital platforms.

2
Model definition

We consider a modified version of the Maki-Thompson (MT) rumor spreading model, where each individual (node
i
) can be in one of three states: ignorant (
X_{i}=1
), spreader (
Y_{i}=1
), or stifler (
Z_{i}=1
) with
X_{i}+Y_{i}+Z_{i}=1
. These are modeled as Bernoulli random variables taking values in
\{0,1\}
. Transitions between states occur as independent Poisson processes on an undirected network defined by adjacency matrix
A=(A_{ij})
. The transition rules are as follows:

\displaystyle Y+X

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\lambda}Y+Y

(spreading)

\displaystyle Y+Y

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\alpha}Z+Y

(stifling by another spreader)

\displaystyle Y+Z

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\alpha}Z+Z

(stifling by a stifler)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\delta}X

(forgetting)

In the standard MT model, the forgetting rate
\delta=0
, leading to infinitely many absorbing states. In the modified MT model,
\delta>0
introduces a spontaneous transition from stifler to ignorant, resulting in a unique absorbing state (rumor-free).

The system evolves as a continuous-time Markov chain on the constrained state space
\{0,1\}^{3N}
, with transition rates determined by pairwise interactions. Exact analysis becomes intractable for large
N
, so we consider a first-order approximation by taking expectations of the indicator variables:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}=\delta z_{i}-\lambda\sum^{N}_{k=1}A_{ki}x_{i}y_{k}\\
\frac{dy_{i}}{dt}=\lambda\sum^{N}_{k=1}A_{ki}x_{i}y_{k}-\alpha\sum^{N}_{k=1}A_{ki}y_{i}(y_{k}+z_{k})\\
\frac{dz_{i}}{dt}=-\delta z_{i}+\alpha\sum^{N}_{k=1}A_{ki}y_{i}(y_{k}+z_{k})\end{array}\right.

where
x_{i}(t)=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}(t)]
,
y_{i}(t)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(t)]
and
z_{i}(t)=\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}(t)]
. This approximation captures the average behavior of the system while accounting for local network structure via the adjacency matrix.

3
Results

3.1
Controlling the lifetime of the rumor

In practical settings, such as mitigating misinformation, prolonging beneficial awareness campaigns, or designing resilient communication systems, being able to control how long a rumor persists in a network is highly valuable. This motivates the study of interventions that modify the system’s behavior without altering global parameters like the transmission rate.

To explore this, we investigate whether small, localized changes in the initial conditions can systematically influence the lifetime of a rumor, defined as the time until all spreaders disappear and the system reaches an absorbing state. The lifetime is not only an important observable itself, but also correlates with the effective reach and impact of the rumor in finite systems.

3.1.1
Increasing the lifetime

Firstly, we consider a simple intervention: adding a second initial spreader, placed at random distance from the first, as shown in Fig.
1
A. This minimal change leads to a substantial increase in the average lifetime of the rumor. In the subcritical regime, where spreading is scarce and localized, each spreader initiates an independent cascade, and the system only reaches the absorbing state once both cascades have terminated. Therefore, the total lifetime can be approximated as the maximum time of two independent exponentials:

T_{\text{two independent spreaders}}\approx\frac{3}{2}T_{\text{single spreader}}

(4)

This behavior is supported by the analytical lifetime approximation developed in
Ferraz de Arruda et al 2022

i

1.

Node
i

j

\left\langle{\tau}_{1}\right\rangle

2.

One of the two nodes becomes a stifler,
\left\langle{\tau}_{2}\right\rangle

3.

The second node becomes a stifler,
\left\langle{\tau}_{3}\right\rangle

4.

One of the stiflers forgets the rumor and becomes ignorant,
\left\langle{\tau}_{4}\right\rangle

5.

The remaining node also forgets,
\left\langle{\tau}_{5}\right\rangle

3.1.2
Reducing the lifetime

While extending the lifetime of a rumor may be desirable in cases like public awareness campaigns, there are many contexts (such as combating misinformation or panic) where the goal is the opposite: to shorten the rumor’s duration and minimize its impact. We now explore whether the lifetime of a rumor can be actively reduced through localized interventions, again without altering the transmission rate
\lambda
.

To this end, we introduce a second spreader in close proximity to the initial one. Specifically, we place the new spreader in a neighboring node before the first spreading event occurs. In the subcritical regime, where spreading is already inefficient, this small change has a significant suppressive effect. Because the two spreaders are so close, they are likely to interact early. This increases the likelihood of early extinction, effectively bypassing the long transient dynamics characteristic of the subcritical regime conditions.

By placing two spreaders close together, we effectively increase the chance that steps (

B). Analytically, we approximate it by adding a probability that the two spreaders interact and on average the process finish earlier. The full derivation can be found in Appendix

3.2
Autocorrelation patterns of the rumor model

Now we investigate whether early warning signals can provide insights into the dynamics of rumor spreading. One of the most commonly used statistical indicators for detecting critical transitions is the autocorrelation
(
O’Regan and Drake 2013
;
Ullon and Forgoston 2023
)
, which measures the correlation of a signal with a delayed version of itself. As a system approaches a critical point, its recovery rate from perturbations typically slows down, leading to an increase in autocorrelation. This phenomenon, known as critical slowing down (CSD), makes autocorrelation a powerful tool for anticipating shifts in critical complex systems.

The autocorrelation as a function of the spreading rate (
\lambda
) at multiple lags of the density of spreaders starting from a single spreader. The red dashed line indicates the average lifetime (

\delta=0.89,\alpha=0.5
for a network of size
N=1000
and
\langle k\rangle=10
.

We begin by analyzing the autocorrelation of the density of spreaders at different lags (the time separation between two observations) across varying spreading rates to explore early warning signals in different regimes of the model. Additionally, we examine the average lifetime of the rumor as a marker to identify the critical point (
\lambda_{c}
). As expected, the autocorrelation increases near the
\lambda_{c}
, consistent with critical slowing down (see Fig.
3
).

However, an important subtlety arises in the subcritical regime, where the system evolves very slowly due to the rarity of spreading events. In this regime, autocorrelation remains artificially high, not because the system is approaching a transition, but because it is barely changing. This slow behavior leads to high autocorrelation values that can be misleading if interpreted in isolation.

This finding suggests that autocorrelation alone is not a reliable early warning signal in rumor models, especially in the subcritical phase, and must be interpreted alongside other indicators and in the context of the system’s dynamical regime.

3.3
Distinguishing organic growth from an external impulse

We investigate whether EWS can provide insights into the dynamics of rumor spreading. Specifically, their ability to distinguish between the different mechanisms that drive rumor propagation, such as an organic growth of rumor transmissibility and an external impulse or broadcast. To test this, we designed two scenarios that lead to a similar spreader density timeseries. The first is a density pulse that simulates the intrusion of new spreaders interacting with the rumor, a mechanism related to astroturfing or external shocks in general. The second is an abrupt increase in the spreading rate that transitions the system from a subcritical to a supercritical regime. In both experiments, we use two indicators as EWS to track changes in the system. One is the autocorrelation, which we compute from the density of spreaders over time, capturing how similar the system’s state is between successive time intervals. The other is the mutual information between node states, which quantifies the amount of information obtained about one node by observing the other (see Fig.
4
A).

To simulate the first scenario, that corresponds to an organic growth of the interest in the rumor, we have tuned the transmissibility parameter instantaneously from the subcritical to the supercritical regime. Regarding the second scenario, representing an external impulse, we have applied an external injection of spreaders. For this scenario, we applied the artificial injection in different ways. In one hand, taking into account differences in the activity of the added spreaders, we have two cases: one where spreaders are permanent (they continuously stay active) and the other where spreaders behave like normal agents, following same rumor rules. In the other hand, taking into account the location of the added spreaders: one where spreaders are injected at random locations and the other where they are added next to spreader nodes (see Fig.
4

We observe that for the transmissibility increase, the mutual information has a sudden increase just at the perturbation and remains high. Also, the autocorrelation has a peak just at the perturbation and it relaxes to a value higher than before the perturbation. For the artificially added spreaders case we see that just at the perturbation there is an abrupt change in the spreaders density timeseries and also in the analyzed indicators for both experiments. Specifically, the mutual information has a sudden increase just at the perturbation and remains high for the transmissibility increase case and also for the added permanent spreaders. If we look at the autocorrelation, we see that just at the perturbation it has a different behavior for each way of injecting spreaders. When the injection is done with permanent spreaders, a pronounced peak is seen and then it stabilizes at a higher or lower value depending on the location. Conversely, when the injected spreaders behave like normal population (transient), we see that the autocorrelation takes longer to stabilize, and the behavior differs in localized spreaders, which has a drop instead of a peak.

The key challenge is to distinguish, by analyzing the time series of spreaders after the perturbation, whether the perturbation comes from an internal transition or an external influence. However, we note that the changes produced remain ambiguous, since the dynamics of the autocorrelation in the two experiments are very similar.

Interestingly, when analyzing the autocorrelation as a function of the lag, we see that it differs for the two scenarios. In Fig.

3.3.1
Estimating the spreading rate at the steady-state

In this section, we developed an analytical approximation for the autocorrelation to explain the origin of oscillations found in the simulations.

Locally, an individual node in the network can be in different states throughout the process repeatedly so that the spreading state
V
at two different time points can be autocorrelated. The autocorrelation of the infection state of a node
j
between time
s
and
t
is:

\displaystyle R_{j}(s,t)\triangleq\frac{E[V_{j}(s)V_{j}(t)]-E[V_{j}(s)]E[V_{j}(t)]}{\sqrt{Var[V_{j}(s)]Var[V_{j}(t)]}}

(8)

In a first-order approximation we assume that the probabilities are independent, for Bernoulli random variables
E[V_{i}(t)V_{j}(t)]=E[V_{i}(t)]E[V_{j}(t)]
. Here, we use the N-intertwined mean-field approximation (NIMFA)
(
Van Mieghem 2014
)
that allows us to write effective rates
(
Liu and Van Mieghem 2018
)
for each node transition. In the steady-state, we can write effective spreading (
\tilde{\lambda}_{\infty}
) and stifling (
\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}
) rates for each node:

\tilde{\lambda}_{i\infty}\equiv\lambda\sum^{N}_{k=1}A_{ki}y_{i\infty}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{\alpha}_{i\infty}\equiv\alpha\sum^{N}_{k=1}A_{ki}(y_{k\infty}+z_{k\infty})

(9)

The spreading probability of node
i
for
i=1,...,N
follows the governing equation:

\frac{dv_{i}(t)}{dt}=\left[1-v_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{\delta}v_{i}(t)\tilde{\alpha}\right]\tilde{\lambda}-v_{i}(t)\tilde{\alpha}.

(10)

Then, for this approximation we can write an effective infinitesimal generator of the continuous-time Markov processes:

Q_{i\infty}=\begin{pmatrix}-\tilde{\lambda}_{i\infty}&\tilde{\lambda}_{i\infty}&0\\
0&-\tilde{\alpha}_{i\infty}&\tilde{\alpha}_{i\infty}\\
\delta&0&-\delta\end{pmatrix}

(11)

that allows us to write the steady-state transition probability matrix:

P_{i\infty}(h)=e^{Q_{i\infty}h}=U_{i}e^{\Lambda_{i}}U^{-1}_{i}.

(12)

where
U_{i}
and
\Lambda_{i}
are the eigenvector matrix and the diagonal eigenvalue matrix of
Q_{i\infty}h
, respectively. The eigenvalues are:

\lambda_{2,3}=-\frac{1}{2}(\delta+\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\alpha})\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\delta+\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\alpha})^{2}-4(\tilde{\lambda}\delta+\tilde{\alpha}\delta+\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{\alpha})}\equiv a\pm w

(13)

where
a=-\frac{1}{2}(\delta+\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\alpha})
and
w=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\delta+\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\alpha})^{2}-4(\tilde{\lambda}\delta+\tilde{\alpha}\delta+\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{\alpha})}
.

The autocorrelation function for the fraction of infected nodes
V_{j\infty}(t)
is:

\begin{split}R_{i\infty}=\frac{E[V_{i\infty}(t)V_{i\infty}(t+h)]-E[V_{i\infty}(t)]E[V_{i\infty}(t+h)]}{Var[V_{i\infty}(t)]}=\frac{E[V_{i\infty}(t)V_{i\infty}(t+h)]-v^{2}_{j\infty}}{v_{i\infty}-v^{2}_{i\infty}}\end{split}

(14)

The joint expectation
E[V_{i\infty}(t)V_{i\infty}(t+h)]
is:

\begin{split}E[V_{i\infty}(t)&V_{i\infty}(t+h)]=Pr[V_{i\infty}(t+h)=1|V_{i\infty}(t)=1]Pr[V_{i\infty}(t)=1]=\\
&=v_{i\infty}Pr[V_{i\infty}(t+h)=1|V_{i\infty}(t)=1]=v_{i\infty}P_{i\infty}(h)_{2,2}\end{split}

(15)

By doing some calculations (see Appendix
8
) we get the following expression,

\displaystyle P_{i\infty}(h)_{2,2}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\alpha}\delta+\tilde{\lambda}\delta}\left[\delta\tilde{\lambda}+\sqrt{{(\tilde{\alpha}(\tilde{\lambda}+\delta))}^{2}+{\left(\frac{\tilde{\alpha}(\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\lambda}-\tilde{\lambda}^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}\delta-\delta^{2})}{w}\right)}^{2}}e^{ah}\left(\cos{(wh)}-\phi\right)\right]

(16)

and the autocorrelation is:

R_{i\infty}=\frac{1}{1-v_{i\infty}}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\lambda}+\tilde{\alpha}\delta+\tilde{\lambda}\delta}\left[\delta\tilde{\lambda}+\sqrt{{(\tilde{\alpha}(\tilde{\lambda}+\delta))}^{2}+{\left(\frac{\tilde{\alpha}(\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\lambda}-\tilde{\lambda}^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}\delta-\delta^{2})}{w}\right)}^{2}}e^{ah}\left(\cos{(wh)}-\phi\right)\right]-v_{i\infty}\right)

(17)

where

\phi=\arctan{\left(\frac{\tilde{\alpha}(\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\lambda}-\tilde{\lambda}^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}\delta-\delta^{2})}{w\tilde{\alpha}(\tilde{\lambda}+\delta)}\right)}

(18)

that corresponds to a damped oscillator when the eigenvalues become complex (see Section
3.3.3
).

3.3.2
Solution for a random regular network

Now, we want to find the values of the effective spreading (
\tilde{\lambda}_{\infty}
) and stifling (
\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}
) rates at the steady-state for a random regular network. The stationary values
x_{\infty},y_{\infty}
and
z_{\infty}
are calculated from the approximation for a random regular network, where we assume all nodes are statistically identical:
x_{i}=x,y_{i}=y
and
z_{i}=z
. Also,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{ki}=k
. In the steady state:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0=\delta z-\lambda kxy\\
0=\lambda kxy-\alpha ky(y+z)\\
0=-\delta z+\alpha ky(y+z)\end{array}\right.

Substituting and applying
x+y+z=1
we get:

x_{\infty}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\lambda}\qquad y_{\infty}=\frac{\lambda\delta}{\alpha\lambda k+\alpha\delta+\lambda\delta}\qquad z_{\infty}=\frac{\alpha\lambda^{2}k}{(\alpha+\lambda)(\alpha\lambda k+\alpha\delta+\lambda\delta)}

(22)

and finally substituting from (

Case study

In this final section, we apply our method to a real-world dataset: the Twitter activity surrounding the announcement of the Higgs boson discovery, collected between July 1 and July 7, 2012
(
De Domenico et al 2013
)
. This dataset offers a valuable opportunity to analyze the spread of a rumor and collective behavior during a high-impact, real-time event.

Figure 11:
It shows the tweet count timeseries for each group of daily new users from the Higgs boson discovery Twitter dataset. The data includes the activity surrounding the discovery between July 1 and July 7, 2012. The official announcement took place at day 4th.

Our goal is to infer wether the internal dynamics of rumor transmission changed following the external shock (the official announcement of the discovery on July 4th). Specifically, we want to determine whether the observed increase in activity reflects a true increase in transmissibility among users, or whether it was driven primarily by an influx of new participants driven by widespread media coverage. In other words, we aim to distinguish between an intrinsic change in the way the rumor spread and a superficial increase in volume caused by external exposure.

Fig.
11
shows a collection of time series, each representing the activity over time of a group of users who joined the conversation on the same day. This visualization allows us to track how each group of new users contributed to the overall rumor spread. We observe that users who joined in the early days continued tweeting throughout the week, and that the announcement on July 4th led to both a sharp increase in activity and a large influx of new users.

In order to analyze this scenario from the perspective of the methodology presented before, we segmented the new spreaders time series using a breakpoint detection method
(
Truong et al 2020
)
, allowing us to isolate the system’s behavior before and after the announcement. For each segment, we calculated the autocorrelation at different lags (Fig.
12
). Notably, after the peak in activity, the oscillation period in the autocorrelation signal shortens, a pattern which according to our analysis, is compatible with an increase in transmissibility. This suggests that, beyond the external amplification from media coverage, the internal spread of the rumor on Twitter became more intense.

This method provides a form of nowcasting: it allows us to infer real-time shifts in the system’s internal state with minimal data. In practice, we only need to observe half of an oscillation period to estimate transmissibility. Remarkably, our results align with those obtained from more computationally intensive Monte Carlo simulations on the same dataset
(
Ferraz de Arruda et al 2018
)
, but our approach is significantly simpler and more efficient to implement.

5
Discussion

This study advances our understanding of rumor spreading in complex networks and provides practical insights that are applicable to real-world social systems. It demonstrates that early warning signals, such as autocorrelation, can be used not only to detect the onset of viral behavior in rumors but also to infer shifts in the underlying transmission dynamics. Additionally, we propose strategies for increasing or reducing the lifespan of a rumor, with the goal of either amplifying or limiting its influence.

In the context of rumor control, our analytical and simulation-based analysis shows that small, targeted interventions can significantly alter a rumor’s lifetime. These interventions involve introducing external spreaders at specific locations in the network. We observe that the rumor lasts longer when the new spreader is placed far from existing spreaders. This separation leads to the formation of independent spreading processes that are less likely to interfere with each other, thereby prolonging the rumor. In contrast, when the new spreader is placed close to an existing one in a subcritical regime, where the rumor is slow and reaches few nodes, the lifetime is shortened. In this case, the proximity increases the chance of interaction between spreaders, accelerating the end of the rumor. As part of this approach, we are also able to estimate the expected timing of key events, such as the appearance of a second spreader or the first stifler in the network, which can further guide intervention strategies. This information can be used to design more effective interventions, such as improving the reach of awareness campaigns that struggle to reach a wide audience or reducing the lifespan of harmful content that is unlikely to go viral. Based on our results, we observe that extending the lifespan of a rumor is generally easier than shortening it, which aligns with intuitive expectations.

We also examined whether a rise in activity following an external event, such as a media broadcast or astroturfing campaign, can be distinguished from a genuine increase in internal transmissibility. Using autocorrelation as an indicator, we found that different spreading mechanisms leave different statistical signatures. When new spreaders are introduced near already active nodes, the autocorrelation increases, making it difficult to tell whether the increase results from external input or higher transmissibility. We also observed that the behavior of the system differs depending on whether the added spreaders remain permanently active or follow the same dynamics as the rest of the population. In the latter case, the autocorrelation decays more slowly after the external perturbation.

However, measuring autocorrelation at lag 1 alone is not sufficient to clearly distinguish between these cases. To better distinguish between the two cases, we analyzed autocorrelation across multiple time lags. This revealed a key distinction: when transmissibility is high, the autocorrelation displays oscillations across lags, a behavior not present in externally driven cases. Using a mean-field approximation, we derived an analytical expression that captures this behavior as a damped oscillator. Although the oscillations are strongly damped in the analytical model, the period aligns closely with what we observe in simulations. This result not only allows us to distinguish between organic growth and an external impulses, but also shows that the mean-field equations, despite not capturing the phase transition, still match the oscillation period observed in exact stochastic simulations. Moreover, this correspondence allows us to estimate the critical point from the period of these oscillations.

To validate these findings, we applied our method to a real-world dataset: the Twitter activity surrounding the announcement of the Higgs boson discovery in July 2012. We aimed to determine whether the spike in activity following the announcement was driven solely by media amplification or also reflected an increase in internal transmissibility. By measuring autocorrelation at multiple lags before and after the peak, we found a higher frequency of oscillations after the announcement, which is consistent with an increase in transmissibility.

In summary, we have shown that the lifetime of a rumor can be controlled by the targeted placement of spreaders within a network. Depending on where the added spreader is introduced, such interventions can either extend or reduce the lifespan of a rumor. We have also demonstrated that analyzing autocorrelation across time lags can help distinguish between internally driven growth in transmissibility and externally introduced activity. Specifically, only the former leads to oscillatory behavior in the autocorrelation, a pattern that can be captured analytically using a mean-field approximation.

These results not only contribute to the theoretical understanding of rumor spreading in networks but also have broader implications for digital communication environments. By identifying how rumor volume and visibility can be artificially amplified, our findings help explain how “false volume” can be created in online platforms. In doing so, we highlight mechanisms that are often employed by powerful actors, and facilitated by large technology platforms, to manipulate online discourse using fake accounts and coordinated activity. This challenges the notion of social networks as neutral spaces for information exchange and underscores the importance of developing tools to detect and mitigate such manipulation.

We use an optimized Gillespie algorithm
(
Gutjahr et al 2021
)
to simulate the exact stochastic trajectories of the continuous-time Markov process
(
Gillespie 1977
)
.

To compute the lifetime of finite realizations, each run is initialized with a single randomly chosen spreader and the rest of the population as ignorants. The simulation proceeds until the system reaches an absorbing state
(

In order to collect significant statistics in the subcritical regime where the system would rapidly reach the absorbing state and to approximate the steady-state behavior, we apply the quasi-stationary (QS) method
(
de Oliveira and Dickman 2005
)
. With it, we prevent the system from being absorbed by reintroducing a saved active configuration when the absorption state is reached.
