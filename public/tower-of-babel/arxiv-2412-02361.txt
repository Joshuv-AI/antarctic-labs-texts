Chiral Anomalous Magnetohydrodynamics in action: effective field theory and holography
Matteo Baggioli, Yanyan Bu, Xiyang Sun

Abstract
Chiral Anomalous Magnetohydrodynamics (CAMHD) provides a low-energy effective framework for describing chiral fluids in the presence of dynamical electromagnetic fields and axial anomaly. This theory finds applications across diverse physical systems, including heavy-ion collisions, the early universe, and Weyl/Dirac semimetals. Along with Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) effective theories, holographic models serve as a complementary tool to provide a systematic formulation of CAMHD that goes beyond the weak coupling regime. In this work, we explore holographic models with U ​ ( 1 ) A × U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1)_{A}\times U(1) symmetry, where the electromagnetic U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) field is rendered dynamical through mixed boundary conditions applied to the bulk gauge field and the axial anomaly is introduced via a Chern-Simons bulk term. Through a detailed holographic SK analysis, we demonstrate that the low-energy effective action derived from this model aligns precisely with the SK field theory proposed by Landry and Liu and, in fact, it generalizes it to scenarios with finite background axial field. This alignment not only validates the holographic model but also paves the way for its use in exploring unresolved aspects of CAMHD, such as the recently proposed chiral magnetic electric separation wave and nonlinear chiral instabilities.

Introduction

Hydrodynamics describes late-time and long-distance dynamics of conserved quantities around thermal equilibrium and represents in this sense an effective theory where ‘fast’ degrees of freedom are integrated out
[
1
]
. In this limit, a finite temperature chiral fluid in presence of dynamical electromagnetic fields and with Adler-Bell-Jackiw anomaly
[
2
]
is effectively described by chiral anomalous magnethodydrodynamics (CAMHD), that includes the magnetic fields as slow variables together with the other conserved charges. Importantly, the Adler-Bell-Jackiw (ABJ) anomaly breaks the conservation of axial current. Hence, CAMHD is valid only in the limit where axial charge can be treated as an approximately conserved quantity or, in other words, when the axial charge relaxation time is long compared to the typical microscopic timescale – the
quasi-hydrodynamic
regime
[
3
]
.

In the weak magnetic field regime, CAMHD has been discussed at length in the past

[
11

proposed a description in terms of a global one-form symmetry associated to the conservation of the magnetic flux
[
12
]
. In their approach, they were able to reproduce some aspects of chiral anomalous MHD phenomenology from an effective theory viewpoint, including the chiral separation and magnetic effects. Additionally, in that formalism the effects of one-loop level magnetohydrodynamic fluctuations was also studied in
[
13

. An alternative route has been advanced by Landry and Liu
[
14
]
using a more classical formulation in terms of gauge fields. They incorporated the chiral magnetic effect, the chiral separation effect and the chiral electric separation effect in an effective theory language as well. Interestingly, they also predicted the emergence of a novel collective excitation named the
chiral magnetic electric separation wave

[
14

Despite these two recent developments, a complete effective field theory description of CAMHD, including also velocity and temperature fluctuations, is still missing. Historically, holography has always been a good complementary tool to understand the structure of effective field theories and to build corresponding microscopic models that are valid beyond the weak coupling regime. Chiral hydrodynamics in the presence of strong magnetic fields has been recently discussed in the context of holography and the predictions from hydrodynamics have been matched to the holographic results

Effective field theory for chiral anomalous magnethodydrodynamics

Consider a macroscopic system at finite temperature, whose microscopic degrees of freedom include charged chiral fermions interacting with an electromagnetic field. The microscopic description involves massless QED defined by the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm micro}=\bar{\psi}{\rm i}\gamma^{\mu}(\partial_{\mu}+{\rm i}ea_{\mu})\psi-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu},

(2.1)

where
\psi
is the massless charged fermion field,
a_{\mu}
is the electromagnetic gauge potential and
F_{\mu\nu}
the corresponding field strength. Moreover,
e
is the fermion charge. The theory described by Eq. (
2.1
) has a conserved vector current operator and an anomalous chiral current operator

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\hat{J}^{\mu}=0\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad{\rm with}\qquad\hat{J}^{\mu}\equiv\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\psi,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\hat{J}_{5}^{\mu}=-\frac{e^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}\qquad{\rm with}\qquad\hat{J}_{5}^{\mu}\equiv\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi.

(2.3)

In terms of the microscopic degrees of freedom, the partition function is given by

\displaystyle Z=\int_{\rho_{0}}[D\psi][Da_{\mu}]e^{i\int_{\mathcal{C}}d^{4}x\mathcal{L}_{\rm micro}},

(2.4)

where
\rho_{0}
is the density matrix of a thermal state. Therefore, in Eq. (
2.4
) the integration shall be understood along the Schwinger-Keldysh closed time contour
\mathcal{C}
.

In the long-wavelength and long-time limits, the effective description of this system is chiral anomalous magnetohydrodynamics (CAMHD). Using the Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) formalism, the partition function in Eq. (
2.4
) can be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle Z=\int[Da_{\mu}][Da_{\rm a\mu}][D\phi][D\phi_{a}][D\varphi][D\varphi_{a}]e^{iS_{\rm eff}[A_{\mu},A_{\rm a\mu},C_{\mu},C_{\rm a\mu}]}.

(2.5)

The effective action
S_{\rm eff}
depends on the following gauge invariant combinations

\displaystyle A_{\mu}=a_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\phi,\qquad\qquad A_{{\rm a}\mu}=a_{{\rm a}\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\phi_{\rm a},

\displaystyle C_{\mu}=\mathcal{A}_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\varphi,\qquad\qquad C_{{\rm a}\mu}=\mathcal{A}_{{\rm a}\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\varphi_{\rm a}.

(2.6)

Here,
\phi
and
\varphi
are emergent collective variables which will be used to parameterize the currents’ expectation values
J^{\mu}=\langle\hat{J}^{\mu}\rangle
and
J_{5}^{\mu}=\langle\hat{J}_{5}^{\mu}\rangle
. Indeed,
\phi
and
\varphi
could be thought of as the dynamical degrees of freedom corresponding to the densities
J^{0}
and
J_{5}^{0}
, respectively. The latter are usually taken as hydrodynamic variables in formulating chiral MHD. The background axial gauge potential
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}
acts as an external source for the chiral current
J_{5}^{\mu}
. All other variables have a subindex
a
and represent the noisy counterparts characteristic of a finite temperature system. Given Eq. (
2.6
), the path integral in Eq. (
2.5
) can be further simplified into
[
14
]

\displaystyle Z=\mathcal{N}\int[DA_{\mu}][DA_{{\rm a}\mu}][D\varphi][D\varphi_{\rm a}]e^{{\rm i}S_{\rm eff}[A_{\mu},A_{{\rm a}\mu},C_{\mu},C_{{\rm a}\mu}]},

(2.7)

where
\mathcal{N}
is an overall constant. Moreover,
A_{\mu}
is essentially the macroscopic gauge field in the medium.

In Ref.
[
14
]
, the effective action was split into an anomalous part
I_{\rm anom}
and an invariant part
I_{\rm inv}
with respect to axial gauge transformations,

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}=I_{\rm anom}+I_{\rm inv},

(2.8)

where

\displaystyle I_{\rm anom}=c\int\left(\varphi_{{\rm a}}F\wedge F+2\varphi F\wedge F_{\rm a}\right).

(2.9)

Here, the anomaly coefficient
c=-e^{2}/(4\pi^{2})
. The invariant part
I_{\rm inv}=\int d^{4}x\mathcal{L}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle nA_{{\rm a}0}+n_{5}\partial_{0}\varphi_{\rm a}-H_{i}B_{{\rm a}i}+2c\partial_{0}\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B}\varphi_{\rm a}-(2c\mu_{5}-b_{00})B_{i}A_{{\rm a}i}

\displaystyle+{\rm i}(r^{-1})_{ij}A_{{\rm a}i}\left(TA_{{\rm a}j}+{\rm i}\partial_{0}A_{j}\right)+{\rm i}\kappa_{ij}\partial_{i}\varphi_{\rm a}\left(T\partial_{j}\varphi_{a}+{\rm i}\partial_{j}\mu_{5}\right)

\displaystyle+{\rm i}\lambda_{ij}\left((T\partial_{i}\varphi_{\rm a}+{\rm i}\partial_{i}\mu_{5})A_{{\rm a}j}+\partial_{i}\varphi_{\rm a}(TA_{{\rm a}j}+{\rm i}\partial_{0}A_{j})\right)

\displaystyle+\gamma_{ij}(\partial_{i}\varphi_{\rm a}\partial_{0}A_{j}-\partial_{i}\mu_{5}A_{{\rm a}j})+\mathcal{A}_{{\rm a}0}J_{5}^{0}+\mathcal{A}_{{\rm a}i}J_{5}^{i},

(2.10)

where

\displaystyle n=-\frac{\partial\mathbb{F}}{\partial A_{0}},\qquad n_{5}=-\frac{\partial\mathbb{F}}{\partial\mu_{5}},\qquad H_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathbb{F}}{\partial B_{i}}\equiv\frac{B_{i}}{\mu},

\displaystyle r_{ij}=r_{\parallel}h_{i}h_{j}+r_{\perp}\Delta_{ij}+r_{\times}\epsilon_{ijk}h_{k},\qquad\Delta_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-h_{i}h_{j},

\displaystyle\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_{\parallel}h_{i}h_{j}+\kappa_{\perp}\Delta_{ij}+\kappa_{\times}\epsilon_{ijk}h_{k},\qquad\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_{\parallel}h_{i}h_{j}+\lambda_{\perp}\Delta_{ij}+\lambda_{\times}\epsilon_{ijk}h_{k},

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}=\gamma_{\parallel}h_{i}h_{j}+\gamma_{\perp}\Delta_{ij}+\gamma_{\times}\epsilon_{ijk}h_{k},\qquad\lambda_{ij}^{\pm}\equiv\lambda_{ij}\pm\gamma_{ij},

\displaystyle J_{5}^{0}=n_{5},\qquad J_{5}^{i}=(b_{50}-2cA_{0})B_{i}-\kappa_{ij}\partial_{j}\mu_{5}-\lambda_{ij}^{-}\partial_{0}A_{j}+\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_{j}(mB_{k}).

(2.11)

Here,
\mathbb{F}
can be interpreted as the equilibrium free energy density, and
h_{i}\equiv B_{i}/B
. All coefficients should be understood as functions of
A_{0},\mu_{5},B^{2}
except for
b_{00},b_{50}
that are constant.

Our objective is to reproduce this EFT structure directly from the holographic model. To avoid clutter, all the technical derivations are relegated in the next Section
3
. Here, we limit ourselves to report and discuss the main results.

We derive the effective action
S_{\rm eff}=\int d^{4}x\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}
using the holographic Schwinger-Keldysh formalism

In the present work, we focus on the MHD regime assuming that various dynamical variables scale as

\displaystyle\partial_{0}\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),\quad\partial_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1}),\quad A_{0},~C_{0}\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0}),\quad A_{i},~C_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}),

3
Holographic derivation

Here, we provide all the technical details of the holographic computations and derive the effective action presented in the previous Section.

3.1
Holographic setup

The holographic model involves a vector gauge field
V_{M}
and an axial gauge field
L_{M}
whose dynamics are described by the following bulk action

\displaystyle S_{0}=\int d^{5}x\sqrt{-g}

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{4}\mathds{V}_{MN}\mathds{V}^{MN}-\frac{1}{4}\mathds{L}_{MN}\mathds{L}^{MN}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\epsilon^{MNPQR}L_{M}\left(3\mathds{V}_{NP}\mathds{V}_{QR}+\mathds{L}_{NP}\mathds{L}_{QR}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\,\,\left.+\alpha_{1}\left(\mathds{V}_{MN}\mathds{V}^{MN}\right)^{2}+\alpha_{2}\mathds{V}_{AB}\mathds{V}^{BC}\mathds{V}_{CD}\mathds{V}^{DA}\right],

(3.1)

where
\mathds{V}_{MN}=\nabla_{M}V_{N}-\nabla_{N}V_{M}
,
\mathds{L}_{MN}=\nabla_{M}L_{N}-\nabla_{N}L_{M}
, and
\epsilon^{MNPQR}
represents the Levi-Civita tensor in 5D AdS space. The vector field and axial field interact via a gauge Chern-Simons term inducing a chiral anomaly in the boundary theory. In addition, quartic terms for the vector gauge field
V_{M}
are included to generate non-anomalous nonlinear terms in the boundary action, see (
2.17
). Practically, the anomaly coefficient
\kappa
and the coupling constants
\alpha_{1,2}
are assumed to be much smaller than one, validating our perturbative calculations. One may wonder whether we need to add further higher-order terms in the bulk model (
3.1
) in order to generate further non-linearities in the boundary action. Indeed, this is unnecessary as seen from (
2.18
): quartic terms in the boundary action can be generated by cubic interactions in the bulk (i.e., chiral anomaly terms
\sim\kappa
). More generally, an interacting term in the bulk will generate same order non-linearities in the boundary action (via contact-type Witten diagram in the AdS) or induce higher-order non-linearities in the boundary action (via exchange-type Witten diagram in the AdS).

We consider a Schwarzschild-AdS
5
black brane geometry

\displaystyle ds^{2}=g_{MN}dx^{M}dx^{N}=2dvdr-r^{2}f(r)dv^{2}+r^{2}\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j},

(3.2)

where

,
x^{M}=(r,x^{\mu})
with

the holographic radial coordinate, and
x^{\mu}=(v,x^{i})
represent the boundary spacetime coordinates. The location of the event horizon is at

which is related to the boundary temperature via

. In order to derive the SK effective action on the boundary, we use the holographic prescription
[

]
for the SK closed time contour: analytically continue the radial coordinate

\displaystyle A_{{\rm s}\mu}=A_{{\rm s}\mu}[\mathfrak{J}_{{\rm s}\mu}],\qquad\qquad\mathfrak{L}_{{\rm s}\mu}=\mathfrak{L}_{{\rm s}\mu}[C_{{\rm s}\mu}].

\displaystyle S_{\rm bdy}=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-\gamma}\left[-\frac{\log r}{4}\mathds{V}_{\mu\nu}\mathds{V}^{\mu\nu}-\frac{\log r}{4}\mathds{L}_{\mu\nu}\mathds{L}^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{4}\mathds{V}_{\mu\nu}\mathds{V}^{\mu\nu}\right]\Bigg|_{2}^{1},

It is important to note that, because of the structure of the EFT, we can recycle previous results for the vector bulk field

well studied for the case of charge diffusion, in which a Dirichlet condition was imposed for

A_{{\rm s}\mu}

Additionally, in order to fully determine the bulk solutions, we have to impose an additional condition for the time-components of bulk gauge fields

\displaystyle V_{0}(r=r_{h}-\epsilon,x^{\alpha})=0,\qquad\qquad L_{0}(r=r_{h}-\epsilon,x^{\alpha})=0,

(3.12)

3.2
Bulk solutions

In this Section we solve the dynamical equations (

3.2.1
The scheme of perturbative expansion

Since Eqs. (
3.8
) are highly nonlinear, we search for perturbative solutions through a double expansion scheme.

First, recall that the anomaly coefficient
\kappa
, and coupling constants
\alpha_{1,2}
are assumed to be much smaller than unity. Thus, we search for perturbative solutions to the bulk equations (
3.8
) of the form

\displaystyle V_{\mu}=V_{\mu}^{(0)}+\lambda\,V_{\mu}^{(1)}+\cdots,\qquad\qquad L_{\mu}=L_{\mu}^{(0)}+\lambda\,L_{\mu}^{(1)}+\cdots

(3.14)

where we introduced a bookkeeping parameter
\lambda
to assist the perturbative expansion. Moreover, we assume the scaling
\lambda\sim\kappa\sim\sqrt{\alpha_{1,2}}
.This assumption is mainly motivated by the quartic terms in the boundary action. From the results (
2.17
)-(
2.19
), we see that there are two type of contributions to quartic terms in the boundary action: those generated by the chiral anomaly effect having an overall coefficient
\kappa^{2}
, and those generated by
\alpha
-terms in bulk action (
3.1
) having an overall coefficient
\alpha_{1,2}
. Thus, from the perspective of quartic terms in the boundary action, we made the scaling assumption
\kappa\sim\sqrt{\alpha_{1,2}}
.
The leading order solutions
V_{\mu}^{(0)}
and
L_{\mu}^{(0)}
obey homogeneous partial differential equations (PDEs), which are the dynamical components of the free Maxwell equations in Schwarzschild-AdS
5
. The nonlinear solutions
V_{\mu}^{(1)}
and
L_{\mu}^{(1)}
obey similar PDEs as those of
V_{\mu}^{(0)}
and
L_{\mu}^{(0)}
, with nontrivial sources to be built from leading order solutions
V_{\mu}^{(0)}
and
L_{\mu}^{(0)}
.

Second, at each order in the expansion (
3.14
) we consider a boundary derivative expansion

\displaystyle V_{\mu}^{(m)}=V_{\mu}^{(m)(0)}+\gamma\,V_{\mu}^{(m)(1)}+\gamma^{2}\,V_{\mu}^{(m)(2)}+\cdots,

\displaystyle L_{\mu}^{(m)}=L_{\mu}^{(m)(0)}+\gamma\,L_{\mu}^{(m)(1)}+\gamma^{2}\,L_{\mu}^{(m)(2)}+\cdots

(3.15)

where
\gamma\sim\partial_{\mu}
is assumed to assist the derivative expansion. Working in the MHD regime (
2.13
), the derivative expansion (
3.15
) could be simplified.

Through the double expansion (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), the nonlinear PDEs of (
3.8
) turn into a set of ordinary differential equations which we schematically write here

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\left[r^{3}\partial_{r}V_{0}^{(m)(n)}\right]=j_{0}^{(m)(n)},\qquad\qquad\partial_{r}\left[r^{3}f(r)\partial_{r}V_{i}^{(m)(n)}\right]=j_{i}^{(m)(n)},

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\left[r^{3}\partial_{r}L_{0}^{(m)(n)}\right]=l_{0}^{(m)(n)},\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\partial_{r}\left[r^{3}f(r)\partial_{r}L_{i}^{(m)(n)}\right]=l_{i}^{(m)(n)}.

(3.16)

Here, the source terms can be read off by plugging (
3.15
) into (
3.8
), and they are indeed functionals of the lower order solutions.

3.3
Computation of the boundary action

With the perturbative solutions obtained in section

Notice that

and

are linear in the boundary data

,

and

are quadratic in the boundary data

,

\bullet

The quadratic Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{eff}^{(2)}

The quadratic Lagrangian is simply obtained by computing

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}^{(2)}=\int_{\infty_{2}}^{\infty_{1}}dr\sqrt{-g}\left(-\frac{1}{4}\mathds{V}_{MN}\mathds{V}^{MN}-\frac{1}{4}\mathds{L}_{MN}\mathds{L}^{MN}\right)\bigg|_{V_{\mu}\to V_{\mu}^{(0)},\,\,L_{\mu}\to L_{\mu}^{(0)}}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm bdy},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm bdy}
is Lagrangian associated with the boundary action (
3.10
),
i.e.
,
S_{\rm bdy}=\int d^{4}x\mathcal{L}_{\rm bdy}
. Apart from a slight difference in the boundary term, namely the third term in Eq. (
3.10
), the quadratic Lagrangian was previously computed to second order in derivatives of boundary data
[

,
52
,
30
]
. Recycling those results and truncating them to order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
in the MHD regime (

), we eventually produce the results summarized in Eq. (

).

\bullet

The cubic Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{eff}^{(3)}

For the nonlinear corrections
V_{\mu}^{(1)}
and
L_{\mu}^{(1)}

. As a result, the nonlinear solutions

and

are not needed for calculation of those cubic terms in the boundary action. So, the cubic Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{eff}^{(3)}
is simply the bulk Chern-Simons term with the linear solutions plugged in

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm eff}^{(3)}=\int_{\infty_{2}}^{\infty_{1}}dr\sqrt{-g}~2\kappa~\epsilon^{MNPQR}\left[3L_{M}^{(0)}\nabla_{N}V_{P}^{(0)}\nabla_{Q}V_{R}^{(0)}+L_{M}^{(0)}\nabla_{N}L_{P}^{(0)}\nabla_{Q}L_{R}^{(0)}\right].

(3.23)

Indeed, it turns out that we only need

The anomalous quartic Lagrangian

The last part of the boundary action we derived corresponds to the quartic terms fully generated by the chiral anomaly. Through the Chern-Simons term in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm anom}^{(4)}=\int_{\infty_{2}}^{\infty_{1}}dr\sqrt{-g}~\frac{\kappa}{2}\epsilon^{MNPQR}

\displaystyle\,\left.+2L_{M}^{(0)}\mathds{L}_{NP}^{(1)}\mathds{L}_{QR}^{(0)}\right]

\displaystyle\left[2V_{M}^{(1)}\nabla_{N}V_{P}^{(0)}\nabla_{Q}L_{R}^{(0)}+L_{M}^{(1)}\nabla_{N}V_{P}^{(0)}\nabla_{Q}V_{R}^{(0)}\right.

4
Summary and Outlook

In this work, we have constructed a holographic model for chiral anomalous magnetohydrodynamics in
3
+
1
dimensions. This was achieved by combining the introduction of a 5D bulk Chern-Simons term with modified boundary conditions for the EM U(1) bulk gauge field. By using holographic Schwinger-Keldysh techniques, the SK effective action of the boundary field theory is derived and proved to exactly match the field theory construction proposed by Landry and Liu
[
14
]
. Importantly, we generalize the SK effective action in Landry and Liu
[
14
]
where the background axial gauge field was set to zero for convenience. Here, we provide a complete action also in presence of the latter.

Apart from confirming the validity of the EFT action in
[
14
]
and the correct nature of the holographic model, our results open the path towards the exploration of CAMHD in the strong coupling and strong magnetic field regime using holography. In particular, it would be interesting (I) to confirm the existence of a
chiral magnetic electric separation wave
and study its dynamics in the strong
B
limit and (II) to explore the chiral instabilities expected at nonlinear level. A first step would be to obtain the quasinormal mode spectrum of the system and study the real-time dynamics in this holographic model along the lines of similar studies in holographic chiral systems
[
53
,
54
]
.

At the order in the magnetic field considered in this work, we can already anticipate some results regarding the
chiral magnetic electric separation wave
.

\displaystyle\chi_{5}\equiv\frac{\partial n_{5}}{\partial\mu_{5}}=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{\beta^{2}}+\theta_{1}B^{2}+\theta_{1}\mathcal{B}^{2},

\displaystyle(r^{-1})_{\parallel}=\frac{\pi}{\beta}+\frac{8\beta}{\pi}(2\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\left(4A_{0}^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}B^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)+\frac{8\beta^{3}}{\pi^{3}}\alpha_{2}B^{2}+2\theta_{3}\beta B^{2}+\theta_{3}\beta(\mathcal{B}^{2}+\frac{\mathcal{B}_{i}\mathcal{B}_{j}}{B_{i}B_{j}}B^{2}),

In absence of background axial gauge field,
\mathcal{B}_{i}=0

and, hence,
v_{z}=0
. On the other hand, we find that

\Gamma=\frac{2c^{2}}{\pi^{3}T^{3}}B^{2}+\dots

(4.3)

A_{0}=0
). Therefore, we can already conclude that, in absence of background axial field, and in the limit of small magnetic field, the
chiral magnetic electric separation wave
is not a propagating wave but rather a purely decaying damped excitation. It would be interesting to perform a full quasinormal mode analysis in the large magnetic field to ascertain whether this changes in such a limit.

We expect that the introduction of a background axial gauge field will have several physical consequences that can be systematically studied using our framework. Despite a complete study on this point is beyond the scope of this work, we can anticipate one interesting effect caused by the presence of a background axial gauge field and related to the dynamics of the chiral magnetic electric separation wave discussed above. In particular, as evident from the equations above, the background axial magnetic field
\mathcal{B}_{i}
significantly affects the wave’s dispersion relation, modifying both the damping rate and the wave velocity. In fact, in the regime of weak magnetic field, the wave velocity is exactly zero if the background axial gauge field is turned off. We do expect more physical consequences of this sort.

We leave this and other interesting questions for the future.
