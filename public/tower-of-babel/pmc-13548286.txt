Targeting the PD-L1-induced PDK4/GLS metabolic axis overcomes anti-PD-1 resistance in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Wenxin Lian, Hui Yang, Yuqi Zhang, Chenchen Liu, Chunyao Fang, Mengyue Xu, Yixing Yang, Yan Wang, Runqiu Jiang, Jing Xu, Wen Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment is a pivotal barrier to effective immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). While programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) is well characterized as a ligand inhibiting T-cell function, its intrinsic ‘reverse signaling’ role in regulating tumor metabolism and shaping the immune landscape remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated the metabolic determinants of resistance to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1) therapy and the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsIntegrated metabolomics and transcriptomics were performed on tumor samples from patients with non-small cell lung cancer and cell lines. Mechanisms were delineated using RNA sequencing, cleavage under targets and tagmentation assays, metabolic flux analysis, and coculture systems. The therapeutic efficacy of targeting metabolic effectors was evaluated in syngeneic mouse models and correlated with immune profiling.ResultsWe identified a distinct metabolic signature characterized by aberrant pyruvate accumulation in patients resistant to anti-PD-1 therapy. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that antibody-mediated ligation of PD-L1 triggers an intrinsic endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response via the PERK–ATF4–CHOP axis. ATF4 acts as a transcriptional activator that directly upregulates pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) (blocking pyruvate oxidation) and glutaminase (GLS) (promoting glutaminolysis), creating a ‘dual-hit’ metabolic rewiring that drives intracellular pyruvate build-up. Subsequently, tumor-secreted pyruvate is taken up by tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) via MCT1, inducing mitochondrial reactive oxygen species accumulation and driving them into a state of cellular senescence. These senescent TAMs upregulate PD-L1 via STAT3 signaling, thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive feedback loop. Pharmacological inhibition of PDK4 and GLS effectively abolished pyruvate accumulation, prevented macrophage senescence, and restored CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity.ConclusionsOur study identifies a novel ‘PD-L1–ER stress–pyruvate–macrophage senescence’ axis as a key mechanism underlying primary resistance to ICB. These findings highlight the non-canonical reverse-signaling function of PD-L1 in metabolic remodeling and propose that targeting the PDK4/GLS-dependent pyruvate surge offers a promising therapeutic strategy to sensitize tumors to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Tumor microenvironment - TME, Lung Cancer, Immunotherapy, Macrophage

Introduction

Despite the paradigm shift driven by anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) therapies in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), primary resistance remains a critical bottleneck, often attributed to the immunosuppressive metabolic landscape within the tumor microenvironment (TME).1–5 While PD-L1 is canonically characterized as a ligand that suppresses T cells, emerging evidence suggests it also functions as a receptor, transmitting intrinsic ‘reverse signals’ that regulate tumor cell biology.6–9 However, a critical knowledge gap remains: whether this PD-L1 reverse signaling actively reprograms tumor metabolism and how such metabolic alterations subsequently reshape the immune microenvironment to foster resistance.

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer. While extensive research has focused on lactate, the immunomodulatory role of pyruvate—a central node linking glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle—remains significantly underappreciated.10–14 We hypothesized that under persistent immune pressure, tumor cells might activate primary stress responses, such as endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, to rewire pyruvate metabolism.15 This metabolic shift could potentially convert pyruvate from a mere fuel source into a bioactive signaling molecule that mediates dynamic intercellular communication.

Here, we systematically delineate a novel metabolic-immune regulatory axis driven by PD-L1 reverse signaling. We demonstrate that PD-1-mediated engagement of PD-L1 triggers a PERK–ATF4-dependent ER stress response in tumor cells. This signaling cascade orchestrates a ‘dual-hit’ metabolic rewiring by upregulating pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4) (blocking pyruvate oxidation) and glutaminase (GLS) (fueling glutaminolysis), leading to the aberrant accumulation and secretion of pyruvate. Crucially, we identify secreted pyruvate as a key driver of macrophage senescence in the TME. These senescent macrophages subsequently upregulate PD-L1, establishing a vicious positive feedback loop that reinforces immunosuppression. Our findings reveal the ‘PD-L1–ER stress–pyruvate–macrophage senescence’ axis as a driver of primary resistance and propose targeting the PDK4/GLS pathway as a strategy to resensitize tumors to immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Patient cohorts and clinical sample collection

We collected puncture tumor tissue specimens from 10 patients with NSCLC receiving first-line anti-PD-1 therapy in the Department of Oncology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University for metabolomics. Detailed clinical and pathological characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized in online supplemental table S1. These fresh puncture specimens were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection. All specimens underwent histopathological examination and grading by experienced pathologists. Additionally, we retrieved 50 paraffin-embedded sections preserved in the Department of Pathology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University for multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining. The detailed clinical characteristics of these patients are provided in online supplemental table S2. The treatment protocol of this study obtained informed consent from all patients and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 2024-SR-886).

Animal models and in vivo therapeutic studies

The animal study protocol was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: IACUC-2412043). Animal husbandry was conducted following the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. All mice were housed in air-filtered, temperature-controlled (22–24°C), light-controlled rooms with humidity between 40% and 70%, and allowed free access to a standard diet. The urethane model is a classical system widely used to study inflammation and immune microenvironment-related lung tumorigenesis and recapitulates key immune–metabolic interactions, making it suitable for investigating PD-L1-mediated metabolic rewiring and immunotherapy resistance. For the urethane-induced NSCLC model, mice were intraperitoneally injected with urethane at an inducing concentration of 1 g/kg dissolved in saline (twice a week for a total of 8 weeks). Post-treatment, we performed survival analysis on the mice. Murine Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were used for in vivo studies in immune-competent C57BL/6 mice, enabling assessment of immunotherapeutic efficacy and tumor–immune interactions in a fully functional immune system. LLC cells were subcutaneously injected, and treatment was initiated when tumors reached the indicated size. Furthermore, we observed tumor growth and conducted H&E staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, and mIF staining on sections to assess treatment efficacy.

Cell lines and cell culture conditions

Multiple human NSCLC cell lines (H1299 and SK-MES-1) were used for mechanistic in vitro studies. THP-1 cells were employed as a standardized human monocyte/macrophage model to investigate the effects of pyruvate and tumor-derived signals on macrophage senescence and immune modulation. Murine LLC cells were used for in vivo studies in immune-competent C57BL/6 mice, allowing evaluation of immunotherapy efficacy and tumor–immune interactions within a fully functional immune system. These cell lines were obtained from Shanghai Kuisai Biotechnology and cultured in medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO, Grand Island, New York, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (GIBCO) in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at 37°C with 5% CO2.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA from cells or tissues was extracted using Trizol reagent or an equivalent RNA extraction reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer or an equivalent device (A260/A280), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer or a comparable platform to calculate the RNA integrity number. mRNA enrichment/fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification were performed using a commercial library preparation kit (NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit). Libraries that passed quality assessment by the Bioanalyzer were subjected to high-throughput sequencing. Raw sequencing data (raw reads) were first subjected to quality control (QC) and filtering to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. Quality assessment was performed using Fast-QC, and read trimming was carried out using Trimmomatic or Cut-adapt. Clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using HISAT2 or STAR. Gene-level read counts were quantified using feature-Counts or HTSeq, and an expression matrix was generated. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis using the R package clusterProfiler, with false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 considered statistically significant. Enrichment results were visualized as bubble plots or bar charts to illustrate key biological processes and signaling pathways. To avoid potential bias introduced by relying solely on differential gene thresholds, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was further performed on the entire gene expression matrix. Significantly enriched gene sets were defined based on an FDR q-value <0.25, as recommended by GSEA, or according to specific study requirements.

Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis

Fresh tumor biopsy specimens from human patients with NSCLC were collected and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80°C until metabolite extraction. Metabolites were extracted using a standardized protocol, and metabolomic profiling was performed on a UPLC-ESI-Q-Orbitrap-MS system (ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography, Shimadzu Nexera X2 LC-30AD, Shimadzu, Japan) coupled with a Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, USA). For liquid chromatography separation, samples were analyzed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1×100 mm, 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, Massachusetts, USA) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (100% acetonitrile). The gradient program was: 0% B for 2 min, linear increase to 48% B over 4 min, then to 100% B over 4 min, maintained at 100% B for 2 min, followed by a rapid decrease to 0% B in 0.1 min and a 3-minute re-equilibration. The heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source parameters were: spray voltage 3.8 kV (positive)/3.2 kV (negative), capillary temperature 320°C, sheath gas (N2) flow 30 arb, auxiliary gas flow 5 arb, probe heater temperature 350°C, S-Lens RF level 50. Full mass spectrometry (MS) spectra were acquired over m/z 70–1050 at 70 000 resolution (m/z 200), and MS/MS spectra at 17 500 resolution (m/z 200) using stepped normalized collision energies of 20, 30, and 40. Maximum injection times were 100 ms (MS) and 50 ms (MS/MS), with an isolation window of 2 m/z for MS2. QC samples were prepared by pooling aliquots of all samples. Blank samples (75% acetonitrile (ACN) in water) were also included during acquisition. Raw MS data were processed using MS-DIAL for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. Metabolite identification was based on accurate mass (mass tolerance <10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra (mass tolerance <0.02 Da), matched against HMDB, MassBank, other public databases, and an in-house metabolite standard library. Only variables with ≥50% non-zero measurements in at least one group were retained for analysis. The full list of detected and quantified metabolites, including annotations and relative abundance values, is provided in online supplemental table S3.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation assay

This study used the Vazyme Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) Assay Kit (TD904) (online supplemental table S8) to investigate protein–DNA interactions. Briefly, cells were collected and nuclei were extracted. Nuclei were then incubated with ConA Beads Pro, using the beads to bind the target protein and facilitate the transposase reaction. Antibodies specifically recognizing the target protein were used. After primary and secondary antibody incubation, pA/G-Tnp Pro transposase was added to perform DNA cleavage. During this process, the transposase cleaves DNA near the target sites and adds adapter sequences to both ends. After DNA fragmentation, DNA was purified using DNA Extract Beads Pro, followed by library amplification. Finally, the generated library was amplified by PCR, purified, and quality-checked. Experimental procedures followed the kit’s standard protocol using appropriate antibodies and DNA fragmentation conditions to ensure efficient and specific library construction.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the transcriptional regulatory effects of the ER stress-related transcription factor ATF4 on the promoters of the target genes PDK4 and GLS, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed in SK-MES-1 cells. SK-MES-1 cells were seeded into 24-well plates and cotransfected with plasmids when cell confluence reached approximately 80%. Firefly luciferase reporter plasmids containing the PDK4 or GLS promoter fragments were cotransfected with a Renilla luciferase plasmid as an internal control, together with an ATF4 overexpression plasmid, while ensuring that the total amount of DNA was kept constant in each well. Transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 hours of transfection, cells were treated with the indicated stimuli according to the experimental design for an additional 24 hours. Following treatment, the culture medium was removed and cells were gently washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then lysed with lysis buffer by gentle shaking at room temperature for approximately 15 min. Cell lysates were collected for luciferase activity measurement. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (online supplemental table S8) on a multimode microplate reader. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the Firefly/Renilla ratio and normalized to the control group. All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using the Super Fast Pure Cell RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for quantitative PCR (qPCR) reagent (Vazyme). Subsequently, qPCR detection was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Kit (Vazyme) on an Applied Biosystems 7900HT system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The 2−ΔΔCt method was finally applied to determine mRNA levels, with ACTB used as the internal reference. All primers used in this study are listed in online supplemental table S5.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Total protein was extracted from NSCLC tissues and cells using a Total Protein Extraction Kit (KGB5303-10; KGI Biotechnology, China), with all procedures performed on ice to prevent protein degradation. The extracted proteins were separated using a 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel rapid preparation kit (PG113; EpiZyme). After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (3010040001; Roche) (standard wet transfer conditions: 300 mA, 40 min; membrane required preactivation with methanol). The membrane was then blocked with blocking buffer (P0023B; Beyotime) at room temperature for 30 min. After blocking, the membrane was incubated with primary antibody diluted in dilution buffer at 4°C overnight. The next day, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding species-specific secondary antibody at 25°C for 2 hours, followed by visualization of protein bands using a Super ECL detection system. Detection reagent (36208ES60; YEASEN, Shanghai, China) and a chemiluminescence gel imaging system (Vilber, Paris, France) were used, with β-actin serving as the internal loading control. The separated proteins were then analyzed by western blotting using the respective antibodies. All antibodies used in this study are listed in online supplemental table S6.

IHC and immunofluorescence staining

Tissue samples used for IHC and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde immediately after collection. The tissues were then subjected to routine dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding, and sectioned into 4-μm-thick paraffin sections. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) with heating in a microwave oven or pressure cooker. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were washed with Tris-buffered saline (TBS). For IHC, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. For both IHC and IF, sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin or normal serum at room temperature for 30–60 min to reduce non-specific binding. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After washing with TBS the following day, IHC sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, and counterstained with hematoxylin. IF sections were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies in the dark and counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. After staining, IHC sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin, whereas IF sections were mounted using an antifade mounting medium. Images of IHC-stained sections were acquired using a bright-field microscope or a whole-slide scanning system, while IF-stained sections were imaged using a fluorescence or confocal microscope under identical exposure settings. All antibodies used in this study are listed in online supplemental table S6.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

For cellular senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) detection, SA-β-gal staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After staining, cells were kept in a 37°C incubator without CO2 for 12–16 hours, followed by observation under an optical microscope.

Stable isotope tracing and metabolic flux analysis

To investigate the metabolic profile of tumor cells following stimulation with PD-1 recombinant protein, tracing was performed using ¹³C-labeled glucose and ¹³C-labeled glutamine (online supplemental table S7), respectively. First, glucose-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) medium was prepared using universally labeled ¹³C glucose to produce [U-¹³C]glucose DMEM medium at a final concentration of 4.5 g/L. Cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes, and when the cell confluence reached 50%, the normal DMEM medium was replaced with the [U-¹³C]glucose DMEM medium, followed by further incubation for 24 hours. After incubation, the cell culture plates were washed with PBS, and cells were quickly scraped into centrifuge tubes, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellets were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80°C. Samples were analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Subsequently, glutamine-free DMEM medium was prepared using [U-¹³C]-labeled glutamine to produce complete medium containing 2 mM [U-¹³C]glutamine. Cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes, and when the cell confluence reached approximately 80%, the original medium was replaced with the labeled medium described above and incubated at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 6 hours. After incubation, cells were washed twice with precooled PBS, quickly scraped, transferred into precooled centrifuge tubes, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellets were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for further use. Metabolite extraction and analysis of isotopic labeling enrichment were performed using LC-MS/MS.

Glucose uptake assay

To detect the glucose uptake capacity of tumor cells, the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG was used. Briefly, cells were starved in glucose-free medium for 24 hours, followed by incubation with 50 µM 2-NBDG at 37°C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, cells were thoroughly washed with ice-cold PBS to stop the reaction and remove extracellular dye. Subsequently, the intracellular fluorescence intensity of 2-NBDG was measured by fluorescence microscopy to quantitatively analyze cellular glucose uptake activity.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

To evaluate changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) of tumor cells, 5,5′,6,6′-Tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) was employed. Briefly, after treatment, cells were incubated with a working solution containing 10 µM JC-1 in serum-free medium at 37°C for 20 min. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with prewarmed 1× JC-1 staining buffer to remove excess probe. Subsequently, observation and detection were immediately performed using fluorescence microscopy. Under normal ΔΨm (high potential), JC-1 forms aggregates within the mitochondria, emitting red fluorescence (excitation/emission wavelengths: 525/590 nm); when the membrane potential decreases, JC-1 remains in monomeric form, emitting green fluorescence (excitation/emission wavelengths: 490/530 nm). Changes in ΔΨm were quantitatively analyzed by calculating the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity. The entire experiment was carried out under light-protected conditions.

Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test

The Seahorse XF Mitochondrial Stress Test (103015-100; Agilent Technologies) (online supplemental table S8) was used to measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR). OCR measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2×10⁵ cells were seeded into an XF microplate. One hour before the assay, the medium was replaced with Seahorse assay medium (103575-100 Seahorse XF DMEM Medium, 103577-100 Seahorse XF Glucose Solution, 103578-100 Seahorse XF Pyruvate Solution, 103579-100 Seahorse XF Glutamine Solution), and the plate was incubated in a non-CO2 incubator. The Mitochondrial Stress Test was performed following sequential injections of 1.5 µM oligomycin, 1 µM carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A.

Pyruvate concentration measurement

For pyruvate content detection in tumor tissues, tumor tissues were removed and placed in precooled PBS, washed, then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Precisely 100 mg of tumor tissue was weighed and homogenized on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. Pyruvate content was detected according to the instructions of the Pyruvate Assay Kit (BC2205; Solarbio). Data were captured using a multifunctional microplate reader by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 520 nm, and pyruvate concentration was quantified according to the kit instructions.

Flow cytometry analysis

All reagents and antibodies used for flow cytometry analysis were purchased from BioLegend. For in vitro cell staining, harvested cells were incubated with target antibodies in staining buffer (BD Stain Buffer) for 1 hour. For intratumoral flow cytometry, tumors were collected, minced into small pieces, and incubated in precooled PBS containing collagenase, hyaluronidase, and DNase for 1 hour. The tissue fragments were then passed through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Lymphocytes were isolated using a Mouse Lymphocyte Separation Kit and incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in staining buffer protected from light for 1 hour. Fluorescence was detected using a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (V.10.8.1). All antibodies used in this study are listed in online supplemental table S6.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS V.28.0 (SPSS), GraphPad Prism V.9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, California, USA), Origin 2024 (OriginLab, Northampton, Massachusetts, USA), and R software (V.4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) curves were plotted using Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank test). All assays were performed at least three times, and data were presented as mean±SD. Unless otherwise indicated, all statistical analyses were performed using data obtained from independent biological replicates. Quantitative data from experiments were derived from biological replicates rather than technical replicates. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation analyses were used to assess correlations between continuous variables and ordinal variables, respectively, and a two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups. The unpaired two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze single-cell sequencing data. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 (*p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ns, not significant). The sample size was determined based on the levels and consistency between different groups. Unless stated otherwise in the figure legends, the statistical test used was the Student’s t-test.

Results

PD-L1-mediated pyruvate accumulation drives primary resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy

To elucidate the metabolic determinants underlying immunotherapy resistance, we performed metabolomic profiling on tumor samples derived from patients with NSCLC undergoing anti-PD-1 therapy (figure 1A). Principal component analysis revealed a distinct metabolic segregation between treatment-sensitive (responders) and resistant (non-responders) patients (figure 1B). Differential metabolite analysis identified 15 significantly altered metabolites between the two groups (online supplemental table S3). Among these candidates, pyruvate was one of the most significantly upregulated metabolites in the resistant cohort. Given that pyruvate serves as a central metabolic intermediate linking glycolysis and the TCA cycle16 and plays a pivotal role in metabolic reprogramming during tumor progression and immune regulation, this observation prompted us to further investigate its potential contribution to immunotherapy resistance. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted ‘pyruvate metabolism’ and ‘glycolysis or gluconeogenesis’ as among the most significantly altered pathways (figure 1C). Subsequent quantitative analysis confirmed that intratumoral pyruvate concentrations were significantly elevated in the resistant group compared with the sensitive group (figure 1D).

We next investigated the correlation between pyruvate levels and the immune microenvironment. IF staining and correlation analysis revealed that pyruvate content was positively correlated with the expression of PD-L1 and the CD8+ T-cell exhaustion markers PD-1 and TIM3, while displaying a significant negative correlation with the cytotoxic marker granzyme B (GZMB) (figure 1E,F). These clinical data suggest that pyruvate accumulation is a hallmark of an immunosuppressive, resistant microenvironment.

To determine whether the interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 directly drives this metabolic shift, we treated lung cancer cell lines with recombinant PD-1 protein in vitro to mimic PD-1 ligation. The results showed that exposure to recombinant PD-1 significantly elevated intracellular pyruvate levels (figure 1G). Importantly, this elevation was abrogated in PD-L1 knockdown cells (shCD274 sequences listed in online supplemental table S4), confirming that the observed pyruvate accumulation is dependent on PD-L1 signaling (figure 1H,I).

To address whether pyruvate accumulation in the TME is influenced by PD-L1 expression in NSCLC models with negative PD-L1 expression, we generated PD-L1-depleted mimic TPS=0 tumors, PD-L1 knockout was established using sgCD274 (sequences listed in online supplemental table S4). As shown in online supplemental figure 1A,B, PD-L1 expression was efficiently abolished in SK-MES-1 and H1299 cells. Under this PD-L1-negative condition, PD-1 stimulation failed to promote pyruvate accumulation in either SK-MES-1 or H1299 cells, as evidenced by significantly reduced pyruvate concentrations in sgPD-L1 cells compared with the corresponding controls (online supplemental figure 1C,D). Consistently, in the subcutaneous LLC tumor model, IF staining confirmed the loss of PD-L1 expression in LLC-sgPD-L1 tumors (online supplemental figure 1E,F), and these PD-L1-negative tumors exhibited markedly lower intratumoral pyruvate levels than LLC-sgCtrl tumors (online supplemental figure 1G). These results suggest that pyruvate accumulation in the NSCLC TME is dependent, at least in part, on tumor cell PD-L1 expression, and that tumors with negative PD-L1 expression are less likely to sustain PD-1/PD-L1-associated pyruvate enrichment.

Finally, to verify the functional impact of pyruvate on therapeutic efficacy, we used both subcutaneous xenograft and urethane-induced autochthonous lung cancer mouse models undergoing anti-PD-1 therapy. Exogenous supplementation of pyruvate significantly compromised the antitumor efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment, leading to increased tumor burden (figure 1J–Q) and shortened survival (figure 1R). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PD-L1-mediated pyruvate accumulation contributes to primary resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy.

PD-L1 reverse signaling induces ER stress via the PERK–ATF4–CHOP axis

To delineate the molecular mechanisms governing PD-L1-mediated metabolic reprogramming, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on SK-MES-1 cells treated with recombinant PD-1 protein or a control (figure 2A). Heatmap visualization revealed that PD-1 treatment induced a distinct shift in the gene expression profile of tumor cells (figure 2B). KEGG pathway analysis identified ‘protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum’ as the top enriched pathway among differentially expressed genes (figure 2C). Furthermore, GSEA corroborated the activation of ER stress-associated signatures, specifically highlighting the significant enrichment of the ‘ATF4 activates genes in response to ER stress’ pathway (figure 2D).

We subsequently validated these findings at the protein level. Western blot analysis demonstrated that, analogous to the effect of thapsigargin (Thaps; a standard ER stress inducer), treatment with recombinant PD-1 protein significantly elevated the phosphorylation levels of PERK and eIF2α, while concurrently upregulating the protein abundance of ATF4 and CHOP in both H1299 and SK-MES-1 cells. However, the use of shRNA for anti-PD-L1 significantly reduced the protein levels of phosphorylated PERK and eIF2α (figure 2E).17 18 Notably, among the three branches of the unfolded protein response, the PERK signaling axis appeared to be the most predominantly activated. qPCR assays further confirmed a significant surge in the mRNA levels of its key downstream effector, CHOP (figure 2F,G). We further examined whether negative PD-L1 expression is sufficient to prevent activation of downstream ER stress signaling, particularly the PERK–ATF4 pathway. Western blot analysis showed that PD-L1 depletion markedly attenuated PD-1-induced activation of ER stress markers in both SK-MES-1 and H1299 cells, including phosphorylation of PERK and Eif2α, as well as induction of ATF4 (online supplemental figure 2A,B). In contrast, treatment with Thaps restored robust activation of ER stress signaling, confirming that the ER stress machinery remained functionally intact in these cells (online supplemental figure 2A,B). Consistently, qPCR analysis showed that CHOP mRNA was not significantly induced by PD-1 stimulation in PD-L1-depleted cells, whereas Thaps markedly increased CHOP expression in both SK-MES-1 and H1299 cells (online supplemental figure 2C,D). Together, these findings indicate that PD-L1-negative expression prevents PD-1-mediated activation of the PERK–Eif2α–ATF4 ER stress axis, supporting the notion that PD-L1 is required for the downstream ER stress response observed in PD-L1-positive tumor cells.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of this mechanism, we examined tumor tissue samples from the aforementioned cohort of patients with lung cancer. IF staining revealed that tumors with high pyruvate content displayed markedly elevated levels of p-PERK and CHOP expression. Crucially, these regions of high ER stress marker expression spatially colocalized with a phenotype of CD8+ T-cell exhaustion (characterized by high TIM3 and low GZMB expression) (figure 2H,I), suggesting an intrinsic link between tumor ER stress and an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

We next investigated whether the ER stress response is the direct driver of pyruvate accumulation. Consistent with the effects of Thaps, recombinant PD-1 protein treatment significantly increased intracellular pyruvate concentrations (figure 2J,K). Critically, this PD-1-induced pyruvate surge was effectively abrogated by the chemical chaperone 4-PBA or the specific PERK inhibitor PERK-IN-2 (figure 2J,K). Furthermore, Seahorse cell energy metabolism analysis indicated that recombinant PD-1 protein treatment suppressed the mitochondrial OCR in tumor cells, implying a blockade of pyruvate entry into mitochondrial oxidation.19 20 Notably, PERK inhibition was able to restore this metabolic defect (figure 2L–N).

To further verify the specificity of PERK-ATF4 signaling in lung cancer in vivo, we established an LLC tumor-bearing mouse model and treated the mice with the ER stress inducer Thaps or the inhibitor 4-PBA (online supplemental figure 3A). In vivo imaging and tumor weight measurements showed that, compared with the vehicle group, Thaps treatment significantly promoted tumor progression, evidenced by stronger bioluminescence signals and increased tumor mass; conversely, tumor growth was inhibited in the 4-PBA treatment group compared with the Thaps treatment group (online supplemental figure 3B–D). IF staining confirmed the in vivo targeting effect of the drugs: the proportion of p-PERK and CHOP-positive cells significantly increased in the Thaps-treated group, verifying the activation of the ER stress state in vivo (online supplemental figure 3E,G). IHC staining showed that Thaps-induced ER stress significantly reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration within the tumor (online supplemental figure 3F, right panel). Importantly, metabolic analysis revealed that pharmacological induction of ER stress by Thaps alone was sufficient to cause a significant rise in intratumoral pyruvate concentrations (online supplemental figure 3H), establishing a direct causal relationship between ER stress and pyruvate accumulation. Additionally, we assessed changes in the immune microenvironment. Collectively, these results indicate that activation of the ER stress pathway is sufficient to drive metabolic reprogramming and immune exclusion, recapitulating key phenotypes of PD-1 resistance. These data demonstrate that PERK-ATF4-CHOP-mediated ER stress is a requisite condition for PD-L1-induced metabolic remodeling and pyruvate accumulation.

The PERK–ATF4 axis transactivates PDK4 to block pyruvate oxidation and drive metabolic rewiring

To identify the key effector linking ER stress to aberrant pyruvate metabolism, we intersected the list of upregulated genes from our RNA-seq data with the ‘pyruvate metabolism’ gene set. PDK4 emerged as the sole overlapping gene (figure 3A). We first examined whether the expression of PDK4 is regulated by the PD-1/PD-L1–PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 axis. Western blot analysis showed that recombinant PD-1 treatment markedly increased PDK4 protein levels in both SK-MES-1 and H1299 cells. However, PD-L1 knockdown substantially attenuated PD-1-induced PDK4 upregulation. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of PERK by PERK-IN-2 or blockade of integrated stress response signaling by ISRIB also reduced PDK4 expression, whereas Thaps increased PDK4 levels. These results indicate that PD-1/PD-L1 signaling promotes PDK4 expression in lung cancer cells through activation of the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 ER stress pathway (figure 3B,C). Bioinformatics analysis predicted the presence of a canonical ATF4-binding motif within the PDK4 promoter region (figure 3D). Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that recombinant PD-1 protein treatment significantly enhanced PDK4 promoter activity.21 Notably, this enhancement was effectively abrogated by treatment with the PERK inhibitor (PERK-IN-2) or the ISR inhibitor (ISRIB) (figure 3E).

We further validated this regulatory axis at both the mRNA and protein levels. Results from qPCR and IF assays demonstrated that recombinant PD-1 protein significantly upregulated PDK4 expression in tumor cells (figure 3F–I). However, blockade of the PERK–ATF4 signaling pathway (using PERK-IN-2 or ISRIB) significantly attenuated the PD-1-induced upregulation of PDK4 (figure 3F–I).

Next, we established that elevated PDK4 expression is functionally responsible for pyruvate accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction. Treatment with the specific PDK4 inhibitor M77976 completely reversed the intracellular pyruvate accumulation induced by PD-1 (figure 3J,K). Seahorse metabolic analysis further demonstrated that the suppression of basal respiration and maximal respiratory capacity caused by PD-1 treatment was significantly rescued by M77976 (figure 3L–N), indicating that PDK4-mediated inhibition of the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex is critical for this metabolic blockade.

We then mapped the metabolic flux landscape using [U-13C]-glucose and [U-13C]-glutamine tracing technologies (figure 3O).22 Glucose tracing revealed that in the recombinant PD-1 protein treatment group, glucose-derived pyruvate (M+3) significantly increased, whereas citrate (M+2) entering the TCA cycle significantly decreased, substantiating the blockade of pyruvate entry into mitochondria by PDK4 (figure 3P). Intriguingly, glutamine tracing demonstrated a significant increase in glutamine-derived TCA cycle intermediates (such as succinate M+4 and malate M+4) as well as pyruvate (M+3) in the PD-1 treated group (figure 3Q). This indicates that following the blockade of glycolytic carbon entry into the TCA cycle by PDK4, tumor cells undergo metabolic reprogramming, shifting their dependency towards glutamine anaplerosis for survival and generating additional pyruvate via the malic enzyme pathway.

Finally, we assessed glucose uptake capacity and mitochondrial function across different treatment groups (online supplemental figure 4A). 2-NBDG fluorescence assays showed that recombinant PD-1 protein treatment significantly reduced glucose uptake in tumor cells (online supplemental figure 4A,B), aligning with our previous observation of a metabolic shift from glycolysis to glutaminolysis. However, combined treatment with the PERK inhibitor (PERK-IN-2) restored glucose uptake to near-normal levels (online supplemental figure 4B), indicating that the substrate preference was successfully corrected.

Furthermore, we evaluated ΔΨm using the JC-1 probe. The results showed that cells in the PD-1 treated group exhibited evident mitochondrial depolarization (increased green fluorescent monomers and decreased red fluorescent aggregates), suggesting impaired mitochondrial function (online supplemental figure 4C,D). Importantly, the addition of PERK inhibitor (PERK-IN-2) significantly restored ΔΨm (increased JC-1 aggregate/monomer ratio) (online supplemental figure 4C,D). These results further corroborate that tumor cells undergo ER stress-mediated metabolic rewiring on PD-1 engagement.

ATF4 directly transactivates GLS to promote glutaminolysis and pyruvate production

To further elucidate the molecular basis of the ‘glutamine addiction’ metabolic signature observed in tumor cells under PD-1 treatment, we first analyzed intracellular metabolite alterations. Targeted metabolomics profiling revealed that recombinant PD-1 protein treatment resulted in a significant depletion of intracellular L-glutamine, accompanied by a marked increase in its downstream metabolites, L-glutamic acid and α-ketoglutarate (figure 4A,C). Notably, the glutamate-to-glutamine ratio was significantly elevated in the PD-1 treated group (figure 4B), strongly indicating the activation of GLS-mediated glutaminolysis.

Given the previously established activation of the PERK–ATF4 pathway, we hypothesized that ATF4 might directly regulate GLS expression. To test this, we performed CUT&Tag assays.23 24 Sequencing peaks clearly demonstrated a significant enrichment of ATF4-binding signals at the GLS gene promoter region following recombinant PD-1 protein treatment (figure 4D,E), providing direct evidence that GLS is a transcriptional target of ATF4.

We initially evaluated the impact of the PD-1/PD-L1–PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 signaling axis on GLS expression by using western blot analysis. Our results demonstrated that PD-1 treatment led to an upregulation of GLS expression, whereas this effect was effectively abrogated by shRNA-mediated PD-L1 knockdown, pharmacological inhibition of PERK, or treatment with ISRIB (figure 4F,G). We subsequently validated this regulatory mechanism through a multidimensional approach. Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that the GLS promoter contains functional ATF4-binding sites, and its promoter activity was significantly enhanced by PD-1 treatment (figure 4H,I). Consistently, recombinant PD-1 protein treatment significantly upregulated GLS expression at both mRNA and protein levels (figure 4J–M). However, blockade of the upstream PERK-ATF4 signaling using a PERK inhibitor (PERK-IN-2) or an ISR inhibitor (ISRIB) effectively abolished this upregulation (figure 4J–M), substantiating the existence of the PD-L1–PERK–ATF4–GLS regulatory axis.

Finally, to determine whether GLS-mediated glutaminolysis represents another critical source driving pyruvate accumulation, we used the specific GLS inhibitor CB839. The results showed that CB839 treatment significantly attenuated the intracellular pyruvate levels induced by PD-1 compared with the control group (figure 4N,O). Furthermore, we corroborated the clinical relevance of these findings by analyzing patient specimens, confirming a positive correlation between PDK4/GLS expression levels and pyruvate content in tumor tissues (figure 4P,Q). Integrating these findings with the results from figure 3, we conclude that PD-L1 reverse signaling orchestrates pyruvate accumulation via a ‘dual-hit’ strategy: simultaneously ‘blocking the exit’ via PDK4 (inhibiting TCA cycle oxidation) and ‘fueling the supply’ via GLS (promoting glutamine conversion). These two mechanisms synergistically drive metabolic reprogramming and the formation of a drug-resistant microenvironment (figure 4R).

Tumor-secreted pyruvate drives macrophage senescence and PD-L1 upregulation via the MCT1–ROS–STAT3 axis

To explore how tumor-derived pyruvate remodels the immune microenvironment, we first treated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with tumor cell lysates and performed RNA-seq (figure 5A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that signaling pathways related to ‘cellular senescence’ and ‘reactive oxygen species (ROS)’ were significantly enriched in the treated immune cells (figure 5B), suggesting that pyruvate may induce senescence in specific immune cell subsets. To determine which immune cell populations are most responsive to pyruvate accumulation, PBMCs isolated from healthy donors were treated with pyruvate and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry (online supplemental figure 5A). Assessment of intracellular ROS levels revealed that pyruvate treatment induced only a modest increase in ROS-positive lymphocytes, with approximately 11.3% of cells exhibiting ROS accumulation. In contrast, monocytes displayed a markedly elevated ROS response, with approximately 88.1% of cells becoming ROS positive following pyruvate exposure (online supplemental figure 5B–D). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that the proportion of ROS-positive cells was significantly higher in monocytes than in lymphocytes after pyruvate treatment (online supplemental figure 5E). These findings indicate that monocytes are substantially more sensitive to pyruvate-induced oxidative stress than lymphocytes, suggesting that pyruvate accumulation may preferentially modulate the monocyte/macrophage compartment within the immune microenvironment through ROS-dependent mechanisms. To validate this process, we detected the mRNA levels of ROS-related genes and senescence marker genes in macrophages derived from THP-1 cells (online supplemental figure 6). Abusalamah et al demonstrated that pyruvate modulates macrophage inflammatory responses during influenza A virus infection, highlighting the context-dependent immunoregulatory functions of pyruvate.25

We validated this finding in the human monocyte/macrophage cell line THP-1. Confocal microscopy imaging demonstrated that pyruvate treatment induced significant ROS accumulation (indicated by green fluorescence) and enhanced SA-β-gal activity (figure 5C,D). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in both ROS levels (figure 5E) and the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells (figure 5F). Furthermore, mIF staining of mouse subcutaneous tumors revealed that the classic cell cycle arrest protein p16 (a senescence marker) was significantly upregulated following pyruvate treatment (figure 5G).

To dissect the underlying molecular mechanism, we sought to determine the necessity of pyruvate entry and ROS induction. Whether in vitro or in vivo, treatment with the MCT1-specific inhibitor AZD3965 (blocking pyruvate uptake) or the ROS scavenger ROS-IN-1 significantly inhibited the pyruvate-induced enhancement of SA-β-gal activity and p16 upregulation (figure 5C–G,I). These results indicate that tumor-secreted pyruvate enters macrophages via the MCT1 transporter and drives cellular senescence by inducing mitochondrial ROS accumulation.

We further corroborated this phenomenon in clinical specimens. mIF staining showed that in patient tumor tissues with high pyruvate content, macrophages (CD68+) exhibited significantly elevated expression of the senescence marker p16 (figure 5H,J).

Finally, we investigated the functional consequences of macrophage senescence. Pyruvate-induced senescent macrophages displayed significantly upregulated transcription and protein expression of interleukin 6 (IL-6) (figure 5K) and PD-L1 (figure 5L–N), accompanied by elevated levels of STAT3 phosphorylation (figure 5O). Notably, treatment with the senolytic drug ABT737 (which specifically eliminates senescent cells) or a ROS inhibitor effectively abolished the upregulation of IL-6, PD-L1, and p-STAT3 (figure 5K,O). Further analysis of clinical specimens confirmed that in the TME of patients with NSCLC who had high pyruvate levels, macrophages undergo senescence accompanied by PD-L1 upregulation (figure 5P). Collectively, these data unveil a malignant intercellular communication mechanism: tumor-secreted pyruvate causes metabolic poisoning and senescence in macrophages, and these senescent macrophages subsequently upregulate PD-L1 via the ROS–STAT3 pathway, thereby assisting the tumor in maintaining an immunosuppressive microenvironment (figure 5Q).

Given that pyruvate has been reported to regulate PD-L1 expression through the BMP4–SMAD1/5–IRF1 signaling axis in certain cellular contexts,26 we next investigated whether this pathway contributes to pyruvate-mediated macrophage reprogramming. To this end, THP-1-derived macrophages and bone marrow-derived macrophages were treated with pyruvate, followed by assessment of BMP4–SMAD1/5–IRF1 pathway activation. However, pyruvate treatment failed to induce BMP4 expression, SMAD1/5 phosphorylation, IRF1 accumulation, or PD-L1 upregulation in either macrophage model (online supplemental figure 7A,B). These findings suggest that the BMP4–SMAD1/5–IRF1 signaling pathway is not activated by pyruvate in macrophages and therefore is unlikely to mediate the macrophage phenotypes observed in our study. Consistent with previous reports demonstrating context-dependent immunomodulatory functions of pyruvate, our data indicate that the biological effects of pyruvate on tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) occur through mechanisms distinct from the BMP4–SMAD1/5–IRF1 axis. Instead, the subsequent experiments support a model in which pyruvate promotes macrophage dysfunction predominantly through ROS accumulation and senescence-associated reprogramming.

Targeting the PDK4/GLS–pyruvate axis overcomes resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy by remodeling the immune microenvironment

To translate our mechanistic findings into potential therapeutic strategies, we evaluated whether pharmacological targeting of PDK4 and/or GLS could sensitize tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo. We established LLC tumor-bearing mouse models and randomized them into cohorts receiving anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy, or combinations with the PDK4 inhibitor (M77976) and the GLS inhibitor (CB839) (figure 6A).

Tumor growth kinetics revealed that anti-PD-1 monotherapy exhibited limited tumor-suppressive efficacy compared with the control group, consistent with the intrinsic immune resistance of the LLC model. However, coadministration of M77976 or CB839 significantly inhibited tumor growth (figure 6B,C). Notably, the triple combination regimen (anti-PD-1+M77976+CB839) demonstrated the most potent antitumor efficacy, leading to near-complete tumor stasis. This suggests that simultaneous blockade of pyruvate generation (via GLS) and the restoration of its oxidative disposal (via PDK4 inhibition) exert a synergistic therapeutic effect.

To confirm on-target engagement, we assessed intratumoral metabolite profiles. The results confirmed that the combination therapy effectively abrogated the intratumoral pyruvate accumulation, restoring levels to baseline (figure 6D).

Subsequently, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the immune microenvironment following metabolic intervention. mIF staining and quantification demonstrated a significant reduction in the infiltration of senescent macrophages (F4/80+p16+) in the combination therapy group compared with the anti-PD-1 monotherapy group (figure 6E,F), indicating that metabolic intervention successfully disrupted the ‘pyruvate–macrophage senescence’ immunosuppressive axis. Flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of PD-L1+ macrophages decreased in the tumors of mice treated with the combination therapy (figure 6G,H).

Finally, immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses indicated that this microenvironmental remodeling significantly reinvigorated CD8+ T-cell antitumor activity (figure 6I,J). Specifically, flow cytometry analyses indicated the proportion of exhausted T cells (CD8+ TIM3+) was markedly reduced in the combination therapy group (figure 6K,L). Collectively, these in vivo data provide compelling evidence that depleting TME pyruvate via dual inhibition of PDK4 and GLS effectively prevents macrophage senescence and restores T-cell function, thereby overcoming primary resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy.

To determine whether therapeutic anti-PD-L1 ligation induces PD-L1-associated biological responses comparable to those triggered by physiological PD-1-mediated engagement of PD-L1, we systematically evaluated its effects on pyruvate metabolism, ER stress signaling, and macrophage senescence. αPD-L1 treatment did not significantly alter pyruvate concentrations in either SK-MES-1 or H1299 cells (online supplemental figure 8A,B), nor did it affect intratumoral pyruvate accumulation in vivo (online supplemental figure 8H). Consistently, αPD-L1 treatment failed to activate the PERK-mediated ER stress response, as evidenced by the absence of changes in P-PERK, P-eIF2α, ATF4, CHOP, and other ER stress-related markers in both cell lines (online supplemental figure 8C,D). Moreover, expression of the downstream metabolic mediators PDK4 and GLS remained largely unchanged following αPD-L1 treatment (online supplemental figure 8E,F).

We next investigated whether αPD-L1 therapy could promote the immunosuppressive phenotype associated with senescent TAMs. Notably, neither the abundance of F4/80+P16+ macrophages nor the proportion of senescent macrophages within the TME was altered following αPD-L1 treatment (online supplemental figure 8I,J). Furthermore, blockade of pyruvate metabolism using either the PDK4 inhibitor M77976 or the GLS inhibitor CB839 did not provide additional therapeutic benefit when combined with αPD-L1 treatment (online supplemental figure 8K–N). Collectively, these findings indicate that anti-PD-L1 therapy does not trigger pyruvate accumulation, ER stress activation, or macrophage senescence, and therefore does not engage the PD-1-driven metabolic–stress signaling cascade identified in our study. These observations highlight that the PD-1/PD-L1–PERK–ATF4–PDK4/GLS axis represents a unique mechanism underlying primary resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy rather than a general consequence of PD-1/PD-L1 pathway blockade.

Discussion

Our study uncovers a novel mechanism of primary resistance to immunotherapy driven by PD-L1 reverse signaling, providing a metabolic explanation for treatment failure. We systematically delineate a signaling cascade extending from immune checkpoint engagement to TME remodeling—specifically, a ‘metabolic rewiring–immunosuppression’ axis. We demonstrate that PD-1 ligation to PD-L1 triggers ER stress, which promotes metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells via the upregulation of PDK4 and GLS. This metabolic shift leads to the accumulation of pyruvate within the TME, causing senescence in TAMs. These senescent TAMs subsequently reinforce the immunosuppressive niche by upregulating surface PD-L1 expression. This mechanism suggests that immunotherapy does not merely unleash T cells from inhibition; rather, under persistent therapeutic pressure, it induces profound metabolic adaptation in tumor cells. This provides a rational explanation for the clinical observation that a subset of patients initially benefit from therapy but eventually develop primary resistance.27–29

Previous research on the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway has focused almost exclusively on its inhibitory effects on T-cell function, while the active response of tumor cells in this interaction has been largely overlooked.30–32 A key finding of our study is that PD-1/PD-L1 engagement not only ‘turns off’ T cells but simultaneously ‘turns on’ intrinsic stress signaling pathways within tumor cells. Although literature suggests that the highly conserved intracellular domain of PD-L1 possesses signal transduction potential, its biological function has lacked systematic elucidation. Our study is the first to explicitly link PD-L1 reverse signaling to PERK-ATF4-dependent ER stress, revealing that immune checkpoint antibody therapy may mimic persistent immune pressure, thereby triggering a stress response in tumor cells. Importantly, our findings further suggest that PD-L1 expression is not merely a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response but also a functional determinant of downstream metabolic signaling. Tumors with high PD-L1 expression possess a greater capacity to transduce PD-L1 reverse signaling on PD-1 engagement, resulting in stronger activation of the PERK–ATF4 pathway and the downstream PDK4/GLS–pyruvate axis. Consistent with this concept, PD-L1-depleted tumors generated using sgPD-L1 (mimicking TPS=0 status) exhibited marked attenuation of ER stress activation and metabolic remodeling, indicating that the integrity of this signaling cascade depends on tumor cell PD-L1 expression. These observations provide a mechanistic explanation for how different PD-L1 expression states may influence tumor metabolic adaptation and immune microenvironment remodeling. This finding provides a new theoretical basis for understanding the distinct biological consequences induced by antibody-based therapies versus small-molecule interventions at the cellular level.

Regarding the characteristics of metabolic reprogramming associated with primary resistance, we observed a highly coordinated ‘dual-regulation’ strategy in tumor cells, rather than simple metabolic suppression. On one hand, PERK-ATF4 signaling significantly upregulates PDK4, which inhibits PDH activity to restrict pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle, thereby reducing the mitochondrial oxidative burden. On the other hand, it upregulates GLS to enhance glutaminolysis, providing an alternative carbon source (anaplerosis) to the TCA cycle to sustain essential survival needs. This metabolic mode of ‘throttling the outflow while opening the source’—inhibiting glucose oxidation while enhancing glutamine compensation—represents an adaptive survival strategy of tumor cells under immune attack. However, the inevitable consequence of this strategy is the blockade of pyruvate export and its massive intracellular accumulation, laying the critical groundwork for subsequent microenvironmental remodeling.

It is noteworthy that metabolic reprogramming in NSCLC is influenced by multiple factors, including oncogenic signaling, microenvironmental stress, and genomic alterations. Among these, mutations in metabolic regulators such as LKB1 (STK11) have been reported to alter glucose and glutamine metabolism, reshape the tumor immune microenvironment, and contribute to resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. These findings highlight the complex and multifactorial nature of metabolic adaptation in NSCLC. However, no LKB1-mutant cases were identified in our metabolomic discovery cohort, suggesting that the PD-L1–ER stress–PDK4/GLS–pyruvate axis described in this study is not solely attributable to known genomic drivers of metabolic reprogramming. Instead, our data support a previously unrecognized mechanism in which PD-L1 reverse signaling directly couples immune checkpoint engagement to tumor metabolic remodeling and subsequent immunosuppressive microenvironment formation. Importantly, our findings do not exclude the possibility that established metabolic regulators, including LKB1, may interact with or modulate this pathway. Rather, they suggest that metabolic rewiring associated with primary immunotherapy resistance can arise through multiple mechanisms, of which PD-L1-mediated reverse signaling represents a distinct and previously underappreciated contributor. Future studies integrating metabolomics, genomic profiling, and spatial immune analyses will be necessary to determine how PD-L1 reverse signaling intersects with other metabolic regulatory networks and to identify additional mechanisms driving metabolic adaptation and immunotherapy resistance in NSCLC.

The core innovation of this study lies in redefining pyruvate: we identify it not merely as a metabolic intermediate, but as a signaling moiety with potent immunoregulatory functions. Distinct from the well-established role of lactate in immunosuppression, we consistently found—in both NSCLC clinical samples and functional assays—that pyruvate is significantly enriched in immunotherapy-resistant tumors and directly acts on TAMs. Mechanistically, excessive pyruvate uptake induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS overproduction in macrophages, driving a cellular senescence phenotype. These senescent-like macrophages exhibit typical senescence-associated secretory phenotype features, significantly upregulating PD-L1 expression via the secretion of IL-6 and activation of the IL-6–JAK–STAT3 pathway. This discovery extends the concept of ‘metabolite-mediated immune regulation’ and offers a mechanistic explanation for the clinical phenomenon of ‘high PD-L1 expression with lack of response to immunotherapy.’

From a translational perspective, our study underscores that immunotherapy resistance is not solely a failure of the immune system but is accompanied by the activation of specific metabolic adaptation programs. This insight renders metabolic intervention a rational and necessary complementary strategy. We demonstrate that specific inhibitors targeting PDK4 or GLS can effectively deplete TME pyruvate levels, thereby disrupting the pro-resistant metabolic-immune positive feedback loop. Notably, the GLS inhibitor CB-839 has already entered multiple clinical trials,33–35 providing a realistic foundation for the clinical implementation of ‘metabolic-immune combination therapy.’ Compared with broad-spectrum metabolic inhibition, targeting specific metabolic nodes may enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity. An additional implication of our findings is that physiological PD-1 engagement and therapeutic PD-L1 blockade may exert fundamentally distinct biological consequences despite involving the same receptor–ligand pair. In our model, physiological PD-1 binding to tumor cell PD-L1 initiates reverse signaling, activates PERK–ATF4-dependent ER stress, and drives metabolic rewiring that promotes pyruvate accumulation and macrophage-mediated immunosuppression. This process provides a mechanistic framework for understanding primary resistance, whereby pre-existing PD-1/PD-L1 interactions within the TME contribute to the establishment of a metabolically adapted and immunosuppressive state before effective antitumor immunity can be restored. In contrast, anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies primarily function by preventing ligand engagement and may therefore be less likely to trigger the same reverse-signaling-dependent metabolic program. This distinction may partly explain why simultaneous targeting of both PD-1 and PD-L1 has shown limited additional clinical benefit in many settings and highlights the unique nature of the PD-L1 reverse-signaling pathway identified in this study. Collectively, these observations suggest that metabolic rewiring downstream of physiological PD-1/PD-L1 engagement represents a previously underappreciated mechanism of primary immunotherapy resistance and a potentially actionable therapeutic vulnerability.

Although this study systematically elucidates the PD-1/PD-L1-driven metabolic-immune regulatory network, certain limitations remain. These include the inherent differences between murine models and the human immune system, as well as the need for larger scale, prospective clinical studies to validate the stability and specificity of pyruvate as a biomarker for predicting immunotherapy efficacy and prognosis. Overall, our study proposes a new conceptual framework: tumor cells actively shape an immunosuppressive microenvironment through metabolic reprogramming as a crucial mechanism for evading immune surveillance. The ‘ER stress–PDK4/GLS–pyruvate–TAM senescence–immunosuppression’ axis defined here expands our understanding of tumor metabolic plasticity and provides novel potential targets for precision immunotherapy and resistance intervention.
