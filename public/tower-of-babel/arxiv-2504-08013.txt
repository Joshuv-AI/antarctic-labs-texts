Abstract
. The stability problem in Ulam’s sense has recently been explored in locally convex cone environments, as shown in [ 8 , 9 , 10 ] . In continuation of this research direction, our work examines the stability properties of the quadratic functional equation 2 ​ f ​ ( x + y 2 ) + 2 ​ f ​ ( x − y 2 ) = f ⁡ ( x ) + f ⁡ ( y ) 2f\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+2f\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)=f(x)+f(y) in such structures. Our investigation both strengthens the mathematical underpinnings of stability theory and provides new perspectives on interactions between certain operators and locally convex spaces. A cone is a mathematical structure defined by a set

\mathscr{P}
equipped with two operations: addition, denoted as
(a,b)\mapsto a+b
, and scalar multiplication, denoted as
(\lambda,a)\mapsto\lambda a
, where

\lambda
is a non-negative real number. The addition operation must satisfy the properties of associativity and commutativity, and there exists a neutral element

0\in\mathscr{P}
such that:

•

(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)

for all

a,b,c\in\mathscr{P}
,

•

a+b=b+a

for all

a,b\in\mathscr{P}
,

•

a+0=a

for all

a\in\mathscr{P}
.

Scalar multiplication must adhere to the standard associative and distributive rules, meaning:

•

\lambda(\mu a)=(\lambda\mu)a\quad\text{\rm for all}~\lambda,\mu\geqslant 0~~\text{\rm and}~~\text{\rm all}~a\in\mathscr{P}
,

•

(\lambda+\mu)a=\lambda a+\mu a\quad\text{\rm for all}~\lambda,\mu\geqslant 0~~\text{\rm and}~~\text{\rm all}~a\in\mathscr{P}
,

•

\lambda(a+b)=\lambda a+\lambda b\quad\text{\rm for all}~\lambda\geqslant 0~~\text{\rm and}~~\text{\rm all}~a,b\in\mathscr{P}
,

•

1a=a\quad\text{\rm for all}~a\in\mathscr{P}
,

•

0a=0\quad\text{\rm for all}~a\in\mathscr{P}
.

This structure ensures that the set
\mathscr{P}
behaves consistently under both addition and scalar multiplication, maintaining the necessary algebraic properties.
Vector spaces defined over the field of real numbers naturally qualify as cones under this definition. However, cones differ from vector spaces in two key ways: first, they do not require the existence of additive inverses for their elements, and second, scalar multiplication is restricted to non-negative real numbers. Unlike in vector spaces, the property

0a=0
must be explicitly stated for cones, as it does not automatically follow from the other defining conditions. This distinction highlights the broader nature of cones compared to vector spaces, as they relax certain structural requirements while maintaining specific algebraic properties. This concept is illustrated by a straightforward example from [31]. Consider the set
\mathscr{P}=\{0,1\}
with addition defined as
0+0=0,~0+1=1+0=1
, and

1+1=1
. Scalar multiplication by non-negative real numbers is defined as

\lambda a=a
for all
a\in\mathscr{P}
and
\lambda\geqslant 0
. Clearly, all the previously mentioned axioms hold except for the last one, and in this case,

0.1=1
. It can be readily demonstrated that the neutral element
0\in\mathscr{P}
for the addition operation is unique. The inclusion of the final axiom is crucial, as it ensures the property
\lambda 0=0
holds for all
\lambda\geqslant 0
(see
[
13
, Proposition 1.1.1]
).

A subcone
\mathscr{Q}
of a cone
\mathscr{P}
is defined as a non-empty subset of
\mathscr{P}
that remains closed under both the addition operation and scalar multiplication by non-negative real numbers. In other words, if
a,b\in\mathscr{Q}
and
\lambda\geqslant 0
, then
a+b\in\mathscr{Q}
and
\lambda a\in\mathscr{Q}
.

It is important to note that the (algebraic) cancellation law, which asserts that

a+c=b+c~~\text{\rm for}~a,b,c\in\mathscr{P}~~\text{implies}~a=b,

is not required in general. This property holds true if and only if the cone

can be embedded into a real vector space, as demonstrated in

A

As equality in
\mathscr{P}
is obviously such an order, all results about ordered cones apply to cones without

Every ordered vector space inherently satisfies the conditions to be considered a preordered cone. Additionally, the cones

Every subcone of a preordered cone

Let

A subset
E
of a preordered cone
(\mathscr{P},\leqslant)
is called decreasing (or increasing) if, for

and some

(or

\mathscr{P}\setminus E

of a decreasing subset

\mathscr{P}
is increasing. To see why, suppose

for

and

, and since

is decreasing, we get

, which contradicts
b\in\mathscr{P}\setminus E
. A similar argument shows that the complement
\mathscr{P}\setminus E
of an increasing set
E\subseteq\mathscr{P}
is decreasing.

An element
\nu\in\mathscr{P}
is called an upper bound (or lower bound) for a subset

E

if
a\leqslant\nu
(or
\nu\leqslant a
)

A subset
E

(\mathscr{P},\leqslant)
is said to be order convex if, for any

c\in\mathscr{P}
and

a\leqslant c\leqslant b
implies that

c\in E
. When an order convex set is multiplied by a positive scalar, the resulting set remains order convex. Additionally, the intersection of any collection of order convex sets is also order convex.

A subset
\mathcal{V}
of a preordered cone
\mathscr{P}
is referred to as an
abstract 0-neighborhood system

(i)

0<v

for all

(ii)

For any

and

w\leqslant v
;

(iii)

and

u,v\in\mathcal{V}

and

\lambda>0
.

The elements

\mathscr{V}
define upper and lower neighborhoods for the elements of

\displaystyle v(a)

\displaystyle=\{b\in\mathscr{P}_{1}:~b\leqslant a+v\}\quad(\text{\rm upper neighborhood}),

\displaystyle(a)v

\displaystyle=\{b\in\mathscr{P}_{1}:~a\leqslant b+v\}\quad(\text{\rm lower neighborhood}).

All upper neighborhoods

are decreasing convex subsets of

and all

are increasing convex. The symmetric neighborhoods

(1)

(2)

\lambda(a)v=(\lambda a)(\lambda v)

Each of the three topologies on

\mathscr{P}
can rightly be described as locally convex. In the upper topology, any open subset of

\mathscr{P}
is decreasing, as it is formed by unions of neighborhoods of its elements. Similarly, in the lower topology, all open subsets of

\mathscr{P}
are increasing. Consequently, the complements of these open sets exhibit the opposite behavior: subsets closed in the upper topology are increasing, while those closed in the lower topology are decreasing. This duality arises from the relationship between open and closed sets in these topologies.

The study of functional equations and their stability has been a central topic in mathematical analysis for several decades. Among these, the quadratic functional equation, which arises naturally in various areas of mathematics and physics, has garnered significant attention.

Let
\mathscr{P}
and
\mathscr{Q}
be cones. A function
f:\mathscr{P}\to\mathscr{Q}
is referred to as a quadratic mapping if it satisfies

f(x+y)+f(x-y)=2f(x)+2f(y)

for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}
such that
x-y\in\mathscr{P}
. The study of functional equations has been a fundamental area of mathematical research, with applications in various fields such as physics, engineering, and economics. Among these, the quadratic functional equation holds significant importance due to its connection with quadratic forms, inner product spaces, and orthogonal additivity. The stability of functional equations, particularly in the sense of Hyers-Ulam, has been a major topic of investigation since the mid-20th century, leading to profound developments in both pure and applied mathematics. The quadratic functional equation was first systematically studied by Jordan and von Neumann
[
5
]
in the context of characterizing inner product spaces. They proved that a normed space satisfies the parallelogram law (and hence is an inner product space) if and only if its norm satisfies the quadratic functional equation. This established a deep link between quadratic functions and geometric structures.

The stability problem for functional equations originated from a question posed by Ulam
[
15
]
in 1940, concerning the approximation of homomorphisms. Specifically, Ulam asked: If a function approximately satisfies a functional equation, can it be approximated by an exact solution? In 1941, Hyers
[
6
]
provided a partial answer for additive Cauchy equations in Banach spaces, marking the birth of Hyers-Ulam stability theory.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X
be a normed space,
Y
a Banach space, and
f:X\to Y
a mapping satisfying

\|f(x+y)-f(x)-f(y)\|\leqslant\varepsilon

for some constant
\varepsilon>0
and for all
x,y\in X
. Then the limit

A(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(2^{n}x)}{2^{n}}

exists for each
x\in X
, and
A:X\to Y
is the unique additive function satisfying

\|f(x)-A(x)\|\leqslant\varepsilon

for all
x\in X
.

The stability theory of functional equations has witnessed significant developments through several key contributions.
In 1950 Aoki
[
1
]
extended Hyers’ theorem to additive mappings, marking an important generalization. Two decades later, Th.M. Rassias
[
11
]
achieved a crucial advancement by removing the boundedness restriction on the Cauchy difference for linear mappings. This work was further refined by Găvruţa
[
4
]
who introduced general control functions to bound the Cauchy difference. These theoretical breakthroughs have successfully extended the stability concept to various other functional equations.
The stability of the quadratic functional equation was first investigated by Skof
[
14
]
and Cholewa
[
3
]
, who proved that if a function

f
from an abelian group
(G,+)
to a Banach space
Y
satisfies the inequality

\|f(x+y)+f(x-y)-2f(x)-2f(y)\|\leqslant\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
and for all
x,y\in G
, then there exists a unique quadratic function

Q:G\to Y
such that
\|f(x)-Q(x)\|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
x\in G
.

In this work, we investigate the stability of quadratic mappings in the setting of locally convex cones. These structures generalize locally convex topological vector spaces by incorporating order-theoretic and convexity properties. Specifically, a locally convex cone is equipped with a preorder and a system of convex neighborhoods, which allows for a natural extension of classical functional-analytic results to ordered convex structures. The study of quadratic operators in such cones not only extends the classical theory but also provides new insights into the behavior of these operators in ordered and convex structures.

The primary motivation for this work stems from the need to understand the stability properties of quadratic operators in non-linear and non-Archimedean settings. While the stability of quadratic mappings in Banach spaces and normed spaces has been extensively studied, the extension of these results to locally convex cones remains relatively unexplored. This paper aims to fill this gap by establishing Hyers-Ulam type stability results for quadratic operators in locally convex cones. Our approach leverages the structure of these cones, particularly their symmetric topology and the properties of bounded elements, to derive stability theorems.

Our results generalize and extend previous work on the stability of quadratic mappings, providing a unified framework for studying these operators in locally convex cones. The techniques employed in this paper are rooted in the theory of locally convex cones and functional analysis, and they offer a new perspective on the stability of quadratic operators in ordered and convex structures.

Main Results

The following lemmas have essential roles in our main results.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
be a separated locally convex cone, and let
a,b\in\mathscr{P}
. If
a\leqslant b+v
and
b\leqslant a+v
hold for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, then
a=b
.

Proof.

To prove
a=b
, it suffices to show that
\overline{a}=\overline{b}
. Let
x\in\overline{a}
. Then
x\leqslant a+\frac{1}{2}v
for all
v\in\mathscr{V}
. Since
a\leqslant b+\frac{1}{2}v
, it follows that
x\leqslant b+v
for all
v\in\mathscr{V}
, which implies
x\in\overline{b}
. This demonstrates that
\overline{a}\subseteq\overline{b}
. By a similar argument, we can show that
\overline{b}\subseteq\overline{a}
, leading to
\overline{a}=\overline{b}
. Because
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
is a separated locally convex cone, we conclude that
a=b
.
∎

Corollary 2.2

Let
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
be a separated locally convex cone, and let

\displaystyle\frac{f(2x)}{4}\leqslant f(x)+\frac{\lambda+1}{4}v.

Using mathematical induction on

, we extend the result to the following inequalities:

(2.8)

\displaystyle f(x)\leqslant\frac{1}{4^{n}}f(2^{n}x)+\frac{1}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n}}\right)(\lambda+1)v,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{n}}f(2^{n}x)\leqslant f(x)+\frac{1}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n}}\right)(\lambda+1)v

for all

and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To establish (

as a direct consequence of (
2.6
). Next, assume that (
2.8
) is valid for some
n
and all

. Then

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{f(2x)}{4}+\frac{\lambda+1}{4}v\quad\text{(by \eqref{t1.5})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}f(2^{n+1}x)+\frac{1}{12}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n}}\right)(\lambda+1)v+\frac{\lambda+1}{4}v\quad\text{(by \eqref{t1.6})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}f(2^{n+1}x)+\frac{1}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}\right)(\lambda+1)v

for all

. This completes the proof of (
2.8
) for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
To prove (
2.9
), we begin by observing that the inequality holds for

n=1
as a direct result of (
2.7
). Now, suppose that (
2.9
) is true for some
n
and all

. Starting from (
2.9
), we derive:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}f(2^{n+1}x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{f(2x)}{4}+\frac{1}{12}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n}}\right)(\lambda+1)v\quad\text{(by \eqref{t1.6+})}

\displaystyle\leqslant f(x)+\frac{\lambda+1}{4}v+\frac{1}{12}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n}}\right)(\lambda+1)v\quad\text{(by \eqref{t1.5+})}

\displaystyle=f(x)+\frac{1}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}\right)(\lambda+1)v

for all

. This establishes (

) for all

.
Replacing

with
2^{m}x
in (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) and multiplying both sides by

(2.10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{m}}f(2^{m}x)\leqslant\frac{1}{4^{n+m}}f(2^{n+m}x)+\frac{\lambda+1}{3\times 4^{m}}v,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{n+m}}f(2^{n+m}x)\leqslant\frac{1}{4^{m}}f(2^{m}x)+\frac{\lambda+1}{3\times 4^{m}}v

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
and all

.
Given that
v
is bounded, Lemma
2.3
ensures that the sequence

converges to zero as

.
Let

an arbitrary element. Then, there exists a natural number

such that

Using this result together with (
2.10
) and (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{m}}f(2^{m}x)\leqslant\frac{1}{4^{n+m}}f(2^{n+m}x)+u,\quad\frac{1}{4^{n+m}}f(2^{n+m}x)\leqslant\frac{1}{4^{m}}f(2^{m}x)+u

for all

This implies that the sequence

forms a Cauchy sequence in with respect to the symmetric topology. Because

2.9
), we deduce

, where

.
We now prove

is a quadratic mapping on
\mathscr{P}_{1}
. Let
x,y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}

Replacing

with

for all

. We can find

\displaystyle 2Q\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+2Q\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)\leqslant Q(x)+Q(y)+7u,

\displaystyle Q(x)+Q(y)\leqslant 2Q\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+2Q\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)+7u.

2.1
, we infer that

Corollary 2.8
.

Consider a locally convex cone
(\mathscr{P}_{1},\mathscr{V}_{1})
and a separated full uc-cone
(\mathscr{P}_{2},\mathscr{V}_{2})
, where
\mathscr{P}_{2}
is additionally equipped with the structure of a real vector space. Suppose
(\mathscr{P}_{2},\mathscr{V}_{2})
is complete with respect to its symmetric topology. If for some
\varepsilon>0
, a given mapping
f:\mathscr{P}_{1}\to\mathscr{P}_{2}
satisfies the following condition:

2f\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+2f\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)\in\varepsilon v(f(x)+f(y))\varepsilon v

for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
with
x-y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
, where

denotes the generating element of
\mathscr{V}_{2}

satisfying

Q(x)\in\left(\frac{r\varepsilon}{3}v\right)\left(f(x)-\frac{1}{3}f(0)\right)\left(\frac{r\varepsilon}{3}v\right),\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}

for all
r>1
.

Proof.

The symmetric topology on
\mathscr{P}_{2}
is known to be Hausdorff (see
[
7
]
, I.3.9), which implies that the pair
(\mathscr{P}_{2},\mathscr{V}_{2})
becomes a Banach space when endowed with the norm

\rho
defined as:

\rho(a)=\inf\{\mu>0:~\mu^{-1}a\in v(0)v\},\quad a\in\mathscr{P}_{2}.

Furthermore, the vector space structure of
\mathscr{P}_{2}
guarantees that all its elements are bounded. Consequently, the quantity

\rho(a)
remains finite for every
a\in\mathscr{P}_{2}
.
From our initial assumptions, we obtain

2f\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+2f\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)-f(x)-f(y)\in(\varepsilon v)(0)(\varepsilon v)

for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
with
x-y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. This relationship can be alternatively formulated as

\rho\left(2f\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)+2f\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)-f(x)-f(y)\right)\leqslant\varepsilon

for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
with
x-y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. By employing Hyers’ approach and taking
y=0
, we obtain

\rho\left(4f\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)-f(x)-f(0)\right)\leqslant\varepsilon,\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Hence,

(2.16)

\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}f(2^{n+1}x)-\frac{1}{4^{m}}f(2^{m}x)+\sum_{k=m}^{n}\frac{1}{4^{k+1}}f(0)\right)\leqslant\sum_{k=m}^{n}\frac{\varepsilon}{4^{k+1}}

for all

and
n\geqslant m\geqslant 0
. Based on (

), we observe that the sequence

is Cauchy in the Banach space

(\mathscr{P}_{2},\rho)
. Since the space
\mathscr{P}_{2}
is complete with respect to the norm
\rho
, this sequence must converge. This allows us to introduce a well-defined mapping
Q:\mathscr{P}_{1}\to\mathscr{P}_{2}
through the pointwise limit:

Q(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{4^{n}}f(2^{n}x),\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Setting
m=0
and letting

approach infinity in (

) yields

\rho\left(Q(x)-f(x)+\frac{1}{3}f(0)\right)\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{3},\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Consequently, for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
and

, we have
Q(x)-f(x)+\frac{1}{3}f(0)\in\frac{r\varepsilon}{3}(v(0)v)
. This implies that

Q(x)\in\left(\frac{r\varepsilon}{3}v\right)\left(f(x)-\frac{1}{3}f(0)\right)\left(\frac{r\varepsilon}{3}v\right),\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1},~r>1.

∎
