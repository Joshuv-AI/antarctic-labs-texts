Evaluating a virtual facilitation workshop with antimicrobial stewardship teams within a cluster randomized stepped-wedge trial
Authors: Sara Malone, Kelly Bono, Jacqueline M. Saito, Shawn Rangel, Jingxia Liu, Jason G. Newland, Virginia McKay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial stewardship programs (ASP) often function naturally as facilitators within clinical hospital settings, by working with individuals and teams to reduce unnecessary antibiotics. Within implementation science, facilitation has been studied and evaluated as an implementation strategy that can accelerate and improve fidelity to implementation efforts. This study describes a novel, virtual facilitation strategy developed and served as an intervention within the optimizing perioperative antibiotics for children trial (OPERATIC trial). This paper: (1) describes ASP team’s preferences for and use of a facilitation workshop and (2) describes sustained use of facilitation skills throughout the study period.MethodsStudy participants included antimicrobial stewardship team members from the nine children’s hospitals that participated in this study and completed facilitation training. All individuals who completed facilitation training were asked to evaluate the training through an online survey. Additionally, site leads were interviewed by the site coordinator every other month and asked about their team’s use of facilitation skills throughout the rest of the study period. Survey data were managed and coded in R, and qualitative interview data were analyzed using rapid methodology.Results30 individuals, including both physicians and pharmacists, completed the evaluation. Individuals largely rated themselves as novice facilitators (53%). Individuals reported satisfaction with virtual facilitation and identified different components of the workshops as valuable. An additional 108 interviews were performed throughout the study period. These interviews found that facilitators reported using all skills throughout the study period and described varied use of skills over time. All nine sites applied facilitation strategies, team building techniques, and communication/conflict skills at some point during the intervention phase.ConclusionWe describe the use of virtual facilitation as an acceptable and appropriate strategy to enhance facilitation skills for ASP teams working to reduce unnecessary postoperative antibiotics. Participants reported different useful components of facilitation training and described using differing facilitation skills throughout the trial. Overall, the use of facilitation skills continued throughout the duration of the study period. This paper outlines how facilitation training can be conducted virtually in a way that is feasible and acceptable to clinicians.Trial registrationNCT04366440, April 24, 2020.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11714-5.

Background

Facilitation is described as intentional support provided by an individual to provide necessary resources to enhance care delivery and help overcome barriers to implementation [1, 2]. Although the mechanistic pathways remain undetermined, the strategy has been theorized and studied to enhance the uptake of evidence-based care. Examples where facilitation has improved the uptake of evidence-based care include care coordination [1], cardiovascular disease treatment [3], and mental health care integration [4]. Facilitation can be carried out by individuals within the organization where an intervention is being implemented, by individuals external to the organization and/or by those implementing an evidence-based intervention [4–6]. Conceptual work within the field of implementation science, such as the Integrated Promoting Action on Research in Health Services (i-PARIHS) framework, helps define facilitation, promote skills inherent to a good facilitator, and identify conceptual domains that should be targeted by facilitators to improve the implementation of evidence-based care and overcome implementation barriers [7–10]. However, little is known about how facilitation can promote de-implementation, or the removal of practices that lack evidence or are known to cause harm within healthcare [11]. Furthermore, facilitation as either an implementation or de-implementation strategy may look different, and by extension providing training on facilitation may require different skills depending on the context and the health issue of focus [12]. As such, it is important to document facilitation strategies to subsequently understand their potential impacts.

A looming public health crisis where successful de-implementation is critical is antimicrobial overuse. To combat this in hospital settings, antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs), often consisting of infectious diseases clinicians, nurses, and pharmacists, intervene and optimize appropriate antibiotic prescribing, to reduce excessive antibiotic use [13–15]. ASP teams act as natural facilitators, using a variety of implementation strategies to enhance appropriate prescribing across multiple different hospital contexts [16, 17]. These ASPs focus their work at multiple levels, including interventions targeted at clinicians such as audit and feedback, hospital-wide initiatives like treatment protocols requiring antibiotics, and even system-level interventions like prompts and decision support within the electronic health record [18, 19]. Yet, training for ASP teams beyond the biomedical and epidemiological aspects of antibiotic use is virtually non-existent. This remains true despite literature demonstrating the need for social persuasion and navigation of interpersonal dynamics when attempting to change clinical practice within the hospital [20–23]. Because ASP teams are already positioned to be facilitators for implementing evidence related to antibiotic use, understanding how best to cultivate facilitation skills within ASPs can augment existing efforts by these teams and can enhance their influence within hospital care delivery.

Our primary objective with this evaluation is to enhance our understanding of the ASP team’s preferences for and use of a facilitation workshop designed to aid the de-implementation of unnecessary post-operative antibiotics within the pediatric surgical setting. This facilitation workshop was developed and served as an intervention within the optimizing perioperative antibiotics for children trial (OPERATIC trial). For this study, our team developed a virtual facilitation workshop for local hospital antimicrobial stewardship team members to reduce inappropriate post-operative antibiotic prescribing, a known source of excessive antibiotic use in pediatric hospitals [24, 25]. This manuscript reports on the design and content of the facilitation workshops and presents the evaluation of the workshops.

Methods

The OPERATIC trial is a stepped-wedge cluster randomized control trial with nine hospitals, where hospitals were randomized into three clusters (three hospitals per cluster) [26]. This trial consisted of six steps lasting six months with all clusters starting in the control arm. The first cluster was switched to the intervention arm of the trial and participated in the facilitation workshop at step 2, the second cluster at step 3, and the third cluster at step 4. Throughout the study, data were collected evaluating post-operative antibiotic use, strategies used to reduce antibiotic use, and clinicians’ perceptions of the acceptability, feasibility, and appropriateness of the intervention. More details about the overall trial design and data collection approach can be found in our previously published protocol [26]. This analysis used clinicians’ facilitation evaluation data to assess knowledge, skills acquired, and satisfaction with the facilitation workshops. We also used data collected every other month focusing on participants use of materials and skills targeted during the facilitation workshop. The Institutional Review Board approved all evaluation protocols.

This study was conducted at nine children’s hospitals in the US that are members of the SHaring Antimicrobial Reports for Pediatric Stewardship (SHARPS) Collaborative [25] and the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Project-Pediatrics (NSQIP-P) [27]. All hospitals in this study have an active ASP team that includes, at a minimum, a pediatric infectious diseases physician and pharmacist. Hospitals received annual compensation for participating in the study.

Participants

Antimicrobial stewardship team members from the nine hospitals participated in this study. Site leads at each hospital were identified and were responsible for providing a roster of ASP team members at their site. The study team then emailed each of the team members and invited them to participate in the facilitation training. These individuals included physicians, pharmacists, nurses, and other individuals affiliated with the antimicrobial stewardship teams.

Workshop format

These workshops were delivered weekly across one month and consisted of four two-hour virtual sessions, delivered in May 2021, November 2021, and May 2022. The workshop included lectures, discussions, role playing, and networking. Experts in pediatric infectious diseases and implementation science facilitated the sessions. The goal of these sessions was to train ASP teams to act as facilitators in their respective organizations, and each of the sessions was designed to target an aspect of our theoretical model of facilitation. The i-PARIHS framework as well as a review of other facilitation trainings within implementation science informed this training [10].

Content

The sessions included the following agenda topics: supporting literature for appropriate post-operative antibiotic use, data assessment and interpretation, implementation science principles, communication and conflict, and presenting data/data visualization (Supplementary Material 1). The workshop included discussion among participants about leadership, organizational initiatives, and data reporting. An overview of implementation science principles, specifically focused on facilitation as an implementation strategy, was provided during the first session. Following, in the data assessment and interpretation session, we presented information about NSQIP-P data reports, accessing data, and using data for site specific evaluation. In communication and conflict, we discussed different styles of communication between team members as well as information on conflict styles and approaches to interprofessional conflict. Finally, we discussed site capacity for improvement as well as methods to present data in a persuasive manner. Upon workshop completion, worksheets recapping key educational components were developed and distributed to the sites (Supplementary Material 2).

Data collection

First, immediate evaluation data were collected at the conclusion of the workshop from each participant (post-facilitation workshop evaluation). Second, site leads were interviewed every other month on their use of facilitation and their ongoing impression of the facilitation workshop after completing the intervention (bimonthly process interview).

Measures

Post-facilitation workshop evaluation

To assess the facilitation workshop, participants received a link in their email immediately after the final session to complete an electronic survey on REDCap [28]. The survey was adapted from a prior measure evaluating workshop-based practice facilitation resources [8]. Participants were asked to rate overall satisfaction and perceived individual expertise, with 1 indicating very poor and 5 indicating very good. Questions, on a Likert scale, asked how helpful they believed each component of the facilitation workshop to be with 1 indicating very poor – 5 indicating very good; to rate the overall workshop on the length and utility; and about their anticipated future use of the different components of the workshop, with 1 indicating very unlikely – 5 indicating very likely. Participants were asked to provide any additional commentary or feedback through four open-ended questions: (1) what were the most important things you learned (2) , what else would you add to the workshop (3)? , what would you change about the workshop?, and (4) do you have any other comments/thoughts? The survey took about 10 min to complete.

Bi-monthly process interview

Every other month after facilitation, the research team coordinator conducted an interview with the site lead from each site which was scheduled at the site lead’s convenience and conducted via web conference or telephone. During each interview, the site lead was asked about their use of specific facilitation skills and ongoing feedback about the facilitation workshop. A structured interview guide was utilized (Supplementary Material 3). A research team member probed each component delivered during facilitation workshops (data use, data presentation, capacity building, communication/conflict, implementation strategies) during every interview. Interviews lasted approximately 30 min. A research team member took detailed notes in a structured REDCap form during the interview. Due to the nature of these calls, the interviews were not recorded or transcribed. Information about each site’s activities was summarized and site leads reviewed the notes and summaries with the study coordinator at the end of the study period.

Data analysis and management

Data were managed in REDCap and analyzed in RStudio [29]. All survey responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Open ended survey questions were analyzed qualitatively. Two members (SM, VM) of the study team independently reviewed the responses and conducted separate content analyses, and one investigator (SM) developed codes for categories generated from the data [30, 31]. The two investigators discussed and resolved the codes.

Finally, the longitudinal interview data were analyzed using rapid qualitative methods [32–34]. One coder analyzed the questions that individuals answered following the switch into the intervention arm, developed a matrix codebook and then reviewed interview notes to categorize the responses. Individual responses were noted as using each facilitation skill during the interview timeframe or not, and then any extra information was reviewed and categorized. These results were reviewed with the primary facilitation team after completion.

Results

Facilitation workshop evaluation

The facilitation workshop was attended by 36 individuals, with 30 (83%) of them completing the evaluation. There were at least two individuals from each institution that completed the evaluation, such that the responses represented all hospitals participating in the intervention. Individuals who completed the post-facilitation evaluation consisted of 19 physicians (63%), 10 pharmacists (33%), and one person who identified as “other” ASP clinician (3%). Individuals largely rated themselves as novice (53%) or experienced (47%) in facilitation, with none self-identifying as experts.

When individuals were asked about their satisfaction with the workshop, individuals had a median score for the overall workshop of 4 (range 3–5), indicating generally positive satisfaction with the workshop. Participants also agreed that the facilitation workshops could be helpful in planning for the implementation of other programs and practices (median 4, range 3–5). When individuals were asked about their anticipated future use of the workshop components (Table 1), individuals reported a high likelihood that they would use skills from the workshop. High variability from component to component indicated that different individuals found different aspects of the workshop more useful, although each component appeared valuable overall based on aggregated responses.

When asked about the format of the workshop, most individuals indicated that they thought the workshop was the correct length (67%), with 33% stating they would like the workshop to be shorter. When asked about specific teaching elements individuals found useful, individuals reported that both case studies (median 4, range 3–5) and role play (median 4, range 3–5) aided their understanding of the content the most.

Workshop commentary and feedback

Participants described the fundamentals of facilitation and the interpersonal components of the workshop (communication and conflict) as the most useful components of the workshop. We found that the feedback and strategies utilized were similar across hospital settings. Over time, individuals reported using different skills but there was not a pattern to the change over time and instead the use of skills were setting and opportunity dependent. For instance, individuals would use data visualization at times when necessary to present at team meetings, while using communication skills consistently throughout the study period. These were described as foundational concepts to their work, but areas for which they had not received training previously. Teams described this session as useful in how they think about working with other teams, and also in helping consider their own team dynamics and functioning. One participant stated “I enjoyed learning about the various communication styles and conflict styles. Talking through with participants and realizing the types preferred with members of my own ASP team was interesting…”. Another session that was described as useful was data visualization, with one participant specifically stating “I learned about different data visualization tips to make my points clearer”. Participants reflected that it was especially useful to understand different aspects of data visualization and how to communicate findings to various partners.

Individuals identified more applied content to improve the workshop. Recommendations for future workshops included the addition of more role play, a review of examples and group troubleshooting, and a discussion of each institution’s efforts and places for improvement. Additionally, participants requested additional resources in various forms, including handouts, additional follow-up meetings, and copies of information technology resources or presentations.

Participants discussed similar themes when asked about workshop areas they would change, including access to additional resources or more time for application of the concepts through role play and discussion. Participants expressed preferences for logistic changes, such as having the workshop in person or at a different time of the day. Participants described the large time commitment to the workshops and ways to potentially reduce the time burden on ASP teams.

Finally, when asked about additional comments, participants described how the workshops helped facilitate their team functioning and prioritization. Participants reflected on the time spent together as a team and commented that using icebreakers was helpful to build comfort as a group.

Process interview evaluation

The response rate for the process interviews was 100% over the study period. There were 108 overall interviews that were analyzed, representing interviews every other month throughout the intervention period for each site. All nine sites reported using facilitation strategies, team building techniques, and communication/conflict skills at some point during the intervention phase. Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles were asked about as a representation of a specific improvement strategy presented during a workshop throughout the trial (Table 2). Only eight of the sites reported the use of PDSA cycles as a specific tool for reducing post-operative antibiotic use, with an overall frequency of 27 time points out of the 99 possible instances.

When asked to elaborate on the employed facilitation strategies, site leads discussed the use of data in their work and how they were considering analyzing and presenting data to various surgical groups and hospital quality improvement teams. Site leads described the new incorporation of data sources, primarily from the NSQIP-P database that they had been trained on (described above), in their presentations. Additionally, many sites incorporated data visualization and reporting dashboards that were accessible to clinicians. When asked about communication strategies, site leads mentioned their approaches to communication, including their intentionality in meeting planning and preparation of agendas prior to meeting with surgical partners. All the sites also reported planned approaches to getting buy-in and steps they took to build stronger team relationships. Finally, site leads described logistical planning that had changed because of the facilitation workshops, including an ongoing focus on implementation planning.

Discussion

We describe our provision of a virtual facilitation training to ASP teams to enhance their facilitation skills within pediatric hospitals to reduce unnecessary post-operative antibiotic prophylaxis. Our work provides preliminary evidence that facilitation is a strategy that ASP teams find acceptable, and provides skills that teams continue to use over time. Our participants reported finding different aspects of facilitation valuable and employing different facilitation skills at different points in the trial. Team behavior change and use of facilitation continued throughout the duration of the study period. Participants described using facilitation skills at every time point after facilitation training but used different components. This suggests that our virtual facilitation training is a sustainable implementation strategy, even with no additional doses of facilitation training provided to the teams. Furthermore, participants reported that they would be willing to apply these skills to other initiatives in their work. This indicates that facilitation training may have additional benefits beyond our targeted focus of reducing excessive postoperative prophylaxis to improve internal facilitation skills of individuals implementing new evidence-based programs within complex hospital systems.

This study used traditional facilitation workshop components and refined them for delivery to (1) a hospital clinician audience and (2) an exclusively virtual platform. While some of the content in the facilitation training was tailored to our specific context and health concern (i.e., the use of NSQIP-P data to understand excessive postoperative surgical prophylaxis in pediatric hospitals), we feel that the general format (reviewing evidence needs, clinician needs, and contextual influences) is adaptable and valuable for multiple situations.

This study demonstrates how facilitation training conducted virtually is feasible and acceptable to clinicians. While external facilitation, where someone from outside of an organization supports the implementation of evidence-based practice, is routinely done remotely [6, 35, 36], there are very few examples of conducting training for internal facilitators remotely. This workshop enhances our understanding of the skills and concepts that are acceptable to facilitators within implementation efforts, including communication and conflict training, data presentation, and understanding effective persuasion techniques. Additionally, this provides evidence that supports facilitation as a useful strategy for de-implementation and adds to documented use of facilitation throughout the study period.

Although our original intent was to conduct the workshop in person, the move to a virtual format provided several benefits: (1) enhanced engagement, as participants from all three hospitals in a cluster attended together and discussed problems from their varied experiences; (2) a more cost-effective approach compared to having facilitation trainers travel to each site; and (3) more scalable approach when disseminating this effective workshop to more hospital ASP teams. Respondents reported good overall satisfaction with the workshop. Taken together, this suggests that virtual facilitation is a cost-effective strategy for providing facilitation training.

Feedback on the workshop highlighted ways that this workshop could be improved, including adding practical content and potentially shortening sessions. Based on our experience, we recommend that virtual facilitation sessions should follow a structured agenda, be thematically organized, and be well timed. While participants rated sessions as useful and acknowledged the use of different skills at various points throughout the intervention period of the study, we were only able to address some of the recommendations provided, such as developing fact sheets so that individuals could quickly refer back to materials. Other recommendations could be prospectively integrated in future trainings, including more exercises, opportunities for discussion, and information distribution prior to each session. Maintaining training opportunities in data use/presentation, communication/conflict, team interactions, and tools for rapid improvement would be useful as a supplement and would encourage the use of facilitation skills beyond one-time training [12, 37, 38].

Virtual facilitation training holds promise for future scale up, but this workshop and evaluation have limitations. First, the evaluation was limited to the nine sites involved in this study, all of which are academic children’s hospitals. Virtual facilitation workshops likely have different barriers when conducted in other settings that would need to be analyzed and accounted for. Additionally, this training took place over eight hours, a large time commitment for busy clinicians and practitioners. Identifying time-efficient approaches to conduct these workshops will benefit the broader application of this workshop in future implementation.

While this study addresses the feasibility and self-reported use of facilitation skills following virtual training, future work is needed to link this training to the implementation and clinical outcomes of the evidence-based intervention which we intend to examine in subsequent analyses. If the outcomes of these analyses support the continued use of virtual facilitation training, these programs could be scaled in numerous ways – including to different populations, new evidence-based implementation efforts, other geographical locations, and into non-academic settings.

Conclusion

Facilitation is promising as a de-implementation strategy. Our virtual facilitation workshop provides an adaptable template for others who may be interested in using a similar approach to address either implementation or de-implementation in complex health systems. Additional evaluation linking facilitation to implementation and clinical outcomes is needed to further support the utility of the approach.
