Lung cancer in patients with scleroderma-associated interstitial lung disease: Case series and implications for screening
Authors: Omar El Fadel, Konstantinos-Dionysios Alysandratos, Finn Hawkins, Divya Shankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients with scleroderma associated interstitial lung disease (SSc-ILD) are at an increased risk for lung cancer, necessitating a high index of suspicion for timely detection. This case series describes three patients with SSc-ILD who developed lung cancer, highlighting diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in detecting and managing malignancy within abnormal lung parenchyma.MethodsElectronic medical records were reviewed by a team of physicians. Clinical, radiographic, and pathologic data from each patient's disease course were synthesized to illustrate diagnostic workup and management decisions.ResultsThe series includes two former smokers with minimal smoking history and one never-smoker. Lung cancer stages at diagnosis ranged from IA to IVb. Positron emission tomography imaging was instrumental in raising suspicion for malignancy and selecting diagnostic sites. Pausing immunosuppression led to ILD progression for all patients.ConclusionThis case series emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for lung cancer in patients with SSc-ILD, including among minimal or non-smokers, and highlights the urgent need for future research into risk factors and mechanistic pathways, screening strategies, and management approaches for lung cancer in SSc-ILD.Keywords: Scleroderma, Interstitial lung disease, Lung cancer

Key messages:

Introduction

Patients with scleroderma are at four-fold increased risk for lung cancer compared to the general population [1,2]. Those with interstitial lung disease associated with their scleroderma are thought to be at even higher risk [3]. However, the specific factors contributing to this increased risk remain incompletely understood. Abnormal lung parenchyma in patients with SSc-ILD complicates both screening and evaluation of pulmonary nodules, for which no established screening recommendations or optimal imaging intervals exist [4,5]. Additionally, management of SSc-ILD immunosuppression after a lung cancer diagnosis poses challenges. Immunosuppressive therapies used to treat SSc-ILD can aggravate chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression and potentially worsen cancer outcomes, but reducing or pausing immunosuppression risks accelerating ILD progression [[6], [7], [8]]. The objective of this case series is to describe the diagnostic and therapeutic trajectories of three patients with SSc-ILD who developed lung cancer at a tertiary, academic, safety-net center, in order to highlight real-world considerations in the evaluation and management of these patients.

Methods

Electronic medical records were reviewed by a team of physicians. Demographics, smoking history, follow-up frequency, treatment regimens, radiographic monitoring, cancer screening, and cancer diagnostic data were extracted. Relevant information was synthesized. Written consent was obtained from all patients or next of kin if deceased. As this case series includes only three cases, it was exempt from institutional review board approval per institutional policy, and case descriptions were reviewed with the Boston Medical Center privacy officer.

Case report 1

•A never-smoker in their 30s was diagnosed with SSc-ILD and initiated on mycophenolic acid and nintedanib. Approximately six months after initiating treatment, surveillance chest CT identified an enlarging 1.5 cm sub-pleural right lower lobe nodular opacity. Given growth of this nodule, a PET/CT was obtained which showed intense FDG avidity concerning for malignancy. An initial transthoracic biopsy was non-diagnostic, yet persistent concern based on nodule characteristics and interval growth prompted thoracic surgical referral. Resection confirmed stage IA acinar-predominant adenocarcinoma. After treatment, the patient underwent surveillance imaging every three months and remained in remission for over a year until a chest CT revealed loculated pleural effusions and thickening. PET/CT showed FDG avidity of both pleural effusions and a repeat transthoracic biopsy confirmed recurrent acinar adenocarcinoma (PD-L1 90%, NGS with MET amplification). The patient was diagnosed with stage IV disease and initially treated with a MET inhibitor followed by chemoradiation. Mycophenolic acid was paused for one year after initial diagnosis, but ILD progression off immunosuppression led to reinitiation with clinical improvement.

Case report 2

•A former smoker (5 pack-year history) in their 60s was diagnosed with SSc-ILD and managed with mycophenolate. Five years after diagnosis, surveillance chest CT identified a 1.9 cm right lower lobe nodule with central necrosis. PET/CT showed right lower lobe and right hilar FDG avidity. Transthoracic biopsy revealed poorly differentiated lung carcinoma, but EBUS for staging was negative for mediastinal and hilar lymph node involvement. Follow-up PET/CT demonstrated new FDG-avid hepatic and osseous lesions. Given the discrepancy between EBUS results and PET/CT findings, biopsy of the more accessible hepatic lesion was pursued rather than repeat thoracic or nodal sampling for greater diagnostic certainly. This confirmed metastatic disease with stage IVB lung squamous cell carcinoma. Chemotherapy was initiated and mycophenolate was held for four months and then restarted at the same dose due to ILD progression. The patient developed severe respiratory failure attributed to worsening ILD and cancer progression and passed away approximately 7 months after their lung cancer diagnosis.

Case report 3

•A former smoker (15 pack-years) in their 60s with SSc-ILD was managed with mycophenolate and monitored with chest CT every 6 months. Two years after diagnosis, surveillance CT revealed a 4.2 cm nodule in the left major fissure. PET/CT demonstrated FDG avidity in the nodule and additional rib lesions. Given the accessibility of the osseous site, needle biopsy of a rib lesion was pursued, confirming invasive stage IVB squamous cell carcinoma with a lung primary. The patient began chemotherapy and palliative radiotherapy and achieved remission. Immunosuppression was held for three years but then resumed due to ILD progression leading to secondary pulmonary hypertension, intermittent hospitalizations, and uncontrolled symptom burden.

Discussion

We describe three cases of patients with SSc-ILD at a tertiary, academic, safety-net center who developed lung cancer while undergoing monitoring and treatment for SSc-ILD. Our cases highlight 1) the emergence of lung cancer in patients with SSc-ILD, 2) the diagnostic challenges that arise when evaluating new findings in the setting of abnormal parenchyma, and 3) the challenges of managing immunosuppression for SSc-ILD in the setting of a cancer diagnosis.

The development of lung cancer in patients with SSc-ILD has been hypothesized to reflect the combined effects of chronic inflammation, fibrosis-related architectural distortion, and immune dysregulation [[1], [2], [3]]. Persistent epithelial injury and fibrotic remodeling may promote malignant transformation even in the absence of significant smoking exposure, as supported by translational studies demonstrating that repetitive epithelial stress and oxidative injury within fibrotic lung tissue create a pro-oncogenic microenvironment [9,10]. In systemic sclerosis specifically, disease-specific factors including chronic autoimmune inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and distinct autoantibody profiles may further compound this risk beyond fibrosis alone [11]. Epidemiologic studies confirm elevated lung cancer risk in patients with SSc-ILD independent of smoking, implicating the inflammatory and fibrogenic milieu as a driver [[1], [2], [3],12]. The inclusion of a young never-smoker with early-stage disease and the minimal smoking history of the other two cases underscores that smoking may not be the sole determinant of carcinogenesis in SSc-ILD [2,3]. Collectively, our cases and prior literature highlight the need for vigilance for lung cancer even among SSc-ILD patients with lower traditional risk profiles.

Although current practice guidelines acknowledge the increased lung cancer risk in SSc-ILD, they make no specific recommendations for screening [12]. Diagnosing lung cancer in SSc-ILD is challenging due to parenchymal abnormalities obscuring new lesions [4,5]. Most of the patients described in our series had initial negative biopsies. Therefore, a high degree of suspicion, appropriate imaging, and lower thresholds for pursuing additional invasive diagnostics are likely essential to diagnose lung cancer in patients with SSc-ILD. In our series, careful longitudinal imaging review and the use of PET/CT were critical in identifying subtle changes suggestive of malignancy, illustrating how functional imaging can complement anatomic assessment in the SSc-ILD patient population with abnormal lung parenchyma. Importantly, active ILD-related inflammation can itself cause elevated FDG avidity, adding to the inherent difficulty of diagnosing malignancy in this population. The heterogeneity in patient age, smoking history, and disease stage at diagnosis in our cases also highlights the limitations of applying general population screening intervals to SSc-ILD, suggesting the need for risk-stratified screening approaches tailored to SSc-ILD rather than applying general population protocols. Additionally, in safety-net settings such as ours, lung cancer detection may be further challenged by competing acute health needs and social determinants of health impacting follow-up [13]. Our cases also highlight the importance of multidisciplinary care. In all cases, patients were followed by both pulmonary and rheumatology and close collaboration allowed for efficient diagnostic evaluation.

The management of lung cancer in patients with ILD is further complicated by their increased susceptibility to treatment-related toxicities, such as ILD exacerbation following surgery and radiation- or immunotherapy-induced pneumonitis [14,15]. However, existing literature and practice guidelines do not make clear recommendations on how to manage ILD in the context of concurrent cancer diagnosis. Some studies suggest that 13immunosuppression should be held during cancer treatment, as it may hamper anti-tumor immune responses [6,7]. Yet, in the case of SSc-ILD, the proven effectiveness of immunosuppression raises concern that interrupting therapy may come at the cost of worsening ILD [16]. This risk is illustrated in our cases, as most patients had immunosuppression initially held during lung cancer treatment and subsequently suffered ILD progression, requiring therapy to be resumed.

When ILD treatment is continued or resumed during active cancer therapy, treatment selection requires special consideration because medications commonly used for ILD may interact with anticancer therapies or increase treatment-related toxicities. Mycophenolate, the immunosuppressant most often used in SSc-ILD, works mainly by limiting the growth of immune cells. Because chemotherapy also acts on rapidly dividing cells, the two work through overlapping pathways and together can worsen bone marrow suppression and infection risk [17]. [18] Tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 blocker now approved for SSc-ILD, may offer a more targeted alternative to broad immunosuppression, though there is little evidence to guide its use during active cancer [19]. Nintedanib, by contrast, is not an immunosuppressant and might itself be active against non-small cell lung cancer, so it can usually be continued during cancer treatment [20]. For these reasons, restarting immunosuppression during cancer treatment calls for close monitoring of blood counts and coordination across teams. These experiences highlight the therapeutic tension clinicians face in balancing oncologic control with preservation of lung function, an area where evidence remains limited, and management is often individualized through multidisciplinary discussion.

The small sample size and descriptive nature of this case series precludes inference regarding lung cancer risk, causality, or underlying biological mechanisms in SSc-ILD. Nonetheless, these cases highlight several important areas for future research, including identifying molecular mechanisms and clinical, genetic, or environmental risk factors that may contribute to lung cancer in SSc-ILD and establishing best practices for lung cancer screening and immunosuppression management during cancer therapy. Addressing these questions will require large, longitudinal studies with adequate follow-up as well as dedicated mechanistic investigations, which are beyond the scope of this case series. Beyond their research implications, these cases have educational value for clinicians and trainees by illustrating the diagnostic reasoning, multidisciplinary collaboration, and careful longitudinal surveillance needed to detect malignancy in the context of abnormal lung parenchyma.
