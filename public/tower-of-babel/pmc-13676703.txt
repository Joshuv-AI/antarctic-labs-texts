Protocol for mapping sister chromatid exchanges and structural variations using sci-L3-strand-seq
Authors: Shiyang He, Peter Chovanec, Yi Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnderstanding spontaneous genome rearrangement requires mapping both error-free and mutational repair outcomes, particularly error-free sister chromatid exchanges (SCEs). Here, we present a sci-L3-Strand-seq protocol capturing SCEs across thousands of single cells from 12–16 independent samples in one experiment. We detail steps for cell labeling, sample banking, nucleosome depletion, in situ combinatorial barcoding, enrichment of BrdU-labeled cells, and nascent strand ablation. We then detail linear amplification, reverse transcription, and second-strand synthesis. Libraries are typically sequencing-ready within three days from frozen cells.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Chovanec et al.1

Before you begin

Genome integrity is maintained through multiple DNA repair pathways, yet errors in these processes can give rise to structural variations (SVs) that drive cancer development and other genetic diseases.1 Mitotic crossovers (COs), which arise from homologous recombination (HR) during S and G2 phases, can produce a spectrum of genomic rearrangements ranging from error-free sister chromatid exchanges (SCEs) to large-scale copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (cnLOH), copy number variations (CNVs), inversions, translocations, aneuploidy, and uniparental disomy (UPD).2 Studying these events genome-wide at the single-cell level has historically been limited by throughput and cost constraints of existing methods.3,4

Strand-seq is a powerful sequencing-based approach that exploits the semi-conservative nature of DNA replication: cells are pulse-labeled with bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) for one cell division, and the subsequent degradation of BrdU-containing nascent strands allows selective sequencing of the parental template strands.4,5,6 This enables the detection of SCEs, which appear as strand-state switches between Watson (W) and Crick (C) orientations, as well as other SVs that alter strand directionality or copy number. However, the original Strand-seq protocol is limited to low throughput (tens to hundreds of cells per experiment), relies on PCR-based amplification, and is costly.5,7

We previously developed the sci-L3 suite of methods, which combines single-cell combinatorial indexing (sci) with linear amplification (L) and three levels (L3) of barcoding to achieve high-throughput, low-bias single-cell sequencing.8 Here, we describe the full experimental protocol for sci-L3-Strand-seq (Figure 1), an extension of the sci-L3 framework to template strand sequencing, which enables the profiling of thousands to millions of single cells at a cost of $0.10–0.20 per cell.1,9 Key innovations include the use of Tn5 transposome-based genome fragmentation (which preserves template strand directionality and enables digital copy number counting), IVT-based linear amplification (which reduces amplification bias compared to PCR), and an enzymatic nascent strand ablation step combining UV treatment with USER enzyme (uracil DNA glycosylase, UDG, plus endonuclease VIII, EndoVIII) to achieve high-quality strand-specific libraries.Figure 1Overview of sci-L3-Strand-seq protocolCells are fixed and harvested to do tagmentation and 2 rounds of barcode adding, followed by FACS sorting and IVT. The NGS library is built from RNA and sequenced by Illumina Novaseq.

This protocol describes the specific steps for using BJ-5ta, HAP1, and Patski cell lines. All cells should be actively cycling and handled under subdued lighting throughout to protect BrdU-incorporated DNA from photodegradation.

Innovation

Sci-L3-Strand-seq extends the sci-L3 combinatorial indexing framework to template strand sequencing. Key innovations include: (1) IVT-based linear amplification replacing PCR to reduce amplification bias; (2) a combinatorial three-level barcoding scheme (Tn5 tagmentation × ligation × second-strand synthesis) enabling profiling of up to 1M single cells per experiment; (3) an enzymatic nascent strand ablation step combining UV irradiation with USER enzyme (UDG + EndoVIII) for high-fidelity strand-specific library generation; and (4) digital copy number counting from overlapping Tn5 insertion patterns. Together, these advances enable genome-wide mapping of SCE, cnLOH, CNV, inversions, translocations, aneuploidy, and UPD for mammalian cells.

Step-by-step method details

Day 1: Nucleosome depletion and the first two rounds of barcoding

Timing: 8–10 h

Timing: 8–10 h

In day 1 experiments, we process cells from frozen nuclei to sorted nuclei after the first two rounds of split-and-pool barcoding, including the following sub steps: nuclei isolation, nucleosome depletion, Tn5-based first-round barcoding, ligation of the second-round barcodes, and FACS sorting.

Expected outcomes

Sci-L3-Strand-seq generates high-quality, strand-specific single-cell whole-genome sequencing libraries. Key quality metrics and expected outcomes are summarized below.

Library yield and quality

IVT RNA yield: 2–10 ng/μL per well (after cleanup) for Strand-seq libraries. Strand-seq yields ∼3–5× less RNA than WGS because only template strands are amplified.

Duplex DNA yield after SSS: 3–30 ng per 300 human nuclei.

Average cell recovery: approximately 90% of FACS-sorted cells yield usable libraries (789 out of 850 sorted in pilot experiments), consistent across cell lines.

Sequencing and coverage metrics

Sequencing depth: 30,000–100,000 paired-end read pairs per cell (sub-saturation sequencing for cost efficiency). A median of ∼50,000 read pairs per diploid cell is used routinely.

Genome coverage: 0.27–1% median coverage per diploid cell at standard sequencing depth; up to 2% coverage at 300,000+ read pairs.

Background estimate: median background (fraction of reads mapping in the non-template direction) of 3.3–4.9% for high-quality cells. Cells with background >0.08, or >15% (haploid) and >75% (diploid) strand-neutral regions are filtered.

SCE detection

Expected SCE rates: median of 2–3 SCEs per haploid genome per cell division in wild-type human cell lines (HAP1: median 3; BJ-5ta and Patski diploid cells: median 2/haploid genome).

SCE resolution: 128–225 kb median resolution at standard sequencing depth.

Strand information quality: >58% of sorted cells pass QC filters and exhibit clear strand-specific profiles with identifiable SCEs.

Structural variant detection

Inversions: detectable from opposite strand reads on WW or CC chromosomes (heterozygous inversions appear as WC blocks; homozygous inversions flip strand state). Global inversions shared across cell lines are reliably identified.

Translocations: identified from positive Pearson correlation of strand states between translocated chromosome regions across hundreds of cells.

cnLOH and UPD: identified through haplotype-aware segmentation and SVM-based classification using phased haplotype fractions and digital copy number counts from overlapping read patterns.

Phasing accuracy: 98.6% average phasing accuracy for Patski mouse cells (with known haplotypes) when using 931+ cells; comparable performance for human BJ-5ta cells.

Limitations

Sci-L3-Strand-seq requires cells to complete one round of DNA replication to incorporate BrdU; non-cycling or very slowly dividing cells will yield low BrdU-incorporation efficiency and poor FACS enrichment. The protocol is optimized for mammalian cells; adaptation to other organisms may require adjustment of labeling, FA fixation, nucleosome depletion, and FACS gating conditions. Library yield per cell is inherently lower than in PCR-based whole-genome methods because only template strands are amplified by IVT. Resolution of SCE and SV detection is limited by sequencing depth (128–225 kb median resolution at standard depth). Finally, haplotype-phasing accuracy improves with cell number; accurate phasing of heterozygous SVs requires hundreds to thousands of cells.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Poor or absent nucleosome depletion; Tn5 insertion fails (Related to steps 6–15). Possible reason is that the SDS concentration is too low, incubation time too short, or formaldehyde over-fixation.

Potential solution

Confirm SDS concentration is 0.3% final. Optimize fixation to 1–1.5% formaldehyde. Do not exceed 10 min fixation. Ensure Glycine quenching is thorough: ensure Glycine concentration is correct: the glycine-to-formaldehyde molar ratio (quenching agent to fixative) ranges from 3:1 to 4:1. Ensure the quenching time is 5 min.

Problem 2

Low tagmentation efficiency (related to steps 16–22). Possible reason is that the Transposome is not properly assembled or too old.

Potential solution

Assemble fresh transposomes. Verify Tn5 adapter annealing. Use freshly thawed EZ-Tn5.

Problem 3

No BrdU-quenched population visible by Hoechst (Related to steps 34–36). Possible reason is that Insufficient BrdU incorporation; light exposure degraded BrdU.

Potential solution

Verify BrdU concentration (40 μM). Ensure 24 h labeling. If certain cells grow slow, the labeling time needs to extend to at least one cell cycle but not reach two cycles. Protect all steps from light. Check DAPI control for an unquenched baseline (see Figure 2).

Problem 4

No strand information: background estimate >0.08 in all cells (related to steps 41–48). Possible reasons are that UV alone is insufficient; USER enzyme inactive or not added.

Potential solution

Confirm USER is added after UV. Use fresh USER aliquot. Both UV AND USER+UGI steps are required, neither alone is sufficient.

Problem 5

Very low RNA yield (<0.5 ng/μL after cleanup) (Related to step 49–61). Possible reasons are the IVT is inhibited, T7 promoter is damaged, or gap filling is incomplete.

Potential solution

Ensure Bst deactivation (80°C for 10 min). Check that the UGI step was performed. Use a fresh rNTP mix. Extend IVT to 16 h.

Problem 6

Low cDNA yield after RT (related to steps 61–78). Possible reasons are that the RNA is degraded during cleanup or RT primer not annealing.

Potential solution

Use fresh Superase-In throughout. Verify RNA Qubit reading before RT. Rapid cooling (pre-chilled water bath) is critical at Step 61.
