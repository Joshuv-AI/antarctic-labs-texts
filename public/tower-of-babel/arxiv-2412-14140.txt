GLIDER: Grading LLM Interactions and Decisions using Explainable Ranking
Darshan Deshpande, Selvan Sunitha Ravi, Sky CH-Wang, Bartosz Mielczarek, Anand Kannappan, Rebecca Qian

Abstract
The LLM-as-judge paradigm is increasingly being adopted for automated evaluation of model outputs. While LLM judges have shown promise on constrained evaluation tasks, closed source LLMs display critical shortcomings when deployed in real world applications due to challenges of fine grained metrics and explainability, while task specific evaluation models lack cross-domain generalization. We introduce GLIDER, a powerful 3B evaluator LLM that can score any text input and associated context on arbitrary user defined criteria. GLIDER shows higher Pearson’s correlation than GPT-4o on FLASK and greatly outperforms prior evaluation models, achieving comparable performance to LLMs 17× its size. GLIDER supports fine-grained scoring, multilingual reasoning, span highlighting and was trained on 685 domains and 183 criteria. Extensive qualitative analysis shows that GLIDER scores are highly correlated with human judgments, with 91.3% human agreement. We have open-sourced GLIDER to facilitate future research. 1 1 1 https://huggingface.co/PatronusAI/glider

Introduction

The ever improving capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened a wide variety of use cases for their applications such as tool use
Schick et al. (2024)
;
Qin et al. (2023)
, long context reasoning
Kuratov et al. (2024)
and many more. With such a steep growth in the field, practitioners are struggling to effectively evaluate LLMs on potential downstream use cases
Gao et al. (2024)
.

Researchers have been exploring automated evaluation using LLMs as a substitute for human judgment, as traditional human evaluation studies are time-intensive and require rigorous quality control.
Dong et al. (2024)
;
Wang et al. (2024)
;
Son et al. (2024)
. The wide-scale pretraining along with excellent instruction following capabilities
Yin et al. (2023)
;

has made closed-source LLMs a default choice for open ended text evaluation in recent years
Zheng et al. (2023)
;
Bavaresco et al. (2024)
.

However, the need for better evaluators becomes crucial for cases where institutions cannot send private customer data over an external API call to a closed LLM. Previous works
Son et al. (2024)
;
Kim et al. (2024b)
show that open source LLMs still fall short of closed-source enterprise LLMs for generalizable evaluation tasks. While active efforts are being made to improve performance with custom fine-tuned LLMs
Vu et al. (2024)
;
Wang et al. (2024)
, these models are simply too large and expensive for users to efficiently deploy as fast guardrails. Furthermore, prior work like
Kim et al. (2024b)
;
Vu et al. (2024)
mainly assesses model output and evolving needs now call for a more generalizable model that can evaluate all aspects of model interactions including inputs, outputs, metadata, contextual information and more. Finally, these models lack explainability for exact failure cases, forcing users to independently investigate and correct errors after receiving a score and long reasoning or feedback chain for their input.

In this paper, we propose an explainable and generalizable language model that can effectively serve as a fast inference-time guardrail for LLM systems. These are our findings:

1.

Small Language Models (SLMs) trained and aligned using a large variety of synthetic evaluation scenarios can achieve performance comparable to LLMs 17x it’s size.

2.

More focused reasoning chains and explainability highlights improve multi-metric evaluations, thereby reducing number of model calls required during inference for individual metric evaluations.

3.

Small finetuned models are able to preserve multilingual reasoning skills from their pretraining phase.

4.

Explainability in the form of text highlight spans and formally structured reasoning chains leads to better performance across several subjective and factuality-centric benchmarks.

Relevant Work

Open ended text evaluation has been a challenging problem for the community
Celikyilmaz et al. (2020)
. Early works applied for this task utilized ROUGE
Lin (2004)
, BLEU
Papineni et al. (2002)
or embedding based metrics such as the BERTScore
Zhang et al. (2019)
or COMET
Rei et al. (2020)
. While these metrics work well for tasks such as translation or summarization evaluation, they are not generalizable for subjective evaluation criteria and do not produce human comprehensible outputs. This has driven a shift towards the adoption of LLMs as judge models
Zheng et al. (2023)
;
Zhu et al. (2023)
.

LLMs as judge models

Zhu et al. (2023)
were one of the first to study the capabilities of fine-tuned LLMs for text evaluation, soon followed by
Laskar et al. (2023)
who studied LLM evaluations and found that they fail at factuality based evaluations. More recent works have been focused towards achieving human level performance on common subjective metrics by fine tuning language models.
Deriu and Cieliebak (2019)
trained a regression model on conversational dialogues to show that human-level performance can be achieved using language modeling. But due to the absence of customization,
Ye et al. (2023)
aimed at adding specificity to the evaluation metrics and
Dong et al. (2024)
to finer rubrics. The Prometheus models
Kim et al. (2023)
;
Kim et al. (2024b)
set a benchmark with judge models outperforming GPT-4 on pairwise and pointwise ranking tasks for subjective rubrics.
Kim et al. (2024a)
further studied the performance decrease with increase in subjectivity and created the Prometheus-BGB model which is the current state of the art on this benchmark. More recently,
Vu et al. (2024)
have displayed the importance of variety of training data, covering 102 different training tasks. Several recent works such as
Deshpande et al. (2024)
;
Lee et al. (2024b)
improve judge performance with external augmentations and checklists which further motivates the use of high quality reasoning chains and human guidance to train these models.

Small models as capable reasoners and judges

Several of these fine-tuned state of the art models above are excessively large (in the range of 70B or more parameters) and are difficult to deploy on consumer hardware. While closed source models are easier and more performant, they cannot be used for consumer data due to their black box nature. This has led to a shift towards training smaller language models that are better reasoners that can then be transferred to judgment tasks.
FlowAI (2024)
fine-tuned a phi-3.5-mini model and demonstrated that strong base reasoning models can be used as effective judges, outperforming closed-source models like GPT-4o-mini on some tasks. GLIDER improves upon this by increasing performance on structured data and enabling evaluations that go beyond the traditional scope of model inputs and outputs.

Explanability in evaluators

While several works from
Kim et al. (2023)
;
Vu et al. (2024)
;
Wang et al. (2024)
;
FlowAI (2024)
integrate a feedback in the form of a chain-of-thought-like reasoning
Wei et al. (2022)
, these fine tuned models do not provide explainability that can help distinguish input sample failures from the judge model’s own internal reasoning failures. As observed by
Lv et al. (2024)
, highlight spans can help models focus and avoid losing context while also providing additional explainability and performance improvements. Similar behavior was seen by
Li et al. (2024b)
for AI paraphrased text detection. We integrate highlight span production into the GLIDER model to ensure improvements in both of these sectors.

3
Dataset Generation

We use a mixture of synthetic datasets and openly available datasets to train GLIDER. We first create a detailed taxonomy of potential metrics that we want to cover along with their definitions (
Appendix D
). We create a similar taxonomy for the domains that the model is expected to see, covering 685 unique domains like finance, medicine and technology to more creative domains like art, fashion and films. This is done to ensure generalizability in the GLIDER model. We use the Llama-3.1-70B model for the curation of the data points because of its strong instruction following capabilities and open license
Dubey et al. (2024)
. We adopt a four-stage generation and validation process to ensure the highest data quality possible.

Evaluation text generation:

To ensure that the model does not overfit to a single evaluation field like user input or model output, we diversify our dataset by forcing associations arbitrarily between random tag names representing inputs, outputs, contexts and gold answers (
subsection C.2
). This process randomly assigns number of required words in the generation, the scoring scale (binary for point and pairwise, 1-3 or 1-5 Likert scale) and whether the generation should be a code or a piece of text in accordance with
Appendix D
.

To prevent repetitive generations, we vary the temperature between 0.8 and 1 and
top_p
between 0.9 and 1. Following
Ge et al. (2024)
, we define an elaborate system prompt that provides an ideal personality to the generator model. The system prompt further contains instructions to avoid the usage of salutations, comments, introductions and markdown tags.

Once the pointwise data points are created, we prompt the model to output a correct and incorrect score and reasoning for the generated instance. We encourage the model to actively simulate the behavior of a human annotator and output in bullet point format which improved generation quality in our prompt tuning experiments (
subsection C.2
). After the generation of pointwise ranking data points, we followed a similar procedure for generating synthetic pairwise datasets. Based on the metric, we ask the generator Llama-3.1-70B model to produce sample pairs that differ in terms of quality according to the metric provided (
subsection C.2
).

Validation of scores and reasoning chains:

All our data points contain a chosen response, which is the desired output, and a rejected response containing an undersired or incorrect output. This pairwise data generation is used for RLAIF alignment training phase later
Lee et al. (2024a)
;
Wang et al. (2024)
where we use the rejected samples to lower their probabilities and increase the probabilities of the chosen samples. Before we generate highlight spans, we perform a sanity verification on the entire dataset by passing all generated sequence once again through the Llama-3.1-70B model. The LLM is given a persona of an experienced data curator and verifier which helps it produce more direct and accurate responses, following
Li et al. (2024a)
who show that LLMs are capable of providing high quality verifications. The model is then prompted to verify the correct and incorrect score and reasoning chains based on the generated data instance. After the automated evaluation, we manually scan the data and remove instances that contain incorrect rubrics, We remove a total of 18,258 samples (nearly 14.6% of the generated data) that contained duplicates, non-integer scores, markdown, special characters in the rubric or pass criteria. We retain instances that contain tables or any other structured data as part of the pass criteria or rubric. This decision was made for two reasons: a) this enables the automation of pass criteria and rubric generation where users of the model can prompt the LLM to generated these for their personal use case, thereby saving effort and minimizing domain shift between inference and training tone and formats b) previous models proposed by
FlowAI (2024)
;
Kim et al. (2024b)
are not robust to such structured data and we want to encourage robustness in this sector.

Generating highlight spans:

With existing models only targeting performance improvements, aspects that influence the model’s decision are not explicitly clear. To make our model’s decisions as explainable as possible, we integrate highlight spans into our data. Highlight span extraction has been applied to fields such as AI text detection
Li et al. (2024b)
where it not only brings more explainability by highlighting areas of AI text but also improves performance. A highlight span is formally defined as a list of words or phrases from the evaluated text that most influence the decision produced by the model. We directly prompt Llama-3.1-70B to generate highlight spans with the definition above (details in
subsection C.2
). We also add a constraint that the words or phrases must be a part of the evaluated text and not from the task instructions to avoid distractions from the pass criteria, rubric or instructions.

Data adaptation from external sources:

Along with the synthetic data points, we also leverage some existing human and synthetically annotated and verified datasets. We list the full set of eight external datasets along with the number of samples used for training in the
Table 8
. Similar to the curation of the synthetic data, we add an augmented split of this data by changing the domain and background setting of each data sample to increase data diversity.

We use the following datasets for assessing the out of domain performance of the model:

Pointwise ranking datasets:

We use the following pointwise datasets for our evaluation:

1.

FLASK

Ye et al. (2023)
: This dataset contains 80 base test prompts and 80 instance and skill specific, fine grained rubrics. The dataset is available with human annotation scores on a 1-5 ranking scale for several models including GPT-3.5-Turbo
OpenAI (2023)
, Llama-2-Chat-13B
Touvron et al. (2023)
and others. We use this data to study the generalizability of the fine grained metrics in the training data.

2.

Feedback Bench

Kim et al. (2023)
: contains a set of 1000 data points, that include a set of 200 instance and situation specific instructions. This is the in-domain test set for the Prometheus models
Kim et al. (2024b)
and we include it to check the performance of GLIDER on these instance specific texts.

3.

Summeval

Fabbri et al. (2020)
: Contains summaries generated using 16 models for 100 different source news articles. The dataset is annotated by 3 expert annotators. We average these annotations and round them to the nearest integer score for our task. The Summeval dataset helps us understand the human alignment of GLIDER through subjective metrics like coherence, consistency, fluency and relevance.

4.

BigGen Bench

Kim et al. (2024a)
: Contains 765 instances crafted with a human-in-the-loop process spanning across 77 tasks. The dataset also contains a multilingual subset with text in 10 different languages which we use to evaluate the generalization of GLIDER to other langauges from its pretraining.

Preference datasets

•

HH Eval

Askell et al. (2021)
: This dataset contains 58 preference pairs for harmfulness, 59 pairs for helpfulness, and 61 pairs for honest responses. The dataset additionally contains an
Other
split but since there is no concrete pass criteria for these, we have excluded this split from our test sets. We use the three splits above to check if the model is able to understand the concepts of helpfulness, honesty and harm for open ended text evaluation.

•

MT Bench

Zheng et al. (2023)
: The MT-Bench contains 3,300 pairwise annotated human preferences. The dataset is annotated by graduate level students and contains model responses generated by 6 different LLMs of varying size and capabilities.

•

Reward Bench

Lambert et al. (2024)
: This dataset is aimed at evaluating the quality of reward models on chat, reasoning and safety. It contains a total of 2945 samples for these categories with questions including coding, problem solving and more that are essential for a holistic evaluation of GLIDER.

•

Live Bench

White et al. (2024)
: Considering the potential of contamination or over-optimization of several of the datasets above, we include the Live Bench instruction following subset. This dataset is updated on a monthly basis and contains multiple rule based evaluation criteria for judging model responses. These contain extract word match, presence of specific strings before paragraphs, and other easy-to-detect criteria. Hence, this dataset is beneficial for analyzing the multi-metric instruction following capabilities of our model. We use the November 2024 release of this dataset for our runs.

•

Multilingual Reward Bench

Gureja et al. (2024)
: This is a multilingual version of the Reward Bench dataset above. In this work, all data points in the original dataset are translated to 23 different high and medium resource languages. We use this dataset to study the multilingual preferences of GLIDER.

Baselines and Model selection

We pick three state of the art closed source language models including GPT-4o (2024-11-20 version) and GPT-4o-mini (2024-07-18 version)
OpenAI (2024)
, and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (2024-10-22 version)
Anthropic (2024)
as baselines for our study. From the available open source models, we include the Llama-3.1-70B
Touvron et al. (2023)
and Qwen-2.5-70B
Team (2024)
because of their strong generalization and instruction following performance
Fourrier et al. (2024)
. We also include Prometheus-2 7B and 8x7B versions
Kim et al. (2024b)
that support pairwise and pointwise ranking tasks due to their fully open source nature and strong judge performance. Finally, we include the FlowAI-Judge-v0.1
5
5

5

https://huggingface.co/flowaicom/Flow-Judge-v0.1
due to its comparable size and state of the art performance on several ranking datasets.

For our training, we select the Phi-3.5-mini-instruct
Abdin et al. (2024)
, similar to
FlowAI (2024)
, due to its strong reasoning, instruction understanding capabilities and small size. Using this model, we perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for one epoch. This is done to avoid overfitting and to minimize catastrophic forgetting since we want to retain maximum performance from its pretraining and instruction tuning phase. Following this run, we align this model with the APO zero loss
D’Oosterlinck et al. (2024)
since our synthetic data contains noise and APO has been shown to be more robust in such situations
D’Oosterlinck et al. (2024)
. In addition to this preference optimization loss, we add a standard cross entropy term, similar to
Wang et al. (2024)
, ensuring that the model continues to capture data nuances in the alignment phase. This can be formulated as below:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}=-\beta[\nabla_{\theta}(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x))\delta(r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))\\
\underbrace{-\nabla_{\theta}(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x))\delta(r_{\theta}(x,y_{l}))]}_{APO}\\
-\underbrace{\alpha[\nabla_{\theta}(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x))]}_{NLL}\end{split}

Here, the base policy (
\pi_{\theta}
) and reference policy (
r_{\theta}
) both refer to the SFT GLIDER model,
y_{w}
and
y_{l}
refer to the winning and losing outputs and
\beta
and
\alpha
refer to the scaling terms for both loss terms. We observed that the initial supervised fine tuning phase helps boost performance significantly for smaller models like ours as compared to larger models (greater than 7B parameters) as shown by
Wang et al. (2024)
.

Training setup

We load and train our models using Hugging Face
accelerate

Gugger et al. (2022)
and
trl

von Werra et al. (2020)
on 8xH100 GPUs for approximately 5 hours using FSDP
Zhao et al. (2023)
. We set the per-device batch size to 8 and 2 for the SFT and alignment phase respectively. Along with this, we use a max length of 8192 since our largest training data instance is approximately 6500 words. We set the learning rate to
5\times 10^{-5}
and
5\times 10^{-7}
for each phase of training and a cosine scheduler with a decay of 0.05. We use a
\beta

\alpha
of 1. All results in the following sections were extracted using a temperature of 0 and a

RQ1: Can GLIDER compete with LLMs and existing judge models?

As observed in
Table 1
, our model achieves state of the art performance on the FLASK benchmark, beating GPT-4o while still performing close to models 17
\times
its size on the Feedback Collection dataset. Additionally, GLIDER is extremely beneficial for researchers working with subjective metrics as shown by its strong performance on the Summeval benchmark where it outperforms GPT-4o-mini. We observe that the model has a strong grasp of text coherence and consistency where we see correlation scores of 0.462 and 0.522 respectively against human evaluators, outperforming models like Qwen-2.5-72B.

Analyzing the performance on pairwise ranking datasets in
footnote 4
, we observe that GLIDER not only outperforms all existing open judge models, but also compares to GPT-4o-mini with a difference of less than a single F1 score point. This proves that the generalizable synthetic training is highly effective for SLM evaluators.

RQ2: Can GLIDER perform effective multi-metric evaluation?

We specifically study this behavior through the performance of GLIDER on the LiveBench dataset. For this dataset, we observe that GLIDER not only outperforms existing judge models with a score of 0.654, but also much larger LLMs like GPT-4o-mini and Qwen-2.5-72B with F1 scores of 0.481 and 0.485 respectively on multi-instructional judgment criteria. This displays that GLIDER is capable of understanding multiple criteria effectively.

RQ3: Can GLIDER trained on monolingual data generalize to other languages?

Referencing to
Table 3
, we can see that our GLIDER model outperforms Llama-3.1-70B on average performance. Along with this result, as backed by
footnote 4
, we can see that the model is able to retain its strong reasoning performance from its pretraining, achieving a 90% F1 score on the reasoning subset of the Reward Bench in
Table 3
and
Table 4
. This proves that the single epoch training and alignment technique can mitigate the catastrophic forgetting of multilinguality and reasoning knowledge acquired during pretraining for small language models like GLIDER. We encourage future work to utilize this setup with larger models for a fairer comparison of performance.

RQ4: Do highlight spans improve the performance of GLIDER?

As seen in
footnote 4
, removing the highlight spans from the pairwise training data leads to an overall drop of 0.05 pearson correlation between human and model judgments. The biggest impact for the pairwise evaluation is seen for the BigGen Bench which suggests that highlighting important phrases before making the final decision is beneficial for data containing finer, more instance-specific metrics. Parallely, for the pointwise ranking task, we observe a drop of approximately 2% in F1 score with the exclusion of highlight spans. The largest performance degradation was seen on the LiveBench dataset which suggests that multi-metric evaluations are benefitted from the inclusion of highlights. The overall observation in
Table 1
and
footnote 4
suggests that the impact of performance of the highlight spans is more prominent in the more subjective pointwise ranking tasks as compared to pairwise ranking which suggests that the phrase extraction helps the model focus at certain aspects of the text before making the final scoring decision. This is inline with the findings of
Li et al. (2024b)
where performance of subjective, AI text detection improves with explicit extraction of highlight spans.

Qualitative Analysis

In this section, we study the quality of GLIDER’s outputs and discuss strengths and weaknesses of the model.

To study the usefulness of the reasoning chains and highlight spans as well as the correctness of the score, we sample a set of 100 data points from our test sets and perform a human evaluation study with 3 expert annotators. More details about the human study are provided in
subsection C.4
. As observed in
Table 6
, the model outputs receive average agreement scores of 91%, 90% and 91% for each of the binary metrics. We achieved an overall Krippendorrf’s alpha
Krippendorff (2018)
of 0.838 for the study displaying the high quality of annotations. A key observation from human annotations is the high relevancy of highlight spans generated by the model which shows that the highlights spans are useful and highly relevant for user analyzing judge performance.

Next, we perform a qualitative study on the model’s failure cases (
Table 5
) to analyze its strengths and weaknesses. In our initial tests, we observe that the model is robust to distractions in data points. An example of this distraction in a RAG context is shown in the first row of
Table 5
where it is given counterfactual information about the author of the Harry Potter series that contradicts its pretraining knowledge from the real world data. To further study the model’s biases more closely, we tested it on common logical fallacies

where it achieved an accuracy of 95.6%. On deeper analysis, we noted that the model fails at cases like
"People who drive big cars probably hate the environment"
that are popular opinions but not strictly factual. In the second example in
Table 5
, we observe an infrequently appearing failure case where the pass criteria is ill-defined with respect to the data because of underspecification. Here, the
MODEL OUTPUT
tag in Turkish asks if a medicine is prescribed to children under the age of 11 and this text is judged on the appearance of misinformation. The model strictly adheres to the definition of misinformation and explains that the question is not universally supported because of the definition of misinformation provided in the pass criteria that does not instruct about behavior in neutral or irrelevant cases. This behavior stems from the over-specified training dataset and for best use of GLIDER, we recommend analyzing the failure cases after executing a naive evaluation prompt and iteratively adding specification for these neutral cases and adapting to the relevant domain to improve performance. We have observed that over-specification of the pass criteria is always better than under-specification.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we fine-tune and align a phi-3.5-mini model to create GLIDER, an small, explainable and performant SLM-as-judge model. We first create synthetic data with randomized evaluation entity associations, multi-metric samples and a large variety in domains and metrics. Through our results, we show that GLIDER outperforms closed source models such as GPT-4o-mini and models 17
\times
its size. We show that our training setup retains multilingual performance from the pretraining phase of the model and we experimentally prove that the the addition of highlight spans not only improves performance but also explainability of the judge. We peform an exhaustive human evaluation study on our data and model output quality to show high inter-annotator agreement in favor of GLIDER.

Limitations

While GLIDER is an explainability centric model, there are instances where the model may produce incorrect highlight spans that point to the words or phrases in the prompt instead of the model output. This behavior has been studied in the past by
Chen et al. (2023)
and GLIDER has not been extensively tested for these cases. Moreover, the weaknesses of the phi-3.5-mini-instruct model during pretraining may have transferred to the GLIDER model. Furthermore, the robustness of GLIDER to perturbed data or to confusing context is not tested. We leave this robustness study to future work.

Ethics Statement

While we have used Llama-3.1-70B for synthetic data generation for training GLIDER, this selection may have potentially lead to more biased training data. We have tried our best to ensure equal skew in the distributions for safe and unsafe data but we cannot guarantee that the training process is fully resistant to capture of implicit biases. Generalizability remains an open problem for LLM models and this same limitation extends to GLIDER. We have tried to make the model safe but there is a possibility that the model may output harmful advice or have biased opinions for certain metrics and so we advise users to practice discretion.
