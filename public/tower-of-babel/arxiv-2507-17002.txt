Certain Genus 3 Siegel Cusp Forms with Level are Determined by their Fundamental Fourier Coefficients
Sidney Washburn

Abstract
We prove that vector-valued genus 3 3 Siegel cusp forms for Γ 0 3 ​ ( N ) \Gamma_{0}^{3}(N) with certain nebentypus are determined by their fundamental Fourier coefficients, assuming N N is odd and square-free. A key step in our proof involves strengthening the known corresponding genus 2 2 result. More precisely, we show that genus 2 2 Siegel cusp forms for Γ 0 2 ​ ( N ) \Gamma_{0}^{2}(N) with certain nebentypus are determined by their fundamental Fourier coefficients whose discriminants are coprime to N N . We also prove that Jacobi forms of fundamental index with discriminant coprime to the odd level N N are determined by their primitive theta components.



1
Introduction

1.1
Overview

Nonvanishing results for Fourier coefficients of Siegel modular forms have seen many interesting applications in recent years. One such application is with regards to
L
-functions attached to Siegel cusp forms. Pollack proved in
[
10
]
that, assuming the existence of a nonzero Fourier coefficient corresponding to a maximal order in a quaternion algebra, the Spin
L
-function of genus
3
, level
1
Siegel cusp form has a functional equation and meromorphic continuation. The nonvanishing of such a Fourier coefficient in level
1
was established by Böcherer and Das in
[
5
]
, rendering Pollack’s result unconditional.

This paper makes progress towards the analogous question in level greater than
1
. Precisely, what conditions on the level and the cusp form do we need to guarantee the existence of a nonzero Fourier coefficient corresponding to a maximal order in a quaternion algebra? As an application of this statement in higher level, Eischen, Rosso, and Shah in
[
7
]
proved the algebraicity of special values of the Spin
L
-function for genus
3
Siegel cusp forms of arbitrary level. Their work is conditional on the aforementioned nonvanishing condition. To what extent can we make their algebraicity result unconditional?

Our main result in this paper (see Theorem
3.1
for the precise statement) is that with assumptions on the nebentypus and level, genus
3
Siegel cusp forms are determined by their
fundamental
Fourier coefficients. Fundamental Fourier coefficients do not necessarily correspond to maximal orders, but they are still of great importance. As an example, the nonvanishing of fundamental Fourier coefficients has implications for the existence of Bessel models (see Remark 1.1 in
[
11
]
). Böcherer and Das (
[
5
]
) proved that Siegel cusp forms are determined by their fundamental Fourier coefficients in level
1
. Anamby (
[
3
]
) extended their result to arbitrary level when the genus is
2
. This paper is a first step towards answering the analogous question for arbitrary level in genus
3
.

1.2
Proof Strategy and Difficulties

We give an overview of the proof. The strategy is the same as in
[
5
]
. However, we ran into a couple difficulties employing their method, which we explain in the paragraphs proceeding. Let
F
be a nonzero genus
3
Siegel cusp form (satisfying the conditions of Theorem
3.1
). The overview is as follows. Via the Taylor development, we construct a genus
2
Siegel cusp form,
G
, whose Fourier coefficients are essentially the Fourier–Jacobi coefficients of
F
. We then apply Theorem
3.2
to
G
so as to acquire a nonzero Fourier–Jacobi coefficient,
\varphi
, with desirable properties. We then appeal to Theorem
3.3
to acquire a nonzero ”primitive” theta component
h_{\mu}
, an elliptic cusp form that is attached to
\varphi
. By the primitivity of
h_{\mu}
, we are able to transfer nonvanishing results from the elliptic setting (for
h_{\mu}
) to nonvanishing results of the original Siegel setting (for
F
) and complete the proof.

The difficulties arising for us in the situation of higher level are twofold. First, the Fourier–Jacobi coefficients of fundamental index are
not
enough to establish a correspondence between the fundamental Fourier coefficients of a Siegel cusp form and the odd square-free Fourier coefficients of an elliptic cusp form. In our case, we need a Fourier–Jacobi coefficient which not only has fundamental index, but whose index
also
has discriminant coprime to the level. This extra condition is trivial in the level
1
setting worked out in
[
5
]
and led us to prove Theorem
3.2
, in which we prove that genus
2
cusp forms (with the same assumptions on the nebentypus) are determined by their fundamental Fourier coefficients whose discriminants are coprime to the level.

The second difficulty arises in proving Theorem
3.2
. Specifically, when passing from the Fourier–Jacobi coefficient to an elliptic cusp form with desirable properties. In both
[
5
]
and
[
3
]
, the respective authors achieve the passage to elliptic cusp forms via the existence of nonzero ”primitive” theta components. Primitive theta components are sufficient to guarantee the nonvanishing of a fundamental Fourier coefficient, but they are
not
enough to ensure that the discriminant is coprime to the level. Indeed, the components alone are not modular with respect to a ”large enough” congruence subgroup to say anything with respect to the level. To circumvent this, we use a twisted Eichler–Zagier map (cf.
[
11
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
13
]
) to acquire an elliptic cusp form that inherits the nebentypus from the initial Siegel cusp form. The idea of using the Eichler–Zagier map is not new and appeared, for example, in
[
11
]
where Saha proved that genus
2
newforms of square-free level and even weight are determined by their fundamental Fourier coefficients. A weakness here, apparant in Saha’s result, is that the Eichler–Zagier map alone can only say something meaningful in
even
weight. This is because the Eichler–Zagier map
vanishes
when the weight is odd. Inspired by the work of Skoruppa (
[
13
]
) and Anamby and Das (
[
4
]
), we got around this by twisting the Eichler–Zagier map by a Dirichlet character with the appropriate parity.

As a last remark, we discuss the problem which originally inspired this work: the nonvanishing of a Fourier coefficient corresponding to a maximal order in a quaternion algebra. Let
F=\sum_{T}a_{F}(T)q^{T}
be the Fourier expansion of a genus
3
Siegel cusp form
F
. A Fourier coefficient
a_{F}(T)
corresponds to a maximal order in a quaternion algebra when the discriminant of
T
is an odd prime (
[
5
]
). Our result does not achieve this, unfortunately. The method of passing to a primitive theta component can seemingly at best, provide Fourier coefficients with discriminant of the form
p\cdot d
, where
p
is an odd prime coprime to the level and
d
is a divisor of the level. The obstruction to improving our main result is that it is not known (to the author’s knowledge) if half-integral weight newforms are determined by their prime Fourier coefficients.

1.3
The Question of Higher Genus and Imprimitive Character

A natural question with our results is how we might extend our result to genera greater than
3
. Currently, our genus
3
result is
not
strong enough to push to higher genera. The roadblock here is the existence of a Fourier–Jacobi coefficient with fundamental index having discriminant coprime to the level. The existence of such a Fourier–Jacobi coefficient in genus
3
is contingent upon the analogous genus
2
result. A proof in the genus, say
4
, setting will require a strengthening of the genus
3
case in the same way that we strengthened the genus
2
case here.

In this paper, we were able to strengthen known genus
2
results because there are simply more tools available in genus
2
. For example, the well-known fact that a primitive binary quadratic form represents infinitely many primes allows one to find a Fourier–Jacobi coefficient whose index is odd, prime, and coprime to the level,
without
appealing to an inductive argument. The prime index is crucial in proving injectivity of the twisted Eichler–Zagier map (see Proposition
5.5
).

Another natural question that arises here is the necessity of the assumption on the nebentypus in Theorem
3.1
. We suspect that any assumption on the nebentypus is unnecessary and that it should suffice for the Siegel cusp form to simply be a newform. The obstacle we faced that led to taking on the assumption for the nebentypus lies with the construction of genus
n-1
cusp forms from genus
n
cusp forms via the Taylor development. Even if one starts with a newform, it is not clear if the resulting genus
n-1
form is also a newform or has a nonzero newform component. Our assumption on the nebentypus forces both to be new, allowing us to sidestep this issue.

1.4
Structure of the Paper

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we explain and write down the basic notation and prerequisites needed to understand the proofs and results. In Section
3
, we state the main theorems to be proven. In Section
4
, we prove Theorem
3.3
, whose statement is essential for the passage to a desirable elliptic cusp form in both proofs of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
. The proof combines calculations of Böcherer and Das
[
5
]
and Anamby
[
3
]
. Several propositions and lemmas are then collected in Section
5
. These intermediary results are necessary for the proof of Theorem
3.2
in Section
6
. It is here that we prove the injectivity of a twist of the Eichler–Zagier map. Finally, we prove Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
in Sections
6
and
7
, respectively.

2.1
Basic Notation

Throughout, we use
\exp(\bullet):=e^{2\pi i\cdot\text{tr}(\bullet)}
. The transpose of a vector or matrix

{}^{t}x

R^{n}:=R^{(n,1)}
. We will always take the principal square root defined on arguments in
(-\pi,\pi)
, so that
\sqrt{\bullet}
only returns complex numbers with postive real part and is holomorphic away from the negative real axis.

We write
\Lambda_{n}
(resp.
\Lambda_{n}^{+})
to denote the set of
n\times n
half-integral, symmetric, positive semi-definite (resp. positive definite) matrices. A matrix is half-integral if all of the entries are half-integers, except for the diagonal, whose entries are integers. Equivalently, a symmetric matrix
T
is half-integral if the quadratic form corresponding to
2T
represents only even numbers. For
T\in\Lambda_{n}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we write
T[x]:=\ ^{t}xTx
. Given
T\in\Lambda_{n}
, the
content
of
T
, denoted
c(T)
, is the maximal integer
c
so that
c^{-1}\cdot T
is half-integral.
T
is called
primitive
when
c(T)=1
.

The discriminant of
T\in\Lambda_{n}
is the quantity

\text{disc}(T)=d_{T}:=\begin{cases}\det(2T)&n\ \text{is even}\\
\frac{1}{2}\det(2T)&n\ \text{is odd}\end{cases}

T
is called fundamental if
d_{T}
is odd and square-free. Note that fundamental
T
are automatically primitive.

For a Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
N
, we will write
\mathfrak{f}_{\chi}
to denote the conductor of
\chi
.

Frequently, we will use a block decomposition of an
n\times n
matrix into an upper left
1\times 1
block and a lower right
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block. Unless stated otherwise, we will use fraktur letters to denote the lower right
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block. e.g.

Z=\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast\\
\ast&\mathfrak{Z}\end{pmatrix},\ T=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
*&\mathfrak{T}\end{pmatrix}

2.2
Siegel Modular Forms

Fix an
m
-dimensional complex vector space
V
and let
\rho:\text{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\text{GL}(V)
be a polynomial representation on
V
. For such a
\rho
, there exists a maximal integer
k=k(\rho)
so that
\det^{-k}\otimes\rho
is polynomial.
k(\rho)
is called the
determinantal weight
of
\rho
. Let
N>0
be an integer,
\chi
a Dirichlet character mod
N
. The Siegel upper half-space,
\mathbb{H}_{n}
, is the collection of symmetric complex matrices having positive definite imaginary part:

\mathbb{H}_{n}:=\{Z=X+iY=\ ^{t}Z\ :\ X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{(n,n)},\ Y>0\}

When
n=1
, this is the usual upper half-space in
\mathbb{C}
and we omit the
n
from the notation (i.e.
\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{H}_{1}
).

A holomorphic function
F:\mathbb{H}_{n}\to V
is called a Siegel modular form of genus
n
, automorphy factor
\rho
, with respect to a congruence subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})}
if

F|_{\rho}g=F\ \forall g\in\Gamma.

Here
g=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}
is a block deccomposition of
g
into
n\times n
blocks and
F|_{g}
is the ”slash” operator defined as

(F|_{\rho}g)(Z):=\rho(CZ+D)^{-1}F(g\langle Z\rangle)

with
g\langle Z\rangle:=(AZ+B)(CZ+D)^{-1}
. When
n=1
, we require the usual condition of holomorphicity at infinity. There are three congruence subgroups we frequently consider throughout this paper:

\Gamma^{n}(N):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})}\ :\ \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{mod}\ N\right\}

\Gamma_{1}^{n}(N):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})}\ :\ \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&\ast\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{mod}\ N\right\}

\Gamma_{0}^{n}(N):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})}\ :\ \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast\\
0&\ast\end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{mod}\ N\right\}

When
n=1
, we omit it from the notation, e.g. we write
\Gamma_{0}(N):=\Gamma_{0}^{1}(N)
.

We say a Siegel modular form
F
for
\Gamma_{1}^{n}(N)
has nebentypus
\chi
if
F
satisfies the further condition:

F|_{g}g=\chi(\det(D))\cdot F\ \ \forall\ g\in\Gamma_{0}^{n}(N)

Siegel modular forms have Fourier expansions of the form

F(Z)=\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}a_{F}(T)q^{T},

where
q^{T}:=\exp(TZ)
and
a_{F}(T)\in\mathbb{C}
.
F
is called a cusp form when
a_{F}(T)\neq 0
implies
T>0
. We write
S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{0}^{n}(N),\chi)
to denote the space of Siegel cusp forms with automorphy factor
\rho
and nebentypus
\chi
.

2.4

\phi(\tau,z+x\tau+y)=\exp(-T(\ ^{t}x\tau x+2\ ^{t}xz))\phi(\tau,z)

3.

For every

c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\phi|_{k,T}g=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}^{(1,n)}}c(n,r)q^{n}\zeta^{r}

\begin{pmatrix}n&r/2\\
{}^{t}r/2&T\end{pmatrix}\geq 0

Here,

A Jacobi form

\begin{pmatrix}n&r/2\\
{}^{t}r/2&T\end{pmatrix}>0

. Property

\phi(\tau,z)=\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}/2T\mathbb{Z}^{n}}h_{\mu}(\tau)\Theta_{\mu,T}(\tau,z)

where
\displaystyle\Theta_{\mu,T}(\tau,z):=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\exp(T(\ ^{t}(\ell+\tilde{\mu})(\ell+\tilde{\mu})\tau+2\ ^{t}(\ell+\tilde{\mu})z))

\tilde{\mu}:=(2T)^{-1}\mu

The modularity properties of the theta components

\Theta_{\mu}|_{n/2,T}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\

(1)

2.5
The Fourier–Jacobi Expansion

Siegel modular forms and Jacobi forms are naturally connected via the
Fourier – Jacobi expansion
. Assume
n>1
and let
F\in S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{0}^{n}(N),\chi)
be a Siegel cusp form with Fourier coefficients
a_{F}(T)
. Break the input variable
Z\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
and the indexing half-integral matrix
T
into
1\times 1
and
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block matrices as follows:

Z=\begin{pmatrix}\tau&z\\
{}^{t}z&\mathfrak{Z}\end{pmatrix},T=\begin{pmatrix}t&r/2\\
{}^{t}r/2&\mathfrak{T}\end{pmatrix}

Here
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
,
\mathfrak{Z}\in\mathbb{H}_{n-1}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}^{(1,n-1)},t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},r\in\mathbb{Z}^{(1,n-1)},\mathfrak{T}\in\Lambda_{n-1}
. The Fourier–Jacobi expansion of
F
is the Fourier expansion of
F
in the variable
\mathfrak{Z}
:

F(Z)=F(\tau,z,\mathfrak{Z})=\sum_{\mathfrak{T}\in\Lambda_{n-1}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)q^{\mathfrak{T}}

The Fourier coefficients, now functions
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{(1,n)}\to V
, are called the
Fourier–Jacobi coefficients
of
F
. Crucial for us is the Fourier expansion of
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
:

\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}^{(1,n)}}a_{F}\begin{pmatrix}n&r/2\\
{}^{t}r/2&\mathfrak{T}\end{pmatrix}q^{n}\zeta^{t}

When
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
is scalar-valued, the theta components
h_{\mu}
of
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
have the following Fourier expansions

h_{\mu}(\tau)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{F}\begin{pmatrix}n&{}^{t}\mu/2\\
\mu/2&\mathfrak{T}\end{pmatrix}q^{n-T^{-1}[\mu/2]}

When
F
is scalar-valued, the
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
are also scalar valued. In the vector-valued case, the following proposition will be repeatedly used throughout the paper to reduce ourselves to a scalar-valued Jacobi form:

Proposition 2.1
.

(Proposition 3.4 in
[
5
]
)
Let
F\in S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{0}^{n}(N),\chi)
be a nonzero Siegel modular form. If
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
is a nonzero Fourier–Jacobi coefficient of
F
, then there exists a nonzero scalar-valued component of
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
which is a scalar-valued Jacobi form of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, index
\mathfrak{T}
, nebentypus
\chi
, and weight
k^{\prime}\geq k(\rho)
.

Proof.

Böcherer and Das prove this in
[
5
]
in the case of level
1
. The proof for the case of general level is identical and we refer the reader to
[
5
]
for the proof.

Proof of Theorem
3.3

For convenience, we recall the statement of Theorem

Theorem 3.3

\varphi:\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}

\Gamma_{0}(N)
, nebentypus
\chi

5.1
Primitive Fourier Coefficients

The following lemma is useful for finding a Fourier–Jacobi coefficient with desirable properties later.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
F\in S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{0}^{n}(N),\chi)
be a nonzero cusp form of arbitrary genus
n
and nebentypus
\chi
. Assume that
\mathfrak{f}_{\chi}=N
. Then there exists a primitive
T\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}
so that
a_{F}(T)\neq 0
.

Yamana
[
14
]
proved this in the case when
F
is scalar-valued. The proof here is identical except for an extra step to reduce to the scalar-valued setting. We repeat the proof for convenience.

Proof.

Consider the Fourier–Jacobi expansion
\displaystyle F(Z)=\sum_{\mathfrak{T}\in\Lambda_{n-1}^{+}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)q^{\mathfrak{T}}
. Pick any
\mathfrak{T}
so that
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}\neq 0
. By Proposition
2.1
, we may assume
V=\mathbb{C}^{m}
and pick a nonzero scalar-valued component of
\varphi_{T}
which is still a Jacobi form. Henceforth, we abuse notation and write
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
for this scalar-valued component. Now consider the Taylor expansion of
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
in a neighborhood around
z=0
:

\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}}\frac{1}{\lambda!}\left(\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)|_{z=0}\right)\cdot z^{\lambda}

For a multi-index
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{n-1}
, let
\nu(\lambda):=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}
denote the total degree of
\lambda
. Put
\nu_{0}:=\text{min}\left\{\nu(\lambda)\ :\ \frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)|_{z=0}\neq 0\right\}
and fix any
\lambda
with
\nu(\lambda)=\nu_{0}
and
\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)|_{z=0}\neq 0
. Define
\displaystyle f(\tau):=\dfrac{1}{\lambda!}\left(\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)|_{z=0}\right)
.

The modularity condition of
\varphi_{T}
is as follows:

\varphi_{T}\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac{z}{c\tau+d}\right)=\chi(d)(c\tau+d)^{k}\exp\left(T\cdot\frac{c\ ^{t}zz}{c\tau+d}\right)\varphi_{T}(\tau,z),\ \forall\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(N)

Taylor expanding each side around
z=0
, we find that
f
is modular with respect to
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, having nebentypus
\chi
and satisfying the function equation:

f\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right)=\chi(d)(c\tau+d)^{k+\nu_{0}}f(\tau)

The
n
-th Fourier coefficient of
f
is given by

a_{f}(n)=\frac{1}{\lambda!}\sum_{t_{2}}a_{F}\begin{pmatrix}n&t_{2}\\
&T\end{pmatrix}(2\pi it_{2})^{\lambda},

where the sum runs over all
1\times(n-1)
rows
t_{2}
whose entries are half-integers so that
\begin{pmatrix}n&t_{2}\\
&T\end{pmatrix}
is positive definite. If
F
has no nonzero primitive Fourier coefficients, then
a_{F}\begin{pmatrix}n&t_{2}\\
&T\end{pmatrix}
vanishes whenever
(n,c(T))=1
. By Lemma 3 of
[
14
]
, this implies
F=0
, a contradiction. Hence, some
a_{f}(n)\neq 0
with
(n,c(T))=1
and so some
a_{F}\begin{pmatrix}n&t_{2}\\
&T\end{pmatrix}
does not vanish and this Fourier coefficient is primitive.

∎

(-\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon}N\tau+1)^{-k+1/2}\sum_{\eta}\varepsilon(\eta)h_{\eta}\left(\frac{\tau}{-\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon}N\tau+1}\right)=\frac{1}{4p}\sum_{\mu\ \text{mod}\ 2p}\left(\sum_{\eta\ \text{mod}\ 2p}\varepsilon(\eta)G(-\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon}N,2(\mu-\eta),4p)\right)h_{\mu}(\tau)

It is here that we split into two cases.

1.

\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon}=1
. In this case, the inner sum on the right-hand side becomes

\sum_{\eta\ \text{mod}\ 2p}G(-N,2(\mu-\eta),4p)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{4p}\exp\left(\frac{-N\ell^{2}+2\mu\ell}{4p}\right)\sum_{\eta\ \text{mod}\ 2p}\exp\left(\frac{-2\eta\ell}{4p}\right)

The expression
\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\ \text{mod}\ 2p}\exp\left(\frac{-2\eta\ell}{4p}\right)
evaluates to
0
if
\ell\neq 0
mod
2p
. Otherwise, it evaluates to
2p
. So

\sum_{\eta\ \text{mod}\ 2p}G(-N,2(\mu-\eta),4p)=2p\exp\left(\frac{-N(2p)^{2}+2\mu(2p)}{4p}\right)+2p\exp\left(\frac{-N(4p)^{2}+2\mu(4p)}{4p}\right)=4p

Hence,
h|_{k-1/2}V=h
, finishing the proof of this case.

2.

\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon}=p
. The inner sum is

\sum_{\eta\ \text{mod}\ 2p}\varepsilon(\eta)G(-pN,2(\mu-\eta),4p)

The quadratic Gauss sum
G(-pN,2(\mu-\eta),4p)
vanishes if
\mu\neq\eta
mod
p
and is equal to
pG(-N,2(\mu-\eta)/p,4)
otherwise
[
3
]
. So the inner sum evaluates to

\sum_{\eta\ \text{mod}\ 2p}\varepsilon(\eta)G(-pN,2(\mu-\eta),4p)=\frac{1}{4}(\varepsilon(\mu)G(-N,0,4)+\varepsilon(\mu-p)G(-N,2,4))=\varepsilon(\mu)

Hence,
h_{\varepsilon}|_{k-1/2}V=h_{\varepsilon}
and this case is complete.

Now, given that
\phi\neq 0
, we claim that
h_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
if and only if
\varepsilon(-1)=\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}
. This can be seen by considering the Fourier coefficients of
h_{\varepsilon}
directly. Consider
\phi(\tau,-z)
. From (1), we have
\phi(\tau,-z)=\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}\phi(\tau,z)
. Combining this with
\Theta_{\mu}(\tau,-z)=\Theta_{-\mu}(\tau,z)
, we find that
h_{-\mu}=\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}h_{\mu}
. Recall that
h_{\mu}
has Fourier expansion
h_{\mu}(\tau)=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{\mu}(n)q^{n}
, where
c_{\mu}(n)=c\left(\frac{n+\mu^{2}}{4p},\mu\right)
and
c(n,r)
is the
(n,r)
-th Fourier coefficient of
\phi=\sum_{n,r}c(n,r)q^{n}\zeta^{r}
. So
h_{-\mu}=\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}h_{\mu}
implies
c_{-\mu}(n)=\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}c_{\mu}(n)
.

The Fourier expansion
h_{\varepsilon}
is:

h_{\varepsilon}(\tau/(4p))=\sum_{\mu}\varepsilon(\mu)h_{\mu}(\tau)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{\mu:\mu^{2}=-n\ \text{mod}\ 4p}\varepsilon(\mu)c_{\mu}(n)\right)q^{n}

The
n
-th Fourier coefficient of
h_{\varepsilon}
is thus

c_{\mu}(n)+\varepsilon(-\mu)c_{-\mu}(n)=c_{\mu}(n)+\varepsilon(\mu)\varepsilon(-1)\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}c_{\mu}(n)=(1+\varepsilon(\mu)\varepsilon(-1)\chi(-1)(-1)^{k})c_{\mu}(n),

where
\mu
is any choice of square root of
-n
mod
4p
. Clearly, the
n
-th Fourier coefficient on
h_{\varepsilon}
vanishes if
\varepsilon(-1)\neq\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}
. Otherwise, it is equal to
2\varepsilon(\mu)c_{\mu}(n)
. By Theorem
3.3
, there exists
\mu
coprime to
2p
so that
h_{\mu}\neq 0
. In particular, there exists
n
so that
c_{\mu}(n)\neq 0
and hence
2\varepsilon(\mu)c_{\mu}(n)\neq 0
since
\varepsilon(\mu)\neq 0
as well.

Finally, if
\phi
is cuspidal, then each of the theta components are cuspidal (by direct inspection of the Fourier coefficients) and hence
h_{\varepsilon}
is cuspidal.

∎

A couple remarks on the proof and the implications for the proof of Theorem
3.2
:

Remark
.

From the proof of Proposition
5.5
, one sees that the theta components alone are modular for the group
\Gamma(4pN)
. Altogether, via the Eichler–Zagier map, their modularity is improved to
\Gamma_{0}(4pN\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon})

with nebentypus involving

\chi
. The inherited nebentypus allows us to control where the resulting elliptic cusp form
h_{\varepsilon}
lands. Importantly, it allows us to ensure that
h_{\varepsilon}
is a
newform
.

Remark
.

Proposition
5.5
should also be true in the setting of arbitrary matrix index
T
, under the assumption that
\mathrm{disc}(T)
is an odd prime. We did not pursue writing this down. If Theorem
3.1
is improved to nonvanishing of Fourier coefficients
a_{F}(T)\neq 0
with
\text{disc}(T)
odd, prime, and coprime to the level
N
, then the same arguments appearing in this paper will allow one to generalize to genus
4
(and likely arbitrary genus).

6
Proof of Theorem
3.2

With all the preparations in place, we are now ready to prove Theorem
3.2
. We recall the statement here:

Theorem 3.2:
Let
F\in S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{0}^{2}(N),\chi)
be a nonzero cusp form of odd and square-free level
N
and genus
2
. Assume
k(\rho)\geq 3
. Assume
\chi
satisfies conditions
(1)
and
(2)
as in Theorem
1
. Then there exists a fundamental
T\in\Lambda_{2}^{+}
so that
a_{F}(T)\neq 0
and
(\text{disc}(T),N)=1
.

Proof.

Consider the Fourier and Fourier–Jacobi expansion of
F
:

F(Z)=\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{2}^{+}}a_{F}(T)q^{T}=\sum_{n>0}\varphi_{n}(\tau,z)q^{n}

Here, we are using the block decomposition
T=\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast\\
\ast&n\end{pmatrix}
.

By Lemma
5.1
, there exists
T
primitive so that
a_{F}(T)\neq 0
. It’s well-known that primitive binary quadratic forms represent infinitely many odd primes. In particular, there exists
U\in\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
so that
{}^{t}UTU=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
*&p\end{pmatrix}
, where
p
is an odd prime not dividing
N
and
p>3
. Hence, the
p
-th Fourier–Jacobi coefficient
\varphi_{p}\neq 0
is nonvanishing because
a_{F}(T)=\rho(U)a_{F}(^{t}UTU)
implies
a_{F}(^{t}UTU)\neq 0
.

By Proposition
2.1
,
\varphi_{p}
has a nonzero scalar-valued component which is a Jacobi form. As usual, we abuse notation and refer to this scalar-valued component as
\varphi_{p}
. Consider now the theta expansion of
\varphi_{p}
:

\varphi_{p}(\tau,z)=\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}/2p\mathbb{Z}}h_{\mu}(\tau)\Theta_{\mu}(\tau,z)

By Proposition
5.5
, we may pick a Dirichlet character
\varepsilon
mod
p
so that
h_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
. In particular, we may pick
\varepsilon
so that the conductor of
\varepsilon\chi_{p}^{-1}\chi
is divisible by
p
. Note this is possible because we chose
p>3
and so in particular, there are at least two distinct characters satisfying
\varepsilon(-1)=\chi(-1)(-1)^{k}
. Theorem
3.2
follows from the following claim: There exists
n
coprime to
4N
so that
a_{h_{\varepsilon}}(n)\neq 0
.

We first show Theorem
3.2
follows from the claim. Define

g:=\sum_{(n,4N)=1}a_{h}(n)q^{n}.

g\neq 0
by the claim. By Lemma
5.2
,
g
has level
16p\mathfrak{f}N^{3}
. If there exists
n
with
(n,p)=1
and
a_{g}(n)\neq 0
, then Lemma
5.4
applies and we acquire a nonzero Fourier coefficient
a_{h}(m)
odd and square-free and coprime to
4pN
. Otherwise, by Lemma
5.3
, we have
g(\tau)=g_{p}(p\tau)
for some
g_{p}\in S_{k-1/2}(\Gamma_{0}(16\mathfrak{f}N^{3}),\varepsilon\chi\chi_{p}^{-1}\varepsilon_{p})
. Here we split into two cases:

\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon}=1
: In this case, the Fourier coefficients of
g_{p}
are supported away from the level
16N^{3}
. So Lemma
5.4
applies and there exists
m
odd and square-free with
a_{g_{p}}(m)\neq 0
.

\mathfrak{f}_{\varepsilon}=p
: In this case, we claim that there exists
m
coprime to
p
so that
a_{g_{p}}(m)\neq 0
. Indeed, if not, then by Lemma
5.3
, the conductor of
\varepsilon\chi\chi_{p}^{-1}\epsilon_{p}^{2}=\varepsilon\chi\chi_{p}^{-1}
divides
16pN^{3}/p=16N^{3}
. But this is false by our choice of
\varepsilon
. Hence, the modular form
g_{p}^{\prime}:=\sum_{(n,p)=1}a_{g_{p}}(n)q^{n}
is nonzero with Fourier coefficients supported away from the level
16p^{2}N^{3}
. Lemma
5.4
applies and so we acquire
m
odd and square-free with
a_{g_{p}^{\prime}}(m)\neq 0
.

From the arguments above, we have shown that there exists
(m,4N)=1
with
m
odd and square-free so that
a_{h_{\varepsilon}}(m)\neq 0
. We see that
a_{F}(S)=a_{F}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{m+\mu^{2}}{4p}&\mu/2\\
\mu/2&p\end{pmatrix}\neq 0
and
\text{disc}(S)=\det(2S)=m
is odd, square-free, and coprime to
N
.

Finally, we prove the claim.

We proceed by sieving away the Fourier coefficients divisible by primes dividing
4N
. This idea has appeared in various places (e.g.
[
11
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
). Write
4pN=4q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{t}
, the prime factorization of
4N
. Put
g_{-1}:=h
and define
g_{0}:=\sum_{(n,2)=1}a_{h}(n)q^{n}
. We claim
g_{0}\neq 0
. Indeed, by Lemma
5.2
, if
g_{0}=0
, then
2|N
, which is false.

By Lemma
5.2
, we have
g_{0}\in S_{k-1/2}(\Gamma_{0}(16pN),\chi\chi_{p}^{-1})
. Define for
0\leq i\leq t
,
g_{i}:=\sum_{(n,q_{i})=1}a_{g_{i-1}}(n)
. We claim that
g_{i}\neq 0
for each
i
. Indeed, assume
g_{i}\neq 0
for some
0\leq i<t
. By repeated application of Lemma
5.2
,
g_{i}
is modular with respect to the group
\Gamma_{0}(16pN(q_{1}\cdots q_{i})^{2})
. If
g_{i+1}=0
, then by Lemma
5.3
,
\chi\varepsilon_{q_{i+1}}
has conductor dividing
16pN(q_{1}\cdots q_{i})^{2}/q_{i+1}
. This is impossible however because the latter expression is not divisible by
q_{i+1}
while the conductor is. So claim is proven.

∎

7
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Now that Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
on hand, we can now prove Theorem
3.1
. We recall the statement for convenience:

Theorem 3.1
:
Let
F\in S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{0}^{3}(N),\chi)
be a nonzero Siegel cusp form of odd and square-free level
N
and genus
3
. Assume
k(\rho)\geq 3
. Further assume the following conditions on
\chi
:

1.

\mathfrak{f}_{\chi}=N
.

2.

For each
p|N
,
p
divides the conductor of
\chi\epsilon_{p}
, where
\epsilon_{p}
is the natural quadratic character attached to the number field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p})
.

Then there exists a fundamental
T\in\Lambda_{3}^{+}
so that
a_{F}(T)\neq 0
.

Proof.

For
Z\in\mathbb{H}_{3}
, write
Z=\begin{pmatrix}\tau&z\\
{}^{t}z&\mathfrak{Z}\end{pmatrix}
as before. Taylor expand
F
around
z=0
:

F(\tau,z,\mathfrak{Z})=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}F_{\lambda}(\tau,\mathfrak{Z})z^{\lambda}.

Let
\nu_{0}
be the minimal integer so that there exists
\lambda
with
\nu(\lambda)=\nu_{0}
and
F_{\lambda}\neq 0
. Define a vector-valued function
F^{\circ}:\mathbb{H}_{1}\times\mathbb{H}_{2}\to V\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[x_{2},x_{3}]_{\nu_{0}}
as follows

F^{\circ}(\tau,\mathfrak{Z}):=\sum_{\lambda:\nu(\lambda)=\nu_{0}}F_{\lambda}(\tau,\mathfrak{Z})\otimes x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}x_{3}^{\lambda_{3}}

Consider the embedding

(\cdot)^{\downarrow}:\text{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow\text{Sp}_{6}(\mathbb{Z}),g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&&0&\\
&a&&b\\
0&&1&\\
&c&&d\end{pmatrix}=g^{\downarrow}.

Proposition (
[
5
]
Prop 3.1):
We have the following transformation law for
F^{\circ}
:

(F|_{\rho}g^{\downarrow})^{\circ}(\tau,z)=\rho\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&cz+d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\otimes\text{Sym}^{\nu_{0}}(cz+d)^{-1}F^{\circ}(\tau,g\langle z\rangle).

Here,
\text{Sym}^{\nu_{0}}
is the symmetric power representation of
\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
on
\mathbb{C}[x_{2},x_{3}]_{\nu_{0}}
given by
(A\cdot f)(x):=f(xA)
.

Let
\rho^{\prime}
denote the restriction of
\rho
to the embedding

\text{GL}_{2}\hookrightarrow\text{GL}_{3},g\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&g\end{pmatrix}.

Then, restated, the proposition simply says that

(F|_{\rho}g^{\downarrow})^{\circ}(\tau,z)=(F^{\circ}|_{\rho^{\prime}\otimes\text{Sym}^{\nu_{0}}}g)(\tau,z)

When
g\in\Gamma_{0}^{n-1}(N)
, we have by the assumption on
F
that
F|_{\rho}g^{\downarrow}=\chi(\det(d))F
. So together with the proposition, we have that for every fixed
\tau
,

F^{\circ}|_{\rho^{\prime}\otimes\text{Sym}^{\nu_{0}}}g=\chi(\det(d))F^{\circ}

In particular,
F^{\circ}\in S_{\rho^{\prime}\otimes\text{Sym}^{\nu_{0}}}^{2}(\Gamma_{0}^{2}(N)),\chi)
is a non-zero, genus
2
Siegel cusp form with nebentypus
\chi
. Because
k(\rho^{\prime}\otimes\mathrm{Sym}^{\nu_{0}})\geq k(\rho)\geq 3
, we know by Theorem
3.2
that
F^{\circ}
has a non-zero fundamental Fourier coefficient
a_{F^{\circ}}(\mathfrak{T})
with
(\text{disc}(\mathfrak{T}),N)=1
.

Consider now the Fourier–Jacobi expansion
F(Z)=\sum_{\mathfrak{T}\in\Lambda_{2}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}(\tau,z)q^{\mathfrak{T}}
. A simple calculation gives that the
\mathfrak{T}
-th Fourier coefficient of
F^{\circ}
is given by

a_{F^{\circ}}(\mathfrak{T})=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{1}{\lambda!}\left(\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}\right)|_{z=0}(\tau)\otimes x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}

So
a_{F^{\circ}}(\mathfrak{T})\neq 0
implies
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}\neq 0
. Hence, there exists a nonzero Fourier–Jacobi coefficient
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
with fundamental index
\mathfrak{T}
whose discriminant is coprime to
N
.

By Proposition
2.1
,
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
has a nonzero scalar-valued component which is a Jacobi form satisfying the conditions of Theorem
3.3
. We abuse notation as usual and refer to this nonzero component as
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
. By Theorem
3.3
,
\varphi_{\mathfrak{T}}
has a nonzero theta component
h_{\mu}\neq 0
so that
\mathfrak{T}^{-1}[\mu/2]
has denominator equal to
\text{disc}(\mathfrak{T})
. The Fourier expansion of
h_{\mu}
is given:

h_{\mu}(\tau)=\sum_{\ell>0}a_{F}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&{}^{t}\mu/2\\
\mu/2&\mathfrak{T}\end{pmatrix}\exp((\ell-\mathfrak{T}^{-1}[\mu/2])\tau)

From the formula
\det\begin{pmatrix}\ell&{}^{t}\mu/2\\
{}^{t}\mu/2&\mathfrak{T}\end{pmatrix}=(\ell-\mathfrak{T}^{-1}[\mu])\det(\mathfrak{T})
, we see that an odd and square-free Fourier coefficient of
h_{\mu}(d_{\mathfrak{T}}\cdot\tau)
is exactly a fundamental Fourier coefficient of
F
. Hence, we must prove that
h_{\mu}
has a nonzero odd and square-free Fourier coefficient.

First, note that
h_{\mu}
is modular with respect to the group
\Gamma_{1}(d_{\mathfrak{T}}N)\cap\Gamma^{1}(d_{\mathfrak{T}})
(see the proof of Proposition
5.5
). Here
\Gamma^{1}(d_{\mathfrak{T}})\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is the subgroup of matrices which are strictly lower triangular modulo
d_{\mathfrak{T}}
. Hence
h_{\mu}(d_{\mathfrak{T}}\tau)
is modular with respect to
\Gamma_{1}(d_{\mathfrak{T}}^{2}N)
. We now have two cases:

Case 1:
The Fourier coefficients of
h_{\mu}(d_{\mathfrak{T}}\tau)
are supported away from the level
d_{\mathfrak{T}}^{2}N
. In this case, Theorem 4.6 of
[
5
]
applies and we are done.

Case 2:
The Fourier coefficients of
h_{\mu}(d_{\mathfrak{T}}\tau)
are
not
supported away from the level. Note that because
\mathfrak{T}^{-1}[\mu/2]
has denominator
\mathrm{disc}(\mathfrak{T})
, the Fourier coefficients
are
supported away from
d_{\mathfrak{T}}
. To proceed, we apply the same sieving procedure we’ve seen in the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Let
N=p_{1}\cdots p_{t}
be the prime factorization of
N
and put
g_{0}:=h_{\mu}(d_{\mathfrak{T}}\tau)
. For
i\geq 0
, define
g_{i+1}(\tau)
as follows:

g_{i+1}(\tau)=\begin{cases}g_{i}\left(\dfrac{\tau}{p_{i+1}}\right)&\text{if}\ a_{g_{i}}(n)=0\ \text{whenever}\ (n,p_{i+1})=1\\
\\
\displaystyle\sum_{(n,p_{i+1})=1}a_{g_{i}}(n)q^{n}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

By classical old and newform theory (see e.g. Lemma
1
and
2
in
[
14
]
),
g_{t}
is nonzero of level equal to some divisor of
d_{\mathfrak{T}}^{2}N^{3}
. The Fourier coefficients are given by
a_{g_{t}}(n)=a_{g_{0}}(\ell n)
, where
\ell
is some divisor of
N
. Furthermore, the Fourier coefficients of
g_{t}
are supported away from
d_{\mathfrak{T}}^{2}N^{3}
and in particular, supported away from the level. So by Theorem 4.6 in
[
5
]
,
g_{t}
has nonzero odd and square free Fourier coefficients. Because
N
is odd and square-free, the corresponding Fourier coefficient of
F
is also odd and square-free and so Theorem 1 is proved.

∎
