The anatomical and radiologic relationship of the superior longitudinal system with the middle cerebral artery branches
Authors: Mehmet E. Akyuz, Mehmet H. Sahin, Ufuk Temtek, Egemen Gok, Abdullah Keles, Selin Bozdag, Ufuk Erginoglu, Hakan H. Kadıoglu, Ugur Ture, Mustafa K. Baskaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe superior longitudinal fasciculus/arcuate fasciculus (SLF/AF) connects the temporal and frontal lobes through the parietal region. Although well-studied, the SLF/AF arterial supply and 3D-relationship with the middle cerebral artery (MCA) have not been reported. We examined this using cadaveric fiber dissection and tractography to provide neurosurgical-relevant 3D information.MethodsTen cadaveric hemispheres were removed, vascular tissues cleaned, and fiber dissection performed. In another 10 hemispheres, artery-preserving dissections were carried out in situ. Diffusion Tensor Imaging tractography and Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) of 10 healthy individuals were 3D reconstructed with consistent nomenclature.ResultsFiber dissections revealed the superior longitudinal complex (SLF-C), the superior longitudinal fasciculus II (SLF-II), the superior longitudinal fasciculus III (SLF-III), and the arcuate fasciculus (AF) fiber tracts in vascular and meningeal cleared hemispheres. These fiber tracts were also revealed in five heads with intact MCA arterial structures from the MCA bifurcation to the medullary branches. This provided visualization of the SLF/AF and its 3D relationship with the MCA. SLF-C, SLF-II, SLF-III, and AF were shown individually and together with tractography of healthy individuals. The 3D relationship between these fiber tracts, the MCA and its branches was demonstrated by overlaying MRA vascular segmentations onto tractography images.ConclusionThis is the first report showing the SLF/AF and MCA relationship as a whole, from its cortical to its medullary branches, by overlaying tractography with MRA reconstructions. This 3D analysis provides valuable insights into their spatial organization to enhance surgical practice and understand the vascular associations of the SLF/AF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-026-06931-2.

Introduction

The superior longitudinal fasciculus/arcuate fasciculus (SLF/AF) is the largest association fiber system connecting the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes in the ipsilateral perisylvian area. The superior longitudinal system plays an important role in language function, while also contributing to the regulation of motor behavior, working memory, and the transmission of somatosensory information, and connecting cortical areas that give rise to cortico-cerebellar or cortico-ponto-cerebellar projections [12, 27, 36].

Since the early seventeenth century and continuing to the present day, the SLF/AF has been investigated by anatomists and described using a variety of nomenclatures [5, 23, 27, 29, 35, 37]. This tract has been described as both a single bundle of fibers as well as consisting of different components [49, 52]. Recent advances in tractography have provided in vivo information about the anatomy of this fiber tract. While these studies have contributed to SLF/AF nomenclature, its component subdivisions, and to its anatomical pathway, controversy still continues [6, 25, 52].

The brain's arterial supply has also been extensively studied by anatomists and neurosurgeons [1, 16]. Such studies have particularly focused on the pial network along the cortical surface and the leptomeningeal arteries supplying deep brain structures, with the exception of one study which examined the arterial supply of the entire white matter13. However, this work did not examined the specific fiber tracts and their arterial supply, nor did it demonstrate the relationship between white matter fibers and vascular structures [42].

Previous studies investigating the relationship between the middle cerebral artery (MCA) and white matter fibers have primarily focused on the cortical terminal branches of the M1–M2-M3 segments [11, 43, 48]. Inspired by these studies, our objective was to investigate the relationship between the SLF/AF pathway and the M3-M4 branches of the MCA, a region that has received no attention to date.

The SLF/AF, the largest fiber system crossing one hemisphere of the brain, may be the most affected fiber bundle when performing surgery for intrinsic brain tumors. To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies that show the SLF/AF arterial supply. The present study is the first that use a unique white matter dissection technique to preserve arterial structures and anatomical landmarks to reveal the SLF/AF and their arterial relationships from the origin of the MCA to where it reaches the SLF/AF. Our hope is that this will provide neurosurgeons with an improved understanding of the 3-dimensional (3D) anatomical architecture of the SLF/AF with respect to arterial surgical landmarks, to enable safer surgical navigation in this sensitive region.

Methods

Cadaveric studies were performed at the University of Wisconsin School of Medicine and Public Health and at the Atatürk University School of Medicine. Cadaveric studies were exempted for both institutions. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) studies were performed at Ataturk University with ethical approval obtained from Atatürk University (Meeting No: 4; Decision No: 88, 292). No MRI data were acquired or analyzed at the University of Wisconsin.

Ten human heads (20 hemispheres), previously fixed by immersion in 10% formalin for a minimum of 8 weeks, were investigated. The mean age of the donors was 62 years (range 58–74 years; 6 male, 4 female). Prior to vascular preparation, the fixative solution was replaced with 70% ethyl alcohol.

The common carotid arteries, vertebral arteries, and internal jugular veins were carefully dissected and cannulated using silicone tubing secured with silk sutures. The vessels were irrigated under low pressure with tap water for five days. Small cervical vessels were clamped, and the epidural and subdural spaces were tamponed to prevent leakage.

Silicone (3110 RTV silicone rubber; Dow Corning, Midland, MI, USA) was mixed with thinner (polydimethylsiloxane 200 fluid, 20 CST; Dow Corning). For arterial injection, a thinner-to-silicone ratio of 2:1 was used; for venous injection, a 1:1 ratio was used. Powder pigment (red for arteries and blue for veins) was added until a vivid color was achieved.

The colored silicone was injected first into the arterial system and subsequently into the venous system. Injection was performed slowly and under low pressure; in cases of pressure build-up, the injection was temporarily halted and then resumed gradually. Perforating arteries required prolonged low-pressure injection to achieve adequate perfusion [7]. The causes of death were not due to cranial pathologies. The formalin-fixed heads were stored at −16 °C for 2 weeks according to the Klinger method [23]. The specimens were then washed under running water, thawed, and stored in 70% alcohol throughout all dissections.

Dissection of arachnoid mater, arteries, veins, and gray and white matter were performed under a Carl Zeiss OPMI-1 microscope (Oberkochen, Germany) using a microdissector, wooden spatula, microhook and microscissors. Scalpels and high-speed surgical drills (Midas Rex® Legend, Minneapolis, MN) were used for skin, bone and dura removal. Hemispheres of five of the heads were removed from the skull. The arachnoid, artery and vein tissues of these hemispheres were excised and prepared for standard white matter dissection. With the other 5 heads specimens, the skin, subcutaneous tissue, muscle, bone, dura, arachnoid membrane, and veins on the supratentorial area were removed. For topographic advantage in these specimens, white matter dissections were planned on the skull with the ear helix preserved. Graded white matter dissections were performed along the anatomical axes from lateral to medial. During dissection, arterial structures identified while progressing from lateral to medial were secured with a needle, thereby preserving their location and depth. All stages of the dissection were photographed using a Canon® 200 d camera EF-S with an 18–55 mm f/3.5–5.6 DC III lens, and with a Canon® Macro Art Lens EF 100 mm f/1:2.8 lens, and a Ring Light M4 84-Watt light source.

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) and Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) was obtained from 10 healthy individual volunteers without any intracranial pathology at Atatürk University, School of Medicine. MRIs of these 10 individuals were taken with the same device (Siemens® Magneta Skyra 3.0 T, Erlangen, Germany), with DTI and MRA images also taken in the same session. A multi-shell diffusion scheme was used, and the b-values were 1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm2. The number of diffusion sampling directions were 90, 90, and 90, respectively. The in-plane resolution and slice thickness were both 1.25 mm (TR = 5500 ms, TE = 89 ms, resolution = 1.25 mm × 1.25 mm, FoV = 210 mm × 180 mm, matrix = 144 × 168). The DSI data were reconstructed using the generalized q-sampling imaging approach using a diffusion distance ratio of 1.2 as recommended by the original study [56]. DTI images were preprocessed using MRTrix3 [47]. The vascular segmentations of the MCA from MRA images were then obtained by using 3D Slicer (https://www.slicer.org/) [21].

Gadolinium contrast-enhanced time of flight TOF 3D MRI sequences were used as a basis for segmentation of the cerebral vasculature. In the initial phase of pre-processing, we employ 3D Slicer (https://www.slicer.org/), a freely available segmentation package, to perform image segmentation. An initial mask was established by defining an intensity threshold. Subsequently, manual editing was conducted using a paintbrush tool within the software. This manual correction process was carried out in stages; the tasks included removing small islands, filling holes, and most importantly, painting fine vessels. After mask editing, the finalized vessel geometries were extended by six times the vessel diameter at each model’s inlet and outlet(s) using a Python script available in Vascular Modeling ToolKit (VMTK, version 1.4).

Tractographies were obtained from the preprocessed DTIs using the automatic tracking module in DSI-Studio (https://dsi-studio.labsolver.org/) [55]. Following the auto-tracking process, some erroneous fiber tracts were identified. To address this issue, we manually refined the tractographies using the DSI-Studio manual dissection tool ensuring that only anatomically relevant fiber tracts were retained.

Tractographies obtained using DSI-Studio were exported as 3D OBJ models. To visualize these tractography models in the same space as the vascular segmentation viewed with 3D Slicer, adjustments were required due to differences in affine matrices and voxel sizes between the MRI used for tractography and the MRI used for vascular segmentation. To address these discrepancies, we applied transformations to the OBJ models using Python’s standard math module from its library (https://www.python.org). First, we computed the scaling factor based on the voxel size differences between the two MRIs and rescaled the OBJ models accordingly. Then, by performing calculations based on the affine matrices, we adjusted the positions of the tractography models to correctly align them with the vascular segmentation. After verifying the topological correspondence between the tractographic models and vascular segmentation, we incorporated additional cortical segmentations, including the operculum, the precentral gyrus (PrG), and the postcentral gyrus (PoG), to enhance the representation of their spatial relationships with other cortical regions.

The gyrus walls over the decorticated areas were completely removed and the SLF-C, SLF-II, SLF-III were exposed. We named the portion of the SLF from the precentral gyrus to the MFG as the SLF- II, the portion up to the IFG as the SLF-III, and the parts extending from the precentral gyrus to the temporoparietal areas as the SLF-complex, in combination with the SLF/AF group. The SLF-C fibers between the SMG and PrG were removed layer by layer, revealing the distinction between the SLF-C and the more deeply located fibers thought to be the AF (Fig. 1e).

After the MCA bifurcation, the medullary branches originating from the cortical branches of the inferior and superior divisions extend toward the white matter fibers. Anatomically and radiologically, the projections of the cortical branches onto the SLF/AF were demonstrated, and the medullary branches extending to the fibers were identified in the anatomical study. The U-fibers were then removed, and the SLF-C, SLF-II and SLF-III could be viewed along with the medullary brunches of the MCA extending to these fibers. It was observed that the medullar branches of the p.fra.a and the p.cen.a reached the SLF-II and SLF-III located in front of the PrG. Also noted was that the SLF-II, which ends more anteriorly, receives branches from the o.fr.a. It was also observed that the medullary branches of the cen.a, the ant.p.a, the pos.p.a, the ang.a and the t.occ.a reached the SLF-C. Also observed was that the cortical arteries were in proximity in the TTG, PTe, ANG, SMG, PoG and POp projections, and there was a dense medullary arterial network reaching the SLF-C in this region. The decorticated gyrus walls of the IFG and STG facing the insula were then shortened by sharp dissections, and the superior trunk and inferior trunk of the middle cerebral artery on the insula were demonstrated. (Fig. 2d). The SLF-C fibers between the SMG and PrG were removed and the fibers that were presumed to be the AF were located deeper under this region, showing their relationship with the medullary branches of the MCA (Fig. 2e-f). However, it was technically very difficult to demonstrate the medullary branches extending from the MCA to the SLF/AF radiologically; this relationship was demonstrated in cadaver specimens.

The sulcal landmarks of the lateral cortical surface were systematically used to evaluate the topographic distribution of the M4 cortical branches and their relationship with the underlying white matter pathways. In this context, the superior frontal sulcus (sfs), inferior frontal sulcus (ifs), precentral sulcus (pcs), central sulcus (ce), postcentral sulcus (pocs), intraparietal sulcus (ips), lateral sulcus (ls), and superior temporal sulcus (sts) served as consistent anatomical reference points for correlation with the SLF/AF projections (Fig. 1a, 2a).

A particularly dense medullary arterial network was observed at the distal end of the lateral sulcus, extending through the temporoparietal region between the sts and ips and reaching the interwoven fiber bundles of the SLF and AF up to the level of the ce (Fig. 2b–e).

In addition, the orbitofrontal artery (o.fr.a), prefrontal artery (p.fr.a), and precentral artery (p.cen.a) were predominantly identified within the frontal projection zone between the sfs and ifs, where their medullary branches were observed to reach the SLF-II. More inferiorly, within the frontal projection zone between the IFS and LS, the medullary branches of these arteries extended toward the SLF-III and the frontal segment of the AF (Fig. 2e).

These sulcal landmarks further provided reliable cortical entry references for predicting the location of the deep white matter pathways and their medullary arterial supply, thereby improving the potential microsurgical applicability of the anatomical findings.

Discussion

Advancements in DTI and tractography techniques have led to some variations in the terminology used for the arcuate fasciculus, the superior longitudinal fasciculus, and their subcomponents. Some authors have defined the AF as a subcomponent of the SLF [8, 9, 14, 20, 28, 52, 58], while others consider the SLF and AF as two different components wherein they define the fibers providing the frontal-parietal connection as the SLF and the fibers providing the frontal–temporal connection on the same line as the AF [20, 40, 46, 52].

The reasoning for considering the SLF and AF as different fiber structures was thought to be because the SLF shows approximately equal density in the two hemispheres, while in contrast, the AF has a higher fiber density in the left hemisphere than in the right hemisphere, thereby leading to its acceptance as a different component [30, 38, 46, 51, 52]. In addition, studies that indicate SLF/AF function have reported that the AF is related to language processing, while the SLF is related to spatial attention, speech articulation and higher motor functions [18, 40, 54, 57].

In white matter fiber dissection, it is not possible to distinguish intertwined white matter pathways where they intersects. In postmortem white matter dissections, 3D reconstructions based on DTI and MRIs serve as a useful guide, enabling the partial separation of fiber groups during dissections in these intersection regions. For our anatomical dissections and tractographic images, we adopted a nomenclature in line with the definitions of the AF, SLF, and their subgroups as outlined by Makris et al. [25].

Arterial supply and relationship to the SLF/AF pathway

The arterial supply of the brain varies substantially between individuals as has been demonstrated in many studies, although almost all prior works have focused on gray matter [45, 50]. The arterial supply of the white matter fibers of the internal capsule, the anterior commissure, the corpus callosum and the optic radiation have been demonstrated in a small number of studies, but overall, studies of the white matter arterial supply are very limited [4]. The cerebral cortex receives its blood supply from the adjacent pial network, but the situation is more complex for white matter that is distant from the brain surface [41].

There are few studies that show the arterial supply of the SLF/AF fiber system. In these works, the main arteries are stained in various colors and cross-sectional vascularization is shown without preserving the integrity between the micro-arteries and the main arteries [42]. Although these anatomical studies are important, from a neurosurgeon's perspective 2-dimensional studies are insufficient for the surgical understanding of association pathways.

Whether vascular, oncologic or functional surgery, surgeons often have to face the cortex and its associated vessels. In line with the basic principle of microneurosurgery where the goal is minimal damage, surgeons perform surgery by preserving vascular structures as much as possible. In microneurosurgery, vessels are the most important guideposts and in most of the surgical approaches, these vessels are preserved and followed.

Previous microanatomical studies have already provided important insights into the contribution of deeper MCA branches to white matter vascularization. Delion and Mercier demonstrated that long insular perforators arising from the M2 segment, the M2–M3 junction, and occasionally the M3 segment may extend toward the centrum semiovale and reach major white matter pathways, including the AF and occipitofrontal fasciculus. Similarly, Türe et al. emphasized that preservation of the long insular arteries is critical, as their injury may result in infarction involving the corona radiata. In the present study, however, we specifically focused on the sulcal and subcortical relationship of the M4 cortical branches with the SLF/AF complex along the lateral hemispheric surface, as this aspect has not been previously demonstrated in detail [11, 48].

In this study, we demonstrated the microvascular supply of the SLF/AF by the MCA using a different method. The cortical branches of the MCA were followed deeper, revealing the micro-arterial connections over a large area of the SLF/AF. This anatomical dissection technique was preferred to ensure topographic dominance. Thus, the micro-arteries reaching the SLF/AF from the cortical branches of the MCA were preserved together with the main arterial connections, where they are shown herein in 3D anatomy for the first time.

Vascular structures exhibit substantial variability throughout the body. It is well established that precise delineation of vascular territories cannot be reliably achieved using a limited number of subjects. Furthermore, territorial boundaries differ among individuals and are influenced by vascular health status and hemodynamic conditions. In addition, because arterial injection in this study was performed globally rather than selectively into individual cerebral arteries, precise inter-arterial territorial boundaries were not intended to be defined.

The SLF/AF is a broad fiber system extending from the temporal to the frontal lobe, and its vascular supply in the perisylvian region is provided predominantly by branches of the MCA. Although the names and numbers of cortical branches may vary depending on anatomical variation and individual hemodynamic conditions, the aim of this study was to outline the medullary branches reaching the SLF/AF from the cortical branches of the MCA and to demonstrate their three-dimensional anatomical relationship. Detailed territorial mapping and quantitative assessment of vascular boundaries would require selective injection techniques and represent a distinct line of investigation that may be addressed in future studies.

The AF is the main fiber structure connecting Wercincke's area to Broca's area, and its damage results in a conductive aphasia disconnection syndrome, a language disorder characterized by repetition disorder, fluent paraphasic speech and an absence of auditory comprehension [2, 13]. The SLF-III has a similar function to the AF in the left hemisphere and plays a role in speech, while in the right hemisphere it has a functional role in spatial awareness [53]. The SLF-II has a role in speech motor planning and/or syntax processing in the left hemisphere, while the right hemisphere has functional role in fast preferential visuospatial processing [53]. The SLF-I has been described as a dorsal component of the associative system, coursing through the corona radiata and the superior lateral projections of the corpus callosum. However, its distinct anatomical identity remains debated, as some recent studies suggest that its close spatial relationship to the cingulum may render it difficult to distinguish as a separate tract [19].

The SLF/AF fiber system crosses the parietal lobe and connects the frontal and temporal lobes and thus covers a large area. If surgery is to be performed in this large region with safety and without postoperative sequelae, both the anatomical position of the fiber structures should be known very well, and care should be taken to protect the vascular structures associated with these fiber structures. In this anatomical study, both the SLF/AF were revealed and demonstrated by preserving the anatomical landmarks and the relationship with the entire vascular network up to the microvascular structures reaching the fibers. In addition, our dissections showed that the vascular structures were densely located especially in the temporoparietal, SMG and ANG regions. In the present study, the sulcal anatomy of the lateral cortical surface was additionally demonstrated to further delineate the topographic relationship between the course of the M4 cortical branches and their medullary extensions toward the SLF/AF complex. Although the use of sulcal anatomical landmarks has long been an established principle in neurosurgical planning, the aim of this study was to evaluate these cortical reference points together with the vascular structures and the underlying white matter pathways. One of the notable findings of our study was the presence of a prominently dense medullary arterial network within the temporoparietal transition zone, particularly at the distal end of the ls and between the sts and ips. This dense vascular network was shown to extend up to the level of the ce, reaching the interwoven fiber bundles of the SLF and AF. This observation suggests that the temporoparietal perisylvian corridor may represent a distinct anatomical region where vascular structures and eloquent white matter pathways converge in close proximity. Vascular preservation is of great importance in performing surgery in this region similar to surgeries in the insular region [26, 48]. This anatomical study is thus intended to provide a beneficial guide for safe surgery.

Limitations

The MCA has many anatomical variations. Due to the limited number of cadavers studied, it is not possible to demonstrate the breadth of variations. Another technical limitation is that not all cadavers injected with colored silicone have the same filling quality. This limitation predictably manifests itself most frequently in the subcortical thin vessels. The uniform preservation of each of the arterial thin branches is another technical limitation for cadaveric dissections. For this reason, images were obtained by partially preserving the cortical-subcortical vessel connections to the SLF/AF region. In addition, one limitation of this study is that the deeper contribution of long insular perforators from the M2–M3 segments to the SLF/AF, as reported in previous microanatomical studies, was not directly visualized using our current dissection method. Accordingly, the present findings primarily represent the cortical and subcortical medullary vascular relationships of the M4 branches along the lateral hemispheric surface.

Just as there are variations in the vessels, there are variations in the fiber tracts from person to person, and in the same person. in the form of different thickness, density and orientations on the right and left sides. We preferred to use tractographic and angiographic images that were similar to our anatomical dissections in terms of both side and orientation. However, the variation of both fiber tracts and arterial structures creates limitations in radiological images.

Both anatomical and radiological studies can be examined segmentally in multiple spatial planes. The relationship between the SLF/AF and the fine branches of the MCA can be better demonstrated in this way. This aspect can be considered in future studies.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, the relationship between the SLF/AF and vascular structures are demonstrated herein for the first time using 3D anatomical dissection. We show the relationship of the MCA to the SLF/AF as a whole from the point where it leaves the bifurcation to where it reaches the fiber pathway. We note that there is a dense arterial structure in the temporoparietal, ANG and SMG region, and care should be taken for vascular protection in this region.
