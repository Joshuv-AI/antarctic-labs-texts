Clinical practices, self-confidence, and perceived barriers in tracheostomy care among respiratory therapists in Saudi Arabia: a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdulelah M Aldhahir, Ahmed H Alasimi, Yunus A Jothmi, Muhannad N Khowaji, Abdullah A Najaei, Jehad M Rayyani, Ammar N Alwadani, Abdullah A Alqarni, Jaber S Alqahtani, Rayan A Siraj, Munyra Alhotye, Mohammed A Reyany, Mohamed H Hakamy, Mohammad A Shawany, Ali Hakamy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTracheostomy care is a critical aspect of respiratory therapy, requiring specialized knowledge and adherence to standardized protocols to ensure optimal patient outcomes. However, variations in practice and a lack of formalized guidelines may contribute to inconsistencies in tracheostomy management. Therefore, this study aimed to assess clinical practices, self-confidence, and perceived barriers in tracheostomy care among respiratory therapists (RTs) in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive survey was distributed from June to October 2024 among RTs in Saudi Arabia. Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were performed to identify key trends and associations.ResultsA total of 1,012 RTs participated in the study, with the majority being male (588, 58.1%). Tracheostomy care was available in 90.7% of hospitals, although 22.4% reported the absence of specific protocols. Formal training in tracheostomy care was limited, with only 42.3% of RTs receiving 1–5 h of instruction. Attitude of evidence-based practices was generally positive, as 59.9% of respondents felt up to date with current guidelines. Confidence levels in managing tracheostomy patients were notably low (20.9%) and slightly higher for ventilator-assisted patients (23.6%). To enhance skills, 53.6% of RTs visited specialist centers, and many participated in conferences (52.2%) and workshops (49.2%). The most significant barriers to incorporating tracheostomy care into RT services included lack of knowledge (70.7%), inadequate training (59.7%), and the absence of standardized protocols (59.6%).ConclusionAlthough RTs demonstrated a solid understanding of tracheostomy care, variations in clinical practices and confidence levels were observed, primarily due to the absence of standardized protocols and formal training. Implementing targeted educational initiatives and developing clear protocols could significantly improve the quality of tracheostomy care and enhance patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08471-2.Keywords: Tracheostomy, RT, Saudi arabia, Mechanical ventilation

Introduction

Tracheostomy is a surgical procedure that involves the creation of an opening in the trachea, primarily indicated for patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation [1]. While the timing and technique of tracheostomy can influence patient outcomes, effective management depends largely on healthcare providers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) in delivering routine care [2]. Managing patients with tracheostomy is complex and requires a thorough understanding of essential clinical tasks, including suctioning, stoma care, tube changes, and emergency management of complications, which are critical for maintaining patient safety and preventing adverse outcomes [3]. Inadequate practices may result in severe complications, including airway obstruction, infection, tracheoesophageal fistula, tracheal stenosis, bleeding, and accidental decannulation [4]. These risks highlight the need for vigilant care, adherence to standardized management strategies, and rigorous training for healthcare providers, particularly respiratory therapists who are often directly responsible for tracheostomy care [5, 6].

The necessity of standardized protocol for tracheostomy care has been emphasized by the Global Tracheostomy Collaborative (GTC), which advocates interprofessional collaboration, guideline implementation, and competency-based training to enhance patient safety and clinical outcomes [7]. This approach is particularly pertinent in Saudi Arabia, where the healthcare system is evolving, and the demand for skilled practitioners in tracheostomy care is increasing [8, 9]. Evidence suggests that the integration of structured guidelines, educational workshops, and simulation-based learning can significantly improve respiratory therapists’ practical skills, confidence, and adherence to best practices [10–14].

Despite the recognized importance of tracheostomy care, there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding the specific knowledge and practices of respiratory therapy staff in Saudi Arabia. Research indicates that many healthcare providers lack sufficient training and confidence in managing tracheostomy patients, which can lead to adverse outcomes [15, 16]. Therefore, this study aimed to fill this gap by assessing clinical practices, self-confidence, and perceived barriers in tracheostomy care among RT staff in Saudi Arabia.

Materials and methods

Study design and study population

Between the 5th of June 2024 and the 9th of October 2024, a cross-sectional survey was distributed to RTs in Saudi Arabia via the Saudi Society for Respiratory Care (SSRC) and multiple social media platforms.

Sampling strategy

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. Practicing RT staff in Saudi Arabia were invited to access the survey link through social media platforms and the professional committees of the SSRC. Written informed consent was obtained at the beginning of the survey by including a mandatory question: “Do you agree to participate?“. To maintain data integrity, the survey platform implemented restrictions to prevent duplicate responses, allowing each participant to submit their answers only once. The study’s objectives and inclusion criteria were explicitly outlined in the invitation letter accompanying the survey. Eligible participants included all currently practicing RTs in Saudi Arabia, while students and internship-year RTs were excluded.

Study instrument

A previously questionnaire was adopted from similar studies that examined the attitudes, self-confidence, and practices of nursing staff in tracheostomy care [9, 17]. In this study, tracheostomy care refers to specific clinical tasks including suctioning, tube changes, stoma care, and emergency management of tracheostomy-related complications. The questionnaire was structured into three distinct sections to comprehensively assess various aspects of the participants’ background and experience. The first section gathered demographic information, including age, gender, hospital sector, geographical location, academic qualifications, degree location, and years of clinical experience. The second section focused on attitudes and practices related to tracheostomy care, exploring factors such as the type of patient population managed, the clinical unit in which participants worked, the amount of time dedicated to tracheostomy care, and the number of tracheostomized patients managed by the RTs. The third section assessed training and confidence in tracheostomy care, addressing key aspects such as the number of formal training hours received during undergraduate studies and after obtaining the RT degree. It also included questions on clinical supervision hours, the availability of competency-based training or professional development programs, and whether the participants remained up to date with the latest tracheostomy care guidelines.

To ensure relevance for the RT population in Saudi Arabia, the questionnaire was adapted by adding RT-specific items, including whether tracheostomy care is part of the participants’ scope of practice, the existence of hospital protocols or guidelines for tracheostomy care, participation in tracheostomy-related professional development activities such as workshops or conferences, and perceived barriers to incorporating tracheostomy care as part of RT-provided services. Content validity was established through review by a panel of five RT experts with extensive clinical and academic experience in airway management. Minor modifications were made based on expert feedback, and the questionnaire was pilot-tested with 15 respiratory therapists to assess clarity, comprehension, and time required for completion. The final version was deemed appropriate and relevant for the target population.

Sample size

Based on a total population of 5,462 RTs in Saudi Arabia [18], the required sample size was calculated to be 359 respondents, assuming a 95% confidence level, a standard deviation of 0.5, and a 5% margin of error.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Jazan University (Reference No. REC-45/11/1132).

Statistical analysis

Data were collected electronically using Microsoft Excel and subsequently imported into SPSS software, version 28 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the dataset, with continuous variables presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests were performed to examine the associations between participants’ confidence levels and categorical variables, including hospital sector, geographical region, years of clinical experience, patient population, and clinical department, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic data of the study participants

A total of 1,012 respiratory therapists participated in the study, with a mean age of 28.7 years (SD = 4.1). The majority were male (58.1%), worked in governmental hospitals (76.8%), and held a bachelor’s degree (82.5%). Most respondents (46.6%) had 1–5 years of clinical experience, and 86.6% obtained their last degree in Saudi Arabia. Tracheostomy care was available in 90.7% of hospitals, though 22.4% lacked specific protocols. Care frequency per shift varied, with 32.6% providing it twice per shift (Table 1).

Clinical training, updates, and confidence among study participants

The findings indicate that clinical exposure to tracheostomy management varied among respondents, with 42.2% dedicating 11–25% of their time to it, and 35.3% allocating 1–10% of their time. Regarding experience with tracheostomized and ventilator-assisted patients, 45.9% worked with 1–10 patients, and 41.1% managed 11–50 cases. However, 4.0% reported no experience. Formal training was limited, with 42.3% receiving 1–5 h of instruction and 10.4% receiving no training. Similarly, postgraduate clinical supervision was minimal, as 37.5% had only 1–5 h of supervision, and 6.6% received none. A formal competency training program was not universally available, with 24.5% of departments lacking one.

Attitude of evidence-based practices was generally positive, with 59.9% of respondents feeling up to date. However, only 50.3% felt informed about advancements in tracheostomy technology. Regarding training preferences, 32.9% favored postgraduate courses, 31.7% preferred undergraduate coursework, and 26.6% supported internship rotations. However, 8.8% felt no additional training was needed. Support from multidisciplinary teams was a key factor, with 86.1% reporting expert clinical support. Confidence levels varied, with 41.5% feeling neutral about managing tracheostomy patients, while 20.9% felt confident and 11.3% very confident. Confidence was slightly higher in managing ventilator-assisted patients, with 23.6% feeling confident and 14.2% very confident (Table 2).

A significant association was found between participants’ demographic characteristics and their confidence in managing patients with tracheostomies. Confidence levels differed notably across hospital sectors, with those working in governmental hospitals reporting higher confidence compared to those in private sectors (p < 0.001). Similarly, geographical location showed a significant relationship with confidence, where participants from the Eastern and Western regions were more confident than those from the Southern and Northern regions (p < 0.001). Years of clinical experience also had a significant effect (p < 0.001), as RTs with 1–5 years of experience demonstrated higher confidence compared with newly graduated or less experienced staff (Table 3).

Tracheostomy professional development activities

Data analysis showed that more than half of the RTs (53.6%) visited specialist centers to gain hands-on experience and exposure to advanced tracheostomy care techniques. Moreover, attending conferences (52.2%) and workshops (49.2%) were also common methods for professional development, providing RTs with opportunities to learn from experts and network with peers. Additionally, 41.3% participated in teleconferences, allowing them to engage in remote learning and discussions on tracheostomy-related topics. A smaller percentage (29.3%) of RTs became members of tracheostomy groups, which could offer ongoing support, shared experiences, and collaborative learning opportunities (Fig. 3).

Barriers of including tracheostomy care as a part of RT services

The findings demonstrated that RTs faced several challenges in providing optimal tracheostomy care, with lack of knowledge (70.7%) and inadequate training (59.7%) being the most significant barriers. Another critical barrier was the absence of protocols (59.6%), suggesting a lack of standardized guidelines that could ensure consistency in care practices. Additionally, work overload (48.5%) was reported as a significant issue, which may limit the time and attention RTs can dedicate to tracheostomy patients. Resource-related challenges also played a role, with a shortage of tracheostomy care kits (36.5%) hindering effective management and diversity in tracheostomy tubes (17.5%) adding complexity to patient care (Fig. 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the clinical practices of tracheostomy care among RT staff in Saudi Arabia. The findings indicate that RTs predominantly provide care for adult and elderly patients, whereas pediatric and adolescent populations are less frequently managed. Tracheostomy care was a commonly provided service, although some hospitals lacked specific protocols for this procedure. While the majority of RTs demonstrated awareness of and adherence to evidence-based practices, clinical exposure to tracheostomy care varied considerably, largely due to the absence of formal training and structured competency programs. Professional development activities, such as visiting specialist centers, attending conferences, and participating in teleconferences, were commonly pursued to enhance clinical proficiency. However, several barriers to optimal tracheostomy care were identified, including knowledge deficits, inadequate training, the absence of standardized protocols, excessive workload, and limited resources.

In this study, our findings revealed significant variations in tracheostomy management practices among respiratory therapy staff across different clinical settings in Saudi Arabia. In ICUs, respiratory therapists frequently manage tracheostomy patients, though not exclusively, reflecting the critical nature of tracheostomy care in this high-acuity environment. This is consistent with global trends, where ICUs are often the primary setting for tracheostomy care due to the high prevalence of mechanically ventilated patients requiring prolonged respiratory support [19]. Similarly, the involvement of respiratory therapists in emergency departments and inpatient wards highlights their vital role in acute care settings, where timely and effective tracheostomy management is crucial for patient outcomes [20]. In contrast, tracheostomy care in outpatient settings was remarkably less reported, with only a small proportion of therapists providing it regularly. This difference probably reflects the differing patient populations and care needs in non-acute settings, where tracheostomy patients may require less intensive monitoring and intervention. This finding is consistent with studies from other regions, which have also reported a lower frequency of tracheostomy care in outpatient environments compared to acute care settings [21–23].

Despite a majority of respiratory therapists (59.9%) reporting that they feel up to date with current tracheostomy care guidelines, only 20.9% expressed high confidence in performing tracheostomy-related procedures. This contrast highlights a critical gap between perceived knowledge and actual self-efficacy in clinical practice. Such discrepancies suggest that while RTs may be theoretically aware of evidence-based guidelines, this awareness does not necessarily translate into confidence or proficiency in practical skills [24]. Similar findings have been reported in studies assessing the competency of healthcare professionals, where high levels of self-perceived knowledge were not consistently associated with procedural confidence or performance accuracy [9, 25]. This may reflect limitations in experiential learning opportunities, insufficient exposure to hands-on training, or the lack of standardized simulation-based programs designed to reinforce clinical competencies [26]. This mismatch requires targeted educational strategies that not only disseminate theoretical knowledge but also enhance psychomotor skills through structured workshops, competency assessments, and supervised clinical practice.

Variations in tracheostomy management practices can be significantly attributed to differences in institutional protocols, staffing levels, and access to specialized equipment. Research indicates that implementing standardized protocols and fostering multidisciplinary collaboration can significantly enhance tracheostomy care quality and improve patient outcomes [8, 27, 28]. In Saudi Arabia, these challenges are particularly pronounced due to inconsistencies in care protocols, especially in resource-limited settings, leading to significant disparities in management practices across healthcare institutions [29]. Furthermore, the infrequency of tracheostomy care in outpatient settings suggests potential gaps in follow-up care for patients transitioning from acute to non-acute environments, which is essential component in preventing complications such as infections and tube dislodgement [30, 31]. Additionally, studies show that healthcare providers with greater experience and direct involvement in tracheostomy management exhibit higher levels of confidence and competence [8, 28]. This correlation highlights the critical need for continuous education and hands-on training to ensure best practices are consistently applied in clinical settings [31].

Regarding tracheostomy professional development activities, our study revealed that RTs in Saudi Arabia demonstrate a proactive approach to tracheostomy-related professional development, heavily utilizing hands-on experience at specialist centers and attending conferences and workshops. This reflects a commitment to enhancing clinical competencies and staying updated with best practices, consistent with findings from other regions [21, 32]. Teleconferences offer a valuable, flexible learning option, particularly relevant in overcoming geographical barriers. However, membership in tracheostomy-specific groups or associations remains low, indicating a potential area for improvement in promoting professional community and support [33]. Compared to high-income countries, where institutional support for specialized training is more prevalent, Saudi Arabian RTs adapt through resource-conscious strategies like teleconferences and specialist center visits [27].

A considerable proportion of RTs identified a lack of knowledge and inadequate training as their primary obstacles. This finding aligns with existing literature, which highlights knowledge gaps as a prevalent issue in respiratory therapy and suggests that without comprehensive protocols and training resources, the quality of tracheostomy care may decline, ultimately affecting patient outcomes [34]. Previous studies have similarly reported widespread inconsistencies in guideline adherence among RTs, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the demand for mechanical ventilation and tracheostomy management increased [35]. The absence of standardized protocols further exacerbates these challenges, as evidence indicates that a lack of clear guidelines contributes to variability in practice and potential inconsistencies in patient care. This underscores the need for well-defined, multidisciplinary guidelines to ensure uniformity in the emergency management of tracheostomy procedures, particularly in high-risk pediatric populations [36].

Strength and limitations

This study represents the first comprehensive assessment of respiratory therapy staff clinical practices regarding tracheostomy care in Saudi Arabia. With a large sample size (N = 1,012, approximately 20% of the national RT workforce), it provides valuable insights into existing variations in tracheostomy care practices and underscores the need for standardized guidelines and protocols. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, potentially overrepresenting respondents more engaged with professional networks or social media. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data raises the possibility of recall and social desirability bias, which may lead to an overestimation of knowledge and confidence levels. Moreover, the cross-sectional design further limits the ability to infer causality between knowledge, clinical practices, and clinical outcomes. Future studies should incorporate objective assessments or practical skill evaluations to more accurately measure competencies. Moreover, employing advanced causal inference frameworks, such as Target Trial Emulation (TTE), could help explore potential causal relationships between professional attitudes, adherence to best practices, and patient outcomes, thereby strengthening the clinical relevance of future findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant variations in the clinical practices and self-confidence of respiratory therapy staff in Saudi Arabia regarding tracheostomy care. Despite the widespread availability of tracheostomy services in hospitals, many RTs reported a lack of standardized protocols and insufficient formal training. These gaps can lead to suboptimal patient outcomes, underscoring the need for improved educational programs and competency-based training. To enhance tracheostomy care in Saudi Arabia, it is essential to implement standardized training programs, integrate simulation-based learning, and establish national clinical protocols. Regular competency assessments should be conducted to ensure adherence to best practices, while fostering interprofessional collaboration among RTs, nurses, and physicians can improve patient outcomes.
