Exploring future synergies for large-scale structure between gravitational waves and radio sources 2026 Exploring future synergies for large-scale structure between gravitational waves and radio sources –
Stefano Zazzera, José Fonseca, Tessa Baker, Chris Clarkson

Abstract
Future third-generation gravitational wave detectors like the Einstein Telescope (ET) and Cosmic Explorer (CE) are expected to detect millions of binary black hole (BBH) mergers. Alongside these advances, upcoming radio surveys, such as the Square Kilometer Array Observatory (SKAO) will provide new sets of cosmological tracers. These include mapping the large-scale distribution of neutral hydrogen (H i ) using intensity mapping (IM) and H i and radio continuum galaxies. In this work, we will investigate synergies between gravitational waves (GW) and radio tracers through a multi-tracer approach. We first forecast the precision on the clustering bias of GWs by cross-correlating data from an ET-like detector with an SKAO IM survey. Our results indicate that this approach can constrain the GW clustering bias to within 2 % \displaystyle 2\% up to z = 2.5 \displaystyle z=2.5 . Additionally, we explore the potential of a triple cross-correlation using GWs, IM, and photometric galaxies from a survey like LSST. This multi-tracer method enhances constraints on the magnification lensing effect, achieving percent-level precision, and allows for a measurement of the Doppler effect with approximately 15 % \displaystyle 15\% uncertainty. Furthermore we show for the first time that this method could achieve the precision required to measure subdominant gravitational potential contributions to the relativistic corrections, which had thought to be below cosmic variance. Our analysis highlights the potential of cross-correlations between GWs and radio tracers to improve constraints on astrophysical properties of BBHs, measure relativistic effects, and perform null tests of GR in cosmological scales.

. From the several surveys to be conducted by the SKAO, one will be able to trace the Large-scale structure of the universe with three distinctive tracers of dark matter: H

In addition, some radio galaxies do not present any emission line, only emission in the continuum. While getting redshift information is limited one can still perform cosmological tests with such catalogues

)
. The Medium-Deep Band 2 Survey will cover

\displaystyle 10,000

\displaystyle 20,000,\text{deg}^{2}

\displaystyle z\sim 0.35

\displaystyle z\sim 3

\displaystyle z\sim 2

(
Yahya et al. 2015
)
.

Cross-correlating GWs with radio tracers of large-scale structure offers a promising avenue for investigating both cosmology and the properties of BBH populations. By comparing the clustering of GWs with H
i
galaxies, H

(

)
. They showed that this kind of analysis will be able to distinguish between black hole mergers of astrophysical or primordial origin. Further,

This paper builds upon previous work that examined cross-correlation with GWs

;

;
Mukherjee et al. 2020b
;
Mukherjee et al. 2020a
;

, for example with galaxy surveys
(

;
Scelfo et al. 2020
;
Balaudo et al. 2024
;
Pedrotti et al. 2025
)
and intensity mapping
(
Scelfo et al. 2023
)
. In particular, we extend the analysis we presented in
Zazzera et al. 2024a
where we produced forecasts on different combinations of GWs detectors, and current and future galaxy surveys such as LSST
(
LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration 2012
;
LSST Science Collaboration et al. 2021
;
LSST Science Collaboration et al. 2009
)
by incorporating SKAO radio surveys as well. Here we are following a framework set up in
Fonseca et al. 2023
;
Namikawa 2021
;
Balaudo et al. 2024
where the clustering of GWs is treated in luminosity distance space (LDS). In fact, unlike galaxy surveys or intensity mapping, GWs from BBH mergers do not carry information on the redshift of the source, but rather a
\displaystyle D_{L}
measurement; that is, unless an electromagnetic counterpart is detected.

Furthermore, the clustering of GWs in LDS is affected by three important parameters, similarly to what happens in redshift space (RS) for other tracers like galaxies. The GW clustering bias describes how GWs trace the underlying dark matter distribution, analogous to the bias of galaxy tracers. Additionally, the limited sensitivity of GW detectors introduces observational effects captured by the magnification bias
(
Maartens et al. 2021
)
. The magnification bias
\displaystyle s^{GW}
gauges the amplitude of corrections to the number density contrast arising from gravitational lensing, which can enhance or diminish the observed number of GWs by magnifying sources into or out of detectability.
The evolution bias
\displaystyle b_{e}^{GW}
, on the other hand, accounts for the redshift evolution of the intrinsic merger rate and the detector’s ability to observe the cosmic evolution of the sources. Further analysis and modelling of the magnification and evolution biases of GWs is described in
Zazzera et al. 2024b
. These parameters are directly linked to the properties of the BBH population producing the GWs observed, and so measuring them would imply constraining these properties. In particular,
\displaystyle s^{GW}
and
\displaystyle b_{e}^{GW}
depend on the intrinsic merger rate and chirp mass distribution, whilst measuring the clustering bias would directly link the distribution of BBHs to that of the underlying dark matter in the Universe, potentially acting as probe to differentiate between black holes living in dark matter halos and those in isolation, whether of primordial origin or else
(
Scelfo et al. 2018
;
Scelfo et al. 2020
)
.

First we look at how combining data from different GW detector and radio surveys can improve constraints on the GW clustering bias. Then, focussing on cross-correlating ET-like data with an IM survey, we produce forecasts on the detection of different relativistic effects that affect the observed number count fluctuation and thus the angular power spectrum, namely the lensing correction, luminosity distance space distortions (LSDs) and Doppler term. Finally, we introduce a novel approach using a three-tracer correlation between GWs from an ET-like detector, H
i
intensity mapping from an SKA1-MID-like survey, and galaxies from an LSST-like survey to forecast constraints on clustering, magnification and evolution biases of GWs and on the measurements of the relativistic effects.

2
Number Count Fluctuation

In this section we briefly summarise the relevant equations describing the number counts (or density contrast) and the relativistic effects in both luminosity distance and redshift space. However, a more complete description is either found in Appendix
A
or in
Zazzera et al. 2024a
.

We firstly define the linearly perturbed line element in the conformal Newtonian gauge, used to derive the results listed below:

ds^{2}=a^{2}(\eta)\left[-(1+2\Psi)d\eta^{2}+(1-2\Phi)\delta_{ab}dx^{a}dx^{b}\right]\,,

(1)

where
\displaystyle\Phi
and
\displaystyle\Psi
are the metric potentials and
\displaystyle\eta
is conformal time.

The observed density contrast for tracer A, measured through the distance type
\displaystyle X
(i.e. either redshift
\displaystyle z
or luminosity distance
\displaystyle D_{L}
) can be expressed in terms of different contributions as
(
Bonvin 2008
;
Challinor & Lewis 2011
;
Fonseca et al. 2023
)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{n},X)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle b\delta_{n}+A^{X}_{GRV}\partial_{r}(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})+\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}{\rm d}r\ \frac{A^{X}_{L}}{\bar{r}}\Delta_{\Omega}(\Phi+\Psi)

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+A_{D}^{X}(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})+g^{X}(\Psi,\Phi,r)\,.\

In order, we have the matter density contrast with
\displaystyle\delta_{n}
being the density contrast in Newtonian gauge and
\displaystyle b
the clustering bias of the tracer in question; then follows distortions from the gradient of radial velocity, lensing magnification, Doppler term and the final term,
\displaystyle g^{X}(\Psi,\Phi,r)
is a function grouping together further subdominant relativistic corrections (consisting of the metric potentials and related derivatives and integrals), with
\displaystyle r
being the comoving position
\displaystyle r=\int{\rm{d}}z^{\prime}/H
. Different tracers are measured through a different distance measure; for instance galaxies are redshift tracers, whereas GWs are measured in luminosity distance space. Thus, depending on which tracer is used, the amplitudes of each contribution to the number counts have different forms. An interested reader can find the full expression for each in both redshift space (RS) and luminosity distance space (LDS) in Appendix
A
.

Notably,
\displaystyle A_{L}
and
\displaystyle A_{D}
(and several terms inside
\displaystyle g^{X}(\Psi,\Phi,r)
) are found to be dependent on two other tracer-specific parameters,
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle b_{e}
. These are, respectively, the magnification and evolution biases of a given tracer. An in-depth analysis of these parameters for galaxies and intensity mapping is presented in
Maartens et al. 2021
, and for GWs in
Zazzera et al. 2024b
.

From here, one can expand these fields into spherical harmonics and construct the angular power spectrum.
As the data is usually binned into distance intervals, we express the angular power spectrum for the
\displaystyle i
th and
\displaystyle j
th bin as:

C^{ij}_{\ell}=4\pi\int{\rm d}\ln k\ \Delta^{A,i}_{\ell}(k)\Delta^{B,j}_{\ell}(k)\ {\cal P}_{\cal R}(k)\,,

(3)

where
\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\cal R}(k)
is the power spectrum of primordial perturbations and
\displaystyle\cal R
is the curvature perturbation. This is effectively cross-correlating counts of tracer
\displaystyle A
in the
\displaystyle i
th bin with those of tracer
\displaystyle B
in bin
\displaystyle j
. The functions
\displaystyle\Delta^{A,i}_{\ell}(k)
include the transfer functions needed to relate the primordial perturbations to the contributions to the observed number counts in
Equation 2
, and can be found in
Fonseca et al. 2023
for a tracer in luminosity distance space.

3
Surveys

In this section we provide the properties of the surveys and tracers used, in both the radio and GW domains. We address expressions for the number densities, and clustering, magnification and evolution biases.

3.1
Radio tracers

We consider three different radio tracers in this paper: H
i
galaxies, radio continuum galaxies and H
i
intensity mapping.

For the former two the approach is almost identical to the one taken in
Zazzera et al. 2024a
.
We start by modelling the number density of observed sources for H
i
galaxies, which is dependent on the flux density threshold
\displaystyle S_{\rm rms}
and on the observed line profile, i.e. how well the hydrogen spectral line is resolved. The resulting flux threshold becomes
(
Maartens et al. 2021
)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle S_{c}(z)=S_{\rm rms}(z)\frac{N_{\rm cut}}{10}\,.\

(4)

In order to model the number density of H
i
galaxies we follow the results from
Yahya et al. 2015
, where the authors used the S
3
-SAX simulation
(
Obreschkow et al. 2009
)
. Here, each galaxy has a redshift, H
i
luminosity and line profile, allowing for the extraction of the following fitting formula as a function of redshift and detection threshold:

\displaystyle\displaystyle N_{g}^{\textrm{H{i}}}(z,S_{c})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle 10^{c_{1}(S_{c})}z^{c_{2}(S_{C})}\exp{[-c_{3}(S_{c})z]}\quad\rm{deg}^{-2}\,.

(5)

The parameters
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2}
and
\displaystyle c_{3}
depends on the flux sensitivity
\displaystyle S_{c}
used, and are reported in Table 3 of
Yahya et al. 2015
. We adopt
\displaystyle S_{c}=100
for SKAO1, and
\displaystyle S_{c}=5
for SKAO2
(
Yahya et al. 2015
;
Santos et al. 2015a
)
.

The clustering bias of H
i
galaxies is calculated with the same simulation, resulting in:

\displaystyle\displaystyle b^{\textrm{H{i}},\rm gal}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{4}(S_{c})\exp[c_{5}(S_{c})z]\,,

(6)

for which we use the coefficients
\displaystyle c_{4}
and
\displaystyle c_{5}
from Table 3 in
Yahya et al. 2015
.
This is then inserted in
Equation 2
to relate the number counts fluctuation to the underlying dark matter fluctuation
\displaystyle\delta_{N}
.

Magnification and evolution bias are then evaluated following
(
Maartens et al. 2021
)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle s^{\textrm{H{i}},gal}=-\frac{5}{2}\frac{\partial\log N_{g}^{\textrm{H{i}}}}{\partial\log S_{c}}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{e}^{\textrm{H{i}},gal}=\frac{\partial\log N_{g}^{\textrm{H{i}}}}{\partial\log a}\,.

(8)

For our second radio tracer, radio continuum galaxies parameters are instead found in Table 3 in
Bacon et al. 2020
for the
Wide Band 1 Survey
. This lists the galaxy number density, clustering bias and magnification bias at different redshift bins. To obtain the evolution bias we compute a numerical derivative of the resulting number density with respect to redshift, similarly to
Equation 8
.

Our third radio tracer is H
i
intensity mapping. H
i
intensity mapping differs to standard galaxy surveys as the method does not measure individual objects, but rather the total
\displaystyle 21
cm emission in each voxel (i.e. a three-dimensional pixel given by telescope beam and frequency channel width). The H
i
brightness temperature at redshift
\displaystyle z
and in direction
\displaystyle\bm{n}
is proportional to the observed number of H
i
emitters per redshift per solid angle
\displaystyle N_{\rm{\textrm{H{i}}}}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{\textrm{H{i}}}(z,\bm{n})\propto\frac{N_{\rm{\textrm{H{i}}}}(z,\bm{n})}{D_{A}^{2}(z,\bm{n})}\,,

(9)

where
\displaystyle D_{A}
is the area distance.
This is poorly constrained by current observations and we use the fit
Santos et al. 2017
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{\textrm{H{i}}}=0.055919+0.23242z-0.024136z^{2}\,.

(10)

However,
Equation 9
implies conservation of surface brightness, yielding no magnification bias. In fact, for IM
Equation 2
is slightly different
(
Hall et al. 2013
)
but one can recover it using the expression in Redshift Space setting

\displaystyle\displaystyle s^{\textrm{H{i}}}=\frac{2}{5}\,.

(11)

To calculate the evolution bias for intensity mapping, we follow
Maartens et al. 2021
, noticing that
Equation 9
implies that at the background level we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{\rm{\textrm{H{i}}}}\propto\frac{n_{\rm{\textrm{H{i}}}}r^{2}H^{-1}}{a^{2}r^{2}}\propto\frac{(1+z)^{2}}{H}n_{\rm{\textrm{H{i}}}}\,,

(12)

where
\displaystyle n_{\rm{\textrm{H{i}}}}
is the comoving number density of H
i
emitters in the source rest-frame. Thus we can write
(
Fonseca et al. 2018
;
Jolicoeur et al. 2021
)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{e}^{\textrm{H{i}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\log n^{\textrm{H{i}}}}{\partial\log(1+z)}

(13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\log T_{\textrm{H{i}}}}{\partial\log(1+z)}-\frac{\partial\log H}{\partial\log(1+z)}+2\,.

These parameters are all inserted in
Equation 2
through the amplitudes of the corrections terms such as
\displaystyle A_{L}
and
\displaystyle A_{D}
, which are expressed fully in Appendix
A
.

3.2
GWs

To model the population of GW sources we follow the prescriptions already provided in
Zazzera et al. 2024a
.

In this work we focus on GWs emitted by a population of binary black holes (BBHs). We do not focus on binary neutron stars (BNs) or neutron star-black hole (NSBH) mergers for simplicity. Accounting for them would require separate modelling of the intrinsic merger rate and chirp mass distribution for each, and consequently of their related magnification and evolution bias, together with assuming a form of their clustering bias. Furthermore, as the forecasted number of detections are significantly higher for BBHs with ET
(
Punturo et al. 2010
;
Iacovelli et al. 2022
;
Branchesi et al. 2023
)
, we chose to only consider these events.
We assume these to follow a standard Madau-Dickinson rate from
Ye & Fishbach 2021
;
Madau & Dickinson 2014
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle R_{GW}(z)=R_{0}\frac{(1+z)^{2.7}}{1+(\frac{1+z}{2.9})^{5.6}}\,,\

(14)

with
\displaystyle R_{0}
providing the merger rate at
\displaystyle z=0
, given by
Abbott et al. 2021
;
LIGO-VIRGO-KAGRA Collaboration et al. 2022
as
\displaystyle R_{0}=23.9
Gpc
-3
yr
-1
. The values used in
Equation 14
are not fully known and in this paper we fix them to follow a regular Madau-Dickinson model. This assumption relies on the fact that astrophysical BBHs are the results of stellar processes, and would therefore follow a rate akin to the stellar formation rate. We can then set the amplitude
\displaystyle R_{0}
to match LVK observations, and when 3G detectors will come online we will have better constraints on the rest of the parameters. Modifications to the merger rate will result in different values of the magnification and evolution biases, thus impacting the angular power spectrum of GWs. Moreover, it will yield a new estimate of the shot noise.

The number density of observed GW sources follows previous studies
(
Zazzera et al. 2024b
;
Oguri 2018
)
as:

n_{\rm GW}(z)=\tau\frac{R_{GW}(z)}{1+z}\int{\rm d}\mathcal{M}\ \phi(\mathcal{M})\,S(\rho_{th};\mathcal{M},z)\,,

(15)

where
\displaystyle\tau
is the observation time of the detector,
\displaystyle R_{GW}(z)
is the intrinsic merger rate in
Equation 14
,
\displaystyle\phi(\mathcal{M})
is the chirp mass
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
distribution, with

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\equiv\frac{(m_{1}m_{2})^{3/5}}{(m_{1}+m_{2})^{1/5}}\,.\

(16)

The survival function
\displaystyle S(\rho_{th};\mathcal{M},z)
simply imposes whether an event of chirp mass
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
at redshift
\displaystyle z
is detected assuming a certain SNR threshold
\displaystyle\rho_{th}
, and contains the power spectral density of the experiment, thus making each bias detector-dependent. For an O
\displaystyle 5
-like experiment
(
Abbott et al. 2020
)
we assume a detector comprising of advanced LIGO
(
Aasi et al. 2015
)
, Virgo
(
Acernese et al. 2014
)
and KAGRA
(
Abbott et al. 2016a
)
, whereas for ET we assume an L-shape detector with
\displaystyle 10
km arms
1
1

1

The corresponding sensitivity curve for ET is found at
https://apps.et-gw.eu/tds/?content=3&r=18213

.
An interested reader can find a full explanation of the survival function

Following
Zazzera et al. 2024b
, the magnification and evolution biases for each GW experiment are defined as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle s^{GW}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{1}{5}\frac{\partial\log n_{GW}}{\partial\log\rho_{th}}\bigg|_{a}\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{e}^{GW}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log n_{GW}}{\partial\log a}\bigg|_{\rho_{th}}\,,

(18)

where
\displaystyle\rho_{th}
is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) threshold of detection, which we set to
\displaystyle\rho_{th}=8
. This is used instead of the flux, commonly used in the galaxy counterpart.

Finally, the redshift dependence of the clustering bias is modelled as in
Zazzera et al. 2024a
as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle b^{GW}=B(1+z)^{\alpha}\,.

(19)

where
\displaystyle B
and
\displaystyle\alpha
are now key parameters of interest to be constrained with our cross-correlation observables.

is then cast into

4
Fisher formalism

The construction of the Fisher matrices employed in this paper follows Section 4 of
Zazzera et al. 2024a
. It hold for a GW surveys as well as any galaxy survey in the optical or in the radio. Therefore, we only report the most significant equations and changes when including H
i
IM into the tracer’s pool.

In a multi-tracer approach, using a GW survey and an H
i
survey, we can represent schematically the data covariance matrix as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ell}(z_{i},z_{j})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{\ell,ij}^{\textrm{H{i}},\textrm{H{i}}}&\Gamma_{\ell,ij}^{\textrm{H{i}},\rm GW}\\
\Gamma_{\ell,ij}^{\rm GW,\textrm{H{i}}}&\Gamma_{\ell,ij}^{\rm GW,GW}\ \end{bmatrix}\,,

(20)

The data covariance is defined as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma^{ij}_{\ell}=C_{\ell}^{ij}+\mathcal{N}^{ij}\,,

(21)

where
\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{ij}
is the angular power spectrum of bins
\displaystyle i,j
(
Equation 3
), and
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{ij}
is the related noise power spectrum, generally independent of the multipole
\displaystyle\ell
.
The noise angular power spectrum for a discreet tracer is dominated by the shot noise:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{A}_{i}=\frac{1}{N^{A}_{i}}\,,

(22)

where
\displaystyle N^{A}
is the number of objects (galaxies or GWs) per steradian in the
\displaystyle i
-th bin

\displaystyle\displaystyle N^{A}_{i}=\int{\rm d}z\ n^{A}(z)W(z,z_{i};\Delta z_{i},\sigma_{i}^{z})\,.

(23)

Here,
\displaystyle n^{A}
is the comoving number density of objects as function of redshift,
\displaystyle W
the window function centred at
\displaystyle z_{i}
, bin size
\displaystyle\Delta z_{i}
, and redshift scatter
\displaystyle\sigma^{z}_{i}=\sigma_{0}(1+z)
.

Whilst the above is applied for H
i
galaxies and continuum, for H
i
intensity mapping the shot noise is negligible. The relevant noise term is the instrumental variance in a
voxel
. The noise contribution to the angular power spectrum is:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\rm instr}=T_{\rm sys}^{2}\frac{\Omega_{\rm surv}}{2N_{\rm dish}\tau\Delta_{\nu}}\,,

(24)

where
\displaystyle T_{\rm sys}
is the system temperature,
\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm surv}
the sky coverage,
\displaystyle N_{\rm dish}
the number of dishes employed,
\displaystyle\tau
the integration time and
\displaystyle\Delta_{\nu}
the bandwidth of each frequency channel.

The window function
\displaystyle W
to define the binning is then given by a combination of error functions following
(
Ma et al. 2006
;
Viljoen et al. 2021
)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle W(z,z_{i};\Delta z_{i},\sigma_{i}^{z})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\erf\left(\frac{z_{i}+\delta z_{i}-z}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{i}^{z}}\right)-\erf\left(\frac{z_{i}-\delta z_{i}-z}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{i}^{z}}\right)\right]\,.

(25)

For galaxies and GWs
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{z}
is large enough to produce a Gaussian-like window; however, in the case of H
i
intensity mapping, redshift uncertainty is much lower, thus yielding a smoothed top-hat window function.

Relativistic Effects

6.1
Measurement of GR effects

We further explore the synergies between GWs and radio sources and turn our attention to forecasting different relativistic effects. We compute this by coupling the terms in
Equation 2
to a dummy amplitude
\displaystyle\epsilon
which we set to
\displaystyle 1
, implying:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{\delta}+\epsilon_{LSD}\Delta_{LSD}+\epsilon_{L}\Delta_{L}+\epsilon_{D}\Delta_{D}+\epsilon_{P}\Delta_{P}\,.

(30)

Therefore, similarly to what has been done in
Zazzera et al. 2024a
, we forecast measurements of LSD, Lensing, Doppler and further GR corrections (mostly potentials, hence the subscript
\displaystyle P
) by measuring if we can constrain the respective amplitudes
\displaystyle\epsilon
. Note that
\displaystyle\epsilon_{P}
is coupled to the last term in
Equation 2
. Considering the results of the previous section, we only forecast these measurements for an ET
\displaystyle\times
H
i
IM-like cross-correlation. We present the contour ellipses in
Figure 3
. The parameters selected for this analysis are then
\displaystyle\theta\in\{B,\alpha,\epsilon_{L},\epsilon_{LSD},\epsilon_{D},\epsilon_{P}\}
, allowing to check for degeneracies between clustering bias parameters and relativistic effects.

In fact, one can immediately see that
\displaystyle B
and
\displaystyle\alpha
, which build the clustering bias of GWs, are degenerate with both the lensing measurement
\displaystyle\epsilon_{L}
and strongly with the measurement of the luminosity distance space distortions,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{LSD}
.

The confidence on the measurement of the relativistic effects is greatly increased with
\displaystyle 5
years of GW data; for instance the error on
\displaystyle\epsilon_{L}
shrinks of a factor of
\displaystyle 10
to roughly
\displaystyle 10\%
. Notably, the signal on the LSD contribution, i.e. the radial velocity effect, is particularly strong, reaching an error of
\displaystyle\sim 7\%
with
\displaystyle 5
years-worth of data. Doppler and further relativistic corrections remain unobservable instead.

It is interesting to compare these results with those presented with a cross-correlation of GWs from a third generation detector with photometric galaxies from an LSST-like survey
(
Zazzera et al. 2024a
)
. Whilst the lensing appears more easily detectable through correlations with galaxies (reaching and error of
\displaystyle\sim 3\%
), the error on
\displaystyle\epsilon_{LSD}
is improved by a few percent with cross-correlations with intensity mapping, going below the
\displaystyle 10\%
error from the previous study. In the case of intensity mapping, lensing is suppressed as hinted in
section 3
. Therefore, the
\displaystyle C_{\ell}
in
Equation 20
will include lensing-lensing correlations only in the lower right quadrant, i.e. the auto-correlation of GWs. In fact, as
\displaystyle\Delta_{L}^{\textrm{H{i}}}=0
, both auto-correlation of H
i
lensing and cross-correlation H
i
lensing
\displaystyle\times
GW lensing will yield zero. Therefore, the signal on the lensing correction,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{L}
is greatly reduced. However, this implies then that the contribution to the number count fluctuation in
Equation 2
given by the LSD term is much more significant, yielding in fact a stronger signal than the cross-correlation with galaxies.

8
Discussion and conclusions

In this paper we analysed synergies between future GW experiments and upcoming radio surveys. In particular, we presented forecasts on the clustering, magnification and evolution bias of gravitational waves from binary black hole mergers, and on the detectability of different relativistic effects.

We established that cross-correlating GW data from a third-generation ground based detector and an intensity mapping survey such as SKA1-MID would produce high quality constraints on the clustering bias of GWs, achieving an error on the amplitude of
\displaystyle b^{GW}
lower than
\displaystyle 2\%
. Interestingly, intensity mapping showed the best synergy with GW experiments, resulting in good precision on the amplitude of
\displaystyle b^{GW}
when cross-correlating with data from an O
\displaystyle 5
-like experiment as well, achieving precision of about
\displaystyle 5\%
using the predicted full length of the O
\displaystyle 5
run. This can be explained by the large redshift range covered by an intensity mapping survey such as SKA1-MID. In fact, this would cover a range
\displaystyle 0<z<3
, perfectly overlapping with the range covered by ET, and covering the entirety of that of O
\displaystyle 5
. The more bins overlap and the larger the signal on the density term in
Equation 2
will be, which is the dominant term in the number counts fluctuation (at least until high redshifts). Additionally, the shot noise for an SKAO intensity mapping survey is predicted to be advantageous compared to other tracers such as radio galaxies, thus improving the error on the parameters considered.

Further, we also compare our forecasted results with a previous analysis cross-correlating GWs with photometric galaxies from an LSST-like survey
(
Zazzera et al. 2024a
)
. We show that cross-correlating GWs and intensity mapping can yield better results on the clustering bias of gravitational waves, especially when considering the error on
\displaystyle b^{GW}
as a function of redshift. In
Figure 2
we show that simply cross-correlating
\displaystyle 1
year of GW data with an intensity mapping survey such as SKA1-MID can produce constraints similar to those obtained by cross-correlating
\displaystyle 5
years of GW and photometric galaxy data. Additionally, we forecast that with
\displaystyle 5
years of observation, an ET
\displaystyle\times
H
i
correlation would yield a
\displaystyle 2\sigma
precision of less than
\displaystyle 2\%
up until
\displaystyle z=2.5
.

We also produce constraints on the magnification and evolution biases of GWs. Initially, cross-correlating GWs with intensity mapping produced results comparable to the ones obtained by cross-correlating with photometric galaxies
(
Zazzera et al. 2024a
)
. However, significantly better results are obtained with a triple correlation of tracers, i.e. GWs, intensity mapping and photometric galaxies. Using all three tracers, the uncertainty on the values of
\displaystyle s^{GW}
and
\displaystyle b_{e}^{GW}
is reduced by a factor of
\displaystyle 10
. As these parameters are dependent on both the intrinsic merger rate of BBH mergers and on their chirp mass distribution, constraining them would provide a direct handle on constraining these intrinsic properties of BBHs.

Zazzera et al. 2024b
showed that two slightly different primary mass distribution,
Power-Law + Peak
(used in this paper) and
Broken Power-Law
, result in almost identical expressions of the biases, thus having a much less significant impact. Therefore, constraining the values of
\displaystyle s^{GW}
and
\displaystyle b_{e}^{GW}
would yield a consistency check with the measured merger rate of BBHs.

Using a cross-correlation of these three different tracers, we also forecast constraints on different relativistic effects, namely the lensing correction, luminosity distance space distortions, Doppler and subdominant GR effects. Significant constraints can be achieved for the lensing correction also with only the galaxy autocorrelation or the cross-correlation of photometric galaxies and intensity mapping. However, adding gravitational waves increases the precision on the measurement of these effects by a large amount. We forecast an uncertainty on the measurement of the lensing term of less than
\displaystyle 2\%
, and the possibility of measuring the Doppler term with around
\displaystyle 15\%
error, thus at a
\displaystyle 6.7\sigma
level. Furthermore we show for the first time that the gravitational potential terms can be detected in LSS (at
\displaystyle 3\sigma
), which are below the cosmic variance limit for two tracers. This is an important result, as it opens up the possibility of testing gravity in large scale structure studies with GWs. Different modified gravity theories would result in an alternative expression for the number counts (as investigated in e.g.
Balaudo et al. 2024
), especially affecting terms such as the lensing term. Measuring the latter would then help constrain different modifications of gravity.

To conclude, we highlight a synergy between intensity mapping and future GW experiments, particularly as cross-correlations of the two tracers would yield precise measurements of the clustering bias of gravitational waves. Furthermore, we showed that cross-correlating GW, intensity mapping and photometric galaxies altogether would produce tight measurements of the lensing effect on the number counts (
\displaystyle<2\%
) and other relativistic effects such as the luminosity distance space distortions and Doppler term.

Appendix A
Amplitudes

In this section, we show the number counts fluctuation for both redshift tracers and luminosity distance tracers. First we write out the general expression again for clarity:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{n},X)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle b\delta_{n}+A^{X}_{GRV}\partial_{r}(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})+\int_{0}^{\bar{r}}{\rm d}r\ \frac{A^{X}_{L}}{\bar{r}}\Delta_{\Omega}(\Phi+\Psi)

(32)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+A_{D}^{X}(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{n})+g^{X}(\Psi,\Phi,r)\,.\

Then, we list the amplitudes for a redshift space tracer like galaxies or H
i
intensity mapping, i.e.
\displaystyle X=z
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{GRV}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{\rm{RSD}}=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{D}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{5s-2}{\bar{r}\mathcal{H}}-5s+b_{e}-\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{L}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(5s-2)\frac{\bar{r}-r}{r}\,.

(35)

We note that
\displaystyle\bar{r}
is the source’s position and
\displaystyle r
the integral’s comoving distance, and
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle b_{e}
the magnification and evolution biases.

For a tracer living in luminosity distance space such as GWs, the amplitudes of the corrections to the number counts instead read:

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{GRV}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{LSD}=-\ \frac{2\gamma}{\mathcal{H}}\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{D}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1-2(\gamma+\beta)\,,

(37)

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{L}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\frac{\bar{r}-r}{r}\right)(\beta-2)+\frac{1}{1+\bar{r}\mathcal{H}}\right]\,\ .

(38)

Here we define
\displaystyle\gamma\equiv\bar{r}\mathcal{H}/(1+\bar{r}\mathcal{H})
, and

\beta\equiv 1-5s^{GW}+\gamma\left[\frac{2}{\bar{r}\mathcal{H}}+\gamma\left(\frac{\cal{H}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{H}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\bar{r}\cal{H}}\right)-1-b_{e}^{GW}\right]\,,

(39)

where
\displaystyle s^{GW}
and
\displaystyle b_{e}^{GW}
are the magnification and evolution biases, respectively.
