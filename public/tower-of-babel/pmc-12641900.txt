Interaction effects of dietary carotenoid intake and gamma-glutamyl transferase level on the prevalence of stroke in older adults
Authors: Shitu Zhuo, Biyue Zhang, Xinxia Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe possible interaction between dietary carotenoid intake and blood gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) levels in patients with stroke has not been well studied. We investigated the effect of the interaction between dietary carotenoid intake and blood GGT levels on the prevalence of stroke.MethodsUsing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2003–2018, we conducted a cross-sectional study that included individuals residing in the United States and aged ≥60 years. Univariate logistic regression, multivariate-adjusted logistic regression, and restricted cubic spline methods were used to assess the correlation between GGT levels, dietary carotenoid intake, and the prevalence of stroke. Finally, the relative excess risk of interactions, attributable proportion, and synergy index were used to assess additive interactions.ResultsIn total, 11,146 participants were enrolled in this observational study. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline models adjusted for all covariates revealed that GGT was nonlinearly and positively associated with the prevalence of stroke. In contrast, carotenoid intake was linearly and negatively associated with stroke prevalence. Further, the additive interaction results showed a synergistic effect of low dietary carotenoid intake and high blood GGT levels on the prevalence of stroke.ConclusionsFor individuals residing in the United States and aged ≥60 years, blood GGT levels were positively correlated and dietary carotenoid intake was negatively correlated with the prevalence of stroke. Low dietary carotenoid intake and high blood GGT levels have a synergistic effect on the prevalence of stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01139-z.

Introduction

Stroke is the third leading cause of death globally and a major cause of disability [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease, the incidence of stroke has increased by 85% since 2019 [3]. In 2019, 12.2 million cases of stroke were reported, resulting in 6.55 million stroke-related deaths and the loss of 143 million disability-adjusted life years due to stroke [3]. In addition, the annual global burden of stroke exceeds $89.1 billion, burdening individuals and systems [4].

Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) is a marker of cholestasis [5] and an established risk factor for cerebrovascular disease [6, 7]. GGT is primarily found in the liver but is widely distributed in the brain [8]. Previous epidemiological studies have revealed a correlation between GGT and the risk of stroke [9, 10]. A meta-analysis of several prospective studies demonstrated a strong correlation between GGT and stroke [11]. Moreover, similar associations were observed in three recently published cohort studies on GGT and the prevalence of stroke; however, two were based on Asian populations [10, 12], and the remaining study revealed a significant association in only female participants [9].

Carotenoids, which mainly include α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, and lutein/zeaxanthin, are widely found in fruits, vegetables, and some seaweeds [13]. The human body cannot synthesize these carotenoids. Therefore, the only way to obtain them is through food [14]. Carotenoids have many functions, including anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory actions, anti-oxidative stress, and immune regulation [15], and have been associated with chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, cancer, and asthma [16–19]. Increased carotenoid intake has a protective effect against stroke [20, 21]. A meta-analysis of prospective studies involving 1,989 stroke cases revealed that carotenoids significantly reduce the prevalence of stroke by 19.3%, even after adjustment for confounders [20]. However, other studies reported different conclusions [22, 23]. A prospective cohort study involving 34,492 postmenopausal women showed no correlation between carotenoid levels and the prevalence of stroke [22]. Therefore, the relationship between carotenoid intake and a reduced prevalence of stroke remains controversial.

Dietary carotenoids have oxidative and anti-inflammatory effects, which may indirectly affect blood GGT levels by regulating body metabolism and improving oxidative stress, thereby having an impact on the prevalence of stroke. Concurrently, blood GGT level is an indicator of liver function, the body’s metabolic state, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation. Abnormal blood GGT levels may affect the absorption and metabolism of dietary carotenoids and their physiological functions.

Previous observational studies have suggested that dietary carotenoid intake and blood GGT levels are associated with the prevalence of stroke [20, 21]. To date, no study has investigated the presence of a synergistic relation between them. We investigated the relationship between dietary carotenoid intake and blood GGT level, their interaction, and the prevalence of stroke in older adults based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

The NHANES is a cross-sectional study conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) [24]. It utilizes a complex, multistage, stratified probability sampling methodology to gather data on the health and nutrition of the population of the United States (US) [25]. The NHANES was approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all the study participants. The present study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

We used the NHANES data from 2003 to 2018 pertaining to 15,381 participants aged ≥60 years. We excluded participants with missing data on stroke (n=45), GGT levels (n=1740), and carotenoid intake (n=2125). We also excluded participants with missing data on covariates, including marital status (n=5), education (n=19), body mass index (BMI) (n=207), smoking status (n=8), hypertension (n=2), and coronary heart disease (CHD) (n=84). Assessment of stroke

Stroke status was assessed using the self-reported Medical Status Questionnaire data. Survey participants were asked whether they had been informed by a doctor or other healthcare professional that they had experienced a stroke. Those who responded positively were classified as having experienced a stroke. Conversely, participants who responded negatively were classified as not having experienced a stroke. To ensure the reliability of self-reported data, the NHANES study involved face-to-face interviews conducted by trained researchers [26] to reduce the effects of recall bias or misclassification.

Assessment of GGT

GGT is an enzyme present on the exterior of the cell membrane, and serves as an indicator for hepatic cholestasis [27, 28]. For the NHANES, non-fasting blood samples were collected from participants at mobile examination centers. The blood samples were processed, refrigerated, and transported to a collaborating laboratory for analysis. The DxC800 system (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA) was used to measure the GGT level using the enzymatic rate method. GGT levels were analyzed as continuous variables and grouped into quartiles: Q1 (<4–15 U/L), Q2 (15–20 U/L), Q3 (20–30 U/L), and Q4 (≥30 U/L), with Q1 being used as the reference group. According to the NHANES laboratory manual [29], 36 U/L is considered the upper threshold for GGT level in the general population. Therefore, participants in Q4 (≥30 U/L) were classified as having high GGT levels, which is close to the clinical cutoff, while those in Q1–Q3 (<30 U/L) were considered to have normal GGT levels.

Dietary carotenoid intake assessment

In the NHANES, participants underwent two 24-hour dietary recalls, and the data were used to estimate energy and nutrient intake. A dietary investigator conducted the initial dietary recalls at a mobile examination center through face-to-face interactions. The second dietary recall was conducted via telephonic interviews 3–10 days later. In this study, the dietary carotenoids included α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, and lutein/zeaxanthin. Dietary carotenoid intake was calculated by averaging the intake of the five carotenoids estimated across the two dietary recall interviews. Dietary carotenoid intake was analyzed as a continuous variable and categorized into quartiles: Q1 (2–2919.38 mcg/day), Q2 (2919.38–6212.50 mcg/day), Q3 (6212.50–12079.25 mcg/day), and Q4 (12079.25–465710 mcg/day).

Covariates

The covariates were selected based on previous studies [26, 30–32]. For the NHANES, data were collected on the participants’ demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, health status, and biomarkers through questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Age, sex, and race are strongly associated with the prevalence of stroke. Carotenoid intake varies with age, sex, and cultural dietary habits; furthermore, the prevalence of stroke varies with age [26, 30–32]. Educational level and marital status are strongly associated with health behaviors that directly influence the prognosis of cardiovascular disease. Data pertaining to these variables were obtained through participant self-reports. Educational level was categorized as high school, less than high school, or more than high school. Smoking status was categorized into three groups: non-smoking (smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes), former smoking (smoked more than 100 cigarettes but has quit), or current smoking (smoked more than 100 cigarettes and still smokes). Alcohol intake was estimated by calculating the mean intake from the two 24-hour dietary recalls. Physical activity was categorized as active or inactive. According to the US Department of Health and Human Services physical activity guidelines for Americans, individuals with at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week are considered physically active, and those with less that this recommended cutoff are considered inactive. Obesity is a key mediator of inflammation and insulin resistance, and both these pathways are associated with stroke. BMI was calculated by dividing weight by the square of height [33]. Co-morbidities such as hypertension [34], hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and coronary artery disease are also risk factors for stroke and may interact with the carotenoid/GGT pathway through endothelial dysfunction or systemic inflammation. Diabetes mellitus was defined as follows: hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, diagnosis of diabetes mellitus by a physician, or any self-reported use of insulin or other anti-diabetic medications [35]. Hypertension was defined as the average of three blood pressure measurements, with systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mmHg, the use of anti-hypertensive medications, or a self-reported history of hypertension [36]. The diagnosis of hyperlipidemia was mainly based on the following indicators: triglyceride level ≥150 mg/dL, total cholesterol level ≥200 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein level ≥130 mg/dL, or high-density lipoprotein level <40 mg/dL for men and <50 mg/dL for women [37]. Lipid-lowering drug prescriptions were also used as indicators of a diagnosis. The participants were asked whether they had received a diagnosis of CHD from a physician or any other healthcare practitioner. If they answered yes, they were considered to have CHD. Cancer diagnosis was assessed primarily through participants’ self-reports based on questions about a cancer diagnosis made by a physician. If the participants answered yes, they were categorized as having cancer; if they answered no, they were categorized as not having cancer. These factors may influence the association of dietary carotenoids with GGT level in stroke risk and, therefore, the inclusion of these covariates could help in minimizing confounding effects.

Statistical analysis

In accordance with the NHANES analysis guidelines, we applied sample weights to account for the intricate survey design [38]. To ensure accurate national estimates for the US, we used second-day diet weights as subsample weights to represent the smallest subsample of the study population. To evaluate the baseline characteristics, we present the measured data as means and standard errors (analyzed using weighted t-tests). Furthermore, we present count data as sample numbers and component proportions, with comparisons conducted using the chi-square test. Carotenoid intake in the diet and GGT levels were stratified into quartiles (Q1: <25%; Q2: 25–50%; Q3: 50–75%; Q4: ≥75%), assigning Q1 as the reference category. Initially, univariate logistic regression analyses were performed, followed by the inclusion of statistically significant variables in the multivariate logistic regression models (Supplementary Table 1 in Additional file 1). The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity between variables [39]. The primary objective of this study was to explore the relationship between dietary carotenoid intake, GGT level (examined as a continuous variable and in quartile groups), and the prevalence of stroke. To this end, three multifactorial logistic regression models were formulated. The first model was developed without any adjustments for confounders. The second model incorporated adjustments for age, sex, and race. Finally, the third model included additional adjustments for BMI, educational level, marital status, smoking habits, alcohol consumption status, physical activity, and comorbidities such as CHD, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, cancer, and hypertension. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression using three knots (10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) was used to assess the dose–response relationship between dietary carotenoid intake and GGT level in patients with stroke. The optimal number of knots was determined based on the Akaike information criterion. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, sex, race, BMI, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and CHD stratification factors. We applied Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons between multiple subgroup analyses. Finally, the presence of interactions was assessed using an additive-interaction model. The presence of synergistic additive interactions was determined by assessing the interactions using various measures, including the relative excess risk due to the interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (S). If both the RERI and AP values were positive and distinct from 0, and the S value exceeded 1 without equaling it, this signified the presence of a synergistic additive interaction. When RERI is >0, the interaction is positive, and when RERI is <0, the interaction is negative. AP refers to the proportion of cases attributable to the interaction in the presence of the two exposure factors; the larger the value, the greater the contribution of the interaction to the pathogenesis of the disease. S measures the degree of synergy between the two exposure factors, with S>1 indicating a synergistic effect and S<1 indicating the opposite effect. Sensitivity analyses were performed using the multiple imputation method to compensate for missing data based on five imputed datasets (M=5) and 20 iterations of predictive mean matching.

R software (version 4.2.2) was used to perform all NHANES analyses. The “survey” package was used to account for the complex survey design and sampling weights of the NHANES data; the “rms” package was used to construct and validate the regression models; the “ggplot2” package was used for data visualization; and the “epiR” package was used to construct the interaction models. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

We enrolled 11,146 participants aged ≥60 (mean 69) years (representing 53,872,222 adults in the US), including non-stroke (n=10214) and stroke (n=932) groups (Table 1). Stroke prevalence was approximately 7.24%. Participants who had experienced a stroke were typically older, non-Hispanic black, married, had lower educational levels, and were less physically active compared to those who had not experienced a stroke. Further, they were more likely to have a history of smoking, whether previous or current, and were more likely to have diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and CHD, which are common risk factors for stroke. Compared with participants in the non-stroke group, participants in the stroke group had higher GGT levels but a lower dietary carotenoid intake. Association between GGT level and stroke

To explore the association between blood GGT levels and the prevalence of stroke, multivariate logistic regression modeling was used (Table 2). When GGT level was analyzed as a continuous variable, it exhibited a positive correlation with the prevalence of stroke. This correlation persisted even after adjustments for other variables. Subsequently, GGT level was assessed as a categorical variable. In the model without adjustments, elevated levels of GGT (Q4) were associated with an increased prevalence of stroke (compared to Q1). Notably, in both the partially and fully adjusted models, the prevalence of stroke was higher in the highest GGT-level quartile (Q4) than in the lowest GGT-level quartile (Q1) (Q3: odds ratio [OR]=1.75, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.28, 2.40; Q4: OR=1.51, 95% CI: 1.10, 2.06). In the subsequent RCS model, GGT level nonlinearly correlated with the prevalence of stroke (p=0.007, p=0.012), with an inflection point of 36 U/L (Fig. 2). When the GGT level was less than 36 U/L, the prevalence of stroke increased and was strongly and positively correlated with higher GGT levels; when the GGT level was greater than 36 U/L, the positive correlation became weaker. To investigate the correlation between blood GGT levels and the prevalence of stroke across different populations, subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, race, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, and CHD (Fig. 3). Compared with their effect in the normal population, elevated GGT levels had no significant association with the prevalence of stroke in individuals with hypertension, diabetes, or CHD (all p for interaction>0.05). Further, no significant interactions were observed among age, race, or BMI. However, a positive correlation between elevated GGT levels and the prevalence of stroke was observed among female participants. Association between dietary carotenoid intake and the prevalence of stroke

When analyzed as a continuous variable, dietary carotenoid intake was significantly and inversely associated with the prevalence of stroke in the unadjusted and covariate-adjusted logistic regression models (all p<0.05). When carotenoid intake was presented as a categorical variable (quartiles) (Table 2), it was associated with a reduced prevalence of stroke in Q3 and Q4 as compared to that in Q1. Specifically, in Model 1, a 42% reduction in the prevalence of stroke was observed with each unit increase in carotenoid intake (0.58 [0.45, 0.74]), whereas Models 2 and 3 revealed reductions of 37% (0.63 [0.49, 0.81]) and 24% (0.76 [0.59, 0.97]), respectively. An RCS analysis utilizing Model 3 was conducted to elucidate the dose–response relationship between carotenoid intake and the prevalence of stroke, it indicated a consistently negative linear correlation between carotenoid intake (p=0.001) and the prevalence of stroke (p=0.052). The analysis was stratified according to age, sex, race, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and CHD (Fig. 3). The results demonstrated that dietary carotenoid intake was consistently associated with the prevalence of stroke in different populations. No significant interactions were observed between the subgroups (p>0.05 for all interactions). After Bonferroni correction, all p-values remained nonsignificant (Supplementary Table 3 in Additional file 1). It should be noted that all subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature, and therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Effect of interaction between GGT level and dietary carotenoid intake on the prevalence of stroke

In the additive interaction model, we categorized participants in GGT-level Q4 as the group with high levels of GGT (≥30 U/L), whereas those in Q2, Q3, and Q4 were defined as having normal levels (<30 U/L). Carotenoid intake Q1 was defined as the group with a low intake, whereas Q2, Q3, and Q4 were categorized as one group with a normal intake (Fig. 4). The evaluation of the presence of additive interactions by calculating the RERI, AP, and S revealed interaction indexes of 1.49 (95% CI: 0.52, 2.47), 0.49 (95% CI: 0.36, 0.62), and 3.73 (95% CI: 2.59, 5.38), respectively (Table 3). RERI reflects the magnitude of the additional effect of the combination of the two exposure factors over the sum of the individual effects and can be used to determine the direction of the interaction. These findings indicate that high GGT levels and a low carotenoid intake have a significant synergistic effect on the prevalence of stroke. Additive interactions persisted after partial and complete adjustments for covariates. Discussion

This cross-sectional study of individuals residing in the US and aged ≥60 years was designed to investigate the association between blood GGT levels, dietary carotenoid intake, and the prevalence of stroke. The results revealed that blood GGT levels were positively associated with the prevalence of stroke, whereas dietary carotenoid intake was negatively associated with the prevalence of stroke. Additionally, high serum GGT levels and a low carotenoid intake had a synergistic effect on the prevalence of stroke. Therefore, based on the associations between diet, blood GGT levels, and the prevalence of stroke presented in this study, further studies are required to develop suitable interventions.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the interaction effects of blood GGT levels and dietary carotenoid intake on the prevalence of stroke in people in the US and aged ≥60 years. These results corroborate the findings of several previous studies showing that elevated GGT levels are associated with an increased prevalence of stroke [11, 40, 41]. A case–cohort study of 27,548 middle-aged participants revealed a 1.20-times increase in the prevalence of stroke per 1-unit change in GGT level [11]. The long follow-up duration and large sample size of the current study increase the reliability of those results. The consistency of these findings supports the association between GGT levels and the prevalence of stroke. A separate cohort study involving participants from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study entailed a median follow-up duration of 24.2 years and reported that GGT is positively associated with all stroke events, including a dose–response relationship with ischemic stroke [42]. Therefore, GGT levels are positively correlated with the prevalence of stroke. The main function of GGT is to cleave glutathione; however, glutathione prevents oxidative stress [43]. Therefore, an elevated GGT level is a sign of insufficient anti-oxidants and increased oxidative stress [44]. Additionally, GGT is directly involved in the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis through its pro-oxidative and pro-inflammatory effects, leading to plaque instability and an increased prevalence of stroke [45, 46]. Our results demonstrated that elevated GGT levels are associated with an increased likelihood of stroke. Identical outcomes were obtained using a logistic regression analysis. These findings imply that GGT level can be used as a potential biomarker for predicting the prevalence of stroke and for early intervention and prevention. In clinical practice, healthcare providers should pay closer attention to changes in GGT levels among older adults, combined with their dietary carotenoid intake, to identify individuals at a higher risk of stroke in advance. Hence, personalized preventive and intervention measures, such as targeted dietary advice or more intensive health monitoring plans, can be formulated, which are expected to improve patient prognosis and reduce the disease burden of stroke on individuals and society.

In the subgroup analyses stratified by sex, an interaction between elevated GGT level and the prevalence of stroke was initially observed among women. However, after Bonferroni correction, this result was no longer statistically significant. This may suggest that the interaction was limited by sample size or effect size. In future studies, researchers may consider increasing the sample size of women or combining biological markers to further explore sex differences in GGT level and the prevalence of stroke. Further, a larger sample would provide more statistical power to detect potentially significant interactions. Incorporating additional biomarkers related to sex, such as sex hormones or other sex-specific physiological indicators, could help uncover the underlying mechanisms that drive sex-related differences in the association between GGT level and stroke prevalence. Although stratified analyses were performed across various subgroups, the lack of statistically significant interaction terms indicates that the observed associations should be interpreted as exploratory. Such analyses were conducted primarily to assess the consistency of the associations rather than to draw definitive conclusions regarding effect modification. Future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs are needed to validate these findings.

Lycopene is an important intermediate in carotenoid synthesis [47]. Previous evidence indicates that lycopene offers several cardio–cerebrovascular benefits, including anti-atherosclerotic, anti-oxidant, and lipid-modulating effects, as well as improved endothelial function, with favorable efficacy in stroke prevention [47]. A cohort study confirmed that higher serum carotenoid concentrations are associated with a reduced prevalence of stroke [16]. Lutein/zeaxanthin intake is inversely proportional to the prevalence of stroke because lutein/zeaxanthin may improve vasodilatation and endothelial function [48]. In addition, a meta-analysis of 69 prospective studies revealed a negative correlation between the intake of anti-oxidants such as β-carotene and lycopene and the prevalence of stroke [49]. These studies emphasized the potential of dietary carotenoids as anti-oxidants in reducing the prevalence of stroke. Oxidative stress is a disturbance in the pro-oxidant–anti-oxidant balance that plays an important role in the development of stroke [50]. First, oxidative stress can lead to the impairment of vascular endothelial function and the excessive production of oxygen free radicals, causing vasoconstriction, increased permeability, and promoting thrombosis and stroke [51]. Additionally, oxidative stress can directly affect the structure and function of the vascular wall, thereby promoting atherosclerosis formation and development [52]. Carotenoids, which are naturally occurring anti-oxidants, may exert neuroprotective effects by preventing lipid peroxidation, scavenging free radicals, and reducing reactive oxygen species-induced damage [53]. Our results are consistent with those of previous studies. However, several other studies have reported inconsistent views [22, 23]. A Mendelian randomization study in individuals with a European ancestry found that genetically determined anti-oxidant levels (lycopene and β-carotene) are not causally associated with a lower risk of ischemic stroke [23]. Therefore, anti-oxidant supplementation is unlikely to offer a clinical benefit in the prevention of ischemic stroke. Additionally, a prospective cohort study of 34,492 postmenopausal women found that a high intake of dietary carotenoids was not associated with a reduced risk of stroke [22]. Similarly, Ascherio et al. investigated the relationship between carotenoid intake and the risk of stroke in 43,738 men aged 40–75 years and without a history of cardiovascular disease or diabetes, and found that among carotenoids, only lutein was associated with the risk of stroke; however, this association weakened after adjustment for other dietary factors [54]. Possible reasons for these inconsistent results are differences in the study population and sex distribution. Moreover, most previous studies were focused on serum carotenoid levels or the association between specific carotenoids (lycopene or β-carotene) and cerebrovascular diseases. Currently, research on the association between dietary carotenoid intake and the prevalence of stroke is limited. In this respect, our study revealed a negative correlation between dietary carotenoid intake and the prevalence of stroke.

This study not only enriches the evidence regarding the relationship between carotenoid intake and stroke prevalence but also deepens our understanding of the underlying mechanisms. It aligns with previous research in demonstrating the role of carotenoids as anti-oxidants in reducing stroke risk, yet the differences in results highlight the complexity of this relationship due to variations in study populations and designs. For instance, while some studies were focused on genetic determinants or specific subgroups, our research, based on a nationally representative NHANES database and considering the interaction with GGT levels, provides a more comprehensive view of the relationship between dietary carotenoid intake and stroke prevalence among older adults.

We also investigated the effect of the interaction between dietary carotenoid intake and blood GGT levels on the prevalence of stroke. We found a synergistic interaction between low dietary carotenoid intake and high GGT levels during stroke. Previous evidence indicates that elevated GGT levels are markers of anti-oxidant deficiency and oxidative stress [44]. Dietary carotenoids act as anti-oxidants that inhibit oxidative stress and inflammatory responses and regulate lipid metabolism [53]. Dietary carotenoid intake and serum GGT levels may collectively affect the prevalence of stroke by influencing oxidative stress and lipid metabolism. This discovery of the synergistic interaction between dietary carotenoid intake and GGT levels is a novel insight emphasizing the importance of considering the interplay among multiple factors rather than focusing solely on individual risk factors to better understand the stroke pathogenesis. Therefore, dietary carotenoid intake may contribute to the reduction ina elevated GGT levels and provide a reference point for subsequent exploration of strategies related to the prevalence of stroke reduction. A multicenter epidemiological study involving 3,128 participants arrived at a similar conclusion, stating that serum GGT levels are inversely associated with anti-oxidant intake, including carotenoid intake [55]. Further consideration may be given to increasing the dietary intake of carotenoids and regular analysis of serum GGT levels in older adults to provide a reference point for exploring the relevant pathways to reduce the prevalence of stroke.

This study has several strengths. First, it is based on the nationally representative NHANES database, which allows access to data pertaining to a relatively large sample of older adults. Simultaneously, large samples minimize the bias caused by insufficient sample sizes. Second, previous studies have demonstrated that dietary carotenoid intake and blood GGT levels are independent risk factors for stroke. However, few studies have explored the potential interaction between these two factors in patients with stroke. Our findings suggest a synergistic effect between low carotenoid intake and high GGT levels, further emphasizing their combined impact on the prevalence of stroke.

However, this study also has some limitations. First, the diagnosis of stroke in this cross-sectional study was based on a self-reported questionnaire, which may have been affected by recall bias. Such recall bias could either attenuate or inflate the true associations. Therefore, clinically verified stroke diagnoses should be used in future studies to ensure more reliable and valid results. Second, it was not possible to distinguish whether the stroke type was ischemic or hemorrhagic. Therefore, the NHANES stroke diagnosis may have included both. The inability to distinguish between stroke types may limit our understanding of the relationship of dietary carotenoid intake and serum GGT levels with different types of stroke. Therefore, future studies should distinguish between stroke subtypes to better elucidate the specific effects and potential mechanisms of dietary carotenoid intake and GGT on stroke. Third, the study’s cross-sectional design allows only for establishing associations between dietary carotenoid intake or blood GGT level and stroke; it does not permit the inference of causality nor does it rule out the possibility of reverse causality. Consequently, the cross-sectional study design limited our ability to extrapolate the influence of study factors on outcomes and acquire a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of disease progression. Hence, caution should be exercised while interpreting the results of this study. Future studies using prospective cohort designs or Mendelian randomization approaches are warranted to better elucidate the causal links among these variables. Fourth, although 24-hour dietary recalls are widely used in nutritional epidemiological studies, this method may not accurately reflect individuals’ long-term dietary exposure. Dietary habits may change owing to various factors, such as seasons, health conditions, and psychological states. Additionally, individuals may not be able to accurately recall their past dietary intake and food types, leading to uncertainty in the data. The use of plasma carotenoid level as a biomarker should be considered in future research to improve exposure assessment. Although some confounders were controlled in this study, residual confounders (e.g., genetic predisposition) may have affected the presented results and should be evaluated in future studies. Although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, genetic factors, dietary supplement use, and liver disease could not be accounted for due to missing data. The absence of these variables may result in residual confounding, potentially leading to an overestimation or underestimation of the actual association between GGT, carotenoid intake, and stroke risk. Although the NHANES provides a wealth of valuable information, findings from nutritional epidemiological studies should not be used in isolation to inform interventions. Nevertheless, the present findings have some translational potential. For older adults at risk of stroke, we suggest that the intake of carotenoid-rich foods, such as carrots, spinach, and oranges, should be increased appropriately. Additionally, GGT level can be used as an auxiliary indicator for stroke risk assessment in this population. Abnormally elevated serum GGT levels may indicate underlying metabolic disorders or liver function abnormalities, which are associated with the prevalence of stroke. Clinicians shuld use this indicator to inform patient management and lifestyle guidance. Fifth, dietary carotenoid intake was categorized into quartiles based on the distribution of intake levels in the NHANES study population. Currently, there are no standardized clinical thresholds for dietary carotenoid intake, as reference values for optimal or inadequate intake have not yet been established in the general adult population. Therefore, the quartile-based method is a common and accepted approach in epidemiological studies for classifying intake levels and exploring associations with health outcomes. Finally, we herein only analyzed total carotenoid intake and did not explore the specific dose–response relationship between individual carotenoids (e.g., lycopene, β-carotene) and stroke risk. In the future, researchers should consider analyzing the effects of different carotenoids separately to obtain more targeted nutritional recommendations.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that dietary carotenoid intake and blood GGT levels are independent risk factors for stroke in older adults in the US. Moreover, low dietary carotenoid intake and high blood GGT level synergistically affect the prevalence of stroke. These associations should be validated in future studies, including in interventional studies.
