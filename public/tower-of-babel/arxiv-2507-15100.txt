Filling the Gap: Is Commonsense Knowledge Generation useful for Natural Language Inference?
Chathuri Jayaweera, Brianna Yanqui, Bonnie Dorr

Abstract
Natural Language Inference (NLI) is the task of determining the semantic entailment of a premise for a given hypothesis. The task aims to develop systems that emulate natural human inferential processes where commonsense knowledge plays a major role. However, existing commonsense resources lack sufficient coverage for a variety of premise-hypothesis pairs. This study explores the potential of Large Language Models as commonsense knowledge generators for NLI along two key dimensions: their reliability in generating such knowledge and the impact of that knowledge on prediction accuracy. We adapt and modify existing metrics to assess LLM factuality and consistency in generating in this context. While explicitly incorporating commonsense knowledge does not consistently improve overall results, it effectively helps distinguish entailing instances and moderately improves distinguishing contradictory and neutral inferences.



1
Introduction

Natural Language Inference (NLI),
also known as Recognizing Textual Entailment (RTE),
is the task of determining, given a
sentence pair—a premise (P) and a hypothesis (H)—whether the premise semantically entails the hypothesis (Entailment), contradicts it (Contradiction), or neither (Neutral). Earlier definitions specify
RTE
as
the directional relationship between P and H, where
Entailment

holds, if
a typical reader
equipped with general language understanding and background knowledge, would infer that H is likely true upon reading P.

There is little guidance on the amount of common world and language knowledge a system
needs to accurately determine the inference relationship between a premise and hypothesis
(
Zaenen et al. 2005
)
.

Manning 2006

argues that the goal
of NLI is to model the inferences made by a typical, informed, attentive reader
using everyday
background and real-world knowledge.
This perspective supports the development of models that emulate natural human inferential processes, which depends on
incorporating commonsense knowledge into their design.

Premise:
A woman is chatting as she drinks her coffee.

Hypothesis:
The woman is silent.

Commonsense knowledge:
When someone is chatting, they are not silent.

Commonsense knowledge refers to practical information about how the world works, typically shared by most people

(
Bhargava and Ng 2022
)
. In this paper we refer to
Commonsense Knowledge
as an axiom, i.e., a basic, self-evident principle or rule, expressed in natural language, that reflects widely held understanding and helps make sense of
everyday situations without requiring specialized expertise or formal proof. Most
adults possess a large number of
axioms
guiding daily reasoning.

Figure

1
shows a premise-hypothesis pair from the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) dataset
Bowman et al. 2015

that relies on
commonsense knowledge, in the form of a natural language axiom, to predict the correct inference label. We define
an accurate NLI system to be one that
incorporates implicit, axiomatic knowledge into its computations to emulate typical human inference.
Given the
centrality of commonsense reasoning to NLI and
related tasks,
many efforts
aim to construct
structured commonsense representations. Still, it remains unclear whether existing resources
fully capture what is truly required.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs),
trained on large volumes of human-generated text suggests
they
encode significant
commonsense knowledge.
These models
may be capable of generating relevant commonsense axioms
for any premise-hypothesis pair.
Incorporating such axioms
into NLI predictions may
improve the LLM accuracy.
Building on
these intuitions, we
investigate two research questions: 1) Can LLMs
reliably generate
commonsense axioms for NLI? 2) Does
incorporating these
axioms improve LLM prediction accuracy?

To answer these questions,
we introduce modified evaluation criteria for assessing
the reliability of LLM-generated
commonsense
axioms, adapting
existing factuality and consistency metrics
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
.
We also evaluate how
incorporating such knowledge
into the premise-hypothesis pairs
affects LLM inference predictions.
Our contributions are as follows.

•

We introduce evaluation criteria tailored to measure factuality and consistency of
LLM-generated commonsense axioms
for NLI.

•

We evaluate the performance of the Llama-3.1-70B model
Grattafiori et al. 2024

in generating
commonsense axioms
from
premise-hypothesis pairs using our new criteria.

•

We show that, while incorporating
commonsense knowledge does not consistently
improve
overall accuracy, it effectively helps
distinguish
entailment and moderately improves identifying contradictary
and neutral cases.

2
Related Work

Since the introduction of the NLI task, researchers continue to debate what kinds of background knowledge are required.
This section reviews efforts to
incorporate commonsense and other supporting knowledge into NLI models.

2.1
Necessity for additional background knowledge in NLI

Some
premise-hypothesis pairs require different types of background knowledge,
classified into three categories: linguistic, common, and commonsense
(
Storks et al. 2020
)
. Linguistic knowledge requires
understanding syntactic and semantic structures. Common knowledge refers to
widely known facts, often stated explicitly. Commonsense knowledge
is the implicit, intuitive understanding that
humans use to interpret everyday situations.

To account for semantic variations between sentence pairs, many NLI systems
use external linguistic knowledge resources such as WordNet
Miller 1995
and hand-crafted inference rules
Chen et al. 2018
.
However, many implementations also benefit from
need for
broader
world knowledge,
including geographic and spatial understanding, for example,

The cat sat on the mat
implies
The mat was under the cat
. Our study explores whether LLMs can
generate suitable commonsense knowledge to meet this requirement.

2.2
NLI systems with external knowledge

Several systems explicitly incorporate external commonsense knowledge to improve NLI performance.

Camburu et al. 2018

investigate whether providing
a prior explanation alongside the
premise-hypothesis pair improves
inference prediction.
Similarly, we
examine how adding
commonsense knowledge
impacts
prediction accuracy.

Nguyen and Hatua 2024
extend the e-SNLI dataset by using its highlighted keywords
to retrieve relevant commonsense information from ConceptNet
Speer et al. 2017
and Google search
summaries.
However, this approach relies on manually labeled keywords
to extract appropriate
commonsense information.

ExBERT
Gajbhiye et al. 2021
generate contextual representations that integrate
commonsense knowledge from ConceptNet and Aristo Tuple KGs
Mishra et al. 2017
. ERNIE-NLI
Bauer et al. 2021
generate knowledge embeddings
using heuristic-based polarity assignments between concept pairs. However, these approaches do not guarantee
relevance of constructed knowledge to the specific premise-hypothesis pair.

2.3
LLMs for commonsense knowledge generation

Given their extensive pretraining on a variety of training data, LLMs already encode substantial background knowledge, enabling the use of commonsense knowledge across multiple domains.
Wei et al. 2024
explore LLMs in commonsense question answering, by
guiding them to generate high-quality, effective knowledge.

Lin 2024
generates
synthetic, persona-based commonsense knowledge to support open-domain dialog systems. These efforts highlight LLMs’ potential
as commonsense knowledge generators.
Zheng et al. 2024
define evaluation criteria to assess the factuality and consistency of LLMs as knowledge sources
for commonsense question answering. We adapt their metrics to the NLI task and introduce modifications suited to its structure.

Results and Analysis

This section performs a comprehensive analysis of results obtained from the three evaluation processes explained in Section

5.1
Factuality and Consistency Analysis

Factuality metrics evaluate how accurately the model generates
commonsense axioms for a given premise-hypothesis pair. Correct and Wrong rates (CR and WR) measure the proportion of accurate versus inaccurate axioms over all those generated.
The NCR, ranging from -1 to 1, indicates how often the model favors accurate over inaccurate axioms.

As shown in Table
2
,
P3
-generated axioms yield
higher overall NCR value than
P1
axioms, for both SNLI and ANLI datasets. However, Figures
6
-
8
reveal that

P3
-generated axioms
tend to restate premise-hypothesis content,
whereas
P1
axioms are structured as standalone commonsense statements.
Although
P1
axioms lack factuality due to over-generalization, their format aligns more closely with intended knowledge insertion.
The higher
P3
factuality appears to result from paraphrasing rather than true knowledge generation.

Within inference classes, Table
3
shows that
P1
-generated axioms surpass
P3
-generated axioms in NCR for
Entailment
cases in ANLI, while their performance is comparable in SNLI.

The consistency metrics evaluate the model’s ability to consistently generate the same commonsense axiom for a given premise-hypothesis pair across multiple runs. C
correct
and C
wrong
represent average consistency for accurate and inaccurate axioms, respectively. NCCR measures whether the model more consistently generates
correct commonsense axioms
than incorrect ones.

Similar to the
factuality results, consistency scores show that

P3
-generated axioms are more consistent across runs, in both correct and incorrect cases.
P3
also achieves a higher NCCR.
However, Table
3
shows that for most inference classes,

P1
-generated axioms exhibit higher average consistency for both correct and incorrect axioms in SNLI.
This suggests that the model
is more consistent in generating
P1
-style axioms for SNLI, but its lower factuality scores reduce
its NCCR.
Furthermore,
P1
-generated axioms also report higher NCCR for entailment in ANLI dataset significantly surpassing
P3
-generated axioms.

6
Conclusion

This study explores the reliability of LLMs as commonsense knowledge generators for NLI, and the effect of incorporating such knowledge into inference
prediction. Using the
Llama-3.1-70b-Instruct model
and two benchmark datasets (SNLI and ANLI),
we demonstrate that adding generated commonsense axioms improves NLI performance, particularly in identifying entailing premise-hypothesis pairs in both datasets. To a lesser extent we also demonstrate prediction improvements for contradictions (in SNLI) and neutral instances (in ANLI).

We assess the reliability of LLM-generated commonsense knowledge through factuality and consistency evaluations,
using a modified version of the metrics proposed by
Zheng et al. 2024
and incorporating the Claude-3.5-Sonnet model as a judge. We generate axioms for SNLI and ANLI datasets using the Llama-3.1-70b-Instruct model. Results show that pre-generated axioms
(
P1
) offer higher consistency across most inference classes in SNLI and higher factuality entailments in ANLI, compared to those generated post-prediction (
P3
).

These findings highlight the potential of commonsense knowledge to improve NLI performance, while also underscoring the importance of ensuring its accuracy for overall performance improvements.

Future Work

Future research will focus on improving the accuracy and reliability of commonsense axiom generation
for NLI.
We plan to explore strategies to guide
LLMs toward producing
highly factual and consistent axioms.
We will also examine
the faithfulness of
inference predictions to pre-prediction commonsense axioms and
assess how well
post-prediction explanations align with model outputs.

Additionally, we aim to extend this work by evaluating a broader range of LLMs and their capabilities in
generating high-quality commonsense knowledge. Relatedly, we are interested in building new resources to support commonsense reasoning.

Ultimately, the aim is to contribute to more robust and interpretable NLI systems that better emulate
natural human inference.

Limitations

This study examines the use of
LLMs to generate
commonsense axioms for premise-hypothesis pairs to assist in identifying the correct inference relationship.
to support inference classification.
While we evaluate this approach empirically,
several limitations remain.

First, our evaluation relies on an LLM to assess
the helpfulness and semantic similarity of generated commonsense axioms without full human
annotation.
Although we manually reviewed a small subset of outputs,
the overall accuracy of LLM-generated ratings is not
comprehensively validated. Nonetheless, our initial reviews suggest that the assessments are generally reliable.

Second,
we evaluate
the reliability and impact of LLM-generated commonsense axioms
using a single model.
While the results demonstrate potential,
testing across
a broader range of LLMs would provide a more robust validation.

Third, our analysis focuses on factuality and consistency but does not evaluate
faithfulness of LLM predictions to pre-prediction axioms
or
alignment
between post-prediction axioms and model outputs.
We plan to
address these aspects in future work.

Finally, our study is limited to
the English language. All
findings are validated solely
in English, which narrows the generalizability of our conclusions.
We aim for this work to serve as a foundation for future research in multilingual NLI settings.

Ethics Statement

Our study uses LLM-generated commonsense axioms in its experiments. Some examples reveal a tendency toward overgeneralization,
which may reflect harmful assumptions. A manual review of a small sample suggests that these overgeneralized axioms are typically rated
low in factuality, leading to incorrect predictions. Nonetheless, we
do not conduct a full audit of potential harms and acknowledge this is an area for future examination.

We use publicly available datasets—SNLI and ANLI—under
CC BY-SA 4.0 and CC BY-NC 4.0 licenses, respectively.
We ensure proper attribution and rely on the datasets’ original anonymization and ethical review procedures.
Although we do not explicitly filter generated axioms for
personally identifiable information (PII), the prompts are designed to elicit generalized knowledge rather than personal content, making PII inclusion highly unlikely.
The prompt details are included in the study.

We use the Llama-3.1-70b-Instruct model to generate axioms and the
Claude-3.5-Sonnet model to evaluate them, both accessed via institution-internal API (not third party) in inference-only mode. No models were trained as a part of this study. The total cost of API usage was under $100.

We used AI assistance for stylistic edits (e.g., paraphrasing or synonym suggestions), but all substantive content was authored by the research team.
