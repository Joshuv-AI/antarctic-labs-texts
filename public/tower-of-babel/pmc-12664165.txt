Sociodemographic and ecological factors associated with malaria and malaria recurrence risk in children in Msambweni, Kwale County, Kenya
Authors: Jael Sagina Amugongo, A D LaBeaud, Carren Bosire, Donal Bisanzio, M Nzaro, Moses Ngari, D M Vu, Bryson A Ndenga, Francis M Mutuku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health issue where poverty and inadequate healthcare infrastructure intersect. This study assessed sociodemographic and ecological factors influencing malaria risk in Msambweni, Kwale County, Kenya.MethodsA child cohort (aged 1–18 years) presenting with acute febrile illness at Msambweni County Referral Hospital from 2014 to 2018 was enrolled, supplemented by a nested case–control study. Data were collected through caregiver interviews and household observations. Malaria was diagnosed by light microscopy (Giemsa-stained thick and thin smears). Recurrent malaria was defined as ≥ 2 confirmed infections within the study period.ResultsThere were 2610 febrile illness visits by 2371 children; 40.6% (1059/2610) had malaria parasitaemia. A single visit occurred among 2169/2371 (91.5%), while 174, 19 and 9 children made two, three and four visits, respectively. Overall, 992/2371 (41.8%, 95%CI 39.8–43.9%) children experienced malaria, and 60/2371 (2.5%, 95%CI 1.9–3.2) had recurrent malaria; 51 had one, 8 had two and 1 had three recurrent episodes. In multivariable analysis, malaria risk was associated with older age (2–5 vs. < 2 years: aRR 1.38 [95%CI 1.09–1.75]; ≥ 5 vs. < 2 years: aRR 1.57 [95%CI 1.23–2.00]). Protective factors included higher maternal education (secondary and above: aRR 0.66 [95%CI 0.50–0.87]), piped/well water inside the compound (aRR 0.78 [95%CI 0.65–0.94]), and electricity/solar lighting (aRR 0.61 [95%CI 0.50–0.74]). Recurrent malaria was linked to poor-quality housing, particularly dirt floors and proximity to mosquito breeding sites such as rice fields. Increased malaria risk was also associated with poor ventilation and limited windows.DiscussionTargeted vector control near rice fields and consistent use of insecticide-treated nets remain crucial. Housing improvements, including window screening and better roofing, may reduce mosquito-human contact. The shift toward increased malaria among school-aged children highlights the need to strengthen interventions targeting this group.ConclusionsInvestments in housing, maternal education, and WASH infrastructure can enhance malaria control efforts and strengthen existing interventions.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum, Malaria risk, Malaria recurrence, Intervention

Background

Malaria remains a public health challenge that disproportionately affects vulnerable populations particularly where poverty and inadequate health care infrastructure converge to facilitate it’s spread [1]. According to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) malaria report of 2024, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases with 597,000 malaria death cases with majority of the cases occurring in sub-Saharan Africa (sSA) [2]. In Kenya, about 70% of the population are reported to be at risk for malaria with 13 million living in endemic areas according to the Kenya malaria indicator survey of 2020 [3]. Compared to 2020, the number of malaria cases in Kenya are increasing [2] despite high coverage with core interventions. The stalling or increase in malaria cases in Kenya and in many other sSA countries is attributed mainly to insecticide resistance [4, 5] and changing human and mosquito vector behaviour leading increased human-vector contact in early and/or late in the morning [6–8].

On a global scale, transmission risk for malaria is highly variable across the country with environmental factors such rainfall and temperature being key facilitators of this heterogeneity [9]. At local level, besides human and vector behaviours [7, 10], a wide range of other factors including cultural and socioeconomic status drive the sustained transmission patterns [11, 12]. Several studies have explored risk factors for recurrent malaria, with age and socioeconomic status appearing as the most common drivers of malaria recurrence [13, 14]. Some malaria immunity develops in individuals with continued exposure to malaria parasites. Often, these individuals become asymptomatic carriers of asexual parasites [15, 16]. The interaction between increasing age with parasitaemia results in higher asymptomatic malaria compared to symptomatic malaria. The probability of recurring malaria is likely to be higher in poor households where exposure to a mosquito bite is higher [13]. Generally, there is a very high overlap between drivers of malaria and recurring malaria. Understanding the sociodemographic and ecological factors associated with malaria and recurrent malaria risk is crucial for developing targeted intervention strategies.

Sociodemographic factors refer to the individual and household level factors that affect asymptomatic and symptomatic malaria risk positively or negatively. These factors include level of education of the parent/guardian, child sex, socioeconomic status, house characteristics (roof, wall and floor materials, presence of eaves, number of windows, presence of window and door screens), density of structures, family size, not using insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs), level of ITNs ownership and previous history of malaria episode [11, 17–22]. Other factors at household level that are constantly associated with malaria include source of drinking water, source of lighting and availability of sanitation facilities. Families with no access to electricity, use of unprotected source of drinking water and without flush toilet are considered to have low socioeconomic status and have increased risk to malaria [23, 24]. Indeed, a recent study in Congo reported that poor water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) contributed to increased malaria rates because of increased mosquito bites everywhere [25].

Availability of breeding habitats close to human habitation has long been demonstrated as one of the important ecological risk factors for malaria transmission. People living in households close to larval habitats such water pools, streams and rice paddies are associated with increased malaria risk [11, 26–29]. The ability of water to pool and persist long enough to be productive larval habitats for malaria vectors is not a function of rainfall alone. It is modulated by human activities, elevation and water quality [30]. Presence of bushes around household is associated with increased risk of malaria [13, 31]. Living in households proximate to banana plantains and swampy areas has also been associated with increased malaria risk in Ghana [32]. A study in Ethiopia demonstrated that female Anopheline malaria vectors of all physiological states preferred to rest in banana plantains located close to household [33] explaining the association between banana plantains and malaria risk. Other ecological risk factors for malaria include topographical characteristics such as ruggedness, elevation, aspect, and slope [34–36].

Development of effective malaria interventions requires a thorough understanding of drivers of risk. This is due to increasing confounding factors that drive continued transmission such as increasing insecticide resistance in vector populations coupled with climate and environmental changes that have shown to increase and provide habitats for mosquito breeding [37, 38]. In this study, the identification of sociodemographic and ecological factors associated with malaria risk in Msambweni, Kwale County, Kenya were evaluated. Information generated from the study will be utilized in developing targeted and effective malaria control strategies.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in Msambweni (4.48°S, 39.48°E), a rural site located approximately 60 km south of the port city of Mombasa. The area of study is found in the south coast of Kenya in Kwale County. Kwale County borders Tanzania to the southwest and the Indian Ocean to the east. The area is hot and humid year-round with a range of annual mean temperature of 23 °C to 34 °C and an average humidity range of 60–80%. The altitude ranges from 0 to 464 m above sea level. There are two rainy seasons, April to June and October to November, but rain falls nearly every month, especially nearer to the coastline. The total precipitation varies from 900 to 1500 mm per year along the coastal belt to 500–600 mm as one moves further away from the coastal belt. Malaria is endemic to the study areas and the predominant malaria vectors include include Anopheles funestus, Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.) and Anopheles arabiensis [39]. Most residents in Msambweni are fishermen and subsistence farmers.

Study design and participants

The current study was nested within a larger investigation on dengue, chikungunya, and malaria, which enrolled a child cohort (ages 1 to 18) from 2014 to 2018 across four sites: Chulaimbo and Kisumu town (Kisumu County), and Ukunda and Msambweni (Kwale County) [6, 40–54]. For this study, data was exclusively drawn from the Msambweni site in Kwale County. The participants were residents of villages within the catchment area of Msambweni County Referral Hospital (MCRH), which they primarily used, and were originally enrolled in a broader research study on dengue, chikungunya, and malaria.

Study sample and procedures

Two main study procedures were conducted:

Identification of risk factors for malaria and recurrent malaria

During the study period of the larger dengue, chikungunya, and malaria study, 2371 children aged 1–18 years visited the hospital with acute febrile illness and were enrolled in the study from approximately 30 surrounding villages. More child hospital visits were recorded from villages closer to MCRH. At each visit, a thick and thin blood smear for malaria parasite examination was prepared, stained with 2% Giemsa, and read by a laboratory technologist at MCRH. Children with positive parasitaemia tests were treated with artemether-lumefantrine following standardized Kenyan Ministry of Health protocols and hospitalized for severe illness. Demographic, socioeconomic, and housing quality data was collected on all participants (2371) to assess the risk factors for malaria infection and recurrent malaria using a structured questionnaire at the time of study visit. The variables assessed included age of the child, education level of the mother, sex, type of house roof, type of the house floor, type of house windows, number windows, number of rooms in the house, number of people in the house, number of people sleeping in the same room, type of latrine used, source of house lighting, source of drinking water, and asset index.

Identification of ecological risk factors for recurrent malaria

Locations of all households of The 2371 were mapped using global positioning system (GPS). The location data was used to assess the spatial pattern of malarial fevers. Of The 2371 children, 202 made at least two sick visits to the hospital with fever and 33.6% (68/202) were positive for malaria during any of the visits. A group of 202 houses were identified to be able to understand the socioeconomic and ecological risk factors for recurring malaria through a case–control sub study. Households of 66 of the 68 children who had positive malaria test were identified and acted as ‘cases’ in the sub-study. An equal number of controls were matched to the cases by village. The ‘controls’ were children who made more than one sick visit to the hospital with fever during the study period but with a negative malaria test. All the case and control households were visited to collect ecological and demographic characteristics data. During the visit, a record was made of any potential Anopheles breeding site within 200 m, the density of houses within a radius of 50 m, type of dominant vegetation within 50 m of the case, or control houses. The quality of the housing structures including features such as wall materials, presence or absence of eaves and ceiling as well as mosquito exposure behaviour were assessed.

All participants recruited in the original study from Msambweni site in Kwale County were included in this study. No new power calculation was performed.

Data analysis methods

Ecological, environmental, and household characteristics data were collected through interviewing the household head and observation recorded in the dengue, malaria, and chikungunya study database. Household assets (TV, livestock, telephone, radio, bicycle, motor vehicle) and housing structure variables were used to create a household wealth index using the principal component analysis (PCA) method and categorized into tertiles.

Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation (sd)) or median (Interquartile Range (IQR)) depending on underlying distribution. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies with their respective percentages. Children diagnosed with malaria at any clinic visit were considered to have malaria and included in the risk of malaria regression analysis. A child was considered to have recurrent malaria if they had at least two episodes of malaria. Children with recurrence malaria during follow-up were reported as proportion of all the children in the study and the respective 95% binomial exact confidence interval. To identify demographic and ecological factors associated with malaria and recurrent malaria, binomial regression using generalized linear models with binomial probability distribution and log link function was performed. Univariate regression models were run for each independent variable. A backward stepwise approach was used to retain only variables with a P < 0.1 in the multivariable model [55]. Regression coefficients transformed into Risk Ratios (RR) were reported from the generalized linear models.

In the case–control study, medians were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test and categorical data were compared using chi-square test of association between cases and controls. To identify factors associated with being a case, binary logistic regression model was used. In the univariate analysis, each independent variable was included in the model separately and reported as crude Odds Ratios (COR) and their 95% confidence intervals. To perform multivariable regression analysis, all the independent variables were included and used a backward stepwise approach to retain only variables with a P < 0.1 but reported variables with P < 0.05 in the final model. The area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to measure the performance of the final multivariable regression model [56]. Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 and/or 95% confidence interval. Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA Version 17.0 [57].

The spatial pattern of malarial fevers was assessed using a hotspot analysis based on the Getis-Ord (Gi*) local spatial clustering test [58]. The results of the Gi* local spatial test were used to identify hotspot and cold spot villages. Hotspots were those villages with a significant higher number of malarial fevers compared to other villages. Cold spots were those villages with a significant lower number of malarial fevers compared to other villages. The significance of the computed Gi* was estimated by comparing observed values to the random case distribution (null hypothesis) by randomly re-assigning the cases. The statistical significance calculation was based on 100,000 Monte Carlo randomizations (p < 0.05, with Bonferroni correction). GeoDa [59] and QGIS [60] were used to perform the spatial analysis.

Results

Study participant characteristics

The characteristics of The 2371 child participants and those who tested positive for malaria are shown in Table 1. Overall, 252/2371 (11%), 1183/2371 (50%) and 936/2371 (39%) were aged < 2, 2‒5 and ≥ 5 years, respectively. The majority of the mothers of the participating children had attained primary education and higher. The ratio of male: female participants was 52:48. The children lived in relatively large houses (≥ 4 rooms) with floors and roofs made of mainly dirt and natural materials respectively. Only a small proportion of the houses did not have windows, and the majority of the windows present were screened. The most common source of drinking water was a public well or river or water harvested from rain. Kerosene powered lanterns were the most popular sources of lighting.

Malaria occurrence

During the 5-years of the study, 2371 child participants aged 1‒18 years visited the hospital (MCRH) and were enrolled in the study. Overall, 2610 acute febrile illness visits were made by The 2371 children. Of The 2610 visits, 40.6% (1059/2610) had malaria parasitaemia. The majority of the participants (83%, 2169/2371) visited the hospital once; 174 children visited the hospital twice, 19 visited the hospital thrice and nine visited the hospital four times. Malaria parasites were detected in 39.8% (864/2169) children who visited the hospital once. Of the 174 children who visited the hospital twice with febrile illness, 25% (44/174) had malaria on both visits, 35% (60/174) had malaria on one of the visits and 40% (70/174) were negative for malaria on both visits. Of the 19 children who visited the hospital thrice with febrile illness, 15.8% (3/19) had malaria on all three visits, 68.4% (13/19) had malaria on one or two of the visits and 15.8% (3/19) were negative for malaria on all three visits. One out of the 9 who visited four times with febrile illness was negative for malaria parasites on all the 4 visits, while the rest had malaria parasites on at least one of the visits. Overall, a malarial episode was detected in 992/2371 (41.8%, 95%CI 39.8‒43.9%) of the study participants and during 1059/2610 (40.6%, 95%CI 38.7–42.5%) of the visits.

Risk of malaria

In the univariate models examining overall risk of malaria, older age was associated with higher risk of malaria (2‒5 Vs < 2 years: CRR 1.38 (95%CI 1.13‒1.67), ≥ 5 Vs < 2 years: CRR 1.42 (95%CI 1.16‒1.73)). The type of house roof (CRR 1.69 (95%CI 1.49‒1.90)), number of rooms in the house and number of people in the house were positively associated with a malaria episode during the study duration. Higher mother education compared with none, were negatively associated with malaria (primary education CRR 0.84 (95%CI 0.75‒0.94) and secondary education CRR 0.46 (95%CI 0.37‒0.58)). Other variables negatively associated with overall risk of malaria were type of latrine (pit and flush versus none/bush), type of house floor (cement/tiles versus dirty/wood), source of drinking water (piped/well inside the compound), source of house lighting (electricity/solar) and number of windows (≥ 3 versus < 3) Table 2.

In the multivariable model, older children were associated with higher risk of malaria (2‒5 Vs < 2 years: aRR 1.38 (95%CI 1.09‒1.75), ≥ 5 Vs < 2 years: aRR 1.57 (95%CI 1.23‒2.00)). Mother education (secondary and above aRR 0.66 (95%CI 0.50‒0.87)), piped/well inside the compound source of water (aRR 0.78 (95%CI 0.65‒0.94)) and electricity/solar source of house lighting (aRR 0.61 (95%CI 0.50‒0.74)) were negatively associated with malaria at the time of study recruitment (Table 2).

Malaria recurrence

A total of 202/2371 (8.5%) child participants had at least one other occurrence of acute febrile illness documented by a repeat clinic visit after recruitment. Of The 2371 children, 60 (2.5%, 95%CI 1.9‒3.2) had recurrence of malaria; 51, 8 and 1 children had one, two and three recurrent malaria episodes, respectively.

In the univariate models of recurrent malaria, age and sex were not significantly associated with recurrence malaria. Maternal education (secondary education Vs none CRR 0.31 (95%CI 0.09‒0.99)), type of latrine (pit latrine), type of house floor (cement/tiles Vs dirt/wood CRR 0.46 (95% CI 0.25‒0.85)) and source of house lighting were negatively associated with recurrent malaria.

However, in the multivariable model most of the effect in the univariate models attenuated. Only type of house floor (cement/tiles Vs dirt/wood aRR 0.46 (95%CI 0.23‒0.91)) was associated with recurrent malaria (Table 2).

Spatial patterns of malarial fevers

Hotspots of fevers and malarial fevers overlapped, but almost all malarial fevers were far from the coastline and highly proximate to potential breeding sites and rice fields. Demographic and ecological characteristics of the sub-study participants

A total of 66 heads of households (HOH) participated in case–control interviews to collect data on demographic characteristic and in direct observations of ecological characteristics. Of these households, 33 (50%) were cases and 33 (50%) controls. The median age [IQR] of the HOH was 37 [29 to 45] years for cases and 36 [31 to 45] years for controls respectively. There were 26 (79%) and 31 (94%) females among cases and controls, respectively. The majority of the interviewed HOH had primary level education; 19 (58%) and 22 (67%) among the cases and controls, respectively. All HOH participants were living in their own houses. A summary of the demographic and ecological characteristics studied in the case–control arm of this study is presented in Table 3. Discussion

Malaria risk factors continually change depending on transmission dynamics, human behavior, and mosquito vector behavior changes in the face of ongoing interventions. The study examined sociodemographic and ecological factors associated malaria infection among children aged 1 to 18 years in Msambweni Sub-County, Kenya. Malaria was associated with increasing age of the child, lower education level of the mother/guardian, and poverty. Children from poor households with poor quality houses i.e. dirt/earth floors and with no or very few windows were also at higher risk of malaria infection. In addition, recurrent malaria risk was associated only with poor quality housing specifically dirt/earth floor and location of the house proximate to breeding sites (rice fields). Enhancing water and sanitation infrastructure, housing quality, improved education, and overall investments in improving socioeconomic development is suggested as part of the wider strategy to control malaria [12, 61].

Close proximity to a rice field was positively associated with recurrent malaria confirming previous observations in this study area [40]. Villagers in the study area practice rainfed rice irrigation where the rice fields are flooded during rainy season and remain flooded for several months. The rice fields act as important sources of malaria vectors for the neighbouring villages for the 3–4 months when they are flooded [62]. A recent review reported a two-fold increase in malaria risk because of a six-fold exposure risk to malaria vectors in rice growing villages compared non-rice growing [63]. The importance of rice fields as a malaria risk factor is growing as the intensity and burden of malaria decreases [63]. Larviciding using either chemical or bacterial larvicides is effective but labour- and cost-intensive [63]. Targeted mosquito control, particularly near rice fields, and the use of insecticide-treated nets should be intensified.

Numerous recent studies have demonstrated that housing improvements are highly protective against malaria [19, 22, 64–66]. In this study, having ‘improved floor’ or floor made of cement/tiles was both negatively associated with malaria episode and recurrent malaria. Often low-quality houses have floors made of ‘dirt’ or ‘earth’ [67–69]. While the connection between floor type and malaria risk is unclear, it is anticipated that dirt/earth floors provide suitable indoor resting environment for malaria vectors [69]. In general, improved housing minimizes mosquito vector entry into the houses and thus reducing mosquito-human contact [65]. Malaria risk was lower in houses with ≥ 3 windows though the associations were not significant. Many windows in a house are likely to increase airflow in and out thus improving thermal comfort and in turn may enhance compliance with use of insecticide treated nets (ITNs) [64, 70].

Experimental studies have further indicated that increased ventilation dilutes the carbon dioxide concentration indoors, therefore making the houses less attractive to mosquito and hence significantly reducing mosquito entry [71, 72]. These results reinforce the importance of improved housing in supplementing the existing key malaria control interventions (ITNs and IRS). Modest house improvements such as window screening, repair of holes on walls, door covers, closing or screening of eaves, and better roofs [17] are recommended in the study area to ameliorate existing malaria control efforts and will also likely protect against other vector-borne diseases [73].

Malaria infections were observed to increase with increasing age in agreement with previous studies in the study area [40, 74] reporting increasing malaria from 0 to 20 years, followed by a sharp decline [40]. Within the under-fives, lower malaria risk was observed in children below 2 years compared those 2‒5 years. Younger children have minimal exposure to night mosquito bites because they are likely to sleep under a bed net on their mother’s bed [75–77]. The age-related malaria risk shift observed towards school-going children (≥ 5 years) presents the need for redesigning of the current interventions to ensure that all ‘high risk’ age-groups are targeted without compromising the focus to protect relatively naïve under-fives group.

In this study, using piped or having a well inside the compound were significantly associated with reduced risk of Plasmodium falciparum malaria. A meta-analysis study involving data from 23 sub-Saharan African countries reported significantly reduced malaria risk in households using piped water when compared with those using both protected and unprotected water sources [24]. Surface water and public well sources have been associated with increased risk of malaria in studies in Ethiopia [78] and Tanzania [79]. The connection between surface water and increased malaria risk is not straight forward. Surface water often creates pools that can potentially become larval habitats for malaria vectors [80, 81]. The authors speculate that the households using surface water are located proximate to the larval habitats created by surface waters and thus have higher probability of mosquito bite exposure. The association between public wells and increased risk of malaria is also not clear. Water storage in households is a common practice in the study area as it would be expected in communities with unreliable water supply. However, water storage was ruled out as explanation for increased risk of malaria in houses using public wells. This is because previous studies in the study area rarely reported immature stages of malaria vectors in stored water [82]. Some water usually drips on the ground after every public well user and eventually forms pools around the public well that can act as larval habitats. Clearly, further research is required to understand the nexus between increased malaria risk and source of domestic water.

In this study socioeconomic status as estimated from household assets did not show associations with occurrence of P. falciparum malaria. However, other predictors that are indirectly related to socioeconomic status; source of drinking water and source of lighting resulted in significant associations with malaria occurrence. Households with no sanitation facilities and those using pit latrines reported higher malaria episodes compared to those using flush toilets although these differences were not significant. Participants with access to electricity as source of lighting, protected source of drinking and flush toilet are considered to have high socioeconomic status. A low malaria risk was clearly demonstrated with high socioeconomic status. These results are consistent with findings from other studies across Sub-Saharan Africa where it is postulated that people with relatively higher SES are more likely to access and utilize available malaria prevention and control services [24]. Systematic review and meta-analysis results strongly propose investments in socioeconomic development as a malaria intervention [12, 61]. Improvements in SES indicators such education, quality of housing, employment opportunities, income security among others affords individuals ability to access malaria preventive measures. These findings amplify this growing line of thought that investments in socioeconomic development should be considered as an overarching malaria intervention.

Use of electricity as the source of lighting was associated with decreased risk of malaria in this study. Available literature associated use of electricity with increased malaria risk [23, 83, 84], with suggestions that there is increased exposure to mosquito bites because mosquitoes are generally attracted to light [85]. Other than a recent study in Tanzania that showed low exposure to mosquito bites in houses using electricity compared to nonelectrified houses [10], there likely are no studies that had previously observed reduced malaria risk with electricity use. High socioeconomic status is generally associated low risk of malaria and access to electricity can be considered as a proxy for a higher economic status in most rural communities [12, 61].

Education level of the mother is another SES indicator that is widely associated with reduced malaria risk in children under 5 years of age [86, 87]. In the current study children whose mothers had secondary and above level of education were strongly associated with lower malaria risk compared to illiterate mothers or those with primary education. Educated mothers are more knowledgeable about malaria prevention and signs and thus more likely to use malaria preventive measures such as ITNs and seek health care services early [77, 86, 87]. Additionally, educated mothers are more likely to have economic and considerable decision-making autonomy thus able to swiftly deal with health issues affecting their young children [87].

Some limitations were identified in this study. First, detection of parasitaemia was by microscopy. Microscopy, though considered the “gold standard” of malaria diagnosis is variably known to miss a substantial proportion of microscopic parasitaemia [88, 89]. The study, therefore, may have failed to identify key risk factors due to the under detection of children with microscopic parasitaemia. Despite the limitations of microscopy in detecting low levels of parasitaemia, the results remain valid as they were obtained using standardized methods and interpreted by trained personnel. Additionally, complementary diagnostic approaches can help confirm findings, ensuring reliability and accuracy. Secondly, there is a possibility of a certain degree of misclassification of cases and controls in the sub study. This misclassification might affect the identification of risk factor for recurrent malaria. Finally, changes in demographic profiles of respondents and ecological factors between the time of clinic visits and the time the case–control sub study was conducted may also have influenced identification of the risk factors. The original study was not powered to examine factors associated with recurrence malaria. It is likely this analysis was underpowered.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study findings suggest that intensified interventions including larval source management targeting rice paddies, modest house improvements and targeting school-going children will result in significant declines in malaria in the study area. To support ongoing malaria interventions, enhancing water and sanitation infrastructure, housing quality, improved education, and overall investments in improving socioeconomic development is proposed as part of the wider strategy to control malaria.
