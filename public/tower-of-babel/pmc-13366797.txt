BIRC6 prevents GSDME-mediated pyroptosis and promotes cisplatin resistance via a non-canonical UBC domain-dependent steric hindrance
Authors: Ting Zhang, Miao Chen, Peng-Wei Luo, Hui-Juan Hou, Xin Wang, Yi-Fan Ma, Teng Liu, Shi-Hua Deng, Dong-Ming Wu, Ying Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyroptosis, particularly gasdermin E (GSDME)-mediated programmed cell death, has emerged as a crucial determinant of chemosensitivity and antitumor immunity. Although baculoviral inhibitor of apoptosis protein (IAP) repeat-containing protein 6 (BIRC6) is a known inhibitor of apoptosis, its potential role in regulating the pyroptotic switch and cisplatin resistance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains unclear. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying BIRC6-mediated regulation of pyroptosis and cisplatin resistance in LUAD.MethodsWe integrated bioinformatics analysis, proteomics, and structural modeling with CRISPR-Cas9-mediated mutagenesis to dissect the interplay between BIRC6 and pyroptosis. Subsequently, we functionally validated the interaction between BIRC6 and pyroptosis using cisplatin-resistant LUAD cell lines and xenograft models.ResultsWe observed that BIRC6 was aberrantly upregulated in cisplatin-resistant LUAD tissues and correlated with poor clinical prognosis. Functionally, while BIRC6 knockdown resensitized resistant LUAD cells to cisplatin by restoring GSDME-dependent pyroptosis, its overexpression in sensitive cells stifled this pathway to confer resistance. Mechanistically, rather than promoting ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation, BIRC6 physically interacted with the N-terminal pore-forming domain of GSDME via its ubiquitin-conjugating domain. Structural analysis revealed that this interaction created steric hindrance that masked the specific caspase-3 cleavage site on GSDME, thereby preventing the occurrence of pyroptosis. Disruption of the BIRC6-GSDME interface effectively restored pyroptosis and triggered robust inflammatory cell death, which subsequently enhanced cisplatin efficacy in vivo.ConclusionOur findings uncovered a non-canonical mechanism wherein BIRC6 structurally locks GSDME in an inactive state to confer cisplatin resistance. Consequently, targeting the BIRC6-GSDME interface offers a novel therapeutic strategy to trigger pyroptosis and overcome chemotherapy resistance in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02952-7.Keywords: BIRC6, Cisplatin resistance, GSDME, Lung adenocarcinoma, Pyroptosis

Background

According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) constituting the most prevalent histological subtype [1, 2]. Despite significant advancements in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, cisplatin-based chemotherapy remains the cornerstone treatment for a substantial proportion of patients, particularly those lacking actionable driver mutations or those progressing to advanced stages even after targeted interventions [3, 4]. However, cisplatin efficacy is severely limited by the development of acquired resistance, which occurs in approximately 70% of initially responsive patients and results in poor clinical outcomes [5–7]. Although classical resistance mechanisms—such as enhanced DNA repair [8, 9], cell cycle dysregulation [10, 11], and drug efflux pumps [12]—have been extensively characterized, recent paradigms suggest that the failure of chemotherapy lies in the dysregulation of terminal cell death execution pathways [13–16].

Pyroptosis, a pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death, has emerged as a critical determinant of chemotherapeutic sensitivity [17]. Unlike apoptosis, which is immunologically silent, pyroptosis is characterized by plasma membrane rupture and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [18, 19]. This process triggers immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby activating a robust antitumor immune response within the tumor microenvironment [20, 21]. In non-phagocytic cancer cells, pyroptosis is primarily executed by gasdermin E (GSDME). Under chemotherapeutic stress (e.g., cisplatin treatment), activated caspase-3 cleaves GSDME to release its cytotoxic N-terminal domain (GSDME-N), which perforates the cell membrane [22–24]. Consequently, high GSDME expression is often correlated with better response to chemotherapy and immunotherapies [25, 26]. Conversely, silencing or inactivation of the GSDME pathway represents a potent mechanism of drug resistance, allowing tumor cells to evade immune surveillance and survive cytotoxic insults [27, 28]. However, the precise molecular mechanism underlying post-translational suppression of GSDME activation in tumor cells remains poorly understood.

Baculoviral inhibitor of apoptosis protein (IAP) repeat-containing protein 6 (BIRC6), a member of the IAP family, is a multidomain protein featuring an N-terminal baculoviral IAP repeat (BIR) domain that modulates apoptosis and a C-terminal ubiquitin-conjugating (UBC) domain that regulates ubiquitination [29, 30]. This unique dual-domain architecture makes BIRC6 a specialized regulator of cell death pathways. Structurally, BIRC6 forms a megadalton-scale complex that creates a central cavity for substrate binding, acting as a stress-responsive hub [31]. BIRC6 antagonizes apoptosis by directly inhibiting caspases or promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of pro-apoptotic factors, such as caspase-3, -7, and second mitochondria-derived activator of caspases (SMAC) [32, 33]. Given that caspase-3 is the molecular switch that cleaves GSDME to initiate pyroptosis, BIRC6 is theoretically positioned to regulate the transition between apoptosis and pyroptosis. However, previous literature was predominantly focused on the BIRC6-Caspase axis. Therefore, whether BIRC6 can bypass the upstream enzymes and directly interact with GSDME to physically block pyroptotic execution remains unknown.

Notably, BIRC6 has been implicated in the progression of various malignancies, including prostate and lung cancers, where its overexpression correlates with poor prognosis [34, 35]. Nevertheless, its specific role in cisplatin resistance, via the modulation of pyroptosis, remains unknown. Therefore, we hypothesized that BIRC6 functions as a direct checkpoint for GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in LUAD. Accordingly, in this study, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which BIRC6-mediated modulation of GSDME-dependent pyroptosis influences cisplatin resistance in LUAD and to evaluate its potential clinical implications using integrated proteomic and structural biology approaches. Our findings will provide new insights into identifying promising therapeutic targets to overcome cisplatin resistance in LUAD.

Methods

Cell culture and establishment

Human LUAD cell lines (A549 and H1650), human A549 cisplatin-resistant cells (A549/CR), and normal human lung epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) were purchased from ProCell Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). All cell lines used in this study were verified using short tandem repeat DNA profiling. The H1650 cell line was maintained by routine passaging every 3–4 days. Exponentially growing H1650 cells were initially exposed to 5 µM cisplatin for 24 h to develop cisplatin resistance. Subsequently, the drug-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium to allow recovery. After the cells resumed normal proliferation, they were passaged and retreated with 5 µM cisplatin for another 24 h. This dual-exposure protocol at each concentration was repeated while progressively escalating cisplatin concentrations in a stepwise manner up to 80 µM. Consequently, a stable cisplatin-resistant H1650 subline (H1650/CR) was successfully established through repeated cycles of medium replacement and passaging under selective pressure.

All cell lines, except A549/CR and H1650/CR cells, were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. A549/CR cells were maintained in a medium containing 3.3 µM cisplatin according to the manufacturer’s instructions, whereas H1650/CR cells were maintained in medium containing 5 µM cisplatin. The exposure duration was consistently set to 24 h for all subsequent in vitro functional assays involving cisplatin treatment. All media and supplements were purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Cell viability assays

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 10⁴ cells per well) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. After treatment with or without cisplatin, cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Total RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from cells using a Total RNA Extraction Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and RNA reverse transcription was performed using an iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [36]. Subsequently, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) amplification was performed using a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System using SYBR Green Premix (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The relative gene expression level was calculated by normalizing the transcript level to the housekeeping gene β-actin using the comparative threshold cycle number 2-ΔΔCq method. The primers used in this experiment are as follows: BIRC6 forward, 5′-CAGCAGCTCTTATCAGCATGT-3′ and reverse, 5′-AACTGTGGCCCACTTAGCAAC-3′; GSDME forward, 5′-ATGTTTGCCAAAGCAACCAGGA-3′ and reverse, 5′-TCATGAATGTTCTCTGCCTAAAGCACA-3′; β-actin forward, 5′-CCTGGCACCCAGCACAAT-3′ and reverse, 5′-GGGCCGGACTCGTCATAC-3.’

Western blotting

The samples for western blotting were prepared as previously described [36]. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-Gsdme + Gsdme-N (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab222408, 1:1,000), anti-Birc6 (Bethyl, Montgomery, TX, USA; A300-367 A, 1:5,000), anti-ProCaspase3 + CleavageCaspase3 (Abcam, ab13847,1:500), anti-interleukin (IL)-1β (Abcam, ab283818,1:1000), anti-Vinculin (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA; 66305-1-Ig,1:5,000), and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, 1:20,000), at 4 °C overnight. The following day, membranes were incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (SA 00001–1 (mouse); SA 00001–2 (rabbit); 1:10,000; all from Proteintech Group, Inc.) at 25 °C for 2 h. The membranes were then visualized and analyzed using the Quantity One 5.2 software system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) in combination with ECL detection reagents (EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA).

Scanning electron microscopy

LUAD cells were placed on coverslips, allowed to adhere, and gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The PBS was discarded, and 3% glutaraldehyde was added to the cells. The samples were then washed three times with ultrapure water for 10 min each. After fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1–2 h, the cells were washed three times with ultrapure water for 10 min each. The cells were then dehydrated using an alcohol gradient with concentrations of 30–50%, 70%, 90%, and 100% (100% concentration was changed three times) for 15 min each time. The coverslips were dried in a critical point dryer, glued to the sample stages with a conductive adhesive, and gold sputtered in an ion sputter. Images were captured using a JSM-IT700HR scanning electron microscope (SEM; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The overall view was first observed at low magnification for each sample, and the selected areas were subsequently imaged to observe specific lesions.

Flow cytometry

Pyroptosis was measured by flow cytometry using the Annexin V-PE/7-AAD Detection Kit (KeyGEN, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the samples were assessed using a flow cytometer (FACSCalibur; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and the data were processed using flow cytometry software.

Lactate dehydrogenase release assay

LUAD cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated overnight in a cell incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released in the cell supernatant, and LDH released in the mouse serum from eyeballs. All measurements were performed with the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The levels of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in cell-culture supernatants and mouse ocular serum, as well as high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in mouse ocular serum, were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Mlbio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, VD, USA). Standard curves were generated using reference standards to determine target concentrations.

Vector construction and cell transfection

Three specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting human BIRC6 (TargetSeq: shRNA1: CTGCTGTGGATAAAGTTATAT; shRNA2: ACAGCTCAAAGATGGTTTAAA; and shRNA3: TTTATTAGCTGGAGGTTTATT) and a negative control shRNA (TargetSeq: TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT) were designed and cloned into a pGMLV-SC5 RNAi lentiviral vector (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) to silence the BIRC6 gene. Lentiviral particles were then produced and transduced into target cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol to establish stable BIRC6-knockdown cell lines. Similarly, three shRNAs targeting human GSDME (TargetSeq: shRNA1: GATGATGGAGTATCTGATCTT; shRNA2: GGATTGTGCAGCGCTTGTTTG; and shRNA3: GCTTTAGGCAGAGAACATTCA) and a negative control shRNA (TargetSeq: TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT) were designed and cloned into a pGMLV-SC5 RNAi lentiviral vector (Genomeditech) to silence the GSDME gene. Lentiviral particles were then produced and transfected into target cells, according to the manufacturer’s protocol, to establish stable GSDME-knockdown cell lines.

Stable BIRC6-overexpressing cell lines A549-BIRC6 and H1650-BIRC6 were then generated using the dCas9-SAM system. Lentiviruses expressing dCas9-VP64, MS2-P65-HSF1, and gRNA were separately constructed by Genomeditech. The gRNAs for BIRC6 overexpression were designed to target the following sequences: H_BIRC6 sgRNA1-SAM-Puro, CCAGGCTCACAGGAAGAAAC; H_BIRC6 sgRNA2-SAM-Puro, CCGCCACTCCCCAGGCTCAC; H_BIRC6 sgRNA3-SAM-Puro, GAACCGCTGCAAAAGGCCTC. We also established control cell lines A549-Vector and H1650-Vector by transfecting the parental cells with the dCas9-SAM system. The gRNA in this system was designed to target the sequence ACGGAGGCTAAGCGTCGCAA.

CRISPR/Cas9 interference for BIRC6 mutation

The BIRC6 mutation cell line was generated by As-Crispr Bioscience (Shenzhen, China) using the CRISPR/Cas9 Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) system. Briefly, two optimized guide RNAs targeting Exon 71 of the human BIRC6 gene (gRNA-A1: 5’-CAACACTCAAAGGTTTGAGGCGG-3’ and gRNA-A2: 5’-TCAAGAAACACAGGTAACGGTGG-3’) were designed and synthesized. The Cas9 protein and gRNAs were incubated to form RNP complexes and subsequently electroporated into A549/DDP cells using the Neon Transfection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Following electroporation, cells were seeded into 96-well plates for single-colony isolation. To verify the knockout efficiency, genomic DNA was extracted from the expanded single colonies, and the target region was amplified by PCR (Forward primer: 5’-TGTTTGCGTGAGGCTAGATTTG-3’; Reverse primer: 5’-GTAAAGACTTTTATTCACAGGCCAA-3’). The PCR products were then subjected to Sanger sequencing. Clones identified with homozygous deletion mutations in the BIRC6 gene were selected for subsequent functional assays.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer and treated with primary antibodies with gentle stirring at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, protein A/G agarose (Beyotime) beads were used for co-immunoprecipitation according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The samples were then separated by 12% and 6% SDS-PAGE for Western blot detection.

Confocal microscopy

Cells were fixed in pre-cooled 4% formaldehyde for 10 min and washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 5% goat serum albumin for 40 min, followed by incubation with GSDME (1:200) and BIRC6 (1:200) primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:500) thereafter. Finally, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used as a mounting medium to stain the cell nuclei. Images were captured using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Mouse xenograft model

This study was performed in strict accordance with the Regulations for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Guideline for Ethical Review of Animal (China, GB/T 35892 − 2018). All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Use Committee of the Chengdu Medical College (approval number CMC-IACUC-2021024). Female BALB/c nude mice (4–5 weeks of age, 14–16 g) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) and housed in a constant temperature and humidity environment (temperature 24 ± 2°C, humidity 50% ± 10%) under a 12-h light/ dark cycle. The mice were acclimatized to the environment for at least 7 days before the experiment and had free access to food and water. Thereafter, 2 × 10⁶ cells (A549, A549/CR, and A549/CRBIRC6−MT) were injected into the right axilla of nude mice, and cisplatin was administered when the subcutaneous tumor volume reached 100–150 mm³. Mice with established tumors were then randomized into control and treatment groups, which were injected with PBS and 0.8 mg/kg cisplatin once a week, respectively. Tumor growth was measured every seven days using vernier calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V=(a×b2)/2, where a and b are the maximum and minimum diameters (mm), respectively. After 28 days, the mice were deeply anesthetized by a single intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium 50 mg/kg body weight (WKQ-0028128; Vicky Biotechnology Chengdu, China) for at least 10 min, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure humane euthanasia. Subcutaneous tumors were collected for final volume and weight measurement and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, and eyeball blood was extracted to evaluate the serum levels of inflammatory factors.

Immunohistochemistry

A human LUAD tissue microarray (Cat. No.: TFLungade-01) was purchased from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd. Tissue microarray sections were dewaxed and subjected to high-pressure antigen retrieval, and IHC staining was performed as described previously [37]. Subsequently, BIRC6 expression was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system incorporating staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells for the intensity score (0: negative, 1: weak/light yellow, 2: moderate/yellow-brown, or 3: strong/brown) and extent score (0: < 5%, 1:5–25%, 2: 25–50%, 3: 51–75%, or 4: > 75% positive cells). The final immunoreactivity score was calculated by multiplying the intensity and extent scores (range: 0–12), with scores < 2 and > 2 classified as low and high expression, respectively. All slides were independently evaluated by two blinded pathologists with excellent interobserver agreement.

Additionally, subcutaneous tumor sections were washed with PBS, dehydrated in an alcohol gradient, and dewaxed in xylene before blocking with an antifade medium. The sections were imaged using an Olympus XI71 microscope (Tokyo, Japan) and images were processed using the DPC controller software (Olympus). All images were acquired under standardized conditions and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software (US NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence

Tissue sections were washed with PBS, dehydrated, and dewaxed. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling the samples in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), followed by blocking with 5% goat serum at 37 °C for 1 h. The sections were then incubated with BIRC6 (1:200), GSDME (1:200) or calreticulin (Proteintech, 27298-1-AP, 1:200) primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight in a humid box. On the following day, the slides were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h, and stained with DAPI (1:5,000, Solarbio Co., Ltd., China) for 5 min. Finally, the sections were washed with PBS and treated with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium. Fluorescent images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). In vitro experiments were performed in triplicate with independent biological replicates. All animals were randomly allocated to the experimental groups. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality, with normally distributed data expressed as means ± standard errors of the mean. Parametric Student’s t-tests (two-tailed, unpaired) were used in case of equal variances, whereas Welch’s corrected t-tests were used for unequal variances, for comparisons between two groups of normally distributed data. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

BIRC6 overexpression correlates with cisplatin resistance and poor prognosis in LUAD

Bioinformatics analysis of RNA sequencing data (GSE157692) revealed that BIRC6 was a significantly upregulated in cisplatin-resistant LUAD cells compared to that in sensitive controls (Fig. 1A). This finding was experimentally corroborated in our established cisplatin-resistant cell lines (A549/CR and H1650/CR), which exhibited markedly elevated BIRC6 protein and mRNA levels compared to their parental counterparts (Fig. 1B, C). Subsequently, we analyzed a human LUAD tissue microarray (TFLungade-01) to validate the clinical relevance of this finding. IHC staining revealed that BIRC6 expression was significantly upregulated in cisplatin-resistant tumor tissues compared to that in primary tissues and was positively correlated with advanced tumor stage (Fig. 1D). Chi-square analysis revealed significant associations between elevated BIRC6 expression and advanced clinical stage (P = 0.013) and post-chemotherapy recurrence (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further confirmed BIRC6 as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival (OS) (Table 2). Additionally, high BIRC6 expression was associated with significantly worse OS (log-rank P < 0.0001; Fig. 1E). This prognostic correlation was further validated in a GEO dataset (GSE50081), where high BIRC6 expression predicted poor clinical outcomes (log-rank P < 0.0001; Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings suggest that BIRC6 is a critical determinant of cisplatin resistance and a prognostic biomarker in LUAD.

BIRC6-high cisplatin-resistant LUAD cells display minimal GSDME-mediated pyroptosis

Initial characterization of our cell models confirmed a robust chemoresistant phenotype. A549/CR and H1650/CR cells exhibited significantly higher cisplatin IC50 values (146 µM and 63 µM, respectively) than the parental cells (8 µM and 5 µM), yielding resistance indices > 10 (Figure S1A, E). Therefore, baseline cisplatin doses of 8 µM and 5 µM were employed in subsequent related assays. Morphologically, cisplatin treatment induced characteristic pyroptotic features in sensitive parental cells, including cell swelling and large plasma membrane bubbles. In contrast, BIRC6-high resistance cells maintained membrane integrity (Fig. 2A), a phenotype confirmed using scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 2B).

Mechanistically, sensitive and resistant LUAD cell-lines expressed comparable levels of GSDME (Figure S2); however, their response to cisplatin diverged significantly. Cisplatin treatment triggered robust caspase-3 activation and GSDME-N generation in sensitive cells, leading to the increase of Annexin V-PE /7-AAD double-positivity and LDH release. Conversely, these pyroptotic hallmarks were barely observable in resistant LUAD cells (Fig. 2C–G). Furthermore, consistent with the inflammatory nature of pyroptosis, cisplatin treatment induced the secretion of IL-1β in sensitive cells but not in resistant cells (Fig. 2H). These data suggest that cisplatin resistance in LUAD may be driven by a blockade of GSDME-mediated pyroptosis and its associated immunogenic signaling.

BIRC6 knockdown restores cisplatin sensitivity by unleashing pyroptosis

We established stable BIRC6-knockdown clones (shBIRC6#1 and shBIRC6#2) in A549/CR and H1650/CR cells to evaluate the functional impact of BIRC6 and successfully verified the knockdown efficiency by RT-qPCR and western blotting (Fig. 3A, B). BIRC6 depletion drastically reversed cisplatin resistance, reducing the IC50 values from 144 µM to 12 µM and 9 µM in A549/CR cells, and from 60 µM to 10 µM and 7 µM in H1650/CR cells (Fig. S1B). Therefore, 12 µM and 10 µM of cisplatin were utilized as the baseline doses in all subsequent experiments. Upon cisplatin exposure, BIRC6-knockdown cells re-acquired sensitivity, exhibiting typical pyroptotic morphology characterized by membrane pores observed under a SEM (Fig. 3C, D). Functionally, BIRC6 knockdown significantly increased the population of Annexin V-PE /7-AAD double-positive cells and LDH release following cisplatin treatment (Fig. 3E-G). Molecularly, BIRC6 depletion restored caspase-3 activation and GSDME cleavage (Fig. 3H, I), concurrently reinstating IL-1β secretion (Fig. 3J). These results demonstrate that BIRC6 is a functional barrier to pyroptosis, and its removal resensitizes resistant LUAD cells to cisplatin-induced death.

BIRC6 overexpression drives cisplatin resistance by actively suppressing pyroptosis

Subsequently, we established stable BIRC6-overexpressing A549 and H1650 cell lines to further verify our previous findings and rigorously validate the role of BIRC6 as a negative regulator of pyroptosis. Successful BIRC6 overexpression was confirmed via RT-qPCR and western blotting (Figure S3A, B), with the BIRC6-1 clone selected for subsequent assays. BIRC6-overexpressing cells exhibited substantially elevated cisplatin IC50 values from 9 µM to 53 µM in A549 cells, and from 7 µM to 30 µM in H1650 cells (Fig. S1C). Accordingly, the baseline sensitive doses of cisplatin (9 µM and 7 µM, respectively) were applied in the ensuing functional experiments.

Upon cisplatin treatment, BIRC6-overexpressing cells exhibited marked resistance to drug-induced cytotoxicity and completely lacked the characteristic pyroptotic morphology observed in Vector control cells (Figure S3C, D). Consistent with this phenotypic resistance, BIRC6 overexpression significantly attenuated the proportion of Annexin V-PE/7-AAD double-positive cells and the release of LDH (Figure S3E–G). At the molecular level, elevated BIRC6 expression effectively blunted caspase-3 activation and GSDME cleavage (Figure S3H, I), which concomitantly suppressed the secretion of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β (Figure S3J). Collectively, these gain-of-function results corroborate our previous data, demonstrating that BIRC6 upregulation inherently stifles pyroptosis, thereby driving the development of cisplatin resistance in LUAD cells.

BIRC6 physically masks the GSDME cleavage site via its UBC domain

Next, we investigated the molecular basis of pyroptosis suppression. Sequencing analysis ruled out genetic mutations in the GSDME coding region in resistant cells (Figure S4A), indicating a post-translational mechanism. Accordingly, the protein-protein interaction network analysis predicted a potential association between BIRC6 and GSDME (Figure S4B, C). Therefore, we commissioned Wecomput Technology Co., Ltd. to perform in silico structural docking using MODELLER and AMBER to define this interaction. The structural model revealed that the UBC domain (D4498−4820) of BIRC6 spatially engaged with GSDME, resulting in steric hindrance that specifically shielded the caspase-3 cleavage site (D267−270) of GSDME (Fig. 4A, B). This physical masking prevented the generation of the pore-forming GSDME-N fragment.

Subsequently, this model was validated by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, which detected a strong physical interaction between endogenous BIRC6 and GSDME, specifically in cisplatin-resistant cells, whereas binding was negligible in sensitive cells (Fig. 4C–F). Confocal microscopy further confirmed substantial colocalization of BIRC6 and GSDME in the cytoplasm of resistant cells (Fig. 4G–N). Collectively, these data demonstrate a novel mechanism wherein BIRC6 acts as a structural lock, directly binding GSDME to prevent its activation.

Targeted disruption of the BIRC6-GSDME interface reverses cisplatin resistance by reactivating GSDME-dependent pyroptosis

We utilized CRISPR-Cas9-mediated site-directed mutagenesis to disrupt the GSDME-binding interface within the BIRC6 UBC domain (exon 71, D4672–4697) in resistant cells (A549/CRBIRC6−MT) to confirm that the physical interaction via the UBC domain is causal (Fig. 5A). The successful establishment of the BIRC6-mutation cell line was validated by genomic PCR and Sanger sequencing (Figure S5). As predicted, the BIRC6 mutant failed to bind with GSDME in Co-IP assays (Fig. 5B, C) and showed a loss of colocalization (Fig. 5D). Additionally, the IC50 value significantly decreased from 140 µM in A549/CRBIRC6−WT cells to 9 µM in A549/CRBIRC6−MT cells; therefore, 9 µM of cisplatin was used for subsequent functional assays (Figure S1D).

We then divided the A549/CRBIRC6−MT cells into two functional arms using shRNAs to explicitly evaluate the restoration of pyroptosis and cisplatin sensitivity (Fig. 6A). First, in the control group (sh-NC), cisplatin treatment of A549/CRBIRC6−MT cells triggered robust caspase-3 activation and substantial GSDME cleavage (Fig. 6B). Consistent with these biochemical alterations, these cells exhibited typical pyroptotic morphology, with flow cytometry analysis revealing a marked increase in the population of Annexin V-PE/7-AAD double-positive cells (Fig. 6C, D). The release of LDH and the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β were also increased in the control group (Fig. 6E, F). This directly demonstrated that disrupting the BIRC6-GSDME interface successfully reactivated the pyroptotic pathway and sensitized the mutant cells to cisplatin.

Second, we established stable GSDME-knockdown clones (shGSDME) with a A549/CRBIRC6−MT background, as verified by RT-qPCR and western blotting, to confirm that this restored sensitivity was exclusively driven by GSDME (Fig. 6A). Notably, upon cisplatin treatment, silencing GSDME completely abolished the previously restored GSDME cleavage, the typical pyroptotic morphological, the population of Annexin V-PE/7-AAD double-positive cells, and the release of LDH and IL-1β (Fig. 6B–F). Thus, by effectively reversing the sensitizing effect of the BIRC6 mutation, these findings conclusively demonstrated that the UBC domain-mediated physical sequestration of GSDME is the primary mechanism underlying BIRC6-driven resistance.

BIRC6 mutation restores cisplatin efficacy and triggers antitumor inflammation in vivo

Finally, we established subcutaneous xenograft models using A549, A549/CRBIRC6−WT, and A549/CRBIRC6−MT cells to validate our in vitro findings (Fig. 7A). While parental A549 xenografts were sensitive to cisplatin, A549/CRBIRC6−WT tumors remained highly refractory. Notably, tumors harboring the interaction-deficient mutant (A549/CRBIRC6−MT) were resensitized to cisplatin, displaying marked tumor regression comparable to the parental control tumors (Fig. 7B–D). Evaluation of the tumor microenvironment revealed that this restored efficacy was intrinsically linked to pyroptosis-driven inflammation. Cisplatin treatment resulted in increased release of LDH and IL-1β in the A549 and A549/CRBIRC6−MT tumors, but not in the A549/CRBIRC6−WT resistant tumors (Fig. 7E, F). Additionally, IF demonstrated the abrogation of BIRC6-GSDME colocalization in the mutant A549/CRBIRC6−MT tumors compared to the strong interaction retained in the resistant A549/CRBIRC6−WT counterparts (Fig. 7G).

Furthermore, IHC analysis revealed that cisplatin reactivated Caspase-3 and triggered GSDME cleavage specifically in the A549/CRBIRC6−MT group. This was accompanied by a surge in the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β and the DAMP molecule HMGB1 (Fig. 8A). Given the crucial role of DAMPs in antitumor immunity, we evaluated the membrane exposure of calreticulin, a canonical marker of ICD essential for dendritic cell recruitment and antigen presentation. IF staining demonstrated robust surface calreticulin upregulation in the parental and mutant tumors (A549 and A549/CRBIRC6−MT) following cisplatin treatment, confirming the induction of ICD (Fig. 8B). Systemically, serum analysis revealed that effective tumor regression in the A549 and A549/CRBIRC6−MT groups was correlated with a significant release of immunogenic cytokines (HMGB1, IL-6, and TNF-α) (Fig. 8C–E).

Collectively, these in vivo data elegantly demonstrate that uncoupling the BIRC6-GSDME interface overcomes cisplatin resistance by reigniting pyroptosis. This process subsequently drives ICD, serving as a critical bridge between local pyroptotic cell death and systemic antitumor immunity.

Discussion

Cisplatin resistance remains a notable barrier to effective management of LUAD, with the majority of patients eventually succumbing to disease progression despite initial responses [2, 5]. Although classical resistance mechanisms—such as enhanced DNA repair and drug efflux—have been extensively mapped [8, 12], the failure of terminal cell death execution is increasingly recognized as a core driver of therapeutic failure [13–16]. In this study, we identified a novel structural mechanism of chemoresistance: the direct sequestration of GSDME by BIRC6. Our results demonstrated that BIRC6 does not merely inhibit upstream apoptotic signaling but actively shields GSDME from activation, thereby blocking the switch to pyroptosis and preventing the induction of antitumor immunity.

BIRC6 has been implicated as a critical regulator of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance in various malignancies, including prostate cancer, leukemia and ovarian cancer [34, 38, 39]. Consistent with these observations, our clinical analysis of LUAD cohorts revealed that BIRC6 was significantly upregulated in cisplatin-resistant tissues and served as an independent prognostic factor for poor OS and post-chemotherapy recurrence (Fig. 1, Table 1). These findings position BIRC6 as a high-value therapeutic target and a biomarker for stratifying patients who are unlikely to benefit from standard chemotherapy. However, unlike previous studies that attributed BIRC6 function primarily to its anti-apoptotic role via caspase inhibition [31, 40], our data suggest that in the context of cisplatin resistance, its regulation of pyroptosis is equally, if not more, critical.

The distinction between apoptosis and pyroptosis is fundamental to understanding chemotherapeutic efficacy and overcoming drug resistance. Historically, classical chemotherapeutics, including cisplatin, were thought to eliminate cancer cells primarily by inducing apoptosis. Apoptosis is an immunologically “silent” or tolerogenic process characterized by membrane blebbing, cell shrinkage, and the formation of apoptotic bodies, which are rapidly cleared by phagocytes without eliciting an inflammatory response [22]. In contrast, pyroptosis is a highly lytic and immunogenic form of programmed cell death. Mechanistically, in the presence of GSDME, activated caspase-3 pivots the cell death trajectory from traditional apoptosis to pyroptosis by cleaving GSDME to generate pore-forming N-terminal fragments [24]. This leads to rapid cellular swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and the massive release of DAMPs, such as HMGB1, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β [20, 21]. Consequently, while apoptosis often fails to stimulate the tumor microenvironment, pyroptosis effectively bridges local cell death to systemic immunity, turning immunologically “cold” tumors “hot” by recruiting immune effectors [20, 26].

In light of these distinct features, our study provides direct evidence that cisplatin-sensitive LUAD cells undergo robust GSDME-dependent pyroptosis, while BIRC6-high resistant cells remain refractory (Fig. 2). Notably, GSDME expression remained comparable between groups; rather, cisplatin resistance stemmed from a specific post-translational blockade of GSDME cleavage. Accordingly, targeted BIRC6 knockdown successfully dismantled this blockade, reactivating the pyroptotic machinery and immunogenic cytokine release to overcome resistance (Fig. 3). Conversely, BIRC6 overexpression in sensitive cells stifled pyroptosis and conferred resistance, firmly establishing BIRC6 as a critical barrier to pyroptosis and a key driver of chemoresistance.

The most significant finding of our study is the elucidation of the structural basis governing pyroptosis inhibition in LUAD (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). While the UBC domain of the giant E3 ligase BIRC6 is canonically known for catalyzing ubiquitination [29, 30], we uncovered a critical non-canonical, ubiquitination-independent function: it physically masks the specific caspase-3 cleavage site (D267−270) on the GSDME N-terminal domain. Our CRISPR-Cas9 mutagenesis assays confirmed that unlocking this specific physical interface is both necessary and sufficient to resensitize cells to pyroptosis. More importantly, this spatial overlap presents a unique structural constraint: mutating the BIRC6-binding site on GSDME itself is not a viable strategy to reverse resistance, as it would inherently destroy the overlapping caspase-3 cleavage motif and permanently disable pyroptotic execution [22, 24]. Therefore, this strict directional specificity highlights the BIRC6-GSDME interface—specifically by targeting the upstream BIRC6—as an exclusive therapeutic vulnerability to reactivate pyroptosis.

The translational relevance of this mechanism extends beyond direct cytotoxicity, offering a profound immunogenic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Specifically, the in vivo disruption of the BIRC6-GSDME interface effectively converted immunologically “inert” resistant tumors into highly inflammatory lesions characterized by robust ICD [20, 21]. By triggering the surface exposure of calreticulin—a potent “eat me” signal for innate phagocytes—alongside the massive release of DAMPs and proinflammatory cytokines (HMGB1, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) [40], BIRC6-targeted pyroptosis actively fosters a highly pro-inflammatory local milieu (Figs. 7 and 8). While our athymic nude mouse model precludes the direct evaluation of downstream adaptive T-cell responses, the observed innate immune activation, mediated by functionally intact dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, is highly clinically relevant. In an immunocompetent setting, this cascade of DC-activating signals serves as the essential prerequisite for antigen cross-presentation and subsequent T-cell priming [41]. Consequently, targeting BIRC6 provides a dual therapeutic advantage by combining direct tumor cell killing via pyroptosis with the creation of an immunogenic microenvironment that holds the potential to drive durable systemic antitumor immunity.

Despite these promising findings, our study has certain limitations. First, while computational and biochemical analyses strongly support the BIRC6-GSDME interface, high-resolution structural validation (e.g., Cryo-EM) would provide definitive atomic-level insights [42]. In this context, given the massive size and complexity of BIRC6, whether distinct point mutations or stress-induced post-translational modifications could drive dynamic conformational changes that further fine-tune this binding affinity remains to be explored. Second, while we observed a robust induction of inflammatory cytokines indicative of ICD, our reliance on an immunodeficient nude mouse model precludes the direct evaluation of adaptive immune activation, such as CD8+ T cell infiltration. Therefore, future studies employing immunocompetent syngeneic models are therefore required to fully characterize the immunological benefits of targeting BIRC6. Finally, whether this non-canonical resistance mechanism is broadly conserved across other GSDME-expressing malignancies remains an open question, representing a compelling avenue for future exploration.

Conclusions

This study uncovered a novel structural mechanism driving cisplatin resistance in LUAD, wherein BIRC6 functions as a direct checkpoint for GSDME-dependent pyroptosis. Our results demonstrated that BIRC6 utilizes its UBC domain to create steric hindrance that masks the caspase-3 cleavage site on GSDME, thereby locking tumor cells in a non-pyroptotic state (Fig. 9). Thus, our findings provide a rationale for developing peptide mimetics or small molecules that specifically disrupt the BIRC6-GSDME interface to unleash pyroptosis and overcome chemotherapy resistance.
