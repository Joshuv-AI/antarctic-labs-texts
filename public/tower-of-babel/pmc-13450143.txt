MGCL-ST: multi-view graph contrastive learning for spatial transcriptomics imputation
Authors: Jiazhou Chen, Weitian Huang, Xiaojia Chen, Siqi Ding, Yi Liao, Shangyan Cai, Hongmin Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-resolution, continuous spatial gene expression profiling is critical for dissecting complex tissue microenvironments, yet current spatial transcriptomics (ST) platforms suffer from spatial gaps and insufficient resolution. Here, we present MGCL-ST, a multi-view graph contrastive learning method for super-resolution ST imputation. By jointly modeling local and global spatial graphs with histological features derived from a pathology foundation model, MGCL-ST accurately imputes unmeasured gene expression across the tissue space. Evaluated across three diverse platforms, MGCL-ST outperforms state-of-the-art methods in imputation accuracy and spatial clustering, significantly enhancing biological interpretability and enabling the precise mapping of tumor microenvironments. MGCL-ST is available at https://github.com/guxin2002/MGCL-ST.Keywords: Contrastive learning, Gene expression imputation, Super-resolution

Background

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a vital role in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response, owing to its substantial heterogeneity and intricate spatial architecture [1, 2]. Although single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides high-resolution gene expression profiles, it lacks spatial context, limiting its ability to characterize spatial heterogeneity in the TME [3]. The emergence of spatial transcriptomics (ST) has revolutionized this field by enabling spatially resolved gene expression profiling, providing a powerful tool to investigate the spatial organization of the TME [4]. However, mainstream ST platforms, such as 10x Visium, offer relatively low resolution (with spot diameters of 55 μ m) and contain missing measurements, resulting in incomplete spatial coverage. These limitations hinder the accurate identification of fine-grained TME structures, such as tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) [5], and the mapping of spatial immune cell distributions [6]. Consequently, the low resolution and spot gaps restrict the ability to annotate critical TME regions, including tumor margins and cores, and immune components like tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and TLSs, thereby impeding a comprehensive understanding of TME functions [7].

Recently, subcellular-resolution spatial transcriptomics technologies such as MERFISH and SeqFISH [8] have emerged, offering gene expression profiling at single-cell and subcellular resolution. While these technologies hold great promise for resolving the complex spatial architecture of the TME [9], they remain limited by low measurement sensitivity, high cost, and complex experimental protocols, hindering their scalability and widespread use. Additionally, their restricted sample throughput limits applicability to large-scale translational studies. Thus, despite their high-resolution potential, these techniques are often underutilized due to practical and logistical constraints.

To overcome these limitations, computational super-resolution approaches (e.g., imputation beyond the raw spatial transcriptomics resolution) have emerged as promising alternatives. These methods integrate low-resolution spatial transcriptomic data with high-resolution histological images [10] to enhance gene expression at the pixel level, enabling the accurate annotation of tumor boundaries, cellular regions, and immune structures within TME [11]. Such techniques leverage the abundance of legacy low-resolution data while revealing the spatial heterogeneity and molecular mechanisms underlying the TME at higher resolutions, contributing significantly to immunotherapy and precision oncology [12]. However, current methods still face key challenges. For instance, BayesSpace [13] performs spatial decomposition from the spot to subspot level, yet cannot impute transcriptomic signals in the gaps between spots in low-resolution data. It is restricted to spot-covered areas, causing bias in unmeasured regions. XFuse [14] generates super-resolution predictions but suffers from two major shortcomings: high computational cost due to repeated Monte Carlo sampling during training and inference, and limited accuracy in predicting highly variable genes, as it uses gene expression data only as weak supervision without fully leveraging spatial and expression-level relationships. TESLA [15], while efficient, relies solely on color information from histopathology images and overlooks critical spatial–contextual cues such as texture and cellular morphology, resulting in suboptimal imputation performance. These drawbacks hinder the comprehensive exploration of TME spatial heterogeneity at single-cell resolution [16].

To this end, we propose MGCL-ST, a novel framework for spatial transcriptomics imputation. As illustrated in Fig. 1, MGCL-ST introduces two core innovations. First, to mitigate the noise inherent in raw spatial transcriptomics data, MGCL-ST adopts a contrastive learning framework based on multi-view neighborhood graphs, which include a local adjacency graph and a global diffusion graph constructed using Personalized PageRank (PPR). This framework enables the model to enhance spatial gene expression that is robust to noise and consistent with biological structure. Second, for super-resolution imputation, MGCL-ST employs a dual-distance neighbor selection strategy that combines two-dimensional spatial distance with histology-aware feature similarity. The latter is computed using features extracted by the Virchow2 model [17], a vision transformer pretrained on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained tissue images. This hybrid distance metric allows the model to identify appropriate neighbors from sequenced spots and propagate gene expression to unmeasured regions in a spatially coherent manner. These components enable MGCL-ST to produce a super-resolution spatial gene expression map, supporting fine-scale analysis of tumor subregions, immune cell distributions [18], and tertiary lymphoid structures [19] within TME.

Methods

Problem statement

The goal of MGCL-ST is to integrate spatial transcriptomics (ST) data with high-resolution histological images to perform super-resolution gene expression imputation. As illustrated in Fig. 1, MGCL-ST consists of two main components: (1) learning enhanced spatial gene expression through multi-view graph contrastive learning, and (2) conducting super-resolution gene expression imputation guided by a dual-distance neighbor detection strategy.

In the first stage (shown in Fig. 1a), MGCL-ST takes a preprocessed gene expression matrix as input, where N denotes the number of spatial spots and d is the number of genes. The raw ST data are used to construct multi-view neighborhood graphs, including a local adjacency graph A and a global diffusion graph S computed using PPR. These graphs encode both local spatial connectivity and broader semantic relationships. Then, a self-supervised contrastive learning module (see Multi-view graph contrastive learning for gene expression representation section) is employed to learn noise-robust latent spatial representations . This is accomplished by pulling together cross-view representations of spatially adjacent nodes while pushing apart those of corrupted or non-neighboring samples. Eventually, enhanced spatial gene expressions are reconstructed through decoding the latent spatial representations.

In the second stage (shown in Fig. 1b), MGCL-ST performs super-resolution gene expression imputation by integrating spatial transcriptomics with histological information. Specifically, it adopts a dual-distance neighbor detection strategy (see Dual-distance neighbor detection strategy for gene expression imputation section) that combines two-dimensional spatial distance with histological feature distance , the latter computed using embeddings extracted by the Virchow2 model pretrained on H&E-stained tissue images. The hybrid distance d enables the model to identify reliable neighbors from sequenced spots and effectively impute gene expression at unmeasured locations. As a result, MGCL-ST supports the generation of super-resolution spatial gene expression maps, facilitating the identification of TLSs and enabling downstream tasks such as spatial clustering.

Datasets

The data used in this study primarily originates from multiple slices obtained from the 10X Visium platform [20], mouse brain data collected using the Visium HD platform [21], and tumor-related data provided from the Xenium platform [22, 23].

On the 10X Visium platform, we employ a benchmark spatial transcriptomics dataset derived from the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). This dataset includes 12 tissue sections collected from three individuals, with each individual contributing four sections spaced at intervals of 10μ m and 300μ m. Each section contains between 3,498 and 4,789 spatial barcoded spots. MGCL-ST takes spatial gene expression data and high-resolution histological images as inputs. The raw gene expression counts are first preprocessed using the SCANPY [24] package, which includes log transformation and library size-based normalization to eliminate technical noise and batch effects. Subsequently, the data is standardized with zero mean and unit variance to ensure comparability across different samples. Following normalization, the top 3,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) are selected as model inputs, as they capture the most informative biological variability across spatial spots.

For the Visium HD dataset, we select a mouse brain section with a prominently annotated hippocampal region. The Visium HD platform employs ultra-dense sampling, capturing approximately 11 million spatial barcodes per tissue section, with no inter-spot gaps. Each spatial unit corresponds to a 2μ m × 2μ m square region densely packed with oligonucleotide probes. Owing to its full-tissue coverage, the high-resolution gene expression map produced by Visium HD serves as a valuable reference, allowing quantitative assessment via Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC). To simulate a low-resolution setting, we preprocess the Visium HD data through the following steps. The Visium HD data are segmented following the 10X Visium data acquisition specifications, with spatial transcriptomic information used to map each data point to its location within the tissue slice. The tissue is partitioned into circular spots with a radius of 55μ m, spaced 100μ m apart. For spots encompassing multiple square regions, gene expression values are summated as , where represents the expression from the i -th square region and N denotes the total number of square regions within the spot. Edge-detected square regions are included in the spot area. The resulting low-resolution spatial transcriptomics data, together with the high-resolution histological images, are used as input for MGCL-ST. This configuration supports super-resolution gene expression imputation, enabling the model to leverage both spatial location and image-derived structural features for enhanced resolution and biological fidelity.

In addition, we incorporate spatial transcriptomics data from the Xenium platform, including one colorectal cancer (CRC) sample and one breast cancer sample, to enhance the diversity and depth of our analysis. The CRC sample [22] provides high-resolution spatial gene expression profiles of human CRC tissue sections, enabling detailed characterization of tumor microenvironment heterogeneity—such as the spatial distribution of cancer cells, immune cells, and stromal components. The breast cancer sample [23] comprises expression data for 313 curated genes, pathologically annotated regions include ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) subtypes, invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), and rare boundary cells at the myoepithelial border. Consistent with the processing pipeline for Visium HD data, both Xenium samples are subjected to identical preprocessing steps to ensure data consistency and comparability across platforms.

Finally, we incorporate a spatial transcriptomics dataset from cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) [25] to investigate intra-tumor heterogeneity, spatial immune cell distributions, and the localization of TLSs within TME. This dataset is sequenced using the 10X Visium platform, and the spatial resolution is relatively low, with only 658 spatial barcoded spots per section, making it difficult to resolve fine-grained immune structures such as TLSs. Similar to the 10X data, the raw gene expression counts are preprocessed using the SCANPY [24] package, involving log transformation, library size-based normalization, and scaling to a standard distribution with zero mean and unit variance. The top 3,000 HVGs are selected as input features to capture the biological variability of the tumor tissue. MGCL-ST is then applied to this dataset to perform super-resolution gene expression imputation, enabling super-resolution analysis of tumor regions, immune cell distributions (e.g., B cells, CD4 T cells, dendritic cells), and TLS prediction, thereby providing deeper insights into the TME’s functional characteristics and immunotherapy response mechanisms.

Model architecture

MGCL-ST takes preprocessed gene expression data, spatial neighborhood graphs, and histological images as input. The gene expression matrix is log-transformed, normalized, and standardized, while spatial relationships are encoded through multi-view graphs that capture both local and global dependencies. Leveraging multi-view graph contrastive learning [26], the model learns noise-robust gene expression representations by perturbing input features and aggregating neighborhood information through Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs). To support super-resolution gene expression imputation, MGCL-ST integrates spatial transcriptomics data with histological image features. The resulting gene expression maps provide fine-grained spatial resolution and biological coherence.

Dual-distance neighbor detection strategy for gene expression imputation

After obtaining enhanced gene expression data through contrastive learning, we further process the captured regions in histological images to achieve super-resolution gene expression imputation. First, we employ Canny edge detection for contour segmentation to isolate biologically valid tissue regions and eliminate background noise. We then perform subregion partitioning with 4× super-resolution, decomposing the image into finer subregions to capture high-resolution cellular morphology. For instance, when processing 10X Visium spots mapped to 240× 240 pixel regions in histological images, we divide the entire image into contiguous 60× 60 pixel subregions. This approach ensures that the structural details of individual cells and local tissue microenvironments are preserved.

To obtain semantic representations of each image region, we adopted the Virchow2 model due to its state-of-the-art performance in computational pathology and mixed magnification adaptability—pretrained on 3.1 million diverse whole-slide images. Virchow2 ensures consistent and high-quality feature representation across multi-platform spatial transcriptomics data. Both spot regions and subregions were resized to 224× 224 pixels using bilinear interpolation to avoid structural distortion, followed by ImageNet-style normalization to align with the model’s pretraining distribution. Results

Experimental setup

In our experiments, the multi-view graph contrastive learning module contains a GCN encoder and a GCN decoder for each view. Each GCN encoder consists of a graph convolutional layer that maps the raw features to a 64-dimensional latent feature representation, followed by a ReLU non-linear activation function. Similarly, each GCN decoder is also a one-layer GCN that reconstructs the 64-dimensional latent embeddings back to the original gene expression feature dimensions. Regarding the hyperparameter selection, a grid search strategy is applied to determine the optimal value of parameters and based on the imputation performance (SSIM score). In the following experiments, we found that the performance of our model is not overly sensitive to the exact choice of and , demonstrating good robustness and generalizability of the proposed MGCL-ST framework. Evaluation of spatial clustering performance on super-resolution gene expression

Here, we evaluate the spatial clustering performance of MGCL-ST as a downstream task following super-resolution gene expression imputation. A common clustering algorithm, Leiden [33], is employed to categorize the super-resolution gene expression map calculated using our method into distinct groups. Experiments are conducted on 12 tissue slices from the 10X-DLPFC dataset. We compare our approach against seven state-of-the-art methods: BayesSpace [13], SEDR [34], STAGATE [35], ConST [36], MuCoST [37], SpaGCN [38], and GraphST [27]. The clustering performance is quantified using Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI), based on 10 replicate tests.

Figure 4 shows the clustering results (ARI score in Fig. 4a and NMI score in Fig. 4b) of all methods. It is clear that: (1) MGCL-ST achieves the best overall performance, with visibly higher medians on both ARI and NMI metrics, indicating its superior accuracy and robustness across all tissue slices. (2) MGCL-ST attains a median ARI of 0.582 and a median NMI of 0.665. Compared with the state-of-the-art baseline GraphST, which achieves a median ARI of 0.537 and an NMI of 0.616, our method outperforms it by 4.5 and 4.9 percentage points, respectively. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of MGCL-ST in generating biologically coherent clusters with high consistency and information retention.

Moreover, to evaluate the spatial clustering performance of MGCL-ST across different spatial transcriptomics platforms on irregular tumor tissue, we run all methods on a Xenium breast cancer data. We selected three regions of interest (ROIs) manually annotated by expert pathologists: ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), and benign (B), as the ground truth regions (Fig. 6a). Figure 6 shows the clustering performance of all methods qualitatively and quantitatively. We can see that: (1) Compared to the other seven benchmark methods, MGCL-ST achieves the best clustering performance in terms of the ARI (Fig. 6b) and NMI (Fig. 6c) score, illustrating that MGCL-ST maintains high accuracy and robustness even on irregular and complex tumor tissue sections. (2) MGCL-ST attains median scores of 0.693 for ARI and 0.543 for NMI, while the second-best method (MuCoST) achieves a median ARI score of 0.648 and a median NMI value of 0.479. This represents an improvement of 4.5 and 6.4 percentage points, respectively. (3) In Fig. 6a, other methods tend to produce discrete and discontinuous spatial domains, often containing substantial noise. In contrast, our proposed method generates spatial domains with strong internal consistency, smooth and continuous boundaries, which are well-aligned with tumor-related regions. Notably, MGCL-ST further decomposes the DCIS region into two distinct subregions (Cluster 2 and Cluster 6). We observe that these clusters are highly consistent with the previously reported DCIS #1 and DCIS #2 regions [23], indicating our method’s capability to reveal subtle differences between tumor subregions. These results demonstrate the superiority and robustness of MGCL-ST under different spatial transcriptomics platforms and different tissue types.

Tertiary lymphoid structure detection on super-resolution gene expression

After verifying the strong super-resolution imputation capability of MGCL-ST in Spatial transcriptomics super-resolution evaluation and Evaluation of spatial clustering performance on super-resolution gene expression sections, we further apply the model to a cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) tissue sample to investigate whether the reconstructed super-resolution gene expression map can facilitate downstream tasks such as cell type annotation and tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) region identification.

Specifically, we use the super-resolution gene expression matrix output by MGCL-ST as the input for TLS-related analysis. First, we select highly variable genes associated with tumor cells as marker genes to annotate tumor regions based on their expression patterns. By integrating histological images with the super-resolution gene expression, we perform unsupervised deep representation learning to conduct spatial clustering, followed by iterative refinement to generate a high-confidence spatial cluster map. Based on gene expression intensities, we automatically identify tumor-enriched regions and construct cell density maps. We then annotate tumor-enriched areas accordingly. Next, by incorporating both cluster density and morphological structure, we apply edge detection to subdivide the tumor region into core and peripheral zones, providing structural context for downstream differential expression analysis. The results are presented in Fig. 7a, which shows the histological image of the CSCC sample, the expression pattern of a tumor meta-marker gene, the tumor region identified by MGCL-ST, and the delineation of core and peripheral subregions. The predicted tumor areas closely align with manually annotated histological regions, confirming the spatial accuracy of the reconstructed gene expression. Notably, even for subspots located at a considerable physical distance from their neighboring spots, the model identifies relevant neighbors based on morphological similarity derived from the pre-trained histological image model, thereby achieving precise and biologically meaningful expression imputation.

The MGCL-ST model reveals intra-tumor heterogeneity and immune landscape features in a CSCC tissue section. a Histology image (first), tumor meta-marker gene expression (second), tumor regions (third), and tumor core–edge separation (fourth), illustrating spatial tumor organization. b Representative genes enriched in the tumor core (IGFBP2, CCNB1) and tumor edge (CXCL12, IL2RB) identified by MGCL-ST, highlighting molecular differences across regions. c Super-resolution distributions of B cells, CD4 T cells, dendritic cells, and CXCL13 characterized by MGCL-ST, showing the immune microenvironment within the CSCC section. d TLS score heatmap and (e), AUC score calculated by MGCL-ST, providing a quantitative measure of lymphoid aggregation across the cutaneous CSCC tissue section

Following region partitioning, we apply a combination of expression thresholds, inter-region proportion comparisons, and fold change filters to identify representative differentially expressed genes between the tumor core and periphery. As shown in Fig. 7b, we visualize the spatial distributions of selected candidate genes, including IGFBP2 and CXCL12. The results demonstrate that IGFBP2 and CCNB1 are predominantly expressed in the tumor core, while CXCL12 and IL2RB are enriched in the peripheral zone patterns that are highly consistent with their known biological functions. This agreement supports the model’s effectiveness in reconstructing high-resolution spatial expression profiles with biological fidelity.

We then extend a similar strategy to immune cell annotation. Using canonical marker genes, we construct spatial density maps for B cells, CD8 T cells, dendritic cells, and CXCL13-expressing cells. As shown in Fig. 7c, these immune cell subsets are predominantly located around the tumor boundary, exhibiting an immune activation pattern characteristic of TLS-enriched regions. Based on these spatial distributions, we calculate TLS scores to quantify the enrichment of TLS across the tissue section and visualize the inferred TLS-enriched areas. This analysis lays the foundation for subsequent binary mask generation and ROC-based quantitative evaluation. Figure 7d exhibits the TLS score heatmap. The left panel highlights a predicted high-score TLS region (blue box) that overlaps significantly with a manually annotated dense lymphocyte aggregate, suggesting high model confidence. In contrast, the yellow box area corresponding to a region with sparse lymphocyte presence receives a lower TLS score. The center panel shows the ground truth mask, while the right panel presents the predicted TLS heatmap. The spatial consistency between these maps further confirms the robustness and accuracy of MGCL-ST in TLS spatial localization tasks. Figure 7e presents a quantitative assessment of TLS detection performance. MGCL-ST achieves an AUC of 0.7953 in distinguishing TLS-positive regions from TLS-negative regions, indicating strong predictive capability.

In summary, the application of MGCL-ST to CSCC reveals its capacity to characterize intra-tumor heterogeneity, map immune cell distributions, and identify TLS regions with high spatial precision. Through the integration of spatial coordinates and latent feature similarities, the model reconstructs gene expression patterns that align closely with histological architecture and immunological organization. These findings support its ability to capture biologically relevant structure within the tumor microenvironment at subspot resolution.

Ablation study

To assess the effectiveness of the key components in our proposed framework, we conduct a comprehensive ablation study centered around three core modules: the diffusion graph structure, the graph-based contrastive learning module, and the histology-guided neighbor selection mechanism for super-resolution interpolation. To this end, we construct four model variants: the full model (with all modules enabled), the model without the diffusion graph (w/o Diffusion), the model without the contrastive learning module (w/o Contrast), and the model without histological features (w/o HistoFeat). All models are trained under identical settings on 10X Visium slices 151673 and 151675, with downstream clustering used as the evaluation task. Performance is measured using several evaluation metrics: Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), accuracy (ACC), normalized mutual information (NMI), homogeneity, completeness, Fowlkes–Mallows index (FMI), and V-measure.

Table 2 shows the spatial clustering results of all model variants on slices 151673 and 151675. We can find that the full model achieves superior clustering performance than other model variants in all evaluation metrics, indicating each module plays a critical role in enhancing the model’s performance. Specifically, the model without contrastive learning (w/o Contrast) exhibits the most significant performance degradation on slice 151673. Its ARI score drops to 0.4970, which is considerably lower than both w/o Diffusion (0.5461) and w/o HistoFeat (0.5283). This trend is consistent across other metrics such as ACC, NMI, Homogeneity, Completeness, FMI, and V-measure. These results underscore the critical role of the contrastive learning module in denoising, maintaining local spatial consistency, and enhancing representational quality. Next, excluding histological features also negatively impacts the clustering performance. Compared to the full model, the ARI score of the counterpart method (w/o HistoFeat) decreases from 0.5922 to 0.5283. This demonstrates that the morphological context provided by histological images significantly contributes to accurate expression reconstruction, especially in regions with ambiguous spatial boundaries where Euclidean distance alone is insufficient. Ultimately, compared with the model without the diffusion graph (w/o Diffusion), the MGCL-ST achieves a 4.6% performance improvement in ARI score, indicating the importance of global structural modeling for learning spatially coherent and discriminative representations. Similar experimental results can also be observed on slice 151675.

In summary, the diffusion-based global modeling, histology-aware spatial guidance, and contrastive representation learning in our MGCL-ST are complementary and synergistic. Their integration substantially improves both the structural fidelity and biological plausibility of reconstructed gene expression maps, leading to more accurate and interpretable clustering outcomes.

Discussion

The primary challenge in spatial transcriptomics remains the inherent trade-off between spatial resolution and comprehensive tissue coverage, which restricts the accurate characterization of complex biological structures such as the tumor microenvironment. In this study, we demonstrated that MGCL-ST effectively bridges this gap by providing a morphology-aware, semantically consistent framework for super-resolution gene expression imputation. We validated MGCL-ST across major ST platforms, including 10X Visium, Visium HD, and Xenium. In structural fidelity, gene-level expression correlation, and downstream spatial clustering, our method consistently surpasses state-of-the-art baselines such as TESLA and BayesSpace.

A key driver of MGCL-ST’s superior performance is the integration of physical spatial proximity with histological feature similarity, extracted via the Virchow2 vision transformer, to guide neighbor selection. This dual-distance strategy proved essential for preserving intricate anatomical boundaries. For instance, in the Visium HD mouse brain dataset, MGCL-ST accurately recovered the highly specific layered architecture of the hippocampus, successfully capturing subtle spatial expression patterns for regional marker genes like ZBTB20 and PROX1. Furthermore, in highly heterogeneous tumor tissues, this morphology-guided approach mitigated the boundary fragmentation and noise commonly produced by traditional methods. In the Xenium breast cancer dataset, our model yielded biologically plausible spatial domains and successfully delineated distinct ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) subregions that aligned perfectly with pathological annotations.

Beyond imputation accuracy, the reconstructed super-resolution maps generated by MGCL-ST significantly enhanced deep biological interpretation. When applied to a cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) sample, the model enabled the high-resolution mapping of spatial immune cell distributions, such as B cells, CD4 T cells, and dendritic cells. Crucially, MGCL-ST precisely localized tertiary lymphoid structures, achieving a high predictive AUC of 0.7953, confirming its utility in analyzing immune architecture and potential immunotherapy response mechanisms. Our ablation studies further validated that the diffusion-based global structural modeling and the histology-aware neighbor selection act synergistically; their combination is vital for achieving high structural fidelity and meaningful spatial disentanglement.

Despite these strong results, the current study has certain limitations. First, the performance of MGCL-ST currently relies on manual hyperparameter tuning, such as utilizing a grid search to determine the optimal contrastive weighting parameters and the exact number of neighboring spots required for edge versus internal subregions. Second, the model’s computational complexity increases with tissue size and resolution, which may pose challenges for large-scale whole-slide analysis.

Conclusions

In this paper, we propose MGCL-ST, a unified computational model for super-resolution spatial transcriptomics imputation. By jointly modeling local spatial proximity and global semantic structure through contrastive learning, and by leveraging a pre-trained pathology foundation model to inform neighbor selection, MGCL-ST effectively generates accurate, noise-robust spatial gene expression maps. We have evaluated the power of MGCL-ST in super-resolution gene expression imputation, spatial clustering, immune cell annotation, and tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) identification on real datasets. More consistent and reasonable results were achieved by our proposed MGCL-ST model, than by the existing state-of-the-art methods, such as TESLA. The future work will focus on developing an end-to-end generative model that jointly learns from expression patterns and histological context. This advancement will aim to reduce the reliance on manual hyperparameter tuning and further enhance the model’s generalizability across diverse tissue types and transcriptomic platforms.
