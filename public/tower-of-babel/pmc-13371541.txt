Hemoglobin glycation index correlates with the prognoses of critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia: a retrospective cohort study from the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Jinhui Zhang, Zhenkui Hu, Chao Song, Degang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemoglobin glycation index (HGI), a novel metabolic marker, has demonstrated significant associations with adverse clinical outcomes in multiple major diseases. However, the correlation of HGI with mortality in hyperlipidemic populations has rarely been studied. This cohort study sought to elucidate the link of HGI with short-term mortality of critically ill individuals with hyperlipidemia.MethodsData regarding patients diagnosed with hyperlipidemia were retrospectively retrieved from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database and stratified into four groups on the basis of the quartiles of continuous HGI. The main endpoints were in-hospital mortality and 28-day mortality. We utilized various statistical.ResultsAn overall of 3,229 participants (57.9% male) with a median age of 71.01 years (IQR: 61.72, 80.63) were included. K-M survival analysis demonstrated that patients with higher HGI were associated with decreased mortality risks (log-rank p < 0.001). Fully adjusted Cox regression analyses indicated that the elevated HGI was independently associated with reduced risks of both in-hospital mortality (HR 0.833, 95% CI 0.763-0.910, p < 0.001) and 28-day mortality (HR 0.858, 95% CI 0.793-0.927, p < 0.001). RCS analyses confirmed linear relationships of ascending HGI with decreased risks regarding in-hospital and 28-day mortality. Additionally, incorporating HGI into existing models improved short-term mortality prediction performance. Subgroup analysis further showed a strong link of HGI with mortality among patients aged ≥ 65 years and those without diabetes.ConclusionsThe study underscored that a lower HGI was significantly associated with a higher risk of short-term mortality among critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02316-9.

Introduction

Hyperlipidemia, defined as a common metabolic disorder marked by abnormal concentrations of circulating lipoproteins in the bloodstream [1–3], is closely linked to multiple metabolic conditions, including hypertension, coronary heart disease, cancer, and chronic kidney disease [4–6]. It is estimated that over 100 million individuals in the United States, approximately 53% of the adult population, presented with elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [7]. Over the past decade, the prevalence of dyslipidemia among Chinese adults has more than doubled, reaching a rate of 41.9% [8]. In 2017, elevated LDL-C was responsible for 862,759 deaths in China, representing 8.25% of total mortality and 19.71% of cardiovascular disease-related deaths [9]. Given that hyperlipidemia significantly elevates the risk of cardiovascular events, stroke, and mortality worldwide [10–12], there is a critical need for reliable prognostic biomarkers to identify and manage risk factors to improve clinical outcomes.

Studies have shown that insulin-mediated metabolic dysregulation in diabetic patients was often associated with lipid metabolism disorders, which could contribute to the onset of hyperlipidemia [13]. For these individuals, rigorous glycemic management has been demonstrated to be effective in reducing serum triglyceride (TG) concentrations and diminishing the risk of diabetes-related complications and mortality [14, 15]. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), an indicator of long-term glycemic regulation, is strongly linked to hyperlipidemia [16], as insufficient glycemic control drives lipoprotein glycation, thereby elevating atherogenic potential [17]. Nevertheless, HbA1c measurements are subject to variability due to factors such as the lifespan and turnover of red blood cells and transmembrane glucose gradients [18, 19]. The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI), introduced by Hempe et al. in 2002, was designed to overcome these limitations by quantifying individual HbA1c variation through computing the difference between measured and predicted HbA1c [20]. Apparent correlations between HGI and the risks as well as diabetic complications, including mortality, cardiovascular disease, and microvascular complications have already been reported [21, 22]. HGI also demonstrates prognostic significance across various cardiovascular diseases, such as heart failure, myocardial infarction, and coronary artery disease [23–25]. Despite these findings, studies investigating the prognostic utility of HGI in hyperlipidemia, particularly in critically ill patients, remains inadequately explored. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between the baseline HGI at ICU admission and the short-term prognosis of critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia.

Methods

Data source

Data for this retrospective cohort study were derived from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1) database, which encompasses high-caliber and comprehensive medical information of over 90,000 ICU admissions at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology from 2008 to 2022. One author, Jinhui Zhang, was responsible for extracting the data after fulfilling the necessary requirements for database access. As the database was de-identified to ensure patient information was anonymized, individual consent was waived.

Study population

The diagnoses of hyperlipidemia in this study were ascertained based on the codes of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes: ICD-9 (2722 and 2724) and ICD-10 (E782, E784, E7849, and E785). The exclusion criteria comprised: (1) patients aged < 18 years at admission; (2) patients with multiple admissions, retaining only data from the initial ICU admission; (3) patients lacking data on HbA1c or fasting blood glucose (FBG) upon first-day ICU admission; (4) patients with ICU length of stay less than 24 h; (5) patients with a history of malignancy, autoimmune diseases, anemia, hemoglobinopathies, end-stage renal disease, severe liver disease, major blood loss, or transfusions. Ultimately, the analysis cohort consisted of 3,229 participants (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

PostgreSQL software (version 13.7.2) was used to extract data. The demographic characteristics included age, gender, weight, smoking, and race. Clinical parameters included severity of illness scores, vital signs, comorbidities, medication at discharge, laboratory parameters, and clinical outcomes. All vital signs and laboratory data were obtained during the first 24 h following ICU admission, before the initiation of any therapeutic interventions. According to prior studies using MIMIC-IV data [26], blood glucose levels exhibit a circadian trough at 03:00 AM. Therefore, glucose measurements recorded between 02:00 AM and 04:00 AM were extracted as surrogate “fasting” levels when assessing baseline glucose metabolism. To minimize potential bias, variables with a missing rate exceeding 20% were excluded from the analysis to ensure data completeness. Variables with less than 20% missing values were imputed using a random forest methodology for imputation implemented in the missForest package of R software [27].

Definition of exposure variables and outcome events

The HGI was derived as described in previously published protocols [21, 28]. A linear regression model was constructed using data from all participants to examine the relationship between FPG and HbA1c levels. The resulting regression equation was: Predicted HbA1c = 0.0093 × FPG (mg/dL) + 5.170. HGI was defined as the difference between measured and predicted HbA1c values. The correlation between HGI and HbA1c was illustrated in Fig. 2. The main endpoints were in-hospital mortality and 28-day mortality. Data regarding patient mortality was sourced from hospital records and the United States Social Security Administration’s Death Master File.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses of this study were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0, USA), Graph prism (version 9.0, USA) and R software (version 4.1.3, Austria). Continuous variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR, 25th-75th percentiles) and were compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests, while categorical variables were represented as absolute numbers with percentages and analyzed through chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. We utilized the Boruta algorithm to rank features according to their predictive importance for the main endpoints. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival curves and log-rank tests were employed to evaluate the cumulative incidence of endpoint events, with stratification based on HGI quartiles. Multivariable analyses employed three levels of adjustment: Model 1: Unadjusted. Model 2: Adjusted for age, gender, race, weight, smoking. Model 3: Fully adjusted for Model 2 covariates plus myocardial infarction, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, APSIII, Charlson score, SBP, DBP, statins, insulin, oral hypoglycemic agents, WBC, and PLT. The selection of covariates including age, gender, race, weight, smoking, myocardial infarction, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, APSIII, Charlson score, SBP, DBP, statins, insulin, oral hypoglycemic agents, WBC, and PLT were based on clinically relevant baseline variables and those with a univariate relationship to the outcomes, with collinearity controlled (VIF < 5). A restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model with four knots was employed to explore the potential non-linear relationship between continuous HGI and mortality risk. To evaluate the improvement in predictive accuracy when incorporating HGI into the existing prognostic scoring system, we employed the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, quantifying the enhancement via the area under the curve (AUC). Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by age (≤ 65 and > 65 years), gender, and the presence or absence of comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, myocardial infarction, and heart failure. Statistical significance was established at two-sided p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study individuals

The study cohort comprised 3,229 patients, with a median age of 71.01 years (IQR: 61.72, 80.63), including 1,869 (57.9%) males. The in-hospital and 28-day mortality rates were 8.9% and 12.3%, respectively. Baseline characteristics of the patients, stratified by HGI quartiles, were detailed in Table 1. The median HGI values for each quartile were − 1.182 (IQR: -1.479, -1.001), -0.639 (IQR: (-0.744, -0.528), -0.144 (IQR: -0.285, 0.068), and 1.607 (IQR: 0.772, 2.927), respectively. There were significant differences in age, weight, race, APSIII, SAPSII, SOFA score, LODS score, Charlson score, OASIS, temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, SBP, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, insulin, oral hypoglycemic agents, white blood cell (WBC), platelet (PLT), red blood cell (RBC), albumin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), FBG, and HbA1c among the four groups (all p < 0.05). Compared to those in the lower HGI quartile, patients in the higher quartile experienced lower in-hospital mortality (14.5% vs. 6.8% vs. 8.4% vs. 5.7%, p < 0.001) and 28-day mortality (18.0% vs. 12.4% vs. 11.0% vs. 7.7%, p < 0.001).

Clinical outcomes

The Boruta analysis (Fig. 3) suggested that HGI was one of the key factors in predicting in-hospital and 28-day mortality in patients with hyperlipidemia. K-M analysis indicated significant differences in cumulative mortality across the four HGI quartile groups (Fig. 4), with patients in the lowest quartile exhibiting a significantly higher risk of both in-hospital and 28-day mortality (log-rank p < 0.001). The results from the univariate Cox regression analysis were presented in Supplementary Table 1, while the results of the Variance Inflation Factors Analysis were shown in Supplementary Table 2, confirming the absence of multicollinearity among the variables. To assess the independent association between HGI and in-hospital mortality, three progressively adjusted models were constructed to explore this relationship. When analysing HGI as continuous variable, it demonstrated a significant association with in-hospital mortality across all models: the unadjusted model (HR 0.818, 95% CI 0.768–0.872, p < 0.001), the partially adjusted model (HR 0.801, 95% CI 0.739–0.867, p < 0.001), and the fully adjusted model (HR 0.833, 95% CI 0.763–0.910, p < 0.001). When HGI was analyzed as an categorical variable, patients in the highest quartile exhibited a 59.2% reduction risk of in-hospital mortality compared to those in the lowest quartile in the unadjusted model (HR 0.408, 95% CI 0.290–0.575; p < 0.001), which reduced to 53.5% in the partially adjusted model (HR 0.465, 95% CI 0.327–0.660; p < 0.001) and 48.9% in the fully adjusted model (HR 0.511, 95% CI 0.343–0.762; p < 0.001), with a decreasing trend in mortality risk as HGI increased (Table 3). Significant negative trends were observed for 28-day mortality.

Additionally, the RCS regression model showed significant linear links of elevated HGI with decreased risks of both in-hospital and 28-day mortality (all p for non-linearity > 0.05), as shown in Fig. 5. To assess their predictive performance, three models for mortality risk were evaluated using ROC curve and calibration analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1). For in-hospital mortality, the discriminative ability of the models improved progressively: the AUC values for the unadjusted model (Model 1) showed an AUC of 0.605 (95% CI 0.575–0.635), the partially adjusted model (Model 2) demonstrated enhanced discrimination with an AUC of 0.727 (95% CI 0.700-0.754), and the fully adjusted model (Model 3) achieved the highest AUC of 0.837 (95% CI 0.816–0.858). Calibration analysis further indicated that the predicted probabilities from Model 3 were closely aligned with the actual probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 2). Similar trends were observed for 28-day mortality, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2.

Given that ICD-based diagnosis of hyperlipidemia may miss some cases, we assessed the extent of underdiagnosis using available blood lipid laboratory results. The results showed that among patients meeting biochemical diagnostic criteria [29] (defined as serum cholesterol levels ≥ 4.7 mmol/L, triglyceride levels ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, or low-density lipoprotein levels ≥ 4.1 mmol/L), 1.2% remained unrecorded by ICD codes. A sensitivity analysis was therefore performed using a biochemically defined hyperlipidemia variable (including both diagnosed and undiagnosed cases). The results (Supplement Table 3) remained consistent, supporting the robustness of our findings.

Subgroup analysis

To assess the prognostic significance of HGI for mortality endpoints across various subclasses of our population, we performed subgroup analyses stratified based on age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, myocardial infarction, and heart failure (Fig. 7). Our findings revealed that elevated HGI was consistently related to decreased risks of in-hospital mortality among most subgroups, including gender, hypertension, and heart failure, with no significant interactions observed (p for interaction > 0.05). Notably, the predictive value of the HGI seemed to be more prominent in patients aged > 65 years [HR (95% CI) aged > 65 years 0.67 (0.59–0.76) vs. aged ≤ 65 years 0.95 (0.85–1.09), p for interaction < 0.001] and those without diabetes [HR (95% CI) without diabetes 0.59 (0.48–0.72) vs. with diabetes 0.84 (0.78–0.89), p for interaction = 0.005]. This pattern was consistently obtained in stratified analyses of HGI and 28-day mortality

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to retrospectively examine the association between the HGI and clinical prognosis in a United States cohort of critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia. Our analysis demonstrated that HGI was independently associated with in-hospital and 28-day mortality, and this association remained statistically significant even after rigorous adjustment for multiple potential confounders. The results also revealed an inverse linear relationship between HGI and the risk of mortality. Subgroup analyses further indicated that this correlation was notably stronger in patients > 65 years and those without diabetes.

Prior studies have indicated that hyperlipidemia may exacerbate vascular endothelial dysfunction, leading to an elevated risk of cardio-cerebrovascular complications in the diabetic population [30]. Diabetes comorbid with hyperlipidemia exacerbates the progression of atherosclerosis and amplifies cardiovascular risk [31]. Importantly, enhanced glycemic control contributes to the partial alleviation of diabetic dyslipidemia, highlighting an interdependent relationship between glucose and lipid metabolism [32]. HbA1c, a well-established biomarker for the screening, diagnosis, and long-term glucose monitoring of diabetes, exhibits a strong correlation with various lipid parameters [33]. Previous studies have established positive correlations between HbA1c levels and both TC and LDL-C in diabetic populations [34, 35]. Additionally, elevated HbA1c is linked to both elevated TG and reduced HDL-C, further aggravating lipid dysregulation in diabetes [36]. However, HbA1c measurements can be affected by several physiological and pathological factors, such as mean erythrocyte lifespan, transmembrane glucose transport, enzymatic function, and genetic factors [18, 19, 37, 38]. By incorporating both long-term (HbA1c) and short-term (FPG) glycemic markers, the emerging HGI metric offers a more comprehensive assessment of glycemic status and has gained recognition for its prognostic value across multiple diseases in recent clinical studies [39–42]. For instance, Zhang et al. reported an association between high HGI and increased 10-year all-cause mortality in a Chinese cohort [43], while another study demonstrated its association with higher 28-day and 365-day mortality in sepsis patients [44]. However, the relationship between HGI and prognosis may not be uniform across all populations. Several studies have reported that a low HGI can also serve as a predictor of poor outcomes in specific patient groups [45, 46]. Cao et al. observed that, among critically ill myocardial infarction patients, HGI was inversely associated with all-cause mortality [24]. Tong et al. found that lower HGI was associated with increased 30-day and 1-year mortality in ischemic stroke patients [47], while Liu et al. identified low HGI as an independent risk factor for both 28-day and 90-day mortality in patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) [48]. Cheng et al. showed a significant relationship between elevated HGI and lower risks of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with acute decompensated heart failure [49]. Wei et al. suggested that a low HGI was identified as a potential biomarker for the assessment of short- and long-term mortality risk in critically ill patients with coronary heart disease [25]. These findings imply that HGI serves as a marker for a patient’s glycemic metabolic status and may offer insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying patient prognosis. Using data from the MIMIC-IV database, our study of 3,229 critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia found that lower HGI was significantly linked to elevated risks of both in-hospital and 28-day mortality. Our research specifically focused on critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia, a population that typically experiences high acute stress at disease onset, exhibiting more pronounced stress-induced hyperglycemia [50]. This stress hyperglycemia may substantially elevate the HbA1c values estimated from blood glucose levels, leading to a relative decrease in the HGI. The optimal HGI range for hyperlipidemic populations remains to be determined through further investigation in larger cohorts. Discrimination and calibration analyses of the Cox proportional hazards model performance confirmed that the progressive improvement in both discrimination and calibration with successive adjustments underscored the robustness of our fully adjusted model for mortality prediction in critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia

This study further validated the linear relationship between the HGI and short-term mortality in critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia. RCS analyses indicated a significant inverse linear correlation of HGI with in-hospital and 28-day mortality. Previous studies have shown varied associations between HGI and mortality across various critical illness populations. For instance, Ge et al. revealed a strong inverse linear association between HGI and both 28-day and 360-day mortality in Trauma/Surgical Intensive Care Units (TSICU/SICU) patients [51]. Lin et al. identified a U-shaped relationship between HGI and 3-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in diabetic patients with coronary artery disease [46]. Wang et al. reported a J-shaped correlation between HGI and 1-year mortality in critically ill heart failure patients [52]. Among patients with acute coronary syndrome, Li et al. observed a U-shaped correlation between HGI and the occurrence of MACEs [53]. Furthermore, Huang et al. observed that HGI had an L-shaped association with short-term outcomes (30-day and in-hospital mortality), whereas its relationship with 1-year mortality followed a reverse J-shaped pattern in patients with acute ischemic stroke [54]. These findings suggested that the relationship between HGI and clinical outcomes differed across disease contexts. While the mechanisms driving the inverse linear association between HGI and mortality risk in hyperlipidemic patients are not yet fully elucidated, several potential factors have been proposed. Firstly, a low HGI state may result in the abnormal upregulation of NADPH oxidase system activity, leading to promote reactive oxygen species generation, anaerobic glycolysis, lactic acidosis, and initiating an oxidative stress response. This mechanism not only impairs vascular endothelial function but also promotes the release of inflammatory factors through the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [55–58]. However, direct evidence supporting this pathway in the context of low HGI is currently lacking. Secondly, a low HGI may indicate impaired glycation processes, which could be associated with underlying chronic diseases or nutritional deficiencies that reduce patient resilience [59, 60]. Whether such impaired glycation directly contributes to higher mortality remains to be determined. Thirdly, a reduced rate of HbA1c associated with low HGI might lead to an extended lifespan of hemoglobin, thereby increasing its oxidative modification and blood viscosity [24]. Furthermore, a lower HGI may indicate increased metabolic instability, characterized by insulin resistance, glycemic variability, and impaired energy metabolism. These conditions theoretically can exacerbate oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, ultimately leading to endothelial dysfunction [24, 28, 61]. We emphasize that these proposed pathways are hypotheses generated from our observational findings rather than established mechanisms.

Our subgroup analysis revealed that a higher HGI correlated with reduced in-hospital and 28-day mortality in non-diabetic patients with hyperlipidemia. This observation could be attributed to the more rigorous glycemic monitoring and management typically implemented for diabetic patients, thereby potentially attenuating the prognostic utility of HGI in this subgroup. Previous studies have yielded conflicting results regarding the prognostic value of HGI in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations. Cao et al. demonstrated an association between HGI and 30-day mortality in patients with myocardial infarction, regardless of diabetes status [24]. Liu et al. reported a more pronounced relationship between HGI and outcomes in non-diabetic patients compared to diabetic patients with AKI [48]. Conversely, Cheng et al. found that among patients with acute decompensated heart failure, HGI was linked to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in diabetic patients but not in non-diabetic patients [49]. These findings indicated significant variability in the relationship between diabetes status and HGI across different patient populations, highlighting the need for further research to clarify how diabetes or the absence thereof influences the prognostic value of HGI in patients with hyperlipidemia. Additionally, our analysis identified an interaction between HGI and in-hospital and 28-day mortality within age subgroups, revealing that a higher HGI was significantly associated with a reduced risk of short-term mortality risk in elderly patients with hyperlipidemia (aged > 65 years). This observation underscored the clinical significance of HGI in elderly populations; however, the specific biological mechanisms linking age stratification and HGI remain elusive and necessitate further investigation. Severity of illness scores and their use in predicting outcomes have gained considerable favor worldwide and have been proven effective for predicting mortality in ICU patients [62–64]. However, it remains unclear if the addition of HGI can enhance mortality predictions in ICU patients with hyperlipidemia based on these severity scores. In this study, we examined the improvement in predicting short-term mortality for critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia by adding HGI to existing risk scores. The results demonstrated that incorporating HGI consistently enhanced the predictive ability of all evaluated models.

However, we must acknowledge several limitations. First, despite employing multivariable adjustments and conducting subgroup analyses to mitigate potential confounding factors, the single-center design of our study may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Second, HGI was only assessed at the time of patient admission, whereas continuous glucose monitoring during ICU admissions could provide valuable insights into dynamic monitoring of the HGI and their relationship with outcomes. Third, we only used MIMIC-IV database from a U.S. population for the study, which may limit our result extrapolating to other countries or ethnic groups. Fourth, the study excluded patients lacking data on HbA1c or FBG upon first-day ICU admission, which could introduce a selection bias towards patients with diabetes or those suspected of having diabetes, limiting the generalizability of the study’s findings to the broader population of patients with hyperlipidemia. Fifth, Given the database limitations, it was not possible to confirm whether all glucose measurements were obtained from fasting individuals. Finally, while this study focused on short-term mortality as the primary outcome, it did not assess cardiovascular-specific mortality or other endpoints, such as readmission rates and complications. Future research should expand analyses to these outcomes using larger cohorts or extend follow-up periods.

Conclusions

This study highlighted the HGI as a potential predictor of short-term mortality in critically ill patients with hyperlipidemia and exhibited a negative linear relationship. More large-scale studies could be conducted to assess whether recognition or intervening on decreased HGI can yield enhanced clinical outcomes in these populations.
