The effect of internet-based acceptance and commitment therapy on sleep quality and insomnia in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jiaying Fu, Xiaohong Zhang, Yufei Qiu, Qianqian Luo, Rao Liu, Jiali Liu, Yake Yue, Juan Gu, Mengjie Tong, Yong Zhou, Fen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsomnia and poor sleep quality are common sleep-related problems in adults and are associated with impaired quality of life, psychological distress, and increased physical health burden. Although cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as the first-line non-pharmacological treatment for chronic insomnia, internet-based Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (iACT) has increasingly been explored as an alternative or complementary digital psychological intervention for improving sleep-related outcomes. To systematically evaluate the effects of iACT on sleep quality and insomnia-related outcomes in adults. PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and Embase were systematically searched from inception to December 6, 2025. We included randomized controlled trials evaluating internet-based Acceptance and Commitment Therapy in adults that reported validated outcomes for sleep quality or insomnia. Standardized mean differences were pooled using a random-effects model, and risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Eight randomized controlled trials met the inclusion criteria. Five studies were included in the meta-analysis of sleep quality outcomes and three in the meta-analysis of insomnia outcomes. Compared with passive control conditions, iACT was associated with better sleep quality outcomes (SMD = − 0.92, 95% CI − 1.45 to − 0.38; p < 0.001) and reduced insomnia symptoms (SMD = − 0.40, 95% CI − 0.64 to − 0.16; p < 0.001). Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested possible variation in sleep quality outcomes according to intervention duration, participant characteristics, and delivery format, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously. iACT may have potential to improve sleep quality and insomnia-related outcomes in adults. However, the available evidence remains limited by heterogeneous populations, intervention characteristics, delivery formats, and overall low certainty of evidence. Introduction

Sleep problems are among the most common health complaints in adults, with insomnia and poor sleep quality being particularly prevalent and burdensome1,2. Insomnia, one of the most common sleep disorders, is characterized by difficulty initiating sleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening accompanied by daytime impairment3,4. Epidemiological studies suggest that approximately 10% −15% of adults experience chronic insomnia, while 25% −35% experience occasional or transient insomnia symptoms5. Both insomnia and poor sleep quality are associated with impaired daytime functioning and increased psychological distress, including anxiety and depressive symptoms6,7. They are also linked to a range of adverse physical health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and diabetes8–11. In addition, sleep-related problems impose considerable economic costs through increased healthcare utilization and reduced work productivity12,13. Given the high prevalence and multidimensional burden of sleep-related problems, identifying safe, accessible, and effective non-pharmacological interventions remains an important public health priority.

Current treatments for insomnia and related sleep complaints mainly include pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches3. Sedative-hypnotic drugs (such as benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepine drugs) can improve sleep in the short term, but their long-term use may bring adverse reactions such as tolerance, dependence, cognitive function impairment, and the risk of falls14–16. As a result, non-pharmacological interventions have become increasingly important in the management of persistent sleep problems. Among these, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as the first-line treatment for adults with chronic insomnia and has demonstrated robust efficacy in meta-analytic evidence17,18. CBT-I typically includes a combination of techniques, such as cognitive restructuring, stimulus control, sleep restriction, relaxation training, and sleep hygiene education, with the aim of modifying maladaptive sleep-related cognitions and behaviors and restoring healthier sleep patterns19–21. However, psychological treatments remain unavailable or difficult to access for some individuals because of practical barriers such as financial cost, limited provider availability, and time demands22. Moreover, even when patients report that they are following CBT-I principles, closer assessment often reveals lapses in adherence to behavioral recommendations23. Thus, although CBT-I remains the standard treatment, its mechanism and implementation demands may not fully address the needs of all adults with sleep-related difficulties.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) is a third-wave cognitive behavioral therapy grounded in contextual behavioral science that emphasizes psychological flexibility and encourages individuals to respond to internal experiences in an accepting rather than controlling manner while engaging in meaningful actions guided by personal values24. The development and persistence of sleep-related difficulties, most notably insomnia, are closely linked not only to maladaptive sleep behaviours, but also to excessive efforts to control sleep, heightened concerns regarding the consequences of insufficient sleep, and persistent attempts to regulate wakefulness and physiological arousal25. These processes closely align with the core therapeutic targets of ACT, which focuses on reducing experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion while promoting acceptance and cognitive defusion in response to distressing thoughts and bodily sensations24,26. In this sense, ACT offers a conceptually distinct therapeutic pathway for sleep-related difficulties. Rather than directly aiming to eliminate insomnia symptoms, ACT seeks to reduce individuals’ struggle with sleep-related internal experiences by enhancing acceptance, cognitive defusion, and values-based action24–27. This therapeutic orientation may be particularly relevant for individuals with high levels of sleep-related worry, perfectionistic tendencies, experiential avoidance, or broader emotional distress, and may also be more acceptable to patients who find highly structured behavioral prescriptions difficult to sustain. In recent years, ACT has increasingly been applied in interventions for insomnia, and existing studies suggest that ACT delivered in face-to-face, group-based, or self-help formats may improve insomnia severity, sleep quality, and related emotional distress to some extent28. This suggests that ACT may provide a different psychological treatment path for insomnia treatment from traditional CBT-I.

With the development of digital health technologies, ACT has increasingly been adapted into internet-delivered formats, with emerging evidence supporting its efficacy and acceptability for chronic pain and transdiagnostic mental health outcomes such as depressive and anxiety symptoms and psychological distress29,30. Compared with traditional face-to-face treatment, internet-delivered ACT may improve treatment accessibility by reducing time and geographical constraints, and may offer potential cost-effectiveness in some clinical contexts31. Moreover, internet-based ACT has been shown to influence ACT-related psychological process variables, including psychological flexibility, mindfulness, valued living, and cognitive defusion32. In the field of sleep, randomized controlled and online self-help studies have begun to examine the use of iACT in insomnia and related sleep problems, providing preliminary support for its possible role in reducing insomnia symptoms33.

Although iACT has shown promise in improving insomnia and related sleep problems, the current literature remains inconsistent in two key respects. First, studies vary in their target populations and intervention focus: some include individuals with formal or subclinical insomnia, whereas others assess sleep outcomes in broader clinical or occupational populations, and not all interventions are specifically designed to target sleep problems. Second, there is substantial heterogeneity in study design and outcome assessment, including differences in delivery format, therapist support, follow-up duration, and sleep-related outcome measures. These inconsistencies limit the comparability and interpretability of the evidence. Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis is needed to synthesize the available randomized controlled trials and provide clearer evidence for clinical practice and future research.

Method

The Cochrane Interventional Measures Systematic Review Manual34 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) criteria35 were followed in the conduct of this investigation. The review protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251250721). This study did not have a previously published protocol.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and Embase from database inception to December 6, 2025. The search strategy combined terms related to sleep problems (i.e., insomnia, sleep disorders, sleep problems), intervention methods (internet-based or web-based or online or self-help or digital or mobile phone or smartphone-based ACT), and study design as randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Additionally, the reference list of previous reviews on iACT intervention measures was manually retrieved. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A study was included in the analysis if it satisfied the following requirements: (1) Participants: adults aged 18 years or older, including individuals with insomnia, subclinical insomnia, poor sleep quality, or sleep-related outcomes assessed within broader clinical or occupational populations; (2) Intervention: Internet-based Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (iACT) delivered through online platforms, mobile applications, or other digital media, with Acceptance and Commitment Therapy being the core treatment component; (3) Control group: Passive control group (such as waiting list, routine care, no treatment); (4) Outcome indicators: The study should report at least one outcome related to sleep quality or insomnia, assessed using validated tools; (5) Study design: RCTs.

We excluded studies involving participants with severe mental disorders (e.g., psychosis), primary sleep disorders other than insomnia (e.g., primary sleep disorders other than insomnia (e.g., sleep apnea), or other conditions that could substantially confound the assessment of sleep-related outcomes.

Data extraction

This study was independently completed by two authors (JYF and QQL) for the systematic search of multiple databases, using the preset search strategy to screen relevant literature. All retrieved literature was centrally managed using EndNote 21 (Clarivate) and duplicate literature was removed. Subsequently, the relevance of the literature was evaluated based on the title and abstract, and if there were disagreements, discussions were held until consensus was reached. On this basis, full-text retrieval and review of the initially qualified literature were conducted (JYF and QQL). Finally, the inclusion of literature was determined based on the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. During the review process, if there were inconsistent opinions, a third reviewer (FY) participated in the decision-making. The data extraction of included literature was also independently executed and mutually checked by two reviewers, and the extracted information mainly included: first author, publication year, country, sample size, average age, baseline data, subject characteristics, intervention content, and outcome measure tools.

Quality assessment

The updated Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2, version 2) method was used to evaluate each study’s risk of bias36. Five different domains of risk of bias were evaluated, including the randomization process, deviation from the expected intervention, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reporting results.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Stata (version 17.0; StataCorp) and Review Manager (version 5.4; Cochrane Collaboration). Standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to pool sleep-related outcomes measured with different instruments37. The random effects model was used in a meta-analysis38. The statistical heterogeneity between the studies was evaluated using the I2 statistic. The magnitude of heterogeneity was determined based on the I² value: 25% was considered low, 50% moderate, and 75% high39. This study also performed subgroup analyses to investigate potential explanations for the heterogeneity in order to determine the likely sources of variability among the studies. In order to evaluate the robustness of the research findings, sensitivity analysis was also carried out by gradually removing the included studies40. Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed because the number of studies included in each meta-analysis was small. Methods such as funnel plots and Egger’s test are generally considered unreliable when the number of studies is limited, typically fewer than 10 studies41.

Results

Study selection

A total of 3,737 records were retrieved from the five databases, 988 duplicates were removed, and 2749 records were retained. 2672 items with obviously inconsistent titles and abstracts were screened out. The remaining 77 articles were downloaded for full-text reading to evaluate their eligibility. Among them, 69 articles were excluded, as follows: not RCT (n = 8), research protocol (n = 5), not iACT (n = 30), full text unavailable (n = 2), not eligible comparators (n = 4), incomplete relevant data (n = 4), no insomnia outcome (n = 10), Reviews (n = 2). Eventually, 8 studies were included in the final review and meta-analysis because they satisfied the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the literature screening process and results.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 1198 participants from 8 studies were involved. 572 were allocated to the control group and 626 to the iACT group at random. These studies were published between 2019 and 2025, mainly from Finland33,42,43, China44–47, and Sweden29. The smallest sample size was 72, and the largest was 323. All studies adopted a randomized controlled trial design to evaluate the intervention effect of Internet-based Acceptance and Commitment Therapy on insomnia. Among the included studies, five contributed data to the meta-analysis of sleep quality outcomes, whereas three contributed data to the meta-analysis of insomnia outcomes. The baseline severity of insomnia was reported inconsistently in the studies. The intervention duration ranged from 5 weeks to 14 weeks. Literature quality evaluation

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool Version 2 was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies (Figs. 2 and 3). Overall risk of bias was assessed in 5 domains, with 3 studies rated as low (37.50%) and 5 as moderate (62.50%). Significant bias risk was detected in the measurement of the outcome domain, mainly due to the use of self-report questionnaires as the main outcome in the studies. All evaluations were independently completed by two authors (JYF and QQL), and any disagreements were resolved by the corresponding author (FY) to ensure the reliability of the evaluation process. Overall, the credibility of the evidence included in this meta-analysis was considered relatively low (Tables 2 and 3).

Treatment analysis

Sleep quality

A total of 5 RCTs were included in the meta-analysis of sleep quality outcomes. The pooled results suggested that iACT was associated with better sleep quality outcomes compared with control conditions (SMD = − 0.92, 95% CI − 1.45 to − 0.38; p < 0.001). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 92.30%; p < 0.001; Fig. 4).

Forest plot of the meta-analysis of sleep quality.

To further examine potential heterogeneity, exploratory subgroup analyses were carried out according to intervention duration, participant characteristics, and delivery format. Because only a small number of studies were available within each subgroup, these findings should be viewed cautiously and treated as preliminary signals rather than confirmatory evidence. In terms of intervention duration, studies lasting no more than 1 month yielded a pooled estimate of SMD = − 1.52 (95% CI − 1.74 to − 1.30; p < 0.001), whereas studies with longer durations yielded a pooled estimate of SMD = − 0.51 (95% CI − 0.81 to − 0.21; p < 0.001). With regard to participant characteristics, studies involving nurses showed an effect estimate of SMD = − 1.27 (95% CI − 1.78 to − 0.76; p < 0.001), while studies involving adults with sleep-related problems showed an estimate of SMD = − 0.33 (95% CI − 0.65 to − 0.02; p = 0.040). For delivery format, the guided subgroup, which was represented by a single study, showed an effect estimate of SMD = − 1.63 (95% CI − 1.89 to − 1.38; p < 0.001), whereas self-help interventions showed an estimate of SMD = − 0.73 (95% CI − 1.24 to − 0.22; p = 0.005, Table 4). Overall, the current data do not support definitive conclusions about genuine subgroup differences.

Insomnia

A total of 3 RCTs were included in the meta-analysis of insomnia outcomes. The pooled results showed that iACT reduced insomnia symptoms compared with control conditions (SMD = − 0.40, 95% CI − 0.64 to − 0.16; p = 0.001; Fig. 5). The random-effects model analysis revealed no significant heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 0.00%; p = 0.50; Fig. 5), indicating relatively consistent effects across studies.

Forest plot of the meta-analysis of insomnia.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the influence of individual studies on the overall effect size, sensitivity analyses were performed for both sleep quality and insomnia outcomes by sequentially excluding each study. After each exclusion, the pooled effect remained in the same direction for both outcomes (Figs. 6 and 7), suggesting that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall results. These findings support the robustness of the pooled estimates.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 8 randomized controlled trials on the effects of internet-based Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (iACT) on sleep quality and insomnia in adults. The pooled findings suggested that iACT may improve sleep quality and reduce insomnia symptoms compared with passive control conditions. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the included studies were heterogeneous in their populations, intervention characteristics, delivery formats, and outcome measures, and the overall certainty of evidence was low. Accordingly, the present review provides preliminary rather than definitive evidence for the role of iACT in improving sleep quality and insomnia.

A plausible explanation for the observed improvements in sleep quality and insomnia is that iACT may work by interrupting the cognitive-emotional processes that help maintain sleep difficulties. Contemporary models of insomnia suggest that poor sleep is often sustained not only by the sleep disturbance itself, but also by repeated efforts to control sleep, heightened monitoring of wakefulness and fatigue, and persistent worry about the consequences of insufficient sleep25,48. These responses may paradoxically increase pre-sleep arousal and further impair both subjective sleep quality and insomnia symptoms49. In this context, ACT-based interventions may be beneficial because they encourage individuals to reduce their struggle with sleep-related internal experiences rather than intensify attempts to eliminate them. This interpretation is supported by prior intervention research showing that ACT can improve sleep quality while reducing experiential avoidance and difficulties in emotion regulation50, as well as by recent mechanism-oriented evidence indicating that online ACT may alleviate subjective sleep complaints through reductions in thought suppression and depressive symptoms42. iACT delivers ACT-based therapeutic components through internet-based formats, which may improve intervention accessibility and scalability51.

The exploratory subgroup analyses may provide some preliminary clues regarding possible sources of heterogeneity, but they should be interpreted cautiously.

Exploratory subgroup analyses of sleep quality suggested a possible difference according to intervention duration, with shorter interventions showing a numerically larger pooled effect than longer interventions. However, because each subgroup contained only a small number of trials, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence that shorter interventions are preferable. It may reflect random variation, study-level differences, or limited statistical power. Some previous studies may provide relevant context for this observation. For example, prior research has suggested that adherence to behavioral sleep recommendations is an important factor associated with outcomes in behavioral sleep interventions52,53, and that maintaining structured treatment recommendations over time may be difficult for some individuals, particularly when fatigue or low motivation is present54. At the same time, existing evidence does not necessarily indicate that shorter ACT-based interventions are superior. Some brief internet-delivered ACT studies have reported improvements in sleep-related outcomes over six-week treatment periods42, while ACT-based insomnia interventions have also shown sustained benefits at three- or six-month follow-up55. Therefore, the pattern observed in the present review may reflect not only intervention duration itself, but also differences in adherence, intervention burden, and treatment completion. Further studies are needed before any firm conclusions can be drawn regarding the role of intervention duration in sleep quality outcomes.

The exploratory subgroup analysis of sleep quality suggested that studies conducted among nurses with occupational stress or poor sleep quality yielded a numerically larger pooled effect than studies conducted in general adult samples with sleep-related problems. This pattern may be partly related to the distinctive occupational and psychosocial context of nurses. Sleep problems in this population are often intertwined with shift work, irregular work schedules, high workload, occupational stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and stress-related arousal56–58. These factors may create a clinical profile in which sleep disturbance is closely linked to broader cognitive-emotional processes, including rumination, emotional exhaustion, and difficulty disengaging from work-related stress45. ACT may therefore be relevant for this population because it targets individuals’ responses to distressing internal experiences rather than focusing solely on symptom elimination. In addition, as health-care professionals, nurses may be more familiar with structured health-related information and self-management-oriented programs, which could facilitate engagement with digital intervention content; however, such engagement may still depend on digital health literacy, perceived usability, and intervention acceptability59. Moreover, the studies included in this review enrolled participants with poor sleep quality or marked occupational strain, which may have provided greater room for observable improvement. Nevertheless, given the small number of studies within each subgroup and differences in intervention format, comparator conditions, baseline symptom severity, and outcome measurement, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Further research is needed to clarify whether participant characteristics moderate the effects of iACT on sleep quality outcomes.

In the present review, the exploratory subgroup analysis suggested a numerically larger pooled effect on sleep quality in guided formats than in self-help formats. Existing evidence suggests that human guidance may enhance adherence, sustain engagement, reduce attrition, and support completion of treatment programs60,61. In behavioral sleep interventions, adherence is important because benefits often depend on repeated practice and sustained implementation of therapeutic strategies52,62. More broadly, human guidance in digital psychological interventions may enhance adherence, reduce attrition, and support continued engagement60, whereas self-help online ACT formats may rely more heavily on participants’ autonomous motivation and sustained use of intervention materials63. Therefore, the numerically larger effect observed in Guided formats may reflect greater support for participants’ engagement with and implementation of ACT-based content, rather than differences in the core ACT principles themselves. Given the very limited number of studies in this subgroup, we emphasize that this result represents only an exploratory descriptive observation and must be interpreted with caution.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. First, it focuses specifically on iACT and provides a quantitative synthesis of randomized evidence on sleep quality and insomnia in adults. Second, the review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and the protocol was registered in PROSPERO, which enhanced the transparency and methodological rigor of the research process. Third, the certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach, which strengthened the overall assessment of the findings. In addition, the review explored potential sources of heterogeneity through subgroup analyses, providing directions for future study design and population selection.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the number of included studies was small, which limited statistical power, constrained the reliability of subgroup analyses, and precluded formal assessment of reporting bias. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed in the meta-analysis of sleep quality outcomes, suggesting that the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution rather than as a uniform effect across studies. This heterogeneity may be related to differences in target populations, intervention focus, delivery format, therapist support, baseline sleep status, and outcome measures. In addition, not all included studies enrolled participants with clinically diagnosed insomnia; several studies included broader populations, such as nurses with poor sleep quality, adults with persistent physical symptoms, patients with lung cancer, or adults with chronic pain. Therefore, the findings should not be directly generalized to clinically diagnosed insomnia populations, and future studies should more clearly distinguish insomnia-specific populations from broader sleep-related populations. Finally, most studies relied on self-report sleep measures and did not include objective sleep indicators, which may increase susceptibility to measurement bias. Finally, follow-up periods were limited, and the long-term sustainability of the observed effects remains uncertain.

Conclusions

In summary, iACT may improve sleep quality and reduce insomnia symptoms in adults. However, the available evidence remains limited by substantial heterogeneity in study populations, intervention characteristics, delivery formats, and outcome measures, as well as by the inclusion of some non-insomnia-specific populations and the overall low certainty of evidence. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary rather than definitive. Future well-designed randomized controlled trials with more clearly defined target populations, more consistent intervention protocols, and longer follow-up periods are needed to further clarify the role of iACT in improving sleep quality and insomnia.
