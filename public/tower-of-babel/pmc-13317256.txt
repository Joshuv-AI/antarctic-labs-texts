Relationship between the advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) and all-cause and cause-specific mortality among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD): a population-based study
Authors: Zhuanbo Luo, Peixu Chen, Luting Chen, Hongying Ma, Chao Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is viewed as a significant health problem, and the prognosis of patients with this disease is strongly influenced by the inflammatory response and nutritional well-being of the body. The advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) provides a complete measurement of these two factors. Although the ALI has promising applications, its relationship with the prognosis of COPD patients remains unexplored. This research sought to bridge this knowledge gap by investigating the connection between the ALI and the outcomes of COPD patients.MethodsThis study retrospectively investigated 2,884 COPD patients who were admitted to the respiratory department because of acute exacerbation. The study period extends from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2022. All these COPD patients subsequently received follow-up, and the cause-specific and all-cause mortality of these patients was reported. Kaplan‒Meier analysis was employed to investigate the associations of the ALI with all-cause mortality and mortality resulting from specific causes among COPD patients. In addition, univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to explore this association in further detail after adjusting for various confounding variables. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to assess the nonlinear relationships of the ALI with all-cause and cause-specific death rates among COPD patients. In addition, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the validity of the findings.ResultsIn total, 2,884 patients with COPD were recruited. A greater ALI was strongly associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality and mortality resulting from respiratory and cardiovascular illnesses specifically among patients with COPD. The findings of the RCS analysis indicated a reverse J-shaped, nonlinear relationship between ALI and all-cause mortality among COPD patients, and an inflection point was identified at 95 (p for nonlinearity <0.0001). The inflection point of the J-shaped pattern indicates the ALI that is associated with the lowest risk of mortality. For ALIs less than 95, an increase of 10 units in the ALI was associated with a 14% reduction in the possibility of all-cause mortality (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.81–0.92; p for trend=0.01). However, when the ALI was greater than 95, a 10-unit increase in the ALI resulted in a 5% increase in the likelihood of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.01–1.07; Ptrend=0.01). Similar J-shaped patterns were observed for deaths related to cardiovascular and respiratory illnesses, in which context the inflection points were 97 and 96, respectively. These findings were consistent across various medical history and demographic subgroups and remained stable during the sensitivity analysis.ConclusionThis study revealed a unique relationship between a high ALI and a low risk of death among COPD patients. Additionally, the relationships between the ALI and mortality (both all-cause and from specific causes) exhibited nonlinear, J-shaped patterns. These findings indicate the potential for maintaining ALI within a specific range to improve long-term survival outcomes for patients with COPD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04324-w.Keywords: ALI, COPD, All-cause mortality, Cause-specific mortality, Inflammation, Nutritional status

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a prevalent chronic lung condition that involves ongoing airflow restriction because of abnormalities in the airways and alveoli, resulting in persistent respiratory symptoms such as coughing, phlegm production, and difficulty breathing [1]. COPD is now a significant health problem worldwide, causing substantial medical, societal, and financial challenges [2, 3]. In 2015, COPD led to approximately 3 million deaths and thus represented the third most common cause of death worldwide [4]. Therefore, it is crucial to promptly identify COPD patients at high risk of mortality to address this critical clinical concern.

The development of COPD is a multifaceted process influenced by a combination of genetic and environmental factors [5]. One of the main drivers of COPD progression is the inflammatory immune response, which triggers a cascade of cellular reactions and serves as a crucial mechanism [6]. Many inflammatory cells migrate to the airways and become activated, which in turn contributes to triggering and intensifying the immune and inflammatory responses of the body [5]. Previous studies have shown that the presence of these cells may damage structural cells of the airways, such as epithelial cells, parenchymal cells and matrix cells, with the extent of COPD closely tied to the degree of inflammation in the airways [7]. In the development of COPD, neutrophils are key players, serving as the body’s initial line of defense against lung infections by releasing signaling molecules that recruit other cells from the immune system, including monocytes or macrophages, to the location of infection, triggering a broader immune response [8, 9]. Elevated neutrophil counts are a common characteristic among all individuals with COPD. Previous studies have indicated that higher levels of inflammatory markers, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), could be associated with an increased likelihood of more severe symptoms and death in COPD patients [10, 11]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation can also have far-reaching consequences, contributing to decreased albumin levels, weight loss, and insulin resistance, a condition triggered by inflammatory substances such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and C-reactive protein (CRP) [12, 13]. Notably, nutritional indicators such as albumin levels and body mass index (BMI) have been reported to be closely linked to the outcomes of people with COPD and asthma [14, 15]. As such, inflammation can affect the prognosis of individuals with COPD both directly and indirectly by influencing their albumin level and BMI. Thus, using markers of inflammation alone to predict the outcome of COPD patients might not be adequate, which emphasizes the importance of developing new, inclusive measures for assessing patient outcomes.

In 2013, Jafri and his colleagues identified the advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) as a novel approach for predicting outcomes in individuals with advanced lung cancer. By considering factors such as BMI, albumin levels, and the NLR, this index provides valuable information about the inflammatory and nutritional conditions of patients. Originally intended for predicting tumor progression in lung cancer patients, multiple studies have validated its effectiveness in predicting the prognosis of those with lung cancer [16–18]. Moreover, the ALI has demonstrated potential in predicting outcomes in patients with a range of other conditions, such as gastric cancer [19], diabetes [20], heart failure [21], B-cell lymphoma [22], and esophageal cancer [23]. Nevertheless, extensive research on how the ALI is related to the outcomes of individuals with COPD is lacking.

To bridge this knowledge gap, this research investigated the relationship of the ALI with the risk of mortality from all causes, as well as from specific causes, among individuals with COPD and to provide guidance on optimal treatment and care strategies for these patients.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study was performed at the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University, involving patients with COPD who experienced acute exacerbations requiring hospitalization between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2022. In cases when patients had multiple hospitalizations during this period, only data from the initial admission were collected. The study focused on adults aged 20 and older with a confirmed COPD diagnosis, complete data to calculate the ALI, and available mortality records. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University (2025-069RS) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was conducted in accordance with local legislation and institutional requirements. The Ethics Review Committee waived the requirement of written informed consent for participation from the participants or their immediate relatives because of the retrospective nature of the study.

First, data were collected from 3,322 individuals. Of these individuals, 31 were removed because they did not provide information on albumin, BMI, neutrophils, or lymphocytes. Another 246 participants with conditions such as active tuberculosis, asthma, bronchiectasis, malignancy, connective tissue disease, liver failure, or renal failure were also excluded. Furthermore, 15 participants were eliminated from the study because of incomplete information on their follow-up duration and survival outcomes, while another 146 patients lacked necessary data on relevant covariates, leading to their exclusion from the analysis. As a result, the final study sample consisted of 2,884 participants who met the eligibility criteria. A step-by-step overview of the participant selection process and the criteria used for exclusion and inclusion can be found in Fig. 1. COPD was defined as a ratio of less than 0.70 between the forced expiratory volume in 1 s after bronchodilator treatment (FEV1) and the forced vital capacity (FVC) [24].

Determination of the ALI

The ALI was computed by considering three key indicators: the albumin level (Alb) (expressed in grams per deciliter), BMI, and NLR. The calculation involved multiplying the Alb level by the BMI and then dividing the result by the NLR (ALI = Alb*BMI/NLR). COPD patients were then divided into four categories based on their ALI scores: quartile 1, 2, 3, and 4.

Assessment of mortality

This study focused on mortality rates as the main result, covering deaths from all possible causes and two specific causes. The study categorized cause-specific mortality according to the ICD-10 classification system, specifically analyzing deaths related to cardiovascular conditions (classified under codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69) and respiratory conditions (classified under codes J09-J18, J40-J47). The information was gathered from telephone follow-ups or electronic medical records until December 31, 2023. Following a review of all medical records, COPD patients were monitored after being discharged from the hospital. The study’s observation period started the moment they were enrolled and ended on the day of their death or on the 31st of December in 2023 in the case of those who were still alive.

Definitions of covariates

To minimize the impact of confounding variables, we intentionally selected relevant factors based on their clinical significance and previous studies. We gathered baseline data on participants through surveys and medical evaluations, which included details such as age, sex (male or female), education level (beyond high school, high school graduate, or less than high school), and BMI categories (< 25.0, 25.0–29.9, or > 30.0 kg/m2). Additionally, we investigated the marital status of the participants and divided them into three categories: married or living with a partner, widowed or divorced, and never married. Similarly, we classified participants’ smoking behaviors as never smoked, former smoker, or current smoker, based on their self-reported lifetime cigarette consumption [25]. Their drinking habits were also divided into never drank, former drinker, or current drinker, with specific criteria for each category based on how often and how much alcohol was consumed [26, 27].

Pulmonary function was evaluated through spirometry once patients were in stable condition and could perform a spirometer test prior to discharge from the hospital. Patients were also tested for airflow reversibility by administering a short-acting beta-2 agonist (SABA). Upon admission to the hospital, arterial blood samples were promptly obtained and examined from patients with COPD. Additionally, crucial measurements, including complete blood cell count parameters and Alb levels, which are necessary for determining the ALI, were also collected on the first day of hospitalization.

The presence of specific medical conditions, such as diabetes mellitus (DM), high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease (CVD), was also determined. The presence of diabetes was validated through the diagnosis of a doctor, a random blood glucose concentration of 11.1 mmol/L or higher, a two-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) result of 11.1 mmol/L or higher, or the use of diabetes medication or insulin [28]. Hypertension was defined as having a systolic blood pressure of 130 mmHg or above or a diastolic blood pressure of 80 mmHg or above or being on medication for managing blood pressure [28]. People with a medical background of heart failure, heart attack, coronary heart disease or angina as confirmed by a doctor were classified as having cardiovascular disease [28].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in this study were performed with R software (version 4.3.3). A p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as the means with standard deviations, whereas nonnormally distributed data are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. The results concerning categorical data are presented in terms of the number of occurrences and proportion of the total. To determine whether differences between groups were statistically significant, categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, whereas continuous variables were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

We utilized Kaplan‒Meier analysis to investigate the connection between the ALI and mortality rates in patients with COPD. The patients were divided into four groups based on the ALI, and their survival rates were compared using log-rank tests. Furthermore, both univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to delve deeper into the association of the ALI with all-cause mortality and two specific causes of mortality among patients with COPD while controlling for potential confounding variables. The analysis yielded hazard ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Three analytical models were developed: a crude model that was not adjusted; model 1, which accounted for demographic factors, including sex, age, and education; and model 2, which provided a more detailed analysis by building upon model 1 and incorporating additional variables, including lifestyle habits, marital status, body mass index, and medical history of high blood pressure, heart disease, and diabetes.

We employed a multifaceted approach, combining restricted cubic spline (RCS) and multivariable Cox regression with adjustments, to investigate the nonlinear relationships of the ALI with the mortality rates due to all causes and specific causes among individuals with COPD. When nonlinear relationships were detected, a recursive algorithm was used to identify the inflection points. To investigate the relationships between specific thresholds and outcomes in further detail, piecewise Cox regression models were used. We also conducted a multivariable Cox regression analysis in which each participant’s ALI score was divided by 10 and treated as a continuous variable, allowing us to evaluate the effect of a 10-unit increase in the ALI on the mortality rates from all causes, cardiovascular diseases and respiratory diseases in patients with COPD. Through this analysis, we aimed to offer more detailed insight into how alterations in the ALI affect the prognosis of individuals with COPD, considering both quartile ALIs and every 10-unit increase in the ALI. Our results could assist healthcare professionals in making informed decisions and dynamically assessing the prognosis of COPD patients based on their ALI.

To validate our results, we performed sensitivity analyses, including subgroup and interaction analyses, to investigate possible interactions between ALI levels and stratified variables. We also excluded patients who passed away during the initial two years of observation to reduce the impact of reverse causality bias. Furthermore, we focused the study specifically on individuals aged 45 and above to examine the connection between the ALI and mortality rates among patients with COPD.

Significant differences were observed when the different ALI quartile groups were compared. In contrast to the lowest quartile (Q1), the higher quartiles (Q2, Q3, and Q4) generally comprised younger individuals with higher BMIs, a greater percentage of males, and a greater proportion of individuals who had never smoked. Laboratory findings also revealed distinct patterns, with higher quartiles exhibiting elevated PaO2, albumin, lymphocyte, platelet, and ALI levels but decreased PaCO2, white blood cell, neutrophil, and NLR levels. In terms of pulmonary function, these groups had significantly higher values for FEV1, FEV1 (%pred) and FEV1/FVC. Moreover, higher ALIs were associated with a lower incidence of cardiovascular disease and diabetes. Kaplan‒Meier analysis

In this research, the Kaplan‒Meier method was used to investigate the connection between ALI and death rates from various causes in COPD patients. During a medium observation period of 3.57 years, there were 196 fatalities, 93 of which were attributed to cardiovascular issues and 83 of which were attributed to respiratory problems. The findings indicated that individuals with COPD in the highest ALI quartile had a significantly lower risk of mortality from all causes (log-rank test p = 0.002; Fig. 2A), as well as from respiratory diseases (log-rank test p = 0.03; Fig. 2B) and cardiovascular diseases (log-rank test p < 0.001; Fig. 2C).

The ALI and mortality

A Cox regression analysis, as presented in Table 2, was performed to investigate the relationships of the ALI with mortality rates in adults with COPD, considering all-cause, cardiovascular, and respiratory disease-related deaths. The analysis revealed a significant negative association between higher ALIs and reduced mortality rates from all causes in COPD patients, with both unadjusted and adjusted models showing a consistent trend. Specifically, compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the second, third, and fourth quartiles (Q2, Q3, and Q4) had hazard ratios (HRs) (95% CIs) of 0.66 (0.42,0.80), 0.51 (0.32,0.73), and 0.42 (0.30,0.90), respectively, in the fully adjusted model 2 (P for trend = 0.03). Similar findings were observed for cardiovascular disease-related mortality, with elevated ALIs predicting a reduced risk of death, as evidenced by HRs (95% CIs) of 0.66 (0.44, 0.86), 0.56 (0.47, 0.72), and 0.48 (0.38, 0.72) for Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively (P for trend = 0.001). A comparable pattern was observed for respiratory disease-related mortality, thus suggesting that higher ALIs were associated with a significantly lower risk of death from respiratory diseases, with HRs (95% CIs) of 0.69 (0.42, 0.87), 0.52 (0.33, 0.65), and 0.41 (0.30, 0.64) for Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively (P for trend = 0.001) in model 2.

Nonlinear relationships

After all relevant variables were accounted for (model 2), an RCS analysis was performed to illustrate the connection between the ALI and mortality rates from various causes in individuals with COPD. The findings revealed a nonlinear association between the ALI and overall mortality, with an inflection point at 95 (p for nonlinearity < 0.0001) (Fig. 3A). A similar J-shaped pattern was also observed for mortality attributed to cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, with notable inflection points at 97 (p for nonlinearity < 0.0001) and 96 (p for nonlinearity = 0.0006) (Figs. 3B-C), respectively.

Adjustments were made to account for sex (female or male), age (< 40, 40–60 or > 60), education level (high school, less than high school or above high school), smoking status (never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker), drinking status (current drinker, former drinker or never drinker), marital status (married/living with partner, never married or widowed/divorced), BMI (< 25.0, 25.0–29.9 or > 30.0 kg/m2), and history of hypertension (yes or no), hyperlipidemia (yes or no), cardiovascular disease (yes or no) and diabetes (yes or no)

For ALIs less than 95, a 10-point increase in the ALI corresponded to a 14% decrease in the risk of mortality from any cause. Conversely, when the ALI was above 95, a 10-point increase in the ALI was linked to a 5% increased risk of mortality from any cause. A similar trend was observed for deaths related to cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. When the ALI was below 97 and 96, a 10-point increase in the ALI was associated with a 13% and 12% lower risk of mortality due to cardiovascular and respiratory conditions, respectively. However, when the ALI exceeded these thresholds, a 10-point increase in the ALI led to a 2% and 3% greater risk of mortality related to cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, respectively. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The data presented in Table 4 reveal a substantial link between ALI quartiles and all-cause mortality among different demographic subgroups. This association was particularly pronounced in certain populations, including those aged 60 and older, individuals of both sexes, those in a marital or partnered relationship, and those with higher education levels, as well as current or former smokers, former or never alcohol users, those without hypertension or CVD, and those with a history of diabetes (Table 4). The trend was statistically significant (p < 0.05). Conversely, no significant interactions were observed between the ALI and subgroup variables regarding mortality rates in individuals with COPD, as all interaction p values exceeded 0.05. Additional subgroup analyses, detailed in Supplementary Tables S1A-B, did not reveal any significant interactions in mortality rates due to respiratory or cardiovascular diseases.

Discussion

This research first explored the connection between the ALI and mortality rates among individuals with COPD, utilizing a comprehensive and representative dataset. After accounting for various factors, analysis using the Cox model revealed that increased ALIs were linked to reduced mortality rates in COPD patients due to all causes, as well as cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. The RCS analysis suggested that the ideal ALI threshold for reducing mortality risk was 95 for overall mortality, 97 for cardiovascular-related mortality, and 96 for respiratory-related mortality. A unique J-shaped curve indicated a nonlinear association, thus revealing a beneficial relation between the ALI and mortality until the ALI reaches a certain point. More precisely, an increase of 10 units in the ALI was connected to a 14% decrease in overall mortality risk, along with decreases of 13% for cardiovascular-related mortality risk and 12% for respiratory-related mortality risk. Conversely, exceeding the inflection point was associated with increased mortality risk. The findings were consistent across various demographic subgroups and remained robust in sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that the ALI, which combines information on inflammation and nutrition, could be a useful indicator for predicting mortality risk in individuals with COPD. Moreover, clinicians can determine the optimum ALI for reducing the risk of mortality and create individualized treatment strategies by identifying the point at which the J-shaped relationship changes direction.

While reviewing the relevant literature, we observed that baseline ALI values vary significantly depending on the target disease [29]. We speculated that this was likely related to differences in nutritional status and baseline inflammation levels caused by different diseases. For example, COPD typically caused chronic, persistent, low-grade airway inflammation, whereas cancer often induced a systemic inflammatory response that was far more intense and complex than that seen in COPD, primarily through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [30]. Patients with advanced lung cancer frequently developed “pulmonary cachexia” due to increased respiratory energy expenditure and difficulty eating, manifesting as decreased BMI and serum albumin levels. Even patients with seemingly normal BMI may suffer from severe sarcopenia [31].

Persistent inflammation has been identified as a contributing factor to numerous chronic health issues, including diabetes, certain cancers, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory diseases [32]. In the lungs, sustained inflammation in the airways plays a crucial role in the onset and progression of disorders such as COPD [33]. This chronic inflammation is characterized by the triggering of inflammatory processes and the degradation of bodily tissues. COPD, a degenerative inflammatory lung disease, is strongly associated with smoking [34], which triggers persistent and gradually intensifying inflammation and immune system dysfunction in the lungs, driving the development and progression of the disease [35]. In the context of COPD, alveolar macrophages are essential for promoting inflammation by releasing chemokines that attract neutrophils and lymphocytes, thus leading to their accumulation in the airway lining [36]. Prolonged inflammation can hasten protein breakdown, leading to lower albumin levels. Furthermore, inflammation can trigger insulin resistance, decrease appetite, and hinder nutrient absorption, ultimately leading to weight loss [37, 38]. Prior studies have highlighted a strong connection between the nutritional status of individuals with COPD and their long-term outcomes [14].

The ALI evaluates an individual’s inflammation and nutritional status by considering serum albumin levels, body mass index (BMI), and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). Unlike other indices, the ALI uniquely incorporates the BMI to evaluate nutritional health, which is crucial given that a low BMI is a well-known indicator of malnutrition [39]. A previous study revealed that a low BMI prior to surgery is a significant predictor of patient outcomes in those with gastric cancer, highlighting the importance of nutritional status [40]. Similarly, presurgery albumin levels have been recognized as a reliable prognostic indicator in patients with gastric cancer, with low albumin levels being the most accurate predictor of short-term outcomes [41]. By considering both inflammation and nutrition, the ALI provides a precise prediction of outcomes in patients with disease, which has been confirmed in different illnesses, both cancer-related and noncancer-related disorders. However, its relationship with mortality risk in COPD patients was previously unknown. Our study revealed a significant link between higher ALIs and lower death rates from various causes, including cardiovascular and respiratory conditions, among individuals with COPD. This correlation remained steady across the various subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses, thus emphasizing the reliability of the ALI as a tool for predicting COPD patient outcomes.

Initial analysis revealed that the relationship between the ALI and mortality in patients with COPD followed a nonlinear, inverted J-shaped pattern. This connection can be viewed from three key angles. First, the NLR serves as an indicator of the body’s inflammatory response, with elevated neutrophil levels suggesting widespread inflammation and decreased lymphocyte levels indicating impaired immune regulation. As a result, higher NLRs may provide insight into the immune system’s condition during ongoing inflammation [42]. In a prospective study by Lee et al., the NLR was found to be a reliable predictor of COPD exacerbation and hospitalization due to respiratory problems among COPD patients [43]. Paliogiannis and colleagues reviewed previous studies and emphasized the utility of the NLR in predicting acute exacerbation of COPD and mortality [44]. Notably, the general population typically has an average NLR of 1.65 to 2.11 [45, 46], whereas the mean NLR of the COPD patients in this study was 2.53. Leukocytes that have been activated cause the release of reactive oxygen species via neutrophils and cytokines, exacerbating inflammation throughout the body [47]. These data collectively suggest that patients with COPD who have elevated NLRs are more likely to experience poor outcomes. Our research revealed that as the NLR decreased from the highest group (Q1) to the lowest group (Q4), corresponding declines occurred in overall mortality rates and deaths specifically attributed to cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. As a result, our analysis of the ALI led us to hypothesize that a lower NLR was the main factor contributing to the long-term decrease in mortality rates, both overall and specific to certain causes, among individuals with COPD.

Second, albumin has traditionally served as an indicator for assessing an individual’s nutritional well-being. Studies have previously established a link between decreased albumin levels and an increased incidence of complications in patients with COPD and asthma [14, 15]. Notably, albumin has been shown to possess anti-inflammatory properties, which can significantly affect patient outcomes. Research has shown that individuals with higher albumin levels tend to have lower levels of proinflammatory markers, such as TNF and CRP, than those with lower albumin levels have [48]. As a result, elevated albumin levels not only reduce the risk of complications in patients with COPD but also contribute to a more favorable prognosis by mitigating inflammation. This research revealed a steady increase in albumin across the quartiles from Q1 to Q4, which corresponded to a continuous decline in overall mortality and mortality related to cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. As a result, the hypothesis emerged that elevated albumin levels in the ALI primarily contributed to a sustained decrease in mortality risk for COPD patients from both overall and specific causes.

Moreover, the BMI is frequently utilized as an indicator to evaluate a person’s nutritional well-being. A BMI of 30 or above is typically used to classify individuals as obese. Studies have consistently reported that obesity poses a substantial risk for chronic respiratory disorders such as asthma and COPD [49, 50]. Nevertheless, the relationship between BMI and health outcomes in patients with chronic respiratory diseases is complex and has been a topic of debate. Interestingly, some studies suggest that obesity may have a protective effect against mortality in patients with COPD, a phenomenon known as the “obesity paradox” [51, 52]. Research on the relationship between obesity and death rates in people with COPD has yielded inconsistent and contradictory results [53]. Various hypotheses have been proposed to elucidate the “obesity paradox”, but a consensus remains elusive. A prevailing explanation suggests that current obesity measures may not adequately differentiate between body fat and muscle mass [52, 54]. An alternative perspective attributes the obesity paradox to the influence of external factors, such as smoking, with research suggesting that the obesity paradox is not observed in nonsmokers [55]. Additionally, several investigations overlook potential biases associated with varying levels of obesity. Some studies have demonstrated that the connection between BMI and COPD outcomes is more complex and multifaceted than a straightforward causal link. A comprehensive review of existing studies revealed that the link between BMI and mortality rates in COPD patients follows a nonlinear pattern, with a BMI of 30 kg/m2 corresponding to the lowest mortality rate [56]. Interestingly, a BMI above 32 kg/m2 did not appear to have any significant effect on mortality [56]. Similar findings were reported in another study, which reported a BMI of 32.4 kg/m2 as the point at which mortality rates were lowest [57]. For patients with a BMI below this threshold, all-cause mortality rates decreased as the BMI increased, but above this point, no correlation was observed [57]. But a recent study indicated that when BMI exceeds approximately 35 kg/m², the protective effect on respiratory mortality disappeared, and the risk began to increase [58]. Despite this, it couldn’t be overlooked that obesity altered the clinical course of COPD by increasing the muscular effort required for ventilation, thereby exacerbating dyspnea [59]. Since obese COPD patients required greater muscular effort to maintain ventilation during exercise, it was reasonable to expect that, compared with non-obese COPD patients, they were more likely to experience disabling dyspnea early in exercise, leading to increased mortality risk [60]. Given these findings, our results suggest that BMI has a significant effect on the complex relationship between the ALI and mortality among COPD patients, characterized by an inverse J-shaped curve.

The current investigation has several limitations that need to be recognized. First, there was a significant amount of variability in patients’ medication usage throughout the follow-up period, and there were incomplete or missing medical treatment records for the patients enrolled during the inclusion period. As a result, the influence of medical treatment as a confounding factor could not be ruled out in this study. Second, owing to the observational nature of this study, it was not feasible to definitively establish a direct cause-and-effect connection between the ALI and mortality among patients with COPD. Additionally, the ALI employed in this study was based on a one-time assessment of complete blood count (CBC) parameters and serum albumin, which might not provide a comprehensive picture of the participants’ health status, potentially introducing bias. Finally, despite our attempts to address various factors that could introduce bias in this study, there may still be unidentified factors impacting the results.

Conclusions

In summary, this research revealed a previously unknown connection between elevated ALIs and reduced mortality rates from various causes, including all causes and cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, in patients with COPD. Notably, the relationship between the ALI and mortality risk followed an inverse J-curve pattern, with optimal ALIs of 95, 97, and 96 associated with the lowest mortality risk for overall, cardiovascular, and respiratory causes, respectively. These findings indicate the potential for maintaining ALI within a specific range to improve long-term survival outcomes for patients with COPD. Overall, future research with a prospective design and larger sample sizes that accounts for a wider range of confounding factors is necessary to clarify the causal mechanisms and verify the role of the ALI in COPD.
