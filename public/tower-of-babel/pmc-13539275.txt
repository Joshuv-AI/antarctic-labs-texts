Effect of Food on Balcinrenone/Dapagliflozin Pharmacokinetics and the Pharmacokinetics of Balcinrenone When Dosed with a P‐gp Inhibitor
Authors: Anna L. Eriksson, Deeyen Karsanji, Amy H. Shi, Joanna Parkinson, Maria Heijer, Edward Austin, Bosse Lindmark, Charlotta Vedin‐Nilsson, Judith Hartleib‐Geschwindner, Maria Leonsson‐Zachrisson, Hans Ericsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBalcinrenone (AZD9977) is a novel selective non‐steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist with a distinct mode of action being developed as a fixed‐dose combination with the sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor dapagliflozin for the treatment of heart failure with impaired kidney function, and chronic kidney disease. In this Phase 1 randomized open‐label three‐way crossover study we investigated the effect of food on balcinrenone/dapagliflozin pharmacokinetics, and the pharmacokinetics of balcinrenone when dosed with a P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) inhibitor. Fourteen healthy participants were administered an oral capsule of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin 40 mg/10 mg in three dosing periods: fasted (reference), fed (high‐fat, high‐calorie meal) and with a P‐gp inhibitor (quinidine 300 mg × 2). Balcinrenone exposure was comparable in the fed and fasted states (geometric mean ratios [GMRs] [90% CI]: maximum plasma concentration [Cmax] 1.05 [0.88, 1.25]; area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time 0 to infinity [AUCinf] 1.12 [1.06, 1.19]). In the fed state, dapagliflozin AUCinf was comparable to the fasted state (GMR [90% CI] 1.05 [1.01, 1.09]), whereas Cmax was decreased (GMR [90% CI] 0.59 [0.51, 0.69]), in line with previous dapagliflozin food interaction studies. Co‐administration with quinidine increased balcinrenone exposure: GMRs (90% CI) 1.48 (1.24, 1.76) and 1.24 (1.17, 1.31) for Cmax and AUCinf, respectively, but AUC fold increase was <2, the level used for classification of sensitive P‐gp substrates. All interventions were well tolerated. In conclusion, this study supports dosing of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin without regard to food. Balcinrenone is not considered a sensitive P‐gp substrate. No P‐gp based dosing precautions are warranted based on this study.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a serious condition associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, and a significant burden on global health care systems. 1 Patients with HF have a high prevalence of comorbid conditions, with impaired kidney function being one of the most serious. 2 Concomitant impaired kidney function negatively impacts prognosis and complicates HF treatment. 3 Although mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) are an important treatment for HF 4 , 5 they are markedly underused, particularly in patients with impaired kidney function due to safety concerns of hyperkalemia and an initial reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate. 6 , 7

Balcinrenone (AZD9977) is a novel, selective, non‐steroidal MRA with a differential mode of action relative to approved MRAs that is being developed as a fixed‐dose combination with the sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) dapagliflozin for the treatment of patients with HF and impaired kidney function, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). In animal models, balcinrenone reduces albuminuria and improves kidney and cardiac pathology with similar efficacy to approved MRAs while lacking the increase in urinary sodium/potassium ratio seen with traditional MRAs. 8 This suggests a mechanism‐based reduced risk for potassium retention and a potential for lower risk for hyperkalemia.

The efficacy and safety of balcinrenone in combination with dapagliflozin was investigated in patients with HF and CKD in the Phase 2b MIRACLE trial, 9 and is currently being evaluated in a Phase 3 study in patients with HF and impaired kidney function (BalanceD‐HF; ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06307652). In the Phase 2b MIRO‐CKD study in patients with CKD, balcinrenone/dapagliflozin was superior to dapagliflozin alone in reducing albuminuria, and the effects on potassium were minor. 10

Dose‐proportional pharmacokinetics of balcinrenone was observed following single doses of 5 to 200 mg. At doses >200 mg the pharmacokinetics was less than dose proportional, suggesting solubility‐limited absorption. 11 This was supported by data from a subsequent bioavailability study where administration of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin 150 mg/10 mg together with a high‐fat, high‐calorie meal resulted in approximately 60% higher maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) and 20% higher area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), but a lower trough concentration (Ctrough) compared with dosing in the fasted state [NCT04798222]. 12 Although absorption of balcinrenone continues throughout the intestine, 11 when high doses are given in the fasted state absorption is still incomplete. In contrast, when balcinrenone is dosed together with food a larger proportion of the dose is dissolved and absorbed in the upper part of the intestine. This results in higher Cmax and higher total absorption as assessed by AUC. The amount of drug reaching the lower parts of the intestine and being absorbed there is smaller when dosed together with food, resulting in a lower Ctrough. However, based on results from the MIRACLE study, 9 the highest dose currently developed is balcinrenone/dapagliflozin 40 mg/10 mg in a capsule containing balcinrenone and dapagliflozin immediate‐release pellets. It is possible that the impact of food is smaller for these lower doses, but no bioavailability study has yet been undertaken with this dose, in a formulation very similar to the to‐be‐marketed formulation.

In the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) study absolute bioavailability for balcinrenone was 52%, protein binding was 81.5%, clearance was 14.2 L/h, and elimination half‐life as determined after intravenous administration was 4 h. Metabolism, primarily mediated by CYP3A4, was the major elimination route. The exposure to circulating balcinrenone metabolites was low and contribution of metabolites to the pharmacodynamic response was deemed to be negligible. 13

In the ADME study, renal clearance was 5.4 L/h. 13 In a renal impairment study, participants with severe renal impairment had 50% lower total apparent balcinrenone clearance, and consequently two‐fold higher AUC, compared with controls with normal renal function. 14 Renal clearance of balcinrenone exceeds maximal glomerular filtration rate (ADME study: fu × GFR, i.e., 0.185 × 7.5 = 1.4 L/h), indicating elimination via active tubular secretion. In vitro cell line transporter data suggested that P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) and/or organic anion transporter 3 (OAT3) could be mediators of this active secretion. Balcinrenone is a P‐gp substrate in vitro in monolayers of polarized Madin‐Darby canine kidney cells transfected with multidrug resistance 1 gene (MDCK‐MDR1). 13 However, in a subsequent in situ study (human jejunum Ussing chamber) balcinrenone was not shown to be a substrate of intestinal efflux transporters, including P‐gp, with an efflux ratio of <2.0 (Supplemental Appendix). This aligns with the dose linear exposure between 5 and 200 mg balcinrenone and similar renal clearance over the dose range of 5‐1200 mg. 11 , 15 Furthermore, in the ADME study, the fraction absorbed of a 100 mg oral balcinrenone dose was estimated to >94% indicating no or low impact of intestinal P‐gp on the extent of absorption. 13

Patients with HF and impaired kidney function are often treated with many concomitant drugs. 2 , 3 Understanding the drug–drug interaction (DDI) potential of balcinrenone is therefore essential for managing the drug safely. 2 In a DDI study co‐administration of balcinrenone with itraconazole for five consecutive days to healthy volunteers resulted in a 2.2‐fold increase in Cmax and a 3.1‐fold increase in AUC (unpublished data, NCT03843060). Itraconazole is a strong inhibitor of CYP3A4 and P‐gp. 16 , 17 The findings from this study are in line with balcinrenone being metabolized by CYP3A4, but if balcinrenone is a P‐gp substrate, inhibition of P‐gp could also contribute. A dedicated DDI study with a more specific P‐gp inhibitor, such as quinidine, 16 could help in understanding the impact of P‐gp on the PK of balcinrenone.

The purpose of this study was, after a single oral dose of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin in healthy participants, to assess the effect of food on the PK of balcinrenone and dapagliflozin in the fed and fasted state and to assess the effect of the P‐gp inhibitor quinidine on the PK of balcinrenone. The safety and tolerability of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin with and without food, and with and without concomitant quinidine were monitored.

Methods

This study was reviewed and approved by Wales Research Ethics Committee 1, Cardiff, the United Kingdom, and was conducted at one study site in the United Kingdom (Parexel Early Phase Clinical Unit, London). The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06979388). All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki, with the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice, with all applicable regulatory requirements, and with the AstraZeneca policy on bioethics.

Study Participants

Eligible participants were aged 18‐55 years, weighed ≥50 kg, and had a body mass index (BMI) of 18‐30 kg/m2. Participants were to be in good general health based on medical history, physical examination, vital signs, and laboratory screening. Females of childbearing potential were required to use an approved method of highly effective contraception and to have a negative pregnancy test at screening and check‐in to the clinical unit.

Individuals with history or presence of gastrointestinal, hepatic, or renal disease or any other condition known to interfere with ADME of drugs as well as individuals with a history of long QT syndrome, history of additional risk factors for QT prolongation, or any clinically significant abnormalities on 12‐lead ECG at the Screening Visit were not eligible. Use of prescribed or nonprescribed medications (other than paracetamol and hormonal contraceptives), herbal remedies, and large doses of vitamins and minerals were not allowed from 2 weeks, and drugs with enzyme‐inducing properties from 3 weeks, prior to the first administration of study intervention.

Study Design

This was a randomized, single‐dose, open‐label, three‐period, three‐treatment, three‐way crossover study aimed to assess the effect of food on balcinrenone/dapagliflozin pharmacokinetics and the pharmacokinetics of balcinrenone when dosed with a P‐gp inhibitor in healthy adults (Figure 1). The design followed regulatory guidance for food‐effect and drug interaction studies. 16 , 18

Potential participants were screened up to 4 weeks prior to study intervention, with the potential to extend the screening period by a maximum of 14 days for logistical reasons (e.g., delay in receiving medical history from the general practitioner) if necessary. Admission to the study site occurred on Day −1. On Day 1 participants were randomized to one of the six treatment sequences (ABC, ACB, BCA, BAC, CAB, or CBA). Each treatment was separated by a washout period of minimum 72 h, thus each participant received study intervention on Day 1, Day 4, and Day 7. Discharge occurred on Day 10, at least 72 h after the last administration of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin. There was a final follow‐up telephone call within 5 to 7 days after the last balcinrenone/dapagliflozin administration (Days 12 to 14).

For fasting conditions, participants were required to fast overnight for at least 10 h before and 4 h after dosing. For fed conditions, the same fasting requirements applied except for a high‐fat, high‐calorie meal (∼50% fat, 800 to 1000 calories) which was served 30 min before dosing and was consumed entirely within 30 min. Study drugs were administered together with 240 mL of water.

PK Assessments

In each treatment period, blood samples for analysis of balcinrenone were collected pre‐dose and 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h after dosing. Blood samples for analysis of dapagliflozin were collected at the same timepoints but only during Treatments A and B. Urine samples for analysis of balcinrenone were collected 0‐6, 6‐12, and 12‐24 h after each dosing.

Balcinrenone and Dapagliflozin Quantitation

Balcinrenone concentrations in plasma and urine and dapagliflozin concentrations in plasma were determined using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry according to validated and published methods. 13 , 15 , 19 , 20 The lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) of balcinrenone was 2.0 nmol/L in plasma and 10 nmol/L in urine and LLOQ for dapagliflozin in plasma was 0.2 ng/mL. Results from in‐study quality control samples, calibration standards, and incurred sample re‐analyses indicated acceptable assay performance for all analytes.

Pharmacokinetic Evaluation

The pharmacokinetic parameters of the concentration data for balcinrenone and dapagliflozin were derived using non‐compartmental methods in Phoenix WinNonlin Version 8.5 (Certara Inc., Princeton, NJ).

Primary parameters included Cmax and AUC from time zero to infinity (AUCinf) and to last measurable concentration (AUClast). Secondary parameters included time to Cmax (tmax), elimination half‐life associated with terminal slope of a semi‐logarithmic concentration–time curve (t½λz), apparent total body clearance (CL/F), and apparent volume of distribution based on the terminal phase (Vz/F). Non‐renal clearance (CLNR) was the difference between CL/F and renal clearance (CLR) and was calculated for balcinrenone only.

The pharmacokinetic set included all randomized participants who received at least one dose of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin and had at least one evaluable (including concentrations reported as <LLOQ after the first dose) PK concentration. All calculations were based on actual post‐dose sampling times. Individual concentrations below the LLOQ of the bioanalytical assay were reported as NQ.

Statistical Analyses

It was estimated that 12 evaluable participants would provide a confidence interval (CI) with relative precision (ratio between upper and lower limits of the CI) of 1.56, based on an intra‐subject coefficient of variation (CV) of 25%. The intra‐subject variability of AUCinf and Cmax had been estimated to be <25% in previous studies with balcinrenone/dapagliflozin. To account for potential discontinuations, 14 subjects were to be randomized in this study.

The log‐transformed primary PK parameters (AUCinf, AUClast, and Cmax) were analyzed using a linear mixed effect model with treatment, period, and sequence as fixed effects and participants nested within sequence as random effect. Least‐square means (including two‐sided 95% CIs) for each treatment, as well as least‐square mean difference (including two‐sided 90% CIs) for Treatment B (fed) or Treatment C (dosed with quinidine) versus Treatment A (fasted state, reference) were estimated. The results were back‐transformed to provide geometric least‐squares means by treatment, geometric least‐squares mean ratios for the treatment comparisons, and corresponding 90% CIs which were compared to the pre‐specified interval (0.80 to 1.25) for conclusion of no food effect on balcinrenone/dapagliflozin PK or no effect of a P‐gp inhibitor on balcinrenone PK.

Safety Assessments

Safety and tolerability evaluations included the incidence, intensity, and severity of adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs), physical examinations, monitoring of vital signs, 12‐lead electrocardiography, and laboratory tests (blood and urine). AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA; v28.0). AEs were collected from Day 1 throughout the study and including the follow‐up telephone call. SAEs were recorded from the time of signing of the Informed Consent Form. The investigator assessed the causal relationship between treatment and any AEs.

Results

Three participants were randomized to each of the treatment sequences ABC and CAB, and two participants were randomized to each of the treatment sequences ACB, BAC, BCA, and CBA, respectively. All participants were included in all analyses.

Safety

A single oral dose of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin (40 mg/10 mg) was safe and well tolerated in healthy adult participants when administered either under fasted or fed conditions or when administered with quinidine. No SAEs were reported, and no participants terminated the study early due to an AE. All AEs were mild or moderate in intensity, and all were reported as resolved at the end of the study. In total, 11 (78.6%) participants experienced at least one AE during the study. The incidence of AEs was 5 (35.7%), 7 (50.0%), and 8 (57.1%) participants following administration of Treatments A (fasted, reference), B (fed), and C (dosed with quinidine), respectively. The most frequently reported AE by preferred term was headache (6 [42.9%] participants; Table 6). No clinically significant trends or shifts from baseline were reported for laboratory results, vital signs, or electrocardiographic findings.

Discussion

Meal‐dependent dosing recommendations can influence adherence and clinical usability of a drug, making it important to establish and clearly describe the impact of food on the drug to ensure appropriate dosing guidance. In this study, we evaluated the potential impact of food (high‐fat, high‐calorie meal) on the pharmacokinetics of balcinrenone and dapagliflozin when given as a capsule containing immediate‐release pellets in a fixed‐dose combination of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin 40 mg/10 mg. In addition, previous data did not clearly establish whether balcinrenone is a P‐gp substrate. Consequently, food effect and P‐gp interaction assessments were combined within this single study. This integrated design allowed the fasted arm to serve as a shared reference for both evaluations which reduced overall study cost and minimized the number of healthy participants exposed to study drug.

Despite high variability in the half‐life of balcinrenone, all pre‑dose balcinrenone concentrations were below the LLOQ. The apparent variability in half‐life may reflect the relatively flat terminal portion of the concentration–time curve, where plasma concentrations approach the LLOQ. Any detectable dapagliflozin levels were well under 5% of Cmax. We therefore consider the 72‑h washout between interventions sufficient.

The food effect for balcinrenone had previously been explored only with an earlier formulation and at higher doses, where solubility‐limited absorption was observed in the fasted state. 12 These earlier findings highlighted the need to assess the food effect at a clinically relevant dose and with a formulation very similar to the to‐be‐marketed formulation. Dapagliflozin food effect had been studied at the same dose as in the present work, but with a tablet formulation rather than the pellets in capsule formulation co‐administered with balcinrenone evaluated in the present study. 21 Because balcinrenone/dapagliflozin is intended for patient populations where flexible and uncomplicated dosing is important, confirmation that the fixed‐dose combination can be administered without regard to meals is of practical and regulatory relevance. The results from the current study therefore provide important evidence to support dosing recommendations and future labeling.

The balcinrenone PK parameters in the fed versus fasted state derived in the present study are different from findings in previous food interaction studies of balcinrenone, where higher doses were tested. 12 At the dose investigated in this study (40 mg), there was no indication of solubility‐limited absorption observed previously at higher doses. Except for a delay in Cmax, other parameters including decline in balcinrenone plasma concentrations and derived PK parameters were similar when dosed in the fed and in fasted conditions. The dapagliflozin PK results were virtually identical to the results reported in a food interaction study where a tablet formulation was used. 21 The current results confirm that balcinrenone/dapagliflozin can be administered without food guidance. These results also support the dosing regimen applied in the ongoing Ph3 study (NCT06307652), Balanced‐HF, and the completed Ph2b study, MIRO‐CKD. Both of these studies include the balcinrenone/dapagliflozin 40 mg/10 mg capsule formulation, which can be administered with or without food. 10

In the part of the study evaluating whether balcinrenone is a P‐gp substrate, co‐administration with the P‐gp inhibitor quinidine produced only minor changes in balcinrenone exposure. These changes are not considered to be clinically meaningful, and the approximately 25% increase in AUC is below the criteria of AUC fold increase ≥2 with quinidine co‐administration used for classification of sensitive P‐gp substrates in the ICH M12 guideline on drug interaction studies. 16 Furthermore, quinidine is also reported to be a weak inhibitor of CYP3A4, 22 the main enzyme responsible for balcinrenone metabolism. It is therefore possible that inhibition of CYP3A4 is implicated in the minor effects on balcinrenone exposure. Renal clearance of balcinrenone was not affected by quinidine co‐administration. Taken together, the findings suggest a limited net impact of quinidine‐sensitive pathways on balcinrenone exposure.

Earlier in vitro findings suggested that balcinrenone may be a P‐gp substrate, 13 but in situ data from the Ussing chamber experiment (Supplemental Appendix) and clinical data suggest that P‐gp has no or minor impact on the fraction absorbed of balcinrenone. A low efflux ratio (1.8) was observed in the Ussing chamber experiment, indicating that balcinrenone is not a substrate of efflux transporters, including P‐gp, when evaluated in human jejunum.

Dose‐proportional pharmacokinetics were observed over the 5 to 200 mg dose range in the single ascending dose study. 11 In the human ADME study, only 6% of drug‐related materials was associated with balcinrenone in feces, 13 suggesting that the fraction absorbed is greater than 94%. In addition, absorption is rapid when balcinrenone is given as an oral suspension. 15 Collectively, these findings together with the results from the quinidine DDI study demonstrate that efflux transporters do not restrict the absorption of balcinrenone in humans, and that P‐gp does not contribute to balcinrenone excretion in the kidney to a clinically meaningful extent.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in healthy volunteers rather than in the target population of patients with HF and CKD. Food effects and transporter‐mediated disposition may differ substantially in these patients due to comorbidities and concomitant medications. Pharmacokinetic sampling in Phase 2 and 3 trials will allow population PK evaluation of the extent to which the present findings translate to patients. Second, quinidine plasma concentrations were not measured, precluding confirmation of adequate transporter inhibition and assessment of interindividual variability in quinidine exposure. A 600 mg quinidine dose, administered either as a single dose or in divided doses, is commonly used in DDI studies. 23 , 24 , 25 In the present study, 300 mg quinidine was administered 1 h before and 3 h after investigational product dosing to maintain intestinal P‐gp inhibition during the anticipated absorption window. Finally, the study was designed to quantify a potential food effect and a potential interaction with the P‐gp inhibitor quinidine with a certain precision under the conditions studied but not designed to mechanistically explore or exclude small‐to‐moderate transporter‐mediated effects.

Conclusion

The results from this study confirm that balcinrenone/dapagliflozin 40 mg/10 mg in a formulation containing immediate‐release pellets in a capsule and very similar to the to‐be‐marketed formulation, can be administered without meal‐related restrictions.

This study also confirms that balcinrenone is not a sensitive P‐gp substrate, as co‐administration with the P‐gp inhibitor quinidine produced only small and clinically insignificant impact on exposure of balcinrenone in the current study. These small changes do not warrant P‐gp based dosing precautions.

Together, these findings support simple, unrestricted administration of balcinrenone/dapagliflozin with respect to food and drugs affecting P‐gp. This provides a simple regimen for patients as well as key data to inform future labeling and clinical use.
