Efficient Quadratic Corrections for Frank-Wolfe Algorithms
Jannis Halbey, Seta Rakotomandimby, Mathieu Besançon, Sébastien Designolle, Sebastian Pokutta

Abstract
We develop a Frank-Wolfe algorithm with corrective steps, generalizing previous algorithms including blended pairwise conditional gradients and fully-corrective Frank-Wolfe, and propose a highly efficient corrective algorithm in the case of convex quadratic objectives based on linear optimization or linear system solving, akin to Wolfe’s minimum-norm point. Beyond optimization problems that are directly quadratic, we revisit two algorithms, split conditional gradient for the intersection of two sets accessible through linear oracles, and second-order conditional gradient sliding, which approximately solves Variable-Metric projection subproblems, proposing improvement of the algorithms and their guarantees that may be of interest beyond our work, and we leverage quadratic corrections to accelerate the quadratic subproblems they solve. We show significant computational acceleration of Frank-Wolfe-based algorithms equipped with the quadratic correction on the considered problem classes.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider convex constrained optimization problems of the form

\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}{}}f(\mathbf{x}),

where
{\mathcal{X}}{}
is a compact convex set and
f
is a convex differentiable function.
A particularly interesting class of first-order methods for this setting are
Conditional Gradient (CG) or Frank-Wolfe (FW) methods
(
Levitin and Polyak 1966
;
Frank and Wolfe 1956
)
.
One major advantage of these methods is that they only access
{\mathcal{X}}{}
by calling a Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) optimizing linear subproblems over the set and constructing solutions as convex combinations of the resulting vertices.
In particular, these methods avoid costly projection steps while ensuring an
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
convergence rate in general and linear convergence in specific settings.

Over the years, several FW variants were designed to exploit the so-called
active set
, i.e., the set of vertices constructing the current iterate, the oldest and most well-known being the Away-step Frank-Wolfe (AFW)
(
Guélat and Marcotte 1986
)
.
By storing the set of vertices and their weights in the convex combination, these methods reduce the number of LMO calls significantly and reach linear convergence rates on polytopes under some conditions where standard FW remains at a rate of
\Omega(1/t)
.
Furthermore, they have been shown to produce sparse solutions (constructed as convex combinations of few iterates), which motivated the strong interest in FW methods for sparse optimization and machine learning applications.
The Fully-Corrective Frank-Wolfe (FCFW) algorithm presented in some form in
Wolfe 1976
;
Holloway 1974
and coined in
Lacoste-Julien and Jaggi 2015
drives this approach to its limit
by computing a minimizer of the objective over the convex hull of the active set after each FW step.
Besides its favorable convergence properties, FCFW is also known to produce sparse solutions.
Furthermore,
Zhou et al. 2018
showed that an FCFW variant can be viewed as dual to a limited-memory version of Kelley’s cutting plane method,
a connection that has been exploited in multiple other contexts (see, e.g.,
Mazanti et al. 2024
;
Oustry and Cerulli 2024
;
Besançon and Kurtz 2024
).
Nonetheless, FCFW is often computationally impractical since minimizing over the convex hull of the active set can be as hard as the original problem.

In this work, we generalize the idea of corrective steps on the active set to a new framework,
Corrective Frank-Wolfe (CFW)
,
and design two new
Quadratic Correction (QC)
steps tailored to quadratic objective functions.
While there exist many applications of quadratic problems like matrix recovery
(
Garber et al. 2019
)
, spectral clustering
(
Ding et al. 2022
)
and entanglement detection
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
,
we also revisit two algorithms based on FW benefiting from QC and improve their theoretical guarantees (with improved rates and applicability to new function classes).

Split Conditional Gradient (SCG)

(
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
)
tackles optimization problems over finite intersections of convex sets via splitting avoiding potential intractable linear minimization over the intersection.
SCG is inspired by the alternating linear minimization algorithm (ALM)
(
Braun et al. 2023
)

and solves the problem by alternating between update steps for iterates on the respective sets.
Each iterate minimizes an auxiliary quadratic objective inferring an increasing penalty on the squared distance to the other set.
Besides the computational benefits of the quadratic correction, we also prove a faster convergence rate for SCG.

Finally, we consider
Second-Order Conditional Gradient Sliding (SOCGS)

(
Carderera and Pokutta 2020
)
which follows an inexact Newton approach, adapting the conditional gradient sliding of
Lan and Zhou 2016
with a quadratic approximation of the original problem at each iteration.
The method enjoys very fast convergence rates and has been shown to outperform first-order Frank-Wolfe variants on various problems.
However, the main computational bottleneck of SOCGS is solving the quadratic subproblems which can be significantly accelerated using our proposed quadratic corrections.
Furthermore, we establish the global linear convergence rate of SOCGS for generalized self-concordant functions
(
Sun and Tran-Dinh 2019
)
without requiring global Lipschitz smoothness or strong convexity.

Related work

The literature on Frank-Wolfe methods is vast and we only cover a few related methods here, for a more comprehensive overview we refer to
Braun et al. 2022
.
The most closely related prior work is that of
Lacoste-Julien and Jaggi 2015
who also present, besides the already mentioned AFW and FCFW, a Pairwise Frank-Wolfe (PFW) and an approximate variant of Fully-Corrective Frank-Wolfe (AFCFW).
Additionally, they introduce a generalized version of the MNP algorithm
(
Wolfe 1976
)
for corrections in AFCFW, called MNP-Correction.
Furthermore, we follow the idea of
Braun et al. 2017
lazifying the corrective step framework, which was already applied in
Tsuji et al. 2022
to Blended Pairwise Conditional Gradients (BPCG), which will be the main baseline for our experiments.

Contributions

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

Corrective Frank-Wolfe

We introduce Corrective Frank-Wolfe (CFW), a new framework for FW variants that perform corrective steps on the active set and prove linear convergence for smooth, sharp functions over polytopes.
Our results also directly extend to sharp functions of lower order leading to accelerated convergence rates via standard arguments (see
Braun et al. 2022
) which is however beyond the scope of this paper.
The corrective step is selected depending on the local pairwise gap
and performs either a descent step yielding sufficient progress or a drop step decreasing the active set size.
We show that active set-based methods like AFW, PFW and BPCG fit into the corrective step framework.
Furthermore, we introduce a lazified variant of CFW, which avoids LMO calls at iterations where corrective steps are taken.
Thus, unlike AFCFW, our method not only performs FW steps when beneficial, but also calls the LMO only when necessary.

Quadratic Corrections

We introduce two new Quadratic Correction (QC) steps tailored for quadratic objective functions.
The first one, QC-LP, is a direct linear program solving the quadratic problem over the convex hull of the active set via relaxed optimality conditions.
It converges within a single step to the optimal value as soon as a sufficient number of vertices of the optimal face are contained in the active set.
The second type, QC-MNP, is a specialized variant of the Minimum-Norm Point (MNP) algorithm
(
Wolfe 1976
)
solving the quadratic problem over the affine hull.
In contrast to the MNP-correction from
Lacoste-Julien and Jaggi 2015
, QC-MNP solves only one quadratic problem at each iteration.
This is both computationally more efficient and avoids dropping too many potentially good vertices from the active set.
Both steps are approximate variants of a FCFW step, but come only with the cost of solving a linear program or linear system, respectively.
For both quadratic corrections, we present a hybrid method combining these corrections with local pairwise steps following the implementation of the BPCG
(
Tsuji et al. 2022
)

yielding a very efficient update scheme for quadratic problems.

Theoretical results for SCG and SOCGS

Besides the practical benefits of the new quadratic corrections for SCG and SOCGS, we also present new theoretical results.
Firstly, we prove that SCG converges for a smaller step size yielding a faster rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t})

compared to the original rate of
\mathcal{O}(\ln(t)/\sqrt{t})
provided in
(
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
)
.
This closes the gap to the original FW method which has a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t})
for the underlying non-smooth problem.
Secondly, we show that the projected variable-metric method in SOCGS converges globally for generalized self-concordant functions.

Experiments

We demonstrate the excellent computational benefits of the new quadratic corrections on a variety of problems including sparse regression, entanglement detection, projections onto the Birkhoff polytope and tensor completion problems.
Further experiments show that quadratic corrections also improve the convergence of ALM and SCG.
For SOCGS, the quadratic corrections enable us to solve the quadratic subproblem efficiently.

We will now present the Corrective Frank-Wolfe (CFW) algorithm given in

CFW provides a general framework for FW methods that perform corrective steps on the active set.
Unlike AFCFW, CFW does not perform an FW step in each iteration but compares the so-called local pairwise gap
\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{a}_{t}-\mathbf{s}_{t}\right\rangle

with the global Frank-Wolfe gap
\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle
to decide whether to perform a corrective step or an FW step.
The criteria for corrective steps are specified in

performing either a descent step yielding sufficient primal progress or a drop step decreasing the size of the active set while not increasing the objective value.
In contrast to AFCFW, we omit any conditions on the away gap and the primal progress is only compared to the local pairwise step instead of the global FW step.
Particularly, the local update steps in PFW, BPCG and FCFW meet the criteria of a corrective step.

2.2
Quadratic corrections

In this section, we introduce two types of corrective steps for the CFW algorithm, which are especially suited for quadratic problems: Quadratic Correction LP (QC-LP)
and Quadratic Correction MNP (QC-MNP).
Here, we assume that
f
is a convex quadratic function, i.e., can be written as

f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\rangle+c,

with
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix.
This can be extended to general finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces in which case
\mathbf{A}
is a symmetric linear operator.

The Quadratic Corrections (QC) are inspired by the FCFW algorithm which solves the minimization problem
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\conv(S)}f(\mathbf{x})
in each iteration.
Unfortunately, minimizing quadratic functions over polytopes such as
\conv(S)
is computationally demanding (we discuss it in Remark
3
).
To circumvent this difficulty, the QC algorithms instead exploit the finiteness of
S
and relax this problem into minimizing the function
f
over the
affine hull
of
S
,

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{aff}(S)}f(\mathbf{x})\;\big(\leqslant\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\conv(S)}f(\mathbf{x})\big),

(5)

and reconstruct a point in
\conv(S)
.

Solving the minimization problem over the affine hull

Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{aff}(S)}f(\mathbf{x})
.
First-order optimality conditions imply that
\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),\mathbf{d}\right\rangle\geqslant 0
for any feasible direction
\mathbf{d}
.
The feasible directions in
\mathrm{aff}(S)
are spanned by
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}
for
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in S
.
Thus, the first-order optimality conditions are equivalent to the system of equalities,

\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}\right\rangle=0\quad\forall\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in S,

which is equivalent to the gradient at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
being orthogonal to the affine space.
Writing
\mathbf{x}^{*}
in barycentric coordinates,
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in S}\boldsymbol{\lambda}[\mathbf{v}]\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, yields

\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{*})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}+\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\mathbf{b}
.
By fixing an anchor
\mathbf{w}\in S
, we obtain the following
equivalent linear system for the minimization problem (
5
):

\left\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}\right\rangle=0\quad\forall~\mathbf{v}\in S\setminus\{\mathbf{w}\},\quad\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in S}\boldsymbol{\lambda}[\mathbf{v}]=1.

(6)

Remark 3
.

Applying first-order optimality conditions
to
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{\conv}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\conv(S)}f(\mathbf{x})
with the barycentric coordinates yields

\left\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right\rangle\geqslant 0
for all
\mathbf{v}\in S
,
which is a quadratic inequality system in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
that can be as hard to solve as the original problem.
However, if
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{\conv}
lies in the relative interior of
\conv(S)

6

To ensure that the new weights
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are non-negative, we consider two approaches.

Quadratic Correction through Linear Programs

The QC-LP approach assumes enforces the non-negativity directly,
yielding

\left\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}\right\rangle=0\quad\forall\mathbf{v}\in S\setminus\{\mathbf{w}\},\quad\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in S}\boldsymbol{\lambda}[\mathbf{v}]=1,\quad\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geqslant 0.

(7)

The LP is only feasible if the affine minimizer lies inside
\conv(S)
.
Otherwise, we perform a local pairwise step as a cheap fallback option.
The method is presented in
Algorithm

3
.

Quadratic Correction through the Minimum-Norm-Point algorithm

The second approach, QC-MNP, is inspired by the Minimum-Norm-Point algorithm in
Wolfe 1976
.
Instead of enforcing the non-negativity directly, one considers the line segment between the weights of the current point and the affine minimizer.
If at least one of the new weights is negative, we perform a ratio test to find the intersection of the line segment with the
|S|
-simplex.
This step will drop at least one atom from the active set, improving sparsity.
However, there is no guarantee that this atom does not belong to the optimal face.

Unlike the MNP-Correction step from
Lacoste-Julien and Jaggi 2015
, QC-MNP will stop after a single pullback, alleviating the need to solve multiple linear systems in a single iteration.
Furthermore, this also avoids dropping more than one atom in a single iteration, which can be problematic in some cases.
The QC-MNP method is presented in

3
Accelerated algorithms through quadratic corrections

In this section, we leverage quadratic corrections for two classes of algorithms that require solving quadratic subproblems with FW variants.

3.1
Split conditional gradient

Frank-Wolfe methods come with one drawback that can restrict their applicability — the necessity of a linear minimization oracle.
This poses a problem especially for optimization problems over intersections of convex sets, since there is in general no efficient way to minimize a linear function over them.

Braun et al. 2023
introduced a new algorithm, Alternating Linear Minimization (ALM), for finding a point in the intersection of two convex sets
P
and
Q
, drawing a parallel with von Neumann’s alternating projection.
ALM alternates between Frank-Wolfe steps solving

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in P,\mathbf{y}\in Q}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2},

and therefore avoiding projections in an FW-like manner.

Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
extended their approach to optimization problems over intersections by penalizing the distance,

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in P,\mathbf{y}\in Q}F_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=f\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}}{2}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}.

Their method, Split Conditional Gradient (SCG) achieves a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}\left(\ln t/\sqrt{t}\right)
for the primal gap of
F_{\lambda}

(
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
, Theorem 3.3)
.
We show convergence for a smaller step size
\gamma_{t}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)}
yielding an improved convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{t}\right)
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L
-smooth,
P,Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be non-empty compact and convex sets with diameters
D_{P}
and
D_{Q}
such that
P\cap Q\neq\emptyset
.
For
t\geqslant 0
, set
\lambda_{t}=\ln(t+2)
,
(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*},\mathbf{y}_{t}^{*})=\argmin_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in P\times Q}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
For step sizes
\gamma_{t}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)}
, the iterates of SCG satisfy

0\leqslant F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t})-F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*},\mathbf{y}_{t}^{*})\leqslant\frac{(D_{P}+D_{Q})^{2}(L+1)+\sqrt{2}c_{f}}{\sqrt{t+2}}

(8)

for all
t\geqslant 0
, where
c_{f}=\max_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{y}})\in P\times Q}f\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}}{2}\right)-f\left(\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{2}\right)<\infty
.

The proof for the more general case of finite intersections over Hilbert spaces is given in
Appendix

C
.

3.2
(Second-order) conditional gradient sliding

Another class of algorithms that benefit from quadratic corrections are sliding methods,
specifically Conditional Gradient Sliding (CGS)
(
Lan and Zhou 2016
)
and Second-Order Conditional Gradient Sliding (SOCGS)
(
Carderera and Pokutta 2020
)
.
These methods reduce the number of first-order oracle calls by iteratively solving quadratic problems with a FW variant.
Unlike regular FW variants, CGS requires only
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
gradient calls to achieve
\varepsilon
-optimality, matching existing lower bounds.
SOCGS achieves a linear rate for strongly convex functions over polytopes, replacing the Euclidean projection subproblem in CGS with a projected variable-metric problem.
The convergence of CGS and SOCGS was analyzed on globally smooth, strongly convex functions.
We extend this rate to generalized self-concordant functions in
Appendix

D
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a new framework for conditional gradient methods, Corrective Frank-Wolfe, that uses corrective steps on the active set to improve the convergence while avoiding additional LMO calls.
Instances of the proposed framework provide state-of-the-art convergence rates for sharp functions over polytopes.
The proposed quadratic corrections show to be very effective in practice outperforming methods of comparable convergence both in number of iterations and time.
This allows us to revisit and accelerate previous algorithms, split conditional gradient and second-order conditional gradient sliding, by applying quadratic corrections on the arising subproblems.

N_{\text{FW}}^{i}
and
N_{\text{desc}}^{i}
be the number of FW and descent steps in the
i
-th epoch, the iterations between
t_{i-1}
and
t_{i}
.
In order to bound the number of epochs
T
necessary to achieve
\varepsilon
primal accuracy, we first bound the number of gap steps
N_{\text{gap}}
.

Let
u=t_{i}+1
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,N_{\text{gap}}\}
be the iteration after a gap step.
Note that
\mathbf{x}_{u}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{i}}
and thus
\mathbf{v}_{u}=\mathbf{v}_{t_{i}}
.
By convexity and the optimality of
\mathbf{v}_{u}
, we have

f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f^{*}\leqslant\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{u}),\mathbf{x}_{u}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\rangle\leqslant\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{u}),\mathbf{x}_{u}-\mathbf{v}_{u}\right\rangle\leqslant\frac{2\Phi_{u}}{J}\leqslant 2\Phi_{u}.

(25)

By definition of
\Phi_{0}
this also holds for
u=0
.
Furthermore, the gap step always halves the value of
\Phi
, so we have
\Phi_{u}=\Phi_{t_{i}+1}=2^{-i}\Phi_{0}
.
By bounding the right-hand side of
Equation

25
by
\varepsilon
we get

N_{\text{gap}}\leqslant\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{2\Phi_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil.

(26)

With
N_{\text{drop}}\leqslant N_{\text{FW}}
we have that

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\leqslant N_{\text{FW}}+N_{\text{desc}}+N_{\text{gap}}+N_{\text{drop}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2N_{\text{FW}}+N_{\text{desc}}+N_{\text{gap}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{gap}}}2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}+N_{\text{gap}}.

(27)

In the remainder of the proof we find upper bounds for
2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}
by lower bounding the progress of FW and descent steps
for both given cases of
f
. First we consider the case where
f
is convex and smooth.

Let
t\in(t_{i},t_{i+1})
, so
\Phi_{t}=\Phi_{u}
.
If we perform a FW step, we have that
\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle\geqslant\Phi_{t}/J=\Phi_{u}/J
.
By
Equation

18
and
Equation

20
we then have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle^{2}}{2LD^{2}},\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{v}_{t}\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\Phi_{u}}{2J}\min\left\{\frac{\Phi_{u}}{LD^{2}J},1\right\}.

(28)

Next we consider the case of a descent step, i.e.
\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{a}_{t}-\mathbf{s}_{t}\right\rangle\geqslant\Phi_{t}
.
Together with the definition of the descent step, we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{a}_{t}-\mathbf{s}_{t}\right\rangle^{2}}{2LD^{2}}\geqslant\frac{\Phi_{u}^{2}}{2LD^{2}}.

(29)

We use now
Equation

25
as an upper bound of the primal progress in the
i
-th epoch, and we use
Appendix

B
and
Equation

29
to lower bound the primal progress.
Let
I=\lceil\log_{2}\frac{\Phi_{0}}{LD^{2}J}\rceil
.
For
i<I
we have that
\Phi_{0}\geqslant 2^{i}LD^{2}J
and thus
\Phi_{u}\geqslant LD^{2}J
.
Then we get with
Equation

25
,

Appendix

B
and
Equation

29
,

\displaystyle 2\Phi_{u}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f^{*}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f(\mathbf{x}_{t_{i+1}})

\displaystyle\geqslant N_{\text{FW}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}}{2J}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}^{2}}{2LD^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant 2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}}{4J}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}J}{2}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{u}\left(2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}\frac{1}{4J}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\frac{J}{2}\right),

and thus

2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\leqslant\max\left\{8J,\frac{4}{J}\right\}.

(30)

For
i\geqslant I
we have that
\Phi_{u}\leqslant LD^{2}J
and thus with
Equation

25
,

Appendix

B
and
Equation

29
,

\displaystyle 2\Phi_{u}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f^{*}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f(\mathbf{x}_{t_{i+1}})

\displaystyle\geqslant N_{\text{FW}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}^{2}}{2J^{2}LD^{2}}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}^{2}}{2LD^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{u}^{2}\left(2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}\frac{1}{4LD^{2}J^{2}}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\frac{1}{2LD^{2}}\right)

Thus we have

2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\leqslant\frac{2^{i}}{\Phi_{0}}\max\left\{8LD^{2}J^{2},4LD^{2}\right\}.

(31)

We can now bound the number of total iterations using
Appendix

B
and
Equation

26
,

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{gap}}}2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}+N_{\text{gap}}

\displaystyle\leqslant I\cdot\max\left\{8J,\frac{4}{J}\right\}+\sum_{i=I}^{N_{\text{gap}}}\frac{2^{i}}{\Phi_{0}}\max\left\{8LD^{2}J^{2},4LD^{2}\right\}+N_{\text{gap}}

\displaystyle\leqslant I\cdot\max\left\{8J,\frac{4}{J}\right\}+(2^{N_{\text{gap}}+1}-1)\frac{1}{\Phi_{0}}\max\left\{8LD^{2}J^{2},4LD^{2}\right\}+N_{\text{gap}}

\displaystyle\leqslant I\cdot\max\left\{8J,\frac{4}{J}\right\}+(2^{\log_{2}\frac{2\Phi_{0}}{\varepsilon}+2}-1)\frac{1}{\Phi_{0}}\max\left\{8LD^{2}J^{2},4LD^{2}\right\}+\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{2\Phi_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leqslant I\cdot\max\left\{8J,\frac{4}{J}\right\}+\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\max\left\{8LD^{2}J^{2},4LD^{2}\right\}+\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{2\Phi_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil.

Therefore, we have
\varepsilon\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)
.

Consider now the case where
f
is
(c,\frac{1}{2})
-sharp and
{\mathcal{X}}
is a polytope.
Let again
u=t_{i}+1
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,N_{\text{gap}}\}
be the iteration after a gap step.
Then we have

\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{u}),\mathbf{a}_{u}-\mathbf{v}_{u}\right\rangle\leqslant\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{u}),\mathbf{a}_{u}-\mathbf{s}_{u}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{u}),\mathbf{x}_{u}-\mathbf{v}_{u}\right\rangle<\Phi_{u}+\Phi_{u}/J\leqslant 2\Phi_{u}.

With
Lemma

6
we have that

f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f^{*}\leqslant\frac{c^{2}\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{u}),\mathbf{a}_{u}-\mathbf{v}_{u}\right\rangle^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\leqslant\frac{4c^{2}\Phi_{u}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}.

(32)

With this new upper bound on the primal gap, we can improve now the bound on
2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}
for
i\geqslant I
, namely by a constant independent of
i
.
Using
Equation

32
,
Appendix

B
and
Equation

29
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{4c^{2}\Phi_{u}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f^{*}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathbf{x}_{u})-f(\mathbf{x}_{t_{i+1}})

\displaystyle\geqslant N_{\text{FW}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}^{2}}{2J^{2}LD^{2}}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\frac{\Phi_{u}^{2}}{2LD^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\Phi_{u}^{2}}{LD^{2}}\left(\frac{2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}}{4J}+\frac{N_{\text{desc}}^{i}}{2}\right).

Consequently, we have

2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}\leqslant\frac{4c^{2}LD^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\max\left\{4J^{2},2\right\}.

The bounds for the case
i<I
in
Equation

30
stay unchanged.
Together with
Appendix

B
and
Equation

26
this yields

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{gap}}}2N_{\text{FW}}^{i}+N_{\text{desc}}^{i}+N_{\text{gap}}

\displaystyle\leqslant I\cdot\max\left\{8J,\frac{4}{J}\right\}+(N_{\text{gap}}-I)\cdot\frac{4c^{2}LD^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\max\left\{4J^{2},2\right\}+N_{\text{gap}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}N_{\text{gap}}\leqslant C_{1}\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{2\Phi_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,

where
C_{1}=1+\max\left\{\max\left\{8J,\frac{4}{J}\right\},\frac{4c^{2}LD^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\max\left\{4J^{2},2\right\}\right\}
.
∎

Appendix C
Split Conditional Gradient

In this section we consider the Split Conditional Gradient (SCG) method of
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
, the algorithm is presented in
Algorithm

6
.

Algorithm 6

Split Conditional Gradient (SCG)

1:

Convex, smooth function
f
, weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset\mathopen{]}0,1\mathclose{]}
with
\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}=1
, start vertex
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}

2:

\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\leftarrow\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}\mathbf{x}_{0}

3:

for

t=0,1,\dots

do

4:

Choose penalty parameter
\lambda_{t}>0

5:

Choose step size
\gamma_{t}\in\mathopen{]}0,1\mathclose{]}

6:

\mathbf{g}_{t}\leftarrow\nabla f(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t})

7:

for

i\in I

do

8:

\mathbf{v}_{t}^{i}\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{t}+\lambda_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t}),\mathbf{v}\right\rangle

9:

\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}+\gamma_{t}(\mathbf{v}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})

10:

end

for

11:

\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}\leftarrow\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}

12:

end

for

We prove the convergence rate of the SCG method stated in
Theorem

5
in a more general setting, i.e., for arbitrary Hilbert spaces and any finite intersection.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space, let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}^{m}
be the product space of
\mathcal{H}
.
We denote the components of
\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
as
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{m})
.
Let
\boldsymbol{D}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\mid\mathbf{x}^{1}=\mathbf{x}^{2}=\dots=\mathbf{x}^{m}\}
denote the diagonal space of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
.
Furthermore, let
I=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and let
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a selection of weights such that
w_{i}>0
for all
i\in I
and
\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}=1
.
The averaging operator is defined as
A\colon\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathcal{H}\colon\mathbf{x}\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}\mathbf{x}^{i}
.

Proposition 7
.

[
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
, Proposition 2.13]

Let
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, let
({\mathcal{X}}_{i})_{i\in I}
be a finite selection of non-empty, compact and convex sets of
\mathcal{H}
.
For every
\lambda\geqslant 0
, set
F_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=f(A\mathbf{x})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\dist_{\boldsymbol{D}}^{2}(\mathbf{x})
.
Suppose that
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\nearrow\infty
. Then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}F_{{\lambda_{t}}}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigcap_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}f(\mathbf{x}).

Theorem 8
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L
-smooth and
let
({\mathcal{X}}_{i})_{i\in I}
be a finite selection of non-empty, compact and convex sets of
\mathcal{H}
with diameters
\{R_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\bigcap_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}\neq\emptyset
.
For
\lambda\geqslant 0
, set
F_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=f(A\mathbf{x})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\dist_{\boldsymbol{D}}^{2}(\mathbf{x})
,

\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x})
and
H_{t}=F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*})
.
For
\lambda_{t}=\ln(t+2)
and the step size
\gamma_{t}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)}
for all
t\geqslant 0
, the iterates of SCG satisfy

0\leqslant H_{t}\leqslant\frac{2R^{2}(L+1)+\sqrt{2}c_{f}}{\sqrt{t+2}}

(33)

for all
t\geqslant 0
, where
c_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\max_{x,y\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}f(Ax)-f(Ay)<\infty
and
R^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}R_{i}^{2}
.

Furthermore, we get that
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}f(\mathbf{x})
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\dist_{\boldsymbol{D}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=0
.
In particular, any accumulation point of the sequence
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}
of
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
lies in
\bigcap_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}

and satisfies
f(A\mathbf{\tilde{x}})=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}f(\mathbf{x})
.

Proof.

The proof is inspired by the proof of
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
.
However, the key difference is to analyze an alternative auxiliary objective.

\widetilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{F_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})}{\lambda}=\frac{f(A\mathbf{x})}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\dist_{\mathbf{D}}^{2}(\mathbf{x}).

We will see that one can find smaller upper bounds on the primal gap
\widetilde{H}_{t}=\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*})
.
However, even for
{\lambda_{t}}\widetilde{H}_{t}=H_{t}
we achieve faster rates of convergence.
Note that

\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}),

since
F_{\lambda_{t}}
and
\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}
only differ in scaling.
Furthermore, the function
\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}
is
\left(\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1\right)
-smooth.

Analogous to Lemma 3.2 in
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
, we first prove a bound on the primal gap
\widetilde{H}_{t}
.
Using smoothness, the FW update rule, the optimality of the FW vertex
\mathbf{v}_{t}
and convexity we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\right),\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}\left\langle\nabla\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{v}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1}{2}\gamma_{t}^{2}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}\left\langle\nabla\widetilde{F}_{{\lambda_{t}}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1}{2}\gamma_{t}^{2}R^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}\left(\widetilde{F}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}\right)-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right)+\gamma_{t}^{2}R^{2}\frac{\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1}{2}

where
R^{2}
is an upper bound on

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\bigcap_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
, see
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
.

Adding
\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*})
to both sides leads to

\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*})\leqslant(1-\gamma_{t})\underbrace{(\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}))}_{\widetilde{H}_{t}}+\gamma_{t}^{2}R^{2}\frac{\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1}{2}.

Together with the definition of
\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}
and the optimality of
\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*}
yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}_{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\right)-\widetilde{F}_{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}^{*})+\left(\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t+1}}}-\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t}}}\right)\left(f(A\mathbf{x}_{t+1})-f(A\mathbf{x}_{t+1}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}^{*})+\left(\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t}}}-\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\right)\left(f(A\mathbf{x}_{t+1}^{*})-f(A\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}^{*})+c_{f}\left(\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t}}}-\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*})+c_{f}\left(\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t}}}-\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1-\gamma_{t}\right)\widetilde{H}_{t}+\gamma_{t}^{2}R^{2}\frac{\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1}{2}+c_{f}\left(\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t}}}-\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\right),

(34)

where
c_{f}=\max_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}f(A\mathbf{x})-f(A\mathbf{y})<\infty
.
The result in
Equation

34
is analogous to the result in Lemma 3.2 in
Woodstock and Pokutta 2025
.
However, it involves a difference of inverse values of
{\lambda_{t}}
, which will be the key difference here.
This allows us to use a larger step size
\gamma_{t}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)}
for the SCG method.

For the given
{\lambda_{t}}=\ln(t+2)
we obtain

\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t}}}-\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\leqslant\frac{{\lambda_{t+1}}-{\lambda_{t}}}{{\lambda_{t}}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\frac{1}{t+2}}{\ln(t+2)^{2}}=\frac{1}{(t+2)\ln(t+2)^{2}}.

(35)

We can now show by induction that

0\leqslant\widetilde{H}_{t}\leqslant\widetilde{G}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{2R^{2}(L+1)+\sqrt{2}c_{f}}{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)}.

(36)

For
t=0
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\widetilde{F}_{\lambda_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(A\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(A\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})}{\lambda_{0}}+\frac{\dist_{\boldsymbol{D}}^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\dist_{\boldsymbol{D}}^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*})}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{c_{f}}{\ln(2)}+\frac{R^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2R^{2}(L+1)+\sqrt{2}c_{f}}{\sqrt{2}\ln(2)}=\widetilde{G}_{0}.

The induction hypothesis together with
Equation

34
and
Equation

35
yield

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-\gamma_{t})\widetilde{H}_{t}+\gamma_{t}^{2}R^{2}\frac{\left(\frac{L}{{\lambda_{t}}}+1\right)}{2}+c_{f}\left(\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t}}}-\frac{1}{{\lambda_{t+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)-2}{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)}\widetilde{G}_{t}+\frac{2R^{2}(L+1)}{(t+2)\ln(t+2)^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}c_{f}}{(t+2)\ln(t+2)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)-2+1}{(t+2)\ln(t+2)^{2}}\cdot(2R^{2}(L+1)+\sqrt{2}c_{f})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+3}\ln(t+3)}\cdot(2R^{2}(L+1)+\sqrt{2}c_{f})=\widetilde{G}_{t+1}.

To finish the induction, it is left to show that

\frac{\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)-1}{(t+2)\ln(t+2)^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+3}\ln(t+3)}.

Consider

\displaystyle\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)-\sqrt{t+1}\ln(t+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{(t+2)^{\sqrt{t+2}}}{(t+1)^{\sqrt{t+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\left(\left(\frac{t+2}{t+1}\right)^{\sqrt{t+2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(t+1)^{\sqrt{t+2}-\sqrt{t+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{t+2}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{t+1}\right)-(\sqrt{t+2}-\sqrt{t+1})\ln(t+1)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{t+2}}{t+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\phi(t).

The function
\phi(t)
is monotonic decreasing and satisfies
\phi(t)\leqslant 1
for
t\geqslant 1
.
Thus, we have

\sqrt{t+2}\ln(t+2)-1\leqslant\sqrt{t+1}\ln(t+1),

(37)

for all
t\geqslant 1
.
One can even see that
Equation

37
holds for
t=0
.
It is now only left to show that

\sqrt{t+1}\ln(t+1)\sqrt{t+3}\ln(t+3)\leqslant(t+2)\ln(t+2)^{2}.

One can easily see that

\sqrt{t+1}\sqrt{t+3}=\sqrt{t^{2}+4t+3}\geqslant\sqrt{t^{2}+4t+4}=t+2.

Furthermore, using twice the concavity of
\ln
yields

\displaystyle\ln(\ln(t+1)\cdot\ln(t+3))

\displaystyle=\ln(\ln(t+1))+\ln(\ln(t+3))

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\ln\left(\frac{\ln(t+1)}{2}+\frac{\ln(t+3)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\ln\left(\ln\left(\frac{t+1}{2}+\frac{t+3}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2\ln(\ln(t+2))=\ln(\ln(t+2)^{2}),

and thus

\ln(t+1)\cdot\ln(t+3)\leqslant\ln(t+2)^{2}.

This concludes the induction. Using
H_{t}={\lambda_{t}}\widetilde{H}_{t}
yields the proposed upper boundary

H_{t}={\lambda_{t}}\widetilde{H}_{t}\leqslant\frac{2R^{2}(L+1)+\sqrt{2}c_{f}}{\ln(t+2)}\in\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right).

Together with
Proposition

7
we get that
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
exists
and that

\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{\lambda_{t}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{*})=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\bigtimes_{i\in I}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}f(\mathbf{x}).

As
{\lambda_{t}}\to\infty
, we have
\dist_{\boldsymbol{D}}^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\to 0
.
Thus, every accumulation point

D.1
Presentation of the SOCGS algorithm

Introduced in
Carderera and Pokutta 2020

a smooth strongly convex function

with Lipschitz continuous Hessian

over a polytope
\mathcal{X}
.
For each iteration

is minimized over the polytope
\mathcal{X}
using a projection-free method. Minimizing such quadratic forms over a polytope amounts to a
Projected Variable-Metric
(PVM) algorithm
Nesterov 2018

Ben-Tal and Nemirovski Spring 2023
(we state PVM in Algorithm

To do so, a Hessian oracle

The quadratic approximation
1
1

1

\mathbf{H}

Section

E.2
Entanglement detection

In the second experiment we consider bipartite entanglement detection.
Solving this problem is equivalent to projecting a given state onto the set of separable states,

\mathcal{S}_{AB}=\conv\{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{A}\otimes\boldsymbol{\rho}_{B}:\boldsymbol{\rho}_{A}\in\mathbb{D}(A),\boldsymbol{\rho}_{B}\in\mathbb{D}(B)\},

where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product and where
\mathbb{D}(A)
and
\mathbb{D}(B)
are the sets of density matrices on systems
A
and
B
respectively, i.e., (hermitian) positive semidefinite matrices with unit trace.
Consequently, the projection problem can be written as

\min_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\|_{F}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
is the given state.
In our experiments, we consider a family of bipartite
3\times 3
entangled states proposed in
Horodecki 1997
.
Given a parameter
a\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, these states are defined by

\boldsymbol{\rho}^{a}_{H}=\frac{1}{8a+1}\begin{pmatrix}a&0&0&0&a&0&0&0&a\\
0&a&0&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&a&0&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&a&0&0&0&0&0\\
a&0&0&0&a&0&0&0&a\\
0&0&0&0&0&a&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&\frac{1+a}{2}&0&\frac{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}{2}\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&0&a&0\\
a&0&0&0&a&0&\frac{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}{2}&0&\frac{1+a}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(60)

The positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion yields necessary and sufficient conditions for systems of the sizes
2\times 2
and
2\times 3
to be separable; however, it is only necessary for higher-dimensional systems
[
Horodecki et al. 1996
]
.
For
a\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{[}
, the entangled states
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{a}_{H}
are not detected by PPT
Horodecki 1997
, making them weakly entangled and thus harder to detect, which justifies our choice.

Liu et al. 2025
consider adding white noise to the state, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}=v\boldsymbol{\rho}^{a}_{H}+\frac{1-v}{9}\mathbf{I},

(61)

for a given noise level
v\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
.
In
Figure

6
we present the results for different noise levels
v\in\{0.95,1.0\}
for a fixed state with parameter
a=0.5
.
We used again a correction interval of
N=1
for QC-MNP and
N=10
for QC-LP.
Comparing the two plots, one can see that adding white noise decreases the distance to
\mathcal{S}_{AB}
and therefore leads to smaller primal values.
Further experiments show that for
v<0.944
, the state becomes separable.
Furthermore, BPCG is reaching optimality for the pure state case
v=1
within the time limit of 2000s, but not in the noisy case, indicating that adding white noise makes the problem more challenging.
Interestingly, QC-LP is performing worse than BPCG for the pure state. This is because the LP solved by QC-LP is often infeasible, leading to computational overhead without any benefit in terms of primal progress.
On the other hand, QC-MNP is reaching optimality in both cases by far the fastest. This indicates that QC-MNP is more suited than QC-LP for non-polytope domains like the set of separable states

E.3
Projection onto the intersection of the Birkhoff polytope and a shifted
\ell_{2}
ball

Experiment details

The Birkhoff polytope
B(n)
is the set of all
n\times n
doubly stochastic matrices and its vertices are permutation matrices and therefore particularly sparse.
Furthermore, one can solve linear minimization problems over the Birkhoff polytope with a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
using the Hungarian algorithm
[
Combettes and Pokutta 2021
]
.

For shifting the center of the
\ell_{2}
ball, we first sample vertices
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{m}

of the Birkhoff polytope by calling the LMO with uniform random directions
\mathbf{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{d}_{m}
.
The ball is then shifted to
\mathbf{s}=\bar{\mathbf{v}}-c\frac{\bar{\mathbf{d}}}{\|\bar{\mathbf{d}}\|_{2}}

where
\bar{\mathbf{v}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{v}_{i}
and
\bar{\mathbf{d}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{d}_{i}
.
The radius of the ball is set to
r=1
, such that the ball and the polytope intersect if and only if
c\leqslant 1
.
Note the diameter of the Birkhoff polytope is
\sqrt{2n}
and therefore much larger than the given radius of the ball.
This setting can be understood as a projection onto the Birkhoff polytope with some noise or flexibility in the projection direction.

The number of sampled vertices
m
controls the dimension of the face where
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
is located, i.e., the number of non-zero entries in
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
.
In particular, the expected number of non-zero entries in
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
is

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{\mathbf{v}}\|_{0}\right]=n^{2}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{m}\right)\approx n^{2}\left(1-e^{-m/n}\right)=n^{2}\cdot q,

for
m=-n\ln(1-q)
.
Let
B_{2}(r,\mathbf{c})
denote the
\ell_{2}
ball of radius
r
and center
\mathbf{c}
.
The problem we consider is,

\min_{\mathbf{X}\in B(n)\cap B_{2}(r,\mathbf{s})}f(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_{0}\|_{F}^{2},

where
\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is sampled with uniform distributed entries over
[0,1]
.

Since the SCG method alternates between updating the point on the Birkhoff polytope and the shifted
\ell_{2}
ball,
we use a cyclic block-coordinate scheme
[
Lacoste-Julien et al. 2013
,
Beck et al. 2015
]
with different update steps on the two sets.
While we perform vanilla FW steps for the
\ell_{2}
ball, we compare different methods for updating the point on the Birkhoff polytope.
Just like in the other experiments, we compare BPCG with the mentioned hybrid methods.
Note, we use the new penalty schedule
{\lambda_{t}}=\ln(t+2)
proposed in
Theorem

5
, but not the proposed monotonic step size.
BPCG relies on step sizes that consider the current FW gap or pairwise gap to perform proper update steps on the active set.
The monotonic step size would lead to suboptimal updates and thus give the QC methods an advantage as they do not depend on any line search.
Consequently, we use the new secant line search proposed by
Hendrych et al. 2025
.

The gradients
