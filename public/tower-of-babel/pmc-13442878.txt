ABSTRACT
Background:Long COVID is a complex condition where symptoms persist for more than 3 months after SARS-CoV-2 infection and affects an estimated 5-30% of individuals. While persistent inflammation has emerged as an important feature of this condition, it is unclear if immune responses from COVID-19 vaccination or SARS-CoV-2 re-infection exacerbate or mirror the initial inflammatory responses.Methods:We quantified 182 inflammatory and neurology-related proteins in plasma using multiplexed affinity proteomics. Plasma samples from the COVID PROFILE cohort conducted in Victoria, Australia, were collected 6-9 months after first infection, but before COVID-19 vaccination from individuals who had recovered from COVID-19 (n = 21) or from individuals with long COVID (n = 12). To establish baseline plasma profiles, protein levels were benchmarked against unvaccinated, SARS-CoV-2 naive individuals (n = 24). In addition, we performed longitudinal analysis in a subset of individuals (n = 34), where paired samples collected 2-4 weeks after a third COVID-19 vaccine dose and after SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection were available to assess inflammatory and neurology protein plasma levels after antigen exposure in these contexts.Results:In this cohort Boruta feature selection and lasso regression models identified IL-20, HAGH, NAAA, CLEC10A, LXN, and MCP-1, TRAIL, G-CSF, NBL1, and CCL23 as best discriminating proteins when comparing the long COVID group to groups of either healthy or COVID-19 recovered. Notably, longitudinal analysis indicated differences in the levels of a subset of plasma proteins following primary infection compared to after COVID-19 booster vaccination and breakthrough infection within the groups.Conclusions:These findings suggest that there is an altered immune response outcome primarily observed in individuals with long COVID upon re-exposure.

Introduction

After initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, a significant proportion of individuals develop persistent systemic symptoms that may continue for months. This condition is commonly referred to as long COVID or post-acute sequelae of COVID-191,2. One of the major challenges in the field is the insufficient understanding of the underlying pathophysiology of this condition, coupled with the absence of reliable biomarkers that could be used in standardised diagnostic tools. Currently, the diagnosis of long COVID is predominantly based on clinical evaluation. The lack of standardised molecular and quantitative diagnostic criteria has impeded accurate estimation of long COVID prevalence. However, available estimates suggest that long COVID affects 10–30% of non-hospitalised individuals and as many as 50–70% of those who were hospitalised during acute infection2,3. Although COVID-19 vaccination may reduce the risk, the incidence of developing long COVID post-vaccination is still estimated to be 10–12%2,4,5.

While the precise mechanisms underlying long COVID is poorly understood, distinct patterns of immune activation have been observed in affected patients and persist for several months after SARS-CoV-2 infection6–10. These patterns are evident both at the cellular level9,11,12 and through changes in soluble factors in the bloodstream. The latter includes elevated or altered concentrations of circulating inflammatory mediators9,10,13,14, complement proteins6,15, and neuro-inflammatory molecules10,16, and inflammatory markers have been associated with a range of persistent long COVID symptoms17. Collectively, these studies have provided essential insights into dysregulated processes that may underlie long COVID. However, many of these long COVID studies have been conducted in populations with high community transmission of SARS-CoV-2, raising concerns about the potential confounding effects of ongoing viral exposure(s), particularly on inflammatory biomarker measurements. Moreover, studies that have monitored inflammatory and neurology plasma protein profiles longitudinally in long COVID individuals remain limited6,8,10,15,18.

In this study, we leveraged access to samples collected from an Australian population with low community transmission during the two first years of the COVID-19 pandemic19. Using a multiplexed affinity proteomics approach, the Olink Target Proximity Extension Assay (PEA, Olink) and longitudinal plasma samples from initially SARS-CoV-2 naïve individuals, COVID-19 recovered and long COVID individuals, we explored inflammatory and neurology-related plasma protein profiles not only following a primary infection with the SARS-CoV-2 ancestral strain, but also after COVID-19 booster vaccination and breakthrough infections. Our study design provided a rare opportunity to understand how circulating protein profiles after vaccination and re-exposure in the same individual compare to plasma profiles after primary SARS-CoV-2 infections in long COVID individuals.

We identify 14 top inflammatory and neurology-related proteins that are associated with either long COVID or recovery. A subset of these proteins best discriminates long COVID patients from healthy or recovered individuals, suggesting potential ongoing dysregulated processes 6–9 months post-infection. Longitudinal analysis reveals partial resolution of elevated plasma protein levels following COVID-19 booster vaccination, but notably, inflammatory and neurology-related protein patterns after primary infection are not replicated following a subsequent breakthrough infection within the long COVID group. In the longitudinal analysis, we also observe differential plasma protein levels in the COVID-19 recovered group of individuals compared to healthy SARS-CoV-2 naïve individuals. Our analyses provide a pivotal understanding of vaccine-associated and subsequent SARS-CoV-2 infection-induced responses in long COVID and COVID-19 recovered individuals, suggesting an altered immune response outcome in these individuals upon re-infection.

Methods

COVID PROFILE study cohort and samples

Whole blood was collected in EDTA vacutainers as part of COVID PROFILE, a community-based longitudinal cohort study conducted in Victoria, Australia, from October 2020 to April 202319. Plasma was collected upon centrifugation of whole blood at 300 g for 10 min and was removed from the top layer and frozen at minus 80 °C until use. The study was approved by the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute (#20/08) and Melbourne Health (RMH69108) Human Research Ethics Committees. All subjects provided written informed consent to participate in this study, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were enrolled based on SARS-CoV-2 infection status and initially categorised as COVID-19 recovered or SARS-CoV-2 naïve (confirmed by SARS-CoV-2 PCR/serology and referred to as healthy). Demographic data and detailed clinical information on COVID-19 symptoms, co-morbidities, vaccination and breakthrough infections were collected at enrolment and during follow-up visits. Primary vaccination consisted of either two doses of the BNT162b2 mRNA vaccine (COMIRNATY, Pfizer/BioNTech), or ChAdOx1 adenoviral vectored vaccine (Oxford-AstraZeneca), followed by a third mRNA vaccine consisting of either BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273 (Moderna Tx Spikevax). Due to the emergence of the long COVID condition, additional follow-up surveys were conducted to identify participants that were experiencing long COVID. These surveys collected comprehensive information on symptom duration and individuals with long COVID were identified in accordance with WHO definition: symptoms persisting or development of new symptoms 3 months after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, with these symptoms lasting for at least 2 months with no other explanation1. All other participants experienced COVID-19 symptoms that lasted <90 days post-diagnosis and were classified as COVID-19 recovered. We performed a nested-case control study20 in which plasma collected at enrolment for healthy (n = 24) and 6–9 months after primary COVID-19 diagnosis for COVID-19 recovered (n = 21) and long COVID individuals (n = 12) were used for exploratory cross-sectional data analyses. The samples included for this immunological sub-study were selected from the larger Victorian cohort study based on availability of a sample collected 6–9 months post primary infection (recovered and long COVID participants) but prior to receiving any COVID-19 vaccination (all participants). All participants that fulfilled these criteria were included in the cross-sectional analyses and no age, sex and comorbidity matching were performed. Eighty three percent (10/12) of long COVID individuals reported at least one persistent symptom which is recognised to be a neurological long COVID symptom (fatigue, brain fog, headaches, loss of smell and/or taste and myalgia), the other two individuals experienced persistent respiratory issues. In a subset of individuals (n = 34), additional paired samples collected at 2–4 weeks after a third dose of COVID-19 vaccine and post-breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection were used for analysis and only individuals with all three time points were included in the longitudinal analysis.

Luminex multiplex assay

A SARS-CoV-2 multi-antigen serology assay was used to detect SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG antibodies against Spike, receptor binding domain (RBD) and nucleoprotein (NP) antigens as described previously21. Briefly, fluorescent and magnetic beads were coated with antigens of interest and incubated with individual plasma samples to allow binding of antigen-specific antibodies. Bound antibodies were detected using a secondary anti-human IgG fragment crystallisable region (Fc) antibody conjugated to phycoerythrin (PE) and analysed using the MAGPIX® system. The levels of antigen-specific antibodies were reported as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Plate-to-plate variation was adjusted for by using a five-parameter logistic regression model algorithm based on standard curves and quality control (QC) samples. Each sample was run in a single well.

Affinity proteomics assays

Protein biomarkers were quantified using the Proximity Extension Assay at the WEHI Genomics facility, on the Olink Target platform using Inflammation (product number 95302) and Neurology (product number 95801) panels (Olink Proteomics, Sweden) for a total of 182 protein targets. Briefly, 1 µL of each plasma sample per panel was incubated with a multiplexed set of two oligo-labelled detection antibodies per protein target, which after proximity extension can form a specific DNA barcode22. The barcodes were pre-amplified, then detected by qPCR using 96.96 protein expression integrated fluidic circuits (IFCs) on a BioMark HD with HX controller (Fluidigm/Standard BioTools, USA). Detection cycle threshold or C(t) values were then processed to normalised protein expression (NPX) values by normalising to the internal Extension Control and the external Inter-plate Control using Olink® Signature software, and batch correction was applied by intensity normalisation and the OlinkAnalyse R package23. The values are expressed in arbitrary NPX units that are specific to the Olink Proteomics platform and are on a log2x scale.

Samples were randomised across batches, and data were normalised by intensity normalisation as per recommendations by OlinkAnalyze24. Bridging samples were included to validate that appropriate data normalisation and reproducible detection occurred across batches (supplementary Fig. S1).

Proteins detected at or below the calculated Limit of Detection were retained in analysis, as per recommendations by OlinkAnalyze25. No samples or assays failed in detection or calculating NPX, and any samples flagged by NPX software due to the Olink internal controls were indicated in the data file under “QC Warning”. These samples were further assessed for quality by principal component analysis (PCA) and deviation from the median (Supplementary Fig. S2; top panel) before inclusion in the dataset for analysis. All samples were included in the final dataset. Data were also assessed for plate-to-plate variation and data variance explored by PCA plots depicting PC1 vs PC2 and PC3 vs PC4 of all samples included in the cross-sectional analysis stratified based on disease status (healthy, COVID-19 recovered and long COVID individuals; supplementary Fig. S2 middle and bottom panels).

Statistical analyses

Participants were stratified according to SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19 booster status. Statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.2 (2023-10-31) for macOS using the lme4, ggplot2, ggeffects, ggthemes, patchwork, OlinkAnalyze, performance, Boruta, glmnet, glmnetUtils rpart, rattle, e1071, corrplot and corrr packages.

Descriptive statistics were used to compare participant demographics, clinical characteristics, and exposure factors (Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test for continuous or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables). Multinomial log-linear regression models via neural networks using the “multinom” function from the “nnet” package in R was performed for analysis of the demographic features. A Healthy group was set as the reference for the outcome measure and compared to individuals who recovered or developed long COVID. P values were calculated using Wald tests.

Both unsupervised and supervised machine learning algorithms for protein analyte selection were applied, selecting models that demonstrated a relatively good fit. We employed distinct analytical approaches for cross-sectional and longitudinal data due to the differing structure and objectives of each dataset. Machine learning models were used in the cross-sectional analysis to explore potential discriminative protein signatures, while linear mixed-effects models were applied to longitudinal data to account for repeated measures, within-subject variability over time, and determine the effect of booster doses and re-infections on plasma protein and antibody profiles. To identify potential protein biomarkers, we used a combination of penalised regression (lasso) and a feature selection algorithm, using glmnet and Boruta packages in R. The glmnet fitted a generalised linear model via penalised maximum likelihood (lasso, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) to enhance the prediction accuracy and interpretability of the resulting statistical model26,27. The incorporated model fitted a traditional logistic regression model for the log-odds with function glmnet(x, y, standardise = TRUE, alpha = 1, family = “binomial”, intercept = T); where the x variable was standardised prior to fitting the model sequence and alpha = 1 for the lasso penalty. The model demonstrated superior performance when employing a lasso penalty (alpha = 1) compared to a ridge penalty (alpha = 0) within the elastic-net regularisation path for regression. We performed a split-sample approach (including k-fold cross-validation to compute the average error and standard deviation over the folds for lasso regression analysis). Analyte selection also involved the Boruta package in R, which uses wrapper algorithms coupled with random forest classifiers with Bonferroni multiple comparisons adjustment (p ≤ 0.01 threshold)28. The number of trees in the forest was set to 1000. The overall accuracy rate is computed along with a 95 percent confidence interval (95% CI) for this rate (using exact binomial test; binom.test function), and unweighted Kappa statistics are also calculated. Both accuracy and kappa range from −1 to 1. A value of 1 indicates perfect agreement, while a value of −1 indicates complete disagreement. A or true positive (TP); B or false negative (FN); C or false positive (FP), D or true negative (TN).

F1 score = (1 + beta2) × precision × recall/((beta2 × precision) + recall), where precision = A/(A + B) and recall = A (A + C).

Kappa = (Observed agreement − chance agreement)/(1 − chance agreement) or

Kappa = 2 × (TP × TN – FN × FP)/(TP + FP) × (FP + TN) + (TP + FN) × (FN + TN)

Sensitivity = A/(A + C)

Specificity = D/(B + D)

Accuracy = (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN).

Boxplots were plotted displaying the distribution of data; with the minimum value, the first quartile (Q1), the median, the third quartile (Q3), and the maximum value. The olink_anova_posthoc was used to perform post-hoc ANOVA test using the function emmeans from the R library emmeans with Benjamini & Hochberg p value adjustment per group contrast (by OlinkID) at confidence level 0.95, p value annotations: “***“=0.001, “**“=0.01, “*“=0.05.

We employed recursive partitioning and regression trees (rpart) to identify protein analytes predictive of COVID-19 status (long COVID and COVID-19 recovered, long COVID and healthy, and COVID-19 recovered and healthy) before vaccination (n = 57). Models were constructed using the rpart and fancyRpartPlot functions in R to understand the hierarchical relationships and thresholds of protein analytes, extracted by lasso and Boruta random forest, that differentiated COVID-19 status (long COVID and COVID-19 recovered, long COVID and healthy, and COVID-19 recovered and healthy). Decision boundaries, generated using support vector machines (SVM) algorithms from the e1071 package in R29, were plotted to distinguish between the groups (healthy, recovered, and long COVID) based on the selected protein concentrations.

Longitudinal data (n = 34) was analysed by olink_lmer function, which fitted a linear mixed-effects model for each protein analyte or antibodies as outcome measure. It uses the lmer function from the R library lmerTest and the anova function from the R library stats. Adjusted p values are calculated using the p.adjust function from the R library stats with the Benjamini & Hochberg method. The threshold for significance is p < 0.05. The olink_lmer_posthoc function is used to plot significant proteins. It performs a post-hoc analysis based on a linear mixed-effects model using the lmer function. Immune dynamics were plotted using linear mixed-effects models fitted by the lmer function of the lme4 package. The resulting linear equation of the model was y = α + β × Group × Condition + (1|Participant), where group is disease status and condition are exposure factors. Randomisation was not utilised, as the study aimed to compare individuals with long COVID with those recovered or uninfected or healthy controls. Data collection and analysis were not conducted in a blinded manner with respect to the experimental conditions. Multiple testing correction (Benjamini–Hochberg or Bonferroni) was applied for each analysis where appropriate. Olink-generated data were adjusted for 182 proteins, and during post-hoc analysis multiple testing correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied for comparisons between groups and for selected proteins in the figure. For longitudinal mixed-effects model analyses, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was first applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) in the main model to identify significant proteins among the 182 tested. Subsequently, the Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied during pairwise comparisons.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 57 participants (aged 20 to 68 years; 38 females, 67%) enrolled in a longitudinal cohort conducted from October 2020 to April 2023 were included in this study. The participants were categorised into three groups: healthy SARS-CoV-2 PCR/serology confirmed uninfected individuals (n = 24; age 20–68 years, median age 37; 14 (58%) females), COVID-19 recovered (n = 21; age 21–66 years, median age 43; 17 (81%) females) and long COVID (n = 12; age 30–67 years, median age 56; 7 (58%) females) with no significant sex difference between groups but median age higher in the long COVID group (p = 0.035) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1). In addition to univariate analysis, multinomial regression analysis also identified age and comorbidity differences between the groups (Supplementary Table S1). Most participants experienced mild to moderate primary COVID-19 disease, with only 9.5% of individuals of the COVID-19 recovered group requiring hospitalisation due to severe disease (Supplementary Data 1). Mild to moderate disease included acute disease that involved one or multiple of the following symptoms (cough, fever, chills, night sweats, sore throat, runny or stuffy nose, tiredness, loss of smell, shortness of breath, myalgia, headache, nausea, vomiting, or other including brain fog and gastrointestinal issues) but did not result in hospitalisation A range of co-morbidities was observed among the healthy (13%), COVID-19 recovered (62%) and long COVID (50%) participants. Half (50%, 6/12) of individuals diagnosed with long COVID reported an alleviation of symptoms during follow-up (four became asymptomatic before COVID-19 boosters and two before re-infection). Conversely, the remaining cohort (50%) continued to experience long COVID symptomatology both after receiving a booster vaccination and breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection. The most common symptom reported in this study cohort was fatigue, brain fog and loss of smell and taste (Fig. 1). Of the 57 participants, longitudinal analysis was performed on 34 participants with paired blood samples at three time points: pre-vaccination; 2–4 weeks after receiving a booster COVID-19 vaccine dose; and either after a new SARS-CoV-2 infection (healthy group) or after re-infection (COVID-19 recovered or long COVID groups). Identification of best discriminating inflammatory and neurology-related proteins in plasma distinguishing healthy, COVID-19 recovered individuals and long COVID sufferers

Persistent inflammation has been proposed as a key mechanism underlying long COVID, as it may explain the varied manifestations associated with this condition. To determine whether long COVID individuals in our low transmission cohort align with previous observations of lasting inflammation and whether signature inflammatory and neurology-related protein profiles tested in this exploratory study can be detected for long COVID, we profiled the levels of 182 plasma proteins using multiplexed affinity proteomics. These plasma samples were collected at enrolment for healthy (n = 24) and 6–9 months after primary COVID-19 diagnosis for COVID-19 recovered (n = 21) and long COVID individuals (n = 12). All plasma was collected prior to COVID-19 vaccination for this cross-sectional analysis. Initial analysis mapped the overall plasma protein profiles of each individual (supplementary Fig. S3). However, no significantly differentially expressed proteins were observed in a univariate analysis after multiple comparison adjustments. We also used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to associate disease status with specific groups of proteins (Supplementary Fig. S4). While these GSEA results also did not reach statistical significance, the indicated pathways based on the plasma protein levels, highlight potentially ongoing processes in both long COVID and COVID-19 recovered groups at 6–9 months following primary SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, to further understand which analytes equated to the best discriminating proteins between healthy, COVID-19 recovered, and long COVID groups, lasso regression analysis was also performed. First, we investigated plasma profile differences between long COVID and COVID-19 recovered groups. Lasso regression identified four top protein analytes associated with long COVID (NBL1, IL-20, LXN, MCP-1), with an accuracy of 0.55 (95%CI: 0.24–0.83) (Fig. 2B and Table 1). In addition, Boruta random forest, recursive partitioning and regression trees were conducted to identify proteins predictive of COVID-19 status. Consistent with lasso regression analysis, NBL1 and IL-20 were identified as top features with both Boruta random forest (Fig. 2A) with similar accuracy of 0.55 (0.23–0.83; Table 1) and recursive partitioning and regression trees (Accuracy = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.72–0.97; and F1 score = 0.91) (Fig. 3A and Table 1). Visual representation of selected features from lasso regression analysis and Boruta random forest are illustrated in Fig. 2C, showing the direction of differential protein levels between the groups identified in the models. This indicated that the estimated average IL-20 concentration was elevated in the long COVID group compared to the COVID-19 recovered group (Fig. 2C). Best features identified by selected models at the pre-vaccination time point, and models' accuracy

Using the same approach, analysis of plasma protein profiles between the long COVID and healthy groups identified four best discriminating proteins (IL-20, CLEC10A, CCL23 and TRAIL) using lasso regression analysis (Fig. 2E) with accuracy of 0.67 (0.35–0.91; Table 1), and collectively five top predictive proteins (HAGH, G-CSF and NAAA) with Boruta random forest (Fig. 2D) with accuracy of 0.75 (0.43–0.95; Table 1) and (HAGH, CLEC10A and NAAA) using decision tree (Accuracy = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.74–0.97; and F1 score = 0.92; Fig. 3C). Visual representation of top features from lasso regression analysis and Boruta random forest are illustrated in Fig. 2F, and G showing the direction of differential protein levels between the groups identified in the models. The healthy group was observed to have lower estimated average levels of HAGH and CLEC10A concentrations, but higher TRAIL concentration compared to individuals with long COVID (Fig. 2F, and G). Specifically, the decision tree analysis indicated that individuals with HAGH NPX levels >5.4 and/or CLEC10A >4.3 and NAAA <2.9 had a higher predicted probability of suffering from long COVID compared to the healthy group (Fig. 3B).

Finally, we assessed plasma protein profile differences between the COVID-19 recovered and healthy groups. In this comparison, a distinct set of proteins were identified (ARTN, CST5, FGF-19 and MCP-1) using lasso regression (Fig. 2I) with an accuracy of 0.56 (0.31–0.79) (Table 1). Additionally, predictive proteins consisted of HAGH, DNER, as determined using Boruta random forest (Fig. 2H) with accuracy of 0.72 (0.47– 0.90), and HAGH, MCP-1 and ARTN identified using a decision tree (Accuracy = 0.8, 95% CI: 0.65–0.90; and F1 score = 0.82; Fig. 3E, and F). Visual representation of top features from lasso regression analysis and Boruta random forest are illustrated in Fig. 2J, and K, showing the direction of differential protein levels between the groups identified in the models. This depicted that the healthy group had higher estimated average concentrations of DNER than the COVID-19 recovered. Individuals with HAGH NPX levels of >5.3 and/or MCP-1 <12 and ARTN >−0.15 had a higher probability of being COVID-19 recovered than healthy in the tree-based model (Fig. 3C).

As age and comorbidity were identified as independent or explanatory variables between groups, we also further extended the analysis to include age and comorbidity as features in the Boruta and lasso regression analyses (Supplementary Table S2). Results from employing models with 184 features (182 proteins, age and comorbidity) indicated that in lasso regression analysis comparisons of recovered vs. long COVID and Healthy vs. recovered, yielded overall consistent predictors from both analyses with or without age and comorbidity as variables. NBL1 was identified in the lasso regression analysis as an additional feature comparing Healthy vs. long COVID when including age and comorbidity. However, the overall model accuracy remained similar. The Boruta analysis when age and comorbidity was included identified (i) NAAA as an additional feature when comparing recovered vs. long COVID, albeit with similar model accuracy, (ii) age and IL5 as additional features but NAAA was not included when comparing Healthy vs. long COVID with slightly improved model accuracy and (iii) comorbidity and IL-17C was identified as additional features when comparing Healthy vs. recovered but accompanied by slightly decreased accuracy (Supplementary Table S2).

Collectively based on our models with and without accounting for demographic variables, we observed, with modest accuracy, that distinct plasma protein profiles and levels could be detected in groups of individuals in this cohort with different levels of SARS-CoV-2 exposure (healthy vs. previously SARS-CoV-2-infected) and varying disease outcomes (COVID-19 recovered vs. long COVID), even long after SARS-CoV-2 acute infection and diagnosis.

Lower Spike-specific IgG antibody levels following breakthrough infection in long COVID individuals and COVID-19 recovered individuals compared to healthy newly infected individuals

Humoral responses to COVID-19 vaccines and SARS-CoV-2 infections after vaccination (breakthrough infections) result in high levels of antigen-specific antibodies30–32. However, this response is poorly documented in individuals with long COVID9,33–37. To gain insight into the overall responsiveness to COVID-19 booster vaccination and SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection in our cohort, we measured SARS-CoV-2-specific anti-Spike and anti-NP antibody responses in all individuals (n = 57) prior to COVID-19 vaccination, but 6–9 months after infection in COVID-19 recovered and long COVID individuals (Fig. 4; left panels). To align antibody level measurements in our Luminex assay (MFI) with an accredited assay, we correlated MFI read-outs from our assay with readouts (OD450 nm) of the Wantai ELISA for available samples (n = 25) that matched the individuals and time point of the cross-sectional analysis of this study. The two assays showed a high positive Spearman correlation (r = 0.9; p < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. S5) of IgG antibodies directed against the receptor binding domain (RBD) of the Spike protein. Longitudinal analysis of antibody levels was also performed (Fig. 4; right panels) using paired samples from individuals (n = 34) at three time points collected at pre-vaccination (pre-vax), 2–4 weeks after the third COVID-19 vaccine dose (post-booster), and 2–4 weeks after either new SARS-CoV-2 infection (healthy group) or being re-infection (COVID-19 recovered or long COVID groups; post(re)infection). As expected prior to vaccination (pre-vax), individuals previously infected with SARS-CoV-2 had significantly higher Spike and NP-specific antibodies than healthy individuals (Fig. 4, p < 0.001 and Supplementary Table S3). Following booster vaccination, all three groups showed high levels of Spike-specific antibodies (Fig. 4; top right panel, p < 0.001). At this time point, as expected, NP-specific antibodies remained higher in the COVID-19 recovered and long COVID groups compared to the healthy group (Fig. 4; bottom right panel). After breakthrough infection, all groups had increased NP-specific antibody levels (Fig. 4; bottom right panel). However, long COVID and COVID-19 recovered vaccinees had higher NP-specific antibodies following breakthrough infection than newly infected vaccinees (healthy) (p < 0.001). In contrast, Spike-specific antibodies in the latter group were higher compared to antibody levels measured following re-infection in the COVID-19 recovered and long COVID groups (Fig. 4, top right panel, p < 0.033 and 0.030, respectively). We also explored group demographics and time since primary diagnosis relative to SARS-CoV-2-specific antibody responses at baseline (cross-sectional analysis, Supplementary Tables S4 and S5) and after boosters and breakthrough infections (longitudinal analysis; mixed effects models, Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). These additional analyses showed that age, sex, comorbidity and time since diagnosis did not have a substantial effect on SARS-CoV-2 antibody levels at baseline (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5) but that age was only relevant for Spike-specific antibody responses post-boosters (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). Differential plasma protein levels after COVID-19 booster vaccination, primary and breakthrough infections within groups of long COVID and COVID recovered individuals

Vaccines are designed to induce short-term inflammatory responses in order to achieve desired immune responses that ultimately provide protection from invading pathogens. However, the impact of vaccine-induced inflammatory responses in individuals that are indicated to experience dysregulated inflammatory activity, such as those with long COVID, is not well-established. Similarly, the understanding of inflammatory reactions following breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection in long COVID individuals is lacking. To investigate these specific aspects, we analysed longitudinal and paired samples from individuals (n = 34) across three time points as previously (pre-vaccination, post-booster and post-infection/re-infection). In this mixed-effects model analyses, we extracted seven significant proteins (LAT, CASP-8, SIRT2, 4E-BP1, CCL4, STAMBP and HAGH) from the 182 measured inflammatory and neurology-related protein levels using the Olink proteomics assays. Six of these proteins (LAT, CASP-8, SIRT2, 4E-BP1, CCL4 and HAGH) were indicated to be either different between groups or across time points within a group (Fig. 5). Apart from HAGH, these proteins did not overlap with the identified proteins in the cross-sectional analysis. We found that SIRT2 and 4E-BP1 concentrations were higher in the healthy group compared to COVID-19 recovered pre-vaccination (p = 0.03 and p = 0.02, respectively; Fig. 5). Notably, SIRT2 and 4E-BP1 NPX values were similar between the long COVID and healthy groups at pre-vaccination. However, after breakthrough infection, the levels of these two proteins in both long COVID and healthy groups were significantly lower than at pre-vaccination and aligned with the COVID-19 recovered group. Similar patterns where protein levels after breakthrough infection were significantly lower than at pre-vaccination were observed with LAT (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S19), although differences were only noted between time points within groups. The protein HAGH, previously identified as one of the best discriminating protein marker at pre-vaccination for both COVID-19 recovered and long COVID groups using Boruta random forest and lasso regression analysis, was not elevated following breakthrough infections in this longitudinal analysis (Fig. 5). In healthy individuals, CCL4 levels decreased after breakthrough infection, but remained similar for the three sample events in the COVID-19 recovered and long COVID groups (Fig. 5C). Finally, caspase-8 (CASP-8) was significantly higher in the long COVID group at pre-vaccination compared to the healthy group but not the COVID-19 recovered group. However, this finding should be validated in other larger cohorts. Interestingly, CASP-8 protein levels observed at pre-vaccination was not replicated by either COVID-19 vaccination or a breakthrough infection in the long COVID and COVID-19 recovered groups and was not induced after these exposure events in the healthy group (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table S17). To investigate the impact of demographic variables in the longitudinal analysis, we included sex, age, and co-morbidities variables in the mixed-effects models for plasma profiles after booster vaccination and breakthrough infections for SIRT2, CASP-8, 4E-BP1, LAT, CCL4, and HAGH (supplementary Tables S10–S15). This showed that comorbidity was only relevant for HAGH and CASP-8 proteins. Discussion

In this study, we focused on establishing the inflammatory and neurology-related plasma protein profiles after initial infection and whether inflammatory reactions are mirrored or exacerbated post-COVID-19 booster vaccination and re-infection using longitudinal samples. Our models suggest that a subset of neurology-related and inflammation proteins in plasma 6–9 months after primary infection in both long COVID and recovered individuals showed an ability to discriminate between groups of individuals with different levels of SARS-CoV-2 exposure (healthy vs. previously SARS-CoV-2-infected) and disease trajectory (recovered vs long COVID) in our cohort. However, further follow-up confirmation studies will be required to validate this finding. Notably, in our longitudinal analysis, the levels of a small group of plasma proteins were not reproduced after breakthrough infection, particularly in long COVID individuals.

We used affinity proteomics to establish inflammatory and neurology-related plasma protein profiles in our cohort sample set. This highly sensitive multiplexed Proximity Extension Assay (PEA, Olink Proteomics) has increasingly been utilised to both investigate plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) profiles following COVID-19 in the search for potential biomarkers for long COVID16,38–41. These studies have collectively identified proteins that can be used to classify long COVID, but also show that different markers can be related to specific long COVID subtypes based on symptoms. Similarly, using the same methodology, a panel of plasma proteins from our cross-sectional analysis (NBL1, IL-20, LXN, MCP-1, CLEC10A, CCL23, TRAIL, HAGH, G-CSF and NAAA) were identified using our models to be predictors of long COVID and observed to best discriminate the long COVID group from either healthy or COVID-19 recovered individuals. Of note, decreased levels of TRAIL, a cytokine with anti-inflammatory properties, in CSF was previously associated with long COVID and align with our finding in plasma16. However, while some of our proteins (CASP-8 and TRAIL) have been reported to be differentially expressed across these specific studies using the Olink assay, the remaining proteins in our panel, were not among the proteins previously identified in plasma as predictive of long COVID using this methodology. It is possible that the divergent panel of proteins reported between the studies is due to the different COVID-19 sub-groups included for comparison. These previous studies focused on differences between acutely ill and/or hospitalised individuals with long COVID sufferers, whereas our study focused on individuals after recovery from mild to moderate disease and long COVID individuals. Importantly, our samples were collected within a similar timeframe after diagnosis for both groups. Nevertheless, each study underscores the complexity of long COVID and highlights the importance of considering various comparisons to comprehensively unravel serological features of this condition.

We found that IL-20 was a prominent feature of long COVID in our cohort, as this protein was identified to be associated with long COVID using both models when comparing to both healthy and COVID-19 recovered. IL-20 is a pleiotropic cytokine with potent inflammatory properties and has been positively associated with the severity of SARS-CoV-2 infection42. The observation of increased IL-20 in long COVID individuals is intriguing as IL-20 exerts its effects through the activation of signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins and specifically via the type I IL-20 receptor complex, which is composed of IL20RA and IL20RB subunits. Cytokines that use the IL-20 receptor complex are believed to be linked to the development of chronic inflammation and autoimmune diseases43 with increased IL-20 concentrations linked to autoantibody production in rheumatoid arthritis patients44. This raises the intriguing possibility that dysregulation of the IL-20 pathway, characterised by enhanced IL-20 levels, might contribute to the development of autoimmune features known to occur in a subset of long COVID individuals45,46. IL-20 also induces synovial fibroblasts and kidney mesangial cells to produce pro-inflammatory molecules, including MCP-147,48 a protein that was also found in our study to be a feature of long COVID.

Given that neurological symptoms, including fatigue and cognitive dysfunction (memory difficulties, attention deficits and sleep disturbances), are common in long COVID and were frequently reported by our patients, we also quantified protein biomarkers with known relation to neurological disease. Interestingly, increased levels of HAGH have been linked to low cognitive function, whereas an inverse relationship of lower levels of LXN, LAT and NAAA have been associated with higher cognitive function in older adults with metabolic syndrome49. Apart from LAT, using our models HAGH, LXN and NAAA were identified as best discriminating proteins that were all elevated in the long COVID group in our cross-sectional analysis. However, HAGH does not appear to be an indicator of long COVID, as increased HAGH was reported in mild neuro-COVID cases16, but was not a predictor of long COVID in that study. Similarly, we also observed that in the context of this study, HAGH was not specifically a feature of long COVID, but rather a feature of SARS-CoV-2 infection, as HAGH was a top protein for both COVID-19 recovered and long COVID individuals. Nevertheless, based on the literature on neurological disease associated with these proteins and the identification of the same proteins in our models suggests that a signature of neurological alteration after SARS-CoV-2 infection may remain detectable after extended times. While this should be verified in a larger cohort, features within this signature may prove useful in monitoring cognitive functions in affected individuals.

A noteworthy, and to our knowledge, unique aspect of our study was the access to paired samples that allowed analysis of inflammatory profiles after COVID-19 booster vaccination and breakthrough infections in groups of healthy, COVID-19 recovered and long COVID individuals. Using a mixed-effects model on samples from a subset of individuals, we found that before vaccination, protein levels were elevated in plasma of long COVID individuals compared to healthy (CASP-8; p = 0.047), a difference which was not noted in the cross-sectional analysis. Similarly, plasma protein levels for a number of proteins (HAGH, LAT, 4E-BP1 and SIRT2) were also elevated prior to vaccination compared to subsequent time points in the long COVID group. Interestingly, the protein level of CASP-8 normalised to ranges similar to those observed in the healthy and COVID-19 recovered groups after the COVID-19 booster vaccine. Whether this normalisation is a vaccine-induced effect in long COVID individuals remains to be established and could be achieved by comparing plasma protein profiles immediately before and after vaccination. Alternatively, it is possible that the underlying factors driving protein overexpression may gradually diminish over time, as suggested by previous research18. It is also possible that the observed changes in certain proteins (such as CASP-8, HAGH and LAT) correlate with the resolution of symptoms within our cohort. However, due to the limited sample size, we were unable to thoroughly investigate this potential association. It should also be noted that CASP-8 and HAGH protein levels in the long COVID group were not significantly different from the COVID-19 recovered group at pre-vaccination in this study cohort and analysis. However, importantly, we established that the levels of HAGH and CASP-8 were not exacerbated nor mirrored by re-infection in this cohort. Of note, none of the healthy individuals and COVID-19 recovered individuals developed long-term symptoms upon breakthrough infections while enrolled in the study.

Furthermore, we observed that protein levels for SIRT2, 4E-BP1 and CCL4 in long COVID individuals and healthy SARS-CoV-2 naïve controls were similar before vaccination. In contrast, COVID-19 recovered individuals had lower levels of these specific molecules, especially for SIRT2 and 4E-BP1. The similarity in this profile between long COVID individuals and healthy individuals, and their difference from COVID-19 recovered individuals, is an interesting observation that could suggest a potential lack of downregulation of these proteins in response to primary infection in long COVID individuals. In support of this is the decreased levels of both 4E-BP1 and SIRT2 in response to infection, as reflected in previously SARS-CoV-2 naïve healthy individuals. This is particularly interesting to observe with 4E-BP1 and SIRT2 where inhibition of either protein has been linked to antiviral effects50–52. However, it should be noted that again, there was no significant difference between the COVID-19 recovered and long COVID group for these proteins, and intriguingly, the same pattern was not observed upon re-infection in long COVID individuals. Larger cohorts will be required to determine whether the levels of these proteins indicate a potential dysregulation of antiviral processes typically induced to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 replication in long COVID individuals and whether 4E-BP1 and SIRT2 detected in plasma correlates to intracellular levels and activity.

Finally, we assessed SARS-CoV-2-specific Spike and NP IgG antibody levels in all study participants. Elevated SARS-CoV-2-specific antibody titres have previously been reported in long COVID individuals9,12,53. Our group had associated these elevated titres with long COVID symptoms such as fatigue54 in a different low transmission setting cohort, and some studies suggest a potential role of viral persistence in driving these responses55,56. Other studies have found no significant difference in antibody titres between individuals with long COVID and those who have recovered41,57. It has been proposed that this may reflect two main patterns of antiviral immune responses in long COVID individuals, representing robust and strong cellular and humoral responses similar to those seen in people who have fully recovered and lower antiviral responses, but still with detectable antigen-specific CD4 + T cells and/or antibodies58. In our cohort, we observed no substantial difference in Spike and NP-specific IgG antibodies in the long COVID group compared to the recovered group 6–9 months after primary infection.

There are some differences and limitations in our study, which may be reflected in the protein biomarkers discovered. Samples were selected based on the availability of specimens that met the WHO long COVID definition and had been collected at a specific timepoint after diagnosis. Additionally, for the longitudinal analysis, we were restricted by the samples available for individuals that had also received a COVID-19 booster vaccination and had had a breakthrough infection while enroled in the study. Due to the specific set of samples and time points involved, we were unable to find appropriate independent study data for validation of our models. Furthermore, the sample size was small, especially in the long COVID group, and our sample set was also heterogeneous in terms of long COVID symptoms. Consequently, this limited the statistical power of our analyses, particularly for subgroup analyses and examining differing long COVID trajectories. This limitation means that non-significant univariate test findings may mask true associations between variables, necessitating cautious interpretation of regression estimates following booster and (re-)infection. Additionally, observed baseline differences between the groups were noted to have some confounding effects in the predictive models and mixed-effects models. Larger cohort sizes are needed in future studies to validate these findings and improve the generalisability of the identified signatures. Furthermore, we lack information on the specific variant responsible for breakthrough infections. Although the timing of these breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infections in our cohort aligns with epidemiological data indicating that most community infections at the time were due to the Omicron variant59,60, a definitive conclusion on breakthrough infective strains cannot be drawn. Finally, our analyses do not reveal how or whether our putative biomarker proteins are mechanistically linked to long COVID, and further studies need to be conducted to explore their functional role in this context. Additionally, significant proteins identified using the Olink platform in our study were not confirmed in additional assays. However, other studies have performed such validation with conventional assays and found well-correlated results39,61.

In conclusion, we identified several protein markers that were associated with long COVID in our cohort, which can be further explored to discern long COVID disease subsets. Our longitudinal analysis demonstrated that COVID-19 booster vaccination efficiently induced high levels of antigen-specific antibodies without resulting in exacerbation of inflammatory and neurology-related plasma protein levels. Notably, we observed that plasma profiles following SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection in long COVID individuals diverged from the primary infection that lacked features of previously noted plasma protein levels. This was also observed for some of the proteins in the COVID-19 recovered group. These findings provide intriguing and valuable insight into immune response outcomes, especially to SARS-CoV-2 re-infections. This is of particular relevance in individuals with long COVID, where such observations upon validation in larger cohorts may facilitate long COVID management strategies in the future for this poorly understood condition.
