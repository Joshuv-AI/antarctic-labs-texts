Efficacy of a synthetic collagen-based sealant (TachoSil®) in preventing cerebrospinal fluid leak following planned and incidental durotomies in spine surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Pedro David Delgado-López, Ane Barreras-García, Ana Sabel Herrero Gutiérrez, Antonio Montalvo-Afonso, Rubén Diana Martín, Javier Martín-Alonso, Vicente Martín-Velasco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage is a significant complication following planned or incidental durotomies during spinal surgery. While primary suturing remains the gold standard for dural closure, it is common to necessitate adjunctive reinforcement techniques. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of a synthetic collagen-based sealant patch (TachoSil®) in preventing CSF leaks in a large cohort of patients undergoing spinal surgery. A retrospective analysis was conducted in whom TachoSil® was used as a dural sealant, either following planned (n = 12) or incidental (n = 42) durotomies, or prophylactically (n = 31) after decompression for critical lumbar canal stenosis. The primary endpoint was the prevention and resolution rate of CSF leaks. Secondary endpoints included reoperation rates and postoperative complications. A total of 85 patients (6.8%) met the inclusion criteria (mean age, 64.7 ± 15.4 years). TachoSil® was used as monotherapy in 39 patients and in combination with primary suturing or other sealants in 46 patients. Overall, 66 patients (77.6%) did not require surgical reintervention. TachoSil® monotherapy was associated with a significantly higher success rate (89.7%) compared to combined techniques (68.2% for TachoSil® plus suturing and 60.0% for TachoSil® plus other sealants; p = 0.041). Excluding its prophylactic use (either planned or incidental durotomy, n = 54), its efficacy in avoiding reintervention was 86.7% for TachoSil® alone, 77.3% for TachoSil® plus suture, and 64.7% for TachoSil® plus other combinations of sealants (p = 0.67). Postoperative complications occurred in 22 patients (25.9%), primarily including persistent CSF fistula, pseudomeningocele formation, and surgical site infections. TachoSil® demonstrated high efficacy as a standalone dural reinforcement method and when combined with suture or other sealant techniques in preventing CSF leaks following spinal surgeries involving dural compromise.Keywords: TachoSil, Incidental durotomy, Cerebrospinal fluid leak, Spinal surgery, Collagen-based sealant, Dural repair

Introduction

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage is a significant complication that can arise following both intentional and incidental durotomies during spinal surgery [1, 2]. The incidence of incidental or accidental dural tears ranges from 1.6 to 17% depending on the type of technique [1, 3–6]. Such leaks are associated with a spectrum of clinical manifestations, including orthostatic headaches, nausea, and, in severe cases, meningitis due to the potential ingress of pathogens [7–9]. The presence of a CSF fistula not only exacerbates patient morbidity but also poses challenges in postoperative management, often necessitating prolonged hospitalization and additional interventions [8, 9]. Incidental durotomy and subsequent CSF leak can also result in medical malpractice claims [10].

Various techniques have been employed to achieve dural closure and mitigate the risk of CSF leakage [11]. Primary suturing remains the gold standard, aiming to restore the dural integrity directly. However, this approach can be technically demanding, especially in minimally invasive spine surgery [12], and in cases where the dural edges are friable or the tear is irregular [13]. Adjunctive measures, such as the application of fibrin sealants, have been utilized to reinforce the suture line and promote hemostasis [14]. Nonetheless, fibrin sealants may lack sufficient mechanical strength to withstand the pulsatile nature of CSF pressure, potentially leading to persistent leaks [15, 16]. Synthetic and biological patches have also been introduced to augment dural repair. While these patches can provide additional support, concerns regarding biocompatibility, risk of infection, and potential for adhesion formation have been reported [14].

TachoSil® (Baxter, Illinois, USA), a collagen-based fibrin sealant patch, has emerged as a promising alternative for dural repair in spinal surgery [17]. Upon application, TachoSil adheres to the tissue surface, forming a stable fibrin clot that facilitates hemostasis and seals dural defect. Its ease of use and rapid action make it particularly advantageous in complex surgical scenarios where traditional suturing is challenging [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of TachoSil in reducing CSF leak rates and minimizing associated complications [19–24]. The objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of TachoSil in preventing CSF fistulas following spinal surgery. To our knowledge, this represents one of the largest series of spinal surgery patients treated with this sealant reported to date.

Methods

Study design and inclusion criteria

We conducted a retrospective study including all consecutive patients undergoing spinal surgery in which a dural opening—either incidental or intentional—was encountered, and where dural closure was reinforced with a TachoSil® patch to achieve watertight sealing and prevent postoperative CSF leakage. The study received approval from the local institutional review board.

Eligible cases comprised both elective and emergency spinal surgeries, performed via any surgical approach that resulted in dural exposure. All patients included exhibited intraoperative dural compromise, encompassing planned durotomies, unintended dural tears during decompression and/or fusion procedures, and incomplete dural openings associated with arachnoid herniation—whether or not frank CSF leakage was observed. We also used this sealant in patients in which, following decompression, the dura appeared extremely attenuated, translucent, and friable, but without overt CSF extravasation. No specific exclusion criteria were applied. We studied the cohort of patients in which the dural tear was managed with Tachosil® alone or in combination with other sealant procedures. Between September 2021 and March 2025, 1,254 spinal procedures were performed. A total of 85 patients (6.78%) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study.

Clinical data and study endpoints

Demographic and clinical variables were retrospectively recorded, including age, sex, surgical indication and type of intervention, type of durotomy (no durotomy, incidental or intended durotomy), type of dural affectation (either no evident dural tear, only dural tear without arachnoid disruption -arachnoid bubble-, or clear dural opening with CSF egress), dural repair technique (Tachosil® alone or combined with other measures), intraoperative strategy for leak management, need for reintervention, length of hospital stay (after first and second or subsequent operations), occurrence of postoperative complications, and whether the CSF leak resolved after the first or the second operation.

The primary objective was to evaluate the efficacy of TachoSil® collagen sponge patches in reinforcing dural repair and preventing/resolving postoperative CSF leakage. Secondary endpoints included rates of reoperation and postoperative complications.

Surgical technique for TachoSil® application and wound closure

Dural repair techniques were selected at the discretion of the attending neurosurgeon. When feasible, primary closure was attempted using non-absorbable monofilament sutures (4 − 0 or 5 − 0) under microscopic magnification. Adjuncts such as fibrin sealants (e.g., Tisseel®, DuraSeal®), autologous fat grafts, and TachoSil® patches tailored to the dimensions of the dural defect were variably applied. Valsalva maneuvers were routinely employed to confirm watertight closure intraoperatively.

In a subset of patients (n = 31) undergoing decompressive surgery for critical lumbar canal stenosis, in whom the dura appeared extremely attenuated, translucent, and friable, but without overt CSF extravasation, prophylactic reinforcement with TachoSil® was undertaken to explore its potential role in preventing delayed CSF leakage (Fig. 1).

Sagittal and axial MRI images (Fig. 4A) show critical lumbar canal stenosis at the L4L5 level with almost complete compression of the thecal sac. After surgical decompression (Fig. 4B), the dura mater shows a band of deformation due to the severe compression (white arrow) with very weakened areas, almost transparent (green arrow), due to the expansion of the previously compressed dural tissue. In Figs. 4C and 4D, it is shown how we can reinforce the weakened dura mater with Tachosil® patches covering the entire exposed dura mater, in a theoretical attempt to prevent late CSF fistulas

A soft wound drain with gentle suction was routinely placed and removed within 24–48 h postoperatively. Multilayer wound closure was standardized using barbed suture (e.g., 1 − 0 Stratafix™) for the muscle-fascia layer, followed by layered closure of subcutaneous and subdermal tissues with 3 − 0 Vicryl®, and final skin approximation using a running monofilament suture (3 − 0 or 4 − 0) [25].

Postoperative follow-up and management of CSF leak

All patients were monitored during hospitalization and at subsequent outpatient visits. In cases where CSF leakage occurred, initial management included strict bed rest and avoidance of physical exertion, in combination with local reinforcement of the wound closure using additional sutures at the site of CSF egress.

If conservative measures failed, surgical re-exploration was indicated (Fig. 2). Reoperations were conducted under general anesthesia and involved reopening of the wound, identification of the dural defect, and primary closure whenever possible. TachoSil® patches were subsequently applied over the suture line or dural defect to ensure watertightness (Figs. 3 and 4). No postoperative drains were employed in reoperations.

During the surgical revision of the pseudomeningocele wound, a small rounded dural dehiscence approximately 1 mm in diameter (Fig. 2A) was observed on the lateral aspect of the thecal sac, in a location difficult to access for suturing. The defect was partially sutured under the microscope using 6/0 monofilament and a Tachosil® patch was applied over it (Fig. 2B). The surgical wound was sutured with a continuous barbed suture of 1/0 in the muscle-fascial plane. CSF leak–related complications—including orthostatic headache, nausea, vomiting, or infection—were managed following established clinical protocols, including bed rest, deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis, analgesics, antiemetics, fluid administration, and targeted antibiotic therapy as clinically indicated. Cases of wound or CSF infection (e.g., meningitis) were initially treated with empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics and later tailored based on culture and sensitivity results.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24 statistical software. A descriptive analysis was conducted for quantitative variables (mean, standard deviation, range) and qualitative variables (absolute and relative frequencies). Success rates between dural repair methods were compared using the Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were less than 5. A univariate analysis of demographic and surgical variables was performed to identify factors associated with incidental dural rupture. Continuous variables were analyzed using either the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A binary logistic regression model was subsequently constructed, including variables with p < 0.10 in the univariate analysis, to identify independent predictors of dural rupture. Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI 95%). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 85 patients undergoing spine surgery were included, with a mean age of 64.7 ± 15.4 years (median age 67 years, range 44–86 years). All patients were treated with Tachosil®. The most common type of intervention was laminectomy (76.5%), followed by laminectomy with fixation (11.8%) and tumor resection (10.6%). The surgical approach was posterior in 98.8% of cases. Surgical indications included: spinal canal stenosis (n = 51), spinal intradural tumor resection (n = 14), either lumbar or cervical disc herniation (n = 10), decompression and fusion for lumbar spondylolisthesis (n = 7), and dorsal or lumbar fracture repair surgery (n = 3).

Out of these 85 patients, 42 had incidental durotomy (49.4%), of which 32 exhibited frank CSF egress during surgery and 10 presented with dural tear and the formation of an arachnoid bubble without overt CSF leak. In 12 patients, durotomy was performed intentionally (14.1%). The remaining 31 patients (36.5%) did not experience frank dural tear during surgery and no evident CSF leak occurred, but were otherwise treated with Tachosil® alone to reinforce the extremely thinned dura after decompression. Overall, some form of dural disruption occurred in 54 patients (63.5%).

The demographic data of the cohort, clinical outcomes, and hospital stay are summarized in Table 1. Reintervention was required in 19 patients (22.4%), primarily due to persistent CSF fistula. The main postoperative complications (22 patients, 25.9%) were recurrent CSF fistula, surgical wound infection, pseudomeningocele, postural headache (CSF hypotension syndrome), and epidural hematoma. The mean length of stay for the first intervention was 7.0 ± 9.1 days (range: 3–60 days), and for the second or subsequent interventions, it was 2.5 ± 6.7 days.

When comparing the efficacy of different repair methods (Fisher’s exact test), the use of Tachosil as monotherapy showed a significantly higher rate of CSF fistula resolution compared to combinations of Tachosil + suturing or Tachosil + other sealants (p = 0.041). No statistically significant differences were observed between the combined methods (Tachosil + suturing vs. Tachosil + sealants, p = 0.68).

Regarding the efficacy of dural repair methods, the use of Tachosil® as monotherapy was associated with the highest clinical success rate (89.7%). In comparison, techniques combined with suturing or sealants presented lower resolution rates (68.2% and 60.0%, respectively). The statistical analysis revealed significant differences favoring Tachosil monotherapy over the other combinations (p = 0.041) (Table 4).

Excluding patients in which prophylactic patching was used (n = 31), the efficacy of the patch in avoiding reoperation in the remaining 54 cases was 86.7% for Tachosil® alone (n = 15), 77.3% for Tachosil® plus suture (n = 22), and 64.7% for the remaining combinations of patching and other sealants (n = 17).

A separate analysis (Table 3) was performed in patients with incidental durotomy and in those with intentional durotomy. In incidental durotomies, the reoperation-avoidance rate was 72.7% for suture + TachoSil® (n = 11) and 86.7% for TachoSil® alone (n = 15) (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.67). In intentional durotomies, the reoperation-avoidance rate was 81.8% for suture + TachoSil® (n = 11) and no TachoSil® alone was used in this subset. Regarding prophylactic use of TachoSil®, only 2 cases of reoperation were needed, both in patients in which TachoSil® was used to reinforce the dura.

Among the subset of patients in which Tachosil was used prophylactically (n = 31), only 2 patients needed reoperation; one because of CSF leak occurring 3 weeks postoperatively and another presenting pseudomeningocele without external egress of CSF. Both cases of occult dural tear resolved after the second operation.

Postoperative complications and hospital stay

Overall, postoperative complications occurred in 22 patients (25.9%), with the most frequent being persistent CSF fistula, surgical wound infection, pseudomeningocele, postural headache (CSF hypotension syndrome), and epidural hematoma. The overall incidence of postoperative complications related to CSF fistula was 17.6%, with notable cases of subcutaneous CSF collections (5 cases of pseudomeningocele) and isolated cases of infection (4 patients that resolved with broad spectrum and subsequently directed antimicrobial therapy). The mean hospital stay was 9.2 ± 4.7 days, significantly increased in patients with persistent fistula (mean 14.8 days vs. 7.8 days; p < 0.01).

Factors associated with dural rupture

The univariate analysis (Table 5) identified older age (over 75 years, p = 0.04) and the type of intervention (higher risk in tumor resection surgeries; p = 0.02) as factors significantly associated with the occurrence of incidental durotomy. Additionally, the need for complex dural repair techniques (combined use of Tachosil® with suturing or sealants) was associated with a higher incidence of dural rupture (p = 0.03). In the multivariate analysis using logistic regression, age (OR 1.05; 95% CI 1.01–1.11; p = 0.03), and tumor resection (OR 3.2; 95% CI 1.2–8.4; p = 0.01) were confirmed as independent predictors of incidental dural rupture (Table 6).

Occult dural tears

In a single patient (1.2%), a delayed CSF fistula occurred with a large pseudomeningocele, despite no dural injury being present during the intervention and no CSF leakage observed during surgery. This patient presented 2 months later with severe headache and radicular pain upon standing. Reintervention was required, with sealing using sutures plus Tachosil®, leading to complete resolution of the fistula, as shown in Figs. 2, 3 and 4. Another patient without intraoperative dural disruption developed wound CSF leakage that ultimately resolved with reoperation and new TachoSil® patching.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, a total of 85 patients undergoing spine surgery were treated with Tachosil®. Incidental durotomy occurred in 42 patients (49.4%), with 32 showing frank CSF egress during surgery. The use of Tachosil as monotherapy demonstrated a significantly higher success rate in resolving CSF fistulas, both in incidental and intended durotomies, compared to combined methods (89.7% vs. 68.2%), likely attributable to a certain selection bias (combined methods might have been used in more complex cases). Postoperative complications occurred in 25.9% of patients, with recurrent CSF fistula being the most frequent. Reintervention was required in 22.4% of cases, primarily due to persistent fistula. All cases resolved after either primary or secondary surgery. Factors such as older age, tumor resection, and the use of complex dural repair techniques were associated with higher risk of incidental durotomy. As expected, the mean hospital stay was significantly longer for patients with persistent fistula.

CSF leakage remains a significant and potentially debilitating complication in spinal surgeries involving intentional or incidental durotomies. The use of adjunctive sealing materials, such as TachoSil®—a collagen-based hemostatic patch coated with human fibrinogen and thrombin—has gained increasing attention for its potential to improve dural sealing and reduce postoperative CSF fistulas. TachoSil® is a sterile, ready-to-use surgical patch composed of an equine collagen matrix coated on one side with human coagulation factors—fibrinogen and thrombin. Upon contact with bodily fluids, especially blood or CSF, the thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin, initiating a clotting cascade that results in the adhesion of the patch to tissue surfaces and the creation of a watertight seal [26]. Its flexibility and conformability make it particularly useful in the constrained and irregular anatomical regions of the spinal canal.

Clinical efficacy of collagen patch for the prevention of CSF leak

Several clinical studies have investigated the efficacy of TachoSil® in preventing postoperative CSF leakage in spinal procedures (Table 7). In a recent retrospective review, Chaliparambil et al. [19] assessed 56 patients undergoing spinal surgery with incidental or intentional durotomies sealed with TachoSil®. Only 1.8% developed radiographically evident CSF collections, and none required reoperation for dural closure failure within 90 days. This suggests excellent short-term efficacy and safety. Similarly, in a series by Gazzeri et al. [17]., seven patients with incidental dural tears during anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) were treated with TachoSil® and fibrin glue. The intraoperative CSF leakage was successfully controlled in all cases, with no postoperative leakage or infections noted during a six-month follow-up [17]. Another study by Montano et al. [24]., focusing on intradural spinal tumor resections, found only one postoperative CSF leak in 35 patients managed with TachoSil® and fibrin glue, which resolved without surgical intervention. In our series, the application of TachoSil® helped to avoid reintervention in a large proportion of patients, yet nearly 10% needed subsequent revision surgery.

Comparison with other dural closure techniques

The current gold standard for dural repair remains primary suturing. However, this technique is not always feasible, especially in minimally invasive or anterior approaches. Sealants and patches are increasingly used as adjuncts or alternatives. In a systematic review by Than et al. [27]., it was shown that the use of sealants including TachoSil® did not significantly reduce leak rates when used alone but were useful when combined with suturing. Conversely, another systematic review by Choi et al. [1]. reported that combining sutures with biologic patches (including TachoSil®) resulted in the lowest rate of CSF fistulas (5.5%) compared to sutures alone (17.6%) or sutures with sealants (13.7%). In vitro studies also provide insight into mechanical properties. A pressure resistance study by van Doormaal et al. [28]. demonstrated that TachoSil® withstands intracranial pressures up to approximately 20 cmH₂O, but may not be sufficient under higher-pressure gradients, particularly in lumbar spine or intradural procedures. Our study aligns with these previous reports and highlights the capacity of TachoSil® to prevent CSF leak even in monotherapy.

TachoSil® offers several advantages that make it a valuable adjunct in neurosurgical procedures. Its primary strengths include ease of use and time efficiency. The product can be applied rapidly without the need for complex instrumentation or additional suturing, which may contribute to a reduction in operative time and anesthesia-related risks. Moreover, TachoSil® facilitates effective sealing in anatomically complex regions. Its conformability enables adherence over irregular surfaces or anatomically challenging areas, such as the anterior dura or lateral recesses. The collagen-based matrix is fully absorbable over several weeks, thereby reducing the long-term risk of foreign body reactions and eliminating the necessity for subsequent removal.

Nonetheless, certain limitations must be considered. Cost represents a significant drawback: TachoSil® is substantially more expensive than traditional suturing techniques, raising concerns about its cost-effectiveness, particularly in resource-limited settings. Additionally, the product is not optimal for managing high-pressure CSF leaks, as its sealing capacity may be compromised under sustained pressure gradients or in the presence of large dural defects without concurrent suturing [28]. Another critical limitation is the absence of large-scale randomized controlled trials. Despite encouraging preliminary outcomes, most of the available evidence is derived from retrospective analyses or small case series, which limits the strength and generalizability of current conclusions. With regard to safety, postoperative infections, foreign body reactions, and allergic responses associated with TachoSil® are rare. In the referenced studies, no infections or allergic complications were reported following its use [17, 19, 24]. Furthermore, the reoperation rate due to CSF leak-related complications was zero in the TachoSil®-treated cohorts, supporting its reliability when applied appropriately.

TachoSil® represents an effective and safe adjunct in managing dural tears during spinal surgery. When used alone or as a supplement to primary suturing, it provides a reliable watertight seal, potentially reducing postoperative CSF fistula formation, infection, and the need for reoperation. Its rapid application, biocompatibility, and hemostatic properties make it a valuable tool in both open and minimally invasive approaches. Nevertheless, economic factors and the need for high-quality randomized trials must be addressed to refine clinical guidelines for its optimal use.

Delayed CSF leak without intraoperative dural tear (occult dural tear)

Postoperative CSF leakage may occur even in the absence of an intraoperative dural breach or visible CSF extravasation [29–31]. This phenomenon is likely attributable to small, unrecognized dural microtears incurred during surgical manipulation or to inadvertent puncture by residual osseous spicules following decompressive procedures [31]. Elevated intracranial or intraspinal pressure may further predispose patients to the development of postoperative CSF fistulas. We postulate that individuals with severe lumbar spinal stenosis—characterized by markedly diminished canal diameter—are particularly susceptible to occult dural injuries. In these cases, the chronically compressed dura mater becomes attenuated and structurally fragile, rendering it more vulnerable to disruption upon re-expansion during decompression. This susceptibility may be exacerbated in taller individuals, in whom the hydrostatic component of CSF pressure within the caudal spinal canal is inherently greater. In such patients, despite the absence of visible intraoperative CSF leakage, we advocate for prophylactic dural reinforcement using a hemostatic and sealing agent like TachoSil® to mitigate the risk of delayed postoperative CSF egress (Fig. 1). Yet, further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Limitations

This study has limitations that may prevent the generalization of its results. It is a retrospective study where clinical data was obtained from patient medical records which introduces an inherent bias related to the quality of such medical records. Although all interventions were performed by the same five neurosurgeons in a single center, variability in surgical techniques and discretion in applying intraoperative sealing methods for dural tears may have introduced some confounding factors and variability. Furthermore, some clinical variables that may predispose to CSF fistula in spine surgery (such as pre-existing CSF hypertension and possibly body mass index) were not measured, which could introduce variability in the results and the efficacy of sealants. Although all cases of fistula were resolved, we cannot quantify the individualized influence on the efficacy of dural repair of the various methods used (suturing, Tachosil®, and other sealants). In this study we could not compare patching against suture alone (control group), which limits direct comparisons with standard dural repair techniques. In fact, our findings suggest that patching alone may be superior to other combinations techniques, likely reflecting a plausible selection bias, that is, a tendency towards using combined therapies in more complex cases. Finally, we propose a pathophysiological hypothesis for the occurrence of delayed fistulas in patients where no CSF egress was observed during surgery (occult dural tears), which should be tested in studies specifically designed for this purpose. These studies should ideally include the broadest possible population data regarding the occurrence of delayed fistulas in various patient subgroups, adjusted for individual clinical factors and likely radiological factors (preoperative severity of the dural sac compression), as well as personal predispositions for the occurrence of CSF fistula after spine surgery.

Conclusion

This study confirms the high efficacy of collagen patches (TachoSil®), either alone or in combination with other sealing methods, in preventing reoperations due to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, both in patients with intentional and incidental durotomies, following spinal interventions. The preventive use of these patches in patients with particularly fragile dura following spinal canal decompression, even in the absence of overt CSF leakage, may represent a novel indication in light of their demonstrated efficacy, although adequately powered comparative studies are required to confirm their clinical utility.
