Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals immune cell dysfunction and rewired interaction networks in the peripheral blood of active tuberculosis patients
Authors: Yanling Wei, Hongqiu Pan, Fuhui Lu, Xiaowei Deng, Jianming Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains a global health threat with poorly understood immune mechanisms. This exploratory study employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 3 active TB (ATB) patients and 2 healthy controls (HC) to dissect immune cell dysfunction and communication networks. Data were processed using CellRanger, Seurat, and Harmony for quality control, batch correction, and clustering, with cell types annotated via marker genes and SingleR. Functional analyses included cytotoxicity, antigen presentation scoring, differential gene expression, and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, while CellChat mapped ligand-receptor interactions. Results revealed altered immune cell composition in ATB, including increased myeloid cells/platelets and reduced NK cells. CD8 + effector T cells (CD8_Te) and CD16bright CD56dim NK cells (NK_CD56dim) exhibited reduced expression of cytotoxicity-associated genes, while Naïve B cells (Naïve_B)and dendritic cells(DC) showed impaired antigen presentation. RNA metabolism pathways were upregulated alongside suppressed immune regulation. Cell communication analysis identified weakened CD8 + T cell incoming signals, enhanced B/NK cell signaling, and intensified monocyte-driven interactions. TGF-β pathway enrichment and defective MHC signaling disrupted network dynamics, suggesting RNA metabolism dysregulation and TGF-β-mediated immunosuppression as key mechanisms. These findings provide insights that may help inform future studies on TB immunopathology and treatment development. Limitations include reliance on peripheral blood, small sample size, and lack of protein-level validation. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection, remains a major global public health threat and the leading cause of death from bacterial infectious diseases [1]. The 2024 Global Tuberculosis Report estimates that approximately one-quarter of the world’s population harbors latent Mtb infection, with over 10 million individuals developing active TB (ATB) annually [2, 3]. Although the host immune system employs multiple pathways to combat Mtb infection, 5–10% of infected individuals progress to ATB during their lifetime. The lack of an effective vaccine - the currently available Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine exhibits only 0–80% protective efficacy - further impedes TB control efforts [4]. Mtb, a facultative intracellular pathogen with a complex cell structure, interacts dynamically with host anti-Mtb immune responses, ultimately shaping the progression and outcome of TB [5]. A comprehensive understanding of Mtb pathogenesis and its interplay with human immune responses is urgently needed. Yet, the dynamic features of interactions between Mtb and host immune cells remain incompletely elucidated.

Peripheral blood, a critical component of the human immune system, reflects the overall efficacy of host anti-infective immunity through its immune cell composition and functional states [6]. As an easily accessible clinical sample with high immune cell content, peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets are vital for monitoring immune responses. Analysis of peripheral blood immune cells provides insights into coordinated immune responses against pathogen infections. Although previous studies have reported transcriptional profiles of whole blood or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in TB patients, conventional bulk transcriptome sequencing fails to resolve cell subtype-specific features, limiting the exploration of heterogeneous immune cell responses [7–9]. Identifying changes in immune cell distribution and gene expression profiles between healthy controls (HC) and ATB patients is crucial for understanding how Mtb infection impacts the host immune system.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), a powerful tool for dissecting immune cell responses, offers novel perspectives for unraveling host-pathogen interactions and has been widely applied in infection research [10–12]. By enabling high-resolution characterization of cell types, scRNA-seq reveals unique functional states of individual cell populations and elucidates heterogeneity in gene expression patterns [13]. In this study, we performed scRNA-seq analysis on PBMCs from HC and ATB patients to systematically map high-resolution transcriptional landscapes of peripheral blood immune cells. Combined with cell-cell communication network analysis, we explored Mtb infection-induced alterations in immune cell functionality. Our findings demonstrate impaired immune cell function and disrupted interaction networks in ATB patients, potentially identifying novel targets for TB immunotherapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from Zhenjiang Third People’s Hospital. The inclusion criteria for ATB cases were as follows: (1) typical active pulmonary tuberculosis lesions observed on chest imaging; (2) positive sputum bacteriological tests; (3) anti-tuberculosis medication use for ≤ 7 days; (4) age ranging from 18 to 70 years. Individuals with hepatic/renal dysfunction, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, diabetes, or those who were pregnant or lactating were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent. Clinical trial number: not applicable (non-interventional study involving only biospecimen collection for molecular analyses).

Peripheral blood collection and PBMC isolation

After obtaining informed consent, 10 mL of venous blood was drawn from the antecubital vein into EDTA anticoagulant tubes. PBMCs were isolated using Ficoll-Paque Plus reagent (GE Healthcare), washed twice with PBS, and cryopreserved for subsequent analysis.

Single-cell RNA library preparation and sequencing

Cell suspensions were barcoded using the 10× Chromium Single Cell platform. scRNA-seq libraries were generated with the Chromium Single Cell 3′ V3.1 Reagent Kit (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) on a Chromium Controller (10× Genomics). Final libraries were quantified using the Qubit High-Sensitivity DNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and size distribution was assessed with a High-Sensitivity DNA Chip on Qsep100 (Bioptic). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with 150 bp paired-end reads.

Single-cell transcriptome data processing

Raw scRNA-seq data for two HC PBMC samples (SRR10038994 and SRR10038995) were downloaded from the NCBI SRA database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) under accession SRP247583 [11]. Raw sequencing data from 3 ATB patients and 2 HC were processed using CellRanger (10X Genomics, v8.0.1) with the GRCh38 reference genome. Quality control and filtering of low-quality cells were performed using the Seurat R package (v5.1.0) [14]. Potential doublets were detected and removed using Scrublet, yielding a single-cell expression matrix for downstream analysis [15].

The expression matrix was normalized and standardized. Harmony was applied for batch effect correction to correct batch effects caused by sampling time, sequencing batches, or storage methods. Highly variable genes were identified using the “FindVariableGenes” function in Seurat. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on highly variable genes, followed by UMAP and t-SNE for 2D visualization [16, 17].

Cell annotation and marker gene identification

Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat, which employs a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test to assess differential expression between clusters. Parameters were set as follows: log2 fold change (log2FC) >0.25 threshold, expressed in at least 25% of the cells in the cell type. Cell types were annotated using SingleR, published references, and the CellMarker 2.0 database [18].

Bulk sequencing data processing

The raw data of GSE19491 were downloaded from the GEO database using the GEOquery package (v2.72.0). The CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm was employed to evaluate the relative infiltration abundance of immune cell clusters in a cohort of 58 ATB patients and 36 HC. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to calculate the differences in relative infiltration abundance between ATB and HC groups. Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) were calculated using the limma package (v3.60.3). Genes with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and absolute log2FC > 1 were identified as differentially expressed.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

DEGs were identified using the “FindMarkers” function in Seurat. Genes were considered DEGs if they met the following criteria: (1) detected in >10% of cells in at least one group, (2) >25% difference in expression percentage between groups, and (3) absolute average log2FC >1. Adjusted p-values (padj) were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, with significance defined as padj < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.12.6) to explore the functional implications of DEGs [19]. Pathways with adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched and visualized as dot plots.

Cellular scoring

The “AddModuleScore” function in Seurat was used to evaluate the expression levels of predefined gene sets in cell clusters. Genes associated with activation and antigen presentation were collected from previous studies [20]. Phagocytosis-related genes (GO:0006909) were used to calculate phagocytosis scores. Cytotoxicity was defined by cytotoxicity-related genes (PRF1, IFNG, GNLY, NKG7, GZMB, GZMA, GZMK, GZMH, GZMM, KLRK1, KLRB1, KLRD1, CTSW, CST7).

Cell-cell communication analysis

CellChat (v1.6.1) was employed to analyze intercellular communication networks [21]. A CellChat object was constructed using normalized scRNA-seq data and cell annotations. Interactions were referenced from the CellChatDB human database. Overexpressed ligand-receptor pairs and pathways were identified using “identifyOverExpressedGenes” and “identifyOverExpressedInteractions”. Communications with < 10 interactions were excluded. Pathway-level communication probabilities were aggregated, and network centrality metrics were computed to assess signaling strength.

Statistical analysis

Ro/e (observed-to-expected) ratios were calculated to evaluate enrichment, where Ro/e > 1 indicates enrichment and Ro/e < 1 indicates depletion. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions. Group differences were assessed using unpaired Student’s t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. All analyses were performed in RStudio (v4.4.1). Two-tailed p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Exploratory scRNA-seq profiling reveals altered immune landscapes in TB

We analyzed scRNA-seq data from PBMCs of 3 ATB patients and 2 HC, capturing 52,292 cells across 5 samples (Table 1). After quality control and batch effect correction, 46,309 cells (19,221 from HC and 27,088 from ATB) were retained for downstream analysis (Fig. 1A). Unsupervised clustering partitioned the cells into 15 distinct clusters, which were annotated into 5 major cell types, including B cells, T cells, natural killer cells (NK), myeloid cells, and platelets, based on canonical marker genes and cluster-specific upregulated genes (Fig. 1B–D). Consistent with peripheral blood composition, T cells dominated the immune cell population, while B cells, NK cells, and platelets accounted for smaller proportions (Fig. 1E). Compared to HC, ATB patients exhibited increased proportions of myeloid cells and platelets but decreased NK cells, with stable T and B cell levels (Fig. 1F). Subclustering of major cell types was performed for detailed characterization.

Next, we identified 102 upregulated and 37 downregulated DEGs in ATB from bulk RNA-seq data (Fig. 2B). Mapping these DEGs to cell types annotated in the scRNA-seq data revealed their relative enrichment in myeloid cells, suggesting that myeloid cells may play an important role in ATB pathogenesis (Fig. 2C). Functional enrichment analysis of these bulk-derived DEGs revealed that upregulated genes were primarily associated with pathways such as defense response to viruses and regulation of innate immune responses, while downregulated genes were enriched in pathways like B cell receptor signaling and lymphocyte differentiation. Notably, both upregulated and downregulated genes were enriched in innate immune activation signaling pathways, indicating potential immune dysregulation in ATB patients (Fig. 2D).To further explore cell type-specific biological variations linked to ATB progression, we re-clustered and analyzed myeloid cells, T cells, NK cells, and B cells from the scRNA-seq data to obtain finer-resolution insights.

Functional changes of myeloid cells

Monocytes play an essential role in the pathogenesis of TB. Transcriptomic analysis of myeloid cells identified three monocyte subpopulations and one dendritic cell (DC) subset. Based on the expression levels of typical marker genes CD14 and FCGR3A, monocyte subpopulations were categorized into classical monocytes (Classical_Mono), intermediate monocytes (Intermediate_Mono), and non-classical monocytes (Non-classical_Mono) [22] (Fig. 3A, B). The histograms describe the distribution of Classical_Mono, Intermediate_Mono, Non-classical_Mono, and DC in each sample (Fig. 3C). Compared to HC, Intermediate_Mono was increased in ATB patients, while Classical_Mono showed slight fluctuation, accompanied by a decrease in Non-classical_Mono and DC proportions (Fig. 3D).

DC are involved in antigen presentation and play essential roles in innate and adaptive immunity [23]. The antigen presentation score of cells showed that DC had higher antigen presentation scores than other myeloid cell subsets. The antigen presentation score of Non-classical_Mono did not differ significantly between ATB and HC groups. In contrast, in the ATB group, the antigen presentation scores of DC and Classical_Mono were significantly lower than those of the HC group, while the antigen presentation score of Intermediate_Mono was higher than in HC (Fig. 3E). Monocytes are professional phagocytes essential in defending against pathogens such as Mtb [24]. The phagocytosis scores showed that the phagocytosis scores of Intermediate_Mono and DC were not significantly different between ATB and HC groups. In contrast, in the ATB group, the phagocytosis scores of Classical_Mono and Non-classical_Mono were higher than those of the HC group(Fig. 3F).

Transcriptomic profiling of myeloid cell functional alterations revealed distinct GO enrichment patterns between ATB and HC groups (biological processes terms). In DC, biological processes such as nucleocytoplasmic transport, protein polyubiquitination, negative regulation of the catabolic process, and RNA splicing were upregulated in the ATB group compared to the HC group. Conversely, processes including negative regulation of viral genome replication, positive regulation of innate immune response, positive regulation of response to biotic stimulus, and cell killing were downregualated (Fig. 3G). For Classical_Mono subsets in the ATB group, processes such as macroautophagy, intracellular receptor signaling pathway, and regulation of innate immune response were upregulated. However, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, leukocyte-mediated immunity, and cell killing were downregulated (Fig. 3H). In Intermediate_Mono subsets, upregulated genes were enriched in biological processes like intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathways and integrated stress response signaling. Downregulated genes were associated with proton motive force-driven ATP synthesis, though the downregulated pathways did not show statistical significance (padj > 0.05). In Non-classical_Mono, transcriptional regulation-related biological processes and pathways related to the regulation of innate immune responses were significantly upregulated, while immune-related pathways were downregulated (Figure S1). These transcriptional patterns collectively suggest that myeloid cells in ATB patients may exhibit a complex phenotype characterized by impaired antigen-presenting function, enhanced phagocytic compensation, and metabolic-immune imbalance.

Functional changes in T cells

T cells play a crucial role in controlling TB infections by participating in the destruction of macrophages infected with Mtb [23, 25]. We extracted T cells and re-clustered them into subsets. Based on typical marker genes, T cells were classified into seven subsets, including CD4 + naive T cells (CD4_Naïve), CD8 + naive T cells (CD8_Naïve), CD4 + memory T cells (CD4_Tm), CD8 + effector memory T cells (CD8_Tem), CD8 + effector T cells (CD8_Te), mucosal-associated invariant T cells (MAIT), and regulatory T cells (Treg) (Figs. 4A, B). The histograms illustrate the general distribution of T cell subsets across each sample (Fig. 4C). Compared to HC, the proportion of CD4_Tm, CD4_Naïve, Treg, and CD8_Tem cells was increased in the peripheral blood of ATB patients, while the proportion of CD8_Naïve and CD8_Te cells was reduced (Fig. 4D).

Subsequently, we performed functional scoring on each T cell subset in both groups. T cells play an essential role in controlling Mtb infection through cytotoxicity, particularly CD8 + T cells, which can induce apoptosis of infected cells through granule-mediated mechanisms or Fas-Fas ligand interactions [26, 27]. Consequently, cytotoxicity function scores were evaluated for T cell subsets. The results revealed that CD8_Te cells exhibited higher cytotoxicity scores than other T cell subsets. However, in the ATB group, the cytotoxicity of CD8_Te and other subsets was significantly lower than in the HC group (Fig. 4E). Additionally, the cell activation scores of CD8_Te and related subsets in the ATB group were markedly reduced (Fig. 4F). While CD8_Te in ATB patients maintained relatively elevated cytotoxic potential compared to other subsets, their transcriptional profile suggests a possible functional attenuation that might limit their anti-Mtb efficacy.

Transcriptional profiling of T cell subsets revealed distinct functional patterns between ATB patients and HC. Compared to the HC group, regulation of RNA splicing, RNA localization, and regulation of the mRNA metabolism process were upregulated in CD8_Te of the ATB group. Moreover, CD8_Te in the ATB group exhibited downregulation of key signaling pathways including: immune response − activating signaling pathway, immune response − regulating cell surface receptor signaling pathway, and antigen receptor − mediated signaling pathway (Fig. 4G). Similar transcriptional patterns were observed in other T cell subsets (Figure S2). These results suggest that T cells may upregulate transcriptional regulatory pathways to maintain homeostatic equilibrium. However, transcriptional signatures of functional impairment in T cell subsets and downregulation of critical immune pathways may hinder their ability to exert practical anti-tuberculosis effects.

Functional changes of NK cells

T cells play a crucial role in protective immunity against Mtb infection, while NK cells are increasingly recognized as key components of the innate immune response to Mtb infection [28]. NK cells mediate cytotoxicity and cytokine signaling, enabling the elimination of Mtb-infected cells through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and non-MHC-restricted cytotoxic mechanisms, thereby serving as a bridge between innate and adaptive immune responses [29].NK cells were extracted and re-clustered into subpopulations. Based on typical marker genes, NK cells were divided into three subpopulations: CD16bright CD56dim NK cells (NK_CD56dim), CD16dim CD56bright NK cells (NK_CD56bright), and proliferative NK cells (NK_Pro) (Fig. 5A, B). The histogram illustrates the general distribution of the three NK cell subpopulations in each sample (Fig. 5C). Compared to HC, the proportion of NK_CD56bright in peripheral blood was reduced in ATB patients, while the proportion of NK_Pro was increased, while NK_CD56dim proportions remained relatively stable (Fig. 5D).

E Cytotoxicity scores of NK cell subpopulations in the ATB and HC groups. F Cell activation scores. The cytotoxic potential is also a key mechanism of NK cell-mediated protection [30]. Therefore, we evaluated the cytotoxic function of NK cell subpopulations in ATB and HC groups. The cytotoxicity scores demonstrated that NK_CD56dim had a higher cytotoxicity score than the other subpopulations, and the cytotoxicity of NK_CD56dim and other subpopulations in the ATB group was significantly lower than in HC (Fig. 5E). There were no significant changes in the cell activity of NK_CD56dim and NK_CD56bright in the ATB group, while the activation score of NK_Pro was higher than in the HC group (Fig. 5F).

We further used GO enrichment analysis to explore NK cell subpopulations’ functional changes. In NK_CD56dim of the ATB group compared to HC group, we observed transcriptional upregulation of these key biological processes, including regulation of RNA splicing, regulation of mRNA metabolic process and regulation of innate immune response. Downregulated genes in NK_CD56dim of the ATB group were mainly enriched in biological processes like negative regulation of neuronal apoptosis, leukocyte-mediated immunity, and immune response − activating signaling pathway. However, these downregulated pathways were not statistically significant (padj > 0.05) (Fig. 5G). In NK_CD56bright, biological processes like regulation of innate immune response were upregulated, while purine metabolism processes were downregulated. In NK_Pro, transcription regulation-related biological processes were significantly upregulated, and immune response activation-related biological processes were downregulated (Figure S3). These findings collectively suggest that NK cell subsets exhibit transcriptional signatures of functional impairment, with potential compensatory interactions among subsets. However, such compensation may be insufficient to restore the overall anti-tuberculosis immune response fully.

Functional changes of B cells

B cells are primarily involved in humoral immunity, and a comprehensive analysis of cellular and humoral immunity can help better understand the immune response to TB. Currently, the role of B cell-mediated immunity in the response to Mtb infection is poorly understood. Therefore, we analyzed the scRNA-seq results of B cells in the immune response to Mtb. B cells were extracted and re-clustered into subgroups. Based on the expression of typical marker genes, three B cell subgroups were identified, including Naïve B cells (Naïve_B), Memory B cells (Memory_B), and Plasma B cells (Plasma_B) (Fig. 6A, B). The histograms show the distribution of Naïve_B, Memory_B, and Plasma_B in each sample (Fig. 6C). Compared to HC, the proportion of Naïve_B in ATB patients was increased, while the proportion of Memory_B and Plasma_B decreased (Fig. 6D).

B cells play a critical role during Mtb infection by performing multiple effector functions, including antibody production, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion. They are essential for modulating immune responses against Mtb [31, 32]. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that specific immunoglobulin genes, such as IGLC 2, IGHA 1, IGHA 2, IGHG 1, and IGHG 2, tend to be downregulated in Plasma_B in the ATB group. However, these changes were not statistically significant (padj >0.05) (Fig. 6E). B cells are also antigen-presenting cells; they capture and internalize antigens through surface immunoglobulins, then present them on their surface as MHC II peptide complexes, which activate CD4_Naïve [33, 34]. The antigen_presentation scores showed that Naïve_B, Memory_B, and Plasma_B in the ATB group exhibited significantly lower antigen presentation scores compared to the HC group (Fig. 6F).

Memory_B play a critical role in adaptive immunity by rapidly differentiating into Plasma_B upon re-exposure to the same antigen, producing large quantities of antibodies.GO enrichment analysis showed that in the ATB group, regulation of RNA splicing, regulation of mRNA metabolic process, and intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway were significantly upregulated in Memory_B. Biological processes such as humoral immune response, B cell differentiation, and regulation of innate immune response were significantly downregulated (Fig. 6G). Naïve_B demonstrated similar alterations. In contrast, Plasma_B showed significant upregulation of processes like positive regulation of innate immune response and activation of the innate immune response (Figure S4). These transcriptional patterns suggest a potential downregulation of B cell functional pathways in ATB patients, particularly in regulating humoral immune responses.

Cell communication network analysis

The interactions between immune cells play a critical role in immune cell activation. During Mtb infection, numerous key interactions occur between Mtb and immune cells, as well as among different immune cells [35]. PBMCs were reclassified into B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, NK cells, monocytes, DC, and platelets. The CellChat package was used to infer the interactions between immune cells in the peripheral blood of ATB patients and HC. Cell communication results showed that the number and intensity of intercellular communications in the ATB group were higher than in the HC group. We compared the strength of incoming and outgoing signals between cell clusters in ATB and HC groups to identify cell populations with significant signal transmission or reception changes between ATB and HC groups. We observed that CD8 + T cells, NK cells, B cells, and monocytes were the central cell populations affected in the ATB group. Compared to the HC group, the strength of incoming signals in CD8 + T cells was weakened in the ATB group, while the strength of incoming signals in B cells and NK cells was enhanced. The strength of both incoming and outgoing signals in monocytes was enhanced (Fig. 7A), indicating that the innate immune response in monocytes is enhanced in the ATB group. At the same time, the function of CD8 + T cells is impaired.

To further investigate the interactions between monocytes and other cells in ATB and HC groups, we found that the communication intensity between monocytes and monocytes, NK cells, and B cells was higher in the ATB group than in the HC group (Fig. 7B). Next, we analyzed the ligand-receptor interactions between monocytes and other immune cells. The results showed that when monocytes acted as ligand cells, the strength of MHC-II signaling received by CD4 + T cells was reduced, whereas the intensity of MHC-I signaling received by NK cells was enhanced. When monocytes acted as receptor cells, the communication strength with other cells through TGFB1- (TGFBR1 + TGFBR2) and TGFB1- (ACVR1B + TGFBR2) was enhanced, the interaction strength of ICAM signaling between monocytes—mediated by ICAM1-SPN、ICAM1-ITGAL was also enhanced (Fig. 7C).

Subsequently, we compared the overall relative intensity of recognized signaling pathways in each cell cluster based on aggregated incoming and outgoing signals. We ranked critical signaling pathways based on the differences in overall communication probability between ATB and HC groups in specific networks (Fig. 7D). Revealing significant enrichment of the TGF-β signaling pathway in the ATB group.

Previous findings indicated reduced antigen presentation scores in the ATB group. However, overall pathway ranking analysis revealed no significant differences in MHC-I or MHC-II signaling between the ATB and HC groups (Fig. 7D). Further analysis of MHC-I and MHC-II signaling demonstrated that CD8 + T cells in the ATB group received diminished MHC-I signals. In contrast, NK cells exhibited increased MHC-I signal reception (Fig. 7E). Concurrently, CD4 + T cells showed significantly weakened MHC-II signal reception, reflecting a pronounced imbalance in intercellular communication networks among immune cells (Fig. 7F). In summary, these results strongly suggest that the communication network between immune cell clusters in the ATB group is disrupted.

Discussion

TB remains a persistent threat to human health, yet our understanding of the host immune response to Mtb infection is limited. Using scRNA-seq, we mapped the transcriptomes of peripheral blood immune cells in HC and ATB patients, revealing differences in immune cell subpopulations and their functions between ATB patients and HC. Furthermore, we applied cell-cell communication analysis to uncover the interactions among peripheral blood immune cells. Overall, Mtb significantly impacts the functional status of immune cells, and these cells regulate the immune response to Mtb through post-transcriptional mechanisms, although the immune response remains insufficient. This immune imbalance may be a key mechanism in the progression of Mtb infection to active disease.

Hillman et al. [36] found that the monocyte/lymphocyte ratio in ATB patients increased before starting anti-tuberculosis treatment. Additionally, Cai et al. [11] compared scRNA-seq data of PBMCs from HC, LTBI, and ATB individuals, confirming a significant reduction of several lymphocyte subpopulations in ATB patients. Our study also shows an increase in myeloid cell proportions and a decrease in lymphocytes, such as NK cells, in ATB patients, further supporting this finding.

Previous studies have found that ATB patients exhibit downregulated HLA-DR expression in monocytes, leading to reduced antigen-presenting efficacy [37]. Balboa et al. [38, 39] also discovered that the ability of monocytes from ATB patients to differentiate into functional DC is impaired, and the function of antigen-presenting cells is compromised. In the ATB group, DC exhibited lower transcriptional scores for antigen processing and presentation ability, which may directly lead to insufficient activation of CD4 + T cells, thereby weakening the adaptive immune response against tuberculosis. Monocytes exhibit significant subset-specific functional differentiation in ATB. The antigen presentation score of Classical_Mono is reduced, but the phagocytosis score is increased. Biological processes such as lymphocyte-mediated immunity and cytotoxicity are downregulated, which may further weaken the body’s anti-tuberculosis immune response. The antigen presentation score of Intermediate_Mono is elevated, and pathways related to integrated stress response are significantly upregulated. These cells may maintain intracellular homeostasis by activating the “integrated stress response” pathway. However, suppressing “proton-motive force-driven ATP synthesis” suggests mitochondrial dysfunction, limiting the persistence of their immune function. The heterogeneous changes in monocyte subgroups reflect the delicate balance between pathogen clearance and the prevention of immunopathological damage.

CD8_Te as the main cytotoxic T cell subset, retain significant cytotoxic potential in ATB, but their cytotoxicity and activation scores are significantly reduced, potentially due to “functional exhaustion” caused by chronic antigen stimulation. Consistent with our findings, a bronchoalveolar lavage fluid study also suggests reduced activity in biological pathways across multiple T-cell subsets in ATB patients [40]. Lyu et al. [41] found that compared to HC, cytotoxic cells in ATB exhibit reduced cytotoxic activity, increased expression of exhaustion-related genes in CD8 + T cells, downregulated IFN-γ response capacity in CD4 + T cells, downregulated immune-positive regulatory function of T cells, and impaired T cell functionality. Wang et al. [42] also found that NK cells exhibited altered quantities, abnormal receptor expression, and weakened cytotoxic activity. The reduced cytotoxicity scores of CD8_Te and NK_CD56dim may lead to compromised immune coordination against Mtb infection, providing a favorable niche for Mtb survival and proliferation.

The antigen-presenting function of B cell subsets in the ATB group is downregulated, weakening the initiation and maintenance of humoral immune responses. Meanwhile, the upregulation of pathways, such as innate immune response regulation in Plasma_B, may serve as a compensatory mechanism to compensate for the deficiency in adaptive immune responses by enhancing innate immune responses.

Previous studies have shown that the levels of TGF-β are increased in ATB patients [43, 44]. Su et al. [45]found that Mtb can suppress the expression of MHC-II molecules and antigen processing, affecting the presentation to CD4 + T cells. Our study found that TGF-β signaling is significantly increased in the ATB group, while CD4 + T cells receiving MHC-II signals are significantly weakened, providing further evidence for previous research. The increase in TGF-β expression can inhibit the synthesis of glutathione, impairing early innate immune responses and suppressing the adaptive immune responses of T cells and B cells, thus providing a favorable survival environment for Mtb [46, 47]. Additionally, we observed enhanced intercellular communication among monocytes in the peripheral blood of ATB patients. Interestingly, a recent study suggested increased cell-cell communication between macrophages and other cell types in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from ATB patients [48]. This study demonstrates that CD8 + T cells receive fewer MHC-I signals, whereas Wang et al.‘s [49] research revealed that CD8 + T cells in severe ATB patients exhibit stronger MHC signal responses. This contradictory observation might be attributed to heterogeneity in disease stages among the studied populations. In summary, the intercellular interaction network in ATB shows significant changes, with abnormal enrichment of TGF-β signaling pathways and defects in MHC molecule signaling, creating a microenvironment conducive to Mtb persistence and jointly driving immune escape and the chronic process of tuberculosis.

Despite the insights provided by this study into the complex impact of tuberculosis on the immune system, there are several limitations. First, the study primarily focuses on peripheral blood samples, which may not fully reflect changes in localized immune responses (e.g., in the lungs). Second, the functional analysis is mainly based on transcriptomic data, without direct validation of changes at the protein level or cellular function. Future studies should combine multi-omics data (such as proteomics and metabolomics) and functional experiments (such as cytotoxicity and activation assays) to more comprehensively reveal the mechanisms underlying the impact of tuberculosis on the immune system.

Additionally, this study highlights the significant impact of Mtb on cell-cell communication, but the specific mechanisms still require further investigation. Future research could focus on the role of key signaling pathways, such as TGF-β, in Mtb immune evasion and evaluate their potential as therapeutic targets. Lastly, the dataset in this study consists of relatively small sample sizes, and individual differences may affect statistical power. The findings should be validated in larger clinical cohorts to assess their feasibility as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for tuberculosis.

Conclusion

Using single-cell transcriptomics, this study uncovered dynamic immune imbalance in ATB patients. Although immune cells activated transcriptional responses to Mtb, the resultant immune response was impaired, manifesting as a “low-efficiency” exhausted phenotype. Disrupted cell communication networks further weakened anti-Mtb immunity. The combined effects of immune suppression and host tolerance strategies may play a key role in tuberculosis progression. Future studies should integrate multi-omics data from local tissues with functional experiments to target key pathways, potentially uncovering novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
