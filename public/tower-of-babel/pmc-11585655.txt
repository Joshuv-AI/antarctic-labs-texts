Charge-trap synaptic device with polycrystalline silicon channel for low power in-memory computing
Authors: Min-Kyu Park, Joon Hwang, Soomin Kim, Wonjun Shin, Wonbo Shim, Jong-Ho Bae, Jong-Ho Lee, Seongjae Cho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Processing-in-memory (PIM) is gaining tremendous research and commercial interest because of its potential to replace the von Neumann bottleneck in current computing architectures. In this study, we implemented a PIM hardware architecture (circuit) based on the charge-trap flash (CTF) as a synaptic device. The PIM circuit with a CT memory performed exceedingly well by reducing the inference energy in the synapse array. To evaluate the image recognition accuracy, a Visual Geometry Group (VGG)-8 neural network was used for training, using the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research (CIFAR)-10 dataset for off-chip learning applications. In addition to the system accuracy for neuromorphic applications, the energy efficiency, computing efficiency, and latency were closely investigated in the presumably integrated PIM architecture. Simulations that were performed incorporated cycle-to-cycle device variations, synaptic array size, and technology node scaling, along with other hardware-sense considerations.

Introduction

Modern computing workloads are dominated by data-intensive applications, such as deep learning, which demand accelerated computing capabilities at a lower energy budget. The remarkable success of the latest deep neural network (DNN) applications in diverse fields, such as computer vision, language translation, and audio processing, has further increased the demand for efficient hardware support for newer and larger DNN models. Huge language models, such as WuDao 2.0 (1.75 trillion parameters), GPT-3 (175 billion parameters), and Bloom (176 billion parameters), are already stretching the limits of modern computing capabilities1. Although hardware architectural innovations such as tensor processing units (TPU) and graphical processing units (GPU) have constructively supplemented state-of-the-art DNN computations, the inherent serialism in data transfer between memory and processing units has severely constrained further progress. This architectural design flaw is widely known as the Von Neumann bottleneck or memory wall. An alternative computing paradigm that relies on merging the processing and memory domains is processing-in-memory (PIM) computing2–4. The PIM was developed in the 1970s with the aim of reducing data shuttling latency and thereby shifting computations within a storage unit. In recent years, PIM research has focused on accelerating multiply-and-accumulate (MAC) operations, which are the core mathematical operations in DNN algorithms. A plethora of novel nonvolatile memories, such as resistive random-access memories (RRAMs), phase change memories (PCM), magnetic RAMs (MRAMs), and ferroelectric RAMs (FRAMs), have emerged as synaptic devices owing to their fast and low-power operation capabilities5–15. Flash memory devices have also received considerable attention because of their CMOS compatibility, mature technology, excellent reliability, multilevel operation, and scalability16–18. NAND and NOR flash memories have become the mainstay of commercial memory technologies with ultrahigh-density storage capabilities. The recent development of three-dimensional (3D) NAND flash memories has further increased research interest in these technologies as prospective devices for PIM architecture realization.

The fundamental element behind these architectures is the charge trap (CT) memory, which works on the basis of threshold voltage (VT)modulation based on the trapping and detrapping of electrons during program and erase operations. By varying the channel conductance through VT modulation, the charge trap flash (CTF) device can be used effectively as a synaptic device to realize the PIM architecture. Several groups have reported CTF-based arrays for both convolutional and spiking neural networks19,20. However, these early results clearly lack more detailed system-level evaluations and benchmarks.

In this study, we fabricated a low-power CTF with a poly-Si channel and evaluated its performance as a synapse element in PIM-based system-level simulations. The experimentally measured device characteristics were used for system-level simulations with the necessary peripheral circuitry on a 22-nanometer CMOS technology node21. A more complex image-recognition test using the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research (CIFAR)-10 dataset was evaluated with the PIM circuit architecture comprising all peripheral circuitry. Further, efforts have been dedicated to more practical and substantial evaluations of the system in terms of energy efficiency, latency in data transfer, and area occupancy by the system-level simulation tasks. Additionally, the effect of nonideal behaviors in the CTF are considered for evaluating a presumably designed PIM architecture. Finally, the CTF device system-level performance was evaluated at lower technology nodes to examine the scaling trends of the device.

Results and discussion

Device characteristics & operation

A schematic of the CTF device and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the fabricated CTF device are shown in Fig. 1a22. Note that a detailed explanation of device fabrication steps is explained in the “Methods” in Section. The fabricated device is a thin-film transistor (TFT) type. The device utilizes poly-silicon as the channel material, which allows for relatively low current flow, suitable as a PIM device. As depicted in the TEM image in Fig. 1a, the source and drain are electrically isolated, and the device features a p-type doped body. By providing holes, this p-body plays a crucial role in memory operations, particularly during the erase (ERS) operation. Note that the height of the source and drain electrodes is lower than that of the p-body, resulting in a portion of the poly-Si channel near the source and drain being curved. This structural characteristic causes the electric field to concentrate in the curved channel, enhancing gate controllability and providing a significant advantage is resisting short channel effects. Figure 1b shows the measured output characteristics of the fabricated CTF synaptic device, which exhibited saturation characteristics. The channel length and width were both set to 1 μm. The synaptic device can withstand a higher tolerance to unexpected drain voltage (VDS) variations when the device is in saturation mode. Figure 1c shows the transfer characteristics of the fabricated CTF device. The transfer characteristics revealed that increasing the gate voltage (VGS) increases the drain current (ID), which is typical of an n-type MOS device. During device programming, as the positive gate voltage is applied with both the body and source grounded, electrons tunnel from the channel to the CT layer through the Fowler-Nordheim (FN) tunneling process, as schematically shown in Fig. 2a. The trapped electrons in the CT layer screen the effective gate electric field, thereby increasing VT. Similarly, charges stored in the CT layer can be detrapped and tunneled back into the channel during the erase state by applying a negative gate voltage (Fig. 2b). The amount of charge stored in the CT layer can be modulated based on VGS, which can be used to adjust the conductance or synaptic weight of the device.

Neural network design and PIM architecture

The synaptic weights extracted from the LTP/LTD characteristics were further used for off-chip training of a hardware sense neural network using a PIM circuit design. A Visual Geometry Group (VGG)-8 neural network architecture was envisaged for this purpose using the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research (CIFAR)-10 dataset as network input. The neural network architecture is schematically shown in Fig. 4a. The VGG-8 neural network has six convolutional layers and two fully-connected layers. The CIFAR-10 dataset is a widely used three-channel (RGB) color image database with 50,000 training and 10,000 test images with a 32 × 32 resolution.

Schematic of the (a) VGG-8 neural network architecture. (b) convolutional layer operation and corresponding MAC operation executed through VMM operations inside a simple 3 × 3 PIM subarray.

The images belong to one of the 10 output classes: airplanes, automobiles, birds, cats, deer, dogs, frogs, horses, ships, and trucks. Network training uses the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function during forward propagation. The loss is computed using the cross-entropy loss function, whereas the weight update is performed based on the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm. A batch size of 200 and an initial learning rate of 1.0 were used in the simulations. A decreasing learning rate strategy (lrDecay) is used during the VGG-8 training process25. lrDecay is a popular technique that is widely used in deep learning applications to improve the convergence of the network training process. This technique involves a large initial learning rate, which is decayed by a certain factor at predefined epochs. The advantage of this approach is that the initially large learning rate escapes accidental local minima at the beginning epochs during the SGD optimization. However, a high learning rate can overshoot the global minimum if it is maintained throughout the training process. Hence, adjusting to a lower learning rate can help the SGD optimization to effectively converge to the global minima26. The VGG-8 training process started with an initial learning rate of 1.0, which subsequently decreased to 1/8 after 200 epochs of training. In addition, Weights, Activations, Gradients, and Errors (WAGE) method is employed to quantize the input, weight, and output values27. WAGE applies a uniform quantization strategy where the first step is the shifting of each element by a constant value to ensure the data falls within the desired integer limits. Then, a linear constriction process is performed where the values are scaled and rounded to fit within the previously chosen bit precision. Note that for the simulation, 5-bit precision is used for the weights, and 8-bit precision is used for the input and the output values.

The MAC operation between the input vector and vectors in the weight matrix essentially constitute both convolutional layers and linear layers28. For each layer in the VGG8 model, the weights are mapped as the CTF cell conductance, where each junction between a row and a column crossing in a subarray represents a CTF device, as shown in Fig. 4b. During the inference operation, the input vector or the activation output from the previous layer is passed as parallel row-wise voltages. At each cross point of the sub-array, the input voltage is multiplied by the CTF device conductance, which forms the multiply operation in MAC calculation. Finally, the current at the output of each column is the total current summed up using Kirchhoff’s current law. This summation constitutes the accumulation in the MAC operation. The sub-array thus implements a one-shot implementation of the MAC operations at each column endings. Figure 4b shows the schematic of a convolutional layer operation and its weight kernels being mapped to a sample 3 × 3 subarray in the PIM simulation.

The PIM chip-level architecture is illustrated in Fig. 521. The chip-level architecture has been hierarchically structured into tiles, which are further divided into processing elements (PEs) that constitute the final synaptic subarrays. To represent negative weights, two PEs in a pair represent positive and negative weights. The outputs of PEs are hierarchically transferred to digital modules through global buffers and then subtracted to get partial sums considering the sign of the weights. The partial sums are digitally accumulated to acquire the final MAC results. For neural network training applications, we require access to individual analog states of each memory cell. In order to have such precise control of the different analog states of the CTF device, we need to employ a selector transistor-based pseudo-crossbar array architecture. The main advantage of the selector transistor is that any arbitrary CT flash device can be programmed without interference and power leakage to half-selected cells29. The chip architecture considers various device non-idealities, peripheral circuit limitations, such as analog-to-digital converter (ADC) resolution, and technology scaling parameters. For technology scaling, the following parameters were added based on the predictive SPICE technology model: interconnect metal wire thickness, DC supply voltage (VDD), logic transistor threshold voltage (VT), transistor gate length, fringe capacitance, on (and off) current of the NMOS and PMOS transistors and the NMOS-to-PMOS transistor size ratio. For the proper functioning of a PIM circuit, peripheral circuits, including multiplexers (MUX), ADCs, buffers, interconnects (copper for the 22-nm technology node), adders, and shift-registers, are included. Parallel read-out schemes are regularly used, in which flash ADCs are responsible for receiving partial sums along the columns owing to MAC operations. ADCs have high precision and, hence, occupy a relatively large area. The adders and shift registers were used for the shift-and-sum of the input data over multiple input cycles. To reduce the on-chip memory requirements for large amounts of batch-wise data generated during the feedforward process, the PIM architecture relies on off-chip DRAM.

The energy consumption has similar components at each of the hierarchical structures of tiles, PEs, and subarrays. At the subarray level, the energy is the sum of energy consumed by the CTF synaptic subarrays and the energy consumed by each of the sub-circuit modules. For each of these subcircuits, the main energy component is the dynamic energy consumption of the constituent logic gates. For a logic gate with capacitance (C) and bias voltage (VDD), the energy can be taken as CVDD2. The total subcircuit energy can then be calculated by summing up the gate energy consumption at each node multiplied by the number of times the subcircuit module has been accessed. For the synaptic array energy calculation, EnergyCTF from (4) is multiplied by the number of CTF devices in the subarray. A similar procedure is then repeated for the PE level and the tile level energy calculation to obtain the total chip energy. In addition, to calculate the compute efficiency, the total chip area is first calculated based on the CTF subarray area (based on 1T1R) and the logic gate dimensions as per the technology node used. Then, the number of MAC operations is calculated per second, which is computed in terms of Tera operations per second (TOPS). TOPS divided by the total chip area gives the area normalized compute efficiency. The energy efficiency is also similarly calculated by dividing the TOPS by the total chip energy computed, as explained above.

System-level simulations

For the PIM system performance evaluation, DNN + NeuroSim V2.0 package was adopted21. The energy calculation methodology adopted has been previously compared and validated with SPICE-based simulations30. The synaptic sub-array size was assumed to be 64 × 64, and a 5-bit analog-to-digital circuit (ADC) was used, which acts as the neuron circuit along the end of each synapse sub-array column. A column-sharing approach is used for the ADC, such that eight subarray columns share a single ADC. This is because the peripheral ADC size is too large, compared to the synapse subarray column pitch. Figure 6a shows the pattern recognition accuracy of the VGG-8 neural network based on the CTF PIM. The accuracies were reported as the average of five consecutive runs with different seed values. A high-test accuracy of 91.3% was obtained for the CTF device, which is a small accuracy drop compared with that obtained from purely software-based training (92.5%). An abrupt jump was observed at 200 epochs in both the device- and software-based training processes owing to the learning-rate decay strategy used. At 200 epochs, the sudden learning rate decay helps the SGD algorithm find the global minima and, hence, attain a better test accuracy for image recognition. The effect of device nonidealities on PIM performance was of critical interest in several recent research reports30. The inset of Fig. 6a shows the effect of the test accuracy on the CTF cycle-to-cycle variations (C2C) in the conductance. The C2C variations were computed in terms of the standard deviation of each conductance value from the original value. The test accuracy remained above 90% until a C2C variation of 0.02, which reveals a high robustness of the fabricated CTF device for PIM implementation. Especially, the proposed charge trap synaptic device exhibits sufficiently low C2C variation to achieve high accuracy. Neural network quantization has also been extensively studied owing to its applicability to the development of compact neural networks. Although several techniques, such as pruning and singular value decomposition, already exist for neural network size reduction, quantization of weights and gradients has been widely adopted owing to their universality and exceptional performance retention31. Figure 6b shows the test accuracy variation of the CT flash device with bit quantization of weights and gradients during the inference process. It is shown that the device performs reasonably well above the 3-bit quantization. Performance may be further enhanced by employing techniques such as quantization-aware training. The distributions of energy and area consumption by various hardware units are depicted in Fig. 7a–b, respectively. The energy distribution was calculated by computing the dynamic energy consumed at every node by the total capacitance across a logic gate and the chip drive voltage, which was then summed to calculate the total energy consumed by a peripheral unit in a single operation. This value was then multiplied several times depending on the number of operations. As shown in Fig. 7a, the ADCs, interconnects, and accumulation circuits consume the largest share of the energy consumed by the PIM chip. In comparison, a synaptic array consumes minimal energy. Similarly, the bulk of the chip area is occupied by ADCs and interconnects, whereas the synaptic array occupies only a very small area. This also indicates that the ADCs and the interconnect architecture should be further optimized for developing energy- and area-efficient PIM systems. We also benchmarked our system-level simulations with previously reported results using different types of synaptic devices, as summarized in Table 130–39. As shown in the table, the fabricated CTF device performs exceedingly well in terms of energy efficiency and latency, compared to other devices. The high energy efficiency may be due to the low read energy for a synaptic event of the CTF device, which is approximately 14.94 fJ, whereas the low latency may be attributed to the narrow-read pulse width of 100 ns used for each read operation. We also observed that the computing efficiency, which is an important parameter for evaluating the area efficiency, is better for the CTF device than for the RRAM and SRAM devices. The effect of the synaptic subarray size variation on the computational efficiency and energy efficiency is shown in Fig. 8a. As the synapse subarray size decreased from 256 × 256 to 16 × 16, the number of tiles in the PIM chip correspondingly increased from 15 tiles to 646 tiles. Hence, we observed that the reduction in synapse array size does not have a beneficial effect on the computation and energy efficiency because of the increase in tiles needed to store the small-sized synapse arrays. This can also be verified from the chip area and energy, which increase with the synapse-array size reduction, as shown in Fig. 8b. Similarly, the energy and computing efficiency also suffer from a reduced synapse array size owing to multiple additional ADCs (see Fig. 8c) required for the small-sized synapse sub-arrays. Technology scaling is an integral part of evaluating the PIM performance for current and future technological requirements. We evaluated the performance of the CTF device with technology nodes ranging from 45 to 10 nm. As previously discussed, the technology nodes were adapted into the PIM simulations using scaled logic and interconnect specifications from the predictive SPICE models. As shown in Fig. 9a, both the computation and energy efficiency increase with the technology node scaling to 10 nm. This may be attributed to the reduced area and energy consumption of the peripheral circuits owing to the low-power logic transistors in scaled technology nodes. This is also supported by the overall reduced energy consumption of the PIM chip with technology scaling, as shown in Fig. 9b. Although technology scaling does not directly affect the synapse subarrays, the energy consumption is observed to decrease owing to the effect of the synapse array switch-matrix energy reduction with technology node scaling.

Conclusion

In this study, a more detailed and realistic system-level evaluation was performed for a presumed PIM architecture based on poly-Si CTF memory synapses fabricated for ultralow-power operation. A VGG-8 neural network structure was used for the hardware-sense simulation of the designed PIM architecture incorporating nonideality in synaptic weight modulation. It is evident from the system-level simulation that the CTF synapse array consumes extremely low energy and space, compared to other circuital elements that make up the neuromorphic PIM chip. The study of weight quantization reveals important information regarding the compressibility of neural networks. The synaptic subarray and technology node variations had a significant effect on the overall functioning of the PIM circuit. Judging from the high CIFAR-10 image-recognition accuracy of 91.31%, high-speed, and ultra-low-power operation capability, the poly-Si CTF memory is a plausible candidate for the hardware-oriented PIM architecture for chip implementation.

Methods

Device fabrication

The CTF synaptic device was fabricated through 0.5-µm Si CMOS processing. The channel material was polycrystalline Si deposited through low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD), by which the inference energy can be drastically lowered. The tunneling oxide (3.6 nm), CT nitride layer (5.3 nm), and Al2O3 as the blocking oxide (6.2 nm) were deposited sequentially by thermal dry oxidation, LPCVD, and atomic-layer deposition (ALD), respectively.

Electrical characterization

All the electrical characteristics of the CTF device were obtained at room temperature and inside an electrically shielded probe station. The device characteristics were measured using a Keithley 4200 A-SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer.

Simulation environment

System-level simulations were carried out in a 32-core workstation with one of the most recent processor products, AMD Ryzen 9 Processor, 64-GB DRAM, and a high-capacity graphic processing unit (GPU), NVIDIA RTX 3090.
