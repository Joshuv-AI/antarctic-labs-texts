𝓟 3 \bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3} : Toward Versatile Embodied Agents
Shengli Zhou, Xiangchen Wang, Jinrui Zhang, Ruozai Tian, Rongtao Xu, Feng Zheng

Abstract
Embodied agents have shown promising generalization capabilities across diverse physical environments, making them essential for a wide range of real-world applications. However, building versatile embodied agents poses critical challenges due to three key issues: dynamic environment perception, open-ended tool usage, and complex multi-task planning. Most previous works rely solely on feedback from tool agents to perceive environmental changes and task status, which limits adaptability to real-time dynamics, causes error accumulation, and restricts tool flexibility. Furthermore, multi-task scheduling has received limited attention, primarily due to the inherent complexity of managing task dependencies and balancing competing priorities in dynamic and complex environments. To overcome these challenges, we introduce 𝓟 3 \bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3} , a unified framework that integrates real-time perception and dynamic scheduling. Specifically, 𝓟 3 \bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3} enables 1) P erceive relevant task information actively from the environment, 2) P lug and utilize any tool without feedback requirement, and 3) P lan multi-task execution based on prioritizing urgent tasks and dynamically adjusting task order based on dependencies. Extensive real-world experiments show that our approach bridges the gap between benchmarks and practical deployment, delivering highly transferable, general-purpose embodied agents. Code and data will be released soon.

Introduction

Embodied agents are intelligent systems that engage with their environment in real time through a physical or virtual body.
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
The development of such agents aims to bridge perception, reasoning, and action, enabling seamless interaction with the environment.
Prior research in embodied intelligence and multi-modal perception has achieved promising results across various tasks
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Tan et al. 2025
;
Tan et al. 2024
)
.
However, these works rely solely on feedback from tools, resulting in incomplete environment observations, predefined toolkits, and limited task settings. These limitations create a significant gap between agent capabilities and real-world task demands.
To address this challenge and develop versatile embodied agents, a more generalized and flexible architecture is needed.

Specifically, a key limitation of prior embodied agents
(
Yang et al. 2025
;
Tan et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Fan et al. 2025
)
stems from their passive environmental perception mechanism, assuming their actions are the only factors affecting the environment. For example, during a cleaning task, a robot may fail to adapt if a person suddenly moves furniture, disrupting its plan. Such failures reveal agents rely on task feedback from predefined toolkits (e.g., how objects are moved) but fail to detect real-time changes caused by external factors, such as human or other agent actions. This severely restricts their capacity for autonomous environmental awareness and adaptive decision-making in dynamic scenarios.
Moreover, previous embodied agents require feedback from tool agents, limiting their operation to predefined toolkits. However, many real-world edge devices, such as Internet of Things (IoT) components or basic mechanical devices like motors, lack bidirectional communication by design
(
Aouedi et al. 2024
;
Vermesan et al. 2020
)
. Some IoT devices are deliberately limited to unidirectional communication for security
(
Gusita et al. 2025
;
Huang, Liu, and Tao 2019
)
, while resource constraints may also hinder support for complex protocols
(
Gaina et al. 2024
)
, further restricting agent interaction. This gap in compatibility severely hinders the practical deployment of existing embodied agents.

To address these issues, we introduce a perception module that actively detects dynamic changes in the environment, including those caused by the agent’s own actions, human behaviors, other agents’ activities, and dynamic alterations in context.
In contrast to previous systems with fragmented perception
(
Yuan et al. 2025
;
Nazarczuk et al. 2024
)
, our design decouples the perception module from specific tool agents and treats it as an always-on centralized service that continuously observes and perceives environmental cues, thereby enabling effective deployment of embodied agents in real-world environments. These cues are stored and periodically fed into the Visual Language Models (VLMs)
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
, where context engineering is applied to perceive dynamic events in the scene, identify tasks actively, and check if the scene graph needs updating. This architecture brings the following benefits: (1) agents can detect changes that tool-based systems would miss, enhancing its ability to respond to emergencies; (2) agents can actively propose tasks based on its continuous observations, such as room cleaning or emergency handling; and (3) agents can seamlessly integrate with any tool, even those lacking standard feedback mechanisms.

Building on this, the introduction of active tasks (autonomously identified based on robot perception) and diverse tool plug-ins significantly expands the range of executable tasks and available tools. This broader scope makes the weaknesses of earlier task-planning methods even more apparent, further underscoring their limitations.
Specifically, earlier task planning methods typically follow a fixed first-in-first-out manner, lacking the ability to manage task executions based on user preferences or task dependency.
For example, such systems would insist on finishing room tidying before addressing urgent events like a fire.

To overcome these shortcomings and build versatile embodied agents, it is crucial plan and manage omni-tasks (i.e., various types of tasks including active, passive, and scheduled tasks) by considering dependencies, user priorities, and environmental context. They must support adaptive execution, interruptions, and resumptions.
In response, we propose a novel task planning module that jointly processes all types of tasks. This module evaluates task priorities and dependencies, while also incorporating a task memory to store pending, scheduled, and interrupted tasks. By doing so, it allows the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework to effectively handle complex task scheduling in the real world. After the planning phase, tasks that are ready for execution are allocated to the appropriate tool agents for immediate action.

In summary,
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework delivers the following key contributions:

•

Omni-task Perception
: We introduce a unified perception module that actively detects real-time environmental changes, allowing the agent to autonomously identify various types of tasks based on dynamic context and environmental shifts, without relying on passive feedback.

•

Plug-any-tool Agent
: By removing the need for tool feedback, we enable seamless integration with a wide range of devices, including those without standardized interfaces, enhancing the agent’s flexibility and compatibility with diverse real-world tools.

•

Multi-task Planning
: Our dynamic scheduler efficiently manages multiple tasks, autonomously arranging them based on real-time context and dependencies, ensuring flexible task execution in complex environments.

•

Real-world deployments
: We evaluate the strong performance of the proposed framework by testing it through real-world deployments, demonstrating its ability to handle dynamic, real-world challenges effectively.

Embodied System.

Recent advances in embodied intelligence have focused on developing integrated, modular, and scalable robotic systems. Early frameworks like the Robot Operating System (ROS)

(

(

and AllenAct
(
Weihs et al. 2020
)
emphasize interoperability with both simulated and physical environments, providing flexible infrastructures for learning-based embodied tasks. Concurrently, generalist agents such as Being-0

Yuan et al. 2025
)
, Gemini Robotics
(
Team et al. 2025a
)
and
\pi_{0}

(

strive to integrate vision, language, and action, demonstrating strong generalization across various physical tasks without the need for explicit retraining.
Despite recent advances, many systems still struggle with limited perception and poor tool compatibility. They often overlook external factors, relying solely on task feedback to infer scene changes, thus resulting in partial observations and accumulated errors. Moreover, their dependence on fixed hardware and skill sets hinders adaptation to novel tools or unstructured tasks. In contrast, we propose a unified perception module and a plug-any-tool architecture that actively detects scene dynamics and supports seamless integration of diverse tools and tasks, significantly enhancing the agent’s adaptability, versatility, and scalability in real-world deployments.

Embodied Tasks.

Most existing embodied tasks focus on passive task execution, where agents follow explicit human instructions rather than acting autonomously. Paradigms like Vision-Language Navigation
(
Qi et al. 2025
;
Peng et al. 2025
)
and Vision-Language Action
(

;
Team et al. 2025b
;
Xu et al. 2025

reflect this trend, focusing on instruction-following in structured settings with predefined goals.
However, by centering on user-provided commands, these frameworks offer no mechanisms to generate or pursue tasks autonomously, limiting their adaptability in dynamic environments.
In contrast, active tasks, such as emergency response
(
Cani et al. 2025
)
or room cleaning
(
Kant et al. 2022

, require agents to perceive their surroundings, identify task opportunities, and act without explicit guidance. Nevertheless, most existing systems
(

;
Li et al. 2024
)
for active tasks are constrained by limited scene scopes and rely on text-based inputs, which oversimplify the complexities of real-world interactions. Our work addresses these limitations by introducing a robust framework that supports both passive and active tasks.
By integrating a real-time task planner, we make agents more capable of handling various types of tasks in open environments.

Method

In this section, we introduce the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework: versatile embodied agents for real-time omni-task
p
erception, open-ended tool
p
lug-in, and multi-task
p
lanning. The overall framework is illustrated in Figure
2
.

To keep aware of scene changes in the scene caused by both the agent’s own actions and external factors, we decouple the perception capability from each tool agents and integrate them into a centralized and unified Perception Module. The module leverages a state-of-the-art vision-language model with context engineering to proactively observe scene dynamics, detect events, propose active tasks, and update the scene graph. By eliminating the reliance on tool agents’ feedback, the system gains compatibility with a broader range of real-world devices and environments. Moreover, this design allows seamless integration of diverse tools, including those lacking native feedback capabilities.

Meanwhile, to effectively handle task ordering, we adopt an LLM for task planning. This planner collaboratively organizes active tasks proposed by the perception module, triggered by a timer from the task memory, and passive tasks from users’ instructions.
All of these tasks then go through a joint evaluation to determine their importance, and determine the tasks to execute immediately according to their priorities and dependencies. Selected tasks will be assigned to tool agents by a dispatcher for execution.

Finally, during execution, although tool agents no longer provide feedback to agents, their impact on the environment is captured by the perception module, which further aids the decision-making process of the next step.

Perception Module

The perception module takes a video stream as input to observe the environment. As each new frame arrives, a scene graph generator is applied to update the scene graph, capturing the spatial relationships between objects. The updated scene graph is then stored in the spatial memory.

Subsequently, the frame is sent to visual memory, where it undergoes processing through context engineering framework. Here, the frame is parsed into a vision-language model alongside carefully crafted system prompts. This interaction generates context-aware task proposals, which are contextually aligned with the robot’s current operational state. These proposals are combined with the robot’s current situation to localize where the corresponding task is expected to occur. The resulting (task, situation) pairs are then recorded in the “pending” split of the task memory for future scheduling.

Although the video stored in the visual memory enriches perception with historical context, it also yields significant storage and processing overhead. To handle this issue, we employ a gradual frame-level down-sampling strategy to compress the video over time. Once a frame surpasses its time limit in visual memory, it undergoes scene captioning via the VLM. The captions, summarizing key events in the scene, are then recorded in the event history within the memory, ensuring maximum informational retention.

The architecture of the perception module and related memory splits are shown in Fig.
3
. By adopting such architecture, our model is capable of proactively capturing environmental changes, proposing active tasks, and supporting omni-task perception.

Tool Plug-ins

In previous embodied agents, navigation agents (i.e., VLN models) and manipulation agents (i.e., VLA models) serve as two foundational tools for task execution. In
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
, navigation and manipulation agents are treated as essential agents as they control the movements and actions of the robot directly.
Apart from these mandatory agents, our framework further enables the usage of a large variety of tools in the real world, from IoT devices to web agents, without requiring bidirectional feedback loops. Through context engineering techniques (e.g., dynamic scene summarization and relevance filtering), the agent can maintain a concise, high-value memory of past observations, enabling it to issue commands like turning off lights or adjusting air-conditioning, without physically moving when the user leaves for work. Similarly, by collecting timely web-agent updates (such as a weather forecast predicting rain), it can advise the user to bring an umbrella.
Finally, as the perception module eliminates the requirements for tool agents’ feedback, users can also register customized tools into the toolkit. Specifically, the user can register a tool agent by specifying the function of the tool agent and its corresponding function call interface. Such a design enables anything that is controllable in the scene to be plugged into our framework.

For safety and reliability, the commands sent to the manipulation agent are validated beforehand. These checks are not saved to memory as the isolation mechanism prevents conflicts and interference between information and helps control the context length.
To further mitigate context-window limitations, we employ hierarchical memory buffers and on-the-fly summarization: the current query and previous urgent events will be fully sent to the planner, while long-term patterns are archived in an external knowledge store and retrieved. This guarantees that only the most relative information occupies the LLM’s prompt, preserving low latency and robust performance.

Task Planner

To address the shortcomings of first-in-first-out scheduling in embodied robotic, we propose a novel task planning module that jointly considers the importance of different tasks and handles task dependencies. Specifically, the input tasks for planning can be categorized into three types, namely, passive tasks, active tasks, and scheduled tasks. Passive tasks (e.g., “open the window”) are triggered by users’ instructions or questions, while active tasks (e.g., tidy up the room) are proposed by the perception module and stored in the task memory. For scheduled tasks (e.g., “turn on the air-conditioner at 5 p.m” and “monitor the patient’s temperature hourly”), they are registered by the user and also stored in the task memory. Scheduled tasks are triggered by the timer according to users’ instructions. When any task is triggered, all tasks that can be executed instantly (i.e., not pending for the timer) are sent into an LLM for task priority and dependency evaluation. By retrieving event history in the scene from the memory using RAG, the scheduler can obtain related key events and previous states for better judgment. Finally, tasks with higher priority and that are available for execution will be dispatched through the dispatcher.

Active Perception Capability Evaluation

As the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework requires the perception module to observe and propose potential tasks in the environment, it is vital that the VLMs used for the perception module can meet the requirement. To evaluate such a capability, we built the Active Task Perception Benchmark. The benchmark consists of 272 snapshots in first-person videos from either publicly available datasets
(
Dai et al. 2017
;
Yeshwanth et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Baruch et al. 2021
)
or original videos. The snapshots are preprocessed by Qwen2.5-VL
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
to generate task proposals and bounding boxes for reference, and are gone through manual checks to ensure data quality.

In our benchmark, we focus on three categories of tasks that are common in household scenarios, namely debris cleaning, item organization, and safety checks. Additionally, to prevent the situation where the model achieves high performance by arbitrarily guessing tasks, we add negative samples where the room is tidy (i.e., no task should be proposed).
As the perception module should be adaptable to all open-ended tasks, we utilize several off-the-shelf VLMs trained on either general datasets or embodied tasks and evaluate them under a zero-shot setting. Finally, we measure performance by category accuracy using ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo-1106
(
openai 2021
)
for judgment and the average number of proposals on negative samples.

1
shows that QwenVL-max
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
strikes the best balance between positive samples and negative samples, achieving a high overall accuracy of 77.09%, demonstrating that the model can satisfy the requirements for the perception module well. Apart from QwenVL-max, models like Cosmos-Reason1-7B
(
Azzolini et al. 2025
)
and RoboBrain

Real-world Deployment Evaluation

To thoroughly validate the versatility of the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework, we designed 11 real-world embodied tasks for the robots to execute. These tasks are categorized into independent tasks and competing tasks. The independent tasks are designed to assess the framework’s ability to utilize diverse tools and can be executed independently without interference from other tasks. In contrast, competing tasks involve executing multiple tasks on limited resources, which is used to evaluate the framework’s capacity to perform task planning.

Independent Tasks.

To evaluate
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
’s tool integration and execution capabilities, we designed six distinct tasks, encompassing various types of tool agents, including VLN, VLA, IoT, and web modules. These tasks reflect real-world scenarios in which the robot must collaborate with multiple tools to achieve its goals. Details for each task are listed in the Technical Appendix.
During the experiment, each task is given sufficient time for execution. Empirically, all successful attempts can be accomplished within one minute, which is acceptable in real-world applications.

Results in table
2
demonstrate that the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework exhibited robust performance across these tasks, showcasing its versatility in integrating diverse tools. For IoT-related tasks (e.g., Task 2), the framework achieved high success rates, indicating proficiency in interacting with smart devices. Similarly, Task 1 (pick up scrap paper) was autonomously executed, highlighting the framework’s ability to integrate basic tools for active perception. Additionally, more complex tasks, such as Task 6 (“Move the cardboard box and place an order for delivery”), require the combination of navigation, manipulation, and web ordering. These multi-step operations were also carried out effectively, further illustrating the framework’s ability to combine tools and modules for complex task completion.

While our
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework performed well across various tasks, certain challenges remain. On one hand, due to the imperfect performance (e.g., the failure cases in Table
1
and Technical Appendix) of LLMs for the perception and planning modules, the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework cannot always generate optimal answers. On the other hand, the robots’ unstable hardware has also caused multiple failures during the experiment (e.g., unstable network connections and overheating protections). Thus, with improvements of individual modules,
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
can further enhance its adaptability and effectiveness in more complex real-world scenarios.

Competing Task.

In the next set of experiments, we evaluated the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework’s ability to handle tasks competing for limited resources, which poses a harder challenge by incorporating individual task execution with complex task planning requirements.
While independent tasks stem from a single command and can be broken into sub-tasks without conflict, competing tasks involve concurrent execution with interdependent priorities.
For example, in Task 11, the robot was given up to seven different instructions, each requiring decomposition into smaller sub-tasks and careful prioritization. The complexity increases when new instructions are introduced during the execution of existing tasks, as seen in figure
4
. In this case (Task 9), the robot was initially tasked with placing scrap paper in a trash can, but a higher-priority instruction “Go to the lab and take a picture of my files in the cabinet now” was issued. To accomplish the new mission, the robot should seamlessly switch between tasks, complete the high-priority instruction, and then return to finish the suspended task of disposing of the scrap paper. Therefore, a key feature in this process is the robot’s dynamic scheduler, which ensures that tasks are executed in the proper order.

Table
2
highlights the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework’s success in managing competing-task execution. Tasks 7 and 8 showed high success rates, attributed to the clean and simple environment that minimized irrelevant task detections. However, Tasks 9, 10, and 11 presented greater challenges, as active tasks were introduced mid-execution, requiring real-time adjustments in the robot’s planning and task execution. Task 11, in particular, involved picking up a knocked-over cup actively, requiring the robot to adapt its actions and modify its plan on the fly, resulting in a slight drop in performance due to the increased complexity. Further task details are provided in the Technical Appendix.

Nonetheless, our
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework demonstrated robust competing-task management throughout. Its dynamic scheduler and task memory allowed robots to efficiently manage task execution despite interruptions and new instructions. This capability mirrors human-like reasoning, where task prioritization and adaptation to changing conditions are crucial for successful execution in real-world environments.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
, a framework designed to advance embodied agents for versatile, real-world applications. Through a unified perception module that actively identifies tasks, we eliminate the reliance on restrictive tool feedback. This architectural innovation enables a truly plug-any-tool capability, allowing seamless integration with any controllable device. We pair this with a dynamic multi-task scheduler that intelligently plans, prioritizes, and manages concurrent tasks, handling dependencies and interruptions. Extensive real-world experiments confirm that
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
successfully bridges the gap between benchmarks and practical deployment, delivering a significantly more versatile and adaptable embodied agent. It also provides a foundation for scalable embodied data collection.

Data Collection

When building the benchmark, we collect a series of first-person videos from publicly available datasets
(
Dai et al. 2017
;
Yeshwanth et al. 2023
;

To ensure that the videos can reflect real-world scenarios, we filter the videos and preserve those with appropriate lengths and numbers of objects.
Then, for each video, we take a snapshot every 3 seconds.
By applying Qwen2.5-VL
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
and manual check, we select a subset of 272 snapshots belonging to the following categories:

•

Debris Cleaning: Removes all non-functional surface contaminants—both loose solid fragments and adhered liquids or stains. Simple removal or wiping restores cleanliness, excluding functional items or safety hazards.

•

Item Organization: Involves functional items with a defined purpose or designated location (e.g., clothing, toys, utensils, books) that are misplaced or disorganized. These are not debris (non-functional) or contaminants (stains). The task is to return items to their intended spots or arrange them in an orderly manner.

•

Safety Check: Addresses conditions posing immediate or potential risks of harm (e.g., injury, fire, damage). These override other categories if they introduce danger (e.g., a puddle is a stain if dried but a safety hazard if wet/slippery). The task is to flag or assess such risks.

•

Negative Sample: the room is tidy and no outstanding task should be proposed.

Appendix C
Qualitatve Results

In this section, we provide additional qualitative results for our experiment on real-world deployments. The demonstrations are shown in Fig.
8
.

The tasks in the figure verify that our
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework can actively perceive and flexibly adjust to the environment, perform complex task planning including interruption and resumption, and exhibit strong robustness to external disturbance. These advantages further indicate that the framework can address various issues and demonstrate strong performance in diverse real-world scenarios.

Video demonstrations are available in the Multimedia Appendix of the Supplementary Materials.

Figure 8:
Qualitative results for real-world deployments. In the first task, the robot is given the instruction to “go to the large wooden table in the lab and grasp a pack of tissues.” When the robot approaches the table, it perceives that it is still some distance away from the table and performs a minor adjustment by moving forward. This task demonstrates that our
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework can help the robot adjust flexibly according to the complex environment. In Task 2, the robot is given an instruction to take the mouse to the office and then given another instruction with higher priority to suspend the previous task and return to the table to take a picture. When the robot receives the second instruction, the planner module in the
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework interrupts the low-priority task and temporarily puts it back in the task memory. When the latter task is finished, the suspended task is resumed automatically, showing strong capability in handling multiple tasks. Finally, in the last task, when the robot is grabbing the mouse, an external disturbance (human activity) happens. As the perception module detects the unexpected outcome, it pauses the original workflow and reverts to the grabbing process, verifying that our
\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{3}
framework is robust to external disturbance.
