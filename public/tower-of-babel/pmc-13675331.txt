Pharmacological modulation of cGAS-STING-NLRP3 signaling by nano-immunomodulators in Alzheimer and Parkinson disease
Authors: Mohammed A. Abdel-Rasol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroinflammation is a central driver of neurodegeneration in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD), driven by interconnected pathways involving microglial state dysregulation, inflammasome activation, cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) signaling, impaired mitophagy, CD33-TREM2 imbalance, gut-brain axis disruption, and peripheral immune infiltration across a compromised blood–brain barrier (BBB). Conventional anti-inflammatory therapies remain limited by poor CNS delivery, inadequate cell specificity, and the inability to simultaneously modulate multiple inflammatory pathways. Nano-immunomodulators (NIMs) have emerged as a promising strategy to modulate CNS immunity in preclinical models. This review critically evaluates recent advances in NIM-based strategies for AD and PD, including intrinsically therapeutic nanomaterials, targeted delivery systems, and precision gene-regulatory platforms. Intrinsically therapeutic materials, such as polydopamine nanoparticles and biomimetic nanozymes, provide antioxidant and mitochondrial protective functions, whereas BBB-targeted systems, including nanobody-based platforms and engineered exosomes, enhance targeted CNS delivery. Nanotheranostics approaches integrate imaging capabilities with stimuli-responsive release, while precision interceptors aim to simultaneous regulate pathological processes such as amyloid-beta (Aβ)-associated inflammation and cGAS-STING activation. Emerging gene-delivery strategies, including TREM2 mRNA and CD33-targeting lipid nanoparticles, offer opportunities for microglial reprogramming but remain largely restricted to proof-of-concept and early preclinical stages. The review further discusses AI-driven nanomedicine optimization and advanced human-relevant models, including brain-on-a-chip systems and three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting, as tools to improve translational prediction. Currently, AI approaches primarily support candidate selection, formulation optimization, and mechanistic prediction rather than clinical validation. Major challenges for clinical advancement include achieving precise modulation of microglial states beyond the simplified M1/M2 framework, developing context-dependent regulation of NLRP3 and cGAS–STING pathways, establishing gut–CNS therapeutic strategies, improving pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-based validation platforms, and defining long-term CNS safety frameworks. By integrating molecular mechanisms, AI-assisted nanomedicine, nanoparticle-enabled immune and gene modulation, and advanced preclinical models, this review provides a mechanistic and translational framework for advancing next-generation NIM strategies in AD and PD. This integrated perspective addresses the limitations of previous single-domain analyses focused either on neuroinflammation or nanotechnology and outlines key considerations for translating NIMs from experimental platforms toward clinical applications.

Methodology

This narrative review evaluates nano-immunomodulators (NIMs) targeting neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD). The initial PubMed search was conducted in January 2026, followed by supplementary targeted searches during manuscript preparation and revision to incorporate relevant literature published later in 2026. The search covered English-language publications from 2020 to 2026.

The search strategy combined disease-related terms, including “neuroinflammation,” “Alzheimer’s disease,” “Parkinson’s disease,” “blood-brain barrier,” “microglial polarization,” “NLRP3 inflammasome,” “cGAS-STING pathway,” “TREM2,” “mitophagy,” and “gut-brain axis,” with technology-related keywords including “nano-immunomodulators,” “polydopamine nanoparticles,” “biomimetic nanozymes,” “exosome nanocarriers,” “nanobody-drug conjugates,” “nanotheranostics,” “gene-delivering nanoparticles,” “AI-driven nanomedicine,” “brain-on-a-chip,” and “3D bioprinted brain tissue.”

Literature selection involved sequential assessment of titles, abstracts, and full-text articles for relevance to the review objectives. Publications were eligible if they addressed (i) neuroinflammatory mechanisms associated with AD or PD, (ii) NIM platforms or enabling technologies relevant to neuroimmune modulation, or (iii) experimental and translational evidence supporting precision nanomedicine approaches. Studies unrelated to AD/PD neuroinflammation, irrelevant to NIM strategies, published in languages other than English, or providing insufficient methodological information were excluded.

Selected studies were organized into four thematic domains: (1) molecular mechanisms of neuroinflammation, (2) NIM platform design and engineering, (3) emerging therapeutic technologies, and (4) translational challenges. The level of evidence was assessed qualitatively according to study type and experimental strength, prioritizing peer-reviewed original studies, mechanistically supported preclinical investigations, and clinically relevant evidence. Conceptual frameworks, computational predictions, and early proof-of-concept technologies were considered separately from experimentally validated approaches.

Because this manuscript is a narrative review rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, formal risk-of-bias assessment was not performed. However, platform-specific limitations, including synthesis reproducibility, batch variability, protein corona effects, biodegradation, immunogenicity, manufacturing scalability, and long-term CNS safety, were considered during the critical synthesis. Limitations of this review include potential publication and language bias and the predominance of preclinical evidence, as many emerging NIM approaches, including AI-optimized formulations, engineered exosomes, and gene-based microglial reprogramming systems, remain at early translational stages.

Introduction

Neuroinflammation is a fundamental immune response, primarily mediated by microglia and astrocytes, that becomes chronically pathological in neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) (Adamu et al. 2024; Shi and Yong 2025). This pathological shift is characterized by a predominance of pro-inflammatory M1 microglial polarization, reactive astrogliosis, and NLRP3 inflammasome hyperactivation, which collectively drive sustained IL-1β release and accelerate neuronal damage. Furthermore, a compromised blood–brain barrier (BBB) exacerbates CNS degeneration by permitting peripheral immune cell infiltration (Ajoolabady et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2025a; Beltran-Velasco and Clemente-Suárez 2025).

BBB disruption in NDs reflects progressive neurovascular unit dysfunction rather than an isolated structural defect (Sweeney et al. 2018a, 2018b; Uprety et al. 2021). Chronic exposure to Aβ, α-synuclein, mitochondrial injury, and systemic inflammatory mediators activates endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, and microglia, driving oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and impaired barrier-supportive signaling (Nehra et al. 2022; Lau et al. 2024; Knox et al. 2022). These processes disrupt tight-junction proteins, including claudin-5, occludin, and zonula occludens-1, increase endothelial transcytosis, and weaken the basement membrane through matrix metalloproteinase-mediated extracellular matrix degradation (Ning et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2020; Montagne et al. 2020). Pericyte dysfunction and astrocytic end-foot reactivity further destabilize the endothelium and impair neurovascular coupling, while altered transport and efflux systems reduce BBB selectivity and clearance of neurotoxic molecules (Brown et al. 2019; Díaz-Castro et al. 2023; Storck et al. 2018). Increased BBB permeability then facilitates the entry of plasma proteins, inflammatory mediators, and peripheral leukocytes into the CNS, further activating microglia and sustaining a self-reinforcing cycle of neuroinflammation (Galea 2021; Merlini et al. 2019; Haruwaka et al. 2019).

Because conventional systemic anti-inflammatory therapies often lack sufficient CNS specificity and may produce off-target adverse effects, safer and more precise immunomodulatory strategies are needed (Xu et al. 2025b). Nanomedicine offers a potential strategy to overcome these limitations by using engineered nanomaterials to enhance BBB transport and enable spatially and temporally controlled delivery of immunomodulatory agents to brain-resident glial cells. However, therapeutic efficiency depends on nanoparticle composition, physicochemical properties, and targeting strategies. Nanoplatforms also enable stimulus-responsive and combinatorial delivery of anti-inflammatory agents, neuroprotective compounds, and gene-based therapeutics, supporting their potential use in precision interventions for neurodegenerative diseases (Wang et al. 2025a).

This review provides a mechanistic analysis of nano-immunomodulators (NIMs) as potential therapeutic platforms for AD and PD (Cayero-Otero et al. 2018; Yang 2025; Tziveleka et al. 2025). Although both disorders involve chronic neuroinflammation, their pathological landscapes differ substantially (Heneka et al. 2025; Tansey et al. 2022; De Marchi et al. 2023). AD is characterized primarily by Aβ accumulation and tau pathology, accompanied by neuroinflammatory changes in cortical and hippocampal regions and early BBB disruption (Scheltens et al. 2021; Pascoal et al. 2021; Nation et al. 2019). In contrast, PD is driven mainly by α-synuclein aggregation, nigrostriatal degeneration, and inflammatory remodeling of the substantia nigra and associated basal ganglia circuits (Bloem et al. 2021; Tan et al. 2020; Jewell et al. 2022). These distinct microenvironments shape the timing and nature of immune responses, including peripheral immune-cell infiltration, microglial activation states, and responsiveness to nanomedicine-based therapies (Chen et al. 2023; Paolicelli et al. 2022; Lei et al. 2024a). Accordingly, this review examines shared inflammatory pathways, particularly NLRP3 and cGAS–STING as convergent neuroimmune targets, while addressing their disease-specific roles in AD and PD.

The review is structured around four interconnected objectives. First, it defines the molecular basis of chronic neuroinflammation, including microglial activation states, inflammasome signaling, cGAS–STING activation, impaired mitophagy, gut–brain axis dysregulation, and the CD33–TREM2 regulatory axis, thereby establishing a framework for rational NIM design. Second, it evaluates advances in NIM platforms, including direct immunomodulators, targeted delivery systems, and nanotheranostics, emphasizing their ability to overcome BBB transport limitations, improve cell-type specificity, and enable stimulus-responsive cargo release. Third, it examines emerging technologies that may accelerate translation, including AI-guided nanoparticle optimization, human-relevant preclinical models such as brain-on-a-chip and 3D bioprinting, and gene-delivery strategies for microglial reprogramming. Finally, it identifies major knowledge gaps and translational challenges to define a roadmap for advancing NIM-based precision neuroimmunotherapy toward clinical application. Technologies are considered according to developmental stage, distinguishing clinically evaluated or experimentally validated approaches from preclinical proof-of-concept strategies requiring further optimization and validation.

This review is positioned within the rapidly expanding 2024–2026 literature addressing individual aspects of nanomedicine and neuroinflammation. Despite these advances, a critical gap remains in integrating neuroimmune mechanisms with nanomedicine engineering within a unified therapeutic framework. Existing reviews generally address either the molecular mechanisms of neuroinflammation or the design and delivery characteristics of nanoplatforms, with limited analysis of their intersection. This review addresses this gap by mapping major NIM classes including direct immunomodulators, BBB-targeted carriers, nanotheranostics, pathway-specific nanointerceptors, and gene-delivery lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) onto key inflammatory networks such as cGAS–STING–NLRP3, the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway, and the CD33–TREM2 regulatory axis (Table 1). By integrating AI-driven nanoplatform design, advanced preclinical models, molecular mechanisms, and translational considerations, this review provides a comprehensive framework for advancing precision nanomedicine in neurodegenerative diseases.

Neuroinflammation mechanisms and pathways

Microglial state plasticity

Microglial activation in AD and PD is increasingly recognized as a dynamic, heterogeneous process rather than a binary transition between M1 and M2 phenotypes (Ransohoff 2016; Deczkowska et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2025a; Keren-Shaul et al. 2017). Although the M1/M2 classification provides a simplified distinction between pro-inflammatory and reparative responses, single-cell and transcriptomic analyses have identified a spectrum of context-dependent microglial states (Mathys et al. 2019; Hammond et al. 2019; Krasemann et al. 2017). These include homeostatic microglia, disease-associated microglia (DAM), activated response microglia (ARM), interferon-responsive populations, and other transcriptionally distinct phenotypes shaped by disease progression and local environmental signals (Yang et al. 2025a; Friedman et al. 2018; Xue and Wu 2025; Varsamos et al. 2025).

Pro-inflammatory stimuli, including IFN-γ and TNF-α, induce microglial programs involving NF-κB and STAT1 signaling, cytokine production, reactive oxygen species generation, and inflammasome activation. In contrast, PPAR-γ, TGF-β, and TREM2 signaling supports phagocytosis, metabolic adaptation, homeostatic functions, and tissue repair (Varsamos et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2026; Guo et al. 2024a; Masuda et al. 2020). These states are neither discrete nor mutually exclusive, as individual microglia may simultaneously exhibit inflammatory, phagocytic, metabolic, and repair-associated transcriptional programs depending on disease stage and microenvironmental conditions (Li and Barres 2018; Rana et al. 2026).

Accordingly, therapeutic strategies should move beyond the simplified goal of inducing an M1-to-M2 transition and instead regulate microglial state balance. Inflammasome activation

The NLRP3 inflammasome, comprising NLRP3, the adaptor protein ASC, and caspase−1, is a central mediator of chronic neuroinflammation in neurodegenerative diseases (Mustafa et al. 2025; Voet et al. 2019; Halle et al. 2008). Disease-associated protein aggregates can trigger its activation, with Aβ-driven signaling particularly implicated in AD and α-synuclein-associated activation more prominent in PD (Ising et al. 2019; Codolo et al. 2013; Gan et al. 2026). Mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS further enhance inflammasome activity by promoting mitochondrial damage and mtDNA release (Moustakli et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2011; Shi et al. 2015).

Following inflammasome assembly, caspase−1 processes pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature inflammatory forms and cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), initiating pyroptosis (Xu et al. 2025b; Kayagaki et al. 2015; Ding et al. 2016). The resulting GSDMD N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-NT) forms membrane pores that disrupt cellular integrity, causing pyroptotic cell death and release of IL-1β, IL-18, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including HMGB1, extracellular ATP, and mtDNA (Kaczmarek et al. 2013; Bours et al. 2006). These signals activate pattern-recognition and purinergic receptors on neighboring microglia and astrocytes, further promoting inflammasome priming and activation (Liddelow and Barres 2017; Broz and Dixit 2016).

Non-canonical inflammasome signaling provides an additional route to GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis (Kesavardhana et al. 2020). Murine caspase−11 and its human counterparts, caspase−4 and caspase−5, directly cleave GSDMD independently of caspase−1 and can subsequently promote NLRP3 activation (Franklin et al. 2014). Together, these pathways establish a self-amplifying inflammatory circuit in which pyroptotic cell death drives cytokine and DAMP release, sustaining inflammasome activation in surrounding cells (Decout et al. 2021).

Gut-brain axis

Dysbiosis of the gut-brain axis systemically drives neurodegeneration (Wang et al. 2025a; Chen et al. 2025). Environmental or dietary stressors enrich pathogenic bacteria that secrete lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and deplete neuroprotective short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Palanivelu et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025a). This metabolic imbalance elevates systemic cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), degrades endothelial tight junctions, and compromises the BBB, facilitating the CNS influx of peripheral immune cells (Loh et al. 2024; Chakrabarti and Chattopadhyay 2025).

Furthermore, altered vagal signaling impairs neurotransmitter synthesis (serotonin, GABA, dopamine) (Santos et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023). Peripheral immune cell infiltration

BBB disruption facilitates pathological recruitment of peripheral leukocytes into the CNS (Tumpa et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2021). This breakdown reflects coordinated neurovascular unit dysfunction, in which inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress impair endothelial tight-junction proteins, including claudin-5, occludin, and zonula occludens-1 (Langen et al. 2019). Concurrent matrix metalloproteinase activity degrades the basement membrane and extracellular matrix, while pericyte dysfunction, astrocytic end-foot impairment, increased transcellular transport, and altered efflux mechanisms further reduce BBB selectivity (Yuan et al. 2025). Collectively, these changes increase the entry of circulating immune cells and inflammatory mediators, thereby amplifying CNS inflammation (Santos-Lima et al. 2022).

Among infiltrating immune populations, neutrophils are recruited early through CXCL1 and IL-8 signaling and contribute to oxidative stress and the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) (Nowaczewska-Kuchta et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025b). T cells subsequently infiltrate the parenchyma, producing granzyme B and promoting pro-inflammatory microglial polarization (Yang et al. 2025b; Van Hove et al. 2025). Metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial quality control

Microglial metabolism and mitochondrial quality control strictly dictate neurotoxicity (Huang et al. 2024; Nirakis et al. 2025). Pro-inflammatory stimuli upregulate HIF-1α in M1 microglia, shifting metabolism to glycolysis. This elevates ROS production, chronically activating the NLRP3 inflammasome (Tian et al. 2025b; Rosencrans et al. 2025). Normally, PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy clears damaged mitochondria (Zhang et al. 2025a; Zhou et al. 2025). However, AD and PD severely impair this process; the resulting accumulation of defective mitochondria causes cytosolic mtDNA efflux, engaging the cGAS-STING pathway to amplify oxidative stress and microglial activation in a self-reinforcing cycle (Li et al. 2025a; Quan et al. 2025).

Novel immune signaling

The cGAS-STING pathway

This cascade mechanistically links mitochondrial dysfunction to NLRP3 hyperactivation. Cytosolic DNA from impaired mitophagy or aggregate-induced cellular stress activates cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), which synthesizes cGAMP to bind endoplasmic reticulum-resident STING (Jauhari et al. 2026; Zhao et al. 2026). This engagement induces IRF3 nuclear translocation, driving type I interferon (IFN-β) and pro-inflammatory cytokine transcription (Qi et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025b). The CD33-TREM2 axis

Microglial function is strongly shaped by the inhibitory CD33 and activating TREM2 receptors in AD, whereas PD more prominently involves microglial responses to α-synuclein, altered dopamine metabolism, and region-specific innate immune activation (Satoh et al. 2025; Abdulkhaliq et al. 2026a). CD33 binding triggers an ITIM motif (recruiting SHP-1/2 phosphatases) to suppress immune activation; its pathological overactivation limits aggregate clearance and correlates with AD risk (Che et al. 2026; Lin et al. 2025a). Conversely, TREM2 signaling via the PI3K pathway promotes phagocytosis, Aβ clearance, and M2 polarization (Huang et al. 2026a; Liu et al. 2014). Modulating this axis using targeted nanomedicines such as TREM2 mRNA-delivering lipid nanoparticles or siCD33-loaded carriers represents a precise strategy to restore microglial homeostasis (Fig. 5). The complex interplay between microglial dysregulation, inflammasome activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, gut-brain axis disruption, and peripheral immune infiltration establishes a self-amplifying neuroinflammatory network that drives neurodegenerative progression. Latest advances in nano-immunomodulator design and function

Nano-immunomodulatory strategies for neurodegenerative diseases encompass diverse but interconnected platform classes with distinct therapeutic mechanisms. Intrinsically active nanomaterials, including polydopamine nanoparticles and nanozymes, reduce oxidative stress and modulate redox-sensitive inflammatory pathways. Targeted nanocarriers, such as nanobody-functionalized systems and engineered exosomes, enhance BBB penetration and enable cell- or pathology-specific delivery. Drug-loaded and stimuli-responsive nanoplatforms provide controlled release of immunomodulatory cargos in response to disease-associated signals, including ROS, pH changes, and protease activity.

Precision pathway-interceptor systems enable targeted regulation of key inflammatory networks, particularly NLRP3 inflammasome and cGAS–STING signaling, while nucleic acid delivery platforms, especially lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), support microglial reprogramming through mRNA, siRNA, and gene-editing approaches. Collectively, these advances extend nanomedicine beyond general inflammatory suppression toward integrated strategies combining antioxidant activity, targeted delivery, pathway-specific modulation, and long-term immune regulation.

Direct immunomodulatory nanomaterials

Building on the molecular targets discussed in Sect. "Neuroinflammation mechanisms and pathways", this section examines nanoplatforms designed to regulate specific pathological mechanisms, with Table 5 summarizing the corresponding mechanism-platform relationships. Nanomaterials with intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity represent a promising approach for chronic neuroinflammation in AD and PD. Rather than promoting a simplified M1-to-M2 transition, these platforms may regulate microglial state plasticity by suppressing persistent inflammatory programs while supporting homeostatic, phagocytic, and repair-associated functions. Unlike passive delivery systems, intrinsically active nanomaterials directly reduce oxidative stress, modulate pro-inflammatory signaling, and support neuronal preservation without requiring encapsulated therapeutic cargos. Representative examples include polydopamine nanoparticles (PDNPs) and biomimetic nanozymes.

Polydopamine nanoparticles

Polydopamine nanoparticles (PDNPs) exhibit intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity primarily through the redox properties of polymerized polydopamine (Ryu et al. 2018). Their abundant catechol and quinone groups provide strong radical-scavenging and electron-donating capacities, enabling ROS neutralization and reduction of oxidative stress (Wang et al. 2026a). By limiting ROS accumulation, PDNPs can attenuate oxidative stress-driven inflammatory signaling and protect CNS cells from oxidative damage (Wang et al. 2026a).

Beyond their antioxidant effects, PDNPs have been reported to suppress NF-κB signaling and modulate pro-inflammatory microglial responses, potentially favoring more homeostatic or repair-associated states within the broader activation continuum (Duță et al. 2025). Preclinical studies indicate that PDNP-based formulations can reduce ROS levels, attenuate microglial activation, and decrease inflammatory cytokine production in models of CNS injury and neurodegeneration, supporting their potential as redox-modulating nanotherapeutics (Ceccarelli et al. 2025).

PDNP surface chemistry may also facilitate interactions with inflammatory microenvironments and indirectly reduce inflammasome priming and downstream cytokine amplification (Wang et al. 2026b). Biomimetic nanozymes

Biomimetic nanozymes are engineered nanomaterials that mimic the catalytic activities of natural enzymes under physiological conditions (Chen et al. 2026). In neuroinflammatory settings, these platforms are primarily designed to replicate antioxidant enzymes, particularly superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), enabling catalytic removal of superoxide radicals and H2O2 (Godugu et al. 2025; Amniouel et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025b). Through sustained ROS scavenging, nanozymes can reduce oxidative stress and preserve mitochondrial function (Xia et al. 2025).

Beyond redox regulation, certain nanozyme platforms have been reported to enhance mitophagy and promote clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby limiting cytosolic accumulation of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and other damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate innate immune pathways such as cGAS–STING (Xia et al. 2025). By simultaneously targeting oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, these systems may attenuate self-amplifying inflammatory circuits and promote less inflammatory, more phagocytic microglial states (Zha et al. 2024) (Table 3).

Preclinical studies have shown that biomimetic nanozymes provide catalytic ROS regulation, preserve mitochondrial function, reduce mtDNA-associated innate immune activation, and improve neuroprotective outcomes in inflammatory and neurodegenerative models (Xia et al. 2025). Collectively, these findings support their potential as enzyme-mimetic nanotherapeutics targeting interconnected oxidative and mitochondrial mechanisms underlying neuroinflammation (Zha et al. 2024).

Targeted delivery platforms

The BBB remains a major obstacle to effective therapeutic delivery in AD and PD (Serrano et al. 2025). Targeted nanomedicine platforms, including nanobody-functionalized systems and engineered exosomes, are being developed to improve CNS delivery, enhance spatial selectivity, and reduce systemic off-target exposure (Boado 2025).

Nanocarriers can interact with or cross the BBB through several complementary mechanisms (Serrano et al. 2025). Receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) exploits endogenous endothelial receptors, including the transferrin receptor, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), and insulin receptor (Mehdizadeh et al. 2025). Functionalization with specific ligands, peptides, or antibody fragments facilitates receptor binding, endothelial internalization, vesicular trafficking, and BBB transport (Liu et al. 2025c).

Adsorptive-mediated transcytosis (AMT) relies on electrostatic interactions between positively charged nanocarrier surfaces and negatively charged endothelial glycocalyx components, promoting nonspecific endocytosis and transcellular transport (Li et al. 2024). However, excessive surface charge may reduce targeting specificity, increase protein adsorption, and contribute to vascular toxicity (Rautio et al. 2013).

Carrier- or transporter-mediated strategies exploit endogenous nutrient transport systems, such as those for glucose and amino acids, by incorporating transporter-recognized substrates or mimetic ligands into nanocarrier designs (Mehdizadeh et al. 2025). Their effectiveness depends on transporter specificity, competition with endogenous substrates, and preservation of ligand recognition after nanocarrier modification (Choi et al. 2012).

Cell-mediated delivery, often termed a “Trojan horse” strategy, uses migratory cells such as monocytes, macrophages, or stem cells to facilitate nanomedicine transport across the BBB (Mohammed et al. 2025). Although potentially advantageous under inflammatory conditions that increase immune-cell trafficking, this approach is limited by variable biodistribution, inadequate control of cellular migration, and potential effects on inflammatory recruitment (Jo et al. 2015).

BBB-targeted delivery efficiency is influenced by nanocarrier physicochemical properties, including size, shape, surface charge, ligand density, colloidal stability, and protein-corona composition (Ceña and Játiva 2018).

Although optimized surface engineering can improve BBB interactions, no universal size or charge parameters ensure successful transport across different platforms (Wang et al. 2025c). Delivery outcomes are also affected by ligand–receptor interactions, disease-associated BBB alterations, species differences, and the experimental model used (Lee et al. 2026).

Preclinical evaluation of BBB-targeted nanomedicines commonly assesses brain-to-blood distribution, endothelial uptake, transendothelial transport, parenchymal localization, and uptake by target CNS cells, together with BBB integrity and therapeutic efficacy (Zhang et al. 2025c).

Relevant outcomes include modulation of inflammatory cytokines, enhanced clearance of pathological aggregates, and improvements in functional or behavioral measures (Zeng et al. 2026).

Despite substantial progress, clinical translation remains limited by interspecies differences, disease-dependent BBB heterogeneity, peripheral organ accumulation, and the lack of standardized methods for quantifying brain delivery (Lee et al. 2026). Nanobody (sdAb)-based systems

Nanobodies (single-domain antibodies) are compact (~ 15 kDa) camelid-derived antibody fragments that offer enhanced tissue penetration and potential advantages for BBB delivery (Lafon et al. 2025; Naeem et al. 2024). Their lack of an Fc region reduces Fc-mediated immune activation and may limit immunogenicity-related concerns (Tsitokana et al. 2023). These small binding domains can selectively recognize disease-associated targets, including Aβ and α-synuclein aggregates, with reduced steric constraints compared with conventional antibodies.

When conjugated to nanocarriers, nanobodies can improve accumulation of therapeutic cargos at pathological sites, enabling more selective intervention while potentially reducing systemic exposure (Liu et al. 2025c) (Table 3). In human BBB models and preclinical neurodegenerative disease studies, nanobody-functionalized platforms have demonstrated enhanced receptor-mediated transcytosis, increased brain accumulation, and selective targeting of pathological protein conformations, supporting their role as promising BBB-shuttling ligands for precision CNS delivery (Esparza et al. 2023; Jootar et al. 2026; Sun et al. 2025a).

Exosome-based nanocarriers

Natural, cell-derived exosomes offer high biocompatibility and BBB-crossing capabilities, making them ideal CNS drug vectors (Huang et al. 2026b; Onohuean et al. 2025). Their lipid bilayer encapsulates diverse cargoes (miRNAs, small molecules, neurotrophins), while surface functionalization directs them to specific brain cells (Évora et al. 2025; Feng et al. 2025). Engineered exosomes can deliver miRNAs to silence pro-inflammatory microglial genes and suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation, restoring immune balance (An et al. 2025; Shahlaei et al. 2025). They can also simultaneously co-deliver pathology inhibitors (targeting tau or Aβ) and neurotrophins to address multiple neurodegenerative mechanisms concurrently (Abdulkhaliq et al. 2026b; Kudpaje et al. 2025; Ke et al. 2026) (Table 3).

Preclinical exosome studies have shown that engineered vesicles can increase CNS delivery of miRNA and protein cargoes, suppress microglial inflammatory signaling, and improve disease-related phenotypes in experimental AD and PD models, underscoring their translational value as naturally derived drug carriers (Huang et al. 2026b; Salarpour et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024).

Nanotheranostics

Nanotheranostic platforms integrate diagnostic imaging and targeted therapy to overcome limitations of conventional treatments, including poor specificity and limited CNS penetration (Zhao et al. 2025a; Sun et al. 2025b). Diagnostic components commonly include MRI contrast agents, such as iron oxide and gadolinium-based materials, or fluorescent probes for real-time visualization of neuroinflammatory processes (Kochman et al. 2026; Araujo et al. 2026; Prasanth et al. 2025). These systems can detect disease-associated features, including Aβ plaques, tau aggregates, and α-synuclein pathology, as well as microenvironmental signals such as local acidosis, elevated ROS levels, and increased matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity, enabling spatial and temporal characterization of pathological regions (Zhao et al. 2025a; Sun et al. 2025b; Araujo et al. 2026; Prasanth et al. 2025; Ballance et al. 2019).

Therapeutic components are often engineered for stimulus-responsive release triggered by disease-specific cues (Liu et al. 2026a). pH-sensitive coatings promote cargo release in acidic environments, ROS-responsive linkers activate under oxidative stress, and enzyme-responsive systems using proteases such as MMPs or cathepsins enable localized delivery at active pathological sites (Zhang et al. 2023a, 2023b; Guo et al. 2024b). These strategies improve spatial and temporal control of therapeutic exposure, potentially enhancing efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity (Zhang et al. 2023a).

By combining diagnostic monitoring with microenvironment-responsive drug release, nanotheranostic platforms enable imaging-guided anti-inflammatory intervention (Zhao et al. 2025b). Pathological signals used for lesion detection can also serve as activation triggers, allowing localized delivery of immunomodulatory agents such as MCC950 and other neuroprotective cargos while minimizing off-target effects (Tian et al. 2025c). However, most nanotheranostic systems remain at an early preclinical stage, with integrated imaging and therapeutic performance demonstrated mainly in rodent and ex vivo models rather than human CNS platforms (Zhao et al. 2025b) (Table 3).

Preclinical studies have shown that nanotheranostic approaches can combine real-time visualization of pathological lesions with localized therapeutic release, providing proof-of-concept for spatiotemporally controlled intervention in neuroinflammatory disease models (Zhao et al. 2025b).

Precision pathway interceptors in neurodegenerative diseases

Precision pathway interceptors are engineered nanomaterials designed to disrupt self-amplifying molecular circuits that sustain chronic neuroinflammation by targeting multiple pathological nodes simultaneously (Xie et al. 2023). One example is the AD Aβ–STING axis, in which Aβ accumulation can activate microglial cGAS–STING signaling, impair phagocytic function, and reinforce pro-inflammatory microglial states (Chung et al. 2024). The ASSIST system (Aβ-targeting, STING-inhibiting, and immunomodulatory nanomaterials) represents a proof-of-concept approach within this category (Hettiarachchi et al. 2019).

ASSIST nanoparticles have been reported to target Aβ plaques while co-delivering components that suppress STING activation (Mitchell et al. 2021). This dual-action strategy may reduce inflammatory cytokine production and promote a more balanced microglial phenotype; however, its therapeutic potential remains limited by insufficient in vivo validation and clinical evidence (Chung et al. 2024).

Although these platforms demonstrate the potential of precision nano-immunomodulation, their translation requires assessment beyond therapeutic efficacy, including manufacturing scalability, biological complexity, safety, and regulatory considerations (Topal et al. 2020). Preclinical studies of ASSIST-like and related multi-target nanointerceptors have shown reduced Aβ-associated inflammatory signaling, enhanced phagocytic activity, and improved microglial functional balance, supporting the feasibility of simultaneously targeting pathological aggregation and innate immune activation (Lin et al. 2025b; Ren et al. 2025; Younis et al. 2022).

Limitations and translational challenges of current NIM platforms

Despite their mechanistic advantages, current NIM platforms face substantial platform-specific barriers to clinical translation (Gkogkos et al. 2023). Polydopamine nanoparticles and biomimetic nanozymes provide intrinsic catalytic and antioxidant activities; however, their synthesis is highly sensitive to reaction conditions, including pH, oxygen availability, and precursor concentration, which can affect particle size, surface properties, and biological activity (Battaglini et al. 2025). Their reactive surfaces may also promote protein corona formation in biological fluids, altering targeting efficiency, biodistribution, and ROS-scavenging performance (Sun et al. 2024; Mei et al. 2025). Moreover, the long-term biodegradation and clearance of carbon- and metal-based nanozyme systems remain insufficiently characterized, raising concerns about accumulation after repeated administration (Toader et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2022).

Nanobody-based platforms offer high targeting specificity and reduced Fc-mediated immune activation but may still induce anti-drug antibody responses after repeated exposure (Ackaert et al. 2021; Pauw et al. 2023; Xenaki et al. 2021). Their small size also promotes rapid renal clearance, necessitating half-life extension strategies that increase design complexity (Ackaert et al. 2021; Peng et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2023). Exosome-based carriers provide high biocompatibility but are limited by donor-cell variability, inconsistent cargo loading, and the lack of standardized production and quality-control procedures (Koh et al. 2023; Palakurthi et al. 2024). Their complex biological composition further complicates regulatory classification and manufacturing standardization (Lankoff et al. 2024).

Nanotheranostic systems face additional challenges because their multifunctional architectures often combine imaging agents, targeting ligands, therapeutic cargos, and responsive components (Shanahan et al. 2025; Crist et al. 2021). This complexity increases manufacturing demands, cost, and batch variability, while the long-term safety of retained imaging materials, including metal-based contrast agents, requires further evaluation (Li et al. 2026). Similarly, precision pathway interceptors such as ASSIST-like systems remain largely confined to early preclinical validation (Xie et al. 2023; Joseph and Nance 2022; Stucchi et al. 2026). Novel approaches and models in nano-immunomodulators

Artificial intelligence-driven nanomedicine

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are emerging as powerful tools for accelerating NIM design in AD and PD by linking nanoparticle properties with biological outcomes (Krsek et al. 2025; Hosni et al. 2025). Supervised ML models, including random forests and gradient-boosting algorithms, can integrate physicochemical descriptors such as core composition, size, aspect ratio, surface charge, ligand density, and protein corona characteristics with experimental parameters, including BBB permeability, endothelial viability, microglial uptake, and cytokine responses (Li et al. 2025b; Sela et al. 2025). These approaches identify nanoparticle features associated with improved BBB transport and reduced toxicity, enabling rational optimization of NIM formulations beyond empirical screening (Li et al. 2025b; Tomitaka et al. 2023; Su et al. 2025).

ML-based ligand prediction platforms further support the selection of targeting molecules by evaluating peptides, antibodies, and aptamers for affinity and specificity toward disease-relevant targets, including Aβ plaques, α-synuclein aggregates, and microglial receptors such as TREM2 and CX3CR1 (Bansal et al. 2025; Rock et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2026b).

In parallel, deep learning and network-based approaches can integrate single-cell transcriptomic and proteomic datasets to characterize immune-cell states, identify therapeutic targets, and guide nanomedicine design (Xu et al. 2025c; He et al. 2024a, 2024b).

AI-driven workflows have also been applied in preclinical AD and PD nanomedicine research to prioritize nanoparticle formulations with favorable BBB properties and identify targeting strategies for advanced platforms, including exosome-mimetic vesicles and gene-delivery lipid nanoparticles (Krsek et al. 2025; Tomitaka et al. 2023). Collectively, these approaches provide a data-driven framework for improving the precision, efficiency, and translational potential of NIM development (Krsek et al. 2025; Hosni et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2026).

Next-generation pre-clinical models

To overcome the interspecies limitations and predictive failures of traditional animal models, emerging platforms accurately replicate human neurobiology.

Brain-on-a-chip

Brain-on-a-chip systems are preclinical microfluidic in vitro models that reconstruct the human neurovascular unit (NVU) using human-derived cells. By simulating blood flow and incorporating disease markers, they enable high-throughput, real-time evaluation of nanoparticle BBB permeability. In the context of neuroinflammatory nanomedicine, NVU chips have been applied to systematically compare BBB traversal and neurovascular toxicity across different NIM formulations, quantifying changes in transendothelial electrical resistance, tight-junction protein expression, and cytokine profiles in response to polymeric nanoparticles, exosome-mimetic vesicles, and lipid-based carriers before progression to in vivo AD/PD models (Ferrari et al. 2025; Mao et al. 2024) (Table 7).

3D bioprinted brain tissues

To overcome the limitations of conventional 2D cultures, 3D bioprinting enables the integration of NVU components within tunable, brain-mimetic matrices that better preserve native cellular phenotypes (Lee et al. 2021). These models reproduce essential multicellular interactions, including neuron-glia signaling pathways involving NF-κB, NLRP3, and cGAS–STING, while incorporating perfusable channels to evaluate BBB function under inflammatory conditions (Wang et al. 2025d; Aiyegbusi et al. 2026). The introduction of disease-relevant stimuli, such as Aβ or α-synuclein, further enables modeling of pathological metabolic changes and spatial assessment of stimuli-responsive nanocarrier behavior (Qiu et al. 2025; Baghirov 2025; Tang et al. 2025) (Table 7).

In NIM development, 3D bioprinted brain tissues provide human-relevant platforms for evaluating nanoparticle effects on microglial activation states, neuronal viability, and BBB integrity under AD- and PD-like inflammatory environments. These systems support dose–response analysis and multidimensional safety evaluation of advanced NIM platforms, including stimuli-responsive and gene-delivery nanocarriers, within a physiologically relevant neurovascular context (Wang et al. 2025d; Aiyegbusi et al. 2026; Qiu et al. 2025; Baghirov 2025; Tang et al. 2025) (Table 7).

Convergent technologies

Neuroinflammatory nanomedicine is shifting toward programmable systems designed to actively restore microglial phagocytosis and metabolism, rather than merely suppressing cytokines.

Nanobody-drug conjugates

Camelid-derived nanobodies efficiently target cryptic CNS epitopes (e.g., conformation-specific oligomers) without Fc-driven immunogenicity, making them promising candidates for BBB-directed delivery. However, nanobody-drug conjugates remain largely at the preclinical stage. Conjugating them to drug-loaded nanoparticles enables precise dual-target localization. For example, pairing an anti-aggregate nanobody with an NLRP3 inhibitor (e.g., MCC950) halts localized NF-κB signaling and oxidative stress at sites of maximal inflammasome priming (Sela et al. 2025; Rafiei et al. 2025). Advanced constructs utilize BBB shuttles and microenvironment-responsive linkers, though rapid renal clearance and durable CNS exposure remain key challenges (Feiten et al. 2026; Liao et al. 2025; Han et al. 2024).

Gene-delivering nanoparticles

Gene-delivery platforms, particularly lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), use engineered ionizable lipids and targeting ligands to enhance microglial uptake and promote endosomal escape in early preclinical systems (Bai et al. 2025; Saber et al. 2024; Penney et al. 2024). These approaches aim to modulate disease-relevant regulatory pathways, including the CD33–TREM2 axis. TREM2 mRNA delivery has provided proof-of-concept evidence for enhancing phagocytic microglial functions through TYROBP–SYK–PI3K–AKT signaling while reducing NLRP3- and cGAS–STING-associated inflammatory responses in experimental models (Roamcharern and Yubolphan 2026). Conversely, targeted siRNA or CRISPR-based strategies have been explored to suppress inhibitory CD33 signaling; however, their long-term safety, durability, and cell-type specificity remain unresolved (Roamcharern and Yubolphan 2026).

Co-delivery strategies combining TREM2 enhancement with CD33 inhibition represent an emerging approach to microglial reprogramming. However, efficient BBB delivery, endosomal escape, and determination of safe therapeutic windows for immune modulation require further validation in human iPSC-derived models and translational studies (Roamcharern and Yubolphan 2026). Research gaps and future prospects

Despite significant advances in NIM development for AD and PD, major translational and mechanistic challenges remain, requiring coordinated progress across materials science, neuroimmunology, artificial intelligence, and clinical research. Many current NIM platforms are limited by complex multicomponent architectures, restricted manufacturing scalability, and insufficient batch-to-batch reproducibility. Variations in core composition, surface functionalization, and protein corona formation can substantially affect BBB transport, biodistribution, and immune responses.

Many preclinical nanoplatforms also accumulate substantially in peripheral organs, particularly the liver and spleen, with prolonged retention in the reticuloendothelial system, raising concerns about long-term safety and potential disruption of physiological microglial functions. Additional challenges include heterogeneous particle size distributions and incomplete characterization of degradation products, which complicate regulatory assessment and prediction of chronic CNS effects.

Addressing these platform-level limitations will require standardized physicochemical characterization, scalable manufacturing approaches, and comprehensive long-term toxicological evaluation. Resolving these challenges alongside remaining mechanistic uncertainties will be essential for developing safe, reproducible, and clinically translatable NIM-based neuroimmunotherapies for AD and PD.

Microglial heterogeneity and NIM selectivity

Current NIMs should move beyond the binary M1/M2 paradigm and instead account for a broad continuum of microglial states, including disease-associated microglia DAM, activated response microglia ARM, homeostatic microglia, and other transcriptionally defined subpopulations identified through single-cell transcriptomics. Shifting the M1-to-M2 balance risks suppressing neuroprotective phenotypes. Future NIMs must selectively engage pathological subtypes at single-cell resolution using specific surface markers and transcription factor profiles.

Dual targeting of the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 node

Although cGAS-STING activation cross-primes the NLRP3 inflammasome, intensifying neurodegeneration, no clinically validated nanoplatform simultaneously inhibits both pathways in vivo. Future combinatorial interceptors must co-deliver STING inhibitors and NLRP3 antagonists (e.g., MCC950) at disease-specific stoichiometric ratios, potentially utilizing cGAMP-triggered release.

Gut-brain neuroinflammatory axis

Current nanomedicines address the gut or CNS in isolation, limiting their capacity to modulate dysbiosis-driven systemic-to-central inflammatory relays. Future progress is most likely to come from combining gut-directed anti-inflammatory interventions, SCFA-supportive approaches, and BBB-crossing nanocarriers within a staged therapeutic framework. At present, microbiome-responsive nanomedicine should be regarded as a promising but still early-stage concept, requiring mechanistic validation in longitudinal models that track both microbial composition and CNS inflammatory readouts.

Modeling the peripheral-CNS immune interface

Current preclinical tools (e.g., static brain-on-a-chip or 3D tissues) fail to replicate the dynamic, multi-stage extravasation of peripheral immune cells, hindering the evaluation of NIMs targeting chemokine-dependent infiltration (e.g., blocking CCR2 or CXCR1/2). To address this, platforms must integrate perfusable vascular channels populated with PBMCs alongside CNS constructs. Crucially, these systems need a dynamically responsive BBB that degrades progressively under inflammatory stress (cytokines, ROS, MMPs) to mirror human pathology. Furthermore, models must incorporate iPSC-derived cells from patients with distinct genetic variants (e.g., ApoE4, LRRK2) to capture genotype-stratified infiltration dynamics absent in animal models.

Microglial metabolic reprogramming as a target

Activated microglia shift to glycolysis via HIF-1, driving ROS overproduction and NLRP3 activation while impairing mitophagy. NIMs that reprogram metabolism toward oxidative phosphorylation remain exploratory, although this axis is increasingly supported by preclinical evidence. Future work should prioritize metabolically informed nanoplatforms that can be tested in iPSC-derived systems and organoid-based models before translation to in vivo disease settings.

CD33-TREM2 axis: clinical translation

Co-delivering CD33 silencers and TREM2 mRNA via a single nanoparticle remains untranslated. Major barriers include undefined stoichiometric balances to avoid maladaptive overactivation, low endosomal escape efficiency in microglia, and poor CNS-selective delivery across early-disease BBBs. Future research must define safe upregulation windows, utilize inflammation-responsive promoters, and validate constructs in iPSC-derived models capturing variants like TREM2 R47H.

AI-driven NIM design: from prediction to validation

A gap exists between in silico AI predictions and in vivo validation, as training datasets often derive from simplified models lacking neurovascular complexity. In the near term, AI should be used mainly for formulation ranking, feature selection, and hypothesis generation rather than as a substitute for experimental validation. Federated or multimodal learning pipelines integrating 3D bioprinting, organoid pharmacokinetics, and human neuroimaging may improve predictive accuracy, but these approaches still require extensive benchmarking against robust preclinical data.

Nanotheranostics: real-time monitoring

Existing nanotheranostics lack spatial resolution to detect individual plaques or Lewy bodies in vivo, and their responsive release mechanisms are not yet validated in human brain tissue. The most realistic next step is to refine multimodal imaging-enabled systems that combine established diagnostic probes with preclinical stimulus-responsive release, while avoiding assumptions of immediate clinical utility. Development should prioritize reproducibility, sensitivity to disease-associated microenvironments, and compatibility with longitudinal safety assessment.

Long-term safety and regulatory translation

Most preclinical studies assess short-term outcomes, leaving the chronic consequences of CNS nanoparticle accumulation unknown. Future priorities include longitudinal biocompatibility studies in aged disease models and establishing standardized regulatory frameworks (aligned with FDA and EMA guidelines). Additionally, scalable synthesis protocols for complex constructs (nanobody conjugates, exosome carriers) are prerequisite for Phase I/II clinical trials.

Comparative perspective with existing therapeutics

The translational potential of NIMs can be better understood by comparison with current and investigational therapies for AD and PD (Lei et al. 2024b; Kim and Lee 2024). Conventional anti-inflammatory agents, including corticosteroids and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, can reduce systemic inflammation but are limited by poor CNS selectivity, incomplete modulation of disease-relevant pathways, and systemic toxicity (Mallick et al. 2025; Coll et al. 2015). Similarly, small-molecule inhibitors of inflammatory signaling pathways often show promising in vitro activity but face limited BBB penetration, pharmacokinetic instability, and insufficient in vivo durability (Coll et al. 2015).

Direct NLRP3 and STING inhibitors offer promising strategies for targeting upstream inflammatory amplification pathways; however, their development is constrained by incomplete target validation, context-dependent pathway functions, and concerns about long-term immune modulation (Zheng et al. 2024; Gu et al. 2024).

Monoclonal antibodies provide high target specificity and have advanced neurodegenerative disease research, particularly for extracellular pathological targets (Lei et al. 2024b; Pardridge 2023). However, their large molecular size, high production costs, limited BBB penetration, and dependence on receptor-mediated transport restrict their ability to modulate intracellular inflammatory pathways (Pardridge 2023; Mahanta et al. 2025). In contrast, NIM platforms can integrate targeting ligands, protective cargo encapsulation, BBB-targeting strategies, and stimulus-responsive release within a single system (Kim and Lee 2024; Ralvenius et al. 2024). This flexibility enables potential co-delivery of anti-inflammatory agents, RNA therapeutics, or pathway inhibitors to specific CNS cell populations, including microglia and endothelial cells (Kim and Lee 2024; Mahmoudi et al. 2023). However, these advantages remain largely theoretical because most NIM platforms are still in preclinical development and face challenges in manufacturing reproducibility, batch consistency, protein corona formation, long-term CNS safety, and regulatory standardization (Gkogkos et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023c).

Conventional small molecules and selected monoclonal antibodies remain the most clinically mature approaches because of established manufacturing processes and regulatory frameworks (Gkogkos et al. 2023; Lei et al. 2024b). NIMs may provide greatest value where conventional strategies are insufficient, particularly for multi-target modulation, localized BBB-directed delivery, and stimulus-responsive regulation of interconnected inflammatory pathways such as cGAS–STING and NLRP3 (Ralvenius et al. 2024; Đorđević et al. 2022). Thus, nanomedicine is not intended to universally replace existing therapies but to overcome specific biological and pharmacological limitations of single-agent conventional approaches (Gkogkos et al. 2023; Clinical trial landscape, regulatory considerations, and barriers to translation

Despite mechanistic advances in NIM platforms, their clinical translation in AD and PD remains at an early stage (Gkogkos et al. 2023; Song et al. 2024). Most approaches discussed in this review should therefore be considered preclinical or proof-of-concept rather than established therapeutic modalities (Gkogkos et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025d). To date, no nanoparticle-based immunomodulatory therapy has reached late-stage clinical validation for neurodegenerative indications (Gkogkos et al. 2023; Sol et al. 2025).

However, several nanocarrier platforms have entered early clinical evaluation (Kochman et al. 2026; Zheng et al. 2022) In AD, the liposomal formulation APH-1105 (NCT03806478) is being evaluated for intranasal delivery in mild-to-moderate disease, while tau-targeting liposomal vaccines ACI-35.030 and JACI-35.054 (NCT04445831) have advanced to Phase 2 studies (Kochman et al. 2026; Andreadou et al. 2026). In PD, the catalytic gold nanocrystal formulation CNM Au8 completed a Phase 2 trial (NCT03815916, REPAIR PD), and the PEGylated glutathione liposome 2B3-101 (Talineuren, NCT04976127) is undergoing Phase 1 evaluation to enhance BBB delivery (Kochman et al. 2026).

Polymeric PLGA nanoparticles delivering anti-tau siRNA (NCT04294499) remain in early clinical development (Kochman et al. 2026; Zheng et al. 2022). Collectively, these studies demonstrate the feasibility of nanocarrier-based approaches but focus primarily on safety, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy rather than disease-modifying outcomes (Andreadou et al. 2026).

Exosome-based nanotherapies remain less advanced despite their potential as BBB-compatible carriers for RNA therapeutics (Huang et al. 2026b). Clinical studies of exosome platforms in neurodegenerative diseases are limited to early-phase safety and feasibility assessments, with no exosome-based NIM yet evaluated specifically for neuroinflammatory modulation in AD or PD (Gao et al. 2025b). This limited progression reflects persistent challenges in reproducibility, cargo loading, manufacturing scalability, and quality control (Gao et al. 2025b). Similarly, BBB-targeted nanobody and nanobody–drug conjugate systems remain predominantly preclinical despite their favorable targeting properties and smaller molecular size than conventional antibodies (Ma et al. 2025b; Regulatory translation of NIM platforms is further complicated by the lack of dedicated approval pathways for CNS-directed nanomedicines. Regulatory agencies, including the FDA and EMA, currently evaluate these products within existing drug, biologic, or combination-product frameworks, which may not fully accommodate complex multifunctional nanocarriers. Exosome-based products present additional classification challenges because of their biological origin and variable composition, complicating chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) requirements. Increasing emphasis is placed on standardized characterization of nanoparticle properties, including size distribution, surface charge, protein corona composition, and longitudinal biodistribution, although many academic NIM platforms have yet to generate these datasets.

Four major barriers currently limit clinical translation. First, human-relevant BBB targeting efficiency remains insufficiently demonstrated because rodent models may not accurately predict human CNS delivery. Second, reproducible GMP-compatible manufacturing remains challenging, particularly for complex systems such as exosomes and nanobody conjugates. Third, limited long-term data on CNS biocompatibility, degradation, and clearance complicate chronic safety assessment. Fourth, the high production costs and prolonged development timelines of multifunctional theranostic and precision-interceptor platforms may limit investment compared with established small molecules and monoclonal antibodies. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated efforts across nanomaterial engineering, regulatory science, and clinical development to establish standardized evaluation frameworks and translational pathways for CNS-directed nano-immunotherapies.

Critical appraisal of translational success and failure

Although NIMs have generated substantial mechanistic evidence in AD and PD, clinical translation remains limited by unresolved challenges in manufacturing reproducibility, CNS exposure, biodistribution control, and long-term safety. Many preclinical studies demonstrate target engagement in simplified cellular or rodent models, but these findings may not translate to human BBB physiology, aging-associated inflammatory environments, or chronic treatment conditions. Moreover, the structural complexity of multifunctional nanocarriers increases risks of batch variability, protein corona effects, and regulatory uncertainty, limiting near-term clinical advancement.

Among current platforms, intrinsically active systems such as polydopamine nanoparticles and selected biomimetic nanozymes may have relatively greater translational potential because of their simpler design, broader antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms, and reduced dependence on complex biological cargos. In contrast, exosome-based carriers, nanobody–drug conjugates, and gene-delivery nanoparticles offer greater biological specificity but face substantial challenges in manufacturing standardization, purification, cargo loading, and stability. Nanotheranostic systems and precision pathway interceptors remain promising but are less mature because of their complex architectures, increased production requirements, and regulatory challenges.

Several molecular targets also require careful therapeutic positioning because of their context-dependent functions. NF-κB is a key inflammatory regulator but also supports essential cellular homeostatic processes, making broad inhibition potentially harmful. Similarly, cGAS–STING and NLRP3 contribute to innate immune defense and cellular stress responses; therefore, complete and prolonged suppression may impair physiological immune functions. The CD33–TREM2 axis remains incompletely defined, as the optimal level of TREM2 activation needed to enhance phagocytosis without inducing maladaptive microglial responses is unknown. Likewise, although PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy is an attractive target, the relative benefits of direct pathway activation versus indirect mitochondrial protection require further clarification.

Approaches with limited success include nonspecific systemic anti-inflammatory therapies lacking CNS selectivity, single-target interventions that fail to address the interconnected nature of mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammasome activation, and microglial dysregulation, and nanoplatforms that produce transient molecular effects without sustained functional improvement. Several proof-of-concept nanoconstructs have also failed to advance because of insufficient delivery efficiency, inconsistent performance across disease models, or unresolved safety concerns related to long-term CNS retention and immune modulation.

Overall, successful translation will likely depend on balancing mechanistic sophistication with manufacturing feasibility, biological safety, and regulatory compatibility. Simpler, well-characterized, and functionally integrated systems may ultimately achieve greater clinical impact than highly complex multicomponent platforms unless advances in production, safety assessment, and regulatory frameworks progress in parallel. Summary and conclusion

Integrating mechanistic understanding of chronic neuroinflammation including microglial state plasticity, NLRP3 activation, cGAS–STING signaling, impaired mitophagy, CD33–TREM2 dysregulation, peripheral immune infiltration, and gut–brain axis alterations with advanced NIM platforms provides a framework for developing multi-target and stimulus-responsive neuroimmunotherapies. However, therapeutic strategies will require disease-specific optimization because AD and PD differ in pathological substrates, anatomical vulnerability, and inflammatory organization. Current NIM approaches, including direct immunomodulatory nanomaterials, targeted nanocarriers, nanotheranostics, precision pathway interceptors, and gene-delivery lipid nanoparticles, demonstrate mechanistic feasibility primarily in preclinical models, while clinical translation remains limited.

Future progress will depend on addressing key challenges, including precise modulation of microglial subtypes beyond the M1/M2 classification, context-dependent regulation of pathways such as NLRP3 and cGAS–STING, effective gut–CNS targeting strategies, and definition of long-term CNS safety profiles. A more accurate therapeutic framework should regulate microglial functional states across the continuum of homeostatic, disease-associated, inflammatory, and repair-associated phenotypes rather than promote a simplified M1-to-M2 transition.

Ultimately, next-generation NIM-based therapies will require patient-stratified and genotype-informed approaches that incorporate disease-associated factors, including variants affecting pathways such as TREM2 and CD33, alongside individual biological characteristics such as microbiome composition. Human-relevant models, including iPSC-derived systems, brain-on-a-chip platforms, and 3D bioprinted tissues, may improve translational prediction. Nevertheless, clinical advancement will depend on prioritizing nanoplatforms with robust manufacturing processes, reproducible biological performance, favorable safety profiles, and durable in vivo efficacy. Addressing these requirements will be essential for translating nano-immunomodulators from mechanistic concepts into clinically viable precision neuroimmunotherapies.
