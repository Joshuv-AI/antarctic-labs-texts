Experiences and challenges of caregivers of children with cerebral palsy in Magba subdivision, Cameroon: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Helen Lonn, Antor Odu Ndep, Bernadine Nsa Ekpenyong, Lynn Cockburn, Awa Jacques Chirac, Agho Tsangue Glory Tchiaze, Golda H M Jingkuo, Eucheria E Abua, Msughaondo Check Isho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCerebral palsy (CP) is one of the most common childhood neurodisability globally and disproportionately affects children in low- and middle-income countries. In Cameroon, limited epidemiological data, weak rehabilitation infrastructure, and entrenched sociocultural beliefs shape how CP is understood and managed. Children with CP often require lifelong support, placing substantial physical, emotional, and economic demands on family-caregivers, most commonly mothers. Understanding caregivers’ lived experiences within specific cultural and resource-limited contexts is critical for informing inclusive and effective interventions. This study explored the lived experiences and challenges of family-caregivers of children with CP in Magba Subdivision, West Region of Cameroon.MethodThis study employed a qualitative exploratory design using in-depth interviews and inductive content analysis. Participants were family caregivers of children with CP, purposively recruited through community-based rehabilitation (CBR) services. In-depth, face-to-face interviews were conducted in English or local languages, audio-recorded, transcribed, and translated. Data were analysed using inductive content analysis following Elo and Kyngäs’ approach. Findings were interpreted using Raina et al.’s multidimensional caregiving model.ResultsAll participants, aged 15–49 years, were family caregivers of children with CP, aged 4–15 years. Six interrelated themes emerged: (1) sociocultural challenges, including stigma, discrimination, and harmful spiritual beliefs framing CP as witchcraft, ancestral punishment, ‘snake’, or ‘marine spirit’; (2) economic constraints arising from inability to engage in paid work and the absence of social protections; (3) physical caregiving burden characterised by exhaustion, chronic pain, and musculoskeletal strain; (4) inadequate specialized services and health information; (5) limited social/family support; and (6) limited access rehabilitation services. These challenges intensified caregiver isolation and emotional distress.ConclusionCaregiving for children with CP in Magba is shaped by intersecting sociocultural, economic, and systemic factors that extend beyond individual coping capacity. Strengthening culturally sensitive community-based rehabilitation, improving access to early diagnosis and rehabilitation, and implementing disability- and gender-responsive social protection policies are essential to reduce caregiver burden and promote inclusive child and family wellbeing in Cameroon.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Caregiver burden, Sociocultural beliefs, Community-based rehabilitation

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is an umbrella term describing a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture, caused by non-progressive interference in the developing brain. It is widely recognized as the most prevalent disability in the paediatric population and the leading cause of motor impairment among children globally [1, 2]. Risk factors for CP may occur before, during, or after pregnancy and birth, and include, but are not limited to, birth asphyxia, intracranial haemorrhage, hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), intrauterine hypoxia, premature rupture of membranes (PROM), placental abnormalities, preterm or post-term birth, prolonged labour, neonatal infections, and respiratory distress syndrome [3, 4]. Globally, CP affects males more frequently than females and is disproportionately prevalent in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [5, 6]. Over the past 15 years, CP prevalence in high-income countries (HICs) has decreased by 40%, from 2.1 to 1.6 cases per 1000 live births. Conversely, expanding registry data indicates a high prevalence in LMICs at 3.4 per 1000 live births [4].

In Cameroon, reliable national data on the incidence and prevalence of CP are scarce. However, available hospital-based studies and disability-focused organisations suggest that CP represents one of the leading causes of childhood neurodisability in the country [7, 8]. The burden of disability is exacerbated by a complex range of social influences, nested environmental interactions, and cultural narratives surrounding illness and health. Social integration and participation of children with CP and their families are affected by cultural perceptions of CP. In rural and semi-rural settings such as the Magba Subdivision in the West Region of Cameroon, limited access to specialised health services, delayed diagnosis, and sociocultural interpretations of disability, including spiritual or traditional beliefs, significantly shape caregiving experiences [9, 10]. CP causes permanent impairments in posture, movement, and functional independence, limiting children’s ability to perform activities of daily living such as feeding, bathing, dressing, and mobility. Motor impairments are often accompanied by challenges in cognition, communication, perception, behaviour, seizures, and musculoskeletal complications [11, 12].

Due to these functional limitations and participation restrictions, many children with CP are dependent, sometimes entirely, on family caregivers for long-term care, often far exceeding the caregiving demands associated with typically developing children [13, 14]. Family caregivers, predominantly mothers, bear the social burden of blame and shame, further compounded by access to therapy and assistive devices [15]. For these caregivers, this prolonged responsibility can be physically, emotionally, and economically burdensome, negatively affecting their health and well-being [16, 17]. These challenges are often amplified in resource-limited settings like rural Cameroon, where health infrastructure, social protection systems, and disability-inclusive services remain underdeveloped [18, 19]. Understanding the experiences of family caregivers raising children with CP and their cultural complexities is crucial within these resource-limited contexts, where research on this topic remains limited despite rising prevalence and unique contextual factors. A growing body of African research demonstrates the unique cultural, socioeconomic, and services-access challenges faced when caring for a child with CP in diverse African contexts [20–22].

This study was therefore designed to explore the lived experiences and challenges of 12 family caregivers of children with CP in Magba Subdivision, West Region of Cameroon. Understanding these context-specific challenges is critical for informing culturally relevant interventions, strengthening caregiver support systems, and guiding disability-inclusive health and social policies in Cameroon.

Methods

Study design

This study was informed by a phenomenological research approach, which guided the exploration of caregivers’ lived experiences of raising children with CP. Phenomenology is particularly suited for understanding how individuals understand, interpret, and assign meaning to their experiences within specific social, cultural, and emotional contexts [23]. This approach acknowledges that caregiving experiences are deeply personal, shaped by caregivers’ relationships with their children, community/social expectations, and available support systems [24, 25].

While a phenomenology approach informed the study’s epistemological stance and framing of the research questions [24, 26], an inductive content analysis was employed as the method for data analysis [27]. Caregiving is not a uniform process; rather, it is uniquely experienced and interpreted by each individual [28, 29]. The chosen design, therefore, allowed for both depth of meaning and structured interpretation of patterns across participants. The study also drew on the multidimensional caregiving model proposed by Raina and colleagues [30], focusing on three core domains: background/context, caregivers’ strain, and coping/supportive factors. This framework supported the interpretation of findings by situating caregivers’ lived experiences within a broader personal, social, and systemic influences.

Setting

The study was conducted in Magba Subdivision, located in the Noun Division of the West Region of Cameroon. Recruitment took place through community-based rehabilitation services that serve as a referral point for surrounding rural communities. CBR services provide basic rehabilitation and follow-up care for children with neurodevelopmental disabilities, including CP. The population in Magba Subdivision is predominantly engaged in subsistence farming and small-scale trading and commerce. Christianity and Islam coexist alongside strong traditional belief systems, including ancestral worship and spiritual interpretations of disability (Lonn et al., 2026 – under review for publication). Such beliefs often influence how disability is perceived, explained, and managed within families and communities. The first author, a public health professional with experience in child rehabilitation in the region, was familiar with the local context but had no prior relationship with study participants.

Participants

Participants were family caregivers of children diagnosed with CP living in the study area. Participants were eligible if they lived with and provided daily care for a child under 18 years of age who had been clinically diagnosed with CP by a qualified medical doctor or a community-based rehabilitation (CBR) worker, with confirmation documented in medical records.

Sampling

Purposive sampling was used to identify family caregivers who could provide relevant accounts of caring for a child with CP. Caregivers were eligible if they lived with and provided daily care for a child under 18 years of age who had confirmed diagnosis of CP. Sampling continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes emerge from successive interviews [31, 32].

Recruitment

Participants were recruited through routine physiotherapy and medical follow-up provided by the CBR services in Magba subdivision. During scheduled outreach visits, a physiotherapist identified potential eligible caregivers based on the child’s diagnosis recorded in the medical files, as all children identified and diagnosed with CP are registered with the CBR services in the region. Registration included documentation of the child’s diagnosis, age, sex, clinical state, type of CP, and possible rehabilitation plan. Eligible caregivers were then approached in person by the member of the research team, who explained the study aims, procedures, and voluntary nature of participation. The study aims were explained to eligible participants, including voluntary nature of participation and that declining to participate would not affect their child’s care. Consent was obtained through signature or thumbprint prior to data collection.

Data collection

Demographic information was collected from all participants, including caregiver age, educational level, marital status, occupation, and child’s age. These characteristics provided contextual depth for interpreting caregiving experiences.

An interview guide was developed by the lead researcher (HL) based on the study aims, existing and prior field experience in CBR. This document guided the interviews with flexibility for probing and follow-up questions based on participants’ responses [33]. Interviews were conducted by two persons: the lead researcher (HL), a public health professional with prior experiences in CBR and qualitative research in Magba Subdivision, and a Research Assistant (NV), a CBR field worker, who had received training in qualitative interviewing techniques prior to data collection. HL and NV are fluent in English and French and familiar with local sociocultural context, which facilitated cultural sensitivity and engagement with participants. An interpreter was employed in cases of a complete interview in the local language.

Interviews were conducted in English, French or the local language (Bamoun) commonly spoken in the area, depending on participants’ preferences. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants consent and lasted between 15 and 30 min. The relatively short duration reflected practical considerations including participants’ caregiving responsibilities and the need to minimize participant burden. Caregivers had lived with the caregiving roles for several years, were therefore able to articulate rich, focused narratives within shorter timeframes. Interviews were guided by open-ended, experience-focused questions and supported by probing questions to draw out meaning, emotions, and interpretations. Field notes were taken to document non-verbal clues, expressions, and contextual observations. Interviews conducted in local languages were transcribed verbatim and translated to English, with translations cross-checked by a bilingual expert to ensure accuracy and preservation of meaning. Data saturation was obtained within this interview duration with no new themes emerging in later interviews.

Data analysis

Transcripts were analysed using a combination of inductive and deductive context analysis following the approach described by Elo and Kyngäs [27]. Content analysis is a systematic technique for compressing many words of text into fewer content categories based on explicit process [27]. Inductively, transcripts were anonymised using participant codes (e.g. M1, M2). The research team read the transcripts multiple times to familiarise themselves with the data and compared transcripts with audio recordings for accuracy.

We generated codes inductively by highlighting key ideas in the data. These codes were then copied and organized into subcategories according to their relationships. Subcategories with similar meanings were further aggregated into broader categories. Throughout the analysis, emerging categories were continuously compared with all interview transcripts and accompanying fieldnotes to ensure that findings were grounded in the complete body of data and accurately represented caregivers’ experiences.

A deductive analytic approach was applied using the multidimensional caregiving model proposed by Raina et al. [27]. The emerging themes were mapped onto the model’s core domains namely; background and context, caregiver strain, and coping or supportive factors, to support the interpretation and organization of findings.

Background and context

This domain captures the broader sociocultural and economic characteristics of the caregiving setting.

Sociocultural challenges

Family caregivers, predominantly mothers, reported widespread stigma and discrimination within their communities. Many described being blamed, isolated, or subjected to demeaning comments rooted in cultural beliefs that associated disability with curses, ancestral punishment, witchcraft, or spiritual causes. Some caregivers avoided important social gatherings such as funerals, weddings, and church activities due to fear of ridicule and emotional distress.

Several caregivers reported that community members referred to their children as a “snake” or “marine spirit,” leading them to seek confirmation or treatment from traditionalists. One mother recounted how her family pressured her to “return” her four-year-old child, who they believed to be a “snake”, to the river. “Whenever I go out with my child, people use negative language on me such as ‘mother of snake ' or ‘mother of marine spirit’ which I do not like (M2: 28 years).”

Another mother recounted how her daughter was labelled ‘Evil’ or ‘Witch’ and therefore should be left in the forest, “The community labelled my child as evil and suggested that I should abandon my daughter in the forest. My family members claimed it was a punishment for failing to perform traditional rites to appease the ghost of my deceased mother”, she recounted (M3: 38 years).

Another mother narrated her distressing experience rooted in cultural beliefs surrounding CP and marine spirits. She recounted, “Upon discovering that my child exhibited significant physical disabilities, precisely the inability to talk, drooling, and weakness in his limbs that prevented him from walking for five years, my family members suggested that the child was a manifestation of a marine spirit. According to prevailing cultural and family norms, I was instructed by family members to place him by the riverbank, so as to facilitate his return to his marine world” (M6: aged 39 years).” These beliefs often undermined caregivers’ confidence and contributed to emotional suffering.

While there is widespread social stigma and discrimination within the communities, some of the family caregivers also experienced internalized, self-imposed stigma following their cultural and social narratives. ““I just think this is witchcraft because I am surprised why my child will be like this”. (M9: 35 years). One mother recounted how family members made her understand that she had a strange child which they did not want in the family lineage. She said she had to believe the family history narrated to her. “I strongly believe that it is the marine spirit that possessed my child” (M5: 29 years).

Economic constraints of caregiving

Some of the mothers lacked sufficient support for child care and were unable to engage in formal employment outside the home to sustain personal livelihoods. Absence of reliable caregiving prevented participation in community programs or services. Physical caregiving demands, such as transporting the child imposed time and energy costs that reduced time available for income-generating work. This economic constraint was further complicated by the caregiver’s responsibilities for other children. “I don’t have anyone to assist me take care of the child. I cannot also find work out of home to make a living for myself. I cannot even go out for any program because there is no one to take care of her. Even if I have to go to the farm, I will need to carry her and I can only do something when she is asleep. Sometimes, even carrying her to the farm is so challenging because I have another child that is still small, and I cannot carry the two of them alone. You see, it is very difficult for me” (M11: 40 years). The combined caregiving load imposed considerable constraints on mobility, employment opportunities, and participation in external activities, contributing to ongoing distress. The absence of consistent social protection or disability-specific financial support further compounded these challenges.

Caregiver strain factors

Physical care burden

Participants described caregiving as physically exhausting, particularly tasks involving lifting, bathing, and transporting their children. As children grew older and heavier, caregivers experienced chronic pain, fatigue, and musculoskeletal problems. Some relied on pain medication to cope with daily physical demands. One caregiver clearly articulated, “I usually feel tired due to carrying him always. It is a problem lifting him. I sometimes get some body pain due to carrying him because he will not sit, so I do all the time I am carrying him. I pray he can walk someday. If not, he will be a burden while growing up because I cannot carry him” [13].

Inadequate specialized services and health information

Many caregivers reported receiving limited information from health professionals regarding CP, its causes, prognosis, and management. Some also reported receiving information from health professionals in ways that were not easily understood, and often did not corporate local beliefs about CP. This information gap contributed to confusion, fear, and susceptibility to harmful cultural explanations. Some caregivers sought information independently through the community meetings. The Magba subdivision lacked dedicated services and the tools needed to diagnose CP in children. Participants reported that many healthcare providers didn’t seem equipped to recognize CP early in a child’s development. As a result, some children underwent procedures that were not helpful or were sent for tests and treatments that would not change their situation.

One mother narrated, “my baby seemed weak and couldn’t hold up his head at four months, I consulted, yet I was being sent to different clinics with varying explanations. At first, I was told that the child was just weak and would improve with time. But as the child’s condition didn’t change, I received another diagnosis, epilepsy, because seizures appeared. I visited multiple facilities with inconsistent diagnoses. It wasn’t until a final doctor explained that my child had CP, a lifelong condition, and that the focus would shift to rehabilitation, an acknowledgement that brought a deep, painful realization about the costs and the ongoing impact on my family.” (M5:32 years).

Coping and supportive factors

Limited social and family support

Most caregivers reported minimal support from spouses, extended family, or others in their community. Some fathers were described as emotionally or practically absent, leaving mothers to shoulder caregiving responsibilities alone. A mother narrated, “My husband blamed me for not adhering to the midnight ritual of abandoning my child in the forest. Consequently, he labelled the care of our child as solely my responsibility. Accepting this challenge, I took on the role of primary caregiver, frontline warrior, and advocate for my son with limited or no support” (M7: 26 years). This limited social and family support heightened caregivers’ emotional stress and anxiety about the future.

Limited access to rehabilitation services

This theme, regarding limited access to rehabilitation, is not only about whether services exist but also about whether families could reach them. Mothers reported that access to the limited rehabilitation services was hindered by poor transportation infrastructure and the associated costs of travel to hospital facilities. The burden was compounded by the child’s weight and spasticity, which made manual handling and transport physically challenging. In some instances, families lacked the financial resources to cover transportation expenses, further limiting timely access to necessary care. “My daughter is already tall and very heavy, making it difficult for me to carry her on my back. The only available means of transportation with her is a motorcycle, as it becomes difficult to place her on a bicycle for long distances. It gets worse during the rainy seasons when roads are really bad and transportation costs are increased.” (M1: 42 years).

Discussion

This study highlights the multidimensional challenges faced by caregivers of children with CP in Magba Subdivision, Cameroon. Caregivers’ experiences were shaped by sociocultural stigma, compounded by economic hardship, physical strain, and limited access to rehabilitation services. These findings align with existing literature from other low-resource settings and underscore the urgent need for individual, community, and national-level interventions [19, 35, 36].

At the individual level, caring for a child with CP in Magba Subdivision is characterized by intense physical, emotional, and economic strain, borne almost exclusively by mothers. The predominant roles that women take in caregiving highlights deeply-rooted gender norms that frame disability as primarily a maternal responsibility [37–39]. Many caregivers were within the economically productive age range (15–49), yet caregiving demands restricted their abilities to pursue education, employment, and income-generating activities, reinforcing cycles of poverty and dependence.

Physical caregiving burden emerged as a major challenge. Mothers reported pain, fatigue, and musculoskeletal problems resulting from manual lifting, bathing, dressing, and transporting children as they grew older and heavier. This is consistent with existing African literature [13, 40–42]. These findings indicate that caregivers’ health is both a casualty and a determinant of child wellbeing, yet remains largely overlooked in clinical rehabilitation services in African settings [43].

Psychological distress, discrimination by family and community members, and internalized stigma further compounded an individual’s burden. Exposure to persistent stigma and cultural narratives portraying children with CP as ‘spirits’ or the result of curses led some mothers to internalize blame and question the legitimacy of their child’s humanity or their own moral standing. Such internalized stigma undermines coping capacity, increases emotional isolation, and may delay care-seeking, aligning with studies that highlight internalized stigma as a barrier to timely intervention [44, 45].

Limited access to accurate health information left caregivers confused and vulnerable to harmful interpretations. Delayed diagnosis, inconsistent medical explanations, and lack of counselling intensified uncertainty and fear. When caregivers eventually received a CP diagnosis, it often came without adequate guidance on prognosis, rehabilitation pathways, or long-term planning, increasing anxiety about the future. Findings from other African settings report that caregivers perceived poor communication and insufficient information from health systems, alongside poor referral pathways which can undermine caregivers’ engagement [14, 36, 46].

At the community level, caregivers’ experiences were shaped more by collectively enforced norms that define how disability is interpreted and managed. Consistent with Fonzi et al. [35], caregivers in this study describe pervasive stigma rooted in cultural and spiritual interpretations of disability, with mothers bearing disproportionate blame and social isolation. However, while Fonzi et al. primarily emphasized stigma as an interpersonal and emotional burden, this study highlights how such beliefs are collectively enforced within the community, actively influencing caregiver practices through social pressure, social isolation, and calls for ritual responses. Similarly. Loutou et al. [47], highlighted structural barriers to care and emotional burden of caregiving of children with CP, which aligns with caregivers’ accounts of economic hardship and fragmented services in this study. This study emphasizes the interaction between social stigma and weak health and educational systems, showing how cultural beliefs not only coexist with systemic gaps but exacerbate caregivers’ marginalization, and constrain their engagement with formal services [35, 47]. Children with CP were frequently labelled as ‘snakes,’ ‘marine spirits.’ evil,’ or ‘witch,’ framing disability as a spiritual anomaly rather than a medical condition. These beliefs justified extreme social responses, including calls for ritual abandonment, social exclusion, and moral condemnation of mothers. Community stigma significantly restricted caregivers’ social participation. Many mothers avoided public spaces, religious gatherings, and community events due to fear of ridicule or verbal abuse. This social withdrawal reduced access to informal support networks and reinforced isolation. Stigma was not limited to the wider community but often originated within the families, where elders or relatives exerted pressure on mothers to comply with traditional practices. Gender dynamics further intensified community-level challenges. Fathers were frequently absent or disengaged, particularly when mothers resisted ritual abandonment of the child. This withdrawal left mothers socially and economically exposed, reinforcing the perception that caregiving and its consequences was solely the mother’s burden. Informal support systems which are typically central to caregiving in African contexts, were therefore weakened or rendered inaccessible by stigma and fear. Community silence, avoidance, or tacit endorsement of harmful practices perpetuated caregivers’ vulnerability.

At the national level, the findings reflect systemic gaps in health, rehabilitation, and social protection systems that exacerbate caregiver burden similar to what was reported by Name in South Africa [18]. The absence of early diagnosis capacity for CP in primary and district health facilities resulted in delayed and fragmented care pathways. Many caregivers reported visiting multiple facilities with inconsistent diagnoses before receiving confirmation of CP, often after several months or years. The lack of specialized services and trained personnel in Magba Subdivision reflects broader inequalities in Cameroon’s health system, where neurodevelopmental disabilities receive limited attention [43] Inappropriate investigations and treatments not only placed financial strain on families but also delayed access to meaningful rehabilitation services. Access to rehabilitation was further constrained by poor transportation infrastructure, seasonal road conditions, and high travel costs. For children with spasticity and limited mobility, travel itself became a barrier to care, particularly in rural settings reliant on motorcycles. These structural barriers effectively exclude children from continuous care and rehabilitation. The absence of disability-specific social protection policies left families without financial relief or caregiving support. Caregiving labour, largely unpaid and invisible, remains unrecognized within national welfare frameworks. This policy gap disproportionately affects mothers and perpetuates intergenerational poverty.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study provides contextually grounded insights into the lived experiences of caregivers of children with CP in a rural Cameroonian setting, where evidence remains scarce. The qualitative design enhanced in-depth exploration of the intersections between sociocultural, personal, and economic realities, with health system constraints. The application of a multidisciplinary caregiving model proposed by Raina et al., () represents a novel contribution in this context. While the model has been widely used in HICs, its use here extends its applicability and highlights both its relevance and its limitations in low-resource culturally complex environments.

Limitations

A major limitation of this study is the relatively short duration of interviews, which may have constrained the depth of exploration of complex caregiving experiences. While interviews were focused and yielded rich accounts, longer or repeated interviews may have yielded deeper exploration of sensitive topics such as cultural interpretations of disability and family dynamics. Future research should consider extended and longitudinal qualitative approaches to capture the complex and evolving nature of caregiving experiences over time.

There is also potential for social desirability bias as participants may have moderated their responses regarding culturally sensitive beliefs or practices, given that the interviewers had a healthcare background; the use of peer researchers might yield different insights.

The process of translating interviews from local languages into English may have resulted in subtle loss of meaning, especially for culturally embedded expressions. Although translations were crosschecked, some nuances may not have been fully preserved.

Though Raina et al.[30], provided a useful structure for organizing findings, certain dimensions of caregiving in this context, particularly spiritual interpretations of disability and collective family dynamics, were not fully captured within this domain. This gap required interpretive flexibility and suggests that the model may benefit from adaptation when applied in similar settings.

The lead researcher’s background as a public health professional with experience in CBR may have influenced both data collection and interpretation. Familiarity with the local context facilitated engagement and contextual understanding but also may have shaped how participants framed their responses and how data were interpreted. Efforts were made to minimise bias through the use of open-ended questions, probing, reflexive note-taking, and team-based discussions during analysis.

Conclusion

This study highlights the profound and intersecting burdens experienced by family caregivers of children with CP in Cameroon, shaped by entrenched sociocultural beliefs, economic constraints, physical strain, and limited access to accurate information and rehabilitation services. Although the findings are context-specific, they provide important insights for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers seeking to develop locally grounded, culturally responsive support systems for families affected by CP in Cameroon.
