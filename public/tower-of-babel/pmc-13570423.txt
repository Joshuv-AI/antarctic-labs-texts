Latent Classes of Inflammation–Nutrition–Metabolism Status in Maintenance Hemodialysis: Associations with Arteriovenous Fistula Function and Cardiovascular Prognosis
Authors: Shanshan Sun, Xiaoyan Yu, Ying Shi, Ruinuo Hu, Shuangyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify heterogeneous subgroups of maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients by integrating inflammatory, nutritional, and metabolic indicators using latent class analysis (LCA), and to explore the associations of different subgroups with arteriovenous fistula (AVF) function and cardiovascular prognosis.MethodsThis single-center cohort study enrolled 180 MHD patients from January 2022 to December 2025. Baseline data on inflammatory (hs-CRP, NLR, PLR), nutritional (albumin, prealbumin, BMI), and metabolic (calcium, phosphorus, iPTH) indicators were collected. LCA was used to identify patient subgroups. A 1-year prospective follow-up recorded composite endpoint events (AVF dysfunction, major adverse cardiovascular events, all-cause death). Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox regression identified prognostic differences. A nomogram was constructed using LASSO regression.ResultsThree subgroups were identified: high-inflammation and low-nutrition type (26.1%), metabolism-dominant type (40.0%), and relatively stable type (33.9%) (entropy=0.84). The high-inflammation and low-nutrition type showed the lowest AVF blood flow and the highest venous pressure (P<0.001). At 1-year follow-up, this type had the highest incidence of composite endpoint events (46.8%) and all-cause mortality (23.4%) (adjusted HR=3.82, 95% CI: 1.56–9.34, P=0.003). The nomogram showed good discrimination (C-index=0.78, 95% CI: 0.72–0.84) and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P=0.31).ConclusionMHD patients exhibit three distinct subgroups based on inflammation–nutrition–metabolism status. The high-inflammation and low-nutrition type is independently associated with worse AVF function and poorer cardiovascular prognosis. The LCA-based nomogram may assist in precision risk stratification.

Introduction

Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients commonly experience three interrelated pathological conditions: chronic inflammation, malnutrition, and metabolic disorders. These conditions interact to form a complex network that substantially increases the risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality.1 Malnutrition affects 18–75% of dialysis patients and is closely linked to protein-energy wasting (PEW);2 cardiovascular mortality in this population is 10–20 times higher than in the general population.3 Meanwhile, as the “lifeline” of MHD patients, arteriovenous fistula (AVF) dysfunction occurs in 20–50% of cases.4 A nationwide cohort study in Japan demonstrated that AVF use was associated with the lowest risk of all-cause mortality compared with other vascular access types, reinforcing AVF as the preferred access for MHD patients.5 The pathogenesis of AVF dysfunction involves multiple factors, including chronic inflammation and CKD–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD).6 Studies have shown that C-reactive protein, parathyroid hormone, and albumin are independently associated with AVF dysfunction.7

However, most previous studies have analyzed these indicators as independent factors, overlooking the complex interplay among inflammatory, nutritional, and metabolic systems and the heterogeneous nature of the MHD population. Latent class analysis (LCA) has been increasingly applied in nephrology research to identify clinically meaningful patient subgroups.8–10 For instance, LCA has been used to classify depressive symptoms8 and intrinsic capacity9 among MHD patients, revealing distinct heterogeneity within this population. Nevertheless, studies employing LCA to integrate multidimensional inflammation–nutrition– metabolism indicators and to examine their associations with AVF function and cardiovascular prognosis remain scarce.

The variables selected for LCA in this study—hs-CRP, NLR, and PLR for inflammation; albumin, prealbumin, and BMI for nutrition; and calcium, phosphorus, and iPTH for metabolism—were chosen based on their established roles as clinically accessible and prognostically relevant markers in the hemodialysis population.1,3,6,7 These indicators are routinely measured in clinical practice, facilitating potential translation of the LCA findings into bedside application.

Therefore, this study hypothesized that MHD patients could be classified into distinct subgroups based on their integrated inflammation–nutrition–metabolism profile, and that these subgroups would differ significantly in AVF function and cardiovascular outcomes. To test this hypothesis, we applied LCA to integrate multidimensional indicators from the inflammatory, nutritional, and metabolic systems to identify heterogeneous subgroups of MHD patients. Through prospective follow-up, we examined the differences in AVF function and cardiovascular prognosis among latent classes, and constructed a risk stratification nomogram to support precision risk assessment and individualized management.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a two-phase design: the first phase was a retrospective cross-sectional baseline assessment, and the second phase was a prospective cohort follow-up. In the first phase, baseline clinical data, laboratory indicators, and arteriovenous fistula function parameters of patients who received maintenance hemodialysis at our center from January 2022 to December 2025 were retrospectively collected for latent class analysis. In the second phase, patients were prospectively followed from the date of baseline data collection (the most recent assessment within each patient’s routine dialysis care) through December 2026 to observe clinical outcomes. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University (approval number: 2026-K029). This study was designed as a retrospective analysis of routinely collected clinical data (2022–2025) combined with prospective follow-up (2025–2026); the Ethics Committee provided retrospective approval for the overall study protocol in 2026. All patients provided written informed consent. The reporting of this study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies.11

Study Population

Sample Source

A total of 180 patients who received maintenance hemodialysis at the hemodialysis center of our hospital between January 2022 and December 2025 were enrolled as study subjects.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥18 years and ≤80 years; (2) regular hemodialysis for ≥3 months; (3) dialysis frequency of 2–3 times per week, 4 hours per session; (4) use of a native arteriovenous fistula as vascular access; (5) complete clinical data including measurements of inflammatory, nutritional, and metabolic indicators; (6) completion of color Doppler ultrasound assessment of arteriovenous fistula function at baseline; (7) signed informed consent and voluntary participation.

Exclusion criteria: (1) acute kidney injury or dialysis duration <3 months; (2) use of a central venous catheter or synthetic graft as vascular access; (3) concomitant acute infection, active malignancy, severe liver dysfunction (Child-Pugh class C); (4) major cardiovascular events (acute myocardial infarction, stroke) within the previous 3 months; (5) autoimmune diseases or long-term use of immunosuppressants; (6) pregnancy or lactation; (7) expected survival <1 year or planned kidney transplantation; (8) >30% missing data for key indicators.

Criteria for Discontinuation and Dropout

(1) lost to follow-up or voluntary withdrawal during follow-up; (2) transferred to another institution for dialysis and could not be followed further; (3) underwent kidney transplantation; (4) change of vascular access type for reasons not related to the study; (5) found to have violated inclusion criteria or met exclusion criteria.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was calculated considering both latent class analysis and survival analysis requirements. According to the rule of thumb for latent class analysis, each categorical variable requires 10–20 cases; with 9 indicators and 15 cases per indicator, at least 135 cases were needed. Based on our center’s previous data and literature,9 the incidence of composite endpoint events in the high-risk and low-risk groups was approximately 45% and 12%, respectively. Setting α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, Freedman’s formula gave a required sample size of 167 cases. For the development of the nomogram prediction model, the events-per-variable (EPV) criterion was considered; with approximately 50 composite endpoint events anticipated and 5–7 candidate predictors, the EPV of 7–10 met the minimum recommended threshold for reliable logistic regression model development. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was determined to be 180 cases.

Baseline Data Collection

General Clinical Data

General clinical data were collected through the electronic medical record system and dialysis records, including demographic characteristics (sex, age, ethnicity, education level), dialysis-related information (dialysis vintage (months), dialysis frequency, dry weight (kg), dialysis adequacy index Kt/V), primary kidney disease (diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, chronic glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, etc), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, heart failure, etc), and medications (erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, iron supplements, antihypertensives, glucose-lowering drugs, antiplatelet agents, etc).

Inflammatory Markers

Venous blood collected before dialysis was used for inflammatory marker measurements. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured by immunoturbidimetry using a Beckman AU5821 automatic biochemical analyzer. The normal reference range was 0–3 mg/L, with stratification: ≤3 mg/L = normal, 3–10 mg/L = mildly elevated, >10 mg/L = markedly elevated. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was automatically calculated from complete blood count performed on a BC6000 hematology analyzer as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count. The normal reference range was 0.78–3.53, and >5 was considered abnormally elevated. The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) was similarly calculated from the complete blood count. The normal reference range was <150, and >180 was considered abnormally elevated.

Nutritional Assessment

Nutritional status was assessed by serum albumin, prealbumin, and body mass index. Serum albumin (ALB) was measured using a Beckman AU5821 biochemical analyzer (normal range 35–55 g/L). Nutritional status was graded as: ≥40 g/L = normal, 35–40 g/L = mild malnutrition, 30–35 g/L = moderate malnutrition, <30 g/L = severe malnutrition. Prealbumin (PA) normal range was 200–400 mg/L, and <200 mg/L indicated malnutrition. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2) after measuring height (cm) and post-dialysis dry weight (kg). BMI categories followed the criteria for Chinese populations: <18.5 kg/m2 = underweight, 18.5–23.9 kg/m2 = normal, 24–27.9 kg/m2 = overweight, ≥28 kg/m2 = obese. The use of Chinese-specific BMI cutoffs (24 kg/m2 for overweight and 28 kg/m2 for obesity) rather than the WHO international standards (25 and 30 kg/m2) was based on evidence that the latter underestimates obesity-related risks in Asian populations.12

Metabolic Indicators

Metabolic parameters were measured in venous blood collected before dialysis under fasting conditions. Serum calcium (Ca) was measured using a Beckman AU5821 analyzer (normal range 2.11–2.52 mmol/L); according to KDIGO guidelines, the target range for dialysis patients was 2.10–2.37 mmol/L. Serum phosphorus (P) normal range was 0.85–1.51 mmol/L, with a target range of 0.81–1.45 mmol/L for dialysis patients. Intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH) was measured by electrochemiluminescence using an ACCESS immunoassay analyzer (normal range 15–65 pg/mL). According to KDOQI guidelines, the target range for dialysis patients was 150–300 pg/mL, with stratification: <150 pg/mL = too low, 150–300 pg/mL = at target, 300–600 pg/mL = mildly elevated, >600 pg/mL = markedly elevated. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) normal range was 3.9–6.1 mmol/L; the target range for diabetic patients was 4.4–7.0 mmol/L. The rationale for selecting the nine variables for latent class analysis was based on their established prognostic relevance in the hemodialysis population. hs-CRP, NLR, and PLR were chosen as inflammatory markers because they are routinely measured, cost-effective, and have been independently associated with cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in MHD patients. Albumin, prealbumin, and BMI represent core components of nutritional assessment; hypoalbuminemia and low BMI are well-recognized predictors of adverse outcomes in dialysis patients.12 Calcium, phosphorus, and iPTH are the primary biomarkers of CKD–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD), which has been linked to vascular calcification and cardiovascular mortality. These nine indicators are all obtained from routine clinical practice, facilitating potential translation of the LCA findings into bedside application.

Arteriovenous Fistula Function Assessment

Arteriovenous fistula function was assessed by color Doppler ultrasound performed before dialysis. Baseline ultrasound assessment was completed at the time of enrollment. During the follow-up period, all patients underwent standardized ultrasound surveillance every 3 months, consistent with KDOQI recommendations for regular assessment of AV access function. Access flow was calculated by measuring the vessel diameter (D, cm) and time-averaged velocity (TAV, cm/s) 2–3 cm above the anastomosis using the formula: Access flow = π × (D/2)2 × TAV × 60 (mL/min). A functional fistula was defined as access flow ≥500 mL/min; <500 mL/min indicated fistula dysfunction. The diameter of the arterialized venous segment (normal range 5–8 mm), peak systolic velocity (PSV), and end-diastolic velocity (EDV) were also recorded. Venous pressure was measured as the average of three consecutive dialysis sessions from the dialysis treatment records. The normal range was 120–180 mmHg, and >200 mmHg suggested venous stenosis.

Latent Class Analysis

Latent class analysis was performed using Mplus 8.3 software. Models with 2 to 6 classes were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation, with 500 random starts and 50 final optimizations. The optimal number of classes was determined by evaluating the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy (values >0.80 indicating good classification), Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR; P<0.05 supporting a k-class model over a k-1 class model), bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and class sample size (each class ≥5% of the total sample). Classification quality was assessed by posterior probabilities; an average posterior probability >0.85 indicated high classification accuracy. For latent class characterization, class-specific profile plots were first drawn. Subgroups were named based on (1) the distribution of standardized Z-scores of each indicator across classes, and (2) clinical pathophysiological meaning. Naming was performed independently by two researchers blinded to the data, and disagreements were adjudicated by a third senior expert. Differences in continuous variables among classes were compared using analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Prospective Follow-Up and Endpoint Events

Follow-up lasted 1 year from the date of baseline data collection, with follow-up visits scheduled at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. The baseline data collection date for each patient was defined as the date of the most recent routine laboratory assessment at enrollment; prospective follow-up commenced immediately thereafter and continued until December 2026. Patient status was verified by querying the electronic medical record system, dialysis center records, telephone follow-ups, and, when necessary, contacting the community health service center. For quality control, telephone follow-ups used a standardized questionnaire and were audio-recorded; 10% of recordings were randomly selected for verification. All endpoint events were adjudicated by two independent physicians according to predefined criteria; disagreements were resolved by discussion or third-party arbitration.

Endpoint definitions: The composite primary endpoint included the first occurrence of any of the following: arteriovenous fistula dysfunction, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), or all-cause death. This composite endpoint was chosen because AVF dysfunction, MACE, and all-cause death all represent terminal clinical manifestations of the dysregulated inflammation–nutrition–metabolism network, sharing common pathophysiological pathways including endothelial dysfunction, accelerated atherosclerosis, and a prothrombotic state. However, recognizing the clinical heterogeneity of these outcomes, we also performed separate secondary analyses for (1) AVF dysfunction alone and (2) MACE or all-cause mortality, as suggested.

Arteriovenous fistula dysfunction was defined as: (1) ultrasound-detected access flow <500 mL/min on at least two consecutive examinations; (2) need for endovascular intervention (balloon angioplasty, stenting); (3) need for surgical reconstruction of the fistula; (4) non-recanalizable fistula thrombosis requiring change of vascular access.

MACE was defined as: (1) acute myocardial infarction (according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction); (2) ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke (confirmed by CT or MRI of the brain); (3) cardiovascular death.

All-cause death was confirmed by triple verification: hospital death records, public security household registration system, or direct confirmation by family members. Secondary endpoints included hospitalization for heart failure, severe arrhythmias, and fistula-related complications (infection, pseudoaneurysm, steal syndrome, etc).

Follow-up data were recorded using a standardized form, including: time of endpoint event (accurate to the day), event type and diagnostic basis (laboratory tests, imaging findings, pathology reports, etc), treatment modality (medical therapy, interventional therapy, surgery), changes in inflammatory-nutritional-metabolic indicators during follow-up (every 6 months), dialysis adequacy (Kt/V), changes in residual kidney function, and new comorbidities or medication adjustments.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics: Normality of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared among groups using analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For continuous variables with missing data <30%, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed. The proportion of missingness for each key variable was as follows: hs-CRP 4.4% (8/180), NLR 3.3% (6/180), PLR 3.3% (6/180), albumin 2.2% (4/180), prealbumin 5.0% (9/180), BMI 1.7% (3/180), calcium 2.8% (5/180), phosphorus 2.8% (5/180), and iPTH 6.1% (11/180). Missing data were assumed to be missing at random. We generated 20 imputed datasets. Parameter estimates from the 20 imputed datasets were combined using Rubin’s rules13 to obtain pooled estimates and standard errors that account for the uncertainty due to missing data. Variables or individuals with ≥30% missing data were excluded.

Survival analysis: Kaplan-Meier curves were used to plot event-free survival for different latent classes, and differences were compared using the Log rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to analyze the effect of latent class on prognosis. Univariate analysis included latent class, age, sex, dialysis vintage, diabetes, hypertension, and coronary artery disease. Variables with P<0.10 were entered into a multivariate stepwise regression model to calculate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals.

Prediction model development and evaluation: Variable selection was performed using LASSO regression (10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal λ). The nomogram was constructed by integrating latent class typing with selected independent predictors using the rms package in R software. Model performance was assessed by: (1) discrimination: Harrell’s C-index (>0.75 considered good); (2) calibration: calibration curve and Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P>0.05); (3) clinical utility: decision curve analysis (DCA). Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates). External validation was not performed in this study due to the single-center design; this limitation is acknowledged and will be addressed in the Discussion section.

Results

Study Flow and Patient Disposition

A total of 216 patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis at our center between January 2022 and December 2025 were initially assessed for eligibility. Of these, 36 patients were excluded for the following reasons: 14 had a central venous catheter or synthetic graft as vascular access, 9 had acute infection or active malignancy, 7 had incomplete baseline data (>30% missing for key indicators), 4 were aged >80 years, and 2 had expected survival <1 year. The remaining 180 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. All 180 patients completed baseline assessment and latent class analysis. During the 1-year prospective follow-up, 8 patients (4.4%) were lost to follow-up, and 2 deaths (1.1%) could not be confirmed for cause and were treated as censored observations at the time of last contact, yielding a follow-up completion rate of 94.4%. Latent Class Model Fitting and Subgroup Identification

Nine indicators (hs-CRP, NLR, PLR, albumin, prealbumin, BMI, serum calcium, serum phosphorus, iPTH) were used as manifest variables to fit latent class models with 2 to 5 classes. Model fit indices, including AIC, BIC, aBIC, entropy, LMR, and BLRT, collectively supported the 3-class solution as the optimal model. For the 3-class model, the LMR test (P = 0.008) and BLRT (P < 0.001) significantly favored it over the 2-class model, whereas the 4-class model showed a non-significant LMR (P = 0.12) and a decline in entropy to 0.79, indicating overfitting. The entropy of the 3-class model was 0.84, and the average posterior probabilities for the three subgroups were 0.91, 0.88, and 0.93, respectively, indicating good classification quality.

Based on the indicator distribution of the 3-class model, the 180 patients were divided into three subgroups: Class 1 – High-inflammation and low-nutrition type (n = 47, 26.1%): this subgroup was characterized by markedly elevated inflammatory markers (hs-CRP and NLR) together with substantially reduced nutritional indicators (albumin, prealbumin, and BMI), representing a catabolic state. Class 2 – Metabolism-dominant type (n = 72, 40.0%): this subgroup showed prominent elevations in iPTH and serum phosphorus, with relatively preserved nutritional status, indicating a predominant mineral–bone disorder phenotype. Class 3 – Relatively stable type (n = 61, 33.9%): this subgroup maintained all nine indicators within near-normal ranges, representing the most physiologically balanced profile.

A profile plot illustrating the standardized Z-scores of the nine indicators across the three latent classes is presented in Figure 2. Prognostic Follow-Up Results

Among the 180 patients, 170 completed the 1-year follow-up (follow-up rate: 94.4%). The median follow-up duration was 356 days (IQR: 298–365).

Survival Analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significant difference in cumulative event-free survival among the three subgroups (log-rank χ2 = 18.67, P < 0.001). The high-inflammation and low-nutrition type had the poorest survival, with a median event-free survival of 312 days (95% CI: 268–356 days). The 1-year event-free survival rates for the metabolism-dominant and relatively stable types were 82.5% and 90.2%, respectively, with median survival not reached in either group (Figure 3). Comparison of Predictive Performance

Compared with a baseline model incorporating only traditional risk factors (age, diabetes, hypertension), the nomogram incorporating latent class typing showed a significantly higher C-index (0.78 vs 0.65, P = 0.008). The net reclassification improvement (NRI) was 0.32 (95% CI: 0.11–0.53, P = 0.003), and the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) was 0.09 (95% CI: 0.04–0.14, P < 0.001), indicating that latent class typing substantially enhanced the predictive capability beyond traditional risk factors.

Given the relatively small number of events in certain subgroup analyses, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and the model’s generalizability awaits external validation in larger, multi-center cohorts.

Discussion

Identification and Clinical Significance of Inflammation–Nutrition–Metabolism Latent Classes

The primary objective of this study was to apply latent class analysis to integrate multidimensional indicators of inflammation, nutrition, and metabolism, thereby identifying heterogeneous subgroups among maintenance hemodialysis patients. The three-class solution—comprising a high-inflammation and low-nutrition type (26.1%), a metabolism-dominant type (40.0%), and a relatively stable type (33.9%)—was supported by favorable model fit indices (entropy = 0.84; average posterior probabilities > 0.88), indicating robust classification.

This finding aligns with the growing body of evidence demonstrating the utility of data-driven approaches in capturing patient heterogeneity in the dialysis population. Chen et al employed LCA to classify depressive symptoms in 1090 Chinese hemodialysis patients, identifying three distinct depression subgroups and demonstrating that malnutrition and comorbidity burden were key determinants of class membership.14 Similarly, a recent LCA study of intrinsic capacity in 307 elderly MHD patients revealed three distinct phenotypes, with hs-CRP and parathyroid hormone identified as significant predictors of class assignment.15 Extending these applications, our study demonstrates that LCA can effectively integrate pathophysiological domains—inflammation, nutrition, and metabolism—to uncover biologically meaningful phenotypes that may inform clinical risk stratification.

The high-inflammation and low-nutrition type—characterized by elevated hs-CRP and NLR alongside reduced albumin, prealbumin, and BMI—represents a clinical manifestation of the malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome (MICS). This phenotype is consistent with the malnutrition-inflammation-atherosclerosis (MIA) syndrome paradigm, in which chronic inflammation and protein-energy wasting mutually reinforce each other through bidirectional pathways. A recent study by Tian et al16 demonstrated that the simultaneous presence of malnutrition, inflammation, and fluid overload was associated with the highest mortality risk in hemodialysis patients (HR = 8.91), underscoring the prognostic significance of this syndromic clustering. Beyond the dialysis population, albumin-based inflammatory indices—such as the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) and the CALLY index—have also been proposed as integrated markers of systemic inflammatory and metabolic burden. Ertekin et al demonstrated the clinical relevance of these indices in a prospective cohort of third-trimester pregnant women with dyspnea, where albumin-based inflammatory markers correlated with aortic elastic properties and provided prognostic information beyond individual biomarkers.17 These findings, although derived from a non-dialysis population, support the broader concept that combining albumin with inflammatory markers captures a systemic inflammatory–nutritional state that may transcend specific disease contexts.

The metabolism-dominant type (40.0%), distinguished by marked elevations in iPTH and serum phosphorus with relatively preserved nutritional status, suggests that CKD–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) may constitute a pathophysiological pathway partially independent of MICS. This observation is consistent with the concept that disordered mineral metabolism—through mechanisms including vascular calcification and direct inflammatory effects of phosphate and calciprotein particles—can contribute to adverse outcomes even in the absence of overt malnutrition.18

Baseline Arteriovenous Fistula Function Across Latent Classes

Arteriovenous fistula blood flow differed substantially across the three subgroups, with the highest values observed in the relatively stable type, intermediate values in the metabolism-dominant type, and the lowest values in the high-inflammation and low-nutrition type. Venous pressure showed the opposite gradient. These differences are consistent with the distinct pathophysiological profiles of each subgroup.

The association between the high-inflammation and low-nutrition phenotype and impaired AVF function may be explained by several mechanisms. Chronic inflammation is known to impair endothelial function and induce a prothrombotic state, which may interfere with adaptive remodeling of the fistula after creation. Previous studies have reported that each unit increase in CRP and erythrocyte sedimentation rate is associated with increased risk of fistula failure.19 Additionally, hypoalbuminemia has been linked to reduced vascular repair capacity and diminished local anti-infection capability, potentially accelerating fistula function loss. The mean hs-CRP level of 18.5 mg/L and albumin level of 31.5 g/L observed in this subgroup fall within ranges previously associated with adverse vascular access outcomes.20

In the metabolism-dominant type, the elevated serum phosphorus (2.15 mmol/L) and iPTH (500.4 pg/mL) suggest that CKD-MBD-related vascular calcification may contribute to impaired fistula function. Hyperphosphatemia can drive transdifferentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells toward an osteoblast-like phenotype, leading to medial calcification.21 Multivariate analyses have identified elevated phosphorus and iPTH as independent risk factors for AVF failure.22 The intermediate fistula blood flow in this subgroup—better than the high-inflammation and low-nutrition type but worse than the relatively stable type—suggests that metabolic disturbances may exert a more gradual or less severe impact on AVF function compared with the combined inflammatory and nutritional derangements.

Cardiovascular Prognosis Across Latent Classes

During the 1-year follow-up, the high-inflammation and low-nutrition type exhibited the highest incidence of composite endpoint events (46.8%) and all-cause mortality (23.4%), whereas the metabolism-dominant type (20.8% and 1.4%) and the relatively stable type (11.5% and 0%) showed progressively lower event rates. After adjustment for potential confounders, the high-inflammation and low-nutrition type remained independently associated with composite endpoint events (adjusted HR = 3.82, 95% CI: 1.56–9.34, P = 0.003).

This gradient of risk is consistent with the established prognostic significance of MICS in the dialysis population. The MIA syndrome describes a vicious cycle in which malnutrition, inflammation, and atherosclerosis mutually reinforce one another. Chronic inflammation can accelerate cardiovascular disease through multiple pathways—including endothelial activation, induction of a procoagulant state, promotion of monocyte-to-foam cell transformation, and acceleration of atherosclerotic plaque progression—while protein-energy wasting depletes muscle mass and reduces serum albumin, thereby impairing the body’s reparative capacity.23,24 The 23.4% 1-year mortality rate observed in the high-inflammation and low-nutrition type falls within the range reported in previous MICS studies, supporting the clinical relevance of this phenotype.

The intermediate prognosis of the metabolism-dominant type (20.8% composite events) suggests that CKD-MBD-related metabolic disturbances may confer cardiovascular risk that is appreciable but less severe than the combined burden of inflammation and malnutrition. This is consistent with studies demonstrating that hyperphosphatemia and elevated iPTH are associated with vascular calcification and cardiovascular events, but that the addition of inflammatory and nutritional derangements substantially amplifies risk.25 Notably, Hou et al recently demonstrated in a multicenter cohort of 795 incident hemodialysis patients that incorporating inflammatory (NLR, PLR) and nutritional (albumin) biomarkers into traditional risk models substantially improved long-term mortality risk stratification. Their finding that NLR exhibited the highest predictive accuracy across 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals further validates the selection of these markers in our LCA.

Age and diabetes history were also identified as independent predictors of composite endpoint events in multivariate analysis, consistent with their well-established roles as traditional cardiovascular risk factors in the CKD population.

Risk Prediction Model Based on Latent Class Typing

A nomogram incorporating six LASSO-selected predictors (latent class type, hs-CRP, NLR, albumin, iPTH, and diabetes history) was constructed to predict 1-year composite endpoint risk. The model demonstrated a Harrell’s C-index of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.72–0.84), with bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.76, indicating acceptable discrimination. Calibration was satisfactory (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.31), and decision curve analysis suggested net benefit across threshold probabilities of 15%–65%. Compared with a baseline model containing only traditional risk factors, the incorporation of latent class typing significantly improved predictive performance (C-index: 0.78 vs 0.65, P = 0.008; NRI: 0.32, 95% CI: 0.11–0.53; IDI: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.04–0.14).

These performance metrics are comparable to those reported in recent prediction models for dialysis patients. A systematic review by Gan et al examined nine cardiovascular event prediction models in dialysis patients and reported AUCs ranging from 0.70 to 0.88, with age, diabetes, CRP, and albumin emerging as the most common predictors.26 The predictors selected in our model are consistent with these findings. However, most existing models rely solely on traditional risk factors without accounting for patient heterogeneity.27 Our findings suggest that incorporating LCA-derived phenotypes may enhance risk stratification by capturing the syndromic clustering of inflammation, nutrition, and metabolism that is not adequately reflected by individual biomarkers.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, it is the first to systematically apply LCA to integrate multidimensional indicators of inflammation, nutrition, and metabolism in MHD patients, identifying three phenotypes with distinct pathophysiological characteristics. Second, the prospective follow-up design allowed us to examine the associations between these phenotypes and clinically meaningful outcomes, including AVF dysfunction, MACE, and all-cause mortality. Third, the construction of a nomogram incorporating LCA-derived typing provides preliminary evidence for a novel approach to risk stratification that may complement traditional prediction models.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size (n = 180), while adequate for LCA methodology and survival analysis, precludes independent external validation of the prediction model. Second, all data were derived from a single dialysis center, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability; the findings require validation in multicenter, geographically diverse cohorts. Third, AVF dysfunction was diagnosed based on ultrasound findings and clinical intervention records; some early functional impairments may not have been captured in a timely manner. Fourth, although multivariate analysis adjusted for measured confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Fifth, the follow-up duration of 1 year, while sufficient to observe differences in composite endpoints, may not capture longer-term outcomes. Finally, as an observational study, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the observed associations.

Conclusion

In this single-center cohort study, latent class analysis of inflammatory, nutritional, and metabolic indicators classified 180 maintenance hemodialysis patients into three subgroups with distinct clinical and pathophysiological profiles: a high-inflammation and low-nutrition type, a metabolism-dominant type, and a relatively stable type. The high-inflammation and low-nutrition type was associated with the poorest arteriovenous fistula function and the highest 1-year rates of composite endpoint events and all-cause mortality. After adjustment for potential confounders, this phenotype remained independently associated with an elevated risk of composite endpoint events (adjusted HR = 3.82, 95% CI: 1.56–9.34). A nomogram incorporating LCA-derived typing demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration in internal validation. These findings suggest that MHD patients exhibit meaningful heterogeneity based on the integrated inflammation–nutrition– metabolism network, and that LCA-derived phenotypes may assist in risk stratification. However, given the single-center design and relatively limited sample size, the clinical utility of the proposed model requires confirmation in larger, multi-center prospective cohorts before broader application.28 Future studies with extended follow-up and external validation are needed to further evaluate the generalizability and predictive performance of this approach.
