Virtual reality-assisted neuromuscular training effects on agility performance and injury prevention in basketball athletes: a controlled laboratory experiment
Authors: Liang Jiang, Yachao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To examine the effectiveness of virtual reality-assisted neuromuscular training interventions on agility performance and injury prevention indicators in basketball athletes compared to conventional training approaches. A 12-week controlled laboratory experiment was conducted with 70 university-level basketball athletes (age 20.95 ± 1.5 years) randomly allocated to VR-assisted neuromuscular training (n = 35) or conventional training control groups (n = 35). Comprehensive assessments included agility tests (T-test, 5-10-5 shuttle), functional movement screening, biomechanical analysis, and neuromuscular function parameters measured at baseline, mid-intervention, and post-intervention. The VR training group demonstrated 7–10% greater pre-to-post improvements in agility performance compared with the control group across multiple agility measures (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.89–1.34). Neuromuscular function parameters showed 15–30% greater pre-to-post improvements in the VR group relative to controls, including enhanced dynamic balance (pre-VR: 2.34 ± 0.41, post-VR: 1.67 ± 0.28; pre-control: 2.31 ± 0.38, post-control: 2.02 ± 0.35; between-group difference: − 0.35, 95% CI [− 0.52, − 0.18], effect size = 1.24, p < 0.001), improved joint position sense (23.4 vs. 11.8% improvement, 95% CI for difference [8.2%, 15.0%], p = 0.002), reduced dynamic knee valgus angles (18.7 vs. 8.2% reduction, 95% CI for difference [6.1%, 14.9%], p < 0.001), and increased reactive strength index (27.2 vs. 12.3% increase, 95% CI for difference [9.5%, 20.3%], p < 0.001). Movement quality scores and biomechanical injury risk markers consistently favored the VR intervention. Virtual reality-assisted neuromuscular training yields superior improvements in both agility performance and biomechanical injury risk markers compared to conventional training approaches in basketball athletes, supporting its integration into comprehensive athletic development programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48999-x.

Introduction

Basketball imposes extraordinary demands on agility and neuromuscular control. Cutting, jumping, and pivoting maneuvers load the musculoskeletal system in ways that link biomechanical capacity directly to both performance gains and injury susceptibility1–3. Epidemiological data indicate that lower extremity injuries account for roughly 60–70% of all basketball-related injuries, with ankle sprains and anterior cruciate ligament tears ranking among the most consequential4–8. Many of these injuries originate from deficient neuromuscular control during high-velocity directional changes and landing maneuvers—precisely the movements that also determine on-court agility9–11. Neuromuscular training, which targets the coordinated interplay between neural pathways and muscular activation through plyometric exercises, balance challenges, proprioceptive drills, and core stability work, has shown consistent promise for both performance enhancement and injury risk reduction across age groups and competitive levels in basketball and other team sports1,12–17. Yet conventional approaches carry well-documented limitations: inconsistent exercise standardization, restricted objective feedback, and insufficient motivational scaffolding can constrain the magnitude and durability of adaptations18–20.

Virtual reality technology offers a compelling avenue for overcoming these constraints. By pairing head-mounted displays with motion sensors, haptic actuators, and adaptive difficulty algorithms, VR platforms create controlled, reproducible training environments that deliver real-time biomechanical feedback while sustaining athlete engagement through gamified scenarios21–25. Research on body visualization within VR has further demonstrated that immersive self-representation can influence motor execution quality across different populations26. Intervention studies spanning racquet sports, martial arts, and team ball games have reported improvements in reaction time, movement precision, and sport-specific decision-making when VR was integrated with standard training protocols27–32. More recently, artificial intelligence modules embedded within VR systems have begun to tailor training parameters to individual biomechanical profiles, further amplifying the potential for personalized motor learning33. Despite this growing evidence base, independent replication remains limited, and few controlled experiments have examined whether VR-assisted neuromuscular training can simultaneously improve agility performance and modify biomechanical injury risk markers in basketball athletes23,27,34.

This study therefore examines the effectiveness of a 12-week VR-assisted neuromuscular training program on agility performance and biomechanical injury risk markers in university-level basketball players. We assessed agility through a battery of validated field tests (T-test, 5-10-5 shuttle, Illinois agility test, hexagon test), quantified neuromuscular function via dynamic balance, proprioceptive accuracy, muscle response time, and co-contraction indices, and evaluated biomechanical injury risk through knee valgus angle, landing mechanics, and force symmetry measures. We hypothesized that the VR-assisted group would demonstrate significantly greater pre-to-post improvements than a conventionally trained control group in both agility outcomes and neuromuscular injury risk indicators, owing to the enriched sensory feedback and adaptive task complexity afforded by the VR environment.

Methods

Research design and participant selection

We employed a controlled laboratory experimental design with a parallel-group comparative approach to evaluate VR-assisted neuromuscular training effects on basketball athletes’ agility and movement quality35. The experiment ran within university sports science laboratory facilities from September to December 2023. Participants were randomly assigned to either the VR-assisted training group or the conventional training control group upon enrollment, with both groups undergoing identical baseline assessments prior to the 12-week intervention period.

Participant selection criteria were established to ensure homogeneous subject characteristics while maintaining adequate representation of the target basketball athlete population36. Inclusion criteria required participants to be competitive basketball athletes aged 18–25 years with a minimum of two years of organized basketball training experience and current participation in university-level basketball programs. Additional inclusion requirements specified that participants must demonstrate normal range of motion in all major joints as assessed through standardized flexibility testing protocols and exhibit no movement restrictions that would preclude safe participation in high-intensity training activities.

Exclusion criteria were implemented to eliminate potential confounding variables that could influence outcome measurements or compromise participant safety during experimental procedures35. Participants were excluded if they reported any recent lower extremity injuries within the preceding six months that required modification of training activities or resulted in missed training sessions exceeding one week duration. Additional exclusion criteria included current participation in other research studies involving exercise interventions, use of performance-enhancing substances or medications that could affect neuromuscular function, and inability to comply with the complete 12-week intervention protocol due to scheduling conflicts or other commitments37.

Sample size determination relied on statistical power analysis grounded in prior research on similar training interventions in athletic populations38. We adopted an effect size of 0.65, reflecting a moderate-to-large estimate consistent with meta-analytic findings on neuromuscular training effects on agility and balance performance in athletes39,40, with alpha set at 0.05 and desired power of 0.80. This analysis indicated a minimum of 28 participants per group; we recruited 35 per group to accommodate potential attrition during the 12-week protocol36.

The randomization procedure employed a computer-generated random number sequence created by an independent statistician not involved in participant recruitment or data collection to ensure unbiased group allocation while maintaining balanced group sizes throughout the recruitment process35. Block randomization with varying block sizes (ranging from 4 to 8) was implemented to prevent predictable allocation patterns while ensuring approximately equal group distributions as enrollment progressed, with stratification by playing position (guards, forwards, centers) to enhance baseline comparability37. The randomization sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by the statistician and stored in a locked cabinet accessible only to the enrollment coordinator, who opened each envelope only after a participant completed baseline assessments and confirmed availability for the complete intervention protocol38. Allocation concealment was verified by having research assistants guess group assignment prior to envelope opening, with accuracy rates not exceeding chance levels.

Blinding implementation followed established protocols for exercise intervention research, with outcome assessors maintained blind to group allocation throughout all testing sessions39. Assessors were physically separated from training staff, conducted assessments in different laboratory rooms, and had no access to training schedules or session attendance records. To verify assessor blinding integrity, we asked assessors to guess each participant’s group allocation after post-intervention testing; their accuracy (48.5%) did not differ from chance (50%), confirming successful blinding. Statisticians performing primary analyses were also blinded to group allocation, receiving coded datasets (Group A and Group B) until all pre-specified analyses were completed. While complete participant blinding was not feasible due to the overt nature of VR equipment, participants were informed that the study was comparing two evidence-based training approaches without specific details regarding expected outcomes, hypothesized superiority of either intervention, or the novel nature of VR training36. Data analysts remained blinded to group allocation until completion of all statistical analyses to prevent potential bias in data interpretation and result presentation.

Research ethics approval was obtained from the University Sports Science Research Ethics Committee prior to participant recruitment and data collection initiation37. The investigation was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles and institutional guidelines for research involving human participants in exercise science contexts38. All participants provided written informed consent following comprehensive explanation of study procedures, potential risks, and expected benefits associated with participation in the experimental protocol39.

Virtual reality training system and intervention protocols

The virtual reality training system utilized in this investigation comprised a comprehensive hardware and software configuration specifically designed for sports performance applications and neuromuscular training enhancement41.

The hardware platform consisted of a high-resolution head-mounted display unit (HTC Vive Pro, HTC Corporation, Taiwan) with 2160 × 1200 pixel resolution and 90 Hz refresh rate, integrated with precision motion tracking sensors (Xsens MTw Awinda, Xsens Technologies B.V., Netherlands) capable of detecting millimeter-level positional changes and angular velocity measurements up to 2000 degrees per second. The hybrid IMU–optical setup was validated against a gold-standard optical motion capture system (Vicon Nexus, Oxford Metrics Ltd., UK), yielding r = 0.97 for joint angle measurements and root mean square error < 2.3 mm for positional tracking42,43. We note that independent validation studies of Xsens IMU systems in sport-specific jump-landing and change-of-direction tasks have reported highly comparable sagittal-plane waveforms relative to Vicon, though frontal- and transverse-plane agreement can be more variable43.

The system incorporated wireless inertial measurement units (IMUs) sampling at 100 Hz attached to key anatomical landmarks including the pelvis, thighs, shanks, and feet to provide real-time biomechanical feedback and movement pattern analysis throughout training sessions, with sensor fusion algorithms implementing Kalman filtering to minimize drift (reported drift < 1.5° per minute)44. Ground reaction forces were measured using dual force plates (AMTI OR6-7, Advanced Mechanical Technology Inc., USA) sampling at 1000 Hz, synchronized with IMU data via a hardware trigger system ensuring temporal alignment within 1 millisecond. Electromyographic activity was recorded using wireless surface EMG sensors (Delsys Trigno, Delsys Inc., USA) with 2000 Hz sampling rate, synchronized with kinematic and kinetic data through the integrated data acquisition system. Haptic feedback devices (bHaptics TactSuit, bHaptics Inc., South Korea) provided vibrotactile cues with intensity controlled via software algorithms based on real-time biomechanical deviations from target movement patterns.

Software functionality encompassed a custom-developed basketball-specific training platform that incorporated evidence-based neuromuscular training protocols with gamification elements including real-time scoring systems (awarding points for movement accuracy, speed, and technique quality), progressive achievement badges (bronze, silver, gold levels for mastering specific movement patterns), personalized leaderboards comparing individual performance against previous sessions, and immediate visual and auditory rewards for successful task completion to maintain participant engagement and motivation throughout the intervention period46. The software featured adaptive difficulty algorithms that automatically adjusted training parameters based on individual performance progression, ensuring optimal challenge levels while maintaining movement quality standards47. Real-time biofeedback capabilities provided immediate visual and auditory cues to guide proper movement execution, with color-coded joint angle indicators and trajectory guidance systems that facilitated motor learning and movement pattern refinement.

The neuromuscular training content design incorporated four primary training categories: dynamic balance challenges, reactive agility scenarios, plyometric landing mechanics, and sport-specific movement patterns41. Dynamic balance training modules presented participants with unstable virtual environments requiring continuous postural adjustments while maintaining specific stance positions or executing controlled movements42. Reactive agility components featured unpredictable visual stimuli that triggered rapid directional changes, simulating game-like decision-making scenarios while emphasizing proper cutting mechanics and deceleration techniques44.

Plyometric training elements within the VR environment focused on landing mechanics optimization through visual feedback regarding joint positioning, ground reaction forces, and movement symmetry during various jumping and landing tasks45. Sport-specific training scenarios replicated common basketball movement patterns, including defensive slides, offensive cuts, rebounding sequences, and transition movements, all while providing real-time biomechanical guidance to promote optimal movement execution46.

A detailed comparison of training protocols between experimental and control groups is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Both groups received identical supplementary sessions twice weekly, consisting of general basketball conditioning and skill practice.

Training intensity and frequency parameters were established based on established neuromuscular training principles and previous research examining optimal adaptation responses in athletic populations41. The experimental group participated in 60-minute VR-assisted neuromuscular training sessions three times per week, with additional supplementary sessions twice weekly focusing on strength and conditioning components42. Training intensity was systematically progressed using a periodized approach, beginning with 70% of maximum effort during weeks 1–4, progressing to 80% during weeks 5–8, and reaching 90% maximum effort during the final four weeks of intervention44.

The control group followed an equivalent training volume utilizing conventional neuromuscular training methods that have been previously validated in basketball athlete populations45. Traditional training approaches included balance board exercises, agility ladder drills, plyometric jumping protocols, and standard basketball skill development activities46. Training sessions were matched for duration, frequency, and overall energy expenditure to ensure that any observed differences between groups could be attributed specifically to the VR-assisted training methodology rather than training volume disparities47.

Comprehensive safety protocols were implemented throughout the intervention period to minimize risk and ensure participant wellbeing during all training activities41. VR training sessions were conducted under direct supervision by certified strength and conditioning specialists with experience in virtual reality applications and neuromuscular training protocols42. Emergency stop procedures were established for the VR system, allowing immediate session termination if participants experienced discomfort, disorientation, or any adverse responses to the virtual environment44.

Motion tracking calibration procedures were performed at the beginning of each session to ensure accurate biomechanical feedback and prevent potential movement misinterpretation that could lead to improper technique reinforcement45. Regular system maintenance and software updates were conducted to maintain optimal performance and prevent technical malfunctions that could compromise training effectiveness or participant safety46. Additionally, progressive familiarization sessions were implemented during the first week of intervention to allow participants to adapt to the VR environment and develop confidence with the technology before advancing to more complex training scenarios47.

Testing indicators and data collection

The comprehensive agility assessment protocol incorporated multiple validated testing procedures designed to evaluate different aspects of basketball-specific movement performance and neuromuscular control capabilities48. The T-agility test served as the primary assessment tool for multidirectional movement speed, requiring participants to complete a standardized course involving forward sprinting, lateral shuffling, and backward running components while maintaining maximum effort throughout the 10-meter course49. The 5-10-5 shuttle run protocol provided additional assessment of acceleration, deceleration, and change of direction abilities, with participants required to complete rapid directional changes over specified distances while minimizing total completion time.

Supplementary agility assessments included the Illinois agility test and hexagon test protocols, which evaluated complex movement patterns involving multiple directional changes and reactive responses to external stimuli50. While the selected agility tests (T-test, 5-10-5 shuttle) represent well-established and validated two-dimensional assessment tools with strong reliability coefficients, we acknowledge that more recent three-dimensional agility assessments have been developed to capture the full spatial complexity of multidirectional sports movements, which may offer enhanced ecological validity for basketball-specific performance evaluation34. These assessments were selected based on their established reliability coefficients exceeding 0.90 and their demonstrated sensitivity to training-induced adaptations in athletic populations51. All agility tests were performed on standardized gymnasium surfaces with consistent environmental conditions to minimize measurement variability and ensure reliable inter-session comparisons.

The injury risk evaluation framework utilized a comprehensive movement screening approach that combined quantitative biomechanical assessments with standardized functional movement quality ratings48. The Functional Movement Screen protocol provided systematic evaluation of fundamental movement patterns, with certified assessors rating performance quality on established 0–3 point scales for seven distinct movement tasks49. Landing Error Scoring System assessments examined jump-landing mechanics through standardized video analysis procedures, with trained evaluators identifying movement deviations associated with increased injury risk during bilateral drop-landing tasks50.

Biomechanical measurement protocols incorporated three-dimensional motion analysis utilizing high-speed camera systems operating at 240 frames per second to capture detailed kinematic data during functional movement tasks51. Force plate technology measuring ground reaction forces at 1000 Hz sampling frequency provided quantitative assessment of loading patterns, asymmetries, and impact characteristics during jumping, landing, and cutting maneuvers48. Electromyographic assessment of muscle activation patterns utilized wireless surface electrodes positioned over key lower extremity muscle groups to evaluate neuromuscular coordination and timing characteristics during dynamic movement execution49.

The comprehensive testing indicator system, encompassing agility, neuromuscular function, and biomechanical risk assessments, is detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Standardized data collection procedures were implemented to ensure measurement consistency and minimize sources of variability that could compromise data quality or interpretation51. All testing sessions were conducted at identical times of day to control for circadian rhythm influences on performance, with participants required to maintain consistent sleep schedules and avoid intense physical activity for 24 h prior to assessment sessions48. Environmental conditions including temperature, humidity, and lighting were maintained within specified ranges throughout the testing period to eliminate potential confounding variables49.

Each assessment session followed a fixed procedural sequence to balance fatigue control with measurement consistency. After a standardized warm-up (10 min light aerobic activity followed by 5 min dynamic stretching and sport-specific movement preparation), participants proceeded through four testing blocks. Block 1 comprised agility assessments (T-test, 5-10-5 shuttle, Illinois agility test, hexagon test, 3-cone drill, and pro agility test), with each test performed twice and the best trial recorded; a 3-minute seated rest separated individual tests within this block. Block 2 involved functional movement screening (FMS seven-movement battery and Landing Error Scoring System), allowing 2 min between tasks. Block 3 consisted of neuromuscular function tests (Y-balance test, single-leg balance, drop jump for reactive strength index, and isokinetic strength testing), separated by 3-minute rest intervals. Block 4 comprised biomechanical analysis tasks (bilateral drop-landing from 40 cm for kinematic, kinetic, and EMG recording; defensive slide cutting task for frontal-plane knee assessment), with 5-minute rest between tasks to minimize fatigue carryover. Block order was held constant across all sessions to maintain comparability, while test order within each block was randomized across participants using a Latin-square design. Pre-testing preparation protocols required participants to complete standardized warm-up procedures consisting of 10 min of light aerobic activity followed by dynamic stretching and sport-specific movement patterns50. Testing order was randomized within each assessment session to prevent systematic fatigue effects from influencing results, with mandatory rest periods of 3–5 min between individual tests to ensure adequate recovery51. All assessment procedures were conducted by trained research personnel who demonstrated inter-rater reliability coefficients exceeding 0.90 for subjective scoring measures.

Neuromuscular function assessment incorporated multiple calculated indices to quantify biomechanical risk factors and neuromuscular control capabilities. The Stability Index (SI) quantified dynamic balance performance using center of pressure data according to the formula: SI = √[(ΣΔx²/n) + (ΣΔy²/n)], where SI represents the stability index in millimeters, Δx and Δy are center of pressure deviations in mediolateral and anteroposterior directions (mm), and n is the number of data points sampled at 100 Hz during 30-second balance trials52. Lower SI values indicate superior balance control.

Proprioceptive threshold detection capability was assessed using the angular displacement sensitivity equation: θ_threshold = arctan(Δd/r), where θ_threshold is the minimum detectable angular displacement (degrees), Δd is the linear displacement threshold (mm), and r is the radius of joint rotation (mm)53. The joint rotation radius (r) was measured individually for each participant using anthropometric assessment of the relevant joint (knee and ankle), with segment lengths determined from bony landmark palpation and digital caliper measurement to ensure person-specific calculation accuracy. Measurements were obtained during passive joint repositioning tasks with participants blindfolded, detecting threshold accuracy within 0.5° resolution.

Muscle activation coordination was quantified using the Co-contraction Index (CCI): CCI = (EMG_A × EMG_ANT)/(EMG_A + EMG_ANT) × 100, where CCI is the co-contraction index (%), EMG_A is the normalized agonist muscle activation (% of maximum voluntary isometric contraction, MVIC), and EMG_ANT is the normalized antagonist muscle activation (% MVIC)52. EMG signals were band-pass filtered (20–450 Hz), full-wave rectified, and smoothed using a 50ms moving average window. MVIC normalization was performed using the peak 500ms window from three maximal contractions with 120 s rest between trials. CCI calculations utilized the 200-millisecond window centered on peak ground reaction force during landing tasks.

Landing biomechanics were evaluated using the Frontal Plane Projection Angle (FPPA): FPPA = arctan(d_knee/d_ASIS), where FPPA is the frontal plane projection angle (degrees), d_knee is the mediolateral knee displacement from the midline (cm), and d_ASIS is the vertical distance from the anterior superior iliac spine to the ground (cm)53. FPPA was calculated at the instant of peak knee flexion during drop-landing tasks, with larger angles indicating greater knee valgus collapse.

Reactive strength capabilities were assessed using the Reactive Strength Index: RSI = Jump Height / Ground Contact Time, where RSI is dimensionless, jump height is measured in meters from flight time calculations, and ground contact time is measured in seconds from force plate data52. Higher RSI values indicate superior stretch-shortening cycle efficiency and neuromuscular power.

Force production symmetry was evaluated using the Limb Symmetry Index: LSI = (Weaker Limb / Stronger Limb) × 100, where LSI is expressed as a percentage, with values approaching 100% indicating better bilateral balance and reduced injury risk53. LSI was calculated for multiple variables including peak vertical ground reaction force, peak power output, and reactive strength index.

Ground reaction force attenuation was quantified using the Loading Rate: LR = ΔF/Δt, where LR is the loading rate (N/s or bodyweights/s), ΔF represents the change in vertical ground reaction force (N or bodyweights), and Δt is the corresponding time interval (s)54. Loading rate was calculated during the initial 50 milliseconds following ground contact during landing tasks, with lower loading rates indicating better shock absorption capabilities.

All kinematic data were processed using a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a 10 Hz cutoff frequency following residual analysis. Joint angles were calculated using a modified Helen Hayes marker set and inverse kinematics algorithms implemented in Visual3D software (C-Motion Inc., USA). Coordinate system definitions followed ISB recommendations with the laboratory global coordinate system oriented as: X-axis (mediolateral), Y-axis (anteroposterior), Z-axis (vertical).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis procedures incorporated both descriptive and inferential statistical approaches to address the research objectives and hypotheses48. Baseline group comparability was assessed using independent samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, with statistical significance set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed)49. Primary outcome analyses utilized mixed-model repeated measures ANOVA (SPSS Statistics version 28.0, IBM Corp., USA) to examine group-by-time interactions with the following model structure: fixed effects for group (VR vs. Control), time (baseline, week 6, week 12), and group×time interaction; random intercept for participants; and unstructured covariance matrix selected based on Akaike Information Criterion. Assumption testing included Mauchly’s test for sphericity with Greenhouse-Geisser correction applied when violated (ε < 0.75). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni correction procedures to control familywise error rate across multiple comparisons50. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d with pooled standard deviation: d = (M₁ - M₂) / SD_pooled, where M₁ and M₂ are group means and SD_pooled is the pooled standard deviation, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects respectively51. Missing data (3.0% of total observations) were handled using restricted maximum likelihood estimation within the mixed model framework, which utilizes all available data under the missing at random assumption without requiring complete case analysis or imputation. Sensitivity analyses using complete case analysis and last observation carried forward yielded substantively identical results (differences < 2% in effect estimates).

Results

Participant characteristics and training compliance

Seventy-six basketball athletes were screened; 70 met inclusion criteria and completed baseline assessments. Study completion reached 95.7% (67/70), with two VR-group participants withdrawing at week 6 (academic conflicts) and one control participant discontinuing at week 9 (relocation). No withdrawals were attributed to adverse events or training intolerance55,56.

Agility performance

Mixed-model repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant group-by-time interactions for all primary agility measures (all p < 0.001). The VR group demonstrated 7–10% greater pre-to-post improvements than controls across the test battery, with large effect sizes throughout (Cohen’s d = 0.78–1.34). On the T-agility test, the VR group improved by 8.2% versus 4.1% for controls (p < 0.001, d = 1.23). The hexagon test yielded the largest between-group difference (9.9% vs. 5.5%, p = 0.001, d = 1.34). Detailed pre- and post-intervention comparisons appear in Fig. 4; Table 258,59.

Neuromuscular function and injury prevention markers

The VR group achieved 15–30% greater improvements than controls across neuromuscular parameters, with significant group-by-time interactions for all measures (all p < 0.001). The largest between-group differences emerged for the reactive strength index (27.2% vs. 12.3% improvement, d = 1.67), co-contraction index reduction (25.2% vs. 9.6%, d = 1.45), and proprioceptive accuracy (22.4% vs. 10.7%, d = 1.12). Dynamic knee valgus angles decreased by 18.7% in the VR group compared to 8.2% in controls (p < 0.001, d = 0.76). Full results are presented in Fig. 5; Table 352–54.

Figure 5 displays percentage improvements across eight neuromuscular parameters, showing consistent VR-group advantages. Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed normal distributions at both time points for all parameters (all p > 0.15), and no individual values exceeded ± 2.5 SD from group means54.

Discussion

Several mechanisms likely underlie the superior neuromuscular adaptations in the VR group. The enriched proprioceptive feedback delivered through visual and haptic channels promoted heightened joint position awareness and refined movement control52, while real-time biomechanical cues accelerated motor learning beyond what conventional coach-observation approaches typically achieve53. Reduced muscle response times and improved intermuscular coordination patterns point toward central neural adaptations and enhanced neuromuscular efficiency, consistent with established neuroplasticity frameworks that link repetitive, feedback-rich practice to structural and functional neural reorganization54,60–64. The movement variability inherent in VR scenarios likely fostered robust motor schema development, improving the transferability of learned patterns to diverse athletic contexts52,53. We emphasize, however, that these mechanistic inferences rest on peripheral and behavioral measures; direct cortical-level evidence through neuroimaging was beyond this study’s scope.

The exceptionally large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 1.2 for several outcomes, up to 27.2% RSI improvement) warrant careful interpretation. Part of the VR group’s advantage may reflect a “learning to test” effect, whereby participants became increasingly efficient within VR-mediated movement contexts, inflating apparent gains beyond purely physiological adaptation. Although all post-intervention agility tests were conducted outside the VR environment using standard field protocols, the VR group’s extensive exposure to similar movement patterns during training may have conferred a practice-specificity advantage that is difficult to separate from genuine neuromuscular improvement.

Our VR group’s agility improvements (7–10% beyond controls) exceed those typically reported for conventional neuromuscular training alone. A meta-analysis of integrative neuromuscular programs found pooled effect sizes of SMD = 0.26 for jump performance and moderate effects for sprint and agility tasks40, while a study of neuromuscular training in elite soccer players reported effect sizes of d = 0.63–0.88 for reactive agility over six weeks65. Our larger effects may reflect the additive benefit of immersive feedback on top of the neuromuscular exercises themselves, a possibility consistent with cognitive-motor dual-task training frameworks43. The improvements in biomechanical injury risk markers—reduced dynamic knee valgus, improved force symmetry, enhanced landing mechanics—represent meaningful progress, though we reiterate that these remain surrogate markers rather than actual injury incidence data. Such improvements are consistent with injury prevention frameworks established for team sports17,19.

Conclusion

This 12-week controlled laboratory experiment demonstrates that VR-assisted neuromuscular training produces superior improvements in agility performance (7–10% greater gains) and biomechanical injury risk markers (15–30% greater gains) compared to conventional training in university-level basketball athletes, with large effect sizes across most outcomes66. From a theoretical standpoint, the results suggest that combining real-time biofeedback with immersive environmental simulation can accelerate motor learning beyond conventional training, consistent with augmented feedback-driven skill acquisition frameworks67.

From a practical standpoint, these findings provide evidence-based justification for integrating VR platforms into comprehensive basketball training programs. The capacity to address performance enhancement and injury prevention simultaneously may improve training efficiency across competitive levels68.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The 12-week duration may not capture long-term skill retention or transfer to competitive performance. The sample comprised exclusively university-level athletes aged 18–25, limiting generalizability to other age groups, competitive levels, or sports. Laboratory-based assessments, while controlled, may not fully replicate competitive conditions69. Critically, we measured surrogate biomechanical injury risk markers rather than actual injury incidence; future studies with minimum 12-month surveillance and larger samples (estimated n > 300 per group) are needed to validate the injury prevention potential. The large effect sizes raise the possibility of environment-specific learning effects, and single-site recruitment limits geographical generalizability. The absence of a detraining follow-up and the impossibility of participant blinding in an exercise trial represent additional constraints69.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs examining training effect retention, dose-response relationships for VR session parameters, and generalizability across diverse athletic populations. Integration of advanced biomechanical analysis with VR systems and development of sport-specific modules beyond basketball represent promising directions66,67.

In sum, this investigation provides robust evidence supporting VR-assisted neuromuscular training as an effective approach for enhancing basketball athlete agility and reducing biomechanical injury risk markers, while acknowledging the need for longer-term, multi-site replication with actual injury surveillance outcomes68,69.
