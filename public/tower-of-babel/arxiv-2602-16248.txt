Abstract
. We show that the generating series of the number of pairs of geodesics on a compact Shimura curve with given discriminants and intersection angle are coefficients of a non-holomorphic Siegel modular form, a theta lift of the constant function. This retrieves and generalizes counting results of Rickards via the Siegel-Weil formula. More generally, we study the genus two theta lift of Maass forms on this Shimura curve and prove a Fourier-Taylor expansion in terms of some generalized Whittaker functions. We also provide a geometric interpretation of all Fourier coefficients of these theta lifts in terms of averages of geodesic Taylor coefficients over special cycles.



1.
Introduction

Computing the number of closed geodesics and of Heegner points of given discriminant on a Shimura curve and understanding the distribution of these subsets has a long history. An incomplete account starts for the modular curve with Siegel’s estimate
[
42
]
, continues with the equidistribution results by Linnik
[
30
]
and Duke
[
9
]
and includes numerous quantitative versions exploring subconvexity bounds, see e.g. the survey
[
32
]
. A key step in the latter approaches is to interpret the sum of the evaluations of a Maass form at Heegner points of discriminant
D
as the
D
-th Fourier coefficient of a Maass form of weight
1/2
, in fact a theta lift, following ideas of Maass and studied in detail in this non-holomorphic context by Katok-Sarnak
[
19
]
.

Counting pairs of geodesics.

Similar questions for the number and distribution of intersection points of
two
geodesic curves with given discriminant are much less explored. (For references to counting and equidistribution sorted by length see ‘Related results’ below.) One of the few results in this direction is the work of Rickards (
[
40
]
, see also
[
39
]
for the case of the modular curve), who computed the number of intersection points by studying pairs of optimal embeddings of quadratic number fields into quaternion algebras. More precisely, under some ‘admissibility’ condition on the discriminants he showed that this number admits a nice expression (that we recall in Theorem
2.6
) in terms of local factors. One of our motivations was to provide an expression in the general case together with an explanation for this factorization.

Let
B
be an indefinite quaternion algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
which is not globally split.
Fix an Eichler order

in

.

The number

is a product of local factors, explicitly for

Note that the Whittaker function satisfies the transformation law

(1.1)

\displaystyle W_{T,\infty}(av\,{}^{t}a)

\displaystyle\;=\;\det(a)^{3/2}\cdot W_{{}^{t}aTa,\infty}(v)

for

, and for

.

A theta lift.

To prove the theorem we consider the rational quadratic space
V
given by the trace zero elements of
B
equipped with the negative of the reduced norm as a quadratic form. The signature of
V
is
(2,1)
and its general spin group
\operatorname{GSpin}(V)
is isomorphic to the group
B^{\times}
of invertible elements of
B
.
The intersection

L\;=\;V\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{M}}

defines an even lattice.
The Siegel theta function
\theta_{L}^{(2)}(\tau,z)
of genus
2
associated with the lattice
L
(and the standard Gaussian on
V(\mathbb{R})
) is a non-holomorphic automorphic form which is invariant under
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}
in the ‘orthogonal’ variable
z\in\mathbb{H}
and a modular form of weight
1/2
in the ‘metaplectic’ variable
\tau\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
for a congruence subgroup
\widetilde{\Gamma}
of the metaplectic group of genus
2
.

For a Maass form
f
of weight
0
for the group
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}
with Laplace eigenvalue
\lambda
we consider the theta integral

(1.2)

\displaystyle I(\tau,f)\;=\;\int_{\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}\backslash\mathbb{H}}f(z)\theta_{L}^{(2)}(\tau,z)\,d\mu(z),

where
d\mu(z)
denotes the usual invariant measure on

\mathbb{H}
. It defines a Siegel Maass form of weight
1/2
for the group
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, whose infinitesimal character we determine in Proposition
3.1
.

Theorem
1.1
will be proved by carefully analyzing the theta integral in the special case when
f
is constant. In fact, the Maass form
G
is given by the theta integral
I(\tau,1)
, which is just the average of the theta function over the Shimura curve
X
and can be evaluated by means of the Siegel-Weil formula. In this way
G
can be identified with a specific Siegel Eisenstein series of weight
1/2
and genus
2
, see Theorem
5.1
. Its Fourier coefficients can be computed in terms of local Whittaker functions, and the quantity
R_{T}
arises as a products of local non-archimedian Whittaker functions in Theorem
5.2
. Employing explicit formulas for these due to Kudla and Yang
[
26
,
47
]
we derive the result of Rickards. Moreover, this approach allows for a generalization to more general level structures.

General input and generalized Whittaker functions.

However, this argument involving the Siegel-Weil formula is limited to the case when
f
is constant. The main part of Section
3
is devoted to the case of a general Maass form
f
. This will also lead to a second proof of (large part of) Theorem
1.1
. We now turn to a description of our main result in the general case. It turns out that it requires a finer analysis of the coefficients
F_{T}(v)
in the Fourier expansion

F(\tau)\;=\;\sum_{T\in\operatorname{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})}F_{T}(v)\,\mathbf{e}(\operatorname{tr}(Tu))

of a Siegel Maass form
F
of genus
2
.
For simplicity, in this exposition we only consider the case when the index matrix
T
is positive definite, referring to Section
3.1
for general regular
T
.

The special orthogonal group
\operatorname{SO}(T)
of the positive definite quadratic form
T
acts on the space of smooth functions
h:\mathbb{H}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
h(\tau+b)=\mathbf{e}(\operatorname{tr}(Tb))h(\tau)
and having a fixed infinitesimal character. Consequently, the Fourier coefficient
F_{T}(v)
has an expansion
in terms of the characters of the compact abelian group
\operatorname{SO}(T)
.
Fix a matrix
B\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
T={}^{t}BB
. This determines an isomorphism
\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\operatorname{SO}(T)
,
a\mapsto{}^{t}\!Ba\,{}^{t}\!B^{-1}
, and an isomorphism of the character group of
\operatorname{SO}(T)
with
\mathbb{Z}
. We obtain an expansion

F_{T}(v)\;=\;\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{T,n}(v),

where
F_{T,n}(v)
transforms according to the
n
-th character of
\operatorname{SO}(T)/\{\pm 1\}
, see (
3.6
). In particular, the
T
-th Fourier coefficient
I_{T}(v,f)
of our theta integral (
1.2
) possesses such an expansion, whose coefficients we denote by
I_{T,n}(v,f)
.

In Section
3.4
we construct an explicit Whittaker function

\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,n}(v,s)

for
v
a positive definite
(2\times 2)
-matrix and
s\in\mathbb{C}
. Here the superscript refers to the case the
T
has two
positive
eigenvalues. In the body of the text, where we consider general
T
, there will be further cases depending on the signature of
T
.
Under right translation by
a\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
the function
\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,n}(v,s)
satisfies the transformation law

\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{Ba,n}(v,s)\;=\;\det(a)^{-1}\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,n}(av\,{}^{t}a,s),

analogous to (
1.1
).
Under left translation of
B
by
\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
it transforms with the character of index
n
, see Remark
3.13
. Moreover, it is holomorphic in
s
and invariant under the transformation
s\mapsto 1-s
. The function
\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,n}(v,s)\mathbf{e}(\operatorname{tr}(Tu))
is an eigenfunction of the invariant differential operators on Siegel Maass forms of weight
1/2
induced by the Casimir elements of the symplectic group. Similar Whittaker functions were studied by Niwa in weight zero for positive definite
T
and by Moriyama for integral weights and general
T
, see e.g.
[
34
]
,
[
33
]
.

We prove that the coefficient
I_{T,n}(v,f)
of the theta integral is a constant multiple of

\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,n}(v,s)
, where we have written the Laplace eigenvalue of
f
as
\lambda=s(1-s)
.
We show that the constant of proportionality can be described in terms of geodesic Taylor coefficients of
f
at the intersection points of geodesics on
X
with characteristic matrix as in Theorem
1.1
.

Geodesic Taylor expansion.

To describe this in more detail, recall that the Cartan decomposition of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
implies that any
z\in\mathbb{H}
can be written in the form

z=k_{\theta}a_{t}i
, where

a_{t}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad k_{\theta}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}

with
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\theta\in[0,\pi)
. The pair
(t,\theta)
defines the standard geodesic polar coordinates of
z
centered at
i
, where
t
is the geodesic distance from
z
to
i
, and
2\theta
the angle determined by the geodesic ray
i\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
and the geodesic ray from
i
to
z
.
The Maass form
f
has a geodesic Taylor expansion at the point
z_{0}=i
of the form

f(z)\;=\;\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}(z_{0})P^{-|n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))\cdot e^{2in\theta},

where
P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(x)
denotes the classical Legendre function, and the
n
-th coefficient
f_{n}(z_{0})
at
z_{0}
is given by Proposition
3.10
, see also
[
11
]
.
More generally, if
z_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
is any point, we choose a translation matrix
g\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
z_{1}=gi
. We obtain geodesic polar coordinates centered at
z_{1}
by writing
z=gk_{\theta}a_{t}i
. Then
t
denotes again the hyperbolic distance from
z
to
z_{1}
, and
2\theta
the angle determined by the geodesic ray
g(i\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1})
and the geodesic ray from
z_{1}
to
z
. Note that the angle actually depends on the choice of
g
, not only on
z_{1}
. The geodesic Taylor expansion of
f
at the point
z_{1}
(with respect to
g
) is defined as the geodesic Taylor expansion of
f\mid g
at
i
. The corresponding Taylor coefficients are denoted by
f_{n}(z_{1})
.
Our main result for positive definite
T
is now as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let the notation be as above.
The
n
-th coefficient of
I_{T}(v,f)
is given by

\displaystyle I_{T,n}(v,f)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,n}(v,s)\cdot\sum_{x\in R_{T}}f_{-2n}(z_{x}).

Here, for every
x\in R_{T}
we have written
z_{x}
for the Heegner point determined by the intersection of the two geodesics defined by
x
. Moreover, the geodesic polar coordinates at
z_{x}
are defined with respect to a specific translation matrix
g_{x}
(depending on the choice of
B
), see Theorem
3.14

for details.
In particular, the
0
-th coefficient is given by the average value

\displaystyle I_{T,0}(v,f)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,0}(v,s)\cdot\sum_{x\in R_{T}}f(z_{x}).

Remark 1.3
.

i)
An analogous result also holds in the case when
T
is indefinite, corresponding to the cases (ii)–(iv) of Theorem
1.1
. Then the appropriate coordinates are given by the distance of a point
z\in\mathbb{H}
from the geodesic
\gamma
appearing in these cases, and the intersection point of
\gamma
with the geodesic defining the distance. The quotient of
\gamma
by its stabilizer in
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}
is isomorphic to
S^{1}
, and consequently there is again an expansion of
f
in terms of the corresponding characters with coefficients depending on the distance. The analogue of Theorem
1.2
involves twisted cycle integrals of
f
over the image of
\gamma
in
X
and an archimedian Whittaker function
\mathcal{W}^{+,-}_{B,n}(v,s)
, see Theorem
3.19
for details.

ii) The coefficients
I_{T}(v,f)
for singular
T
turn out to be given by the average values of
f
over Heegner points or geodesic cycles of fixed discriminant (determined by the trace of
T
),
see Theorems
3.23
and
3.24
. In this way we recover the modularity result for such averages due to Katok–Sarnak
[
19
]
.

iii) In the special case when
f=1
, we have
s=0
and the Whittaker function
\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,0}(v,0)
reduces to the Whittaker function
W_{T,\infty}(v)
of Theorem
1.1
. The geodesic Taylor coefficients
f_{n}(z)
vanish for all
n\neq 0
, and
f_{0}(z)=1
at all points
z\in\mathbb{H}
. Hence, we recover the modularity assertion of Theorem
1.1
. However, this does not give the explicit formula for
R_{T}
in terms of non-archimedian local Whittaker functions.

To prove the theorem, we decompose the Fourier coefficient
I_{T}(v,f)
into orbital integrals and expand these with respect to the characters of
\operatorname{SO}(T)
.
It turns out that the corresponding integrals also make sense if
f
is replaced by a spherical function on
\mathbb{H}
. We employ this fact, together with properties of the Weil representation, to construct the Whittaker functions
\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{B,n}(v,s)
in Theorem
3.12
. Next we use the fact that the group
\operatorname{SO}(T)
is naturally embedded into both, the Levi factor of
\operatorname{Sp}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and the orthogonal group
\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{R})
, and that the corresponding actions on
V(\mathbb{R})^{2}
are compatible, see Lemma
3.7
.
With these preparations, the orbital integrals can be computed in terms of the geodesic Taylor expansions of
f
.

Hecke action.

Let
p
be an odd prime which is coprime to the discriminant of
B
and the level of the Eichler order. If
f
is an eigenform of the Hecke operator
T_{p}
, then the theta integral
I(\tau,f)
will again be a Hecke eigenform, now for the two non-trivial Hecke operators
\widehat{T}_{p,0}^{(2)},\widehat{T}_{p,1}^{(2)}
that we have in genus two. In Proposition
4.4
we give the explicit relation of these Hecke operators on
I(\tau,f)
with the action of
T_{p}
on
f
. The proof is an application of the method of Rallis, extended by Zhuravlev to include the metaplectic case. In fact, Zhuravlev gives maps that intertwine the action of Hecke operators on theta functions with the Hecke action on the Schwarz functions via the Weil representation. Moreover the Rallis-Zhuravlev map intertwines the symplectic and orthogonal Hecke operators inside the Weil representation. In Section
4
we combine these ideas to ready-to-use formulas and cross-check them with the genus one theta lift via the Siegel-
\Phi
-operator, see Proposition
4.6
.

Related results.

We remark that all the above questions on counting of closed geodesics on
any
compact Riemann surface according to
length
have been intensely studied, too, starting with Sarnak’s work
[
41
]
. There is no analog of special points like Heegner points in this context, though. Analogous questions on counting and equidistribution of intersection points have been investigated in
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
, and also
[
44
]
, however without taking any properties of the intersection angle into account. Most recently, these type of results have been upgraded to give explicit error terms in
[
43
]
and also in
[
20
]
. Since it is difficult to compare discriminant and length on Shimura curves, we make no attempt to directly relate the two kinds of results.

The distribution of intersection
angles
has been studied by Pollicott–Sharp
[
35
]
and Herrera
[
15
]
in the general hyperbolic context and by Jung–Sardari
[
18
]
in the arithmetic context (in fact: the modular surface case), solving the distribution conjecture from
[
39
]
.

An old theorem of Birkhoff identifies the number of intersection points of two geodesics with the
linking numbers
of the links formed by the geodesic and its time reverse. This fact is employed by Duke, Imamoglu and Toth in
[
8
]
to compute linking numbers of modular knots in terms of generalized Dedekind symbols. The latter are related to geodesic cycle integrals of weakly holomorphic modular functions on the modular curve
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
. Generating series of such cycle integrals were studied earlier in
[
7
]
, where the main result can be viewed as an analogue of
[
19
]
for weakly holomorphic modular forms. It states that the simultaneous generating series of cycles integrals and CM values of a given weakly holomorphic cusp form of weight zero is a harmonic Maass form of weight
1/2
. See also
[
3
]
for an approach via the regularized theta correspondence.
It is an interesting question whether the results of the present paper admit a generalization to meromorphic (or, in the modular curve case, weakly holomorphic) modular forms.

One motivation for the work of Rickards came from the work of Darmon and Vonk on a real quadratic analogue of singular moduli.
Rickards stresses in
[
40
, Section 2.5]
the analogy of his formula for the (unsigned) total intersection number of geodesics cycles of positive discriminants
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
with the formula of Gross-Zagier for the norms of differences of singular moduli
[
14
]
of negative discriminants. In particular, both have in common a decomposition according to ‘
b
-linking’. In the Gross-Zagier case the contribution of the
b
-linked part is determined by the arithmetic degree of a special
0
-cycle
Z(T)
, where
T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}D_{1}&b\\
b&D_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, on the arithmetic surface given by the modular curve
X(1)
over
\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})
, which in turn can be expressed as the
T
-th Fourier coefficient of the derivative of an incoherent genus
2
Siegel Eisenstein series of weight
3/2
.
In our interpretation of Rickards’ result (see Theorem
5.2
), the contribution of the
b
-linked part is given by the degree of a special
0
-cycle
Z(T)
on the complex Shimura curve associated to
B
, which in turn can be expressed as the
T
-th Fourier coefficient of the value of a coherent genus
2
Siegel Eisenstein series of weight
1/2
.

Darmon-Vonk propose that a real quadratic analogue of the differences of singular moduli is given by the evaluation of rigid meromorphic cocycles for the group
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}[1/p])
at real multiplication points. They show that the valuations at primes
q
are related to certain
signed
intersection numbers of geodesic cycles on Shimura curves. It would be interesting to investigate whether there is a variant of our theta correspondence approach involving signed intersections that could lead to a connection between the conjectural arithmetic intersection numbers of
[
5
, Conjecture 3.27]
and (derivatives of incoherent) Siegel Eisenstein series of genus
2
.

2.
Geodesic cycles and Heegner points

2.1.
The setup

Let
B
be an indefinite quaternion algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
D(B)
be the discriminant of
B
, that is, the product of all primes
p
for which
B_{p}=B\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is a division algebra.
For
x\in B
we denote by
\operatorname{tr}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}
the reduced trace and by
\operatorname{N}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}
the reduced norm. The vector space

(2.1)

\displaystyle V\;=\;\left\{x\in B\mid\;\operatorname{tr}(x)=0\right\}

together with the quadratic form
Q(x)=-\operatorname{N}(x)=x^{2}
is a rational quadratic space of signature
(2,1)
. It is anisotropic if and only if
D(B)\neq 1
. The corresponding bilinear form is
(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}(xy)
.
We view the group
H=B^{\times}
as an algebraic group over
\mathbb{Q}
. The action of
H
on
V
by conjugation induces an isomorphism
H\cong\operatorname{GSpin}(V,Q)
. Over
\mathbb{R}
, the space
V(\mathbb{R})
is given by the real
2\times 2
matrices of trace
0
, and
Q(x)=-\det(x)
. Moreover, we have

H(\mathbb{R})=\operatorname{GSpin}(V,Q)(\mathbb{R})\cong\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
For any subfield
F\subset\mathbb{R}
, we write

H(F)^{+}\;=\;\{h\in H(F)\mid\;\det(h)>0\}.

The hermitian symmetric space associated with
H
can be realized as the Grassmannian

\mathcal{D}\;=\;\{z\subset V(\mathbb{R})\mid\;\text{$\dim(z)=1$ and $Q\mid_{z}<0$}\}

of negative lines in
V(\mathbb{R})
. It is isomorphic to the complex upper half
plane
\mathbb{H}
via

(2.2)

\mathbb{H}\ni z=x+iy\,\mapsto\,\mathbb{R}\cdot\frac{1}{y}\left(\begin{matrix}-x&|z|^{2}\\
-1&x\end{matrix}\right)\in\mathcal{D}\,.

Here the natural action of
H(\mathbb{R})^{+}
on
\mathcal{D}
is identified with the action
on
\mathbb{H}
by fractional linear transformations. To describe the action of the full group
H(\mathbb{R})
on
\mathbb{H}
, we consider the involution

denotes the stabilizer of

in
\Gamma

The following result can be proved in the same way as Proposition

Proposition 3.2
.

Let

We state some basic properties of orbital integrals, which will be used for the computation of the Fourier coefficients of the theta integral. The group

acts on

(viewed as row vectors) by multiplication from the right, that is,

(3.26)

\displaystyle x\mapsto xa\;=\;(x_{1},x_{2})a

The group

(3.27)

For these actions, the following relations are easily verified.

Proposition 3.3
.

Using the above notation we have

\displaystyle I(xa,v,f)

\displaystyle\;=\;I(x,av\,{}^{t}a,f),

(3.29)

\displaystyle I(h.x,v,f)

3.3.1.
The Siegel-
\Phi
operator

Holomorphic Siegel modular forms posses a Fourier expansion around the maximal dimensional cusp of the Siegel spaces and the constant terms of this Fourier expansion is obtained by the classical Siegel-
\Phi
-operator, taking plainly the limit of the Siegel modular form along the block matrices
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tau_{1}&0\\
0&iv_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{r-1}\times\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{H}^{r}
as
v_{2}\to\infty
. It sends Siegel modular forms of genus
r
to Siegel modular forms of genus
r-1
, and in fact Hecke eigenforms to Hecke eigenforms, see e.g.
[
21
, Section II.5]
,
[
1
, Section 2.3.4 and 4.2.3]
.

In the general context of Siegel Maass forms we are not aware of a general definition of a Siegel operator. This is related to the interesting problem of computing an expansion as in Theorem
1.2
and determining the coefficient growth for
any
Siegel Maass form.

Instead we give an ad hoc definition on the subspace consisting of theta integrals. In our setting, we define the
Siegel-
\Phi
operator
by

(3.30)

(\Phi\theta_{L}^{(r)})(\tau_{1},z):=\lim_{v_{2}\to\infty}v_{2}^{-1/2}\theta_{L}^{(r)}(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tau_{1}&0\\
0&iv_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right),z),

where
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tau_{1}&0\\
0&iv_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{r-1}\times\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{H}^{r}
.
It is clear the limit eliminates all
x\in L^{r}
with the last
coordinate non-zero, and the existence of the limit is justified by the explicit formula

(3.31)

(\Phi\theta^{(r)})(\tau_{1},z)\;=\;\lim_{v_{2}\to\infty}v_{2}^{-1/2}\theta_{L}^{(r)}(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tau_{1}&*\\
*&iv_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right),z)\;=\;\theta_{L}^{(r-1)}(\tau_{1},z)

for any
r\geq 1
. This also justifies our ad-hoc definition:

Proposition 3.4
.

The Siegel-
\Phi
-operator on theta integrals defined by

(3.32)

\Phi I^{(r)}(\tau_{1},f)\,:=\,\lim_{v_{2}\to\infty}v_{2}^{-1/2}I^{(r)}(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tau_{1}&*\\
*&iv_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right),f)

converges and satisfies

(3.33)

\Phi I^{(r)}(\tau_{1},f)\;=\;\Phi I^{(r-1)}(\tau_{1},f)

for any Maass form
f
of weight
0
.

Remark 3.6

The group
\operatorname{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

Lemma 3.7
.

If

Since

\displaystyle(ke_{1}k^{-1},ke_{2}k^{-1})

∎

Proposition 3.8
.

For any smooth and bounded function

on
\mathcal{D}
and for

the orbital integral satisfies

(3.38)

I(e,k^{2}v\,{}^{t}k^{2},f)\;=\;I(e,v,f\mid k).

We compute

\displaystyle I(e,k^{2}v\,{}^{t}k^{2},f)=I(ek^{2},v,f)=I(k\cdot e,v,f)=I(e,v,f\mid k),

Note that the reflection

\displaystyle I_{n}(e,v^{*},f)

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
x,x^{\prime}\in V(\mathbb{R})^{2}
such that
Q(x)=Q(x^{\prime})\in\operatorname{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is invertible.
Then there exists a
g_{1}\in H(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
x=g_{1}x^{\prime}
. It is unique up to multiplication by an element of the center of
H(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We denote by
\langle x\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}x_{1}+\mathbb{R}x_{2}
the subspace of
V(\mathbb{R})
generated by
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in V(\mathbb{R})^{2}
. By assumption,
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
x^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})
are pairs of linearly independent vectors. Let
h
be the linear map defined by

h:\langle x^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\to\langle x\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},\quad h(x_{1}^{\prime})\;=\;x_{1},\quad h(x_{2}^{\prime})\;=\;x_{2}.

Since
Q(x^{\prime})=Q(x)
, this is an isometry of quadratic spaces. Since
Q(x)
is invertible, the quadratic spaces are non-degenerate.

According to Witt’s Theorem there exists an element
h_{1}\in\operatorname{O}(V)(\mathbb{R})
extending

, that is,

h_{1}\mid_{\langle x^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}}=h
. This element is unique up to multiplication from the left with the reflection
\sigma
that fixes
\langle x\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
and acts by multiplication by
-1
on the orthogonal complement. In particular, replacing
h_{1}
by
\sigma\circ h_{1}
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that
h_{1}\in\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{R})
. Then
h_{1}
is the unique
element of
\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
h_{1}\mid_{\langle x^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}}=h
.

Now any preimage
g_{1}\in H(\mathbb{R})
of
h_{1}
under the natural surjective map
H(\mathbb{R})\to\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{R})
has the required properties.
∎

Note that in Lemma
3.9
the reduced norm of
g_{1}
may be negative. For example, this is the case if
x=(e_{1},e_{2})
and
x^{\prime}=(e_{1},-e_{2})
, where
g_{1}
is a scalar multiple of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

Since
T
is positive definite, there exists a
B\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that

. If
\widetilde{B}\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a second matrix with this property, then
\widetilde{B}=kB
with

.
The pair

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})\;=\;(e_{1},e_{2})B\in V(\mathbb{R})^{2}

satisfies
Q(x^{\prime})=T
. If
x\in V(\mathbb{R})^{2}
also satisfies

3.9
there exists a

such that

x\;=\;g_{x}(e_{1},e_{2})B.

3.4.1.
Geodesic Taylor expansion of a Maass form

We will express the coefficients of the Fourier expansion of the theta integral in terms of
geodesic Taylor coefficients of the Maass form
f
at Heegner points. Here we recall some facts on geodesic Taylor expansions, following
[
11
, Section 1]
, see also e.g.
[
17
, Chapter 1.3]
.

Consider the group
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\subset H(\mathbb{R})
and its Cartan decomposition
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})=KAK
, where

(3.41)

A\;=\;\{a_{t}\mid\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}\quad\text{with}\quad a_{t}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix},

and
K=\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The map

(3.42)

\displaystyle[0,\pi)\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{H},\quad(\theta,t)\mapsto k_{\theta}a_{t}i

is injective and has as its image the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
with the point
i

removed. Here
k_{\theta}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in K
as before. Hence any
z\in\mathbb{H}
can be written in the form

z=k_{\theta}a_{t}i

with
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\theta\in[0,\pi)
. The pair
(t,\theta)
defines the standard geodesic polar coordinates of
z
centered at
i
, where
t
is the geodesic distance from
z
to
i
, and
2\theta
the angle determined by the geodesic ray
i\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}
and the geodesic ray from
i
to
z
. According to
[
17
, Chapter 1.3]
, the invariant measure
d\mu(z)=\frac{dx\,dy}{y^{2}}
becomes in these coordinates

d\mu(z)\;=\;(2\sinh t)d\theta\,dt.

Moreover, the invariant Laplacian (
3.20
) is expressed as

(3.43)

\displaystyle\Delta\;=\;-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{\cosh t}{\sinh t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{(2\sinh t)^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}.

By pull-back via (
3.42
), we may view any function
f
on
\mathbb{H}

as a function of
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, which is
\pi\mathbb{Z}
-periodic in
\theta
. Hence it has a
geodesic Taylor expansion at the point
z_{0}=i
of the form

(3.44)

f(k_{\theta}a_{t}z_{0})\;=\;\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n,t}(z_{0})e^{2in\theta},

where the coefficients are given by

(3.45)

\displaystyle\qquad f_{n,t}(z_{0})\;=\;\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f(k_{\theta}a_{t}z_{0})e^{-2in\theta}\,d\theta.

The shape of the Fourier coefficients has for instance been determined by Fay. Recall from Appendix
B
the definition of Legendre’s
P
-function
P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(u)
, which we will use on the interval
u\in(1,\infty)
and for
s\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Suppose that
f
is an eigenfunction of the hyperbolic Laplacian
\Delta
with eigenvalue
\lambda=s(1-s)
. Then the standard geodesic Taylor expansion of
f
at
z_{0}=i
has the form

(3.46)

\displaystyle f(z)\;=\;\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}(z_{0})P^{-|n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))\cdot e^{2in\theta},

where the
f_{n}(z_{0})
are unique complex coefficients and
P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(u)
denotes Legendre’s
P
-function.

Proof.

Using the Laplacian in polar coordintes (
3.43
), the expansion (
3.44
), and the fact that
f
is an eigenfunction, we obtain for the coefficients
f_{n,t}(z_{0})
the differential equation (
B.3
) with
u=\cosh(t)
and parameters
\nu=-s
(or equivalently
s-1
),
\mu=|n|
. This implies that
f_{n,t}(z_{0})
is a linear combination of the Legendre
P
-function and the Legendre
Q
-function. Since the
Q
-function is not regular at
t=0
, the coefficient of
Q
-function vanishes. Hence

f_{n,t}(z_{0})\;=\;f_{n}(z_{0})P^{-|n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))

for a unique coefficient
f_{n}(z_{0})
.
∎

More generally, if
z_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
is any point, we choose a translation matrix
g\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
z_{1}=gi
. We obtain geodesic polar coordinates centered at
z_{1}
by writing
z=gk_{\theta}a_{t}i
. Then
t
denotes again the hyperbolic distance from
z
to
z_{1}
, and
2\theta
the angle determined by the geodesic ray
g(i\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1})
and the geodesic ray from
z_{1}
to
z
. Note that the angle actually depends on the choice of
g
, not only on
z_{1}
. The geodesic Taylor expansion of
f
at the point
z_{1}
(with respect to
g
) is defined as the geodesic Taylor expansion of
f\mid g
at
z_{0}=i
. The corresponding Taylor coefficients are denoted by

f_{n,g}(z_{1})=(f\mid g)_{n}(z_{0})
, or simply by
f_{n}(z_{1})
if the choice of
g
is clear from the context. Hence, we have the expansion

(3.47)

\displaystyle f(z)\;=\;\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n,g}(z_{1})P^{-|n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))\cdot e^{2in\theta},

where

f_{n,g}(z_{1})P^{-|n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f(gk_{\theta}a_{t}i)e^{-2in\theta}\,d\theta,

and
t
denotes the hyperbolic distance from
z
to
z_{1}
and
2\theta
the angle as described above.

Remark 3.11
.

i) Let the notation be as above. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
z_{1}=gi=gk_{\alpha}i
. The corresponding geodesic Taylor coefficients of

satisfy

f_{n,gk_{\alpha}}(z_{1})\;=\;e^{2in\alpha}f_{n,g}(z_{1}).

ii) The geodesic Taylor coefficients can also be computed using differential operators, see e.g.
[

, Theorem 1.2]
.

3.4.2.
Computation of the Whittaker coefficients

We are now ready to compute the Whittaker coefficients

in (

) it suffices to determine the analogous coefficients
I_{n}(x,v,f)
of the orbital integrals for

.

Using the coordinates of Section

, we consider the function

h(k_{\theta}a_{t}z_{0})=P^{-|2n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))e^{-4in\theta}

on
\mathcal{D}
. It is an eigenfunction of
\Delta
with eigenvalue

.
In view of Remark

, we may use the orbital integral

to construct a Whittaker function on

.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. The function

(3.48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{n}(v,s)\,:=\,\det(v)^{1/2}e^{2\pi\operatorname{tr}(v)}\cdot I_{n}(e,v,P^{-|2n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))e^{-4in\theta})

is a Whittaker function of index
(1,n)
and weight
1/2
. More precisely, it has the following properties:

(i)

it has moderate growth,

(ii)

it satisfies

D_{i}\left(\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{n}(v,s)\mathbf{e}(\operatorname{tr}u)\right)\;=\;\lambda_{i}\cdot\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{n}(v,s)\mathbf{e}(\operatorname{tr}u)

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
as in Proposition
3.1
,

(iii)

it satisfies
\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{n}({}^{t}k_{\theta}vk_{\theta},s)=e^{2in\theta}\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{n}(v,s)
for
k_{\theta}\in\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

(iv)

and
\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{n}(v^{*},s)=\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{-n}(v,s)
.

Furthermore, it has the integral representations

(3.49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{+,+}_{n}(v,s)

\displaystyle=\;2(\det v)^{1/2}e^{2\pi\operatorname{tr}(v)}\int\limits_{t>0}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}\!\sinh(t)P^{-|2n|}_{-s}(\cosh{t})

\displaystyle\times\exp(-4in\alpha)\,\exp(-4\pi\operatorname{tr}(Q((a_{t}^{-1}k_{\alpha}^{-1}e)_{z_{0}^{\perp}})v))\,d\alpha\,dt\,.

The argument of the second exponential factor can be computed and will be abbreviated by

(3.50)

\displaystyle\Upsilon

\displaystyle:=\,\Upsilon_{t,\alpha}(v):=\operatorname{tr}(Q((a_{t}^{-1}k_{\alpha}^{-1}e)_{z_{0}^{\perp}})v))

\displaystyle=\;v_{1}^{2}((c^{2}-s^{2})^{2}+4c^{2}s^{2}\cosh(t)^{2})+v_{2}^{2}((c^{2}-s^{2})^{2}c^{2}s^{2}+4\cosh(t)^{2})

\displaystyle=\;4\cosh(t)^{2}(v_{1}^{2}c^{2}s^{2}+v_{2}^{2})+(c^{2}-s^{2})^{2}(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}c^{2}s^{2})

where
c=\cos(\alpha)
and
s=\sin(\alpha)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.12
.

The properties (i)–(iii) follow immediately from Proposition
3.2
and Remark
3.5
. Property (iv) is a consequence of (
3.40
). It remains to prove the integral representation. In fact, we will show slightly more generally, that if
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{C}
is any smooth eigenfunction of
\Delta
with eigenvalue
\lambda=s(1-s)
, then

(3.51)

\displaystyle I_{n}(e,v,f)

\displaystyle=2f_{-2n}(z_{0})\int\limits_{t>0}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}P^{-|2n|}_{-s}(\cosh{t})e^{-4in\alpha}\,e^{-4\pi\Upsilon}\sinh(t)\,d\alpha\,dt.

The assertion then follows by specializing
f
to the function

P^{-|2n|}_{-s}(\cosh(t))e^{-4in\theta}
.

To show (
3.51
), we deduce by (
3.37
) and Proposition
3.8
that

\displaystyle I_{n}(e,v,f)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}I\left(e,{}^{t}k_{\theta}vk_{\theta},f\right)e^{-2in\theta}\,d\theta

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}I\left(e,v,f\mid k_{-\theta/2}\right)e^{-2in\theta}\,d\theta

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}I\left(e,v,f\mid k_{\theta}\right)e^{4in\theta}\,d\theta.

We use the geodesic polar coordinates (
3.42
) around
z_{0}

to express
I(e,v,f)
as follows:

\displaystyle I(e,v,f)

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{H}}f(z)e^{-4\pi\operatorname{tr}(Q(e_{z^{\perp}})v)}\,d\mu(z),

\displaystyle\;=\;2\int_{t>0}\int_{\alpha=0}^{\pi}f(k_{\alpha}a_{t}z_{0})e^{-4\pi\Upsilon}\,\sinh(t)\,d\alpha\,dt.

Putting this into the integral for
I_{n}(e,v,f)
we get

\displaystyle I_{n}(e,v,f)

3.5.1.
Geodesic cartesian coordinate expansion of a Maass form

In the so-called Flensted-Jensen coordinates, see Remark
1.3
i) for the geometric interpretation, we write
g=a_{b}\rho_{t}k_{\theta}\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\rho_{t}
defined in (
3.9
).
This gives a decomposition
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})=ARK
with
R=\{\rho_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
In these coordinates, the Laplacian
\Delta
and the Haar measure are

(3.56)

\Delta\;=\;-\frac{1}{4}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}+2\tanh(2t)\partial_{t}+\frac{4\partial_{b}^{2}}{(\cosh(2t))^{2}}\right),~d\mu(z)\;=\;\frac{dxdy}{y^{2}}\;=\;2(\cosh 2t)dtdb.

Let
f
be a Maass cusp form on
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
with eigenvalue
\lambda=s(1-s)=\frac{1}{4}+r^{2},r\geq 0
under
\Delta
. The subgroup
A\cap\Gamma
is infinite cyclic. Choose a generator
a_{b_{0}}
with
b_{0}>0
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we are interested in understanding the function

(3.57)

C_{n,f}(g)\,:=\,\frac{1}{b_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\cdot b_{0}}f((a_{b}g)\cdot i)\mathbf{e}(-nb/b_{0})db

on
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Since
a_{b+b^{\prime}}=a_{b}\cdot a_{b^{\prime}}
, this function can be decomposed as

C_{n,f}(a_{b^{\prime}}g)\;=\;\mathbf{e}(nb^{\prime}/b_{0})C_{n,f}(g)

for all
b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. Also,
C_{n,f}
is right
\operatorname{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant. So we define the following function

(3.58)

c_{n,f}(t)\,:=\,\mathbf{e}(-nb/b_{0})C_{n,f}(a_{b}\rho_{t}k_{\theta})\;=\;\frac{1}{b_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\cdot b_{0}}f((a_{b}\rho_{t})\cdot i)\mathbf{e}(-nb/b_{0})db.

Notice that

(3.59)

\begin{split}c_{n,f}(-t)&\;=\;C_{n,f}(\rho_{-t}k_{\theta})\;=\;C_{n,f}(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\\
&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\rho_{{t}}k_{-\theta}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\\
&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right))\;=\;c_{n,\sigma(f)}(t),\end{split}

where
\sigma(f)(z)=f(-\overline{z})
. For an even Maass form
f
, i.e. if
\sigma(f)=f
, the function
c_{n,f}(t)
is even in
t
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Using
(\Delta-\lambda)f=0
, we see that
c_{n,f}
satisfies the differential equation

(3.60)

\left(\partial_{t}^{2}+2\tanh(2t)\partial_{t}+\frac{-4(2\pi n/b_{0})^{2}}{(\cosh(2t))^{2}}+4\lambda\right)c_{n,f}(t)\;=\;0.

We make a substitution
u=\tanh(2t)
, which implies
e^{4t}=\frac{1+u}{1-u}
, and set

\widetilde{c}_{n,f}(u)\,:=\,\cosh(2t)^{1/2}c_{n,f}(t).

This turns the equation above into the Legendre differential equation (
B.3
) with parameters
\mu=\pm ir=\pm(s-\frac{1}{2})
and
\nu=-\frac{1}{2}\pm 2n\pi i/b_{0}
. In particular, we define

(3.61)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{n,s}^{\pm}(x)&\,:=\,\frac{1}{C^{\pm}}\left((P^{\mu}_{\nu}(x)+P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(x))\pm(P^{\mu}_{\nu}(-x)+P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(-x))\right),\\
C^{+}&\;=\;2(P^{\mu}_{\nu}(0)+P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(0)),~\quad C^{-}\;=\;2((P^{\mu}_{\nu})^{\prime}(0)+(P^{-\mu}_{\nu})^{\prime}(0))\end{split}

for
-1<x<1
. Then
P^{\mu}_{\nu}(u)
and
Q^{\mu}_{\nu}(u)
are linear combinations of

\mathbb{P}^{\pm}_{n,s}(u)
. It is clear that the function
\mathbb{P}^{\pm}_{n,s}
satisfies the following symmetries

(3.62)

\mathbb{P}^{\pm}_{-n,s}(x)\;=\;\mathbb{P}^{\pm}_{n,s}(x),\quad\mathbb{P}^{\pm}_{n,s}(-x)\;=\;\pm\mathbb{P}^{\pm}_{n,s}(x).

Putting these together gives us the following result.

Proposition 3.16
.

For a Maass cusp form
f
with eigenvalue
s(1-s)
, let
c_{n,f}(t)
be the function defined in (
3.58
). Then

(3.63)

c_{n,f}(t)\;=\;\cosh(2t)^{-1/2}\left(\gamma_{f,n}\mathbb{P}^{+}_{n,s}(\tanh(2t))+\gamma^{\prime}_{f,n}\mathbb{P}^{-}_{n,s}(\tanh(2t))\right),

where the constants
\gamma_{f,n},\gamma^{\prime}_{f,n}\in\mathbb{C}
are given by

(3.64)

\begin{split}\gamma_{f,n}&\;=\;\frac{1}{b_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\cdot b_{0}}f(a_{b}\cdot i)\mathbf{e}(-nb/b_{0})db,\\
\gamma^{\prime}_{f,n}&\;=\;\frac{-i}{b_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\cdot b_{0}}(\partial_{z}f)(a_{b}\cdot i)\mathbf{e}(-nb/b_{0})ie^{b}db.\end{split}

Remark 3.17
.

Since
f
has weight 0, the derivative
(\partial_{z}f)(z)
is modular of weight 2. If we set
z=a_{b}\cdot i=e^{b}i
, then
ie^{b}db=dz
and the integral expression of
\gamma^{-}_{f,n}
is the cycle integral of the differential form
(\partial f)(z)dz
against the exponential function.
Using these coefficients, we have the following expansion

(3.65)

f\left(e^{b}\frac{i\cosh t+\sinh t}{i\sinh t+\cosh t}\right)\;=\;\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\gamma_{f,n}\mathbb{P}^{+}_{n,s}(\tanh(2t))+\gamma^{\prime}_{f,n}\mathbb{P}^{-}_{n,s}(\tanh(2t))\right)\frac{\mathbf{e}(nb/b_{0})}{\cosh(2t)^{1/2}}.

Proof of Proposition
3.16
.

Let
f^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{2}(f\pm\sigma(f))
.
From (
3.60
) and the subsequent discussion, we obtain (
3.63
) with some constants
\gamma_{f,n},\gamma^{\prime}_{f,n}
independent of
t
. Since
c_{n,f^{\pm}}(-t)=\pm c_{n,f^{\pm}}(t)
by (
3.59
), decomposing
c_{n,f}(t)=c_{n,f^{+}}(t)+c_{n,f^{-}}(t)
and using the parity of
\mathbb{P}^{\pm}_{n,r}
from (
3.62
) gives us

c_{n,f^{+}}(t)\;=\;\cosh(2t)^{-1/2}\gamma_{f,n}\mathbb{P}^{+}_{n,s}(\tanh(2t)),\quad c_{n,f^{-}}(t)\;=\;\cosh(2t)^{-1/2}\gamma^{\prime}_{f,n}\mathbb{P}^{-}_{n,s}(\tanh(2t)).

To determine
\gamma_{f,n}
, we simply put
t=0
and use
f^{+}(a_{b}\cdot i)=f(a_{b}\cdot i)
. To determine
\gamma_{f,n}^{-}
, we look at the first term of the Taylor expansion at
t=0
. This finishes the proof.

4.
The action of the Hecke algebra

The goal of this section is to show in Proposition
4.4
that the action of the Hecke operators on the theta integrals can be expressed in terms of the usual Hecke action on the Maass forms. As usual, we mainly focus on
r=2
, but for comparison we also record the case
r=1
and check compatibility under the Siegel operator. The method is an application of the strategy of Rallis-Zhuravlev. As in these sources we restrict
throughout this section to primes

.

As guide to notation, straight letters
\widehat{T}_{p,i},T_{p,i}
denote Hecke operators expressed in global Hecke rings, whereas calligraphic letters
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{p,i},\mathcal{T}_{p,i}
denote Hecke operators expressed in local Hecke rings. Decorations by a hat denote Hecke operators on the metaplectic side, whereas undecorated
T
’s denotes Hecke operators on the orthogonal side, making use of the fact that we do not need here Hecke operators for the symplectic group.

Proof.

Remark 4.7

Using the action of the Siegel-

5.
A computation using the Siegel-Weil formula

In this section we consider the theta integral
I(\tau,f)
for the constant function
f=1
. Our general results of Section
3
imply in this case that the theta integral is a Siegel Maass form of weight
1/2
and that the cardinalities of the sets
R_{T}
occur as (non-archimedean parts of) its Fourier coefficients.
We give a different proof of this fact by means of the Siegel-Weil formula. It identifies the theta integral as a specific Siegel Eisenstein series. We compute its Fourier coefficients using explicit formulas of Kudla and Yang for local Whittaker functions. This leads to a new proof and a generalization of the result of Rickards on the number of intersection points of
b
-linked geodesics, stated as Theorem
2.6
in Section
2.1
.

5.1.
Siegel Eisenstein series

We begin by recalling some facts about Siegel Eisenstein series following
[
24
]
and
[
4
]
, specialized to genus
r=2
. Recall the notation set up for the Weil representation in Section
3.2
. In particular,
F=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for a place
p\leq\infty
or the ring
\mathbb{A}
of adeles of
\mathbb{Q}
.
For a character
\chi
of
F^{\times}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
, let
I(s,\chi)
be the degenerate principal series representation of
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,F}
consisting of smooth functions
\Phi(s)
on
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,F}
satisfying

\Phi([n(b)m(a),z]g,s)\;=\;z\,\chi(\det a)\gamma(\det a,\frac{1}{2}\psi)^{-1}|\det a|^{s+\rho_{2}}\Phi(g,s),

where
\rho_{2}\;=\;\frac{3}{2}
and
m(a),n(b)\in\operatorname{Sp}_{2}(F)
are as in the beginning of Section
3.2
. Such a section
\Phi
is called standard, if its restriction to
\widetilde{K}_{F}
is independent of
s
. Here, for
p<\infty
, we denote by
\widetilde{K}_{F}
the preimage of
\operatorname{Sp}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
under the double covering map
\operatorname{pr}:\operatorname{Mp}_{2,F}\to\operatorname{Sp}_{2}(F)
, and for
p=\infty
the preimage of the maximal compact subgroup
\operatorname{U}(2)
, and the product of these if
F=\mathbb{A}
.

If
T\in\operatorname{Sym}_{2}(F)
is a symmetric matrix, the Whittaker function of
\Phi
with respect to
T
is the function

(5.1)

W_{T}(g,s,\Phi)\;=\;\int_{\operatorname{Sym}_{2}(F)}\Phi(wn(b)g,s)\psi(-\operatorname{tr}(Tb))\,d_{\psi}b,

on
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,F}
, where
d_{\psi}b
is the self-dual Haar measure on
\operatorname{Sym}_{2}(F)
with respect to the pairing
(b_{1},b_{2})\mapsto\psi(\operatorname{tr}(b_{1}b_{2}))
. It has the transformation behavior

(5.2)

\displaystyle W_{T}(n(b)m(a)g,s,\Phi)

\displaystyle\;=\;\psi(\operatorname{tr}(Tb))\chi(a)^{-1}|a|^{\rho_{2}-s}\gamma(a,\frac{1}{2}\psi)\cdot W_{{}^{t}aTa}(g,s,\Phi).

Here we have shortened
\gamma(a,\frac{1}{2}\psi)=\gamma(\det a,\frac{1}{2}\psi)
and similarly for
\chi(a)
. When
F=\mathbb{R}
and
\det a>0
, then
\gamma(a,\frac{1}{2}\psi)=1
. If
F=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
we usually write
\Phi_{p}
for sections of
I(s,\chi)
for
p\leq\infty
, and similarly write
W_{T,p}(g,s,\Phi_{p})
for the corresponding Whittaker functions.

We now define Eisenstein series on
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}}
. Recall that there is a unique splitting homomorphism
\operatorname{Sp}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})\to\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}}
to the covering
\operatorname{pr}:\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}}\to\operatorname{Sp}_{2}(\mathbb{A})
. We denote its image by
G_{\mathbb{Q}}
and define
P_{\mathbb{Q}}=\widetilde{P}_{\mathbb{A}}\cap G_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
\Phi=\prod_{p\leq\infty}\Phi_{p}\in I(s,\chi)
be a standard factorizable section of the induced representation of
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}}
. The corresponding Eisenstein series is defined by

(5.3)

\displaystyle E(g,s,\Phi)\;=\;\sum_{\gamma\in P_{\mathbb{Q}}\backslash G_{\mathbb{Q}}}\Phi(\gamma g,s),

where
g\in\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}}
. It converges normally for
\Re(s)>\rho_{2}
and has a meromorphic continuation in
s
to the whole complex plane. Moreover, it satisfies a functional equation relating values at
s
and
-s
. The Eisenstein series has a Fourier expansion of the form

(5.4)

\displaystyle E(g,s,\Phi)\;=\;\sum_{T\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})}E_{T}(g,s,\Phi).

When
T\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
is non-singular, the
T
-th Fourier coefficient factorizes,

(5.5)

\displaystyle E_{T}(g,s,\Phi)\;=\;\prod_{p\leq\infty}W_{T,p}(g_{p},s,\Phi_{p}),

into a product of local Whittaker functions as in (
5.1
).

5.2.
The Siegel-Weil formula

We use the Siegel-Weil formula to compute the theta integral
I(\tau,1)
defined in (
3.21
) for the constant function
1
. Since
V
is anisotropic over
\mathbb{Q}
the theta integral converges. In this case its Fourier coefficients (
3.24
) can be written as

(5.6)

\displaystyle I_{T}(v,1)

\displaystyle\;=\;(\det v)^{1/2}e^{2\pi\operatorname{tr}Tv}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in R_{T}\end{subarray}}I(x,v,1)

\displaystyle\;=\;(\det v)^{1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in R_{T}\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}e^{-2\pi\operatorname{tr}((Q(x_{z^{\perp}})-Q(x_{z}))v)}\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle\;=\;(\det v)^{1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in R_{T}\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash H(\mathbb{R})^{+}}\varphi_{\infty}(v,h^{-1}x)dh,

where
\varphi_{\infty}(v,x):=e^{-2\pi\operatorname{tr}((Q(x_{z_{0}^{\perp}})-Q(x_{z_{0}}))v)}
as in (
3.17
), and
2dh=d\mu(z)d\theta
is normalized as in
[
26
]
.
Here the factor
\pi
is the volume of
\mathrm{PSO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cong Z(\mathbb{R})\backslash H(\mathbb{R})^{+}
since
\mathcal{D}\cong\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{PSO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

We now construct the standard factorizable section used as input for the Eisenstein series. Recall that there is an
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,F}
-equivariant map

(5.7)

\displaystyle\lambda:S(V^{2}(F))\rightarrow I(0,\chi_{V}),\quad\lambda(\phi)(g)\;=\;\big(\omega(g)\phi\big)(0),

from the space of Schwartz functions to the induced representation associated with the quadratic character
\chi_{V}
of
V
.
We will also denote by
\lambda(\phi)
the associated standard section in
I(s,\chi_{V})
with
\lambda(\phi)(g,0)=\lambda(\phi)(g)
.

Let
\widehat{L}=L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
, and write
\varphi_{L}=\operatorname{char}(\widehat{L}^{2})\in S(V^{2}(\mathbb{A}_{f}))
for the characteristic function of
\widehat{L}^{2}
.
Applying the intertwining operator
\lambda
, we obtain a corresponding section
\lambda(\varphi_{L})
of the induced representation of
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}_{f}}
. At the archimedian place the Gaussian
\varphi_{\infty}\in S(V^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
transforms in weight
1/2
under
K_{\infty}
. Its image under the intertwining operator
\lambda
is the standard section
\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2}
of weight
1/2
in the induced representation of
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{R}}
(normalized such that
\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2}(1)=1
). The product

\lambda(\varphi_{L})\otimes\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2}

defines a factorizable standard section in the induced representation of
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}}
.
We will often view the corresponding Siegel Eisenstein series

E(g,s,\lambda(\varphi_{L})\otimes\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2})

on
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{A}}
as a function on the Siegel upper half space
\mathbb{H}_{2}
.
To this end, for
\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
with real part
u
and imaginary part
v
, we choose
a\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
with
a\,{}^{t}a=v
and let
g_{\tau}=n(u)m(a)\in\operatorname{Sp}_{2,\mathbb{R}}
.
Then the Eisenstein series

(5.8)

\displaystyle E(\tau,s,\lambda(\varphi_{L})\otimes\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2})\;=\;(\det v)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\cdot E(g_{\tau},s,\lambda(\varphi_{L})\otimes\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2})

is a (non-holomorphic) Siegel modular form of weight
1/2
and genus
2
for a congruence subgroup of
\operatorname{Mp}_{2,\mathbb{Q}}
. The following result is now a consequence of the Siegel-Weil formula, see e.g.
[
23
]
,
[
46
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
\Gamma=H(\mathbb{Q})^{+}\cap K\subset H(\mathbb{Q})^{+}
is an arithmetic subgroup such that the image of the open compact subgroup
K\subset H(\hat{\mathbb{Q}})
under the spinor norm is
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
.
Let
\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Gamma})
be the volume of
X_{\Gamma}
with respect to the measure
d\mu(z)
.
The above Eisenstein series is holomorphic in
s
at
s=0
. The theta integral
I(\tau,1)
is given by

\frac{2}{\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Gamma})}I(\tau,1)=E(\tau,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L})\otimes\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2}).

In particular, for the Fourier coefficients in (
3.22
) we have

(5.9)

\frac{2}{\operatorname{vol}(X_{\Gamma})}I_{T}(v,1)\;=\;(\det v)^{-\frac{1}{4}}W_{T,\infty}(m(a),0,\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2})\cdot\prod_{p<\infty}W_{T,p}(1,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L_{p}})).

Using the local archimedean Siegel-Weil formula in
[
26
]
, we can express the size of the set
R_{T}
in (
3.25
) in terms of values of local Whittaker functions at non-archimedean places.

Let
K=K_{\mathfrak{M}}\subset H(\mathbb{A}_{f})
by the compact open subgroup defined in (
2.5
) and put
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}=H(\mathbb{Q})^{+}\cap K_{\mathfrak{M}}
. Recall from (
2.6
) that the corresponding Shimura curve
X_{\mathfrak{M}}=X_{K_{\mathfrak{M}}}
is connected.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
T
be invertible, and let
R_{T}
be the set of representatives defined in (
3.25
).
Then

(5.10)

|R_{T}|\;=\;2^{3/2}\pi\operatorname{vol}(X_{\mathfrak{M}})\prod_{p<\infty}W_{T,p}(1,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L_{p}})).

Proof.

We employ the local Siegel-Weil formula, which expresses orbital integrals associated with Schwartz-Bruhat functions in terms of local Whittaker functions.
According to
[
24
, Corollary 5.3.5]
we have

(5.11)

\displaystyle\prod_{p<\infty}W_{T,p}(1,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L_{p}}))\;=\;C_{\infty}(V,\alpha,\beta,\psi)^{-1}\cdot\prod_{p<\infty}O_{T,p}(\varphi_{L,p})\,.

Here the local orbital integral on the right hand side is defined by

O_{T,p}(\varphi_{L,p})\;=\;\int_{\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{Q}_{p})}\varphi_{L_{p}}(h^{-1}x_{0})\,d_{p}h

if there exists an
x_{0}\in V^{2}(\mathbb{Q})
with
Q(x_{0})=T
, and by
0
if there exists no such
x_{0}
. Here we have used that
\varphi_{L,p}
is even, and therefore the
\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
-orbital integral is equal to the

\operatorname{O}(V)(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
-orbital integral. Finally the measures are normalized as in
[
24
, page 124]
, and the constant
C_{\infty}(V,\alpha,\beta,\psi)
is defined in Proposition 5.3.3 of
[
24
]
. It is independent of
T
and of the choice of the Schwartz function in the argument.

Let
\bar{K}\subset\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{A}_{f})
be the compact open subgroup which is given by the image of
K=K_{\mathfrak{M}}\subset H(\mathbb{A}_{f})
under the projection
H(\mathbb{A}_{f})\to\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{A}_{f})
and put

\bar{\Gamma}\,:=\,\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{Q})\cap(\operatorname{SO}(\mathbb{R})\times\bar{K})\;=\;\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{M}}^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}.

Since
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{M}}^{\times}
contains an element of reduced norm
-1
, the group
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}/\{\pm 1\}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{M}}^{1}/\{\pm 1\}
is a subgroup of
\bar{\Gamma}
of index
2
. Since
\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{A}_{f})=\operatorname{SO}(\mathbb{Q})\bar{K}
,

[
24
, Proposition 5.3.6]
implies that

\displaystyle\prod_{p<\infty}O_{T,p}(\varphi_{L,p})

\displaystyle\;=\;\operatorname{vol}(\bar{K})\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in V^{2}(\mathbb{Q})/\bar{\Gamma}\\
Q(x)=T\end{subarray}}\varphi_{L}(x)

\displaystyle\;=\;\operatorname{vol}(\bar{K})\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in L^{2}/(\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}/\{\pm 1\})\\
Q(x)=T\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{vol}(\bar{K})\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in L^{2}/\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}\\
Q(x)=T\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{vol}(\bar{K})\cdot|R_{T}|.

Here we have also used that the stabilizer
\operatorname{SO}(V)(\mathbb{Q})_{x}
of any
x\in V^{2}(\mathbb{Q})
with
Q(x)=T
is trivial and that
\{\pm 1\}\subset\Gamma_{\mathfrak{M}}
acts trivially on
V(\mathbb{Q})
.
Inserting the latter formula into (
5.11
), we obtain

(5.12)

\displaystyle\prod_{p<\infty}W_{T,p}(1,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L_{p}}))\;=\;\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\bar{K})}{2C_{\infty}(V,\alpha,\beta,\psi)}\cdot|R_{T}|.

According to equations (5.3.46) and (5.2.11) of
[
24
]
, the constant on the right hand side is given by

\displaystyle C_{\infty}(V,\alpha,\beta,\psi)\;=\;2^{-1}C_{\infty}(V)\;=\;2^{1/2}\gamma_{\infty}(V)^{2}D(B)^{2}.

As our choice of
w
agrees with the one in
[
26
]
, the constant
\gamma_{\infty}(V)^{2}
here is 1, and differs from the one in
[
24
]
by a factor of
-1
(see the paragraph above Theorem 5.2.7 on page 116 of
[
24
]
).
Moreover, by Lemma 5.3.9 (iii) of
[
24
]
and (
2.7
), we have

\operatorname{vol}(\bar{K})\;=\;\pi^{-1}D(B)^{2}\operatorname{vol}(X_{\mathfrak{M}},d\mu)^{-1}.

In fact, this is first seen if
\mathfrak{M}=1
, and then a simple index argument shows that it also holds for general
\mathfrak{M}
. Consequently, we get

\frac{C_{\infty}(V,\alpha,\beta,\psi)}{\operatorname{vol}(\bar{K})}\;=\;2^{1/2}\pi\operatorname{vol}(X_{\mathfrak{M}},d\mu).

Putting this into (
5.12
) concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

The formula of Theorem
5.2
can also be deduced from the global Siegel-Weil formula, together with a comparison of asymptotics as
v
tends to infinity (in a suitable direction). Since this argument aligns nicely with the one given in
[
19
]
we briefly explain it here in the case when
T
is positive definite.

Combining (
3.24
) and (
5.9
), we find

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\operatorname{vol}(X_{\mathfrak{M}})}I_{T}(v,1)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{2}{\operatorname{vol}(X_{\mathfrak{M}})}(\det v)^{1/2}e^{2\pi\operatorname{tr}Tv}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in R_{T}\end{subarray}}I(x,v,1)

\displaystyle\;=\;(\det v)^{-\frac{1}{4}}W_{T,\infty}(m(a),0,\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2})\cdot\prod_{p<\infty}W_{T,p}(1,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L_{p}})).

We write
T={}^{t}BB
with
B\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
as before and specialize to

v=rB^{-1}\,{}^{t}\!B^{-1}
with positive real
r
. In particular, we consider the archimedian Whittaker function at the argument
m(\sqrt{r}B^{-1})
. Applying Proposition
3.3
, we find for
x\in R_{T}
that

I(x,v,1)\;=\;I(x,rB^{-1}\,{}^{t}\!B^{-1},1)\;=\;I(e,r1_{2},1).

On the other hand, by
[
4
, Lemma 4.2]
, we have

W_{T,\infty}(m(\sqrt{r}B^{-1}),0,\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2})\;=\;|B|^{-3/2}W_{1,\infty}(m(\sqrt{r}1_{2}),0,\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2}).

Inserting these identities, we obtain

(5.13)

\displaystyle\phantom{\;=\;}\frac{2}{\operatorname{vol}(X_{\mathfrak{M}})}|R_{T}|e^{4\pi r}I(e,r1_{2},1)

\displaystyle\;=\;r^{-3/2}\prod_{p<\infty}W_{T,p}(1,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L_{p}}))\cdot W_{1,\infty}(m(\sqrt{r}1_{2}),0,\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2}).

From the definition of
I(e,r1_{2},1)
one can derive the asymptotic behavior

(5.14)

\displaystyle I(e,r1_{2},1)\;=\;\frac{1}{4r}e^{-8\pi r}(1+O(r^{-1}))

as
r\to\infty
. Moreover, using similar arguments as in
[
4
, Section 4]
, it can be shown that

(5.15)

\displaystyle W_{1,\infty}(m(\sqrt{r}1_{2}),0,\Phi_{\infty}^{1/2})\;=\;\sqrt{2}\pi\sqrt{r}e^{-4\pi r}(1+O(r^{-1}))

as
r\to\infty
. Plugging (
5.14
) and (
5.15
) into (
5.13
) and taking the limit
r\to\infty
, we derive

\frac{1}{2\operatorname{vol}(X_{\mathfrak{M}})}|R_{T}|\;=\;\sqrt{2}\pi\prod_{p<\infty}W_{T,p}(1,0,\lambda(\varphi_{L_{p}})),

which yields the desired formula for
|R_{T}|
.

5.3.
Non-archimedian Whittaker functions

Let
p
be a finite prime and

non-singular. For

odd, or for

with
\det(T)\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and

, the matrix

Appendix B
Some special functions

This appendex summarizes properties of special functions, solutions to some standard differential equations, and in particular normalizations from various sources, most frequently relying on
[
6
]
and
[
13
]
.

Hypergeometric functions

The hypergeometric function
[
6
, Section 15]

(B.1)

F(a,b,c;z)\;=\;\sum_{i\geq 0}\frac{(a)_{i}(b)_{i}}{(c)_{i}}\,z^{i}\,,

where
(x)_{i}=\Gamma(x+i)/\Gamma(x)
denotes the Pochhammer symbol, is the unique (up to scale)
regular solution near
z=0
of the hypergeometric differential equation

(B.2)

z(1-u)\frac{d^{2}{w}}{dz^{2}}+(c-(a+b+1)z)\frac{d{w}}{dz}-abw\;=\;0\,.

Legendre functions

Legendre’s
P
-function
P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(u)
and
Q
-function are a basis of solutions of the second order linear differential equation
[
6
, Section 14.1]

(B.3)

\frac{d^{2}{\varphi}}{du^{2}}+\frac{2u}{u^{2}-1}\frac{d{\varphi}}{du}-\Bigl(\frac{\nu(\nu+1)}{u^{2}-1}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{(u^{2}-1)^{2}}\Bigr)\varphi\;=\;0\,.

More precisely,
P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(u)
is the solution which is regular for
\mu\geq 0
at
u=1
. It is given on the interval
u\in(1,\infty)
and
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
as

(B.4)

P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(u)\;=\;\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+\mu)}\Bigl(\frac{u-1}{u+1}\Bigr)^{\mu/2}F(1+\nu,-\nu,1+\mu,(1-u)/2)\,.

In fact by
[
6
, Eq. 14.8.7]

\lim_{u\to 1^{+}}P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(u)\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+\mu)}\Bigl(\frac{u-1}{2}\Bigr)^{\mu/2}

for
\mu\neq-1,-2,-3,\dots
. The sign change in the upper index is given
by
[
6
, Eq. 14.9.13]

P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(u)\;=\;\frac{\Gamma(1+\nu-\mu)}{\Gamma(1+\nu+\mu)}P^{\mu}_{\nu}(u)

whenever
\nu
is such that the
\Gamma
-function has no poles. We do not need the normalization of the
Q
-function
Q^{\mu}_{\nu}(u)
, but see
[
6
, Section 14.3]
.

Whittaker functions

The Whittaker differential equation is the second order differential equation

[
6
, (13.14.1)]

(B.5)

\frac{d^{2}{W}}{dy^{2}}+\Bigl(-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\lambda}{y}+\frac{1/4-\mu^{2}}{y^{2}}\Bigr)W\;=\;0.

There is a unique solution of this differential equation, the Whittaker function
W_{\lambda,\mu}(y)
, whose asymptotic behaviour is (see
[
6
, (13.14.21)]
)

(B.6)

W_{\lambda,\mu}(y)\,\sim\,e^{-y/2}y^{\lambda}\qquad(y\to\infty),

see e.g.
[
13
, Chapter 9.220]
or
[
6
, Section 13.14]
for series representations and integral representations.

There are various integral relations between Legendre and Whittaker functions,
for example we need

(B.7)

\int_{w>0}P_{\nu}^{0}(\sqrt{1+w})\frac{e^{-\beta w}}{\sqrt{1+w}}\,dw\;=\;\beta^{-3/4}e^{\beta/2}W_{-1/4,\nu/2+1/4}(\beta)\,.

This integral is for instance evaluated in
[
13
, Chapter 7.146]
(however citing incorrectly from
[
10
, Formula 180(8)]
with index
+1/4
). The above formula leads to the correct asymptotic behavior.)
