White matter integrity buffers the impact of screen time on inhibitory control in youth
Authors: Moonyoung Jang, Seung Yun Choi, Jungyoun Janice Min, Kyung Hwa Lee, Seunghui Han, Kahyun Choi, Ji Yeon Kim, Grace Chung, Dongil Chung, Sang Ah Lee, Jiook Cha, Jae-Won Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Excessive screen time is associated with reduced inhibitory control, a core component of executive function in children and adolescents. However, whether neurobiological factors buffer this effect remains unclear. The aim of this cross-sectional study was to investigate the relationship between screen time and inhibitory control and whether white matter integrity, a marker of axonal coherence, moderates this relationship in children and adolescents. A total of 150 participants aged 7–17 years completed the Stroop Color and Word Test, which was administered to assess inhibitory control. The participants underwent diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging to enable evaluation of white matter integrity. Screen time was determined using caregiver reports. White matter integrity was indexed using fractional anisotropy (FA) in eight tracts: the bilateral superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) I/II, SLF III, arcuate fasciculus, and cingulum bundle. Whether white matter integrity moderates the relationship between screen time and inhibitory control was analyzed. Longer screen times were associated with weaker inhibitory control. FA in the right SLF I/II, SLF III, and arcuate fasciculus was inversely related to screen time. The right SLF I/II significantly moderated the relationship between screen time and inhibitory control. The effect of increasing screen time on reducing inhibitory control was observed only at lower FA levels. The moderating effect was not significant for any other tract. These findings indicate that white matter integrity may reflect neurocognitive resilience to screen time-related reductions in inhibitory control. The right SLF I/II FA may reflect individual differences in susceptibility to the cognitive correlates of screen exposure, highlighting the relevance of white matter integrity in understanding the variability in the neurocognitive outcomes associated with screen time.

Introduction

Advancements in digital devices, such as smartphones, tablets, and laptops, have significantly increased access to digital technology across all age groups. Notably, screen use has increased prominently among youth (Trott et al., 2022), a pattern attributed to the growing prevalence of screen-based learning; the digitization of peer interactions through social networking platforms; and adolescents’ increasing reliance on digital media to fulfill emotional needs such as stress relief (Camerini et al., 2023, Piccerillo and Digennaro, 2025, Nesi et al., 2018). While certain forms of screen use may offer educational, social, or recreational benefits, growing evidence suggests that excessive or unregulated screen use may pose risks to neurodevelopment and mental health in youth (Stiglic and Viner, 2019). Indeed, increasing evidence links excessive screen time in youth to developmental delays (Madigan et al., 2019), cognitive impairments (Law et al., 2023), sleep disturbances (Brosnan et al., 2024), attentional difficulties (Li et al., 2021), heightened impulsivity (Lissak, 2018), and high risk for internalizing and externalizing psychopathology (Eirich et al., 2022).

A particularly concerning report indicates that increased screen time is associated with reduced inhibitory control (Law et al., 2023, Mallawaarachchi et al., 2024, Wallace et al., 2023). This is especially problematic, as reduced inhibitory control impairs youths’ ability to regulate screen time, potentially creating a vicious cycle wherein poor self-regulation increases screen use (Wallace et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023). Inhibitory control is a critical construct in youth mental health research as it is a foundational component of the executive function system, and its impairment can lead to cascading disruptions in higher-order cognitive processes, including working memory, attentional shifting and planning (Barkley, 1997, Nigg, 2000, Miyake and Friedman, 2012). Deficits in inhibitory control have been closely linked to core symptoms, observed in prevalent adolescent psychiatric conditions, such as inattention and impulsivity, represented by attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (Beyens et al., 2018, Hwang et al., 2016).

However, the neurobiological factors that may buffer the negative effects of screen use on inhibitory control remain unknown. One promising candidate may be white matter integrity. Given the neuroplastic nature of the developing brain, environmental factors, such as screen exposure, may significantly influence white matter development (Ferschmann et al., 2022, Hutton et al., 2020). In particular, prior studies have suggested that increases in screen time are associated with reduced white matter integrity, potentially through pathways involving insufficient sleep and diminished interpersonal interactions (Hutton et al., 2020, Zou et al., 2021). Moreover, white matter structure has been reported to be associated with inhibitory control (Parlatini et al., 2023, Gagnon et al., 2022). Importantly, higher white matter integrity may facilitate more efficient neural communication, preserving inhibitory control despite environmental challenges (Filley, 2010), and can thus serve as a neurobiological marker of resilience that buffers the impact of excessive screen use (Eaton et al., 2022, Bolsinger et al., 2018). Consequently, the association between screen use and inhibitory control may vary with individual differences in white matter integrity. White matter integrity is most commonly indexed by fractional anisotropy (FA), a metric derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) reflecting the directionality and coherence of white matter fibers (Basser et al., 1994). Although FA can be extracted from multiple white matter tracts, we focused on the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), cingulum bundle, and arcuate fasciculus, which are major frontoparietal and frontotemporal pathways implicated in executive functioning. These tracts facilitate communication between prefrontal control regions and distributed cortical and subcortical areas, thereby supporting attentional regulation, cognitive control, and response inhibition (Parlatini et al., 2023, Gagnon et al., 2022).

To our knowledge, no previous study has examined white matter integrity as a moderating factor in the relationship between screen time and inhibitory control in children and adolescents. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate (1) the association between screen time and inhibitory control, (2) the correlations between FA values with screen time and inhibitory control, and (3) whether the integrity of white matter tracts related to response inhibition moderates this association. We hypothesized that greater screen time would be associated with poorer inhibitory control, and that higher white matter integrity would exert a protective effect, buffering against the negative impact of screen use on inhibitory control (Fig. 1). We assessed screen time in youth aged 7–17 years and measured their inhibitory control using the Stroop test (Stroop, 1935). We first explored the correlation between screen time and Stroop performance, and between FA values with screen time and inhibitory control. Then we conducted a moderation analysis to examine whether the association between screen time and inhibitory control was moderated by white matter integrity using FA values extracted from eight white matter tracts (bilateral superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) I/II, SLF III, arcuate fasciculus, and cingulum bundle) implicated in response inhibition.Fig. 1Conceptual model illustrating the moderating role of white matter microstructure on the association between screen time and inhibitory control.

Methods

Participants

A total of 180 individuals (mean [SD] age = 14.12 [2.13] years, 94 female) were enrolled between February 2022 and September 2024 through Seoul National University and the Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology from the WECANDOIT project (Wellness Effects on Childhood and Adolescent Neurocognitive Development Outcomes and Individual Trajectories) (Min et al., 2025).

All participants completed diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and structured questionnaires. The study included school-enrolled children and adolescents aged 7–17 years. The exclusion criteria included participants with (1) diagnosed intellectual disability; (2) prior clinical diagnosis of a psychiatric or learning disorder; (3) insufficient fluency in Korean to complete assessments reliably; (4) color vision deficiencies; or (5) contraindications for MRI scanning, including metallic implants or possible pregnancy.

Due to an unexpected shutdown and resulting damage to the MRI scanner during the participant recruitment phase, MRI data could not be obtained for 26 participants. Additionally, four participants were excluded from the MRI analysis due to contraindications, such as piercings or excessive head motion during scanning. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 150 participants were included in the final analysis (mean [SD] age = 13.63 [1.94] years, 80 female), and their ages and sex distribution did not differ significantly from the 30 excluded participants.

All participants and their legal guardians provided written informed consent. The study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University (IRB Nos. 2112/004–006 and 2202/003–003).

Assessment of screen time and inhibitory control

To assess youths’ screen time, we adapted the Screen Time Questionnaire developed for the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Bagot et al., 2022). The adapted parent-report measure comprises 23 items that need to be completed by the caregiver. Among these, 12 items assessed the daily average time spent on screen-based activities during weekdays and weekends, including: (1) watching TV or movies, (2) watching online videos, (3) playing video games, (4) texting via phone, tablet, or computer, (5) using social media, and (6) engaging in video chatting. An additional six items assessed problematic behaviors related to the child’s screen media use, including craving, withdrawal-like symptoms, dependence, functional impairment and impaired control, as perceived by the parent. Four other items measured parental concerns and perceptions regarding the child’s online environment, such as exposure to harmful content, inappropriate behavior and excessive engagement. Finally, one item asked parents to indicate which type of screen media their child used most frequently (non-scalar). A composite variable representing overall screen time was computed by summing all scalar items, excluding the non-scalar item indicating the most frequently used media type. For each screen-based activity, caregivers rated the child’s average daily duration of use separately for weekdays and weekends using a 7-point ordinal scale: 1 = none, 2 = <30 min, 3 = 30 min to < 1 h, 4 = 1 to < 2 h, 5 = 2 to < 3 h, 6 = 3 to < 4 h, and 7 = ≥4 h. These ordinal ratings were summed across items to derive a composite index of overall screen time.

To evaluate inhibitory control, the Stroop Color and Word Test (Stroop, 1935) was administered by trained clinical psychologists following standardized procedures. The Stroop interference score was derived by subtracting the raw reaction time obtained on the Color Naming task from that on the Color-Word Reading task. The resulting score was then converted into an age-normed T-score using the normative data provided in the Stroop test administration and scoring manual (Golden et al., 2003). This T-score was used as a standardized index of inhibitory control, reflecting the ability to inhibit automatic responses to conflicting stimuli. Specifically, a higher interference score reflects poorer inhibitory control.

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) acquisition

Diffusion-weighted images were acquired on a Siemens 3 T MAGNETOM TrioTim scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. A spin-echo echo-planar imaging (SE-EPI) sequence with multiband acceleration (factor = 3) was used to enable rapid acquisition with whole-brain coverage. Acquisition parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 3200 ms, echo time (TE) = 110 ms, and isotropic voxel size = 2.3 × 2.3 × 2.3 mm³ . Diffusion sensitizing gradients were applied using multiple b-values: 0 and 1000 s/mm² (20 directions), 2000 s/mm² (30 directions), and 3000 s/mm² (64 directions) (Min et al., 2025).

dMRI preprocessing

All preprocessing steps were conducted using the Brainlife.io platform (Hayashi et al., 2024), an open-source cloud platform designed to support reproducible, scalable, and shareable neuroimaging analysis. Diffusion volumes acquired at the three b-values, all with left–right (LR) phase encoding, were merged into a single multi-shell dataset using the merge DWI app. In addition to the primary DWI images, a reverse phase-encoded (RL direction) b= 0 image (i.e., blip image) was provided to correct for susceptibility-induced distortions, using the mrtrix3 preprocess app. The preprocessing pipeline included denoising, Gibbs ringing artifact removal, Rician background noise suppression, and alignment of DWI data to the T1-weighted structural image via boundary-based registration (BBR) from FSL. Bias field correction was performed using the N4 algorithm implemented in ANTs. Whole-brain probabilistic tractography was then performed using the MRtrix3 - Whole-brain Anatomically-Constrained Tractography (WMC ACT) app, employing the iFOD2 (probabilistic Fiber Orientation Distribution) algorithm with the following tractography parameters: minimum streamline length of 5 mm, maximum length of 250 mm, curvature threshold of 45°, and a total of 1 million streamlines generated per subject. Streamline weights were computed using Spherical-deconvolution Informed Filtering of Tractograms 2 (SIFT2), implemented via the Compute streamline weights using SIFT2 app. The whole brain tractography was segmented into major white matter tracts using the White Matter Anatomy Segmentation app, which is based on the white matter parcellation approach proposed by Bullock et al. (2019). The Remove Tract Outliers app was used to iteratively eliminate streamlines that deviated from tract-like morphology based on their distance from the fiber core and mean length, ensuring anatomical coherence across all classified white matter tracts. Finally, diffusion metrics such as FA were extracted along the trajectory of each tract using the Tract Analysis Profiles app, and the resulting tract profiles were averaged to yield a mean FA value for each tract, which was used for subsequent statistical analyses. A detailed list of all preprocessing applications used in this pipeline is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

White matter tract selection

Tracts previously implicated in executive functions, particularly response inhibition, attentional control, and top-down cognitive regulation, were selected (Bagautdinova et al., 2023, Blakemore and Choudhury, 2006, Ribeiro et al., 2024). These included the left and right SLF I/II, SLF III, arcuate fasciculus, and cingulum bundle. Statistical analyses

To test potential sex effects, we first explored sex differences in screen time, Stroop interference score, and white matter FA values using independent samples t-tests. Given the wide range of ages, we also examined whether these variables were associated with age using Pearson correlation analyses. To test our primary hypothesis that screen time is associated with Stroop interference scores, we evaluated a partial correlation between screen time and Stroop interference scores while controlling for age and sex. Additionally, partial correlations were conducted to examine the associations between FA values and Stroop interference scores, as well as between FA values and screen time, while controlling for age and sex.

To test whether white matter integrity moderates the relationship between screen time and inhibitory control, we conducted moderation analyses using the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Hayes, 2012). The Johnson-Neyman technique was applied to identify regions of significance in the interaction between screen time and FA. Conditional effects were examined at ±1 standard deviation from the mean FA to aid interpretation. To control for Type I error arising due to multiple comparison testing, the Bonferroni correction was applied. Since eight white matter fasciculi were tested, the Bonferroni corrected significance threshold was set at p = 0.00625 (derived by dividing the significance threshold 0.05 by 8). All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (version 29.0.1.0, IBM Corp.).

Finally, several supplementary analyses were conducted to examine the robustness and specificity of the primary findings. First, to assess whether moderation effects differed by screen activity subtype (e.g., playing videogames, texting, and watching television), screen activities were classified into two categories (active and passive activities) based on their functional characteristics and inhibitory control demands (Marin-Dragu et al., 2023). Video games, texting, and video calls were classified as active screen activities, whereas television and online video viewing were classified as passive screen activities; social media use was excluded due to its mixed active and passive features (Verduyn et al., 2015, Trifiro and Gerson, 2019). Second, to evaluate whether the association between screen time and inhibitory control varied as a function of chronological age, an additional moderation model including a screen time × age interaction term was tested. Age-stratified subgroup analyses were also conducted by dividing participants into younger (<12 years) and older (≥12 years) groups, corresponding to the typical onset of early adolescence (Encyclopaedia,). The younger (<12 years) and older (≥12 years) subgroups comprised 28 and 122 participants, respectively. Third, sensitivity analyses were performed with pubertal development included as an additional covariate, operationalized using a composite score derived from parent-reported Pubertal Development Scale (PDS) items (Pan et al., 2025). This score was calculated by summing ratings across relevant items, including height growth, skin changes, body hair growth, breast development, voice change, facial hair growth, and menarche status, as applicable.

Associations of white matter integrity with screen time and inhibitory control

Greater FA values in the right SLF I/II (partial r = –.197, p = .017), right SLF III (partial r = –.167, p = .043), and right arcuate fasciculus (partial r = –.220, p = .007) were significantly correlated with reduced screen time. No significant associations were observed for the left SLF I/II, SLF III, arcuate fasciculus, or bilateral cingulum (all ps >.05). Notably, FA values were not significantly correlated with Stroop interference scores after controlling for age and sex.

Moderation analysis: relationship between screen time and response inhibition moderated by white matter integrity

We observed a significant interaction for the right SLF I/II, which remained significant after Bonferroni correction (ΔR² =.049, b = –7.02, p = .005) (Table 2). This indicated that the FA in the right SLF I/II significantly moderated the association between screen time and inhibitory control. Johnson–Neyman analysis revealed that the association between screen time and Stroop interference score was significant only at low FA in the right SLF I/II (–1 SD; b = 0.32, SE = 0.07, p < .001), not at high FA (+1 SD; b = 0.02, SE = 0.08, p = .83) (Fig. 4). Interactions with the right cingulum (p = 0.015) and right (p = .016) and left arcuate fasciculi (p = .046) did not survive Bonferroni correction (Supplementary Table 2). Additional and exploratory analyses

3.5.1. The moderation effects of screen time subtype. When screen time subtype was divided into active and passive activity subtypes, moderation effects of white matter integrity were more evident for active screen activities, whereas comparable effects were less consistently observed for passive screen activities. The interaction effects observed for active screen activities did not survive Bonferroni correction; however, they demonstrated patterns consistent with the primary findings. These results are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

3.5.2. The moderation effects of age. To examine whether the association between screen time and inhibitory control varied as a function of chronological age, an additional moderation analysis including a screen time × age interaction term was conducted. This interaction was not statistically significant (p = .766), indicating that the association did not differ systematically by age (Supplementary Table 4). In age-stratified subgroup analyses, statistically significant effects were observed in the subgroup aged 12 years and older, whereas no significant effects were found in the subgroup younger than 12 years. In the analysis restricted to participants aged 12 years and older, the screen time × right SLF I/II FA interaction remained significant after Bonferroni correction, and the overall pattern of findings was consistent with the primary results. The results of the age-restricted analysis are presented in Supplementary Table 5.

3.5.3. Controlling for pubertal development. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for pubertal development yielded results comparable to the primary models. This analysis showed an overall pattern consistent with the primary findings; however, the interaction effects did not survive Bonferroni correction. The results are presented in Supplementary Table 6.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationship between youth screen use and inhibitory control, and tested whether white matter integrity moderated this relationship. Greater screen time was associated with poorer inhibitory control. Furthermore, white matter integrity of the right SLF I/II moderated this association.

As we hypothesized, longer screen time was correlated with reduced inhibitory control. This result aligns with previous studies suggesting that youth who spend more time on screens tend to exhibit weaker inhibitory control (Law et al., 2023, Mallawaarachchi et al., 2024, Wallace et al., 2023). Specifically, one meta-analysis reported that longer screen viewing time, including exposure to TV and video, was associated with poorer cognitive outcomes, including diminished response inhibition (Mallawaarachchi et al., 2024). Another study found that screen time measured during infancy was correlated with poor performance on the Stroop test at nine years of age (Law et al., 2023). Furthermore, excessive use of digital media may displace cognitively enriching activities, such as physical exercise, sleep and interpersonal interactions, that are essential for the development of executive functions, including the capacity for inhibitory control (Thorell et al., 2024). Therefore, this finding suggests that regulating screen use may help promote normative development of cognitive functions, particularly during adolescence, which represents a critical period for cognitive maturation.

White matter integrity in the right SLF I/II may play an important role in moderating vulnerability to the cognitive effects of screen exposure. Given that the SLF I/II supports top-down regulation and attentional control (Ribeiro et al., 2024), youth with lower integrity in this tract may have compromised axonal coherence and reduced conduction efficiency (Filley, 2010), rendering them more susceptible to disruptions in inhibitory control posed by excessive screen time. In contrast, individuals with higher white matter integrity of the SLF I/II may exhibit greater neurocognitive resilience (Koc et al., 2020), maintaining efficient signal transmission and cognitive control despite similar screen exposure (Filley, 2010). Such inter-individual variation in white matter FA likely reflects the combined influence of genetic factors and developmental experiences across childhood and adolescence. Notably, frontoparietal white matter tracts such as the SLF continue to undergo protracted maturation throughout late childhood and adolescence, including ongoing myelination and strengthening of long-range connections, which are critical for the development of executive function and cognitive control (Lebel et al., 2008). Resilience-focused neuroimaging studies further suggest that frontoparietal white matter pathways may be shaped by potentially modifiable factors such as sleep, physical activity, and cognitive engagement (Eaton et al., 2022, Bolsinger et al., 2018), although this cannot be tested in the current cross-sectional design. In line with this mechanistic framework, exploratory analyses indicated that moderation effects were more evident for active screen activities, which place greater demands on response selection and cognitive control, than for passive screen activities. This result also indicates that screen time subtype should be considered in future research examining the effects of screen time on cognitive function in youth.

Although multiple tracts (SLF III, arcuate fasciculus, and cingulum) have been implicated in inhibitory control, only the SLF I/II showed a statistically significant moderating effect in the present study, possibly due to the more direct involvement of this tract in executive functions compared to the other tracts (Schmahmann et al., 2007, Urger et al., 2015); however, this interpretation warrants further investigation in larger samples. In addition, the moderating effects observed in the present study were confined to right-hemispheric tracts. Although hemispheric lateralization was not a primary hypothesis, this pattern may align with prior evidence suggesting a relative right-hemispheric involvement in inhibitory control, warranting further investigation (Casey et al., 1997, Schrammen et al., 2020).

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether screen exposure alters inhibitory control and white matter integrity, or vice versa. The relatively wide age range of the sample may be another limitation, as screen media preferences and the developmental significance of screen time are likely to differ across childhood and adolescence. Second, although additional age-related analyses suggested that the primary findings were more evident in adolescents, future studies with age-specific characterization of screen use are needed to better capture developmental differences. Third, although younger children are unlikely to provide accurate self-reports, older adolescents may spend considerable time away from their parents, making it difficult for parents to precisely monitor their screen time. Therefore, incorporating both parent and self-reports may yield a more accurate estimate of actual screen use. In addition, screen time was assessed using questionnaire-based reports rather than objective or device-based measures, which may be subject to recall bias and limit precision in estimating actual screen exposure. Finally, inhibitory control was assessed using a single Stroop measure, which may not fully capture this multifaceted construct. Future studies should incorporate prospective designs, multi-informant assessments of screen use, and diverse measures of executive function to enhance generalizability and interpretability. Additionally, although pubertal development assessed using the questionnaire-based measures reported by parents did not alter our main findings, the absence of objective biological or hormonal indices may limit the precision with which individual variability in pubertal maturation may influence the association between screen time, white matter integrity, and executive function.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths, including a relatively large sample size for diffusion MRI analyses in youth and a comprehensive analytical approach that went beyond main effects by formally testing moderation effects with appropriate correction for multiple comparisons. To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that white matter integrity moderates the association between screen time and inhibitory control. Our finding extends previous research by identifying a neural correlate of inter-individual differences in vulnerability to screen time-related cognitive disruption. Practically, it underscores the potential utility of diffusion-based markers in characterizing neurocognitive heterogeneity and identifying protective behaviors that may promote resilience in the context of screen exposure. Future longitudinal studies should test whether white matter integrity prospectively moderates the impact of screen use on inhibitory control and examine modifiable behaviors, such as sleep, physical activity, and structured offline engagement, that may strengthen neurocognitive resilience during development. Taken together, the present findings highlight the importance of considering individual neurobiological differences when evaluating the cognitive effects of screen use in youth.
