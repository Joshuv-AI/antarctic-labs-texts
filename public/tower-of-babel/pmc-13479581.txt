STING‑regulated Ca²⁺–TGF‑β axis drives airway remodeling and lung function decline in asthma: a multi‑omics mechanistic study
Authors: Zhiqin Zhu, Qingao Xiao, Runjin Cai, Enyu Qi, Xiaoxiao Gong, Jiale Tang, Xiaozhao Li, Juntao Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe progressive lung function decline in severe asthma is poorly explained by traditional inflammation‑centered paradigms. Calcium (Ca²⁺) dysregulation and transforming growth factor‑β (TGF‑β) signaling have been implicated, but the molecular link between them and their upstream regulators remain elusive.MethodsWe integrated clinical CT imaging, proteomics (6 asthma vs. 6 healthy controls), public transcriptomic data (GSE63142, GSE68479), single‑cell RNA‑sequencing (GSE193816, GSE185048), and functional studies in STING knockout mice and airway epithelial cells. GO and KEGG enrichment, LASSO regression, Spearman correlation, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, co‑immunoprecipitation, western blot, mitochondrial function assays (MitoSOX/JC‑1), Fura‑2 calcium imaging, RNA‑seq and ELISA were used to systematically evaluate the role of the STING‑PPM1F‑Ca²⁺‑TGF‑β axis in asthma airway remodeling.ResultsUsing logistic regression, we found that disease severity, age, pulmonary function parameters and eosinophil count independently predicted the presence of tracheal cartilage calcification, with the model achieving excellent discrimination (AUC > 0.92). Proteomics identified PPM1F, CAMK2G, MICU2 and CIB1 as key calcium‑metabolism proteins co‑enriched in calcium signaling and TGF‑β pathways; PPM1F expression strongly correlated with reduced FEV₁% (r = − 0.94, p = 0.005) and recurrent exacerbation risk (p < 0.01). Functionally, OVA induced time‑dependent mitochondrial ROS elevation and membrane potential dissipation, triggered cytosolic mtDNA release, and activated STING downstream p‑TBK1 signaling. STING activation directly mobilized intracellular Ca²⁺ via both store release and extracellular influx, as demonstrated by Fura‑2 imaging. STING knockout or silencing abolished OVA‑induced PPM1F upregulation and TGF‑β/Smad activation, and attenuated airway pathology. Co‑immunoprecipitation confirmed direct PPM1F‑TGF‑β physical interaction. Single‑cell transcriptomics revealed SFTPC⁺ epithelial cell expansion and high NKX2‑1/FOXA2 activity; multiplex immunofluorescence further identified SCGB1A1⁺SFTPC⁺ double‑positive cells in asthmatic airways, suggesting a potential phenotypic transition of club cells toward an AT2-like state.ConclusionsThe STING‑regulated Ca²⁺–TGF‑β axis, activated by mitochondrial stress‑induced mtDNA release, is associated with airway remodeling and lung function decline in asthma. PPM1F is a potential biomarker for asthma severity and recurrent exacerbations; targeting STING or PPM1F may offer new therapeutic strategies for severe asthma.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00940-y.Keywords: Asthma, Calcium signaling, TGF-β, PPM1F, STING, Multi-omics

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease characterized by airway hyperresponsiveness, variable airflow limitation, and airway remodeling, often accompanied by chronic airway inflammation, significantly impacting the health and quality of life of hundreds of millions of people worldwide [1, 2]. While standard therapies such as inhaled corticosteroids effectively control symptoms in most patients, individuals with severe asthma often experience progressive lung function decline and poor treatment response—a phenomenon driven by irreversible airway remodeling [3, 4]. Uncovering the molecular mechanisms underlying this progressive functional deterioration remains a central challenge in asthma research.

Calcium (Ca²⁺) signaling has long been recognized as a core component regulating airway tone and reactivity [5–7]. As the ultimate messenger for airway smooth muscle contraction, Ca²⁺ not only directly contributes to airway hyperresponsiveness but also mediates mucus hypersecretion via calcium-activated chloride channels (e.g., hCLCA1/mCLCA3) and plays a broad role in inflammatory cell activation and cytokine release. In recent years, growing evidence has indicated that asthmatic patients often exhibit systemic calcium metabolism dysregulation [8–10]. For instance, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 levels show a positive correlation with blood calcium and phosphorus concentrations, and vitamin D supplementation has been shown to simultaneously improve calcium-phosphate metabolism and immune-inflammatory markers [5, 11]. including altered vitamin D metabolism and ryanodine receptor dysfunction. Notably, clinical imaging studies have sporadically reported pulmonary calcification in chronic airway diseases—such as high-attenuation areas (CT value > 100 HU) in allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis [7, 9]. However, whether a quantitative relationship exists between the extent of pulmonary calcification and the degree of lung function impairment in asthma, and what molecular mechanisms might explain such a link, remain unexplored. Upon activation by cytosolic DNA, STING recruits and activates TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), a key downstream signaling kinase that phosphorylates interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) and drives type I interferon responses. Beyond its canonical role in innate immunity, the STING-TBK1 axis has recently been implicated in non-immune processes, including autophagy and cell death.

Meanwhile, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) acts as a “dual regulator” in asthma airway inflammation and remodeling, with effects extending beyond conventional pro- or anti-inflammatory roles [12, 13]. In the airway microenvironment of asthmatic individuals, particularly those with an allergic phenotype, TGF-β1 expression is significantly elevated and drives disease progression through both Smad-dependent and non-Smad signaling pathways [14–16], operating at multiple levels: it directly recruits eosinophils, induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated pathway activation, and strongly promotes fibrosis by driving fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation, leading to excessive extracellular matrix deposition and airway wall thickening [17–19]. Notably, the TGF-β3 isoform has also been shown to regulate airway autophagy and MUC5AC mucus secretion via the TGF-β3/Smad2/Smad3 axis, further underscoring its role in shaping asthma heterogeneity [20, 21]. However, the precise regulatory logic of TGF-β within the genetic background and local microenvironment of asthma remains poorly understood.

Intriguingly, recent studies have revealed a close “bidirectional crosstalk” between calcium metabolism and TGF-β signaling, forming a sophisticated regulatory network. On one hand, TGF-β promotes extracellular calcium influx by modulating L-type and T-type voltage-gated calcium channels, thereby activating downstream ERK1/2 and PKCα signaling to drive specific gene expression [22–24]. On the other hand, intracellular Ca²⁺ serves as a key second messenger that enhances TGF-β transcriptional activity via calmodulin-dependent mechanisms—such as activation of calcineurin and CaMKIV—establishing a positive feedback loop [25, 26]. This interactive mechanism has been shown to play a central role in physiological processes like chondrogenesis and bone regeneration, suggesting that similar functional coupling may exist in the airway microenvironment [25–28]. We hypothesized that disruption of calcium homeostasis and its interplay with TGF-β signaling may constitute a key pathogenic axis driving lung function decline in asthma. It is important to distinguish between macroscopic tissue calcification, which we assess clinically via CT imaging as a potential biomarker of systemic calcium dysregulation, and the dynamic intracellular Ca²⁺ fluxes that constitute the signaling axis we investigate here. While both may arise from underlying disturbances in calcium homeostasis, they represent distinct biological phenomena.

In this study, we integrated clinical imaging, proteomics, and single-cell transcriptomics to systematically investigate this hypothesis. We first established a quantitative relationship between tracheal cartilage calcification and lung function impairment, constructing a predictive model with excellent clinical performance. We then identified key calcium metabolism-related proteins—PPM1F, CAMK2G, MICU2, and CIB1—that converged on both calcium and TGF-β signaling pathways, defining a “Calcium-TGF-β axis.” Using in vitro functional studies and STING knockout mice, we demonstrated that STING acts as an upstream regulator of this axis, with PPM1F physically interacting with TGF-β and mediating TGF-β-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Single-cell transcriptomics further revealed that this axis orchestrates epithelial cell reprogramming and dominates intercellular communication networks. Our findings uncover a novel mechanism linking calcium dysregulation to TGF-β-driven airway remodeling and identify PPM1F as a promising prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target for severe asthma.

Experimental procedures

Ethics and informed consent

This study recruited asthma patients aged 18–70 years diagnosed according to the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2023 guidelines from Xiangya Hospital, Hunan, China. Exclusion criteria were: (1) concomitant other respiratory diseases such as emphysema, bronchiectasis, pneumonia, tuberculosis, interstitial lung disease, non‑asthmatic chronic cough or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) severe systemic diseases such as heart failure, malignancy or severe mental disorders. Healthy controls (≥ 18 years) were recruited from the hospital’s health examination center, with no respiratory diseases, normal chest imaging and pulmonary function. All subjects underwent comprehensive pulmonary function and blood tests. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (approval No. 2022020475).

Study design and data collection

We prospectively enrolled 99 subjects, including healthy individuals and asthma patients, who visited the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine of Xiangya Hospital between January 2022 and December 2025. All patients met the GINA diagnostic criteria for asthma and signed written informed consent. Imaging data: Chest non‑contrast CT scans were performed at the end of deep inspiration using a Siemens SOMATOM Definition AS 64‑slice spiral CT scanner. Tracheal cartilage calcification value was measured using Syngo.via post‑processing workstation. A region of interest (ROI, approximately 10 mm²) was placed at the most calcified area of the tracheal cartilage ring, and the mean CT value (Hounsfield units, HU) was recorded. The ROI was placed at the most calcified area to capture the most representative calcification signal for each subject. While using averaged measurements from multiple regions could potentially reduce sampling bias, we chose a single ROI at the maximum calcification site to ensure consistency and reproducibility across all subjects, as this approach has been commonly used in previous CT‑based calcification studies. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that multi‑region averaging may provide a more comprehensive assessment and should be considered in future studies. Pulmonary function tests: Lung function parameters, including FEV₁, FVC, FEV₁/FVC, PEF, MEF₇₅, MEF₅₀, MEF₂₅ and MVV, were measured using a Jaeger MasterScreen spirometer, strictly following ATS/ERS standard operating procedures. Laboratory indicators: Peripheral venous blood was collected to measure eosinophil count (EOS) using an automated hematology analyzer (SF-3000, Sysmex Corporation, Japan). Clinical data: Demographic characteristics, disease severity grade, comorbidities and medication history were systematically collected.

Predictive model construction and validation

Data processing was performed using R 4.4.1. Missing values were imputed by multiple imputation (MICE package). LASSO regression (glmnet package) with 10‑fold cross‑validation was used for variable selection. Selected variables were incorporated into multivariable logistic regression. Model performance was evaluated by ROC curves (pROC package), calibration curves (rms package) and decision curve analysis (rmda package). Internal validation was performed by bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations).

Proteomics analysis

Airway mucosal biopsy tissues from 6 asthma patients with severely impaired lung function and 6 healthy controls were selected from the cohort of 99 subjects. Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer, quantified by BCA assay, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by a nanoElute liquid chromatography system coupled with a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer in DIA mode. Raw data were searched against the human UniProt database using Spectronaut Pulsar (v16.0). Filtering criteria: FDR < 1%, p < 0.05 and fold change > 1.2 or < 0.833. Protein identification was performed by searching the raw data against the human UniProt database using Spectronaut Pulsar (v16.0). The search parameters included a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm and a fragment mass tolerance of 0.05 Da. Protein validation was based on a minimum of two unique peptides per protein, with a peptide confidence score > 0.99 and a protein confidence score > 0.99. Only proteins with a q-value < 0.01 (corresponding to FDR < 1%) were retained for downstream analysis. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package. For GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, significantly enriched terms were defined using a p‑value threshold of < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. To correct for multiple comparisons, p‑values from the Spearman correlation analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini‑Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. After FDR correction, the correlations of PPM1F, MICU2 and CIB1 with FEV₁% remained significant (q < 0.05), while other associations did not reach significance. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between protein expression and lung function parameters. The 6 asthma patients and 6 healthy controls for proteomic analysis were selected based on extreme phenotypes of lung function (FEV₁% predicted, with values below 60% for the asthma group and above 90% for the control group). To assess the robustness of the Spearman correlations given the small sample size (n = 6), we calculated 95% confidence intervals for each correlation coefficient using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution were used as the lower and upper bounds of the confidence interval.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence validation

Airway tissues from an additional 12 subjects (6 asthma patients and 6 healthy controls) were randomly selected from the remaining cohort (after excluding the 12 subjects used for proteomics) for immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence validation. The airway mucosal tissues were collected by direct scraping of the airway epithelium using a sterile cytology brush during bronchoscopy. Paraffin‑embedded airway sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: PPM1F (Proteintech, 17020‑1‑AP, 1:200), MICU2 (Proteintech, 20859‑1‑AP, 1:150), CAMK2G (Proteintech, 12666‑2‑AP, 1:100) and CIB1 (Proteintech, 11823‑1‑AP, 1:150). IHC used HRP‑labeled secondary antibodies and DAB staining; IF used Cy3/FITC‑labeled secondary antibodies, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired with a Nikon Eclipse Ni‑U microscope and analyzed with ImageJ.

GEO dataset validation

The GSE63142 (20 healthy controls, 72 non‑severe asthma, 56 severe asthma) and GSE68479 (10 non‑exacerbation, 12 recurrent exacerbation) datasets were downloaded. Data were normalized and analyzed using R. Kruskal‑Wallis test was used for multi‑group comparisons, and Wilcoxon rank‑sum test for pairwise comparisons. Visualization was performed with ggplot2. For integrated analysis of transcriptomic datasets, batch effects were corrected using the ComBat method implemented in the sva package.

Single‑cell RNA sequencing

We integrated two independent single‑cell transcriptomic datasets: GSE193816 (4 asthma patients and 4 non‑asthma controls, lower airway mucosal cells obtained by endobronchial brushing) and GSE185048 (10 completely healthy volunteers, same source of brushing). Data were processed using Seurat (v4.3.0), including SCTransform normalization, PCA and UMAP dimensionality reduction, and cell type annotation using SingleR (v2.2.0). Cell‑cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat (v1.6.1). Batch effects between the two datasets were corrected using Seurat’s integration workflow (FindIntegrationAnchors + IntegrateData) prior to downstream analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

Human bronchial epithelial BEAS‑2B cells (ATCC, CRL‑9609) were cultured in BEGM complete medium at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. CCCP (HY-100941, MCE, USA) was used for 20 min as a positive control for mitochondrial stress. Ovalbumins (OVA, HY-W250978, MCE, USA) was used to stimulate cells for 24 h to mimic asthma‑related pathological stimulation.

Immunofluorescence co‑localization

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde(HY-Y0333, MCE, USA), permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‑100, and incubated with primary antibodies against PPM1F (Proteintech, 17020‑1‑AP, 1:200) and TGF‑β1 (Santa Cruz, sc‑130348, 1:100) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488/594‑conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (HY-D2868, MCE, USA). Images were acquired with a Nikon A1R confocal microscope and analyzed with ImageJ.

Co‑immunoprecipitation

Cell lysates (500 µg protein) were incubated with 2 µg of anti‑PPM1F (Proteintech, 17020‑1‑AP) or rabbit IgG control (Santa Cruz, sc‑2027) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz, sc‑2003) for 4 h at 4 °C. Bound proteins were eluted and subjected to Western blot using anti‑TGF‑β1 (Santa Cruz, sc‑130348) and anti‑PPM1F antibodies.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer and quantified by BCA assay. Equal amounts (30 µg) were separated by SDS‑PAGE(Cat: G4770, Solarbio) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: PPM1F (Proteintech, 17020‑1‑AP, 1:1000), TGF‑β1 (Santa Cruz, sc‑130348, 1:500), p‑Smad2 (HUABIO, ET1612-32, 1:1000), p‑Smad3 (HUABIO, HA750172), STING (CST, 13647, 1:1000), Smad2 (HUABIO, ET1604-22) and Smad3 (HUABIO, ET1607-41) GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004‑1‑Ig, 1:5000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‑conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, 1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using ECL substrate and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ.

Mitochondrial function assays

MitoSOX staining: Cells or freshly isolated primary airway epithelial cells were incubated with 5 µM MitoSOX (HY-D1055, MCE, USA) and Hoechst 33,342 (HY-15559, MCE, USA) for 10 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa). JC‑1 staining: Cells were incubated with JC‑1 working solution (HY-15534, MCE, USA) for 20 min at 37 °C, washed, and the red/green fluorescence ratio was analyzed by flow cytometry. CCCP (10 µM, 20 min) was used as a positive control.

Animal experiments

Animal grouping: Eight‑week‑old male wild‑type (WT) C57BL/6 and STING knockout (STING KO) mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6/group): WT control, WT + OVA, STING KO control, and STING KO + OVA. Asthma model: Mice were sensitized on day 0 by subcutaneous and intraperitoneal injection of OVA (Sigma, A5503, 20 µg) mixed with aluminum hydroxide (Imject Alum, Thermo Fisher, 2 mg) as an adjuvant. From day 7 to day 13, mice were challenged every other day (days 7, 9, 11 and 13) with 1% OVA aerosol for 30 min via ultrasonic nebulization. Control mice received PBS alone using the same schedule. Sample collection: Mice were euthanized by gradual CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death, 24 h after the last challenge. For CO₂ euthanasia, mice were placed in a dedicated induction chamber and exposed to a gradual fill rate of 20% of the chamber volume per minute with 100% CO₂, following the AVMA guidelines. The time from the onset of unconsciousness to respiratory arrest was approximately 3–5 min. Immediately after confirming death by cervical dislocation, lung, kidney, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and primary airway epithelial cells were collected. The entire procedure from euthanasia to tissue snap-freezing or fixation was completed within 5 min to minimize post-mortem artifacts. The entire procedure from euthanasia to tissue snap-freezing or fixation was completed within 5 min to minimize post-mortem artifacts.

Histological analysis

Lung and kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm sections. Sections were stained with H&E and PAS (Sigma, 395B). Images were acquired by light microscopy and analyzed using ImageJ for quantification of inflammatory infiltration and goblet cell hyperplasia.

Isolation of primary airway epithelial cells

Mouse trachea and main bronchi were digested in 0.1% protease XIV (Sigma, P5147) at 4 °C. Epithelial cells were gently scraped, filtered through a 70 μm strainer, centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, and resuspended for subsequent experiments.

Real‑time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol and reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, RR037A). qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A) and 0.2 ml White PCR 8-Strip Tubes (403012, NEST Biotechnology)on a LightCycler 480 II system. The mRNA expression levels of PPM1F and the release of cytosolic mtDNA were quantified by real-time quantitative PCR. For mouse tissue samples, Ppm1f was detected using the following primers: forward 5‘-AATTTCCTCTGGGTGCCTCT-3’ and reverse 5‘-ATCAAGAGCTGTCCCAGGTTG-3’, with Gapdh as the internal control (forward 5‘-CCTGCGACTTCAACAGCAAC-3’ and reverse 5‘-TGGGATAGGGCCTCTCTTGC-3’). For BEAS-2B cell samples, mtDNA (D-loop region) was detected using forward 5‘-GCCCATGACCAACATAACTG-3’ and reverse 5‘-CCTTGACGGCTATGTTGATG-3’, with ACTB as the internal control (forward 5‘-GAGAAAATCTGGCACCACACC-3’ and reverse 5‘-GGATAGCACAGCCTGGATAGCAA-3’). All primer pairs were validated for amplification specificity by melting curve analysis prior to use, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method.

ELISA

BALF supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Mouse TGF‑β1 levels were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (R&D, MB100B) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissues of WT + OVA and STING KO + OVA mice (n = 6/group). Libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw data were processed with fastp, aligned to the GRCm38 genome using HISAT2, and quantified with featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (|log2FC| ≥ 1, padj < 0.05). KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 or R 4.1.0, and results are expressed as mean ± SD. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene‘s test before statistical comparisons. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t‑test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non‑normally distributed data. For multi‑group comparisons, one‑way or two‑way ANOVA was performed for normally distributed data, followed by Tukey‘s or Bonferroni’s post‑hoc test for multiple comparisons; for non‑normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. A p‑value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001). For multiple comparisons, p‑values were adjusted using the Benjamini‑Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method to control the FDR at 0.05. Adjusted p‑values (q‑values) were calculated for the Spearman correlation analyses between the 8 proteins and 3 lung function parameters.

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Significant differences in asthma severity distribution were observed between the training and validation sets (p < 0.001). In the training set: grade 0 (healthy control) 8 (15.7%), grade 1 19 (37.3%), grade 2 7 (13.7%), grade 3 4 (7.8%), grade 4 7 (13.7%), grade 5 6 (11.8%). In the validation set: grade 0 7 (14.6%), grade 1 3 (6.3%), grade 2 7 (14.6%), grade 3 4 (8.3%), grade 4 7 (14.6%), grade 5 20 (41.7%). The proportion of severe asthma (grades 4–5) in the validation set was 56.3%, significantly higher than the 25.5% in the training set.

In terms of pulmonary function, the training set had a median FEV₁% predicted of 79.9% (IQR: 61.8–93.15) and the validation set 57.8% (IQR: 46.98–63.63) (p < 0.001). Mean FVC% predicted was 82.08 ± 8.98% in the training set and 79.66 ± 7.43% in the validation set (p < 0.001). Median FEV₁/FVC was 82.7% (IQR: 65.55–96.05) in the training set and 72.5% (IQR: 43.05–94.69) in the validation set (p < 0.001). PEF (L/s) median: training 4.91 (IQR: 3.76–5.94), validation 2.79 (IQR: 1.94–3.96) (p < 0.001). Small airway indices were also significantly different (all p < 0.001). Sex distribution differed significantly (p = 0.012), while age (p = 0.119) and eosinophil count (p = 0.367) did not differ significantly between the two sets.

These systematic differences indicate that the validation set included more severe asthma patients with worse lung function, suitable for evaluating model generalizability in severe disease, while the training set had a more balanced distribution from healthy controls to various asthma grades, providing a good basis for model training. However, we acknowledge that the random split without stratification led to an imbalanced distribution of asthma severity between the training and validation sets. This imbalance may potentially affect the generalizability of the model, and we have addressed this limitation in the Discussion section.

Imaging characterization and construction and validation of the predictive model

Computed tomography (CT) images showed distinct tracheal cartilage calcification patterns between patients with normal lung function and those with very severe impairment. Representative CT images from a patient with very severe ventilatory dysfunction showed significant calcification foci with a value as high as 769.8 HU (Fig. 1A), whereas a patient with normal lung function showed a calcification value of 123.3 HU (Fig. 1B), suggesting that the degree of tracheal cartilage calcification may be related to disease severity. To build a robust and parsimonious predictive model, we used LASSO regression for variable selection. The LASSO coefficient path plot shows the trajectory of variable coefficients versus log(λ) (Fig. 1C). The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined by 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 1D). The λ value yielding the minimum binomial deviance (λ.min) was selected, under which 6 variables had non-zero coefficients and were included in the final model.

Performance validation of the predictive model

The logistic regression model built based on the LASSO-selected predictors showed excellent discriminatory ability. The areas under the ROC curve for the training set and validation set were 0.923 (95% CI: 0.856–0.989) and 0.920 (95% CI: 0.807-1.000), respectively (Fig. 1E), indicating that the model accurately distinguishes patients with different prognoses. Calibration curves assessing the agreement between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes showed that the curves for both the training set (Fig. 1F) and the validation set (Fig. 1G) closely followed the ideal reference line. Quantitative indicators showed that the calibration slope was 1.000 and the intercept was 0.000 for both datasets; the Brier scores were 0.102 and 0.097, respectively, confirming excellent calibration. Decision curve analysis evaluated the clinical utility of the model across different threshold probabilities (Fig. 1H). The results showed that within a reasonable threshold range, using this model for clinical decision-making provided a higher net benefit than the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies, demonstrating its potential clinical application value. The final predictive model formula is: p = 1 / (1 + exp(-9.31599–1.10988*Disease_classification + 0.11102*Age − 0.04682*FEV1% − 0.01804*FEV1%/FVC% + 0.78136*PEF + 2.00794*EOS)). Thus, disease severity, age, lung function parameters and eosinophil count are independent predictors of tracheal cartilage calcification in asthma. The model may help estimate individual calcification risk. To assess whether the imbalanced severity distribution affected model performance, we performed a sensitivity analysis by re-evaluating the model within severity-matched subgroups. The results showed that the AUC remained acceptable but was somewhat lower than the overall AUC, suggesting that the original performance may be slightly overestimated. These findings underscore the need for external validation of the model in future independent cohorts. We acknowledge that the imbalanced distribution of asthma severity between the training and validation sets may lead to overestimation of model performance. Therefore, the model’s generalizability should be validated in prospective, independent cohorts in future studies.

Proteomics reveals coordinated dysregulation of calcium‑TGF‑β axis in asthmatic airway epithelium and its association with lung function decline

To explore the molecular mechanisms of lung function impairment, we performed proteomics on airway mucosal biopsy tissues. Samples were selected from 6 asthma patients and 6 healthy controls from our established cohort of 99 subjects (including 84 asthma patients and 15 healthy controls). To investigate the potential functions and clinical significance of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in asthmatic airway epithelium, we first performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on all DEPs (Fig. 2A and B). GO analysis showed that DEPs were significantly enriched in cadherin binding, cytoskeleton organization and other biological processes. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of the TGF‑β signaling pathway (hsa04350), suggesting coordinated dysregulation of calcium‑related functions and the TGF‑β pathway in asthma.

To further visualize the positions of key proteins in the signaling pathways, we constructed a curated sub‑network derived from the KEGG calcium signaling pathway (hsa04020), focusing on the nodes that were differentially expressed in our proteomics data (Fig. 2C). Figure 2C shows that calcium/calmodulin‑dependent protein kinase IIγ (CAMK2G) is a core node of the calcium signaling pathway. Meanwhile, our KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 2B) independently confirmed significant enrichment of the TGF‑β signaling pathway. Although CAMK2G is not a direct component of the TGF‑β pathway, existing literature indicates extensive crosstalk between calcium and TGF‑β signaling: TGF‑β can mobilize intracellular calcium stores via non‑canonical pathways, inducing changes in Ca²⁺ concentration and thereby activating downstream effectors (such as CaMKII); conversely, abnormal calcium signaling can also regulate TGF‑β ligand expression and receptor sensitivity. As a hypothesis-generating observation, this network suggests potential involvement of calcium signaling in asthmatic airway remodeling, but should not be interpreted as definitive pathway activation without further functional validation.

Notably, recent studies have revealed that STING (stimulator of interferon genes) is an emerging node linking calcium signaling to inflammation/remodeling processes. STING can respond to intracellular DNA damage signals and directly regulate calcium release through endoplasmic reticulum calcium channels (such as TRPV2), thereby participating in calcium signal transduction; at the same time, the STING signaling axis has been shown to be closely related to TGF‑β‑driven epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT) and airway remodeling. Our proteomics data showed that STING protein was significantly highly expressed in the calcification group (fold change = 3.77, p = 0.0075), suggesting its aberrant activation in asthmatic airways (Table 2).

Identification of calcium metabolism-related proteins by proteomics

To further evaluate the direct association between the above‑mentioned differential proteins and clinical lung function decline, we performed Spearman rank correlation analysis between eight target proteins (PNPLA4, PPM1F, CAMK2G, MICU2, CIB1, COMMD5, STING, SMAD2, Table 2) and three key lung function parameters (FEV₁% predicted, FVC% predicted, FEV₁/FVC ratio). The results are presented as a heatmap (Fig. 2E).

PPM1F showed the strongest and most consistent negative correlation with lung function parameters: it was significantly negatively correlated with FEV₁% predicted (r = ‑0.94, p = 0.005) and with FVC% predicted (r = ‑0.83, p = 0.041), while its negative correlation with FEV₁/FVC ratio did not reach statistical significance (r = ‑0.31, p = 0.540). This correlation suggests that elevated PPM1F protein expression is associated with more severe ventilatory dysfunction in asthma patients. While this finding is hypothesis-generating, it points to the potential pathological significance of PPM1F within the context of the calcium-TGF-β axis, a role that is further investigated in our subsequent functional experiments. To assess the robustness of these correlations given the limited sample size (n = 6), we performed bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) to calculate 95% confidence intervals for each Spearman correlation. For the strongest associations identified, the bootstrap 95% CI for PPM1F with FEV₁% was − 0.994 to − 0.486 (r = − 0.94, p = 0.005), and for MICU2 and CIB1 with FEV₁% were 0.486 to 0.994 (r = 0.94, p = 0.005) for both proteins. Bootstrap resampling provided supportive evidence for the stability of these key correlations within this exploratory cohort, although the wide confidence intervals for some non-significant associations underscore the inherent limitations of the small sample size.

STING protein also showed a negative correlation trend with lung function parameters: it was negatively correlated with FEV₁% predicted (r = ‑0.71), FVC% predicted (r = ‑0.60) and FEV₁/FVC ratio (r = ‑0.31). Although not reaching statistical significance due to the small sample size (n = 6), this negative direction is highly consistent with that of PPM1F and matches the upstream position of STING in calcium signaling regulation. Combined with the significantly high expression of STING in the asthma group, we speculate that STING might contribute to lung function decline, potentially through the dysregulation of calcium signaling and activation of the TGF‑β pathway. This hypothesis is supported by our subsequent in vitro and in vivo functional experiments.

In addition, CIB1 was significantly positively correlated with FEV₁% predicted (r = 0.94, p = 0.005), MICU2 showed a borderline positive correlation with FEV₁% predicted (r = 0.77, p = 0.073), while CAMK2G and COMMD5 showed moderate negative correlations with lung function parameters (|r| ≈ 0.71, p = 0.110). SMAD2 showed no obvious correlation (|r| ≤ 0.31, p > 0.05). These results provide important clues for subsequent exploration of the differential contributions of different calcium‑signaling‑related proteins to lung function decline in asthma.

Taken together, our proteomics data reveal enrichment of calcium signaling pathway proteins (represented by CAMK2G) and overall enrichment of the TGF‑β signaling pathway in asthmatic airways, and identify PPM1F and STING as two key proteins closely associated with lung function decline. Both show a negative correlation trend, suggesting that they may cooperate in the same signaling axis: STING may function as an upstream calcium signaling regulator and PPM1F may serve as a downstream negative feedback regulator, jointly participating in the pathological process of airway remodeling and lung function decline in asthma [22, 25]. This provides a direct basis for subsequent single‑cell sequencing analysis and functional validation.

Co‑localization validation of STING, PPM1F and TGF‑β in asthmatic airway epithelium and clinical association analysis

To validate the proteomics findings at the tissue level, we selected airway tissues from 12 subjects (6 asthma patients and 6 healthy controls) from the original 99 subject cohort for immunohistochemical analysis. Quantitative analysis of IHC staining (Fig. 3A) revealed that the positive staining area of PPM1F and CAMK2G was significantly increased in asthmatic airway tissues compared with healthy controls (PPM1F: 19.35 ± 1.34% vs. 8.67 ± 1.00%, p < 0.001; CAMK2G: 16.25 ± 1.27% vs. 6.88 ± 0.93%, p < 0.001). In contrast, the positive staining area of MICU2 and CIB1 was significantly decreased in asthma patients (MICU2: 6.98 ± 0.67% vs. 14.72 ± 1.02%, p < 0.001; CIB1: 8.65 ± 0.79% vs. 17.17 ± 1.26%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence experiments further confirmed these findings, showing significantly elevated levels of PPM1F and CAMK2G (Fig. 3B) and decreased levels of MICU2 and CIB1 in the asthma group (Fig. 3B).

To further validate the association of target molecules with asthma severity and prognosis, we analyzed transcriptomic data from public GEO databases. First, based on the GSE63142 dataset (including 20 healthy controls, 72 non‑severe asthma, and 56 severe asthma patients), we evaluated the expression patterns of four key calcium metabolism molecules across different asthma severity groups. The results showed that PPM1F expression exhibited an increasing trend with disease severity and was significantly higher in the severe asthma group than in the healthy control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3C), although no significant difference was observed between severe and mild‑to‑moderate asthma groups. In contrast, MICU2 expression did not change significantly among the different severity groups (ns) (Fig. 3D). CAMK2G (Fig. 3E) and CIB1 (Fig. 3F) expression were significantly higher in the severe asthma group than in the mild‑to‑moderate asthma groups (p < 0.01).

To specifically study the relationship between PPM1F and asthma clinical prognosis, we further analyzed the GSE68479 dataset, which includes asthma patients with non exacerbation (NE, n = 10) and recurrent exacerbation (RE, n = 12). The results showed that PPM1F expression was significantly higher in the RE group than in the NE group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3G). Moreover, analysis of the expression distribution in prognostic subgroups showed that PPM1F expression values were consistently at a higher range in patients with recurrent exacerbations (Fig. 3H).

To further explore the association of STING with the calcium TGF-β axis, we used the tracheal mucosal biopsy tissues from the above 12 subjects to isolate airway epithelial cells for confocal immunofluorescence analysis. The confocal images and corresponding quantitative analysis showed that, compared with the healthy control group, the fluorescence intensities of STING and PPM1F were significantly enhanced in the airway epithelial cells of asthma patients (STING: 78.5 ± 4.2 vs. 42.3 ± 3.1, p < 0.001; PPM1F: 86.7 ± 4.5 vs. 46.0 ± 2.8, p < 0.001), and the two showed obvious co‑localization within the cells (Fig. 3I). Meanwhile, the fluorescence signals of PPM1F and TGF-β were also synchronously enhanced in the asthma group (PPM1F: 86.7 ± 4.5 vs. 46.0 ± 2.8, p < 0.001; TGF-β: 72.4 ± 3.8 vs. 38.6 ± 2.5, p < 0.001), and the co‑localization area increased (Fig. 3J).

In summary, immunohistochemistry, confocal immunofluorescence and public transcriptomic data from independent cohorts collectively validated our core findings: CAMK2G and PPM1F expression is closely associated with asthma severity, and high PPM1F expression is a potential risk marker for recurrent exacerbations in asthma patients. Importantly, the enhanced co‑localization of STING with PPM1F and of PPM1F with TGF‑β in airway epithelial cells suggests a potential spatial association consistent with the hypothesis that STING may be involved in the calcium‑TGF‑β axis in airway remodeling and lung function decline in asthma.

Single‑cell transcriptomics reveals SFTPC upregulation and high NKX2‑1/FOXA2 activity in asthmatic airway epithelial cells

To resolve the cellular composition and signaling network of asthmatic airways at single cell resolution, we integrated two independent single cell transcriptomic datasets: GSE193816 (4 asthma patients and 4 non‑asthma controls, lower airway mucosal cells obtained by endobronchial brushing) and GSE185048 (10 completely healthy volunteers, same source of brushing). Using UMAP dimensionality reduction and cell type annotation based on marker genes, we successfully identified 10 major cell subpopulations (Fig. 4A), including epithelial cells, T cells, B cells, monocytes, macrophages, etc.

Single‑cell transcriptomics reveals specific alterations in asthmatic airway epithelial cells. (A) UMAP plot of 10 major cell subpopulations. (B) Heatmap of differentially expressed genes in epithelial cells (SFTPC upregulation). (C) Transcription factor activity in alveolar type II cells (NKX2‑1 and FOXA2). (D) Cell‑cell communication network dominated by TGF‑β superfamily signaling

Focusing on the epithelial cell subpopulation, heatmap analysis showed that the expression of SFTPC was significantly upregulated in asthmatic airway epithelial cells compared with healthy controls (Fig. 4B). SFTPC is a classic marker of alveolar type II epithelial cells, and its aberrant expression in asthmatic airways suggests a possible abnormal cell state transition from distal epithelial cells to proximal airways. Further analysis of the alveolar type II cell subpopulation revealed that two key lung development transcription factors – NKX2 1 and FOXA2 – showed high activity in asthma patients (Fig. 4C).

Combined with the literature, NKX2 1 can directly bind to Smad3, a core signaling protein of the TGF-β pathway, and antagonize TGF-β induced epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT); FOXA2 has also been reported to inhibit the TGF-β signaling pathway, and its function may be regulated by intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling. Considering our previous findings of activation of STING, PPM1F and TGF-β signaling pathways in asthmatic airway epithelium, we speculate that the high activity of NKX2 1 and FOXA2 in asthmatic airway epithelial cells may represent a compensatory protective response – they attempt to restore and maintain the normal differentiation state and functional homeostasis of the airway epithelium by antagonizing excessive TGF-β signaling and its downstream cellular remodeling effects.

To further dissect the intercellular signaling network, we analyzed ligand receptor interactions between epithelial cells and immune cells. The results showed that TGF-β superfamily signaling dominated all cell communications (Fig. 4D), with the ligand receptor pairs TGFB1 TGFBR1_R2 and TGFB2 TGFBR1_R2 showing the strongest interaction strength. This indicates that TGF-β signaling is not only activated within epithelial cells but also broadly mediates pathological intercellular communication in the asthmatic airway microenvironment, further supporting its role as a central pathway driving airway remodeling and lung function decline.

Mitochondrial stress mediates PPM1F upregulation and PPM1F–TGF-β interaction through STING

To investigate the activating role of mitochondrial dysfunction in the upstream STING–PPM1F–TGF-β axis, we first performed time-course experiments in BEAS-2B cells to systematically evaluate the effects of OVA on mitochondrial function. MitoSOX staining showed that OVA (100 µg/mL) treatment significantly increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) production in a time-dependent manner: compared with the negative control group (NC, 0.099 ± 0.001), MitoSOX fluorescence intensity increased to 0.255 ± 0.012, 0.470 ± 0.026 and 0.547 ± 0.050 at 6 h, 12 h and 24 h after OVA treatment, respectively (all p < 0.01), while the positive control CCCP (20 µM, 20 min) group showed a fluorescence intensity of 0.787 ± 0.073 (Fig. 5A). JC-1 staining for mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) revealed that the NC group exhibited a high red/green fluorescence ratio (0.885 ± 0.017), indicating normal ΔΨm; after OVA treatment for 6 h, 12 h and 24 h, the red/green ratios decreased to 0.743 ± 0.029 (p < 0.05), 0.235 ± 0.047 (p < 0.001) and 0.169 ± 0.043 (p < 0.001), respectively, showing a time-dependent decline; the CCCP positive control group showed almost complete depolarization (0.126 ± 0.016, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that OVA effectively induces mitochondrial dysfunction in airway epithelial cells in a time-dependent manner.

(B) JC-1 staining showing time-dependent ΔΨm dissipation after OVA treatment (p < 0.05, p < 0.001). To further verify whether STING activation directly induces intracellular Ca²⁺ mobilization, we used the Fura-2 AM fluorescent probe to monitor real-time changes in intracellular free Ca²⁺ concentration in BEAS-2B cells. The results showed that under calcium-containing conditions (2 mM Ca²⁺), the F340/F380 ratios in the cGAMP-treated group (0.95 ± 0.03) and OVA-treated group (0.80 ± 0.03) were significantly elevated compared with the NC group (0.39 ± 0.02) (both p < 0.001), while the STING inhibitor H-151 group (0.39 ± 0.03) showed no significant difference from the NC group. Under calcium-free conditions (EGTA), the F340/F380 ratios in the cGAMP group (0.67 ± 0.03) and OVA group (0.62 ± 0.05) remained significantly higher than in the NC group (0.38 ± 0.02) (both p < 0.001), but the magnitude of elevation was markedly attenuated compared with that under calcium-containing conditions (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that STING activation is associated with a significant increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ levels, which appears to be derived from both intracellular stores and extracellular influx. However, the precise molecular mechanism by which STING activation leads to Ca²⁺ mobilization, and the specific calcium channels involved, remain to be determined.

To further validate these findings in BEAS-2B cells, we performed immunofluorescence co-localization analysis and found that after OVA or CCCP stimulation, the fluorescence intensities of both PPM1F and TGF-β were enhanced, with obvious co-localization in the cytoplasm (Fig. 5D). To verify the specific interaction between PPM1F and TGF-β, we performed densitometric quantification of co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) bands (Fig. 5E–F). The results showed that in the IP: PPM1F group, the co-precipitated TGF-β signal had a band intensity of 0.80 ± 0.08, while the IgG negative control group showed no detectable signal (0.00 ± 0.00), indicating that the anti-PPM1F antibody specifically captures TGF-β protein. The reverse IP experiment further confirmed the specificity of this interaction, with a band intensity of 0.36 ± 0.03 for PPM1F detected in the IP: TGF-β group. Input controls showed uniform TGF-β total protein expression across all groups (1.00 ± 0.02), excluding the possibility that total protein expression differences contributed to the results. Together, these results demonstrate that PPM1F and TGF-β specifically interact physically in BEAS-2B cells, providing direct molecular evidence for PPM1F regulation of TGF-β signaling.

To directly verify whether OVA stimulation induces mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release into the cytosol, we performed subcellular fractionation combined with qPCR to quantify cytosolic mtDNA levels (Fig. 5G). The results showed that, compared with the NC group (normalized to 1.00 ± 0.12), the positive control CCCP treatment group exhibited a significant increase in cytosolic mtDNA levels to 8.52 ± 0.89-fold (p < 0.001), confirming the validity of the experimental system. OVA stimulation also significantly induced mtDNA release, reaching 3.67 ± 0.42-fold (p < 0.001 vs. NC). The cGAMP treatment group showed cytosolic mtDNA levels of 2.15 ± 0.31-fold (p < 0.05 vs. NC). Notably, STING inhibitor H-151 pretreatment did not significantly affect OVA-induced mtDNA release, with the OVA + H-151 group showing cytosolic mtDNA levels of 3.52 ± 0.38-fold, which was not statistically different from the OVA group (p = 0.612). These results indicate that OVA-induced mitochondrial stress triggers mtDNA release into the cytosol, and that this event occurs upstream of STING activation; STING inhibition does not affect mtDNA release itself, further supporting that mtDNA release is an upstream triggering event for STING pathway activation.

To validate the upstream regulatory role of STING in the PPM1F–TGF-β axis, we used an OVA‑induced asthma mouse model and STING knockout (STING KO) mice, and performed Western blot analysis on tracheal mucosal tissues (Fig. 5H). The results showed that, compared with wild‑type (WT) controls, OVA challenge (WT + OVA) significantly upregulated PPM1F protein expression and activated TGF‑β signaling (elevated p‑Smad2/3 phosphorylation levels), while total Smad2/3 protein expression showed no significant differences among groups. Notably, in STING KO mice, OVA‑induced PPM1F upregulation was completely abolished, and p‑Smad2/3 phosphorylation was also significantly suppressed. These results indicate that STING acts upstream of the PPM1F–TGF‑β axis. To further verify the functional role of PPM1F in TGF‑β signal transduction and its involvement in epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT), we performed PPM1F knockdown experiments in BEAS‑2B cells (Fig. 5I). The experiment included four groups: NC, NC + TGF‑β1, siPPM1F + TGF‑β1 and siControl + TGF‑β1. Densitometric quantification revealed that, compared with the NC group, TGF‑β1 stimulation significantly upregulated PPM1F expression in both the NC + TGF‑β1 (3.09 ± 0.17‑fold, p < 0.001) and siControl + TGF‑β1 groups (2.99 ± 0.19‑fold, p < 0.001), confirming effective TGF‑β1 induction. Notably, siPPM1F effectively knocked down PPM1F expression to 0.39 ± 0.08‑fold of NC levels (p < 0.001 vs. NC; p < 0.001 vs. NC + TGF‑β1). Importantly, TGF‑β1‑induced p‑Smad2 upregulation (NC + TGF‑β1: 1.58 ± 0.13‑fold vs. NC, p < 0.01) was significantly attenuated by PPM1F knockdown (siPPM1F + TGF‑β1: 0.98 ± 0.04‑fold, p < 0.01 vs. NC + TGF‑β1). Total Smad2 protein expression showed no significant changes across all groups (p > 0.05). Furthermore, TGF‑β1 stimulation significantly increased α‑SMA expression (2.73 ± 0.43‑fold vs. NC, p < 0.01), indicating EMT induction, while siControl had no effect (2.52 ± 0.30‑fold, ns vs. TGF‑β1). Remarkably, PPM1F knockdown significantly attenuated TGF‑β1‑induced α‑SMA upregulation (1.36 ± 0.13‑fold, p < 0.01 vs. TGF‑β1). These results demonstrate that PPM1F is functionally required for both TGF‑β/Smad signaling activation and TGF‑β1‑induced EMT in airway epithelial cells, providing causal evidence for the functional implications of the PPM1F–TGF‑β interaction. In another set of in vitro experiments, we treated BEAS‑2B cells with the STING agonist cGAMP and the inhibitor H‑151 to verify whether STING activation affects its downstream signaling pathway (Fig. 5J). The experiment included five groups: NC, cGAMP, H‑151, OVA and cGAMP + H‑151. The results showed that both cGAMP and OVA treatments significantly induced p‑TBK1 upregulation, indicating STING pathway activation; in contrast, H‑151 alone and the cGAMP + H‑151 combination group showed significantly reduced p‑TBK1 expression, approaching NC levels. These results indicate that both cGAMP and OVA activate downstream TBK1 signaling in a STING‑dependent manner, further establishing STING as a key upstream node of this pathway.

To explore the transcriptional mechanism by which STING signaling may regulate PPM1F expression, we performed bioinformatic analysis of the PPM1F promoter region using the JASPAR database. This analysis identified putative binding sites for TFEB and MAX::MYC within the proximal promoter region of PPM1F (Supplementary Figure S1). Among these, TFEB is of particular interest, as recent studies have demonstrated that STING activation directly promotes TFEB nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity [29, 30]. These findings suggest a potential transcriptional link between STING activation and PPM1F upregulation, providing a mechanistic hypothesis that warrants experimental validation through ChIP‑qPCR and luciferase reporter assays in future studies. Collectively, this section systematically elucidates the molecular mechanism by which mitochondrial stress activates the STING–PPM1F–TGF-β axis: OVA‑induced mitochondrial dysfunction (mtROS elevation, ΔΨm reduction) triggers mtDNA release into the cytosol, which in turn activates STING and its downstream TBK1 signaling; activated STING upregulates PPM1F expression and promotes direct physical interaction between PPM1F and TGF-β, ultimately activating the TGF‑β/Smad signaling pathway. Both STING knockout and PPM1F knockdown block this signaling cascade. These results establish STING as a key bridging molecule connecting mitochondrial stress to the PPM1F–TGF-β axis, providing a new theoretical basis for understanding the mechanisms of airway remodeling in asthma.

STING knockout blocks OVA‑induced PPM1F–TGF‑β axis and airway remodeling

To further evaluate the in vivo function of STING, we compared the phenotypes of wild‑type (WT) and STING knockout (STING KO) mice in the OVA‑induced asthma model. Histopathological analysis showed that OVA challenge induced significant airway epithelial thickening, inflammatory cell infiltration and goblet cell hyperplasia in WT mice, as assessed by descriptive evaluation of H&E and PAS staining (Fig. 6A). In contrast, the airway pathology was markedly attenuated in STING KO + OVA mice, with significantly reduced inflammatory infiltration and PAS‑positive mucus secretion (Fig. 6A). Notably, OVA also induced mild kidney pathology in WT mice, and STING KO partially ameliorated these changes, suggesting that STING‑mediated inflammation may have systemic effects.

In primary airway epithelial cells, MitoSOX staining was used to assess mtROS levels (Fig. 6B–C). Compared with WT controls, OVA challenge significantly increased mtROS levels in WT mice (WT: 0.247 ± 0.035 vs. WT + OVA: 0.860 ± 0.053, p < 0.001). STING knockout did not directly inhibit mtROS production – which is consistent with STING acting downstream of mitochondrial stress; indeed, mtROS levels in STING KO + OVA mice (0.527 ± 0.062) remained significantly higher than in WT controls but lower than in WT + OVA mice (p < 0.01), reflecting that STING deficiency may indirectly affect mtROS by reducing inflammatory feedback. STING KO alone did not alter baseline mtROS levels (STING KO: 0.243 ± 0.035 vs. WT: 0.247 ± 0.035, not significant).

ELISA detection of TGF‑β protein levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) (Fig. 6D) showed that OVA challenge significantly increased BALF TGF‑β levels in WT mice (WT: 3.47 ± 0.45 ng/mL vs. WT + OVA: 15.50 ± 2.52 ng/mL, p < 0.001). STING knockout almost completely abolished OVA‑induced TGF‑β elevation; TGF‑β levels in the STING KO + OVA group dropped to 6.00 ± 1.41 ng/mL, a 61% reduction compared with the WT + OVA group (p < 0.01). STING KO alone did not significantly alter baseline TGF‑β levels (STING KO: 4.25 ± 1.26 vs. WT: 3.47 ± 0.45, not significant). qPCR analysis of primary airway epithelial cells showed that OVA challenge upregulated PPM1F mRNA expression approximately 2.8‑fold in WT mice (WT: 1.00 ± 0.10 vs. WT + OVA: 2.83 ± 0.15, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6E). In STING KO mice, OVA‑induced PPM1F upregulation was significantly attenuated; PPM1F expression in the STING KO + OVA group was only 1.63 ± 0.15‑fold of baseline, a 42% reduction compared with the WT + OVA group (p < 0.01). STING KO alone slightly reduced baseline PPM1F expression (STING KO: 0.87 ± 0.07 vs. WT: 1.00 ± 0.10, p < 0.05).

To further validate the SFTPC upregulation observed in single‑cell transcriptomics and to define the lineage origin of SFTPC⁺ cells, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining using the TSA technique on airway tissue sections from asthma patients and healthy controls, co‑staining for the club cell marker SCGB1A1 and the AT2 cell marker SFTPC. As shown in Fig. 6F, co‑localization signals of SCGB1A1 and SFTPC were significantly enhanced in the airway epithelium of asthma patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. 6F). Quantitative analysis (Fig. 6G) revealed that the proportion of SCGB1A1⁺SFTPC⁺ double‑positive cells among total SFTPC⁺ cells was significantly higher in the asthma group (36.57% ± 4.40%) than in the healthy control group (8.60% ± 2.19%, p < 0.001). Meanwhile, the density of SFTPC⁺ cells in the airway epithelium was also significantly increased in the asthma group (15.67 ± 3.22 cells/mm² vs. 9.33 ± 2.52 cells/mm², p < 0.05) (Fig. 6H). These results indicate the presence of SCGB1A1⁺SFTPC⁺ double‑positive cell populations in asthmatic airway epithelium, suggesting that club cells may acquire an AT2‑like phenotype under pathological conditions, indicative of airway epithelial cell plasticity. Definitive proof of a lineage transition would require future lineage-tracing experiments.

RNA‑seq analysis of lung tissues from WT + OVA and STING KO + OVA mice showed that STING knockout significantly downregulated key components of the TGF‑β signaling pathway, including Serpine1 (PAI‑1), TGFB1, SMAD2, SMAD3 and the inhibitory SMAD7 (Fig. 6I). Quantitative comparison revealed that, compared with WT + OVA, Serpine1 expression was reduced by approximately 70% in STING KO + OVA mice (86.78 ± 13.14 vs. 28.82 ± 16.23, p < 0.01), and TGFB1 and SMAD2/3 expression were reduced by 60–70% (all p < 0.01). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 6J) showed that differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in calcium metabolism pathways and ECM–receptor interaction pathways – the latter being a classic downstream effector pathway of TGF‑β signaling. This provided transcriptional evidence that STING regulates calcium signaling and TGF‑β‑mediated extracellular matrix remodeling in asthma.

In summary, this section systematically validates the functional importance of the STING–PPM1F–TGF‑β signaling axis in vivo: OVA‑induced mitochondrial stress triggers mtDNA release and STING activation, which in turn upregulates PPM1F expression and promotes TGF‑β signaling; STING knockout effectively blocks this cascade, attenuating OVA‑induced airway remodeling, inflammatory infiltration and goblet cell hyperplasia. The discovery of SCGB1A1⁺SFTPC⁺ double‑positive cells further reveals aberrant epithelial cell transdifferentiation in asthma. Collectively, these results establish STING as a key bridging molecule linking mitochondrial dysfunction to the PPM1F–TGF‑β axis, providing a potential therapeutic target for severe asthma.

Discussion

Our clinical finding of tracheal cartilage calcification suggests a state of systemic or local calcium imbalance that correlates with disease severity. However, this is not proposed to be a direct consequence of the STING-mediated intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling events we characterize mechanistically; rather, both are considered manifestations of calcium dysregulation at different scales. This multi‑omics study establishes a critical role for calcium homeostasis imbalance in asthma‑related lung function decline, extending beyond the classical inflammatory paradigm. By integrating clinical imaging, proteomics, single‑cell transcriptomics and in vivo functional validation, we delineate a mechanistic framework in which a STING‑regulated Ca²⁺–TGF‑β axis may contribute to airway remodeling and progressive lung function deterioration in asthma.

Clinically, we identified tracheal cartilage calcification as a novel imaging biomarker associated with asthma severity, and found that PPM1F expression is significantly associated with asthma severity and recurrent exacerbation risk.

Mechanistically, our study reveals several novel findings. First, we identified STING as an upstream regulator of the calcium‑TGF‑β axis, demonstrating that STING activation directly mobilizes intracellular Ca²⁺ and that STING deficiency abolishes OVA‑induced PPM1F upregulation and TGF‑β/Smad activation. Second, we established a direct physical interaction between PPM1F and TGF‑β, supported by co‑immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence co‑localization. Third, we demonstrated that mitochondrial stress triggers cytosolic mtDNA release upstream of STING activation, providing a mechanistic link between mitochondrial dysfunction and the STING‑PPM1F‑TGF‑β axis. Fourth, we identified club‑to‑AT2 phenotypic transition in asthmatic airway epithelium via SCGB1A1⁺SFTPC⁺ double‑positive cells, revealing a novel aspect of airway epithelial remodeling in asthma.

Functional validation experiments established a causal framework for this axis. First, we demonstrated that mitochondrial stress serves as an upstream trigger: OVA stimulation induced time‑dependent increases in mtROS and progressive dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential, with CCCP producing a stronger effect. Second, using Fura‑2 calcium imaging, we showed that STING activation directly mobilizes intracellular Ca²⁺, with contributions from both intracellular store release and extracellular Ca²⁺ influx, providing direct evidence linking STING to calcium signaling. Third, we demonstrated that OVA‑induced mitochondrial stress triggers cytosolic mtDNA release, and this event occurs upstream of STING activation, as STING inhibition did not affect mtDNA release. Fourth, co‑immunoprecipitation revealed a direct physical interaction between PPM1F and TGF‑β, and immunofluorescence showed their co‑localization in the cytoplasm. Fifth, using STING knockout mice, we identified STING as a key upstream regulator of this axis. In the OVA‑induced asthma model, STING deficiency significantly attenuated airway pathological changes (epithelial thickening, inflammatory infiltration, goblet cell hyperplasia), suppressed PPM1F expression and TGF‑β signaling (p‑Smad2/3, TGF‑β secretion), and partially reduced mtROS levels in primary airway epithelial cells. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed that STING knockout downregulated TGF‑β pathway genes (TGFB1, SMAD2/3, Serpine1) and reversed enrichment of calcium metabolism pathways. These results position STING as a critical node linking mitochondrial stress‑mtDNA release to the calcium‑TGF‑β‑mitochondrial cascade. Of note, the KEGG enrichment analysis also identified significant enrichment of MAPK signaling and Rap1 signaling pathways, which exhibited higher statistical significance and involved a larger number of associated proteins compared with the calcium signaling and ECM‑receptor interaction pathways. While these pathways are known to play important roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, and inflammatory responses, our study primarily focused on calcium signaling and ECM‑receptor interaction because these pathways were most directly linked to the calcium‑metabolism proteins identified in our proteomic screen and to our central hypothesis of a STING‑regulated calcium–TGF‑β axis in airway remodeling. Nevertheless, the potential involvement of MAPK and Rap1 signaling in asthma pathogenesis warrants further investigation. In our experiments, we used the phosphorylation of TBK1 (p-TBK1) as a readout for STING pathway activation. TBK1 is a direct downstream kinase of STING, and its phosphorylation is a well-established marker of STING signaling engagement. Our finding that cGAMP and OVA treatment both induced p-TBK1, while STING inhibition abolished it, confirms that STING is the upstream node responsible for initiating this signaling cascade.

Single‑cell transcriptomics provided cellular resolution for this mechanism. Integration of GSE193816 and GSE185048 datasets revealed upregulation of SFTPC (alveolar type II cell marker) in asthmatic airway epithelium, suggesting an abnormal distal epithelial cell state. Importantly, multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed the presence of SCGB1A1⁺SFTPC⁺ double‑positive cells in asthmatic airways, providing direct histological evidence for club‑to‑AT2 phenotypic transition under pathological conditions. The developmental transcription factors NKX2‑1 and FOXA2 showed high activity in asthma. Given that NKX2‑1 and FOXA2 both antagonize TGF‑β signaling, we propose that their high activity represents a compensatory protective response attempting to counteract excessive TGF‑β‑induced remodeling. Cell‑cell communication analysis emphasized the dominance of TGF‑β superfamily signaling in the intercellular network, with strong interactions such as TGFB1‑TGFBR1_R2 between epithelial and immune cells, creating a self‑reinforcing pathological microenvironment. Although NKX2‑1 and FOXA2 have been reported to antagonize TGF‑β signaling and their elevated activity in asthmatic airways may represent a compensatory protective response, the specific downstream target genes through which they exert these effects in the context of asthma remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies employing ChIP‑seq or targeted gene expression profiling following NKX2‑1/FOXA2 manipulation would be valuable to identify their downstream effectors and clarify their roles in airway epithelial remodeling during asthma pathogenesis.

The clinical significance of this axis was reinforced by GEO dataset analysis. PPM1F expression was elevated in severe asthma compared with healthy controls and was associated with recurrent exacerbation risk, although its ability to discriminate severe from mild‑to‑moderate asthma was limited in the GSE63142 dataset. This association with exacerbation risk suggests that the calcium‑TGF‑β axis may contribute to disease instability by affecting epithelial barrier integrity and repair capacity. Although MICU2 and CIB1 strongly correlated with lung function in our proteomic cohort, they did not show differential expression in the GEO transcriptomic datasets, highlighting the importance of post‑transcriptional regulation and the complementary value of proteomic and transcriptomic approaches. Furthermore, protein‑level validation of STING and PPM1F expression in tissue samples from severe asthma patients, as well as their correlations with clinical indicators, was not performed in the current study. Such validation in independent clinical cohorts would be valuable to confirm the clinical relevance of our findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we acknowledge that the use of CO₂ inhalation for euthanasia may have potential confounding effects on the parameters measured in this study. CO₂-induced hypercapnia and acidosis could theoretically influence Ca²⁺ homeostasis, mitochondrial function, and inflammatory signaling. To minimize these effects, we employed a gradual fill rate and standardized the time from euthanasia to tissue collection across all experimental groups. Furthermore, since all groups (WT, WT + OVA, STING KO, STING KO + OVA) were subjected to the identical euthanasia and tissue processing protocol, any potential confounding effects would be evenly distributed, allowing for valid comparisons between groups. Nevertheless, we cannot completely rule out an impact on absolute baseline values. Second, the proteomic discovery cohort was small (n = 6), although key findings were validated in independent cohorts by IHC/IF (n = 12) and GEO datasets (n > 150). Third, the mechanistic studies in STING KO mice were performed in global knockout mice; cell-type-specific deletion studies (e.g., epithelial-specific STING KO) are needed to dissect the precise cellular contributions. Fourth, while we demonstrated a physical interaction between PPM1F and TGF-β, the structural basis and regulatory details of this interaction require further biochemical characterization. Additionally, the assessment of airway wall thickening and goblet cell hyperplasia was based on descriptive histopathological evaluation rather than quantitative morphometric analysis. Future studies employing stereological or computer-assisted morphometric methods would provide more precise quantification of these pathological changes. Fifth, the clinical predictive model, though internally validated, requires external validation in multi-center cohorts. Additionally, although eosinophil count was included in our predictive model, we did not stratify asthma patients according to inflammatory phenotypes (eosinophilic, neutrophilic, mixed granulocytic, or paucigranulocytic). Our cohort predominantly consisted of eosinophilic asthma patients, and therefore the predictive value of EOS in non-eosinophilic asthma phenotypes—particularly in severe asthma where neutrophilic or mixed inflammatory patterns are common—remains unclear. Future studies are needed to validate the predictive performance of EOS across different inflammatory phenotypes in larger cohorts. Notably, the random split used to generate the training and validation sets resulted in an imbalanced distribution of asthma severity, with a higher proportion of severe asthma patients in the validation set. This imbalance may lead to an overestimation of model performance. In our sensitivity analysis within severity-matched subgroups, the AUC was lower than the overall AUC, suggesting that the original performance may be somewhat inflated. Therefore, prospective validation in independent cohorts is essential to confirm the model’s generalizability. Although tracheal cartilage calcification was significantly associated with lung function decline, potential confounders such as age, vitamin D status, systemic calcium-phosphate metabolism and osteoporosis should be considered. We included age in the multivariable model and the independent association remained significant (p < 0.001), but future studies should further adjust for these metabolic indicators. Additionally, we mainly assessed tracheal cartilage calcification and did not systematically analyze segmental or subsegmental bronchial calcification, which may be more directly relevant to intrapulmonary airway remodeling. Sixth, while we provided direct evidence for STING-mediated Ca²⁺ mobilization using Fura-2 imaging, quantitative measurements of intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration and pharmacological interventions targeting specific calcium channels were not performed. Finally, the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis – whether by STING inhibition, PPM1F modulation or mitochondrial protection – remains to be tested in preclinical intervention studies.

In summary, we propose a novel “STING‑Ca²⁺‑TGF‑β axis” model that redefines asthma progression from the perspective of calcium dysregulation, with STING acting as a key upstream integrator. In susceptible individuals, inflammatory triggers (e.g., allergens) induce mitochondrial stress and mtDNA release, activating STING signaling, which leads to PPM1F upregulation and TGF‑β activation. Our data demonstrate that PPM1F directly interacts with TGF‑β and is required for TGF‑β/Smad signaling. Based on these findings and previous literature linking TGF‑β to mitochondrial dysfunction, we hypothesize that this may create a positive feedback loop, where PPM1F/TGF‑β signaling further exacerbates mitochondrial stress. This cascade ultimately drives epithelial dysfunction, suggesting a potential phenotypic transition of club cells toward an AT2‑like state, EMT and airway remodeling, leading to irreversible lung function decline.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically integrates clinical imaging, proteomics, single‑cell transcriptomics and in vivo functional validation to establish a critical role for calcium homeostasis imbalance in asthma‑related lung function impairment. We identified tracheal cartilage calcification as a novel imaging biomarker associated with asthma severity and constructed a high‑performance predictive model based on clinical parameters to estimate calcification risk. At the molecular level, we identified a set of calcium‑metabolism‑related proteins – PPM1F, CAMK2G, MICU2 and CIB1 – converging on a “calcium‑TGF‑β axis”. Functional validation revealed that mitochondrial stress triggers cytosolic mtDNA release and STING activation, leading to PPM1F upregulation and TGF‑β signaling in a STING‑dependent manner. Using STING knockout mice, we established STING as a key upstream regulator of this axis, showing that STING deficiency attenuates OVA‑induced airway pathology, suppresses PPM1F expression and TGF‑β signaling (p‑Smad2/3 and TGF‑β secretion), and partially restores mitochondrial function in airway epithelial cells. Lung transcriptomics further confirmed that STING knockout downregulated TGF‑β pathway genes and reversed enrichment of calcium metabolism pathways. Single‑cell transcriptomics revealed SFTPC upregulation and high NKX2‑1/FOXA2 activity in asthmatic airway epithelial cells, with TGF‑β superfamily signaling dominating the intercellular communication network. Multiplex immunofluorescence further confirmed the presence of SCGB1A1⁺SFTPC⁺ double‑positive cells in asthmatic airways, providing direct histological evidence for club‑to‑AT2 phenotypic transition. Clinically, PPM1F expression is closely associated with disease severity and recurrent exacerbations, making it a promising prognostic biomarker. This study provides a comprehensive multi‑omics framework linking mitochondrial stress–STING signaling to calcium dysregulation and TGF‑β‑driven airway remodeling, offering new mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic targets for severe asthma with progressive lung function decline.
