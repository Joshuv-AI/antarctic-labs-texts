Mechanisms of gut microbiota metabolite-mediated gut-heart Axis in heart failure: An integrative study based on network pharmacology☆
Authors: Feng Tan, Yuan Cheng, Jianwei Yan, Zeqi Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) is a prevalent terminal-stage cardiovascular disease worldwide. Recent years have seen growing research on the gut-heart axis, yet the role of gut microbiota-derived metabolites in HF pathogenesis remains insufficiently explored.MethodsThis network pharmacology study screened overlapping targets of heart failure and gut metabolites via gutMGene, GeneCards and OMIM databases, then carried out PPI, enrichment, M-S-M-T network construction, as well as drug-likeness and toxicity prediction of metabolites.ResultsWe identified 47 candidate targets at the intersection of HF-related genes and gut metabolite-associated targets. PPI network analysis suggested that AKT1, TNF, TP53, IL6, and PPARG may represent key hub targets. Functional enrichment indicated potential associations with the NF-κB and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. The M-S-M-T regulatory network results show that PPARG is the hub target with the most extensive connections. Drug-likeness and toxicity predictions suggested favorable in silico profiles for five metabolites, including 3-indolepropionic acid (IPA) and indole-3-lactic acid (ILA).ConclusionThis network pharmacology study screened core genes, metabolites and pathways associated with the gut-heart axis in heart failure, providing hypothetical support for subsequent experimental research on intervening heart failure via gut microbiota-derived metabolites.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a cardiovascular syndrome resulting from structural or functional cardiac impairments that compromise ventricular filling or ejection, and it constitutes the terminal phase of numerous cardiac disorders. Heart failure affects more than 56 million people worldwide, and its prevalence is expected to continue increasing in the coming decades. HF is a leading cause of cardiovascular mortality and represents a substantial global health burden [1], [2], [3]. Despite advances in socioeconomic conditions and medical care, which have enabled extensive clinical and research efforts into chronic HF management, diagnosis, and treatment, its incidence and mortality rates remain persistently high. Consequently, the prevalence of HF is projected to increase, presenting a growing public health challenge.

The pathogenesis of HF is highly complex and multidimensional. Current understanding identifies myocardial remodeling, excessive neuroendocrine system activation, and immune-metabolic inflammatory dysregulation as core pathological mechanisms. Despite this, clinical management of HF remains significantly limited. Existing therapeutic regimens can delay disease progression and alleviate symptoms, but they cannot achieve a cure. Furthermore, treatment strategies remain centered on post-onset intervention, lacking effective methods for primary prevention.

Recent research into the gut-heart axis has suggested that gut microbiota and their metabolites may be associated with the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular diseases [4]. This perspective provides a potential framework for investigating HF-related mechanisms. Patients with HF have been reported to exhibit altered gut microbiota composition, reduced microbial diversity, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and changes in circulating microbiota-derived metabolites [5]. Studies have shown that significant changes occur in the gut microbiota of HF patients, characterized by an imbalance in the ratio of beneficial bacteria (such as Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides) and harmful bacteria (such as Proteobacteria) [6], [7]. Meanwhile, different metabolites of the gut microbiota exhibit a clear bidirectional regulatory effect on cardiac function—harmful metabolites such as trimethylamine N-oxide, lipopolysaccharide [8], [9], and the newly discovered uremic toxin indoxyl sulfate play important roles in the progression of HF [10]. Conversely, beneficial metabolites produced by probiotic intestinal bacteria through fermenting dietary fiber and other substrates, such as short-chain fatty acids, 3-indolepropionic acid (IPA), and indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), not only provide energy for intestinal epithelial cells and strengthen the integrity of the intestinal barrier, but also exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, metabolic regulatory, and vascular tone regulatory effects through the blood circulation, thereby playing a cardioprotective role [6], [11], [12]. Clinical studies have found that certain probiotics, which are live microorganisms conferring health benefits on the host when supplied in sufficient doses, reduce inflammation in patients with HF [13], [14], [15], and animal studies demonstrate that regulating the intestinal microbiota improves cardiac function and survival in HF models [16]. This evidence indicates that gut microbiota and their metabolites hold considerable therapeutic potential for HF, underscoring the importance of these microbial communities in human health and disease. However, current research on the gut-heart axis primarily examines the downstream effects of microbial metabolites, while the initiating mechanisms of gut microbiota dysbiosis in HF remain unclear. The specific molecular mechanisms, core targets, and key signaling pathways through which gut microbiota metabolites regulate HF are not fully elucidated. A more comprehensive understanding of active gut microbiota metabolites and their potential pharmacological mechanisms in HF requires further investigation.

As an interdisciplinary field, network pharmacology can be used to screen disease-related candidate compounds and targets by analyzing interactions within complex biological systems, making it a useful exploratory tool for studying the potential regulatory networks of gut microbiota-derived metabolites. Consequently, this study adopted a network pharmacology approach, focusing on gut microbiota-derived metabolites and using databases such as SEA, STP, gutMGene, PubChem, CTD, GeneCards, and OMIM for integrative analysis. We systematically explored the predicted associations between these metabolites and HF-related targets and signaling pathways. A microbiota-substrate-metabolite-target (M-S-M-T) regulatory network was constructed to provide a theoretical and hypothesis-generating framework for future mechanistic validation. This work may offer a novel perspective for further research on the gut-heart axis in HF.

Methods

Identification of gut microbiota metabolites and targets

In this study, gut microbiota metabolites and human intestinal targets were obtained from the gutMGene database, which is a comprehensive resource library collecting the association information of gut microbiota-metabolites-host genes. Subsequently, the metabolites were uploaded to the PubChem database to obtain their corresponding Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) format. Based on the regulatory targets of enriched metabolites identified in the SEA and STP databases for intracellular signaling–a common approach in target screening for metabolic regulation and mechanistic studies–we incorporated the target data for intestinal microbial metabolites, with the species specified as “Homo sapiens”. This integrated target set served as a reliable prediction to support the identification of signaling pathway-related targets. Then, the targets of gut microbiota metabolites from the SEA database and STP database were intersected using Venn diagrams to obtain common targets for further analysis.

Screening of disease-related genes and determination of intersection genes

Using “heart failure” as the keyword, HF-related disease genes were searched through the GeneCards database, OMIM, and CTD database (URLs of disease databases are shown in Table 1). Genes covered by multiple databases were retained using Venn diagrams. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses

The intersecting genes were uploaded to the DAVID database for Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis (including three dimensions: biological process [BP], cellular component [CC], molecular function [MF]) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. Statistical analysis: entries meeting the criterion of P value (P.adjust) < 0.05 were included as significantly enriched entries, and bioinformatics tools were used to visualize the results.

Construction and analysis of PPI network

The intersecting genes of gut microbiota metabolites and HF key targets were uploaded to the STRING database to construct a PPI network; then, the degree centrality (DC) of nodes was calculated using the CytoNCA plugin of Cytoscape, and genes with top DC values were selected as core targets.

Evaluation of druglikeness and toxicity

The druglikeness and toxicity of the identified key metabolites were measured through the SwissADME platform and ADMETlab platform respectively (URLs of the SwissADME platform and ADMETlab platform are shown in Table 1). Druglikeness was determined according to Lipinski's five rules: (1) molecular weight ≤500 Da (Dalton); (2) lipophilic-hydrophilic partition coefficient ≤5; (3) number of hydrogen bond acceptors ≤10; (4) number of hydrogen bond donors ≤5; (5) number of rotatable bonds (RB) ≤10. Then, toxicity parameters were evaluated by ADMETlab 3.0, including human hepatotoxicity (H-HT), drug-induced liver injury (DILI), carcinogenicity, and hERG channel blocking activity.

Results

Identification of gut microbiota metabolite-associated targets potentially linked to HF

First, using the SEA and STP databases, this study obtained 797 and 1291 targets related to gut microbiota-derived metabolites and human intestinal metabolism, respectively. The 570 overlapping targets were identified as the main predicted metabolite-associated targets (Fig. 1A). Next, disease-related genes were cross-verified using the CTD, GeneCards, and OMIM databases, yielding five high-confidence intersecting genes (Fig. 1B). Finally, a three-way Venn analysis of the STP-SEA combined dataset, the gutMGene database, and HF-related genes was performed to further connect the pre-screened gene pool with HF and gut microbiota-related functional genes, ultimately identifying 47 candidate core targets (Fig. 1C). Construction of PPI network

The 47 candidate core targets were submitted to the STRING platform for PPI network analysis, and node centrality was visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape 3.7.1. The HF-related PPI network included 40 nodes and 566 edges. The PPI network analysis suggested four major functional modules: an immune-inflammatory module, including TNF, IL6, TLR4, NLRP3, and IL1B; a signal transduction module, including AKT1, MAPK family members, and GSK3B; an apoptosis-related module, including TP53, BCL2, and CASP3; and a metabolic regulation module, including PPARG, GHRL, and NOS3. These modules may be associated with inflammatory signaling, cellular survival, apoptosis, metabolic regulation, and vascular function in HF-related biological processes (Fig. 3A-C). Identification of key gut microbiota-derived metabolites linked to core HF targets

To clarify which gut microbiota-derived metabolites were most closely associated with the core HF-related genes, we further mapped the metabolite–target relationships obtained from the gutMGene, SEA, and STP databases onto the five hub targets identified in the PPI network, namely AKT1, TNF, TP53, IL6, and PPARG (Fig. 3A-C). This analysis identified several candidate metabolites potentially linked to these core targets, including tryptophan-derived indole metabolites, fatty acids, and polyphenol-related metabolites. Among them, IPA, ILA, linoleic acid, luteolin, and tryptamine were prioritized as key metabolites in this study because they were predicted to be connected to core HF-related targets and were further supported by favorable predicted drug-likeness and toxicity profiles (Fig. 4). Specifically, indole-derived metabolites such as IPA and ILA were mainly associated with inflammatory and oxidative stress-related targets, whereas linoleic acid and luteolin were linked to metabolic and inflammatory regulatory targets such as PPARG, AKT1, IL6, TNF, and TP53. These findings suggest that gut microbiota-derived metabolites may be involved in HF-related immune-inflammatory, oxidative stress, and metabolic pathways through their predicted associations with these hub genes. "M-S-M-T" network analysis

This study constructed a M-S-M-T network to delineate the predicted relationships among gut microbiota, substrates, metabolites, and HF-related targets. The network included 74 gut microbial taxa, 29 substrates, 49 metabolites, and five hub genes (Fig. 4). Representative microbial taxa included Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Bifidobacterium bifidum, Bifidobacterium longum, and Escherichia coli. Representative substrates included rutin, tryptophan, and linoleic acid, while representative metabolites included polyphenols such as quercetin and luteolin, indole derivatives, and fatty acids such as linoleic acid and palmitic acid. Among the five hub genes, PPARG showed the most extensive predicted connections with metabolites and gut microbiota, suggesting that it may represent an important hub in the predicted M-S-M-T network. AKT1, TNF, IL6, and TP53 were also connected with specific metabolite-microbe axes. Together, these components form an integrated predictive network that may help prioritize candidate metabolites and targets for subsequent experimental validation.

Discussion

Imbalanced gut microbiota homeostasis has been associated with the onset and progression of HF, and gut microbiota-derived metabolites may serve as important mediators of host-microbiota interactions within the gut-heart axis [17]. Using network pharmacology, this study integrated data from SEA, STP, gutMGene, PubChem, CTD, GeneCards, and OMIM databases to identify candidate regulatory targets and pathways.

This study used SEA and STP to predict potential targets of gut microbiota-derived metabolites. SEA provides target information based on chemical similarity, whereas STP predicts potential protein targets based on molecular structure and similarity to known ligands. The intersection of the SEA and STP databases provided a set of predicted targets with potential structural or functional associations with gut microbiota-derived metabolites. The gutMGene database was subsequently employed because it contains manually curated associations among gut microbiota, metabolites, and host genes. By performing three-way Venn analysis of the STP-SEA combined target set, gutMGene functional gene set, and HF-related genes, we prioritized 47 candidate core targets that may be related to both gut microbiota-derived metabolites and HF pathogenesis. This multi-database cross-verification strategy may improve the reliability of candidate target prioritization, although these predicted associations require experimental validation.

PPI network analysis suggested that Akt1, Tnf, Tp53, Il6, and Pparg may represent core hub genes through which gut microbiota-derived metabolites may be involved in the pathogenesis of HF. These genes were connected with biological processes involving metabolic regulation, cell survival, inflammatory response, and apoptosis [18]. AKT1 is a central component of the PI3K-Akt pathway, which has been implicated in cardiomyocyte survival, glucose metabolism, and responses to ischemic or hypoxic stress [19], [20]. In the present network analysis, AKT1 appeared as an important hub gene, suggesting that AKT-related signaling may be involved in the predicted metabolite-target network. Previous studies have suggested that indole-derived metabolites may be associated with oxidative stress responses and cardiometabolic regulation [21], [22]. However, whether IPA directly modulates AKT1-related signaling in HF requires further experimental validation.

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) are important mediators of chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction in HF. Intestinal barrier impairment in HF may permit endotoxin translocation into the circulation, thereby activating inflammatory pathways such as TLR4/NF-κB and promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [23], [24]. In the present analysis, TNF and IL6 were identified as hub targets in the predicted network, suggesting that inflammatory signaling may be an important link between gut microbiota-derived metabolites and HF-related pathological processes. Indole-3-lactic acid and other tryptophan-derived metabolites have been reported in previous studies to be associated with immune and inflammatory regulation [25], but their direct effects on TNF- or IL6-related signaling in HF remain to be experimentally validated.

TP53 is involved in the regulation of apoptosis and oxidative stress and has been implicated in cardiovascular injury and remodeling [26]. In HF-related pathological conditions, oxidative stress and hypoxia may be associated with TP53 activation and apoptosis-related signaling [26]. In the present network analysis, TP53 appeared as one of the hub targets, suggesting that apoptosis and oxidative stress pathways may participate in the predicted metabolite-target network. Metabolites such as luteolin and quercetin have been reported to influence oxidative stress and apoptosis-related pathways in previous studies [27], [28]. However, whether these metabolites directly modulate TP53-related mechanisms in HF requires further experimental validation.

PPARG is a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily and is involved in metabolic homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory regulation [29]. In HF, metabolic remodeling and inflammation are important pathological features, and previous studies have suggested that PPARG-related pathways may be associated with myocardial lipid metabolism and insulin resistance [30]. In the present M-S-M-T network, PPARG showed extensive predicted connections with gut microbiota-derived metabolites and microbial taxa, suggesting that it may represent an important hub connecting metabolite-related signals with HF-related metabolic and inflammatory pathways. These findings support PPARG as a candidate target for future mechanistic validation rather than direct evidence of metabolite-based therapeutic intervention.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses suggested that the core targets potentially associated with gut microbiota-derived metabolites and HF were mainly enriched in immune-inflammatory responses, cell survival, metabolism, and oxidative stress, with extensive crosstalk among these biological processes. Among immune-related pathways, Toll-like receptor, IL-17, TNF, and NF-κB signaling showed prominent enrichment, suggesting that inflammatory signaling may represent an important biological context linking gut microbiota-derived metabolites to HF-related pathological processes [31], [32]. In addition, PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling were also enriched, indicating that cell survival, endothelial function, and myocardial remodeling may be involved in the predicted metabolite-target network [33]. These findings provide mechanistic clues for understanding how gut microbiota-derived metabolites may be associated with HF-related immune-inflammatory and metabolic pathways. However, these results are based on database-derived enrichment analyses and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of direct causal regulation.

To clarify the overall relationship between gut microbiota metabolites and HF, we constructed a comprehensive M-S-M-T regulatory network. This network linked gut microbial taxa, including Lactobacillus thermophilus, Escherichia coli, and Enterobacter, with substrates such as tryptophan and linoleic acid; representative metabolites prioritized in this study, including IPA, ILA, linoleic acid, luteolin, and tryptamine; and core HF-related targets including AKT1, IL6, and PPARG. In the present analysis, IL6 and PPARG were identified as hub targets in the predicted network, while previous studies have also implicated IL-6-related inflammatory signaling and PPARG-related metabolic regulation in HF [30], [34]. These database-derived associations suggest that specific gut microbiota-derived metabolites may be connected with HF-related immune-inflammatory and metabolic pathways, including PI3K-Akt and IL-17 signaling. Importantly, the M-S-M-T network should be interpreted as a predictive framework for prioritizing candidate metabolites and targets rather than direct evidence that these metabolites causally regulate HF progression. Further experimental studies are required to validate the metabolite-target interactions and their functional consequences in HF.

The evaluation of drug-likeness and toxicity provides a preliminary basis for prioritizing candidate gut microbiota-derived metabolites for future investigation. In this study, the key metabolites identified in the predicted network, including IPA, ILA, linoleic acid, luteolin, and tryptamine, showed favorable predicted drug-likeness and toxicity profiles in SwissADME and ADMETlab analyses. These in silico findings suggest that the identified metabolites may be suitable candidates for further experimental evaluation.

This study preliminarily reveals the potential regulatory mechanisms of gut microbiota-derived metabolites in HF-related pathways, with several limitations remaining. First, the established M-S-M-T regulatory network only demonstrates correlative relationships rather than direct causality. In vivo and in vitro experiments are required to validate metabolite-target interactions and their functional regulatory effects on HF-associated signaling pathways. Second, the gut-heart axis is a sophisticated cross-organ system integrating neural, immune, endocrine, and metabolic pathways. This study mainly focuses on the metabolic and immune-inflammatory pathways mediated by AKT1, IL6, and PPARG, while the potential involvement of neural and endocrine pathways is not fully explored. Further studies incorporating multiple cross-organ pathways are needed to comprehensively elucidate the gut-heart axis mechanisms. In addition, database-based analyses fail to account for individual variations, including age, sex, underlying diseases, medication history, diet, and gut microbial profiles. Moreover, the primary causes of gut microbiota dysbiosis in HF and the crosstalk between gut microbiota and other organs such as the liver and kidney remain uninvestigated. Furthermore, the optimal administration route, dosage, and treatment duration of the candidate metabolites require further experimental and clinical validation. Collectively, the proposed regulatory network is preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Future studies combining multi-omics strategies and organ-specific functional validation will help refine the comprehensive regulatory mechanism of the gut-heart axis in HF.

Conclusions

This study employed network pharmacology to explore the potential associations between gut microbiota-derived metabolites and HF-related molecular targets and pathways. Key target genes, including AKT1, TNF, TP53, IL6, and PPARG, were identified as central nodes in the predicted network. These targets may be involved in HF-related inflammatory, oxidative stress, and metabolic pathways. In addition, key metabolites with favorable predicted drug-likeness and toxicity profiles, such as IPA and ILA, were identified together with core signaling pathways, including NF-κB and PI3K-Akt. These findings provide a hypothesis-generating framework for future experimental studies on the gut-heart axis in HF, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence that these metabolites can prevent or treat HF.
