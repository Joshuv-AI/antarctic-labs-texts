Sharp regularity of sub-Riemannian length-minimizing curves
A. Socionovo

Abstract
. A longstanding open question in sub-Riemannian geometry is the C ∞ C^{\infty} -smoothness of length-minimizing curves (in their arc-length parameterization). A recent example answered this question negatively, showing the existence of a sub-Riemannian manifold with a length minimizer of class C 2 ∖ C 3 C^{2}\setminus C^{3} . In this paper, we study a broader class of sub-Riemannian structures containing that of the aforementioned example, and we prove that length-minimizing curves are of class C 2 C^{2} within these structures. In particular, the aforementioned example is sharp (within this class of sub-Riemannian structures).



1.
Introduction

One of the most difficult and important open problems in sub-Riemannian geometry is the regularity of geodesics, i.e., of those curves that are length-minimizing for short times (here and hereafter, by regularity of a curve we mean the regularity of its arc-length parameterization). The problem has remained open since the works of Strichartz in
[
20
,
21
]
.

A sub-Riemannian structure is a pair
(M,\mathscr{D})
, where
M
is a smooth manifold,
\mathscr{D}\subset TM
is a smooth bracket-generating distribution (i.e., a distribution satisfying the Hörmander condition). When
\mathscr{D}
is endowed with a smooth metric
g
, we have a sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\mathscr{D},g)
. A
horizontal
(or,
admissible
) curve is an absolutely continuous trajectory in
M
which is tangent to
\mathscr{D}
almost everywhere. The length of horizontal curves is defined in the standard way with respect to the metric
g
and the distance between two points
p,q\in M
is the infimum of the length of horizontal curves joining
p
and
q
. The Chow-Rashewskii and the Hopf-Rinow theorems ensure the existence of geodesics between every pair of points sufficiently close.

Candidate curves to be length-minimizing are called
extremal curves
and they are individuated by some necessary conditions appearing in the Pontryagin maximum principle. Extremal curves are divided in two non-disjoint classes, which are called
normal
and
abnormal
(or
singular
) curves. Normal curves, which are the only ones appearing in Riemannian geometry, are smooth and length-minimizing for short times. Instead, abnormal curves are a priori no more regular than being absolutely continuous, and their length-minimality properties are also unknown. The difficulty of the problem of the regularity of length-minimizing curves lies in the presence of
strictly abnormal
(or
strictly singular
) extremals, i.e., those curves that are abnormal but not normal. We refer the reader to
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
17
,
19
]
for an exhaustive introduction to sub-Riemannian geometry and to the regularity problem of geodesics.

The first example of a strictly abnormal curve that is length-minimizing, was discovered by Montgomery in his ground-breaking work
[
16
]
. After that, many other examples have been found, see for instance
[
1
,
15
]
. All these examples are smooth curves (here and hereafter, by smooth we mean
C^{\infty}
), indicating the profound complexity of the problem. Over the years, some partial results have been found. On the one hand, it was first proved in
[
10
,
14
]
that abnormal length-minimizing curves cannot have corners, and later in
[
11
,
18
]
that they must have a tangent line. In some special cases they are of class
C^{1}
, see
[
4
,
7
,
13
]
, but in general the
C^{1}
regularity remains an open question. On the other hand, necessary conditions for the minimality of abnormal curves can be derived by the differential analysis of the end-point map (see
[
2
, Chapter 8]
for a detailed introduction to the end-point map). This theory is well known up to the second order, see
[
3
,
8
]
, and it has been partially extended to the
n
-th order in
[
5
]
. Finally, an algorithm to produce abnormal curves in Carnot groups of sufficiently high dimension (without any new regularity or minimality result) is given in
[
9
]
.

A recent and significant progress for the theory is the following result. On
\mathbb{R}^{3}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be the vector fields defined by

(1.1)

X_{1}(x):=\partial_{x_{1}},\quad X_{2}(x):=\partial_{x_{2}}+P(x)^{2}\partial_{x_{3}},

where

(1.2)

P(x)=x_{1}^{a}-x_{2}^{b},\quad a,b\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1},

and let
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
be the sub-Riemannian manifold where
\mathscr{D}=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
and
g
is the metric making
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
orthonormal. Let also consider, for
\varepsilon>0
, the curve

(1.3)

\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm,\pm}:[0,\varepsilon]\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm,\pm}(t)=\begin{cases}(\pm t^{q},\pm t,0),\quad b\geq a,\\
(\pm t,\pm t^{q},0),\quad a\geq b,\end{cases}\quad q:=\frac{\max\{a,b\}}{\min\{a,b\}}.

We simply denote by
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+,+}
. If
a
and
b
are not multiples, every curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm,\pm}
is of class
C^{\lfloor q\rfloor}\setminus C^{\lfloor q\rfloor+1}
at
0
in its arc-length reparameterization.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
a=2
and
b\geq 5
is odd, then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is length-minimizing in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
. Moreover, the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is non-smooth at 0.

Theorem
1.1
is proved in
[
6
]
and provides the first ever examples of non-smooth sub-Riemannian length-minimizing curves, answering a longstanding open question in sub-Riemannian geometry. The geodesic with the lowest regularity is of class
C^{2}\setminus C^{3}
and it is obtained for
a=2
and
b=5
(the singularity is at a boundary point).

In this paper, we delve into the reasoning and methodology behind the construction of the examples in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) and we prove that Theorem
1.1
is sharp, in the sense that it is not possible to find a geodesic of class
C^{1}\setminus C^{2}
within these examples. Indeed, in this setting, every length minimizer is at least of class
C^{1}
by
[
13
, Section 4]
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
b>a\geq 3
, or
a=2
and
b=3
, or
a>b\geq 3
, or
b=2
and
a=3
, then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, every curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm,\pm}
is not length-minimizing.

Since every
C^{1}
but non-smooth abnormal curve in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D})
must contain
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
,
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+,-}
,
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{-,+}
, or
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{-,-}
as a subarc (this is a consequence of (
2.19
) and Lemma
2.6
below, and of the arguments used in
[
13
, Section 4]
), we have the following consequence.

Corollary 1.3
.

For all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, every geodesic in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
is of class
C^{2}
.

Another consequence of Theorem
1.1
is the existence of
C^{1}
-rigid curves that are not length-minimizing, a problem that has been pointed out by Montgomenry in his book
[
17
, Chapter 3]
. A curve is
C^{1}
-rigid if it admits a
C^{1}
neighborhood containing no other horizontal curves (up to reparameterization) with the same endpoints. In Lemma
2.13
we prove that, for every
b>a
, the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is
C^{1}
-rigid. As a consequence, we have the following result.

Corollary 1.4
.

For all
b>a\geq 3
and for
(a,b)=(2,3)
, the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is a strictly abnormal and
C^{1}
-rigid curve which is not length-minimizing.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is constructive, as it provides an explicit example of a shorter curve connecting the same points. This kind of construction is, in our opinion, a general interesting tool to understand (as a first analysis) the minimality of abnormal curves. Indeed, in these examples, the shorter curves we construct are a close approximation of normal curves, see
[
6
, Proposition 2.2]
. On the other hand, the proof makes explicit the analytical role of the parameters
a
and
b
, which are in fact the discriminant for both the minimality and the regularity of the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Instead, the intrinsic and geometric nature of these parameters persists as an enigma, and we believe that understanding it is a deep and central question. Finding an abstract argument that unifies all these points –the explicit construction in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, approximation of normal curves, and the interplay between analytical and geometric aspects of the parameters
a
and
b
– would represent, in our opinion, a significant breakthrough for the theory, which may leads either to find an example of a
C^{1}\setminus C^{2}
length-minimizing curve, or to prove the
C^{2}
(or
C^{1}
) regularity, or to find an example of a non-smooth geodesic with internal singular point. Ultimately, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
may open new frontiers of research in sub-Riemannian geometry.

We conclude this introductory part with an outline of the paper.

In Section
2
, we introduce a class of sub-Riemannian structures on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that generalizes those defined in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), see Definition
2.1
, and we study their general properties, including a geometric characterization of abnormal curves in Lemma
2.6
. We also provide some concrete examples and we explain the ideas behind the construction of the examples in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), whose structure (in particular the square of
P
) is inspired by the Liu-Sussmann example in
[
15
]
.

In Section 3, we prove Theorem
1.2
. The proof is written only for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, since the other cases for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+,-}
,
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+,-}
, and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{-,-}
are identical. The proof relies upon the construction of a competing curve which is shorter than
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Since every horizontal curve is implicitly defined by its first two coordinates, the construction of the competitor can be reduced to a constrained problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, see Definition
3.1
. The constraint we obtain is, due to the Stokes theorem, of isoperimetric type with a weighted area, see Lemma
3.2
. The competing curve (actually, its projection onto the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane) is then built as follows: we cut
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
near the origin with a segment connecting 0 to the point
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(\rho)
, for some
0<\rho<\varepsilon
; then we follow the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
up to its end-point; finally, we add the boundary of a square of length
0<\delta<\varepsilon
at the end-point of
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Thanks to Remark
2.4
, the sub-Riemannian length of both
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
and the competitor is measured by the Euclidean length of their projection on the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane. Therefore, the cut produces a gain of length
\Delta L(\rho)
, but it modifies the third coordinate. The square is then needed to restore the third coordinate to 0, despite it wastes an amount of length equal to
\delta
. The proof consists in showing that the parameters
\rho,\delta>0
can be chosen such that the length gained with the cut is greater than that lost with the boundary of the square, i.e., such that
\Delta L(\rho)>\delta
.

2.
Sub-Riemannian structures on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

In this section, we study the class of sub-Riemannian structures on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined in Definition
2.1
below and their general properties, we provide some examples, and we explain the ideas behind the construction of the structure defined in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
).

Definition 2.1
.

On
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the coordinates
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
we define the vector fields

(2.1)

X_{1}(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\quad X_{2}(x)=\varphi(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}+\psi(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}},

where
\varphi,\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R})
are such that
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
satisfy the Hörmander condition. We also define the distribution
\mathscr{D}:=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
and the sub-Riemannian metric
g
that makes
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
orthonormal.

Definition 2.2
.

An absolutely continuous curve
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is
admissible
or
horizontal
in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D})
if there exists a function
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in L^{2}([0,\tau],\mathbb{R}^{2})
, called the
control
of
\eta
, such that

(2.2)

\dot{\eta}(t)=u_{1}X_{1}(\eta(t))+u_{2}X_{2}(\eta(t)),\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,\tau].

By (
2.2
) it follows that a horizontal curve
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and its control
u
satisfy, for almost every
t\in[0,\tau]
,

(2.3)

\dot{\eta}_{1}(t)=u_{1}(t),\quad\dot{\eta}_{2}(t)=u_{2}(t)\varphi(\eta(t)),\quad\dot{\eta}_{3}(t)=u_{2}(t)\psi(\eta(t)).

If
\varphi\circ\eta\neq 0
, then we have
\displaystyle u_{2}(t)=\frac{\dot{\eta}_{2}(t)}{\varphi(\eta(t))}
, for a.e.
t\in[0,\tau]
, and thus the third coordinate of
\eta
is uniquely determined by the first two coordinates of
\eta
via the integral constraint

(2.4)

\eta_{3}(t)=\eta_{3}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\eta}_{2}(s)\frac{\psi(\eta(s))}{\varphi(\eta(s))}ds,\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,\tau]$}.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a horizontal curve in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
and let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be two points. We say that:

(i)

the sub-Riemannian length of
\eta
is

(2.5)

L_{\mathrm{sR}}(\eta):=\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{g\big(\dot{\eta}(t),\dot{\eta}(t)\big)}\,dt;

(ii)

the sub-Riemannian distance between
x
and
y
is

(2.6)

d(x,y):=\inf\{L_{\mathrm{sR}}(\widetilde{\eta})\mid\widetilde{\eta}:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{ is horizontal},\;\widetilde{\eta}(0)=x,\;\widetilde{\eta}(\tau)=y\};

(iii)

\eta
is length-minimizing if for every horizontal curve
\widetilde{\eta}:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\widetilde{\eta}(0)=\eta(0)
and
\widetilde{\eta}(\tau)=\eta(\tau)
, we have
L_{\mathrm{sR}}(\eta)\leq L_{\mathrm{sR}}(\widetilde{\eta})
.

The fact that the distance in (ii) is well defined (i.e.
d(x,y)<+\infty
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
) is a direct consequence of the Hörmander condition and it is well-known as Chow-Rashewskii theorem, see for instance
[
2
]
. Consequently, also the definition of length-minimality in (iii) is well posed.

Remark 2.4
.

If
\varphi\equiv 1
every horizontal curve
\eta
has control
u=(\dot{\eta}_{1},\dot{\eta}_{2})
. In this case, since
g
is the metric making
X_{1},X_{2}
orthonormal, we have that the sub-Riemannian length of
\eta
is the Euclidean length of the plane curve
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
.

In the literature, necessary conditions (of the first order) for horizontal curves to be length-minimizing can be obtained in two different ways. The first possible approach is to study the first differential of the end-point map together with the Lagrange multiplier rules. The other way is to use the Pontryagin maximum principle provided by classical control theory. Both the methods are well illustrated in
[
2
,
3
]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a length-minimizing curve in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
. Then there exists an absolutely continuous function of covectors
p:[0,\tau]\to(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{*}
such that, for
i=1,2
and for all
t\in[0,\tau]
, one of the following conditions holds

(2.7)

\displaystyle p(t)\big(X_{i}(\eta(t))\big)=g\big(\dot{\eta}(t),X_{i}(\eta(t))\big),

(2.8)

\displaystyle p(t)\big(X_{i}(\eta(t))\big)=0,\quad\text{with }p(t)\neq 0.

If
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is horizontal and there exists a function of covectors
p:[0,\tau]\to(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{*}
such that (
2.7
) or (
2.8
) holds, then
\eta
is called an
extremal curve
. Extremal curves satisfying (
2.7
) are called
normal
, while extremal curves satisfying (
2.8
) are called
abnormal
. The conditions in (
2.7
)-(
2.8
) are mutually exclusive only if the function of covectors
p
is fixed, i.e., an extremal curve can be both normal and abnormal with different covectors. A curve that is only normal (resp., only abnormal) is called
strictly normal
(resp.,
strictly abnormal
).

We briefly recall that normal curves are solution to the normal Hamiltonian equations. Here and hereafter, we use the short notation
\varphi_{x_{i}},\psi_{x_{i}}
to denote the partial derivatives of the functions
\varphi
and
\psi
(see Definition
2.1
) with reaspect to
x_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
. Defining the normal Hamiltonian of
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
as

(2.9)

H:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{*}\to\mathbb{R},\quad H(x,p):=\frac{1}{2}p_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}h(x,p)^{2},

where
h(x,p):=p_{2}\varphi(x)+p_{3}\psi(x)
, we have that normal curves with their covectors are solution to the system of equations

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{1}=p_{1}\\
\dot{x}_{2}=h(x,p)\varphi(x)\\
\dot{x}_{3}=h(x,p)\psi(x),\end{cases}\begin{cases}\dot{p}_{1}=-h(x,p)(p_{2}\varphi_{x_{1}}(x)+p_{3}\psi_{x_{1}}(x))\\
\dot{p}_{2}=-h(x,p)(p_{2}\varphi_{x_{2}}(x)+p_{3}\psi_{x_{2}}(x))\\
\dot{p}_{3}=-h(x,p)(p_{2}\varphi_{x_{3}}(x)+p_{3}\psi_{x_{3}}(x)).\end{cases}

We refer the reader to
[
2
,
19
]
for all these facts, including a proof of (
2.10
).

We now focus on the study of the abnormal curves in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D})
, aiming to provide their geometric characterization. As a matter of fact, the abnormal condition (
2.8
) does not depend on the metric. We start by recalling that necessary conditions of the second order for abnormal curves to be length-minimizing, known as Goh conditions, can be obtained by the study of the second-order differential of the end-point map. Moreover, the optimality assumption is unnecessary when
\dim(\mathscr{D}_{x})=2
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus, every abnormal curve
\eta
in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D})
with its function of covectors
p
satisfies

(2.11)

p(t)([X_{1},X_{2}](\eta(t)))=0,\quad\mathrm{for\;all}\;t\in[0,\tau],

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the Lie bracket of vector fields. For more details about Goh conditions and for a proof of (
2.11
) we refer the reader to
[
2
]
.

Combining (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) we deduce the desired geometric characterization for abnormal curves in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D})
.

Lemma 2.6
.

A horizontal curve
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is abnormal in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D})
if and only if it is supported inside the surface

(2.12)

\Sigma_{\mathscr{D}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid\varphi(x)\psi_{x_{1}}(x)-\psi(x)\varphi_{x_{1}}(x)=0\}.

Proof.

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an abnormal curve and let
p=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}):[0,\tau]\to(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{*}
be its covector. By (
2.8
) with
i=1
we get
p_{1}=0
. Then by (
2.8
) with
i=2
and by (
2.11
) we have that
\eta
satisfies, for all
t\in[0,\tau]
,

(2.13)

\begin{cases}p_{2}(t)\varphi(\eta(t))+p_{3}(t)\psi(\eta(t))=0,\\
p_{2}(t)\varphi_{x_{1}}(\eta(t))+p_{3}(t)\psi_{x_{1}}(\eta(t))=0.\end{cases}

Since
(p_{2}(t),p_{3}(t))\neq 0
, we have that for all
t\in[0,\tau]
the determinant of the matrix of the system (
2.13
) is forced to be 0, that is

(2.14)

(\varphi\psi_{x_{1}}-\psi\varphi_{x_{1}})(\eta(t))=0.

Conversely, if
\eta
is supported in
\Sigma_{\mathscr{D}}
then it holds (
2.14
). Thus for every
t\in[0,\tau]
there exists
(p_{2}(t),p_{3}(t))\neq 0
such that (
2.13
) holds. Consequently, the covector
p:[0,\tau]\ni t\mapsto(0,p_{2}(t),p_{3}(t))\in(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{*}
ensures that
\eta
is abnormal.
∎

Definition 2.7
.

The set
\Sigma_{\mathscr{D}}
defined in (
2.12
) is the
Martinet surface
of
\mathscr{D}
.

We conclude this section providing some examples of distributions of the type appearing in Definition
2.1
, which vary depending on the functions
\varphi
and
\psi
. The aim is to study Martinet surfaces and abnormal curves within these examples. In particular, we mainly focus on the case where
\varphi,\psi
are chosen as in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), and we explain through an heuristic argument why the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+,+}
defined in (
1.3
) emerges as a natural candidate to be a non-smooth geodesic for every
b>a\geq 2
and for small
\varepsilon>0
.

The Heisenberg group.
This represents the simplest and most famous example of a sub-Riemannian manifold. It is obtained by setting
\varphi=1
and
\psi=x_{1}
. In this example, the Martinet surface is empty, so there are no abnormal curves. The Heisenberg group has been extensively studied in the literature, and interested readers are directed for example to
[
17
]
.

The Martinet example.
This example involves a slight modification of the Heisenberg group by choosing
\varphi=1
and
\psi=x_{1}^{2}
. Consequently, the Martinet surface is no longer empty and is given by the plane
x_{1}=0
. Thus, for
\varepsilon>0
, the curve

(2.15)

\alpha_{\varepsilon}:[0,\varepsilon]\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\alpha(t)=(0,t,0),

is abnormal. Since
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
is a segment in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, by Remark
2.4
it is optimal in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
for all
\varepsilon>0
, providing an example of an abnormal length-minimizing curve. However, the curve
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
is not strictly abnormal since it is also normal in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathscr{D},g)
with the constant covector
p=(0,1,0)
.

The Liu-Sussmann example.
This example, credited to Liu and Sussmann (see
[
15
]
or
[
17
, Chapter 3]
), consists of a slight modification of the Martinet example to make the curve
\alpha
in (
2.15
) strictly abnormal. The choice of
\varphi
and
\psi
here is
\varphi=1-x_{1}
and
\psi=x_{1}^{2}
. Consequently, the defining function of
\Sigma_{\mathscr{D}}
in (
2.12
) becomes

(2.16)

2x_{1}(1-x_{1})+x_{1}^{2}=x_{1}(2-x_{1}),

implying that the Martinet surface is the union of the two planes
x_{1}=0
and
x_{1}=2
.

The curve
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
2.15
) remains abnormal and, since
\varphi
is no longer equal to 1, the sub-Riemannian length of horizontal curves here is no longer the Euclidean length of their projection onto
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore, the length-minimality of
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
is no longer obvious in this example. In
[
15
]
(see also
[
17
, Chapter 3]
or
[
19
, Section 2.5]
) it is proved that
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
is both strictly abnormal and length-minimizing for small
\varepsilon>0
.

The proof of Liu and Sussmann relies on the fact that the curve
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
is
C^{1}
-rigid.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a horizontal curve with control
u
. For
\delta>0
, define

(2.17)

\begin{split}\mathscr{U}_{\eta,\delta}&:=\big\{\tilde{\eta}:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{ horizontal with control }\tilde{u}\mid\\

\frac{2b+1}{3}>2q-1,

which in fact reads

(3.26)

b+2>3\,\frac{b}{a}.

If the latter equation is satisfied, then we can chose small
\rho,\delta>0
such that
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is not length-minimizing. Indeed, it is enough to choose
\rho>0
small enough such that (
3.24
) is satisfied. This choice implicitly fixes
\delta>0
by (
3.11
) and ensures
L(\omega)<L(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
.

It is very easy to see that (
3.26
) is satisfied if and only if
a\geq 3
, or
a=2
and
b=3
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

We explain how to adapt the proof of Theorem
1.2
in the case
a>b
, exploiting the symmetry within the computations between
a
and
b
. If
a>b
then
q=\frac{a}{b}
, and the competing curve
\omega
is essentially the same: we cut from 0 to
(\rho,\rho^{q})
and we add the rectangle
E_{\delta}=(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{q})+[-\varepsilon\delta,0]\times[0,\varepsilon^{q}\delta]
. Again, we orient
\partial E_{\delta}
such that
{\mathrm{ind}}(E_{\delta})=-{\mathrm{ind}}(U_{\rho})
(see Remark
3.4
), and
\rho,\delta>0
are small parameters to be fixed at the end.

With the same computations as in (
3.12
)-(
3.17
) we get

(3.27)

\Delta_{3}^{\mathrm{cut}}(\rho)=\rho^{2a+1}\int_{0}^{1}(s^{a}-s^{b})^{2}ds\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\Delta_{3}^{\mathrm{cor}}(\delta)=a(b+a)\varepsilon^{2a+q}\delta^{3}(1+o(1)),

where, as before,
\Delta_{3}^{\mathrm{cut}}
is the error produced by the cut and
\Delta_{3}^{\mathrm{cor}}
is the error produced by the rectangle, both in absolute value. The power of
\rho
in
\Delta_{3}(\rho)
replaces
b
with
a
(as expected due to the symmetry in
a,b
), while the slight modification in the power of
\varepsilon
into
\Delta_{3}(\delta)
is irrelevant (
\varepsilon>0
is fixed). Condition (
3.11
) then implies that
\omega
is a competitor as soon as

(3.28)

\rho^{2a+1}=\bar{C}\delta^{3}(1+\bar{f}_{a,b}(\delta)),

for some
\bar{C}=\bar{C}(a,b,\varepsilon)>0
and
\bar{f}_{a,b}(\delta)=O(\delta)
.

The computations for the gain of length in (
3.19
)-(
3.20
) are identical, then (
3.21
) remains the same.

Finally, with similar estimates as in (
3.22
)-(
3.23
)-(
3.24
), we deduce that
\omega
is shorter than
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
as soon as

(3.29)

a+2>3\frac{a}{b},

for a suitable choice of
\rho>0
small enough. Once a small
\rho>0
is fixed, then also the small
\delta>0
is automatically determined. The conclusion is symmetric in
a,b
with respect to the one obtained in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Remark 3.7
.

The statement of Theorem
1.2
is stable with respect to arbitrary increases in the power of the polynomial
P
defining
\psi
. That is, the statement remains true for every choice of
\psi
of the form
\psi=(x_{1}^{a}-x_{2}^{b})^{c}
, for every natural numbers
a,b,c\geq 2
. Indeed, by repeating the same computations as in the proof Theorem
1.2
and in Remark
3.6
, we obtain, in place of (
3.26
)-(
3.29
), that
\omega
is shorter than
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
as soon as

(3.30)

\frac{c}{2}\max\{a,b\}+2>3q.

Since
c\geq 2
the latter equation is implied by (
3.26
) when
b>a
, and by (
3.29
) if
a>b
.
