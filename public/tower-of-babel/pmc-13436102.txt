Diabetes care performance across Europe: a cascade of care approach accounting for population diversity in 10 countries
Authors: Jorik Vergauwen, Philippe Bos, Katrien Danhieux, Josefien Van Olmen, Edwin Wouters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes mellitus is a growing public health concern across Europe, with substantial gaps in diagnosis, treatment, and glycemic control. Despite clinical guidelines, care performance varies widely, even between high-resource countries. This study aims to assess diabetes care performance across ten European countries using a cascade of care framework and to examine whether population characteristics explain cross-country differences.MethodsWe analyzed data from 18,499 individuals aged 50 and older from wave 6 of the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), including self-reported diabetes status, medication use, and HbA1c biomarkers. Diabetes was defined as either self-reported diagnosis, medication use, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%. The cascade of care framework assessed three stages: diagnosis, treatment, and glycemic control. Multivariate logistic regression models adjusted for sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health-related covariates.ResultsDiabetes prevalence ranged from 13% in Denmark to 37% in Spain and Greece, with up to 40% of cases undiagnosed. The share of individuals living with diabetes being diagnosed, in treatment and achieving glycemic control ranges between 35% and 50% depending on the country. Belgium consistently performed well across all stages, while Greece showed strong treatment and control outcomes despite low diagnostic rates. Population health profiles significantly influenced diagnostic effectiveness, with healthier individuals more likely to remain undiagnosed. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, limited indicators of the diabetes care process, and inability to distinguish diabetes types.ConclusionsDiabetes care performance varies markedly across Europe, with diagnosis representing the greatest point of attrition. Population characteristics influence care outcomes, especially at the diagnostic stage. Strengthening early detection and tailoring interventions to country-specific care gaps and population profiles are essential for improving diabetes outcomes and reducing health inequalities. Future research should explore longitudinal care trajectories and system-level determinants of care performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27990-1.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus represents a major public health challenge in Europe and globally, imposing an escalating disease burden as a chronic condition requiring lifelong management. Current estimates indicate that nearly 10% of the European population aged 20–79 years (66 million individuals) is living with diabetes, with projections suggesting this will rise in the coming decades [1]. The most common form of diabetes, type 2 diabetes, accounting for more than 95% of cases [1], predominantly affects middle-aged and older adults and is strongly linked to modifiable lifestyle factors including obesity, tobacco use, poor dietary patterns, and physical inactivity [2, 3].

In addition to the high diabetes prevalence, significant gaps exist throughout the diabetes care continuum. Approximately one in three Europeans living with diabetes remains undiagnosed and therefore outside the healthcare system entirely [1]. Among those who are diagnosed, care management remains suboptimal, with only half achieving target blood glucose levels [4, 5]. Early detection and appropriate management are crucial for preventing or delaying complications [3, 6, 7], yet these care gaps—from delayed diagnosis to inadequate glycemic control—significantly increase the risk of serious complications including cardiovascular disease, diabetic neuropathy, and retinopathy [3, 8].

The cascade of care (CoC) framework provides a comprehensive lens to examine the entire diabetes care trajectory, recognizing that individuals with diabetes must progress through sequential care stages to achieve successful disease management [9–11]. The CoC quantitatively tracks patient retention at each care stage, with its primary strength being the ability to identify specific points of attrition, such as diagnostic effectiveness or treatment adherence [12]. By pinpointing where care breakdowns occur, the CoC approach facilitates systematic comparison of care performance between healthcare systems and ultimately supports the development of targeted interventions and evidence-based policies to address identified gaps.

Current European diabetes research lacks comprehensive cross-country comparisons using the CoC framework. Instead, European comparisons have focused on isolated stages of the care continuum, such as diagnosis [13] or glycemic control [4], providing only fragmented insights into overall care performance. This study therefore employs harmonized multi-country data on individuals aged 50 and above from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) to pursue our first research objective: assessing diabetes care performance across European countries using the CoC framework. Specifically, we compare care performance at three key cascade stages: diagnosis, treatment, and glycemic control.

However, examining care performance gaps alone provides an incomplete picture. Previous research indicates that care disparities are not equally distributed but systematically patterned by lifestyle and health-related factors as well as sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics [14]. Persistent inequalities in diabetes care can in this sense be understood as one expression of broader health inequalities rooted in socioeconomic stratification, with social differentials manifesting at each stage of the diabetes care trajectory—making the cascade a useful lens through which to assess how equitably health systems serve different population groups. Migration background, education, income, and gender are associated not only with higher diabetes risk [2, 15–18], but also with increased likelihood of remaining undiagnosed [19–21]. Among diagnosed individuals, those with lower socioeconomic position demonstrate poorer diabetes control compared to their more advantaged counterparts [22–24]. The combination of delayed diagnosis and ineffective disease management in vulnerable groups creates cumulative disadvantage, exacerbating disparities in diabetes complications [24–26], thereby widening health inequalities.

Population aging, combined with growing sociodemographic diversity and persistent socioeconomic disparities both within and between European countries, challenges healthcare systems’ capacity to effectively manage diabetes along the care continuum. Additionally, European countries differ substantially in population health profiles, including the prevalence of health and lifestyle risk factors that directly influence diabetes care outcomes [27]. These population-level differences may systematically affect countries’ apparent care performance, potentially masking or amplifying healthcare system effectiveness. Moreover, differences in the organization of care between countries—which plays a crucial role from early detection to ongoing care coordination [28, 29]—may further explain variations in both overall care performance and equity outcomes [30–32].

This leads to our second research objective: to examine whether observed differences in care performance between countries can be attributed to variation in population characteristics. By accounting for population diversity—including sociodemographic composition, socioeconomic factors, and health-related factors—this analysis will provide insights into European countries’ underlying capacity to manage diabetes effectively, distinguishing healthcare system performance from population-level influences on care outcomes.

Methods

This study is a secondary analysis, drawing on the rich data infrastructure from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), a population-based panel survey covering most European countries [33, 34]. SHARE follows respondents aged 50 and older biennially, together with their cohabiting partners, collecting detailed longitudinal information on health and well-being, socioeconomic circumstances, and social and family networks. To address our research objectives, we analyze survey data from wave 6 (2015) complemented with objective health biomarkers obtained through dried blood spot (DBS) collection [35, 36]. DBS samples consist of drops of capillary whole blood collected via finger prick.

The analytical sample was derived from the SHARE wave 6 dataset—when the SHARE DBS initiative was implemented across twelve participating countries—through a series of sequential selection steps related to DBS collection eligibility, consent, biomarker validity, and data completeness. A full overview of all selection steps, including the number of respondents excluded at each stage, is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Trained interviewers collected DBS samples from 27,385 eligible respondents using standardized protocols seamlessly integrated into the main face-to-face interview process [37]. Eligibility required respondents to be part of a panel household, to consent to DBS collection, and not to be hindered by medical safety reasons, resulting in a participation rate of 65% among initially selected respondents. All samples underwent comprehensive laboratory processing and rigorous quality control procedures, including systematic validation and statistical adjustment to account for the non-clinical collection environment and ensure cross-national comparability [37, 38]. The SHARE DBS dataset provides seven blood biomarkers covering inflammatory markers, lipoproteins, and key indicators of metabolic and cardiovascular health [36]. For this study, we focus specifically on glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), which reflects average blood glucose levels over two to three months preceding the interview and serves as a reliable biomarker for diabetes diagnosis and management [39]. While we cannot distinguish between diabetes types with the available data, our focus is primarily on type 2 diabetes, which represents approximately 95% of all diabetes cases among adults aged 50 and older [1].

After excluding samples with incomplete or inadequate data collection, 23,482 SHARE wave 6 respondents aged 50 and older had at least one analyzed blood parameter. From this sample, we identified 19,608 individuals with available HbA1c measurements. We excluded France and Israel from the analysis due to data quality concerns. France participated with only four regions in the DBS collection, while Israel exhibited over 50% missing data for the HbA1c parameter. This resulted in an analytical sample of 18,862 individuals across ten countries. After excluding cases with missing information on covariates and weighting variables, our final analytical sample comprises 18,499 respondents (18,731 respondents for unweighted data) from the following European countries: Belgium, Denmark, Estonia, Germany, Greece, Italy, Slovenia, Sweden, Switzerland, and Spain. Hence, 67.6% of the 27,385 respondents who consented to DBS collection were retained in the final analytical sample, with the large majority of attrition occurring at the biomarker stage due to the absence of a valid biomarker or specific HbA1c measurement.

The cascade of diabetes care

We construct a CoC framework in a subsample of individuals living with diabetes, starting from (0) prevalence as the foundation, then assessing three stages of diabetes care: (1) diagnosis, (2) treatment, and (3) control. Prevalence serves as the starting point of our cascade, encompassing all individuals with diabetes regardless of their awareness or management status (27.5% of the sample). We identify diabetes cases through two pathways: self-reported diagnosis or medication use, including reports from previous SHARE waves (18.6% of the sample), and undiagnosed cases with HbA1c levels ≥ 6.5% (≥ 48 mmol/mol) who have never received a medical diagnosis nor reported diabetes medication use (8.9% of the sample) [40]. A recent validation study demonstrates high reliability of the self-reported diabetes and medication use in Danish SHARE data [41]. To assess the robustness of our findings to the ambiguity of the diagnosis item—which combines a formal diabetes diagnosis and a report of elevated blood sugar within a single survey question that cannot be disentangled in the data (cf. Table 1)—we conduct a sensitivity analysis applying a stricter definition of diagnosed diabetes. Under this definition, a respondent is classified as diagnosed only if they report having ever been told by a doctor that they had diabetes or high blood sugar and have used diabetes medication at any point during their SHARE observation period.

The position of diabetes cases within the cascade is defined by the following stages. Diagnosis distinguishes individuals aware of their diabetes status from those remaining undiagnosed. We define diagnosed cases as respondents reporting a medical diabetes diagnosis or current/previous diabetes medication use [13]. Treatment captures active pharmacological management among diagnosed individuals, defined as current diabetes medication use. Control assesses glycemic management effectiveness using age-specific HbA1c thresholds aligned with clinical guidelines. Following standardized recommendations for patients with diabetes, we apply HbA1c targets of < 7.0% (< 53 mmol/mol) for individuals younger than 75 years and < 8.0% (< 64 mmol/mol) for those aged 75 and older, reflecting less stringent goals appropriate for patients with limited life expectancy and potential comorbidities [42, 43].

Covariates of diabetes care

The analysis includes multivariate modeling of the diabetes care cascade to account for population heterogeneity, facilitating cross-national comparisons. We distinguish between three sources of individual covariates: (1) sociodemographic, (2) socioeconomic characteristics, and (3) health-related factors. For categorical covariates, missing information is treated as a separate category to retain the maximum number of cases in the analysis. A descriptive overview of these characteristics by diabetes status is presented in Table 2 (using unweighted data), where individuals without diabetes have non-diabetic HbA1c levels and have never received a diagnosis or used diabetes medication. Individuals with diabetes (i.e., the prevalence group) constitute the subsample of interest for this study.

Sociodemographic factors encompass age, gender (male or female), country of birth (native, born in other European country, born in non-European country, or missing), partnership status (cohabiting partner or not a cohabiting partner), and parental status (parent, not a parent, or missing). Previous studies demonstrate that these characteristics are important correlates of diabetes diagnosis and management [2, 4, 13, 17, 44, 45]. Consistent with this literature, Table 2 demonstrates that individuals with diabetes, particularly those with diagnosed diabetes, are significantly more likely to be older, male, foreign-born, and living without a cohabiting partner.

Socioeconomic characteristics are represented by educational level (low, middle, high, or missing, as recoded from the ISCED97 classification), employment status (employed, not employed, or missing, as recoded from current employment situation), and income quintile. The latter comprises five quintiles (each representing 20% of the income distribution, from lowest to highest) and a missing category. The quintiles are derived from household income, adjusted for household size, and calculated for each country separately to account for varying income levels between countries. Table 2 confirms existing research showing that non-employment and low income are positively associated with diabetes diagnosis [15, 44], while lower educational levels are associated with both undiagnosed and diagnosed diabetes [19, 20].

The following health-related indicators are considered: body mass index (underweight/normal if BMI less than 25, overweight if 25–30, obese if 30 or more, or missing), hypertension (self-reported diagnosis or hypertension medication use), number of comorbidities, handgrip strength (maximum of two measurements per hand), self-rated health (less than good or good or excellent), and number of years since diagnosis. As reflected in Table 2, BMI is a well-established risk factor for diabetes [2, 46], and obesity impedes successful disease management [47, 48]. Hypertension and comorbidities are only introduced in the multivariate models for treatment and control because the causal direction between diagnosis and these factors is unclear. The literature indicates that quality of diabetes care is higher for patients with multiple and related chronic conditions [49, 50], suggesting that both the presence and type of comorbidities play a role in treatment and control. As expected, individuals with hypertension and a higher number of other diagnosed conditions are more likely to have diagnosed diabetes (cf. Table 2). Handgrip strength and self-rated health are introduced as objective and subjective health indicators, respectively, that may influence engagement with diabetes care. Handgrip strength reflects physical vitality and functional capacity [51, 52], while self-rated health captures perceived health status, both measures may signal the likelihood of healthcare utilization [53]. Individuals with poorer physical function or perceived health are more likely to have regular healthcare contact, which could facilitate earlier diabetes detection and more intensive management. Evidence supporting this relationship is confirmed in Table 2, which shows that individuals with diagnosed diabetes have significantly lower grip strength and substantially poorer self-rated health [54, 55]. Finally, duration since diabetes diagnosis is considered in relation to treatment and control outcomes as lifestyle changes in diabetes prove difficult to sustain [56], while poor glycemic control and the risk of complications increase with longer disease duration [4, 57–59].

Statistical analysis

Our analytical strategy uses two complementary approaches to study the cascade of diabetes care. First, we apply a fixed denominator approach to construct the cascade, enabling identification of attrition patterns between sequential stages of the care continuum across countries. This methodology maintains the total diabetes subsample (i.e., prevalence) as the consistent denominator for calculating the proportion of individuals reaching each subsequent cascade stage. The numerator comprises individuals with diabetes who have successfully achieved the given cascade stage along with all preceding stages, ensuring that progression through the cascade reflects cumulative care achievement [12]. Specifically, we examine the share of all individuals with diabetes who are (1) diagnosed, (2) treated, and (3) controlled. This approach facilitates cross-national comparisons of overall care gaps and healthcare system performance. We conduct additional analysis examining glycemic control without requiring the intermediate treatment step. This recognizes that some individuals with diabetes may achieve adequate control through lifestyle modifications alone, particularly in early disease stages.

Second, we examine stage-specific attrition by modeling transitions between consecutive cascade stages as dependent variables and analyzing their associations with covariates. Unlike the fixed denominator approach, this method uses a variable denominator strategy where the subsample reaching each previous cascade stage serves as the denominator, while successful progression to the subsequent stage constitutes the numerator. We study three specific outcomes: (1) diagnosed versus undiagnosed among those with diabetes, (2) treated versus not treated among those diagnosed, and (3) controlled versus not controlled among those treated. This approach provides insights into how stage-specific care gaps relate to population heterogeneity and enables identification of factors relating to transitions at particular points in the care continuum across countries.

Both approaches employ multivariate logistic regression models with country fixed effects to model cascade of diabetes care outcomes. We use country fixed effects rather than random effects given the limited number of countries (n = 10). With this small set of clusters, random-effects models tend to underestimate variance components and produce unreliable standard errors for assessing country differences [60]. For the fixed denominator approach, we calculate country-specific rates for diabetes diagnosis, treatment, and control as predicted probabilities from the regression models, adjusting for population heterogeneity to evaluate the extent to which individuals achieve successful diabetes care across countries. For the stage-specific transition analysis, we examine cross-country differences relative to the balanced grand mean using stage-specific regression models. The resulting adjusted country differences are informative for understanding diabetes care performance net of compositional variation, revealing how cross-country disparities change when accounting for population characteristics.

All analyses are conducted using Stata 18.0 with sample weights that correct for both survey non-response and non-participation in blood sampling (i.e., DBS eligibility, consent, and analyzability) [37]. This study was not pre-registered and does not have a published analysis plan or protocol.

Results

Country descriptives

Figure 1 presents the unadjusted prevalence of undiagnosed, diagnosed and total diabetes by country. The population-weighted average total prevalence across the ten countries in individuals aged 50 and older is 27.5%, comprising 18.6% diagnosed and 8.9% undiagnosed cases. This indicates that diabetes is present in more than one in four individuals aged 50 and older, with approximately 32% diabetes cases remaining undiagnosed. Supplementary Figure S2 presents the corresponding prevalence figure under a stricter diagnosis definition, requiring both a self-reported diagnosis or elevated blood sugar and diabetes medication use. Overall diabetes prevalence remains comparable under this definition (25.2%), while the share of undiagnosed cases increases modestly (9.6%), resulting in about 38% of diabetes cases being undiagnosed. Country-level patterns and relative differences are consistent across both definitions, supporting the robustness of our findings.

Diabetes prevalence varies nearly three-fold across countries, ranging from approximately 13% in Denmark and 15% in Switzerland to 37% in both Greece and Spain—corresponding to roughly one in eight versus more than one in three individuals aged 50 and older. The Mediterranean countries (Spain, Greece, and to a lesser extent Italy and Slovenia) show the highest prevalence, while central and northern European countries (Germany, Estonia, Sweden, Belgium, Switzerland, and Denmark) cluster well below the cross-country average. The same Mediterranean–Northern gradient is visible at both the diagnosed and undiagnosed level: Spain and Greece show the highest rates of both diagnosed (around 22–24%) and undiagnosed (around 14–15%) diabetes, while Denmark, Switzerland, and Belgium consistently show the lowest.

Figure 2 presents the cascade of diabetes care across countries, ordered by decreasing proportion achieving glycemic control. Each bar indicates the share of individuals reaching a particular cascade stage along with all preceding stages. The difference in percentages between bars can be interpreted as attrition between the sequential stages of the cascade.

The proportion of diagnosed cases among the entire diabetes population ranges between 60% and 80% across countries, indicating that 20–40% of individuals with diabetes remain undiagnosed. For most countries, this represents the largest attrition point in the care cascade. Estonia stands out as the exception, achieving the highest diagnostic rate with 80.9% of diabetes cases diagnosed, followed by a cluster of countries in the upper 60s to lower 70s (Belgium, Germany, Sweden, Italy), and a lower group around 60% (Denmark, Switzerland, Slovenia, Spain), with Greece showing the lowest (59.9%).

Once diagnosed, the attrition for treatment ranges between 7.6% points in Greece and 25.8% points in Estonia. In most countries, more than half of individuals with diabetes are receiving treatment, while Switzerland, Italy, and Slovenia fall below 50%.

The transition from treatment to glycemic control (HbA1c < 7.0% or < 8.0% for those aged 75 or older respectively) shows the least attrition across the cascade, with losses ranging between 2.4% points in Greece and 13.8% points in Sweden. Greece (49.9%) and Belgium (49.8%) achieve the highest proportions of controlled cases. Estonia and Spain follow (both above 45%), while Germany, Denmark, and Italy also achieve control in more than four out of ten diabetes cases. Switzerland, Sweden, and Slovenia show the lowest effectiveness in achieving diabetes control (around 35%), with considerable attrition throughout the care cascade.

Individuals with diabetes may achieve adequate glycemic control through different pathways, including lifestyle modifications, without requiring pharmacological intervention. To capture this, Supplementary Figure S3 presents an alternative cascade of diabetes care across countries, examining the direct pathway from diagnosis to glycemic control without requiring the intermediate treatment step. This direct transition shows attrition ranging from 3.3% points in Greece to 15.2% points in Sweden. Estonia demonstrates the highest proportion of diabetes cases that are both diagnosed and under control (73.6%), followed by a cluster of countries between approximately 60% and 65% (Belgium, Italy, Germany). The remaining countries fall in the 50s, with Slovenia showing the lowest performance at 50.3%.

Progressive model adjustment reveals substantial attenuation of Estonia’s advantage. In the fully adjusted model, Estonia’s diagnostic rate converges toward the grand mean, with this regression primarily attributable to adjustments for country of birth and health-related covariates. Two key factors contribute to Estonia’s initially elevated diagnostic rate. First, approximately 30% of prevalent diabetes cases in Estonia comprise individuals born outside Europe (Table S2), with a disproportionate representation of individuals born in Russia, Belarus, and Ukraine among the diagnosed diabetes population, suggesting selective in-migration of diagnosed cases. Second, Estonia exhibits a relatively high prevalence of health-related factors associated with increased likelihood of being diagnosed, including obesity, reduced grip strength, and poor self-reported health status (Tables S1-S2). The analysis indicates that this less favorable health profile among the Estonian diabetes population contributes to the country’s elevated diagnosis rate.

For Belgium and Denmark, adjustment for self-rated health (plus grip strength and BMI for Denmark) substantially increases the estimated diagnostic effectiveness. Given that self-rated health is relatively more favorable in these countries compared to others (Table S2), this suggests that the better health profile of individuals with diabetes in Belgium and Denmark suppresses their relative diagnostic rates. The findings indicate that diagnosis is more frequently missed among individuals with better health parameters, thereby reducing the apparent diagnostic performance in these two countries. Sweden and Switzerland show a similar but less pronounced pattern.

Germany and Slovenia show a different pattern, with a relatively high prevalence of overweight and obesity elevating diagnosis (Table S2). Adjusting for BMI leads to decreasing diagnostic performance compared to others. For Italy, Spain, and Greece we find limited influences of the considered factors.

Taken together, adjustment for individual-level covariates reduces the average absolute country deviation from the grand mean by approximately 4%, indicating that compositional differences in population characteristics account for only a small share of the cross-country variation in diagnosis.

The analysis reveals important individual-level findings regarding sociodemographic and socioeconomic differentials at the diagnosis stage. The unadjusted regression results from Table S1 indicate that individuals aged 60 and older, with lower income, and the unemployed are significantly more likely to be diagnosed compared to remaining undiagnosed. However, when unfavorable health factors predicting diagnosis are introduced into the model, these differentials are substantially attenuated to statistical non-significance, suggesting that younger and higher socioeconomic status individuals tend to have more favorable health profiles that make diagnosis less likely. Additionally, women are more likely to remain undiagnosed compared to men.

The unadjusted country differences reveal extensive treatment of diagnosed cases in Greece, followed by above-average treatment rates in Spain and Belgium. While other countries cluster around the grand mean, Italy and Estonia show relatively strong attrition at the treatment stage, indicating that a more limited proportion of diagnosed individuals receive pharmacological treatment. Notably, Greece, Italy, and Estonia show statistically significant deviations from the grand mean.

After adjusting for population characteristics, between 25% and 40% attrition occurs at the diagnosis stage. Belgium, Denmark, and Sweden demonstrate the highest adjusted proportions of diagnosed cases (above 70%). Other countries fall between 60% and 70%, with Greece at the lower end. The treatment stage further reduces the adjusted probabilities, with countries spanning from 45.9% (Estonia and Slovenia) to 59.6% (Belgium). Attrition from treatment to control remains limited across most countries. The adjusted proportions achieving successful control vary considerably, from approximately one in three individuals in Slovenia to more than 50% in Belgium. Spain and Greece exhibit adjusted control rates approaching 50%. Denmark, Estonia, Germany, Italy, Switzerland, and Sweden are grouped around 40%.

Interestingly, countries vary in the extent to which correcting for population composition affects diabetes care performance. Throughout the cascade, Belgium, Spain, Denmark, Switzerland, and Sweden show improvement in care effectiveness, suggesting that diabetes care is relatively hampered by population characteristics. Particularly, the improved diagnostic capability impacts the entire cascade. For Switzerland, additional analysis indicates that hypertension and other comorbidities (Table S2), both positively related to the sequential care cascade stages, are less frequent in individuals with diabetes and therefore partially explain lower performance. For Spain, we find that it is mostly the younger and female composition of the subsample (Table S2) that diminishes care performance.

For Greece, Italy, and Germany, the population characteristics accounted for do not particularly influence care cascade performance. In Estonia and Slovenia, correcting for compositional effects reduces successful diabetes management. For Estonia, the adjusted diagnosis rate declines by more than 11% points. No specific population characteristics explaining decreased care performance could be identified for Slovenia.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive cross-national assessment of diabetes care performance in Europe using a cascade of care framework. By leveraging harmonized biomarker and survey data from SHARE, we identify substantial variation in diabetes care outcomes across ten European countries and highlight the role of population characteristics in shaping these outcomes.

Our findings reveal that diabetes is present in more than 25% of individuals aged 50 and older across the ten observed countries. We observe significantly higher rates of both diagnosed and undiagnosed diabetes in Mediterranean countries (e.g., 37% in Spain and Greece) compared to northern (e.g., 13% in Denmark) and central European countries (e.g., 25% in Germany), consistent with regional variation patterns reported in other studies utilizing SHARE data [13, 61]. On average, about one in three diabetes cases remains undiagnosed, which is comparable to recent European figures from the International Diabetes Federation [1].

Considerable attrition occurs at each stage of the care cascade, with the most pronounced losses occurring at the diagnosis stage. In most countries, more than 30% of individuals with diabetes remain undiagnosed. This result is consistent with recent research on undiagnosed diabetes in Europe using SHARE data [13], though our findings show slightly lower undiagnosed rates in most countries due to our use of longitudinal information on diabetes diagnosis. These European undiagnosed rates exceed the 28% undiagnosed rate reported in the United States [9], though this figure is based on adults aged 18 and older. Attrition is less pronounced at subsequent stages. On average, almost 60% of individuals aged 50 and older with diabetes achieve glycemic control across countries when assessed independently of pharmacological treatment. Research for the United States from the same source suggests that 46% of individuals with diabetes receive a diagnosis and meet glycemic control targets [9]. According to our results, when combining both treatment and glycemic control, 35% to 50% of individuals aged 50 and older achieve comprehensive diabetes management across countries.

Adjusting for population heterogeneity reveals that some countries’ performance is either bolstered or hindered by their population composition, underscoring the importance of contextualizing care outcomes within demographic, social, and health profiles. Belgium consistently performs well across all stages of the cascade, while Spain and Greece, despite lower diagnostic rates, demonstrate strong treatment and control outcomes. In contrast, countries such as Slovenia, Sweden, and Switzerland exhibit significant attrition at later stages, suggesting challenges in treatment adherence or effectiveness. Importantly, country-specific patterns of attrition suggest that interventions must be context-sensitive. For example, Greece may benefit from enhanced screening and better healthcare accessibility, while Slovenia and Sweden may require targeted support for treatment adherence and glycemic control.

The remaining disparities in the care cascade may be influenced by broader structural and organizational differences in care service delivery and health system characteristics across Europe. There is evidence that integrated care pathways involving both primary and secondary (specialist or hospital-led) care are associated with better glycemic control [62], and that strong primary care—characterized by high accessibility and continuity—is associated with fewer diabetes-related hospital admissions and complications [63]. Primary care system strength across Europe has been classified by Kringos et al. [64, 65], and recent patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) provide a complementary assessment of care quality from the user perspective [66]. Countries with stronger primary care infrastructure and chronic care coordination—such as Belgium, Spain, and Denmark—appear to achieve relatively higher rates of glycemic control, the cascade stage at which performance across all preceding steps cumulates, particularly after adjusting for population characteristics. By contrast, Greece achieves a relatively high adjusted glycemic control rate (more than 49%) among individuals with diabetes despite having a relatively weak primary care system and lower patient-experienced care quality, a pattern consistent with other cross-country research [67, 68]. This suggests that other factors—such as dietary patterns, access to pharmacological treatment, or patient engagement—may contribute to diabetes management outcomes in this context [69]. Countries achieving adjusted glycemic control rates around 40% more often have medium primary care system strength (Germany, Italy, Switzerland, and Sweden), with Slovenia being a notable exception [64]. Despite being considered to have a strong primary care system and an average patient-experienced care quality, Slovenia achieves adjusted glycemic control in only one in three individuals aged 50 and older with diabetes, potentially reflecting identified weaknesses in continuity and comprehensiveness of primary care [64]. These patterns warrant systematic investigation in future research on the relationship between primary care system characteristics and diabetes care performance across European countries.

Importantly, primary care strength is itself shaped by other health system determinants not captured in our analysis, such as the inclusion of diabetes services in health benefits packages, purchasing and provider payment arrangements, and the stewardship of the public–private mix. A deeper understanding of the complex relationship between health system determinants, service delivery process, and diabetes outcomes is a promising direction for future research, for instance through the integration of health system performance indicators with individual-level data [70].

An important finding is the role of health factors at the diagnosis stage of diabetes care. As unfavorable health status and obesity are positively associated with receiving a diagnosis, relatively favorable health profiles of individuals with diabetes hamper diagnostic effectiveness in countries like Belgium, Denmark, and Sweden. This indicates that individuals with more severe or symptomatic disease are more likely to attract medical attention and receive diagnosis, while seemingly healthy cases may evade clinical detection due to fewer overt symptoms or reduced healthcare engagement. Our results therefore signal not only the need for strengthening early detection as a crucial component of the diabetes care trajectory, but also highlight that adopting broader detection approaches rather than focusing solely on high-risk presentations can improve diagnostic effectiveness in certain countries. For seemingly healthy individuals, underestimation of their personal diabetes risk may be misleading, underscoring the critical role of healthcare systems in ensuring timely detection [13]. Conversely, in Estonia, and to a limited extent in Germany and Slovenia, we find the opposite pattern: higher diagnosis rates are partly explained by the less favorable health characteristics of individuals with diabetes in these countries.

The analysis reveals pronounced sociodemographic and socioeconomic variation in diabetes prevalence (e.g., for gender, partnership status, education, and income), with individuals in disadvantaged positions being more likely to have diabetes, whether undiagnosed or diagnosed [15, 19, 21, 44]. However, these differentials manifest differently in the progression through the cascade of care. Compared to individuals with undiagnosed diabetes, those with diagnosed diabetes are more often aged 60 and older, unemployed, and have lower levels of income. Our results may explain this pattern: higher socioeconomic status individuals are more likely to have relatively favorable health profiles, making them less likely to present with overt symptoms and thus more prone to evading clinical detection at the diagnostic stage. Since diabetes develops gradually, younger individuals with higher socioeconomic status may also be at an earlier disease stage and remain unaware of their condition prior to the onset of complications. Longitudinal research would be required to further unpack the underlying mechanisms driving these socioeconomic differences in diabetes diagnosis. Together, these findings suggest that while it is crucial to target vulnerable groups with preventive initiatives, a more privileged group with diabetes may go unnoticed, potentially increasing their risk of disease complications due to delayed diagnosis and treatment.

An additional finding of note is that subsequent stages of the diabetes care cascade—being in treatment and achieving glycemic control—appear less strongly associated with individual characteristics compared to the diagnosis stage. This pattern suggests that the group of diagnosed individuals may represent a selective population that is more actively engaged with the healthcare system. Once individuals enter formal care through diagnosis, individual socioeconomic and health factors appear to play a diminished role in determining treatment status and control outcomes. This selectivity effect indicates that healthcare system factors and clinical protocols may become more influential than patient-level characteristics in driving progression through later stages of the care cascade, highlighting the critical importance of improving diagnostic effectiveness to ensure equitable access to the full continuum of diabetes care.

The interpretation of these results should be considered alongside the study’s strengths and limitations. A key strength of this study is the use of harmonized biomarker and survey data across multiple countries, which enables robust cross-national comparisons of diabetes diagnosis, treatment, and control capabilities. This contrasts with most existing research that either focuses on isolated care components or applies cascade of care approaches using non-harmonized data across different healthcare contexts. The cascade of care approach provides a granular view of care performance across the entire care continuum, and the inclusion of multivariate models that comprehensively account for population characteristics helps disentangle the effects of population composition from system-level performance.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the inability to distinguish between diabetes types may introduce some misclassification, although the focus on older adults likely minimizes this concern. Information on age at diagnosis indicates that less than 5% of diabetes cases in SHARE were diagnosed before age 30, suggesting a low prevalence of type 1 diabetes in the sample [13]. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits our ability to assess care trajectories over time. It also constrains the inclusion of current lifestyle behaviors (e.g. smoking, diet, physical activity) as covariates, since these may be influenced by the diabetes diagnosis itself, and long-term differences in such factors may contribute to residual country variation alongside health system factors. Third, while SHARE provides rich data, some relevant variables are not or are measured with limited precision. The self-reported diagnosis item (“a doctor has ever told you that you had diabetes or high blood sugar”) combines a formal diabetes diagnosis and a report of elevated blood sugar that cannot be disentangled, carrying a risk of modest overestimation of diagnosed prevalence due to reporting of prediabetes or transient hyperglycemia. A sensitivity analysis applying a stricter diagnosis definition requiring both a self-report and evidence of medication use yields substantively consistent results, with the share of undiagnosed cases increasing modestly from 32% to 38%, reinforcing the conclusion that diagnosis represents the primary point of attrition in the cascade. Furthermore, diabetes treatment is narrowly defined, as non-pharmacological management such as lifestyle interventions is not adequately considered, and the available medication data provides limited information on treatment adherence. In addition, important covariates such as healthcare access, risk perception, and health literacy are not accurately captured in the dataset. Similarly, more fine-grained data on ethnicity are not available, and the size of the analytical sample prevents us from capturing heterogeneity within the non-European migrant population by region of origin—a particularly relevant consideration for countries with large and diverse migrant communities. Finally, population-level variation in genetic susceptibility to type 2 diabetes and pharmacogenetic response to antidiabetic treatment may also contribute to cross-country differences, although we lack genetic data to evaluate this empirically. Fourth, while our analytical definition of glycemic control applies age-differentiated HbA1c thresholds in line with international guidelines, national clinical guidelines may vary in how they operationalize treatment targets for older adults, which could introduce some residual noise in cross-country comparisons of control rates. Fifth, the analytical sample may be somewhat healthier than the underlying 50 + population. Proxy interviews and respondents with medical contraindications for blood sampling were not eligible for dried blood spot collection, which removes some of the most frail individuals from the sample. At the same time, voluntary blood sampling and survey participation in general may additionally overrepresent more health-conscious respondents [71]. While the weighting procedures partially correct for differential non-participation, residual selection cannot be fully ruled out. In general, our findings apply to community-dwelling adults aged 50 and older in ten European countries and should not be extrapolated without caution to younger adults, institutionalized populations, or non-European settings.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable evidence for understanding diabetes care performance across European healthcare systems. Our results demonstrate that care performance varies markedly across ten European countries, with the diagnosis stage consistently representing the greatest point of attrition in the care cascade. Population characteristics significantly relate to care outcomes, particularly at the diagnostic stage, where health status and socioeconomic factors play determining roles in the likelihood of diagnosis. However, after accounting for population composition, meaningful differences in healthcare system performance persist, suggesting that structural and organizational factors contribute to cross-country variation in diabetes care effectiveness. These findings underscore that strengthening early detection mechanisms and tailoring interventions to address both country-specific care gaps and diverse population profiles are essential strategies for improving diabetes outcomes and reducing health inequalities. Future studies should explore longitudinal dynamics of the diabetes care cascade, investigate the role of healthcare system characteristics in shaping outcomes, and assess the impact of recent policy reforms. Additionally, qualitative research could illuminate patient and provider perspectives on barriers to care progression [72], particularly in countries with high attrition rates in diabetes care.
