The role of mTORC2/RICTOR in immune cells and inflammatory diseases
Authors: Jingting Xu, Wenxin Zhong, Fei Xin, Fei Xu, Zehang Zheng, Fengjing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundmTORC2, defined by its core component RICTOR, is a key regulator of immune cell function and inflammation, yet its roles have long been underappreciated due to lack of specific inhibitors and in vivo tools.ObjectiveThis review summarizes the current understanding of mTORC2-specific signaling (via AKT, SGK1, and PKC) in regulating the development, differentiation, and effector functions of diverse immune cells, and discusses its emerging roles in immune cell inflammation and tissue-specific inflammation.MethodWe synthesized findings from recent studies using conditional knockout mouse models, pharmacological inhibitors, and clinical samples.ConclusionmTORC2 controls Th1/Th2 differentiation, Treg stability, and memory T cell formation; regulates BCR signaling and plasma cell survival; and modulates macrophage M2 polarization, DC maturation, and NK cell function. In tissue inflammation, mTORC2 plays context-dependent roles—exacerbating osteoarthritis and pancreatitis but restraining pro-inflammatory polarization in colorectal cancer. Dual mTORC1/2 inhibitors (e.g., AZD2014) have shown early promise, though challenges remain regarding efficacy, toxicity, and immunomodulatory complexity.

Introduction

The immune system plays a central role in defending against pathogens and maintaining tissue homeostasis [1]. A normal immune response can clear infections and promote tissue repair, but once dysregulated, it may lead to excessive or persistent inflammation, which in turn contributes to various diseases, such as autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, and even cancer [2]. Therefore, understanding how immune cells are regulated and how inflammatory responses are kept within appropriate limits has long been a focal point of immunological research. Among these regulatory mechanisms, the mTOR signaling pathway has gradually emerged as a key node of considerable interest in recent years [3].

mTOR is an evolutionarily highly conserved kinase that forms two distinct complexes in cells, differing in both structure and function: mTORC1 and mTORC2. mTORC1 is sensitive to rapamycin and primarily regulates protein synthesis and autophagy [4]. In contrast, mTORC2 is generally not directly inhibited by rapamycin; rather than responding to a single extracellular cue, mTORC2 integrates growth factor signaling, phosphoinositide metabolism, mechanical forces and metabolic status to coordinate downstream signaling.It influences cell survival, metabolism, cytoskeletal dynamics, and polarity establishment by phosphorylating members of the AGC kinase family, including Akt (at Ser473), PKC, and SGK [5]. Although both complexes share the catalytic kinase mTOR, they differ substantially in upstream regulation, substrate specificity, metabolic functions and immunological outcomes. A large body of past work has focused on mTORC1, and its role in immune cells, in particular, has been relatively well characterized [6]. In comparison, the role of mTORC2 in the immune system has long received insufficient attention, mainly due to the lack of specific inhibitors and suitable in vivo research tools.

However, over the past decade, with the widespread application of conditional knockout mice (such as Rictor-floxed mice), the unique functions of mTORC2 in immune cells have gradually come to light [7]. In light of these advances, this review aims to summarize the current understanding of mTORC2-specific signaling—primarily through AKT, SGK1, and PKC—in regulating the development, differentiation, and effector functions of various immune cell subsets, including T cells, B cells, macrophages, and innate lymphoid cells. Furthermore, we discuss the emerging roles of mTORC2 in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases such as Immune Cell Inflammation, Tissue-Specific Inflammation. Unraveling the cell-type-specific and context-dependent functions of mTORC2 may not only provide critical insights into immune regulation but also open new avenues for therapeutic targeting of this kinase complex in inflammatory and immunological disorders.

mTORC2: structure and function

MTOR, serving as a central controller of cellular growth and metabolic processes, is a serine/threonine kinase. It is increasingly recognized that the mTOR pathway plays a fundamental role in sensing environmental conditions and orchestrating a wide spectrum of metabolic activities at both the cellular and organismal levels [8]. It is part of two structurally and functionally distinct complexes, mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2), which differ in composition, regulation, and biological functions. mTORC1 and mTORC2 have shared and unique components. Both contain mTOR, DEP domain containing mTOR interacting protein (DEPTOR) and mammalian lethal with SEC13 protein 8 (mLST8). Regulatory-associated protein of mTOR (RAPTOR) and proline-rich AKT substrate of 40 kDa (PRAS40) are unique to mTORC1, whereas rapamycin-insensitive companion of mTOR (RICTOR), stress-activated map kinase-interacting protein 1 (mSIN1) and protein observed with RICTOR (PROTOR) are specific to mTORC2 [9]. Structural studies indicate that the unique arrangement of RICTOR and mSIN1 stabilizes mTORC2 assembly and regulates substrate accessibility, whereas SIN1-mediated phosphoinositide sensing contributes to spatial control of mTORC2 localization and kinase activation [10, 11]. Advances in cryo-electron microscopy have greatly improved our understanding of the architecture, assembly and substrate recognition of mTOR complexes, providing an important structural basis for deciphering mTORC2-specific signaling mechanisms [10, 12]. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies have revealed the structural organization of mTORC2 [10, 13]. Importantly, these studies indicate that mTOR complexes are highly dynamic rather than static assemblies. Conformational rearrangements during complex assembly and activation regulate substrate engagement and kinase accessibility, suggesting that structural flexibility is an integral determinant of mTOR signaling specificity [12, 13].

The mTORC2 complex consists of four core proteins and two non-core components. The core components include mTOR kinase, rapamycin-insensitive companion of mTOR (RICTOR), mammalian stress-activated protein kinase-interacting protein 1 (mSin1) and mammalian lethal with SEC13 protein 8 (mLST8/GβL). The non-core components comprise protein observed with RICTOR (Protor1/2) [14, 15], and DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein (DEPTOR). Among the non-core components, DEPTOR anchors via its PDZ domain and exerts inhibitory functions through its DEP domain [16]. mSin1 acts not only as a scaffold but also negatively regulates mTORC2 via its PH domain and facilitates plasma membrane localization, thereby enhancing PIP3-mediated activation [11]. Similar to mTORC1, membrane localization contributes to spatial regulation of mTORC2 activation. mSIN1 PH domain senses phosphoinositides and facilitates localization of mTORC2 to the plasma membrane, thereby promoting phosphorylation of Akt [11, 17]. Protor1/2 stabilizes the binding between RICTOR and mTOR and is involved in regulating mTORC2-dependent phosphorylation of serum- and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1) [14, 18].

Among the core components, RICTOR plays a pivotal role in complex assembly, intracellular localization, and stability [19], while mLST8 assists in maintaining the stability of the mTOR kinase activation loop and preserves RICTOR-mTOR interaction and complex dynamic equilibrium [20]. Beyond maintaining structural integrity, structural studies suggest that the unique spatial arrangement of RICTOR contributes to substrate accessibility and contributes to the selective phosphorylation and activation of downstream AGC kinases, thereby distinguishing mTORC2 signaling from that of mTORC1 [10, 11, 13].The mTORC2 complex serves as an upstream kinase for the AGC kinase family (e.g., Akt, SGK, and PKC), regulating cell survival, migration, and cytoskeletal organization by phosphorylating their hydrophobic motif (HM) and turn motif (TM) sites [21]. Further studies indicate that Akt activates mTORC2 through a positive feedback mechanism by phosphorylating SIN1 at threonine 86 (T86) [22]. SIN1 plays a critical role in recruiting Akt and SGK to the complex [15], while Protor binds to mTORC2 via RICTOR, and its stability depends on the expression of other components [20]. Notably, activating mutations in the FAT (FRAP-ATM-TRRAP) domain of mTOR can upregulate both mTORC1 and mTORC2 activity, promoting tumorigenesis [23].

As a unique core subunit of mTORC2 [24], loss of RICTOR disrupts complex stability and integrity, thereby impairing mTORC2 signaling [20]. The RICTOR protein, composed of 1,708 amino acids, contains approximately 37 serine/threonine phosphorylation sites predominantly located in its C-terminal region. Among these, Thr1135 has been identified as a growth factor-stimulated and rapamycin-sensitive site, directly phosphorylated by ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1, a downstream effector of mTORC1 [25]. Studies demonstrate that the mTORC2–RICTOR complex mediates phosphorylation of Akt at Ser473 [26], and RICTOR-deficient mouse embryonic fibroblasts completely lack phosphorylation at this site, confirming mTORC2 as the primary kinase for Akt Ser473 [27]. Additionally, RICTOR is involved in maintaining neuronal function and morphology in the murine brain [28, 29]. In cancer, RICTOR copy number gain and protein overexpression are associated with enhanced mTORC2 activity, which has been repeatedly shown to promote cancer cell survival and migration [30].

From a structural perspective, RICTOR interacts extensively with mTOR through multiple α-helical clusters [31]. It comprises an N-terminal Armadillo domain (AD), a middle HEAT-like domain (HD), and a C-terminal domain (CD). The AD consists of nine triple-helical repeats that separate the HD and CD; the CD contains four helical bundles adjacent to a phosphorylation-rich region [25]. The AD binds to mTOR, while the CD occludes the FRB domain of mTOR, enabling RICTOR and SIN1 to prevent the binding of the rapamycin/FKBP12 complex and mTORC1 substrates such as 4EBP1 and S6K [32]. Similar to raptor, RICTOR interacts with mTOR via the HEAT repeat region. Predictive analyses suggest that RICTOR may contain a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain that mediates phosphoinositide binding and membrane localization [33]. Additionally, sequence homology with mammalian ribosomal 39S protein L17 implies potential ribosome interaction [33], though these predicted functions require experimental validation. Notably, the HD of RICTOR contains an ATP-binding site (A-site), although its allosteric regulatory role remains unclear [31].

Beyond its mTORC2-related functions, RICTOR can interact independently with other proteins such as cullin-1, Myo1c, integrin-linked kinase (ILK), and FBXW7 [20, 34], suggesting involvement in mTOR-independent biological processes including protein ubiquitination and degradation [35–37], epithelial-mesenchymal transition [38], cell migration [39], and cortical actin remodeling [40].

Dysregulation of mTORC2 has been implicated in aging-related diseases such as cancer, diabetes, and neurodegenerative disorders [41, 42]. Importantly, mTORC2 regulates autophagy through a mechanism distinct from mTORC1—by suppressing chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) [43]—thereby expanding its role in cellular metabolism and homeostasis maintenance.

mTORC2 functions in different immune cells

T cells

In T cells, mTORC2 is primarily responsible for phosphorylating several key downstream molecules, such as Akt (at serine 473), PKCθ, and SGK1(at serine 422), thereby influencing cell polarity, survival, differentiation direction, and long-term functional maintenance [4, 44]. Regarding the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Th1, Th2, and Th17 subsets, mTORC2 plays a selective and irreplaceable role [45, 46]. Studies have found that it primarily promotes Th1 and Th2 differentiation through the Akt-Foxo1 signaling axis: upon phosphorylation and activation, Akt retains the Foxo1 transcription factor in the cytoplasm, preventing its nuclear entry, thereby relieving Foxo1-mediated repression of T-bet (the master transcription factor for Th1) and GATA-3 (the master transcription factor for Th2) [47]. Conversely, specific deletion of Rictor (a core component of mTORC2) in T cells reduces Akt activity, allowing Foxo1 to enter the nucleus and selectively suppress Th1- and Th2-related gene expression [45, 47]. Interestingly, Th17 differentiation remains largely unaffected or is even slightly enhanced, as Th17 cells primarily rely on the mTORC1-Stat3-RORγt axis. Additionally, mTORC2-activated SGK1 blocks E3 ligase Nedd4-2 to stabilize JunB by phosphorylation at Ser342 and Ser448 [48, 49]. Accumulated JunB specifically binds the IL-4 promoter’s AP-1 site and synergizes with c-Maf to robustly induce IL-4 and other Th2 cytokines, driving Th2 differentiation [50]. Meanwhile, SGK1 partially suppresses Th1 polarization by inhibiting GSK-3β to sustain β-catenin, which upregulates the long TCF-1 isoform to repress Th1 programs [48]. Finally, mTORC2 can regulate proximal TCR signaling and immunological synapse formation via PKCθ, indirectly influencing helper T cell differentiation [51].

Turning to regulatory T cells (Tregs), within Tregs, mTORC2 activity must be maintained at an appropriate level, which is crucial for sustaining Foxp3 expression and suppressive function. Specifically, the mTORC2-Akt signaling axis phosphorylates Foxo1 and Foxo3a, sequestering them in the cytoplasm and preventing their nuclear translocation and subsequent binding to the Foxp3 promoter and conserved noncoding sequence (CNS) elements, thereby abrogating Foxp3 induction [52]. Simultaneously, mTORC2 also influences the recycling and stability of CTLA-4 and CD25 on the Treg surface via PKCθ, further enhancing their contact-dependent suppressive capacity [53]. Moreover, Tregs primarily rely on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) for energy production, and maintaining low-level mTORC2 activity prevents excessive mTORC1 activation that would destabilize Tregs, while providing sufficient Akt-dependent survival signaling to ensure Treg longevity [54].

This concept of metabolic regulation is even more evident in the formation and metabolic reprogramming of memory T cells. The formation of memory T cells (including both CD4+ and CD8 +) is highly dependent on mTORC2 [55, 56]. The core mechanism lies in the precise regulation of Foxo1 inhibition by mTORC2-Akt signaling—when mTORC2 activity is within an optimal range, only a fraction of Foxo1 enters the nucleus, allowing moderate expression of memory-associated genes such as KLF2, Tcf7, and S1pr1 [57–59]. This maintains central memory T cells (Tcm) with high CD62L expression and homing capacity to lymph nodes, while promoting their long-term survival [59].

Meanwhile, mTORC1 exerts its biological functions primarily via phosphorylating ribosomal S6 kinase 1 (S6K1) and eIF4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1), orchestrating cap-dependent translation, cell cycle progression, metabolic reprogramming and effector lineage specification [60, 61].

mTORC1 drives CD4+ Th subset differentiation through strictly mTORC1-specific cascades, with effects distinct from and partially reciprocal to mTORC2 signaling [61]. RHEB-activated mTORC1 represses SOCS3 to amplify IL-12/STAT4 signaling and T-bet induction, promoting Th1 polarization. For Th17 specification, mTORC1 upregulates HIF1α to stabilize lineage-defining RORγt and couple glycolytic reprogramming to Th17 differentiation, a process unaffected by mTORC2 ablation [61, 62]. Conversely, mTORC1 negatively regulates Th2 differentiation, as RHEB deletion enhances STAT6/GATA3 activity to drive Th2 commitment [61].

Additionally, mTORC1 acts as a central metabolic checkpoint and dictates CD8+ T cell effector-memory bifurcation: TCR-activated mTORC1 upregulates MYC/HIF1α to drive glycolytic reprogramming for effector expansion, while its inhibition induces EOMES and fatty acid oxidation to promote memory formation[63–65]. TSC1 loss-induced mTORC1 hyperactivation disrupts naive T cell quiescence [66].In summary, the influence of mTORC2 on T cells can be distilled to one key point: it uniformly regulates Th1/Th2 differentiation, Treg stability, memory T cell formation, and CD8+ T cell functional balance through the Akt-Foxo1 signaling axis, while also modulating immunological synapses and metabolic reprogramming via PKCθ and SGK1, respectively. In comparison, mTORC1 acts as a central regulator of T cell anabolic metabolism and acute effector functions. It exerts biological effects primarily through phosphorylating downstream effectors S6K1 and 4E-BP1 to control cap-dependent translation and cell cycle progression; it selectively drives Th1 and Th17 lineage commitment while suppressing Th2 polarization, and promotes short-lived effector CD8+ T cell generation by enforcing glycolytic reprogramming. TSC1-mediated restraint of mTORC1 activity is also critical for maintaining naive T cell quiescence.Unlike mTORC1, which tends to drive effector cell expansion and focus on "short-term combat capability," mTORC2 is more concerned with cell polarity, long-term survival, and the establishment of memory programs. It can thus be regarded as a key molecular switch guiding T cells from an acute effector state toward immune memory and tolerance.

B cells

The role of mTORC2 in B cells is just as broad and critical as it is in T cells, but with a notable difference in focus. Instead of primarily directing differentiation choices, mTORC2 in B cells tends to control BCR signaling, B cell activation, and the long-term maintenance of terminally differentiated cells [67]. At the heart of BCR signaling and Akt activation, mTORC2 acts as a central hub. When the BCR recognizes an antigen, PI3K gets recruited to the membrane and generates PIP3, which then activates both PDK1 and mTORC2; mTORC2 goes on to fully activate Akt by phosphorylating it at serine 473 (Ser473) [68] and facilitates the folding and maturation of nascent Akt polypeptides via phosphorylating the Akt turn motif, thereby maintaining Akt protein stability [69]. This phosphorylation step does more than just maximize Akt's kinase activity—it also influences which downstream substrates Akt will target. Once fully activated, Akt can phosphorylate a range of effector molecules, including Foxo family transcription factors (Foxo1, Foxo3a, Foxo4), along with GSK3β and PRAS40 [70]. Akt can induces phosphorylation-dependent inactivation of GSK3α/β, which attenuates GSK3-mediated phosphorylation of c-Myc at Thr58/Ser62 residues and the subsequent proteasomal degradation of c-Myc. This post-transcriptionally stabilizes c-Myc protein and promotes metabolic reprogramming of B cells involving both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation [69].

mTORC1 acts as a core hub governing the global B cell transcriptome with bidirectional biphasic effects. It positively regulates cell cycle, metabolism and DNA repair genes to drive proliferation, while indirectly suppressing GSK3α/β kinase activity to repress immune response genes. Functionally antagonistic at the transcriptional level, mTORC1 and GSK3 oppositely regulate 70% of their shared target genes, forming a homeostatic regulatory circuit [71].

The influence of mTORC2 extends across the entire journey of B cell development, activation, and antibody production. From a developmental standpoint, the survival of immature B cells in the bone marrow and their transition into mature B cells depend on the mTORC2-Akt-Foxo signaling axis [72]. Early in B cell development, Foxo1 promotes RAG gene expression and light chain recombination, but once mTORC2 activates Akt, Akt phosphorylates Foxo1 and sends it out of the nucleus, shutting down the recombination process at the right time and preventing the generation of self-reactive B cells [73].

When it comes to antibody production, mTORC2 plays a decisive part in keeping plasma cells and memory B cells alive—an area where B cells clearly differ from T cells. As B cells differentiate into plasma cells, they undergo a dramatic metabolic shift, moving from quiescent oxidative phosphorylation to a high-glycolysis mode during the effector phase to support the massive synthesis and secretion of antibodies [74]. Much like memory formation in T cells depends on mTORC2, the long-term survival of plasma cells also relies heavily on mTORC2-Akt signaling. The underlying mechanisms work like this: mTORC2 activates Akt by phosphorylating it at Ser473, and Akt then inhibits the pro-apoptotic proteins Bad and Bim while turning on anti-apoptotic proteins Mcl-1 and Bcl-xL, which helps plasma cells stick around in bone marrow survival niches for a long time [75, 76].

In a few words,mTORC2 serves as a core regulator that guides B cells from the very first step of antigen recognition all the way through terminal differentiation to the establishment of long-lived immune memory.

In macrophages, mTORC2 is mainly involved in controlling polarization, phagocytosis, and inflammasome activation. Macrophages can polarize toward either the classical (M1) or alternative (M2) pathway, and which direction they take largely determines their role during inflammation and tissue repair [77]. mTORC2 appears to strike a balance here. IL-4 signaling initiates PI3K activation upon engagement of its receptor on macrophages, thereby promoting subsequent activation of mTORC2 [78, 79]. Activated mTORC2 subsequently induces full Akt activation through phosphorylation of its hydrophobic motif at Ser473 [80]. Enhanced Akt activity promotes both the expression of glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) and its translocation to the plasma membrane, thereby increasing glucose uptake and intracellular glucose flux in macrophages. The augmented glucose metabolic activity further supports mitochondrial metabolism, including fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), which are critical metabolic programs required for M2 macrophage polarization [81, 82]. Significantly, the functional significance of this mTORC2-centered metabolic pathway has been demonstrated by genetic studies showing that Rictor deficiency abolishes IL-4-induced FAO, OXPHOS, and M2 polarization. Conversely, enforced Glut1 expression restores glucose metabolic flux and partially rescues the defective M2 differentiation observed in Rictor-deficient macrophages [82]. Collectively, these findings establish the IL-4–PI3K–mTORC2–Akt–Glut1 metabolic axis as a critical regulatory pathway linking nutrient metabolism with macrophage phenotype determination.

Meanwhile, studies indicate that the mTORC2-Akt axis tends to favor M2 polarization—by phosphorylating Akt at Ser473 and turning on downstream signals, it boosts M2 markers like Arg-1 and CD206 while keeping M1-associated pro-inflammatory cytokines in check. When mTORC2 activity goes down, macrophages drift toward M1 instead, pumping out more TNF-α, IL-6, and iNOS [83]. mTORC2 also participates in inflammasome regulation. Evidence shows it can influence NLRP3 inflammasome activation by tweaking mitochondrial ROS production and potassium efflux, which in turn controls the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 [84]. Upon recognition of Toll-like receptor (TLR) ligands, mTORC1 dominates translational reprogramming of cytokines and immune effectors in macrophages [35, 36], mediates HIF1α-dependent aerobic glycolysis to drive proinflammatory polarization [58, 85], and controls NLRP3 inflammasome activation via HK1-induced glycolytic metabolism [82].

Turning to dendritic cells, mTORC2 mainly affects their maturation, migration, and antigen presentation capacity [56]. When dendritic cells mature, they upregulate co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80 and CD86 as well as MHC molecules, and mTORC2-Akt signaling helps drive this process [86]. Antigen presentation by dendritic cells also depends on mTORC2. Beyond just affecting MHC levels, mTORC2 influences phagosome maturation and the efficiency of cross-presentation, which in turn shapes the quality of naïve T cell activation [87]. In dendritic cells upon Toll-like receptor (TLR) engagement, mTORC2 mediates full activation of Akt to govern early glycolytic initiation and de novo fatty acid synthesis, which supports endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus expansion to accommodate cytokine production and secretion. By contrast, mTORC1 drives late-phase glycolytic reprogramming, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression and cell survival maintenance via its downstream effector hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) [88]. Collectively, the two mTOR complexes function synergistically to sustain DC metabolic adaptation, constrain excessive proinflammatory responses, and modulate DC maturation and antigen-presenting capacity [17, 89].

In NK cells, mTORC2 primarily shapes effector functions and metabolic adaptation. NK cells kill target cells directly through the perforin/granzyme pathway and also regulate immunity by releasing cytokines such as IFN-γ [46]. Studies have shown that mTORC2 supports NK cell effector differentiation by E4BP4 and T-bet [90]. Beyond that, mTORC2 plays a critical role in NK cell metabolism. Resting NK cells rely mainly on oxidative phosphorylation for energy, but once activated, they need to switch quickly to glycolysis to support their effector functions [91]. Furthermore, mTORC2 helps regulate the long-term survival of NK cells and the formation of memory-like NK cells, following the same basic principle seen in T cells and B cells for memory formation.

Putting it all together, although mTORC2 has been more extensively studied in adaptive immune cells, a growing body of evidence indicates that it also plays indispensable regulatory roles in innate immune cells. From macrophage polarization and inflammasome activation, to dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation, and to NK cell effector functions and metabolic reprogramming, mTORC2 finely orchestrates the activation, differentiation, and functional execution of these cells through its core downstream signaling axis. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the regulatory network of innate immunity but also offer potential new targets for intervening in diseases such as inflammation, infection, and cancer. However, compared with its roles in adaptive immunity, the precise molecular mechanisms and spatiotemporal specificity of mTORC2 in innate immune cells remain to be further elucidated, which represents an important direction for future research in this field.

The core role of mTORC2/RICTOR in immune cell inflammation

The mTORC2/RICTOR signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating the functions of various immune cells, with its effects exhibiting significant cell-type and context dependency. In macrophages, this pathway contributes to immune homeostasis by modulating polarization states and inflammatory responses. mTORC2 signaling is necessary for the efficient migration of monocytes to inflammatory sites and their full maturation into macrophages [92]. mTORC2 coordinately induces M2 polarization and restricts TLR4-triggered IL-12 secretion through promoting IRF4-mediated metabolic reprogramming and Akt-dependent FOXO1 inhibition [93]. The TLR4–mTORC2–Akt axis serves as a critical signaling pathway that upregulates scavenger receptor expression and promotes lipid accumulation upon LPS stimulation, thereby driving foam cell formation. Inhibition of mTORC2 effectively blocks this process, which cannot be rescued even by exogenous Akt activators, underscoring its indispensable role [94]. Within the tumor microenvironment, mTORC2 activation facilitates the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing tumor growth and metastasis [93]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), mTORC2 is predominantly localized in macrophages within the tumor-adjacent stroma rather than in tumor cells. Its deficiency reprograms macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype and exacerbates intestinal inflammation and tumorigenesis via secretion of SPP1 [95]. Clinical evidence further indicates that low mTORC2 activity, marked by reduced RICTOR expression in macrophages, is significantly associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients [95].

In dendritic cells (DCs), mTORC2/RICTOR acts as a negative regulatory hub to prevent excessive maturation and pro-inflammatory polarization. In a renal ischemia–reperfusion injury model, loss of RICTOR led to upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86) and enhanced migratory capacity in DCs, aggravating local inflammation and tissue damage [96]. Within the central nervous system, mTORC2 negatively regulates inflammation by inhibiting nuclear translocation of FoxO1 in DCs and promotes Th2 differentiation of T cells. mTORC2 deficiency in DCs enhances Th1/Th17 responses, influencing anti-tumor and anti-infection immunity [97].

In invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells, mTORC2 is essential for their development, subset differentiation, and effector functions. Its loss results in early developmental arrest, specifically impedes the formation of the NKT-17 subset (RORγt⁺, IL-17A⁺), and markedly impairs cytotoxic function [98]. Similarly, in natural killer (NK) cells, mTORC2/RICTOR is a key regulator of early development, promoting functional maturation through maintaining T-bet expression and modulating surface receptors (e.g., suppressing CD117 and promoting Ly49 and KLRG1). However, it is dispensable for terminal differentiation, acting in concert with mTORC1 [90].

These findings collectively demonstrate that mTORC2/RICTOR plays a pivotal role in inflammation and immune responses by differentially regulating the development, polarization, migration, and effector functions of various immune cells. Dysregulation of this pathway is implicated in multiple diseases. For instance, in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), sustained activation of mTORC2 disrupts immune complex degradation and establishes a pro-inflammatory positive feedback loop [93], highlighting its potential as an important target for immunotherapeutic interventions.

Mechanisms of mTORC2/RICTOR in the regulation of tissue-specific inflammation

The mTORC2/RICTOR signaling pathway mediates inflammatory responses in a highly context-specific and cell type-dependent manner across different tissues. In osteoarthritis (OA), activation of mTORC2/RICTOR serves as a central link connecting inflammatory stimuli to cartilage destruction. Key OA-related pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β significantly upregulate RICTOR expression and enhance mTORC2 activity, which in turn activates the IκB-α/NF-κB p65 signaling axis [99–101]. This drives the expression of downstream pro-inflammatory factors and catabolic enzymes, exacerbating joint inflammation and matrix degradation [100]. Studies demonstrate that specific inhibition of mTORC2 effectively blocks this NF-κB activation and alleviates inflammatory responses, underscoring its critical role in OA pathogenesis [102].

In hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis (HP), mTORC2 activity is markedly upregulated and exacerbates pancreatic injury through multiple mechanisms: enhancing inflammatory pathway activation; promoting the release of cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α; disrupting lipid homeostasis by inhibiting fatty acid oxidation and promoting lipogenesis, leading to lipotoxicity; and inducing various forms of cell death including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis in acinar cells, thereby amplifying tissue damage. Knockdown of Rictor has been shown to alleviate pancreatic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation via upregulation of PPARα [103].

In adipose tissue, mTORC2/RICTOR acts as a key mediator through which FGF1 ameliorates insulin resistance: FGF1 enhances RICTOR expression and mTORC2 activity in adipocytes, thereby suppressing CCL2 transcription, reducing M1 macrophage infiltration, attenuating inflammation, and improving insulin sensitivity. Adipocyte-specific RICTOR knockout abolishes the metabolic benefits of FGF1 [85].

Within the nervous system, mTORC2/RICTOR modulates the structure and function of nociceptive neurons and contributes to pain signaling: tissue inflammation activates mTORC2 in nociceptive neurons; pharmacological activation of mTORC2 induces structural remodeling—elongation and branching—of peripheral nociceptive endings, leading to persistent pain hypersensitivity. Computational modeling suggests that mTORC2-mediated structural changes suffice to increase neuronal excitability. Conditional knockout of RICTOR in nociceptors prevents inflammation-induced nerve ending remodeling and alleviates inflammatory pain, while a single injection of RICTOR-targeting antisense oligonucleotides produces long-lasting analgesia [104, 105].

In gastric tissue affected by chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG), mTORC2 serves as a critical node within the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, regulating gastric mucosal cell metabolism, proliferation, and apoptosis. It is closely associated with inflammatory progression and carcinogenic transformation. Notably, while its mRNA expression is elevated, protein levels remain unchanged, suggesting the involvement of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms [106].

In the liver, however, the role of mTORC2/RICTOR displays distinct characteristics: although its activity decreases during starvation and cannot be rescued by inflammatory stimuli, it does not contribute to starvation-induced hepatoprotection, indicating a lower functional priority compared to mTORC1 [107].

Together, these findings highlight that the function of mTORC2/RICTOR in tissue inflammation is highly dependent on cell type, microenvironment, and pathological context. Its mechanisms of action are complex and finely regulated, providing a critical foundation for the development of tissue-specific therapeutic strategies.

mTORC2 as a therapeutic target

Therapeutic strategies targeting mTORC2 (mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 2) have gained significant attention in oncology and immune-related diseases due to its central role in cell proliferation, metabolism, and immune regulation. As a core component of mTORC2, RICTOR facilitates downstream signaling through phosphorylation of AKT (Ser473) and SGK, thereby modulating cell survival and metabolic programs. Dysregulated mTORC2 activation is closely associated with cancer progression driven by PI3K-AKT pathway hyperactivation. Unlike first-generation rapalogs that predominantly inhibit mTORC1, second-generation ATP-competitive mTOR kinase inhibitors (e.g., Torin1, PP242, AZD2014) bind the catalytic site of mTOR, simultaneously blocking both mTORC1 and mTORC2 activity. These agents not only suppress global protein synthesis more effectively—achieving nearly 50% inhibition in MDA-MB-361 breast cancer cells—but also circumvent the PI3K-AKT feedback activation commonly induced by rapalogs, offering a promising approach to overcome rapamycin resistance [35, 108].

Preclinical studies further highlight the “oncogene-addicted” nature of mTORC2 in certain cancers. For example, conditional deletion of RICTOR in prostate epithelium shows minimal toxicity in normal tissues but effectively suppresses PTEN loss-driven prostate tumorigenesis. Moreover, RICTOR knockdown selectively impairs viability in cancer cells with hyperactive AKT [108]. Beyond cell-autonomous effects, mTORC2-RICTOR signaling shapes the tumor microenvironment by promoting M2-like macrophage polarization via the PI3K-AKT-IRF4 axis, enhancing glycolytic and fatty acid oxidative metabolism. RICTOR deficiency disrupts the immunosuppressive function of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), restraining tumor growth and moderating aberrant immune responses in parasitic infections, thereby revealing new avenues for cancer immunotherapy and infectious disease modulation [82].

However, the immunoregulatory function of mTORC2 exhibits context-dependent complexity. Karl Katholnig et al. demonstrated that in colorectal cancer (CRC), mTORC2 restrains pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages and maintains their anti-inflammatory phenotype. Its inactivation triggers macrophage reprogramming toward a pro-inflammatory state, characterized by secretion of factors such as SPP1, which exacerbates inflammation and tumor progression. Notably, low RICTOR expression in TAMs correlates with poor prognosis in CRC patients, underscoring the cell type- and disease context-specific impact of mTORC2 activity in macrophage-mediated tumorigenesis [95].

In clinical translation, early-phase trials of dual mTORC1/2 inhibitors such as AZD2014 have shown partial responses in patients with PDGFR-mutant pancreatic cancer and ERBB2-mutant breast cancer, accompanied by significant reduction in AKT Ser473 phosphorylation in tumor biopsies, confirming the feasibility of mTORC2-targeted therapy [109]. Nevertheless, challenges remain: first, inter-trial variability in efficacy may stem from insufficient mTORC1 inhibition potency or divergent pathway dependencies across cancer types [110]; second, balancing specificity and toxicity is difficult—broad mTOR inhibitors like AZD2014 may disrupt physiological mTORC1 functions in normal tissues, leading to fatigue, mucositis, and other adverse events, while truly selective mTORC2 inhibitors (e.g., those disrupting RICTOR–mTOR interaction) remain preclinical and require further validation [108]; third, the dual immunomodulatory roles of mTORC2 in macrophage activation necessitate careful evaluation of potential effects on both antitumor immunity and host defense against infections, especially given its variable functions in innate immune cells across cancer types [82, 95].

In summary, targeting mTORC2 holds clear promise in molecularly selected cancers and immune microenvironment reprogramming. Overcoming current limitations will require refined patient stratification, development of mTORC2-selective inhibitors, and rational combination strategies—such as with immune checkpoint inhibitors—to maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing adverse outcomes.

Summary and future perspectives

In summary, mTORC2 serves as a critical signaling hub whose function is highly dependent on cell type and microenvironment, exerting broad and finely tuned regulatory effects within the immune system. Unlike mTORC1, which primarily drives acute effector responses, mTORC2 is more focused on regulating long-term immune cell functions, including cell survival, polarity establishment, immunological synapse formation, and the maintenance of immune memory. Through its core subunit RICTOR, mTORC2 acts mainly by phosphorylating members of the AGC kinase family—such as Akt, SGK1, and PKC—thereby modulating key transcription factors including Foxo1 and T-bet, and achieving precise control over differentiation, metabolism, and functional status across different immune cell types.

Notably, the functional output of mTORC2 exhibits a high degree of cell type and tissue specificity. In adaptive immunity, it balances the differentiation direction of Th1 and Th2 cells, maintains the stability of regulatory T cells, and ensures the long-term survival of plasma cells. In innate immunity, it finely regulates macrophage polarization, dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation, as well as the effector functions and metabolic reprogramming of natural killer cells. In the context of tissue-specific inflammation, mTORC2 can either exacerbate pathological damage or exert protective effects, depending on the microenvironment—a dual nature consistently observed in conditions such as osteoarthritis, pancreatitis, adipose tissue inflammation, and colorectal cancer. This characteristic not only makes mTORC2 a highly promising therapeutic target but also serves as a reminder that indiscriminate inhibition of this pathway may carry unforeseen risks.

The inflammatory regulatory function of mTORC2 is highly context-dependent, exerting opposing effects across different cell types and pathological microenvironments. In innate immune lineages including macrophages and dendritic cells, mTORC2 predominantly mediates anti-inflammatory homeostatic effects: it drives anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization, restrains excessive DC maturation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and limits aberrant Th1/Th17 immune priming. Myeloid mTORC2 also mitigates intestinal inflammation in colorectal cancer and mediates FGF1-induced anti-inflammatory effects in adipose tissue. In contrast, mTORC2 acts as a pathogenic pro-inflammatory driver in multiple tissue-specific diseases, exacerbating osteoarthritic cartilage destruction, hypertriglyceridemic pancreatitis injury, and systemic lupus erythematosus autoimmune feedback via NF-κB activation and other parenchymal pathways. This functional duality underscores the need for cell-type-specific targeting strategies for safe and precise mTORC2-based therapeutics.

Looking ahead, several key scientific questions remain to be addressed. First, the spatiotemporal dynamics of mTORC2 assembly and membrane localization in immune cells in vivo require further dissection at the single‑cell level. Second, the molecular mechanisms by which mTORC2 integrates signals from various immune receptors—such as TLRs, TCR, BCR, and cytokine receptors—have yet to be elucidated. Third, small‑molecule compounds that selectively inhibit mTORC2 without interfering with the physiological functions of mTORC1 are still lacking, representing a major bottleneck in drug development. Fourth, given the opposing effects of mTORC2 in different tumor microenvironments, future clinical translation will rely on precision medicine strategies, such as stratifying patients based on RICTOR expression levels or phosphorylation status.

In conclusion, an in-depth understanding of the context‑specific functions of mTORC2 across different immune cell types and pathological settings will not only advance our knowledge of immune regulatory networks but also open new avenues for the treatment of inflammatory diseases, autoimmune disorders, and cancer. Future research should integrate genetic, pharmacological, and multi‑omics approaches to systematically unravel the regulatory logic of this important kinase complex. Moreover, the lack of highly selective mTORC2 inhibitors remains a major obstacle for clinical translation, as currently available mTOR-targeting agents frequently affect both mTORC1 and mTORC2, leading to limited therapeutic specificity and potential adverse effects. Future studies should therefore focus on developing selective mTORC2 modulators, identifying reliable biomarkers for patient stratification, and integrating mTORC2-targeted therapies with immunotherapy or metabolic interventions, with the ultimate goal of translating its therapeutic potential into clinical practice..
