Pathological R-loops in bacteria from engineered expression of endogenous antisense RNAs whose synthesis is ordinarily terminated by Rho
Authors: Apuratha Pandiyan, Jillella Mallikarjun, Himanshi Maheshwari, Jayaraman Gowrishankar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn many bacteria, the essential factors Rho and NusG mediate termination of synthesis of nascent transcripts (including antisense RNAs) that are not being simultaneously translated. It has been proposed that in Rho’s absence toxic RNA–DNA hybrids (R-loops) may be generated from nascent untranslated transcripts, and genome-wide mapping studies in Escherichia coli have identified putative loci of R-loop formation from more than 100 endogenous antisense transcripts that are synthesized only in a Rho-deficient strain. Here we provide evidence that engineered expression in wild-type E. coli of several such individual antisense regions on a plasmid or the chromosome generates R-loops that, in an RNase H-modulated manner, serve to disrupt genome integrity. Rho inhibition was associated with increased prevalence of antisense R-loops also in Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae and Caulobacter crescentus. Our results confirm the essential role of Rho in several bacterial genera for prevention of toxic R-loops from pervasive yet cryptic endogenous antisense transcripts. Engineered antisense R-looped regions may be useful for studies on both site-specific impediments to bacterial chromosomal replication and the mechanisms of their resolution.

Introduction

The Rho protein mediates an important and active mechanism of bacterial transcription termination, and is essential for viability in many species, including Escherichia coli [reviewed in (1–8)]. Rho binds to an exposed rhoutilization(rut) sequence of degenerate specificity on nascent RNA trailing an RNA polymerase (RNAP) during the elongation step of transcription, following which it acts along with accessory factors such as NusG to dislodge RNAP from the DNA; the precise molecular mechanism by which Rho’s action leads to transcription termination and to release of RNAP and RNA from DNA is unclear, and several alternative models have been proposed in this regard (9–15).

In view of the existence of transcription–translation coupling in bacteria such as E. coli (1,16–20) for which NusG is also proposed to play a role (21,22), rut sequences in the protein-coding regions of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) are shielded from Rho binding, so that Rho mediates premature termination of only those mRNAs that are not being simultaneously translated (such as those with a nonsense codon mutation in a protein-coding gene or on which there has been stochastic failure of ribosome loading); such premature termination within a gene or operon as a consequence of transcription–translation uncoupling is also referred to as transcriptional polarity (23–25) [reviewed in (1,16,17,23,26)]. Rho also mediates transcription termination of RNAs that are not naturally translated (such as in the 3′-untranslated region of an mRNA, or antisense RNAs), although an active antitermination mechanism operates to ensure that synthesis of ribosomal RNA (which is not translated) is not terminated by Rho’s action.

In Bacillus subtilis, transcription and translation are not strongly coupled (27,28), and it has been proposed that cis-encoded elements define the sites of Rho-mediated transcription termination (27,29). At the same time, the finding from E. coli (30,31) that Rho acts to suppress pervasive antisense transcription has been shown also to be true in the different Gram-positive bacteria, including B. subtilis (27,32), Staphylococcus aureus (33) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (34,35). For E. coli, several models, not mutually exclusive, have been proposed to account for the essentiality of Rho, such as its role in (i) silencing of cryptic prophage genes (36–39), (ii) prevention of replication fork arrest especially at sites of stalled transcription elongation complexes (40–42) or (iii) avoidance of formation of toxic transcription-associated RNA–DNA hybrids or R-loops in the genome (26,30,43–45).

The R-loop is a three-stranded nucleic acid structure that is generated when a nascent transcript is re-annealed with the upstream template DNA strand to form an RNA–DNA hybrid, with the non-template strand now being displaced as single-stranded (ss)DNA [reviewed in (45,46)). The propensity of a nascent transcript to form an R-loop is not universal (47), and is largely determined by the sequence of and features in RNA, including occurrence of G-rich clusters or of trinucleotide repeats (48–52). RNase HI (encoded by rnhA) specifically cleaves RNA in an RNA–DNA hybrid. R-loops can confer cellular toxicity by one or more of the following mechanisms: (i) by acting as roadblocks to transcription (30,53,54); (ii) by engendering transcription–replication conflicts (55–58); and (iii) by priming aberrant DNA replication initiation (59–64).

Two mechanisms, not mutually exclusive, may be envisaged for R-loop generation from nascent untranslated RNAs in the absence of Rho-mediated transcription termination. First, a transcript may by itself directly re-anneal with the template DNA strand in the negatively supercoiled region upstream of the moving RNAP (26,43,45). Second, RNAP in a transcription elongation complex that is uncoupled from translation is more prone to backtracking and arrest (41,65–67), and there is evidence for R-loop formation from its associated RNA when the elongation complex collides with a replisome (41,55,56,68–71).

Three lines of evidence have provided support for the model that it is R-loop avoidance, which explains the essentiality of Rho and NusG in E. coli. (i) Nucleic acid preparations from cells with compromised Rho function exhibit increased reactivity to S9.6 monoclonal antibody, which is specific for RNA–DNA hybrids (30). (ii) Δrho and ΔnusG lethalities are rescued by ectopic expression of an R-loop helicase UvsW (sourced from phage T4) (30,44). (iii) Finally, two novel categories of antisense RNAs, each several hundred in number, have been identified exclusively in Rho-deficient cells: the first occurs only when UvsW helicase is expressed in the cells, whereas the second occurs both in the presence and in the absence of UvsW (30,31). [These two categories are in addition to a third category of antisense RNAs that is constitutively expressed even in Rho-proficient strains (72–75).] The chromosomal regions encoding antisense RNAs of the first category (i.e. those that are expressed only under the combined conditions of Rho deficiency and UvsW helicase expression) were correlated with the hotspots of antisense R-loop formation that have been mapped in the E. coli genome (30,44), and these data have been interpreted as follows (30): (i) that a large number of cryptic antisense loci exist in the E. coli genome [presumably correlated with widespread presence of intragenic promoters oriented in both directions (74,75)], whose expression is ordinarily terminated by Rho so that the antisense RNAs are synthesized only in Rho-deficient cells; and furthermore (ii) that a subset of these antisense RNAs (representing the first category described above) possess the propensity to form R-loops that block further transcription and therefore require unwinding by UvsW helicase for them to be synthesized in quantity and to be identified, while the remainder (constituting the second category) do not form R-loops and hence are detected in Rho-deficient cells without or with UvsW. Several other studies, in E. coli (76–79) and in Vibrio cholerae following sublethal translational inhibition (80), have also suggested that R-loops may be generated when transcription and translation are uncoupled.

However, the interpretations above may not be considered as conclusive, especially since UvsW belongs to a group of helicases that can act not only on R-loops but also at replication forks (81–84); it is therefore possible that Rho’s essential function is to maintain replisome progression without necessarily invoking an R-loop avoidance mechanism (40). It is also known that Rho deficiency itself can confer pleiotropic effects on cells (2).

In this work, we show that the engineered overexpression in E. coli, on plasmids or the chromosome, of several of these otherwise cryptic endogenous antisense loci of the first category (i.e. those which are predicted to generate R-loops in Rho-deficient cells) confers toxicity and loss of genome integrity even in Rho-proficient strains; the effects are RNase H-modulated, indicating that R-loops are being generated under these conditions (85). Our findings therefore confirm the existence of significant numbers of R-loop forming natural antisense RNAs whose synthesis is being ordinarily curtailed by Rho. We have also extended these findings to two other Gram-negative bacteria, Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae and Caulobacter crescentus, to show in them that Rho inhibition is associated with increased R-loop prevalence that can putatively be mapped to antisense hotspots across the genome. Finally, our work offers a new experimental approach based on transcription-associated R-loops to provoke site-specific impediments to replisome progression on the chromosome, which is different from those based on protein roadblocks (86,87), ectopic Ter sites (88), palindrome DNA cleavage (89) rrn operon inversions (68) or other head-on transcription (55) that have been described earlier.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Escherichia coli strains are listed in Supplementary Table S1, and their derivatives were grown in LB medium (90) at 37°C unless otherwise indicated. Where appropriate, the medium was supplemented with ampicillin (Amp), kanamycin (Kan) or trimethoprim (Tp) (each at 50 μg/ml), and Xgal at 25 μg/ml. Supplementation with isopropyl β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG) was at 1 mM.

The knockout mutants ΔrnhA, ΔpcnB, ΔrapA, ΔsbcC, ΔrhlE, ΔeutT, ΔentE, ΔcstA and ΔilvD were from the Keio collection (91). Mutations in rnhA are synthetic lethal with mutations impeding DNA damage repair (92,93); a few groups have also reported that rnhA single mutants grow very poorly in rich medium (94) and rapidly accumulate suppressors (80). On the other hand, many other groups, including our own, have not previously observed any other major growth defects associated with just lack of RNase HI (30,44,57,63,64,95–97), which was also the experience in this study with ΔrnhA derivatives of XL1-Blue recA+. We also independently analyzed several publicly available whole-genome sequence (WGS) data for rnhA mutants (63,96) and did not detect any evidence (in the form of increased frequency of either single nucleotide polymorphisms or indels) for accumulation of suppressor mutations in these populations.

Caulobacter crescentus and X. oryzae pv. oryzae strains were provided by Anjana Badrinarayanan and Subhadeep Chatterjee, respectively, and their growth conditions were as described (98,99). Supplementation of cultures with the Rho inhibitor bicyclomycin (BCM) for each of the bacteria was at the indicated concentrations.

Plasmids

Previously described plasmids include (salient features in parentheses): pKD13 (KanR AmpR), pKD46 (AmpR) and pCP20 (CmR AmpR) for chromosomal recombineering and site-specific excision between a pair of FRT sites, as described (100); pTrc99a (AmpR, for IPTG-induced expression of cloned DNA fragments) (101); pSK760 (AmpR, ColE1 plasmid with cloned rnhA+ gene) (76); pET28b (KanR, ColE1-derived plasmid) (Novagen, USA); and pHYD5701 (TpR, single-copy-number plasmid with recA+ lacZ+) (95).

Plasmids pHYD7527 and pHYD7528 are pKD13 derivatives with a cloned Ptac sequence at a single site in two orientations, whose construction is described below. Construction of plasmids by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and cloning of different antisense regions into the pTrc99a vector is described in the Supplementary Text; the plasmids are listed in Supplementary Table S2, and the oligonucleotide primers used for generating PCR amplicons for the cloning are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Plasmid DNA supercoiling assays

Transformants of XL1-Blue (102) with pTrc99a-derived plasmids were grown to an A600 of ∼0.6 without or with terminal exposure to IPTG for 2 h. Approximately 2 μg of plasmid DNA from each culture was subjected to electrophoresis at 3 V/cm for 16–22 h on 1% agarose gels with the indicated concentrations of chloroquine, as described (62,103).

Recombineering of Ptac at different chromosomal loci

We first cloned the Ptac sequence in two orientations into plasmid pKD13 by annealing a pair of complementary oligonucleotide primers JGAH068-JGAH069 comprising the tac sequence (Supplementary Table S3) and ligating the DNA duplex (with 5′-GATC overhangs) into the BamHI site of pKD13. From the transformants recovered in strain S17-1 λpir (104), two plasmids pHYD7527 and pHYD7528 were identified to carry the cloned Ptac sequence reading, respectively, away from (‘Out’ orientation) or toward (‘In’ orientation) the KanR gene of pKD13. The orientation of Ptac in the plasmids was established by PCR with primer pairs JGAH070-JGAH072 (positive for pHYD7527) and JGAH071-JGAH072 (positive for pHYD7528); the primer sequences are given in Supplementary Table S3.

Plasmids pHYD7527 and pHYD7528 were then used as templates for recombineering of Ptac into different sites on the chromosome, the details of which are described in the Supplementary Text. Expression of the λ Red genes for recombineering was achieved with the use of either plasmid pKD46 (100) or a strain carrying mini- λ-Tet prophage (105).

Blue–white assay to determine RecA dependence for viability of recombineered strains

The blue–white assay, which was employed to establish whether the Ptac-recombineered strains were RecA-dependent or -independent for viability, has been described earlier (95,104). Briefly, Ptac-recombineered derivatives of XL1-Blue (which is lacZΔM15 and recA1) carrying the single-copy-number recA+ lacZ+ TpR shelter plasmid pHYD5701 were plated for single colonies on LB-Xgal medium. Since the plasmid is spontaneously lost from ∼20% of cells in a culture, RecA independence of viability was established by the presence of white colonies on plating. On the other hand, if blue colonies alone were obtained (despite the absence of Tp in the plates), it was concluded that the recombineered strain is RecA-dependent for viability.

WGS for DNA copy number analysis and mutation identification

The methods for WGS and analysis were essentially as described (95,104). Genomic DNA was extracted by the phenol–chloroform method from cultures of XL1-Blue strain derivatives grown with IPTG supplementation to early exponential phase, and subjected to Illumina-based WGS (Medgenome Labs, Bengaluru, India) followed by copy number analysis after alignment to the MG1655 reference sequence (accession number U00096.3); three chromosomal deletions were identified as common to all the sequenced strains (relative to the reference sequence), between nucleotide coordinates 62402-88275, 1196376-1211408 and 4540757-4597433, respectively. WGS data were also used for de novo contig assembly and identification of novel junction sequences as described in (106).

WGS following bisulfite treatment

The protocols for preparation of total nucleic acids from cultures, treatment (without denaturation) with sodium bisulfite, and subsequent whole-genome amplification were as described (44). (Exposure to bisulfite provokes C-to-T changes in ssDNA.) WGS was performed on an Illumina platform, and the reads were aligned and quantitated (using individual genes or open-reading-frames (ORFs) as units) with the aid of the Bismark software (107); the reference sequences used were those of E. coli strain MG1655, X. oryzae pv. oryzae strain BXO1 and C. crescentus CB15 (accession numbers U00096.3, NZ_CP033201.1 and AE005673.1, respectively). The Bismark algorithm determines, from the aggregate population of reads, base counts for each C residue of the reference sequence in the following categories: (i) unchanged; (ii) converted to T on top strand (‘C-to-T’); or (iii) converted to T on bottom strand (‘G-to-A’). For each ORF, the bisulfite reactivity of its top and bottom strands was then computed as the ratio of its C-to-T and G-to-A base counts, respectively, to its total base counts (i.e. the sum of unchanged, C-to-T and G-to-A base counts).

Conversion of ‘color space’ sequence reads to ‘base space’ sequence reads

Data from an earlier study (44) of WGS following bisulfite treatment of non-denatured nucleic acid preparations from E. coli cultures, which had been deposited in ‘color space’ sequence format in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive, were converted to ‘base space’ sequence format with the aid of the perl script csfq2fq.pl downloaded from https://gist.github.com/pcantalupo/9c30709fe802c96ea2b3. The latter was then analyzed by the Bismark algorithm (107) as described above, using the reference genome sequence of E. coli MG1655 (accession number U00096.3). The bisulfite reactivity percentiles of all ORFs calculated before and after conversion (in both sense and antisense orientations) were highly correlated (R2 > 0.85). To determine bisulfite reactivity at high resolution from these data for each of the different antisense regions, we plotted moving average values using window and step sizes of five and two consecutive C residues, respectively, on the non-template strand of antisense transcription.

Other methods

Procedures for phage P1 transduction (108), pCP20-mediated excision between FRT sites (100), immunoblotting of total nucleic acid preparations with S9.6 monoclonal antibody (30) and determinations of β-galactosidase specific activity in Miller units (90) were as described. Methods for DNA manipulations, PCR and gel electrophoresis were as described in (109).

Results

R-loop toxicity following plasmid-borne expression of endogenous chromosomal cryptic antisense regions

Previous studies have shown that R-loops can be generated from endogenous E. coli transcripts when expressed from plasmids in topoisomerase I-deficient cells but not in a wild-type strain [reviewed in (46)]. In wild-type E. coli, R-loops occur upon heterologous expression of certain mammalian gene regions such as from either an immunoglobulin or a triplet-repeat locus (both of which are known to form R-loops in their native contexts) (48,49). Data from the experiments below indicate that plasmid-borne expression of several endogenous cryptic antisense regions can also generate R-loops in wild-type E. coli.

From among the E. coli chromosomal antisense loci that had been characterized at 100-base resolution in an earlier RNA sequencing study (30) as being predominantly transcribed only under the combined conditions of Rho deficiency and presence of the R-loop helicase UvsW (i.e. falling into category 1 of antisense RNAs as described in the ‘Introduction’), we chose 19 regions (each ∼0.3 or 2 kb long) for cloning downstream of the IPTG-inducible trc promoter of plasmid pTrc99a (101), as described in Supplementary Table S2. All of these regions were above the 85th percentile for antisense bisulfite reactivity (i.e. for occurrence of displaced ssDNA) (44). As discussed below, this criterion (of high antisense bisulfite reactivity percentile) merely represents a first approximation for high R-loop forming propensity, since the antisense percentiles could only be computed for discrete ORFs, whereas antisense transcription may be confined to only one part of an ORF or may even traverse ORF boundaries.

We also cloned three antisense regions from among those encoding the category 2 of antisense RNAs (as described in the ‘Introduction’ section), i.e. whose expression also occurs only upon Rho inhibition but this time without the need for UvsW helicase (30,31). These RNAs were accordingly expected not to exhibit significant R-loop propensity and they were associated with a low bisulfite reactivity percentile (Supplementary Table S2).

As listed in Supplementary Table S2, some of the cloned category 1 and category 2 antisense regions extended across ORF boundaries within an operon. In the descriptions below, each of the plasmid clones is non-redundantly designated by a single ‘b’ number (110) corresponding to the cognate ORF (or one of the ORFs, in the case of clones with antisense regions spanning two or more of them), with ‘AS_’ as prefix.

That a particular phenotype is R-loop associated can be inferred based on its modulation by cellular RNase HI status, i.e. between derivatives that are ΔrnhA, rnhA+ or with engineered increase in RNase HI expression (41,43,48,49,55,57,76–78,80). Of the 19 category 1 antisense regions that were tested in recA+derivatives of strain XL1-Blue after cloning in pTrc99a, 6 (AS_b0059, AS_b0397, AS_b0797, AS_b1077, AS_b2456 and AS_b2723) conferred a marked IPTG-dependent decrease in viability on Amp-supplemented medium, but only so in a ΔrnhA derivative and not the rnhA+ parent (Figure 1A, clone numbers marked in blue; only two of the clones that were negative in this test are shown, marked in black). We interpret these findings as evidence for R-loops being generated from the cognate antisense transcripts, whose persistence in the RNase HI-deficient strain then leads to inhibition of plasmid replication. The findings are also noteworthy in that they represent the first examples of endogenous chromosomal regions that are also able to confer apparent R-loop toxicity upon their engineered expression from a plasmid in wild-type E. coli.

None of the three category 2 antisense regions cloned into pTrc99a conferred growth inhibition in the rnhA+ or ΔrnhA derivatives on IPTG-supplemented medium (data for two of them, AS_b0561 and AS_b2375, are shown in Figure 1A). However, we did not attempt to determine whether the difference in behavior between categories 1 and 2 (upon cloning into pTrc99a) is statistically significant, for which a much larger number of the category 2 antisense regions would need to have been tested.

For one more of the candidate category 1 antisense regions cloned in pTrc99a (AS_b2459, cloned region length 336 bp), IPTG-induced lethality on Amp-supplemented medium was observed in both ΔrnhA and rnhA+ host strains (Figure 1A). We surmise that in this case, R-loops generated from the antisense RNAs on the plasmids are in sufficient excess to overwhelm the capacity of native RNase HI to remove them. Even for this plasmid, modulation of IPTG-dependent lethality by cellular RNase HI status could be demonstrated following introduction into the host strain of a ΔpcnB mutation [which serves to reduce copy number of ColE1 plasmids (43,111,112)]; thus, the plasmid conferred lethality with IPTG in a ΔrnhA ΔpcnB but not an rnhA+ ΔpcnB derivative (Figure 1B, compare with control plasmid AS_b0561).

The observations above, with pTrc99a derivatives bearing cloned endogenous antisense regions, were made in the E. coli strain background of XL1-Blue recA+ (either rnhA+ or ΔrnhA). To test their behavior in other strain backgrounds, we introduced the plasmid derivatives carrying antisense regions AS_b0397 (lethal in ΔrnhA but not rnhA+), AS_b2459 (lethal in both) and AS_b2375 (control, lethal in neither) into ΔrnhA and rnhA+ derivatives of strains MG1655, MC4100 and BW25113. The results, shown in Supplementary Figure S1, indicate that the pattern of IPTG-induced and RNase H-modulated toxicity exhibited by the different cloned endogenous antisense regions is the same across a variety of E. coli strain backgrounds.

Finally, IPTG-dependent lethality associated with the plasmids above was exhibited also on medium without Amp supplementation (data not shown). These findings suggest that excessive R-loop formation on the plasmid not only can interfere with plasmid replication but can also confer cellular toxicity.

Contiguous, or even nested, antisense regions exhibit differences in IPTG-induced toxicity

Among the different antisense regions of category 1 that had been tested above, we noted several instances in which a region conferring IPTG-dependent lethality either was contiguous to or overlapped with another that was not lethal, even though all of them fulfilled the candidate criteria for R-loop formation at their native locations (as detailed in Supplementary Table S2). Three such groups were (see Supplementary Figure S2) (i) AS_b1077 (0.3 kb, lethal in ΔrnhA), which is nested within AS_b1075 (2 kb, not lethal); (ii) AS_b0059 (region 1) (0.3 kb, lethal in ΔrnhA), which is nested within AS_b0059 (region 2) (1.9 kb, not lethal); and (iii) the group of AS_b2459 (0.3 kb, lethal in both rnhA+ and ΔrnhA), which is adjacent to AS_b2458 (0.3 kb, not lethal), which in turn is nested within AS_b2456 (1.9 kb, lethal only in ΔrnhA).

To check whether differences for IPTG-induced lethality within each of these three groups of contiguous or nested antisense regions could be correlated with differences, if any, in bisulfite reactivity patterns (i.e. ssDNA distribution) examined at a higher resolution at their native chromosomal locations than that of discrete ORFs, we determined bisulfite reactivity (as described in ‘Materials and methods’ section) for overlapping windows of five consecutive C residues on the non-template strand of antisense transcription (each thus corresponding to 20 bases approximately of DNA). We also plotted the log2 ratio of antisense RNA abundance, for overlapping 100-base regions at each locus in the Rho-deficient strain with UvsW helicase relative to that in wild type (30); a category 2 region AS_b2375 (that is expressed upon Rho deficiency but does not form R-loops) was included as a negative control.

The results from this analysis are shown in Supplementary Figure S2. As expected, bisulfite reactivity at the negative control locus AS_b2375 was substantially lower than that for the three groups of test regions, whereas the magnitude of antisense RNA expression provoked by Rho deficiency in the former was not inferior to that in the latter (Supplementary Figure S2, compare panel A with panels B–D). Within each of the three groups of cloned test regions, the overall distribution of deep valleys or troughs was apparently more pronounced in plots for the clones that were non-lethal (denoted in gray: AS_b0059 region 2, AS_b2458 and AS_b1075 in, respectively, panels B–D of Supplementary Figure S2), in comparison with that for the clones that conferred lethality (denoted in red: AS_b0059 region 1 in Supplementary Figure S2B; AS_b2456 and AS_b2459 in Supplementary Figure S2C; and AS_b1077 in Supplementary Figure S2D).

These observations were supported also by calculations of area under the curve (AUC) per unit length as a measure of the extent of ssDNA (assumed to be at R-loop sites) at the corresponding loci (values are indicated in Supplementary Figure S2). The AUC per kb was uniformly higher for the loci conferring lethality (>59 000 arbitrary units) than that for the non-lethal loci (<53 000 arbitrary units). Although not conclusive, these results do suggest that subtle differences in bisulfite reactivity may, at least in part, account for the distinction between lethal and non-lethal clones of the category 1 regions. Additional factors that might contribute to the outcome of the plasmid lethality assay are touched upon in the ‘Discussion’ section.

Antisense expression toxicity is not a consequence of interference with cognate sense gene function

In the description above, growth inhibition associated with IPTG-induced expression of the antisense RNAs has been presumed to be a consequence of R-loop formation. On the other hand, an alternative possibility could be that it is a consequence of base pairing between the plasmid-encoded antisense transcript and the corresponding sense mRNA expressed from the chromosome, thereby interfering with the latter’s expression or stability. Indeed, several of the cloned regions that had exhibited IPTG-dependent toxicity are antisense to functionally important genes such as rapA [b0059, encoding an RNAP recycling factor (113,114)], sbcC [b0397, encoding (along with its partner gene sbcD in the same operon) a DNA exo/endonuclease (115,116)] and rhlE [b0797, encoding an RNA helicase (117)].

Given that for none of the seven loci, the antisense RNAs from which were identified in this study to confer lethality when expressed from plasmid pTrc99a, is the individual sense gene knockout lethal (91), it would appear unlikely that interference with sense mRNA function is the cause of antisense transcript toxicity. (Our own WGS experiments, described elsewhere in the text, had also shown that the XL1-Blue strain used in this study carries a promoter-proximal deletion of rapA.) To further confirm that the sense mRNA plays no role in the exhibition of IPTG-induced toxicity, we transformed four of the pTrc99a plasmid derivatives with cloned antisense regions (namely AS_b0059, AS_b0397 and AS_b0797 mentioned above, along with AS_b2459 that had shown toxicity even in rnhA+) into the cognate derivatives carrying the Keio knockout mutations ΔrapA, ΔsbcC, ΔrhlE and ΔeutT, respectively, that had been constructed by P1 transduction into XL1-Blue recA+. In each of the cases, the plasmid transformants exhibited IPTG-induced lethality in a manner that was identical to that in XL1-Blue recA+ itself, namely AS_b2459 conferred lethality in both rnhA+and ΔrnhA derivatives of the ΔeutT mutant, whereas the other plasmids were lethal only in the ΔrnhA derivatives (Supplementary Figure S3). These results served to establish that toxicity is not a consequence of antisense RNA base pairing with its cognate sense mRNA.

An additional line of evidence in support of this conclusion is the fact that the observed IPTG-dependent lethalities were RNase H-modulated, implying that they are associated with formation of RNA–DNA (rather than RNA–RNA) hybrids.

IPTG-induced increased supercoiling in plasmid with cloned antisense region AS_b2459

Plasmid DNA supercoils are constrained upon R-loop occurrence, and the consequent homeostatic adjustment of superhelical density within the cells is manifested as increased negative supercoiling in preparations of plasmid DNA from these cultures that can be detected by chloroquine-agarose gel electrophoresis (46,47,76–79,85,94,118–120). We tested the supercoiling status of the pTrc99a-derived plasmids prepared from cultures of XL1-Blue transformants grown without or with IPTG supplementation. Upon electrophoresis through a chloroquine-agarose gel, a marked increase in negative supercoiling following IPTG addition was observed for the plasmid with cloned antisense locus AS_b2459 of category 1 (that confers lethality with IPTG supplementation even in an rnhA+ strain), whereas supercoiling of the control plasmid with antisense region AS_b2375 (of category 2) was unaltered in the absence or presence of IPTG (Figure 2, compare lane 4 with lanes 1–3; gel image is shown in panel A and densitometric tracing of gel lanes in panel B). The lengths of cloned regions in the two plasmids are 336 and 334 bp, respectively. Similar results obtained from additional independent experiments are shown in Supplementary Figure S4. These findings serve to substantiate the conclusion that it is R-loop generation that is responsible for IPTG-induced lethality with AS_b2459.

R-loops from engineered antisense transcription at chromosomal locus AS_b2459

We chose two category 1 antisense regions AS_b2459 and AS_b0397, whose expression from the plasmid was lethal, respectively, even in rnhA+ or only in ΔrnhA, to recombineer the Ptac promoter sequence at their native chromosomal locations. In each case, the site of chromosomal integration was congruent with the 5′-end of antisense RNA expressed from the cognate pTrc99a-derived plasmid clone. The results for the two loci are described separately in this and the succeeding sections, respectively, but the following features were common to both of them.

All derivatives were ΔlacZ recA1 on the chromosome and carried an unstable single-copy-number ‘shelter’ plasmid with recA+ and lacZ+ genes; cultures of such derivatives typically are comprised of a 80:20 ratio (approximate) of plasmid-bearing and plasmid-free cells that grow as blue (recA+) and white (recA1) colonies, respectively, on Xgal-supplemented plates (‘blue–white’ assay).

Furthermore, although the Ptac promoter used for the chromosomal recombineering was expected to be tightly IPTG-regulated, we observed in a control experiment (wherein this promoter was integrated upstream of native lacZ in each of two different constructs) that it is sufficiently active even in the absence of IPTG (β-galactosidase specific activity of approximately 240 and 1980 Miller units, respectively, without and with 1 mM IPTG; details are provided in the Supplementary Text). The phenotypes described below were unchanged in the presence or absence of IPTG.

Survival in the absence of RecA (i.e. growth of white colonies on Xgal medium) was restored for the Ptac::AS_b2459 (In) derivative (GJ20013) upon heterologous overexpression of RNase HI from a multicopy ColE1-derived plasmid pSK760 (Figure 3B, panel iii). Furthermore, in this situation, the rnhA+-bearing plasmid could no longer be cured from the recA1 derivative even upon introduction of a second plasmid (pET28b, KanR) of the same incompatibility group; thus, none of 40 KanR colonies tested had lost the AmpR-bearing rnhA+ plasmid after introduction of the second KanR plasmid. On the other hand, when a similar experiment was performed as control in the isogenic recA+ derivative (i.e. on a blue colony), 32 of 40 KanR transformants tested had become AmpS, indicating that the rnhA+ plasmid was being lost at high frequency as expected under these conditions. As with RNase HI overexpression, ectopic expression of the R-loop UvsW helicase (30,44,121) was also able to restore RecA-independent viability in the Ptac::AS_b2459 (In) integrant (Figure 3B, panel iv).

In a ΔrnhA derivative of XL1-Blue recA+ (GJ20007/pHYD5701), attempts to introduce the Ptac::AS_b2459 (Out) event by KanR transduction were not successful. Likewise, in the method for obtaining Ptac::AS_b2459 (In) into this strain, the second step of site-specific excision of KanR (from strain GJ20022, which would result in Ptac driving antisense expression at the locus) could not be achieved because of an inability to obtain transformants with plasmid pCP20 (that mediates the excision) into the strain. These results suggest that Ptac-driven expression of AS_b2459 is lethal in a ΔrnhA recA+ strain.

Taken together, these observations indicate that promoter integration at the chromosomal antisense locus AS_b2459 is correlated with generation of R-loops, and that the subsequent outcome is determined by cellular RNase HI status: with multicopy-rnhA+, the R-loops are removed without much ensuing pathology; in rnhA+, genome integrity is compromised most likely as a consequence of transcription–replication conflict at the site of R-loop formation (17,55–57,122–124), necessitating a requirement of recombinational repair for cell viability; and in ΔrnhA, there is persistent R-loop formation at the site that serves to permanently block replisome progression (55).

During the process of routine maintenance of cultures of these recombineered strains, we found that the synthetic lethality phenotype with recA1 was often lost, suggestive of the accumulation of suppressor mutations. One such suppressor was designated GJ20029 (Figure 3B, panel v), and its molecular characterization (as a revertant of sokE::IS150 to sokE+) is described in a later section.

R-loops from engineered antisense transcription at chromosomal locus AS_b0397

For the second antisense region AS_b0397 (which confers lethality when expressed from plasmid pTrc99a only in a ΔrnhA background), the Ptac promoter sequence was recombineered by the two-step method outlined in panel (ii) of Figure 3A (to generate strain GJ20017/pHYD5701). The integration event is designated as Ptac::AS_b0397 (In). In this case, antisense transcription is expected to be co-directional with oriC-initiated replisome progression.

In the blue–white screening assay with recA+ borne on the shelter plasmid, the Ptac promoter integrant designed to express antisense region AS_b0397 failed to yield white colonies at 30°C (Figure 3B, panel vi), indicating that this event also affects genome integrity as to be synthetic lethal with recA1. White colonies were obtained, however, at 37°C (Figure 3B, panel vii). Previous studies have shown that R-loop toxicity is exacerbated at lower temperatures (43,76).

In control experiments for both the chromosomal loci b2459 and b0397 (for which genomic integrations leading to antisense expression had resulted in synthetic lethality with recA1 mutation), we were able to show by the blue–white screening assay that the Keio knockout mutant at each locus (eutT and sbcC, respectively) is not synthetic lethal with recA1 (Supplementary Figure S5). [That mutants in sbcCD are viable in the absence of RecA has also been shown earlier by several groups (89,125,126).] These results served to exclude the possibility that the synthetic lethality observed with recA1 for each of the genomic antisense-expressing integrations is because of the loss of sense gene expression.

Absence of toxicity from engineered antisense transcription at control chromosomal locus AS_b2375

The Ptac promoter was also recombineered for antisense transcription at the control chromosomal locus AS_b2375 by the two-step method depicted in panel (ii) of Figure 3A (to generate strain GJ20015/pHYD5701), and the integration event is designated as Ptac::AS_b2375 (In). As determined by the blue–white screening assay with recA+ on the shelter plasmid, both recA+ and recA1 derivatives were viable with 1 mM IPTG at 30 and 37°C, confirming the absence of significant genome perturbation under these conditions (Figure 3B, data are shown for 30° in panel viii).

Escape replication of defective prophages upon chromosomal overexpression of the R-loop prone antisense loci

We undertook WGS of cells from IPTG-supplemented cultures grown to exponential phase of the strains (all carrying the recA+ plasmid) with Ptacintegrated upstream of the different antisense loci: Ptac::AS_b2459 (Out) and Ptac::AS_b2459 (In); Ptac::AS_b0397 (In) (two replicate cultures); and, as control, Ptac::AS_b2375 (In) (two cultures grown at, respectively, 37 and 30°C). Cultures of the integrant at AS_b0397 were grown at 30°C (since its phenotype of RecA-dependent viability is exhibited only at this temperature), whereas the pair of integrants at AS_b2459 was grown at 37°C. DNA copy number for each of the regions across the entire chromosome was then determined in cultures of the test strains (with Ptac integrated upstream of the R-loop prone antisense loci) relative to that in the control cultures of the Ptac::AS_b2375 (In) integrant grown at the cognate temperature.

The most interesting result from these experiments was that, in all the four test cultures that were RecA-dependent for viability, there was (in comparison with the control cultures) an enrichment in DNA copy number at one or both of a pair of genomic regions that precisely correspond to the positions of two defective (also called as cryptic or ‘grounded’) lambdoid prophages CP4-6 and DLP12 (127,128); the two prophages are located at, respectively, 0.28 and 0.57 Mbp in the genome (Figure 4). Thus, for the Ptac integrants at locus AS_b2459, the copy number of DLP12 prophage was elevated in one culture (In) (Figure 4A) and that of CP4-6 in the other (Out) (Figure 4B). For the integrant at locus AS_b0397, copy numbers of both CP4-6 and DLP12 were elevated in one replicate culture (Figure 4C), whereas that of CP4-6 alone was elevated in the other (Figure 4D). Careful examination of the plots also showed that the copy number of other defective prophages such as e14, CP4-44 and CP4-57 was also modestly elevated in the culture of Ptac::AS_b2459 (Out) that was RecA-dependent for viability (Figure 4B).

The copy number increases described above are indicative of escape replication (also called onion-skin replication) of the defective prophages in a subpopulation of cells in the cultures (129,130). The regulatory mechanisms for prophage escape replication are not fully understood, and there is evidence that several, including CP4-6 and DLP12, are controlled by the Rho factor (36,38). Positive feedback regulatory loops, as in phage λ replication (131), may also be involved to give rise to bistable or all-or-none behavior in individual cells, which may account for differential amplification at the two prophage loci in replicate cultures. Strain background features may also play a role, as we show below that in the MG1655 background it is another defective prophage CPS-53 that exhibits pronounced escape replication upon Rho inhibition. A tentative hypothesis to account for these observations is discussed below.

WGS analysis of suppressors of RecA-dependent viability

We also performed WGS on two suppressor derivatives of the Ptac::AS_b2459 integrants in each of which RecA was no longer essential for viability. The first was a spontaneous mutant of Ptac::AS_b2459 (Out) that had arisen in the course of routine maintenance of the strain, and the WGS analysis indicated that the elevation in DNA copy number at the CP4-6 prophage locus that was observed in the parent had been reversed in this suppressor (Figure 4E).

From the WGS reads of the parent and of this suppressor strain, we also performed de novo genome assembly and determined novel junction sequences by the method of Durrant et al. (106). This analysis showed that whereas the parent had an IS150 insertion in the sokE gene, the suppressor was sokE+; we then performed P1 transduction experiments to demonstrate that it is the sokE locus that is responsible for the difference in RecA dependence for viability between parent and the suppressor strain, and these results are described in the Supplementary Text. Analysis of WGS data of the other sequenced strains in this study showed that all of them were like the parent in harboring an IS150 insertion in sokE. Replacement by P1 transduction of sokE::IS150 by sokE+ in the other Ptac integrants that were RecA-dependent for viability [Ptac::AS_b2459 (In) and Ptac::AS_b0397 (In)] also resulted in restoration of RecA-independent viability in them (described in the Supplementary Text).

The E. coli chromosome bears five hok-sok gene pairs (suffixed A to E) that encode orthogonal toxin–antitoxin systems that are all putatively inactivated in different ways (132,133). The product of each of the sok genes (including sokE) is an antitoxin RNA of around 60 nucleotides. Several laboratory E. coli strains such as MG1655 and BW25113 are sokE+, but the gene is disrupted by IS150 insertion (exactly as in our parental version of XL1-Blue) in more than 400 sequenced natural E. coli isolates, as also in some strains of Shigella flexneri (134–136) (see Supplementary Table S4); sokA of MG1655 is also disrupted by IS150 insertion (133). Although the mechanism by which sokE+ acts as a suppressor was not further investigated in this study, our results do suggest that Ptac-directed chromosomal antisense expression, RecA-dependent viability and prophage escape replication are indeed correlated with one another.

The second suppressor derivative (which was viable in the absence of RecA) that was subjected to WGS was that with RNase HI overexpressed from a plasmid in the Ptac::AS_b2459 (In) integrant. In this case, the DNA copy number at both the CP4-6 and DLP12 prophage loci was elevated (Figure 4F), although the parent itself had shown copy number increase only for DLP12; there was also the expected increase in read numbers mapping to the rnhA locus because of this gene’s additional presence on a multicopy plasmid (not discernible in Figure 4F, in view of the small size of the locus). As discussed below, these findings indicate that although escape replication of the defective prophages may be correlated with elevated transcription of the R-loop prone antisense loci, it is likely not the cause for RecA dependence of viability in the integrant strain.

In summary, the WGS analysis in combination with the phenotype data has shown that Ptac-directed expression of R-loop forming antisense loci on the chromosome is associated with both escape replication of defective prophages and RecA dependence for strain viability, and that RNase HI overexpression apparently mitigates the latter but not the former.

Increased prevalence of R-loops following Rho inhibition in C. crescentus and X. oryzae

To test whether the models developed for Rho’s role in R-loop avoidance in E. coli are extendable to other bacteria, we examined reactivity of total nucleic acid preparations from cultures of C. crescentus and X. oryzae pv. oryzae to the R-loop specific monoclonal antibody S9.6. Rho is essential for viability in both bacteria (137,138). For E. coli, it has earlier been shown (30) that S9.6 reactivity is markedly increased in cultures grown in the presence of a sublethal concentration of BCM, an inhibitor of Rho function in many bacterial species (4).

For both C. crescentus and X. oryzae, we first confirmed their growth sensitivity to BCM at, respectively, 100 and 30 μg/ml. Nucleic acid preparations were then obtained from the cultures grown with sublethal BCM (25 and 15 μg/ml, respectively), as also for E. coli MG1655 as control (with BCM at 25 μg/ml). These preparations exhibited strong signals with S9.6 antibody compared to those from the control cultures without BCM (Figure 5A, top panel, compare columns 1 and 3 from left). In each case, the S9.6 signal was considerably reduced or attenuated upon prior treatment of the preparations with RNase H in vitro (Figure 5A, top panel, compare columns 3 and 4 from left), indicating that the signal was indeed from RNA–DNA hybrids in the preparations. We conclude that R-loop abundance is enhanced upon Rho inhibition in both bacteria, just as is the case in E. coli.

Whole-genome bisulfite mapping for ssDNA distribution in C. crescentus and X. oryzae

As mentioned above, we had previously employed a whole-genome bisulfite mapping strategy in E. coli to identify antisense loci with high propensity for R-loop formation (44), and we undertook a similar approach in this study with C. crescentus and X. oryzae. Non-denatured total nucleic acid preparations from cultures of the two bacteria (along with E. coli as control) grown without or with sublethal BCM were treated with sodium bisulfite, which leads to C-to-T conversions in ssDNA. The preparations were then subjected to WGS in order to infer the distribution of ssDNA regions in the genome. In this set of experiments, we could confirm the finding from an earlier report (37) that DNA copy number of a 10-kb region corresponding to the defective prophage CPS-53 (or KplE1), at the 2.47-Mbp location in the genome, is markedly increased in the Rho-inhibited E. coli culture, i.e. with BCM supplementation (Supplementary Table S5, sheet 1). These data are again indicative of prophage escape replication, similar to that described above for the RecA-dependent Ptac integrant strains.

In addition to ssDNA regions at R-loops that are exposed on the non-template strand behind the transcription elongation complex, ssDNA is also expected to be present on the lagging-strand template during replisome progression. Accordingly, the ranking of bisulfite-reactive regions across the genome was done as in our previous study (44), with genes or ORFs as units, across four separate categories based on combinations of the following: whether the strand is the non-template strand for sense or for antisense transcription, and whether it is the leading- or lagging-strand template during replication. The data for the individual ORFs of E. coli, X. oryzae pv. oryzae and C. crescentus are presented in Supplementary Table S5 (sheets 1, 2 and 3, respectively).

As had been done in our earlier study (44), we then sought to identify the putative sites of antisense R-loop occurrence in the genome as those ORFs that rank high in bisulfite reactivity percentile for the non-template strand of antisense transcription. For each of the three pairs of cultures (of E. coli, X. oryzae and C. crescentus, without and with BCM), the R2 correlation coefficient across the full range of antisense bisulfite reactivity percentiles was relatively modest at 0.51, 0.26 and 0.15, respectively. On the other hand, the correlations became progressively more robust at the higher percentile ranges, as was shown by the following analysis.

For each culture (say, E. coli without BCM), the antisense bisulfite reactivity data were grouped into different quantile ranges such as percentile, quintile, decile, dodecile, quartile and so on. From each such range, the highest-ranking bin was chosen for comparison with its equivalent from the data of the partner culture (in this case, E. coli with BCM). Examples of the highest-ranking bins chosen were >99 for percentile, >95 for quintile, >90 for decile and so on; for each comparison, the number of identical ORFs observed in both sets, as a proportion of that expected by chance, was determined.

As shown in Figure 5B, the observed-to-expected ratio for identical ORFs within each of the pairs, computed as above, increases sharply with increasing threshold of the highest-ranking bin for all three bacteria. The same pattern was noted when the data from this study was compared with that from our earlier study (44) for E. coli cultures without BCM supplementation [Figure 5B, curve marked E. coli (2); R2 = 0.33 for the comparison between the full range of values].

This analysis suggests that, for all three bacteria, the subset of ORFs with high antisense bisulfite reactivity is highly reproducible. Based on the other findings that we have obtained in this and in earlier work (30,44), we believe that at this high percentile range, R-loops are the main and reproducible source of antisense bisulfite reactivity. The data also lend support to our earlier proposal (44) that the order of ranking of R-loop prevalence across different antisense regions is not significantly different between cultures that are proficient or deficient for Rho-dependent transcription termination.

Evidence for R-loop toxicity upon expression of X. oryzae antisense region in E. coli

Our work above had shown that around one-third (7/19) of the tested E. coli antisense regions, selected on the basis of high bisulfite reactivity percentile, confer toxicity associated with R-loop formation upon cloning and expression in plasmid pTrc99a. We undertook similar experiments with four selected antisense regions each from X. oryzae and C. crescentus that exhibited high bisulfite reactivity percentiles.

Of the total of eight antisense regions that were tested from the two organisms after cloning in plasmid pTrc99a (listed in Supplementary Table S2), one (from X. oryzae, AS_BXO1_RS06265) conferred an IPTG-dependent decrease of viability in the ΔrnhA E. coli mutant but not in the rnhA+ parent (both carried the recA+ plasmid pHYD5701); the other seven clones were viable with IPTG in both strains (Figure 5C, data are shown only for derivatives with cloned X. oryzae antisense regions). These findings lend support to the notion that at least some of the antisense regions exhibiting high bisulfite reactivity from these organisms are prone to R-loop formation upon their adventitious expression in E. coli.

Discussion

Synthesis of the vast majority of antisense RNAs in E. coli and several other bacteria is kept under tight check by Rho- and NusG-mediated transcription termination (27,30–35). It is generally assumed that such cryptic antisense RNAs serve no function, and that they represent ‘noise’ of spurious transcription initiated from widely dispersed but weak intragenic promoters in the genome (74,75,139). However, no evidence is available at present either to support or to refute this assumption (e.g. whether any of these antisense transcripts exert a physiological function under environmental conditions that lead to downregulation of Rho activity in the cells).

We have previously presented evidence (30) for the occurrence of two categories of novel antisense transcripts in Rho-deficient E. coli cells, namely those that form and do not form R-loops, respectively. The loci AS_b2459 and AS_b0397 encode examples of the first category (which are detected only with Rho deficiency and presence of UvsW helicase) whereas locus AS_b2375 encodes an example of the second (detected in Rho-deficient cells without or with UvsW helicase). It was suggested in that work (30) that it is the aggregate genome-wide distribution of R-loops at the native category 1 antisense loci that contributes to inviability of a Δrho strain. The present work is complementary to the earlier one in that it has examined the effects of engineered overexpression of individual examples of these two categories of Rho-curtailed antisense RNAs (in Rho-proficient strains), to provide evidence that several of those belonging to the first category generate toxic R-loops both on a plasmid and on the chromosome.

In eukaryotes as well, the Sen1/senataxin factor has been proposed to function much like Rho in bacteria in mediating both transcription termination and prevention of R-loop occurrence from nascent transcripts (140–144). More generally, it is being increasingly recognized that various cotranscriptional processes are important for gene regulation in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes (145,146).

R-loop generation on plasmids from E. coli antisense RNAs

Of the 19 candidate R-loop forming antisense regions (i.e. of category 1) that were selected based on the data from previous whole-genome studies of bisulfite reactivity and of RNA expression, we obtained evidence of R-loop toxicity conferred by seven of them (including AS_b2459 and AS_b0397) upon their cloning and expression from plasmid pTrc99a. (None of the three control antisense regions of the second category, including AS_b2375, conferred toxicity in these experiments.) These represent the first examples in which occurrence of R-loops from endogenous DNA regions cloned on a plasmid has been demonstrated in wild-type E. coli; in previous studies, R-loop formation on plasmids has been shown either in topoisomerase I-deficient E. coli with some endogenous DNA regions (77,94) or in wild-type E. coli with heterologous DNA stretches from other organisms (48,49).

As was stated in the ‘Introduction’ section, there are two current non-exclusive models for cotranscriptional R-loop generation in bacteria. In both models, RNA sequence features (such as G-clusters and trinucleotide repeats) are important for nucleation of R-loops and for their extension (50–52). In the first model, it is primarily negative supercoiling behind the elongating RNAP that facilitates R-loop formation from the nascent transcript, which then leads to transcription–replication conflict (47,77,94), whereas transcription–translation coupling is protective (26,43,45,76,79,80); this model, for example, explains why R-loops accumulate in topoisomerase I-deficient mutants. In the second model, it is transcription–replication conflict associated with RNAP backtracking that precedes and provokes R-loop formation, for which negative supercoiling, RNA sequence propensity and absence of translation are then facilitatory (41,55,68–70); this model would, for example, explain why R-loops accumulate when conflicts between transcription and replication are engineered on a plasmid or the bacterial genome. Either of these models could appropriately explain the occurrence of R-loops from engineered antisense (translation-uncoupled) transcription as was noted in this study. The fact that lethality associated with the plasmid-encoded antisense RNAs was observed even in knockout mutants of the cognate chromosomal genes served to exclude the possibility that toxicity is a consequence of interference with sense mRNA expression from these genes.

We also note that not all category 1 antisense regions conferred R-loop toxicity upon their ectopic expression from plasmid pTrc99a, and that in several instances even contiguous or nested antisense regions (which otherwise fulfilled the candidate criteria) behaved differently in this assay. As suggested above, it is possible that there are subtle differences in bisulfite reactivity between these regions that could account for their different phenotypes. Three additional possibilities, not mutually exclusive, that may explain these findings are (i) that RNA expression levels upon IPTG addition are not uniform across the different plasmid clones, (ii) that initiation of R-loop formation (as opposed to R-loop extension) occurs at specific sequences (147), and that plasmid toxicity occurs when sites of such R-loop initiation are cloned and expressed, or (iii) that the end point of cell lethality used in the assay represents a high threshold for R-loop detection, and that some antisense regions that also generate R-loops fail to meet this threshold level.

Our studies have shown that two other bacteria X. oryzae pv. oryzae and C. crescentus are sensitive to the Rho inhibitor antibiotic BCM, and that R-loop prevalence in them is increased in the presence of sublethal BCM just as is the case in E. coli (30). For these organisms as well, we believe that at least some of the identified antisense regions with high-percentile bisulfite reactivity would represent sites of R-loop formation, which was indeed confirmed for one such locus after its cloning and expression in plasmid pTrc99a.

Escape replication of defective prophages and RecA-dependent cell viability upon Ptac-directed chromosomal expression of R-loop prone antisense regions

The mechanisms underlying escape replication of defective prophages remain to be fully understood. To our knowledge, this phenomenon has been described in E. coli in just two situations that are both in some manner related to perturbations of Rho action. The first is upon Rho inhibition by addition of BCM to wild-type cultures [(37), and this study]. The second is upon Ptac-directed chromosomal overexpression of category 1 antisense loci such as AS_b2459 and AS_b0397, whose synthesis is otherwise ordinarily curtailed by Rho; engineered overexpression from these two chromosomal antisense loci was associated also with RecA dependence for cell viability. Upon RNase HI overexpression in the strain with antisense transcription of the AS_b2459 chromosomal locus, cell viability in the absence of RecA was restored but prophage escape replication did persist. It is also known that an E. coli strain with all its resident prophages deleted is more tolerant than the wild type to loss of Rho function (36,40,44). We propose the following model to explain these findings.

We suggest that the fundamental difference between the two categories of antisense loci whose transcription is provoked in Rho-deficient cells is that it is only category 1 (which includes AS_b2459 and AS_0397) that is susceptible to R-loop formation associated with translation-uncoupled RNAP backtracking and arrest (41,65,66,148). This then leads in cis to serious transcription–replication conflict and the need for RecA-mediated recombinational repair to maintain cell viability.

We further propose that escape replication of defective prophages is an event that is triggered in trans upon expression of these category 1 antisense loci. The phenomenon is apparently an accompaniment to, but independent of, R-loop formation, and the signal linking such antisense expression to escape replication remains to be identified.

According to our model, when RNase HI is overexpressed in cells with Ptac-directed expression of AS_b2459, R-loops associated with the arrested transcription elongation complex are removed so that transcription–replication conflict is mitigated. On the other hand, antisense RNA expression persists so that the postulated signal for prophage escape replication continues to be generated.

One distinct possibility is that it is SOS induction that contributes to defective prophage escape replication. First, it is likely that the occurrence of backtracked RNAP, R-loops and transcription–replication conflict associated with genomic antisense integration (at category 1 loci) leads to generation of RecA-bound ssDNA that would trigger the bacterial SOS response (149). Second, prophage repressor cleavage as part of the SOS response might then be responsible for initiating the positive feedback loops and all-or-none kinetics of prophage induction (131) leading to escape replication. If such prophage induction were to be triggered stochastically in only a subpopulation of cells in a culture (97), it might explain why different patterns of escape replication were obtained even in replicate cultures of the category 1 antisense integrants although the underlying signal was common for all of them. At the same time, it should be noted that SOS induction by itself cannot be sufficient for exhibition of the phenomenon of escape replication, since we have earlier performed similar DNA copy number analyses under other conditions that cause SOS induction without observing evidence for escape replication; these conditions have included chromosome cleavage by I-SceI (104,150), or the presence of mutations in topA (62) or dam (95).

Disruption of genome integrity through site-specific R-loop formation on the chromosome

Co-transcriptional R-loop formation has previously been implicated as an obstacle to DNA replication and as a threat to genome integrity in both bacteria and eukaryotic cells (45,55,56,58,118,146,151–153). It would appear that both co-directional and head-on transcription–replication conflict associated with R-loop formation can affect genome integrity (41,55,56,71,123,124,154), which is supported in this study as well. Of the two recombineered antisense loci, one is expected to generate head-on (AS_b2459) and the other co-directional (AS_b0397) transcription–replication conflict.

The ability to generate site-specific DNA damage on the bacterial chromosome has been useful in experimental studies on the mechanisms both of damage generation and of its repair. Previously described methods for this purpose have included placement of I-SceI sites (104,150), ectopic tus sites (88), a palindrome sequence for cleavage by SbcCD (89) or sites for binding of LacI or TetR protein arrays (86,87). We suggest that the recombineered antisense R-looped loci generated in this work may also serve as suitable tools for use in such studies.
