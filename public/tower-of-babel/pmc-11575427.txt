Fusion of multiple self-diagnostic questionnaires into optimal diagnostic cut-offs and factor analysis for depression characterization of the Korean university student group
Authors: Soojin Lee, Sukhan Lee, Jungsun Lee, Young Tak Jo, Eunil Park, Junyeop Cha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe were interested in developing a methodology for diagnosing the depression status of a focused population group, such as the Korean university student group, with higher accuracy. To this end, we proposed a method of fusing the data collected from multiple depression self-questionnaires aided by a psychiatrist’s diagnosis. In particular, we found that the standard diagnostic cut-offs and factor analysis prepared for a general population by depression self-questionnaires are inadequate for a focused population with its unique cultural background. In this study, a novel approach to optimizing diagnostic cut-offs and generalizing factor analysis for the Korean university student group is presented in the fusion space of multiple self-questionnaires.MethodsWe collected the data from 30 randomly selected Korean university students, over 21 weeks, with the psychiatric evaluation as a reference, then established the optimal cut-off regions in the fused CESD − PHQ9 score space based on the statistical correlation between CES − D and PHQ − 9 and the reference diagnostics. We also re-extracted the factors in the fused CESD − PHQ9 space to expose the key factors that are behind the depression characteristics of the group.ResultsWe verified the existence of a clear correlation between CES − D and PHQ − 9 scores. However, the standard cut-offs of CES − D and PHQ − 9 are found inconsistent with the correlation. The new cut-off regions we obtained in the fused CESD − PHQ9 score space are consistent with the correlation and optimal for the psychiatrist’s diagnosis with the sensitivity and specificity of 80.95% and 89.74%, respectively. Also, we identified that “socio-psychological” and “interpersonal relationship” factors are the major factors for the depression characteristics of the group.LimitationsAlthough the new cut-off regions we presented were based on the incorporation of clinical diagnosis into the fused CESD − PHQ9 score space, further verification with a larger scale of clinical data is helpful.ConclusionWe identified optimal cut-off regions and generalized factor analysis in the fusion space, which can provide more reliable and trustworthy diagnoses. These can serve as a self-diagnostic tool for reliably identifying the depression characteristics of a focused population as well as effectively linking individuals and psychiatrists as an intermediary.Keywords: Korean university student group, Multiple self-questionnaires, CES − D, PHQ − 9, Newly defined cut-off regions, Factor analysis

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of people suffering from depression is about 280 million, which is about 3.8% of the world population. Although it is a relatively common disease compared to other diseases worldwide, if not treated promptly, depression can lead to serious neurological or suicidal problems [1]. According to the recent survey by the Korea Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (KHRA) on the status of depression treatment over the past five years (2017 − 2021), among all age groups, the number of depression patients in the 20s age group increased the most. For example, compared to an overall average annual increase rate of 22.8% between 2017 and 2021, the increase rate of the 20s age group was 127.1%. In fact, the number of depression patients in their 20s was 177,166, comprising 19% of all depression patients in Korea.

Depression is a common mental disorder with a high incidence rate. If it is not detected early or treated properly, the severity of depression can increase and significantly impact daily life. Delay in the diagnosis and treatment of depression increases the risk of recurrence and prolongs the duration of symptoms [2, 3]. In addition, delayed diagnosis and treatment of depression increases the risk of suicide, which is approximately 20 times higher than the risk in the general population [4]. Depression affects both mental health and physical health [5–7]. Depression also significantly impacts daily and social activities, with people with depression reporting lower work productivity and significantly higher rates of absence from work [6, 8, 9].

Especially in populations with low rates of self-reporting of depression, patients are less likely to be self-aware of their current condition. In addition, when patients with depression visit a hospital and meet with a psychiatrist, the accuracy of their diagnosis may be influenced by the patient’s behavior, such as hiding or exaggerating their conditions and symptoms. Experts indicate that self-questionnaires that can be frequently recorded at regular and short intervals in the patient’s daily life may be more desirable for effective diagnosis. As previous studies have shown [10–13], an important issue regarding self-questionnaires is that the diagnostic cut-offs should be set differently among different target populations. Another critical issue is that different self-questionnaires end up with different diagnostic results for the same person. It has been shown that linking multiple questionnaires based on their correlations does not always lead to consistent cut-offs in diagnostic decisions. We propose to solve the above two issues by fusing multiple questionnaires that provide consistency in diagnostic decisions and generality in factor analysis in a population-optimized and psychiatrist-trusted way.

There have been several studies related to depression associated with a particular group, including the university student group. In a study of Korean university students, Cha et al. [14] investigated the mental health status of nursing school students in comparison with general university students in terms of stress, depression, and suicidal ideation. They used the Center for Epidemiologic Studies of Depression (CES − D) [15] to measure depression and found that the average total scores have no statistically significant difference between the two groups. Ji et al. [16] studied the correlation of self-esteem and social support with depression scores from CES − D using university students as subjects. They found that both self-esteem and social support are strongly correlated with depression scores, where social support plays a more significant role. Kim [17] collected the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ − 9) [18] data together with the customized questionnaires related to psychological, environmental, and behavioral factors from a large number of university students to analyze the depression level and factors of the Korean university student group. He found that 19.6% of the subjects require psychiatric treatment for depression, indicating that the depression status among Korean university students is more significant than expected. Na et al. [19] made the Korean version of the Activation for Depression Scale (K − BADS) and validated its effectiveness by studying the correlations of K − BADS with a number of existing questionnaires, such as the PHQ − 9, CES − D, and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD − 7) [20], which were simultaneously collected from a university student population. They verified that K − BADS correlated well with the existing questionnaires, either fully or partially.

Several self-questionnaire studies of depression in other countries have focused on university student groups. Most of these studies translated depression self-questionnaires into their languages to determine if self-questionnaires are valid for university student groups. Adewuya et al. [21] evaluated the validity of the PHQ − 9 and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [22] in a Nigerian university student group. They also assessed the clinical diagnosis through the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) to validate the collected PHQ − 9 and BDI. They found that the PHQ − 9 would be a useful tool for estimating depression among university students in the Nigerian community. Jankowski [23] collected the CES − D and the Composite Scale of Morningness (CSM) [24] in a Polish university student group and analyzed the correlation between morningness and depressive symptoms. Using PCA, they categorized the CES − D into depressive/physical, positive, and interpersonal factors and the CSM into morning affect and circadian preference factors. They found that lower scores of CSM are associated with stronger eveningness, and in particular, lower scores of morning affect tend to be associated with higher CES − D total scores. Suh et al. [25] collected the CES − D−10 [26] from an international university student group in the United States to assess its factor structure and measurement invariance. They proposed a more concise, seven-item, single-factor form of the CES − D (the CES − D Short-form International), specific to international students and can simply diagnose their psychological distress.

Although many correlation studies have been conducted to validate self-questionnaires for their effectiveness as a self-diagnostic tool for depression, little progress has been made on fusing multiple self-questionnaires for possible improvement in diagnosis beyond what individual self-questionnaires can offer. Linking is considered a simplistic approach to fusion, which transforms the scores of one questionnaire into those of another based on the correlation between the two. For example, Choi et al. [10]. developed a cross-walk table based on linking the scores of BDI − II, CES − D, and PHQ − 9 with the t-scores of the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) [27] developed by the National Institutes of Health (NIH). In Ref. [10] , they attempted to link BDI − II, CES − D, and PHQ − 9 with PROMIS with high precision. They found significant correlations between PROMIS scores measured directly in the experiment and those transformed from the BDI − II, CES − D, and PHQ − 9. In addition, several linking studies transform the scores from depression self-questionnaires to PROMIS [11–13]. However, instead of exploiting the information reinforced by multiple questionnaires, linking concerns about the transformation of scores can improve diagnostic decisions and factor analysis. As far as fusion is concerned, Hueniken et al. [28] combined multiple self-questionnaires, CES − D and GAD − 7, into one by selecting important items from the two while transforming the selected items into questionnaires probing the conditions affecting the psychological state of the subject, represented as the values of Shapely Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [29], based on a machine learning approach, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [30]. This approach is mostly concerned with identifying important and independent items from multiple questionnaires to transform their scores into those supplementary to the diagnosis. However, it is limited to elevating fusion into an improvement in the diagnostic decision and factor analysis as a self-diagnostic tool, as exemplified in the following.

References [10–13] raised an issue regarding the completion of multiple depression self-questionnaires by the same individual at the same time, resulting in different diagnoses on each self-questionnaire. We also found the same issue shown in Fig. 1, in which the interval where CES − D and PHQ − 9 are in the same diagnostic level is colored yellow. At the same time, the CES − D and PHQ − 9 values for the Korean university student group are represented by gray color. The results of the two self-questionnaires are not significantly different between the clinical criteria and the Korean university student group distribution for levels 1 (not depressed) and 2 (mild depressed). However, for levels 3 (moderate) and 4 (severe), the clinical criteria and the Korean university student group distribution differ. The PHQ − 9 tends to be diagnosed lower than the CES − D in these levels. Even though the CES − D and PHQ − 9 were measured simultaneously, the results of each self-questionnaire were diagnosed at different levels. This difference in diagnosis can lead to confusion in the decision of depression status, also leaving the person in a dilemma about which self-questionnaire to trust ultimately.

Differences between the clinical diagnostic criteria of CES − D and PHQ − 9 and the diagnosis results in a Korean university student group

In this paper, we propose a novel approach to fusing multiple questionnaires, CES − D and PHQ − 9, that define new cut-offs and major factors in the fusion space pertinent to a particular group. The newly defined cut-offs and factors are consistent for multiple questionnaires and trustworthy with psychiatrists. Based on the proposed fusion approach, we identify the characteristics of the depression status of the Korean university student group. Specifically, a set of new, more generalized, major factors are derived by fusing CES − D and PHQ − 9 questionnaires, by which a specialized factor structure for the Korean university student group is identified. In addition, in the 2D fusion space formed by CES − D and PHQ − 9 scores, four cut-off regions are defined for diagnostic decisions based on the correlation between CES-D and PHQ − 9 scores as well as their variances together with the input from psychiatrists. The newly established cut-off regions can serve as a self-diagnostic tool, particularly for the Korean university student group, as an intermediary diagnosis prior to psychiatric treatment. In addition, we analyzed the samples that belong to individual cut-off regions based on newly defined major factors to identify the depression characteristics of the Korean university student group. We found that “socio-psychological,” “interpersonal relationship,” and “sleep” are the three major factors characterizing the depression status of the Korean university student group. To verify the validity of the identified depression characteristics, we analyzed subjects’ emotional states based on the sentiment statement data of the subjects collected simultaneously with the questionnaires on a weekly basis. A mental health self-monitoring platform for the general population has emerged as crucial, for which self-collected non-biased data from everyday lives for a prolonged period of time play an important role as an intermediary to link to psychiatrists prior to psychiatric screening and therapy. The proposed optimal diagnostic cut-offs and factor analysis based on multiple self-questionnaires could contribute to advancing self-monitoring platforms with more accurate and reliable self-diagnosis as an intermediary.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Sungkyunkwan University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB no. SKKU 2022-01-017).

Recruitment

The Korean university student group in this paper consists of 30 university students in the group of 20s who are enrolled at Sungkyunkwan University in South Korea. They applied through a recruitment announcement posted on the school’s website, and 30 subjects were randomly selected from 187 applicants. Table 1 below shows the attribute analysis for the subject group. The selected 30 university students submitted their data online by completing the CES − D and PHQ − 9 via “Google Forms” every Monday (once a week) from 11 July 2022 to 30 November 2022. The data collected from the 30 subjects for 21 weeks amounted to 586 for CES − D and PHQ − 9, excluding 44 non-responses. In addition to CES − D and PHQ − 9 data, to collect ground truth data, in the 21st week, a psychiatrist interviewed 21 subjects to have the clinical diagnoses of their depression status.

Questionnaires

In this paper, the CES − D and PHQ − 9 self-report questionnaires were used to explore depression in a Korean university student group.

Center of epidemiological studies-depression scale (CES − D)

The CES − D [15] is a self-questionnaire developed by Laurie Radloff in 1977 that was developed for epidemiologic studies rather than clinical use but is now also used in primary care and research. It consists of 20 questions about the daily life of the past week, with each question having a scale of (0 to 3) points. The most notable feature of CES − D is that it contains 4 positive reverse-transformation questions. The CES − D diagnoses the current state through a total score of 20 questions, where (0 − 15) is not depressed, (16 − 20) is mild depression, (21 − 24) is moderate depression, and (25 − 60) is severe depression. The sensitivity and specificity for the CES − D are 95.1% and 85.0%, respectively, when the standard cut-off is 16 or higher. Many versions have been developed since the CES − D has been used for a long time. These include the Center of Epidemiological Studies-Depression Scale for Children (CES − DC) [31], which consists of child-appropriate items to screen for depression in children; the CES − D−10 [25], which is an abbreviated version of the 20-item CES − D with 10 items; and the CESD − R [32], a revised version of the CES − D that is more clinically relevant than the CES − D and that includes anhedonia, psychomotor agitation or retardation, and suicidal ideation.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ − 9)

The PHQ − 9 [18] is a self-questionnaire developed in 2001 that measures depression in adult patients in primary care settings. The main feature of the PHQ − 9 is that the questions are based on the nine diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders − 5 (DSM − 5) [33], which is the current standard for diagnosing mental health. Therefore, unlike the BDI − II and CES − D, it includes questions related to suicide. It consists of 9 questions, each with a scale of (0 to 3) points. The PHQ − 9 responses are for the daily life of the past 2 weeks. It also diagnoses the current depression state based on the total score, where (0 − 4) is not depressed, (5 − 9) is mild, (10 − 14) is moderate, (15 − 19) is moderately severe, and (20 − 27) is severe. Reference [18] shows that the PHQ − 9 is widely used in primary care and has good sensitivity (88%) and specificity (88%) compared to other self-questionnaires; also, the number of questions is relatively small so that it can be used efficiently in clinical practice. Since its development, the PHQ − 9 has been expanded into many versions. These include the PHQ − 2 [34], which initially screens for depression with only two questions about depressed mood and anhedonia out of the original nine questions; the PHQ − 8 [35], which screens for depression with eight questions, excluding the item assessing suicidal ideation; and the PHQ − A(Adolescents) [36], which adapts the PHQ − 9 to assess depression in adolescents.

Statistical analysis for self-questionnaire

The statistical analysis of the collected data of CES − D and PHQ − 9 of Korean university students was performed by using “pandas 1.3.5”, “numpy 1.21.6”, and “factor-analyzer 0.4.1” libraries in Python 3.7.15 environment. Before factor analysis, Bartlett’s and Kaiser − Meyer − Olkin (KMO) tests were performed on the collected data to verify whether the data were suitable for factor analysis. The number of factors was selected by measuring the eigenvalue of each factor in advance and selecting the number of factors with a value of 1 or more, while Varimax was used as a rotation method. In addition, the validity of the selected model was verified through the “convergent validity indicator” and “discriminant validity index”.

Newly defined cut-off regions based on fusing CES − D and PHQ − 9

However, as introduced in the Introduction section, when multiple self-questionnaires are used and completed by the same person simultaneously, different self-questionnaires result in different diagnostic results when their standard cut-off metrics are used for diagnosis. As illustrated in Fig. 1, inconsistent diagnostic results were observed when we used the CES − D and PHQ − 9 for the Korean university student group. In this study, we proposed to fuse the data from multiple questionnaires to derive a consistent result from multiple questionnaires together with trustworthiness with psychiatrists. Specifically, we fused the two self-questionnaire scores in the CESD − PHQ9 score space by applying the data fusion approach of the Covariance Projection Filter (CPF) [37]. The fusion of the CES − D and PHQ − 9 scores, x1 and x2, respectively, takes place in the CESD − PHQ9 score space, x, x=x1,x2T, in such a way that x is projected onto the constraint manifold, Mx, defined as the functional relationship between x1 and x2. The projection onto Mx is intended to find the maximum probability point, x*, of x, that satisfies the constraint, Mx, between x1 and x2 under the uncertainty associated with x. The uncertainty of x is represented as the probability distribution around x, x=x1,x2T, based on the respective statistical variances of x1 and x2. Here, the constraint manifold, Mx, is defined in the fusion space as the correlation line between x1 and x2. Notably, the correlation line obtained between CES-D and PHQ-9 data, as illustrated by the red lines in Figs. 2 and 3, shows a strong Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.8765. On the other hand, the probability distribution of x is defined as a 2D Gaussian distribution with its mean of x and variance of σx1200σx22, where the standard deviations, σx1 and σx2, of x1 and x2, respectively (refer to the ellipses in Fig. 2), were obtained by the respective CES-D and PHQ-9 data collected from individual subjects. The maximum probability point, x*, on Mx for the given x is then the point at which the uncertainty ellipsoid of x meets with Mx in terms of a tangent line (refer to Fig. 2). Based on the above data fusion approach, we established the new cut-off regions based on the 21 pairs of reference CESD−PHQ9 scores for which the psychiatrist diagnoses were available, and the uncertainties associated with CESD−PHQ9 scores from individual subjects. Specifically, we selected the boundary cut-off points from 21 pairs of the reference CESD − PHQ9 scores in such a way as to maximize the sensitivity and specificity of psychiatrist diagnoses and drew the projection lines from the selected boundary points based on the uncertainty ellipsoid associated with the CESD − PHQ9 scores of individual subjects (refer to the dashed lines in Fig. 3). The uncertainty ellipsoid was defined based on the mean standard deviations of CES−D (4.9135) and PHQ−9 (2.1068) scores, which were obtained by averaging the standard deviations of 21-week data from individual subjects. Figure 2 shows the maximum probability points on the correlation line and the associated projection lines for the selected boundary points (marked yellow) and the rest of the 21 pairs of reference CESD−PHQ9 scores (marked blue). Refer to Fig. 3 for the resulting cut-off points on the correlation line as well as the corresponding cut-off regions in the entire CESD−PHQ9 fusion space.

Results

Statistical analysis of the CES − D and PHQ − 9 questionnaire scores

We validated the effectiveness of the sample size collected from 30 subjects. Due to the exponentially decreasing nature of CES − D and PHQ − 9 sample distributions along scores, we adopted a non-parametric null-hypothesis test using the two-sample Kolmogorov − Smirnov test (K − S test). For the two-sample K − S test, we adopted the reference CES − D and PHQ − 9 sample distributions available in the literature that are based on a large scale of data collected from the general population: (1) a CES − D data set collected in Japan with 21,040 subjects [38], and (2) a PHQ − 9 data set collected in the United States with 5,372 subjects [39]. By setting the confidence level of 95% (α=0.05) and the sample size of 30 (n=30), we obtained the critical value of 0.24710 from the K−S value table. On the other hand, we obtained the supremum function value representing the difference between the two sample distributions as 0.1666 for CES−D and 0.1818 for PHQ−9. Since both 0.1666 for CES−D and 0.1818 for PHQ−9 are less than the critical value, the null hypothesis is accepted, implying that the sample size of CES−D and PHQ−9 that we collected with 30 subjects for 21 weeks is effective.

In addition, we analyzed the effect of weekly-repeated sample data on the required sample size based on the sample size metric, E=σN, where E, σ, and N are the error of mean estimate, the sample standard deviation, and the sample size, respectively. The sample size, N, is determined in such a way that E is within 95% of statistical confidence and can be reduced when the sample standard deviation, σ, is reduced for the same E. We recognized that multiple self-questionnaire score data collected weekly repeatedly from the same subject could reduce the standard deviation, σ, involved in the collected samples, as proven by the data fusion principle, resulting in an effect of increased sample size. For instance, a numerical calculation of the impact of repeated samples on σ and, consequently, N for the same E indicates that σ can effectively be reduced by 0.7981 ~ 0.3604 while N can effectively be increased by 55 ~ 234, in case 2 ~ 5 weeks of samples are fused for 30 subjects individually.

Newly defined cut-off regions in the fused CESD − PHQ9 score space

Figure 3 shows the results of re-diagnosing 586 pairs of the (CES − D and PHQ − 9) data collected using the newly defined cut-off regions. In the figure, the black dashed line is the newly defined cut-off regions in the Fused CESD − PHQ9 score space, which score space is defined as level 1, level 2, level 3, and level 4 from the left. In our experiments, we observed a notable frequency of two diagnoses that displayed dissimilar levels of difference in the standard diagnosis results. This frequency comprised a 25.98% difference at level 1 and a 3.75% difference at level 2. However, the biggest feature of the newly defined cut-off is that part of level 2 and all levels 3 and 4 are included for the level 4 cut-off region of CES − D, while the level 2 and 3 cut-off regions are merged into one to be defined as level 2. In PHQ − 9, if the CES − D has a relatively high score in the level 1 cut-off, it is defined as level 2, in level 3, if the CES − D score is relatively higher than that of PHQ − 9, it is defined as level 4.

The cut-off lines dividing cut-off regions are determined so that the sensitivity and specificity associated with the ground truth data provided by a psychiatrist become maximum, where the psychiatrist interviewed 21 subjects to obtain the ground truth diagnostic data. The resulting cut-off lines provide a sensitivity of 80.95% and a specificity of 89.74% with the psychiatrist’s ground truth diagnoses.

Factor analysis from CES − D, PHQ − 9, and in the fused CESD − PHQ9 questionnaire space

The biggest feature of the CES − D and PHQ − 9 data collected through this experiment is that the two self-questionnaires were collected simultaneously on the same day. Taking advantage of the simultaneous collection, we performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-based factor analysis on the CES − D and PHQ − 9 and the Fused CESD − PHQ9. The Fused CESD − PHQ9 uses 20 CES − D items and 9 PHQ-9 items, for a total of 29 items. Before factor analysis, Bartlett’s and KMO tests were performed on the collected data to verify whether the data were suitable for factor analysis. Table 4 confirms that Bartlett’s test and KMO test values for CES − D, PHQ − 9, and Fused CESD − PHQ9 data are suitable for factor analysis. The results of Bartlett’s Test indicated a significant correlation between the variables (p < 0.001) for the CES − D, PHQ − 9, and Fused CESD − PHQ9 data. The KMO values for the CES − D, PHQ − 9, and Fused CESD − PHQ9 were 0.93, 0.89, and 0.94, respectively.

For the 586 CES − D data collected, four factors had an eigenvalue of 1 or higher. After performing factor analysis with the number of factors set to four, Fig. 4a displays the correlation coefficient between each question and the factor. The factor with the highest correlation coefficient is selected for each question; Factor I is items 4, 8, 12, and 16; Factor II is items 5, 7, 9, and 20; Factor III is items 1, 2, 11, 13, 15, and 19, and Factor IV is items 3, 6, 10, 14, 17, and 18. Analyzing newly developed cut-off regions and factors with weekly sentiment description data

Based on the collected CES − D and PHQ − 9 data of the Korean university student group, we defined a major trend line and a correlation between the two and defined newly developed cut-off regions by linking the diagnosis results of psychiatrists from a Fused CESD − PHQ9 perspective.

For a more accurate analysis of the differences and distribution among the six-factor space values, we normalized the factor space values using the eigenvalue of each factor and the number of CES − D and PHQ − 9 items belonging to the factor. For each factor space value of the data, we divided it by the eigenvalue of the corresponding factor and then normalized it by dividing it once more, using the eigenvalue divided by the number of items. This removes the differences in the size of the eigenvalue and the number of items between each factor so that comparisons can be made between factor values under the same conditions.

Figure 7a & b plot a graph representing the normalized factor space value distribution, where Fig. 7a shows the sample distribution of each factor for the 544 samples that belong to the levels 1 and 2 defined by the newly established cut-off regions, while Fig. 7b shows the sample distribution of each factor for the 42 samples that belong to the levels 3 and 4. For each data sample, we divided the normalized factor value into three bins, 0, 1, and 2, depending on whether the value is < − 1 for Bin 0 or > 1 for Bin 2. as illustrated in Fig. 8c. If a sample belongs to Bin 0, Bin 1, and Bin 2, the scores of the self-questionnaire items that correspond to a particular factor are usually (0 or 1), (1 or 2), and (2 or 3), respectively. By analyzing Fig. 7a & b, we can extract the factors that significantly affect the severe and moderate depression status of the Korean university student group. To this end, we observed the ratio between the percentile of Bin 2 for a particular factor in the normal and mild depression samples and the percentile in the case of the moderate and severe depression samples. Specifically, from Fig. 7a & b, we have the percentile ratios of the “socio-psychological,” “interpersonal relationship,” “sleep,” and “depression and appetite” factors as 52/7, 50/13, 60/19, and 60/23, respectively, in decreasing order. This indicates that “socio-psychological” and “interpersonal relationship” factors tend to more significantly affect the depression status of the Korean university student group.

Discussion

Newly developed cut-off regions in the fused CESD − PHQ9 space

In this paper, we found that the trends of the Korean university student group and the criteria for clinical diagnosis were different for both the CES − D and PHQ − 9. Figure 8 shows the 586 pairs of CES-D and PHQ-9 collected in the fused CESD − PHQ9 space. In Fig. 8, the green graph connects the clinical diagnosis levels (1 − 1, 2 − 2, 3 − 3, and 4 − 4) of CES − D and PHQ − 9, and the slope of the (2 − 2 and 3 − 3) levels of the green graph appears to change rapidly. On the other hand, the red graph represents the correlation line for the collected 586 pairs of Korean university student group data, and there is the greatest difference in the 2 − 2 and 3 − 3 levels compared to the clinical diagnosis graph (green). In particular, from the midpoint of the 2 − 2 level, the correlation line of the collected data and the clinical diagnosis graph gradually diverge. Because of this difference, clinical diagnosis levels of the CES − D and PHQ − 9 that are not the same frequently occur. The PHQ − 9 tends to be diagnosed as a lower level than the CES − D; for example, most subjects diagnosed as level 4 (severe) in the CES − D are diagnosed in the PHQ − 9 as level 3 (moderate). When using multiple self-questionnaires, such a discrepancy in diagnostic results between self-questionnaires can confuse users.

Based on this analysis, we present newly defined cut-off regions of the Fused CESD − PHQ9 results by applying a formula that links the data fusion concept of CES − D and PHQ − 9 to the results of the psychiatrists’ diagnoses. Through the newly defined cut-off of the Fused CESD − PHQ9, more trustworthy diagnosis results can be made when the CES − D and PHQ − 9 are used simultaneously, targeting the Korean university student group, and it is expected that it will be much easier for psychiatrists to evaluate the two results together. Furthermore, association with the factor analysis of the Korean university student group could provide psychiatrists with the results of a specialized factor analysis for the Korean university student group.

Caution is warranted when changing crucial metrics, such as the diagnostic cut-offs from the standard. However, we found that the CES − D and PHQ − 9 questionnaires collected simultaneously resulted in inconsistent diagnoses for the same individuals when their standard cut-off metrics were used, although the collected CES − D and PHQ − 9 scores show a well-defined correlation. This led us to develop new cut-off regions in the fused CESD − PHQ9 space to obtain consistent yet more accurate diagnoses by fusing the two data sources. The inconsistency in diagnoses by the standard cut-off metrics of CES − D and PHQ − 9 may be attributed to the fact that the population group, the Korean university student group studied here, is rather focused in terms of its unique environmental and cultural background different from the general population. The proposed methodology of extending cut-off points into cut-off regions in the fused questionnaire score space can contribute to a more reliable and trustworthy diagnosis, particularly for focused population groups. Note that psychiatric diagnoses are subject to certain uncertainties with limited interviews such that caution is warranted in utilizing diagnostic interviews with psychiatrists as a gold standard.

Factor analysis

Factor analysis was conducted on the CES − D, PHQ − 9, and Fused CESD − PHQ9 with the factor − item relationships of their own. Note that the factor − item relationships identified here are not equal to the factor-item relationships reported previously for a general population [40–44] while carrying a unique feature for the Korean university student group. Also, the factor − item relationships from the Fused CESD − PHQ9 are not a simple combination of those from the CES − D and PHQ − 9 but rather a new configuration out of the combination of the CES − D and PHQ − 9 items.

As presented, our CES − D factor analysis identifies the four factors, “positive emotion,” “lethargic,” “physical/behavioral and interpersonal relationships,” and “depression,” in decreasing order of importance. This differs from the CESD − based factor analysis applied previously to a general Korean population [40–43] as “depression,” “positive emotion,” “physical/behavioral,” and “interpersonal relationship,” in decreasing order of importance. The two factors that differ are configured with different factor − item relationships. Our CES − D factor analysis indicates that the items corresponding to the “positive emotion” factor account most significantly for the depression characteristics in the Korean university student group. We consider that this is due to the tendency of Asians to rate their happiness and pleasure rather lower than other cultures. The second major CES − D factor is “lethargic”, which represents drained feelings due to concentration and work difficulties, as well as problem-solving disability or failure, related mostly to the student’s academic studies and employment. Notably, when we identified the factors that account for the depression characteristics of the moderate/severe levels in the same way as we did in the Fused CESD − PHQ9 space (refer to Fig. 7), we found the “lethargic” factor to be the most sensitive in the moderate/severe group. Specifically, in Bin 2 of the “lethargic” factor, the moderate/severe group has a percentile ratio of 79/14 in terms of the normal/mild group.

In the case of the PHQ − 9, two factors, “physical /emotional” and “socio-psychological”, in decreasing order of importance, are identified, while a previous study [44] reported the two factors for a general Korean population, “socio-psychological” and “physical/physiological”, in decreasing order of importance. Note that although the factors from both sources sound similar, their factor − item relationships are not equal. In our finding, items corresponding to depressive mood and physical items were combined into “physical/emotional,” and failure/pessimistic thoughts and suicidal thoughts into “socio-psychological.” While the PHQ − 9 factor analysis provides the decreasing order of importance of “physical/emotional” and “socio-psychological”, we further analyzed the data based on “the percentile ratio” presented above to identify the key factor that is behind the depression characteristics of the moderate/severe cases. As the results, we found that the “socio-psychological” factor is highly sensitive to the moderate/severe group with a percentile ratio of 52/4 in terms of the normal/mild group, whereas the “physical/emotional” factor turned out to be insensitive, with the percentile ratio of 100/100.

The factor analysis in the Fused CESD − PHQ9 questionnaire space results in six factors, “lethargic, physical/emotional,” “socio-psychological,” “depression and appetite,” “positive emotion,” “sleep,” and “interpersonal relationship,” in decreasing order of importance. Note that the factors from the Fused CESD − PHQ9 space are not a simple combination of the CES − D and PHQ − 9 factors but are formed uniquely by the factor − item relationships of their own that represent the depression features associated with the Korean university student group in a more general way. For example, the “lethargic, physical/emotional” factor from the Fused CESD − PHQ9 combines the second factor of CES − D, “lethargic,” with the first factor of PHQ − 9, “physical/emotional.” The “socio-psychological” factor is related to such items as failure, fear, depression, and suicide, which are more comprehensive than those of the PHQ − 9. Moreover, the “depression and appetite,” “sleep,” and “interpersonal relationship” factors in the Fused CESD − PHQ9 have their factor − item relationships formed independently of the CES − D and PHQ − 9 factors.

As shown in Fig. 7, the analysis of the sensitivity associated with individual factors, in particular to the moderate/severe levels, indicates the change of the initial decreasing order of importance into the following: “socio-psychological,” “interpersonal relationships,” “sleep,” and “depression and appetite.” This may show the unique feature of the depression characteristics of the Korean university student group, namely, the influence of stress related to academic studies and employment on the moderate/severe level of depression among the student group. We interpreted this result as follows: Students in their 20s in Korea go through 12 years of basic education from the ages of 8 to 19 and then attend university for at least two more years. However, even after completing all educational courses, Korean university students are still required to study extra courses and take part in external activities to qualify for higher employment standards. According to a survey by Statistics Korea, the number of employed Koreans in their 20s has plummeted to 3,833,000 as of June 2023. As a result of the decline in employment, Korean university students are feeling failure and disappointment and experiencing insomnia due to worries about employment and study difficulties with a fear of an uncertain future. In addition, they are physically tired due to their excessive daily routine for employment and studies.

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a novel method for statistically fusing multiple self-diagnostic questionnaires, aided by diagnostic input from the psychiatrists, into more accurately defined cut-off regions in the fused score spaces and more generalized analyses of factors in the fused questionnaire space. The proposed method could effectively address the inconsistency among the diagnostic cut-offs from the individual questionnaires that appear when multiple questionnaires are collected simultaneously from the same individuals. This inconsistency is expected to be a lot more significant when dealing with a more focused population with a unique cultural background, as we verified in our experiment of characterizing the depression status of the Korean university student group with the simultaneously collected CES − D and PHQ − 9 data on a long-term basis.

On the other hand, the factor analysis conducted in the fused questionnaire revealed that novel factors could emerge with unique factor − item relationships pertinent to the focused population that are different from those obtained separately from individual questionnaires. Consequently, based on the factor analysis in the fused space and applying the proposed percentile ratio analysis, we could identify that “socio-psychological” and “interpersonal relationship” are the most significant factors related to moderate/severe depression cases for the Korean university student group. As a result, we identified the generalized characteristics of depression in the Korean university student group, in particular, stress from academic studies and employment, with feelings of helplessness, failure, and pessimism.

Finally, the proposed method can offer a more accurate and trustworthy self-diagnosis that can serve as an effective intermediary interface between the population and psychiatrists. In particular, we can interpret the major factors currently contributing to the subject’s depressive symptoms with the newly defined cut-off score and provide this factor information as supplemental data to psychiatrists for clinical diagnosis. In the future, we plan to extend the application of the proposed approach to other mental disorder categories, including anxiety and psychotic disorders. In addition, we plan to evaluate the proposed approach in terms of the clinical value of an intermediary interface between the population and psychiatrists.
