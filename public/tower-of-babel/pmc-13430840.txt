From two to one: spatiotemporal mapping of chromatin remodelling in the protamine-lacking early zygote of zebrafish
Authors: Eleonora Pustovalova, Roman Franěk, Essaikiammal Sodalai Muthu Konar, Ondrej Šanovec, Michaela Frolíková, Martin Pšenička, Katerina Komrsková, Dmitrij Dedukh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAt fertilization, two highly specialized gametic genomes must rapidly reprogram into a single totipotent nucleus. In mammals, this transformation is marked by a dramatic protamine-to-histone exchange and pronounced asymmetry between the parental pronuclei. Zebrafish offer a contrasting vertebrate model in which sperm chromatin is already organized in nucleosomes and carries both active and repressive histone modifications, eliminating the need for protamine replacement. This histone-based configuration provides a unique opportunity to examine how parental chromatin transitions are initiated and coordinated directly in vivo. Although zebrafish is a well-established model organism in developmental biology, the detailed cellular and molecular steps of its fertilization process, particularly the timing and coordination of parental chromatin remodelling, remain largely unknown.ResultsUsing immunofluorescence coupled with spinning-disk confocal microscopy, we mapped histone variants and post-translational modifications with temporal sampling at minute-level intervals across 3,549 zebrafish embryos from fertilization to the first cleavage. This analysis resolved the main steps of fertilization, from sperm entry and chromatin decondensation to the formation, expansion, and apposition of the parental pronuclei. Throughout these stages, maternal and paternal genomes were remodelled almost synchronously. Activation-associated marks (H3K4me1/2/3, H3K9ac, H4K12ac) appeared shortly after fertilization, spread to both pronuclei by apposition, and disappeared before mitosis. The histone modification H3K9me3, commonly associated with compact or heterochromatin-like chromatin states, was present from the earliest time point examined and became more prominent during pronuclear maturation, whereas H3K27me3 was not detectable at any stage. H2A.Z showed strong paternal enrichment after sperm entry and was later detected in both pronuclei during apposition with weaker maternal signal. Polar bodies did not show evidence of undergoing the extensive chromatin remodelling observed in the zygotic pronuclei. In gametes, sperm contained core histones and methylation marks but lacked acetylation, indicating a compact, semi-active chromatin state, while unfertilized eggs displayed H3K9me3 and H3S10ph but no activating marks, consistent with meiotic arrest.ConclusionsZebrafish fertilization involves direct, histone-based chromatin remodelling that occurs without protamine replacement and proceeds almost simultaneously in both parental pronuclei. The sperm genome enters fertilization already in a semi-active, histone-bound state, enabling rapid and coordinated remodelling of both parental chromatin. This process results in a transient change in chromatin configuration that is more symmetrical between the parental genomes than that reported in mammalian systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13072-026-00670-5.

Background

Fertilization represents a fundamental biological process underlying sexual reproduction [27, 52]. This process comprises a series of tightly coordinated cellular events, including sperm-egg interactions and membrane fusion, sperm and egg chromatin remodelling, centrosome transmission and aster formation, as well as pronuclear migration, culminating in the union of the parental genomes (e.g., [18, 37, 39]). These steps require radical reprogramming events, as two epigenetically distinct genomes, each shaped by gamete-specific function, rapidly transform into a single totipotent nucleus primed to initiate embryonic development [31, 82]. This transformation involves large-scale changes in chromatin structure, histone composition, and post-translational modifications (PTMs). In mammals and many other vertebrates, the paternal chromatin, initially compacted by protamines, must undergo a dramatic protamine-to-histone exchange immediately after membrane fusion, triggering large-scale remodelling and the establishment of a new epigenetic landscape in the male pronucleus [58]. By contrast, in several fish [60], and amphibians [26], sperm chromatin remains histone-based, offering an alternative model for studying chromatin reprogramming in the absence of protamine replacement. Despite this diversity, the timing and coordination of parental chromatin transitions during the early stages of fertilization remain poorly understood in most vertebrates, particularly in vivo.

The zebrafish (Danio rerio) provides an ideal vertebrate model to address these gaps. First introduced into research in the late 1960s [70], zebrafish combine practical advantages, such as external and rapid embryonic development, transparent embryos, and high genetic tractability [28], with substantial genomic conservation to humans [77]. Although zebrafish have become a key model for genomics, developmental and chromatin biology, the molecular events underlying gamete fusion, pronuclear remodelling, aster formation, and early histone dynamics before zygote formation remain unresolved in vivo.

Unlike mammals, zebrafish sperm is not compacted with protamines. Instead, the paternal genome is packaged in nucleosomes, drawing on somatic-like strategies for chromatin organization, including the use of linker histones and the absence of histone acetylation [60, 86]. While zebrafish cannot address protamine-histone replacement, its nucleosomal sperm chromatin provides a powerful system to study how histone variants and post-translational modifications inherited from gametes are remodelled after fertilization.

Previous studies in zebrafish have demonstrated that the sperm DNA methylome is transmitted to the embryo and guides early developmental programming, suggesting a form of epigenetic memory [23]. The transmission of DNA methylation patterns is relatively well characterized. In contrast, the functional roles of histone marks and variants during early zebrafish development remain much less clear. Transcription before the mid-blastula transition (MBT) is largely promoter-driven, and canonical marks such as H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 are transiently depleted before being re-established at zygotic genome activation [86]. These findings point to a more naïve or undefined chromatin landscape during the cleavage stages, potentially regulated by uncharacterized histone marks, PTMs, or maternally supplied factors.

To delineate chromatin state transitions during fertilization, histone variants and post-translational modifications that encode distinct regulatory and structural functions are particularly informative, yet their earliest dynamics remain incompletely characterized in zebrafish and other teleost [20, 53]. In this species, most insights into histone modifications derive from genome-wide or population-level analyses performed at or after the mid-blastula transition, including ChIP-seq and bulk immunostaining approaches that revealed transient depletion and later re-establishment of canonical marks such as H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 at zygotic genome activation [61, 86]. However, the spatiotemporal dynamics of activation-associated marks (H3K4me1/2/3, H3K9ac, H4K12ac), repressive modifications (H3K9me3, H3K27me3), and the mitotic marker H3S10ph have not been directly visualized during zebrafish fertilization and early pronuclear stages in vivo. The histone variant H2A.Z, which regulates chromatin accessibility, restricts DNA methylation, and is required for early embryonic viability in zebrafish, has been studied primarily through genetic and genomic approaches [2, 44], while its incorporation dynamics during fertilization have not been examined at high temporal resolution.

In zebrafish, the sperm is expected to supply the centrioles that recruit maternal pericentriolar material to rebuild the zygotic centrosome after fertilization. As in sexually reproducing organisms, these centrioles are thought to organize the sperm aster, which guides pronuclear migration and assembly of the first mitotic spindle [4, 47, 62]. Although this has not been demonstrated directly in Danio, it remains an open question whether sperm-derived structures are temporally aligned with chromatin remodelling in a way that promotes parental genome alignment and entry into the first cleavage division.

Here, we present a high-resolution, time-resolved analysis of chromatin remodelling during zebrafish fertilization, capturing the incorporation dynamics of histone variants and PTMs within maternal and paternal pronuclei. Using spinning-disk confocal microscopy combined with immunofluorescence, we characterized a panel of histone marks at minute-scale intervals from fertilization through pronuclear migration and the first mitosis. These findings define, for the first time, the spatial and temporal sequence of chromatin remodelling events during early stages of zebrafish fertilization and establish a reference framework for studying parental epigenetic convergence in vertebrate embryos.

Materials and methods

Fish crosses, gamete collection, and fertilization

Adult zebrafish (five males, five females) were maintained under standard laboratory conditions in a ZebTec Active Blue breeding system at 28 °C on a 14:10 h light: dark cycle. Fish were fed twice daily with commercial dry food (GEMMA Micro 500) supplemented by live Artemia nauplii. Sexually mature males and females were placed in pairs into spawning aquaria the evening before gamete collection, separated by a transparent plastic divider. At the onset of light the following morning, the barrier was removed to induce spawning. Females were carefully observed for oviposition and promptly transferred to anaesthesia (0.05% MS222), and eggs were collected by hand stripping.

Mature males were manually stripped to collect milt under sterile conditions [10]. Sperm from five males was individually collected in 10 µl of Kurokura 180 immobilizing solution, checked for motility after activation using a system water under a bright field microscope, and stored for a short-term prior to oocytes collection. A portion of oocytes containing ~ 200 eggs was mixed with 10 µl of pooled sperm and activated by 100 µl of a system water to ensure consistency across experimental replicates. Fertilized eggs were incubated at 27 °C and monitored until the cleavage stages.

Sperm and eggs fixation and permeabilization

Unfertilized (egg collected prior to exposure to sperm), fertilized (after sperm treatment) eggs and embryos (after first zygotic division) were collected at defined time points (2–75 min post-fertilization), covering the pronuclear and early cleavage stages [29], and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1× PBS (phosphate-buffered saline) at room temperature (RT) for 12–14 h. Samples were then washed three times in 1× PBS (30 min each) and stored in 1× PBS at 4 °C prior to the immunostaining procedure. Sperm samples were fixed in 4% PFA in 1× PBS, smeared on Permafrost-coated slides (Avantor) and incubated in a humid chamber for 20 min at RT. Afterwards slides were once washed in 1× PBS and used for the immunostaining procedure.

Eggs and sperm immunostaining

Before immunostaining, all samples were permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS (sperm preparations for 20–30 min, eggs, and embryos for 1–2 h) to enable antibody penetration. Fertilized eggs and embryos were manually dechorionated using fine forceps before further processing. Specimens were then transferred into 0.5% blocking solution (11096176001, Roche) in 1× PBS and incubated for 60–90 min at RT. Primary antibodies were applied in blocking solution (concentration 1:200) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: H3K9me3 (ab8898, Abcam), H3K9ac (ab10812, Abcam), H3K27me3 (ab6002, Abcam), H2B (ab23913, Abcam), H2A.Z (GTX108273, GeneTex), H4K12ac (ab177793, Abcam), H3S10ph (GTX128116, GeneTex), H3 (ab1791, Abcam), H4 (GTX637870, GeneTex), H3K4me1 (GTX60818, GeneTex), H3K4me2 (GTX121915, GeneTex), H3K4me3 (GTX128954, GeneTex), γ-tubulin (ab27074, Abcam) and α-tubulin (ab7291, Abcam) (Table S1, antibodies validated in [7, 11, 13, 14, 42, 71, 73, 74, 76–78, 81] ). Secondary antibodies in blocking solution (concentration 1:300) included anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (A-11037, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (A-32723, Thermo Fisher Scientific), applied for 12 h at RT. After primary and secondary antibodies, egg and embryo samples were washed three times in 1× PBS for 15 min each and counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL in PBS; Sigma-Aldrich). Slides with sperm were washed in 1× PBS, dehydrated through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 96%), and mounted in Vectashield mounting medium containing 1 µg/mL DAPI (Vector, Burlingame, CA, USA).

Microscopy and image processing

Immunostained embryos were mounted in 1× PBS on glass-bottom dishes (MatTek) and imaged using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2 equipped with a Yokogawa CSU-W1 scanner unit and a PRIME BSI (Teledyne Photometrics) camera). Six to eight embryos were analysed per time point for each antibody (total n = 3,549 embryos), and maternal and paternal pronuclei were evaluated separately when they could be reliably distinguished. A 60× water immersion objective (NA 1.20) was used for high-resolution imaging of nuclear structures. Z-stacks were acquired at 0.5–1 μm intervals to capture the full volume of embryos or individual blastomeres. Fluorophores were excited using 385 nm, 488 nm, and 594 nm lasers, and emission was collected using appropriate bandpass filters. Acquisition settings (laser power, exposure time, and gain) were kept constant for each antibody to allow relative comparison across samples and time points.

Raw image stacks were processed using FIJI/ImageJ (projection type for Z-projection: standard deviation) [63]. For quantitative analysis, fluorescence signal intensity was measured within manually defined nuclear regions of interest (ROIs) based on PTM staining. Depending on developmental stage, ROIs corresponded to individual maternal or paternal pronuclei, fused nuclei, mitotic chromatin, or the second meiotic chromosome set / polar body. When maternal and paternal pronuclei could be clearly distinguished based on morphology and developmental timing, they were quantified separately.

For each ROI, background fluorescence was measured in an adjacent cytoplasmic region lacking detectable nuclear signal. Quantitative values are presented as MFI ratios (ROI-to-background), calculated by dividing nuclear MFI by the corresponding background MFI. This normalization was used to account for local background variation and imaging noise. Quantitative comparisons were restricted to measurements obtained for the same histone mark within the same experiment; absolute signal intensities were not compared between different antibodies.

For each developmental stage, embryos were collected from at least three females, with at least two embryos per stage analysed from each individual fish. When immunofluorescence staining patterns were consistent across all embryos examined at a given stage (e.g. uniform presence or absence of a signal), the sample size was not further expanded. In cases where variability in staining was observed between embryos at the same stage, additional embryos were analysed to confirm the pattern. Final sample sizes for each marker and stage are indicated in Supplementary Tables 2, 3. Statistical comparisons between maternal and paternal pronuclei at the same developmental stage were performed using non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests, with significance assigned at p < 0.05.

Fluorescence intensity measurements are interpreted as relative indicators of detectable nuclear signal and temporal dynamics rather than absolute protein abundance, given potential influences of chromatin compaction, nuclear morphology, and epitope accessibility. In the context of highly condensed meiotic and early pronuclear chromatin, limited epitope accessibility may constrain immunodetection of certain histone marks such that absence of signal does not necessarily reflect true absence of the modification.

Maternal and paternal chromatin were distinguished based on characteristic morphology, cell-cycle stage, and developmental timing, with maternal chromatin identified as the anaphase II chromosome set at fertilization and paternal chromatin as the condensed sperm nucleus and subsequent male pronucleus.

Slides with spermatozoa were captured using a fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ni, Nikon, Japan) equipped with a standard fluorescent filter set at ×1000 magnification, and images were acquired using NIS software (Nikon). Representative images were assembled in Adobe Photoshop (version 26.3.0) or FIJI [63]. All image manipulations were restricted to linear adjustments applied uniformly and preserving original signal integrity.

Results

The fertilization in zebrafish involves rapid and synchronous remodelling of parental chromatin, beginning with sperm entry and continuing through the first embryonic mitosis. To investigate the dynamics of these events in vivo, we analysed sperm and unfertilized eggs along with approximately 3,549 fertilized eggs at 1-5-minute intervals from 2 to 75 min post-fertilization (mpf) (Table S2). Using immunofluorescence combined with spinning-disk confocal microscopy, we tracked a panel of histone PTMs, core histones, and variants across distinct stages of nuclear morphology during fertilization and early zygotic development (Table S2). These included activating marks (H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, H4K12ac), repressive marks (H3K9me3, H3K27me3), the mitotic marker H3S10ph, histone variant H2A.Z and core histones (H2B, H3, H4) (Table S1, S2; Fig. S6).

Pronuclear morphology and microtubule dynamics during early zebrafish fertilization

Maternal and paternal pronuclei became morphologically distinguishable by ~ 2 mpf (Fig. 1A, B) and remained physically separated during stages of chromatin remodelling and pronuclei migration (Fig. 1C-G). In all embryos at ~ 2–3 mpf (n = 170), the two pronuclei were consistently identifiable based on DAPI morphology. The maternal chromatin, derived from the egg, initially exists in anaphase of the second meiotic division. At this stage, tubulin staining revealed the spindle of the maternal pronucleus during the second meiotic division (Fig. 1A, B) and sperm-derived structures, including the retained flagellum (Fig. 1A) and centrosomes (Fig. 1C). Between ~ 3–6 mpf, the oocyte completes meiosis II and extrudes the second polar body, after which the maternal pronucleus becomes morphologically recognizable. From this stage, the maternal pronucleus appears larger and more diffuse than the paternal pronucleus, which originates from the sperm and is initially small and more compact (Fig. 1A-C). At this stage (Fig. 1, A-D), the paternal pronucleus starts to swell but remains rounder and denser compared to the slightly irregular maternal pronucleus. The flagellum was barely visible next to the paternal pronucleus, while the sperm aster first began to form but was faintly detectable in most embryos (Fig. 1D). By eight mpf, both pronuclei enlarged, with the paternal pronucleus swelling faster and soon matching the maternal one in size and chromatin appearance (Fig. 1E). From ten mpf, the pronuclei reached maximal size, with smooth contours and diffuse chromatin (Fig. 1F). At this stage, the aster becomes clearer, extending microtubules across the cytoplasm toward the maternal pronucleus in parallel with its migration (Fig. 1E). During 10–30 mpf, the pronuclei migrate progressively toward each other and align side-by-side but without fusion (apposition), maintaining an undistinguishable size difference (Fig. 1G). By ~ 20 mpf, both pronuclei were positioned side by side; however, the microtubule network was not visible unless the image was overexposed. Finally, by 30–35 mpf, both pronuclei underwent nuclear envelope breakdown, and chromatin condensed synchronously into mitotic chromosomes (Fig. 1H), marking the onset of the first cleavage division (Fig. 1, I). It is accompanied by reorganization of microtubules reorganized into the first bipolar spindle at mitotic entry, occurring at the same time as chromatin condensation (Fig. 1I).

Histone composition in zebrafish sperm

Analysis of zebrafish sperm revealed the presence of a distinct set of core histones and modifications (Table S2, Fig. S1). In total, 600 spermatozoa were analysed (50 spermatozoa per each mark). Core histones H3 and H4 were clearly detected (n = 100), confirming that zebrafish sperm chromatin retains a nucleosomal organization rather than undergoing protamine-based compaction (Fig. S1, A, B). The histone variant H2A.Z was barely observed (Fig. S1, C, n = 50), suggesting potential roles in maintaining chromatin accessibility or marking regulatory regions.

Among methylation marks, both H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 were strongly present (Fig. S1, D, E, n = 100), reflecting a partially active chromatin configuration. In contrast, H3K4me3 was not detected (Fig. S1, F, n = 50), consistent with the lack of canonical active promoter marking in mature sperm. H3K9me3 was robustly detected (Fig. S1, G, n = 50), indicating the presence of heterochromatinised regions, while H3K27me3 was observed only as a discrete, dot-like signal (Fig. S1, H, n = 50), possibly marking a specific repressed locus or compartment. Notably, no signal was detected for H3K9ac or H4K12ac (Fig. S1, I, J, n = 100), indicating that zebrafish sperm chromatin lacks acetylation-associated features typically linked to a more permissive chromatin configuration at this stage.

Histone composition in zebrafish unfertilized eggs

In unfertilized zebrafish eggs (six eggs per each mark, n = 72), the maternal genome exists as a metaphase of the second meiotic division, suggesting that only a limited set of histone signals was present (Fig. S2; Tables S2, S3). Core histones H3 and H4 were readily detected in unfertilized eggs and localized to the condensed meiotic chromosomes (Fig. S2, A, B, n = 12). Among post-translational modifications, the repressive mark H3K9me3 was clearly detectable (Fig. S2, C, n = 6), displaying a strong enrichment, while the mitotic marker H3S10ph was also present (Fig. S2, D, n = 6), indicating condensed chromosomes during meiotic division. No other activating acetylation or methylation marks, as well as the histone variant H2A.Z, were observed in unfertilized eggs (Fig. S2, E-J, n = 48).

Histone variation H2.A.Z and core histones H3/H4 incorporation precedes the acquisition of modifications

The histone variant H2A.Z (Fig. 2, panels A, B, C) was detected in the paternal pronucleus exclusively during 2–5 mpf (Fig. 2, A1, B1, C1, n = 28). Later, during apposition, this modification became visible in both pronuclei, although the signal strength in the maternal pronucleus was significantly lower (Fig. 2, A2, B2, C2, n = 176; Fig. S4; Table S3). Finally, H2A.Z was not detected in both maternal and paternal pronuclei during the early 1-cell stage and later (Fig. 2, A3-5, C3-5, n = 42). Core histone H3 (Fig. S3, panels A, B, C, n = 398; Fig. S4; Table S3) was faintly detectable at sperm entry and during activation (2–5 mpf, Fig. S3, A1, B1, C1, n = 32) and then detected at ~ 50–52 and ~ 63–64 mpf (2–4 cell stages, Fig. S3, A4, B4, C4). Core histone H4 (Fig. S3, panels D, E, F, n = 420; Fig. S4; Table S3) was at the 1-cell stage exclusively (Fig. S3, D3, E3, F3, n = 21), while H2B was not observed at any stage (Fig. S3, panels G, H, I, n = 157; Fig. S4; Table S3).

A transient wave of histone modifications accompanies early pronuclear progression

The repressive mark H3K9me3 (Fig. 2, panels D, E, F; Fig. S5) was already strongly present in paternal and maternal pronuclei from sperm entry (2–3 mpf, n = 14), with a significantly stronger signal in the paternal pronucleus. This modification showed pronounced nuclear enrichment throughout early pronuclear stages (n = 154) and became further concentrated during chromosome condensation at cleavage onset (~ 30–75 mpf, n = 160). In contrast, H3K27me3 was not detected at all stages examined (Fig. 2, panels G, H, I, n = 266; Fig. S5).

Among early-appearing PTMs, the activating marks H3K4me1 (Fig. 3, panels A, B, C), H3K4me2 (Fig. 3, panels D, E, F), and H3K4me3 (Fig. 3, panel G, H, I) were detected in both pronuclei as early as ~ 2–5 mpf. H3K4me1 showed significantly brighter signal in the paternal pronucleus during pronuclear migration and apposition stage, while H3K4me2 was significantly brighter during sperm entry and pronuclei activation stage (Fig. S4). However, H3K4me1/3 were consistently depleted in both pronuclei by ~ 30 mpf in all embryos (n = 503), and H3K4me2 depleted in both pronuclei by ~ 40–45 mpf in all embryos (n = 204, Fig. S4).

H3K9ac (Fig. 4, A-C; Fig. S5) was detectable from sperm entry and was enriched in the maternal pronucleus, whereas the paternal pronucleus showed weaker near background-level signal, with a statistically significant difference in fluorescence intensity between pronuclei (Fig. 4, A1, B1, C1; Fig. S5; n = 21). Mitotic phosphorylation marks onset of first cleavage

The metaphase chromosome marker H3S10ph (Fig. 4, panels G, H, I; Fig. S4) was not detected in maternal and paternal pronuclei during sperm entry, pronuclear migration, and apposition (Fig. 4, G1, H1, I1, G2, H2, I2, n = 126). This modification appeared sharply and synchronously in fused pronuclei at ~ 33 and ~ 60–85 mpf (1-cell and 4-cell stages, Fig. 4, G3-5, H3-5, I3-5, n=211), coinciding with chromosome condensation during prometaphase. This signal was reproducible across all embryos examined at the corresponding stage (Fig. S4; Table S3).

Polar bodies exhibit distinct chromatin profiles

Following sperm penetration, the oocyte completed meiosis II and extruded the second polar body within ~ 5 mpf, which was observed by DAPI in most embryos during 2–35 mpf (≥ 80%, n = 2,960; Figs. 2, 3 and 4, S3, S4; Table S3). Anaphase II set or polar bodies displayed a distinct chromatin profile, retaining core histone H3 (Fig. S3, A-C1, A-C2), the histone variant H2A.Z (Fig. 2, A-C1, A-C2), and several PTMs (H3K27me3, H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K9me3) in approximately 70% of embryos (Figs. 2, 3 and 4; Fig. S4, S5; Table S3). Notably, strong signals for H3K4me1, H3K4me2, and H3K4me3 were detected in all the polar bodies (Fig. 3, S3). H3K9ac was present in the polar body only during sperm entry and activation stages (2–5 mpf, Fig. 4, A-C1, A-C2, n = 20; Fig. S5; Table S3) while H4K12ac was not detected in all the cases (Fig. 4, D-F1, D-F2, D-F3, n = 150). This signal was reproducible across all embryos examined at the corresponding stage (Fig. S4, S5; Table S3).

Discussion

Cytoskeletal dynamics and pronuclear behaviour during zebrafish fertilization

Fertilization in zebrafish proceeds through a tightly timed sequence of events in which parental chromatin remodelling unfolds in parallel with cytoskeletal reorganization. In this study, we provide the first in vivo characterization of these events at minute-scale resolution in a model object, Danio rerio, revealing how the maternal and paternal pronuclei form, migrate, and prepare for the first mitosis. We show that both pronuclei expand and mature almost synchronously after sperm entry, while a sperm-derived aster organized by paternal centrioles forms and extends through the cytoplasm. This finding confirms, with direct microscopic evidence, that zebrafish rely on sperm-derived centrosomes to establish the first zygotic spindle, a process that until now had been assumed but not visualized in this species and in other teleosts. When compared with other model organisms, zebrafish display both conserved and distinctive features of fertilization. In mammals and Drosophila, paternal centrioles are known to organize the sperm aster that drives pronuclear migration [39, 47, 62], and our imaging data in zebrafish support a similar model. However, in contrast to mammals, where remodelling of parental pronuclei occurs asynchronously and involves extensive protamine-histone exchange [1, 59], zebrafish pronuclei remodel in parallel, reflecting the histone-based nature of fish sperm chromatin. Other species, such as Drosophila [72], Caenorhabditis elegans [51], Xenopus [36] , share paternal centriole inheritance during canonical fertilization but also exhibit a capacity for centrosome biogenesis and remodelling, including de novo assembly under specific developmental or experimental conditions. The zebrafish shares the vertebrate mode of paternal centrosome inheritance and exhibits uniquely synchronized pronuclear and cytoskeletal events.

Zebrafish fertilization involves synchronous parental chromatin remodelling

Histone PTMs orchestrate the structural and functional states of chromatin, enabling dynamic transitions between transcriptionally permissive and repressive configurations. Although in mammals, early zygotic reprogramming displays marked asymmetry between parental genomes [1, 59], our data show that zebrafish fertilization follows a largely synchronous remodelling of both pronuclei. This reflects a fundamental difference in sperm chromatin organization: zebrafish sperm DNA is already histone-based rather than protamine-packed [86]. For the first time, we defined temporal phases of chromatin remodelling in zebrafish intact gametes and embryos and showed the rapid appearance of activation-associated marks, including H3K4 methylation (me1, me2, me3) and acetylation (H3K9ac, H4K12ac), along with the core histones H3 and H4 (Fig. 5).

Temporal dynamics of histone modifications and variants during zebrafish fertilization. Quantitative data is presented in Supplementary figures S3, S4 and table S3

In zebrafish, H3K4 methylation marks (me1, me2, me3) emerge within the first ten minutes post-fertilization, with H3K4me1/2 initially enriched in the paternal pronucleus and H3K4me3 in both pronuclei. By ~ 20 mpf, all three marks are detectable in both pronuclei, but they are subsequently lost from both by ~ 30 mpf. Rather than indicating early transcriptional activation, this transient presence of H3K4 methylation likely reflects a brief phase of chromatin relaxation that accompanies pronuclear expansion and reorganization. Such rapid turnover is consistent with the maintenance of a flexible chromatin state prior to the mid-blastula transition, when zygotic genome activation occurs in zebrafish [86]. Unlike zebrafish, mammalian pronuclei accumulate H3K4me3 during migration and maintain this modification even beyond zygotic genome activation [5].

Histone acetylation displayed partially overlapping but distinct dynamics. H3K9ac was detectable from sperm entry and, up to ~ 10 mpf, remained enriched in the maternal pronucleus, with significantly lower fluorescence intensity in the paternal pronucleus, whereas H4K12ac appeared in both pronuclei from ~ 10 mpf and onward to ~ 35–40 mpf. These modifications likely promote chromatin relaxation and nucleosome remodelling, facilitating later zygotic genome activation rather than triggering immediate transcription [85, 90]. Across eukaryotes, histone acetylation is broadly conserved as a mechanism for establishing transcriptional competence by loosening chromatin and marking genomic regions poised for activation [79, 80, 83]. However, the timing and functional context of these events vary: in mammals, H4K12ac is primarily linked to oocyte maturation and centromere organization [80], whereas in fish (our data; [17, 79]) and nematodes [83], acetylation correlates with chromatin accessibility and developmental plasticity, priming the genome for subsequent transcriptional activation. Altogether, these observations suggest that while acetylation-driven chromatin relaxation is broadly conserved across metazoans, in zebrafish it operates as an early, transient mechanism that primes both pronuclei for later genome activation in a manner distinct from mammalian and other vertebrate systems.

The synchronized and rapid acquisition of activation-associated marks observed here implies the involvement of chromatin modifiers that are already present and active in the egg cytoplasm at fertilization [17, 30, 84]. Enzymes responsible for H3K4 methylation, such as SET1/COMPASS or MLL-family methyltransferases, as well as histone acetyltransferases (HATs) capable of depositing marks such as H3K9ac and H4K12ac, are strong candidates, as these factors are maternally deposited in many vertebrates and can act on chromatin on a minute timescale without requiring de novo transcription [17, 33, 64]. In zebrafish, the histone-based organization of sperm chromatin may permit immediate access of such modifiers to both parental genomes, facilitating the largely synchronous remodelling observed [30, 84, 86]. This contrasts with protamine-based systems, such as mammals, where extensive protamine removal and histone incorporation must occur before chromatin-modifying enzymes can act, contributing to pronounced parental asymmetry [9, 32, 87]. Thus, differences in sperm chromatin packaging may fundamentally shape how rapidly and symmetrically chromatin-modifying enzymes operate following fertilization [32, 86, 87]. Identifying the specific regulators involved will require functional approaches beyond the scope of the present study.

Repressive chromatin marks displayed a contrasting behaviour. H3K9me3 was present from the time of sperm entry in both pronuclei, became further enriched during pronuclear migration, and remained prominent during chromosome condensation at the onset of cleavage. This persistence suggests a stabilizing role for H3K9me3 during the extensive structural transitions of the zygotic nucleus, potentially safeguarding repetitive or vulnerable genomic regions prior to the establishment of more permanent heterochromatin domains [69]. In contrast, no evidence of H3K27me3 was detected during zebrafish pronuclear development. This absence may reflect loss or masking of a highly localized modification during sperm chromatin remodelling, or limitations of immunodetection at this stage, and indicates that Polycomb-mediated repression does not contribute to early pronuclear regulation in zebrafish. Comparative studies reveal substantial variation in the timing of H3K27me3 deposition across vertebrates: in Xenopus, this mark appears only after the mid-blastula transition [3], whereas in most mammals it is inherited from the oocyte and participates in early lineage specification via bivalent domains [40]. Rabbits display yet another pattern, with H3K27me3 absent until the morula and blastocyst stages [35]. Together, these comparisons emphasize that while H3K9me3 may act as an early and conserved safeguard of genome integrity, the timing and function of H3K27me3 are highly species-specific. In zebrafish, the absence of Polycomb-mediated repression during pronuclear stages suggests that alternative mechanisms, including DNA methylation, maternally supplied repressors, or selective incorporation of histone variants such as H2A.Z, maintain transcriptional silence until zygotic genome activation [20, 40].

The histone variant H2A.Z exhibited a distinctive but asymmetric pattern across fertilization. In zebrafish, it was present in intact sperm chromatin and paternal pronucleus from the time of sperm entry but not detected in the maternal chromatin. Further, during pronuclear apposition, it appeared in both pronuclei, with stronger signals in the paternal genome. This asymmetry likely reflects fundamental differences between maternal and paternal chromatin, such as their initial compaction state, histone composition, or DNA methylation environment, which influence how and when histone variants like H2A.Z are incorporated after fertilization [21, 44]. Similarly to our data, in mice and other vertebrates, H2A.Z exhibits tightly regulated dynamics, with up to 65% of zygotically activated genes enriched for this variant, emphasizing its conserved role in transcriptional priming and chromatin boundary control [15, 49]. In zebrafish, the maternal paralog H2afv restricts DNA methylation and is essential for normal development [15, 41, 49]. The reduced maternal signal observed in our study may therefore reflect species-specific modes of H2A.Z deposition that modulate the timing and extent of early genome reprogramming.

By contrast, H2B was not detected in our assays during zebrafish fertilization stages, which is consistent with the limited evidence for canonical H2B incorporation in early gametes of some vertebrates [55]. This likely reflects low epitope accessibility or a conserved exclusion of H2B from early chromatin remodelling. Phylogenomic analyses show that, despite strong evolutionary constraint, H2B variants have diversified functionally across vertebrates [55]. This selective handling of histone variants may help ensure proper completion of the first cell cycle, highlighting that even conserved histone families can adopt specialized roles during early development [6, 66].

Stage-dependent differences in the detectability of core histones H3 and H4 during zebrafish fertilization most likely reflect dynamic chromatin remodelling rather than true histone absence [22]. During pronuclear swelling, migration, and fusion, rapid chromatin reorganization associated with transient histone incorporation and large-scale decondensation can limit antibody access to epitopes within the histone fold domain [16, 22, 54]. In contrast, post-translational modifications on flexible N-terminal tails may become accessible earlier and remain detectable even when total histone signals are weak or below detection threshold [1, 8, 54]. The preferential detection of H4 at the 1-cell stage is consistent with progressive histone loading and nucleosome stabilization during pronuclear maturation, as early histone pools are likely sub-stoichiometric or highly dynamic [25, 54, 88]. Accordingly, absence of core histone signal at early stages should be interpreted as limited detectability under identical imaging conditions rather than complete histone loss.

Chromosome condensation marker, H3S10ph, accumulates in prophase, peaks in metaphase, and drastically declines in anaphase, allowing sister chromatid separation [12, 19, 76]. In zebrafish embryos, its absence at the sperm entry stage suggests that maternal chromatin had already started anaphase of the second meiotic division. H3S10ph first appeared at ~ 33 and ~ 60–85 mpf, coinciding with the first and second embryonic mitosis, respectively. In mammals, by contrast, H3S10ph has a dual role: it marks condensed mitotic chromosomes and also associates with actively transcribed regions during interphase, as shown by combined immunostaining and ChIP-based assays [43, 89]. In Xenopus [67] and in Danio (our data) its function remains largely mitotic until zygotic genome activation. These observations suggest that the mitotic role of H3S10ph is evolutionarily conserved, whereas its transcription-associated functions, described in some mammalian systems, have not yet been demonstrated in zebrafish and other fish embryos. Thus, zebrafish emphasize the primacy of H3S10ph as a structural regulator of mitotic entry and an excellent marker for the mitotic divisions.

At the level of the gametes, our analysis of intact sperm shows that zebrafish sperm already contain core histones H3 and H4 and the variant H2A.Z, together with H3K4me1/2 and H3K9me3, indicating a compact and epigenetically structured chromatin state [45, 48]. Unlike mammalian or avian sperm, where DNA is tightly compacted by protamines [46, 56], zebrafish sperm likely begin chromatin remodelling immediately upon activation in water, before fertilization, yielding a partially relaxed paternal genome [86]; [46]. This early remodelling may facilitate rapid pronuclear formation and synchronous fertilization but also increases sperm DNA sensitivity to oxidative or osmotic stress, a trade-off characteristic of externally fertilizing species [34]. In contrast, unfertilized eggs remain meiotically arrested, enriched for H3K9me3 and H3S10ph but devoid of activating marks, reflecting a meiotically arrested chromatin configuration lacking features commonly associated with permissive chromatin [48, 68, 86]. The complementarity between an epigenetically pre-primed paternal genome and a repressed maternal one thus appears to optimize both the speed and fidelity of zygotic genome reprogramming in teleosts.

Distinct chromatin state of polar bodies during zebrafish fertilization

Polar bodies provided a complementary view of epigenetic asymmetry and of chromatin fates that are excluded from embryonic reprogramming. Although polar bodies retained core histones H3 and H4 and the variant H2A.Z in ~ 70% of embryos, they lacked activating acetylation marks and H3K4me3 in more than 90% of cases. Instead, they displayed strong signals for H3K4me1, H3K4me2, and H3K9me3 in more than 80% of embryos, a combination that is most consistent with inheritance from meiotic chromatin rather than de novo modification after fertilization. Importantly, in this context, H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 are not interpreted as indicators of transcriptional activity but as residual chromatin marks maintained on a genome that does not undergo zygotic reprogramming.

Functionally, this repressive-biased chromatin state is consistent with the developmental fate of polar bodies. Polar body chromatin is physically segregated from the zygotic nucleus and is ultimately eliminated rather than subjected to embryonic chromatin remodelling. Stable chromatin compaction together with repressive modifications, including H3K9me3, likely helps maintain this genome in a segregated state that does not undergo reprogramming or contribute to embryonic development. Similar retention of meiotic or heterochromatic histone signatures in polar bodies has been reported in mammals, including mice [24, 75], as well as in Drosophila, where polar body nuclei remain transcriptionally quiescent and do not undergo embryonic chromatin remodelling [39, 57]. Together, these observations support the view that polar body chromatin in zebrafish retains a meiotic, non-embryonic epigenetic state and remains excluded from the chromatin reprogramming that prepares the zygotic genome for development.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that zebrafish fertilization involves direct, histone-based chromatin remodelling that proceeds synchronously in both parental pronuclei, fundamentally differing from the protamine to histone exchange and pronounced asymmetry characteristic of mammalian fertilization. The sperm genome enters the egg already packaged in nucleosomes with a semi-active chromatin signature marked by H3K4me1/2 and H3K9me3 but lacking acetylation, enabling immediate parallel remodelling of both pronuclei upon fertilization. Within minutes of sperm entry, activation marks including H3K4 methylation (me1, me2, me3) and acetylation (H3K9ac, H4K12ac) appear rapidly and spread to both pronuclei during migration and apposition, a transient shift toward a more permissive chromatin configuration. These activating marks prove transient, disappearing before the first mitosis, indicating they prepare the genome for later activation rather than triggering immediate transcription. In parallel, the repressive mark H3K9me3 persists throughout pronuclear maturation and intensifies during chromosome condensation, likely safeguarding genomic integrity and repetitive elements during dynamic structural transitions. The complete absence of H3K27me3 at all stages examined distinguishes zebrafish from mammalian systems and suggests reliance on alternative mechanisms such as DNA methylation, maternal repressive factors, or selective histone variant incorporation to maintain transcriptional silence until the mid-blastula transition.

The asymmetric incorporation of H2A.Z, showing strong paternal enrichment after sperm entry and weaker maternal signals during apposition, further highlights the distinct chromatin states of the two parental genomes and the regulated nature of chromatin remodelling during early development. Our study also provides the first direct microscopic evidence that zebrafish employ sperm-derived centrioles to organize the zygotic aster and coordinate pronuclear migration, linking cytoskeletal dynamics with chromatin remodelling in a tightly synchronized process. The transient nature of activation marks, delayed heterochromatin consolidation, and absence of Polycomb repression collectively indicate that early zebrafish zygotes maintain a structurally open, epigenetically plastic state adapted to rapid cleavage divisions and delayed zygotic genome activation. By maintaining chromatin in a flexible, poised configuration rather than establishing stable regulatory domains prematurely, zebrafish ensure both parental genomes remain competent for large-scale reprogramming until the mid-blastula transition. Our high-resolution temporal mapping establishes a comprehensive reference framework revealing that chromatin remodelling proceeds through discrete, coordinated transitions reflecting both conserved vertebrate mechanisms and teleost-specific adaptations, positioning zebrafish as a valuable comparative model for understanding the mechanistic diversity of fertilization and epigenetic reprogramming across vertebrate lineages.

In summary, zebrafish fertilization features synchronous, histone-based chromatin remodelling without protamine replacement, characterized by transient activation marks, persistent H3K9me3, absence of H3K27me3, and sperm-derived centrosome inheritance, representing a streamlined mode of epigenetic reprogramming adapted to teleost developmental strategies.
