Mavacamten in Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Haoyi Zheng
Published: 2026

Mavacamten, a cardiac myosin inhibitor, has ushered a new era of treatment for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM). It reduces left ventricular outflow (LVOT) gradients, improves functional capacity and peak oxygen uptake, and allows many patients to avoid or defer invasive septal reduction therapy. However, its potent negative inotropic effect has raised concerns regarding cardiac safety, particularly after publication of the ODYSSEY-HCM trial in symptomatic nonobstructive HCM (non-oHCM).1

Unlike the oHCM trials, ODYSSEY-HCM1 did not meet its primary efficacy endpoints (changes in peak oxygen uptake and the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire clinical summary score). On safety, a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50% occurred in 21.5% (62/288) on mavacamten vs 1.7% (5/292) on placebo at week 48; 7 patients reached LVEF ≤30% (none on placebo) and met criteria for permanent discontinuation. Heart failure (HF) events occurred in 6.6% (19/288) vs 1.7% (5/292). Although most patients recovered to ≥50% after treatment interruption, 3 did not, and 2 serious events (cardiogenic shock and cardiac arrest with death) occurred in the mavacamten arm. By contrast, the oHCM trials reported substantially lower rates of these events. In EXPLORER-HCM,2 LVEF <50% occurred in 5.7% (7/123), and no HF event occurred in the mavacamten arm at week 30. In EXPLORER-CN (conducted exclusively in China),3 no patient had an LVEF <50% at week 30. In VALOR-HCM4 (a crossover trial in septal reduction–eligible patients), the incidence of LVEF <50% was 11.1% (12/108), with 2 patients reaching LVEF ≤30% at week 56 (Table 1).

The cause for the higher incidence of LV dysfunction in ODDYSEY-HCM was not reported. oHCM is characterized by sarcomeric hypercontractility due to excessive actin–myosin cross-bridge formation. Mavacamten acts by reducing the number of actin–myosin cross-bridges with resultant negative inotropy.5 Therefore, LVEF reductions reflect an on-target pharmacologic action of mavacamten. A substantial LVEF reduction occurs when the impact of drug exposure exceeds the myocardial physiological contractility reserve. On closer review of these trials, there are 2 potential contributors for the high rate of LV dysfunction in ODYSEY-HCM.

First, baseline LVEF could act as a “safety buffer.” It is noted that the baseline mean LVEF is lower in non-oHCM. Across trials (Table 1), the baseline LVEF was ∼74% in EXPLORER-HCM,2 and ∼78% in EXPLORER-CN, vs ∼68% in VALOR-HCM,4 and ∼66% in ODYSSEY-HCM. ODYSSEY-HCM had the lowest mean baseline LVEF, despite an inclusion criterion requiring an LVEF of ≥60%. Patients with oHCM often have a supernormal LVEF >70% due to sarcomeric hypercontractility. A supernormal LVEF provides a physiological buffer against mavacamten’s on-target negative inotropy; conversely, a lower baseline LVEF, which represents a less sarcometric hyperactivity, reduces that buffer. Thus, an equivalent exposure and equivalent LVEF drops could make more patients cross cardiac safety thresholds. Clinically, an LVEF of 50% in patients with HCM represents serious LV dysfunction analogous to an LVEF of 35% to 40% in patients with systolic HF without HCM.5 This explains why trials excluded patients with LVEF ≤55%. It is noted that in ODYSSEY-HCM, patients whose LVEF decreased to <50% had a baseline LVEF (65.2% ± 4.9%) similar to those who maintained LVEF during follow-up (65.9% ± 3.7%) within the mavacamten group. Detailed characteristics of patients who developed LVEF <50%, including dose distribution and race or ethnicity, were not reported. In addition, differences in underlying pathophysiology and coexisting conditions (eg, atrial fibrillation) may contribute to interindividual susceptibility. Nevertheless, because mavacamten is a potent negative inotrope that reduces contractility by reversing hypercontractility, baseline contractile reserve remains a mechanically relevant factor for the incidence of LVEF < 50% across oHCM trials.

Second, titration strategies differed across trials. In ODYSSEY-HCM (nonobstructive), mavacamten was titrated solely based on LVEF. In VALOR-HCM, dosing was adjusted to reduce the LVOT gradient to the target (ultimately, <30 mm Hg), with LVEF safety checks by echocardiography (hold mavacamten if LVEF <50%). These approaches contrast with EXPLORER-HCM and EXPLORER-CN, where titration incorporated LVOT-gradient targets, plasma concentration (350-700 ng/mL), and LVEF safety. EXPLORER-CN also used a lower starting dose (2.5 mg once daily). In the earlier phase II PIONEER-HCM,6 substantial reductions in LVOT obstruction occurred at plasma concentrations between 350 and 695 ng/mL. Mavacamten decreased LVEF in a concentration-dependent manner. Four patients (36%) had LVEF reductions to 34% to 49% at plasma concentrations of 695 to 1500 mg/mL in this trial. The detailed end-of-study dose distributions were not fully reported for EXPLORER-HCM and EXPLORER-CN; however, ODYSSEY-HCM and VALOR-HCM had a substantial proportion of patients at the 15-mg dose by study end, 21.5% (62/288) and 19.4% (21/108) respectively. Furthermore, differences in higher dose distribution (55% vs 49% on 10 or 15 mg) and baseline LVEF (65% vs 68%) between VALOR-HCM and ODYSSEY-HCM may explain the different incidences of LVEF <50%. In contrast, the available dose distributions from the EXPLORER trials suggest fewer patients who received 15 mg, where most patients remained on 5 to 10 mg (89% in EXPLORER-CN and 81% in EXPLORER-HCM; the percentage of patients receiving 15 mg was not reported). Furthermore, in EXPLORER-CN, mavacamten was initiated at a lower starting dose of 2.5 mg because of concerns of lower body weight and a higher prevalence of poor CYP2C19 metabolizers in the Chinese population. This trial supports the idea that a concentration-guided titration approach can effectively individualize therapy without routine CYP2C19 genotyping. Notably, no LVEF <50% events occurred over 30 weeks in EXPLORER-CN (Table 1). These observations suggest that titration without concentration guidance may increase exposure to higher doses, contributing to a higher incidence of LV dysfunction.

Mavacamten-related systolic dysfunction is generally considered reversible. However, in ODYSSEY-HCM, 3 patients did not regain LVEF ≥50%; 2 serious events occurred among participants randomized to mavacamten: 1 cardiogenic shock and 1 cardiac arrest with death. In addition, 7 patients permanently discontinued treatment after meeting prespecified LVEF stopping criteria, either LVEF ≤30% at any time or 2 separate treatment interruptions for LVEF <50%. Importantly, in oHCM, a return of LVEF merely to ≥50% still represents reduced systolic performance.

Across the 4 pivotal trials, 2 safety observations warrant closer attention. First, VALOR-HCM reported a substantially higher incidence of LV dysfunction and HF. Second, EXPLORER-CN reported no cases of LVEF < 50% or HF. These contrasting findings underscore the critical roles of baseline LVEF and titration strategy in shaping the safety profile of mavacamten.

In clinical practice, the higher risk observed in non-oHCM should not deter appropriate use of mavacamten in oHCM, where benefit is well established and the safety buffer is typically larger. However, it is noteworthy that EXPLORER-CN did not just confirm the similar efficacy from EXPLORER-HCM but also demonstrated a more conservative approach for initiating and titrating mavacamten, which was associated with a highly favorable safety profile. Patients with oHCM who do not exhibit supernormal LVEF are frequently encountered in clinical practice but are under-represented in pivotal trials. A less hyperdynamic LVEF may reflect more advanced remodeling and myocardial fibrosis,7 which in turn may indicate reduced contractile reserve and less tolerance to the negative inotropic effect of mavacamten. In such patients, a more conservative and individualized dosing strategy, similar to that used in the EXPLORER-CN trial, should be adopted to avoid treatment interruption and optimize therapeutic benefit. In summary, mavacamten has transformed the care of oHCM. The excess systolic dysfunction seen in ODYSSEY-HCM reflects a combination of reduced physiological reserve and less constrained titration. By applying individualized, conservative, and, when possible, concentration-guided dosing with rigorous LVEF monitoring, clinicians can minimize risk while preserving the benefits of this agent. Given that mavacamten therapy in oHCM is often long-term or lifelong, maintaining treatment persistence is essential. Therefore, the guiding principle should be consistent across patients: identify the lowest effective dose to balance efficacy with safety.
