Endoscopic intermuscular dissection of a rectal gastrointestinal stromal tumor
Authors: Babak Mirminachi, Albert Ha, Varun Jain, Harkirat Singh, Pil Gyu Hwang, Sultan Mahmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsRectal gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are rare, and optimal management of localized disease remains uncertain. Endoscopic intermuscular dissection (EID) is an emerging minimally invasive technique that allows en bloc removal of selected subepithelial lesions that do not extend beyond the circular layer of the muscularis propria while preserving anorectal anatomy. We present a case of a rectal GIST successfully treated with EID. A 53-year-old man undergoing high-risk colorectal cancer surveillance was found to have a subepithelial rectal lesion 1.5 cm proximal to the dentate line. Endoscopic ultrasound and biopsy confirmed a low-grade GIST. The patient was referred for endoscopic submucosal dissection.MethodsThe procedure began with thermal marking followed by repeated methylene blue injections to maintain a well-defined submucosal plane. Once the lesion was identified as originating from the muscularis propria, the approach was converted to EID, allowing capsule-preserving en bloc resection within the intermuscular space. The defect was subsequently closed with a running endoscopic suturing device.ResultsPathology confirmed a spindle-cell, low-grade GIST with negative margins and no lymphovascular invasion. At 14-month follow-up, there were no delayed adverse events or evidence of recurrence.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of EID for small rectal GISTs originating from the muscularis propria.

Introduction

Rectal gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are rare, representing approximately 0.6% of rectal neoplasms.1,2

Because of GISTs’ low incidence, there is no established consensus regarding the optimal management of localized nonmetastatic rectal GISTs. Surgical resection, including minimally invasive transanal approaches, remains the standard therapeutic modality; however, these techniques often require full-thickness excision and may be associated with adverse events such as anastomotic leakage, surgical site infections, and postoperative functional impairment.3,4

Endoscopic intermuscular dissection (EID) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative that allows en bloc resection while preserving anorectal anatomy. Nevertheless, the literature on the use of EID for rectal GISTs is limited, and no standardized approach has yet been established, to our knowledge. We report on a case of a rectal GIST successfully treated with EID.

Case description

A 53-year-old male underwent colonoscopy for high-risk colorectal cancer surveillance. Colonoscopy revealed a subepithelial lesion in the distal rectum (Fig. 1A). Endoscopic ultrasound suggested a 10-mm oval hypoechoic lesion arising from the submucosal layer, located 1.5 cm proximal to the dentate line (Fig. 1B). Targeted biopsies were performed, and immunohistochemical staining was consistent with a GIST. The patient was subsequently referred for endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD).

A, Colonoscopic view of the rectal subepithelial lesion. B, Endoscopic ultrasound image of rectal subepithelial lesion.

A standard upper endoscope with a clear distal attachment was prepared for ESD (Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org). Thermal marking was applied to mark the borders of the lesion. Dilute methylene blue was injected along the thermal markings to lift the submucosa from the muscularis propria. Importantly, posterior lift was performed first because anterior lift can create a “hill” and obscure visualization. Adequate submucosal lift was achieved.

Initially, a superficial circumferential incision with a needle-knife (Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) was made along the thermal markers. Methylene blue was injected into the submucosal plane throughout the dissection.

The lesion was found to be arising from the muscularis propria layer, and the decision was made to perform intermuscular dissection to remove the lesion en bloc with an intact capsule (Fig. 2). Careful dissection below the circular muscularis plane was carried out, with exposure of the longitudinal muscularis fibers.

Wall layers with exposed intermuscular space illustrated. The curved black arrow indicates the outer longitudinal layer, and the yellow arrow indicates the tumor capsule involving the circular layer.

With steady progres, intermuscular dissection was extended, and the mass was seen beginning to detach from the muscular layer, as cautery was continued as needed. Dissection was continued until the lesion was completely removed, leaving the longitudinal muscle layer intact. The defect was subsequently closed using a running suture applied with an endoscopic suturing device (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA).

The procedure was completed in 58 minutes, and the patient was discharged the same day with 1 dose of ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV as prophylaxis. There were no immediate adverse events. Pathology results revealed a low-grade GIST with spindle cell morphology. The lateral and deep margins were negative, and there were no signs of lymphovascular invasion. A 14-month follow-up revealed a well-healed, healthy-appearing scar and no evidence of disease recurrence.

Discussion

We describe the use of EID for the resection of a rectal GIST. With increasing detection of early colorectal lesions during screening colonoscopy, there is a growing need for techniques that allow resection in deeper layers beyond the submucosa. EID provides access to the intermuscular plane between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers, enabling en bloc removal of lesions arising from the muscularis propria while preserving anorectal anatomy.

Other endoscopic resection techniques include EMR and endoscopic full-thickness resection. However, EMR performed in the distal rectum is associated with significantly lower en bloc resection rates, higher recurrence rates, and markedly lower R0 resection rates compared with ESD.

The full-thickness resection device also presents important limitations in this anatomical location. Its relatively bulky 21- to 23-mm cap reduces maneuverability within the narrow distal rectum, while the limited inner diameter (12 to 13 mm) restricts adequate tissue capture. In addition, use near the dentate line has been associated with significant pain, further limiting its applicability in very distal lesions.5,6

Although the intermuscular space is considerably narrower than the submucosal layer, adequate submucosal injection can expand the plane sufficiently to permit safe dissection. Although evidence on EID specifically for rectal GISTs remains limited, prior reports have demonstrated its feasibility for rectal adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors.7,8

EUS initially suggested that the lesion originated from the submucosa. However, during ESD, it became evident that the tumor extended into the muscularis propria, prompting us to proceed with intermuscular dissection to achieve complete resection. This experience underscores a practical limitation of EUS in accurately defining the layer of origin in subepithelial lesions.

In this case, both the lateral and deep margins were negative. Notably, data from GIST series have demonstrated that margin status does not significantly impact overall survival following resection, suggesting that microscopic margin involvement may not adversely influence long-term outcomes in these patients.9

Complete R0 resection was achieved without adverse events, and the patient remained recurrence-free at 14-month follow-up. These results support EID as a safe, organ-preserving alternative to surgery for selected small, low-risk rectal GISTs that do not extend beyond the circular layer of the muscularis propria.
