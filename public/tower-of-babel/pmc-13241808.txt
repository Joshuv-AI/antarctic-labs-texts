Electric Cataract After High‐Voltage Craniofacial Injury With Preserved Optic Nerve Microvasculature: A Case Report
Authors: Md Iftekher Iqbal, Fariah Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐voltage craniofacial electrical injury may result in delayed cataract without optic nerve damage. Multimodal structural and functional assessment supports accurate prognostication, and when posterior segment integrity is preserved, visual recovery after cataract surgery is typically excellent and sustained.Keywords: case report, cataract, electric injuries, optical coherence tomography angiography, phacoemulsification

Introduction

Electrical injury is an uncommon but recognized cause of ocular morbidity. Cataract formation following high‐voltage (> 1000 V) exposure, particularly when current traverses the craniofacial axis, has been consistently documented [1, 2, 3]. Electrical exposure may not always raise the incidence of long‐term cataracts, according to large cohort studies, but head injuries are more strongly linked to lenticular opacification [4, 5]. This underscores the significance of the electrical pathway in ascertaining ocular consequences.

The interval between electrical injury and cataract formation ranges from rapid opacification to several months [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. This fluctuation indicates a gradual biochemical and metabolic disturbance beyond the immediate heat denaturation of crystallins. Proposed processes encompass direct resistive heating, electroporation‐induced alterations in membrane permeability, calcium‐mediated proteolysis, and oxidative stress [7, 8]. Electric cataracts typically manifest morphologically as anterior subcapsular and cortical opacities, indicating damage to the lens epithelial and superficial fiber layers [3, 4, 5].

Involvement of the posterior segment is less predictable [9, 10, 11]. Optic neuropathy, visual field irregularities, and electrophysiological impairments have been documented even in patients achieving good postoperative visual acuity [6, 12, 13]. Thus, visual acuity alone is inadequate for prognostic prognosis, necessitating a thorough structural and functional assessment [14].

Here, we present a case of delayed electric cataract following high‐voltage craniofacial injury with preserved optic nerve microvasculature demonstrated by multimodal imaging, including spectral‐domain optical coherence tomography (SD‐OCT), optical coherence tomography (OCT) angiography, and automated perimetry, with sustained visual recovery at 12‐month follow‐up.

Case History/Examination

A young boy of 21 years old sustained accidental high‐voltage electrical exposure from a local power distribution line while working outdoors. According to the patient, the electrical current entered the craniofacial region, resulting in severe burns to the face, eyelids, and both upper and lower extremities. Acute burn management was performed at a tertiary center. Over subsequent months, the affected areas developed hypertrophic keloid scars.

Five months after the injury, the patient reported progressive blurring of vision in his left eye. There was no history of prior ocular disease, steroid exposure, or systemic metabolic disorder.

On examination of his left eye, his best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 6/24, and intraocular pressure (IOP) was 16 mmHg. The right eye's BCVA was 6/6 with an IOP of 14 mmHg. External examination revealed healed burn scars without significant eyelid malposition or exposure keratopathy, bilaterally (Figure 1a,b).

Slit‐lamp examination of the left eye demonstrated flower‐shaped anterior subcapsular and cortical lens opacities consistent with electric cataract (Figure 1c). The cornea was clear, and the anterior chamber was quiet. Gonioscopy revealed open angles without peripheral anterior synechiae or angle recession (Figure 1d). The right eye revealed no significant abnormalities in both anterior and posterior segments.

Methods

Phacoemulsification with posterior chamber intraocular lens implantation was performed without complication. Capsulorhexis was well controlled, and zonular integrity was preserved. A foldable intraocular lens was implanted in the capsular bag. Video 1 demonstrates the full surgical procedure.

Conclusions and Results

At 3 months postoperatively, multimodal comprehensive structural and microvascular evaluation was performed. Spectral‐domain optical coherence tomography (SD‐OCT) of the macula demonstrated preserved foveal contour and intact outer retinal layers without intraretinal or subretinal fluid (Figure 2). Structural optic nerve head (ONH) OCT demonstrated a typical neuroretinal rim configuration with a healthy cup‐to‐disc ratio. In all quadrants, peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness was age‐matched normative without focal thinning or superior–inferior asymmetry (Figure 3a). Radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) density was intact in OCT angiography (Angio Disc 4.5 × 4.5 mm protocol), with no focal dropout or hemisphere perfusion imbalance, confirming retained optic nerve microvasculature (Figure 3b). Humphrey Visual Field Analysis (24–2 SITA Standard) showed reliable indices, a Glaucoma Hemifield Test within normal limits, a Visual Field Index of 98%, a mean deviation of −2.70 dB, and a pattern standard deviation of 1.60 dB, without focal defects (Figure 4).

Optical coherence tomography angiography of the optic nerve head. (a) Optic nerve head analysis showing preserved neuroretinal rim configuration. (b) OCTA showing preserved radial peripapillary capillary density without focal perfusion defect and structural peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer thickness maps are within normative limits.

Humphrey visual field testing (24–2 SITA Standard). (a) Automated perimetry demonstrating Glaucoma Hemifield Test within normal limits and absence of focal visual field defects.

BCVA improved to 6/6 (− 0.50 DC at 85°). Clinical follow‐up was documented here for 12 months and he was asked for regular follow‐up annually. Visual acuity remained stable at 6/6, and no secondary glaucoma or clinical signs of optic neuropathy developed.

Discussion

Electric cataract following high‐voltage craniofacial injury is a recognized but uncommon consequence of electrical trauma [1, 2, 12]. The crystalline lens is especially fragile because of its avascular structure, high concentration of crystalline proteins, and reliance on tightly regulated ionic homeostasis. Several mechanisms may cause cataracts after electrical injury. Electroporation disrupts lens epithelial membrane integrity, allowing calcium influx and activation of the proteolytic pathway. Direct resistive heating may denature protein. Oxidative stress increases crystallin aggregation [5, 7, 8]. Delayed presentation, as seen in this case, suggests increasing metabolic impairment beyond the initial electrical assault.

The degree of ocular involvement after electrical shock primarily depends on the current's pathway and the tissue's resistance. Cataract development is more commonly observed when the electrical arc affects the orbit or skull [2, 4, 5]. Even though patients with electric cataract often have good vision after surgery, visual field and electrophysiologic abnormalities may persist if the posterior segment is affected [5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. These findings emphasize that structural lens opacity may not fully reflect the degree of neurological impairment and underscore the need for thorough assessment.

To our knowledge, structural—vascular—functional correlation using OCT, OCT angiography, and automated perimetry in electric cataract has rarely been documented.

SD‐OCT showed intact outer retinal layers and foveal appearance. Some studies describe retinal pigment epithelium and outer retinal changes following electrical injury [5, 9, 10, 14, 16]. The lack of retinal structural anomalies in this case corroborates the favorable visual outcome.

OCT of the optic nerve head (ONH) showed physiological cupping and retained neuroretinal rim structure. All quadrants of peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness were within age‐matched normative limits without sectoral thinning or superior–inferior asymmetry. RNFL thinning is a sensitive marker of axonal injury and may precede visual field defects [17]. The absence of RNFL structural loss excluded optic nerve axonal damage secondary to electrical trauma.

OCT angiography (OCTA) permits noninvasive evaluation of optic nerve head microvasculature, particularly radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) density. In glaucomatous and ischemic optic neuropathies, vessel density decreases and structural axonal damage occurs [18, 19]. Microvascular impairment may result from vascular spasm or endothelial injury from electrical shock. Modern OCTA evidence of electric cataract is scarce; however, this case's retained RPC density confirms intact optic nerve microvasculature.

Automated perimetry demonstrated the Glaucoma Hemifield Test within normal limits and no focal pattern deviation defects. The mild generalized depression (mean deviation −2.70 dB) is consistent with prior media opacity rather than neural dysfunction. According to structural–functional correlation studies, RNFL thinning precedes and correlates with localized visual field loss [17]. Preservation of ONH structure, undamaged microvasculature, and stable visual field findings support the diagnosis of isolated lenticular disease.

Ocular involvement in this patient was restricted to the crystalline lens, despite the presence of hypertrophic keloid and extensive craniofacial burns. According to these findings, the degree of the exterior burn injury is not the main determinant of visual prognosis following high‐voltage electrical injury; rather, the structural and microvascular integrity of the posterior segment is more important. Comprehensive multimodal evaluation using OCT, OCT angiography, and visual field testing may therefore be valuable in prognostication. When posterior segment integrity is preserved, cataract extraction can lead to durable visual rehabilitation with excellent long‐term outcomes.
