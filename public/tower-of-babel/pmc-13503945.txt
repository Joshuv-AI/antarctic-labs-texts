Multi-strategy quantum-inspired dung beetle optimizer for kernel extreme learning machine in slope safety factor prediction
Authors: Zhiyuan Sun, Mingwei Hai, Miao Wang, YanXiu Guo, Bin Zhou, Qi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of the slope safety factor (FS) is crucial for stability assessment and risk-informed decision making in mining and geotechnical engineering, yet practical applications are often constrained by small, heterogeneous datasets and the sensitivity of learning models to hyperparameter settings. This study presents a quantitative FS prediction framework that couples a Kernel Extreme Learning Machine (KELM) with a multi-strategy quantum-inspired dung beetle optimizer (QHDBO) to tune the key hyperparameters (regularization coefficient and kernel parameter) in logarithmic space. Five widely available geotechnical and geometric variables—unit weight (γ), cohesion (c), internal friction angle (φ), slope angle (α), and slope height (h)—are used as inputs, with FS as the target output. To ensure reliable generalization estimates under limited samples, model performance is assessed using strict no-leakage repeated nested cross-validation (5-fold outer CV repeated 30 times) and evaluated by R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE, complemented by engineering-critical error analysis near FS ≈ 1 and bootstrap confidence intervals. Results show that QHDBO-KELM achieves superior accuracy and robustness compared with baseline KELM and several benchmark models (RF, GBDT, MLP, SVR), yielding lower prediction errors and higher goodness-of-fit across diverse data partitions. Paired significance tests over 150 outer folds further confirm that the improvements are statistically significant, indicating that the proposed framework provides an efficient and dependable surrogate tool for slope FS prediction and can be extended to other geotechnical stability-related regression tasks.

Introduction

Slope stability analysis has been a prominent area of research within geotechnical and geological engineering. During activities such as mining, highway and railway construction, water conservancy projects, and other construction endeavors, numerous slopes are formed. The loss of stability in these slopes poses significant risks to economic development as well as to the safety of human lives and property1–4. Therefore, the creation of dependable and efficient models for evaluating and forecasting slope stability is of significant theoretical interest and considerable practical importance5. In engineering applications, the factor of safety (FS) serves as a measure of the margin between resisting and driving forces (or moments) along a potential failure surface. This factor is typically determined through limit equilibrium methods (LEM) based on predefined failure mechanisms or via numerical strength-reduction techniques (such as finite element method (FEM) or finite difference method (FDM)-based strength reduction methods) that iteratively decrease shear strength until a critical state is attained6–8. Beyond LEM and mesh-based numerical techniques, advanced large-deformation and post-failure methods—such as the material point method (MPM), smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH), and discrete element method (DEM)—are capable of representing complex kinematic behaviors and progressive failure mechanisms. However, these approaches generally demand greater data input, calibration efforts, and computational resources9–11. These practical challenges underscore the need for the development of rapid yet reliable surrogate models that can facilitate preliminary screening and informed decision-making, serving to complement rather than supplant mechanistic analyses.

Recent years have witnessed significant advancements in the study of slope stability. Utilizing the area equivalence principle, Wang et al.12 transformed the nonlinear strength envelope into a linear form, thereby deriving equivalent linear Mohr-Coulomb strength parameters and enabling approximate estimation of slope safety factors under nonlinear strength criteria. Yang and Yin13, along with Tang et al.14, employed single-point and multi-point tangent methods, respectively, to represent the global or local shear strength of sliding surfaces through specific tangent points on the nonlinear strength envelope. These approaches, combined with optimization algorithms, facilitated the identification of the most critical tangent strength affecting slope stability. T. Yang et al.15 applied numerical simulations to determine the principal stresses within grid elements under unreduced strength conditions, subsequently deducing the stress state of the sliding surface to calculate local safety factors. Deng et al.16 introduced a novel combined finite-discrete element method (FDEM) to investigate the mechanical behavior, failure mechanisms, and stability of soil-rock mixtures (SRMs). Hussen et al.17 examined the properties of colluvial soils and assessed the stability of landslide-prone slopes using various analytical techniques. Furthermore, Zhang et al.18 proposed a generalized horizontal slice method (GHSM) to evaluate the stability of multilayered slopes and to identify the ultimate failure modes in clay slag receiving areas, based on upper bound analysis theory. In rock slopes, structurally controlled kinematics such as wedge failure remain a key instability mode, and recent work has provided 3D numerical investigation and risk-relevant characterization for wedge-type failures19. Collectively, these studies highlight the importance of nonlinear strength behavior, material heterogeneity, and failure mechanism characterization in stability assessment.

Slope engineering, characterized as an open, uncertain, and nonlinear complex system, is influenced by numerous random and uncertain factors20–22. Consequently, traditional modeling approaches exhibit considerable limitations, often failing to yield optimal predictive outcomes. In recent years, the advent of machine learning (ML) algorithms has facilitated their integration with conventional engineering challenges23,24. For instance, Zhang et al.25 proposed a PINN-based physics-guided framework for meshfree 3D slope stability analysis and extended it to hybrid PINNs for slopes under external excitations, and Li et al.26 developed a deep learning-based probabilistic framework that couples PINN seepage simulation with a CNN surrogate to enable efficient reliability evaluation of spatially variable slopes under saturated seepage conditions. Lin et al.27 compiled ten slope datasets from both domestic and international sources and developed classifiers using the gravitational search algorithm, random forest, support vector machine, and naive Bayesian algorithms; their findings indicated superior performance of the gravitational search algorithm. Karir et al.28 constructed regression models to predict slope safety factors based on support vector machines, artificial neural networks, random forests (RF), and extreme gradient boosting machines, comparing these with numerical simulation results and concluding that tree-based models such as RF and extreme gradient boosting exhibit enhanced predictive accuracy. Utilizing deep learning neural networks, Bui et al.29 developed a slope stability analysis model applied to slope engineering in Vietnam’s Kon Tum area; although this approach was effective for stability analysis, it was comparatively slower than radial basis function neural network models in terms of local approximation and training speed. He et al.30 conducted a comparative study of neural networks, support vector machines, and other algorithms against finite element simulations, revealing that machine learning models not only achieve accurate predictions but also operate with significantly greater computational efficiency than traditional methods. Finally, Zhao et al.31 examined the potential of the relevance vector machine algorithm for slope stability analysis, identifying it as the most effective predictive algorithm among those considered. These works demonstrate that machine learning can serve as a practical supplement to mechanistic analyses by enabling fast screening and rapid updating, particularly when repeated evaluations are required. In recent years, data-driven approaches within geotechnical engineering have progressively evolved from an exclusive emphasis on predictive accuracy toward a comprehensive framework that integrates optimized modeling, uncertainty quantification, and interpretability analysis. Prior studies have implemented hybrid ensemble models for the multi-class classification of liquefaction-induced lateral spreading, thereby demonstrating the potential of ensemble learning combined with statistical posterior analysis in evaluating seismic geotechnical hazards32. Additionally, interpretable artificial intelligence techniques have been utilized to forecast the unconfined compressive strength of microbiologically cemented sand, uncovering nonlinear associations between input parameters and strength outcomes through methods such as feature importance metrics, SHAP values, and partial dependence plots33. The integration of Bayesian optimization with interpretable machine learning has also been applied to predict seismic site amplification effects, highlighting that automated hyperparameter tuning alongside model interpretability strategies enhances both the reliability and transparency of geotechnical predictive models34. Collectively, these investigations underscore that machine learning applications in geotechnical engineering should extend beyond mere predictive performance to incorporate rigorous hyperparameter optimization, model interpretability, statistical robustness, and practical engineering relevance.

Machine learning (ML) techniques have demonstrated growing potential for predicting slope stability; however, their application in practical engineering contexts requires further enhancement, particularly when dealing with the limited and heterogeneous datasets typical of geotechnical engineering. This study is motivated by several considerations. Firstly, a significant number of existing investigations focus primarily on binary classification of stability or qualitative evaluations, whereas engineering design and risk management necessitate accurate quantitative predictions of the factor of safety (FS), especially within the critical decision-making range near FS ≈ 1. Secondly, in regression-based models, prediction accuracy is substantially affected by the selection of hyperparameters, and the generalization capability may fluctuate when datasets are small or derived from diverse sources. Consequently, evaluation methodologies that emphasize robustness beyond a single random data split are warranted. Thirdly, although meta-heuristic algorithms have been employed for hyperparameter optimization, their search processes can prematurely converge or exhibit an unbalanced exploration-exploitation trade-off, potentially resulting in inconsistent tuning outcomes across different data partitions. Together, these gaps indicate the need for a small sample data-oriented, stably generalizable FS quantitative prediction framework, as well as reliable FS predictions under supporting real constraints.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this study introduces a quantitative slope safety factor prediction framework designated as QHDBO-KELM. Within this framework, the Kernel Extreme Learning Machine (KELM) functions as an effective regression model. KELM preserves the advantages inherent to Extreme Learning Machines (ELM), such as rapid training without the need for iterative gradient-based optimization, while enhancing nonlinear representation capabilities through kernel mapping35–39. This approach obviates the requirement for a predetermined number of hidden nodes. The kernel parameters and regularization coefficients are concurrently optimized using a quantum-inspired multi-strategy hybrid dung beetle optimization algorithm (QHDBO)40. The designation “quantum-inspired” refers exclusively to a classical stochastic search technique that draws on principles of probabilistic amplitude representation and quantum rotation perturbation; it does not imply the utilization of quantum computing hardware or the achievement of quantum speedup. This technique enhances the stability of the kernel extreme learning machine (KELM) parameter optimization within an inner-loop cross-validation framework by increasing the diversity of candidate solution distributions, thereby mitigating the likelihood of convergence to local optima and improving the equilibrium between global search capabilities and local exploitation.

The primary contribution of this study is the development and preliminary validation of a QHDBO-KELM modeling framework designed for the quantitative prediction of the slope safety factor (FS). This framework integrates a quantum-inspired multi-strategy dung beetle optimization algorithm, implemented in a classical manner, to simultaneously optimize the regularization coefficient and kernel parameters of the Kernel Extreme Learning Machine (KELM). This approach reduces the dependence on manually set parameters and enhances the stability of parameter optimization, particularly under conditions of limited sample size. To address the variability introduced by single-round data partitioning and parameter tuning, the study employs leak-free repeated nested cross-validation for robust model performance evaluation. Moreover, a thorough assessment of the model’s predictive capabilities is conducted by combining out-of-fold predictions, global error metrics, and detailed error analysis focused on the critical region near FS ≈ 1. The paper also examines the effects of limited sample size, statistical uncertainty, and computational cost on model applicability through the use of learning curves, bootstrap confidence intervals, and an analysis of the trade-off between accuracy and computational time. Collectively, this research offers a data-driven methodology for rapid and reliable estimation of the slope safety factor in scenarios characterized by small-sample, heterogeneous slope data.

Data sources and geotechnical context

Dataset description and definition of FS

Slope stability is governed by factors such as material strength, geometric configuration, and loading and drainage conditions. This research utilizes a dataset consisting of 55 slope cases, which have been collected from previously published studies41–43. For each case, the output variable is the factor of safety (FS) as reported in the original source, typically calculated using Mohr–Coulomb shear strength parameters and a defined analytical approach (e.g., limit equilibrium methods with specified failure mechanisms or numerical strength reduction techniques). In this study, the FS is regarded as the engineering metric derived from these established analytical procedures; the machine learning model developed herein serves as a surrogate predictor aligned with the original analytical framework, rather than as a substitute for mechanistic modeling.

A particular consideration regarding sample size is necessary: with a purely data-driven modeling approach, a sample size of n = 55 remains relatively small. To mitigate the risk of over-dependence on a single random data partition, this study utilizes repeated nested cross-validation to assess generalization performance. Uncertainty is quantified through out-of-fold predictions, the distribution of results across folds, and bootstrap-derived confidence intervals. Concurrently, a learning curve analysis is conducted to investigate the effects of varying training sample sizes on R² and RMSE metrics. Nonetheless, these methodological approaches primarily serve to elucidate performance variability inherent to small sample sizes and do not substitute for larger, more balanced engineering datasets with comprehensive variable documentation. Consequently, Sect. “Conclusions, limitations, and future work” of this paper addresses issues related to model extrapolation and emphasizes the necessity for future data augmentation efforts.

Input selection and physical relevance

In consideration of data availability and physical significance, five input variables were selected for analysis: unit weight (γ), cohesion (c), internal friction angle (φ), slope angle (α), and slope height (h), with the factor of safety (FS) serving as the output variable. These parameters were chosen due to their frequent reporting in both field and laboratory studies, their direct association with classical slope stability mechanics, and their consistent presence across the reviewed literature. Specifically, unit weight (γ) affects the gravitational driving forces; cohesion (c) and internal friction angle (φ) represent the Mohr–Coulomb shear strength parameters governing resistance; and slope angle (α) along with slope height (h) characterize the slope geometry, thereby influencing stress distribution and the potential development of slip surfaces.

Various factors—including pore water pressure, groundwater levels, rainfall infiltration, drainage conditions, seismic influences, stratigraphic configurations, discontinuities, and material anisotropy—play critical roles in determining slope stability. However, these parameters have not been uniformly documented across the reviewed literature cases. Mandating the inclusion of all such variables would further diminish the sample size and compromise the comparability among cases. Consequently, this study focuses on five variables that are relatively consistently reported across sources and exhibit clear mechanical relevance for modeling purposes. Thus, the relationships derived by the model should be interpreted as empirical correlations specific to the current dataset and factor of safety (FS) definitions, rather than as universal principles governing slope stability that fully capture the complex hydro-mechanical-structural interactions. For slopes influenced by groundwater, pore pressure, or structural discontinuities, independent validation through field monitoring and mechanistic modeling remains essential.

The safety factor distribution exhibits a broad range with a prominent peak near 1.5, suggesting that the majority of slopes maintain stability within this interval. The internal friction angle approximates a normal distribution, primarily concentrated between 20° and 40°, implying that most slope soils exhibit moderate to high frictional resistance. Methodology

Kernel extreme learning machine

The Extreme Learning Machine (ELM), introduced by Huang et al.35,36, is a learning algorithm designed for single-hidden-layer feedforward neural networks. The architecture comprises an input layer, a single hidden layer, and an output layer. In comparison to traditional feedforward neural networks, ELM generally offers accelerated training processes, mitigates the likelihood of convergence to local minima, and exhibits reduced sensitivity to the selection of learning rates. These benefits primarily arise from its distinctive training approach: the weights connecting the input layer to the hidden layer, as well as the biases within the hidden layer, are randomly assigned and subsequently held constant, while only the output weights are determined through an analytical solution. Given a predetermined number of hidden neurons, ELM produces a unique solution.

To enhance search stability and prevent premature convergence, this study employs the Quantum Heuristic Multi-strategy Hybrid Dung Beetle Optimizer (QHDBO) framework, as introduced by Zhu et al.40, for the hyperparameter optimization of Kernel Extreme Learning Machines (KELM). The QHDBO framework incorporates three principal enhancements: first, it initializes the population using a set of optimal points to improve coverage and uniformity; second, it modulates the balance between exploration and exploitation through a nonlinear convergence factor combined with a dynamic threshold mechanism; third, it integrates t-distribution mutation, probabilistic perturbation, and rotational update strategies within the iterative process to increase candidate solution diversity and mitigate the risk of entrapment in local optima. It is important to clarify that the term “quantum heuristic” in this context serves as an analogical descriptor within optimization algorithm design. All operations are executed via classical random search techniques on conventional CPU architectures, without involving physical qubits, genuine quantum gate operations, quantum entanglement, or quantum computing hardware. Consequently, this approach should be interpreted as a method to enhance population diversity, global search efficacy, and convergence robustness in classical swarm intelligence algorithms, rather than as a means of achieving acceleration through quantum computation.

QHDBO-KELM coupling and optimization objective

To mitigate the challenges associated with searching and numerical instability arising from KELM hyperparameters spanning different orders of magnitude, the regularization coefficient and kernel parameter are optimized within a logarithmic space. This approach effectively reduces sensitivity to scale variations, enabling the search process to occur within a smoother parameter landscape. Additionally, it facilitates the establishment of search boundaries confined to a reasonable range of magnitudes, which is advantageous in engineering applications. Consequently, this strategy enhances both optimization efficiency and reproducibility. For each outer fold, given a candidate parameter vector, KELM training and validation are conducted on the outer training set, followed by the computation of the average performance across the inner cross-validation (Inner CV) folds, which serves as the fitness measure.

Unlike approaches that focus solely on minimizing training error, this study explicitly defines the optimization objective as maximizing the average R² obtained from inner-layer cross-validation. This formulation encourages the search process to identify parameter combinations that are both more stable and generalizable, thereby mitigating the risk of overfitting during parameter tuning, particularly in scenarios involving small sample sizes. Additionally, to prevent redundancy arising from repeated evaluations of identical parameter sets within the same iteration of the meta-heuristic algorithm, a memoization mechanism is incorporated into the fitness function. This strategy reduces computational overhead and enhances the efficiency of parameter optimization. In the implementation of QHDBO-KELM, a multi-restart strategy is employed to bolster the reliability of global optimization. Once a candidate optimal solution is identified, it undergoes refined adjustment through a deterministic local coordinate search, which progressively decreases the step size in logarithmic space to further minimize parameter fluctuations induced by stochastic search processes. Through this integrated design, QHDBO not only aims to identify solutions with lower error but also strives to discover more robust and generalizable models, thereby aligning the optimization objectives and validation methods with practical engineering requirements.

Strict no-leakage repeated nested cross-validation

Addressing the challenges associated with small sample sizes, the potential occurrence of “lucky partitions” arising from single random splits, and the risk of data leakage introduced by normalization procedures, this study proposes a novel methodological framework. Specifically, the conventional single training/test partition approach is supplanted by a repeated nested K-fold cross-validation (CV) scheme. The outer cross-validation layer employs K = 5 folds, repeated R = 30 times, yielding a total of 150 outer test folds. This extensive partitioning facilitates a robust evaluation of model stability and generalization performance across diverse data splits. Within each outer fold, an inner 3-fold cross-validation is conducted exclusively for hyperparameter tuning of KELM-like models, ensuring that the outer test fold remains entirely excluded from any parameter optimization process. Crucially, all data preprocessing strictly adheres to the leak-free principle of “training set fitting, test set application.” For the KELM family models (including KELM, dbo-KELM, and QHDBO-KELM), feature scaling and label mapping to the interval [-1, 1] are performed using the MAPMINMAX function calibrated solely on the outer training set; the resulting transformation parameters are then applied to the corresponding outer test set. Subsequently, z-score normalization parameters (mean and standard deviation) are computed exclusively from the outer training set and applied to the test set. This procedure effectively eliminates the inadvertent incorporation of test set distributional information during training, which commonly arises when normalization is performed on the entire dataset prior to partitioning. To facilitate unbiased diagnostic evaluation and error analysis, the study further aggregates predicted values for each sample across all repeated outer folds in which it serves as a test instance, thereby generating out-of-fold (OOF) predictions. These OOF predictions enable accurate estimation of test sample errors and support subsequent analyses, including residual distribution assessment, identification of failure cases, and critical interval error analysis near FS ≈ 1. Importantly, this approach methodologically mitigates the optimistic bias associated with repeated use of the full dataset for validation purposes.

Model comparison

To perform a comparative assessment of the predictive capabilities of the proposed QHDBO-KELM model, this study selects several widely utilized machine learning algorithms for benchmarking. Each model is evaluated using an identical outer-layer data partitioning scheme and adheres to a leak-free protocol, wherein preprocessing parameters are exclusively derived from the training subset and subsequently applied to the test subset. Both model training and hyperparameter optimization are restricted to the respective outer-layer training data. The models included in the comparison are KELM, DBO-KELM, QHDBO-KELM, Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and Support Vector Regression (SVR). For KELM, DBO-KELM, and QHDBO-KELM, key hyperparameters were identified through inner-loop cross-validation, whereas RF, GBDT, MLP, and SVR were configured using reproducible experimental protocols within training folds and hyperparameter tuning conducted over a constrained search space. Given the inherent differences in parameter configurations, search domains, training methodologies, and computational resources across these models, the comparative results presented herein primarily serve to demonstrate the relative performance of each algorithm under a consistent outer validation framework and the specific experimental conditions established in this investigation.

Evaluation metrics, statistical tests, and uncertainty quantification

Given that engineering decisions exhibit heightened sensitivity when the factor of safety (FS) approaches 1.0, this study conducts a detailed evaluation of the root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) within the critical band defined by |FS − 1.0| ≤ 0.10. Statistical significance tests

To examine whether variations in model performance predominantly arise from random data partitioning, this study performed paired statistical comparisons across 150 outer-layer test folds. Given that all models within a single outer-layer fold utilize identical training and test sets, fold-by-fold comparisons can partially control for the influence of sample partitioning on the results. Both paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were employed: the paired t-test assessed whether the mean difference in performance between folds significantly deviated from zero, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test provided a nonparametric alternative suitable for situations with minimal assumptions regarding the underlying distribution. It is important to acknowledge that the outer folds generated through repeated nested cross-validation are not fully independent, as different folds share some original samples; consequently, the associated p-values may be somewhat optimistic. Therefore, this study does not rely exclusively on significance testing as the sole criterion for evaluation. Instead, it offers a comprehensive interpretation by integrating average performance metrics, standard deviations, effect directionality, distributional patterns across folds, and practical engineering relevance.

Table 3 presents a comprehensive summary of the overall predictive performance, performance within the FS ≈ 1 critical band, and computational overhead associated with each model, as evaluated through repeated nested cross-validation. The findings indicate that QHDBO-KELM attains the highest mean R² value (0.80334) alongside the lowest root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) across global metrics. This suggests that, given the dataset and validation framework employed in this study, the optimization of KELM hyperparameters via QHDBO significantly enhances overall prediction accuracy. When compared to both the unoptimized KELM and DBO-KELM models, QHDBO-KELM further reduces global error metrics, demonstrating its superiority in the optimization of regularization coefficients and kernel parameters. Within the engineering-sensitive FS ≈ 1 critical range, the interpretation of results warrants greater caution. As shown in Table 3, the multilayer perceptron (MLP) model achieves the lowest RMSE_FS ≈ 1 and MAE_FS ≈ 1 values, outperforming QHDBO-KELM in this localized context. This performance may be attributed to the gradient-based optimization inherent to MLP, which facilitates strong local fitting capabilities for a limited number of boundary samples. However, the stringent critical band defined by |FS − 1.0| ≤ 0.10 encompasses only four original samples, thereby constraining the generalizability of this local advantage. Consequently, it is not justifiable to infer superior overall engineering reliability of MLP based solely on these localized metrics. Conversely, although QHDBO-KELM does not achieve optimal performance within the critical band according to local metrics, it demonstrates robust performance in global measures such as R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. Furthermore, it exhibits relative stability in sensitivity analyses when the critical band is expanded. These observations suggest that QHDBO-KELM offers a well-balanced trade-off between global predictive accuracy and stability within the critical region.

Table 4 presents a detailed comparison of the per-fold error differences between QHDBO-KELM and the baseline KELM. For both RMSE and MAE metrics, the tests consistently produce p-values well below the 0.05 threshold, suggesting that, within the repeated partitioning framework employed in this study, QHDBO-KELM demonstrates a statistically significant and directionally consistent reduction in error relative to KELM. Table 5 further illustrates that QHDBO-KELM shows significant performance differences compared to DBO-KELM, RF, GBDT, MLP, and SVR across multiple global evaluation metrics, including R², RMSE, and MAE, in the majority of cases. However, given the inherent correlation among repeated cross-validation folds and the variability in the number of hyperparameters, search spaces, and tuning budgets across different model families, these statistical findings are interpreted as supportive evidence rather than definitive conclusions. Future investigations may benefit from employing methodologies such as corrected repeated-sampling tests, hierarchical statistical modeling, or multiple comparison correction techniques to derive more robust and reliable statistical inferences.

Runtime comparison and practical deployment considerations

Figure 16 depicts the trade-off between average prediction error and computational time for each evaluated model. The horizontal axis denotes the average runtime per outer fold, while the vertical axis represents the average root mean square error (RMSE). Notably, the QHDBO-KELM model occupies the region corresponding to the lowest prediction errors, achieving the minimum average RMSE among all models tested. This outcome suggests that QHDBO-KELM attains superior predictive accuracy within the experimental framework of this study. However, its computational time exceeds that of KELM, DBO-KELM, Random Forest (RF), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), indicating that the QHDBO hyperparameter optimization incurs additional computational overhead. Conversely, KELM and DBO-KELM demonstrate reduced computation times but at the cost of marginally increased prediction errors. The MLP model exhibits the shortest runtime but suffers from a substantially higher RMSE compared to QHDBO-KELM. Support Vector Regression (SVR) requires the longest runtime without achieving the lowest error, reflecting a relatively unfavorable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency in this experimental context. Both RF and Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT) show higher prediction errors, with GBDT also incurring comparatively longer runtimes. In summary, while QHDBO-KELM does not minimize computational cost, it delivers the lowest overall RMSE within a runtime measured in seconds. Consequently, this model is particularly well-suited for applications such as offline modeling, comparative scheme evaluation, and batch processing, where high predictive accuracy is prioritized and moderate computational expense is acceptable. In contrast, for real-time early warning systems or large-scale rapid screening tasks, model selection should be guided by the available computational resources and the required response times.

Conclusions, limitations, and future work

This study introduces a QHDBO-KELM framework designed for the quantitative prediction of slope safety factors (FS). The framework integrates the Kernel Extreme Learning Machine (KELM) with a classical variant of the Quantum Heuristic Multi-Strategy Dung Beetle Optimization (QHDBO) algorithm to concurrently optimize the regularization coefficient and kernel parameters of KELM. Utilizing a small, heterogeneous dataset consisting of 55 slope instances, the research assesses model performance and uncertainty through rigorous methods including leak-free repeated nested cross-validation, out-of-fold prediction, bootstrap confidence intervals, learning curves, and critical band error analysis. The principal findings are summarized as follows:

Overall Prediction Accuracy and Stability. Across 30 iterations of 5-fold out-of-fold cross-validation, the QHDBO-KELM model demonstrated superior performance on global evaluation metrics, achieving the highest mean R² alongside reduced values of RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. These findings suggest that, within the context of the dataset and experimental framework employed in this study, the optimization of KELM hyperparameters via QHDBO contributes to enhanced overall predictive accuracy and greater stability in parameter search. In comparison to both the standard KELM and DBO-KELM models, QHDBO-KELM exhibited lower error rates, indicating that its multi-strategy search mechanism effectively mitigates sensitivity to individual parameter configurations.Statistical Testing and Uncertainty Interpretation. Given the engineering significance of the region proximate to FS ≈ 1, this study conducted a focused examination of prediction errors within varying critical bandwidths: |FS − 1.0| ≤ 0.10, ± 0.15, and ± 0.20. It was observed that the narrowest band (|FS − 1.0| ≤ 0.10) encompassed only four original samples, suggesting that results within this interval should be interpreted primarily as indicative for near-threshold diagnostic purposes. This study presents several limitations. Although 55 case studies were assessed for robustness through repeated nested cross-validation, out-of-fold prediction, learning curves, and bootstrap confidence intervals, the dataset remains relatively small and heterogeneous. Consequently, the proposed model is primarily suited as a rapid tool for factor of safety (FS) estimation within comparable data domains. Due to inconsistencies in variable reporting within the public literature, this investigation included only unit weight, cohesion, internal friction angle, slope angle, and slope height as input variables, while factors such as pore pressure, groundwater conditions, rainfall, drainage, structural discontinuities, and reinforcement measures were not uniformly incorporated. Therefore, for complex slope stability assessments, a comprehensive approach integrating the limit equilibrium method, strength reduction method, field investigations, and monitoring data remains necessary. Additionally, discrepancies in parameter tuning ranges and computational demands across different model families imply that model comparisons should be interpreted as relative performances under the specific experimental conditions of this study. The limited sample size within the critical FS ≈ 1 zone restricts the evaluation of model performance near this threshold, necessitating further validation with additional critical samples.

Future research directions include: (1) developing a larger, more balanced slope database with comprehensive variable records, emphasizing groundwater, pore pressure, rainfall, drainage, seismic activity, stratigraphy, discontinuities, and reinforcement measures; (2) implementing stratified modeling approaches for earth slopes, rock slopes, homogeneous versus layered slopes, and drained versus undrained conditions to mitigate uncertainties arising from heterogeneous operating environments; (3) conducting targeted data collection or active sampling focused on the critical FS ≈ 1 region to enhance the statistical reliability of near-threshold model assessments; (4) performing systematic hyperparameter optimization across all baseline models within a consistent computational budget to enable fairer model comparisons; (5) applying advanced statistical techniques such as corrected repeated-sampling tests, hierarchical models, and multiple comparison corrections to strengthen the robustness of statistical inferences; and (6) integrating mechanical constraints, physics-inspired features, physics-informed neural networks, or prediction interval methodologies to improve the models’ engineering interpretability, uncertainty quantification, and risk decision-support capabilities. Overall, the QHDBO-KELM model offers a valuable reference tool for rapid FS estimation, preliminary screening, and auxiliary ranking within similar data domains; however, its practical engineering application should be complemented by field investigations, monitoring data, and mechanistic analyses.
