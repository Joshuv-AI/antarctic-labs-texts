Neurodevelopmental disorder–linked Argonaute mutations permit delayed RISC formation and unusual shortening of miRNAs by 3′→5′ trimming
Authors: Andrew Savidge, Huaqun Zhang, Vishal Annasaheb Adhav, Audrey C. Kehling, GeunYoung Sim, Zhangfei Shen, Tian-Min Fu, Kotaro Nakanishi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mutations in Argonaute proteins (AGOs) cause Argonaute syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD), yet the underlying molecular mechanism remains unclear. We determined cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of wild-type (WT) AGO1 and the recurrent, severe causative ΔF180 mutant. AGO1(ΔF180) unexpectedly rearranges the hydrophobic core of the L1 domain to preserve the overall RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISC) architecture and recognize guide RNAs similarly to WT. Functionally, the mutant binds target RNAs like WT but more frequently retains passenger strands, implicating a defect in RISC assembly. Biochemical assays of ΔF180 and L190P mutants revealed normal duplex loading but impaired passenger ejection, exposing guide 3′ ends to 3′→5′ exonucleases and generating abnormally short ~11-nucleotide RNAs. In cells, AGO1(ΔF180), AGO1(G199S), and corresponding AGO2 mutants likewise produced truncated guides. Together, these findings support a model in which defective passenger ejection underlies the pathology of AGO-associated NDDs by rendering microRNAs bound to AGO syndrome mutants susceptible to aberrant 3′ trimming.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are 18 to 23 nucleotide (nt) noncoding RNAs that silence genes posttranscriptionally by providing target specificity to Argonaute proteins (AGOs) within RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISCs) (1). Links between AGO mutations and NDDs were first identified in patients with deletions encompassing AGO1 and AGO3 genes (2) or point mutations in AGO1 gene, including ΔF180, L190P, and G199S (3). Whole-exome sequencing has since revealed de novo AGO1 variants strongly associated with autism spectrum-like disorders, collectively termed Argonaute syndrome (3, 4). Comparative studies also show significant overlap of coding variants in AGO1 and AGO2, with many clustering near the L1 domain (3, 4). Despite this genetic evidence, the mechanistic consequences of pathogenic AGO mutations remain poorly understood.

Results

We selected AGO1(ΔF180), one of the most recurrent NDD mutations (3), and purified it as guide-loaded RISC (5). Cryo-EM structures revealed that AGO1(ΔF180) adopts an architecture similar to AGO1(WT)-RISC (Fig. 1 A and B), with both proteins showing a relaxed conformation compared to the previously reported crystal structure (6). The L1 domain forms the core of the N–PAZ lobe by contacting the N and PAZ domains (Fig. 1 A and B). In the WT structure, F180 participates in a hydrophobic network with V175, W197, T220, and F222, stabilizing a three-layer stack formed by P182, P183, W197, and F222 (Fig. 1D and Movie S1). In AGO1(ΔF180), this aromatic interaction is lost, but nearby residues rearrange to reconstitute the hydrophobic core (Fig. 1C and Movie S2). As a result, AGO1(ΔF180) preserves the overall RISC architecture. This includes intact tryptophan-binding pockets for TNRC6 interaction, consistent with a report that the corresponding pathogenic AGO2 mutant, AGO2(ΔF182), still interacts with TNRC6 proteins (4). Since these pockets form during RISC assembly (5), the structure demonstrates that the mutant still retains the ability to form mature RISC.

Filter-binding assays confirmed that AGO1(WT) and AGO1(ΔF180) loaded with the same 23-nt miR-20a recognized three target RNAs with similar affinities (Fig. 1E). However, when expressed in HEK293T cells, AGO1(ΔF180) bound more passenger strands relative to WT for let-7a (Fig. 1F), a trend also seen in another mutant, AGO1(G199S). These results are consistent with a preceding study in Caenorhabditis elegans where Argonaute-like gene 1 (ALG-1) mutants mimicking the two AGO1 mutants misload miRNAs and repress inappropriate targets (7) or indicate that the passenger strand was not ejected efficiently. Therefore, we hypothesized that the mutants have a deficiency in forming the correct RISC.

We next tested whether defects occur before mature RISC formation. Duplex-loading assays showed that WT and ΔF180 AGO1 incorporate siRNA duplexes equally well (Fig. 2A), but the mutant inefficiently ejected the passenger strand (Fig. 2B). A similar defect was observed for AGO1(L190P) (Fig. 2C). Despite residing in distinct structural regions (Fig. 2D), both mutants slowed RISC maturation after duplex loading, implicating impaired passenger ejection as a common pathogenic mechanism. Passenger removal is known to require the N domain to wedge open the duplex, allowing the guide 3′ end to dock in the PAZ domain (8). To probe this step, we exposed RISC intermediates to the 3′→5′ exonuclease ISG20 (9). With single-stranded guide RNAs, both WT and ΔF180 AGO1 protected the 3′ end (Fig. 2 E and F, Top). When duplex-loaded, WT AGO1 prevented further trimming, indicating that it quickly formed mature RISC (Fig. 2 E, Bottom). In contrast, AGO1(ΔF180) produced 16- and 11-nt fragments because it retained duplexes longer, exposing the guide 3′ end to ISG20 (Fig. 2 F, Bottom). These findings indicate that the mutant lingers in the duplex-bound state (Fig. 2G).

Because cellular cofactors such as chaperones promote RISC assembly (10, 11), we examined whether they could rescue the defect. FLAG-tagged AGO1(WT) or AGO1(ΔF180) was expressed in HEK293T cells with a radiolabeled 23-nt miR-20a duplex. In WT, bound guides largely remained full-length, even with ISG20 coexpression (Fig. 2H). In ΔF180, however, guides were consistently shortened to 19 nt, and ISG20 coexpression further trimmed them to 11 nt (Fig. 2H). Similar results were obtained with AGO1(G199S), as well as with corresponding AGO2 mutants (ΔF182, G201V) (Fig. 2 H and I). Testing with miR-124-3p, a brain-enriched miRNA (12), yielded the same trimming defect (Fig. 2J). Overall, duplexes bound to AGO syndrome mutants are trimmed differently from those bound to their WT counterparts, regardless of the mutation site or miRNA species. This consistent difference further supports the idea that a prolonged duplex-bound state during RISC assembly is a plausible pathogenic mechanism underlying AGO syndrome.

Discussion

ISG20 expression trimmed guide RNAs to 11 nt, likely preventing AGO1 syndrome mutants from completing RISC assembly, since at least 18 nt are required for capture by the PAZ domain (13). Although ISG20 induction in AGO syndrome patients remains to be studied, more abnormally short RNAs would be generated under stress or viral infection, where ISG20 is strongly induced (14, 15); thus, AGO1 mutants may function as miRNA sponges (Fig. 2G). Our results do not exclude the possibility that NDD mutations may impact any downstream events following RISC maturation. But prolonged passenger ejection combined with ISG20 induction could create a detrimental effect that yields unusually short guide RNAs and further compromises RISC maturation, suggesting a plausible pathological mechanism for AGO1-associated NDD.
