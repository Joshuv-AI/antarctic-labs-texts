Improved detection of Listeria monocytogenes outbreaks using whole genome sequencing, Gipuzkoa, Northern Spain, 2010 to 2022
Authors: Pedro Vallejo, Alexandra Moura, Maddi López-Olaizola, Alexandre Leclercq, Diego Vicente, Marc Lecuit, José María Marimón
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundListeria monocytogenes is a major foodborne pathogen responsible for severe infections, mainly affecting pregnant women, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals. In Spain, listeriosis has been insufficiently studied using modern molecular techniques, and genomic data from clinical isolates remain scarce. Long‑term genomic surveillance based on whole genome sequencing (WGS) has not yet been systematically applied. WGS has proven to be a powerful method for outbreak detection and for revealing transmission dynamics that remain hidden to conventional epidemiology. This study aimed to characterise clinical L. monocytogenes isolates from Gipuzkoa, a region in northern Spain, collected over more than a decade, and to investigate their genetic diversity, phylogenetic relationships, and virulence traits.ResultsBetween 2010 and 2022, a total of 131 laboratory‑confirmed human listeriosis cases were diagnosed in Gipuzkoa. Among them, 127 genomes were successfully sequenced and analysed by core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) approaches. Genomic analysis revealed that 47 cases (36%) were part of nine distinct clusters, some extending across periods of up to nine years, while 84 cases appeared sporadic. Importantly, several outbreaks previously considered independent were shown to originate from genetically related strains, highlighting the persistence of specific clones. By comparing our dataset with publicly available international L. monocytogenes genomes, we identified putative foodborne sources for human infections and demonstrated cross‑border connections with lineages circulating in other countries. Additionally, antibiotic resistance determinants were detected infrequently, in line with the low resistance rates described for L. monocytogenes.ConclusionsThis study represents one of the most comprehensive WGS‑based studies of listeriosis to date in Spain. Our findings demonstrate that more than one third of apparently sporadic cases were in fact part of persistent or recurrent genomic clusters, underscoring the hidden transmission potential of this pathogen. WGS analysis revealed links between cases that would not have been detected through routine epidemiology, providing critical insights into outbreak dynamics and long‑term circulation of L. monocytogenes. This regional study highlights the importance of implementing genomic surveillance for listeriosis and integrating all parts of the infection chain — from clinical diagnosis to food production and public health — to better guide responses and strengthen prevention and control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04585-1.Keywords: Listeriosis, Listeria monocytogenes, WGS, One health, Molecular epidemiology, Neurolisteriosis, Bacteraemia

Background

Listeriosis is a zoonotic foodborne disease caused by Listeria monocytogenes with a high case fatality rate and leading to serious consequences for pregnant women, fetuses and newborns [1]. In recent years, the incidence of listeriosis in the European Union (EU) has shown an upward trend, which remains consistent with hospitalizations and mortality rates higher than other foodborne diseases [2, 3]. In Spain, the increasing number of susceptible people, as elderly and immunosuppressed, together with the increasing consumption of ready to eat food, has contributed to the increase in the incidence of listeriosis [3].

Most human cases of listeriosis are thought to be sporadic. However, listeriosis outbreaks affecting many people cause a great social alarm due to the possible fatal consequences, as in Spain, where a listeriosis outbreak in 2019 caused over 3,000 suspected cases, 141 hospitalizations, 34 miscarriages and 3 deaths [4]. Listeriosis is in Spain a mandatory notifiable disease since 2015 and the implementation of human and food epidemiological surveillance is delegated to autonomous communities [5, 6].

The implementation of whole genome sequencing (WGS) as routine typing tool has demonstrated its usefulness in the investigation of L. monocytogenes outbreaks as well as its ability to analyse the population structure and diversity in the surveillance of sporadic cases. Compared to the previous typing techniques such as Pulsed-field Gel Electrophoresis (PFGE) or Multi-locus Sequence Typing (MLST) [7], WGS is more powerful in terms of discriminatory power and evolutionary relationships [8]. WGS also provides a better understanding of the epidemiology of listeriosis, a greater ability to detect clusters of cases and outbreaks and their food origin, and the possibility to determine and study L. monocytogenes genomes. Moreover, the use of WGS makes it possible to better trace the routes of local and widespread transmission of L. monocytogenes.

In Gipuzkoa, (Basque Country, north of Spain), with a population of around 700,000 inhabitants, listeriosis annual incidence is estimated at 2 cases per 100,000 inhabitants in the period 2010–2020 [9], higher than that reported in the EU [3]. In a previous work, we analysed listeriosis epidemiology in Gipuzkoa based on MLST and PFGE analysis [9]. Here we leverage the increased discriminatory power of WGS to uncover the diversity and virulence potential of L. monocytogenes circulating strains and to track how infection clusters spread across different types of clinical infections and patient conditions.

Methods

Case definition

A listeriosis case was defined as a patient fulfilling both clinical and laboratory criteria. Clinical criteria included septicemia, meningitis/meningoencephalitis, or signs of localized infection. Laboratory confirmation required either isolation of L. monocytogenes or detection of its DNA by PCR from sterile sites, or from non-sterile sites within 24 h postpartum in pregnancy-related cases. For non-pregnancy cases, culture from non-sterile sites was also accepted if consistent with the clinical presentation. For pregnancy-related cases, a single episode was counted regardless of whether samples were obtained from the mother, the newborn, or both.

Isolates and typing

The study included all L. monocytogenes isolates collected from human samples of listeriosis cases diagnosed between 2010 and 2022 in Gipuzkoa, Basque Country, Spain. All isolates were identified, typed and genotyped as previously described [9]. Briefly, Gram-positive rods isolated from blood (BACTEC FX blood culture system BD, New Jersey, USA) or from other biological samples (Columbia Agar with 5% Sheep Blood, BD) were identified using the using the API20 Corynebacterium System (bioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France) or, since 2013, by MALDI-TOF using the Flex Analysis and MALDI Biotyper 3.1 software and library (version 3.1.66, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Serotyping was performed using Listeria-O-Antisera (Difco; BD Diagnostics, Sparks, MD, USA). PFGE was performed and interpreted as described [10].

Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS)

Frozen isolates stored at −80ºC were cultured on Columbia Agar with 5% Sheep Blood (BD) and incubated overnight at 35ºC. Bacterial DNA was extracted using the QIAmp® DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA was quantified by Qubit fluorometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and sequenced on Illumina platforms. Paired-end 150 bp libraries were prepared with Nextera XT or Illumina library kits, per manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on Illumina MiSeq, iSeq, and NextSeq 500 platforms (Illumina Inc., San Diego, US).

Raw read qualities were assessed using FastQC v0.11.9 and quality trimming of FASTQ reads were made using Trimmomatic v0.39 and fqCleaner v21.10 with a quality threshold of 20, minimum read length of 30, percentage of confident bases of 50 and minimum read coverage of 70. Trimmed reads were assembled with SPAdes v3.14.0 and filtered reads were mapped in SPAdes assemblies using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) v0.7.17 and SAMtools v1.16. Contigs obtained of assembly were assessed with Quality Assesment Tool for Genome Assembles (QUAST) v5.0.2. Correction and polishing of spades assemblies was made using Pilon v1.23 and contigs of less than 300 nucleotides were discarded for downstream analysis.

Based on Illumina WGS, four isolates from the outbreaks (two containing the phage and two without it) were also sequenced using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) to obtain circularized genomes to better localize the position of the phage within the genome. These libraries were prepared using the Rapid Barcoding kit (SQK-RBK004) and loaded on MinION R9.4.1 flow cell for sequencing. Basecalling was performed from raw FAST5 files using Guppy v6.4.6 in high‑accuracy mode. Adapter trimming and quality filtering (minimum Q9) were conducted with Porechop v0.2.4 and Filtlong v0.2.1, retaining the 90% of reads longer than 1,000 bp. After filtering, the total ONT yield ranged between ~ 0.11–0.64 Gbp per isolate (median read length ≈ 7–10 kbp, N50 ≈ 13–17 kbp, median read quality ≈ 13). Hybrid assemblies (Illumina and Nanopore reads) were performed using Unicycler v.0.5.0 within the Galaxy Europe server (https://usegalaxy.eu) using default parameters. Genome circularisation was assessed using Bandage v0.9.0, confirming the recovery of four complete circular chromosomes. Nanopore sequence reads have been deposited in ENA (BioProject PRJEB98371) together with Illumina datasets.

Sequence analysis

PCR serogrouping, MLST and core genome MLST (cgMLST) analyses were performed using BIGSdb-Lm platform [7] (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/listeria/) v.1.42.0, to determine for each assembled genome its PCR serogroup, MLST sequence type (ST), clonal complex (CC), sublineage (SL) and cgMLST type (CT). Presence of putative phage sequences was screened using PHAge Search Tool with Enhanced Sequence Translation (PHASTEST) [11]. Presence of plasmids and antibiotic resistance genes were assessed using MOB-suite [12] v.3.0.1 and ABRicate [13] v.1.0.1 (against databases of PlasmidFinder [14], ARG-ANNOT [15], CARD [16], Resfinder [17], MEGARES [18], NCBI [19] and BIGSdb-Lm [20]), respectively.

Clusters of isolates likely to share a common epidemiological source were defined using the cut-off of ≤ 7 allelic differences (AD) out of 1748 cgMLST loci as proposed by Moura et al. [7].

For each cgMLST cluster, whole genome single nucleotide SNP (wgSNP) was performed with snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy), using the oldest isolate within each cluster as reference for read mapping.

Results

Between 2010 and 2022, 131 cases of human listeriosis were diagnosed in Gipuzkoa, including 27 pregnancy-related and 104 non-pregnancy-associated infections (Fig. 1A, Table 1). Key demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as outcomes, are summarized in Table 1. In the group of perinatal infections 27 cases occurred, diagnosed in 23 mothers and 4 foetuses. Overall consequences of the cases were 27 mothers and 31 foetuses affected. Of the 131 isolates, the genome of 127 L. monocytogenes was sequenced while four isolates could not be recovered for the study (from 2010, 2013, 2016 and 2019). Most isolates belonged to phylogenetic lineage I, with serogroups IVb and IIb being the most prevalent. Isolates belonged to 23 different clonal complexes defined by MLST and 25 sublineages defined by cgMLST, being Lineage I sublineages SL1/CC1 (22.8%) and SL87/CC87 (17.3%) the most frequent (Fig. 1B, Table 1). The 127 isolates were assigned to 86 different cgMLST types (CTs) (Supplementary Table 1), of which 58 had not been previously reported, 15 had already been reported in Spain and 13 elsewhere. Forty-seven isolates (37%) were grouped in 9 clusters, hereby named HUD #1 to #9, by decreasing order of cluster size, comprising 2 to 18 cases (median 3) and spanning 1 month to 9 years (Table 2). The remaining 80 isolates (63%) were considered as sporadic cases.

The largest cluster detected (HUD#1, cgMLST L1-SL87-ST87-CT4318) comprised 18 cases spanning from 2013 to 2020, with the majority of cases (n = 13) between the last quarter of 2013 and the first of 2014. Among the cases, eight were pregnancy-related, including one chorioamnionitis, three preterm births, and one fetal loss. Additionally, there were 8 cases of bacteremia, one neurolisteriosis, and one gastroenteritis. Five deaths occurred in the first year after infection (FYAI), two of them in the first month after infection (FMAI).

Interestingly, previous PFGE analyses based on SmaI and AscI restriction patterns, split the isolates of 2013 and 2014 into two clusters that differed in the size of a unique band (of around 260 and 300 Kb each of them). While all isolates shared an indistinguishable SmaI pattern, they differed by the AscI profile. As a result, due to the little geographical spread of the cases and the short period of time between them, the two PFGE clusters were considered independent at the time of investigations, and thirteen cases (August 2013 to February 2014) were associated to an outbreak related to a foie grass contamination, as previously published [10]. Results of WGS data analysis show that isolates of both PFGE patterns shared 0–2 cgMLST AD and 0–8 wgSNPs, confirming their closely genetic relationship and constituting only one cluster (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The genomic analyses showed a region spanning approximately 45,000 bp, which contained a putative prophage (50% of identity with LP-030–3, GenBank accession number NC_024384) in 10 strains of this cluster of cases (strains IDs: 103611, 103616, 103617, 103633, 103631, 103627, 103628, 103620, 103624, 103625; Supplementary Table 1). With the support of ONT sequencing, the integration of a phage between two cleavage regions of the enzyme AscI was confirmed, leading in PFGE results to the AscI band of 300 Kb within isolates collected between November 2013 and February 2014 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The acquisition of the phage did not produce changes in the clinical presentation or the severity of the cases.

The cluster with the longest timespan (HUD#4, cgMLST type L2-SL101-ST431,ST2177-CT1687), comprised 5 cases diagnosed 9 years apart (2013–2022), suggesting a long persistence of this strain in the source of infection. Three cases occurred in three consecutive months in 2016, but no common epidemiological data linking the cases nor a possible vehicle of ingestion have been discovered with the epidemiological surveys of the health department of the region (Table 2).

Similarly, for the remaining clusters, despite the carrying out of epidemiological surveys through standardized interviews by local health authorities at the time, no common epidemiologic link was found then or in retrospective analyses.

Genomic analysis also showed the presence of relevant traits of virulence along numerous isolates (eg. LIPI-4 in clusters HUD#1, cgMLST type L1-SL87-ST87-CT4318 and cluster HUD#7, cgMLST type L1-SL87-ST87-CT1742 and in SL219 isolates) and stress survival genomic islands (eg. LGI-3 in cluster HUD#4, cgMLST L2-SL101-ST431,ST2177-CT1687 and SSI-1, mainly in lineage I isolates).

Some isolates (15%) carried plasmids with genes related with stress and antibacterial survival capacities, highlighting cadA (cadmium-transporting ATPase), qacC (quaternary ammonium compound-resistance protein), copB (copper-exporting P-type ATPase B) or ebrB (multidrug resistance protein), among others (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1). Prophages were present in 61.4% isolates (Supplementary Table 1), but did not seem to impact in clinical presentations or bacterial fitness.

As previously reported [9], all isolates were susceptible to all antibiotics used in therapy, including ampicillin, gentamicin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (data not shown). Sporadic benzalkonium chloride resistance genes (bcrABC), erythromycin resistance (ermC) and efflux systems genes (qacA) were found in 9.4% isolates.

Discussion

In 2019, the largest listeriosis outbreak in Spain, affecting mainly the Andalucia region (cgMLST type L1-SL387-ST388-CT8466) caused by a contaminated stuffed pork [4] highlighted the importance of WGS for precise definition of cases and the need for coordinated national genomic surveillance.

Prior to WGS, L. monocytogenes epidemiological investigations were initiated based on serotyping, MLST, and PFGE to search the possible source of infection. That approach in Gipuzkoa had led to the confirmation of only one outbreak linked to a food (foie gras) in 2013–2014 [10] during the same period as this study. Refining analysis using WGS and cgMLST schemes now revealed that 9 outbreaks occurred during the 13 years period, representing the 37% of all the cases, a percentage consistent with other reports of national WGS-based surveillance programs [21]. In this work we adopted the internationally recognized cutoff of 7 or fewer AD to define clusters of isolates likely sharing a common epidemiological source, following reference approaches previously proposed [22]. The 7-AD cutoff has been previously shown to better capture clusters and aligning more closely with epidemiological findings while still maintaining a robust clustering accuracy [7]. Here, cases within clusters were often separated by several years, and no trace-back investigation could be performed, preventing any conclusions about the effectiveness of the use of more restricted cut-offs.

In the case of listeriosis, epidemiological surveys could have limited value due to the time elapsed between consuming the contaminated food and conducting the survey, as the incubation period is highly variable. Furthermore, the wide variety of foods acting as vehicles for the bacteria hampers the association of a case with a specific food. WGS-based clusters should be used routinely in our country to identify the microbiological relationships between isolates, limiting the investigation of epidemiological relations to closely related isolates, to save resources and reduce time of detection of related outbreaks [22]. WGS also allowed to better resolve previous thought independent outbreaks [10] due to PFGE different banding patterns. Retrospective analyses of epidemiological data on cgMLST type L1-SL87-ST87-CT4318 (cluster HUD#1) can now confirm the foie grass as the common source of contamination in the previously described two 2013–2014 outbreaks [23, 24], revealing a larger outbreak than previous thought. As per the outbreak investigation protocols in place at that time, interpretation criteria tolerated up to one or two band differences per enzyme in PFGE. Nevertheless, the divergence observed with AscI was considered sufficient to define two distinct clusters. Our findings highlight a well‑known limitation of PFGE: interpretations may differ when restriction patterns are identical with one enzyme (e.g., SmaI) but show minor variations with another (e.g., AscI), even among epidemiologically related isolates. In the 2013–2014 events, reliance on the AscI profile led to the classification of separate clusters, while subsequent WGS analysis revealed their close genetic relationship and clarified the actual outbreak structure. This observation is consistent with broader national experience, where the transition from PFGE to real‑time WGS has improved cluster delineation, outbreak detection, and reduced the ambiguity inherent to band‑based typing methods. Also, it reinforces the idea that it has to be considered that any outbreak investigation involves both epidemiological and microbiological analysis. Moreover, previous reports of the cgMLST type in Spanish farms [25] suggest a possible common source of dissemination to the food products that caused the largest outbreak in Gipuzkoa, tracing a possible route of transmission in the food chain.

We also observed the long persistence of the cgMSLT type L2-SL101-ST431,ST2177-CT1687 (cluster HUD#4), sampled for up to 9 years, suggestive of persistence in a food processing environment [26], reinforcing the idea that investigations in the food chain to identify the source of contamination should prevent more infections. As described in other works [27], lineage II isolates are more associated with food processing environments due to the presence of genetic traits conferring tolerance to disinfectants and/or metal resistance. Here all L2-SL101-ST431,ST2177-CT1687 isolates carried the virulence factor LGI-3, associated to cadmium resistance and strain persistence [28]. This cgMLST type had been also detected in 2011 in environmental swabs of a diverse food processing environment in Ireland [29], again pointing out that the food chain was a possible source of contamination.

Previously, we had described a large series of cases of ST219 (CC4) cases and observed its strong association to neurolisteriosis (p < 0.001) [9], already described in other studies [7]. With WGS data, we observed that all ST219 cases were genetically independent and not part of a single cluster. In terms of virulence, they all harboured the pathogenic island LIPI-4 that which presence has been shown to increase the tropism to the brain and the placenta of CC4 isolates [30], leading to more severe infections. Consistently, L1-SL87-ST87-CT4318 isolates, that also carried LIPI-4, were strongly associated to pregnancy related infections (p < 0.05).

Interestingly, other cgMLST types identified in this study, as L1-SL87-ST87-CT4318; L1-SL1-ST1-CT288 and L2-SL101-ST431,ST2177-CT1687, have been previously identified in animal farms or wild animals in Spain (Table 2), highlighting the importance of asymptomatic zoonotic reservoirs as source of contamination of the food chain.

A limitation of this study is that, although our genomes were compared with typed Listeria monocytogenes sequences in BIGSdb-Listeria, they were not compared against untyped genomes available in international databases. The scarcity of genomic data from Spain may also conceal possible local links between apparently sporadic isolates. Future analyses should incorporate comparative genomics with publicly available sequences (e.g., NCBI RefSeq or large-scale datasets) to better contextualize the Spanish isolates within the global L. monocytogenes population structure.

Conclusions

This study represents one of the largest studies on listeriosis carried out in Spain including clinical and WGS data. Our findings underline the value of local WGS surveillance and highlight the urgent need for centralized, WGS-based listeriosis surveillance systems. In the context of a globalized food industry, agile surveillance systems are essential. These systems, as part of a "One Health" approach, should routinely monitor listeriosis across clinical, veterinary, and food environments. Such comprehensive monitoring would enable a precise understanding of listeriosis dynamics and facilitate the rapid detection and prevention of outbreaks at national and international levels.
