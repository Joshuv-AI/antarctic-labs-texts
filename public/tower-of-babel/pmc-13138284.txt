Hemodynamic remnant drift after coil embolization: longitudinal silent MRA and computational fluid dynamics analysis of a migratory neck remnant. Illustrative case
Authors: Toru Satoh, Kana Murakami, Megumi Sasaki, Yudai Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDNeck remnants after coil embolization are usually attributed to coil compaction or aneurysm regrowth, but their longitudinal spatial behavior has rarely been examined.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report an unruptured posterior communicating artery aneurysm treated with coil embolization, followed by serial silent MR angiography (MRA) and computational fluid dynamics (CFD). Fusion imaging from an intradome perspective showed progressive migration of a small residual cavity, rather than concentric enlargement, along the aneurysm neck plane at 6, 12, and 18 months. CFD demonstrated that remnant location corresponded to temporal changes in inflow targeting, best reflected by the flow rate ratio, whereas wall shear stress magnitude alone did not correlate consistently with remnant evolution. Directional WSS patterns and streamlines suggested shifting hemodynamic guidance of the residual cavity. Endovascular coil embolization is a widely accepted treatment for intracranial aneurysms; however, neck remnants are not uncommon even after technically successful procedures. While many remnants remain stable or regress, a subset demonstrate delayed enlargement requiring retreatment. Traditionally, such changes have been attributed to coil compaction or insufficient packing density.1–4 Recent advances in noncontrast silent MR angiography (MRA), particularly when combined with MR cisternography and 3D fusion imaging, have enabled high-resolution visualization of coil-treated aneurysms with minimal metal artifacts. In parallel, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has provided quantitative assessment of aneurysmal hemodynamics, including wall shear stress (WSS) and inflow jet behavior.5–9 Despite these advances, the spatial and temporal relationships between postcoiling hemodynamics and remnant evolution remains poorly understood. Specifically, it is unclear whether remnant enlargement occurs at a fixed site or whether hemodynamic vulnerability migrates along the neck plane over time. Here, we present a unique case in which longitudinal silent MRA and CFD analysis demonstrated asymmetric and migratory remnant formation after coil embolization. Based on these observations, we propose a novel concept termed “remnant drift.”

Illustrative Case

A patient with an unruptured left posterior communicating artery aneurysm underwent simple coil embolization. Follow-up silent MRA combined with MR cisternography fusion imaging was performed at 6 (MR6), 12 (MR12), and 18 months (MR18). Three-dimensional reconstructions from an intradome perspective demonstrated dynamic change in remnant morphology. At MR6, a small remnant occupied a limited portion of the neck plane. At MR12, the remnant appeared at a different location along the neck plane rather than enlarging concentrically. At MR18, further protrusion occurred adjacent to the MR12 site, producing an elongated irregular remnant configuration (Fig. 2). The posterior communicating artery was visible at MR6 but was no longer visualized at MR12 and MR18. This finding was considered secondary to progressive thrombosis and flow alteration at the remnant-neck interface, consistent with local hemodynamic instability.

Patient-specific CFD simulations were performed using vascular geometries reconstructed from silent MRA datasets and analyzed with commercial software (Hemoscope version 1.4, EBM and AMIN Corp.), as previously described.8 Hemodynamic parameters included instantaneous WSS magnitude (Pa), WSS directional variability (degrees), and streamline patterns.

CFD analysis demonstrated temporal redistribution of inflow jet impingement zones and regions of elevated WSS directional variability that preceded subsequent remnant formation (Fig. 2). Quantitative analysis of normalized CFD parameters (Table 1) demonstrated progressive enlargement of the remnant volume, increasing from 15.14 mm3 at MR6 to 21.01 mm3 at MR12 and 31.75 mm3 at MR18. Consistent with this volumetric growth, the flow rate ratio—defined as inflow into the remnant relative to the parent artery (mL/min)—increased from 0.32 at MR6 to 0.38 at MR12 and 0.48 at MR18. In contrast, the mean, minimum, and maximum WSS magnitude ratios showed a different temporal pattern without a consistent increase. These findings suggest progressively enhanced hemodynamic communication between the parent artery and the remnant cavity. Discussion

The present case demonstrates that enlargement of a postcoiling neck remnant does not necessarily occur as concentric growth at a single fixed site. Instead, longitudinal fusion imaging revealed sequential positional changes of the residual cavity along the aneurysm neck plane, suggesting spatially migrating hemodynamic vulnerability.1,2,4,6

Because the remnant occupied only a small fraction of the total coiled dome volume, global coil compaction alone is unlikely to explain the observed changes. CFD analysis demonstrated temporal migration of inflow jet targeting and increased WSS directional variability preceding remnant formation.5–11 The flow rate ratio increased in parallel with remnant volume, indicating progressively enhanced hemodynamic coupling between the parent artery and the remnant cavity.

These findings raise the possibility that CFD-derived parameters obtained at the time of initial coiling may help identify aneurysms at risk for delayed recurrence. Although each individual hemodynamic force is modest, the cumulative effect of repetitive focal loading—analogous to repeated mechanical “microjabs”—may induce gradual micro-rearrangement at the coil-wall interface and progressive wall remodeling, eventually manifesting as morphologically apparent remnant enlargement.

We propose the term “remnant drift” to describe this phenomenon, in which hemodynamic vulnerability migrates along the neck plane over time (Fig. 3). Although CFD studies often focus on WSS magnitude, vascular wall stress (VWS) related to systemic blood pressure is orders of magnitude greater than WSS.8 Therefore, WSS patterns may primarily indicate where hemodynamic forces are directed, whereas pressure-related VWS determines how much the vessel wall deforms. This complementary relationship may explain why spatial redistribution of WSS patterns preceded remnant growth in the present case.

Clinically, combined silent MRA and MR cisternography fusion imaging enabled visualization of subtle early remnant changes not readily detected by conventional angiography. Recognition of remnant drift may help identify aneurysms requiring closer surveillance. This concept may also apply to contemporary endovascular techniques, including stent-assisted coiling and intrasaccular devices, whenever microscopic flow communication persists along the neck plane.13–17

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present CFD analysis focused on relative and longitudinal redistribution of hemodynamic parameters rather than absolute quantitative thresholds. Larger studies are required to define predictive cutoff values. Second, although silent MRA provided adequate visualization in this case, depiction capability may be reduced after certain treatments, such as flow diverter placement, potentially limiting applicability. Finally, this report represents a single case, and validation in larger cohorts is required.

Observations

Serial silent MRA and MR cisternography fusion imaging combined with CFD demonstrated that the neck remnant migrated along the neck plane over time rather than enlarging concentrically. Redistribution of inflow jet targeting and increased WSS directional variability preceded remnant protrusion.

Lessons

Neck remnants after coil embolization may evolve through spatial migration of hemodynamic loading rather than concentric enlargement at a fixed site. Longitudinal fusion imaging may detect subtle preclinical changes preceding overt recurrence. Recognition of remnant drift may improve risk stratification and surveillance strategies after endovascular aneurysm treatment.
