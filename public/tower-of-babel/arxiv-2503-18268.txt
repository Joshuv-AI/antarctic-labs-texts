A proof of the multi-component q q -Baker–Forrester conjecture
Yue Zhou

Abstract
. The Selberg integral, an n n -dimensional generalization of the Euler beta integral, plays a central role in random matrix theory, Calogero–Sutherland quantum many body systems, Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equations, and multivariable orthogonal polynomial theory. The Selberg integral is known to be equivalent to the Morris constant term identity. In 1998, Baker and Forrester conjectured a ( p + 1 ) (p+1) -component generalization of the q q -Morris identity. It in turn yields a generalization of the Selberg integral. The p = 1 p=1 case of Baker and Forrester’s conjecture was proved by Károlyi, Nagy, Petrov and Volkov in 2015. In this paper, we give a proof of the ( p + 1 ) (p+1) -component q q -Baker–Forrester conjecture, thereby settling this 26-year-old conjecture. Keywords: Selberg integral; q q -Morris identity; q q -Baker–Forrester conjecture; constant term identity; splitting formula; iterated Laurent series.

1.
Introduction

In this introduction, we first briefly review the main developments in the area of constant term identities.
The study of constant term identities dated back to Freeman Dyson, who conjectured his now famous constant term (ex)-conjecture
[
10
]
in 1962.

Theorem 1.1

(Dyson’s ex-conjecture)
.

For nonnegative integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,

(1.1)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}(1-x_{i}/x_{j})^{a_{i}}=\frac{(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n})!}{a_{1}!\cdots a_{n}!},

where
\displaystyle\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}f
denotes taking the constant term of the Laurent polynomial
f
with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.

Dyson’s conjecture was soon proved by Gunson
[
17
]
and Wilson
[
40
]
. Twenty years later Wilson
received his Nobel Prize in physics, while
[
40
]
is his first publication.
In 1970, using Lagrange interpolation Good
[
16
]
discovered a very elegant one-page proof. Much later, Zeilberger gave the first combinatorial proof using tournaments
[
41
]
.

In 1975, Andrews
[
2
, Page 216]
conjectured a
q
-analogue of the Dyson constant term identity.

Theorem 1.2

(Andrews’ ex-conjecture)
.

For nonnegative integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,

(1.2)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{a_{i}}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{a_{j}}=\frac{(q)_{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}}}{(q)_{a_{1}}(q)_{a_{2}}\cdots(q)_{a_{n}}},

where
(a)_{z}=(a;q)_{z}:=\prod_{j=0}^{z-1}(1-aq^{j})
is the
q
-shifted factorial for a positive integer
z
and
(a)_{0}=1
.

Andrews’ conjecture remained open for ten years until Zeilberger and Bressoud
[
42
]
generalized the above-mentioned combinatorial method from
[
41
]
.
In 2006, Gessel and Xin
[
15
]
obtained a second proof (the first algebraic proof) using iterated Laurent series.
In 2014, Károlyi and Nagy
[
26
]
discovered the “Proof from the book” using the combinatorial Nullstellensatz.
In the same year, Cai
[
8
]
found the first inductive proof by adding an extra parameter to the original problem.

The Laurent polynomials on the left-hand side of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) are usually called the Dyson product and the
q
-Dyson product respectively
[
11
,
14
,
34
,
27
,
39
]
.
Besides the constant term of the
q
-Dyson product, Sills
[
34
]
conjectured three other coefficients.
Lv, Xin and Zhou
[
29
]
proved Sills’ conjectures and obtained all the first-layer coefficients of the
q
-Dyson product.
That is

(1.3)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{x_{j_{1}}^{p_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{t}}^{p_{t}}}{x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{s}}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{a_{i}}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{a_{j}},

where all the
i_{1},\dots,i_{s},j_{1},\dots,j_{t}
are distinct and the
p_{i}
are positive integers.
Here we omit the explicit formula due to its complexity.
Sills’ ex-conjectures correspond to the cases
s,t\leq 2
in (
1.3
).
The equal parameter case, i.e.,
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{n}=c
of (
1.3
) was obtained by Stembridge
[
35
]
in his first-layer formulas for the characters of
\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{C})
.

In 2000, Kadell made a remarkable conjecture
on a symmetric function generalization of the
q
-Dyson identity.
Let
X=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
be an alphabet of countably many variables.
Define the
r
th complete symmetric function
h_{r}(X)

in terms of its generating function as

(1.4)

\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}h_{r}(X)=\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{1}{1-zx_{i}}.

More generally, the complete symmetric function indexed by a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
is defined as

h_{\lambda}:=h_{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda_{2}}\cdots,

where a partition
\lambda
is a weakly decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers such that only finitely many
\lambda_{i}
are positive.
For
a:=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
a sequence of nonnegative integers, let
\boldsymbol{x}^{(a)}
denote
the alphabet

(1.5)

\boldsymbol{x}^{(a)}=(x_{1},x_{1}q,\dots,x_{1}q^{a_{1}-1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n}q,\dots,x_{n}q^{a_{n}-1})

of cardinality
|a|:=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}
, and define
the generalized
q
-Dyson constant term

(1.6)

D_{v,\lambda}(a)=\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}x^{-v}h_{\lambda}\big(\boldsymbol{x}^{(a)}\big)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{a_{i}}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{a_{j}}.

Here
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,

x^{v}
denotes the monomial
x_{1}^{v_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{v_{n}}

and
\lambda
is a partition.
For the constant term (
1.6
),
Kadell formulated the
following conjecture
[
23
, Conjecture 4]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Kadell’s ex-conjecture)
.

For
r
a positive integer and
v
a vector of nonnegative integers such that
|v|=r
,

(1.7)

D_{v,(r)}(a)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{q^{\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}a_{i}}(1-q^{a_{k}})(q^{|a|})_{r}}{(1-q^{|a|})(q^{|a|-a_{k}+1})_{r}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{a_i+\cdots+a_n}{a_i}&\text{if $v=(0^{k-1},r,0^{n-k})$},\\[17.07164pt]
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c}=(q^{n-c+1})_{c}/(q)_{c}
is the
q
-binomial coefficient for nonnegative integers
n
and
c
.

In fact Kadell only considered
v=(r,0^{n-1})
in his conjecture, but the
more general statement given above is what was proved by Károlyi,
Lascoux and Warnaar in
[
27
, Theorem 1.3]
using multivariable Lagrange
interpolation and key polynomials.
For a sequence
u=(u_{0},\dots,u_{n})
of integers, denote
by
u^{+}
the sequence obtained from
u
by ordering the
u_{i}

in weakly decreasing order. Károlyi et al. also
proved a closed-form expression for

D_{v,v^{+}}(a)
in the case when all the parts of
v
are nonnegative integers and have multiplicity one, i.e., all the
v_{i}\geq 0
and
v_{i}\neq v_{j}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
Subsequently, Cai
[
8
]
gave an inductive proof of Kadell’s conjecture.
Recently, we obtained some further results on
D_{v,v^{+}}(a)
, see
[
44
,
45
]
.

In 1982, Macdonald discovered that the equal parameter case of the
q
-Dyson identity, i.e., the
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{n}=c
case, can be formulated as a combinatorial
identity for the root system
\mathrm{A}_{n-1}
.
Then he conjectured a constant term identity for arbitrary root systems
[
30
]
:

Theorem 1.4

(Macdonald’s ex-conjecture)
.

For a nonnegative integer
c
,

\CT\,\prod_{\alpha\in R^{+}}(e^{-\alpha})_{c}(qe^{\alpha})_{c}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{d_ic}{c}.

Here
R
is a reduced irreducible finite root system of rank
r
,
R^{+}
is the set of positive roots and
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}
are the degrees of the fundamental invariants.

Initially, many cases of Macdonald’s conjecture were proved on a case by case basis
[
4
,
13
,
18
,
22
,
43
]
.
A uniform proof for
q=1
was first found by Opdam
[
32
]
using hypergeometric shift operators.
Thirteen years after Macdonald presented his constant term conjecture,
Cherednik
[
9
]
finally gave a case-free proof based on his double affine Hecke algebra. For more on the extensive literature of Macdonald’s constant term conjecture we refer the reader to
[
12
]
and references therein.

Another important generalization of the equal parameter case of the
q
-Dyson identity
is the
q
-Morris constant term identity.

Theorem 1.5

(Morris’ ex-conjecture)
.

For nonnegative integers
a,b,c
,

(1.8)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{x_{0}}{x_{i}}\Big)_{a}\Big(\frac{qx_{i}}{x_{0}}\Big)_{b}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}\Big)_{c}\Big(\frac{qx_{j}}{x_{i}}\Big)_{c}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{(q)_{a+b+ic}(q)_{(i+1)c}}{(q)_{a+ic}(q)_{b+ic}(q)_{c}}.

Since the Laurent polynomial in (
1.8
) is homogeneous in
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
, we can take, for example
x_{0}=1
, without changing
the constant term.
The
q=1
case of (
1.8
) was proved by Morris
[
31
]
in his Ph.D thesis.
He also showed that the
q=1
case is equivalent to the Selberg integral
[
33
]
and conjectured the above
q
-analogue.
It is known that the
q
-Morris identity (
1.8
) is equivalent to Askey’s
q
-Selberg
ex-conjecture. We state the result below.

For
0<q<1
, define the
q
-integral or the Jackson integral as

\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\mathrm{d}_{q}t:=(1-q)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(q^{k})q^{k},

where
f
is a function for which the right hand side converges.
In 1980, Askey
[
5
]
formulated a
q
-analogous conjecture of the Selberg integral
[
33
]
.

Theorem 1.6

(Askey’s ex-conjecture)
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and
\alpha,\beta
be complex parameters such that

\mathrm{Re}(\alpha)>0
,
\mathrm{Re}(\beta)>0
.
Then

(1.9)

\int\limits_{[0,1]^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha-1}(qx_{i})_{\beta-1}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}x_{j}^{2k}(q^{1-k}x_{i}/x_{j})_{2k}\mathrm{d}_{q}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}_{q}x_{n}\\
=q^{\alpha k\binom{n}{2}+2k^{2}\binom{n}{3}}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma_{q}\big(\alpha+kj\big)\Gamma_{q}\big(\beta+kj\big)\Gamma_{q}\big(1+k(j+1)\big)}{\Gamma_{q}\big(\alpha+\beta+k(n+j-1)\big)\Gamma_{q}(1+k)},

where
\Gamma_{q}(z)=(q)_{z-1}(1-q)^{1-z}
is the
q
-gamma function
for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}
.

The
q=1
case of (
1.9
) is the famous Selberg integral
[
33
]
.
When
n=1
, the Selberg integral reduces to the Euler beta integral.
In 1988, Askey’s conjecture was proved independently by Habsieger
[
19
]
and Kadell
[
21
]
. Both Habsieger and Kadell turned the integral (
1.9
) into an equivalent constant term identity (
1.8
). Hence, the identity (
1.8
)
is usually referred to as the Habsieger–Kadell
q
-Morris identity,
while its equivalent integral (
1.9
) is called the Askey–Habsieger–Kadell integral.
For the importance of the Selberg integral, see a review
[
12
]
.

Now we introduce Baker and Forrester’s conjecture on a generalization of the
q
-Morris identity.
Let
n_{0},n_{1},\dots,n_{p}
be positive integers such that
n_{0}+\cdots+n_{p}=n
. Set

N_{0}=\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}
,
N_{1}=\{n_{0}+1,\dots,n_{0}+n_{1}\},\dots,N_{p}=\{n_{0}+\cdots+n_{p-1}+1,\dots,n\}
.
Denote

(1.10)

\varepsilon_{ij}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\ \{i,j\}\subseteq N_{l}\ \text{for some}\ l\in\{1,\dots,p\},\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Define

(1.11)

L_{n_{0},\dots,n_{p}}(a,b,c;x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0}/x_{i})_{a}(qx_{i}/x_{0})_{b}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c+\varepsilon_{ij}}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{c+\varepsilon_{ij}},

and

D_{n}\big((n_{0},\dots,n_{p});a,b,c\big)=\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}L_{n_{0},\dots,n_{p}}(a,b,c;x).

In 1998, Baker and Forrester
[
6
, Conjecture 2.2]
conjectured a recursion
for
D_{n}\big((n_{0},\dots,n_{p});a,b,c\big)
. We will refer to this in detail in Theorem
1.8
.

By the definition of the
\varepsilon_{ij}
in (
1.10
), if
p=0
then all the
\varepsilon_{ij}=0
.
It follows that in the
p=0
case
D_{n}(n_{0};a,b,c)
is just the constant term in the
q
-Morris identity (
1.8
).

There have been several attempts to prove the Baker–Forrester conjecture in the
p=1
case

[
14
,
24
,
25
,
20
]
.
Following their discovery of the “Proof from the book” of the
q
-Dyson theorem using the combinatorial Nullstellensatz
[
26
]
,
Károlyi, Nagy, Petrov and Volkov
[
28
]
proved this case in 2015. We state the result in the next theorem.

Theorem 1.7
.

For
n_{0}+n_{1}=n
,

(1.12)

D_{n}\big((n_{0},n_{1});a,b,c\big)=\prod_{j=2}^{n-n_{0}}(1-q^{j(c+1)})\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{(q^{a+jc+\chi(j>n_{0})(j-n_{0})+1})_{b}(q)_{(j+1)c+\chi(j>n_{0})(j-n_{0})}}{(q)_{b+jc+\chi(j>n_{0})(j-n_{0})}(q)_{c+\chi(j>n_{0})}},

where
\chi(S)
is the truth function which is 1 if
S
is true and 0 otherwise.

Károlyi et al.
[
28
]
actually gave an Aomoto-type
[
1
]
extension of the above theorem by adding
an extra parameter.
Recently, we
[
39
]
obtained several symmetric function generalizations of
Theorem
1.7
and its Aomoto-type extension.

In the general
p+1
case, Baratta
[
7
, Theorem 12]
proved the
a=b=0
case.
In this paper, we prove the
q
-Baker–Forrester conjecture for all
p\geq 1
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Assume
n_{k}=\max\{n_{1},\dots,n_{p}\}
. Then

(1.13)

D_{n}\big((n_{0},\dots,n_{p});a,b,c\big)=\frac{(1-q^{n_{k}(c+1)})(q^{a+(n-1)c+n_{k}})_{b}}{(1-q^{c+1})(q^{(n-1)c+n_{k}})_{b}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{nc+n_k-1}{c}\\
\times D_{n-1}\big((n_{0},\dots,n_{k-1},n_{k}-1,n_{k+1}\dots,n_{p});a,b,c\big).

We refer to
D_{n}\big((n_{0},\dots,n_{p});a,b,c\big)
as
D_{n}(a)
for short throughout this paper if there is no risk of ambiguity.
Note that the value of
k
in the theorem may not be unique, say

n_{k_{1}}=\cdots=n_{k_{s}}=\max\{n_{1},\dots,n_{p}\}
. Then (
1.13
) holds for any
such
n_{k_{i}}
. The original restriction on the
n_{i}
in
[
6
]
is

n_{p}>n_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,p-1
.
However, the assumption stated in the theorem is sufficient to derive a closed-form formula for
D_{n}(a)
. Thus, we propose a slight variation.
Also note that in the definition of
n_{k}=\max\{n_{1},\dots,n_{p}\}
,
n_{0}
is not included in the set
\{n_{1},\dots,n_{p}\}
. As mentioned above, the Morris-type constant term identities are equivalent
to the Selberg-type integrals. Therefore, the recursion given in (
1.13
) suggests
a corresponding integral recursion. However, the integral recursion is too complicated to be included here.

2.
A sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.8

In this section, we provide a sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.8
.
In Subsection
2.1
, we present the main steps of our proof of Theorem
1.8
.
In Subsection
2.2
, we show that
D_{n}(a)
is a polynomial in

.
In Subsection

, we describe the roots of

.
In Subsection

, we demonstrate that it is sufficient to derive an expression for

2.1.
The main steps

The basic idea for proving Theorem
1.8
is the following.
A polynomial of degree at most
d
is determined by
d+1
distinct values.
We obtain (
1.13
) through the next four steps.

(1)

Polynomiality:
View
D_{n}(a)
as a polynomial in
q^{a}
of degree at most
nb
;

(2)

Roots:
Explicitly determine all the roots of
D_{n}(a)

under the assumption that
c
is sufficiently large;

(3)

A non-vanishing value:
Obtain a recursion for
D_{n}(0)
(i.e., the
q^{a}=1
case),
and use this to obtain a recursion for
D_{n}(a)
;

(4)

Rationality:
Extend the recursion for
D_{n}(a)
to all nonnegative integers

c
by a rationality argument.

Below we explain the above steps in further detail.

The first step is routine, see Corollary
2.2
below.
To view a constant term as a polynomial in one parameter originates from
Gessel and Xin’s Laurent series proof
[
15
]
of
the Zeilberger–Bressoud
q
-Dyson identity (
1.2
).
We utilized this idea for obtaining a series of constant term identities in
[
38
,
39
,
29
,
44
,
46
]
.

The second step is quite involved.
We find that
D_{n}(a)
can vanish only when
a
is a negative integer.
Hence, we need to extend the original definition of
D_{n}(a)
to all the integers
a
using the theory of
iterated Laurent series
[
36
]
. For a negative integer
a
, we actually
need to take constant term of a rational function, rather than a Laurent polynomial.
The original Gessel–Xin method can only
deal with the constant term of a rational function, say
f(x_{i})
, with negative degrees.
Here we say that the degree of the rational function
f(x_{i})
is the degree in
x_{i}

of its numerator minus the degree in
x_{i}
of its denominator. We extended their method to deal with
rational functions with non-positive degrees to obtain the first-layer coefficients
of the
q
-Dyson product
[
29
]
. We mentioned this in (
1.3
) in the introduction.
For
D_{n}(a)
, not all the roots can be determined using the
original Gessel–Xin method. This is caused by the non-symmetry of the
x_{i}
’s.
For some roots, we have to tackle
the problem of how to obtain the constant term of a rational function with positive degree.
This has been a long-standing hurdle
[
14
]
.
Recently, we discovered that rational functions of the
q
-Baker–Forrester type with 2 components
(i.e. the
p=1
case of

) can be transformed into Laurent polynomials under certain conditions
[
39
]
.
In this paper, build on this result, we deal with the general case of
p+1
components.
Then, using Cai’s splitting idea
[
8
]
for proving constant term identities,
we find some new splitting formulas for the
q
-Baker–Forrester type Laurent polynomials.
Using these new splitting formulas, we manage to deal with all the
zeros of
D_{n}(a)
.

To explicitly determine the closed-form formula for
D_{n}(a)
as a polynomial,
besides all its roots, we require one extra non-vanishing value.
To achieve this, we find a recursion for
D_{n}(0)
using the above mentioned splitting formulas.
Together with all the roots of
D_{n}(a)
, this completes the recursion (
1.13
)
in Theorem
1.8
for sufficiently large
c
.

The assumption that
c
is sufficiently large is imposed to avoid
multiple roots for
D_{n}(a)
in Step (2).
In Step (4), a rationality result, see Proposition
2.4
, is used to
extend the recursion (
1.13
) to all nonnegative integers
c
.

2.2.
The polynomiality

The next polynomiality result is a special case of
[

, Lemma 2.2]

and
[

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be an arbitrary Laurent polynomial independent of
a
and
x_{0}
.
Then, for fixed nonnegative integers

,

(2.1)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x_{0}/x_{i})_{a}(qx_{i}/x_{0})_{b}A(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

is a polynomial in

of degree at most

.

Then, by taking

A(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}/x_{j})_{c+\varepsilon_{ij}}(qx_{j}/x_{i})_{c+\varepsilon_{ij}}

in Lemma
2.1
, we complete Step (1) in Subsection

. We state the result in the next corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Assume all the parameters of
D_{n}(a)
, except for
a
, are fixed. The constant term
D_{n}(a)
is a polynomial in
q^{a}

with degree at most
nb
.

2.3.
The roots

To describe the roots of
D_{n}(a)
, we need to sort
n_{1},\dots,n_{p}
.
Assume
n_{s_{1}},\dots,n_{s_{p}}
is a rearrangement of
n_{1},\dots,n_{p}

such that
1\leq n_{s_{1}}\leq\cdots\leq n_{s_{p}}
.
Note that if one
n_{i}=0
then it is in fact the
p-1
case.
Therefore, we can assume all the
n_{i}
to be positive integers.
Also note that
n_{0}
is not sorted and it is not included
in the
n_{s_{j}}
.
To explicitly describe all the roots of
D_{n}(a)
, we begin with the patterns
from
p=0,1,2
.

For the
p=0
case, the constant term
D_{n}(n_{0};a,b,c)
vanishes when

Note that

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

and

be nonnegative integers. Then

\sum_{j=0}^{t}\frac{q^{j(n-t)}}{(q^{-j})_{j}(q)_{t-j}}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{t}.

Proof.

We can rewrite the left-hand side of (

\sum_{j=0}^{t}\frac{(-1)^{j}q^{j(n-t)+\binom{j+1}{2}}}{(q)_{j}(q)_{t-j}}=\frac{1}{(q)_{t}}\sum_{j=0}^{t}q^{\binom{j}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{t}{j}(-q^{n-t+1})^{j}.

Using the well-known

(z)_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{t}q^{\binom{j}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{t}{j}(-z)^{j}

\frac{1}{(q)_{t}}\sum_{j=0}^{t}q^{\binom{j}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{t}{j}(-q^{n-t+1})^{j}=(q^{n-t+1})_{t}/(q)_{t}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{t}.\qed

4.
The Gessel–Xin method to evaluate constant terms

In this subsection, we briefly introduce the Gessel–Xin method to evaluate
constant terms of rational functions.

The method is based on the theory of iterated Laurent series. Xin constructed
the field of iterated Laurent series in his Ph.D thesis
[
36
]
.
In the following of this paper, we work in the field of iterated
Laurent series
\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle=\mathbb{C}(\!(x_{n})\!)(\!(x_{n-1})\!)\cdots(\!(x_{0})\!)
.
Elements of
\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle

are regarded first as Laurent series in
x_{0}
, then as
Laurent series in
x_{1}
, and so on.
The application of this theory to constant terms first appeared
in Gessel and Xin’s
[
15
]
Laurent series proof of the Zeilberger–Bressoud

q
-Dyson constant term identity
[
2
,
42
]
.
For more applications to constant terms, the reader could refer to
[
29
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
44
,
46
]
.
The most applicable fact in what is to follow is that the field
\mathbb{C}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
of
rational functions in the variables
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}

forms a subfield of
\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
, so that every rational function can be identified as its unique Laurent series expansion.

In our approach, the next expansion of
1/(1-cx_{i}/x_{j})
for
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}

is fundamental:

\frac{1}{1-cx_{i}/x_{j}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{l\geq 0}c^{l}(x_{i}/x_{j})^{l}&\text{if $i<j$},\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle-\sum_{l<0}c^{l}(x_{i}/x_{j})^{l}&\text{if $i>j$}.\end{cases}

Thus,

(4.1)

\CT_{x_{i}}\frac{1}{1-cx_{i}/x_{j}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i<j$},\\
0&\text{if $i>j$},\\
\end{cases}

where, for
f\in\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
,
the operator
\displaystyle\CT_{x_{i}}
also means to take the constant term
with respect to
x_{i}
.
The constant term operators defined in
\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle x_{n},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
have the property of commutativity:

\CT_{x_{i}}\CT_{x_{j}}f=\CT_{x_{j}}\CT_{x_{i}}f

for any
i
and
j
.
This means that we are taking constant term in a set of variables.
Then
\CT\limits_{x}
is well-defined in this field.

The following lemma has appeared previously in
[
15
]
.
It is a basic tool for
extracting constant terms from rational functions.

Lemma 4.1
.

For a positive integer
m
, let
p(x_{k})
be a Laurent polynomial
in
x_{k}
of degree at most
m-1
with coefficients in

K\langle\!\langle x_{n},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
.
Let
0\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{m}\leq n
such that all
i_{r}\neq k
,
and define

(4.2)

f=\frac{p(x_{k})}{\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1-c_{r}x_{k}/x_{i_{r}})},

where
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
c_{r}\neq c_{s}
if
x_{i_{r}}=x_{i_{s}}
.
Then

(4.3)

\CT_{x_{k}}f=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\[1.0pt]
i_{r}>k\end{subarray}}^{m}\big(f\,(1-c_{r}x_{k}/x_{i_{r}})\big)\Big|_{x_{k}=c_{r}^{-1}x_{i_{r}}}.

Note that we extended Lemma
4.1
to
[
29
, Lemma 3.1]
,
in which
p(x_{k})
is a Laurent polynomial in
x_{k}
of degree at most
m
.
In this paper, we need to deal with the cases when the degree in
x_{k}
of
p(x_{k})
is larger than
m
. This technique first appeared in
[
39
]
.

If
f
is a rational function of the form
(
4.2
) with respect to
x_{0}
. Then we can apply Lemma
4.1

to
f
with respect to
x_{0}
and obtain (
4.3
) for
k=0
.
We can write (
4.3
) as a finite sum of the form

\CT_{x_{0}}f=\sum_{1\leq u_{1}\leq n}f_{(u_{1})}.

Note that
f_{(u_{1})}
may be a sum. It is still a multi-variable rational function without the variable
x_{0}
.
If furthermore, one of the
f_{(u_{1})}
is still of the form (
4.2
) with respect to
x_{u_{1}}
, then we can apply Lemma
4.1
again to the
f_{(u_{1})}
and obtain

\CT_{x_{u_{1}}}f_{(u_{1})}=\sum_{1\leq u_{1}<u_{2}\leq n}f_{(u_{1},u_{2})}.

We can further apply Lemma
4.1
to each
f_{(u_{1},u_{2})}
with respect to
x_{u_{2}}
if applicable, and get a sum.
Continue this operation until Lemma
4.1
is no longer valid to every summand. Finally we write

(4.4)

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}f=\sum_{s\in T}\sum_{1\leq u_{1}<\cdots<u_{s}\leq n}\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})},

where
T
is a subset of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Of course, here
f_{(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})}
may
also be a sum.
We call this operation the Gessel–Xin operation to the rational function
f
, since it first appeared in Gessel and Xin’s paper
[
15
]
.
Though we turn
\CT\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}}f
into a huge sum (
4.4
),
we can determine that all the summand in
(
4.4
) are zeros under certain conditions.
Then we can arrive at a conclusion that

\CT_{\boldsymbol{x}}f=0.

This is the main procedure of the Gessel–Xin method.

5.
The roots

In this section, we determine all the roots of
D_{n}(a)
and prove Lemma
2.3
.

5.1.
Preliminaries

For a positive integer
s
, let
\mathfrak{S}_{s}
be the set of all the permutations of
\{1,2,\dots,s\}
.
For positive integers
r_{0},r_{1},\dots,r_{p}
such that
r_{0}+\cdots+r_{p}=s
,
denote
r=(r_{0},\dots,r_{p})
and

(5.1)

R_{0}=\{1,\dots,r_{0}\},\qquad R_{i}=\{r_{0}+\cdots+r_{i-1}+1,\dots,r_{0}+\cdots+r_{i}\}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,p.

For
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
,
define

(5.2)

N_{w,r}:=\sum_{j=1}^{s}e_{j}(w,r),

where

(5.3)

e_{j}=e_{j}(w,r):=u_{j}(w)+\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big).

Here
w(0):=0
and

(5.4)

u_{j}(w)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $w(j-1)<w(j)$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For example, if
s=10
,
r=(r_{0},r_{1},r_{2})=(3,3,4)
, and
w=(9,10,3,5,6,8,4,2,7,1)
,
then
R_{0}=\{1,2,3\}
,
R_{1}=\{4,5,6\}
,
R_{2}=\{7,8,9,10\}
and
N_{w,r}=8
.
Let

(5.5)

0=w(0)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{1}}w(1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{2}}\cdots\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{s-1}}w(s-1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{s}}w(s)

be a weighted directed path on
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
.
Then we can view
N_{w,r}
as the sum of the weights on this directed path.
Thus, the above example can be represented graphically as follows:

0\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{1}9\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{2}10\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{0}3\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{1}5\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{2}6\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{1}8\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{0}4\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{0}2\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{1}7\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{0}1.

Note that
e_{j}
is determined by
w(j),w(j-1)
and
r
,
and
e_{1}=1
. We call
w(j-1)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{e_{j}}w(j)
an ascending arc if
w(j-1)<w(j)
.

It is not hard to obtain the lower bound for
N_{w,r}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For a positive integer
s
, let
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
,
r=(r_{0},\dots,r_{p})
be a vector of positive
integers such that
|r|=s
,
and
N_{w,r}
be defined in (
5.2
).
Then, for all
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
and any
i\in\{1,\dots,s\}

(5.6)

N_{w,r}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}e_{j}(w,r)\geq\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\},

and

(5.7)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}e_{j}(w,r)\geq\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}-1.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
R_{i}
in (
5.1
).
For a given
l\in\{1,2,\dots,p\}
and any two elements
w(i_{1}),w(i_{2})\in R_{l}
,
we show that the sum of weights that start from
w(i_{1})
and end at
w(i_{2})
must be no less than 1. Denote this sum by
h
.
More explicitly, either
w(i_{1})\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{e_{i_{2}}}w(i_{2})
or

w(i_{1})\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{j}}w(j)\overbrace{\longrightarrow\cdots\mathop{\longrightarrow}}^{h_{1}}w(k)\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{e_{i_{2}}}w(i_{2}),

then
h=e_{i_{2}}\geq 1
for the first case and

(5.8)

h=e_{j}+e_{i_{2}}+h_{1}\geq 1

for the second case.
The first case holds apparently since both
w(i_{1}),w(i_{2})\in R_{l}
.
We show the second case by discussing:
(i)
w(i_{1})>w(j),w(k)<w(i_{2})
;
(ii)
w(i_{1})<w(j),w(k)>w(i_{2})
;
(iii)
w(i_{1})<w(j),w(k)<w(i_{2})
;
(iv)
w(i_{1})>w(j),w(k)>w(i_{2})
.
For (i)–(iii), it is easy to see that
h\geq e_{j}+e_{i_{2}}\geq 1
since there exists at least one ascending arc
between two consecutive elements.
The last case
w(i_{1})>w(j),w(k)>w(i_{2})
.
If one of
w(j)
and
w(k)
belongs to
R_{l}
, then either
e_{j}=1
or
e_{i_{2}}=1
and
h\geq 1
.
Thus, we assume
w(j),w(k)\notin R_{l}
below. In this case
w(j)\neq w(k)
, otherwise
w(i_{1})>w(j)
and
w(k)>w(i_{2})
can not hold both for

w(j)=w(k)\notin R_{l}
.
Since
w(j)\neq w(k)
,
w(i_{1})>w(j)
,
w(k)>w(i_{2})
and
w(j),w(k)\notin R_{l}
, we conclude that
w(j)<w(i_{1})<w(k)
.
Using the fact that
w(j)<w(k)
, there must exist at least one ascending arc between
w(j)
and
w(k)
. Hence
h_{1}\geq 1
.
It follows that
h\geq 1
by (
5.8
).

Let
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
be any two consecutive elements in
R_{l}
.
Using the above fact that the sum of weights that start from
w(i_{1})
and end at
w(i_{2})
must be no less than 1, together with
e_{1}=1
, we have

N_{w,r}\geq r_{l}=|R_{l}|

for any given
l\in\{1,\dots,p\}
.
Then
N_{w,r}\geq\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}
.

By the discuss above, it is clear that for any
i\in\{1,\dots,s\}

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{s}e_{j}(w,r)\geq\max\{r_{1}-1,\dots,r_{p}-1\}=\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}-1.\qed

Note that the lower bound in (
5.6
) can be attained.
To determine the minimum
N_{w,r}
, the approach involves separating the elements of
R_{i}
as much as possible and then sorting these elements in descending order, from largest to smallest.
To demonstrate the corresponding
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
,
we name the elements of
R_{i}
(in an increasing order) as
a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,r_{i}}
respectively.
Note that
|R_{i}|=r_{i}
.
Arrange all the
a_{i,j}
as follows

w^{0}=\big(a_{p,r_{p}},a_{p-1,r_{p-1}},\dots,a_{0,r_{0}},a_{p,r_{p}-1},a_{p-1,r_{p-1}-1},\dots,a_{0,r_{0}-1},\dots\big),

where
a_{p,r_{p}},\dots,a_{0,r_{0}}

are the largest elements in
R_{p},\dots,R_{0}
respectively,
and
a_{p,r_{p}-1},\dots,a_{0,r_{0}-1}

are the second largest elements in
R_{p},\dots,R_{0}
respectively, and so on.
It is not hard to see that
N_{w^{0},r}=\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}
.
For example, if
s=10
,
r=(3,3,4)
,
R_{0}=\{1,2,3\}
,
R_{1}=\{4,5,6\}
and
R_{2}=\{7,8,9,10\}
,
then
w^{0}
in
\mathfrak{S}_{10}
is

(10,6,3,9,5,2,8,4,1,7)
and
N_{w^{0},r}=4=\max\{r_{1},r_{2}\}
.

We present the key lemma, which plays a pivotal role in determining the roots of
D_{n}(a)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For a positive integer
s
, let
b,c,t
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}
be nonnegative integers such that

1\leq k_{i}\leq(s-1)c+b+t
for
1\leq i\leq s
.
Then at least one of the following holds:

(1)

1\leq k_{i}\leq b
for some
i
with
1\leq i\leq s
;

(2)

-c\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c-1
for some
(i,j)
such that
1\leq i<j\leq s

and
\{i,j\}\nsubseteq R_{l}
for all
l=1,2,\dots,p
;

(3)

-c-1\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c
for some
(i,j)
such that
1\leq i<j\leq s

and
\{i,j\}\subseteq R_{l}
for some
l\in\{1,2,\dots,p\}
;

(4)

there exists a permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{s}
and nonnegative integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{s}

such that

(5.9a)

k_{w(1)}=b+d_{1},

and

(5.9b)

k_{w(j)}-k_{w(j-1)}=c+\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big)+d_{j}\quad\text{for $2\leq j\leq s$.}

Here the
d_{j}
satisfy

(5.10)

\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\Big(\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big)+d_{j}\Big)\leq t,

w(0):=0
, and
d_{j}>0
if
w(j-1)<w(j)
for
1\leq j\leq s
.

Note that the
p=0
case reduces to
[
46
, Lemma 8.1]
,
the
p=1
case reduces to
[
39
, Lemma 6.4]
.
Also notice that
d_{1}
is always a positive integer, and if
t=0
then (
5.10
) can not hold. Hence, (4) can not occur if
t=0
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by showing that if (1)–(3) fail then (4) must hold.

Assume that (1)–(3) are all false.
Then we construct a weighted tournament
T
on a complete graph
on
s
vertices, labelled
1,\dots,s
, as follows.
For the edge
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq s
and
\{i,j\}\nsubseteq R_{l}
for any
l\in\{1,2,\dots,p\}
,
we draw an arrow from
j
to
i
and attach a weight
c
if
k_{i}-k_{j}\geq c
.
If, on the other hand,
k_{i}-k_{j}\leq-c-1
then we draw an arrow
from
i
to
j
and attach the weight
c+1
.
Similarly, for the edge
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq s

and
\{i,j\}\subseteq R_{l}
for some
l\in\{1,2,\dots,p\}
,
we draw an arrow from
j
to
i
and attach a weight
c+1
if
k_{i}-k_{j}\geq c+1
,
and we draw an arrow from
i
to
j
and attach the weight
c+2

if
k_{i}-k_{j}\leq-c-2
.
Note that the weight of each edge of a tournament is nonnegative since
c
is a nonnegative integer.

We call a directed edge from
i
to
j
ascending if
i<j
.
It is immediate from our construction that
(i) the weight of the edge
i\to j
is less than or equal
k_{j}-k_{i}
, and
(ii) the weight of an ascending edge is positive.

We will use (i) and (ii) to show that any of the above-constructed
tournaments is acyclic and hence transitive.
As a consequence of (i), the weight of a directed path from
i
to
j
in
T
,
defined as the sum of the weights of its edges, is at most
k_{j}-k_{i}
.
Proceeding by contradiction, assume that
T
contains a cycle
C
.
By the above, the weight of
C
must be non-positive, and hence
0
.
Since
C
must have at least one ascending edge, which by (ii) has a positive
weight, the weight of
C
is positive, a contradiction.

Since each
T
is transitive, there is exactly one directed Hamilton path
P
in
T
,
corresponding to a total order of the vertices.
Assume
P
is given by

P=w(1)\rightarrow w(2)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow w(s-1)\rightarrow w(s),

where we have suppressed the edge weights.
Then

k_{w(s)}-k_{w(1)}\geq(s-1)c+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big),

and thus

\displaystyle k_{w(s)}

\displaystyle\geq k_{w(1)}+(s-1)c+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big)

(5.11)

\displaystyle\geq b+1+(s-1)c+\sum_{j=2}^{s}\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big).

Together with the assumption that
k_{w(s)}\leq(s-1)c+b+t

this implies that
P
has at most
t-1
ascending edges.
Let
d_{1},\dots,d_{s}
be nonnegative integers such that (
5.9
) holds.
Since (1) does not hold and by (
5.9a
),
k_{w(1)}=b+d_{1}\geq b+1
, so that
d_{1}\geq 1
.
For
2\leq j\leq s
, if
w(j-1)\to w(j)
is an ascending edge, then
d_{j}
is a positive integer.
That is, for
2\leq j\leq s
if
w(j-1)<w(j)
then
d_{j}\geq 1
.
Set
k_{0}:=0
.
Since

\sum_{j=1}^{s}(k_{w(j)}-k_{w(j-1)})=k_{w(s)}=b+(s-1)c+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big)\\
\leq b+(s-1)c+t,

we have

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big)\big)\leq t.

Since
d_{j}\geq u_{j}
by the definition of the
u_{j}
in (
5.4
),

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big)\big)\geq N_{w,r},

where
N_{w,r}
is defined in (
5.2
).
Together with Proposition
5.1
yields

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\big(d_{j}+\chi\big(\{w(j),w(j-1)\}\subseteq R_{i}\ \text{for some $i=1,\dots,p$}\big)\big)\geq\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}.

This completes the proof of the assertion that (4) must hold if (1)–(3) fail.
∎

By Lemma
5.2
, we have an essential result to determine the roots
of
D_{n}(a)
.

Corollary 5.3
.

For a positive integer

Take

(5.12)

\vdots\\

$n-n_{w_{p}}+n_{w_{p-1}}+1\leq s\leq n$,}\end{cases}

where
(n_{w_{1}},\dots,n_{w_{p}})
is a permutation of
(n_{1},\dots,n_{p})
in an increasing order.
In particular, if
p=0
, then
t_{s}=0
for
1\leq s\leq n
; if
p=1
, then
t_{s}=0
for

for

.
Denote by
R_{0}=\{1,\dots,r_{0}\}

R_{i}=\{r_{0}+\cdots+r_{i-1}+1,\dots,r_{0}+\cdots+r_{i}\}

for
i=1,\dots,p
.
Then, at least one of the following cases holds:

(i)

1\leq k_{i}\leq b
for some
i
with
1\leq i\leq s
;

(ii)

-c\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c-1
for some
(i,j)

1\leq i<j\leq s

for all

;

-c-1\leq k_{i}-k_{j}\leq c

such that

.

For the

(5.13)

t_{s}=\Big\lfloor\frac{s-n_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}n_{w_{i}}-1}{p-j+1}\Big\rfloor

for
n-\sum_{i=j}^{p}n_{w_{i}}+(p-j+1)n_{w_{j-1}}+1\leq s\leq n-\sum_{i=j+1}^{p}n_{w_{i}}+(p-j)n_{w_{j}}
.
Here we take
n_{w_{0}}=1
.
Note that there are

in (

) with

.

Proof.

Compare with Lemma
5.2
,
it suffices to prove that Case (4) of Lemma
5.2

can not occur under the conditions of this corollary.
We complete this by showing that (
5.10
) can not hold with
t=t_{s}
.
For the
t_{s}=0
case, (
5.10
) can not hold since

.
For the
t_{s}>0

\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}>t_{s}.

Then (

) can not hold.
We achieve this by showing that for a fixed integer
j\in\{1,\dots,p\}

) holds.

To warm up, we first show the

For
j=1

s\geq n-\sum_{i=1}^{p}n_{i}+p+1=n_{0}+p+1

t_{s}=\Big\lfloor\frac{s-n_{0}-1}{p}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lfloor\frac{p+l-1}{p}\Big\rfloor=1+\Big\lfloor\frac{l-1}{p}\Big\rfloor.

To obtain the lower bound of
\max\{r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\}
,
we can imagine that there are
s

. The box zero can contain

balls at most, the box 1 can contain
n_{1}
balls at most,

n_{2}
balls at most, and so on.
The number

is the number of the balls filled in the box

i=1,\dots,p
.
If
