Equidistribution for Tannakian Monodromy Groups
Beat Zurbuchen

Abstract
. We prove that a perverse sheaf on a connected commutatitve algebraic group over a finite field is generically unramified. This implies an equidistribution theorem for Tannakian monodromy groups in previously unavailable generality. We also prove a stratification theorem for exponential sums in families indexed by a scheme and the characters of a connected commutative algebraic group. Our method is based on Tannakian categories introduced by Gabber and Loeser. This method naturally yields fiber functors. We also prove vanishing theorems over a connected commutative algebraic group, classify the negligible sheaves, and prove relative weak propagation theorems for tori.



1.
Introduction

Equidistribution of exponential sums indexed by the characters of a connected commutative algebraic group
G
over a finite field
k
were first studied in this generality by
[
6
]
. The formalism of loc. cit. attaches a geometric and an arithmetic Tannakian monodromy group to a perverse sheaf on
G
to control the equidistribution of the associated Fourier coefficients. This formalism yields equidistribution for continuous trace test functions, but not for arbitrary continuous class functions. Equidistribution is expected to hold more generally, for example, Katz proved equidistribution for all continuous class functions for Tannakian monodromy groups attached to perverse sheaves on
\mathbb{G}_{m}
in
[
12
]
.

The fundamental issue in the formalism of
[
6
]
is that the constructed Tannakian categories are not equipped with fiber functors. They introduce the definition of a
generically unramified sheaf
, i.e. a perverse sheaf whose associated Tannakian category admits sufficiently many fiber functors. Under this assumption, they are able to prove the expected equidistribution theorems in
[
6
, Thm. 4.15, Thm. 4.11]
. The goal of the present work is to construct the required fiber functors for these equidistribution theorems to hold:

Theorem
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({G}).
Then
M
is generically unramified.

In fact, we prove that the ramified locus is contained in a thin proper subset. Conceptually, this says that a perverse sheaf on a group ramifies outside a thin closed subset. This is expected because ramification, in the classical setting, occurs along a closed subset. We write out one consequence of the results proved here for the equidistribution of class functions.

Theorem
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
be pure of weight zero. Suppose
\mathscr{X}\subseteq\widehat{G}
is a complement of a thin proper subset such that
M
is unramified at all
\chi\in\mathscr{X}.
Assume the geometric and the arithmetic Tannakian monodromy group of
M
agree. There exists a compact Lie group
K
and conjugacy classes
\Theta_{M,\chi}\in K^{\sharp}
such that all continuous class-functions
f\colon K^{\sharp}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfy

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathscr{X}(k_{n})|}\sum_{\chi\in\mathscr{X}(k_{n})}f(\theta_{M,\chi})=\int_{K}f(g)dg.

The conjugacy classes
\Theta_{M,\chi}
in the above theorem are related to the perverse sheaf
M
via the Tannakian formalism in the following way. For each
\chi\in\mathscr{X}
and each object
M^{\prime}
in the Tannakian category generated by
M
under convolution, we have the vector space
H^{0}_{c}(G,M^{\prime}_{\chi})\otimes_{\iota}\mathbb{C}
on which
K
acts naturally. The conjugacy class
\Theta_{M,\chi}
acts coincides with that of the Frobenius endomorphism. The Lefschetz trace formula often yields a concrete expression for the trace of such an operator. The terms with
\chi\in\widehat{G}
and
\chi\notin\mathscr{X}
can be bounded by the generalized Riemann hypothesis and the stratification theorem (see below). This allows one to bound these terms with a power saving. Thus Tannakian monodromy groups become a much more flexible tool for the study of families of exponential sums indexed by the characters of a group.

This equidistribution statement can be generalized in various directions. We refer the interested reader to
[
6
, Ch. 4]
, especially
[
6
, Thm. 4.15, Thm. 4.11]
. In this article, the main equidistribution theorems are Theorem
7.17
and Theorem
7.18
.

1.1.1.
Stratification theorems

In
[
6
]
, the authors apply their vanishing theorems to prove stratification theorems for exponential sums indexed by the characters of a group. We can also utilize the vanishing theorem (see below) to obtain stratification theorems for exponential sums. We obtain two improvements over the stratification theorems in loc. cit. First, the ramification loci in the stratification theorem are closed. This is one of the crucial improvements over
[
6
]
that allows for the construction of fiber functors. Secondly, the stratification theorems hold uniformly in a family indexed by a scheme
X
. To our knowledge, this is the first stratification theorem that holds uniformly in families for groups of the form
S\times U
.

Theorem
.

Let
X
be of pure dimension
d
. We assume
G=S\times U
. Let
K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(G\times X)
be a complex of weight
\leq 0
and with perverse amplitude
\leq d
. There exists:

(1)

A stratification by closed subsets

\widehat{U}\times X=Z_{0}\supseteq Z_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq Z_{n-1}\supseteq Z_{n}=\emptyset

such that for all
0\leq i\leq n
the subset
Z_{i}
has codimension
\geq i
.

(2)

A stratification by thin subsets

\mathscr{C}(S)=\Delta_{0}\supset\Delta_{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq\Delta_{n-1}\supseteq\Delta_{n}=\emptyset

such that for all
0\leq j\leq n
the subset
\Delta_{j}
has codimension
\geq j
.

Let
m\geq 1
and
0\leq i,j\leq n-1
. For all
\chi\in\widehat{G}(k_{m})
and
x\in X(k_{m})
with
(\pi_{U}^{*}(\chi),x)\notin Z_{i+1}
and
\pi_{S}^{*}(\chi)\notin\Delta_{j+1}
, we have

\sum_{t\in G(k_{m})}\chi(t)t_{K}(t,x)\ll_{K}|k_{m}|^{(i+j)/2}.

For estimates on the size of proper thin subsets, the reader is referred to Proposition
2.84
. We remark that, in the terminology of
[
6
, Def. 1.23]
, a thin subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
is a finite union of tacs (because
S
is a semiabelian variety). For arbitrary groups, which are not a product, we record an absolute stratification theorem in Theorem
7.13

1.1.2.
Vanishing theorems

In order to prove the existence of the fiber functors and the above stratification theorem, stronger vanishing theorems are required. In particular, constructing fiber functors requires us to prove that the vanishing loci are closed. Since vanishing also has to be proven for the unipotent part, we have to prove relative vanishing theorems for semiabelian varieties. Theorem
3.29
is the main vanishing theorem of this article:

Theorem
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({S\times X})
. There exists a dense open subset
U\subseteq X
and a proper thin subset
\Delta\subset\mathscr{C}(S)
such that:

(1)

The complexes
\pi_{X?}(M_{\chi})
are lisse over
U
and their formation commutes with arbitrary base change
X^{\prime}\rightarrow U
for all
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(S)
.

(2)

The forget supports map

\pi_{X!}(M_{\chi})\rightarrow\pi_{X*}(M_{\chi})

is an isomorphism for all
\chi\notin\Delta
.

(3)

The complexes
\pi_{X?}(M_{\chi})
are perverse for all
\chi\notin\Delta.

We remark, that point (1) holds for all characters on the semiabelian variety
S
. The terminology of a thin subset is borrowed from
[
13
]
.

1.1.3.
Relative weak propagation

In order to construct the desired fiber functors, we have to prove a relative weak propagation theorem. The reason for this is that the relative propagation theorems amplify the vanishing via a spectral sequence argument. The idea is inspired by
[
14
]
, where propagation theorems are established in the absolute case for a semiabelian variety in a stronger form. To our knowledge, propagation theorems were only considered in the absolute case before and relative propagation theorems have not been known to hold. Our first propagation theorem is Theorem
5.6
.

Theorem
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({T\times X})
. Suppose there is a closed subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(T)
such that

{}^{\mathfrak{p}}\mathscr{H}^{0}(\pi_{X!}(M_{\chi}))=0.

for all
\chi\notin\Delta
. Each perverse subquotient
M^{\prime}
of
M_{\overline{k}}
satisfies

\pi_{X?}(M^{\prime}_{\chi})=0

for all
\chi\notin\Delta
(and either choice of support).

This theorem is proven from the relative classification of negligible sheaves, the vanishing theorems, and Artin’s vanishing theorem. We can also obtain a result for perverse degree one, see Theorem
5.7
.

Theorem
.

Let
T
be a torus over
k
and
X
a scheme of finite type over
k
. Let
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
. Suppose there is a closed subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(T)
such that

{}^{\mathfrak{p}}\mathscr{H}^{1}(\pi_{X!}(M_{\chi}))=0.

for all
\chi\notin\Delta
. Then we have

{}^{\mathfrak{p}}\mathscr{H}^{n}(\pi_{X!}(M_{\chi}))=0

for all
\chi\notin\Delta
and
n\geq 1
.

This theorem is proven by induction on the dimension of
T
and the first propagation theorem. We are not sure how the relative propagation theorems generalize to higher perverse degrees nor how they generalize to semiabelian varieties. The application of Artin’s vanishing theorem prevents a direct generalization to semiabelian varieties for the first propagation theorem. We also remark that the argument applies to all fields, not just finite fields, by the results of
[
8
]
, but we do not pursue it here.

1.1.4.
Classification of negligible sheaves

We go into detail about the classification of negligible sheaves. One of the cornerstones of the theory presented here is the classification of negligible sheaves on a constant seimabelian variety over a base scheme. Similar classifications have been achieved for abelian varieties, by
[
13
]
, for tori, by
[
8
]
, and for semiabelian varieties over the complex numbers by
[
14
]
. We prove a relative classification theorem for constant semiabelian varieties over finite fields. The main result is Theorem
4.2
, which states:

Theorem
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({S\times X})
be a geometrically irreducible perverse sheaf, such that there is a character
\nu\in\mathscr{C}(S)
with

[\pi_{X!}(M_{\nu})]=0

in the
K
-group of
X
. There is a quotient
\pi\colon S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
with connected fibers of dimension
d>0
, a perverse sheaf
N\in\text{Perv}({S^{\prime}\times X})
, and a character
\chi\in\widehat{S}(k)
such that

M=\pi^{*}(N)\otimes\mathscr{L}_{\chi}[d].

Moreover, for each
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(S)
we have
\pi_{X!}(M_{\chi})\neq 0
if and only if
\chi\in\overline{\chi}\cdot\pi^{*}(\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime}))
. Dually, we also have
\pi_{X*}(M_{\chi})\neq 0
if and only if
\chi\in\overline{\chi}\cdot\pi^{*}(\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime}))

The argument is based on the classification proven in
[
13
]
. We achieve this by closely following the argument given in
[
8
]
. One of the crucial steps during the induction is the amplification of the absolute classification to a relative classification. Since we are working exclusively over finite fields, we can not reproduce the argument from
[
8
]
and prove the amplification based on a specialization argument.

We remark two things about the classification which appear to be new. First, note that our classification uniquely characterizes the quotient and the character in terms of the ”Fourier support” of the perverse sheaf
M
. The Fourier support plays a central role to our construction of Tannakian category and could be useful in other contexts as well.

Secondly, the classification is arithmetic, in the sense that the quotient and the character descend to the base field
k
. To our knowledge, both of these points have not appeared in the literature before. The condition on the support is sure to generalize to other fields as well. We are not sure whether the descent generalizes to fields other than finite fields because the argument involves the computation of a Galois cohomology group. We also achieve the classification of negligible sheaves on all groups of the form
S\times U
in Theorem
4.13
. In principle, the classification is possible on all connected commutative algebraic groups. For our purposes, this is not required because we can reduce most statements to a product
S\times U
by the structure theorems for algebraic groups.

1.2.
Tannakian categories

We outline the construction of the Tannakian categories, in the hopes of making it more accessible. The primary goal of our construction is that the Tannakian categories come with fiber functors given by Fourier coefficients.

1.2.1.
Unramified characters

This is achieved by giving a new definition of an unramified character. This definition is given by Definition
3.1
. We believe this is the right notion to consider for the construction of fiber functors because the unramified characters can be characterized by the following proposition, at least when the perverse sheaf in question is pure or the group is affine. See Proposition
3.15
for a slightly more general and precise statement. We hope that the following Proposition allows one to find closed unramified loci in practice.

Theorem
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
and
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
a closed subset. Suppose
M
is pure or
G
is affine. The sheaf
M
is
\Delta
-unramified, i.e. unramified at all characters
\chi\notin\Delta
, if and only if:

(1)

The sheaf
M
is weakly unramified at all
\chi\notin\Delta
.

(2)

We have

\text{dim}(H^{0}(G,M_{\chi}))=\text{dim}(H^{0}(G,M_{\chi^{\prime}}))

for all
\chi,\chi^{\prime}\notin\Delta
.

Roughly speaking, this says that a character is unramified if and only if the Fourier coefficient is concentrated in degree zero, pure, and has the “right” dimension, i.e. the Tannakian dimension of
M
. In this way, our definition is almost optimal for constructing fiber functors, because it mainly requires the Fourier coefficient to have the right dimension. We then prove the following general vanishing theorem in Theorem
3.31
.

Theorem
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
. There exists a proper thin subset
\Delta\subset\mathscr{C}(G)
such that
M
is
\Delta
-unramified.

1.2.2.
Tannakian categories

We now describe the construction of the Tannakian categories and the fiber functors in detail. The Tannakian categories we consider here were first constructed in
[
8
, Sec. 3.9]
. As is well-known, the convolution product of two perverse sheaves is, in general, not perverse. However, it is perverse up to certain negligible factors. One constructs a
Tannakian category
with convolution as a tensor product by localizing the derived category at some category of negligible complexes, i.e. complexes which consist of negligible sheaves. One then proves that the
localized convolution product
preserves perversity. Our goal is to localize the Tannakian category in such a way that the Fourier coefficients remain well-defined on the localization.

This is achieved by gaining control over the negligible factors in a convolution product of two perverse sheaves. Let
\Delta\subset\mathscr{C}(G)
be a closed subset. Roughly speaking, we show that the negligible factors in a convolution of two
\Delta
-unramified perverse sheaves have vanishing Fourier coefficients outside
\Delta
. Moreover, we then prove that the convolution product of two
\Delta
-unramified perverse sheaves is
\Delta
-unramified. This is the basic mechanism of our construction, that we now describe in more detail.

Instead of neglecting all perverse sheaves, our primary goal is to only neglect perverse sheaves whose Fourier support is contained in a closed subset
\Delta
. The definition of the Fourier support of a complex
K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(G)
is

\text{FSupp}_{?}(K):=\{\chi\in\mathscr{C}(G)~|~H^{*}_{?}(K,M_{\chi})\neq 0\}^{\text{cl}}

See also Definition
5.1
. Note that we take the closure in the standard topology. An important point in the construction is Theorem
5.4
, which states

\text{FSupp}_{!}(K)=\text{FSupp}_{*}(K).

Based on this equality, we simply write
\text{FSupp}(K)
for the Fourier support. This equality is proven from the classification of negligible sheaves. By Poincare duality, this implies that the Fourier support behaves well with respect to duality

\text{FSupp}(K)=\text{FSupp}(K^{\vee}).

The basic idea is then to localize the category of perverse sheaves at the category

S_{\Delta}:=\{M\in\text{Perv}({G})~|~\text{FSupp}(M)\subseteq\Delta\}.

The Fourier support also behaves well with respect to filtrations of complexes, see Theorem
5.3
. This compatibility of filtrations is also proven from the classification of characters. We do not know how to obtain the compatibility without the classification of negligible sheaves. This compatibility with exact sequences implies that the category
S_{\Delta}
is Serre. Thus we can form the Serre quotient

\text{Perv}_{\Delta}({G}):=\text{Perv}({G})/S_{\Delta}.

We can now form the category

N_{\Delta}:=\{K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(G)~|~\text{FSupp}(K)\subseteq\Delta\}.

and then the Verdier quotient

\text{D}_{\Delta}^{b}(G):=\text{D}_{c}^{b}(G)/N_{\Delta}.

The compatibility of the Fourier support with filtrations implies that
\text{D}_{\Delta}^{b}(G)
is a triangulated category with
t
-structure. The heart of this
t
-structure is naturally equivalent to
\text{Perv}_{\Delta}({G})
. This is of fundamental importance to our construction. The point of the above localization is that the Fourier coefficient

\text{D}_{\Delta}^{b}(G)\rightarrow\text{D}_{\text{coh}}^{b}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}\text{-Vec}),~K\mapsto H^{*}_{c}(G,K_{\chi})

is well-defined for all
\chi\notin\Delta
. These functors will give the desired fiber functors on the Tannakian categories.

1.2.3.
Convolution product

We describe the basic mechanism behind our construction of the convolution product. To obtain the Tannakian categories, we must prove that the localized convolution product of two perverse sheaves is perverse. We proceed (almost) as in
[
8
, Prop. 3.9.3]
. The main point there, roughly speaking, is
[
8
, Prop. 3.9.2]
. Let
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
be a closed subset and
M,N\in\text{Perv}({G})
two
\Delta
-unramified perverse sheaves. We have isomorphisms

H^{*}_{c}(G,(M*_{!}N)_{\chi})\rightarrow H^{*}(G,(M*_{*}N)_{\chi})

for all
\chi\notin\Delta
by the Künneth formula. Morally (and, for tori, literally), Theorem
6.15
says that the above isomorphisms imply that the natural forget supports map

M*_{!}N\rightarrow M*_{*}N

is an isomorphism in
\text{D}_{\Delta}^{b}(G)
. Therefore, all arrows in the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{*}_{c}(G,(M*_{!}N)_{\chi})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{*}_{c}(G,(M*_{*}N)_{\chi})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{*}(G,(M*_{!}N)_{\chi})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{*}(G,(M*_{*}N)_{\chi})}}

are isomorphisms for all
\chi\notin\Delta
. The above criterion for isomorphism allows us to apply the decomposition theorem and the hard Lefschetz theorem to the convolution
M*_{!}N
. This implies that
M*_{!}N
is perverse and that all perverse subquotients of
M*_{!}N
are
\Delta
-unramified.

1.3.
Character theory

We describe the character theory in detail, because it is rather long. This summary, hopefully, makes it more accessible. The first step is to construct the
fundamental group
\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)
of
G
in Definition
2.1
. This is a commutative profinite group. The fundamental group behaves well in exact sequences, see Proposition
2.9
and Proposition
2.11
. It also satisfies a product formula by Proposition
2.10
A
character
is a continuous group morphism

\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)\rightarrow K

to the group of units in a finite extension
K/\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
. The
character group
is denoted
\mathscr{C}(G)
and is the group of all characters as above. The exactness of the fundamental group carries over to the character group by Theorem
8.4
to Propositions
2.18
and Proposition
2.19
. We have the Künneth formula

\mathscr{C}(G\times G^{\prime})=\mathscr{C}(G)\times\mathscr{C}(G^{\prime})

by Proposition
2.20
. Crucially, Proposition
2.19
implies that the map

q^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(G^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(G)

is surjective with finite kernel for an isogeny
q\colon G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
.

Example 1.1
.

Let
G=S
be a semiabelian variety. Proposition
2.35
gives an isomorphism

\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(S)=\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}}).

Hence we recover the characters considered by Gabber-Loeser in
[
8
]
for a torus and the characters considered by Krämer-Weissauer in
[
13
]
for an abelian variety.

Example 1.2
.

If a character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(G)
has finite order, then it “comes from a group morphism”

\chi\colon G(k_{n})\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}^{*}.

by Lemma
2.28
. We see that the characters of finite order coincide with the set of characters
\widehat{G}
considered in
[
6
]
.

Example 1.3
.

When
G=U
is unipotent, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)
is a pro-
p
group. This implies that any character has finite order. By Example
1.2
the set of characters
\mathscr{C}(U)
is equal to the set of characters considered in
[
6
]
. In particular, the set
\mathscr{C}(U)
coincides with set of closed points of the dual group
\widehat{U}_{\overline{k}}
.

We equip the space of characters
\mathscr{C}(G)
with two topologies, the thin and the standard topology. When we say a subset is closed, we always mean closed with respect to the standard topology.

Example 1.4
.

The characters
\mathscr{C}(U)
are in bijection with the closed points of
\widehat{U}_{\overline{k}}
. We define the thin and the standard topology on
\mathscr{C}(U)
by saying a set
Z\subseteq\mathscr{C}(U)
is closed, if it is the set of closed points of a closed subscheme of
\widehat{U}_{\overline{k}}
(see Definition
2.65
).

Example 1.5
.

Let
S
be a semiabelian variety. A subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
is called closed in the thin topology if it is a finite union of tacs (see Definition
2.39
). We call it closed in the standard topology, if it is closed with respect to the Zariski topology constructed in
[
8
]
for a torus (see Section
2.3.3
for a precise definition).

Example 1.6
.

Let
S
be a semiabelian variety and
U
a unipotent group. A subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S\times U)
is called
thin
, if there are tacs
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
and closed subsets
Z_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{C}(U)
such that

\Delta=\Delta_{1}\times Z_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\Delta_{n}\times Z_{n}.

This is precisely the product topology on
\mathscr{C}(S\times U)=\mathscr{C}(S)\times\mathscr{C}(U)
by Proposition
2.74
.

For a general group
G
, we reduce the definition of the topology to a product as follows. There exists an isogeny
q\colon G\rightarrow S\times U
by the structure theorem, Theorem
8.6
. We declare the map

q^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(S\times U)\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(G)

to be a quotient map in the thin and in the standard topology (see Definition
2.78
). This turns out to be a well-defined topology such that
\mathscr{C}(G)
is a topological group in both topologies. In the thin topology, the space
\mathscr{C}(G)
is a Noetherian irreducible space (see Proposition
2.81
). In the standard topology, the space
\mathscr{C}(G)
is a countable disjoint union of irreducible Noetherian spaces (see Proposition
2.80
) Based on this, we define the
dimension
of a subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
to be the Krull dimension in the standard topology (see Definition
2.78
). For any isogeny
q\colon G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
, the induced map

q^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(G^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(G)

is a surjective closed quotient map by Proposition
2.82
. For a closed subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G^{\prime})
, i.e. closed in the standard topology, we have

\text{dim}(\Delta)=\text{dim}(q^{*}(\Delta))

by Proposition
2.83
. For a thin subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
with codimension
d
, we have the estimate

\frac{|\Delta\cap\widehat{G}(k_{n})|}{|\widehat{G}(k_{n})|}\ll_{\Delta}|k|^{-nd}

by Proposition
2.84
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Fundamental group

We construct a fundamental group that classifies the characters of an algebraic group. The construction is such that the arithmetic characters recover the dual group from
[
6
]
. By introducing the fundamental group below, we also subsume all other previously considered definitions for a group
G
.

It is technically convenient to introduce the fundamental group of a disconnected group as well - we shall see, however, that this definition only remembers the connected component (see Corollary
2.7
). This will be helpful in proving Proposition
2.11
, which plays a central role in many of our arguments with the character group.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a possibly disconnected group. For all
m|n
with
n\geq 1
we define the
trace
to be

\text{Tr}_{k_{m}/k_{n}}\colon G(k_{m})\rightarrow G(k_{n}),~x\mapsto\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}m|i|n\\
i>0\end{subarray}}\text{Fr}_{k_{i}}(x).

We define the
fundamental group of
G
to be the limit along the traces

\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G):=\varprojlim_{n}{G}(k_{n}).

This is an inverse limit in the category of commutative profinite groups along the ordered set of positive integers ordered by the divisibility relation.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
G
be a possibly disconnected group over a finite field
k
and
n\geq 1
. There is a map

\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G_{k_{n}})

given by functoriality of the limit. The diagram defining the fundamental group of
G_{k_{n}}
is final in the diagram defining the fundamental group of
G
. Hence the above map is a canonical isomorphism. In particular, the fundamental group does not depend on the basefield
k
.

2.1.2.
Functoriality of the fundamental group

Proposition 2.8
.

Let

and

be possibly disconnected groups over a finite field

and

be a morphism. We have a continuous map

which is the limit of the maps

The fundamental group defines a functor from the category of possibly disconnected groups defined over

to the category of profinite commutative groups.

Proposition 2.9
.

We consider an exact sequence

0\rightarrow G^{\prime}\rightarrow G\rightarrow G^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow 0.

We have a short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G^{\prime})\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow 0

of profinite groups.

Proof.

We have an exact sequence by Corollary
2.5

0\rightarrow G^{\prime}(k_{n})\rightarrow G(k_{n})\rightarrow G^{\prime\prime}(k_{n})\rightarrow 0.

∎

We obtain the product formula.

Proposition 2.10
.

The natural map

\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G\times G^{\prime})\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)\times\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G^{\prime}),~\gamma\mapsto(\pi_{1*}(\gamma),\pi_{2*}(\gamma))

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Indeed, we have exact sequences

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G\times G^{\prime})\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G\times G^{\prime})\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)\rightarrow 0.

∎

The following lemma plays a fundamental role in the theory:

Proposition 2.11
.

We consider an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\Gamma\rightarrow G\rightarrow G^{\prime}\rightarrow 0,

where

is a finite group. We have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G)\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(G^{\prime})\rightarrow\Gamma(\overline{k})\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

By Remark

, it is sufficient if we provide the exact sequence after a finite extension of

. We can pass the exact sequence in Corollary

to the limit and obtain the short exact sequence

Our assumption on the Frobenius action on

implies that the maps in the exact sequence from Corollary

2.2.2.

We now prove a certain lemma, which will allow us to prove that Frobenius invariant linear subsets come from an arithmetic subgroup. We begin by computing the cohomology of the Weil group acting on the character group. We characterize quotients in terms of their character subgroup. This allows us to give a certain characterization of Frobenius invariant subsets inside the character variety.

Proof.

The equality implies

This implies the claim by Lemma

∎

Remark 2.31

2.3.
Semiabelian varieties

We discuss the character theory of a semiabelian variety
S
over a finite field
k
in more detail. We classify the characters by identifying the character group with the Tate group of the semiabelian variety. We then introduce the character variety and recall certain well-known facts about relative Fourier coefficients of semiabelian varieties.

2.3.1.
Characters

We begin by proving the comparison theorem. We compare the fundamental group constructed here to the tame fundamental group. The definition of the tame fundamental group requires us to construct a normal crossing compactification of
S
. We recall a well-known construction and show that all character sheaves are tamely ramified. We prove the comparison following a well-known method.

Definition 2.33
.

We have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow T_{\overline{k}}\rightarrow S_{\overline{k}}\rightarrow A_{\overline{k}}\rightarrow 0.

The scheme
S_{\overline{k}}
is a
T_{\overline{k}}
-torsor over
A_{\overline{k}}
. We can identify
T_{\overline{k}}\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}
for some
n\geq 0
. Note that we have an equivariant simple normal crossing compactification
T_{\overline{k}}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Since this compactification is equivariant, we can construct the compactification

\overline{S}:=\mathbb{P}^{n}\times_{T_{\overline{k}}}S.

This yields a simple normal crossing compactifiction
S\rightarrow\overline{S}
. We consider the tame fundamental group
\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}},e)
with respect to this compactification.

The following fact is crucial to us:

Lemma 2.34
.

Every etale isogeny
S^{\prime}\rightarrow S
extends to a tamely ramified cover

\tilde{S^{\prime}}\rightarrow\overline{S}

for a normal crossing compactification
\tilde{S^{\prime}}
of
S^{\prime}
. In particular, the morphism obtained in Lemma
2.15
factors to give a morphism

\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}},e)\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(S).

Proof.

The exact sequence appearing in the definition of the normal crossing compactification is functorial. In particular, we can diagonalize the map between the tori to obtain a map between compactifications. This map is automatically tame, because it can be factored into an abelian part, which is smooth, and a toric part, which of degree coprime to
p
and etale over
S
. In particular, the Lang torsor is tamely ramified and we obtain the desired morphism of compactifications.
∎

We prove that the map from the tame fundamental group is an isomorphism for semiabelian varieties
S
based on a well-known argument.

Proposition 2.35
.

The above map gives a canonical isomorphism

\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}},e)\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(S).

Proof.

The multiplicativity of the tame fundamental group

\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}}\times S_{\overline{k}},e)=\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}},e)\times\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}},e).

implies that any tame cover of
S_{\overline{k}}
can be equipped with a group structure (see
[
17
, Thm. 5.1]
). In particular, every tame cover can be represented by an isogeny. An isogeny can always be dominated by the Lang torsor (see
[
20
, Ch. VI, §1, 6]
). In particular, the map

\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}},e)\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(S)

is injective by Proposition
2.9
. Since it is surjective by Lemma
2.15
, we obtain the isomorphism.
∎

The character sheaves considered in
[
13
]
and
[
8
]
, are precisely the one-dimensional representations of the tame fundamental group of
G
. This means the character theory presented here agrees with their theory. We note a certain consequence of this theorem (see
[

]
and
[

]
).

Proposition 2.36
.

Let
k^{\prime}/k
be an extension and
K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(S_{k^{\prime}})

\chi(S,K)=\chi_{?}(S,K_{\chi}).

for all

.

We also make use of the cohomology of a semiabelian variety.

Let

be the dimension of

See

2.3.2.
Thin topology

We go on to define the first topology on the space of characters of a semiabelian variety, the
thin topology
. Such subsets have been considered before, for example in
[
5
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
6
]
. In
[
5
]
, these subsets are called quasi-linear, in
[
13
]
they are called thin and in
[
8
]
and
[
6
]
they are called finite unions of translated algebraic cotori. We have chosen the terminology thin because neither finite union of tac nor quasi-linear apply in our setting, especially once we generalize them to arbitrary groups.

Definition 2.39
.

Let
S
be a semiabelian variety over a finite field
k
. A
translated algebraic cotorus (tac)
is a subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
such that there is a group quotient quotient
\pi\colon S_{\overline{k}}\rightarrow S^{\prime}
with geometrically connected fibers and a character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(S)
with

\Delta=\chi\cdot\pi^{*}(\mathscr{C}(S)).

We say a subset is
thin
if it is a finite union of tacs. The
thin topology
is the topology on
\mathscr{C}(S)
where a subset is closed if it is thin.

The following follows from an argument similar to the proof of Lemma
2.29
.

Proposition 2.40
.

Suppose we are given (connected closed) subgroups
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq S
with associated quotients
\pi_{i}\colon S\rightarrow S/S_{i}
and characters
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\in\mathscr{C}(S)
. We define the quotient maps
\pi\colon S\rightarrow S/(S_{1}+S_{2})
. Put
\Delta_{i}:=\chi_{i}\cdot\pi_{i}^{*}(\mathscr{C}(S_{i}))
. All characters
\chi\in\Delta_{1}\cap\Delta_{2}
satisfy

\Delta_{1}\cap\Delta_{2}=\chi\cdot\pi^{*}(\mathscr{C}(S/(S_{1}+S_{2}))).

In particular,
\Delta_{1}\cap\Delta_{2}
is a tac.

Proposition 2.41
.

Let
\Delta_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
be thin subsets indexed by a set
i\in I
. The subset
\bigcap_{i\in I}\Delta_{i}
is a thin subset. In particular, the thin sets form a topology on
\mathscr{C}(S)
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on the dimension of
S
. If
S
has dimension zero, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, let
i\in I
be such that
\Delta_{i}
is a proper thin subset. The subset
\Delta_{i}
is a finite union of tacs, say
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n}
. We can assume
\Delta_{i}=\Delta_{1}
. The claim follows from the induction hypothesis and Proposition
2.40
.
∎

Lemma 2.42
.

Let
\pi\colon S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
a group morphism. The map

\pi^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(S)

is continuous in the thin topology. In particular, the group
\mathscr{C}(S)
is a topological group with respect to the thin topology.

We only prove that the group
\mathscr{C}(S)
is topological.

Proof.

Note that it is a topological group because the diagonal map
S\rightarrow S\times S
induces the addition map on character groups and the inversion induces the inversion map.
∎

Proposition 2.43
.

Let
\pi\colon S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
be a surjective map. The induced map

\pi^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(S)

is closed.

Proof.

We can factor
\pi
into an isogeny and a surjective map with connected fibers. We only consider the case when
\pi
is an isogeny, the other case can be treated by a similar argument.

Suppose
\pi
is an isogeny. Let
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\colon S^{\prime}_{\overline{k}}\rightarrow S^{\prime\prime}
a surjective group morphism with connected fibers. We can factor
\pi^{\prime}
into a square over a finite extension
k^{\prime}/k

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{k^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{\prime\prime\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{\prime\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime\prime\prime}}

such that
\pi^{\prime\prime\prime}
is an isogeny and
\pi^{\prime\prime}
has connected fibers. In particular, the map
(\pi^{\prime\prime\prime})^{*}
is surjective by Proposition
2.19
. We have

\pi^{*}(\chi\cdot\pi^{\prime*}(\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime\prime})))=\pi^{*}(\chi)\cdot\pi^{\prime\prime*}(\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime\prime\prime})).

Hence
\pi^{*}
is closed.
∎

Proposition 2.44
.

A subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
is irreducible in the thin topology if and only if it is a tac.

Proof.

By trivial bounds on the number of points of a tac, we see that a finite union of proper tacs can not cover a tac (see Lemma
2.52
and Lemma
2.76
).
∎

Proposition 2.45
.

The thin topology is Noetherian. In particular, thin subsets admit unique decompositions into tacs.

Proof.

Induction on the dimension of
S
as before.
∎

2.3.3.
Standard topology

We introduce the
standard topology
The Tannakian categories are defined by localizations along closed subsets. This topology is also well-known and has appeared in many other contexts, expecially when considering Mellin transforms on semiabelian varieties. It is analogous to the Zariski topology on the character variety of a complex torus.

Let
S
be a semiabelian variety over a finite field
k
. A character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(S)
can be factored into a pro-
\ell
part
\chi_{\ell}\in\mathscr{C}(S)
, whose monodromy is pro-
\ell
, and a coprime to
\ell
-part
\chi_{{\ell^{\prime}}}\in\mathscr{C}(S)
, whose monodromy is coprime to
\ell
, such that

\chi=\chi_{\ell}\chi_{\text{f}}.

We call the group of characters with pro-
\ell
monodromy by
\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S)
and the group of characters with mondoromy coprime to
\ell
by
\mathscr{C}^{\text{f}}(S)
. We have constructed a decomposition

\mathscr{C}(S)=\mathscr{C}^{\text{f}}(S)\times\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S).

This leads to a decomposition

\mathscr{C}(S)=\bigsqcup_{\chi\in\mathscr{C}^{\text{f}}(S)}\chi\cdot\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S).

The group of pro-
\ell
-characters is identified with the group (the notation is introduced in
8.1
)

\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S)=\mathscr{C}(\pi_{1}^{\ell,\text{char}}).

We define the Iwasawa algebra

R_{S,\text{int}}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}[[\pi_{1}^{\ell,\text{char}}]].

We form the tensor product

R_{S}:=R_{S,\text{int}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}}\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}.

It is well-known that the closed points of
\text{Spec}(R_{S})
identify with the set
\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{C}(\pi_{1}^{\ell,\text{char}}))
. We topologize
\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S)
by equipping it with the Zariski topology coming from the Iwasawa algebra and the above isomorphism. We then equip
\mathscr{C}(S)
with the coproduct topology. The resulting topology is called the
standard topology
. Note that it has countably many connected components.

Remark 2.46
.

A thin subset is closed in the standard topology.

We begin by proving that the maps are continuous.

Lemma 2.47
.

Let
\pi\colon S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
a group morphism. The pullback map

\pi^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(S)

is continuous in the standard topology. In particular, the group
\mathscr{C}(S)
is a topological group.

To evaluate the dimension of the components of a thin subset, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.48
.

Let
\pi\colon S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
be a surjective morphism. The induced map

\pi^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(S)

is a closed immersion.

.

This can be identified, componentwise, with a map on spectra induced by a morphism of rings. The morpshim
\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(S)\rightarrow\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(S^{\prime})
is surjective, which implies that the ring morphism
R_{S}\rightarrow R_{S^{\prime}}
is surjective. ∎

Proof.

The map is induced by a ring morphism of the localized Iwasawa algebra.
∎

Definition 2.49
.

The dimension of a closed subset is defined to be the Krull dimension.

Remark 2.50
.

The Krull dimension of a countable union of irreducible subsets is the supremum over the irreducible components.

Lemma 2.51
.

Let
q\colon S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
be an isogeny. A closed subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime})
satisfies

\text{dim}(q^{*}(\Delta))=\text{dim}(\Delta).

Proof.

The map
q^{*}
is a countable disjoint union of maps on spectra induced by a finite morphism of commutative rings. Hence it preserves dimension.
∎

Lemma 2.52
.

Let
S
be a semiabelian variety of dimension
d=d_{a}+d_{t}
. The dimension of any irreducible component of
\mathscr{C}(S)
(in the standard topology) is
2d_{a}+d_{t}
. In particular, for a quotient
\pi\colon S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
and a character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(S)
, the dimension of any irreducible component of
\chi\cdot\pi^{*}(\mathscr{C}(S^{\prime}))
is
2d^{\prime}_{a}+d^{\prime}_{t}
, where
d^{\prime}=d^{\prime}_{a}+d^{\prime}_{t}
is the dimension of
S^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Proposition
2.37
implies

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(S,e)

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}^{t}(S_{\overline{k}},e)

\displaystyle=\text{Hom}(H^{1}(S,\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}),\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}^{2d_{a}+d_{t}}.

In particular, the ring
R_{S,\text{int}}
can be identified with a power series ring over
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
in
2d_{a}+d_{t}
variables. Hence
R_{S}
has dimension
2d_{a}+d_{t}
(see
[
8
, 3.1]
). The second statement follows from Lemma
2.48
.
∎

The following theorem is used at a crucial point.

Proposition 2.53
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
be a tac and
\Delta^{\prime}\subset\Delta
a proper thin subset. Any irreducible component of
\Delta^{\prime}
is properly contained in an irreducible component of
\Delta
.

Proof.

By passing to an irreducible component of
\Delta^{\prime}
in the thin topology, we can assume
\Delta^{\prime}
is a tac. Note that the dimension of each irreducible components
\Delta^{\prime}
or
\Delta
has the same dimension as
\Delta
or
\Delta^{\prime}
by Lemma
2.52
. Thus any component of
\Delta^{\prime}
has strictly lower dimension than any component of
\Delta
.
∎

Proposition 2.54
.

Let
\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
be tacs. Suppose there exists a closed irreducible subset
Z\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
such that
Z
is an irreducible component of
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
. Then
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The subset
\Delta\cap\Delta^{\prime}
is a tac by Proposition
2.40
and we have
\Delta\cap\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta
. The sets
\Delta\cap\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta
have an irreducible component in common. Thus Proposition
2.53
implies

\Delta\cap\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta.

This means
\Delta\subseteq\Delta^{\prime}
. By symmetry, we obtain
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
.
∎

2.4.
Unipotent groups

We introduce the Fourier transform on a general unipotent commutative groups

2.4.1.
Characters

The identification of characters of a unipotent group with the points of the dual group rests on the following lemma:

Lemma 2.55
.

The group
\pi_{1}^{\text{char}}(U)
is a pro-
p
group. In particular, every character
\psi\in\mathscr{C}(U)
has finite order.

Proof.

There is
n\geq 1

.

We begin by introducing the dual group.

The

The algebraic group

is unique up to unique isomorphism and

is an involutive, contravariant functor.

The following is a reformulation of the construction in

Lemma 2.57
.

Let

. We choose a faithful character
\psi_{0}\colon\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}^{*}
. The character
\psi_{0}
induces a functorial isomorphism

U^{*}(k_{n})=\widehat{U}(k_{n})

for all

\mathscr{L}_{\psi_{0}}
on

\mathscr{L}_{\psi_{0}}|_{U\times\{\psi\}}=\mathscr{L}_{\psi}.

We regard the choice of
\psi_{0}
as fixed throughout and surpress it. We write
\mathscr{L}
for the universal sheaf constructed in the above Lemma. Lemma

allows us to identify the closed points of

with the arithmetic characters of

for a point on

from now on.

2.4.2.
Fourier transform

Definition 2.59
.

Let
X
be a scheme over
k
. The
Fourier transform
is defined to be the functor

\displaystyle\text{FT}_{?}\colon\text{D}_{c}^{b}(U\times X)\rightarrow\text{D}_{c}^{b}(\widehat{U}\times X),~K\mapsto\pi_{\widehat{U}?}(\pi_{U}^{*}K\otimes\mathscr{L}[d]).

We write
\text{FT}:=\text{FT}_{!}
.

We list the basic properties of the Fourier transform (see
[
19
]
).

Theorem 2.60
.

Let
U
be a unipotent group with dimension
d
. Consider
K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(U\times X)
.

(1)

The natural morphism

\text{FT}_{!}(K)\rightarrow\text{FT}_{*}(K).

is an isomorphism.

(2)

We have

\text{inv}^{*}D(\text{FT}(K))=\text{FT}(\text{inv}^{*}D(K))(d).

(3)

We have

K=\text{inv}^{*}(\text{FT}(\text{FT}(M)))(-d).

(4)

The Fourier transform is a
t
-exact equivalence of categories (with respect to the perverse
t
-structure).

(5)

For each closed point
\psi\in\widehat{U}
, we have

m_{\psi}^{*}(\text{FT}(K))=\text{FT}(K_{\psi}).

where
m_{\psi}(x):=x+\psi
is the additive shift.

The Fourier transform is functorial:

Proposition 2.61
.

Let
U
and
U^{\prime}
be unipotent groups of dimension
d
and
d^{\prime}
respectively. Let
\pi\colon U\rightarrow U^{\prime}
be a group morphism. We consider
K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(U\times X)
. We have

\displaystyle\text{FT}(\pi_{!}(K))

\displaystyle=\widehat{\pi}^{*}(\text{FT}(K))[d-d^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\text{FT}(\pi_{*}(K))

\displaystyle=\widehat{\pi}^{!}(\text{FT}(K))(d^{\prime}-d)[d^{\prime}-d]

If we apply this theorem to the multiplication map, then we obtain the following corollary (see Section

)

Corollary 2.62
.

Let
K,L\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(U).
We have isomorphisms

\displaystyle\text{FT}(K*_{!}L)

\displaystyle=\text{FT}(K)\otimes\text{FT}(L)[-d]

\displaystyle\text{FT}(K*_{*}L)

\displaystyle=\text{Hom}(D(\text{FT}(K)),\text{FT}(L))(d)[d]

We also require the functoriality of the Fourier transform in the base space.

Proposition 2.63
.

Let
\pi\colon X\rightarrow Y
be a morphism. Let
K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(U\times X)
and

a lisse sheaf on

. We have

\displaystyle\text{FT}(\pi_{*}(K))

\displaystyle=\pi_{*}(\text{FT}(K))

\displaystyle\text{FT}(\pi_{!}(K))

\displaystyle=\pi_{!}(\text{FT}(K))

\displaystyle\text{FT}(K\otimes\pi_{X}^{*}(F))

\displaystyle=\text{FT}(K)\otimes\pi_{X}^{*}F.

Proof.

The third formula can be verified from the projection formula using the standard argument for proving identities involving Fourier transforms.

We describe the character variety of a unipotent group in terms of its dual.

Lemma 2.64

There is a bijection

U(\overline{k})=\mathscr{C}(U).

which only depends on our implicit choice of character
\psi_{0}

Proof.

We have proven that all characters are arithmetic. Thus

\mathscr{C}(U)=\varinjlim_{n}\widehat{U}(k_{n})=\widehat{U}(\overline{k}).

2.4.3.
Topology

Definition 2.65
.

We say a set
Z\subseteq\mathscr{C}(U)
is closed in the thin and the standard topology if and only if it is the set of closed points of a closed subset
Z^{\prime}\subseteq\widehat{U}_{\overline{k}}
. We define the
dimension
of a closed subset to be its Krull dimension.

Remark 2.66
.

The dimension of a closed subset
Z\subseteq\mathscr{C}(U)
agrees with the Krull dimension of the associated closed subsets
Z^{\prime}\subseteq\widehat{U}
.

Lemma 2.67
.

Let
\pi\colon U\rightarrow U^{\prime}
be a group morphism. The induced map

\pi^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(U^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(U)

is a continuous. In particular,
\mathscr{C}(U)
is a topological group.

Proof.

The morphism
\pi^{*}
can be described by a morphism of spectra induced by a morphism of rings.
∎

Lemma 2.68
.

Let
\pi\colon U\rightarrow U^{\prime}
be a surjective morphism. The map

\pi^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(U^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(U)

is closed.

Proof.

We can factor the morphism into an isogeny and a surjective morphism with closed fibers. For an isogeny, the map
\pi^{*}\colon\widehat{U^{\prime}}\rightarrow\widehat{U}
is surjective, hence flat. Thus it is open. An open morphism of topological groups is closed.
∎

Lemma 2.69
.

Let
q\colon U\rightarrow U^{\prime}
be an isogeny and
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(U^{\prime})
a closed subset. We have

\text{dim}(q^{*}(\Delta))=\text{dim}(\Delta).

Proof.

The morphism of schemes
q^{*}\colon\widehat{U^{\prime}}\rightarrow\widehat{U}
is finite.
∎

2.5.
Character variety

The goal of this section is to introduce the
thin
and the
standard
topology on the character variety. The standard topology behaves much like the topology of a Noetherian scheme, with the exception that there can be countably many irreducible components. The dimension can be defined in terms of the standard topology.

2.5.1.
Topology on Products

We first construct the standard and the thin topology for certain products.

Definition 2.70
.

Let
G=S\times U
be a connected commutative algebraic group over a finite field
k
. The
thin
and the
standard
topology in
\mathscr{C}(G)
are given by the product topologies along the isomorphism

\mathscr{C}(G)=\mathscr{C}(S)\times\mathscr{C}(U).

We define the
dimension
of a closed subset
Z\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
to be the Krull dimension in the standard topology. Unless stated otherwise, a closed subset always means a closed set in the standard topology.

This definition has as an immediate advantage.

Lemma 2.71
.

Let
G=S\times U
and
G^{\prime}=S^{\prime}\times U^{\prime}
be connected commutative algebraic groups over a finite field
k
. Let
\pi\colon G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
be a group morphism. The induced map

\pi^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(G^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(G)

is a continuous map in the thin and the standard topology.

Proof.

The group morphism
\pi
can be written as a product of maps
S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
and
U\rightarrow U^{\prime}
up to a radiciel morphism
U\rightarrow S^{\prime}
. By
2.11
, we can assume the morphism is diagonal. Lemma
2.47
, Lemma
2.42
, and Lemma
2.67
say the compoonents of this map are continuous.
∎

2.5.2.
Topology

We can describe the connected components of the standard topology.

Lemma 2.72
.

Let
G=S\times U
. The character variety splits, in the standard topology, into a disjoint union

\mathscr{C}(G)=\bigsqcup_{\chi\in\mathscr{C}^{\text{fin}(S)}}\chi\cdot(\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S)\times\mathscr{C}(U)).

The product
\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S)\times\mathscr{C}(U)
is a Noetherian topological space. A closed subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
is irreducible if and only if there is a character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}^{f}(S)
, a closed, irreducible subset
\Delta_{S}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\ell}(S)
and an irreducible subset
Z_{U}\subseteq\mathscr{C}(U)
such that

\Delta=\chi\cdot(\Delta_{S}\times Z_{U}).

In particular, the dimension of
Z
is

\text{dim}(Z)=\text{dim}(\Delta_{S})+\text{dim}(Z_{U}).

Proof.

The product of two irreducible Noetherian topological spaces is irreducible and Noetherian.
∎

We can now describe all closed subsets as countable unions of irreducible subsets as in Lemma
2.72
and then determine the dimension as a maximum over all irreducible subset. Note that the decomposition in this Lemma is unique, hence we obtain irreducible components of character sets.

Proposition 2.73
.

Let
G=S\times U
. The character variety
\mathscr{C}(G)
in the standard topology is a countable disjoint union of irreducible Noetherian topological spaces. In particular, each closed subset admits a unique irreducible decomposition.

For the thin topology, we obtain similar statements.

Proposition 2.74
.

Let
G=S\times U
. The character variety
\mathscr{C}(G)
is an irreducible Noetherian space in the thin topology. In particular, each thin closed subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
can be uniquely written as finite a union of sets of the form
\Delta_{S}\times Z_{U}
, where
\Delta_{S}\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
is a tac and
Z\subseteq\mathscr{C}(U)
is the set of closed points of an irreducible closed subset of
\widehat{U}
.

Proof.

The product of two Noetherian spaces is Noetherian by CITE. It is irreducible by CITE. ∎

Remark 2.75
.

The Krull dimension of a countable union of irreducible subsets is the supremum over the irreducible components.

We can estimate the number of points of a thin subset based on its dimension.

Lemma 2.76
.

Let
G=S\times U
be a connected commutative algebraic group over a finite field
k
. Let
\Delta\subset\mathscr{C}(G)
be a thin subset. Suppose
\Delta
has codimension
d
. Then

\frac{|\Delta\cap\widehat{G}(k_{n})|}{|\widehat{G}(k_{n})|}\ll_{\Delta}|k|^{-nd}.

Proof.

A thin subset is a finite union of sets of the form
Z\times\Delta
for a tac
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(S)
and a closed subset
Z\subseteq\widehat{U}
. For
Z
, we can appeal to trivial bounds based on the dimension. For
\Delta
, we can again appeal to trivial bounds and the computation of the dimension given by Lemma
2.52
.
∎

2.5.3.
Isogenies

We record the following crucial corollary.

Corollary 2.77
.

Let
G=S\times U
be a connected commutative algebraic group over a finite field
k
. For an isogeny
q\colon G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
, the induced map

q^{*}\colon\mathscr{C}(G^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(G)

is a closed map in the thin and the standard topology.

Proof.

The isogeny is a product of isogenies. The irreducible subsets in the closed and the thin topology are therefore mapped to closed subsets. The irreducible decompositions constructed above imply that all closed sets are mapped to closed sets.
∎

Given two such isogenies, we can always find a third isogeny to a product dominated by the two. Thus it is sufficient if we prove that an isogeny between algebraic groups of the form

2.6.

During the course of our arguments, it will be convenient to have a shorthand notation for certain functors which appear repeatedly. For this reason, we introduce

Definition 2.85

\text{FM}_{\chi,?}:=\text{FT}(\pi_{?}(K_{\chi}))

\text{FM}_{\chi,?}:=\text{FT}(\pi_{?}(K_{\chi}))|_{\widehat{U}_{\chi,\Delta}}.

In most cases, we surpress

3.
Vanishing theorems

3.1.
Definition of
\Delta
-unramified

The goal of this section is to introduce the notion of a
\Delta
-unramified sheaf for a closed subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
. One of the core insights of this article is that one can give a convenient definition of an unramified character for a perverse sheaf.

Proposition
3.15
will give a more concrete and usable characterization than the definition just below. Proposition
3.15
shows that the unramified characters defined here are precisely those that can give fiber functors on Tannakian categories.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\pi\colon G\rightarrow U
be a morphism, whose restriction to the maximal unipotent connected subgroup of
G
induces an isogeny. Let
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
. We say
M
is
unramified
at a character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(G)
if:

(1)

The natural map

\text{FT}(\pi_{!}(M_{\chi}))\rightarrow\text{FT}(\pi_{*}(M_{\chi}))

is an isomorphism at
0
.

(2)

The complexes
\text{FT}(\pi_{?}({M}_{\chi}))
are perverse in a neighborhood of zero.

(3)

The complexes
\text{FT}(\pi_{?}(M_{\chi}))
are lisse in a neighborhood of zero.

Let
\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{C}(G)
be a subset of
\mathscr{C}(G)
. We say
M
is
\Delta
-unramified
if all
\chi\notin\Delta
are unramified for
M
.

We should get the following right out of the way:

Lemma 3.2
.

The above definition does not depend on
\pi
.

Proof.

If we are given two maps
\pi_{i}\colon G\rightarrow U_{i}
for
i=1,2
as in the above definition, we can find a unipotent group
U
with isogenies
q_{i}\colon U_{i}\rightarrow U
such that
q_{1}\pi_{1}=q_{2}\pi_{2}.

For any perverse sheaf
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
and character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(G)
we have a functorial isomorphisms

\text{FT}((q_{i}\pi_{1})_{?}(M_{\chi}))=q_{i}^{*}\text{FT}((\pi_{i})_{?}(M_{\chi}))

by Proposition
2.61
. Therefore, the sheaf
M
does not ramify at
\chi
“with respect to”
\pi_{1}
if and only if it does not ramify with respect to
q_{1}\pi_{1}
if and only if it does not ramifiy with respect to
\pi_{2}
.
∎

Definition 3.3
.

Let
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
. The sheaf
M
is called
weakly unramified
at a character
\chi\in\mathscr{C}(G)
if the following two conditions are satisfied.

(1)

The natural map

H^{*}_{c}(G,M_{\chi})\rightarrow H^{*}(G,M)

is an isomorphism.

(2)

The complexes
H^{*}_{?}(G,M_{\chi})
are concentrated in degree zero.

The following two examples attempt to highlight the difference and the similarities between the definition of an unramified character and the definition of a weakly unramified character. Once we have developed more theory, we also discuss Example

, which discusses the case

in detail.

Example 3.4
.

Let

a perverse sheaf on

is unramified for

if and only if
\text{FT}(M)
is lisse at
\psi
.

Example 3.5
.

Let

be a semiabelian group over a finite field

and

a perverse sheaf. The sheaf

is unramified at a character

3.3.
Stratifying Fourier Coefficients

The goal of this section is to stratify Fourier coefficients for semiabelian varieties. This is a crucial element in our stratification and vanishing theorems. Note that it is not possible to stratify Fourier coefficients for general groups, because character sheaves for unipotent groups have wild ramification. Similar stratifications were obtained in
[
6
, Ch. 2.4]
. We give the following example showcasing the general behavior of Fourier coefficients of complexes on semiabelian varieties in families.

Example 3.18

Let

. We form the hypergeometric sheaf

\mathscr{H}:=i_{1!}\mathscr{L}_{\psi}*_{!}i_{2!}\mathscr{L}_{\psi}*_{!}\cdots*_{!}i_{n!}\mathscr{L}_{\psi}.

Define

\pi\colon\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m},(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}

For a character
\chi=(\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{n})\in\mathscr{C}(\mathbb{G}_{m}^{n})

\pi_{!}(\mathscr{H}_{\chi})=\text{Hyp}(!,\psi;\chi_{1},\chi_{2},\ldots,\chi_{n};\emptyset).

The sheaf on the right is defined in
[
11
, 8.2]
. It is proven in
[

, Thm. 8.4.1]
, that the Fourier coefficients are lisse. This is the type of behaviour we would like to generalize to arbitrary complexes. Note that the lisseness holds for all characters and no stratification of the character variety is required.

We recall the notion of a stratification and the notion of a tame family (see

5.
Supports

In this section, we study supports of perverse sheaves. We do this first in the absolute case over an arbitrary group. We introduce the Fourier support and establish its basic properties. These basic properties lay the foundation for the theory of
\Delta
-localization because they allow us to define a perverse
t
-structure and a duality theory on the localized categories.

We then study supports in the relative setting. Here, we narrow our focus to tori and prove two propagation theorems. These propagation theorems are central to our construction of Tannakian categories because they will allow us to prove a certain criterion for a morphism to be an isomorphism.

In this section, we begin deviating from the convention of the previous sections with respect to the base field. In particular, this section is written for groups over a finite field or the algebraic closure of a finite field
k
. Strictly speaking, we never consider an algebraic group over the algebraic closure of a finite field but only the base change of an algebraic group to
\overline{k}
while implicitly remembering the group over
k
. This is so we obtain a well-defined notion of quasi-arithmetic complexes.

If the group
G
is defined over the algebraic closure of a finite field, following our convention made at the beginning, the category
\text{D}_{c}^{b}(G)
denotes the category of quasi-arithmetic complexes on
G
. Note that the irreducible constituents of a decomposition series of a perverse sheaf
M\in\text{Perv}({G})
descend to geometrically irreducible perverse sheaves defined over a finite field by definition of quasi-arithmeticity. This makes all the results of the previous section applicable to irreducible perverse sheaves.

5.1.
Fourier support

We now introduce the notion of Fourier support. The Fourier support plays a central role to
\Delta
-localization. The negligible complexes we introduce are defined by a condition on the Fourier support.

Definition 5.1

8.1.
The
\ell
-adic character group

We prove exactness for the group of
\ell
-adic characters of a profinite abelian group. For this section, we denote with
p\in\mathbb{N}
any prime and with
\ell
a fixed prime.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\pi,\pi^{\prime},\pi^{\prime\prime}
be profinite commutative groups. We say a sequence of continuous morphisms

0\rightarrow\pi^{\prime}\rightarrow\pi\rightarrow\pi^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow 0

is exact if and only if it is exact pointwise, i.e. as a sequence of commutative groups.

Recall the following structural theorem, which follows from the classification of finitely generated abelian groups.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\pi
be a profinite commutative group. Then

\pi=\prod_{p}\pi_{p}

where
\pi_{p}
is the maximal pro-
p
-quotient of
\pi
for a prime
p\in\mathbb{N}
. The functor

\pi\mapsto\pi_{p}

is an exact functor from the category of profinite commutative groups to the category of profinite commutative groups.

We introduce the group of
\ell
-adic characters for a topological group.

Definition 8.3
.

Let
\pi
be a topological group. An
\ell
-adic character of
\pi
is a continuous group morphism
\chi\colon\pi\rightarrow K_{\lambda}^{*}
for a finite extension
K_{\lambda}/\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
. We denote by
\mathscr{C}(\pi)
the group of all
\ell
-adic characters.

Our objective is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
, and
\pi^{\prime\prime}
be commutative profinite groups. We assume the maximal pro-
\ell
quotient of
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
and
\pi^{\prime\prime}
is finitely generated. If we are given an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\pi^{\prime}\rightarrow\pi\rightarrow\pi^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow 0.

then we have an exact sequence of commutative groups

0\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(\pi^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(\pi)\rightarrow\mathscr{C}(\pi^{\prime})\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

We have

\mathscr{C}(\pi)=\bigoplus_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{C}(\pi_{p}).

This decomposition splits the sequence on profinite groups and character groups into a direct product and a direct sum of sequences on profinite groups and abstract groups by Lemma
8.2
. Hence we can assume
\pi
is pro-
p^{\prime}
for a prime
p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
p^{\prime}\neq\ell
, the claim follows form the
p
-divisibility of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Suppose
p^{\prime}=\ell
. Let
M_{\lambda}
be the pro-
\ell
factor of the profinite group
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{*}
. Then we put

M:=\varinjlim M_{\lambda}.

The abelian group
M
is an
\ell
-divisible
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
-module. Hence it is an injective
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
-module. The sequence of character groups identifies with the sequence

0\rightarrow\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}}(\pi^{\prime\prime},M)\rightarrow\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}}(\pi,M)\rightarrow\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}}(\pi^{\prime},M)\rightarrow 0.

This sequence is exact because
M
is injective.
∎

8.2.
Structure theorem for algebraic groups

We require the following structural theorem for algebraic groups.

Theorem 8.6
.

There exists an isogeny

G\rightarrow A\times T\times U

such that
U
is a smooth unipotent group.

Proof.

Note that
G
admits an isogeny to a smooth, algebraic group by
[
3
, Prop. 2.9.2]
. Thus we may assume
G
is smooth. By
[
3
, Cor. 5.5]
, there exists an abelian variety
A\subseteq G
and a connected, affine subgroup
L\subseteq G
such that
G=L\cdot A
and
L\cap A
is finite. In particular, the map

G\rightarrow G/L\times G/A

is an isogeny. The group
G/L
admits the isogeny
A\rightarrow G/L
, hence it is an abelian variety. The group
G/A
admits the isogeny
L\rightarrow G/A
, hence the group
G/A
is affine and connected. By
[
3
, Thm. 5.3.1 (2)]
, it is the product of a torus with a unipotent group. We apply
[
3
, Prop. 2.9.2]
to construct an isogeny with
U
smooth.
∎

We prove a Lemma, which is required during the proof of the classification.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
S
be a semiabelian variety of dimension
d=d_{a}+d_{t}
over a finite field
k
. If
d_{t}>0
, there exists a finite extension
k^{\prime}/k
and a surjective group morphism
S_{k^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}
with connected kernel.

Proof.

By Theorem
8.6
, there exists an isogeny

S\rightarrow T\times A.

There exists a finite extension
k^{\prime}/k
such that the torus
T
splits, i.e.

T_{k^{\prime}}\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}^{d_{t}}.

This means we can find a group morphism

S_{k^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}.

Let
S^{\prime}
be the kernel, then we have the isogeny

S_{k^{\prime}}/(S^{\prime})^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}.

Thus

S_{k^{\prime}}/(S^{\prime})^{0}\cong\mathbb{G}_{m},

hence the Corollary.
∎

8.3.
Canonical map

We verify the commutativity of a certain diagram. This section is written for Noetherian schemes, because that is the natural generality for the commutativity of this diagram.

Definition 8.8
.

Let

be a separated morphism of finite type between Noetherian schemes and

(see
[
7
, Ch. 10.1]
). We define the map
f^{*}K\otimes f^{!}L\rightarrow f^{!}(K\otimes L)
as the adjoint of

f_{!}(f^{*}K\otimes f^{!}L)\xrightarrow{\sim}K\otimes f_{!}f^{*}L\rightarrow K\otimes L

where the second map is the counit and the first arrow is the isomorphism coming from the projection formula.

Remark 8.9
.

Let
i_{y}\colon\overline{y}\rightarrow Y
be the inclusion of a geometric point supported at a point
y\in Y
into
y
. This map is not of finite type. Therefore, the functor
i_{y}^{!}
is strictly speaking not defined. We define it as follows: let

be the closed immersion of the reduced subscheme supported over the closure of
\{y\}
. Then we define
i_{y}^{!}K:=i_{y}^{*}i^{!}K
. This also applies to the canonical map.

Remark 8.10
.

When
f
is proper, there are natural transformations
f^{*}f_{!}\rightarrow 1
and
1\rightarrow f_{!}f^{*}
. Using these maps map, one can write the morphism defining the map as

f_{!}(f^{*}K\otimes f^{!}L)\leftarrow f_{!}(f^{*}K\otimes f^{*}f_{!}f^{!}L)\leftarrow K\otimes f_{!}f^{!}L\rightarrow K\otimes L.

The first two maps compose to give the projection formula, hence the composite is an isomorphism.

8.4.
Perverse sheaves

We record a few lemmas on perverse sheaves, since they do not fit the text otherwise. The following lemma is based on
[
10
, Prop. 6.7.1]
.

Lemma 8.13
.

Let
X
be an irreducible geometrically unibranch scheme of dimension
d
. Let
M\in\text{Perv}({X})
be a perverse sheaf on
X
. If the stalks of
M
are concentrated in degree
-d
and have the same dimension at all closed points, then
M
is lisse.

Proof.

Let
U\subseteq X
be a dense open subset where
M
is lisse and denote by
Z:=X-U
the complement of
U
. We consider immersions
i\colon Z\rightarrow X
and
j\colon U\rightarrow X
. We have a triangle

i_{*}i^{!}M\rightarrow M\rightarrow j_{*}j^{*}M\xrightarrow{+}.

The sheaf
i_{*}i^{!}M
is concentrated in non-negative perverse degrees and is concentrated on
Z
. Hence

H^{-d}(i_{*}i^{!}M)=0.

In particular, we obtain an injection

H^{-d}(M)\rightarrow H^{-d}(j_{*}j^{*}M).

The specialization maps on
j_{*}j^{*}M
are injective because
X
is geometrically unibranch. Hence the specialization maps on
H^{-d}(M)
are injective. Note that all the stalks in
H^{-d}(M)
have the same dimension, hence the specialization maps are isomorphisms. Thus
H^{-d}(M)
is lisse and hence
M
is lisse.
∎

We begin by proving the following Lemma:

Lemma 8.14
.

Let
K\in\text{D}_{c}^{b}(X_{\overline{k}})
be a complex with perverse amplitude
[a,b]
. Let
X\rightarrow Y
be a morphism to an irreducible scheme
Y
of dimension
d
. There exists a dense open subset
U\subseteq Y
such that
i_{y}^{*}(M)[-d]
has perverse amplitude
[a,b]
for all points
y\in U
.

Proof.

We can assume
Y
is affine. By Noether normalization, we can assume
Y=\mathbb{A}^{d}
. The problem is Zariski local on
X
, hence we can assume
X\rightarrow Y
is affine. There is a closed embedding
X\rightarrow\mathbb{A}^{N}_{Y}
, hence we can assume
X=\mathbb{A}^{N}_{Y}
. There exists a dense open subset
U\subseteq Y
over which
K
is universally locally acyclic. Proposition
3.11
implies that the canonical map

i_{y}^{*}(M)\otimes i_{y}^{!}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})\rightarrow i_{y}^{!}(M)

is an isomorphism for all

. Let

. Artin’s vanishing theorem (and the existence of systems of parameters) implies that the cohomological amplitude of
i_{y}^{*}
is
[-d,-d+c]
and the cohomological amplitude of

is
[-d+c,-d+2c]
. Smoothness implies

i_{y}^{!}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell})=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{\ell}(-c)[-2c].

The statement follows by comparing the cohomological amplitude of the left- and the right-hand side of the canonical map.

∎
