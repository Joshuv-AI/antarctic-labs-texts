Modulator therapy: rates and stages of respiratory bacteriome restoration in cystic fibrosis patients chronically infected with Pseudomonadota
Authors: Olga L. Voronina, Natalia N. Ryzhova, Marina S. Kunda, Ekaterina I. Ermolova, Elena L. Amelina, Rezuan U. Kagazezhev, Alexander L. Gintsburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic lung infection caused by Pseudomonadota (PCH) in patients with cystic fibrosis (pwCF) is difficult to eradicate. CFTR modulators have a potential role in the prevention of airway infections, but their ability to eradicate chronic infection remains to be investigated.The aim of our study was to evaluate the impact of combination (antibacterial (AT) and modulator (MT)) therapy on the lung bacteriome composition (LBC) in the pwCF cohort.ResultsThe bacteriomes of sputum samples longitudinally collected from 57 Russian adult pwCFs chronically infected with Pseudomonadota CF pathogens (PCH) were analyzed. MT tended to reduce the bacterial load. The LMC did not significantly change in PCH pwCFs that received MT for less than three years. Two-component MT resulted in a temporary decrease in the proportion of the CF pathogen only when combined with a course of AT. Three-component MT has been successful in inducing favorable bacteriome changes (in terms of the abundance and diversity of anaerobic taxa) over a period of more than 3 years but not for all cases of Burkholderiales infection.ConclusionsThe respiratory system damaged by bronchiectasis is susceptible to new infections, so patient management requires constant monitoring of the LBC and replenishment of the therapeutic landscape with both new modulators and new antibacterial drugs.

Introduction

The healthy lung microbiota is characterized by a low bacterial load and a predominance of representatives from the Bacillota and Bacteroidota phyla, with relatively small proportions of Pseudomonadota and Actynomicetota [1]. Typically, the lung microbiome is in an ecologically dynamic state that is inherently shaped by the balance of three processes: microaspiration from the upper respiratory tract, clearance, and replication of local microbes [2]. Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a multisystem autosomal recessive genetic disease in which this balance is disrupted. Mutations in the cftr (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) gene result in an abnormal CFTR chloride channel protein, which is normally needed to regulate the flow of chloride, sodium, and bicarbonate ions across epithelial cell membranes [3]. Dysfunction in the chloride channel causes mucus dehydration and impaired mucociliary clearance. This environment provides a breeding ground for bacteria, increases stimulation of the immune system, and results in chronic lung inflammation. Hyperactive inflammatory responses contribute to a decline in lung function and eventually to respiratory failure. CF pathology is therefore characterized by the progressive loss of lung function through a cycle of infection, inflammation, and tissue damage [4].

The typical bacteria infecting the lungs of people with CF (pwCF), the so-called common CF pathogens, are Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, Achromobacter spp., Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Burkholderia spp. (primarily Burkholderia cepacia complex, Bcc) [5], and nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) [6].

Moreover, mucus hyperviscosity and oxygen consumption by neutrophil influx and bacterial proliferation create anoxic conditions that stimulate anaerobic growth [7]. Anaerobic bacteria may migrate from the oral cavity to the lower airways and then colonize the airways of patients at densities comparable to those of canonical pathogens [8]. Prevotella, Veillonella, Fusobacterium, Atopobium, Peptostreptococcus and Porphyromonas are the most frequently detected genera in the lungs of patients with CF [9]. The role of anaerobes in the lungs is still controversial. Some researchers associate more diverse anaerobes with less lung inflammation and better lung function [7]. Other authors have noted the virulence and resistance factors of anaerobes and their ability to increase the virulence and resistance potential of CF pathogens [10].

Changes in the composition of the lung microbiota become more pronounced with age. The “healthy” microbiome, with the prevalence of oral commensal bacteria found in infants, is disrupted by the dominance of CF pathogens by 3–5 years of age in pwCFs [11]. In adolescents and adults, the dominance of one typical CF pathogen is usually associated with decreased microbial diversity and is positively correlated with worsened lung function, increased inflammation, and increased antibiotic exposure [12].

Although the therapeutic landscape for CF includes the application of mucolytics, respiratory physiotherapy, and antibiotics, the eradication of CF pathogens remains a very complex task. CF pathogens use the following tactic to evade host defenses: switching between extracellular and intracellular lifestyles. They form aggregates in the lumens of the respiratory tract; control autophagy; and shelter inside macrophages, neutrophils, and the pulmonary epithelium, which reduces the effectiveness of standard antibiotic therapy in treating CF [13].

The development of CFTR modulators, which have been approved for use since 2012, has opened new possibilities for improving airway clearance in pwCFs [14]. Ivacaftor (IVA), a potentiator, was the first modulator approved as monotherapy. IVA improves the function of the CFTR protein at the cell surface [15]. Then, correctors that stabilize the conformation, trafficking, and embedding of the CFTR protein at the cell surface are synthesized: lumacaftor (LUM), tezacaftor (TEZ), elexacaftor (ELX), and vanzacaftor; and a new potentiator, deutivacaftor, is approved [16]. Currently, CFTR modulators, which include combinations of one (IVA), two (LUM/IVA and TEZ/IVA), and three (ELX/TEZ/IVA and vanzacaftor/TEZ/deutivacaftor) active components, are available for use. The spectrum of CFTR mutations for which modulator therapy is possible has gradually expanded. Currently, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved an ELX/TEZ/IVA for pwCF with at least one F508del mutation or any of the 271 predefined rare mutations [17]. In a comparative network meta-analysis of 13 studies of CFTR modulators of different combinations, Iftikhar et al. reported that vanzacaftor/TEZ/deutivacaftor and ELX/TEZ/IVA improved the predicted forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), CF questionnaire-revised respiratory domain scores, and sweat chloride levels more significantly than did LUM/IVA, TEZ/IVA, and IVA in pwCFs carrying at least one allele of the F508del mutation [18].

Studies of the effects of modulator therapy on the most common CF pathogen, P. aeruginosa, have demonstrated that despite the success of modulators in restoring CFTR-dependent mucociliary clearance and decreasing P. aeruginosa sputum load, CFTR modulators are not sufficient to eradicate P. aeruginosa in adults [19, 20]. A subset of people continues to experience two or more CF pulmonary exacerbations, requiring courses of intravenous antibiotics or hospitalization per year despite modulator use [21]. Thus, CFTR modulators have a potential role in the prevention of airway infections but not in the eradication of chronic infections [15].

In Russia, the Cystic Fibrosis Patient Registry includes 4,018 living patients with 275 genetic variants of the CFTR gene, among which the most common variant is F508del (51.40%) [22]. Four modulator therapy drugs (IVA, LUM/IVA, TEZ/IVA, and ELX/TEZ/IVA) are used by Russian patients, two of which (LUM/IVA and ELX/TEZ/IVA) are currently registered in the Russian Federation [22]. Our study cohort included 55 adult pwCFs, primarily chronically infected with CF pathogens, representing 20% of adult pwCFs registered in the Moscow region. The aim of our study was to evaluate the impact of combination (antibacterial and modulator) therapy on the lung bacteriome composition in the pwCF cohort.

Materials and methods

Materials

Clinical investigations were carried out within the framework of state assignment No. 056-00066-23-00 (2023–2025) of the Russian Ministry of Health for the N.F. Gamaleya National Research Center for Epidemiology and Microbiology “New approaches to the diagnosis and treatment of bacterial infections in patients with cystic fibrosis, contributing to the reduction of exacerbations of the chronic bronchopulmonary process and stabilization of respiratory function indicators”.

A total of 171 expectorated sputum samples from 55 adult pwCFs, whose characteristics are presented in Table 1, were collected from 2023 to July 2025. For 10 long-term pwCFs, data from previously conducted microbiome studies, starting in 2013, were used (BioProjects PRJNA544655, PRJNA544933, PRJNA545010, PRJNA590873, and PRJNA717158).

The sputum samples were collected during medical care at the Pulmonology Research Institute under FMBA of Russia. The samples were stored at -20 °C until they were delivered to the research laboratory. Sputum samples collection and medical record review were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Research Institute of Pulmonology under FMBA of Russia (protocol No. 04–23, April 24, 2023, and protocol No. 07–24, December 18, 2024) and by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of the N.F. Gamaleya National Research Center for Epidemiology and Microbiology (protocol No. 59, September 8, 2023). Our local ethics committees operate in accordance with national legislation and institutional requirements, based on the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Medical records helped to determine the clinical state at the time of sample collection: B, baseline (out of exacerbation); E, pulmonary exacerbation before antibiotic administration; AT, after antibiotic treatment of pulmonary exacerbation (episodic antibiotic therapy), and disease stage on the basis of percent predicted forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) values on the day of sample collection: early (FEV1 > 70%), intermediate (70% < FEV1 > 40%), or advanced (FEV1 < 40%) [23]. The disease aggressiveness phenotype (mild, moderate, or severe) was determined for 7 pwCFs followed for 5–11 years on the basis of the rate of change in FEV1 [24].

Physicians defined the patient’s clinical condition as pulmonary exacerbation based on the 12 criteria suggested by Fuchs et al. [25], their modification validated by the European Consensus Group [26], and the ECFS best practice guidelines [27]. Chronic infection was defined as recommended in the Registry of Patients with Cystic Fibrosis in the Russian Federation [22]. The presence of chronic Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection was determined by the treating physician using the modified Leeds criteria and/or anti-Pseudomonas antibodies. A patient is considered chronically infected if 50% of sputum cultures collected in the past 12 months (or at least four cultures during this period) were positive. The similar approach used in Germany and North America [28]. Emerging Pseudomonadota, according to the Registry of Patients with Cystic Fibrosis in the Russian Federation [22], refers to intermittent bacterial culture, that is, the periodic (episodic, temporary) detection of a specific microorganism in a patient’s biological material during laboratory testing.

Methods

DNA extraction

After a freeze‒thaw cycle, total DNA from the sputum samples was extracted via the MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA). This Kit enables purification of high-molecular-weight (100–200 kb) DNA via chemical and enzymatic lysis of samples and ensures the removal of contaminants and inhibitors.

Quantitative PCR

Total bacterial DNA levels were determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR) on a LightCycler® 480 II instrument (F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd., Basel, Switzerland). The reactions were carried out via the use of 1× Taqman PCR master mix with 1× LightCycler® 480 ResoLight Dye (F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd., Basel, Switzerland), primers for amplification of the V1‒V5 fragment of 16S rDNA [29], and 2 µL of 2‒10-fold diluted sputum DNA. A 10-fold dilution series of Pseudomonas aeruginosa GIMC5002:PAT-169 (CP043549) genomic DNA was used to construct a calibration curve for calculating the 16S rDNA copy number. To convert the DNA concentration to the 16S rDNA copy number, the presence of four rDNA operon copies per P. aeruginosa genome was considered. All the samples were assayed in duplicate.

Identification of several typical CF pathogens

To control the chronicity of the infection, alleles of genes characteristic of several typical CF pathogens were determined: trpE – P. aeruginosa, gltB, gyrB – Burkholderia, nrdA, nusA – Achromobacter, gapA - Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (the PubMLST database collection, URL: https://pubmlst.org/, accessed on 5 September 2025), adkA – E. coli [30], rpoB, tonB – K. pneumonia (the website of the Klebsiella pneumoniae species complex, URL: https://bigsdb.Pasteur.fr/klebsiella/, accessed on 5 September 2025), and groEL (hsp65) - nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) with primers TB11/TB12 [31]. Additionally, we used primers to variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) loci to confirm the identification of Mycobacterium abscessus: MAB_0030, MAB_1087, and MAB_4284c [32]. S aureus was identified by tuf gene sequencing [33]. Sputum DNA was amplified, and amplicons were sequenced as described previously [34].

Microbiological detection of NTM was performed in the WHO supra-national reference laboratory at the Central TB Research Institute using culture media and conditions detailed in the review of existing solid growth media for mycobacteria culture [35].

Bacteriome sequencing

Libraries of 16 S rDNA fragments, including the V1‒V5 regions of the 16 S rDNA gene, were prepared via the Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) for sequencing on a NextSeq 500/550 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) or Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford Science Park, UK) with the NEB-Next Companion Module v2 for Oxford Nanopore Technologies Ligation Sequencing (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) for sequencing on a GridIon in a Flow Cell R10.4.1 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford Science Park, UK). The sequencing data were deposited in GenBank, BioProject ID: PRJNA717158.

Bacteriome data analysis

Bacteriome data were analyzed via the Microbial Genomics Module of the CLC Genomic Workbench v.21.0.1 (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA). The Greengenes v13_8 database with a similarity level of 97% was used to define operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Nineteen OTUs were categorized as typical CF pathogens: Mycobacterium (Actinomycetota), Staphylococcus aureus (Bacillota), Pseudomonas, Pseudomonadaceae, Burkholderia, Lautropia, Burkholderiaceae, Burkholderiales, Achromobacter, Alcaligenaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, Klebsiella, Stenotrophomonas, Lysobacteraceae, Haemophilus, Pasteurellaceae, Campylobacter, Moraxellaceae, and Alphaproteobacteria (Pseudomonadota). Thirty OUTs were categorized as obligate and facultative anaerobes and named “anaerobes”: Rothia, Atopobium, Actinomyces (Actinomycetota), Capnocytophaga, Prevotella, Bacteroidales, Paludibacter, Porphyromonas, Paraprevotella, Weeksellaceae (Bacteroidota), Oribacterium, Granulicatella-2, Lactobacillus, Gemellaceae, Bulleidia, Aerococcaceae, Catonella, Veillonella, Parvimonas, Planococcaceae, Abiotrophia, Enterococcus, Clostridiaceae, Paenibacillus, Gemella, Alloiococcus, Coprococcus, Peptostreptococcus (Bacillota), and Leptotrichia (Fusobacteriota). The facultative anaerobic genus Streptococcus was analyzed separately.

Shannon’s entropy coefficient was used to assess alpha diversity. The significance of differences in alpha diversity among sample groups was calculated via the Kruskal‒Wallis H test and the Mann‒Whitney U test and confirmed via PERMANOVA (p value < 0.05). Beta diversity was assessed via UniFrac metrics. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and MS Excel statistical functions were used for a detailed analysis of similarity between samples. The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) algorithm was used to detect taxa that are markers of differences between the analyzed groups [36]. Only reliable values of the LDA score (log 10) ≥ 4–5 were taken into account.

XLSTAT 2025.1.3 was used for statistical analysis of the data, performing the Kruskal‒Wallis H test and correlation test with the Pearson correlation coefficient (XLSTAT by Lumivero, URL: https://www.xlstat.com/, accessed on 15 September 2025).

Margalef’s Richness Index (Dmg) was used to measure and compare the anaerobe OTU richness across the CF microbiomes. Dmg was determined by dividing the number of anaerobic OTUs (S) by the natural logarithm of the total relative abundance of the anaerobic OTUs (N): Dmg = S-1/lnN.

Results

Patients in the analysis

The cohort of 55 adult pwCFs included 56% females and 44% males aged 18.1–56.7 years (mean age 30.4 years, median 29.8 years). The patients were grouped by the parameters presented in Table 1. Among the three age groups, the oldest age group was the largest. PwCF in this group had a high proportion of non-F508del/non-F508del mutations, whereas the youngest age group was dominated by the F508del/F508del mutation. The proportion of F508del/non-F508del mutations was high in all age groups. In pwCFs in the oldest age group, advanced disease stage was prevalent, whereas a younger pwCF had a greater proportion of intermediate disease stage. The majority of pwCFs in the younger age groups were receiving modulator therapy (MT), whereas in the older age group, pwCFs with and without MT were represented in approximately equal proportions. PwCFs chronically infected with Pseudomonadota (PCH) were predominant in all age groups. The greatest diversity of Pseudomonadota representatives was found in sputum samples from the oldest age group, pwCF: P. aeruginosa, Bcc, Achromobacter spp., Enterobacteriacae, and H. influenzae. The duration of MT was two to three years in the younger age groups and up to one year in the older age groups. The exception was a patient in the older age group who had received MT for 9 years. PwCFs taking three-component modulators were predominant in the middle-aged and older age groups, whereas in the younger age group, the proportions of two- and three-component modulators were almost equal. Changes in CFTR modulators occurred in all age groups. Only in the oldest age group were three pwCFs noted to refuse to take LUM/IVA due to intolerance. In the younger age group, ELX/TEZ/IVA was discontinued in one CF patient (F508del/non-F508del) because of a lack of efficacy.

Primary characteristics of sputum samples

Sputum samples were primarily assessed for 16S rDNA concentration and the presence of major CF pathogens: P. aeruginosa, Achromobacter spp., Bcc, E. coli, S. maltophilia, NTM, S. aureus, and a rare CF pathogen K. pneumonia.

16S rDNA gene concentration

To assess the bacterial load in sputum samples, the concentration of the 16S rDNA gene was measured in DNA isolated from sputum and expressed as 16S rDNA gene copies per ml of sputum. The samples had the following bacterial load: 6% of samples - an average of 2.9E + 09 (2.0E + 09–5.0E + 09) copies/ml; 64% of samples - an average of 7.0E + 08 (2.0E + 08–1.8E + 09) copies/ml; and 30% of samples - an average of 1.0E + 08 (2.0E + 07–1.9E + 08) copies/ml.

Analysis of the duration of MT in subgroups on the basis of 16S rDNA concentrations revealed that the average duration of MT was 0.5 years in the subgroup with the highest DNA concentration, 1.1 years in the average subgroup, and 1.5 years in the subgroup with the lowest DNA concentration. Thus, the bacterial load tended to decrease with increasing duration of MT in this pwCF cohort.

Typical CF pathogens in sputum samples

Monitoring typical CF pathogens, especially bacteria of the phylum Pseudomonadota, is important for assessing the effectiveness of antibiotic therapy. We used an expression method of amplification and sequencing of taxon-specific targets to identify some relevant CF pathogens and verify the chronicity of infection. The following Pseudomonadota CF pathogens were detected in 80% of the samples: P. aeruginosa (64%), Achromobacter spp. (11%), Bcc (10%), E. coli (9%), S. maltophilia (7%), H. influenzae (4%), and K. pneumoniae (1%). A representative of the phylum Actinomycetota, the NTM Mycobacterium abscessus, was detected in one patient’s sample.

P. aeruginosa isolates from chronically infected pwCFs in the study had different genotypes, with the exception of those from siblings. A more detailed analysis of isolates from three pwCFs included for the first time in a molecular genetic study of respiratory infections, confirmed the genotype differences. The pwCFs were chronically infected with P. aeruginosa ST9 (123-CF), ST198 (137-CF), or ST859 (132-CF).

Most of the samples with BCC contained B. cenocepacia ST709, which is an epidemic clone in Russia [31]. One pwCF was chronically infected with B. multivorans ST835. B. multivorans ST195 and B. cepacia ST9 were detected once. Achromobacters were also diverse and represented by two species and five genotypes: A. ruhlandii ST262 and ST36 and A. xylosoxidans ST207, ST257, and ST346.

The following alleles of the adk gene were identified in samples containing E. coli: 92, 14, and 21. Two pwCFs were infected with E. coli ST648, but the genomes of the isolates differed in that they excluded the presence of a single source of infection [30]. A mixed infection of E. coli and S. maltophilia (gap gene alleles: 22 and 11) was revealed in two pwCFs. Another CF patient had coinfection with K. pneumoniae ST716 and S. maltophilia (gap: 183).

Five pwCFs were long-term infected with H. influenzae (relative abundance 15–62%). Notably, in samples with H. influenzae loads of 50% or more, amplification and Sanger sequencing of 16 S rDNA fragments made it possible to identify this microorganism.

S. aureus, a CF pathogen of the phylum Bacillota, had a high relative abundance (30–94%) in 12 samples from 10 pwCFs, primarily from the P and the NP (with emerging Pseudomonadota and without Pseudomonadota) group, in clinical states of exacerbation (E) or during the course of antibiotic therapy (AT).

Microbiome analysis

Microbiome analysis provides information on the representation of different bacterial taxa in sputum samples, as well as the dynamics of changes in the ratio of taxa in different clinical conditions. To characterize the microbiome of the samples in general, we assessed alpha diversity via Shannon’s entropy coefficient, the relative abundance of CF pathogens, and anaerobes (taxa of obligate and facultative anaerobic bacteria) (Table 2). The facultative anaerobe Streptococcus was assessed separately, as the abnormally high abundance of this taxon (26–77%) in some samples deserves special attention.

The analysis revealed that the relative abundance of CF pathogens was inversely proportional to the Shannon entropy, the relative abundance of anaerobes, and the relative abundance of Streptococcus, with high correlation coefficients. The effect of MT duration on the relative abundance of CF pathogens, anaerobes and Streptococcus was weak, as confirmed by the low values of the correlation coefficient.

The Shannon coefficient, an indicator of alpha diversity, varied in the sputum samples within the range of 0.12–3.73, reaching its maximum value in a 29-year-old patient in the early disease stage, in the baseline clinical state, uninfected with Pseudomonadota pathogens, and receiving MT for approximately one year. The minimum Shannon coefficient value was observed in the oldest patient (56.7 years old), in the early disease stage, in the baseline clinical state, who was chronically infected with P. aeruginosa, and who had received MT for less than one year.

We divided the samples into subgroups on the basis of the Shannon coefficient in increments of one and estimated the marker taxa of each subgroup via the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) algorithm (Fig. 1). In the subgroup with the highest scores, the marker taxa were anaerobes and TM7-3. TM7-3 is a taxon of the kingdom Bacillati, phylum Minisyncoccota [37], previously known as Candidatus Saccharimonadota and candidate phylum radiation (CPR). TM7-3 bacteria are known for their small genome size and limited synthetic ability and are usually obligate symbionts or parasites of other organisms [38]. The highest values of TM7-3 relative abundances (11–23%) were observed in samples from pwCFs with early disease stages, those that were chronically infected with P. aeruginosa, and those that were in the first six months of receiving MT.

The subgroup with a Shannon coefficient of 2.0–2.9 was characterized by the anaerobes Streptococcus, Granulicatella, and Capnocytophaga.

In the subgroup with lower diversity values, the marker taxa were Staphylococcus aureus and Haemophilus. Finally, the subgroup with the lowest Shannon coefficient was distinguished by Pseudomonas and Enterobacteriaceae.

Beta diversity analysis of the samples revealed a disproportionate microbiome composition not only of CF pathogens but also of anaerobic taxa. Principal coordinate analysis (Fig. 2) revealed centripetal movement of the samples with increasing proportions of the CF pathogens. The trajectory of movement is determined by the pathogen taxon. Extremely high relative abundances of CF pathogens were observed in samples from pwCFs infected with Bcc, P. aeruginosa, Achromobacter spp., and Enterobacteriaceae, reaching 98–100%; for S. aureus, the relative abundance was somewhat lower, reaching 84%. The maximum relative abundance of Haemophilus was 62%. The increasing relative abundances of Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Rothia also caused the samples to move away from the center.

Clinical state and sputum microbiome composition

The composition of the sputum microbiome is related to the patient’s clinical state. The relative abundance of CF pathogens was significantly greater in samples obtained during exacerbations (Fig. 3A), whereas the relative abundance of Streptococcus increased in the baseline clinical state (Fig. 3B). The duration of MT was greater in samples obtained in the baseline clinical state than in samples obtained in the exacerbation state or AT clinical state (Fig. 3C).

Only two patients (77-CF and 114-CF) at the first observation point had the highest Shannon entropy index for their microbiome during the study, approximately 2.5 (Fig. 11H, J). Discussion

CFTR modulators have expanded the therapeutic landscape for CF and improved the quality of life for pwCFs affected by modulator therapy (MT). The developers and researchers of CFTR modulators were awarded the Lasker Clinical Research Prize in 2025. However, a complete understanding of the modulator’s impact on lung infections, particularly in chronically infected adult pwCFs, remains unclear. Previous studies of the effects of modulator treatment on the lung microbiome have focused mostly on F508del CFTR homozygous participants and have lasted no more than a year [18, 41, 42].

Our three-year study included 55 adult pwCFs chronically infected with CF pathogens, primarily of the phylum Pseudomonadota. The participants had not only F508del CFTR homozygotes but also F508del CFTR-heterozygous and non-F508del CFTR mutations. The study revealed that with increasing duration of modulator therapy, there was a trend toward a decrease in the bacterial load in sputum samples. A trend toward decreased total bacterial abundance was also shown by Hilliam et al., who studied the bacterial community of the middle meatus of pwCF after 42 months of therapy with the ELX/TEZ/IVA modulator [43]. However, a number of studies characterize the results of modulator therapy on the basis of only the average sputum densities of traditional CF pathogens, noting a 10- to 100-fold reduction in this indicator [41, 44].

In our cohort of 55 adult pwCFs, most of whom were chronically infected with Pseudomonadota, the effect of MT duration on the relative abundance of CF pathogens, anaerobes and Streptococcus was weak, as confirmed by the low values of the correlation coefficient (Table 2). Some pwCFs presented significant fluctuations in typical CF pathogens in sputum samples, whereas other pwCFs presented a consistently high proportion of pathogens in the microbiome of sputum samples. Extremely high relative abundances of CF pathogens were observed in samples from patients infected with Bcc, P. aeruginosa, Achromobacter spp., and Enterobacteriaceae, reaching 98–100%. Neerincx et al. also reported only a temporary reduction in P. aeruginosa abundance in sputum under the influence of LUM/IVA modulator therapy [45].

A decrease in the relative abundance of CF pathogens was correlated with an increase in the Shannon coefficient, an indicator of alpha diversity. Anaerobes were marker taxa for the subgroups of samples with the highest Shannon coefficient values: 3.0 to 3.9 and 2.0 to 2.9 (Fig. 1). In the 3.0 to 3.9 group, anaerobes were complemented by the TM7-3 taxon of the phylum Minisyncoccota, and in the 2.0 to 2.9 group, they were complemented by Streptococcus, a facultative anaerobe considered separately. Samples in which the relative abundance of anaerobes was greater than 10% were found in 81% of pwCFs in the NP group and in 33% of pwCFs in the PCH group, both with and without modulator therapy. In the PCH group, such samples were more often associated with antibiotic therapy, and in the NP group, they were approximately equally common in the baseline and exacerbation states. Overall, across the entire cohort, samples with a relatively high relative abundance of anaerobes were observed in the baseline state (p = 0.015). Only four patients with early or intermediate lung disease in the NP group had a greater relative abundance of anaerobes in the exacerbation state than in the baseline state. The proportion of S. aureus as a CF pathogen in these samples ranged from 2.0 to 52.0%. According to observations by Zhao et al. and Carmody et al., the relative abundance of anaerobic genera increased significantly during exacerbations before episodic antibiotic treatment compared with baseline [23, 46]. A detailed analysis of the patient P2 samples presented by Zhao et al. [46] revealed that the observation is true for samples with a relative abundance of CF pathogens of 40–50% in the baseline state; with an increase in the relative abundance of CF pathogens, the relative abundance of anaerobes during exacerbation becomes minimal. Most patients in our cohort had a high relative abundance of CF pathogens in their sputum samples.

The role of anaerobes in the lung microbiome of pwCFs remains highly controversial. Some researchers have shown that the relative abundance of anaerobes in sputum samples is associated with less inflammation [12] and improved lung function [10]. Other researchers have identified potential mechanisms by which anaerobes can lead to disease progression in the lungs of pwCF: (a) the production of short-chain fatty acids by anaerobes stimulates the release of proinflammatory cytokines from human bronchial epithelial cells [47]; (b) anaerobes degrade mucin and synthesize nutrients for CF pathogens [48]; and (c) anaerobes produce β-lactamases, which mediate antimicrobial resistance [49, 50].

Particular attention should be given to the excessive increase in the proportion of individual taxa of anaerobes with a decrease in the relative abundance of CF pathogens. In our pwCF cohort samples, the proportion of anaerobes reached 40% for Rothia, Prevotella, Capnocytophaga, and Oribacterium; 27–28% for Athopobium and Granulicatella; 23% for TM7-3; 18% for Lactobacillus; and 15% for Bulleidia. Moreover, in healthy individuals, the proportion of the most abundant anaerobe, Prevotella, was 15% in the right upper lobe of the lungs and 19% in the supraglottic space [51]. The proportion of Streptococcus increased even more significantly with a decrease in the proportion of CF pathogens, reaching 23–77% in the cohort samples, compared with 5% in the right upper lobe of the lungs and 13% in the supraglottic space in healthy individuals [51]. Such an abnormal increase in the proportion of some genera of anaerobes at the first stages of reducing the relative abundance of CF pathogens under the influence of complex therapy is the result of the formation of a special microbial community in the pwCF lungs in conditions that support the growth of anaerobic bacteria: secretion of the thick mucus, hypoxia and the presence of anaerobic niches [52]. Normalization of the composition of the microbiome implies the development of the microbial community with a high diversity of both anaerobes and representatives of the Bacillota and Bacteroidota as a whole.

In our study, Streptococcus was the marker taxon in the MT group (Fig. 5) and competed with the Pseudomonadota CF pathogens in the PCH group and with Staphylococcus in the NP group. On the other hand, during exacerbations, in some patients with a low proportion of CF pathogens, the relative abundance of Streptococcus increased to 32–70%. Notably, several studies have shown that the presence of Streptococcus as the predominant genus in the lungs is correlated with exacerbations [53, 54].

Long-term modulator therapy is essential for improving the airway microbiota in pwCFs. A Danish national cohort study of airway pathogens revealed that even three years after ELX/TEZ/IVA initiation in 201 pwCFs over 12 years of age, airway secretions in two-thirds of pwCFs remained culture-positive with the same airway pathogens as before they started ELX/TEZ/IVA [55]. In our cohort of 55 adult patients, the average duration of modulator therapy was 2.1 years (range, 0.23–9.0 years). Therefore, we supplemented the study with data from seven pwCF receiving modulators for more than three years (3.1–9.0).

For patients chronically infected with P. aeruginosa, switching to the three-component modulator ELX/TEZ/IVA, which reduces the proportion of P. aeruginosa, as well as S. aureus and Streptococcus, competitors that replace the main CF pathogen, is important (Fig. 6A, B, C). As a result, the microbiomes with a relative abundance of P. aeruginosa less than 1% and S. aureus ranging from 0.5 to 9% and with a diversity of anaerobes corresponded to the most favorable pulmonary type 4 microbiome, as defined by Widder et al. [56]. We demonstrated an even clearer effect of modulator composition in a group of pwCFs with the F508del/F508del CFTR mutation infected with P. aeruginosa and observed beginning in early adolescence. Among the three pwCFs receiving the dual-modulator LUM/IVA, two twins with moderate lung disease (Fig. 9A, C) had a consistently high relative abundance of P. aeruginosa but an extremely low relative abundance of anaerobes, including Streptococcus, corresponding to pulmonary type 5, according to Widder et al. [56]. However, over the last three years of observation, the patients showed only a slight decrease in the predicted FEV1% and no exacerbations. In the third CF patient who received LUM/IVA, a decrease in the relative abundance of P. aeruginosa occurred only during the course of antibiotic treatment for exacerbation (Fig. 9E). In pwCFs with the F508del/F508del CFTR mutation infected with Burkholderiales (B. cenocepacia ST709–64CF and (A) ruhlandii ST262–106CF), when switching to the three-component modulator ELX/TEZ/IVA, a temporary decrease in the relative abundance of the main CF pathogen was observed, but eradication was not achieved even after six years of modulator therapy in the case of 106-CF (Fig. 9C). Thus, at the last observation point, the microbiome of patient 64-CF temporarily had pulmotype 4, and that of patient 106-CF could be classified as pulmotype 6. In our opinion, such an interpretation of pulmotypes is possible by replacing the position of P. aeruginosa with any Pseudomonadota CF pathogen in the model of pulmotypes by Widder et al. [56]. In the patient with the non-F508del/non-F508del CFTR mutation infected with (B) multivorans ST835, a year and a half after switching to ELX/TEZ/IVA did not lead to a decrease in the relative abundance of the main CF pathogen but predicted that the FEV1% indicators somewhat stabilized at a low value of 37.5, despite episodes of exacerbation (Fig. 9A; Table 4). Overall, in a cohort of 55 adult patients, the relative abundance of CF pathogens was significantly lower, whereas the relative abundance of Streptococcus and the Shannon entropy coefficient were significantly greater in the group of patients receiving the three-component modulator CFTR (Fig. 4). Thus, the composition of the modulator is important for clearing the lower respiratory tract from chronic CF pathogens.

Moreover, observations of the bacteriome of a pwCF who received modulator therapy for 9 years, 4.5 of which were subjected to ELX/TEZ/IVA, revealed that morphological abnormalities in the respiratory system facilitate infection with new CF pathogens (Haemophilus, Mycoplasma, and others), which exacerbates the disease. Thus, in this patient, we continued to observe changes in the bacteriome from pulmotype 4 to pulmotype 5 and back to pulmotype 4 at the last observation point, which presented the highest diversity of anaerobes in the sample.

Metagenomic analysis of sputum samples with the study of the species diversity of anaerobic genera, and separately Streptococcus species, a shared pool of resistance and virulence genes, metabolic pathways, and analysis of bacterial interactions in the microbiome would be advisable as ELX/TEZ/IVA and vanzacaftor/TEZ/deutivacaftor become more readily available to a larger number pwCFs, with expanding the duration of three-component CFTR modulators therapy to three years or more, reducing the relative abundance of the main CF pathogen to at least 40–50% and increasing bacteriome diversity, including the diversity of anaerobes.

Conclusions

The long-term study of adult patients who are predominantly chronically infected with Pseudomonadota CF pathogens with a wide range of mutations describes the structure and dynamics of airway bacterial communities in patients with CF, linking these indicators to changes in clinical status, disease stage, and severity. A key observation is that modulator therapy results in a reduction in the bacterial load without the eradication of typical CF pathogens. Only long-term three-component modulator therapy brings the lung microbiome closer to a relatively favorable state but does not ensure complete restoration of a healthy microbiome. The respiratory system damaged by bronchiectasis is susceptible to new infections, so patient management requires constant monitoring of the microbiome composition and replenishment of the therapeutic landscape with both new modulators and new antibacterial drugs.
