Iodized Salt Consumption Practices and Iodine Status in the Rural Adult Population Near Chennai, India
Authors: Gayathri Gopakumar, Vanishree Shriraam, Aliya Jasmine, Shriraam Mahadevan, Sudha R. Prabhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Despite initiatives, the lack of understanding and improper usage habits for iodized salt are still prevalent. This cross-sectional study was conducted in rural Thiruvallur district of Tamil Nadu to assess the amount of iodine in the salt consumed, determine dietary iodine consumption, urinary iodine concentration, and understand the knowledge and practices related to iodized salt usage.Methods:A questionnaire was designed and validated to assess knowledge and practices of procuring, storing salt, and cooking practices. A standard field-testing kit was used to estimate the salt’s iodine level, and World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines values were taken for adequacy of iodization. Per capita consumption of salt was calculated by dividing the monthly purchase by family members partaking and iodine intake was calculated from a three-day dietary recall using the US FDA database. Spot urinary samples were collected to estimate the Median Urinary Iodine Concentration (MUIC).Results:Out of 650 households, 79% procured packaged salt; 40% were not aware of iodized salt; and 50.4% felt that consuming iodized salt is not important. The majority (91%) of the subjects added iodized salt at the beginning of cooking, 95% covered the storage container, and 32% exposed the salt to heat. Around 31% used uniodized or inadequately iodized salt. More than a quarter (27%) of them had the MUIC in the deficient range.Conclusion:A third of the population continues to use inadequately iodized salt, and the awareness of IDD and the importance of iodized salt is low.

INTRODUCTION

Salt iodization has been implemented in many countries, including India. This was to eradicate iodine deficiency disorder in a much safer, cost-effective, and sustainable way.[1] Universal salt iodization is the most significant step taken as a public measure for eradicating iodine deficiency disorders. The State of Tamil Nadu is the third largest producer of salt in India, but just 61.9% of the state’s population consume iodized salt, making it the state with the least household coverage of adequately iodized salt as per the Indian iodine survey 2018–19.[2]

Even though universal salt iodization has been accepted in all parts of Tamil Nadu and families obtain appropriately iodized salt for consumption, inappropriate storage practices combined with insufficient and incorrect understanding about the significance of iodization can account for the lack of adequate iodization of consumed salt.[3] Poor awareness and practice are observed in every sociodemographic group.[4] There is a dearth of information on household knowledge, attitudes, and practices about this issue. Also, there is scarcity of studies in India measuring dietary iodine intake.

The MUIC is the primary measure of a person’s nutritional iodine status. It is the main variable used to evaluate the effectiveness of iodine supplementation among a population.[5]

This study was therefore carried out to determine the amount of dietary iodine intake, to measure the iodine content of household salt, to determine urinary iodine concentration, and to study the practices concerning the consumption of iodized salt and the association of MUIC with these characteristics and practices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and study area

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the field practice area of a Rural Health and Training centre (RHTC) in Thiruvallur district of Tamil Nadu from October 2021 to March 2022.

Sample size and sampling

This is a part of a larger study for which the sample size was calculated based on the prevalence of thyroid disorders (13.3%) in a study conducted in Kochi on adult subjects.[6] With an allowable error of 20% and α of 95%, the minimum sample size required was 626. All adults in the 39 villages under nine panchayats of RHTC (around 14000 population) formed the sampling frame, and 17 from each village were selected by systematic random sampling. Among the adults who were available in the household at the time of visit, one person was chosen randomly after explaining the study and obtaining written informed consent.

Data collection technique and tools

A structured questionnaire was prepared after a detailed review of the literature from KAP studies on iodization of salt, consumption practices, and iodine deficiency disorders. The face and content validation was then conducted by a panel of three experts in fields of nutrition, public health, and endocrinology. The relevance, clarity, and appropriateness of each of the items were verified for the rural population and modifications on wording and sequencing were conducted based on their feedback.

The questionnaire was then pre-tested in three phases—once among adults attending the rural health centre and twice in the community setting in another primary health centre field area. This helped assess their comprehension and response variations, feasibility of administration in field area as well as identification of certain missing variables. Additional variables, such as use of secondary salt types, sharing of salt with cattle, and culturally specific practices, were incorporated based on pre-test findings. The final version was administered by trained investigators using face-to-face interviews.

The study subject was asked to bring half a tablespoon of all salt samples used in the household in a plate. Iodine estimation was carried out with spot testing kits. The colour chart given in the kit was used to compare and determine the iodine range in the salt sample as 0, 7, 15, and above 30 ppm. According to WHO guidelines, a cutoff fraction of 15 ppm and above was taken as appropriately iodized salt.[3] Salt samples were also collected from street vendors, local markets, and ration shops of the study area and spot testing was carried out. No separate sample size was estimated for this collection.

Per capita consumption of salt was calculated by dividing the monthly purchase of salt in kilograms by the number of family members partaking food, and then, it was divided by approximate days of salt storage. A three-day dietary recall was collected using standard measuring cups and spoons to measure the iodine intake from the regular diet. Dietary intake of Iodine was calculated by using the U.S. Department of Agriculture, FDA database.[7] Per capita iodine intake was calculated by adding dietary iodine from average of three-day dietary recall and iodine from per capita daily salt intake (per capita daily salt intake multiplied by level of iodization). For this, we excluded people with storage of salt for > two months.

Additionally, spot samples of urine were collected in screw-capped plastic containers. MUIC was determined by ammonium persulphate wet digestion method based on Sandell–Kolthoff reaction. Standard operating procedures were followed as per External Quality Urinary Iodine Participation Program (EQUIP). For internal verification of accuracy of the analytical method, analysis of known concentration iodine urine samples was used. The percent coefficient of variation for the UIC assay, calculated as (standard deviation/mean) × 100, was 3.1%, indicating high analytical precision.[8] The concentration cut-off values for public health significance were taken from WHO guidelines.[9] MUIC is considered a reliable indicator for assessing iodine deficiency in populations. It provides a non-invasive, cost-effective method for monitoring iodine status at a population level and has been recognized by the WHO as a key tool in iodine nutrition monitoring for public health purposes.

Statistics

Data were entered and analysed using SPSS version 20.0. Frequency, percentage, and arithmetic mean (SD) and median were used to present the data. Appropriate tests of significance (Chi-square test, Student’s t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA)) were applied wherever necessary at 5% level of significance. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical aspects

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee at Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (IEC-NI/19/NOV/71/101 dated 05.09.2020). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants for participation in the study and the use of the patient data for research and educational purposes. All the procedures followed the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and as revised later.

RESULTS

A total of 630 subjects participated in the study, 82% were women, with a mean age of 46 ± 14.9 years. Most were married (89%), lived in nuclear families (57%) with a median per capita income of Rs. 3333. Figure 1 shows the flowchart of subjects in the study.

Knowledge and attitude toward iodized salt

Awareness of iodized salt was reported by 60% of participants; however, only 24% correctly identified it as salt fortified with iodine. Half considered iodized salt important, and only 16% were aware of the health consequences of iodine deficiency [Figure 2].

Salt purchase and consumption pattern of study participants

More than half the participants (55%) used more than one type of salt. Packaged salt was used by 79%, while the rest bought unpacked crystalline salt. Crystalline salt was predominant salt in 52% households and sea salt was the main source (95%). Purchase of salt was predominantly from local shops (79.3%) at a frequency of 1 kg/month (63%) [Figure 3].

Salt purchase and consumption pattern of study participants

The majority of the subjects added salt during the cooking process (91%); stored for < two months (82%); predominantly stored in covered plastic containers (95%), but 32% exposed the salt to heat.

Iodization level of salt: (n = 580)

The spot testing kit’s iodine test revealed that 69% used adequately iodized salt, 16.4% used non-iodized salt, and 31% consumed salt with excessive iodine [Figure 4].

Level of salt iodization

Non-iodized salt purchased from street vendors was mainly due to its taste (77%), low cost, and easy availability. Some quoted that they ‘are just used to buying that’.

Of the 30 market samples tested, 28.5% of crystalline salts were non-iodized, while all street-vendor samples were non-iodized.

Dietary intake of salt and iodine: (n = 535)

The mean daily per capita uptake of salt was 13.35 ± 8.35 g (Median 11 gms; interquartile range 8.3–16.7). Seventy-three participants (11.6%) reported having cattle in their house and they partook salt with the cattle. The mean iodine uptake from food sources was 25.08 ± 18.24 mcg/day, and total iodine uptake from diet and salt was 52.08 ± 26.48 mcg/day.

DISCUSSION

The intent of this study was to assess salt iodization levels, estimate MUIC, and to evaluate knowledge and practices with respect to iodized salt in a rural area of India.

As per the India Iodine Survey report of 2018–19, the national household coverage of iodised salt was 76.3%, and in Tamil Nadu, was the least at 62%.[2] Although there is some increase in coverage (to 69%) since that survey, the state is far behind the Universal Salt Iodisation (USI) target coverage of 90%. The programme should also regularly check the upper limit of iodine before salt package and distribution to avoid excessive iodisation.

With regard to awareness about iodized salt (60% here), studies from rural Tamil Nadu and Delhi showed similar findings, but this was higher in Kerala.[101112] Crystalline salt bought from local markets and ration shops showed ‘0’ iodine levels frequently (28%). It becomes important for the Government and Program officials to give specific attention to packing of crystal salts and permissible duration of storage.

There is an iodine gap that needs to be bridged (insufficient or excess). There is literature enough to say that even excessive iodine may lead to hyper/hypothyroidism or thyroid autoimmunity in susceptible individuals.[131415] The National programme suggests minimum levels of iodization at production and consumer level but not the maximum level.[16]

There was also a big gap observed from the purchase to the consumption in terms of cooking and storing practices that led to the loss of iodine before consumption. But in this study, we did not see a statistically significant association between the cooking or storage practices of salt and their MUIC except when salt is moist.

There were other socio-cultural practices like sharing salt with the cattle in house, use of salt to ward off evil eye, etc., which made the estimation of per capita consumption difficult.

The results from a National non-communicable diseases monitoring survey, India, estimated the mean daily salt intake as 8.0 g.[17] In this study, it was much higher (13–14 g) than the WHO guidelines as well as the NCD survey, 2023.[1718] The effect of this high salt intake combined with an excessive iodization of salt in 31% subjects needs to be studied.

The dietary intake of iodine was very less. Iodized salt was the primary preventive source. Around 13% of the study subjects still purchase unpackaged uniodized salt from street vendors. Tamil Nadu, being third largest salt producing state in the nation, should try to achieve the USI target of 90% at the earliest and take stringent action against sale of uniodized salt.

Older participants and those with hypertension had a significantly higher mean MUIC than their counterparts in this study.

This is one of the first studies to estimate iodine status comprehensively among the rural adult population.[192021222324] Also, a standard kit was used to estimate the iodine content of the salt purchased and the iodine intake from the regular diet was computed.

One of the study’s limitations is that it is conducted in a single region. There is a female predominance, as, in some families, the males suggested their counterparts to answer, as it was a study on salt consumption and cooking habits. The iodization level of 50 (8%) households could not be assessed.

CONCLUSION

The study reveals that a significant portion of the rural population consumes food with either insufficient or excessive levels of iodized salt. Awareness about the health benefits of iodized salt and cooking-storing practices remain low. MUIC levels were insufficient in a quarter of individuals and excessive in a third. MUIC and iodization levels were not associated. The widespread availability of non-iodized salt underscores the urgent need for stricter regulations. Addressing the issues of both insufficient and excessive iodization through robust monitoring and surveillance is crucial.
