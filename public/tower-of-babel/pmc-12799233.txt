Facilitating postpartum care with a doula and certified nurse midwife in the neonatal intensive care unit: A qualitative analysis of maternal experiences
Authors: Cecelia L. Corson, Ashley E. Martin, Niesha Darden, Laura Walker, Maggie E. Power, Celeste P. Durnwald, Sara C. Handley, Heather H. Burris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMothers of infants in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) are at higher risk of postpartum complications than mothers of well infants. The Postpartum Care in the NICU (PeliCaN) study was an unblinded, randomized controlled trial of doula‐supported, certified nurse midwife–delivered postpartum care for NICU mothers that reduced time to postpartum care for intervention participants. To establish acceptability and identify the most valuable core components of this care model, we conducted a qualitative study to understand the following: (1) the perspective and experience of NICU mothers in the intervention and control groups, (2) their receipt of and associated facilitators and barriers to postpartum care, and (3) the relative contributions of the doula and certified nurse midwife in accessing postpartum care.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews were completed with 20 participants (10 intervention and 10 control) who were NICU mothers of infants born <34 weeks’ gestation, admitted to the NICU, and <2 weeks old. We utilized template analysis, a form of thematic analysis, and an inductive approach to examine, interpret, and identify themes in the data.ResultsWe identified seven primary themes capturing the positive influence and care facilitated by the doula and certified nurse midwife. Four themes were specific to the doula: care coordination, prioritization of NICU mothers’ own postpartum care, resource coordination, and guidance by the doula. Three themes were specific to the certified nurse midwife: efficient, timely care at the bedside; removing travel and scheduling barriers; and personalized care from the midwife.ConclusionsWe identified a variety of ways in which embedding a doula and certified nurse midwife in the NICU to support postpartum care contributed to timely access to postpartum care and improved the postpartum experience for these high‐risk obstetric patients. The synergy of doula‐facilitated care, resource coordination, emotional support, and guidance with personalized certified nurse midwife–delivered essential bedside care that minimized logistical barriers was well received and appreciated by intervention participants. These maternal perspectives support embedding postpartum care in NICUs via policies and integrated healthcare delivery models.

INTRODUCTION

Over the last 20 years the maternal mortality rate has increased and pregnancy‐related deaths have more than doubled, with the majority of deaths occurring during the postpartum period [1]. Large disparities in maternal morbidity and mortality persist, with Black individuals experiencing rates three times higher than white individuals [2]. Parallel disparities are seen in preterm birth rates, which translate into disproportionate representation of Black mothers with infants in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). During pregnancy, most pregnant people attend frequent obstetric care visits (>10 recommended visits for low‐risk pregnancies), which decline abruptly after birth [3, 4]. Obstetric care often focuses on prenatal care and childbirth, yet many maternal mortalities and severe morbidities occur postpartum [5, 6]. Improving access to and receipt of postpartum care is critical to address disparities and improve outcomes for obstetric patients.

NICU mothers are a vulnerable population. Preterm birth is one of the most common indications for NICU admission and mothers of preterm infants are at higher risk of pregnancy and postpartum complications [7]. Nearly half of all preterm births are medically indicated for maternal or fetal well‐being, with many secondary to pregnancy‐specific conditions (e.g., preeclampsia), which necessitate timely postpartum follow‐up [8, 9]. Mothers of NICU infants have three times the prevalence of chronic hypertension, twice the prevalence of diabetes, and are three times more likely to develop postpartum depression or post‐traumatic stress disorder compared to mothers of well infants [7, 10, 11]. Balancing the needs of their hospitalized infant with their own healthcare needs presents many challenges for NICU mothers. Preterm infant hospitalizations may last weeks and mothers often prioritize being with their infant over managing their own health and recommended care [12, 13]. Given these challenges and NICU mothers’ increased susceptibility to postpartum complications, there is an urgent need to improve postpartum care delivery for this population.

The Postpartum Care in the NICU (PeliCaN) trial tested an innovative care delivery model by embedding doula‐coordinated, certified nurse midwife (CNM)‐delivered postpartum care in the NICU for mothers of preterm infants in a diverse, urban, academic hospital [14]. Doulas are uniquely equipped to support NICU mothers through assisting in physical recovery from childbirth (e.g., encouraging postpartum care receipt), serving as an informational resource (e.g., surrounding breastfeeding or pumping), and providing emotional support (e.g., mental health resources) [15, 16, 17, 18]. CNMs are nurses with advanced training in women's reproductive health. They are core obstetric team members in clinics and hospital units who provide pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum care, including family planning services [18]. For NICU mothers randomized to the doula and CNM intervention, time to the first postpartum care visit was significantly earlier than those in the control group (11 vs. 31 days) [14]. While the quantitative data support the effectiveness of PeliCaN, maternal perspective and experience are critical to examine and establish the model's acceptability and most valuable core components. Thus, the objective of this qualitative study was to understand the perspective and experience of NICU mothers in the intervention and control groups, their receipt of and associated facilitators and barriers to postpartum care, and the relative contributions of the doula and CNM in accessing care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Description of intervention

The PeliCaN study was a single‐centered, unblinded, randomized controlled trial that embedded postpartum doula support and CNM care within the NICU for mothers of hospitalized preterm infants from November 2022 to November 2023 [14]. The focus of the intervention was ensuring timely receipt of comprehensive postpartum care, defined as blood pressure measurement and treatment if indicated, mental health screening and referral if needed, and contraceptive services if desired. The study doulas, who had access to the electronic health record (EHR), tracked medical information for participants and their infants, provided lactation support, resource coordination, emotional support, and facilitated postpartum care by directly connecting and coordinating care with the study CNM or participants’ primary obstetric clinician. The CNM delivered bedside and telehealth clinical care, including but not limited to blood pressure monitoring, postpartum mental health assessments, and contraception counseling.

Participants

PeliCaN was conducted in a single, large, urban, academic birth hospital in the MidAtlantic region of the United States. The study team screened the EHR to identify eligible NICU mothers of infants born <34 weeks’ gestation, admitted to the NICU, and <2 weeks old. There were 37 participants enrolled in PeliCaN, 20 in the intervention group and 17 in the control group, all 37 were considered eligible to participate in the qualitative study. Thirty‐two consented to qualitative interviews, of those one was lost to follow up and two experienced infant loss and were not contacted. The lead interviewer contacted the remaining 29 participants with 20 responding and completing interviews.

Interviews

We completed 20 semi‐structured interviews (10 intervention, 10 control participants). All interviews were conducted over the phone by one of the nonclinical authors who is a qualitative researcher with >15 years of experience. Intervention interviews lasted between 25 and 90 min, and control interviews lasted between 25 and 90 min. Video interviews were not considered given privacy concerns and logistical challenges. All PeliCaN study participants who completed a PeliQual interview opted into interviews during their initial PeliCaN consent. At enrollment, participants were informed that they might be contacted for future research activities, including qualitative interviews, and were given the opportunity to decline. When contacted for the interview, the clinical research coordinator obtained informed consent again using an IRB‐approved phone script. The script reviewed the study purpose, risks, potential benefits, alternatives, and participant rights. Participants were given adequate time to ask questions, and interviews began only after verbal consent was provided. The research team (doulas, CNM, physicians, and qualitative researchers) developed the interview guides (Supporting Information S1). We revised our interview guides and iteratively refined questions throughout data collection as needed. During the recorded interviews, participants were asked questions about their NICU experiences and postpartum healthcare. Intervention participants were asked additional questions to elicit feedback on their experiences with the PeliCaN (doula–CNM) intervention. Questions were asked with minimal prompting from the interviewer. While moving through the interview, attention was paid to the narrative and topic flexibility was encouraged. If needed, prompts were used to follow up on questions and probe specific answers to achieve greater depth. Interview files were transcribed by ADA Transcription.

Qualitative analysis

We utilized a template analysis approach, a form of thematic analysis, to examine and interpret our data [19]. Interview transcripts were uploaded to the qualitative analysis software Atlas.ti for management and coding. We employed an inductive approach to analysis and worked within a constructivist framework to understand and report shared meanings, patterns, and cross‐connections in the data. We followed the six steps of template analysis: (1) review of the data to build familiarity, (2) preliminary coding of a subset of data, (3) organization of initial codes into meaningful clusters, (4) development of an initial codebook, (5) application of the codebook to additional data and revision as necessary, and (6) finalization of the codebook and application to the full data set [20, 21]. To ensure codebook appropriateness, the team regularly discussed coding and any observations, questions, and concerns. The qualitative analyst reflected independently upon codes and code reports using memos and additional notes and outlined to document, organize, and categorize thoughts and then discussed these findings with the team. The team collectively constructed and agreed upon themes. Reflexivity was encouraged, acknowledged, discussed, and interpreted with attention paid to the team's role in the analysis [22]. Included quotes are verbatim with the expectation of minor edits to improve clarity. The University of Pennsylvania and Children's Hospital of Philadelphia Institutional Review Boards approved this study.

Reflexivity statement

Our multidisciplinary research team includes people with graduate‐level education and expertise in organizational psychology, qualitative analysis, perinatal epidemiology, health services research, and perinatal care delivery—all focusing on improving care quality, outcomes, and equity for NICU families. Two authors have clinical expertise in neonatology, one in maternal‐fetal medicine, one in midwifery care, and two are experienced postpartum doulas, the latter three were involved in the PeliCaN intervention. All eight team members identify as female with two identifying as non‐Hispanic Black, and six as non‐Hispanic White. All participants identified as “mothers,” thus this language (mother, maternal) is used throughout, although our team recognizes that not all birthing people identify as mothers. Complete reflexivity statements from PeliCaN's lead doula, doula, and CNM are in Supporting Information S2.

The themes are described below, and associated quotes are included in Tables 2 and 3. Benefits of doula support provided to mothers in NICU

Doula Theme 1: Doulas play an important role of coordinating multiple types of care for NICU mothers

Many intervention participants described the key role doulas played in coordinating necessary care for themselves and their infants (Table 2). Intervention participants provided several concrete examples of proactive doula‐facilitated postpartum care: helping schedule appointments; sending appointment and medication reminders; tracking follow‐up; and picking up prescriptions. Participants appreciated that the doula also provided healthcare‐related knowledge and guidance, encouraged continuity of care, and supported postpartum health monitoring and appointment accountability. Participants acknowledged that these actions facilitated their receipt of appropriate, timely, and necessary care. Conversely, control participants reported limited support or guidance in postpartum care coordination. They described challenges making appointments, long lengths of time to see a physician, and the insufficiency of conversations with clinicians, which contributed to struggles in accessing postpartum care.

Doula Theme 2: Doulas encourage NICU mothers to prioritize their own care so they could better care for their infant

Participants often placed their infants’ needs above their own, de‐prioritizing their own health and care. The doula played a crucial role in supporting NICU mothers in balancing their postpartum recovery alongside their infant's hospitalization, reinforcing that their well‐being was essential to their baby (Table 2). Mothers voiced that it was important for them to hear, trust, and learn that receiving necessary postpartum care better equipped them to care for their infants in the NICU and after hospital discharge. Participants identified this theme as a key turning point that motivated them to access postpartum care. While all participants were intently focused on caring for their infant and remaining at their infant's bedside, control participants struggled to balance the time and resources available to do so far more than intervention participants who had doula support. In addition, despite experiencing high emotional strain, control participants focused less on addressing their anxiety and depression due to the lack of time and resources available to them. They described feeling limited by what healthcare and insurance systems recognize as a critical postpartum issue warranting immediate evaluation and treatment.

Doula Theme 3: Doulas play a central role in coordinating various necessary resources for NICU mothers

Participants emphasized the doula's role in facilitating their receipt of and knowledge about practical, tangible, and available resources (Table 2). In addition to lactation support, which is an area of doula expertise, the doulas helped mothers access, navigate, and obtain transportation, parking, food, housing, therapy services, and other items (i.e., pumping bras, car seats). This knowledge of essential resources alleviated many burdens. In contrast, control participants received a standard handout for NICU parents, listing local resources without additional context. Control participants spoke of the need to increase awareness of hospital‐associated and NICU‐provided resources as well as improved communication about why and how they should utilize available resources. These barriers contributed to participants feeling unsupported in the NICU.

Doula Theme 4: Doula's guidance for the NICU mother leads to increased parental trust, confidence, and communication skills

Participants often spoke of the ways in which the doulas approached them with compassion and transparency, establishing trust, and offering guidance, coaching, and emotional and mental support (Table 2). Participants spoke of how their doula coached them on how to communicate with the care team (or communicated on their behalf), listened to their concerns, explained things they did not understand, answered questions about the infant's care, and advocated for their infant. Through this experience, the participants described gaining trust in the doula that created a sense of security regarding the infant's care and increased their confidence and ability to communicate about their infant. Control participants described feeling overwhelmed by the NICU and the details of medical care, leaving them feeling disconnected from their baby and the care plan. Many control participants shared that their emotional experience both pre‐ and post‐birth was dominated by feelings of fear, surprise, confusion, guilt, and blame.

Benefits of CNM care provided to NICU mother

CNM Theme 1: CNM provides essential healthcare for NICU mothers at the infant's bedside

The primary role of the CNM was to deliver postpartum care at the NICU bedside, allowing ongoing bonding and proximity to the infant while addressing important healthcare needs (Table 3). This format was positively received as it facilitated maternal presence at the bedside. Further, participants stated that bedside care ensured they received necessary postpartum care which felt expedited—easier to access, quicker to receive, and more flexible. Seeking and receiving postpartum care for control participants often centered around the standard 6‐week postpartum appointment at their obstetrician's clinic, which they described as feeling rushed and incomplete in addressing all their concerns.

CNM Theme 2: CNM bedside care of NICU mothers addresses logistical barriers in care access

Participants shared that the provision of CNM care was timely and efficient, largely because it removed many logistical barriers to accessing postpartum care (Table 3). Intervention participants highlighted overcoming issues related to time, limited travel resources, challenging care coordination among multiple specialties, appointment scheduling, and general stress related to competing priorities and responsibilities (e.g., infant's health, their health, needs of other children, household responsibilities, employment, etc.). They noted that the CNM's care provided timely screening and interventions (e.g., blood pressure or anti‐depressant medications) and helped them avoid acute care visits. Control participants spoke of significant barriers associated with appointment logistics, including travel challenges, parking issues, poor office staff communication, and lack of childcare, which were reasons they delayed care. Control participants described having an infant in the NICU compounded logistical barriers.

CNM Theme 3: CNM's personable approach to care made NICU mothers feel cared for, advocated for, and comfortable

Participants described how they grew to trust and feel comfortable with the CNM (Table 3). They noted that the CNM distinguished herself from other clinicians through her patience as she listened to and took time with each individual. Participants described the CNM as understanding, empathetic, patient, relatable, and non‐judgmental, all characteristics that made them feel cared for, advocated for, and comfortable. The PeliCaN model created time and space, and participants reported the CNM did not rush appointments and was timely in responding to follow‐up questions and concerns. Conversely, control participants commented on their impersonal postpartum care interactions. Some participants likened care to feeling like “a checklist” or “getting a carwash through an actual machine.” They described generic interactions, which were not often defined by feelings of care or comfort.

DISCUSSION

Through studying NICU mothers’ perspectives, this analysis identified a variety of ways in which embedding a doula and CNM to support postpartum care contributed to timely postpartum care. The synergy of doula‐facilitated care, resource coordination, emotional support, and guidance with personalized CNM‐delivered essential bedside care that minimized logistical barriers was well received and appreciated by intervention participants. The experience of control participants receiving standard care is a stark contrast.

Doulas enhance many aspects of postpartum care, yet there are unique benefits and opportunities for doulas to improve patient experience and care in the NICU for both mothers and infants [23, 24]. For this high‐risk population, doulas can play a direct role in optimizing postpartum recovery through screening for issues related to physical health, mental health, and lactation [23]. Early detection and care coordination surrounding such issues may reduce morbidity as well as healthcare costs (e.g., one intervention participant described starting an antihypertensive) [15, 25]. Study doulas had access to the EHR which they leveraged to follow obstetric and neonatal care plans, track appointments, and reinforce lactation guidance in collaboration with clinicians. We found that when doulas in the NICU act as a supportive and knowledgeable bridge between the mother and infant's care team, communication improved, facilitating maternal understanding and confidence regarding infant care (e.g., medical rounds, interventions, and feeding plan). Clear communication and improved understanding support successful discharges and transitions to home [26, 27]. Beyond health‐related opportunities, doulas can support social risk screening, which is a frequently unmet need in the NICU [28]. While often social workers conduct this work, in this context the doula enhanced screening and resource utilization for intervention participants. Unmet social needs and hardships are associated with worse outcomes for low‐birth‐weight infants, thus early mitigation of these issues is crucial to infant well‐being [29].

The framework in which doulas work shapes their role and relationship with multidisciplinary teams and community organizations. The study doulas worked within a community‐based framework, which moves their role beyond traditional postpartum doulas who primarily provide support in home settings [30]. This approach is grounded in care that is (1) culturally responsive, (2) relationship‐building, and (3) directly collaborates with medical teams (both obstetric, and thus including the CNM, and infant). This embedded model allowed for traditional doula support and extended the standard doula model as new responsibilities and opportunities emerged based on systemic gaps identified through NICU mothers’ experiences. Utilizing the EHR, ensuring mothers had the necessary tools for successful infant feeding, and helping reduce barriers to care are a natural extension of the community doula framework and training. As NICUs and hospitals work to meet families where they are, the influence of doulas is amplified when they are integrated into the system within flexible, community‐based frameworks.

These qualitative data support patient acceptance and appreciation of this embedded doula‐CNM care model, under which intervention participants accessed comprehensive postpartum care significantly earlier than control participants [14]. Intervention participants repeatedly highlighted the CNM's concrete medical interventions, including mention of avoiding emergency department visits. Beyond the potential to decrease postpartum acute care utilization and costs through timely care and preventative measures, co‐locating care in the NICU decreases costs for patients associated with transportation, parking, and childcare, which are often unmeasured. Furthermore, many inpatient obstetric units employ CNMs and these units are often in close proximity to NICUs, which may increase efficiency for CNMs or other obstetric clinicians. In addition to improving access to care, embedded, co‐located care delivery models are an opportunity to efficiently improve patient, and perhaps clinician, experience and outcomes.

Policies, at both the hospital and state levels, are an important opportunity to create, sustain, and scale innovative changes in postpartum care delivery. At the hospital level, hospitals or health systems could consider integrating doulas into hospital‐based and outpatient care models. Prior studies suggest this can be achieved through policies that reduce administrative barriers to doula services (e.g., provider enrollment paperwork), adequately compensating for doula services using sustainable payment structures (e.g., salaries and benefits instead of per client payment), increase diversity of the doula workforce (e.g., improve cultural congruency with patients), and prioritize partnership with the doula community (e.g., collaborate with community doulas to increase accessibility) [31]. While reimbursement remains a commonly cited barrier in the provision of doula services, a prior study of individuals with low incomes found that a doula program demonstrated cost savings to payers, which may motivate changes to current policies and reimbursement models [32]. Further, over 40 states currently offer some level of reimbursement for doula services, which supports the idea that such reimbursement is financially responsible [33].

Limitations

The study's first limitation is that as a single‐center study, generalizability is limited, and quotes may reference specific, local resources. Yet, the primary themes identified from these patient experiences are likely shared with postpartum NICU mothers in other hospitals and communities. Second, given this study only included people who consented to research participation, selection bias may be present. However, the inclusion of both the intervention and control groups provides insight into the standard of care likely experienced by those who did not consent. Third, PeliCaN was focused on postpartum care receipt, thus the perspectives and experiences of the partner or other support people were not included. These people may influence or augment the postpartum experience for NICU mothers and can be explored in future studies.

CONCLUSION

These NICU mothers’ perspectives demonstrate the value of integrated doulas and CNMs, the acceptance of a co‐located postpartum‐neonatal medical care model, and the importance of associated policies and funding structures to facilitate dyadic NICU care. PeliCaN and other innovative, integrative care models may ultimately improve short‐ and long‐term outcomes for mothers, infants, and families.
