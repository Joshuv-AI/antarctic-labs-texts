Nanoscale biomechanical remodeling in human myopic corneal lenticule revealed by quantitative dynamic AFM
Authors: Hai-qiong Deng, Shu Yang, Xin Wang, Yan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncreasing evidence shows that myopia, a prevalent ocular disorder and the leading cause of visual impairment worldwide, is associated with significant alterations in the biomechanical properties of the sclera. However, the role of the anterior ocular segment, particularly the hierarchical collagen structure and biomechanical behavior of the corneal stroma, remains poorly understood in the context of myopia development. There is an urgent need to elucidate the relationship between nanoscale changes in corneal collagen ultrastructure, tissue-level biomechanical properties, and the macroscopic progression of myopia. In this study, we used quantitative dynamic atomic force microscopy and transmission electron microscopy to investigate the ultrastructure and biomechanical properties of the corneal stroma in human myopic eyes. Our results demonstrate that with increasing myopia severity, collagen fibril density progressively decreases, fibril packing becomes sparser, and interfibrillar spacing widens. Nanomechanical characterization further revealed decreased stromal stiffness and reduced mechanical stability, indicating a diminished capacity of the tissue to bear mechanical loads. Collectively, this correlative analysis highlights the pivotal role of corneal stromal nanostructure and biomechanics in the pathological development of myopia, providing new insights that could aid in the characterization, diagnosis, and assessment of the condition.Keywords: myopia, corneal lenticule, collagen ultrastructure, AFM/TEM, biomechanics

Why it matters

Myopia, a type of refractive error, is the most prevalent ocular disease worldwide and a major contributor to the global medical burden of eye disorders, especially in East Asia. This study reveals that increasing myopia severity correlates with sparser, less organized corneal collagen fibrils and reduced tissue stiffness. These nanoscale structural deficits compromise the cornea’s mechanical integrity, linking microscopic changes to macroscopic ocular elongation. By establishing a quantitative structure-mechanics-function correlation, our findings provide a mechanistic basis for myopia progression. These insights could inform earlier diagnosis, enable more accurate risk stratification, and guide the development of targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at preserving ocular biomechanical integrity.

Introduction

Myopia, a type of refractive error, is the most prevalent ocular disease worldwide and a major contributor to the global medical burden of eye disorders, especially in East Asia.1 Individuals with myopia, particularly high myopia, face a significantly elevated risk of visual impairment due to associated pathologies such as retinal detachment, myopic maculopathy, and glaucoma, which can lead to irreversible vision loss.2,3 The cornea, a transparent connective tissue at the front of the eye, serves the critical functions of transmitting external light to the retina and regulating ocular refraction.4 Comprising approximately 80%–85% of the corneal thickness, the stroma forms its middle layer and consists of precisely arranged, parallel collagen fibrils interspersed with keratocytes.5 The cornea is horizontally oval, measuring 11–12 mm horizontally and 9–11 mm vertically.6 Central corneal thickness (CCT) typically ranges from 551 to 565 μm, whereas peripheral corneal thickness ranges from 612 to 640 μm.7 Within the central stroma, collagen fibrils are uniformly spaced and organized into distinct lamellae, each oriented orthogonally to its neighboring layers. The human stroma contains 200–250 distinct lamellae, which vary in thickness. Individual lamellae can reach up to 0.2 mm in width and 2 μm in thickness. This highly ordered, hierarchical structure is directly responsible for the cornea’s unique combination of optical transparency and mechanical resilience.

Recent research has considerably advanced our understanding of corneal stromal mechanics.8,9 Concurrently, growing evidence indicates that myopia, a common ocular disorder, involves significant alterations in the biomechanical properties of the sclera.10,11,12,13 However, the complex hierarchical relationship between structure reorganization and biomechanical remodeling in the human myopic corneal stroma remains unclear. This relationship spans from nanoscale ultrastructural changes and tissue-level biomechanical alterations to the macroscopic development of myopic pathology. This study aims to elucidate these multiscale correlations. Using quantitative dynamic atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), we assessed the ultrastructure and nanomechanical properties of human corneal stroma from individuals with varying degrees of myopia following small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) surgery. Our findings indicate that with increasing degree of myopia, collagen fibril density decreases and interfibrillar spacing widens, compromising mechanical integrity. Correspondingly, stromal stiffness is reduced while tissue deformability increases. Establishing this strong positive correlation between structure, property, and function across multiple scales is crucial for understanding intrinsic alterations in myopia etiology. Furthermore, it provides a foundational framework to assist in the characterization, diagnosis, and clinical assessment of myopia.

Materials and methods

This prospective study examined corneal lenticules obtained from 31 eyes of 23 patients who underwent SMILE surgery for myopia correction. The mean age of the subjects was 27.0 years (range: 17–44 years), with a mean preoperative spherical equivalent (SE) of −4.8 ± 2.0 D. Based on preoperative SE, subjects were categorized into three groups: high myopia (SE ≤ −6.0 D with a mean of −7.0 ± 0.7 D, 11 eyes), moderate myopia (SE = −6.0 to −3.0 D with a mean of −4.3 ± 0.9 D, 10 eyes), and mild myopia (SE = −3.0 to −0.5 D with a mean of −2.6 ± 0.4 D, 10 eyes). Baseline characteristics of subjects in myopia groups are shown in Table S1. All subjects provided written informed consent for participation after being informed of the study’s aim during their initial clinical visit. This prospective study, involving patients undergoing myopia correction, was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Eye Hospital (Tianjin, China) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Corneal lenticules were extracted from a stromal depth of approximately 120 μm during SMILE surgery for myopia correction (Figure 1A). The central thickness of each lenticule (ranging from ∼50 to 170 μm) was determined by preoperative SE ranging from −1.50 to −9.0 D. The average lenticule diameter was approximately 6.6 mm (Figure 1B). Following extraction, each lenticule was dissected from surrounding tissue, marked with gentian violet at the 12 o’clock position for orientation, and temporarily stored at 4°C in a solution of 15% dextran (Solarbio, D8270-10g, Dextran T-500, Beijing, China). All specimens were analyzed within 24 h of preservation.

(C) Diagram showing the five measurement positions (center and four peripheral sites at 3, 6, 9, and 12 o’clock, 2 mm from the center) for anisotropy assessment. (D) Experimental workflow and corresponding ultrastructural and nanomechanical alterations in collagen associated with progressive myopia.

All measurements were performed on the anterior surface of the lenticules post-extraction. To prepare samples for high-resolution AFM imaging, the SMILE-derived stromal tissue was air-dried at 25°C and 30%–40% relative humidity for 24 h to form a thin, stable film on plastic Petri dishes. The corneal stroma topography was characterized using PeakForce Quantitative Nanomechanical Mapping (PeakForce QNM) mode in air with an RTESPA-300 probe (Bruker, Santa Barbara, CA, USA). An initial screening was performed over a 20 × 20 μm2 area, followed by a final high-resolution scan over a 5 × 5 μm2 area at a resolution of 256 lines per frame.

For nanomechanical testing, stromal samples were first attached to 35 mm plastic Petri dishes (Corning, NY, USA) using fast-curing epoxy adhesive (Pattex, Henkel, Düsseldorf, Germany). Each sample was oriented with reference to a gentian violet mark at the 12 o’clock position, ensuring the superior-inferior (SI) axis was aligned vertically. Measurements were conducted in force-volume mode using a BioScope Resolve AFM (Bruker) equipped with a PFQNM-LC-A-CAL cantilever (Bruker) on a 37°C heating stage. Each force-volume map, acquired over approximately 9 min, consisted of a 16 × 16 array of force curves within a 20 × 20 μm2 range. Mechanical characterization began at the stromal center, identified via the AFM motorized stage and inverted optical microscope. Subsequently, four additional peripheral sites located at 3, 6, 9, and 12 o’clock positions, each 2 mm from the center, were mapped to assess stromal anisotropy (Figure 1C). Elastic modulus maps were generated from the force-volume data, and specific pixels were selected for localized analysis of mechanical response. Figure 1D illustrates the experimental workflow and the corresponding ultrastructural and nanomechanical alterations in collagen associated with progressive myopia.

TEM produces a two-dimensional image by projecting electrons through an ultrathin specimen. Fresh corneal tissue samples (three per group) were primarily fixed with freshly prepared 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate (pH 7.0) following extraction. Ultrathin sections were prepared and observed at magnifications of 8,000×, 15,000×, and 40,000× using TEM (JEM-1400Flash, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences in biomechanical properties, fibril diameter, and interfibrillar spacing among the three myopia groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. p-values were calculated using independent t tests among the high, moderate, and mild myopia groups. The correlation between elastic modulus (E) and SE was evaluated using linear regression analysis.

Results and discussion

The study comprised 11, 10, and 10 eyes in the high, moderate, and mild myopia groups, respectively. Baseline clinical characteristics are presented in Table S1. No significant intergroup differences were found in age, sex distribution, CCT, biomechanically corrected intraocular pressure (bIOP), intraocular pressure (IOP), or stiffness parameter A1 (SP-A1) (all p > 0.05).

The geometry of the extracted stromal lenticules is typically thickest centrally and thins toward the periphery, with distinct nasal-temporal (NT; horizontal) and SI (vertical) principal meridians (Figure 1). It is well established that the collagen fibrils in the anterior third of the cornea are highly interwoven, oriented at an angle relative to the corneal surface, and insert into Bowman’s layer.14 However, the extraction process using the all-in-one VisuMax femtosecond laser system disrupts collagen lamellae, resulting in an irregular surface microstructure on the lenticule even with minimal photodisruption.15,16 This disrupted and loosened collagen matrix hinders regular and controllable interaction between the AFM tip and the stromal surface during high-resolution scanning. Therefore, to enable detailed investigation of collagen fibril packing and organization, high-resolution AFM imaging was performed instead on air-dried stromal samples under ambient conditions.

The corneal stroma in mild myopia is primarily composed of numerous fine collagen fibrils organized into lamellae parallel to the corneal surface (Figures 2A and 2D). These long, thin fibrils are densely interwoven at varied angles and consist primarily of type I and V collagen, as established in the literature.17 They exhibit uniform and narrow diameters that are stabilized by intra- and intermolecular covalent crosslinks, conferring mechanical integrity to withstand IOP. This highly interlocked microstructure provides substantial mechanical strength and limits relative micro-deformation. A distinct microstructural gradient was observed across mild, moderate, and high myopia in human corneal stroma (Figures 2 and S1). Compared with mild myopia, stromal collagen fibrils became progressively thicker and more variable in diameter with increasing myopic severity. In high myopia, individual fibrils appeared loosely connected and overlapping, with significantly reduced interlocking across the lamellae. Notably, highly myopic stroma exhibited a marked reduction in collagen fibril density and wider interfibrillar spacing, a space typically occupied by proteoglycans in vivo. This decrease in fibril count, sparser packing, and increased interfibrillar gaps likely stems from reduced collagen synthesis and/or accelerated degradation in progressive myopia. These structural deficiencies in tensile strength may explain the frequent posterior scleral expansion and axial elongation observed in highly myopic eyes under IOP.

AFM topological images of corneal lenticules. Representative AFM morphology scans of the corneal stroma in the (A and D) mild, (B and E) moderate, and (C and F) high myopia groups.

Given the complex hierarchical architecture of the human corneal stroma,18 assessing alterations in collagen fibril morphology at the nanoscale is essential for understanding corresponding changes in tissue-level biomechanics and macroscopic pathology. We employed TEM to quantitatively analyze the precise arrangement of collagen fibrils and their interactions with other extracellular matrix components, such as proteoglycans and glycoproteins. Figure 3 shows a representative TEM image of the corneal stroma, revealing collagen fibrils in parallel arrays with a textured longitudinal structure, as viewed parallel to the anterior surface. The packing and organization of these fibrils systematically changed with increasing myopia severity. Stromal tissue from mild myopic eyes contained densely packed, crimped collagen fibrils (Figures 3A–3C). As myopia progressed to the moderate stage, this parallel structure transformed into a less dense matrix characterized by larger fibril diameters and increased interfibrillar spacing (Figures 3D–3F). This trend intensified in high myopia, where the stromal matrix became notably sparse (Figures 3G–3I).

(C), (F), and (I) show further detail (40,000×) of the regions marked by red dashed circles in (B), (E), and (H). Quantitative analysis of the collagen fiber diameter, Φ, and interfibrillar space, Δ, is presented in Figures 3J and 3K, respectively. A significant difference was observed among the three myopia groups, with both diameter and spacing increasing progressively with myopia severity (Table S2; p < 0.01, determined by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test). The TEM findings are consistent with the high-resolution AFM morphological data, although absolute dimensions measured by AFM were larger. This discrepancy likely stems from meniscus forces during the air-drying process for AFM preparation, which can draw adjacent fibrils together, promoting bundle formation. In this study, AFM topography was used as a complementary, qualitative indicator of surface structure, while TEM provided the quantitative morphological data. Although air-drying during AFM sample preparation is known to induce fibril reorganization, all experimental groups were processed identically. Thus, any drying-related artifacts are expected to be systematic across conditions and unlikely to obscure or artificially generate the group-level trends observed. Nevertheless, the qualitative nature of AFM imaging in this study should be considered when interpreting surface topography.

The corneal stroma, constituting 80%–85% of the corneal thickness, is a collagenous extracellular matrix with high swelling capacity, often described as a fibril-reinforced hydrogel.19 Consequently, its biomechanical properties, particularly elasticity, largely define overall corneal biomechanics. Given that structural alterations are often correlated with mechanical changes, this study further investigated how stromal nanomechanics vary with myopia severity. Using AFM mapping, we analyzed spatial elasticity distributions as an indicator of the tissue’s resistance to localized deformation.

Mechanical measurements were performed by acquiring 16 × 16 arrays of loading-unloading curves in the force-volume map (Figure 4A). The elastic modulus at each pixel was derived by curve fitting the force-displacement data to a spherical Hertzian contact model using in-house MATLAB (MathWorks) code, where the color bar indicates softer regions in dark blue (Figure 4B). Representative force curves for each myopia group are shown in Figure 4C. Although the corneal stroma was modeled as a purely elastic material, a hysteresis loop between loading and unloading curves was observed, most likely due to inherent tissue viscoelasticity. The maximum indentation depth remained below 1 μm, corresponding to less than 2% of the total lenticular thickness (depending on myopia degree ranging from 50 to 170 μm). This shallow indentation ensured that substrate effects were negligible in our experiments. Figure 4D presents the corresponding spherical Hertzian mechanical model fits on a log-log scale. The distribution of elastic modulus in physiological buffer for mild, moderate, and high myopic stroma is shown in Figure 5A and Table S3. The elastic modulus ranged from 7 to 17 kPa, consistent with prior literature.20 The mean elastic moduli were 17.0 ± 2.0 (mild), 13.5 ± 1.5 (moderate), and 7.8 ± 1.0 (high myopia) kPa. Stromal stiffness decreased monotonically with increasing myopia severity, showing a strong negative correlation with SE (R2 = 0.8; Figure 5B). These results indicate that the cornea becomes progressively less rigid and more deformable under mechanical stress as myopia advances. Although lenticule thickness varies with myopia severity, potentially introducing differences in stromal lamellar composition, all AFM measurements were performed at a consistent anterior surface depth (∼120 μm) and with a shallow indentation depth (∼1 μm), which effectively limits the influence of through-thickness heterogeneity. However, we recognize that the full-thickness lamellar architecture may differ between groups; therefore, future studies employing layer-specific mechanical mapping or depth-resolved profiling would be valuable to systematically evaluate the mechanical gradient across the anterior and posterior stroma.

Conclusion

In this prospective study, we employed quantitative dynamic AFM and TEM to investigate the ultrastructure and biomechanical properties of the corneal stromal tissue extracted from patients with varying degrees of myopia via femtosecond laser surgery. Our approach enabled a correlative, quantitative analysis of stromal structure, stiffness, and function. The results demonstrated that corneal stiffness decreases with increasing myopia severity, a finding consistent with prior literature.20 Furthermore, cross-sectional analysis revealed systematic changes in collagen fibril organization: with increasing myopia, fibrils within lamellae exhibited greater diameters and wider interfibrillar spacing. The densely interwoven architecture observed in mild myopia provides substantial mechanical integrity and resistance to micro-deformation.

Our data reveal a strong multiscale correlation: pathological changes in collagen fibril packing at the nanoscale are linked to altered tissue biomechanics, which in turn correspond to the macroscopic development of myopia. Specifically, the observed wider spacing and looser packing of collagen fibrils in higher myopia correlate directly with reduced tissue stiffness. This relationship may be attributed to two interconnected factors. (1) Structural deficiency: in high myopia, reduced fibril density, increased fibril diameter, and wider interfibrillar spaces diminish tensile strength, resulting in lower ocular rigidity, increased deformability, and elongation of the optic disc; (2) Biochemical remodeling: during myopia progression, increased activity of collagen-degrading enzymes and reduced interweaving between collagen lamellae may facilitate fibril sliding and promote permanent tissue deformation. An enhanced understanding of corneal biomechanics is critically important in refractive surgery. This collective and correlative analysis of pathological human stroma advances beyond an empirically driven approach, providing a structural-mechanical framework for predicting corneal behavior. Such insights are essential for elucidating the etiology of myopia development. A limitation of this study is the lack of axial length (AL) data, which are more directly linked to myopic remodeling than SE alone. While SE remains a practical grouping metric and correlates strongly with stromal stiffness in this study, it also depends on corneal power and lens status. Future work incorporating AL will help clarify whether the nanoscale and biomechanical alterations observed here track more closely with refractive error or with axial elongation.
