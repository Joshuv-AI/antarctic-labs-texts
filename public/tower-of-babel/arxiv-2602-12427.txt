A variational critical-state theory of friction
Mary Agajanian, Nadia Lapusta, Anna Pandolfi, Michael Ortiz

Abstract
Friction plays a fundamental role in many natural processes, including earthquakes, landslides, and volcanic eruptions. Earthquakes occur when highly compressed fault surfaces accumulate large enough shear stresses, causing the faults to move relative to one another, or slip. The slip is accommodated within a thin layer of comminuted granular material – called fault gouge – between the fault surfaces. As a result, characterizing the mechanical behavior of fault gouge in response to shear is a major open problem in earthquake source physics. Modeling gouge is complicated by large deformations, inelasticity, rate dependence, and volumetric changes. As such, researchers typically rely on empirical formulations to capture the effective response. Here, we systematically develop a variational, finite-kinematics framework for fault gouge. We first describe a general theory for a rigid-viscoplastic, pressure-sensitive material, where the plasticity evolution follows from the principle of maximum dissipation. Then, we specialize the governing equations for a Cam-Clay material within a shearing and dilating layer. We rely on convexity considerations and experimental observations from consolidation tests of granular layers to calibrate the model and develop explicit solutions for the rate- and state- dependent response of the model to shear tests under constant compressive normal stress and prescribed shearing rate. To validate the model, we select common rate functions and compare numerical material point tests and theoretical solutions to standard laboratory experiments of shearing granular layers. Lastly, we discuss connections of the model to empirical rate-and-state friction laws.



1
Introduction

Large earthquakes occur as shear ruptures along pre-existing faults—weak interfaces within the Earth’s crust
[
58
]
. Repeated sliding of these interfaces produces a finely comminuted granular layer known as fault gouge
[
15
,
30
]
. At seismogenic depths, the gouge layer is highly compacted and undergoes intense shearing due to the relative tectonic motion of the bounding rock masses. The distribution of slip within the gouge may be either homogeneous or localized along discrete shear bands. Furthermore, grain rearrangement during shear induces macroscopic volumetric changes that directly influence the shear resistance of the layer. Geological evidence indicates that, during large earthquakes, faults may slip by several meters within zones less than a millimeter thick, implying extremely large strains across the gouge layer (e.g.
[
16
]
). Accurate continuum modeling of the finite-deformation, shearing, and dilating behavior of such granular layers is therefore essential for elucidating the conditions under which earthquakes nucleate and propagate.

The stress and volumetric response of sheared granular layers have been the focus of extensive laboratory investigations
[
13
,
22
,
23
,
56
,
41
]
. In these experiments, a granular assembly is confined under a prescribed normal stress and sheared under controlled loading protocols at relatively slow shear rates (corresponding to slip rates over the layer of
10^{-8}
to
10^{-3}
m/s). The resulting shear resistance is largely cohesionless and frictional. That is, the ratio of shear to normal stress defines an effective friction coefficient, typically in the range of 0.6–0.85
[
13
]
. Under constant shear loading rates, the shear resistance approaches a rate-dependent steady state. In response to abrupt changes in shear rate, the shear stress exhibits a transient, non-monotonic evolution: an instantaneous rate-dependent jump followed by a gradual evolution with slip toward a new steady-state value
[
23
]
. These so-called velocity-step tests are used to interrogate shear resistance in two important regimes – abrupt changes in shear rates and constant shear rates – and are key to understanding fault gouge behavior during both earthquake nucleation and seismic slip.

Sheared granular layers also exhibit pronounced consolidation- and rate-dependent volumetric changes. At low confinement, the material dilates under shear; at higher confinement, it compacts
[
20
,
10
]
. The transition between these regimes is governed by the pre-consolidation pressure, i. e., the maximum past pressure experienced by the material. The volumetric response is further rate-dependent, with more dilation at higher shear rates
[
40
,
59
]
. This suggests that, at constant loading rates, the layer attains a rate-dependent steady-state volume that increases with shear rate
[
40
,
59
,
39
]
.

A wide range of theoretical models have been proposed to describe the mechanical response of fault gouge and analog materials. In one class of models, the highly-confined, narrow granular layer is interpreted as a frictional interface, with the shear resistance given by empirical rate-and-state friction laws
[
23
,
56
,
41
]
, which have been explained with various micro-physical interpretations (e. g.
[
43
,
32
,
1
]
). Other models of fault gouge and analog materials include inertial rheologies
[
31
,
42
,
6
]
, shear-transformation-zone theories
[
29
]
, extended continuum models
[
19
]
, and micromechanical formulations
[
65
]
. Continuum approaches frequently employ pressure-dependent viscoplastic models such as the Drucker–Prager or Mohr–Coulomb formulations
[
27
,
25
]
. To account for hardening at high pressures, some models augment the yield surface with an elliptic cap
[
24
]
. Other extensions include the effect of grain crushing
[
28
,
62
]
. In most such theories, however, the yield surface is prescribed
a priori
, thereby constraining the possible material response and limiting the adaptability of the formulation to large-strain kinematics.

The long-term bulk mechanical behavior of granular and cohesive-frictional geological materials is often described within the critical-state framework
[
53
,
57
,
44
,
10
]
. These theories are motivated by the empirical observation that, after sufficient shear, a granular material attains a critical state characterized by constant stress and volume during continued plastic deformation. Critical-state plasticity thereby captures the essential features of pressure dependence, yielding, plastic flow, and dilatancy in granular media.

The Cam–Clay model stands as one of the most influential among the various critical-state formulations. Developed at the University of Cambridge in the 1960s, it provides a constitutive framework that unifies compressibility, yielding, and stress-dependent stiffness in natural clays. Foundational works by Roscoe, Schofield, and Wroth
[
54
,
57
,
12
]
established both the experimental basis and theoretical structure of the critical-state concept. The modified Cam–Clay model describes material behavior through an elliptical yield surface in the mean effective stress–deviatoric stress plane, which expands (hardens) or contracts (softens) with plastic volumetric strain. The critical-state line delineates the transition between the hardening and softening responses. Extensions of Cam–Clay to finite deformations have been achieved through the assumption of a multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient
[
9
,
14
,
44
,
8
]
.

In the present study, we adapt the critical-state plasticity framework originally developed for clays and soils to model the response of fault gouge. Our formulation builds upon the variational finite-kinematics framework introduced in
[
45
]
and extended to Cam–Clay plasticity in
[
44
]
. In contrast to
[
44
]
, where the incremental formulation led to timestep sensitivity, we employ a continuous-time approach. By assuming a specific form of the flow rule and deriving the yield surface from the principle of maximum plastic dissipation, we obtain a consistent, discretization-independent formulation. Under the assumption of rigid elasticity, we derive closed-form solutions for the stress response of a sheared and compressed granular layer.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we summarize the key experimental observations that motivate our modeling framework. Section
3
presents the general variational formulation for non-isochoric, rigid-plastic materials and specializes it to Cam–Clay plasticity. Section
4
further develops the theory for a shearing and dilating granular layer, discusses model calibration, and provides closed-form expressions for simple shear. In Section
5
, we present numerical results and compare the model predictions with experimental data and existing theoretical models. Finally, Section
6
summarizes the main findings and outlines directions for future research.

3
Governing equations

We present a general finite-kinematics framework for critical-state plasticity motivated by
[
44
]
. Taking advantage of the variational structure of the governing equations, we identify effective stress-strain relations for a general rigid-plastic material. We then specialize the formulation to describe the shearing and dilating granular materials found in fault zones and their laboratory analogs.

Notational conventions

We adopt the following notational conventions. We denote the
n\times n

identity matrix
by
{\mathbf{I}}
. We define the
inner product
of two
n\times n
matrices
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
as

{\mathbf{A}}\cdot{\mathbf{B}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}B_{ij}.

(1)

The
trace
of an
n\times n
matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
is then,

{\operatorname{tr}\,}{\mathbf{A}}:={\mathbf{A}}\cdot{\mathbf{I}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{ii},

(2)

and the
norm
is

|{\mathbf{A}}|:=\sqrt{{\mathbf{A}}\cdot{\mathbf{A}}}=\sqrt{{\operatorname{tr}\,}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{T})}.

(3)

For any
n\times n
matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
we use the notation

\,\overline{\!{A}}:={\operatorname{tr}\,}{\mathbf{A}},\quad{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}:={\mathbf{A}}-\frac{1}{3}\,\overline{\!{A}}\,{\mathbf{I}},

(4)

to denote the
volumetric
and
deviatoric
parts of
{\mathbf{A}}
, respectively.

3.1
Finite kinematics

We assume a multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient,

{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{F}^{e}}{\mathbf{F}^{p}},

(5)

into an elastic deformation
{\mathbf{F}^{e}}
and a plastic deformation
{\mathbf{F}^{p}}

[
36
]
.

3.2
Rigid elasticity

The yield stress of fault gouge in shear is pressure-dependent. At the depths at which earthquakes occur, the shear and normal stresses are on the order of
10-100
MPa. As the elastic moduli of rock are on the order of
10
GPa, the associated elastic deformation is very small (yield strains of
10^{-4}-10^{-3}
) compared to the finite strains achieved across a shear layer during an earthquake. Thus, we assume rigid elasticity, such that the right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor
{\mathbf{C}}^{e}\equiv{\mathbf{I}}
, or equivalently, the elastic deformation is a pure rotation,

{\mathbf{F}^{e}}={\mathbf{R}^{e}}\in SO(3).

(6)

3.3
Volumetric plasticity

We denote the Jacobian of the deformation by
J:=\det{\mathbf{F}}
. For rigid elasticity,
J^{e}\equiv 1
, and thus
J^{p}=J
. Then, we define the corresponding volumetric plastic strain by

\theta^{p}:=\log J^{p}=\log J.

(7)

3.4
Rate of deformation

The spatial velocity gradient
{\mathbf{L}}=\dot{{\mathbf{F}}}{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}
can be additively decomposed into

{\mathbf{L}}={\dot{{\mathbf{F}}}^{e}}{\mathbf{F}^{e}}^{-1}+{\mathbf{F}^{e}}({\dot{{\mathbf{F}}}^{p}}{\mathbf{F}^{p}}^{-1}){\mathbf{F}^{e}}^{-1}:={\mathbf{L}}^{e}+{\mathbf{L}}^{p}.

(8)

For
{\mathbf{F}^{e}}\in SO(3)
,
{\mathbf{L}}^{e}
is skew-symmetric and, assuming no plastic spin,
{\mathbf{L}}^{p}
is symmetric,

{\mathbf{D}}^{e}:=\operatorname{sym}{\mathbf{L}}^{e}\equiv{\mathbf{0}},\quad{\mathbf{W}}^{p}:=\operatorname{skew}{\mathbf{L}}^{p}\equiv{\mathbf{0}}.

(9)

Then,

{\mathbf{W}}={\mathbf{W}}^{e},\quad{\mathbf{D}}={\mathbf{D}}^{p}.

(10)

3.5
Elastic deformation

Due to the decomposition (
10
), the elastic deformation is the solution of the ODE

\dot{{\mathbf{R}}}^{e}{\mathbf{R}^{e}}^{-1}={\mathbf{W}},

(11)

with initial condition
{\mathbf{R}^{e}}(t=0)={\mathbf{I}}
. Thus,
{\mathbf{R}^{e}}(t)
is given by

{\mathbf{R}^{e}}(t)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{W}}(s)\ ds\right),

(12)

where the matrix exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix is a rotation matrix.

3.6
Flow rule

The classical plastic-flow rules of small-strain rate-independent plasticity are derived from the elastic domain in stress space by invoking a normality rule
[
38
]
. In finite deformations, this approach raises conflicts with requirements of material-frame indifference or objectivity.

To sidestep these difficulties,
45
formulated a kinematical theory of finite-deformation plastic flow that subsumes the classical theory when restricted to small strains and relies on the principle of maximum dissipation to characterize yield, ensuring material-frame indifference. Thus, following
[
45
]
, we write the rate of plastic deformation as

{\dot{{\mathbf{F}}}^{p}}{\mathbf{F}^{p}}^{-1}=\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\,{\mathbf{M}},\quad{\mathbf{M}}^{T}={\mathbf{M}},

(13)

where
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
is the plastic strain rate and
{\mathbf{M}}
is a symmetric kinematic tensor that sets the direction of plastic flow. Taking derivatives in (
7
), the volumetric plastic strain rate is

\dot{\theta}^{p}={\operatorname{tr}\,}\left({\dot{{\mathbf{F}}}^{p}}{\mathbf{F}^{p}}^{-1}\right)=\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\,\,\overline{\!{M}},

(14)

whereupon (
13
) can be rewritten as

{\dot{{\mathbf{F}}}^{p}}{\mathbf{F}^{p}}^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{3}\,\dot{\theta}^{p}\,{\mathbf{I}}+\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\,{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}.

(15)

Specific classes of finite-deformation plastic materials can be conveniently modeled by appending suitable constraints to the tensor
{\mathbf{M}}
, effectively setting forth particular forms of the plastic flow rule
[
45
]
. The most general plastic-flow constraint can be expressed as

g({\mathbf{M}})=0,

(16)

where
g
is a scalar-valued function. Examples of choices of the constraint (
16
) that return classical plasticity models are given in
[
45
]
.

3.7
Maximum dissipation

The rate and direction of plastic flow are posited to maximize dissipation, in accordance with the principle of maximum dissipation
[
38
]
. To this end, one formulates a dissipation function that depends on the kinematic tensor
{\mathbf{M}}
and plastic strain rate
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
, such that minimizing the function with respect to both arguments maximizes the dissipation.

Here, we notice that the plastic-flow constraint, (
16
), sets forth an implicit dependence of
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
on
{\mathbf{D}}
due to the assumption of rigid elasticity. That is, inserting (
13
) into (
16
) and using (
10
) gives

g({\mathbf{R}^{e}}^{-1}({\mathbf{D}}/\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\,{\mathbf{R}^{e}})=0,

(17)

which implicitly defines a function
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})
, as surmised. Hence, the dissipation function can be written entirely in terms of
{\mathbf{D}}
instead of
{\mathbf{M}}
and
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
. In addition, differentiating (
17
) with respect to
{\mathbf{D}}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\mathbf{R}^{e}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial{\mathbf{M}}}\,{\mathbf{R}^{e}}^{-1}-\frac{1}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p\,2}}\Big({\mathbf{R}^{e}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial{\mathbf{M}}}\,{\mathbf{R}^{e}}^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{D}}\Big)\frac{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\partial{\mathbf{D}}}=0,

(18)

whence, solving, the derivative of
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})
follows as

\frac{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\partial{\mathbf{D}}}=\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\Big({\mathbf{R}^{e}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial{\mathbf{M}}}\,{\mathbf{R}^{e}}^{-1}\cdot{\mathbf{D}}\Big)^{-1}\Big({\mathbf{R}^{e}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial{\mathbf{M}}}\,{\mathbf{R}^{e}}^{-1}\Big).

(19)

In formulating the dissipation function, we assume that the stored energy
W^{p}
is solely a function of the plastic volumetric deformation
\theta^{p}
and temperature
T
and define the consolidation pressure
p_{0}(\theta^{p},T)
by

p_{0}(\theta^{p},T):=\dfrac{\partial W^{p}}{\partial\theta^{p}}(\theta^{p},T).

(20)

This assumption is motivated by theories of free volume in granular media and amorphous metals
[
18
,
26
]
and is born out by the validation examples in Section
5
. Likewise, we assume a kinetic potential of the form
{\psi}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p};\theta^{p},T)
, depending on the state only through
(\theta^{p},T)
.

Building on these relations, we postulate a dissipation function of the general form

f({\mathbf{D}};{\bm{\sigma}},{\mathbf{R}^{e}};\theta^{p},T)=-{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot{\mathbf{D}}+p_{0}(\theta^{p},T)\,\dot{\theta}^{p}+{\psi}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p};\theta^{p},T),

(21)

for fixed Cauchy stress tensor
{\bm{\sigma}}
, elastic rotation
{\mathbf{R}^{e}}
, volumetric plastic strain
\theta^{p}
and temperature
T
. In (
21
),
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
and
\dot{\theta}^{p}
are understood to be functions of
{\mathbf{D}}
through (
14
) and (
17
). As a result, both the rate and direction of plastic flow follow from minimizing (
21
) with respect to
{\mathbf{D}}
. The function
p_{0}(\theta^{p},T)
represents a critical or yield pressure for plastic volumetric deformation and
{\psi}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p};\theta^{p},T)
is a dissipation potential, with both to be characterized for specific classes of materials.

For simplicity of notation, we subsequently omit any and all dependencies on the state variables
(\theta^{p},T)
and leave them implicit throughout.

Minimizing
f
with respect to
{\mathbf{D}}
gives the effective stress-strain relation

{\bm{\sigma}}({\mathbf{D}})=\Big(p_{0}+\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}}))\Big)\,{\mathbf{I}}+\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}}))\dfrac{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\partial{\mathbf{D}}},

(22)

where
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})
is defined implicitly by (
17
) and its derivative is given by (
19
). As expected, the stress-strain relations (
22
) have a variational structure

{\bm{\sigma}}({\mathbf{D}})=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{\mathbf{D}}}({\mathbf{D}}),

(23)

with effective dissipation potential

\Psi({\mathbf{D}})=p_{0}\,\overline{\!{D}}+\psi(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})).

(24)

If the dissipation potential
{\psi}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})
is convex, then so is the
effective dissipation potential
\Psi({\mathbf{D}})
. However,
\Psi({\mathbf{D}})
may fail to be differentiable at
{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{0}
, in which case the derivative (
23
) is valid for
{\mathbf{D}}\neq\mathbf{0}
only and must be carefully reinterpreted in the sense of subdifferentials
[
52
]
, which may be set-valued, at
{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{0}
. We recall that the subdifferential of
\Psi({\mathbf{D}})
at
{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{0}
is the set in stress space

\partial\Psi(\mathbf{0}):=\{{\bm{\sigma}}\,:\,\Psi({\mathbf{D}})\geq{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot{\mathbf{D}},\;\,\text{for all}\;{\mathbf{D}}\},

(25)

where we assume that
\psi(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})
is convex and differentiable away from
(0,0)
and
\psi(0,0)=0
. Therefore, we identify the elastic domain with the set (
25
) in stress space.

We note that the elastic domain is generally dependent on the state of volumetric plastic deformation
\theta^{p}
due to consolidation and on the elastic rotation
{\mathbf{R}^{e}}
due to anisotropy.

3.8
Specialization of plastic flow potential to critical state models

Further explicit representations can be derived in special cases. For instance, if the plastic-flow rule is isotropic and independent of the third invariant of
{\mathbf{M}}
, then the general plastic-flow constraint (
16
) specializes to

g(\,\overline{\!{M}},|{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}|)=0.

(26)

Under this assumption, we have the identity

\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial{\mathbf{M}}}=\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial\,\overline{\!{M}}}\,{\mathbf{I}}+\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial|{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}|}\dfrac{{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}}{|{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}|},

(27)

which can be used, e. g., in (
19
).

A further particular example is the Cam-Clay model, which can be characterized by a plastic flow constraint of the form
[
44
]

g(\,\overline{\!{M}},|{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}|)=\dfrac{\,\overline{\!{M}}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}+\dfrac{2}{3}|{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}|^{2}-1=0,

(28)

where
\alpha>0
is an internal friction coefficient related to the friction angle of the Mohr-Coulomb failure criteria
[
44
]
. In this case, the implicit relation (
17
) gives the explicit relation

\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})=\Bigg(\dfrac{\,\overline{\!{D}}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}+\dfrac{2}{3}|{\mathbf{D}}^{\prime}|^{2}\Bigg)^{1/2},

(29)

with derivative

\dfrac{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\partial{\mathbf{D}}}=\frac{1}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})}\Bigg(\dfrac{\,\overline{\!{D}}}{\alpha^{2}}{\mathbf{I}}+\dfrac{2}{3}{\mathbf{D}}^{\prime}\Bigg),

(30)

and the effective stress-strain relation (
22
) takes the form

{\bm{\sigma}}({\mathbf{D}})=p\,{\mathbf{I}}+\dfrac{2}{3}\dfrac{{\mathbf{D}}^{\prime}}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})}\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})),

(31)

where

p=p_{0}+\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}}))+\dfrac{\,\overline{\!{D}}}{\alpha^{2}\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})}\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})).

(32)

Taking norms of the deviatoric part of (
31
) gives the scalar relation

q=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\dfrac{|{\mathbf{D}}^{\prime}|}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})}\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}})),\quad q:=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}|{\bm{\sigma}}^{\prime}|,

(33)

where
q
is the Mises stress. Using the stress invariants (
32
) and (
33
), (
29
) becomes

\alpha^{2}\Big(p-p_{0}-\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}}))\Big)^{2}+q^{2}=\Big(\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\,\overline{\!{D}},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{D}}))\Big)^{2}.

(34)

When
{\mathbf{D}}={\mathbf{0}}
,
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}({\mathbf{0}})=0
and the elastic domain reduces to

\sqrt{\alpha^{2}(p-p_{0})^{2}+q^{2}}-\sigma_{0}\leq 0,

(35)

where the yield surface corresponds to the stress states for which the equality is achieved, and we assume

\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(0,0)=0,\quad\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(0,0)=\sigma_{0}.

(36)

In this case, the yield surface (
35
) is an ellipse and the critical state line is a straight line (Figure
4
).

4
Specialization to a granular layer

To apply the general framework to the granular material found in fault zones, we consider shear and dilation of a granular layer of fault gouge of initial thickness
h_{0}
, shown in Figure
5
. The deformation mapping within the layer has the form

y_{i}(\mathbf{x})=x_{i}+\dfrac{\delta_{i}}{h_{0}}x_{3},

(37)

where
i=1,2,3
. Then, the jump in displacement across the layer is

\delta_{i}=y_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{p}+(h_{0}/2){\mathbf{e}}_{3})-y_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{p}-(h_{0}/2){\mathbf{e}}_{3}),

(38)

where
\mathbf{x}_{p}=(x_{1},x_{2},0)
is the in-plane position vector. Similarly, the thickness of the layer at time
t
is

h:=h_{0}+\delta_{3}.

(39)

Figure 5
:
Shearing and dilating shear layer of fault gouge with initial height
h_{0}
and deformed height
h
.

The deformation gradient follows as

F_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\frac{\delta_{i}}{h_{0}}\delta_{3j},\quad{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{I}}+\frac{{\bm{\delta}}}{h_{0}}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{3},\quad{\mathbf{F}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\delta_{1}/h_{0}\\
0&1&\delta_{2}/h_{0}\\
0&0&1+\delta_{3}/h_{0}\end{pmatrix},

(40)

in indicial, invariant, and matrix notation, respectively, and has determinant

J=\det{\mathbf{F}}=1+\dfrac{\delta_{3}}{h_{0}}=\dfrac{h}{h_{0}}.

(41)

From (
7
), the layer dilation is directly related to the volumetric plastic strain through

\theta^{p}=\log J=\log\dfrac{h}{h_{0}},\quad h=h_{0}\,\exp{\theta^{p}}.

(42)

For the deformation considered, the spatial velocity gradient
{\mathbf{L}}
is

L_{ij}=\frac{\dot{\delta}_{i}}{h}\delta_{3j},\quad{\mathbf{L}}=\frac{\dot{{\bm{\delta}}}}{h}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{3},\quad{\mathbf{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\dot{\delta}_{1}/h\\
0&0&\dot{\delta}_{2}/h\\
0&0&\dot{\delta}_{3}/h\end{pmatrix},

(43)

and the skew-symmetric and symmetric parts are given, respectively, by the spin tensor,

W_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{\delta}_{i}}{h}\delta_{3j}-\frac{\dot{\delta}_{j}}{h}\delta_{3i}\right),\;\;{\mathbf{W}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{{\bm{\delta}}}}{h}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{3}-{\mathbf{e}}_{3}\otimes\frac{\dot{{\bm{\delta}}}}{h}\right),\;\;{\mathbf{W}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&{\dot{\delta}_{1}}/{2h}\\
0&0&{\dot{\delta}_{2}}/{2h}\\
-{\dot{\delta}_{1}}/{2h}&-{\dot{\delta}_{2}}/{2h}&0\end{pmatrix},

(44)

and the rate-of-deformation tensor,

D_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{\delta}_{i}}{h}\delta_{3j}+\frac{\dot{\delta}_{j}}{h}\delta_{3i}\right),\;\;{\mathbf{D}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\dot{{\bm{\delta}}}}{h}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{3}+{\mathbf{e}}_{3}\otimes\frac{\dot{{\bm{\delta}}}}{h}\right),\;\;{\mathbf{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&{\dot{\delta}_{1}}/{2h}\\
0&0&{\dot{\delta}_{2}}/{2h}\\
{\dot{\delta}_{1}}/{2h}&{\dot{\delta}_{2}}/{2h}&{\dot{\delta}_{3}}/{h}\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

At fixed
{\mathbf{F}^{e}}
, the stress tensor finally follows by inserting (
45
) into (
22
). We note that, in general, the in-plane components of the stress tensor are not zero due to the active pressure that results from normal stress.

For a shearing and dilating granular layer,
{\mathbf{D}}
is fully parameterized by the deformation rates

\dot{\gamma}_{i}:=\frac{\dot{\delta}_{i}}{h}.

(46)

Hence, the stress-strain relations (
22
) reduce to functions of the form

{\bm{\sigma}}={\bm{\sigma}}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})={\bm{\sigma}}(\dot{{\bm{\delta}}}/h),

(47)

where the state dependence is implicit. In particular, the tractions

\bm{\tau}:=\bm{\sigma}\,{\mathbf{e}}_{3},

(48)

which are work-conjugate to
\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}
, obey a law of the form

\bm{\tau}={\bm{\sigma}}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})\,{\mathbf{e}}_{3}:=\bm{\tau}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}).

(49)

The work-conjugacy between
\bm{\tau}
and
\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}
has the immediate consequence that the stress-strain law (
49
) derives from a potential, namely,

\bm{\tau}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}),\quad\Phi(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\Psi({\mathbf{D}}),

(50)

with
{\mathbf{D}}
as in (
45
).

When regarded as a relation for
\bm{\tau}(\dot{{\bm{\delta}}})
at given
h
, with the dependence on state implicit, (
49
) expresses a stress-strain law of the type commonly assumed in rate-and-state models of fault shear resistance
[
46
,
47
,
22
,
56
]
. However, it bears emphasis that the shear resistance law thus obtained is not postulated
ad hoc
, but rather derived as the effective behavior of a shearing and dilating layer composed of a granular material.

4.1
Specialization to Cam-Clay

We apply the Cam-Clay plasticity model to the particular deformation of a shearing and dilating granular layer discussed above. Using (
45
) in (
29
),

\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\sqrt{\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{1}^{2}}{3}+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{2}}{3}+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{3}^{2}}{\tilde{\alpha}^{2}}},

(51)

where we write

\tilde{\alpha}:=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\frac{4}{9}\alpha^{2}}},

(52)

and, from (
14
),
\dot{\theta}^{p}=\,\overline{\!{D}}=\dot{\gamma}_{3}
. From (
45
), the corresponding stress-strain relation (
31
) becomes

\displaystyle{\bm{\sigma}}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})

\displaystyle=\left(p_{0}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}))+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{3}}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\alpha}^{2}}-\dfrac{2}{3}\right)\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}))\right)\mathbf{I}

(53)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{3\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{3}+{\mathbf{e}}_{3}\otimes\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})).

As a result, the tractions
\bm{\tau}
are given by

\bm{\tau}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\left(p_{0}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}))\right){\mathbf{e}}_{3}+\frac{1}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})}\left(\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{3}}{\tilde{\alpha}^{2}}{\mathbf{e}}_{3}+\frac{\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}-\dot{\gamma}_{3}{\mathbf{e}}_{3}}{3}\right)\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})),

(54)

and, by symmetry, the lateral stresses are

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})=\Big(p_{0}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}))+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{3}}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\alpha}^{2}}-\dfrac{2}{3}\right)\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}))\Big)\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

(55)

It follows that the confining in-plane stresses are independent of direction, as expected from isotropy.

A number of further restrictions on the layer behavior can be gleaned from standardized experiments on soils and sands. In some cases, such restrictions suffice to fully identify the behavior of the layer, as shown next.

4.2
Oedometer test

Oedometer tests relate the consolidation stress of a granular material to its volume change. Here, we use these experiments to restrict the functional forms of the consolidation pressure
p_{0}
and rate potential
\psi(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})
.

In an oedometer test, the granular material is laterally confined such that
\dot{\gamma}_{1}=\dot{\gamma}_{2}\equiv 0
. The test may be displacement controlled (
\dot{\gamma}_{3}<0
prescribed) or load controlled (
\tau_{3}<0
prescribed). Consider the displacement-control case. For this deformation, (
51
) has the simplified form

\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{\gamma}_{3})=\dfrac{|\dot{\gamma}_{3}|}{\tilde{\alpha}},

(56)

for given
\dot{\gamma}_{3}
, and the only nonzero component of the traction (
54
) is

\tau_{3}(\dot{\gamma}_{3}):=\bm{\tau}(\dot{\gamma}_{3}{\mathbf{e}}_{3})\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{3}=p_{0}+\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{\gamma}_{3}))+\dfrac{1}{\tilde{\alpha}}\operatorname{sgn}(\dot{\gamma}_{3})\dfrac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\dot{\gamma}_{3},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{\gamma}_{3})).

(57)

If we further assume that in the limit
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\to 0
,
\tau_{3}=0
for
\dot{\gamma}_{3}>0
(opening), i. e., cohesive effects are solely viscous, then (
57
) and (
36
) give that the yield stress
\sigma_{0}
is related to the consolidation pressure
p_{0}
through

\sigma_{0}=-\tilde{\alpha}p_{0}.

(58)

Experiments on granular media find a linear relation between the normal strain,
\delta_{3}/h_{0}
, and the logarithm of the normal stress,
\log(-\tau_{3})
, at constant temperature and loading rate
[
7
,
37
,
33
,
17
]
. The consolidation curve shifts with changes in temperature and loading rate
[
61
,
37
,
34
]
. For a fixed vertical strain, the consolidation stress increases at higher strain rates and lower temperatures; i. e., larger normal stresses are required to achieve the same void ratio in the granular material. Additionally,
37
empirically found that, at small strain rates and large normal stresses relative to the pre-consolidation pressure, the normal stress is a separable function of the normal strain and the normal strain rate.

To match these experiments, we assume that
p_{0}
and
{\psi}
have the same
\theta^{p}
and
T
dependence, and choose the functional forms,

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta^{p},T)=\dfrac{p_{c}(T)}{2}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\theta^{p}}{\theta^{p}_{\rm ref}}\right),

(59)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\psi}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p};\theta^{p},T)=\sigma_{0}\,\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}+p_{0}(\theta^{p},T)\,\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\,\dot{\theta}^{p}+\sigma_{c}(T)\,\varphi(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}),

where
p_{c}(T)<0
is the quasi-static pre-consolidation pressure with
\sigma_{c}:=-\tilde{\alpha}p_{c}>0
,

\theta^{p}_{\rm ref}>0
is the reference volumetric strain, and
\tilde{\varphi}
and
\varphi
are rate potentials that are constrained by the convexity of
\psi(\cdot,\cdot)
(
A
) and have to be selected for specific materials.

As a result, inserting the representation (
59
) into (
57
),

\tau_{3}(\dot{\gamma}_{3})=p_{0}+p_{0}(\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})+\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}))-\operatorname{sgn}(\dot{\gamma}_{3})\left(p_{0}+p_{c}\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\right),

(60)

such that in tension,
\operatorname{sgn}(\dot{\gamma}_{3})=+1
,

\tau_{3}(\dot{\gamma}_{3}>0)=p_{0}(\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})+\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}))-p_{c}\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}),

(61)

and in compression,
\operatorname{sgn}(\dot{\gamma}_{3})=-1
,

\tau_{3}(\dot{\gamma}_{3}<0)=p_{0}(2+\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})+\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}))+p_{c}\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}).

(62)

Due to the exponential dependence of
p_{0}
on
\theta^{p}
, there is a linear relation between the vertical strain and the logarithm of the vertical stress, and the effective consolidation pressure is rate-dependent, as desired.

The stress-strain relation (
54
) is fully-calibrated solely considering experimental observations of oedometer tests, but must be solved numerically for a general deformation. In the following section, we make simplifying assumptions motivated by experiments that allow us to develop closed form expressions for
\bm{\tau}(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})
for a shearing granular layer.

4.3
Shear test

We examine the response of the material model to a plane-strain (
\delta_{1}=0
), simple shear deformation, with prescribed shearing rate
\dot{\delta}_{2}>0
and constant normal stress
-\tau_{3}:=\sigma_{n}>0
, with the goal of deriving closed-form expressions for the shear stress and state variable. The condition of constant normal stress is typical in experiments on the shear resistance of gouge layers
[
23
,
56
,
40
,
3
]
to mimic conditions of confinement by overburden on natural faults.

The plastic strain rate in simple shear depends on the shear loading rate and layer dilation rate through

\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}(\dot{\gamma}_{2},\dot{\gamma}_{3})=\sqrt{\dfrac{\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{2}}{3}+\dfrac{\dot{\gamma}_{3}^{2}}{\tilde{\alpha}^{2}}},

(63)

where
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
depends implicitly on
\theta^{p}
due to the Eulerian nature of
\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}}
.

Under plane-strain conditions, the shear tractions from (
54
) are
\tau_{1}=0
and

\tau_{2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}),

(64)

and constant normal stress is enforced by
\tau_{3}=-\sigma_{n}
, or

-\sigma_{n}=p_{0}+\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\dot{\theta}^{p}}}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})+\dfrac{\dot{\theta}^{p}}{\tilde{\alpha}^{2}\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}(\dot{\theta}^{p},\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}),

(65)

where
p_{0}
and
\psi
are given by (
59
). Solving (
65
) effectively provides an evolution equation for
{\theta}^{p}
, which serves as a state variable analogous to that of rate-and-state friction formulations (e. g.
[
23
,
56
,
59
]
), and corresponds to the volume change necessary to maintain the prescribed normal stress
\sigma_{n}
at the plastic strain rate
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
. That is, the derivatives of the stress potential,
\Phi(\dot{{\bm{\gamma}}})
, give both the stress-strain relation and the evolution law for the state variable,
\theta^{p}
.

Specializing for the rate potential in (
59
), (
65
) becomes

\sigma_{0}\left(1+\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\right)-\tilde{\alpha}\sigma_{n}=\left(\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{c}\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\right)\dfrac{\dot{\theta}^{p}}{\tilde{\alpha}\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}-\sigma_{0}\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\left(\dfrac{\dot{\theta}^{p}}{\tilde{\alpha}\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}\right)^{2}.

(66)

4.3.1
Behavior over constant loading windows

We demonstrate that (
66
) results in a decaying exponential dependence of the shear stress with slip, in line with experimental observations from velocity-step tests and previous theoretical work
[
23
,
56
]
. Although it is not possible to derive a closed-form solution for a general rate potential
\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})
and
\varphi(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})
, we make a series of simplifying assumptions that result in an explicit solution of (
66
). Then, using this solution in (
64
) gives the desired result.

To solve (
66
) explicitly, we assume that the shearing rate is much larger than the dilation rate (
|\dot{\gamma}_{2}|\gg|\dot{\gamma}_{3}|
), the volume changes are small (
h\approx h_{0}
), and the viscous stresses are much smaller than the normal stresses (
\sigma_{c}\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\ll\tilde{\alpha}\sigma_{n}
) (
B
). In this regime, (
66
) is well-approximated by

\dot{\hat{\sigma}}_{0}=-\dfrac{{\hat{\sigma}_{0}}-{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}}{t_{c}},

(67)

where we have used (
58
) and (
59
) to write the ODE only in terms of
\sigma_{0}
, and

{\hat{\sigma}_{0}}:=\dfrac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{c}},\quad\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\approx\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\dfrac{\dot{\delta}_{2}}{h_{0}},\quad t_{c}:=\dfrac{\theta^{p}_{\rm ref}/(\tilde{\alpha}\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})}{1+\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})},\quad\hat{\sigma}_{n}:=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{c}},\quad{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}:=\dfrac{\hat{\sigma}_{n}}{1+\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})}.

(68)

For constant loading rate
\dot{\delta}_{2}
in the interval
(t_{0},t)
and initial condition
\sigma_{0}(t_{0})=\sigma_{0}^{\rm init}
, the solution of the ODE (
67
) is given by

{\hat{\sigma}_{0}}={\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}+\left({\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{\rm init}}-{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}\right)\exp{\left(-\dfrac{t-t_{0}}{t_{c}}\right)},

(69)

where, over long loading windows
(t-t_{0})/t_{c}\gg 1
,
{\hat{\sigma}_{0}}
evolves to a rate- and normal-stress-dependent steady-state value,
{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}
. For constant shear rate, time and slip are related through
\dot{\delta_{2}}
, and thus, (
70
) can be understood as a decaying exponential dependence with slip, or

{\hat{\sigma}_{0}}={\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}+\left({\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{\rm init}}-{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}\right)\exp{\left(-\dfrac{\delta_{2}-\delta_{20}}{\delta_{2c}}\right)},

(70)

where
\delta_{2c}
is a rate-dependent characteristic slip distance which follows from substitution into (
68
),

\delta_{2c}:=\dot{\delta}_{2}t_{c}=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}\theta^{p}_{\rm ref}/\tilde{\alpha}}{1+\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})}h_{0}.

(71)

Using (
70
), the layer height and shear stress evolutions follow from straightforward substitutions. The change in layer width, (
42
), is thus

h=h_{0}(2{\hat{\sigma}_{0}})^{-\theta^{p}_{\rm ref}},

(72)

where the layer dilates with slip if
{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{\rm init}}>{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}
, while for
{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{\rm init}}<{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}
, it compacts. Similarly, the shear stress (
64
) is

\tau_{2}=\dfrac{\sigma_{c}(T)}{\sqrt{3}}\left({\hat{\sigma}_{0}}+\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})+\dfrac{\theta^{p}_{\rm ref}}{\tilde{\alpha}}\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\,\dot{\hat{\sigma}}_{0}\right),

(73)

where we have used (
58
) and (
59
). The first term characterizes the change in shear stress with slip: if
{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{\rm init}}>{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}
, the shear stress decreases with slip and the layer softens, while for
{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{\rm init}}<{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}
, it increases and the layer hardens. These trends in consolidation-dependent changes in the layer response agree with the experimental results from Figure
2
. The next two terms describe the rate-dependence of the shear stress, with the last term also describing how far the system is from steady state through
\dot{\hat{\sigma}}_{0}
.

4.3.2
Long-term behavior at the critical state

Laboratory experiments find that a granular sample sheared at a constant loading rate
\dot{\delta}_{2}
evolves to a rate-dependent steady-state layer width
h_{\rm crit}
, which increases with the loading rate
\dot{\delta}_{2}
. Evaluating (
72
) in the limit
{\hat{\sigma}_{0}}\to{\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{c}}
gives

h_{\rm crit}=h_{0}\left(\dfrac{1+\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})}{2\hat{\sigma}_{n}}\right)^{\theta^{p}_{\rm ref}}.

(74)

From (
68
), at constant normal confinement
\sigma_{n}
,
1/\hat{\sigma}_{n}
is smaller for a loosely consolidated material than for a densely consolidated one. When
1/\hat{\sigma}_{n}>2
, corresponding to more consolidated materials, (
74
) predicts dilation. In addition, for
\tilde{\varphi}
monotonically increasing, the layer height increases at faster loading rates. Both predictions agree with the key mechanisms from Section
2
.

At the critical state, the shear stress becomes

\tau_{2}^{\rm crit}=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}\sigma_{n}}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\left(1+\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\right)^{-1}+\dfrac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}}\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\right).

(75)

Interestingly, the last term differs from the typical assumption of Coulomb friction, in which the friction coefficient is independent of normal stress. This additional normal stress dependence in (
75
) occurs when the current confining normal stress is smaller than the pre-consolidation pressure,
\hat{\sigma}_{n}<1
. For small
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}
, the critical state shear stress is approximately given by

\tau_{2}^{\rm crit}\approx\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}\sigma_{n}}{\sqrt{3}}\left(1-\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})+\dfrac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}}\varphi^{\prime}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})\right),

(76)

where the rate effects perturb the friction coefficient around a rate independent value,
\tilde{\alpha}/\sqrt{3}
. Depending on the relative magnitudes of
\tilde{\varphi}
,
\varphi
, and
\hat{\sigma}_{n}
,
\tau_{2}^{\rm crit}
may increase or decrease with loading rate. That is, the response may be
steady-state rate strengthening
or
steady-state rate weakening
. Steady-state rate strengthening results in stable, slow slip under tectonic plate loading, while the weakening of the interface with larger loading rates leads to dynamic ruptures, or earthquakes
[
50
,
35
]
; hence, the ability of the model to capture both behaviors could explain the existence of both stably slipping and stick-slip fault segments.

4.4
Specialization of rate potentials

To illustrate the behavior of the model, we select functional forms for the rate potentials
\tilde{\varphi}
and
\varphi
satisfying the convexity constraints from
A
. We consider two examples,

\displaystyle\text{Case 1: }\quad\varphi(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})=\eta_{1}\dfrac{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{0}}{2}\left(\dfrac{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{0}}\right)^{2},\quad

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})=\frac{\eta_{2}}{\eta_{1}}\dfrac{2\varphi(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}},

(77)

\displaystyle\text{Case 2: }\quad\varphi(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})=\eta_{1}\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{0}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\left(\dfrac{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{0}}\right)^{2}}+\dfrac{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{0}}\sinh^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{0}}\right)\right),\quad

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})=\frac{\eta_{2}}{\eta_{1}}\dfrac{2\varphi(\dot{\varepsilon}^{p})}{\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}},

where the first case corresponds to linear rate-sensitivity and the second is motivated by activation theory
[
49
]
. For small plastic strain rates,
\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}\ll\dot{\varepsilon}^{p}_{0}
, the two rate potentials are identical at leading order. In the following section, we conduct numerical experiments and compare the response of the material model for each case of rate sensitivity.

5

We investigate the response of the material model to tests commonly used to characterize granular media. First, we conduct a numerical oedometer test, in which we increase the normal load at a constant loading rate,
\dot{\sigma}_{ld}

\sigma_{M}

\hat{\sigma}_{n}

To validate the model, we solve the stress-strain relations (

\Delta\delta_{i}

C

6
Conclusions

We have developed a variational, finite-deformation framework for non-isochoric, rigid-plastic materials such as fault gouge. The variational structure of the governing equations admits a potential formulation for the effective stress–strain relations. In the particular case of shearing and dilating granular layers, the shear stresses and evolution of internal variables are prescribed by the choice of the rate potential. By invoking a separation of scales between the shear and dilatant responses, we obtain closed-form expressions for the shear stress and layer dilation in simple shear. Lastly, we demonstrate the ability of the model and theory to capture experimental features from consolidation and shear tests.

Our formulation extends existing descriptions of granular material behavior in several important respects. First, the rate and direction of plastic flow follow from the maximization of a dissipation potential subject to a kinematic constraint. The governing equations are cast in rate form and are therefore independent of numerical discretization. The variational structure guarantees objectivity and provides a consistent extension of yield surfaces to finite deformations. In addition, the effective stress–strain relations emerge from the stationarity of the dissipation functional. This potential-based formulation thus resolves ambiguities concerning the evolution of internal variables for shearing interfaces and offers a clear physical interpretation of the internal variable as the effective consolidation state of the layer. A further advantage of the approach lies in its calibration procedure, which requires only standard consolidation experiments and convexity considerations. Consequently, the predicted response to standard shearing protocols follows naturally from the variational structure.

The model reproduces key qualitative features of experimental observations on shearing granular layers. Using two canonical examples of rate sensitivity in viscoplastic theories, we capture the non-monotonic response of simulated fault gouge under stepped shear loading. The analysis identifies the ratio of pre-consolidation to confinement stress, denoted by
\hat{\sigma}_{n}
, as a principal parameter controlling the shear response. Increasing
\hat{\sigma}_{n}
induces a transition in the rheological behavior, leading to rate weakening—a destabilizing mechanism naturally associated with earthquake nucleation in fault zones.

The proposed framework introduces significant improvements to the empirical rate-and-state description of friction, a model known for reproducing a wide range of earthquake source observations with a minimal parameter set
[
35
,
55
,
58
]
. The rate-and-state parameters are typically inferred only from shear tests and are known to vary with loading history, normal stress, and other factors
[
40
,
5
,
4
,
39
]
, undermining their interpretation as material constants. Additionally, although the effect of dilatancy has been incorporated into rate-and-state friction formulations (e. g.
59
,
60
), it has been done on the basis of a limited set of experiments.

The present variational formulation offers a rational foundation for these empirical laws, provides a framework for including a wealth of additional information to determine the appropriate functional forms and parameters for the shear resistance of granular layers, and suggests a path toward identifying consistent analogues between rate-and-state parameters and the critical-state variables of our model. Systematic comparisons of the developed framework with laboratory experiments under diverse loading protocols, for different minerological compositions of gouge, and at the faster strain-rates characteristic of earthquakes that activate shear-induced heating and hence temperature effects (e. g.
[
63
,
51
,
21
,
64
,
55
]
) constitute a natural continuation of this work.

Finally, the extension of the framework to account for pore fluid effects offers a promising direction. In fluid-permeated fault zones, pore pressure reduces the effective normal stress and thereby facilitates slip. Conventional friction models incorporate this effect via the Terzaghi effective stress, wherein the normal stress is reduced by the pore pressure. This modification arises naturally in our setting through a straightforward adjustment of the dissipation function. More elaborate couplings linking volumetric deformation, pore pressure evolution, and fluid transport would further enhance the model’s fidelity and provide valuable insight into the mechanics of fluid-assisted fault slip.
