Translating between the representations of an acyclic convex geometry of bounded degree Thanks: The authors have been supported by the ANR project PARADUAL (ANR-24-CE48-0610-01).
Oscar Defrain, Arthur Ohana, Simon Vilmin

Abstract
We consider the problem of enumerating the irreducible closed sets of a closure system given by an implicational base. In the context of Horn logic, these correspond to Horn expressions and characteristic models, respectively. To date, the complexity status of this problem is widely open, and it is further known to generalize the notorious hypergraph dualization problem, even in the context of acyclic convex geometries, i.e., closure systems admitting an acyclic implicational base. This paper studies this later class with a focus on the degree, which corresponds to the maximal number of implications in which an element occurs. We show that the problem is tractable for bounded values of this parameter, even when relaxed to the notions of premise- and /conclusion-degree. Our algorithms rely on structural properties of acyclic convex geometries and involve various techniques from algorithmic enumeration such as solution graph traversal, saturation techniques, and a sequential approach leveraging from acyclicity. They are shown to perform in incremental-polynomial time for the computation of irreducible closed sets, and in polynomial time for the construction of an implicational base. Finally, we argue that our running times cannot be improved to polynomial delay using the standard framework of flashlight search. Keywords: algorithmic enumeration, closure systems, acyclic convex geometries, implicational bases, irreducible closed sets, incremental-polynomial, saturation algorithms, solution graph traversal, flashlight search.

Introduction

Finite closure systems
appear in disguise in several areas of mathematics and computer science, including database theory
[
Mai83
,
MR92
]
, Horn logic
[
KKS93
,
CH11
]
,
or lattice theory
[
Bir40
,
DP02
]
, to mention but a few.
A closure system is a family of subsets of some finite ground set being closed by intersection and containing the ground set.
The study of the various representations of closure systems and the algorithmic task of translating between them have received lots of attention in these respective fields, and have been the topic of the Dagstuhl Seminar 14201
[
AIBT14
]
.
The translation task we study in this paper is most importantly known for being a considerable generalization of the notorious and widely open hypergraph dualization problem, while not being known to be intractable, contrary to analogous other natural generalizations such as lattice dualization
[
KSS00
,
BK17
,
DN20
]
.
We redirect the reader to the recent works
[
DS11
,
BK13
,
BMN17
,
DNV21
,
NV23
]
as well as to the surveys
[
Wil17
,
BDVG18
,
Rud17
]
for more information on the topic.

Among the several representations of closure systems, two of them occupy a central role, namely,
irreducible closed sets
and
implicational bases
, whose formal definitions are postponed to Section
2
.
Informally, irreducible closed sets are particular sets of the closure system, while implicational bases are sets of implications of the form
A\to B
, where
A
, the premise, and
B
, the conclusion, are subsets of the ground set.
A closure system has a unique collection of irreducible closed sets, but it can be represented by several equivalent implicational bases.
As noted in
[
Kha95
]
, these two representations are generally incomparable, in size and usefulness.
Notably, there exist instances where the minimum cardinality of an implicational base is exponential in the number of irreducible closed sets, and vice versa.
Moreover, the computational complexity of some algorithmic tasks such as reasoning, abduction, or recognizing lattice properties can differ significantly depending on the representation at hand
[
KKS93
,
BK13
,
MR92
]
.
These observations motivate the problem of translating between the two representations, which has two sides: enumerate the irreducible closed sets from an implicational base, and produce a compact implicational base from the collection of irreducible closed sets.
These problems are respectively denoted by
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
throughout the paper and formally defined in the next section.

Because of the possible exponential gap between the sizes of the two representations, input sensitive-complexity is not a meaningful efficiency criterion when analyzing the performance of an algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
or
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
.
Instead, the framework of algorithmic enumeration in which an algorithm has to list without repetitions a set of solutions, is usually adopted.
The complexity of an algorithm is then analyzed from the perspective of
output-sensitive complexity
, which estimates the running time of the algorithm using both input and output sizes.
In that context, an algorithm is considered tractable when it runs in
output-polynomial
time, that is, if its execution time is bounded by a polynomial in the combined sizes of the input and the output.
This notion can be further constrained to guarantee some regularity in the enumeration.
Namely, we say that an algorithm runs in
incremental-polynomial time
if it outputs the
i^{\text{th}}
solution in a time which is bounded by a polynomial in the input size plus
i
.
It is said to run with
polynomial delay
if the time spent before the first output, after the last output, and between consecutive outputs is bounded by a polynomial in the input size only.
We refer the reader to
[
JYP88
,
Str19
]
for more details on enumeration complexity.

In this work, we investigate
acyclic convex geometries
, a particular class of closure systems.
They are the convex geometries—closure systems with the anti-exchange property—that admit an implicational base with acyclic implication graph.
Acyclic convex geometries have been extensively studied
[
HK95
,
Wil94
,
BČKK09
,
SW14
,
DNV21
]
.
Even in this class though, the two problems
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
have been shown in
[
DNV21
]
to be harder than distributive lattices dualization, a generalization of hypergraph dualization.
On the other hand, the authors in
[
DNV21
]
show that if, in addition to acyclicity, the implication graph admits a rank function, then the problem can be solved using hypergraph dualization.
Using this result, they derive a tractable algorithm for so-called ranked convex geometries admitting an implicational base of bounded premise size.
It should be noted that, even under these restrictions, the problem generalizes the one of enumerating all maximal independent sets of a graph (in fact of hypergraphs of bounded edge size) a central and notorious problem in enumeration
[
TIAS77
,
JYP88
]
.
These results have then been extended to a more general setting in
[
NV23
]
.
Let us finally note that, outside of acyclic convex geometries, the translation task is known to be tractable in modular or
k
-meet-semidistributive lattices
[
Wil00
,
BMN17
]
, or in closure spaces of bounded Caratheodory number
[
Wil17
]
.

In this paper, we continue the line of research of characterizing tractable cases of acyclic convex geometries for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
, typically, by identifying for which parameters
k
we can obtain tractable algorithms for these problems for fixed values of
k
.
We note that in this research, performing in quasi-polynomial time should already be considered a success, since no sub-exponential time algorithms are known to date.
We focus on the degree, which is defined as the maximum number of implications in which an element participates, and its two obvious relaxations which are the premise- and conclusion-degree; these parameters are defined in Section
2
.
The principal message of this work is that bounding one of these two relaxed notions of degree suffices to provide not only quasi-polynomial but polynomial-time bounds for the translation.
More specifically, as the first of our two main theorems, we obtain the following, whose quantitative formulation is postponed to Sections
5
and
6
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is an incremental-polynomial time algorithm enumerating the irreducible closed sets of an acyclic convex geometry given by an acyclic implicational base of bounded premise- or conclusion-degree.

Our algorithms for acyclic convex geometries of bounded degree rely on several steps which combine structure and enumeration techniques.
We give a rough description which highlights the techniques used, and refer to the appropriate sections for more details.

For generating irreducible closed sets from an acyclic implicational base, we use the fact that in a convex geometry, each irreducible closed set is
attached
to a unique element of the ground set; this is detailed in Section
2
.
Then, leveraging from acyclicity, our algorithm relies on a sequential procedure which consists of enumerating, incrementally and in a top-down fashion according to a topological ordering
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of the elements of the ground set, all the irreducible closed sets attached to
x_{i}
.
This step can be seen as enumerating the irreducible closed sets attached to
x_{i}
with additional information as input, namely, the irreducible closed sets of ancestor elements.
Finally, it remains to solve this latter task, which we can do either by bruteforcing the minimal transversals of a carefully designed hypergraph in case of bounded conclusion-degree, or performing a solution graph traversal in case of bounded premise-degree.
In the end, the resulting algorithms run in incremental-polynomial time, where the dependence in the number of solutions arises from the sequential top-down procedure.

We then turn to the opposite translation task of generating a compact implicational base given the irreducible closed sets of an acyclic convex geometry.
To do so, we rely on the concept of critical generators
[
KL84
,
Wil94
,
HK95
]
.
These are known to form an acyclic implicational base of minimum cardinality (in fact, minimum among those with conclusions of size one, or minimum once aggregated, a point that is discussed in Section
3
) in the class of acyclic geometries.
This implicational base, referred to as the critical base, thus constitutes a candidate of choice to be generated from irreducible closed sets.
We obtain the following as our second main theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm constructing the critical base of an acyclic convex geometry of bounded premise- or conclusion-degree given by its irreducible closed sets.

This second algorithm also relies on the top-down approach
except that, this time, we generate all the critical generators of
x_{i}
at each step.
However, since the implicational base is not given, a particular care is devoted to arguing of the choice of the ordering
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and the fact that it can be computed from the irreducible closed sets only.
Then, the enumeration of critical generators is conducted
using a saturation algorithm relying on the existence of an algorithm for the sequential enumeration of irreducible closed sets we obtained in order to prove Theorem
1.1
.
We note that in this direction, our algorithm is much more efficient thanks to the fact that the output has polynomial cardinality.

In order to prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we build upon a number of existing properties of acyclic convex geometries coming from different fields.
This results in a substantial presentation of acyclic convex geometries which aims to be self-contained and accessible.
Besides, we also extend this presentation with a study of the degree in this class.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the concepts and notations used throughout the paper.
Section
3
continues the state of the art via a focus on acyclic convex geometries, and include additional observations that may be of independent interest.
Section
4
presents the top-down approach that we use as the key ingredient in our algorithms.
Sections
5
and
6
are devoted to prove Theorem
1.1
in the respective cases of bounded conclusion- and premise-degree.
Theorem
1.2
is proved in Section
7
.
Finally, we discuss possible generalizations, limitations, and open directions in Section
8
.

Preliminaries

All the objects we consider in this paper are finite.
If
X
is a set,
\mathcal{P}(X)
is its powerset.
The size of
X
is denoted
|X|
.
A
set system
, or
hypergraph
, over a ground set
X
is a pair
(X,\mathcal{F})
where
\mathcal{F}
is a collection of subsets of
X
, that is
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
.
Its
size
is
|X|+|\mathcal{F}|
; note that the binary length of any standard encoding of a hypergraph is polynomially related to its size.
Thus, for simplicity, we shall consider this measure for families as input size in the different problems we will consider.
If
X
is clear from the context, we may identify
\mathcal{F}
with
(X,\mathcal{F})
.
Sometimes, and if there is no ambiguity, we may write a set as the concatenation of its elements, for instance
123
instead of
\{1,2,3\}
.
Similarly, we sometimes withdraw brackets when dealing with singletons, e.g., we may write
\phi(x)
instead of
\phi(\{x\})
.

Independent and hitting sets, hypergraph parameters.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(X,\mathcal{E})
be a hypergraph.
We call
vertices
of
\mathcal{H}
the elements in
X
, and
edges
the sets in
\mathcal{E}
.
We say that
\mathcal{H}
is
Sperner
if any two of its edges are pairwise incomparable by inclusion.
An
independent set
of
\mathcal{H}
is a set
I\subseteq X
that contains no edges of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
E\nsubseteq I
for every
E
in
\mathcal{E}
.
The family of inclusion-wise maximal independent sets of
\mathcal{H}
is denoted
\mathrm{MIS}(\mathcal{H})
.
Formally:

\mathrm{MIS}(\mathcal{H}):=\max_{\subseteq}\{I:\text{$I\subseteq X$, $E\nsubseteq I$ for every $E$ in $\mathcal{E}$}\}.

Dually, a
hitting set
, or
transversal
, of
\mathcal{H}
is a subset
T
of
X
that intersects each edge of
\mathcal{H}
.
The collection of inclusion-wise minimal hitting sets of
\mathcal{H}
is called
\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H})
:

\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}):=\min_{\subseteq}\{T:\text{$T\subseteq X$, $T\cap E\neq\emptyset$ for every $E$ in $\mathcal{E}$}\}.

The hypergraph
\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H})
is usually called the
dual hypergraph
of
\mathcal{H}
.
Observe that
I\subseteq X
is independent if and only if
T:=X\setminus I
is a hitting set.
Consequently,
\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H})=\{X\setminus I:I\in\mathrm{MIS}(\mathcal{H})\}
.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{H}
is not Sperner, then its minimal hitting sets (resp. maximal independent sets) are precisely those of the hypergraph formed by the inclusion-wise minimal edges of
\mathcal{H}
.
The two equivalent generation problems associated to maximal independent sets and minimal hitting sets read as follows:

Maximal Independent Sets Enumeration (
MIS
{\cdot}
Enum
)

Input:

A (Sperner) hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=(X,\mathcal{E})
.

Output:

The family
\mathrm{MIS}(\mathcal{H})
.

Minimal Hitting Sets Enumeration (
MHS
{\cdot}
Enum
)

Input:

A (Sperner) hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=(X,\mathcal{E})
.

Output:

The family
\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H})
.

To date, the best algorithm for these tasks runs in output-quasi-polynomial time and is due to Fredman and Khachiyan
[
FK96
]
.
However, a number of output-polynomial time algorithms have been proposed for particular cases, especially when bounding structural parameters
[
EMG08
]
.
Among these parameters, we will be interested in the
dimension
, which is the size of the largest edge, or the
dual dimension
, being the dimension of the dual hypergraph.

Closure systems, closure spaces.

A
closure system
is a set system
(X,\mathcal{C})
that satisfies
X\in\mathcal{C}
, and
C\cap C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
whenever
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
.
The sets in
\mathcal{C}
are called
closed (sets)
.
A
closure space
is a pair
(X,\phi)
where
\phi\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(X)
is a mapping that satisfies for every
A,B\subseteq X
: (1)
A\subseteq\phi(A)
, (2)
\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(B)
if
A\subseteq B
, and (3)
\phi(\phi(A))=\phi(A)
.
The map
\phi
is a
closure operator
.
Closure spaces and closure systems are in one-to-one correspondence.
More precisely, if
(X,\phi)
is a closure space, the family
\mathcal{C}:=\{C:C\subseteq X,\ \phi(C)=C\}
defines a closure system.
Dually, if
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a closure system, the map
\phi
defined by
\phi(A):=\min_{\subseteq}\{C:C\in\mathcal{C},\ A\subseteq C\}=\bigcap\{C\colon C\in\mathcal{C},\ A\subseteq C\}
is a closure operator whose associated closure system is precisely
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
In this paper, if
(X,\phi)
is a closure space, we call
(X,\mathcal{C})
its
corresponding
closure system, and vice-versa.
Moreover, we always consider without loss of generality that
\emptyset\in\mathcal{C}
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be a closure system.
The poset
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
of closed sets ordered by inclusion is a
(closure) lattice
where
C\land C^{\prime}=C\cap C^{\prime}
and
C\lor C^{\prime}=\phi(C\cup C^{\prime})
.
A
predecessor
of a closed set
C
is an inclusion-wise maximal closed set
F
properly contained in
C
, i.e.,
F\subset C
and for every
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
,
F\subseteq C^{\prime}\subseteq C
implies
C^{\prime}=F
or
C^{\prime}=C
.

Successors
are defined dually.
Let
M\in C
,
M\neq X
.
The closed set
M
is
irreducible
if it is not the intersection of other distinct closed sets, that is,
M=C\cap C^{\prime}
for some
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
only if
C=M
or
C^{\prime}=M
.
Irreducible closed sets are in fact the members of
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
with a unique successor in the poset.
We denote by
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
the family of irreducible closed sets of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
For every
C\in\mathcal{C}
, it is known that
C=\bigcap\{M:M\in\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C}),\ C\subseteq M\}
.
Moreover,
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
is the unique minimal subset of
\mathcal{C}
from which
\mathcal{C}
can be reconstructed using intersections.
Let
x\in X
.
We denote by
\mathrm{irr}(x)
the inclusion-wise maximal closed sets not containing
x
, i.e.,

\mathrm{irr}(x):=\max_{\subseteq}\{M:M\in\mathcal{C},\,x\notin M\}.

Observe that
\mathrm{irr}(x)\subseteq\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
for every
x\in X
.
An irreducible closed set
M
in
\mathrm{irr}(x)
is said to be
attached
to
x
.
In fact, it is well known that
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})=\bigcup_{x\in X}\mathrm{irr}(x)
.
A
(non-trivial) minimal generator
of
x
is an inclusion-wise minimal subset
A
of
X\setminus\{x\}
satisfying
x\in\phi(A)
.
The family of minimal generators of
x
is denoted
\mathrm{mingen}(x)
.
Again, it is well-known
[
MR92
]
that
\mathrm{irr}(x)
and
\mathrm{mingen}(x)
, seen as hypergraphs over
X
, are dual to one another, that is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{irr}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MIS}(\mathrm{mingen}(x)\cup\{\{x\}\}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{mingen}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MHS}(\{X\setminus(M\cup\{x\}):M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)\}).

Let
A\subseteq X
.
An
extreme point
of

is an element
x\in A
such that
x\notin\phi(A\setminus\{x\})

We denote by

In particular, if

.
Note that for every

.
Indeed,

.
Dually, if

for some
C\in\mathcal{C}
, then
x\notin\mathrm{ex}(C^{\prime})
for every

such that

.
If

, a

of

of

.

A closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a

if
\emptyset\in\mathcal{C}
and for every closed set
C

X
there exists
x\notin C
such that

is closed.
The closure system depicted in Figure

is a convex geometry.
Equivalently,
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry if the corresponding closure operator

has the

Convex geometries have been rediscovered in several fields and, as such, enjoy several characterizations

]
.
We summarize some of them in the subsequent statement:

Proposition 2.1

(see e.g.,
[
EJ85
, Theorems 2.1, 2.4]
)
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be a closure system such that

.
The following are equivalent:

(X,\mathcal{C})

2.

the associated closure operator

has the anti-exchange property

3.

each irreducible closed set is attached to a unique element, i.e.,
\{\mathrm{irr}(x):x\in X\}
is a partition of

4.

each closed set

has a unique minimal spanning set, which is
\mathrm{ex}(C)

Let

.
We call

for

a family of sets obtained by choosing, for each

.
Note that since we are dealing with convex geometries, and by Proposition

.
In the following, we note

Observation 2.2

For any

and any selection

We conclude the preliminaries with critical generators.
These have first been defined in terms of circuits of antimatroids (complements of convex geometries) within the context of combinatorial optimization
[
KL84
,
KLS12
,
Die87
]
but they also have been studied from the perspective of closure systems
[
Wil94
,
NK13
,
Wil17
,
YHM17
,
DNV21
]
.
They will play a crucial role in this paper.
Recall that
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry.
Let
x\in X
and let
A\in\mathrm{mingen}(x)
.
The minimal generator
A
is a
critical generator
of
x
if
\phi(A)\setminus\{a,x\}\in\mathcal{C}
for every
a\in A
.
For
x\in X
, we call
\mathrm{critgen}(x)
the set of critical generators of
x
.
Using the properties of extreme points previously mentioned, we can rephrase the definition of critical generators in a way that will be more convenient to use in our approach:

Proposition 2.3
.

The set
A
is a critical generator of
x
if and only if
A=\mathrm{ex}(\phi(A))
and
\phi(A)\in\min_{\subseteq}\{C:C\in\mathcal{C},\ x\in C,\ x\notin\mathrm{ex}(C)\}
.

Proof.

We start with the if part.
By assumption, for every
a\in A
,
x\in\mathrm{ex}(\phi(A)\setminus\{a\})
, i.e.,
\phi(A)\setminus\{a,x\}\in\mathcal{C}
for each
a\in A
.
Hence, we only need to show that
A\in\mathrm{mingen}(x)
.
Observe first that
x\notin A
by definition of
A
.
Now, let
a\in A
.
We have
\phi(A\setminus\{a\})\subseteq\phi(A)\setminus\{a\}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Moreover, since
A
is the unique minimal spanning set of
\phi(A)
by assumption, any subset of
A
must be the unique minimal spanning set of its closure, so in particular
\mathrm{ex}(\phi(A\setminus\{a\}))=A\setminus\{a\}
.
Thus,
x\in\phi(A\setminus\{a\})
would imply that
x\notin\mathrm{ex}(\phi(A\setminus\{a\}))
, as
x\notin A
,
which in turn yields
x\notin\mathrm{ex}(\phi(A)\setminus\{a\})
, a contradiction to the minimality of
\phi(A)
.
We deduce
x\notin\phi(A\setminus\{a\})
and hence that
A\in\mathrm{mingen}(x)
.

As for the only if part,
A
is a minimal generator of
x
, hence it must be the unique minimal spanning set of
\phi(A)
.
Since
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry,
A=\phi(\mathrm{ex}(A))
holds by Proposition
2.1
.
Thus, the predecessors of
\phi(A)
are precisely the sets
\phi(A)\setminus\{a\}
for
a\in A
.
Hence, for any
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C\subset\phi(A)
, there is
a\in A
such that
C\subseteq\phi(A)\setminus\{a\}
.
As
x\in\mathrm{ex}(\phi(A)\setminus\{a\})
,
x\in C
entails
x\in\mathrm{ex}(C)
.
We conclude that
\phi(A)\in\min_{\subseteq}\{C:C\in\mathcal{C},\ x\in C,\ x\notin\mathrm{ex}(C)\}
.
∎

Proposition
2.3
suggests an algorithm to compute a critical generator of an element
x
given a set that generates
x
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be a convex geometry,
x\in X
and
Y\subseteq X
such that
x\notin Y
and
x\in\phi(Y)
.
A critical generator
A
of
x
such that
\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(Y)
can be computed with a number of calls to
\phi
being polynomial in
|X|
.

Proof.

Notice that
x\in\phi(Y)
but
x\notin\mathrm{ex}(\phi(Y))
by definition.
Hence, there exists
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C\subseteq\phi(Y)
and
C\in\min_{\subseteq}\{C:C\in\mathcal{C},\ x\in C,\ x\notin\mathrm{ex}(C)\}
.
Moreover, for each such
C\subseteq\phi(Y)
and each
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C\subseteq C^{\prime}\subseteq\phi(Y)
,
x\notin\mathrm{ex}(C^{\prime})
must hold.

We describe an algorithm to identify a critical generator
A
of
x
included in
\phi(Y)
.
As a preprocessing step, we compute
\phi(Y)
.
Then, given a closed set
C
, we compute its extreme points and its predecessors, that are precisely the sets
\{C\setminus\{y\}:y\in\mathrm{ex}(C)\}
, and we identify the
element
y\in\mathrm{ex}(C)
with the smallest index such that
x\notin\mathrm{ex}(C\setminus\{y\})
.
If no such
y
exists, we output
\mathrm{ex}(C)
.
Otherwise, we repeat the step with
C\setminus\{y\}
as input.
By Proposition
2.3
, and since we check all the predecessors of the input closed set at each step, this procedure will correctly output a critical generator
A
of
x
included in
\phi(Y)
.

Computing
\phi(Y)
requires one call to
\phi
.
For a closed set
C
, finding
\mathrm{ex}(C)
and checking whether
x\in\mathrm{ex}(C\setminus\{y\})
for each
y\in\mathrm{ex}(C)
requires at most
2\cdot|X|
calls to
\phi
.
Since at each step we remove one element from
C
, we will repeat these operations at most
|X|
times.
Hence, the whole algorithm requires at most
1+2\cdot|X|^{2}
calls to
\phi
.
∎

Implicational bases.

An
implication
over
X
is a statement
A\to B
where
A,B
are subsets of
X
.
In
A\to B
,
A
is the
premise
and
B
the
conclusion
.
If
B
is a singleton, that is
B=\{b\}
, the implication
A\to b
is
(right-)unit
.
An
implicational base
(IB) is a pair
(X,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is a collection of implications over
X
.
The IB
(X,\Sigma)
is
(right-)unit
if it consists in unit implications.
The
aggregation
of an IB
(X,\Sigma)
is the IB
(X,\Sigma^{*})
resulting from the unification of the implications with equal premises, i.e.,
\Sigma^{*}:=\left\{A\to B:B=\bigcup\{B^{\prime}:A\to B^{\prime}\in\Sigma\}\right\}
.
Dually, the
unit-expansion
is obtained by replacing each implication
A\to B\in\Sigma
by the set of implications
\{A\to b:b\in B\}
.
Given an IB
(X,\Sigma)
,
|\Sigma|
is the cardinal of
\Sigma
, that is the number of implications in
\Sigma
.
The
size
of the IB is the value
|X|+|\Sigma|
.
The
degree
of an element
x\in X
, denoted
\deg(x)
, is the number of implications of
\Sigma
in which
x
appears.
We refine this notion by defining the
premise-degree
of
x
as
\mathrm{pdeg}(x):=|\{A:A\to B\in\Sigma,\,x\in A\}|
, and its
conclusion-degree
as
\mathrm{cdeg}(x):=|\{A\to B:A\to B\in\Sigma,\,x\in B\}|
.
Then
\deg(x)\leq\mathrm{pdeg}(x)+\mathrm{cdeg}(x)
.
Equality may not hold in general because an element can belong to both premise and the conclusion of an implication.
In this paper though, we avoid this degenerate case by always considering the premise and conclusion of an implication to be disjoint, which can be ensured in polynomial-time without loss of generality with respect to the closure system.
Moreover, we will mainly use the
degree
,
premise-degree
, and
conclusion-degree
of
\Sigma
defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{pdeg}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in X}\mathrm{pdeg}(x),

\displaystyle\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in X}\mathrm{cdeg}(x),

\displaystyle\deg(\Sigma)

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in X}\deg(x).

Note that
\deg(\Sigma)\leq\mathrm{pdeg}(\Sigma)+\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma)
for any
\Sigma
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
X=\{1,2,3,4,5\}
and consider the IB
(X,\Sigma)
given by:

\Sigma=\{4\to 1,25\to 3,12\to 3,15\to 23\}.

We have
\mathrm{pdeg}(2)=2
,
\mathrm{cdeg}(2)=1
,
\mathrm{pdeg}(\Sigma)=\mathrm{pdeg}(2)=\mathrm{pdeg}(1)=2
, and
\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma)=\mathrm{cdeg}(3)=3
.

We now relate IBs to closure systems.
Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an IB.
A set
C\subseteq X
is
closed
for
(X,\Sigma)
if for every implication
A\to B\in\Sigma
,
A\subseteq C
implies
B\subseteq C
.
The pair
(X,\mathcal{C})
where
\mathcal{C}
is the family of closed sets of
(X,\Sigma)
is a closure system.
An IB
(X,\Sigma)
thus induces a closure operator
\phi(\cdot)
that can be computed in polynomial time using the
forward chaining
algorithm.
This procedure, which will be also used in some of our proofs, starts from a set
Y
and produces a sequence of sets
Y=:Y_{0}\subseteq Y_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq Y_{k}:=\phi(Y)
such that
Y_{i}:=Y_{i-1}\cup\bigcup\{B:\text{$A\to B\in\Sigma$, $A\subseteq Y_{i-1}$, $B\nsubseteq Y_{i-1}$}\}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.
On the other hand, any closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
can be represented by several
equivalent
IBs.
An implication
A\to B
is
valid
or
holds
in
(X,\mathcal{C})
if for all
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
A\subseteq C
implies
B\subseteq C
.
Equivalently,
A\to B
holds in
(X,\mathcal{C})
if and only if
B\subseteq\phi(A)
.
For instance,
\Sigma=\{A\to\phi(A):A\subseteq X\}
constitutes an IB for
(X,\mathcal{C})
as well as
\Sigma^{\prime}=\{C\cup\{x\}\to\phi(C\cup\{x\}):C\in\mathcal{C},\,x\notin C\}
(recall that
\emptyset\in\mathcal{C}
by assumption).
Moreover an IB is always equivalent to its aggregation, and to its unit-expansion.
For example, the IB of Example
2.5
is an IB of the closure system of Figure
1
.

The fact that a closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
can be represented by several IBs has lead to the study of a broad range of different IBs and different measures on IBs, as witnessed by two surveys on the topic
[
Wil17
,
BDVG18
]
.
Among possible IBs, the canonical base
[
GD86
]
, the canonical direct base
[
BM10
,
Wil94
]
and the
D
-base
[
ANR13
]
have attracted much attention.
In this paper, we will be interested instead in some measures that allow to compare two equivalent IBs.
Namely, we say that an IB
(X,\Sigma)
, or simply
\Sigma
, is
minimum
if for any IB
(X,\Sigma^{\prime})
equivalent to
(X,\Sigma)
,
|\Sigma|\leq|\Sigma^{\prime}|
.
Similarly, if
(X,\Sigma)
is unit, we say that it is
unit-minimum
if it is cardinality-minimum among equivalent unit IBs.
If
(X,\Sigma)
is an arbitrary IB, then a minimum IB
(X,\Sigma^{\prime})
can be obtained in polynomial time, see e.g.,
[
Mai83
,
Sho86
,
GD86
,
Wil94
]
.
On the other hand, finding an equivalent unit-minimum IB is computationally hard
[
ADS86
]
.
Thus, in general, computing a unit-minimum IB is harder than computing a minimum one.

The
degree
of a closure system is the least integer
k
such that it admits an implicational bases of degree
k
. The premise- and conclusion-degree are defined analogously.

Translation task.

To conclude the preliminaries, we define the two enumeration problems we study in this paper.
These are the problems of translating between an IB and irreducible closed sets, two representations of a closure system.
Formally, the problems read as follows:

Irreducible Closed Sets Enumeration (
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
)

Input:

An implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
.

Output:

The irreducible closed sets of the associated closure system.

Unit-Minimum Implicational Base Generation (
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
)

Input:

A ground set
X
, the irreducible closed sets
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
of a closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
.

Output:

A unit-minimum implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.

We stress the fact that in both problems, only an implicit (and possibly compact) description of the closure system is provided.
Namely, for
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
, only
X
and
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
are part of the input, not
\mathcal{C}
, and a unit-minimum implicational base is to be computed.
Analogously for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
, only
(X,\Sigma)
is given, not
\mathcal{C}
.
Moreover, we note that in the literature, variants of
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
have been considered where the goal is to compute a minimum (not unit-minimum) IB.
However, and as discussed previously, our formulation is harder; see also
[
DNV21
]
.
It is also known that these problems are polynomially equivalent in general; see
[
Kha95
]
for more details.
Finally, we note that in both problems the output may be of exponential size with respect to the input size as suggested by Example
2.6
below.
Hence, these problems are studied through the lens of output-sensitive complexity.

Example 2.6
.

Let
X=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}\cup\{b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\}\cup\{x\}
, and
\Sigma_{1}=\{a_{i}b_{i}\to x:1\leq i\leq k\}
. Denoting

the associated closure system, we have
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C}_{1})=\{X\setminus\{y\}:y\in X,\ y\neq x\}\cup\{\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}:c_{i}\in\{a_{i},b_{i}\},\ 1\leq i\leq k\}
.
Therefore,
|\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C}_{1})|=2^{k}+2k
while

.

Dually, consider the closure system

with irreducible closed sets

.
Then, a unit-minimum IB
(X,\Sigma_{2})
of
(X,\mathcal{C}_{2})
is given by
\Sigma_{2}=\{c_{1}\dots c_{k}\to z:c_{i}\in\{a_{i},b_{i}\},\ 1\leq i\leq k\}
.
We have
|\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C}_{2})|=3k
while
|\Sigma_{2}|=2^{k}
.

3
Acyclic convex geometries

In this section we define and give the main properties of the class of convex geometries we study in this paper:
acyclic convex geometries
.
They have been studied under various names such as poset-type convex geometries
[
SW14
]
,
G
-geometries
[
Wil94
]
, or as acyclic Horn function from the perspective of Horn CNFs
[
HK95
,
BČKK09
]
.

To define acyclic convex geometries, we will use IBs, thus following the line of propositional logic
[
HK95
]
.
To do so, we need the concept of implication graph.
Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an IB.
For the time being,
(X,\mathcal{C})
needs not be a convex geometry.
The
implication graph

[
HK95
,
BČKK09
]
of
(X,\Sigma)
is the directed graph
G(\Sigma)=(X,E)
where there is an arc
ab
in
E
if there exists
A\to B
in
\Sigma
such that
a\in A
and
b\in B
.
The IB
(X,\Sigma)
is called
acyclic
if
G(\Sigma)
is.
In this case,
G(\Sigma)
can be seen as a poset, and naturally induces an
ancestor
relation over
X
defined for all
x\in X
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{anc}(x)

\displaystyle:=\{y:\text{$y\in X$, $x\neq y$, and there exists a directed path from $y$ to $x$ in $G(\Sigma)$}\}.

Equivalently,
\mathrm{anc}(x)
is the set of in-neighbors of
x
in the transitive closure of
G(\Sigma)
.
The descendant relation can be defined dually using out-neighbors.
An element
x
of
X
is thus maximal if it has no ancestors, and minimal if it has no descendants.
As an example, Figure
3
illustrates the implication graph of the IB given in Example
2.5
.

A closure system that admits an acyclic IB is
acyclic
and it is a well-known fact that acyclic closure systems are convex geometries
[
Wil94
,
SW14
]
.

Figure 3:
The implication graph
G(\Sigma)
of the IB
(X,\Sigma)
of Example
2.5
, associated to the closure system in Figure
1
.
This directed graph is acyclic, which makes the closure system an acyclic convex geometry.
The ancestors of
2
, highlighted in green, are
1,4,5
, i.e.,
\mathrm{anc}(2)=\{1,4,5\}
.
Maximal elements are
4
,
5
and the unique minimal element is
3
.
A
topological order
, as we will use later, would be
4,1,5,2,3
in this example.

At first sight, acyclicity seems to be a property of IBs more than a property of the underlying closure system, as when taking two distinct IBs of a closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
, one may be acyclic and not the other. See Example
3.1
.

Example 3.1
.

Let
X=\{1,2,3,4\}
and consider the two IBs
(X,\Sigma_{1}
),
(X,\Sigma_{2})
where:

•

\Sigma_{1}=\{1\to 4,12\to 3,23\to 4\}

•

\Sigma_{2}=\{1\to 4,124\to 3,23\to 4\}

The two implicational bases are equivalent, i.e., they define the same closure system.
However,
G(\Sigma_{1})
is acyclic, while
G(\Sigma_{2})
contains a cycle with arcs
34
and
43
.

Consequently, one might expect that acyclicity does not especially reflects in closed sets more than the fact that the closure system is a convex geometry.
This however is not true.
To see this, consider the implicational base of minimal generators
(X,\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
of a closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
, where
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}}=\{A\to b:b\in X,\,A\in\mathrm{mingen}(b)\}
, which has been studied under various names
[
BM10
]
.
The implication graph
G(\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
, where there is an arc from
a
to
b
if some
A\in\mathrm{mingen}(b)
contains
a
, turns out to be equal to the (inverse of the)
\delta
-relation of lattice theory
[
MC97
,
BM10
]
where
b\delta a
holds in
(X,\mathcal{C})
if there exists
M\in\mathrm{irr}(b)
such that
a\notin M
.
This equality is also a direct consequence of the fact that
\mathrm{irr}(b)
and
\mathrm{mingen}(b)
define dual hypergraphs, as explained in Section
2
.
As was shown by Hammer and Kogan
[
HK95
]
,
G(\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
, or equivalently
\delta
, characterizes the acyclicity of a closure system.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
HK95
, Lemma 5.2]
)
.

A closure system
(X,\mathcal{C})
is acyclic if and only if the implication graph
G(\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
of its implicational base of minimal generators
(X,\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
is acyclic.

Lemma
3.2
has interesting consequences.
First, given the correspondence between
G(\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
and the
\delta
-relation using irreducible closed sets, it shows that acyclicity indeed reflects in (irreducible) closed sets and can be tested in polynomial time given
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
.
Furthermore, it follows that
G(\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
, and hence its associated ancestor relation, can be recovered in polynomial time from
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
too.
To do so, one just has to check, for all
a,b\in X
, whether there exists
M\in\mathrm{irr}(b)
such that
a\notin M
.
As another consequence, any subset
\Sigma
of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}}
produces an acyclic IB.
This holds in particular for IBs
(X,\Sigma)
of
(X,\mathcal{C})
that satisfies
\Sigma\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}}
.
Since such an IB
(X,\Sigma)
can always be obtained from an arbitrary IB of
(X,\mathcal{C})
in polynomial time, checking whether
(X,\mathcal{C})
is indeed acyclic can be done in polynomial time given an arbitrary IB (see Corollary 5.3 in
[
HK95
]
).
Moreover, Boros et al.
[
BČK98
, Theorem 17, Corollary 18]
show that any IB
(X,\Sigma)
of
(X,\mathcal{C})
that satisfies
\Sigma\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}}
enjoys the same ancestor relation as
(X,\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
.
In other words,
G(\Sigma)
and
G(\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}})
have the same transitive closure.
We thus directly obtain a statement that will be used in a few paragraphs.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be an acyclic convex geometry and let
(X,\Sigma)
be an implicational base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
such that
\Sigma\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathrm{mingen}}
.
Then, the ancestor relation associated to
\Sigma
can be computed in polynomial time when given
X
and
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
.

Among the minimal generators of
(X,\mathcal{C})
, we now focus on critical generators, for they play a crucial role in acyclic convex geometries.
If
(X,\mathcal{C})
is an acyclic convex geometry, its

is defined as follows:

\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}:=\{A\to b:\text{$b\in X$ and $A$ is a critical generator of $b$}\}.

As a preliminary remark,

must be acyclic by Lemma

, since critical generators are minimal generators.
A nice result of Hammer and Kogan
[

]

shows that not only

but also that it is unit-minimum and can be obtained in polynomial time from any other IB of

More precisely, they show:

As a consequence, the critical base of an acyclic convex geometry constitutes a valid output to the

Critical Base Generation (

Given that

Corollary 3.5

The ancestor relation of the critical base of an acyclic convex geometry

In the rest of the paper, we will call

As a last property of critical generators, we argue that on top of being unit-minimum, the critical base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
is also minimum in terms of the different measures we introduced in Section

,

To do so, we will rely on a result stating that in a convex geometry

.
This is a consequence of
[

.
It is also directly stated in
[

, as well as in

, Proposition 4.5]
but restricted to acyclic convex geometries.
In view of our claim on degrees though, we need to prove a slightly stronger property stating that these premise and critical generator share the same closure.
We give the statement below and we include for self-containment a proof which differs from the ones presented in

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be a convex geometry and let
b\in X
.
For any implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
of
(X,\mathcal{C})
and any critical generator

D\to B
in

,

and
\phi(A)=\phi(D)

Proof.

Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an IB of
(X,\mathcal{C})
, let
b\in X
and
A
a critical generator of

.
Let
A=A_{0}\subseteq A_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq A_{k}=\phi(A)
be the sequence of sets obtained by applying the forward chaining on

(X,\Sigma)
.
Since
A

but

.
Therefore, there exists some

but

.
Hence, there exists an implication
D\to B\in\Sigma
such that
b\in B
and
D\subseteq A_{i-1}
.
Since
D\subseteq A_{i-1}
and
\phi(A_{i-1})=\phi(A)
, we deduce
\phi(D)\subseteq\phi(A)
.
Now, since
A
is a critical generator of
b
, we have that
\phi(A)\in\min_{\subseteq}\{C:C\in\mathcal{C},\,b\in C,\,b\notin\mathrm{ex}(C)\}
by Proposition
2.3
.
On the other hand, we have
b\in\phi(D)
but
b\notin\mathrm{ex}(\phi(D))
.
We deduce from the minimality of
\phi(A)
that
\phi(A)=\phi(D)
.
As
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry, the unique minimal spanning set of
\phi(D)
is
\mathrm{ex}(\phi(D))=\mathrm{ex}(\phi(A))=A
by Propositions
2.1
and
2.3
.
Hence,
A\subseteq D
holds.

Consequently,
D\to B
is an implication of
\Sigma
such that
A\subseteq D
,
\phi(D)=\phi(A)
and
b\in B
as desired.
This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now proceed to show that the critical base, once aggregated, minimizes all the three measures about degrees.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{C})
be an acyclic convex geometry with aggregated critical base
(X,\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*})
.
For any
\mu\in\{\mathrm{cdeg},\mathrm{pdeg},\deg\}
, we have:

\mu(\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*})=\min\{\mu(\Sigma):\text{$(X,\Sigma)$ is an implicational base of $(X,\mathcal{C})$}\}.

Proof.

We show the statement for each of the three measures.
We start with the equality regarding
\mathrm{cdeg}
, the conclusion-degree.
Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an arbitrary IB of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
We show that
\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*})\leq\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma)
.
By definition of
\mathrm{cdeg}
, it is sufficient to argue that for each
b\in X
,
\mathrm{cdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(b)\leq\mathrm{cdeg}_{\Sigma}(b)
.
Let
b\in X
and let
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
be the critical generators of
b
.
Then
\mathrm{cdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(b)=k
.
By Lemma
3.6
, for each
1\leq i\leq k
there exists an implication
D_{i}\to B_{i}
in
\Sigma
such that
b\in B_{i}
and
\phi(A_{i})=\phi(D_{i})
.
By Propositions
2.1
and
2.3
, each
A_{i}
is the unique minimal spanning set of
\phi(A_{i})
.
Hence,
\phi(A_{i})\neq\phi(A_{j})
for every
1\leq i,j\leq k
such that
i\neq j
.
Since for each
1\leq i\leq k
,
\phi(D_{i})=\phi(A_{i})
, we deduce
\phi(D_{i})\neq\phi(D_{j})
for each
j\neq i
.
Consequently, all implications
D_{1}\to B_{1},\dots,D_{i}\to B_{i}
must be distinct, all the while satisfying
b\in B_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
.
We deduce
k=\mathrm{cdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(b)\leq\mathrm{cdeg}_{\Sigma}(b)
.

We proceed to analyze
\mathrm{pdeg}
, the premise-degree.
Again, we need only to prove that for each
a\in X
,
\mathrm{pdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(a)\leq\mathrm{pdeg}_{\Sigma}(a)
.
Let
a\in X
and let
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
be the (distinct) critical generators to which
a
belongs.
We obtain
\mathrm{pdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(a)=k
because
\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}
is aggregated.
Applying Lemma
3.6
once more, there exists
D_{1}\to B_{1},\dots,D_{k}\to B_{k}
in
\Sigma
such that, for each
1\leq i\leq k
,
a\in A_{i}\subseteq D_{i}
and
\phi(A_{i})=\phi(D_{i})
.
Similarly to
\mathrm{cdeg}
, we obtain
\phi(D_{i})\neq\phi(D_{j})
for all
1\leq i,j\leq k
such that
i\neq j
.
Thus, all implications
D_{1}\to B_{1},\dots,D_{k}\to B_{k}
are distinct, with
a\in D_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
.
We deduce
k=\mathrm{pdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(a)\leq\mathrm{pdeg}_{\Sigma}(a)
for all
a\in X
, and
\mathrm{pdeg}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*})\leq\mathrm{pdeg}(\Sigma)
follows.

As for the degree
\deg
, we have
\deg_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(x)=\mathrm{cdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(x)+\mathrm{pdeg}_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(x)
for each
x\in X
as
x
cannot belong to one of its critical generators by definition.
According to the previous arguments, we thus have
\deg_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*}}(x)\leq\deg_{\Sigma}(x)
for each
x
.
So
\deg(\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*})\leq\deg(\Sigma)
also holds, which concludes the proof.
∎

Moreover, observe that
\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}})=\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{*})
so the critical base, which is not aggregated, is enough for the conclusion degree.
We directly deduce the following for acyclic convex geometries.

Corollary 3.8
.

An acyclic convex geometry has bounded (premise- or conclusion-) degree if and only if its aggregated critical base has bounded (premise- or conclusion-) degree.

To conclude this section, we give further insights on the translation tasks within acyclic convex geometries.
First, thanks to Theorem
3.4
, one can always assume to be given the critical base as an input to
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
in this context.
More importantly, the critical base is, by Theorem
3.4
, a correct solution to
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
, a fact that we will rely upon in Section
7
.

It is shown in
[

, Theorem 3.1]
that in acyclic convex geometries, all three problems
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
,
ACS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
are harder than distributive lattice dualization, a generalization of
MIS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
MHS
{\cdot}
Enum
.
Here, we briefly discuss a simpler reduction that we will later adapt to obtain further hardness results for acyclic IBs with bounded degrees.
Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,\mathcal{E})
be a Sperner hypergraph as an input to

{\cdot}
Enum
.
Let

where

.
The IB

is our input to

{\cdot}

.

may be seen as a poset of height

We proceed to describe the irreducible closed sets of

, the closure system associated to

.
First, each element

is attached to a unique irreducible being

, as

is the conclusion of no implications.
As for

.
Therefore,
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{MIS}(\mathcal{H})\cup\{X\setminus\{v\}:v\in V\}
.
Given that
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
has only a linear number of irreducible closed sets not in

, which can be identified in polynomial time, an algorithm for

{\cdot}

solves
MIS
{\cdot}
Enum
with the same time complexity.
Similarly, an algorithm solving

{\cdot}

given

{\cdot}

.

\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})

.

We conclude these observations with the following.

.

In acyclic convex geometries, the problems

{\cdot}

,

and

are harder than

.

Given

.

.

, we have

.

Theorem 3.10
.

The problem

{\cdot}

is

{\cdot}

-hard even for acyclic implicational bases of conclusion-degree at most 2.

.

4
Top-down procedures

We describe two sequential procedures
to solve
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
in acyclic convex geometries.
Intuitively, the first procedure consists in enumerating, incrementally and in a top-down fashion according to a topological ordering
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of the elements of the implicational base, all the irreducible closed sets attached to
x_{i}
.
The second one proceeds analogously and lists all critical generators of
x_{i}
at each step.

4.1
Irreducible closed sets

Let us focus on irreducible closed sets first.
Recall that the ancestors of an element
x
in an acyclic implicational base are the elements that participate in a path of implications to
x
, and which can be identified in polynomial time; see Section
3
for a formal definition.
We will be interested in solving the following version of
ACS
{\cdot}
Enum
in which we are given ancestors’ irreducible closed sets as additional information.

Attached Closed Sets Enumeration with Ancestors’ solutions (
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
)

Input:

An acyclic implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
, an element
{x\in X}
, and the family
\mathrm{irr}(y)
for every ancestor
y
of
x
in
\Sigma
.

Output:

The set
\mathrm{irr}(x)
.

Clearly, an algorithm for
ACS
{\cdot}
Enum
can be used to solve
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
, while the reverse may not be true.
We motivate this latter problem by showing that we have an incremental-polynomial time algorithm for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
whenever we have one for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
.
Intuitively, our procedure will iteratively use an algorithm for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
on the elements of the ground set, in order, according to an arbitrary topological ordering of the implicational base.
Note that the base case corresponds to computing the irreducible closed set of the maximal elements
x
, i.e., those verifying
\mathrm{irr}(x)=\{X\setminus\{x\}\}
.
In terms of difficulty,
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
may thus be seen as standing as an intermediate problem between
ACS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
in acyclic convex geometries.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
be a function.
Let us assume that there is an algorithm that, given an instance of
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
of size
N
, produces its
i^{\text{th}}
solution in
{f(N,i)}
-time.
Then there is an algorithm that, given an acyclic instance of
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
of size
N^{\prime}
, produces its
j^{\text{th}}
solution in
O(\mathrm{poly}(N^{\prime})+N^{\prime}\cdot f(N^{\prime}+j,j))
time.

Proof.

Let us assume the existence of an algorithm
\mathsf{A}
for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
as required by the statement.
Without loss of generality and for convenience in the rest of the proof, let us assume that
f
is increasing.
We describe a sequential procedure
\mathsf{B}
which produces the
j^{\text{th}}
solution of
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
within the desired time bound.

Let
{(X,\Sigma)}
be an acyclic instance of
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
.
Recall that, for convenience, we view the ancestor relation induced by
\Sigma
as a partial order on
X
, and denote sources of
G(\Sigma)
as maximal elements.
First, we identify each maximal element
a
of
X
, compute
\mathrm{irr}(a)=\{X\setminus\{a\}\}
in
\mathrm{poly}(|X|,|\Sigma|)
time, and mark these
a
as handled.
Every such set is output and stored in memory.
Then, iteratively, given a maximal element
x\in X
among those that are not marked, we identify its ancestors in
\mathrm{poly}(|X|,|\Sigma|)
time, output and store
\mathrm{irr}(x)
using
\mathsf{A}
, and mark
x
.
The procedure stops when every element of
X
has been marked.
In other words, in this procedure, the elements
x
are selected with respect to a topological ordering of
G(\Sigma)
.

Note that we can indeed use
\mathsf{A}
in the last step of our procedure since the sets
\mathrm{irr}(a)
for all ancestors
a
of
x
have been inductively computed by the choice of
x
.
Furthermore, as by Proposition
2.1
the sets
\mathrm{irr}(a)
and
\mathrm{irr}(b)
are disjoint for any two distinct
a,b\in X
, there is no repetition in the outputs of this procedure: each set output by
\mathsf{A}
is a distinct new solution of
\mathsf{B}
.

We now turn to the complexity analysis.
Observe that, except for the executions of
\mathsf{A}
, the different steps of
\mathsf{B}
take
\mathrm{poly}(N^{\prime})
time for
N^{\prime}=|X|+|\Sigma|
.
Let us now focus on the time spent by
\mathsf{B}
in order to output the
j^{\text{th}}
solution in case it is not the irreducible closed set of a maximal element, i.e., when it has been provided by an instance of
\mathsf{A}
.
By assumption, this instance produces the
i^{\text{th}}
irreducible closed set attached to
x
, each time being a new solution of
\mathsf{B}
, in a time which is bounded by
f(N,i)
, for
N
being the sum of the sizes of
X
,
\Sigma
and
\bigcup_{a\in\mathrm{anc}(x)}\mathrm{irr}(a)
.
Note that the latter family has already been output by
\mathsf{B}
at this stage and can be recovered in
\mathrm{poly}(j)
time from the memory.
Thus, and since
f
is increasing, this is bounded by
f(|X|+|\Sigma|+j,j)
for
j
being the total number of solutions output by
\mathsf{B}
so far.
And finally,
\mathsf{A}
will be call only once for each
x
of the ground set.
We conclude to the desired time bound by replacing
|X|+|\Sigma|
by
N^{\prime}
.
∎

We note that an analogous statement to the one in Theorem
4.1
holds by relaxing the required and obtained time bounds to output-polynomial times.
On the other hand, improving the complexity of an algorithm for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
to polynomial delay (or even input-polynomial as we will provide later for the case of bounded conclusion-degree) does not generally ensure polynomial delay (or incremental-linear) for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
, since the size of an instance of
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
grows with the number of already obtained solutions for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
, which typically becomes exponential in the size of the implicational base.

4.2
Critical generators

We now turn on enumerating the critical generators of an acyclic convex geometry.
Analogously as for irreducible closed sets, we proceed in a top-down fashion according to a topological ordering of the ground set, this time related to the critical base we wish to compute.
Only it should be argued that such an ordering can be efficiently computed from the irreducible closed sets, i.e., without the knowledge of the critical base and its implication graph, a fact that follows from Corollary
3.5
.

More formally, we are interested in solving the following enumeration problem, which is an analogue of
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
to critical generators.

Critical Generators of an Element with Ancestors’ solutions (
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Gen
)

Input:

A ground set
X
, the irreducible closed sets
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
of an acyclic convex geometry
(X,\mathcal{C})
,
x\in X
, and the set
\mathrm{critgen}(y)
for every
y\in\mathrm{critanc}(x)
.

Output:

The family
\mathrm{critgen}(x)
.

We stress the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is not given in this problem; only
X
and
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
are given as a representation.
Note that
\mathrm{critgen}(x)
is empty for maximal elements of the
\mathrm{critanc}
relation, which corresponds to the base case of our procedure.
Moreover, sets lying in
\mathrm{critgen}(y)
and
\mathrm{critgen}(y^{\prime})
for distinct elements
y,y^{\prime}\in X
yield distinct implications of the (unit) critical base of
\mathcal{C}
.
Then, following a direct analogue of the proof of Theorem
4.1

we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
be a function.
Let us assume that there is an algorithm that, given any instance of
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Gen
of size
N
, produces its
i^{\text{th}}
solution in
{f(N,i)}
-time.
Then there is an algorithm that, given any acyclic instance of
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
of size
N^{\prime}
, produces its
j^{\text{th}}
solution in
O(\mathrm{poly}(N^{\prime})+N^{\prime}\cdot f(N^{\prime}+j\cdot|X|,j))
time.

4.3
Limitations

Let us end this section by pointing some limitation to the top-down approach.
Note that for implicational bases (or critical base) of height at most two, the inputs of
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
and
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Gen
do not contain any valuable additional information compared to the inputs of
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
.
Indeed, as mentioned earlier, for such bases, each maximal element
a
has a unique, polynomially characterized attached irreducible closed set
X\setminus\{a\}
, due to the fact that
a
has no minimal generators, and in particular no critical generators.
Nevertheless, these instances remain
MIS
{\cdot}
Enum
-hard by Theorem
3.9
.
Thus, in order to expect tractable algorithms using this strategy, one should most probably restrict the closure system to particular cases or bound structural parameters.

5
Bounded conclusion-degree

We show that the top-down approach successfully provides an incremental-polynomial time algorithm for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
whenever the input implicational base has bounded conclusion-degree, proving one of the two cases of Theorem
1.1
.

In the following, let us consider an instance of
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
, i.e., we fix some acyclic implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
, an element
x\in X
, and assume that we are given
\mathrm{irr}(y)
for all ancestors
y
of
x
in
\Sigma
.
Let us consider the hypergraph
of premises of
x
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{x}

\displaystyle:=\{A:A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma,\ x\in B\},\ \text{and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{x}

\displaystyle:=\left(\bigcup\mathcal{E}_{x},\mathcal{E}_{x}\right)\!.

The intuition behind the following lemma is that, in order to get an irreducible closed set attached to
x
, one should remove at least one element
a
in the premise of each implication having
x
in the conclusion, which can be done by considering the intersection with an irreducible closed set attached to
a
.
Recall that
\mathcal{S}(T)
denotes the family of irreducible selections; see Section
2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
{M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)}
there exists a minimal transversal
T
of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
and an irreducible selection
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
such that

M=\left(\bigcap\mathcal{S}\right)\setminus\{x\}.

Proof.

Let
Y:=\bigcup\mathcal{E}_{x}\setminus M
.
Note that for each
A\in\mathcal{E}_{x}
there exists
a\in A
such that
a\in Y
, as otherwise
A\subseteq M
which contradicts the fact that
M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)
as
A
implies
x
.
Thus,
Y
is a transversal of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
.
Let
T\subseteq Y
be any minimal transversal of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
included in
Y
.
Note that for all
a\in T
, as
M
is a closed set and
a\notin M
, there exists a set
M_{a}\in\mathrm{irr}(a)
such that
M\subseteq M_{a}
.
This defines an irreducible selection
\mathcal{S}=(M_{a})_{a\in T}
for
T
, where each such
M_{a}
includes
M
.
Therefore, and as
x\notin M
, we have

M\subseteq(\bigcap\mathcal{S})\setminus\{x\}
.

Now, as every member of
\mathcal{S}
is closed, so is
\bigcap\mathcal{S}
.
Moreover, by definition of
\mathcal{S}
, no premise
A
of
\mathcal{E}_{x}
is contained in such an intersection.
Thus the set
M^{\prime}:=(\bigcap\mathcal{S})\setminus\{x\}
is closed.
By the maximality of
M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)
, and since
M\subseteq M^{\prime}
, we conclude that
M^{\prime}=M
, hence to the desired equality.
∎

Note that, by definition, the number of edges in
\mathcal{H}_{x}
is exactly
\mathrm{cdeg}(x)
, which is at most
\mathrm{cdeg}(\Sigma)
.
Moreover, since the ground set of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
is a subset of the ancestors of
x
, the selections of Lemma
5.1
are part of the input we consider for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
.
We use these observations to derive a polynomial-time algorithm for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
whenever the conclusion-degree of
x
is bounded by a constant.

Theorem 5.2
.

There is an algorithm solving
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
in
N^{O(k)}
time on inputs of size
N
and maximum conclusion-degree
k
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{E}_{x}
has cardinality at most
k
, all possible minimal transversals
T
of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
can be computed in
|X|^{O(k)}
time by bruteforcing all subsets of size at most
k
, and testing in
\mathrm{poly}(|X|+|\mathcal{H}_{x}|)
time, hence
\mathrm{poly}(N)
time, whether the obtained set is a minimal transversal of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
.
Moreover, all possible irreducible selections
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
for each such
T
can be computed in
(\max_{a\in T}|\mathrm{irr}(a)|)^{O(k)}
, hence in
N^{O(k)}
time, since every
a\in T\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{E}_{x}
is an ancestor of
x
, thus the families
\mathrm{irr}(a)
are part of the input.
As the intersections of Lemma
5.1
can be computed in
\mathrm{poly}(N)
time, and as it can be checked in
\mathrm{poly}(N)
time if they are closed sets attached to
x
, we conclude that all
M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)
can be identified in
N^{O(k)}
time, as desired.
∎

We conclude to the following, as a corollary of Theorems
4.1
and
5.2
, and proving the case of bounded-conclusion degree in Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

There is an incremental-polynomial time algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
in acyclic implicational bases of bounded conclusion-degree.

Let us remark that in our algorithm, we actually do not need the knowledge of
\mathrm{irr}(a)
for
all
ancestors of
x
, but only for those in a premise which has
x
in conclusion.
This however makes no asymptotic difference on the final complexity we get for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
.

Also, note that in the proof of Theorem
5.2
we only need a constant upper bound on the size
k
of a minimal transversal of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
, to derive an input-polynomial time algorithm solving
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
.
We immediately derive the following generalization of Theorem
5.3
dealing with hypergraphs of bounded dual-dimension (i.e., admitting minimal transversals of bounded size) who define complement of conformal hypergraphs
[
Ber84
]
, which typically arise in geometric settings.

Theorem 5.4
.

There is an incremental-polynomial time algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
if, for any element
x
of the ground set, the hypergraph of premises having
x
in their conclusion has bounded dual-dimension.

We end the section by showing the limitations of this approach in order to get output-polynomial time bounds whenever we drop the condition of bounded conclusion-degree.

First, note that the key step of the approach is to guess the correct
T\in\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{x})
and
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
as in the statement of Lemma
5.1
, which we do by testing all possibilities in Theorem
5.2
.
However, there are cases where the number of such possibilities is exponential in
|\mathrm{irr}(\Sigma)|
, which typically occurs if minimal transversals of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
are of unbounded size, or if
\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{x})
is of exponential size in
\mathrm{irr}(x)
.

To get one such example, consider
k\in\mathbb{N}
and the implicational base defined by
X:=\{a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{k},b_{k},x\}
and
\Sigma:=\{a_{i}b_{i}\to x:1\leq i\leq k\}\cup\{b_{i}\to b_{i+1}:1\leq i<k\}
; see Figure
6
for an illustration.
Then
\mathrm{irr}(x)=\{\{a_{1},\dots,a_{j}\}\cup\{b_{j+1},\dots,b_{k}\}:0\leq j\leq k\}
so
|\mathrm{irr}(x)|=k+1
, and the other elements in
X\setminus\{x\}
are easily seen to have a single attached irreducible closed set. Therefore,
\mathrm{irr}(\Sigma)
is of polynomial size in
|X|=2k+1
and
|\Sigma|=2k-1
.
However,
|\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{x})|=2^{k}
and computing this set would fail in providing an output-polynomial time algorithm for enumerating
\mathrm{irr}(x)
.

Figure 6:
An instance where
|\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{x})|=2^{k}
but
|\mathrm{irr}(\Sigma)|=3k+1
and
|\Sigma|=2k-1
.

At last, we note that this example has bounded premise-degree.
We will show in the next section that such instances can actually be efficiently solved by solution graph traversal.

6
Bounded premise-degree

We provide an incremental-polynomial time algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
whenever the given implicational base has bounded premise-degree.
Our algorithm relies on the top-down approach from Section
4
and solves
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
relying on the solution graph traversal technique.

In the
solution graph traversal
framework, one has to define a digraph called
solution graph
, whose nodes are the solutions we aim to enumerate, and whose arcs
(F,F^{\prime})
consists of ways of reconfigurating a given solution
F
into another solution
F^{\prime}
.
Then, the goal is not to build the solution graph entirely, but rather to traverse all its nodes efficiently and avoiding repetitions of nodes.
This general technique has been fruitfully applied to numerous enumeration problems, see e.g.,
[
BEG04
,
KBEG08
,
YLLY22
]
, and generalized to powerful frameworks
[
AF96
,
CKS08
,
CGM+22
]
.
In the following we will use the next folklore result which states conditions for such a technique to be efficient; see
[
Elb15
]
for a quantitative statement and a proof.

Theorem 6.1

(Folklore)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
be a solution set (to be enumerated) over a ground set
X
, and let us denote by
N
the size of the input.
Then there is a
\mathrm{poly}(N)
-delay algorithm enumerating
\mathcal{F}
whenever:

1.

one member of
\mathcal{F}
can be found in
\mathrm{poly}(N)
-time

2.

there is a function
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{F})
called
neighboring function
mapping every solution to a set of solutions that can be computed in
\mathrm{poly}(N)
time given
F\in\mathcal{F}

3.

the directed solution graph
(\mathcal{F},\{(F,F^{\prime}):F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(F)\})
is strongly connected

In the following, let us consider an instance of
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
, i.e., we fix some acyclic implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
, an element
x\in X
, and we assume that we are given
\mathrm{irr}(y)
for all ancestors
y
of
x
.

We first argue that Condition
1
holds for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
.
Note that there is a function
\mathrm{{GC}}_{x}
which maps any
Y\subseteq X
such that
x\notin\phi(Y)
to a maximal set
M\supseteq Y
with this property.
We call the obtained set the
greedy completion
of
Y
.
Note that
\mathrm{{GC}}_{x}(Y)
can be computed in polynomial time in the size of
(X,\Sigma)
, and that the obtained set lies in
\mathrm{irr}(x)
.
We derive the following.

Lemma 6.2
.

A first solution
\mathrm{{GC}}_{x}(\emptyset)\in\mathrm{irr}(x)
can be obtained in
\mathrm{poly}(|X|+|\Sigma|)
time.

We now turn on showing the existence of a polynomial-time computable neighboring function satisfying Conditions
2
and
3
.
Given some irreducible closed set
M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)
, and some element
y\in X\setminus M

we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{M,y}

\displaystyle:=\{A:A\rightarrow B\in\Sigma\ \text{with}\ A\setminus M=\{y\},\ B\not\subseteq M\},

\displaystyle V_{M,y}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup\mathcal{E}_{M,y},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{M,y}

\displaystyle:=(V_{M,y},\mathcal{E}_{M,y}).

In other words,
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
is the hypergraph of premises of
\Sigma
not included in
M
, triggered by adding
y
, and with a conclusion not in
M
.
Note that
|\mathcal{E}_{M,y}|\leq\mathrm{pdeg}(y)\leq\mathrm{pdeg}(\Sigma)
.
Also, by the maximality of
M
, triggering any such implication results into implying
x
.
We derive the following observation.

Observation 6.3
.

Every vertex of
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
is an ancestor of
x
.

Regarding Condition
2
, we first need the following statement, which holds the same idea as Lemma
5.1
for bounded premise-degree.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
M,M^{*}
be two distinct sets in
\mathrm{irr}(x)
.
Then there exists
y\in M^{*}\setminus M
,
T\in\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{M,y})
, and
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
such that

M^{*}\subseteq\bigcap\mathcal{S}.

Proof.

Let
y
be an element of
M^{*}\setminus M
which is minimal, with respect to the ancestor order, for this property.
Let
A\in\mathcal{E}_{M,y}
and
A\to B\in\Sigma
such that
B\not\subseteq M
. As
y\in A
, every
b\in B
is a descendant of
y
so
b\notin M^{*}
or
b\in M
by minimality of
y
.
Moreover,
B\not\subseteq M
by definition of
\mathcal{E}_{M,y}
so there is a
b\in B
such that
b\notin M^{*}
.

Therefore, for all
A\in\mathcal{E}_{M,y}
,
A\not\subseteq M^{*}
(
M^{*}
is a closed set). So
V_{M,y}\cap M^{*}
is an independent set of
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
thus its complement is a transversal.
Let
T
be any minimal transversal of
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
included in
V_{M,y}\setminus M^{*}
.
Then, for all
a\in T
,
a\notin M^{*}
so there exists
M_{a}\in\mathrm{irr}(a)
such that
M^{*}\subseteq M_{a}
. For the irreducible selection
\mathcal{S}=(M_{a})_{a\in T}
, one gets
M^{*}\subseteq\bigcap\mathcal{S}
.
∎

We are now ready to define the aforementioned neighboring function.
Given
M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)
and
y\in X\setminus M
such that
x\not\in\phi(y)
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(M,y)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathrm{{GC}}_{x}\left(\left(M\cap\bigcap\mathcal{S}\right)\cup\{y\}\right):\,\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T),\ T\in\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{M,y})\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(M)

\displaystyle:=\displaystyle\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\not\in M\\
x\notin\phi(y)\end{subarray}}\mathcal{N}(M,y)

and prove that Conditions
2
and
3
hold for the latter function.

Observation 6.5
.

By definition of
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
and of a selection,
x\notin\phi\left(\left(M\cap\bigcap\mathcal{S}\right)\cup\{y\}\right)
for all
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
and
T\in\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{M,y})
. Therefore, the greedy completion in the definition of
\mathcal{N}(M,y)
is well defined and
\mathcal{N}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{irr}(x)
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
N
be the sum of the sizes of
X
,
\Sigma
, and
\mathrm{irr}(a)
for all ancestors
a
of
x
.
Then, for all
M\in\mathrm{irr}(x)
,
\mathcal{N}(M)
can be computed in
N^{O(k)}
time where
k
is the premise-degree of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let us first note that
\mathrm{{GC}}_{x}\left(\left(M\cap\bigcap\mathcal{S}\right)\cup\{y\}\right)
can be computed in
\mathrm{poly}(|X|+|\Sigma|+|\bigcap\mathcal{S}|)
time, for any
y
and
\mathcal{S}
.
Note that
\mathcal{E}_{M,y}
has cardinality at most
k
, hence that all possible minimal transversals
T
of
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
can be computed in
|X|^{O(k)}
time by bruteforcing all subsets of size at most
k
, and testing whether the obtained set is a minimal transversal of
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
in
\mathrm{poly}(|X|+|\mathcal{H}_{M,y}|)
time, hence in
\mathrm{poly}(|X|+|\Sigma|)
time.
Moreover, all possible irreducible selections
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
for each such
T
can be computed in
(\max_{a\in T}|\mathrm{irr}(a)|)^{O(k)}
time, hence in
N^{O(k)}
time as families
\mathrm{irr}(a)
are part of the input.
Finally, the last union over
y
multiplies the total time by a factor at most
|X|\leq N
, hence the result.
∎

Note that Condition
2
is ensured by Observation
6.5
and Lemma
6.6
.
We are thus left to prove Condition
3
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
M,M^{*}
be two distinct sets in
\mathrm{irr}(x)
.
Then there exists
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(M)
such that
M\cap M^{*}\subset M^{\prime}\cap M^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
y\in M^{*}\setminus M
,
T\in\mathrm{MHS}(\mathcal{H}_{M,y})
and
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{S}(T)
such that
M^{*}\subseteq\bigcap\mathcal{S}
, as provided in Lemma
6.4
.
Note that
T
may intersect
M\setminus M^{*}
, but not
M^{*}
.
Then

\displaystyle M\cap M^{*}

\displaystyle\subseteq M\cap\bigcap\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\subseteq\left(M\cap\bigcap\mathcal{S}\right)\cup\{y\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathrm{{GC}}_{x}\left(\left(M\cap\bigcap\mathcal{S}\right)\cup\{y\}\right)\in\mathcal{N}(M).

So, by setting
M^{\prime}:=\mathrm{{GC}}_{x}\left(\left(M\cap\bigcap\mathcal{S}\right)\cup\{y\}\right)
and intersecting with
M^{*}
, one gets
M\cap M^{*}\subseteq M^{\prime}\cap M^{*}
.

Finally, let us recall that
y\notin M
by definition but
y\in M^{\prime}\cap M^{*}
by construction and definition. Hence the inclusion is strict.
∎

We derive the following, namely Condition
3
, which is easily proved by induction since
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(M)
has greater intersection with
M^{*}
than
M
has with
M^{*}
.

Corollary 6.8
.

The solution digraph
(\mathrm{irr}(x),\{(M,M^{\prime}):M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(M)\})
is strongly connected.

Combining Theorem
6.1
, Lemma
6.2
,
Lemma
6.6
, and Corollary
6.8
, we conclude to a polynomial-delay algorithm for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
by solution graph traversal for acyclic implicational bases of bounded premise-degree.
This, together with Theorem
4.1
, in turn implies the following theorem, proving the remaining case of bounded premise-degree in Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 6.9
.

There is an incremental-polynomial time algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
on acyclic implicational bases of bounded premise-degree.

As in the previous section, notice that we only needed to bound the size of a minimal transversal of
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
to derive an input-polynomial time algorithm solving
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
.
This gives the following result, analogous to Theorem
5.4
.

Theorem 6.10
.

There is an incremental-polynomial time algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
if, for any irreducible closed set
M
and
y\notin M
, the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{M,y}
has bounded dual-dimension.

7
Generating the critical implicational base

We provide an algorithm which constructs the critical base of an acyclic convex geometry given by its irreducible closed sets, namely solving
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
.
Our algorithm relies on the top-down approach of Section
4.2
, and solves
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Gen
by a saturation procedure which either finds a missing critical generator, or conclude that all critical generators have been found.
It performs in polynomial time whenever the aggregated critical base of the closure system has bounded premise- or conclusion-degree, relying on our algorithms from Sections
5
and
6
, proving Theorem
1.2
.

In the
saturation technique
, the goal is to decide, starting from the empty set as an initial partial solution set, whether a solution is missing, and to produce one such missing solution if it is the case.
This analogue functional problem is usually referred to as the
decision version with counter example
of the enumeration problem.
It is folklore and easily seen that there is an incremental-polynomial time algorithm for an enumeration problem whenever its decision version with counterexample can be solved in polynomial time; see e.g.,
[
Str19
]
for more details and
[
FK96
,
BEGK03
,
MS19
]
for typical applications of this technique.

More formally, in the remaining of the section, we are interested in solving the following decision version with counterexample of
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Gen
.
Let us recall that
\mathrm{critanc}
, the ancestor relation associated to the critical base, can be computed in polynomial time from the irreducible closed sets by Corollary
3.5
.

Critical Generators of an Element with Ancestors’ solutions,

Decision version with counterexample (
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Dec
)

Input:

A ground set
X
, the irreducible closed sets
\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
of an acyclic convex geometry
(X,\mathcal{C})
, an element
x\in X
, the set
\mathrm{critgen}(y)
for every
y\in\mathrm{critanc}(x)
, and a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathrm{critgen}(x)
.

Output:

Yes if
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{critgen}(x)
, a set
A\in\mathrm{critgen}(x)\setminus\mathcal{F}
otherwise.

In the following, let us consider an instance of this problem.
Let us put

\Sigma^{\prime}:=\left\{A\to x:A\in\mathcal{F}\right\}\cup\left\{A\to y:A\in\mathrm{critgen}(y),\ y\in\mathrm{critanc}(x)\right\}.

Note that
\Sigma^{\prime}
defines a subset of the critical base of
(X,\mathcal{C})
.
We will note
(X,\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
the closure system represented by
\Sigma^{\prime}
, and
\phi^{\prime}
its associated closure operator.
Since we are now dealing with two distinct closure systems, we shall write
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
and
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)
instead of
\mathrm{irr}(x)
to distinguish between the two closure systems, and
we do analogously for
\mathrm{critgen}
.

As
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}
, one can notice that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. This little fact will be a key element in the following proofs.

To solve
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Dec
, our strategy is to find a set
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)\setminus\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
using an algorithm for
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
.
If such a
M
exists, we argue that it provides a counterexample
A\in\mathrm{critgen}(x)\setminus\mathcal{F}
(Lemma
7.1
alongside Proposition
2.4
).
If not, we prove that
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{critgen}(x)
(Lemma
7.2
).

Lemma 7.1
.

If there exists
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)
such that
M\not\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
, then there exists
A\in\mathrm{critgen}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
such that
A\subseteq M
and
A\not\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

First, we argue that
M\notin\mathcal{C}
and
x\in\phi(M)
.
Assume for contradiction that
M\in\mathcal{C}
.
Because
x\notin M
and
M\notin\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
by assumption, there exists
y\neq x
such that
M\cup\{y\}\in\mathcal{C}
, as
(X,\mathcal{C})
is a convex geometry.
However, as
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}
, we have
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.
Therefore,
M\cup\{y\}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
holds, which contradicts
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)
.
We deduce
M\notin\mathcal{C}
.
Again as
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
,
\phi(M)\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
holds.
From
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)
and
M\subset\phi(M)
, we derive
x\in\phi(M)
as required.

Since
x\notin M
but
x\in\phi(M)
and
(X,\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}})
is acyclic, there exists an implication
A\to b\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}
with
A\subseteq M
but
b\notin M
such that
A\cup\{b\}\subseteq\mathrm{critanc}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)\cup\{x\}
.
If
b\neq x
,
A\to b\in\Sigma^{\prime}
by definition of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
As
M\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
,
A\subseteq M
thus implies
b\in M
, a contradiction.
We deduce that
b=x
must hold, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

If
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)\subseteq\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
, then
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)=\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
and so
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{critgen}(x)
.

Proof.

First, we prove that
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}(x)=\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
indeed holds.
Let
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
.
Then,
M\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
as
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and there exists
M^{\prime}\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)
such that
M\subseteq M^{\prime}
.
As
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)\subseteq\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
and the members of
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
are pairwise incomparable, we deduce
M=M^{\prime}
and
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)
.

Then, we argue that
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{critgen}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
by contradiction.
Recall that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathrm{critgen}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
by definition of
\mathcal{F}
.
Hence, suppose that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathrm{critgen}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
and let
A\in\mathrm{critgen}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)\setminus\mathcal{F}
.
We first show that
x\notin\phi^{\prime}(A)
. Again, suppose for contradiction that
x\in\phi^{\prime}(A)
.
Then, applying forward chaining on
A
with
\Sigma^{\prime}
will reach
x
.
Since
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}
and
A\to x\notin\Sigma^{\prime}
, applying the forward chaining on
A
with
\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}\setminus\{A\to x\}
will reach
x
too.
Hence, the two IBs
(X,\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}})
and
(X,\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}\setminus\{A\to x\})
are equivalent.
However, this contradicts the fact that
\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}
is unit-minimum by Theorem
3.4
.
We conclude that
x\notin\phi^{\prime}(A)
must hold as expected.
Now,
x\notin\phi^{\prime}(A)
implies that there exists
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)
such that
\phi^{\prime}(A)\subseteq M
.
By assumption,
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
, so that
A\subseteq\phi(A)\subseteq\phi(M)=M
.
However,
x\in\phi(A)
holds as
A\in\mathrm{critgen}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
, which entails
x\in M
, a contradiction with
M\in\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
.
We conclude that such
A
cannot exist, and hence that
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{critgen}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
, which was to be proved.
∎

Before concluding to an algorithm for
CGE
+
A
{\cdot}
Dec
based on
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
, there is one last subtlety to handle: we need to guarantee that for each
y\in\mathrm{critanc}(x)
, the irreducible closed sets
\mathrm{irr}(y)
we provide as an input to
ACS
+
A
{\cdot}
Enum
along with
\Sigma^{\prime}
indeed correspond to the irreducible closed sets attached to
y
in
(X,\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
, i.e., that
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(y)=\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(y)
for each such

Lemma 7.3
.

For every
y\in\mathrm{critanc}(x)
,
\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}}(y)=\mathrm{irr}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(y)
.

Proof.

Because attached irreducible closed sets and minimal generators define dual hypergraphs, it is sufficient to show that
\mathrm{mingen}_{\mathcal{C}}(y)=\mathrm{mingen}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(y)
.
Since

is acyclic, so is

and

follows by definition of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
We deduce that for every

,

.
Again using acyclicity, for each
Z\subseteq\mathrm{critanc}_{\mathcal{C}}(y)
,

if and only if there exists a subset
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}=\{A_{1}\to b_{1},\dots,A_{k}\to b_{k}\}
of implications of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}
where

for all

and

, where

is the closure operator associated to
(X,\Sigma^{\prime\prime})
.
Given that any such
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
satisfies

, we deduce that

if and only if
y\in\phi^{\prime}(Z)

Therefore,

if and only if
A\in\mathrm{mingen}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}(x)

Discussion

In this paper, we provided incremental-polynomial time algorithms for the two translation tasks
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
in acyclic convex geometries of bounded (premise- or conclusion-) degree.
We note that the delay of our algorithms is intrinsically not polynomial since our algorithm rely on a sequential top-down procedure which reduces the enumeration to an auxiliary enumeration problem whose instance grows with the total number of solutions output so far.
This suggests the following question.

Question 8.1
.

Can
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
and
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
be solved with polynomial delay for acyclic convex geometries of bounded degree?

Toward this direction, we now argue that the classic flashlight search framework may not be used in a straightforward way to answer positively to this question.
In the
flashlight search technique
, the goal is to construct solutions one element at a time, according to a linear order
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
on the ground set, and deciding at each step whether
x_{i}
should or should not be included in the solution.
For this approach to be tractable, the framework requires to solve the so-called “extension problem” in polynomial time: given two disjoint subsets
K,F
of the ground set, decide whether there exists a solution including
K
and avoiding
F
.
We refer to e.g.,
[
BEG04
,
MS19
]
for a more detailed description of this folklore technique.
More formally, this technique reduces the enumeration to the following decision problem.

Irreducible Closed Set Extension (
ICS
{\cdot}
Ext
)

Input:

An implicational base
(X,\Sigma)
and
K,F\subseteq X
.

Question:

Does there exist
M\in\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
such that
K\subseteq M
and
M\cap F=\emptyset
?

We show that this problem is
NP
-complete even for acyclic implicational bases of bounded degree, even for
{K=\emptyset}
, suggesting that this framework may not be used in a straightforward way to improve our results.

Theorem 8.2
.

The problem
ICS
{\cdot}
Ext
is
NP
-complete even for acyclic implicational bases with degree, premise-degree, conclusion-degree and dimension (i.e., the maximum size of a premise) simultaneously bounded by 4, 4, 2 and 2, respectively.

Proof.

A polynomial-size certificate for
NP
is a subset
M\subseteq X
for which it is easily checked, in polynomial time, whether
M\in\mathrm{irr}(\mathcal{C})
,
K\subseteq M
, and
M\cap F=\emptyset
.

Regarding completeness, it is known that SAT is
NP
-complete even for instances where each clause contains at most
3
literals, and each variable appears (negatively or positively) in at most
3
clauses
[
GJ79
, Problem L01]
.
Let us consider an instance
\psi
of this problem, with variables
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
and clauses
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
.
For clause
C_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq m
, let us denote by
\ell_{j}^{1},\dots,\ell_{j}^{k}
its
k\leq 3
literals, i.e.,
\ell_{j}^{i}
is either a variable or its negation.

.

We reduce

to an instance

Let us first describe the ground set

.
First, for every variable

,

we create three elements

with

representing

,

representing

, and

v_{i}
and
\overline{v_{i}}

Then, for every clause

We now describe
\Sigma
.
For every

.
For every clause

appearing in position

We complete the construction of

.

Let us consider a positive instance of

{\cdot}

.
Let

Consider the assignment defined by

Lastly, notice that the implications in

Among other directions, we wonder whether our results extend to broader classes of closure systems or to parameters that generalize the degree.
As a first step towards this avenue, we show that when we simply drop acyclicity, then the degree is helpless as far as
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
is concerned.
More precisely, we show in the next theorem that an algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
for closure systems with bounded degree can be used to solve
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
in the general case.

Theorem 8.3
.

There is an output-polynomial time algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
in general whenever there is an output-polynomial time algorithm solving
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
on closure systems with bounded degree.

Proof.

Let
(X,\Sigma)
be an instance of
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
with
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and
\Sigma=\{A_{1}\to B_{1},\dots,A_{m}\to B_{m}\}
.
We build an implicational base
(X^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
of polynomial size and that has bounded degree.
We first describe
X^{\prime}
.
For each
x_{i}\in X
and each implication
A_{j}\to B_{j}\in\Sigma
, we create a gadget element
x_{i}^{j}
if
x_{i}\in A_{j}\cup B_{j}
and we call
X_{i}
the set of resulting elements associated with
x_{i}
, i.e.,
X_{i}=\{x_{i}^{j}:A_{j}\to B_{j}\in\Sigma,\ x_{i}\in A_{j}\cup B_{j}\}
.
Recall that we consider that
B_{j}
and
A_{j}
are disjoint, so that an element can occur only once in an implication.
Therefore,
|X_{i}|=\deg(x_{i})
.
We put
X^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
We now describe
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
For each implication
A_{j}\to B_{j}
of
\Sigma
we create an implication where each element
x_{i}\in A_{j}\cup B_{j}
is replaced by its corresponding gadget
x_{i}^{j}
, that is, we build the implication
A^{\prime}_{j}\to B^{\prime}_{j}
where
A^{\prime}_{j}=\{x_{i}^{j}:x_{i}\in A_{j}\}
and
B^{\prime}_{j}=\{x_{i}^{j}:x_{i}\in B_{j}\}
.
Finally, for each
i
, we consider an arbitrary labeling
y_{1},\dots,y_{k}
of the elements of
X_{i}
and make all of them equivalent by putting in
\Sigma^{\prime}
the cycle of implications
\{y_{1}\to y_{2},\ \dots,\ y_{k-1}\to y_{k},y_{k}\to y_{1}\}
.
This completes the description of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Observe that
(X^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
can be built in polynomial time in the size of
(X,\Sigma)
and that each element in
(X^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
has degree at most 3.
The reduction is illustrated on an example in Figure

.

Note that the mapping
\psi\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

is a polynomial-time computable bijection between the two closure systems
(X,\mathcal{C})
and
(X^{\prime},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
.
Hence, with this bijection, an output-polynomial time algorithm solving

{\cdot}

for

{\cdot}

Theorem
8.3
thus suggests to investigate instead more constrained classes of closure systems.
The top-down strategy we used for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
requires to devise an ordering on the elements of the ground set that provides a meaningful ancestor relation.
Lower-bounded closure systems
[
FJN95
,
ANR13
]
are a well-known generalization of acyclic convex geometries that appears as a natural candidate to investigate.
They are characterized by an acyclic
D
-relation, a subset of the
\delta
-relation (see Section
3
), which suggests that a topological ordering of the elements can be set and used as in our approach.
Besides, closure systems arising from graphs, also known as graph convexities
[
FJ86
]
, may be a source of closure systems where natural orders may be found based on the properties on the underlying graphs.
We note, however, that these closure systems are in general not acyclic.

The algorithm we rely on to solve
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
, through
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
, seems slightly more demanding.
On top of a natural ordering of the vertices, it requires the critical base not only to be well defined, but also to be valid.
Critical generators are initially defined in convex geometries using extreme points, but in arbitrary closure systems, closed sets need not have extreme points, and critical generators may thus not exist.
However, as observed by different authors
[
NK13
,
Wil17
]
, the critical generators of an element could instead be defined as its minimal generators that have the smallest closure inclusion-wise.
This definition makes critical generators of an element exist in any closure system as long as this element admits minimal generators, and it coincides with the original definition in convex geometries.
Nevertheless, even under this definition, the corresponding critical base of an arbitrary closure system needs not to be a valid IB (as it already fails in convex geometries), which prevents the strategy of finding critical generators to answer
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
from applying in general.
Identifying classes of closure systems, or convex geometries, where the critical base is valid is thus an interesting question to investigate in order to study the tractability of
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
.

A possible alternative is to use the lattice theoretic counterpart of critical generators, being
E
-generators
[
FJN95
,
ANR13
]
.
Still, the corresponding
E
-base, much as the critical base, does not always constitute a valid IB of its closure system.
However, in the class of lower bounded closure systems, the
E
-base turns out to be valid.
Together with the fact that lower bounded closure systems are
D
-acyclic, this suggests our approach to
MIB
{\cdot}
Gen
based on the problem
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
, adapted to
E
-generators, might succeed.

As for parameters, an analogue of the notion of degeneracy coming from (hyper)graph theory would be a natural candidate for the generalization of the degree.
Degeneracy in implicational bases can be defined as the smallest left-degree among all possible left-to-right ordering of the vertices.
Dimension, also known as arity in the language of abstract convexity
[
vdV93
]
is another intriguing parameter incomparable to degree.
It is the least integer
k
such that the closure system admits an IB with premises of size at most
k
, which makes it particularly interesting for the translation task as
MIS
{\cdot}
Enum
is tractable for hypergraphs of bounded dimension.
Other parameters coming from convexity such as the Caratheodory number—for which some tractable algorithms can be obtained
[
Wil17
]
—, the Radon number, or the Helly number might also be avenues for future research.

Finally, we note that our algorithm for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
has an
XP
dependence on the conclusion-degree
k
, i.e., it runs in
N^{f(k)}
time for some computable function
f
where
N
is the size of the input plus the output.
This is not due to the top-down approach, that is, Theorem
4.1
, which preserves
FPT
, i.e., running times of the form
f(k)\cdot N^{O(1)}
for some computable function.
This is neither due to the computation of minimal transversals in Theorem
5.2
, as this can be achieved with
FPT
delay parameterized by the number of edges
[
EHR08
]
.
Rather, the bottleneck to
FPT
time is the guess of irreducible selections from Lemma
5.1
.
The same arises in Lemma
6.4
for the premise-degree.
Consequently, and since our algorithm for
CB
{\cdot}
Gen
relies on the ones we devised for
ICS
{\cdot}
Enum
, they also have an
XP
dependence on the parameter.
Thus, it is natural to ask whether any of these algorithms can be improved to run in
FPT
times parameterized by the degree.
