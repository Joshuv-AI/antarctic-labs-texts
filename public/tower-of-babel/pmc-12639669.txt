Comparative analysis of virulence genes, biofilm production, and antibiotic resistance in Escherichia coli from dogs and humans using rep-PCR
Authors: Rasa Zarin, Jalal Shayegh, Somayyeh Hosseinzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEscherichia coli (E. coli) is a major pathogen responsible for urinary tract and gastrointestinal infections in both humans and dogs and is of serious public health concern due to its potential for severe infections. The aim of this study was to compare the pathogenicity of E. coli strains isolated from dogs and humans using repetitive sequence-based polymerase chain reaction (rep-PCR).MethodsA total of 30 fecal samples from visiting dogs and 30 samples from veterinarians and other animal-related personnel in Tehran and Qom, Iran was collected. The presence of E. coli was confirmed by phenotypic and biochemical methods (Gram staining, culture on MacConkey and EMB agar, TSI agar, and IMViC tests) followed by identification of virulence genes (bfpB, elt, stx1, hlyA, fimC) by PCR. Susceptibility to antimicrobials was determined using the Kirby-Bauer method and the ability to produce biofilm was assessed. Finally, all isolates were subjected to genomic fingerprinting using rep-PCR.ResultsOut of 60 fecal samples, only one E. coli isolate per sample was selected for analysis. All 60 isolates were confirmed and the fimC gene was present in all samples, while the bfpB gene was found in 90% of human samples and 46.4% of animal samples. All isolates had the ability to produce biofilm, with 20% of human samples and 56.6% of animal samples produced strong biofilms. Antimicrobial testing showed high resistance to amikacin (96.6–100%) and fluoroquinolones, but susceptible to nitrofurantoin and gentamicin. Over 90% of isolates were multidrug-resistant, indicating treatment challenges. Based on rep-PCR, the isolates are assigned to 12 different clusters. Of the 12 clusters identified, five contained isolates from both humans and dogs, suggesting potential zoonotic transmission.ConclusionsThe results suggest that E. coli from humans and dogs share the same pathogenic characteristics, with notable differences in biofilm production and antibiotic resistance. The genetic relatedness between some isolates highlights the risk of zoonotic transmission, emphasizing the need for continuous surveillance and a One Health approach to control infections.Keywords: Escherichia coli, Rep-PCR, Antibiotic resistance, Biofilm, Dog, Human, One health approach

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a Gram-negative bacterium and a natural part of the normal gut microbiota in humans and many domestic animals, where it typically does not harm its host under normal conditions [1, 2]. However, certain strains of this bacterium are pathogenic and lead to serious and even life-threatening infections. Infections caused by E. coli can include bloody diarrhea, urinary tract infections, meningitis and septicemia [3, 4].

In dogs, this bacterium is often responsible for urinary tract infections, which may be accompanied by symptoms such as fever, weakness and increased heart rate. In severe cases, the infection can lead to fluid accumulation in the pericardium and even death. Escherichia coli infections can be transmitted to humans, especially if people have close contact with infected dogs or the contact with contaminated environment with dog feces. Escherichia coli infections in humans include urinary tract and intestinal infections, which can lead to severe diarrhea and systemic infections [5, 6]. As this bacterium is easily transmitted, the identification and investigation of its pathogenic potential is of particular importance.

In this context, an effective method for identifying and comparing different E. coli strains is the use of repetitive sequence-based polymerase chain reaction (rep-PCR). This technique was selected over other genotyping methods due to its high discriminatory power for epidemiological tracking, cost-effectiveness, and rapid turnaround time [7]. This method allows us to identify genetic differences between strains isolated from different sources, including humans and dogs. Rep-PCR is a molecular technique that uses specific sequences repeated in bacterial genomes to identify genetic patterns and compare strains. It has become a popular tool in microbiology research due to its high accuracy and the short time required to perform. However, it is noted that rep-PCR may have limitations in reproducibility across laboratories compared to whole-genome sequencing, though it remains highly suitable for local outbreak investigations and comparative studies [8–10].

The comparing of E. coli pathogenicity isolated from dogs and humans can provide valuable insights into the epidemiology and transmission of infections by this bacterium [11, 12]. This is particularly crucial in regions like Iran, where studies on human clinical isolates have revealed a high prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli, including strains producing extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) and harboring plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes [13, 14]. This comparison can help to identify virulence factors and antibiotic resistance, providing valuable insights to improve treatment strategies and infection prevention measures. Therefore, this study specifically investigated the pathogenicity of E. coli isolated from dogs and humans using the rep-PCR technique. While the role of E. coli in zoonotic infections is well-established, the influence of local socio-cultural factors on its transmission dynamics and resistance profiles remains poorly understood, particularly in Middle Eastern contexts. To address this gap, this study included samples from two distinct urban environments in Iran: Qom, selected for its unique socio-environmental profile, and Tehran, chosen as a representative major metropolitan hub. This strategic selection enables the investigation of how such socio-environmental contrasts may correlate with variations in E. coli strains. The main objective was to identify genetic differences and pathogenicity levels between isolates from these two sites, thus contributing to a better understanding of the epidemiology of E. coli related infections.

Methods

Sample collection

A total of 30 fecal samples (15 per city) were collected from dogs showing symptoms clinically suggestive of E. coli infection (including acute diarrhea, vomiting, or signs of urinary tract infection such as dysuria) at veterinary clinics in Tehran and Qom, Iran. In addition, 30 fecal samples were collected from veterinarians and other personnel working with animals in the same clinics (15 per city). The samples were collected in a hygienic manner, with one gram taken from normal feces and five grams from diarrheic feces and placed in special sample containers. Phenotypic and biochemical methods were used to confirm the E. coli isolates. The tests included Gram staining (showing Gram-negative rods), culture on MacConkey agar (pink colonies), EMB agar (green metallic sheen), TSI agar (yellow color change indicating sugar fermentation) (Ibresco, Italy), and IMVIC tests (indole positive, methyl red positive, Voges-Proskauer negative, citrate negative). To confirm these results, the textbook Clinical Veterinary Microbiology was consulted [15].

Identification of virulence genes

Escherichia coli was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (Ibresco, Italy), and after incubation at 35 °C for 24 h, DNA extraction was performed using a boiling method [16]. The selected virulence factor genes in this study included bfpB, elt, stx1, hlyA and fimC. These genes were amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to determine their presence or absence. The reactions were performed in a total volume of 25 µl using a thermocycler (CORBETT, Australia). The primers (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran) used and the sizes of the resulting products are listed in Table 1 [17–20]. Thermal cycling conditions for the target genes included initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles consisting of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 63 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 1.5 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. For PCR amplification, specific reference strains were used as positive controls for each target gene: E. coli ATCC 35,401 for elt, E. coli RIGLD-EHEC for stx1, E. coli ATCC 43,887 for bfpB, E. coli 35,218 for hlyA, and a standard laboratory strain of E. coli K-12 (e.g., DH5α) for fimC. All reference bacterial strains were obtained from the Culture Collection of the Research Center for Gastroenterology and Liver Diseases, Shahid Beheshti University of Tehran, Iran. Sterile deionized water served as the negative control in all reactions. The sizes of the bands corresponding to each gene were as follows: bfpB (910 bp), fimC (477 bp), elt (322 bp), stx1 (244 bp), and hlyA (533 bp).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The susceptibility of the bacteria to antibiotics was determined using the modified Kirby-Bauer method based on the guidelines of the National Committee for Clinical Laboratory Standards (NCCLS) for nine antibiotics. After a bacterial suspension was prepared according to the McFarland standard, it was transferred to Mueller-Hinton agar (Ibresco, Italy) plates. Antibiotic discs were then placed on the surface of the plates using sterile forceps. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, resistance or susceptibility was determined by measuring the zone of inhibition with a caliper. The antibiotics used in this study, are commonly prescribed for domestic dogs and including nitrofurantoin (25 µg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (25 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), cefixime (5 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), ofloxacin (5 µg), piperacillin/tazobactam (100 µg), amikacin (30 µg) and cefotaxime (50 µg). The inhibition zones were measured for each antibiotic and the zone diameter breakpoints were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [21]. According to previous studies, an isolate that was resistant to one antibiotic was classified as resistant, while an isolate that was resistant to ≥ 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial categories was considered a Multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolate [22].

Measuring biofilm production

The ability of E. coli isolates to produce biofilm was assessed in the laboratory using scoring methods described in previous studies with minor modifications [23, 24]. Briefly, isolates were incubated overnight at 37 °C in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) (Ibresco, Italy). Following dilution in fresh TSB, 150 µL of this cell suspension was added to a 96-well polystyrene microtiter plate. After a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C, the plates were gently washed three times with 200 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Baharafshan, Iran) and inverted to dry. To fix the biofilms, 100 µL of 96% ethanol was used. After 15 min, the alcohol was removed, and the plates were allowed to air dry. Subsequently, 100 µL of a 2% crystal violet (CV) (Merck, Germany) stain was added to all wells. After 20 min, the plates were rinsed under running water to remove excess dye. The bound dye was then released by adding 150 µL of 33% acetic acid, and the optical density (OD) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ELISA reader (Anthos 2020, Austria). All measurements were performed in quadruplicate and repeated in three separate experiments. Un-inoculated TSB was used as a negative control and 96% Ethanol was used as a blank. The cut-off value for optical density (ODc) was determined as the mean OD value of the negative control group plus one standard deviation. The ability of the tested isolates to form a biofilm was divided into four categories based on the OD values: non-adherent (OD < ODc), weakly adherent (ODc < OD < 2xODc), moderately adherent (2xODc < OD < 4xODc) and strongly adherent (4xODc < OD) [23].

Repetitive sequence-based PCR (rep-PCR)

The reaction was carried out in a final volume of 25 µL in a thermocycler (CORBETT, Australia), whereby for each sample 12.5 µL of the PCR master mix, 1 µL of the primers REP 1 (5’-IIIICGICGICATCIGGC-3’) and REP 2 (5’-ICGICTTATCIGGCCTAC-3’) (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran), each in a final concentration of 0.4 µM, 2 µL extracted DNA and 8.5 µL sterile deionized water were mixed in PCR-specific microtubes. The rep-PCR reaction program included an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 7 min, followed by 35 cycles consisting of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 60 °C for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 8 min, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 8 min. The rep-PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel at a voltage of 95 volts for 40 min and then visualized under UV light using a gel documentation system. Patterns were analyzed with the online data analysis service (http://insilico.ehu.es/dice_upgma/) using the binary scoring method based on the presence (1) or absence (0) of bands [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., USA). Comparison of categorical variables (biofilm formation capacity, antibiotic resistance rates) between human and animal isolates was performed using Chi-square test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Confirmation of the isolates by phenotypic methods

The presence of E. coli was confirmed in all 60 fecal samples (30 from dogs and 30 from humans) using standard phenotypic and biochemical tests. The typical reactions observed were: formation of pink colonies on MacConkey agar; green metallic sheen on EMB agar; yellow color change on TSI agar (indicating fermentation of glucose, lactose, and sucrose); and an IMVIC profile of indole positive, methyl red positive, Voges-Proskauer negative, and citrate negative. Gram staining further confirmed the presence of Gram-negative rod-shaped bacteria.

Identification of virulence genes in samples

The results showed that of the 30 human samples, 27 samples (90%) contained the bfpB gene and all 30 samples (100%) contained the fimC gene. None of the human samples were positive for the genes associated with elt, stx1 or hlyA. In the animal samples, 14 (46.7%) of the 30 samples contained the bfpB gene and all 30 samples (100%) contained the fimC gene. Similarly, none of the animal samples were positive for the genes associated with elt, stx1 or hlyA. The results of the electrophoresis for each gene are shown in Fig. 1.

Ability to produce biofilm from isolates

The data obtained with the ELISA reader showed that the majority of human and animal isolates were capable of producing biofilm with varying degrees. The results revealed that out of 30 human samples, 7 samples (23.3%) were weak biofilm producers, 6 samples (20%) had moderate biofilm production and another 6 samples (20%) had strong biofilm production. In addition, 11 samples (36.7%) from the human group were unable to produce biofilm. The assessment of biofilm formation in animal samples yielded the following results: Of the 30 animal samples, 3 samples (10%) were able to produce weak biofilm, 5 samples (16.7%) produced moderate biofilm, and 17 samples (56.7%) showed strong biofilm production. In addition, 5 samples (16.7%) from the animal group were unable to produce biofilm. A summary of the biofilm production results can be found in Table 2.

Antibiotic susceptibility test

The antibiotic susceptibility profiles for both human and animal samples are presented in Table 3. The antibiotic susceptibility tests of the human samples showed that E. coli isolates had the highest resistance to amikacin (96.6%), followed by ofloxacin (70%), ciprofloxacin (63.3%), cefixime (46.6%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (36.6%), cefotaxime (33.3%), gentamicin (23.3%), nitrofurantoin (13.3%) and piperacillin/tazobactam (3.3%). The highest susceptibility among the isolates was observed against nitrofurantoin and gentamicin (both 76.6%). This was followed by susceptibility to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, piperacillin/tazobactam and cefotaxime (63.3% each), cefixime (26.6%), ofloxacin (20%) and ciprofloxacin (16.6%). Most isolates showed resistance to amikacin, with the exception of one isolate that showed moderate susceptibility to this antibiotic. Of the 30 E. coli isolates, 28 (93.3%) were resistant to ≥ 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial categories and were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates.

Antibiotic susceptibility profiles in human and animal samples

The results of antibiotic susceptibility testing for animal samples showed that E. coli isolates had the highest resistance to amikacin (100%), followed by ofloxacin and ciprofloxacin (both 90%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (56.6%), cefotaxime (50%), cefixime (43.3%), piperacillin/tazobactam (40%), nitrofurantoin (26.6%) and gentamicin (20%). The highest susceptibility among the isolates was observed for gentamicin (73.3%), followed by nitrofurantoin (66.6%), piperacillin/tazobactam and cefotaxime (50% each), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (43.3%), cefixime (23.3%), ciprofloxacin (10%) and ofloxacin (6.6%). None of the isolates from either group showed susceptibility to amikacin. Of the 30 E. coli isolates, 27 isolates (90%) were resistant to ≥ 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial categories and were classified as MDR isolates.

Overall, the observations revealed that both the human and animal groups had the highest resistance to amikacin, with resistance rates of 96.6% in the human samples and 100% in the animal samples. In addition, significant resistance to ofloxacin and ciprofloxacin was found, with resistance rates of 70% and 63.3% in humans and 90% in animals for both antibiotics, respectively. Remarkably, none of the isolates from either group showed susceptibility to amikacin. Conversely, both groups showed the highest susceptibility to gentamicin and nitrofurantoin, indicating a potential treatment option for infections caused by these isolates.

The results obtained from the assessment of biofilm production in the isolates indicate significant differences in biofilm-forming ability between human and animal isolates (p=0.002). In human samples, the highest percentage of biofilm production was attributed to weak biofilms (23.3%). Conversely, in animal samples, the highest percentage of biofilm production was associated with strong biofilms (56.6%).

The results of the antibiogram test reveal a concerning pattern of antibiotic resistance among E. coli isolates obtained from human and animal samples. Both groups of isolates exhibited the highest resistance to the antibiotic amikacin, with 96.6% resistance in human samples and 100% in animal samples. Furthermore, high resistance to ofloxacin and ciprofloxacin was observed in both groups (70% and 63.3% in human samples and 90% in animal samples, respectively); while conversely, isolates exhibited high susceptibility to gentamicin and nitrofurantoin (76.6% and 73.3%, respectively), suggesting these as effective treatment options.

Repetitive sequence-based PCR (rep-PCR)

The results of rep-PCR for human and animal samples were analyzed based on the number of bands and their respective regions. The products of the rep-PCR reaction were subjected to electrophoresis (Fig. 2), and molecular weight markers were used to assess the similarity between isolates, resulting in the generation of a dendrogram. Isolates that showed similar or different banding patterns within one to three bands were considered identical and grouped accordingly. In contrast, isolates that showed more than three different bands were classified as unrelated and assigned to different groups. A total of 60 isolates were analyzed using the rep-PCR method.

In this study, the degree of similarity between the E. coli isolates was assessed, resulting in the identification of 12 clusters. Of these, five clusters contained similar isolates (common type), while seven clusters were unique (unique type) (Fig. 3). Discussion

Escherichia coli is a major pathogen causing urinary, gastrointestinal, and systemic infections, whose study is crucial due to increasing antibiotic resistance and enhanced severity from biofilm production and virulence factors [27]. Dogs can act as hosts for E. coli and potentially transmit infections to humans [12]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the pathogenicity of E. coli strains isolated from dogs and humans using rep-PCR to provide insights for better infection management and control strategies.

The present study found that all fecal samples from dogs and humans contained E. coli, which was confirmed by phenotypic and biochemical methods [15]. Five major virulence genes were examined in the study: bfpB (adhesion and biofilm formation), fimC (bacterial adhesion), elt (heat-labile enterotoxin), stx1 (Shiga toxin) and hlyA (hemolysin). The results showed that 90% of the human samples contained the bfpB gene and 100% contained the fimC gene; in the animal samples, 46.6% had bfpB and 100% had fimC. No samples contained elt, stx1 or hlyA. Type 1 fimbriae are critical for colonization of E. coli, with the fimC gene facilitating the transport of fimbrial components to the outer membrane. This suggests that fimC is essential for adhesion, as it was present in all samples. In addition, Terlizzi et al. (2017) demonstrated that fimC helps in the adhesion of uropathogenic E. coli to the urinary tract epithelium, an important step in urinary tract infections (UTIs) [28]. Malberg Tetzschner et al. (2020) also showed that fimC enhances the adhesion of enterotoxigenic E. coli and thus contributes to diarrheal diseases [29]. The study found that 90% of human samples and 46.6% of animal samples contained the bfpB gene, which encodes a lipoprotein essential for the formation of bundle-forming pili (BFP) in enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC). These pili are crucial for the adhesion of EPEC to intestinal epithelial cells, indicating the pathogenicity of EPEC and the important role of the bfpB gene in infections [30].

Alizade et al. (2019) reported that gastrointestinal pathogenic E. coli had the highest prevalence in the Iranian population based on a meta-analysis of 73 studies [31]. Our study concluded that adherence factors are the most important virulence factors for E. coli infections in both human and animal samples, as only two of five genes examined were present, suggesting similarities between isolates.

The assessment of biofilm production revealed significant differences between human and animal isolates. Weak biofilms were most prevalent in human samples at 23.3%, while strong biofilms were most prevalent in animal samples at 56.6%. These differences may be due to genetic and phenotypic diversity among the isolates, varying environmental conditions, and different immune pressures that affect biofilm production capability. Furthermore, the strong biofilm production in animal isolates may indicate their greater role in pathogenicity and infections associated with these isolates, as biofilms can aid bacterial survival against antimicrobial agents, including antibiotics and the immune system.

The results of the antibiogram showed alarming antibiotic resistance patterns in E. coli isolates from human and animal samples. Of particular clinical concern is the intermediate resistance observed, such as the 20% rate for ciprofloxacin in human isolates, which may indicate a risk of treatment failure under standard dosing regimens and necessitate alternative therapeutic approaches. Both groups showed the highest resistance to amikacin, with 96.6% in humans and 100% in animals, probably due to the frequent misuse of this antibiotic. Resistance to ofloxacin and ciprofloxacin was also high (70% and 63.3% in humans, 90% in animals), indicating widespread quinolone resistance, which is critical for the treatment of urinary tract infections. However, isolates were highly susceptible to gentamicin and nitrofurantoin (76.6% and 73.3%), suggesting that these may be effective treatment options despite the presence of MDR. These results emphasize the need for careful monitoring of antibiotic use and further studies to identify resistance patterns for better infection control strategies. The high prevalence of multidrug-resistant E. coli isolates observed in our study aligns with recent findings from the same region, where Jomehzadeh et al. (2022) reported that 51.6% of uropathogenic E. coli isolates were MDR, highlighting a serious public health concern in Iran [32]. However, our study further reveals that these resistant strains are shared between humans and pet dogs, suggesting that companion animals could serve as a reservoir for the community transmission of MDR bacteria. The horizontal transfer of resistance genes via mobile genetic elements (e.g., plasmids) is a key mechanism facilitating the dissemination of traits such as extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production between hosts, making infection control more challenging [33]. In addition, direct contact between dogs and humans may facilitate the transmission of resistant bacteria and genes.

Statistical analysis revealed that while the overall resistance profile was similar between groups, resistance to Ciprofloxacin (p = 0.021) and Piperacillin/Tazobactam (p = 0.001) was significantly higher in animal isolates. This result is consistent with the results of the study by Stenske et al. (2009). In their study, which focused on fecal samples from 61 pairs of healthy dogs and their owners and 30 healthy humans without dogs as a control group, no differences were found in antibiotic susceptibility test results between dog-owner pairs [34]. In the study conducted by Younis et al. (2015) on 70 fecal samples from dogs and cats with diarrhea in different veterinary clinics and hospitals in Egypt, E. coli was detected in 41 samples, all of which showed multidrug resistance. The results of this study suggest that pets can harbor pathogenic strains of E. coli with high and multidrug resistance, posing a risk of transmission to humans who come into contact with them and making antimicrobial treatment more difficult for these individuals [35]. In the study by Carvalho et al. (2016) which examined fecal isolates of E. coli from 134 dogs and their owners and 44 humans without contact with dogs as a control group in Brazil, the degree of antibiotic resistance was very similar in the isolates from all three groups. The results indicate a possible transmission of resistant E. coli bacteria between humans and animals in a shared environment [36]. In the study by Askari et al. (2021) which investigated the phylogenetic background and antibiotic resistance of E. coli isolates obtained from healthy domestic dogs and their owners in the city of Kerman, the results indicated that domestic dogs should be considered as significant reservoirs for resistant strains of E. coli [37].

The results of the rep-PCR test showed significant genetic diversity among the E. coli isolates from human and animal samples and identified 12 clusters with similar banding patterns. This suggests genetic correlations that can improve the understanding of the pathogenesis and epidemiology of the bacterium. The presence of five common clusters and seven unique clusters indicates a possible relationship between some isolates that may contribute to common infections. In Japan, a study also found that the characteristics of E. coli isolates from dog feces were similar to those of their owners, with transmission occurring in 8.8% of households [38]. The rep-PCR clustering revealed significant associations between genetic profiles and pathogenic potential. Notably, Cluster C, which contained isolates from both humans and dogs exhibiting 100% biofilm formation and multidrug resistance, represents a high-risk clone with potential for zoonotic transmission. These findings highlight the critical link between genetic relatedness and phenotypic resistance patterns, emphasizing the need for targeted surveillance of such high-risk clusters to control E. coli infections effectively.

Conclusion

This study compared pathogenic and genetic characteristics of E. coli from dogs and humans. The high prevalence of virulence genes (fimC: 100%, bfpB: 90% human, 46.6% animal isolates) underscores their role in infection. Significant antibiotic resistance was observed, notably to amikacin (96.6–100%) and ciprofloxacin (63.3–90%), with over 90% of isolates being multidrug-resistant. rep-PCR revealed 12 genetic clusters, with Cluster C showing 100% biofilm formation and MDR, indicating high-risk zoonotic potential. These findings demonstrate shared pathogenic and resistance profiles between canine and human isolates, highlighting the need for coordinated One Health strategies for infection control and antimicrobial stewardship.
