"Feeling that I was Collaborating with Them": A 20 years Systematic Literature Review of Social Virtual Reality Leveraging Collaboration CCS: Human-centered computing Collaborative and social computing CCS: Human-centered computing Human computer interaction (HCI)
Niloofar Sayadi, Sadie Co, Diego Gómez-Zará

Abstract
. As more people meet, interact, and socialize online, Social Virtual Reality (VR) emerges as a promising technology that can bridge the gap between traditional face-to-face and online communication. Compared to traditional screen-based applications, Social VR provides immersive, physical, and three-dimensional social interactions, making it a promising communication tool for enhancing collaborations. Despite the growing interest and research in Social VR, how it has supported collaborations and teamwork has not been explored in depth. To map the existing research in this domain, we conducted a 20-year systematic literature review to characterize how Social VR has been employed for collaboration. After screening 1,123 articles, we identified 51 articles that specifically addressed how Social VR has leveraged collaboration among remote users. Our findings show that Social VR can enhance team collaboration on three key levels: enhancing individual perceptions and experiences within their groups, fostering team dynamics with virtual elements that enable realistic interactions, and employing VR affordances that augment users’ spaces. However, the research requires a deeper understanding of how Social VR can support long-term collaboration and trust-building in collaborative settings. This review highlights the current practices, challenges, and future research opportunities within CSCW, offering insights for theorizing the impact of Social VR on team collaboration and for designing new applications that effectively support remote collaborations.

1.
Introduction

The rise of Social Virtual Reality (Social VR) as a platform for immersive interaction has transformed the way people communicate and collaborate in digital environments. Unlike traditional communication tools such as video conferencing or instant messaging, Social VR uses head-mounted displays (HMDs) to immerse users in three-dimensional spaces, providing a sense of physical presence and embodied interaction
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
. This capacity to simulate real-world experiences has positioned Social VR as a transformative medium for remote collaboration, particularly after the COVID-19 pandemic, which increased the need for robust virtual alternatives to face-to-face interactions
(
Waizenegger et al. 2020
;
Ball 2022
;
Gómez-Zará et al. 2023
)
.

In February 2022, Meta’s social VR platform Horizon Worlds achieved 300,000 active monthly users in the US
(
Heath 2022
)
, and the platform Rec Room surpassed 3 million monthly active users
(
Feltham 2022
)
. These milestones highlight the growing adoption of Social VR as a tool for connection and collaboration. Social VR offers unique affordances that can enhance communication and teamwork among distributed teams, such as full-body avatars, spatial audio, and interactive environments
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Freeman et al. 2022
)
.

Collaboration lies at the heart of human progress. Organizations, teams, and educational institutions rely on effective collaboration to solve complex problems, innovate, and achieve collective goals
(
Olson and Olson 2000
)
. However, traditional remote collaboration tools, while effective for basic communication, struggle to replicate the richness of in-person interactions
(
Freeman and Acena 2021
)
. Research has consistently shown that these tools often fail to support nuanced social dynamics, non-verbal cues, and the trust-building processes that are critical for team success
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
. As hybrid work environments and globalized teamwork become increasingly common, the demand for tools that foster presence, connection, and engagement in remote settings has never been greater.

Despite the growing interest in Social VR, its use in professional and collaborative settings remains unclear. While much of the existing research has focused on its applications in gaming, training, and casual gatherings
(
Bujić et al. 2021
;
Angel-Urdinola et al. 2021
;
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
, the potential of Social VR for enabling collaborative meetings and teamwork has been underexplored. Companies such as Microsoft have attempted to integrate Social VR technologies into professional environments to support meetings, conversations, and teamwork
(
Tilley 2022
;
Roth 2023
)
. However, adoption has often fallen short due to several challenges, including low-quality simulated environments, steep learning curves, cognitive dissonance between physical and virtual worlds, and a lack of applications specifically designed for professional purposes
(
Ashtari et al. 2020
;
Zhao 2011
;
Hubbard and Aguinis 2023
)
.

Moreover, while Social VR platforms like VRChat, HTC VIVE Sync, and Meta’s Horizon Workrooms have demonstrated capabilities for facilitating team collaborations
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
;
Abramczuk et al. 2023
;
Osborne et al. 2023
)
, the literature has yet to fully explore how these technologies support team processes, such as trust-building, long-term collaboration, and group cohesion. These challenges highlight important gaps in both the design and understanding of Social VR as a tool for distributed collaboration. Specifically, there is a need to better characterize how Social VR environments can address these limitations and replicate the richness of in-person interactions in professional settings.

The Computer-Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW) and Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) communities have long sought to develop technologies that support meaningful human interaction
(
Olson and Olson 2000
)
. Social VR offers an opportunity to rethink how digital tools can enhance collaboration in remote and hybrid contexts, bridging the limitations of current systems while using its unique affordances for presence and engagement. By creating immersive environments where users can interact in real-time, Social VR platforms enable remote teams to engage in activities that closely mirror face-to-face collaboration
(
McVeigh-Schultz et al. 2019
)
. Features such as avatar embodiment, shared object manipulation, and spatial awareness provide opportunities for more natural communication and enhanced social presence
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Freeman et al. 2022
)
. Despite these possibilities, critical questions remain about the effectiveness of Social VR in supporting diverse collaborative activities, addressing challenges such as sustaining user engagement, building trust, facilitating natural team dynamics, and overcoming technical limitations that impact presence, usability, and accessibility across distributed teams
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
;
Zhao 2011
)
.

Characterizing studies on Social VR that address collaboration and teamwork is important to understanding its potential as a transformative tool for distributed collaboration. This work provides a comprehensive overview of the current research landscape, identifying critical gaps in areas such as trust-building, user engagement, scalability of teamwork, long-term collaboration, the diversity of study samples, and the design of effective Social VR environments to support various stages of collaborative processes. By synthesizing these findings, we aim to highlight actionable opportunities for future CSCW research and offer design implications for developing effective collaborative VR systems that address the limitations of existing tools. This effort is intended to bridge the gap between theoretical advancements and practical applications, helping organizations and researchers leverage Social VR for more meaningful and productive remote teamwork. Given this motivation, the research questions that guide this study are:

•

RQ1: How have previous studies addressed team collaboration in Social VR?

•

RQ2: What are the specific gaps, future prospects, and research directions in this area?

To answer these research questions, we systematically reviewed the literature on Social VR addressing aspects of collaboration. By conducting this review, we aim to delineate solutions, insights, and recommendations that Social VR technologies have already highlighted and could offer to facilitate their implementation in the context of teamwork. This systematic review included articles published in the IEEE Xplore Digital Library, ACM Digital Library, and Web of Science databases from 2004 to 2023, covering 20 years of research. After screening 1,123 articles, we identified 51 articles that focused on how researchers and practitioners have created new systems to support teams and organizations and enhance teamwork and collaboration in Social VR. Based on this corpus, we conducted a thematic analysis of these papers, identifying three core themes: (1) Individual Perception and Experience of Collaboration in Social VR, (2) Team Dynamics and Collaboration in Virtual Environments, and (3) Enabling Collaboration through Social VR Affordances. These themes map how Social VR can enhance collaboration at the individual, team, and environment levels. Furthermore, this systematic review reveals several research gaps, including insufficient exploration of diverse user populations across demographics and technical expertise, limited understanding of how Social VR supports scalability for larger teams and complex tasks, and a lack of standardized evaluation frameworks for measuring collaboration effectiveness. Additionally, there are gaps in understanding trust-building mechanisms, team dynamics, and the transition from traditional online platforms to Social VR for professional collaboration. As a result, this review provides several research opportunities to understand how Social VR can support larger teams and more complex, open-ended projects that mirror real-world collaborative work. This analysis provides an in-depth review of the current capabilities and opportunities offered by Social VR technologies to people and organizations, pinpointing their limitations and identifying the gaps and emerging needs.

The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, it offers a comprehensive review of the use of Social VR technology to support remote collaboration, encompassing an analysis of applications and their impact on teamwork. Second, it provides a map for future CSCW research and system development in Social VR to enhance teamwork and collaboration. Thus, the findings presented in this study aim to inform CSCW designers and researchers about the opportunities and challenges involved in making Social VR an effective tool for remote collaboration.

2.
Background

We situate our work around prior studies examining (a) teams and technologies to support teamwork, (b) Social VR, and (c) how people have used Social VR technologies to collaborate in the workspace.

2.1.
Teams and Teamwork Technologies

Teams are groups of two or more individuals working interdependently, adaptively, and dynamically toward a common and valued goal, bringing diverse skills, knowledge, and perspectives to address challenging tasks
(
Gómez-Zará et al. 2020
;
Salas et al. 2000
)
. Unlike individuals working alone, teams can bring together people with diverse skills and knowledge to achieve better outcomes
(
Engel et al. 2015
;
Mathieu et al. 2017
)
. Team members work on different parts of a task, relying on each other and sharing responsibility to achieve a common goal. This collaborative approach allows teams to better tackle complex problems
(
Mathieu et al. 2017
;
Gómez-Zará et al. 2020
;
Salas et al. 2000
)
. Previous research shows that successful teamwork relies on clear communication, coordination, and mutual support to ensure individual contributions are aligned with the overall task strategy
(
Mathieu et al. 2017
)
. While this collective approach requires more coordination and collaboration than individual efforts, the result is a higher capacity to manage challenging tasks, particularly when specialized skills are needed
(
Mathieu et al. 2017
)
. The importance of teams is clear as organizational structures have significantly changed, with many moving away from individual roles to team-based systems over the past decades
(
Wuchty et al. 2007
)
. This shift allows companies to be more flexible and successful in reaching shared objectives by focusing on teamwork instead of relying on just individual efforts
(
Arora et al. 2023
;
Mathieu et al. 2017
)
. For instance, in IT organizations, project teams require programmers, designers, and project managers to work interdependently on complex software development projects, where effective communication, task coordination, and shared understanding are essential for delivering successful outcomes
(
Arora et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2023
)
.

The effects of online technologies on teams have been evolving and adapting to their current state-of-the-art technology. New advancements in groupware, intranets, social media platforms, clouding technologies, and videoconferencing platforms have enabled new forms of remote teams and capabilities
(
Gilson et al. 2015
;
Dulebohn and Hoch 2017
;
Jimenez et al. 2017
;
Treem et al. 2020
)
. Currently, hybrid workplaces allow team members to work remotely and also meet in person on specific occasions
(
Gilson et al. 2015
)
. Workers can still be physically in one building but working through online communication. Thus, some scholars have characterized teams according to their levels of virtualness
(
Staples and Webster 2008
)
, which could range from purely face-to-face to only virtual interactions
(
Griffith and Neale 2001
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
. Since team members do not necessarily work in the same physical place, current challenges that teams face reside in coordinating, staying cohesive, and performing effectively
(
Gilson et al. 2015
)
.

Given the increasing levels of virtuality in teams
(
Freeman et al. 2022
;
Bjørn et al. 2014
;
Breideband et al. 2023
)
, remote collaboration tools are now key components for facilitating effective communication and teamwork
(
Cai and Tanaka 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
)
. Instant messaging platforms, like Microsoft Teams and Slack, provide another layer of communication by enabling quick exchanges of information and facilitating asynchronous collaboration
(
Cai and Tanaka 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
)
. These text platforms support various forms of media sharing and integrate with other productivity tools, enhancing workflow efficiency. However, they may contribute to information overload and can sometimes lead to misinterpretations without the contextual cues present in verbal communication
(
Cha et al. 2018
)
. On the other hand, video conferencing platforms, such as Zoom, Skype, and Microsoft Teams, are also part of our current ecosystem of online communication technologies. These platforms support real-time audio and video interactions, creating a shared visual space that enhances user engagement and presence
(
Tang et al. 2023
;
Karis et al. 2016
)
. These tools allow team members to participate in meetings and discussions from different locations, fostering a sense of connection despite physical distance. However, while they offer significant benefits, they often lack the depth of non-verbal cues available in face-to-face interactions, which can hinder social presence and trust-building among team members
(
Freeman and Acena 2021
;
Bente et al. 2008
;
Kanawattanachai and Yoo 2002
)
. In summary, previous research has shown how that remote collaboration can curb creativity
(
Brucks and Levav 2022
)
, ideas
(
Lin et al. 2023
)
, identity
(
Bartel et al. 2007
)
, and cohesion
(
Yang et al. 2022b
)
among teams. These current limitations have motivated CSCW researchers to explore how immersive technologies, such as Social VR, can improve remote collaboration and enhance the quality of remote teamwork.

2.2.
Social VR

Social Virtual Reality (VR) refers to 3D virtual environments where multiple users can interact with each other using VR head-mounted displays (HMDs)
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Freeman and Maloney 2021
)
. Social VR platforms provide a sense of co-presence in which users feel as if they are physically present with others in the same virtual space
(
McVeigh-Schultz et al. 2019
)
. Given these unique affordances, Social VR has the potential to change how people engage, communicate, and collaborate in various activities, from social gatherings and entertainment to educational and professional teamwork settings
(
Deighan et al. 2023
)
. Platforms like VRChat have become popular spaces for individuals to connect in VR environments and foster a sense of community and well-being, particularly with the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
.

Unlike traditional video conferencing applications, Social VR allows people to interact and socialize in more immersive ways
(
Freeman and Acena 2021
)
. Social VR applications can enable remote users to interact within 3D virtual objects in immersive spaces
(
Freeman et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2021
)
, utilize avatars that can differ from their real bodies and appearances, employ 3D interactive tools that break the physical laws
(
Freeman et al. 2022
)
, and help people stay more engaged in the team conversation compared to other mediums
(
Abramczuk et al. 2023
;
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
;
Steinicke et al. 2020
)
. The unique affordances that VR provides can help users to communicate not only with words but also with gestures, body language, and eye gaze with the help of full-body tracked avatars
(
Freeman et al. 2022
)
.

Previous research shows that Social VR can enable people to interact in a similar way to face-to-face settings, helping people build a stronger bond between users
(
Yoon and Leem 2021
)
. For entertainment purposes, VR has demonstrated its potential to enhance shared experiences. For example, Lee et al.
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
developed a VR lobby for digital opera social experiences, offering an environment for future VR theater events. Their work highlights how Social VR can replicate and enrich live entertainment through immersive environments, allowing users to engage with cultural events in novel ways. Beyond entertainment, Young et al.
(
Young et al. 2023
)
demonstrated that Social VR can facilitate remote learning by enabling students to participate in discussions and activities, improving their sense of presence that often traditional online learning platforms do not provide. In another study, Sanaei et al.
(
Sanaei et al. 2023
)
conducted a controlled experiment to compare group interactions in face-to-face (F2F), video conferencing, and VR settings. The study found that interactions in VR led to more engagement among users in performing their collaboration tasks. Furthermore, participants in the VR condition reported feeling a similar sense of presence and connection with their team members as the participants in the F2F settings did. These studies suggest that Social VR can emulate the interactive aspects of in-person interactions that traditional online communication platforms lack.

Although Social VR has seen increasing use across various settings, its application for remote work and its promise as an online communication tool for group settings has not been fully examined.

2.3.
Collaboration Work in Social VR

Collaboration in Social VR refers to two or more people employing a Social VR application to work together and achieve a common goal
(
Freeman et al. 2022
)
. The embodied interactions in Social VR, such as avatar customization, nuanced non-verbal behaviors, and physical interactions, can facilitate a strong sense of being together and foster relationship building among distributed collaborators
(
Freeman and Acena 2021
)
. It also enables remote teams to engage in a wide range of collaborative activities that mirror in-person teamwork, from creative projects and problem-solving to mundane tasks and social experiences
(
Freeman et al. 2022
)
. Furthermore, Social VR allows remote teams to experience the interplay of productivity and sociality that often characterizes co-located collaboration
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
. In one study, Maloney et al.
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
conducted interviews to explore what makes activities on Social VR platforms meaningful to users. They found highlighted immersive interactions through full-body tracking and facilitating more natural and expressive communication as the main advantages of Social VR, overcoming the limitations of 2D video conferencing
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
. In another study, Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2021
)
argues that Social VR affords more natural social interactions than video conferencing, such as the ability for users to organically break off into small groups and interact with virtual objects in the shared environment. Unlike video conferencing, which restricts users to ’talking head experiences’—static, front-facing video feeds—Social VR allows for more physical activities and spontaneous collaborations that naturally arise from users’ interactions
(
Li et al. 2021
)
.

Since the COVID-19 pandemic led to cancel in-person conferences and meetings, many organizers transitioned to online events, often with mixed success in replicating the traditional conference experience
(
Moss et al. 2021
)
. This shift accelerated the adoption of remote collaboration and revealed both the strengths and weaknesses of current video conferencing tools
(
Moss et al. 2021
)
. As a consequence, several tech companies and researcher communities started examining the “Metaverse” as a potential technology for social gatherings and collaboration
(
Cheng et al. 2022
;
Duan et al. 2021
;
Gómez-Zará et al. 2023
)
. Within that spectrum, Social VR platforms gained increasing popularity for enabling people to interact synchronously in 3D virtual spaces. Platforms like “Altspace VR” could effectively create a sense of social presence, allowing remote participants to feel a sense of togetherness in a shared space
(
Pizzolante et al. 2023
)
. However, researchers also pointed out potential challenges, including accessibility issues, privacy concerns, and the need to establish new social norms for virtual interactions
(
Gómez-Zará et al. 2023
;
Moss et al. 2021
)
.

Given this growing research on Social VR and its applications, we aim to map how previous research has studied these technologies in the context of collaborations and teamwork. Although the CSCW community has extensively delved into several questions, analyses, and reflections on Social VR itself, there is still much to be learned about its implications for teams and remote collaborations. As technologies continue to evolve, shape, and influence how people work together
(
Harris et al. 2019
)
, the CSCW community has an opportunity to examine the unique role of Social VR in facilitating remote collaborations, as well as enabling practitioners and designers to build appropriate applications that become effective tools within future technological ecosystems
(
Gonzales et al. 2015
)
. By conducting a systematic literature review, we aim to map the different findings and contexts in which Social VR has been employed to facilitate remote collaborations. As a result, we can uncover potential areas for improvement and highlight design opportunities for future work.

3.
Methodology

We followed a scoping review methodology
(
Arksey and O’Malley 2005
)
to synthesize existing information and reveal trends in using Social VR technologies in collaboration. This methodology allows researchers to map the literature and identify gaps in a specific area of research
(
Arksey and O’Malley 2005
;
Levac et al. 2010
)
. Scoping reviews have several advantages: they are transparent, comprehensive, less prone to bias, and make it easier to reproduce the detailed information reported about each step of the review and how it is conducted
(
Pham et al. 2014
)
. They have gained prominence in the HCI community as they allow for the synthesis and comprehension of the development of different research directions
(
Balsara and Gómez-Zará 2024
;
Cosio et al. 2023
;
Bergram et al. 2022
;
Rogers et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2022
)
. We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to report the methods and results
(
Page et al. 2021
)
. PRISMA covers four stages in the review process: Identification, Screening, Eligibility, and Inclusion. We collected papers and tabulated them in a shared Google Spreadsheet, capturing metadata such as publication year, abstract, and keywords. Once the metadata was recorded, the authors of the articles were hidden to avoid potential bias during the coding phase.

3.1.
Search Strategy and Data Sources

To identify relevant articles for our systematic literature review, we developed a comprehensive search query that could capture the key concepts of our study while balancing sensitivity and specificity. After several iterations, we built our search query by breaking down our research topic into its core components: the technology (i.e., social VR), the activity (i.e., collaboration), and the groups (i.e., teams). For the technology concept, we included several synonyms and related terms to maximize coverage, namely “Social Virtual Reality,” “Social VR,” “Multi-user Virtual Reality,” and “Collaborative Virtual Environments.” We intentionally did not include the term “Virtual Reality” alone, as our focus was on Social VR applications specifically designed to enable interaction and collaboration rather than VR more broadly. For the collaboration concept, we chose a set of keywords focused on people working together: “Collaborative,” “Collaboration,” and “Cooperation.” We chose to add the term “cooperation” alongside “collaboration,” as the two are closely related and often used interchangeably in the literature
(
Stout and Keast 2021
)
. Lastly, for the groups concept, we included the words referring to teams: “Team,” “Teams,” “Teamwork,” “Group,” and “Groups.” These words defined the importance of studies analyzing multiple individuals or users within an unit. We combined the synonyms within each concept using the Boolean OR operator and then linked the three main concepts with the AND operator. As a result, our final query was:
(“Social Virtual Reality” OR “Social VR” OR “Multi-user Virtual Reality” OR “Collaborative Virtual Environments”) AND (Team OR Teams OR Teamwork OR Group OR Groups) AND (Collaborative OR Collaboration OR Cooperation).

We conducted the first step, “Identification,” using the following three databases: IEEE Xplore Digital Library, ACM Digital Library, and Web of Science. We chose these datasets since they cover the leading conferences and journals on human-computer interaction and team research. We searched for articles in these databases based on their titles, abstracts, and full text. We conducted the final search on June 5, 2024. We included articles published between 2004 and 2024, covering 20 years of collaborative Social VR research. We limited the included papers to the last 20 years to ensure that our corpus captured the most recent developments, which led to the significant boom in HMD technologies starting around the 2010s, including HTC Vive and Oculus Rift. We checked earlier studies, which began as early as 1993 in our search scope, and primarily focused on collaboration in virtual environments using screen-based technologies rather than HMDs. For the few early papers involving VR, their contributions were either revisited in later research or limited by the technological constraints of the time, making them less relevant to the modern landscape of Social VR research. We obtained 72 papers from the IEEE Xplore Digital Library, 882 papers from the ACM Digital Library, and 169 papers from the Web of Science database. Since the ACM Digital Library did not allow users to export the results from their website, we manually copied and pasted the ACM Digital Library’s results to a Google Spreadsheet. We exported the results of all these three databases from their website to a CSV file. We then compiled all the results into a single spreadsheet for analysis. In cases where we found two versions of an article (e.g., a conference paper later extended into a journal article), we only included the most recent version to avoid duplication. Based on the titles, we removed only one duplicated article. This left us with 1,123 articles for screening. Fig.
2
depicts the trend of paper counts for each year in our search corpus from 2004 to 2024.

3.2.
Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion
: We included articles that specifically addressed collaboration within Social VR environments, focusing on how Social VR can support people and organizations in collaborating and accomplish work-related tasks. Our inclusion criteria were:

•

Articles must involve two or more human users collaborating in a Social VR environment. Collaboration involves communication, coordination, and sharing of knowledge and resources among participants to achieve a joint objective.

•

Articles must focus on immersive Social VR environments experienced through head-mounted displays (HMDs).

•

The collaborative task or activity must have a clear goal relevant to a professional setting, such as problem-solving, decision-making, or a creative project. Simply meeting, learning, chatting without a shared work-related purpose, or casual socializing were not considered collaborative tasks for this review.

•

We included only full papers published in journals or conference proceedings to focus on more substantial and in-depth contributions to the literature on collaboration in Social VR.

Exclusion
: We decided to exclude articles that were not focused on our research questions and were not accessible to the research team. Our exclusion criteria were:

•

Articles that only examined interactions between virtual agents without any human users. Our interest was in understanding collaboration between real human users in Social VR.

•

Articles where the VR environment included only a single human participant interacting with virtual agents were also excluded, as this does not constitute a collaborative human team.

•

Articles that only examined desktop-based virtual environments or other screen setups.

•

Articles that primarily focused on other forms of extended reality (XR) technologies besides VR, such as augmented reality (AR) and mixed reality (MR). While these XR technologies are related to VR and can also support remote collaboration, they involve different technical setups, interaction modalities, and user experiences. To maintain a clear scope, we limited our review to papers that specifically examined collaboration within fully immersive virtual reality environments using head-mounted displays.

•

Articles primarily focused on (i) educational settings, such as teaching in classrooms or to children; (ii) training either for medical purposes or for specialized fields; and (iii) gaming, whether for entertainment or training purposes. We excluded these papers to concentrate the review on professional work contexts.

•

Workshop papers, magazines, posters, extended abstracts, and thesis papers. While potentially insightful, these formats were often too brief to provide a substantial contribution.

•

Articles written in languages other than English were excluded to avoid potential misinterpretation.

•

Articles that could not be accessed or downloaded from their original published source.

3.3.
Article Selection

The first author of the paper (i.e., the coder) manually screened the titles and abstracts of all 1,123 retrieved articles. The coder proceeded to screen articles for inclusion through a three-stage process. First, the coder reviewed the retrieved articles’ titles and keywords (i.e., level-one screening). The coder retained articles whose titles or keywords met the inclusion and exclusion eligibility criteria, resulting in 602 articles excluded and 521 articles selected for the next stage. Then, in the level-two screening, the coder performed a second review that included the articles’ titles, keywords, and abstracts. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the coder excluded 373 articles and selected 148 articles to read in full. In the third stage (“Eligibility”), the coder read and reviewed each article’s main content in full, excluding 97 articles and selecting 51 articles to build the final corpus. This selection process is explained in more detail in the results section and illustrated in Fig.
4
. The coder then presented the selected articles to the other co-authors, who reviewed the coder’s classification in a second cycle together. All authors agreed to analyze the 51 articles, and the coder continued with the data extraction and synthesis stage. Fig.
3
shows the yearly trend of the 51 articles included in our search corpus. The final list of included articles is available in the Supplementary Materials.

3.4.
Data Extraction and Synthesis

Once the final corpus was established, the coder extracted data to synthesize their characteristics, methods, and findings. Using a Google Spreadsheet, the coder extracted data pertaining to:

•

Year of publication

•

Research type: Bibliographic, Descriptive, Case Study, Laboratory Experiment, Explanatory, or Non-empirical.

•

Methodology: Quantitative, Qualitative, or Mixed Methods.

•

VR device type used in the study (i.e., HTC Vive, Oculus Rifts, Meta Quest)

•

Body tracking (i.e., full body or not)

•

Number of participants recruited in the study.

•

Number of participants per group.

•

Type of experimental task of the study

The coder also used a Google Doc to take open notes and memos that synthesized the key findings of each article. On average, the coder took 15 notes per article. These notes provided more context and details on the technologies, design processes, and results described in each article.

3.5.
Thematic Analysis

After completing the coding process, the authors met again to discuss the initial codes to summarize and integrate the articles’ findings across the corpus. We conducted a thematic analysis to create cohesive themes that described this final corpus
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
. The goal of thematic analysis is to discover themes from the data while minimizing confirmation bias. The coder began by familiarizing themselves with the articles and taking notes on the main contributions of the paper, as well as the ideas mentioned throughout. These notes were compiled in a Google spreadsheet and used to inductively generate codes without trying to fit these codes into preexisting coding frameworks or theories
(
Nowell et al. 2017
;
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
. The codes were not mutually exclusive since the articles could address multiple codes. The coder iterated the initial codes through several rounds of adjustments until no new ideas emerged from them. Following this, the codes were collected into themes, which also underwent iterative rounds of renaming to create more accurate descriptions. Through several meetings, all authors discussed different iterations of the themes to ensure they all agreed upon the definition and boundaries of each theme. Finally, we integrated and synthesized the final themes into three overarching categories and nine subcategories.

4.2.
Thematic Analysis

Our thematic analysis uncovered three main themes that we called (1) “Individual Perception and Experience of Collaboration in Social VR,” (2) “Team Dynamics and Collaboration in Virtual Environments,” and (3) “Enabling Collaboration through Social VR Affordances: Technical Elements and User Interactions.” These themes are evidence of an increasing focus on employing Social VR to support collaboration by leveraging user experience, team dynamics, and environmental affordances. Moreover, they uncover significant gaps and future research directions, especially in understanding specific user interactions and technical elements that could better facilitate these collaborative experiences.

The first theme includes articles addressing the foundational aspects of individual perception and experience within Social VR environments. We divided this theme into two sub-themes: “Am I Really There?” which highlights how Social VR can simulate users’ physical presence, enhancing their interaction and engagement with the collaborative task. The second sub-theme, “How Am I Perceived Here?” explores avatar design and its impact on collaborative dynamics and engagement, emphasizing the importance of avatar customization to shape user interactions and self-expression within virtual spaces.

The second theme centers on team dynamics and collaboration in virtual environments, discussing how Social VR can improve team collaboration through social presence. This theme is further divided into four sub-themes: “Are We Really Together?” which focuses on improving team collaboration through social presence; “Is This Collaboration Real?” which addresses how users perceive the realness of collaboration in VR; “Should I Trust this Person?” which explores trust-building as a critical component of effective virtual collaboration; and “Are We Fully Engaged?” which examines the importance of social engagement in VR collaboration and how different factors influence user involvement.

The final theme examines how collaboration is enabled by Social VR affordances, which are the technical elements and designed interactions that facilitate realistic and functional virtual environments. This theme includes three sub-themes: “Creating Realistic and Functional Virtual Environments,” which discusses the design of virtual spaces that mimic real-world settings to improve user experience; “Facilitating Interaction through Virtual Tools and Objects,” which highlights the importance of object manipulation and tool use in collaborative tasks; and “Enhancing Immersion through Spatial Audio and Movement,” which underscores the role of spatial audio and coordinated movement in providing an immersive collaborative experience.

4.2.1.
Individual Perception and Experience of Collaboration in Social VR

“Am I Really There?”
Sense of Presence as a Foundation for Immersive Collaboration.

Articles in this sub-theme studied the sense of presence and the benefits of Social VR environments. While the sense of presence refers to the subjective experience of being physically present in a computed-simulated virtual environment, social presence (or co-presence) relates to the feeling of being there with other people
(
Oh et al. 2018
)
. In this sub-theme, we focus on the individual level, exploring the sense of presence as a foundational experience for immersive collaboration in Social VR environments. Overall, these identified articles reported that Social VR users can experience high levels of physical presence as they feel a strong sense of “being there.” Users can feel present in a virtual environment when they can turn their intentions into actions
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
, such as grabbing, rotating, moving, and interacting with virtual objects. Additionally, users can feel more present in the Social VR application by navigating through a large virtual space to explore, which is harder to achieve with traditional video conferencing tools
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
. In one study, Olaosebikan et al.
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
, conducted an observational study to study scientific collaboration in VR meeting applications. Participants reported experiencing a greater sense of presence compared to traditional video conferencing platforms because of the immersive qualities of VR. They remarked that the VR application made their interactions more engaging and closely mirrored the dynamics of a live presentation rather than the often static nature of a typical videoconferencing meeting.

Another article demonstrated that the sense of presence in Social VR is influenced by various factors beyond just spatial navigation. Khojasteh et al.
(
Khojasteh and Won 2021
)
conducted a 5-session longitudinal study where participants completed different collaborative VR tasks with new partners each time. They found that as users spent more time working together on collaborative tasks in virtual environments, their sense of presence increased, suggesting that familiarity with the virtual space improves the feeling of “being there.” Yassien et al.’s
(
Yassien et al. 2020
)
systematic review of Social VR literature revealed that self-embodiment is important to establish a strong sense of presence. They found that users can feel more present in Social VR when having a complete virtual body that fully matches their physical movements rather than seeing and perceiving a few parts of their bodies
(
Yassien et al. 2020
)
. Lastly, Torro et al.
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
discussed how creating a strong sense of presence in collaborative VR environments enables users to feel genuinely immersed in the virtual space, which can significantly advance communication and teamwork. When users perceive themselves as truly present, they can interact more naturally as they would in a physical setting. This immersive experience helps users engage more deeply with the virtual environment, enabling them to perform tasks and collaborate with others as if they were physically together. By fostering such an environment, organizations can use VR to simulate and potentially enhance face-to-face interactions.

“How Am I Perceived Here?”
Avatar Design Shaping Collaborative Dynamics and Engagement.

In this sub-theme, articles explored how avatar representation influences individual engagement, experience, and behavior in Social VR environments. The ability to customize avatars enables users to tailor their virtual representation, improving their engagement and potentially shaping their interactions within collaborative experiences. For example, Osborne et al.
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
showed that aesthetic choices in avatar customization–such as stylization and realism–can significantly boost user engagement. This article also reported that many Social VR users enjoy the aesthetic style and level of abstraction they can choose for their avatars, as these elements contribute to the types of social encounters they prefer. The customization process goes beyond mere appearance, often serving as a tool for self-expression and identity exploration in virtual worlds
(
Freeman et al. 2020
;
Osborne et al. 2023
)
.

Another article
(
Freeman et al. 2020
)
explored how Social VR users perceive and experience their avatars by conducting a descriptive study involving 30 semi-structured in-depth interviews. The authors discovered that many users viewed avatar experiences as an engaging and embodied method to explore their identity, particularly beneficial for those questioning their gender or sexual identity
(
Freeman et al. 2020
)
. Users often perceived their avatars as direct extensions of themselves rather than mere representations, demonstrating a strong desire to create avatars that closely resembled their self-image. These virtual representations can help users discover new aspects of themselves or gain confidence to make real-life changes.

In addition to aiding identity exploration, articles showed that gender representation significantly influences an individual’s behavior and self-perception in Social VR, even when the avatar’s gender differs from the user’s real-world gender
(
Yassien et al. 2021
;
Freeman et al. 2020
)
. Yassien et al.
(
Yassien et al. 2021
)
designed an experiment where pairs collaborated on nonverbal virtual car repair tasks in a virtual garage. They changed the partner’s virtual gender while performing the tasks and measured the impact on collaboration quality. The study found that avatar gender seemed more influential than the person’s real gender. When avatar genders matched (e.g., both avatars being male or female), users’ productivity and mutual support were enhanced during collaborative tasks
(
Yassien et al. 2021
)
. Participants interacted naturally even when their avatar’s gender was swapped, highlighting a design opportunity to improve collaborative experiences and mitigate gender stereotypes.

Lastly, articles from this sub-theme showed that avatar design can be broadly categorized into humanoid and non-humanoid types
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. Humanoid avatars are realistic representations, often created by capturing a user’s image or using a photo uploaded to the VR platform. In contrast, non-humanoid avatars are more abstract in design
(
Osborne et al. 2023
;
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
. While photorealistic (humanoid) avatars can potentially help establish trust due to familiarity, they often fall into the “uncanny valley,” causing discomfort and disrupting immersion
(
Osborne et al. 2023
;
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
. Osborne et al.
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
conducted an analysis of various Social VR meeting applications, including VRChat and Spatial, examining design choices and their impact on social interaction. Their research revealed that users often experienced discomfort when interacting with highly realistic avatars that approached but did not quite achieve human likeness, potentially disrupting their sense of immersion
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. This discomfort is attributed to the uncanny valley effect, a phenomenon where an artificial being becomes increasingly realistic until it reaches a point where it feels unsettling or disturbing due to its near-human likeness but noticeable imperfections
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. Creating an avatar that closely resembles a user but still falls short of a true likeness leads to an unsettling uncanny valley effect, as the slight dissimilarities make the avatar feel unnatural. As a consequence, the uncanny valley effect can significantly hinder collaboration in VR environments, reducing users’ sense of presence and leading to less effective communication during virtual collaboration
(
Osborne et al. 2023
;
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
.

4.2.2.
Team Dynamics and Collaboration in Virtual Environments

“Are We Really Together?”
Social Presence Enhancing Team Collaboration.

Articles in this sub-theme described how social presence (i.e., the ability to feel others present in a VR environment) is a key component for creating realistic and engaging Social VR experiences
(
Yassien et al. 2020
;
Khojasteh and Won 2021
)
. Olaosebikan et al.
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
conducted an observational study and interviewed two groups of interdisciplinary scientists who collaborated remotely using commercially available VR meeting applications (i.e., Spatial and Nanome). They analyzed how scientists utilized shared artifacts, embodied interaction, and spatial interaction for ongoing scientific collaboration. Scientists reported they felt others’ presence in the VR applications as in a face-to-face meeting. They noted that giving a talk in VR felt closer to giving an in-person talk compared to giving the same talk but on Zoom
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
. These findings suggest that the immersive nature of VR enhances presence and leads to more engaging collaboration than video conferencing
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
;
Khojasteh and Won 2021
;
Park et al. 2024
)
.

These articles also highlight how avatars can leverage users’ social presence, as both avatar embodiment and representation play a critical role in their interactions and impressions. Young et al.
(
Young et al. 2015
)
show that full-body avatar representation increases social presence and improves the performance of collaborative tasks since it provides users with a more realistic bodily experience that mimics real-world movements and synchronizes with their own actions to enhance the presence of others. In that study, the researchers investigated dyadic high-five interactions in VR, with participants performing high-fives in both collocated (same physical space) and displaced (separate spaces) settings. Their findings showed that full-body avatars not only led to better performance but also improved social presence compared to hands-only avatars
(
Young et al. 2015
)
.

Moreover, the articles also show that the design of the virtual environment is an important factor in boosting social presence. In one article
(
Weissker et al. 2023
)
, the researchers compared four virtual auditorium designs, focusing on features like improved visibility and improved feelings of togetherness. Participants rated auditoriums with round group tables, which promote co-watching and social exchanges, significantly higher on social presence than those without these features.

According to these articles, non-verbal cues such as gaze, facial expressions, gestures, body language, and mood can enhance both social presence and awareness by conveying teammates’ active engagement in the task—far more effectively than lifeless, awkward staring
(
Kimmel et al. 2023
;
Mei et al. 2021
;
Gumilar et al. 2022
)
. Kimmel et al.
(
Kimmel et al. 2023
)
investigated how four types of facial expressions (i.e., neutral, eyes only, mouth only, eyes and mouth) affected social presence in two collaborative VR tasks involving verbal and graphical explanations. They found that users’ facial expressions, especially mouth movements, significantly increased perceived co-presence compared to neutral expressions, as evidenced by higher co-presence scores in the “Networked Minds Social Presence Inventory” and increased gaze duration when mouth movements were rendered on avatars
(
Kimmel et al. 2023
)
. Another study by Gumilar et al.
(
Gumilar et al. 2022
)
compared three different gaze directions (i.e., averted, direct, and natural eye contact) during a collaborative finger-tracking task in VR and found that natural gaze resulted in higher visual coordination and mutual awareness compared to the absence of gaze cues.

Lastly, these articles reveal that the lack of facial expressions and emotional connections in Social VR can significantly decrease the feeling of social presence, resulting in reduced engagement in collaborative activities
(
Mei et al. 2021
;
Kimmel et al. 2023
)
. In a cake co-design study
(
Mei et al. 2021
)
, one of the participants indicated that the lack of avatar reactions made their interaction feel more like a telephone conversation than a face-to-face meeting. Several articles in this sub-theme addressed these challenges by proposing collaborative visualization cues, such as visual indicators (e.g., arrows showing a partner’s movement direction) and symbolic gestures (e.g., a thumbs-up) to their collaborative VR applications to enhance social presence
(
Bovo et al. 2022
;
García et al. 2008
;
Sarmiento et al. 2014
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Kimmel et al. 2023
;
Ide et al. 2020
;
Le Tarnec et al. 2024
)
. Bovo et al.
(
Bovo et al. 2022
)
proposed the Cone of Vision (CoV), a visualization technique that enhances visual coordination and mutual awareness in collaborative exploratory data analysis (EDA) tasks. In their study, they compared three conditions: no CoV, mono-directional CoV in which participants could see only their collaborator’s CoV, and bi-directional CoV in which they could see both their own and their collaborator’s CoV. The results showed that bi-directional CoV led to higher visual coordination, measured by the time spent looking at the same target, while both mono-directional and bi-directional CoV improved mutual awareness of visual attention
(
Bovo et al. 2022
)
. In another study, Ide et al.
(
Ide et al. 2020
)
conducted a within-subject experiment involving brainstorming tasks under three conditions: face-to-face interaction, VR without symbolic gestures, and VR with symbolic gestures. In the third condition, participants could choose from nine types of symbolic gestures for their avatars, including bowing, thumbs up, nodding, and laughing. Participants reported that these symbolic gestures helped them express their intentions and feelings more easily compared to the other conditions
(
Ide et al. 2020
)
. Although these articles have highlighted the importance of non-verbal cues in enhancing social presence in collaboration, they also pointed out that many of these cues still remain underdeveloped in many collaborative Social VR applications
(
Kimmel et al. 2023
;
Argelaguet et al. 2011
;
Gumilar et al. 2022
)
.

In summary, these articles demonstrate the importance of avatar representation, environmental design, non-verbal cues, and visualization techniques in enhancing social presence in Social VR environments.

“Is This Collaboration Real?”
Perceiving Teamwork’ Realness in Social VR

This sub-theme includes articles that focus on how users perceive their collaborations in VR as real
(
Park et al. 2024
)
. According to Torro et al.
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
, one key illusion that contributes to the feeling of realism in VR is the “social presence illusion,” which involves the technology’s capacity to help users perceive other avatars as real social actors. When this illusion is well-supported by the technology, users are more likely to perceive VR interactions as real, allowing for a natural replication of social dynamics and nonverbal communication
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
. In another study, Maloney et al.
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
conducted in-depth interviews with 30 Social VR users to explore what makes activities on these platforms meaningful to them. They expressed a desire for more realistic natural settings that could allow them to experience authentic interactions with their collaborators
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
. For example, the study found that participants asked for more natural thumbs-up gestures rather than the constant use of boxing glove-like hands
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
.

In addition to providing authentic interactions, these articles examined the impact of avatars’ appearance on users’ experiences of realism and the naturalness of their interactions
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. Osborne et al.
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
conducted a landscape analysis of seven commercial VR meeting applications to understand design choices and their impact on social interaction. This study highlighted the challenges of current realistic avatars due to the uncanny valley, where users reported their discomfort when interacting with avatars that appeared almost human but were flawed in certain ways. Some mentioned cases included avatar glitches that led to people having multiple avatars or when users chose an avatar that was somewhat similar to them but still significantly different from their actual appearance
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
.

In their pursuit of realism, Zigbang et al.
(
Park et al. 2024
)
studied how a South Korean company relocated their employees to a metaverse platform called Soma
1
1

1

https://www.somaworld.com/
. Unlike common Social VR applications that support features like tele-transportation, the designers of this virtual workplace opted to emphasize realism by removing those unrealistic features. Instead, the company’s employees could only navigate the virtual workplace by walking through hallways, taking elevators, and moving between spaces. By forcing participants to navigate and walk through this virtual space, employers were more likely to have spontaneous encounters with their colleagues in common areas, increasing social interaction
(
Park et al. 2024
)
. Although this heightened realism fostered a stronger sense of presence, it introduced some logistical inefficiencies, highlighting the balance between immersive experience and workplace productivity that future Social VR applications may need to consider.

“Should I Trust this Person?”
Enabling Trust in Social VR

Two articles within our search scope addressed trust-building in Social VR environments, highlighting both the importance of this topic and the existing research gap
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
;
Yassien et al. 2020
)
. Building and maintaining interpersonal trust through remote working applications is difficult due to the lack of social cues and limited informal interactions
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
.

Torro et al.
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
developed an information systems design theory for social exchange in Social VR-enabled virtual teams. Their study draws from affordance and social exchange theories to explain how Social VR material properties enable and foster social exchange. Their findings suggest that trust in Social VR environments can be built through both peripheral and central routes
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
. While the peripheral route involves initial impressions based on social categories and reputations, the central route involves deeper evaluations of traits like ability, integrity, and benevolence. To support trust-building, Torro et al.
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
recommended designing Social VR applications that facilitate both verbal and non-verbal communication. This approach aligns with the peripheral and central routes of trust-building by enabling users to engage in rich, immersive interactions that can improve initial impressions and deeper evaluations of trustworthiness. For example, they suggested avatars with higher behavioral realism that can enhance mimicry and social bonding activities. Additionally, Torro et al.
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
suggested creating “virtual break rooms” to encourage casual and informal interactions without strict rules or roles. This setting encourages reciprocity, as individuals feel more comfortable expressing themselves and responding to others positively
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
.

The second article shows that respecting personal space in Social VR is another relevant aspect of trust and social bonding
(
Yassien et al. 2020
)
. The researchers found that violations of personal space can lead to discomfort and negative experiences, potentially undermining trust and social bonds
(
Yassien et al. 2020
)
. To examine the effect of personal space on trust, the study recommended designing Social VR environments with mechanisms that predict and prevent collisions or unwanted proximity between avatars. Their research suggested that ensuring users feel secure and respected in their virtual interactions is key to fostering trust
(
Yassien et al. 2020
)
.

Both studies highlight the potential of Social VR to enhance trust-building through features that are not available in traditional online collaboration tools. For instance, avatar customization can encourage self-disclosure, while spatial awareness features can create more natural social interactions
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
;
Yassien et al. 2020
)
.

“Are We Fully Engaged?”
The Importance of Social Engagement in VR Collaboration.

Articles in this sub-theme explored how different factors influence user engagement during collaboration in Social VR. Social engagement directly impacts communication, a key component of successful collaboration
(
Le Tarnec et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
. To strengthen social engagement, articles proposed innovative solutions to further enhance user involvement. Non-verbal behaviors, including facial expressions, gaze, and body movements, play a crucial role in conveying engagement and maintaining interaction in collaborative settings
(
Le Tarnec et al. 2024
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
. One such article, “VRinteract,” involves an augmented avatar model that displays non-verbal engagement behaviors, even when not explicitly performed by the user. The results showed that improving avatars with non-verbal cues significantly increased user engagement during collaboration
(
Le Tarnec et al. 2024
)
. In another example, Xu et al.
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
conducted a qualitative study examining factors influencing social engagement during a collaborative VR travel planning task involving older adults. The results showed a a strong link between conversations about personal lives in VR and social engagement, highlighting the importance of allowing users to share their life experiences
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
.

The visual representation of avatars also plays a key role in attentional engagement, which refers to the level of attention an individual dedicates to their collaborators during interaction
(
Li et al. 2024
)
. In Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2024
)
’s hands-only vs. full-body avatar study, they observed that full-body avatars were perceived as more socially engaged collaborators. Another article developed “CollaVR,” a system to support multiple users in reviewing a VR video together while wearing headsets
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
. The system provided awareness visualizations to help users understand collaborators’ activities and view directions in the headset. View-sharing tools such as ’peek,’ which lets users see each other’s in-headset view, ’follow in time,’ which allows one user to control the video timeline for synchronized playback, and ’slave,’ which synchronizes both the video timeline and view direction with a collaborator for precise coordination, enable users to establish a shared context for collaboration and foster further engagement
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
. Their results show that participants using CollaVR engaged in more implicit communication, spent more time viewing videos together, and used collaborative note-taking compared to the baseline condition, where those collaboration features were absent
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
.

4.2.3.
Enabling Collaboration through Social VR Affordances: Technical Elements and User Interactions.

Creating Realistic and Functional Virtual Environments.

This sub-theme’s articles focused on how the levels of realism affect Social VR users’ experience, presence, and interaction. Realism in virtual environments extends beyond visual fidelity, affecting the sense of co-presence and social interaction of users
(
Mei et al. 2021
)
. High levels of realism in virtual reality workspaces, particularly those that resemble physical office spaces, can boost users’ sense of being in a shared environment with others
(
Park et al. 2024
)
. This increased realism can lead to more natural and engaging social interactions, potentially improving collaboration outcomes
(
Park et al. 2024
;
Mei et al. 2021
)
. Higher realism creates a sense of reality among collaborators, making them feel as if they are working in a real-world scenario, allowing them to perform tasks more naturally
(
Park et al. 2024
;
Mei et al. 2021
)
. In one of the articles, participants of an interview study emphasized the importance of the social atmosphere, preferring environments that resembled real cafes or cozy places
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
. Nevertheless, the realism of Social VR environments has only been addressed in a few studies. Mei et al.
(
Mei et al. 2021
)
’s cake co-designing experiment found that the absence of realistic elements in the work environment led to a reduced sense of presence among users. Missing elements such as virtual baking tools, environmental noise (e.g., the sound of a crowd or background music), and realistic avatars were among the key aspects participants noted as necessary to enhance the sense of presence
(
Mei et al. 2021
)
.

The design of virtual environments significantly influences collaborative experiences and users’ perception of realism in Social VR. Osborne et al.
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
categorized Social VR environments into three types: “Skeuomorphic,” “Experimental,” and “Prefabs-based.” Skeuomorphic environments are pre-built and mimic real-life business settings, such as conference rooms and scrum boards, providing a familiar atmosphere for users. Experimental environments, on the other hand, are highly creative and user-editable, allowing extensive customization and fostering innovation
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. Prefabs-based environments offer pre-built meeting templates that users can personalize using built-in prefabs, striking a balance between structure and customization
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. The article emphasizes the importance of enabling more flexible environmental customization to improve collaboration by allowing users to tailor virtual spaces to their specific needs
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
.

Furthermore, the ability of VR technology to create an environment that responds to users’ sensorimotor actions can make them feel as though they are truly present
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
. When this effect is well-supported, users are more likely to perceive the virtual setting as real, enabling a natural replication of social dynamics and communication
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
.

Private spaces are also essential and resemble real-world scenarios in which groups need privacy for conversation without disturbances
(
Freeman et al. 2022
;
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
. This design aspect increases the realism of the environment, leading to more natural collaboration
(
Freeman et al. 2022
;
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
. Research has explored different workspace configurations, such as public spaces, invisible personal spaces, and visible personal spaces
(
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
. Men et al.
(
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
conducted a study using the “LeMo” system, a Social VR platform for collaborative music creation. Participants used three different space configurations to compose music collaboratively, and the research team analyzed their interactions, preferences, and activity measures. Their findings indicated that personal workspaces are essential for successful collaboration, with a preference for publicly visible personal spaces over invisible ones, suggesting a beneficial balance between privacy and transparency
(
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
.

The articles also show that the size and scale of virtual environments significantly impact collaborative tasks
(
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
;
Freiwald et al. 2020
)
. Articles highlighted the importance of adapting communication tools to the spatial context, calling designers to consider how the scale of virtual spaces affects user interaction and information sharing. Freiwald et al.
(
Freiwald et al. 2020
)
compared three different visual indicator methods for VR collaboration: “Object Highlighting,” which visually emphasizes the object a user is focusing on by altering its appearance; “View Cones,” which represent the boundaries of a user’s field of view; and “Video Mirroring,” which displays a direct video feed of what the user sees in their head-mounted display on a virtual screen. The study tested these methods to determine which is most effective for VR collaboration scenarios. Their findings show that “View Cones” method was perceived to have the highest social presence, and “Object Highlighting” had some situational benefits. Yet, “Video Mirroring” emerged as the overall most effective method for virtual collaboration across different scales and environment sizes because it provided a direct view of the user’s perspective, allowing tasks to be completed accurately and with fewer mistakes
(
Freiwald et al. 2020
)
.

Replicating real-world experiences in Social VR environments is another important consideration to promote collaboration
(
Osborne et al. 2023
;
Saffo et al. 2021
;
Park et al. 2024
)
. Some of the identified articles have successfully replicated in-person experiments in VR settings, demonstrating the potential for VR to serve as a platform for both research and real-world task simulation
(
Osborne et al. 2023
;
Saffo et al. 2021
)
. For instance, Saffo et al.
(
Saffo et al. 2021
)
replicated a qualitative tabletop collaboration study in “VRChat” to demonstrate the practicality, validity, and ethics of conducting VR user studies within a Social VR platform. This ability to recreate physical scenarios in Social VR opens up new possibilities for training, education, and collaborative problem-solving
(
Saffo et al. 2021
)
.

Finally, the design of virtual environments can influence users’ behavioral patterns. Different workspace configurations can lead to distinct territory and working arrangements, such as side-by-side or face-to-face setups
(
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
. In the study on collaboratively composed music by Men et al.
(
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
, seven groups worked side-by-side, facing the same direction towards the music interfaces, while five groups worked face-to-face with music interfaces between them. Side-by-side allowed a shared perspective but unequal access to interfaces, while face-to-face provided equal access and visibility but required specially designed directionless interfaces
(
Men and Bryan-Kinns 2019
)
. These findings highlighted how the virtual environment’s design can subtly shape collaboration dynamics and user interactions.

Facilitating Interaction through Virtual Tools and Objects.

Collaboration in Social VR also depends on the artifacts within the simulated environment
(
Roberts et al. 2004
;
Roberts et al. 2005
)
. The identified articles showed that many collaboration tasks were closely coupled tasks, which were centered on shared object manipulation
(
Otto et al. 2006
)
. Closely coupled collaboration involves a strong connection between object manipulation and human interaction, where participants’ actions are interdependent and require synchronous or sequential interaction with the same or different object attributes
(
Roberts et al. 2004
;
Otto et al. 2006
)
. For these kinds of tasks, effective collaboration relies on having all team members able to perceive and understand the shared objects they are working with.

To facilitate object manipulation tasks and ensure consistent feedback on user actions, one study developed a Gazebo construction task with four object-sharing scenarios: planning the task, passing tools, moving objects together, and assembling components simultaneously
(
Roberts et al. 2004
)
. This approach revealed challenges like network delays, event loss, and inconsistencies in object states between remote users
(
Roberts et al. 2004
)
. Another experiment also focused on manipulating a virtual Gazebo and showed that visual cues—such as arrows indicating movement direction or signals for when an object was close to falling—significantly enhanced task performance and reduced errors
(
García et al. 2008
)
.

In addition to closely coupled tasks, the articles highlighted the importance of supporting object manipulation for other kinds of collaborative tasks. In one study
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
, the researchers conducted an observational study of commercial VR meeting applications and highlighted that physically interacting with data, like writing or handling virtual objects, improved participants’ ability to remember information compared to merely reading from a 2D screen. However, the article also pointed out several limitations due to the technology’s low realism
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
. For instance, the developed white-boarding tools were limited, while sticky notes served as an alternative, only allowing one person to edit at a time, which could hinder effective collaboration
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
.

Lastly, the articles showed a variety of virtual tools that facilitate interaction in Social VR environments. Pens, markers, whiteboards, and laser pointers, among others, play an important role in improving collaborative activities
(
Freeman et al. 2022
)
. For instance, the “Maker Pen” tool allows users to create 3D models, adding a layer of physicality that makes the virtual workspace feel more tangible than simply interacting with a flat-screen
(
Freeman et al. 2022
)
. Yet, the availability and ease of use of these tools vary across platforms. For example, Osborne et al.
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
showed that AltspaceVR provided the lowest quantity of shared tools among six other commercial VR meeting applications. One potential reason could be the inconvenient way of using those tools. Some tools, such as spawning 3D objects, are restricted unless permissions are granted, and presenting in AltspaceVR requires participants to install specific software, which can further complicate the process
(
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
)
.

Enhancing Immersion through Spatial Audio and Movement.

The last sub-theme explores the role of spatial audio and movement in enhancing collaboration within Social VR environments. Immohr et al.
(
Immohr et al. 2023
)
conducted a study comparing VR communication scenarios with spatial and non-spatial audio to face-to-face interactions, measuring social presence and conversational behavior. Although binaural audio showed potential benefits, it did not significantly enhance social presence or alter participant behavior in this specific two-party negotiation task involving apartment room assignments. This outcome suggests that the task might not have fully leveraged spatial audio’s capabilities, indicating a need for further exploration in more dynamic scenarios
(
Immohr et al. 2023
)
. Similarly, Rios et al.
(
Ríos et al. 2018
)
examined locomotor behavior differences between real and virtual environments during a collaborative puzzle-solving task. They tested conditions involving synchronized avatar animations and footstep sounds with participant movements. While the synchronism of animations and footstep sounds did not greatly impact users’ sense of presence or control over their avatars, these features did lead to participants navigating the virtual environment with smaller clearance values, indicating more precise movement in the VR space
(
Ríos et al. 2018
)
.

Movement also plays an important role in enhancing immersion, particularly in collaborative tasks that require joint navigation. While individual navigation is common in Social VR, collaborative tasks demand group coordination, which can be challenging
(
Weissker 2020
;
Weissker and Froehlich 2021
)
. Researchers have proposed various group navigation techniques to address these challenges
(
Weissker 2020
;
Weissker and Froehlich 2021
;
Naef et al. 2004
)
. For instance, Weissker et al.
(
Weissker 2020
)
developed a two-user jumping technique allowing navigators to adjust group formations while specifying targets, augmenting adaptability during jumps. Another study introduced a group navigation method for virtual museum tours using short-distance teleportation, supporting up to ten users with formation-preserving jumps and preview avatars for enhanced comprehensibility
(
Weissker and Froehlich 2021
)
. Lastly, Naef et al.
(
Naef et al. 2004
)
developed the “blue-c API” that supports synchronized navigation, allowing users to move collectively rather than individually, which helps maintain group cohesion and improve the collaborative experience.

Together, these studies highlight the potential of spatial audio and movement synchronization to enhance the immersive and collaborative experience in Social VR, while underscoring the importance of task design and group coordination in fully realizing their benefits.

5.
Discussion

This paper aims to explore how Social VR has been studied over the past two decades as a tool to support collaboration and teamwork. By systematically reviewing the literature, we examined how Social VR applications leverage their unique affordances to address the challenges of remote collaboration and identified critical gaps for future research. We systematically reviewed studies at the intersection of Social VR, teams, and collaboration purposes. We identified 51 relevant research articles that introduced novel technologies, examined the effects of VR on social dynamics, and characterized how the different affordances and elements of Social VR impact collaboration. Through a thematic analysis, we found three comprehensive themes that describe how these articles addressed team collaboration at the individual, group, and environmental levels (See Table
3
). In the following subsections, we elaborate on the key findings of this systematic literature review. In particular, we focus on how the findings outline social VR application within this field, identifying gaps in this corpus, and outlining potential opportunities for future research directions.

5.1.
RQ1: How have previous studies addressed team collaboration in Social VR?

These findings align with early HCI and CSCW research, which highlighted the challenges of distributed collaboration. Olson et al.
(
Olson and Olson 2000
)
emphasized the critical role of spatial and social presence in overcoming the barriers of remote teamwork, a challenge that Social VR is uniquely positioned to address. Using its immersive affordances, Social VR has the potential to build on these early insights and redefine how teams collaborate across distances.

Our review highlights how Social VR distinguishes itself from traditional online communication technologies, particularly in its ability to enhance group interactions through immersive and interactive environments. Unlike tools like video conferencing, Social VR provides a heightened sense of presence and social connection, enabling more natural and engaging collaboration. The identified corpus shows that Social VR applications can expand the current technological ecosystem and provide new tools for remote collaborations. Compared to traditional online communication technologies, the unique affordances and characteristics enabled by Social VR can enhance team collaboration by providing immersive spaces and supporting richer social interactions
(
Maloney and Freeman 2020
)
. For example, Social VR technologies can offer an increased sense of presence and social presence by enabling their physical presence in a simulated place and allowing natural interactions within these places
(
McVeigh-Schultz et al. 2019
)
. In the same manner, the articles posit that Social VR can be helpful for working on coupled tasks and other collaborative tasks that involve manipulating objects, such as problem-solving and co-designing
(
Kocur et al. 2020
;
Mei et al. 2021
;
Osborne et al. 2023
)
, since they can mimic real-world settings better than videoconferencing or 2D screen applications.

Moreover, the articles emphasized avatar customization as a significant factor in shaping collaborative dynamics
(
Freeman et al. 2020
;
Osborne et al. 2023
;
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
. The ability to personalize avatars (including their gender) not only enhances user engagement but also influences how individuals perceive themselves and others within the virtual environment
(
Kimmel et al. 2023
;
Mei et al. 2021
;
Gumilar et al. 2022
)
. This customization supports identity exploration and can impact collaboration quality by affecting trust and reliable interactions
(
Freeman et al. 2020
;
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. Furthermore, the design of social VR environments plays an important role in supporting collaboration. Realistic and functional virtual spaces that replicate physical office settings and provide customization can enhance the sense of presence and improve collaborative outcomes
(
Park et al. 2024
;
Mei et al. 2021
)
. In contrast to other technologies, Social VR offers technical elements such as spatial audio
(
Immohr et al. 2023
)
, movement synchronization
(
Weissker 2020
;
Weissker and Froehlich 2021
;
Naef et al. 2004
)
, and interactive virtual tools that facilitate realistic user interactions
(
Roberts et al. 2005
;
Olaosebikan et al. 2022
;
Freeman et al. 2022
)
. These advantages allow for more natural communication between team members, thus improving the overall collaborative experience.

Overall, the articles have explored the potential of Social VR to transform team collaboration by designing and testing immersive experiences that closely replicate real-world interactions. Realism, presence, and interactive tools are the main affordances examined by these studies, which have focused strongly on differentiating these immersive technologies from traditional online communication applications. Future research should focus on refining these technologies to improve user engagement, trust building, and social presence, ensuring that Social VR can effectively support diverse collaborative activities in various contexts.

5.2.
RQ2: What are the specific gaps, future prospects, and research directions in this area?

While this system literature review highlights numerous contributions provided by the identified corpus, it also reveals multiple research gaps to comprehensively examine the role of Social VR technologies across all stages of collaboration and teamwork processes. Much of the current research has focused on short-term interactions, typically lasting only a few hours or days. This raises the need for future studies to explore how Social VR can sustain long-term collaboration, particularly by developing features that support ongoing communication, relationship building, and task management. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for Social VR’s adoption in organizational settings requiring sustained teamwork. Moreover, most articles in our review centered on small group settings (primarily two users) or specific collaborative scenarios, such as design tasks or problem-solving exercises. While these studies provided valuable insights, they do not fully capture the range of collaborative activities that teams engage in, particularly in larger organizational settings. Future research should explore how Social VR can be scaled to support larger teams and more complex, open-ended projects that mirror real-world collaborative work.

Another identified gap is the lack of standardized evaluation frameworks for assessing collaboration effectiveness in Social VR environments. Current research lacks consistent metrics and methodologies for measuring collaborative success, making it difficult to compare different approaches and solutions. Additionally, most existing studies rely heavily on participant’s self-report measures, which can introduce bias. Incorporating physiological data such as eye tracking or heart rate monitoring could provide more objective insights into user experiences during collaboration.

We found that several articles lacked diversity in their user samples. Many studies involved homogeneous groups, often lacking representation across different ages, cultural backgrounds, and technical expertise levels. This limits the generalizability of these studies’ findings and highlights the need for more inclusive research that considers diverse user populations. Furthermore, while avatar customization is a popular feature in Social VR, its impact on collaboration dynamics is not fully understood. Future research should explore the balance between personalization and standardization in avatars to enhance team cohesion without compromising individual expression.

The inclusion of Social VR in group settings reveals both promising opportunities and significant challenges. While gaming and entertainment have been the primary domains for VR, the articles show how its potential in professional and organizational environments. While in gaming, the realism of avatars might not significantly impact user experience, the degree of realism in professional settings could be essential
(
Ball 2022
)
. Realistic avatars could enhance professional interactions and provide a more authentic representation of participants, thereby improving communication and collaboration. By closely resembling the user’s real-world appearance, these avatars can facilitate more natural interactions. This can be achieved by using VR systems that capture and display facial expressions, especially mouth movements, which significantly increases perceived co-presence compared to static avatars. Incorporating natural eye contact and gaze direction during collaborative tasks helps improve visual coordination and mutual awareness between team members.

Additionally, incorporating features that allow for real-time facial expression tracking and body language mirroring can further enhance the realism and effectiveness of these interactions, making virtual collaboration feel more like face-to-face interactions. However, this raises questions about the appropriate level of realism needed to balance immersion with user comfort and privacy concerns
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
.

The potential of Social VR extends to tasks that benefit from immersive environments, such as prototyping, collaborative design, and object manipulation. These applications leverage VR’s ability to simulate real-world interactions, offering advantages over traditional tools. However, CSCW researchers must carefully identify which tasks are best suited for Social VR and design systems that maximize its strengths while addressing its limitations, such as user fatigue and privacy concerns. These tasks leverage the unique affordances of VR, allowing users to interact with digital objects as if they were physical items. It is important to acknowledge that not all tasks will require such an immersive setting; for instance, analyzing 2D plots or documents may not gain much benefit from a VR context. As such, CSCW researchers and designers need to reflect on which specific activities can benefit from Social VR. Inspired by the identified corpus, we suggest that activities strongly focused on social bonding, coupled tasks, and object manipulation are good candidates for Social VR applications.

Despite the advantages mentioned, Social VR is not without drawbacks. The articles strongly alerted users’ fatigue and dizziness experiences after prolonged use, which can hinder productivity and limit the duration of VR sessions. We suggest developing adaptive systems that allow users to transition between immersive and non-immersive modes based on task requirements
(
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. This transition can be beneficial for tasks that do not require continuous immersion but benefit from periodic engagement, such as brainstorming sessions or design reviews. The immersive environment can provide additional context and depth to the interactions. Moreover, while Social VR can create a sense of closeness among remote participants, it is not designed for scenarios where physical proximity is essential or where users can easily meet in person. Privacy and security considerations in collaborative VR environments remain underexplored, particularly in professional settings where confidentiality and data protection are important
(
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
;
Osborne et al. 2023
)
. Future research should address how to implement robust security measures while maintaining the seamless and immersive nature of Social VR interactions.

Lastly, the identified articles have extensively covered individual and environmental factors in Social VR but have left a considerable gap at the team level, particularly regarding team dynamics (e.g., cohesion, work division, trust), realness, and performance. The “Collaboration Realness” in Social VR involves creating environments that closely mimic real-world settings, which can enhance user engagement and facilitate more natural interactions. This can be achieved through realistic avatars
(
Bai et al. 2024
)
and environments that support authentic social dynamics and nonverbal communication. However, previous research shows several challenges (e.g., the uncanny Valley) to achieve sufficient levels of realism
(
Schwind et al. 2018
;
Osborne et al. 2023
;
Torro and Pirkkalainen 2023
)
. Moreover, building trust on Social VR platforms could incorporate features like avatar customization to encourage self-disclosure and spatial awareness tools to maintain personal space, enhancing users’ comfort and security during interactions.

As VR technologies continue to improve and other innovative technologies appear in the market (e.g., Mixed Reality), future CSCW research needs to move from merely comparing Social VR with traditional online communication systems. Considering Social VR as another complementary tool for online communication, the next steps should be exploring novel uses of immersive technologies for collaboration (e.g., allowing users to customize shared environments with objects that are visible only to them), designing collaborative applications that could break the laws of physics
(
Grønbæk et al. 2024
;
Irlitti et al. 2024
)
, and how the integration of advanced AI models can empower physical and spatial interactions with teams
(
Urban Davis et al. 2021
)
, including human-AI interaction embodied with avatars
(
Malik et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
.

5.3.
Limitations and Future Work

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this paper. First, our systematic review was scoped to three databases only. We did not include articles that could have been indexed in other systems (e.g., Scopus, Google Scholar), have pre-printed versions (e.g., arXiv, SSRN), or were published in non-academic outlets (e.g., blog articles, news, etc.). Incorporating these datasets can be fundamental to expanding our knowledge in Social VR and team collaboration. This limitation could be critical given the fact that collaborative Social VR technologies may not be addressed by academic publications. Second, this paper only included articles published in the last 20 years. A historical review, including earlier work, could provide additional context on the evolution of Social VR collaboration. We acknowledge that studies published before 2004 are important for the development of Social VR technologies for collaboration and teamwork processes. We prioritized recent advancements given the technological VR ecosystem, and future reviews should discuss the importance of research articles published before that year. Third, we only included English-language papers, potentially missing insights from non-English speaking regions. Fourth, our thematic analysis depends strongly on the researchers’ knowledge and perspectives, relying on their interpretations to identify themes. Iterative discussions helped the researchers to consider different perspectives and angles of these articles. Lastly, we did not consider other relevant extended reality technologies (e.g., augmented reality and mixed reality), which have made important contributions to collaboration applications. Future literature reviews could consider how these systems have shaped and influenced team dynamics, as well as their differences with VR applications.

6.
Conclusion

This systematic literature review has provided a comprehensive analysis of how Social Virtual Reality (VR) technologies have been utilized to support collaboration over the past two decades. By examining 51 relevant articles, we identified key themes and insights into the current practices and challenges associated with Social VR in collaborative settings. Our findings highlight the potential of Social VR to enhance teamwork by offering immersive experiences that foster a sense of presence and social connection among users. However, significant gaps remain in understanding long-term collaboration dynamics, trust-building mechanisms, and the impact of avatar customization on team cohesion. Future research should focus on exploring diverse user populations, developing standardized evaluation frameworks, and integrating emerging technologies to optimize Social VR environments for effective team collaboration. By addressing these areas, we hope future CSCW research can leverage Social VR’s unique affordances that facilitate more engaging and productive remote teamwork in various group contexts.
