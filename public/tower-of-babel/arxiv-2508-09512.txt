On Complex Dimensions and Heat Content of Self-Similar Fractals
William E. Hoffer, Michel L. Lapidus

Abstract
. Complex fractal dimensions, defined as poles of appropriate fractal zeta functions, describe the geometric oscillations in fractal sets. In this work, we show that the same possible complex dimensions in the geometric setting also govern the asymptotics of the heat content on self-similar fractals. We consider the Dirichlet problem for the heat equation on bounded open regions whose boundaries are self-similar fractals. The class of self-similar domains we consider allow for non-disjoint overlap of the self-similar copies, provided some control over the separation. The possible complex dimensions, determined strictly by the similitudes that define the self-similar domain, control the scaling exponents of the asymptotic expansion for the heat content. We illustrate our method in the case of generalized von Koch snowflakes and in particular extend known results for these fractals with arithmetic scaling ratios to the generic (in the topological sense), non-arithmetic setting.

Keywords:
Heat content, self-similar fractals, complex dimensions, fractal zeta functions, scaling zeta functions

1.
Introduction

In this work, we study the
heat content
of regions with
self-similar, fractal
boundaries. Given a region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, here an open bounded set, its boundary
\partial\Omega
is said to be self-similar if it the the invariant set of an
iterated function system
consisting only of similitudes of Euclidean space. On such regions, we consider the Dirichlet problem for the heat equation in the sense of Perron-Wiener-Brelot with boundary conditions chosen as a model for heat flow into the region. The heat content is the integral of this solution over the region as a function of the time parameter and we will concern ourselves with the asymptotic expansion of this quantity.

The main results of this work are to provide explicit formulae for the heat content of regions with self-similar fractal boundary in terms of the poles of associated scaling zeta functions and the residues of a heat zeta function, up to an order determined by estimates of the remainder in an initial decomposition. These formulae take the form of a sum over the possible complex dimensions of the boundary with coefficients determined by an associated heat zeta function. We prove these results in the context of solutions to scaling functional equations, for which we state and prove sufficient conditions for requisite asymptotic estimates needed to explicitly compute inverse Mellin transforms in terms of limits of sums of residues of the integrand at poles of a meromorphic function.

1.1.
Main Results

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set and suppose that its boundary
\partial\Omega
is the invariant set of a self-similar system
\Phi
(Definition
2.2.1
) for which
(\partial\Omega,\Omega)
is an osculant fractal drum (Definition
2.2.3
). Let
E_{\Omega}
denote the total heat content (Definition
2.1.2
) of
\Omega
with respect to Problem
2.2
and suppose that it decomposes according to self-similar copies up to the error term

R_{\Omega}(t):=E_{\Omega}(t)-\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}E_{\varphi[\Omega]}(t),

with estimate
|R_{\Omega}(t)|=O(t^{(N-\sigma_{0})/2})
as
t\to 0^{+}
, where
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is the smallest such parameter.

We state and prove two admissibility conditions for this remainder (Theorem
3.2.3
and Theorem
3.2.2
) depending on
\Phi
and
\sigma_{0}
under which the following results hold. In the pointwise setting, the
k^{\text{th}}
antiderivative of the heat content,
E_{\Omega}^{[k]}(t)
, for
k\geq 2
is shown to satisfy

E_{\Omega}^{[k]}(t)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}})}\Res\Bigg(\cfrac{t^{(N-s)/2+k}}{((N-s)/2+1)_{k}}\widehat{\zeta}_{\Omega}(s/2;\delta);\omega\Bigg)+\mathcal{R}^{k}(t),

for all
t\in(0,\delta)
for fixed
\delta>0
and where the remainder term satisfies the estimate
\mathcal{R}^{k}(t)=O(t^{{(N-\sigma_{0})}/2-\varepsilon+k})
as
t\to 0^{+}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. The function
\widehat{\zeta}_{\Omega}
, which we call a heat zeta function (Definition
4.1
), is defined in analogy to a tube zeta function and is given in terms of a scaling zeta function associated to
\Phi
, viz. Theorem
4.1
. Further, this identity is valid for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
when interpreted as a distributional identity, and in particular the heat content itself is given by

E_{\Omega}(t)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}})}\Res\Bigg(t^{(N-s)/2}\widehat{\zeta}_{\Omega}(s/2;\delta);\omega\Bigg)+\mathcal{R}^{[0]}(t).

The pointwise results is the content of Theorem
4.2
and the distributional result (without specialization to the case of
k=0
) is the content of Theorem
4.2
.

In these formulae, the summations are indexed by the possible complex dimensions (Definition
2.2.4
) of
\partial\Omega
relative to
\Omega
in an open right half-plane determined by the order of the estimate on
R_{\Omega}
,
\sigma_{0}
. This set,
\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}})
, is the set of singularities (here, the poles) of the scaling zeta function

\zeta_{\Phi}(s)=\cfrac{1}{1-\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{s}},

where
\lambda_{\varphi}
is the scaling ratio of the similitude
\varphi\in\Phi
. For an osculant fractal drum (with an estimate of the volume in a residual set comparable to that of
R_{\Omega}
), the set of complex dimensions of
\partial\Omega
relative to
\Omega
is exactly a subset of the poles of this scaling zeta function
[
Hof25
, Theorem 5.5]
. These poles are the complex solutions
\omega
of the Moran equation

1=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{\omega}.

These results are obtained through the study of solutions to scaling functional equations, which heat contents are shown to satisfy by virtue of their scaling properties (viz. Proposition
2.1.2
and Corollary
3.1.4
) when there exists a decomposition induced by a self-similar system for the heat content with suitably estimated remainder (cf. Definition
3.1.4
). In fact, they are stated and proved in the context of general solutions to scaling functional equations (viz. Theorem
3.3.1
and Theorem
3.3.2
) with admissible remainders such that the Mellin transform of the remainder term and the scaling zeta function
\zeta_{\Phi}
associated to the SFE are
jointly languid
, a notion we introduce in Section
2.3
. The first admissibility criterion occurs when
\sigma_{0}
, from the remainder estimate, is strictly smaller than a lower bound for the poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, here called the lower similarity dimension of
\Phi
(Definition
3.1.2
). The second criterion is when the scaling ratios of
\Phi
lie in the lattice case (Definition
2.2.2
).

Lastly, we illustrate our results in Section
4.3
in the case of generalized
(n,r)
-von Koch fractals (GKFs), such as the examples in Figure
2
. The admissibility criteria are shown to be satisfied when either
n\geq 5
or in the lattice case (see Definition
2.2.2
). In particular, the ordinary von Koch snowflake (see Figure

) falls in the lattice case. Further, in the lattice case, when the poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
(and thus the complex dimensions of the GKF) are simple, the heat content is given by a distributional expansion of the form

E_{\Omega}(t)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi_{n,r}}(\mathbb{H}_{0})}r_{\omega}\,t^{(2-\omega)/2}+\mathcal{R}(t),

where
r_{\omega}
is a constant determined by the residue of the heat zeta function (Corollary
4.3.3
). The general versions of these results are given in Theorem
4.3.3
and Theorem

to the nonlattice case (see Definition

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The Heat Equation on Bounded Open Sets

2.1.1.
Perron-Wiener-Brelot Solutions

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
be an arbitrary bounded open set. To consider the heat equation on an arbitrary open set, care must be taken to interpret the prescribed boundary conditions. In this work, we study a solution in the sense of Perron-Wiener-Brelot (PWB) per Definition
A.4
, the details of which we include in Appendix
A
. In brief, a PWB solution adapts Perron’s famous method to solving the Laplace equation
[
Per23
]
in the context of the heat equation. It is named additionally for the contributions of Wiener
[
Wie24
,
Wie24a
]
and Brelot
[
Bre55
,
Bre67
]
. For a more thorough background of the contributors to heat potential theory, see Section
A.1
.

The advantage of the PWB approach, over a weak/variational formulation, is that it yields a solution which is pointwise-defined everywhere and continuously differentiable in the interior. Convergence to prescribed boundary values is a pointwise limit, though only on a subset of the boundary called the regular essential boundary (see Section
A.3
).

We restrict our attention to a cylindrical open set, the Cartesian product of a fixed spatial region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(without loss of generality a connected open set) and
(0,\infty)
. The Dirichlet problem for the heat equation is then given by

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u-C\Delta u&=0&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
u&=f&&\text{on }\Omega\times\left\{0\right\},\\
u&=g&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
C
is a positive constant called the diffusivity constant. In order for a solution
u
to exist in the PWB sense, the prescribed boundary values specified by the functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
must be resolutive (per Definition
A.4
). Letting
F:\partial(\Omega\times(0,\infty))\to\mathbb{R}
represent the shared boundary conditions, that is with
F|_{t=0}\equiv f
and
F|_{x\in\partial\Omega}\equiv g
, an equivalent characterization is that
F
is integrable with respect to each element of a family of parabolic measures relative to
\Omega\times(0,\infty)
(per Definition
A.5
).

In what follows, we will specialize to the following boundary conditions. Initially, we suppose that the PWB solution to the heat equation inside of
\Omega
is zero. On the boundary of
\Omega
for all time, we suppose that the boundary
\partial\Omega
is held at a constant temperature, which we suppose to be equal to one. Explicitly,

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u-C\Delta u&=0&&\text{in }\Omega\times[0,\infty),\\
u&=0&&\text{on }\Omega\times\left\{0\right\},\\
u&=1&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

These boundary conditions model the flow of heat into
\Omega
from its boundary, supposing that the edges are held at a constant temperature. These boundary conditions define a resolutive boundary function for any bounded open set, so that a PWB solution exists, and furthermore, they are scale invariant.

To see the former, let
Y=\Omega\times(0,\Omega)
and define the function

F(x,t)=\mathds{1}_{\partial\Omega}(x),

which is a measurable function for each of the parabolic measures
\omega_{Y,p}
(Definition
A.5
),
p\in Y
, since each measure is a Borel measure and
\partial\Omega
is closed (whence its indicator function is measurable). Further,
F
is integrable since the measure of the (essential) boundary (see Section
A.3
) of
Y
is equal to one, i.e.
\omega_{Y,p}(\partial_{e}Y)=1
, since they are probability measures. Thus
F
is parabolically integrable and Problem
2.2
admits a PWB solution.

We also have that the solution to Problem
2.2
is unique due to the results of Widder
[
Wid44
]
since the solution is nonnegative (which can be seen from the minimum principle for the heat equation).

The scale invariance of these boundary conditions will be essential to establishing the relationship between Problem
2.2
and the analogous problem on the image
\varphi[\Omega]
under a similitude
\varphi
of Euclidean space. By (parabolically) scale invariant, we mean that
F(x,t)=F(\varphi(x),\lambda^{2}t)
for any similitude
\varphi
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
whose scaling ratio is
\lambda>0
. That
F
is scale invariant follows immediately from it being piecewise constant (and the fact that
\lambda^{2}\cdot 0=0
). In general, the results of this work may be generalized to parabolically integrable, scale-invariant choices of boundary conditions.

2.1.2.
Heat Content and its Properties

First, we define our main object of study, the heat content of (a measurable subset of) the spatial domain.

Definition 2.1: Heat Content

Let
u_{\Omega}
be the PWB solution to the Dirichlet Problem
2.1
on an open bounded set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with resolutive boundary conditions (per Definition
A.4
). The
heat content

E_{\Omega}
inside any measurable set
F\subseteq\Omega
is defined as

(2.3)

E_{\Omega}(t;F):=\int_{F}u_{\Omega}(x,t)\,dx.

When
F=\Omega
, we write
E_{\Omega}(t)=E_{\Omega}(t;\Omega)
and call it the
total heat content
.

In the case of Problem
2.2
, we note that the heat content
E_{\Omega}(t;F)
is uniformly bounded for all
t\in[0,\infty)
and any measurable set
F
. This follows as a corollary of the maximum principle for the heat equation, the boundedness of the region
\Omega
, and elementary properties of the Lebesgue measure. Since the boundary conditions of Problem
2.2
are bounded by one, we have the following explicit estimate.

Proposition 2.2: Heat Content Boundedness

Let
u_{\Omega}
be the PWB solution to Problem
2.2
. Then for any
t\in[0,\infty)
and any measurable set
F\subseteq\Omega
, we have
|E_{\Omega}(t;F)|\leq\left|\Omega\right|_{N}
.

Next, we deduce a scaling property for the heat content. This will require imposing (parabolically) scale-invariant boundary conditions, the simplest of which are constant boundary conditions.

Proposition 2.3: Heat Content Scaling Property

Let
u_{\Omega}
and
u_{\varphi[\Omega]}
be PWB solutions to Problem
2.1
on a bounded open set
\Omega
and
\varphi[\Omega]
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, respectively, where
\varphi
is a similitude of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with scaling ratio
\lambda>0
. Suppose that
f
and
g
are chosen so that
u_{\Omega}
is unique, e.g. if
f
and
g
are both nonnegative (in which case
u_{\Omega}\geq 0
, which is sufficient by
[
Wid44
]
).

Suppose that the boundary functions
f
and
g
have the following scale invariance properties with respect to
\varphi
: for any
x\in\Omega
,
f
is
scale invariant
(i.e.
f(\varphi(x),0)=f(x,0)
) and
g
is
parabolic scale invariant
(i.e.
g(\varphi(x),\lambda^{2}t)=g(x,t)
). Then

E_{\varphi[\Omega]}(t)=\lambda^{N}E_{\Omega}(t/\lambda^{2}).

Proof.

First, we have that for any
x\in\Omega
and for all
t>0
,

(2.4)

u_{\varphi[X]}(\varphi(x),\lambda^{2}t)=u_{\Omega}(x,t).

To see this, we note that the function
v(x,t):=u_{\varphi[\Omega]}(\varphi(x),\lambda^{2}t)
satisfies the heat equation for any
x\in\Omega
and for all
t>0
, since
(\partial_{t}-\Delta)v=\lambda^{2}(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u_{\varphi[\Omega]}=0
on the points
(\varphi(x),t)\in\varphi[\Omega]\times(0,\infty)
.

Furthermore,
v
satisfies the same boundary conditions as the function
u_{\Omega}
. Let us use the notation in the discussion of Problem
A.3
: let
u_{\Omega}=u_{Y,F}
and
u_{\varphi[\Omega]}=u_{\varphi[Y],F}
, where
Y=\Omega\times(0,\infty)
and where
F
represents respective boundary conditions
f
and
g
when appropriately restricted. Let
p_{n}=(x_{n},t_{n})\to q=(y,s)
be a sequence of points in
Y
approaching the boundary point
q
, and in the case of a semi-singular boundary point we assume that
t\to s^{+}
. Let
p^{\prime}=(\varphi(x_{n}),\lambda^{2}t)
and
q^{\prime}=(\varphi(y),\lambda^{2}t)
denote the corresponding points under parabolic transformation.

By assumption, at any regular boundary point
q
(respectively
q^{\prime}
) we have that

\displaystyle u_{Y,F}(p_{n})

\displaystyle\to F(q)

\displaystyle\text{ as }p_{n}\to q\in\partial_{e}Y,

\displaystyle u_{\varphi[Y],F}(p^{\prime}_{n})

\displaystyle\to F(q^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ as }p_{n}\to q\in\partial_{e}Y.

In other words,
u_{Y,F}
(respectively
u_{\varphi[Y],F}
) converge on the regular essential boundary in the sense of Definition
A.3
. We note that if
q
is a regular boundary point, then so too is
q^{\prime}
for its corresponding boundary. This can be seen from the barrier criterion (see for instance
[
Wat12
, Theorem 8.46]
). Namely, if there is a barrier
w
defined near the point
q
, then the corresponding function
w(\varphi(x),\lambda^{2}t)
will be a barrier at
q^{\prime}
.

By definition,
v(p)=u_{\varphi[Y],F}(p^{\prime})
, so
v(p)\to F(q^{\prime})
. Under the parabolic scale invariance assumption,
F(q^{\prime})=F(q)
. Thus, we conclude by uniqueness that
v(x,t)=u_{\Omega}(x,t)
, which is exactly (
2.4
).
Using properties of the Lebesgue integral and (
2.4
), it follows that

\displaystyle E_{\varphi[\Omega]}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u_{\varphi[\Omega]}(\varphi(x),t)\,d\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{N}\int_{\Omega}u_{\varphi[\Omega]}(\varphi(x),\lambda^{2}(t/\lambda^{2}))\,dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{N}\int_{\Omega}u_{\Omega}(x,t/\lambda^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{N}E_{\Omega}(t/\lambda^{2}).

∎

Note that an important part of this scaling property is that the heat content in a region is related to a
rescaled
problem’s heat content (cf.
E_{\lambda\Omega}(t)
), not a
restricted
heat content (cf.
E_{\Omega}(t;\lambda\Omega)
).

2.2.
Geometry of Self-Similar Fractals

2.2.1.
Self-Similar Iterated Function Systems

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. An
iterated function system (IFS)
on
(X,d)
is a finite set
\Phi
of contraction mappings on
X
. Explicitly, this means that for each map
\varphi\in\Phi
, there exists a constant
r_{\varphi}\in(0,1)
such that for all
x,y\in X
,

d(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))\leq r_{\varphi}\,d(x,y).

Hutchinson showed that there is a unique nonempty set
K=K_{\Phi}
which is closed, bounded, and satisfying

K=\bigcup_{\varphi\in\Phi}\varphi[K],

which is called the attractor or invariant set of
\Phi
; it is compact and obtained as the closure of the set of fixed points of finite compositions of mappings in the IFS
[
Hut81
]
. For example, many classical fractals may be obtained through this framework using the space of nonempty, compact subsets of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
equipped with the Hausdorff metric. This includes Cantor sets, fractal snowflakes, Sierpinski carpets, Menger spongers, and more
[
Fal90
,
Bar88
,
LR24
]
. In what follows, we shall be focused on sets like these in Euclidean space, rather than elements of more general complete metric spaces.

All of these specific examples share an additional property,
self-similarity
. The mappings of an IFS are required to be contractions, but for self-similar (iterated function) systems we shall also require that the mappings are similitudes.

Definition 2.4: Self-Similar System

A
self-similar system

\Phi
on a complete metric space
(X,d)
is a finite set of (nontrivial) contractive similitudes,

\Phi:=\left\{\varphi_{k}:X\to X\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
.

Explicitly, for each of the mappings
\varphi_{k}
,
k=1,...,m
, to be both a contraction and a similitude, we must have that for every
x,y\in X
, there is some
r_{k}\in(0,1)
, called the
scaling ratio
of
\varphi_{k}
, so that

d(\varphi_{k}(x),\varphi_{k}(y))=r_{k}\,d(x,y).

Note that we have ruled out the presence of a constant mapping (by requiring
r_{k}>0
) and have enforced contractivity (by imposing that
r_{k}<1
). In general, a similitude may be defined simply by the equality without the shrinking distance requirement, allowing any positive scaling ratio. We note that maps defined this way are equivalently compositions of isometries—namely, compositions of translations, rotations, and reflections— of Euclidean space and uniform scaling maps.

Given a nonempty compact set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
X
is said to be a
self-similar set
if there exists a self-similar system
\Phi
such that
X
is the attractor of
\Phi
.

2.2.2.
Lattice/Nonlattice Dichotomy

In the study of self-similar fractals with multiple scaling ratios, there is a
lattice/nonlattice dichotomy
in behavior depending on whether or not the distinct scaling ratios are
arithmetically related
or not. This dichotomy has also been called the arithmetic/non-arithmetic dichotomy, and has been discussed in the work of Lalley in
[
Lal88
,
Lal89
,
Lal91
]
, in the work of Strichartz on self-similar measures
[
Str90
,
Str93
,
Str93a
]
, in the work of the second author and collaborators on fractal harps, fractal drums, and the theory of complex dimensions (such as in
[
LvF13
,
LRŽ17
,
Lap93
,
KL93
,
LP10
,
LPW11
,
HL06
,
Lap19
]
), and for the generalized von Koch snowflakes by van den Berg and collaborators in their work on heat content
[
vdB00b
,
vdB00a
,
vdBG98
,
vdBH99
]
, among others. See
[
DGM+17
]
and the references therein for more information about this dichotomy in fractal geometry.

Definition 2.5: Lattice/Nonlattice Dichotomy

A set of (distinct) scaling ratios
\left\{\lambda_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
is said to be in the
lattice case
if the group

G=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}^{\mathbb{Z}}

is a discrete subgroup of the positive real line with respect to multiplication. In this case, there exists a generator
\lambda_{0}
such that every
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{0}^{m_{k}}
for some positive integer
m_{k}
. In this case, the (distinct) scaling ratios are said to be
arithmetically related
.

If
G
is not a discrete subgroup of the positive real line (in which case it is a dense subgroup), then the (distinct) scaling ratios are said to be
non-arithmetically related
. This is called the
nonlattice case
.

Note that if
\Phi
is a self-similar system, the set
\left\{\lambda_{\varphi}\right\}_{\varphi\in\Phi}
is necessarily the set of distinct scaling ratios of the similitudes in
\Phi
, rather than viewed as a multiset. It has cardinality less than or equal to
|\Phi|
. By a slight abuse of language, to say that the scaling ratios of the mappings in
\Phi
are arithmetically related is to say that the
set of
scaling ratios, i.e. the distinct scaling ratios, are arithmetically related.

An important feature of this dichotomy is that the
complex dimensions
of self-similar fractals behave very differently in the lattice and nonlattice case. The structure of complex dimensions has important implications for the asymptotics of the quantities on regions with self-similar fractal boundary. For more information about the structure results in the different cases, see
[
LvF13
, Theorem 2.16]
for the one dimensional setting and
[
LvF13
, Theorem 3.6]
for a generalized result which may be applied to self-similar sets in higher dimensions.

2.2.3.
Relative Fractal Drums

A relative fractal drum (RFD), a notion originally introduced in
[
LRŽ17
]
, is a geometric object that considers a fractal of interest, here the boundary
\partial\Omega
, relative to some set which is close to the fractal in an appropriate sense. For this work, it the relative set simply the interior region
\Omega
on which we study Problem
2.1
and the closeness condition is automatic from the definition of the boundary of a set.

Definition 2.6: Relative Fractal Drum (RFD)

Let
X,\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Suppose that
\Omega
is open, has finite Lebesgue measure, and has the property that there exists
\delta>0
such that

X\subseteq\Omega_{\delta}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,\middle|\,\exists\,y\in\Omega,\,d(x,y)<\delta\right\},

a
\delta
-neighborhood of
\Omega
. Then the pair
(X,\Omega)
is called a
relative fractal drum (RFD)
.

So, the RFDs of this work are of the form
(\partial\Omega,\Omega)
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded open set (hence having finite Lebesgue measure). Note that any
\delta>0
is sufficient for
\Omega_{\delta}
to contain
\partial\Omega
. Often, one may assume that
\Omega
is connected since we may independently study each of the relative fractal drums
(\partial U,U)
for each connected component
U
of
\Omega
. (In other words,
\Omega
may be chosen to be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.) Note that, in general,
\Omega
has at most countably many connected components.

Since our fractal sets (i.e. the boundaries of the given region) will be defined via iterated function systems, we will impose conditions which control the way in which this relative set interacts with the IFS. The first is a separation condition called the open set condition.

Definition 2.7: Open Set Condition

An iterated function system
\Phi=\left\{\varphi_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies the
open set condition (OSC)
if there exists a nonempty, open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(called a feasible open set for
\Phi
) such that:

(1)

U\supset\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{m}\varphi_{i}[U]
;

(2)

For each
i,j\in\left\{1,...,m\right\}
with
i\neq j
,
\varphi_{i}[U]\cap\varphi_{j}[U]=\emptyset
.

We need slightly more information about such a feasible open set when studying an RFD
(X,\Omega)
. Namely, we need to know that the images of this set remain closest to the corresponding images of the attractor under the mapping. This condition, introduced as the osculating set condition in
[
Hof25
]
, ensures that points in the image
\varphi[\Omega]
, for
\varphi\in\Phi
, remain closest to the corresponding image
\varphi[X]
under the same contraction
\varphi
, as opposed to another distinct image.

Definition 2.8: Osculating Sets and RFDs

Let
\Phi=\left\{\varphi_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
be an iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let
X
be its attractor. A nonempty, open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is said to be an
osculating set
for
\Phi
if the following hold:

(1)

\Phi
satisfies the open set condition with respect to
\Omega
;

(2)

For each
i=1,..,m
, if
y\in\varphi_{i}[\Omega]
, then
d(y,X)=d(y,\varphi_{i}[X])
.

A relative fractal drum
(X,\Omega)
is called an
osculant
fractal drum if there exists an IFS
\Phi
for which
X
is its attractor and
\Omega
is an osculating set thereof.

2.2.4.
Tube Zeta Functions and Complex Dimensions

Fractal zeta functions arise out of the use of the Mellin transform to analyze the oscillatory behavior characteristic of fractals in the space of scales. The versions of fractal zeta functions developed for the higher-dimensional theory, distance and tube zeta functions, extend the theory of complex (fractal) dimensions to this setting
[
LRŽ17
]
. In this work, we will focus on relative tube zeta functions, considering a boundary relative to its corresponding interior region.

To start, the
tube function
of the RFD
(X,\Omega)
(also called a relative tube function) is the Lebesgue measure of a tubular neighborhood of
X
intersected with
\Omega
as a function of the distance parameter defining the neighborhood. Explicitly, the tube function of
X
relative
\Omega
is the function

V_{X,\Omega}(t):=\left|X_{t}\cap\Omega\right|_{N},

for all
t>0
. Here, we denote by
\left|\,\cdot\,\right|_{N}
the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and we need only impose that
\Omega
is measurable, since a tubular neighborhood (as a union of open balls) is necessarily an open set, even if
X
itself is arbitrary. For the fractal drums we consider,
\Omega
is open and thus measurable.

A
truncated Mellin transform
is an ordinary Mellin transform but with restriction of the domain to an interval subset of
(0,\infty)
. The Mellin transform is the multiplicative cousin of the Fourier transform, defined for the positive real numbers with the operation of multiplication and the natural Haar measure on this group. In particular, an upper cutoff improves the convergence properties of the transform at the cost of complicating the self-similarity properties of the transform. In general, a truncated Mellin transform can introduce lower or upper cutoffs.

Definition 2.9: Truncated Mellin Tranform

Let
f
be an integrable function on
(a,b)
, where
0\leq a<b<\infty
. Then the
truncated Mellin transform

\mathcal{M}_{a}^{b}[f]
of
f
is the complex analytic function

\mathcal{M}_{a}^{b}[f](s):=\int_{a}^{b}t^{s-1}f(t)\,dt

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
for which this Lebesgue integral converges. We shall further identify
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{b}[f]
with the analytic continuation of this function in the complex plane when there is no ambiguity in the domain.

When
a=0
, we write
\mathcal{M}_{a}^{b}=\mathcal{M}^{b}
.

In the present work, we shall consider only functions which are meromorphic without any accessible logarithmic singularities, so we shall not encounter any ambiguities in the domain induced by the need for branch cuts. For more information regarding (truncated) Mellin transforms and their properties, see Appendix
B
.

Of particular use presently is the scaling property of this Mellin transform.

Proposition 2.10: Scaling Property of Mellin Transforms

Let
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}
denote a truncated Mellin transform with
\delta>0
. Then for any
\lambda>0
and for
f
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that the transform converges,

\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f(t/\lambda^{2})](s)=\lambda^{2s}\mathcal{M}^{\delta/\lambda^{2}}[f](s).

This proposition is a simple corollary of the change of variables formula, but notably the upper cutoff changes. The difference between cutoffs, notably, turns out to be a function which is entire with respect to the complex variable
s
. Note also that we are using a scaling factor of
\lambda^{2}
owing to the scaling properties of heat content, viz. Proposition
2.1.2
.

Putting the notions of a tube function and a truncated Mellin transform together, we may now formally state the definition of the relative tube zeta function.

Definition 2.11: Relative Tube Zeta Function

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum and let
\delta>0
. The
relative tube zeta function

\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
of
X
relative to
\Omega
is given by

\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}(s;\delta):=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[t^{-N}V_{X,\Omega}](s),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
for which the analytic continuation of the integral transform is well defined.

This tube zeta function will typically possess a meromorphic continuation in the complex plane, and its poles are of great importance to the geometry of the fractal.

The singularities of this fractal zeta function are called complex dimensions
[
LvF13
,
LRŽ17
]
. As we show in Section
4
, they will play the role of exponents in explicit formulae for heat content. To formally define complex dimensions, we must specify the window
W
, a subset of the complex plane to which the function permits a meromorphic extension. Additionally, we note that the singularities of the tube zeta function are independent of the cutoff parameter
\delta
, that they are geometric invariants of a given fractal set, and further that the singularities of different variants of fractal zeta functions, most notably the tube and distance zeta functions, are the same
[
LRŽ17
]
.

Definition 2.12: Complex Dimensions of a Set

Let
(X,\Omega)
be a relative fractal drum and let
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
be the relative tube zeta function of
X
. If
W\subset\mathbb{C}
, then the
complex dimensions
of
X
relative to
\Omega
contained in the window
W
, denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{X}(W)
, are the poles of
\widetilde{\zeta}_{X,\Omega}
contained within
W
.

Strictly speaking, other types of singularities are of interest and should be considered as complex dimensions. For the self-similar sets we consider here, however, only poles will occur, and thus we may restrict our definition, for simplicity.

2.3.
Languid Growth

2.3.1.
Background

Languidity refers to growth estimates needed to express the contour integral of an inverse Mellin transform in terms of a sum over the residues of the integrand at its poles in a vertical strip in the complex plane. The notion of languid growth was introduced in
[
LvF00
]
and then refined in
[
LvF13
]
and
[
LRŽ17
]
in the context of proving explicit tube formulae and other geometric, dynamical, and spectral formulae for generalized fractal harps or relative fractal drums, respectively. Strictly speaking, these definitions apply to the geometric object (the harp or the drum) but involve conditions on their associated zeta functions. As in
[
LRŽ17
]
, we extend these definitions to apply to a (zeta) function itself, and the corresponding geometric object, including now a self-similar system, may be defined to be languid when its associated zeta function is languid.

There are two types of languid growth, standard (or weak) languid growth and strong languid growth. We will provide the definitions of both, as the scaling zeta functions of a self-similar system will in fact be strongly languid. The formulae which we will obtain for tube and heat zeta functions by means of solving a general scaling functional equation, however, will be only languid, owing to the nature of the contribution from a remainder term in the (approximate) scaling functional equation. Languid growth is the content of
[
LRŽ17
, Definition 5.1.3]
and
[
LvF13
, Definition 5.2]
and strongly languid growth is the content of
[
LRŽ17
, Definition 5.1.4]
and
[
LvF13
, Definition 5.3]
.

As the names would suggest, strong languid growth implies (weak) languid growth. However, there is an important caveat to this implication. A strongly languid function is only languid with respect to
some
screen (to be defined), not any
specified
screen. In order to compare the languid growth of two different functions, we introduce a notion of
joint languidity
where the
same
screen is used for both functions. This notion will precisely define the joint estimates for the truncated Mellin transform of a remainder term in a scaling functional equation and the associated scaling zeta function needed in Section
3.3
to prove our main results.

2.3.2.
Languidity Hypotheses

In what follows, let
\zeta_{f}
denote a complex function which is holomorphic in the open right half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{D}

and extended by analytic continuation to a domain in
\mathbb{C}
containing the closure of
\mathbb{H}_{D}
. When
\zeta_{f}(s)=\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f](s)
is the truncated Mellin transform of a function
f
, we take
D
to be its abscissa of absolute convergence
\sigma_{ac}
. (See Appendix
B
for more detail in this case.) We assume that
\zeta_{f}
admits analytic continuation to an open connected neighborhood of the set
W\setminus\mathcal{D}_{f}(W)
, where
W\subset\mathbb{C}
and where
\mathcal{D}_{f}(W)
is a discrete subset of
W
at which
\zeta_{f}
may be singular. In the functions we analyze in this work,
\zeta_{f}
will possess a necessarily unique meromorphic continuation in an open connected neighborhood of
W\subset\mathbb{C}
, with possible singularities at the points of
\mathcal{D}_{f}(W)
.

Next, let
S:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded, Lipschitz continuous function. We say that a
screen

\mathcal{S}
is a set of the form

\mathcal{S}=\left\{S(\tau)+i\tau\,\middle|\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

By a slight abuse of notation, we say that the function
S
itself is a screen and identify its graph with the set
\mathcal{S}
, which is a rotated embedding of its graph in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We say that a set
W=W_{S}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is a
window associated to a screen
when

W=\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,\Re(s)\geq S(\Im(s))\right\}

for some screen
S
. Note that
W_{S}
is closed, thus when we consider analytic continuation to this window, we strictly speaking are considering analytic continuation to an open connected neighborhood containing
W_{S}
.

Given a function
\zeta_{f}
, we will impose the following constraints regarding an associated screen and window. Firstly, we assume that
S:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,D]
, where
D\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
\zeta_{f}
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{D}
. When
\zeta_{f}
is a Mellin transform or a tamed Dirichlet-type integral (see
[
LRŽ17
, Definition A.1.2]
or Appendix
B
), we assume that
D=\sigma_{ac}
is its abscissa of absolute convergence. Secondly, we assume that
W_{S}
is contained in the connected open set in
\mathbb{C}
for which the holomorphic continuation of
\zeta_{f}
is defined, except perhaps for a discrete set
\mathcal{D}_{f}(W_{S})
of exceptional points (viz. where the meromorphic continuation of
\zeta_{f}
has poles). Thirdly, we assume that the image of the screen does not intersect this discrete set of possible singularities of
\zeta_{f}
; in particular,
\zeta_{f}
must be meromorphic on a connected open neighborhood of (the image of) the screen
S
without poles on the screen.

For brevity, let us say when these assumptions hold for a given function
\zeta_{f}
(which is assumed to be holomorphic in some open right half-plane and admitting an analytic continuation to a connected open subset of
\mathbb{C}
containing the closure of
\mathbb{H}_{D}
) and a given screen
S
(with window
W_{S}
), that
\zeta_{f}
is
extended with respect to the screen

S
. In the event that
\zeta_{f}
admits a necessarily unique analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{f}(\mathbb{C})
for some discrete set
\mathcal{D}_{f}(\mathbb{C})
(e.g. a meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
), we say that it may be extended with respect to the (formal) screen
S(\tau)\equiv-\infty
.

Lastly, let
\left\{\tau_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a doubly infinite sequence with the following properties:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}=\infty,\text{ }\lim_{n\to-\infty}\tau_{n}=-\infty,\text{ and }\tau_{-n}<0<\tau_{n}\text{ for all }n\geq 1.

For brevity, we will say that
\left\{\tau_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a
sequence of admissible heights
.

Languidity consists of two hypotheses, the first of which concerns power-law or polynomial growth along a sequence of horizontal lines. The horizontal lines each pass from a point on a screen
S
to some point in a half-plane in which the function is holomorphic.

Definition 2.13: Languidity Hypothesis L1

We say that
\zeta_{f}
satisfies
languidity hypothesis L1
with exponent
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and with respect to the screen
S
if the following hold.

Firstly, there must exist some half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{D}
in which
\zeta_{f}
is holomorphic and
\zeta_{f}
must admit an extension to the screen
S
. Secondly, there must exist a positive constant
C>0
, a constant
\beta>D
, and a sequence of admissible heights
\left\{\tau_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
(in the sense of (
2.5
)) such that for any
\sigma\in[S(\tau_{n}),\beta]
,

|\zeta_{f}(\sigma+i\tau_{n})|\leq C(|\tau_{n}|+1)^{\kappa}.

In other words, on every horizontal contour of the form
I_{n}=[S(\tau_{n})+i\tau_{n},\,\beta+i\tau_{n}]
,
\zeta_{f}
has at most power-law
\kappa
growth with respect to the magnitude of its imaginary part
\tau_{n}
as
|\tau_{n}|\to\infty
. Alternatively, on each horizontal contour
I_{n}
,
\zeta_{f}
is uniformly bounded by the constant
C_{n}
, where
C_{n}=O((|\tau_{n}|+1)^{\kappa})
as
|n|\to\infty
.

The second hypothesis concerns power-law/polynomial growth along a vertical curve, the screen
S
. The standard (or weak) version of languidity concerns growth along a single curve, and strong languidity concerns growth along a sequence of such curves moving to the left. These curves are precisely the screens of the window(s) to which
\zeta_{f}
admits an extension.

Definition 2.14: Languidity Hypothesis L2

A function
\zeta_{f}
is said to satisfy
languidity hypothesis L2
with exponent
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and with respect to the screen
S
if there exists a positive constant
C>0
such that for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\tau|\geq 1
,
|\zeta_{f}(S(\tau)+i\tau)|\leq C|\tau|^{\kappa}
.

A
function
which satisfies both hypotheses
L1
and
L2
with respect to the same screen
S
and exponent
\kappa
is said to be languid, or equivalently to have languid growth. A geometric structure (such as a generalized fractal harp, a relative fractal drum, or a self-similar system) may be called languid if its associated zeta function (respectively, its geometric zeta function, its relative zeta function, or its scaling zeta function) is languid, i.e. it satisfies languidity hypotheses
L1
and
L2
.

Definition 2.15: (Standard/Weak) Languidity

We say that
\zeta_{f}
is
languid
with exponent
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
if there exists a screen
S
such that
\zeta_{f}
satisfies both languidity hypotheses
L1
(Definition
2.3.2
) and
L2
(Definition
2.3.2
) with exponent
\kappa
and with respect to the screen
S
.

Given a particular screen
S
, we say that a function
\zeta_{f}
is
languid with respect to

S
(with exponent
\kappa
) if it satisfies these hypotheses (with exponent
\kappa
) with respect to the given screen.

We will not have use of this presently, but one may also say that a function
\zeta_{f}
is languid if there exists some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and some screen
S
for which
\zeta_{f}
is languid with exponent
\kappa
and with respect to
S
.

2.3.3.
Strong Languidity

Strong languidity may be seen as a sequence of languidity conditions. Namely, it concerns languidity along
a sequence of screens

\left\{S_{m}\right\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}

converging to

-\infty
. To be more precise, let
\sup S
denote the supremum of the elements of the range of
S
, i.e.
\sup S:=\sup\left\{S(\tau)\,\middle|\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
. Then a sequence of screens
\left\{S_{m}\right\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to converge to
-\infty
if
\lim_{m\to\infty}\sup{S_{m}}=-\infty
. To be strongly languid, a given function
\zeta_{f}
must necessarily admit an analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
(excepting for a possible discrete set of singularities). In this work, a strongly languid function will be assumed to admit a meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
and this discrete set is the set of its poles.

For this sequence
\left\{S_{m}\right\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
of screens, we will also impose the following. For each screen
S_{m}
, which is by definition Lipschitz continuous, its
Lipschitz constant

K_{m}
is the smallest constant such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that
|S_{m}(x)-S_{m}(y)|\leq K_{m}|x-y|
. We say that the sequence
\left\{S_{m}\right\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
has
uniformly bounded Lipschitz constants
when there exists a constant
K
such that for every Lipschitz constant
K_{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, of
S_{m}
, we have that
K_{m}\leq K
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
. (In other words, the set of all Lipschitz constants,
\left\{K_{m}\right\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded by some finite constant
K
.)

Definition 2.16: Strong Languidity of a Function

Let
\zeta_{f}
be a holomorphic function possessing an analytic continuation (viz. meromorphic continuation) to the whole complex plane except possibly for a discrete set of singular points (viz. the set of poles). Then
\zeta_{f}
is said to be
strongly languid
with exponent
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
if there exists a sequence
\left\{S_{m}\right\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
of screens converging to
-\infty
with uniformly bounded Lipschitz constants such that:

L1

The function
\zeta_{f}
satisfies languidity hypothesis
L1
with respect to each screen
S_{m}
and the (fixed) constant
\kappa
. (Equivalently, it satisfies
L1
with respect to the formal screen
S(\tau)\equiv-\infty
.)

L2
′

There exist positive constants
C
and
B
such that for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\geq 1
,
\zeta_{f}
satisfies

|\zeta_{f}(S_{m}(\tau)+i\tau)|\leq CB^{|S_{m}(\tau)|}(|\tau|+1)^{\kappa}.

Note that the strong languidity condition allows for a prefactor with exponential growth related to
\left\lVert S_{m}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, but is otherwise analogous to
L2
. Condition
L2
′
implies
L2
for each of the screens with finite supremum. Also, without loss of generality, the constant
C
may be chosen to be the same in both hypotheses.

2.3.4.
Strong Languidity of Self-Similar Systems

Self-similar systems and their associated scaling zeta functions will play a critical role in the analysis to follow. As it will turn out, a self-similar system is always strongly languid in the sense that its associated scaling zeta function
\zeta_{\Phi}
is strongly languid with exponent
\kappa=0
. We first state this for scaling operators having positive, integral multiplicities and scaling ratios in
(0,1)
.

Proposition 2.17: Languidity of Scaling Zeta Functions

Let
L:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}M_{\lambda_{i}}
be a scaling operator with distinct scaling ratios
\lambda_{i}\in(0,1)
and positive integral multiplicities
a_{i}
. Then its associated scaling zeta function
\zeta_{L}
is strongly languid with exponent
\kappa=0
as in Definition
2.3.3
.

Proof.

This result is a corollary of
[
LvF13
, Theorem 3.26]
and the discussion in
[
LvF13
, Section 6.4]
, as
\zeta_{L}
is essentially the same as that of a self-similar fractal string (or harp). (Let the length and gap parameters be one.) Note in particular the explicit estimate in
[
LvF13
, Equation 6.36]
used to establish the uniform bounds on screens with arbitrarily small real parts.
∎

For a self-similar system
\Phi
, its associated scaling operator

will always have scaling ratios in
(0,1)
and positive, integral multiplicities. Thus, it is an immediate corollary of Proposition
2.3.4
that the scaling zeta function
\zeta_{\Phi}=\zeta_{L_{\Phi}}
is strongly languid. Extending the convention that a geometric structure itself may be dubbed languid when its associated zeta function is languid, we may say that self-similar systems are languid.

Corollary 2.18: Languiditiy of Self-Similar Systems

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then
\Phi
is strongly languid (with exponent
\kappa=0
) in the sense that its associated scaling zeta function
\zeta_{\Phi}
is strongly languid (with exponent
\kappa=0
) as in Definition
2.3.3
.

2.3.5.

In what follows, we will require the existence of screens

for which two functions
\zeta_{f}
and
\zeta_{g}
are both languid with respect to the

screen

. We shall call this

.

Definition 2.19: Joint Languidity

Let
\zeta_{f}
and

and both admitting a necessarily unique analytic continuation to a half-plane

\sigma_{0}<\sigma_{1}
, except perhaps for a discrete set

or

We say that
\zeta_{f}
and

are
jointly languid
with exponent

if there exists a screen

(contained in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{f,g}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}})
) such that the following hold.

•

\zeta_{f}
and
\zeta_{g}
both satisfy languidity hypothesis

with exponent

and with respect to
S
using this sequence.

•

Both
\zeta_{f}

satisfy languidity hypothesis

with exponent

In this work, the functions we consider will have a necessarily unique meromorphic continuations to

and
\mathcal{D}_{f,g}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}})
is the union of the sets of poles of
\zeta_{f}
and

in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
. In the event that

.

3.
Solutions of Scaling Functional Equations

Scaling operators, scaling zeta functions, and scaling functional equations were studied in

The notion of scaling zeta functions in an earlier form, during the development of what would become fractal zeta functions and complex dimensions, also appears in the work of the second author with E. Pearse and S. Winter

,
LPW11
]

[

These methods have been applied with great success in the realm of fractal geometry, including but not limited to the work of Strichartz on self-similar measures
[
Str90
,
Str93
,
Str93a
]
, the work of the second author on the vibrations of fractal drums

[

]
. Additionally, in the case of von Koch fractals, the renewal theorem has ben used to study the heat content of the von Koch snowflake
[
FLV95
]
as well as of generalized von Koch fractals
[
vdBG98
,
vdBH99
,
vdB00b
,
vdB00a
]
. Furthermore, the notion of a (scaling) functional equation was employed by Deniz, Koçak, Özdemir, and Üreyen in
[

]
to provide a new proof of tube formulae for self-similar sprays (originally established in the work of Lapidus and Pearse

3.1.
Scaling Functional Equations

3.1.1.
Scaling Operators

First, we define scaling operators which act on
C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, where
\mathbb{R}^{+}:=(0,\infty)
. A
pure scaling operator

M_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, shall act by precomposition of the function with scaling, namely

(3.1)

M_{\lambda}[f](x):=f(x/\lambda).

This convention of division by the scaling factor shall be convenient for our applications. A general
scaling operator

L
is any finite linear combination of such pure scaling operators with real coefficients, i.e.
L=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}M_{\lambda_{k}}
, where
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
for each
k=1,...,K
. Later, we will add the constraint that the multiplicities
a_{k}
be positive and integral.

3.1.2.
Scaling Zeta Functions and Similarity Dimensions

Given such a scaling operator
L
, we define a
scaling zeta function
associated to
L
as in
[
Hof25
]
. Given a scaling operator
L=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}M_{\lambda_{k}}
, the
scaling zeta function

\zeta_{L}
associated to
L
is the analytic continuation in
\mathbb{C}
of the function defined by

\zeta_{L}(s):=\cfrac{1}{1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\lambda_{k}^{s}}

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{L}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{L}
the (discrete) set of singularities of
\zeta_{L}
. This function plays a key role in describing solutions to scaling functional equations, owing to its relation to the Mellin transform of such functions and the role of its singularities. For example, the complex dimensions of an osculant, self-similar fractal drum (with appropriate estimates) is a subset of the poles of such an associated scaling zeta function (viz.
[
Hof25
, Theorem 5.5]
).

Given a self-similar system
\Phi
, we can define a scaling operator
L_{\Phi}
associated to
\Phi
(and thus also a scaling zeta function associated to
\Phi
). Letting
\lambda_{\varphi}
denote the scaling ratio of a similitude
\varphi\in\Phi
, we define the
scaling operator

L_{\Phi}

of the self-similar system

\Phi
by

(3.2)

L_{\Phi}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}}.

The scaling zeta function associated to
\Phi
is simply the scaling zeta function associated to this operator
L_{\Phi}
, i.e.
\zeta_{L_{\Phi}}
, which we will denote by
\zeta_{\Phi}
. We note that the multiplicities of
L_{\Phi}
are positive and integral, and that the scaling ratios
\lambda_{\varphi}
lie in
(0,1)
since the mappings in
\Phi
are nontrivial and contractive.

For such scaling zeta functions, we can give a precise bounds for the locations of their poles. For this purpose, we define
upper and lower similarity dimensions
of a self-similar system
\Phi
which will define a vertical strip in the complex plane containing any such singularity.

Firstly, an upper similarity dimension is an extension of the definition of the similarity dimension of a set, defined by means of a Moran scaling equation.

Definition 3.1: (Upper) Similarity Dimension

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system and let
\left\{\lambda_{\varphi}\right\}_{\varphi\in\Phi}
denote the set of scaling ratios of the similitudes
\varphi\in\Phi
. Then there is a unique real solution
D
to Moran’s equation,

(3.3)

1=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{D}.

This number
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
is called the
(upper) similarity dimension
of
\Phi
.

Under the imposition of the open set condition (Definition
2.2.3
), it follows from Moran’s theorem
[
Mor46
]
that the similarity dimension for a self-similar system of mappings in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
coincides with the Minkowski dimension (and with the Hausdorff dimension) of the invariant set of
\Phi
. This invariant set is called the
self-similar set
associated with
\Phi
. Consequently, it follows that
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
satisfies the bounds
0\leq D\leq N
. Further, supposing that
|\Phi|\geq 2
, it follows that
D>0
.

We will also denote
\dim_{S}(\Phi)=\overline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
when we wish to explicitly differentiate the upper similarity dimension from its lower counterpart (to be defined presently). Under the imposition of the open set condition (Definition
2.2.3
), the (upper) similarity dimension of a self-similar system is exactly the similarity dimension of its invariant set by Moran’s theorem
[
Mor46
]
. It is called an
upper
similarity dimension since it equals the largest real part of the poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, with all its other poles having equal or smaller real parts, which follows by
[
LvF13
, Theorem 3.6]
.

Next, we define the
lower similarity dimension
of a self-similar system. The (upper) similarity dimension is a pole of
\zeta_{\Phi}
with largest real part (per
[
LvF13
, Theorem 3.6]
), so the lower similarity dimension will be the infimum of these poles.

Definition 3.2: Lower Similarity Dimension

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system and let
\zeta_{\Phi}
be its associated scaling zeta function (i.e.
\zeta_{\Phi}(s)=(1-\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{s})^{-1}
, where
\lambda_{\varphi}
is the scaling ratio of
\varphi
). Then the
lower similarity dimension
of
\Phi
, denoted by
\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
, is defined by

(3.4)

\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi):=\inf\left\{\Re(\omega)\,\middle|\,\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{C})\right\},

where
\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the set of poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
in
\mathbb{C}
(given by solutions of the equation
\zeta_{\Phi}(\omega)^{-1}=0
).

If
\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{C})
were to contain points with arbitrarily small real parts, then we would have that
\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)=-\infty
. However, we have the following lower bound for
\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
, obtained in
[
LvF13
]
. Given a self-similar system
\Phi
, let
\left\{r_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{M}
denote the
distinct
scaling ratios of the mappings in
\Phi
with corresponding multiplicities
m_{k}
. Suppose that the scaling ratios are ordered by size, i.e.
r_{1}>r_{2}>...>r_{M}
. Then there is a unique real solution
D_{\ell}
to the equation

(3.5)

\frac{1}{m_{M}}(r_{M}^{-1})^{D_{\ell}}+\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}\frac{m_{k}}{m_{M}}\left(\frac{r_{k}}{r_{M}}\right)^{D_{\ell}}=1,

and by
[
LvF13
, Theorem 3.6]
, we have that
D_{\ell}\leq\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
. We summarize these properties of the similarity dimensions as Proposition
3.1.2
, which is an immediate corollary of
[
LvF13
, Theorem 3.6]
, noting that
\zeta_{\Phi}
is the reciprocal of a Dirichlet polynomial.

Proposition 3.3: Similarity Dimension Bounds

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system and let
\zeta_{\Phi}
be is associated scaling zeta function. Let
D_{\ell}
be the unique real solution to
(
3.5
)
corresponding to
\Phi
. Then for any
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{C})
, the set of poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
in
\mathbb{C}
, we have that

(3.6)

-\infty<D_{\ell}\leq\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)\leq\Re(\omega)\leq\dim_{S}(\Phi).

If
|\Phi|\geq 2
, then
\dim_{S}(\Phi)\geq D_{\ell}>0
. Furthermore, under the imposition of the open set condition (Definition
2.2.3
), we have that
\dim_{S}(\Phi)\leq N
.

Note that the upper bound in (
3.6
) is achieved, since
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
is a pole of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, but that the lower bound
D_{\ell}\leq\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
need not be achieved in general. A sufficient condition for this lower similarity dimension to be the infimum of the real parts of the poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
is the full rank nonlattice case (called the generic nonlattice case in
[
LvF13
]
), i.e. when the multiplicative group
G=\prod_{k=1}^{M}r_{k}^{\mathbb{Z}}
has rank
M
. Note that this occurs for Lebesgue almost every tuple of distinct scaling ratios
(r_{1},...,r_{M})\in(0,1)^{M}
.

3.1.3.
Scaling Functional Equations

Given functions
f,R\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and a scaling operator
L
, a scaling functional equation for
f
with remainder
R
is an identity of the form
f=L[f]+R
. If
R\equiv 0
, the scaling functional equation is said to be exact, and if not then it is said to be an approximate scaling functional equation. Given such a scaling functional equation, it can be solved directly by means of truncated Mellin transforms
[
Hof25
]
or it can be converted into an additive functional equation through changes of variables and then solved by the renewal theorem of Feller
[
Fel50
]
.

A scaling functional equation can be established for a quantity
f_{A}(t)=f(t;A)
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
A
is a geometric object such as a relative fractal drum or a subset of Euclidean space, through two elements: a
decomposition induced by a self-similar system

\Phi
and a
scaling law
satisfied by
f
. To the former, a decomposition of
f
induced by
\Phi
is an identity of the form

f(t;A)=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}f(t;\varphi[A])+R(t),

where
R
is some error quantity called a
decomposition remainder
. A scaling law for a family of functions
\left\{f_{A}\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}
is an identity of the form
f(t;\varphi[A])=f(t/\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha};A)
, where
\alpha
is fixed (for tube functions
\alpha=1
and for heat content, per Corollary
3.1.4
,
\alpha=2
) and
\lambda_{\varphi}
is the scaling ratio of
\varphi
. Note that we assume for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
, so too is
\varphi[A]\in\mathcal{A}
. Letting
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
, it follows that
f_{A}
satisfies the scaling functional equation
f_{A}=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f_{A}]+R
.

More precisely, let
\Phi
be a self-similar system on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(or, more generally, on a complete metric space) and let
\mathcal{A}
be a collection of objects with the following closure property: if
A\in\mathcal{A}
, then
\varphi[A]
is well-defined and
\varphi[A]\in\mathcal{A}
for each
\varphi\in\Phi
. For brevity, we will say that
\mathcal{A}
is
\Phi
-closed. Next, let
\left\{f_{A}\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}
be a family of functions
f_{A}:I\to G
for some set
I
and an additive semigroup
(G,+)
. We will identify
f
with the family and write
f(t;A)=f_{A}(t)
for
t\in I
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a self-similar set, let
\Phi
be a self-similar system having
X
as its invariant set, and let
f_{A}(t)=f(t;A)
be a function defined on
\mathbb{R}
and certain subsets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Here, we will only require that if
f_{A}
is defined on
A
, then
f_{\varphi[A]}
is defined for any image
\varphi[A]
of
A
under the maps
\varphi\in\Phi
.

Definition 3.4: Induced Decomposion

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\Phi
-closed collection of objects, and let
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{A}\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}
be a family of functions
f_{A}:I\to G
for some set
I
and an additive semigroup
(G,+)
. Let also
R:I\to G
be a function.

A self-similar system
\Phi
is said to
induce a decomposition
of
f
on
I
with remainder
R
if for all
t\in I
and
f_{A}\in\mathcal{F}
,

(3.7)

f(t;A)=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}f(t;\varphi[A])+R(t).

In this work,
I
is always a subset of
\mathbb{R}
and the codomain is the field
\mathbb{R}
with its standard addition operation. Further,
A
is some geometric structure, either a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
or, more generally, a relative fractal drum
(X,\Omega)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\varphi[A]
is its pointwise image. For a relative fractal drum, we define
\varphi[(X,\Omega)]:=(\varphi[X],\varphi[\Omega])
, noting that this also defines a relative fractal drum.

An induced decomposition will be paired with a scaling law satisfied by the function
f(t;A)
. A linear scaling (invariance) law would be of the form
f(\lambda t;\lambda A)=f(t;A)
, for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. However, depending on the context, the real and set parameters may scale differently. In the case of heat contents, the function will obey a quadratic scaling law:
f(\lambda^{2}\,t;\lambda A)=f(t;A)
. We provide a general notion for defining the scaling relation which allows one to specify the parameter
\alpha
dictating this relationship.

Definition 3.5:
\alpha
-Scaling Law

Let
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f(\cdot;A)\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}
be a family of functions
f_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
where
\mathcal{A}
is a
\left\{\varphi\right\}
-closed collection of objects for any similitude
\varphi
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
(and in this work,
\alpha>0
), we say that
\mathcal{F}
has an
\alpha
-scaling law
if for any similitude
\varphi
with scaling ratio
\lambda>0
and for any
f_{A}\in\mathcal{F}
,
f(t;\varphi[A])=f(t/\lambda^{\alpha};A)
.

We conclude this section with the main idea: a scaling law and an induced decomposition for a family of functions together lead to the scaling functional equation of a single function.

Proposition 3.6: Scaling Functional Equation (SFE)

Let
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{A}\right\}_{A\in\mathcal{A}}
be a family of functions on
I
satisfying an
\alpha
-scaling law. Suppose that
\Phi
is a self-similar system which induces a decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
on
I
with remainder term
R
. Define the scaling operator
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
by
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
.

Then each function
f_{A}\in\mathcal{F}
in the family satisfies the
scaling function equation

f_{A}=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f_{A}]+R
on
I
with error term
R
, which is to say that for all
t\in I
,

f_{A}(t)=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f_{A}](t)+R(t).

We say that
\Phi
induces a scaling functional equation for
f_{A}
with operator
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
.

Proposition
3.1.3
is, in essence, an untangling of definitions meant to lead to a single key concept:
a self-similar system induces a scaling functional equation
for functions with scaling laws. It is, however, a consequence of the definitions that any function in this family satisfies an induced scaling functional equation. The proof is merely to apply the
\alpha
-scaling law to each term of an induced decomposition and then use the definition of the scaling operator
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
to rewrite the decomposition in terms of a single function.

3.1.4.
Scaling Functional Equations for Heat Content

Proposition
2.1.2

-scaling law in the sense of Definition

Corollary 3.7:
2
-Scaling Law of Heat Content

Given any bounded open set

and any similitude
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}

E_{A}(t)
be the total heat content for the PWB solution

of Problem

for each of the sets
\Omega=A
and
\Omega=\varphi^{\circ k}[A]
,

(with respect to the

-closed set

-scaling law in the sense of Definition

, viz. for every

and

(t/\lambda^{2})^{-N/2}E_{A}(t/\lambda^{2})=t^{-N/2}E_{\varphi[A]}(t).

Proof.

The constant, nonnegative boundary conditions of Problem

ensure that

is unique and they are clearly scale invariant and parabolically scale invariant in their respective variables. Because
\varphi
is a similitude, every set
\varphi^{\circ k}[A]
is bounded—using the bound
\lambda^{k}C
, where

Applying Proposition

to an arbitrary set

\displaystyle t^{-N/2}E_{\varphi[A]}(t)=t^{-N/2}\lambda^{N}E_{A}(t/\lambda^{2})=(t/\lambda^{2})^{-N/2}E_{A}(t/\lambda^{2}).

∎

Suppose now that our region
\Omega
has a self-similar boundary
\partial\Omega
corresponding to a self-similar system
\Phi
and that
\Omega
is an osculating set (see Definition
2.2.3
) for

. In order to use the scaling property to obtain a scaling functional equation, we will need an induced decomposition in the sense of Definition

. For later use, we define the remainder which occurs as the difference of the heat content and the sum of scaled copies. Given a suitable estimate of this quantity, we will be able to obtain explicit formulae for the heat content.

Definition 3.8: Decomposition Remainder

Let
u_{\Omega}
be the PWB solution to Problem
2.2
on the bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Suppose that
\partial\Omega
is the attractor of a self-similar system
\Phi
and that the relative fractal drum
(\partial\Omega,\Omega)
is osculant.

We define the
decomposition remainder
of
E_{\Omega}
to be the quantity

(3.8)

R_{\Omega}(t):=E_{\Omega}(t)-\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}E_{\varphi[\Omega]}(t).

The normalized remainder is the quantity
R(t):=t^{-N/2}R_{\Omega}(t)
for
t>0
.

Note that in the case of Problem
2.2
, we can deduce that
R_{\Omega}
is bounded for all

as a corollary of Proposition
2.1.2
. This will provide an upper bound for the abscissa of absolute convergence of its truncated Mellin transform,
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[t^{-N}R_{\Omega}(t)]

. This helps establish some technical preliminaries for later proofs, allowing us to concern ourselves solely with asymptotic estimates of the form
R_{\Omega}(t)=O(t^{{(N-\sigma_{0})}/2})

, for some
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. When

is the smallest such parameter, it corresponds to
\sigma_{0}=2\sigma_{ac}

The most precise results occur when there is no error term

The novelty of this method, though, lies in its ability to handle cases where this is not true, but with explicit estimates for the degree to which the decomposition is not exact, such as in the case of generalized von Koch fractals. The key part of using this scaling function approach is obtaining good estimates for the remainder term (viz. for small values of

3.2.
Admissible Remainders

3.2.1.
Admissibility

As part of our analysis of scaling functional equations, we will be required to show that certain functions are languid (see Section
2.3
). In so doing, we will need to constrain the types of remainders which can appear in order for these results to be established. In this section, we introduce terminology for such
admissible remainders
relative to a given self-similar system as well as some sufficient conditions for admissibility.

The admissibility of a remainder is essentially tied to a need for
joint languidity
. Two functions, the Mellin transform of the remainder
\zeta_{R}
and the scaling zeta function
\zeta_{\Phi}
, must share the same screen and the same horizontal contours on which they are both uniformly bounded. They must both be languid (with exponent
\kappa
) on a shared screen. It is for this reason that we introduced the notion of
joint languidity
(Definition
2.3.5
).

Definition 3.9: Admissible Remainders and Screens

Let
R
be a function such that its truncated Mellin transform
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)
is holomorphic in the half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
.
1
1

1

This occurs, for instance, if
R
is continuous on
(0,\delta]
and
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}})
as
t\to 0^{+}
(per Lemma
B.3
).

Let
L
be a scaling operator with associated scaling zeta function
\zeta_{L}
. We say that
R
is an
admissible remainder
for
L
if there exists a screen
S
such that
\zeta_{L}
and
\zeta_{R}
are jointly languid with exponent
\kappa=0
with respect to
S
in the sense of Definition
2.3.5
. Any such screen
S
is called an
admissible screen
for
R
and
L
(or their respective zeta functions).

Given a self-similar system
\Phi
, we say that
R
is an admissible remainder for
\Phi
if it is admissible for the associated operator
L_{\Phi}
(and similarly for admissible screens).

3.2.2.
Lower Dimension Criterion

The easiest way to obtain a nontrivial admissible screen occurs when
\zeta_{R}
is holomorphic in a half-plane to the left of the vertical strip containing all of the poles of
\zeta_{L}
. This ensures that a screen may be chosen to the left of any pole of
\zeta_{L}
but also in a region where
\zeta_{R}
is holomorphic. Supposing that
L=L_{\Phi}
is the scaling operator associated to a self-similar system, we have that this strip is bounded explicitly by the similarity dimensions of
\Phi
: if
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}
, then
\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)\leq\Re(\omega)\leq\dim_{S}(\Phi)
. So, the first criterion is to ensure an estimate for
R
that guarantees that the abscissa of absolute convergence of
\zeta_{R}
is strictly smaller than the explicit bound
D_{\ell}
for the lower similarity dimension, which is used to estimate
\zeta_{\Phi}
.

Theorem 3.10: Lower Dimension Criterion for Admissibility

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system and let
D_{\ell}\leq\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
be the lower similarity dimension bound as in Proposition
3.1.2
. For any
R\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, if there exists
\sigma_{0}<D_{\ell}
such that as
t\to 0^{+}
,
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}})
, then
R
is an admissible remainder for
\Phi
and any screen of the form
S_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\equiv\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon
, with
0<\varepsilon<D_{\ell}-\sigma_{0}
, is admissible.

Proof.

Let
D_{\ell}\leq\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
be the lower similarity dimension bound for
\Phi
and let
S_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\equiv\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon
be a constant screen in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
, where
\varepsilon>0
is such that
\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon<D_{\ell}
. We will show that
\zeta_{R}
and
\zeta_{\Phi}
are jointly languid with exponent
\kappa=0
on
S_{\varepsilon}
.

To start, we have that
\zeta_{\Phi}
is strongly languid with exponent
\kappa=0
by Corollary
2.3.4
. This guarantees that for any
\sigma_{-}
, there exists a sequence of admissible heights
\left\{T_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
(i.e. with

and

as

with

such that
\zeta_{\Phi}
is uniformly bounded on horizontal contours of the form
[S_{-}(T_{n})+iT_{n},\sigma_{+}+iT_{n}]

\sigma_{+}>\max(\sigma_{-},\dim_{S}(\Phi))
. In particular,
\zeta_{\Phi}
will be uniformly bounded on the subsets
I_{n}:=[\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon+iT_{n},\sigma_{+}+iT_{n}]

L1

\zeta_{\Phi}
.

Next, we note that the estimate on
R

\zeta_{R}

\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
by Lemma

\zeta_{R}
is bounded on the screen
S_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\equiv\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon>\sigma_{0}
as well as on any vertical strip of the form
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon}^{\sigma_{+}}
. This establishes both languidity hypotheses
L1
and
L2
with exponent
\kappa=0
and on the screen
S_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the intervals
I_{n}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon}^{\sigma_{+}}
, with shared sequence of admissible heights.

It remains to show that
\zeta_{\Phi}
is bounded on the screen
S_{\varepsilon}
. To this end, we enforce that
\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon<D_{\ell}
, in which case for any
s
on the screen,
\Re(s)=\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon<D_{\ell}
. Let
\left\{r_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
denote the set of unique scaling ratios of
\Phi
, arranged in decreasing order
r_{1}\geq...\geq r_{M}
, and let
m_{k}
denote the multiplicity of
r_{k}
. Define the function

(3.9)

p(t)=\frac{1}{m_{M}}(r_{M}^{-1})^{t}+\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}\frac{m_{k}}{m_{M}}\left(\frac{r_{k}}{r_{M}}\right)^{t},

which is readily seen to be strictly increasing with range
(0,\infty)
. Note that
p(D_{\ell})=1
by definition. We will obtain a bound for
\zeta_{\Phi}(s)
when
\Re(s)=\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon<D_{\ell}
using this function.

To that end, let
f(s)=\zeta_{\Phi}(s)^{-1}=1-\sum_{k=1}^{M}m_{k}r_{k}^{s}
be the denominator and let
\sigma=\Re(s)
. Then

\displaystyle|r_{M}^{-s}/m_{M}f(s)+1|

\displaystyle=\left|r_{M}^{-s}/m_{M}-\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}m_{k}/m_{M}(r_{k}/r_{M})^{s}\right|

\displaystyle\leq p(\sigma)<p(D_{\ell})=1.

By the reverse triangle inequality, we have that

\displaystyle 1>p(\sigma)\geq\left||r_{M}^{-s}/m_{M}f(s)|-1\right|=\left|r_{M}^{-\sigma}/m_{M}|f(s)|-1\right|,

which may be rewritten as
||f(s)|-m_{M}r^{\sigma}|\leq m_{M}r^{\sigma}p(\sigma)<m_{M}r_{M}^{\sigma}
. Once again using the reverse triangle inequality, we find that the lower bound for
|f|
is furnished by

\displaystyle|f(s)|\geq m_{M}r_{M}^{\sigma}-||f(s)|-m_{M}r^{\sigma}|\geq m_{M}r_{M}^{\sigma}-m_{M}r^{\sigma}p(\sigma)>0.

It follows that when
\Re(s)=\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon
is fixed,
|\zeta_{\Phi}(s)|\leq C_{\Phi}
, with
C_{\Phi}=(m_{M}r_{M}^{\sigma}-m_{M}r^{\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon}p(\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon))^{-1}
. This establishes hypothesis
L2
for
\zeta_{\Phi}
, and thus
\zeta_{\Phi}
and
\zeta_{R}
are jointly languid on the screen
S_{\varepsilon}
(with exponent
\kappa=0
).
∎

In order to accommodate
\alpha
-scaling laws with
\alpha>0
(in the sense of Definition
3.1.3
), we will consider scaling operators of the form
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
, where
\Phi
is a self-similar system. In this case, the zeta function associated to
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
is exactly the function
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
, where
\zeta_{\Phi}(s)
is the scaling zeta function associated to
\Phi
itself.

This change of variables amounts to a simple rescaling of the bound with respect to the lower similarity dimension of
\Phi
a remainder must satisfy in order to be admissible by Theorem
3.2.2
. Namely, when
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{R}})
as
t\to 0^{+}
for some
\sigma_{R}<D_{\ell}/\alpha\leq\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)/\alpha
, we have that
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
has poles in the strip
\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)/\alpha\leq\Re(s)\leq\dim_{S}(\Phi)/\alpha
and
\sigma_{R}
lies strictly to the left of this bound. Equivalently, if we state the estimate for
R
in the form
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}/\alpha})
(as
t\to 0^{+}
), where
\sigma_{0}/\alpha=\sigma_{R}
, then
\sigma_{0}
must satisfy the bound
\sigma_{0}<D_{\ell}
directly. The estimates for
\zeta_{\Phi}
apply to its rescaled analogue, and thus the proof of Theorem
3.2.2
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.11: Rescaled Lower Dimension Criterion

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system and let
D_{\ell}\leq\underline{\dim}_{S}(\Phi)
be the lower similarity dimension bound as in Proposition
3.1.2
. Given
\alpha>0
, define the scaling operator
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
.

For any
R\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, if there exists
\sigma_{R}<D_{\ell}/\alpha
such that as
t\to 0^{+}
,
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{R}})
, then
R
is an admissible remainder for
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
and any screen of the form
S_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\equiv\sigma_{R}+\varepsilon
, with
0<\varepsilon<D_{\ell}/\alpha-\sigma_{R}
, is admissible.

Proof.

By Lemma
B.3
, we have that
\zeta_{R}(s)
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
and thus
\zeta_{R}(z/\alpha)
is holomorphic when
\Re(z)>\alpha\,\sigma_{R}
. Further,
\zeta_{R}(s)
is bounded on any vertical strip of the form
\mathbb{H}_{a}^{b}
,
\sigma_{R}<a\leq b<\infty
, so
\zeta_{R}(z/\alpha)
is bounded on the corresponding strips
\mathbb{H}_{a^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}
,
\alpha\,\sigma_{R}<a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<\infty
.

Since
\alpha\,\sigma_{R}<D_{\ell}
, by repeating the proof of Theorem
3.2.2
(with
\sigma_{0}=\alpha\,\sigma_{R}
) we obtain that
\zeta_{\Phi}(z)
and
\zeta_{R}(z/\alpha)
are jointly languid on screens of the form
S_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\equiv\alpha\,\sigma_{R}+\varepsilon
when
0<\varepsilon<D_{\ell}-\alpha\,\sigma_{R}
. Taking
z=\alpha s
, we see that the functions
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
and
\zeta_{R}(s)
are jointly languid on screens of the form
S^{\prime}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\tau)\equiv\sigma_{R}+\varepsilon^{\prime}
where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/\alpha\in(0,D_{\ell}/\alpha-\sigma_{R})
.
∎

3.2.3.
Lattice Criterion

The next criterion is related to when we have explicit knowledge of the locations of the singularities of
\zeta_{\Phi}
. In particular, in the lattice case (see Definition
2.2.2
), we can explicitly show that all of the poles lie on one of finitely many vertical lines (and are distributed periodically along these lines with a shared period for each line); see for instance
[
LvF13
, Theorem 3.6]
. Thus, we can easily choose screens within this region which will never encounter singularities of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, with distance to any pole bounded by the distance of the real part of the screen to the real part of the closest of the finitely many exceptional points.

Theorem 3.12: Lattice Criterion for Admissiblility

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system and suppose that its distinct scaling ratios
\left\{\lambda_{\varphi}\right\}_{\varphi\in\Phi}
are arithmetically related (see Definition
2.2.2
). Let
R
be a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the estimate that
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}})
as
t\to 0^{+}
, for some
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Then for all but finitely many
\sigma>\sigma_{0}
,
S_{\sigma}(\tau)\equiv\sigma
is an admissible screen. Consequently, there are admissible screens of the form
S_{\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

Proof.

By definition, in the lattice case there exists some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for each
\varphi\in\Phi
, there is a positive integer
k_{\varphi}
such that
\lambda_{\varphi}=\lambda_{0}^{k_{\varphi}}
. Under this assumption, we may explicitly write the denominator of
\zeta_{\Phi}
as the Dirichlet polynomial

P(s)=1-\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{s}=1-\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{0}^{k_{\varphi}s}.

Under the change of variables
s=\log_{\lambda_{0}}z
(so that
\lambda_{0}^{s}=z
), we have that

P(\log_{\lambda_{0}}z)=1-\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}z^{k_{\varphi}}.

This is precisely a polynomial in the variable
z
(since
|\Phi|=n<\infty
), so by the fundamental theorem of algebra it has finitely many roots (exactly
K=\max(\left\{k_{\varphi}\right\}_{\varphi\in\Phi})
) in
\mathbb{C}
.

Denote these roots by
Z=\left\{z_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{K}
. Any solution of
P(\omega)=0
must then be of the form
\lambda_{0}^{\omega}=z_{j}
for some
z_{j}\in Z
. We note that
0\notin Z
since the polynomial equals one when
z=0
, so there exists a branch of the logarithm for which
\log z_{j}
is well defined for each
z_{j}\in Z
and without loss of generality we may find a single branch defined for each
z_{j}\in Z
and for
\lambda_{0}
since
Z
is finite. The logarithm is multivalued, however, with
\lambda_{0}^{\omega}=e^{\omega\log\lambda_{0}}=e^{\omega\log\lambda_{0}+2\pi im}
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. So, we will obtain as solutions to
P(\omega)=0
exactly the points
\omega_{j,m}
of the form
\omega_{j,m}=\log(z_{j})/\log(\lambda_{0})+2\pi im/\log(\lambda_{0})
, where
m\in\mathbb{Z}
is arbitrary.

Observe that there are finitely many real parts,
\sigma_{j}=\Re(\log(z_{j})/\log(\lambda_{0}))
,
j=1,...,K
, at which these poles occur and that the imaginary parts are all distributed with the same period,
2\pi/\log\lambda_{0}
. We will show, starting with hypothesis
L1
, that for any screen of the form
S_{\sigma}(\tau)\equiv\sigma
,
\sigma\neq\sigma_{j}
for each
j=1,...,K
,
\zeta_{\Phi}
is languid with exponent
\kappa=0
with respect to
S_{\sigma}
.

The next two steps of the proof are the same as in the proof of Theorem
3.2.2
. In short, by the strong languidity of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, for any
\sigma_{-}
, there exists a sequence of admissible heights
\left\{T_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
on which
\zeta_{\Phi}
is bounded on horizontal intervals of the form
[\sigma_{-}+iT_{n},\sigma_{+}+iT_{n}]
, where
\sigma_{-}
is arbitrarily small and
\sigma_{+}>\max(\dim_{S}(\Phi),\sigma_{-})
. Choosing
\sigma_{-}=\sigma
establishes hypothesis
L1
with respect to the screen
S_{\sigma}
for
\zeta_{\Phi}
. Secondly, we have that
\zeta_{R}
satisfies hypotheses
L1
and
L2
for the sequence of heights as above on any screen of the form
S_{\sigma}
,
\sigma>\sigma_{0}
, because
\zeta_{R}
is a Mellin transform and is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
.

It only remains to show that
\zeta_{\Phi}
satisfies hypothesis
L2
on
S_{\sigma}
when
\sigma
is not one of the exceptional values
\sigma_{j}
,
j=1,...,K
. We will show that
f(\tau)
is bounded from below by a strictly positive constant on all of
\mathbb{R}
, and thus
\zeta_{\Phi}
will be bounded on
S_{\sigma}
. As we have shown,
P(\sigma+i\tau)\neq 0
whenever
\sigma\notin\left\{\sigma_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{K}
. Let
f(\tau)=|P(\sigma+i\tau)|
. Given any compact interval
[a,b]
, we have that
f(t)
must be nonzero and bounded from below by a strictly positive constant on
[a,b]
. This follows by continuity and the intermediate value theorem, noting that if
f(\tau)=0
, then
P(\sigma+i\tau)=0
, which is a contradiction.

We will use the (multiplicative) periodicity of
f
to show that
f(t)=|P(\sigma+i\tau)|
cannot become arbitrarily small as
|\tau|\to\infty
. Recall that

P(\sigma+i\tau)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{0}^{k_{j}(\sigma_{0}+i\tau)}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lambda_{0}^{k_{j}\sigma}\cdot e^{i(k_{j}\log\lambda_{0})\tau}.

Under the transformation
\tau\mapsto(2\pi m/\log\lambda_{0})\,\tau
, for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have that each exponential is invariant since
k_{j}
is an integer. Thus,
P(\sigma+i\tau)=P(\sigma+i(2\pi m/\log\lambda_{0})\tau)
. Choosing
m=-1
shows us that the function
f(\tau):=|P(\sigma+i\tau)|
is multiplicatively periodic with period
p=-2\pi/\log\lambda_{0}=2\pi/\log\lambda_{0}^{-1}
or a rational multiple thereof, depending on the greatest common divisor of the integers
k_{j}
,
j=1,...,K
. However, if they share a greatest common divisor
GCD
, it is possible to redefine
\lambda_{0}
by
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}=\lambda_{0}^{\text{GCD}}
and obtain a new set of integers without this property. Note that
\log(\lambda_{0}^{-1})>0
.

By the previous argument,
f(\tau)=|P(\sigma+i\tau)|
is bounded from below on any compact interval. So,
f
is nonzero on the interval
[-1,1]
. Now pick one full multiplicative period in
(0,\infty)
, say
[p^{m_{0}},p^{m_{0}+1}]
(if
p>1
) or
[p^{m_{0}+1},p^{m_{0}}]
(if
p<1
). The function
f
must be bounded from below strictly away from zero as this is a compact interval. By periodicity, this same bound applies to any interval of the form
[p^{m},p^{m+1}]
or
[p^{m+1},p^{m}]
, respectively, for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since the function
p\mapsto p^{t}
is surjective onto
(0,\infty)
, this implies that
f
is bounded uniformly from below by a strictly positive constant, the same as the first bound, when
\tau>0
. For
\tau<0
, we can use the starting interval
[-p^{m_{0}+1},-p^{m_{0}}]
or
[-p^{m_{0}},-p^{m_{0}+1}]
, depending on whether
p>1
or
p<1
, and the same argument to deduce that it is also bounded from below by a strictly positive constant on
(-\infty,0)
. Taking the minimum of these three bounds shows that
f(\tau)=|P(\sigma+i\tau)|>C>0
.

It follows that
\zeta_{\Phi}
is bounded from above on
S_{\sigma_{0}}
, establishing hypothesis
L2
. Thus, we have established the joint languidity of
\zeta_{\Phi}
and
\zeta_{R}
on any screen of the form
S_{\sigma}
, where
\sigma>\sigma_{0}
and where
\sigma\notin\left\{\sigma_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{K}
. When choosing a screen
S_{\sigma_{0}+\varepsilon}
, choosing
\varepsilon
with
0<\varepsilon<\min_{j=1,...,K}(|\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{0}|)
is sufficient.
∎

3.3.
Solutions of Scaling Functional Equations

In this section, we will obtain explicit formulae for functions which satisfy a scaling functional equation. Given a scaling operator
L
, a scaling functional equation is a relation of the form
f=L[f]+R
, where
R
is a remainder term. We obtain formulae which are valid up to an asymptotic order determined by estimates of an
admissible remainder
term (in the sense of Definition
3.2.1
). We begin with the statement of our results followed by the discussion and estimates needed for their proof.

We focus on scaling functional equations which are induced by a self-similar system on functions which satisfy certain scaling laws (see Proposition
3.1.3
), as these are the main type of scaling functional equations that we will need for our application to heat content in Section
4
. Explicitly, let
\Phi
be a self-similar system, let
\alpha>0
, and define the scaling operator
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
. The scaling functional equations we study herein will be stated in terms of such scaling operators. Note, though, that any general scaling operator with scaling ratios in
(0,1)
each having positive, integral multiplicity can be written in this form.

There are two types of explicit formulae, namely pointwise and distributional (just as in
[
LvF13
,
LRŽ17
]
). The former have the advantage of being simpler in their statements, but the disadvantage that they
may only be valid for antiderivatives of the function satisfying the scaling functional equation and not the function itself. The distributional explicit formulae we obtain do not have this restriction, but they will require additional terminology to state and interpret in a weak formulation.

3.3.1.
Pointwise Explicit Formulae

The pointwise explicit formulae we establish in this work for solutions to a scaling functional equation, say
f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
, will be valid for its antiderivatives. To that end, we introduce the following notation and convention. Firstly, define
f^{[0]}:=f
. For any
k>0
, we define
f^{[k]}
recursively by integrating the previous antiderivative and imposing the convention that
f^{[k]}(0)=0
. Namely, for
k>0
,

f^{[k]}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}f^{[k-1]}(\tau)\,d\tau.

We will also denote
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)
. In our applications, typically we have some function
F(t)
of interest which must be normalized in order to satisfy a scaling law in the sense of Definition
3.1.3
, so we will have that
f(t)=t^{-\beta}F(t)
for a given parameter
\beta
. For tube functions (cf.
[
Hof25
]
),
\beta
is the dimension
N
and for heat content in Section
4
, it will be
N/2
. When we state the explicit formula, we will do so in terms of the parameter
\beta
, noting that typically
\beta=N
. Let
F^{[k]}
be defined in the same manner as
f^{[k]}
,
k\geq 0
.

As an additional preliminary to stating the result, we define the Pochhammer symbol
(z)_{w}:=\Gamma(z+w)/\Gamma(w)
for
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
. Note that when
w=k
is a positive integer, this simplifies to
(z)_{k}=z(z+1)\cdots(z+k-1)
or, when
w=0
, to
(z)_{0}=1
.

Lastly, we must give meaning to the summations which will appear in what follows. Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a discrete (and hence at most countably infinite) subset with the property that for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathcal{D}_{m}:=\left\{\omega\in\mathcal{D}\,\middle|\,|\Im(\omega)|\leq m\right\}<\infty.

Then we define

\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}}a_{\omega}:=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{m}}a_{\omega}.

Note that this summation is a
symmetric limit
of finite partial sums. That is to say, it is a sum over the elements of
\mathcal{D}
with increasing large absolute values of imaginary parts, but with the upper and lower bounds taken at the same rate. As such, the convergence of these sums is comparatively more delicate; it may be that the limit, when taken independently, does not exist.

Theorem 3.13: Pointwise Explicit Formula

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system,
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f(t)=t^{-\beta/\alpha}F(t)\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. Suppose that
\Phi
induces the scaling functional equation
f=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f]+R
on
[0,\delta]
with admissible remainder term
R
(in the sense of Definition
3.2.1
) with corresponding screen
S
. Let
\sigma_{R}
denote the abscissa of absolute convergence of
\zeta_{R}
,
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
the (upper) similarity dimension of
\Phi
, and suppose
\beta/\alpha+1>\max(D/\alpha,\sigma_{R})
. Lastly, suppose that
S
is contained in the half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
and write
W_{S}
for the window to the right of
S
.

Then for every
k\geq 2
in
\mathbb{Z}
and every
t\in(0,\delta)
, we have that

F^{[k]}(t)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\alpha W_{S})}\Res\Bigg(\cfrac{t^{(\beta-s)/\alpha+k}}{((\beta-s)/\alpha+1)_{k}}\zeta_{f}(s/\alpha;\delta);\omega\Bigg)+\mathcal{R}^{k}(t),

where
\zeta_{f}
is given by (
3.18
). The error term satisfies
\mathcal{R}^{k}(t)=O(t^{\beta/\alpha-\sup(S)+k})
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

Note, in particular, that the order of the remainder directly controls the error of the approximation. As we are considering small values of
t
, the larger the exponent, the better the remainder estimate. The restriction of
k\geq 2
is related to the exponent
\kappa=0
of the languid growth of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, and consequently of
\zeta_{f}
.

3.3.2.
Distributional Explicit Formulae

Put simply, a
distribution
is a functional on a prescribed space of functions called
test functions
. A standard distribution is a functional on the space
C_{c}^{\infty}
of smooth, compactly supported functions on a given domain. The type of distributions we consider here, called
tempered distributions
, will be a subspace of these distributions.

We define as the space of test functions the class of Schwartz functions, or functions of rapid decrease. For a finite domain
(0,\delta)
, just as in
[
LvF13
, Chapter 5]
and
[
LRŽ17
, Chapter 5]
, the space is defined by

(3.10)

\mathcal{S}(0,\delta):=\left\{\psi\in C^{\infty}(0,\delta)\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} &\forall m\in\mathbb{Z},\,\forall q\in\mathbb{N},\text{ as }t\to 0^{+},\\
&t^{m}\psi^{(q)}(t)\to 0\text{ and }(t-\delta)^{m}\psi^{(q)}(t)\to 0\,\end{aligned}\right\}.

Note that
C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\delta)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
, with a continuous embedding, whence by duality the space of functionals on
\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
is a subset of the usual space of distributions, the dual of
C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\delta)
. Typically, for a distribution
F
and a test function
\psi
, we write
F(\psi)=\langle F,\psi\rangle
, denoting by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the natural pairing of
\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
with its dual space.

In this distributional setting, the restriction of
k\geq 2
may be relaxed in Theorem
3.3.1
. However, equality must be interpreted
in the sense of distributions
: we say that
F=G
in the sense of distributions if for any test function
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
,
\langle F,\psi\rangle=\langle G,\psi\rangle
. In addition to the standard definitions for the definitions of differentiation and integration of distributions, we note the following relevant identity regarding residues at a point:

(3.11)

\langle\Res(t^{\beta}G(s);\omega),\psi\rangle=\Res(\mathcal{M}[\psi](\beta+1)G(s);\omega).

See
[
LRŽ17
, Equation 5.2.5]
, as well as the preceding discussion regarding the extension of
\psi
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Secondly, we provide the definition for a distribution
\mathcal{R}
to satisfy the error estimate
O(t^{\alpha})
as
t\to 0^{+}
(defined as in
[
LRŽ17
, Theorem 5.2.11]
). Given a test function
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
and
a>0
, define the new test function
\psi_{a}(t):=a^{-1}\psi(t/a)
. We say that
\mathcal{R}(t)=O(t^{\alpha})
as
t\to 0^{+}
if for all
a>0
and for all
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
,

(3.12)

\langle\mathcal{R},\psi_{a}(t)\rangle=O(a^{\alpha}),

as
t\to 0^{+}
in the usual sense.

Theorem 3.14: Distributional Explicit Formula

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system,
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f(t)=t^{-\beta/\alpha}F(t)\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. Suppose that
\Phi
induces the scaling functional equation
f=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f]+R
on
[0,\delta]
with admissible remainder term
R
(in the sense of Definition
3.2.1
) with corresponding screen

. Let
\sigma_{R}
denote the abscissa of absolute convergence of
\zeta_{R}
,
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
the (upper) similarity dimension of
\Phi
, and suppose
\beta/\alpha+1>\max(D/\alpha,\sigma_{R})
. Lastly, suppose that
S
is contained in the half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
and write
W_{S}
for the window to the right of
S
.

Then for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have that, in the sense of distributions,
F^{[k]}
satisfies

(3.13)

\begin{split}F^{[k]}(t)&=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\alpha W_{S})}\Res\Bigg(\cfrac{t^{(\beta-s)/\alpha+k}}{((\beta-s)/\alpha+1)_{k}}\zeta_{f}(s/\alpha;\delta);\omega\Bigg)+\mathcal{R}^{[k]}(t),\end{split}

as
t\to 0^{+}
for a remainder distribution term
\mathcal{R}^{[k]}
. See (
3.14
) for the explicit identity of action on test functions.

Here,
\zeta_{f}
is as in Corollary
3.3.3
. The distributional remainder term satisfies the estimate
\mathcal{R}^{[k]}(t)=O(t^{\beta/\alpha-\sup(S)+k})
as
t\to 0^{+}
, in the sense of (
3.12
).

Most precisely, (
3.13
) means that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
,

(3.14)

\begin{split}\langle F^{[k]},\psi\rangle&=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\alpha W_{S})}\Res\Bigg(\cfrac{\mathcal{M}[\psi]({(\beta-s)/\alpha+k+1})}{((\beta-s)/\alpha+1)_{k}}\zeta_{f}(s/\alpha);\omega\Bigg)+\langle\mathcal{R}^{[k]},\psi\rangle.\end{split}

While this formulation is less direct compared to the pointwise expansion, it does have the advantage of requiring less regularity to leverage the expansion. Namely, it is valid when
k=0
, yielding a formula (in the sense of distributions) for the function
f
itself.

Corollary 3.15: Distributional Explicit Formula,
k=0

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system,
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f(t)=t^{-\beta/\alpha}F(t)\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. Suppose that
\Phi
induces the scaling functional equation
f=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f]+R
on
[0,\delta]
with admissible remainder term
R
(in the sense of Definition
3.2.1
) with corresponding screen
S
. Let
\sigma_{R}
denote the abscissa of absolute convergence of
\zeta_{R}
,
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
the (upper) similarity dimension of
\Phi
, and suppose
\beta/\alpha+1>\max(D/\alpha,\sigma_{R})
. Lastly, suppose that
S
is contained in the half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
and write
W_{S}
for the window to the right of
S
.

Then in the sense of distributions we have that for
t\in[0,\delta)
,

(3.15)

F(t)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\alpha W_{S})}\Res\big(t^{{\beta-s}/\alpha}\zeta_{f}(s/\alpha;\delta);\omega\big)+\mathcal{R}(t).

The distributional remainder term satisfies the estimate
\mathcal{R}(t)=O(t^{\beta/\alpha-\sup(S)})
as
t\to 0^{+}
, in the sense of (
3.12
).

If we further assume that the poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
in
W_{S}
are simple, then (
3.15
) simplifies to

f(t)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\alpha W_{S})}\Res(\zeta_{f}(s/\alpha;\delta);\omega)\,t^{(\beta-\omega)/\alpha}+\mathcal{R}(t).

In this formulation, and in particular in the case when the self-similar system induces simple poles of its zeta function, the formula is a simple expansion with constant coefficients and powers determined by the poles
\omega
. The computation of the residues, however, is not a trivial matter and beyond the current scope of this work. Corollary
3.3.2
is a direct application of Theorem
3.3.2
.

3.3.3.
Formulae for the Zeta Functions

We will discuss the solution of scaling functional equations by means of truncated Mellin transforms (see Definition
2.2.4
and Appendix
B
). If what follows, let
\delta>0
be fixed and let
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}
be the truncated Mellin transform on
(0,\delta)
. Given a function
f
which is locally (Lebesgue) integrable on
(0,\delta)
, define
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta):=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
for which the integral is convergent. We say that
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)
is a
tamed Dirichlet-type integral (DTI)
(viz.
[
LRŽ17
, Definition A.1.2]
) if
d\mu(t)=f(t)dt
is a local complex measure (i.e. its restriction to any compact subset is a complex measure) on
[0,\delta]
and if there exists
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that the defining integral is convergent (and hence holomorphic) on
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
. See Appendix
B
for more information. For example, if
f
is integrable, bounded away from
0
, and
f(t)=O(t^{-\sigma})
as
t\to 0^{+}
then
\zeta_{f}
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
(viz. Lemma
B.3
).

Given a scaling operator
L:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}M_{\lambda_{k}}
, define

(3.16)

\xi_{L,f}(s;\delta):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\lambda_{k}^{s}\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{k}}[f](s).

If
\Phi
is a self-similar system, then define
\xi_{\Phi,f}:=\xi_{L_{\Phi},f}
, where
L_{\Phi}
is the scaling operator associated to
\Phi
. Lastly, we recall that
\zeta_{L}
is the scaling zeta function of
L
, with singular set
\mathcal{D}_{L}
(see Section
3.1
).

The following is essentially
[
Hof25
, Theorem 4.8]
, although we take the opportunity to state the theorem with slightly more precise and slightly more general hypotheses.

Theorem 3.16: Zeta Functions of Solutions to General SFEs

Let
L:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}M_{\lambda_{k}}
be a scaling operator and
\Lambda=\max\left\{\lambda_{k}^{-1}\right\}_{k=1}^{m}
.
Fixing
\delta>0
, let
f
and
R
be integrable functions on
(0,\Lambda\delta)
and
(0,\delta)
, respectively, and suppose that
\zeta_{f}(s;\Lambda\delta):=\mathcal{M}^{\Lambda\delta}[f](s)
and
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta):=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)
are tamed DTIs (viz.
[
LRŽ17
, Definition A.1.2]
), with respective abscissae of absolute convergence
\sigma_{f}
and
\sigma_{R}
, respectively.
Let
L:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}M_{\lambda_{k}}
be a scaling operator for which
f
satisfies the scaling functional equation
f=L[f]+R
for all
t\in(0,\delta)
.

Then for all
s\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{C})
,

(3.17)

\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)=\zeta_{L}(s)(\xi_{L,f}(s;\delta)+\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)),

where
\xi_{L,f}
, defined as in (
3.16
), is an entire function. Further,
\zeta_{f}
is holomorphic in the half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\max(D,\sigma_{R})}
, where where
D
is the unique real pole of
\zeta_{L}
, and admits a meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
with any poles contained in
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}})
. Further, these poles are independent of the choice of
\delta
, i.e.
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)
and
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta^{\prime})
have the same poles for any
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,\delta)
.

Proof.

As tamed DTIs, there exist
\sigma_{f},\sigma_{R}\in[-\infty,\infty)
for which
\zeta_{f}(s;\Lambda\delta)
and
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)
are holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{f}}
and
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
, respectively, with the convention that
\mathbb{H}_{-\infty}=\mathbb{C}
. Consequently, they are both holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{M}}
where
\sigma_{M}:=\max(\sigma_{f},\sigma_{R})
.
Additionally, we have that by Lemma
B.4
, for each
M_{\lambda_{k}}
,

\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[M_{\lambda_{k}}[f]](s)=\lambda_{k}^{s}\mathcal{M}^{\delta/\lambda_{k}}[f](s).

Note that each function
\mathcal{M}^{\delta/\lambda_{k}}[f]
,
k=1,...,m
, is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{f}}
since
\zeta_{f}(s;\Lambda\delta)
is assumed to be a tamed DTI for the largest interval of the form
(0,\delta/\lambda_{k})
,
k=1,...,m
. (It follows from Lemma
B.3
that the difference between
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta_{1})
and
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta_{2})
is an entire function for any
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,\Lambda\delta)
, so integration over a subset yields a function with the same holomorphicity properties.)

With these holomorphicity properties, the proof of
[
Hof25
, Theorem 4.8]
is applicable without further modification, noting that
E(s)=\xi_{L,f}(s;\delta)
and
\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{R}
. The independence of the poles of
\zeta_{f}
on the value of
\delta
may be seen as a corollary of Lemma
B.3
.

∎

We now specialize to operators of the form
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
, where
\Phi
is a self-similar system and
\alpha>0
. We require that
\alpha>0
, so that
\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}\in(0,1)
for each
\varphi\in\Phi
. When
\alpha=1
, this is exactly the scaling operator
L_{\Phi}
associated to
\Phi
. For other values of
\alpha
, the effect on the associated zeta function is simply a rescaling of the input relative to
\zeta_{\Phi}
, namely
\zeta_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}}(s)=\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
. (This is an immediate consequence of the definition of these functions and elementary properties of exponents.) Note that it follows that if
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
is the similarity dimension of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, an upper bound for the real parts of its poles by Proposition
3.1.2
, then
\zeta_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}}
is holomorphic in the half plane
\mathbb{H}_{D/\alpha}
. Further, the sets of poles are directly related: any pole
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}
exactly corresponds to the pole
\omega/\alpha\in\mathcal{D}_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}}
.
These observations together yield the following corollary of
[
Hof25
, Theorem 4.8]
.

Corollary 3.17: Zeta Functions of Solutions to Self-Similar SFEs

Let
f,R\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and suppose that there exist
\sigma_{R},\sigma_{f}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{R}})
and
f(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{f}})
as
t\to 0^{+}
. Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system, let
\alpha>0
, and let
{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
. If
f
satisfies the scaling functional equation
f={L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}}[f]+R
for all
t\in(0,\delta)
, then

(3.18)

\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)=\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)(\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)+\zeta_{R}(s;\delta))

and is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\max(D/\alpha,\sigma_{R})}
, where
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
. Further,
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)
is meromorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
, it has poles in a subset of
\alpha^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}})
, and these poles are independent of
\delta
.

We note that the assumptions on
f
and
R
are sufficient to ensure that
\zeta_{f}
and
\zeta_{R}
are tamed DTIs (viz. as a corollary of Lemma
B.3
), as they are holomorphic in the half-planes
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{f}}
and
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
, respectively. These values of
\sigma_{f}
and
\sigma_{R}
give upper bounds for the abscissa of absolute convergence of
\zeta_{f}
and
\zeta_{R}
, respectively. So long as
\sigma_{f}
and
\sigma_{R}
are chosen to be the minimal such exponents for which these estimates hold, they will equal their respective abscissae of absolute convergence. (See Appendix
B
for more detail.)

When
L=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
, we note that (
3.16
) is explicitly the entire function

(3.19)

\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha s}\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}[f](s).

In this setting, it will be convenient to state the estimate for
R
as
t\to 0^{+}
in the form
R(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}/\alpha})
, where
\sigma_{R}=\sigma_{0}/\alpha
. Under the change of variables
s^{\prime}=s/\alpha
, a pole
\omega^{\prime}=\omega/\alpha\in\alpha^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}})
of
\zeta_{f}(s^{\prime};\delta)
corresponds to the pole
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}})
of
\zeta_{f}(s/\alpha;\delta)
. Thus, in Section
3.3
when we consider poles of
\zeta_{f}
in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
, under these conventions we will be considering the poles of
\zeta_{\Phi}
in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
. In Section
4.4
, we will see that this change of variables ultimately clarifies the direct connection of spectral complex dimensions (regarding heat content and heat zeta functions) to the geometric complex dimensions (defined by fractal zeta functions such as the tube zeta function).

The next step for solving the scaling functional equation
f=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f]+R
is to apply the Mellin inversion theorem to (
3.18
), which is the content of
[
Hof25
, Theorem 4.9]
. Note the convention regarding the contour integral, in that

\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}f(z)\,dz:=\lim_{T\to+\infty}\int_{c-iT}^{c+iT}f(z)\,dz,

which may also be considered a principal value integral (e.g. as in
[
Gra10
]
). We state the formula using the notation of Corollary
3.3.3
(i.e. in terms of
\zeta_{\Phi}
,
\zeta_{R}
, and
\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}
) here as a proposition for later reference.

Proposition 3.18: Mellin Inversion Formula

Let
f,R\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system, let
\alpha>0
, and let
{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}}:=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}M_{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}
. Suppose that
f
satisfies the scaling functional equation
f={L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}}[f]+R
for all
t\in[0,\delta]
and that
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[R](s)
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}}
. Lastly, let
D=\dim_{S}(\Phi)
and
c>\max(D/\alpha,\sigma_{R})
. Then for any
t\in(0,\delta)
,
f
is given by:

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}[\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)(\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)+\zeta_{R}(s;\delta))](t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}t^{-s}\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)(\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)+\zeta_{R}(s;\delta))\,ds.

It remains to evaluate this contour integral by means of the residue theorem. Strictly speaking, it requires a sort of unbounded residue theorem, using a sequence of regions for which the integrand has finitely many poles (on which the residue theorem applies) that converge to the region containing all of the countably many singularities. The conditions of languidity are necessary in this process, used to estimate contour integrals over pieces of the boundaries of regions involved in this process. This method developed to establish explicit formulae for fractal tube functions in
[
LvF13
, Chapter 5 and Chapter 8]
and then, with refinements to the proof, used to prove explicit formulae for relative tube functions in
[
LRŽ17
, Chapter 5]
.

3.3.4.
Languidity from Scaling Functional Equations

We will need growth estimates for
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)
when
f
is the solution of a scaling functional equation. In particular, using the formula in Corollary
3.3.3
, we show that
\zeta_{f}
is languid provided some control over the remainder term itself.

Theorem 3.19: Languidity of Zeta Functions from SFEs

Let
\Phi
be a self-similar system, let
\alpha>0
, and
f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. Suppose that
\Phi
induces the scaling functional equation
f=L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}[f]+R
on
[0,\delta]
with admissible remainder term
R
for
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
(in the sense of Definition
3.2.1
) and let
S
be an admissible screen.

Then the zeta function
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)=\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f](s)
is also languid with exponent
\kappa=0
with respect to
S
.

Proof.

Because
R
is an admissible remainder for
L_{\Phi}^{\alpha}
with admissible screen
S
, we have that
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
and
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)
are jointly languid (with exponent
\kappa=0
) with respect to this screen
S
. We have that
\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}
is an entire function by Corollary
3.3.3
and explicitly we show that
\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}
is bounded on any vertical strip of the form
\mathbb{H}_{a}^{b}=\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,a<\Re(s)<b\right\}
.

In order to do so, it suffices to find positive constants
C_{\xi},A_{\xi}
such that

|\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)|\leq C_{\xi}A_{\xi}^{|\Re(s)|},

as this function is bounded when
|\Re(s)|
is bounded as is the case with vertical strips.

To that end, let
\sigma=\Re(s)
and define
\Lambda_{+}=\max(\left\{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}\right\}_{\varphi\in\Phi})
and
\Lambda_{-}=\min(\left\{\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}\right\}_{\varphi\in\Phi})
. Then we have that

\displaystyle|\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha\sigma}\left|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}[f](s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max(\Lambda_{+}^{\sigma},\Lambda_{-}^{\sigma})\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\left|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}[f](s)\right|.

By Corollary
B.3
, we have that each Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}^{\delta/\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha}}[f]
is bounded on any vertical strip of the form
\mathbb{H}_{a}^{b}
. It follows that a finite sum of such functions is bounded on any such vertical strip by the sum of these constants, which we denote by
C_{\xi}
. Choosing
A_{\xi}=\max(\Lambda_{-},\Lambda_{+},\Lambda_{-}^{-1},\Lambda_{+}^{-1})
allows us to write that
A_{\xi}^{|\sigma|}\geq\max(\Lambda_{+}^{\sigma},\Lambda_{-}^{\sigma})
, and thus obtaining the desired bound. It follows that
|\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}|
is bounded in any vertical strip.

Now let
\left\{\tau_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of admissible heights shared jointly by
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
and
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)
, which exists by fiat. We have that each of these respective functions is uniformly bounded on all of the horizontal contours
I_{n}=[S(\tau)+i\tau_{n},c+i\tau_{n}]
, for some particular
c
sufficiently large so that
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
and
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)
are holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{c}
. Since
\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}
is bounded on the strip
\mathbb{H}_{\inf(S)}^{c}
, it follows that it is uniformly bounded on all of these intervals
I_{n}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\inf(S)}^{c}
. It follows that
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)=\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)(\xi_{\Phi,f}(s;\delta)+\zeta_{R}(s;\delta))
is uniformly bounded on these intervals. This establishes hypothesis
L1
for the screen
S
.

For hypothesis
L2
, we note that
\zeta_{R}(s;\delta)
and
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
are bounded on
S
by assumption of their joint languidity. Because
S
is contained in the strip
\mathbb{H}_{\inf(S)}^{\sup(S)}
(noting that screens are by definition bounded), we have that
\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}
is bounded on
S
. The boundedness of
\zeta_{f}
on this screen thus follows. Thus,
\zeta_{f}
is languid with respect to the same screen
S
(with exponent
\kappa=0
).

∎

3.3.5.
Proof of the Explicit Formulae

With the languid growth conditions of
\zeta_{f}
having been established, we may now complete the proofs of the main theorems, Theorems
3.3.1
and
3.3.2
. We shall treat the proofs of these theorems together as they require similar setup and estimates. The main differences come in the application of different theorems to provide the relevant result. Starting from the result of Proposition
3.3.3
, we will compute the resulting contour integral by the methods in
[
LRŽ17
, Theorem 5.1.11]
and
[
LRŽ17
, Theorem 5.2.2]
for the pointwise and distributional explicit formula, respectively.

Proof.

Fix
c
such that
\beta/\alpha+1>c>\max(D/\alpha,\sigma_{R})
. By Proposition
3.3.3
, we have that for any
t\in(0,\delta)
,

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}t^{-s}\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)(\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)+\zeta_{R}(s;\delta))\,ds,

where
\zeta_{f}
,
\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}
, and
\zeta_{R}
are as in Corollary
3.3.3
. By Theorem
3.3.4
, we have that
\zeta_{f}
is languid with exponent
\kappa=0
on the admissible screen
S
.

Noting that
F(t)=t^{\beta/\alpha}f(t)
, we have that

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}t^{\beta/\alpha-s}\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)(\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}(s;\delta)+\zeta_{R}(s;\delta))\,ds.

It is this expression that is analogous to
[
LRŽ17
, Equation (5.1.18)]
, but with
\beta
in place of
N
,
\alpha=1
, and
\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)
in place of the relative tube zeta function
\widetilde{\zeta}_{A,\Omega}(s;\delta)
. Under these conditions,
[
LRŽ17
, Theorem 5.1.11]
and
[
LRŽ17
, Theorem 5.2.2]
are essentially applicable with only minor modifications and justifications required in the general case.

Firstly,
[
LRŽ17
, Proposition 5.1.8]
is applicable, where the interchange of the order of integration may be justified by the explicit formula for
\zeta_{f}
(cf. Corollary
3.3.3
) and explicit bounds for the constituent functions. The uniform estimates for
\zeta_{R}
and
\xi_{L_{\Phi}^{\alpha},f}
follow from Corollary
B.3
.

For an estimate of
\zeta_{\Phi}
, note that when
\sigma=\Re(s)>D/\alpha
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha s}\right|\leq p(\alpha\sigma):=\sum_{\varphi\in\Phi}\lambda_{\varphi}^{\alpha\sigma}<p(D)=1,

using the fact that
p
is strictly decreasing. It follows that there is a uniform bound (based on
c>D/\alpha
) for
\zeta_{\Phi}(\alpha s)
when
\Re(s)=c
. Put together, we obtain that the function
t^{\beta/\alpha-s}\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)
, viewed as a multivariate function with variables
t
and
s
, is integrable for
(t,s)\in[0,\delta]\times(c-i\infty,c+i\infty)
provided that
\beta/\alpha+1>c>D/\alpha
. The assumption that
\beta/\alpha+1>c
ensures that
t^{\beta/\alpha-s}
is integrable as
t\to 0^{+}
. Next,
[
LRŽ17
, Lemma 5.1.10]
(with
\kappa=0
) is directly applicable based on the languidity of the function
\zeta_{f}
(see Theorem
3.3.4
) followed by
[
LRŽ17
, Theorem 5.1.11]
. This establishes the proof of Theorem
3.3.1
.

Next, this integrability argument is also necessary to prove Theorem
3.3.2
, namely to justify the use of the Fubini–Tonelli theorem in
[
LRŽ17
, Equation (5.2.10)]
(in the proof of
[
LRŽ17
, Theorem 5.5.5]
). In this case, because
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(0,\delta)
, we may write the corresponding integral in question as

\displaystyle\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(t)\frac{t^{\beta/\alpha-s+k}}{(\beta/\alpha-s+1)_{k}}\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\int_{0}^{\delta}\psi(t)\frac{t^{\beta/\alpha-s+k}}{(\beta/\alpha-s+1)_{k}}\zeta_{f}(s;\delta)\,dt.

We claim that once again, the integrand is integrable over the product space, that is, for
(t,s)\in[0,\delta]\times(c-i\infty,c+i\infty)
. Firstly, we have the same estimates for
\zeta_{f}
as before. For the Pochhammer symbol, we may write that

\displaystyle|(\beta/\alpha-s+1)_{k}|

\displaystyle\geq|\beta/\alpha-c+1|\cdot|\beta/\alpha-c+2|\cdots|\beta/\alpha-c+k-1|

\displaystyle\geq|\beta/\alpha-c+1|^{k},

noting that on the vertical lines
z=\beta/\alpha-c+k+i\mathbb{R}
, the closest point to the origin occurs exactly on the real axis. The second estimate follows from using the fact that
\beta/\alpha-c+1>0
is the smallest term in the product. This gives a uniform upper bound for
|(\beta/\alpha-s+1)_{k}^{-1}|
. Lastly, the power of
t
may be arbitrary since
\psi
is a function of rapid decrease: for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
t^{\gamma}\psi(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

The final step is a minor change of variables. Rather than indexing the sum by the poles of
\zeta_{f}
in
W_{S}
, we note that as long as
\Re(s)>\sigma_{R}
, the abscissa of absolute convergence of
\zeta_{R}
, a pole of
\zeta_{f}
only occurs at
s
if
\omega=\alpha s
is a pole of
\zeta_{\Phi}
. In other words, we have that

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{D}_{f}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}})}g(s)=\sum_{\omega/\alpha\in\mathcal{D}_{f}(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{R}})}g(\omega/\alpha)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{H}_{\alpha\sigma_{R}})}g(\omega/\alpha).

Since we assume that

lies in the half-plane

, this same argument applies with respect to the window

and its scaled transformation

. This establishes the proof of Theorem

.

∎

4.2.
General Heat Content Results for Self-Similar Sets

We now state some general results regarding pointwise and distributional explicit formulae for the heat contents of open bounded regions

whose boundary

is a self-similar set and for which
(\partial\Omega,\Omega)
is an osculant fractal drum. The pointwise expansions require integrating the heat content sufficiently many times to improve regularity of the function to be obtained. Meanwhile, the distributional identities (while less direct) remove this technical hurdle.

First, we state the pointwise identity, given in terms of antiderivatives of the heat content. We denote by
E_{\Omega}^{[k]}
the
k^{\text{th}}
antiderivative of the heat content defined so that
E_{\Omega}^{[k]}(0)=0

and for

by

E_{\Omega}^{[k]}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}E_{\Omega}^{[k-1]}(\tau)\,d\tau.

As an additional preliminary to stating the result, we define the Pochhammer symbol
(z)_{w}:=\Gamma(z+w)/\Gamma(w)
for
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
. Note that when
w=k
is a positive integer, this simplifies to
(z)_{k}=z(z+1)\cdots(z+k-1)
or, when
w=0
, to
(z)_{0}=1
. Lastly, we note that the summations to follow are defined as symmetric limits of the sums taken over values of
\omega
with bounded imaginary parts for increasingly large upper bounds.

Theorem 4.4: Heat Content, Pointwise Expansion

Let
E_{\Omega}
be the heat content of the PWB solution to Dirichlet Problem
2.2

\Phi

By Proposition

.

To define an

(4.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{n,r}&:=\left\{\varphi_{L},\varphi_{R},\psi_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},k=1,...,n-1\right\},\\
\varphi_{L}&:=S_{\ell},\\
\varphi_{R}&:=T_{(\ell+r,0)}\circ S_{\ell},\\

Appendix A
Perron-Wiener-Brelot Solutions

A.1.
History of the PWB Method for the Heat Equation

Perron introduced his famous method for solving the Dirichlet problem for Laplace’s equation
[
Per23
]
. The basic premise is to obtain the solution, a harmonic function, as a supremum over a family of subharmonic functions. This method was studied by Wiener, who in particular defined a generalized solution as one obtained in this way with limits converging to the prescribed boundary values at regular boundary points and proved a criterion for the convergence of such solutions to the prescribed boundary value conditions
[
Wie24b
,
Wie24
]
.

Wiener’s criterion established existence of such generalized solutions, and the uniqueness of such solutions (namely, that such a solution is identically zero when all its limits approaching regular boundary points are zero) was established by Kellogg
[
Kel28
]
and Evans
[
Eva33
]
. Brelot’s contributions include simplifying this proof
[
Bre55
]
as well as developing a general theory and extending the method (cf.
[
Bre67
]
). His general study in potential theory was already inspired by others, notably including the work of Doob
[
Doo66
]
. The potential theory was further developed and axiomatized by Bauer
[
Bau66
]
and Constantinescu and Cornea
[
CC72
]
.

The application of the PWB method to the heat equation relates to the probabilistic approach, owing to the connection of this equation to Brownian motion. This connection was discovered as early as the work of Bachelier in mathematical finance
[
Bac00
]
, and it was independently analyzed by Einstein
[
Ein56
]
and Smoluchowski
[
Smo06
]
. Einstein’s work especially was a large inspiration for Wiener’s mathematical development of the theory of Brownian motion
[
Wie23
,
Wie24a
,
Wie76
]
. It is for this reason that Brownian motion may be instead called a Wiener process.

The analysis of the heat equation on an arbitrary bounded open set in Euclidean space, using the probabilistic approach, is due to Doob
[
Doo55
]
. He obtained the solution in the sense of Perron and Wiener, using the martingale analogues of subharmonic functions, though he did not provide details for the classification of regular and irregular boundary points. Evans and Gariepy proved an analogue of Wiener’s criterion for the classification of regular and irregular boundary points for solutions of the heat equation
[
EG82
]
. Watson would later show that Doob’s approach is equivalent to the later developments in the theory, including filling in details not originally provided by Doob
[
Wat15
]
. For a modern reference, we recommend Watson’s book on the subject
[
Wat12
]
.

Lastly, we note that the Dirichlet problem for the heat equation requires some extra assumptions in order to be well posed. (A Dirichlet problem is well posed if there exists a unique solution and, further, this solution depends continuously on the initial conditions.) As was discovered by Tychonoff, functions with growth larger than quadratic exponentials with certain initial conditions can yield nonunique solutions, but under the restriction to this growth condition, solutions are unique
[
Tyc35
]
. Widder further analyzed the problem and showed that nonnegative solutions are unique
[
Wid44
]
. For more information about the (non)uniqueness, we recommend Widder’s paper.

In the simplest case, if we consider

However, for the study of the heat problem on an arbitrary open set, we will need to consider functions which are not necessarily continuously differentiable. Thus, we will need an analogue of the heat operator that does not require differentiability, as we cannot define

(A.2)

\mathscr{M}_{p_{0},r}[u]=\mathscr{M}[u;\partial E_{p_{0},r}]:=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{N/2}}\int_{\partial E_{p_{0},r}}Q(p-p_{0})u(p)dS(p).

The last preliminary is to define the properties that our functions must satisfy in lieu of continuous differentiability. We first define the notions of upper and lower
semicontinuity
: a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\to[-\infty,\infty]
is
upper semicontinuous
on a set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
if for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
\left\{p\in Y\,\middle|\,f(p)<a\right\}
is a relatively open subset of
S
(i.e. open in the subspace topology it inherits from
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
). We say that an extended real-valued function
f
is
lower semicontinuous
if
-f
is upper semicontinuous.

Next, we say that an extended real-valued function
f:\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\to[-\infty,\infty]
is
upper finite
on a set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
if
f[Y]\subseteq[-\infty,\infty)
and
lower finite
on
Y
if
f[Y]\subset(-\infty,\infty]
. Note that when
f
is both upper and lower finite, it is simply a real-valued function.

We may now define hypertemperatures and hypotemperatures.

Definition A.2: Hypertemperatures

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
. We say that an extended real-valued function
f
is a
hypertemperature
on
Y
if
f
is
lower finite
,
lower semicontinuous
, and with the following property. For any
p\in Y
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
r<\varepsilon
such that

f(p)\geq\mathscr{M}[f;\partial E_{p,r}].

Let
\text{Tem}_{+}(Y)
denote the set of all hypertemperatures on
Y
.

The efficient way to define when a function
f
is a hypotemperature is to require that
-f
be a hypertemperature. However, for the sake of completeness and establishing notation, we will give an independent definition and let this fact be an immediate corollary.

Definition A.3: Hypotemperatures

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
. We say that an extended real-valued function
f
is a
hypotemperature
on
Y
if
f
is
upper finite
,
upper semicontinuous
, and with the following property. For any
p\in Y
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
r<\varepsilon
such that

f(p)\leq\mathscr{M}[f;\partial E_{p,r}].

Let
\text{Tem}_{-}(Y)
denote the space of all hypotemperatures on
Y
.

Ultimately, when we look for solutions of the heat equation, we will consider the collection of all hypertemperatures and hypotemperatures that satisfy the prescribed boundary conditions. By taking an infimum of hypertemperatures or a supremum of hypotemperatures, then ideally we arrive at the same function which now satisfies the boundary conditions and will be a
temperature
, satisfying the heat equation proper.

A.3.
Classification of Boundary Points

In what follows, let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
be an arbitrary open set. We consider the boundary of
Y
relative to the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, so that
\infty\in\partial Y
exactly when
Y
is unbounded. With this proviso,
\partial Y
is otherwise the standard Euclidean boundary of
Y
. For an arbitrary open set
Y
, it turns out that we cannot prescribe boundary conditions on all of
\partial Y
. This is to be
reasonably expected
, however.

Consider the simple problem of a one-dimensional rod given by the interval
[a,b]
and suppose that we wish to consider the problem for time
t\in[0,1]
. Suppose now to look for solutions of the heat equation
\partial_{x}^{2}u=\partial_{t}u
in the set
(a,b)\times(0,1)
. Prescribing conditions on the boundary of
[a,b]\times[0,1]
amounts to three types of constraints. On the set
(a,b)\times\left\{0\right\}
, we prescribe the initial amount of heat on the inside of the rod at time
t=0
. On each of the sets
\left\{a\right\}\times[0,1]
and
\left\{b\right\}\times[0,1]
, we are prescribing the temperature values of the endpoints of the rod. Lastly, on the boundary piece
(a,b)\times\left\{1\right\}
, we are prescribing the future temperature of the inside of rod at time one. However, in this deterministic model, the conditions at time
t=0
and on the boundary for all time
t\in[0,1]
will determine the future temperature; it cannot be arbitrarily chosen.

This leads us to a general classification of the types of boundary points of
\partial Y
. In order to do so, let us establish some notation regarding half-balls with respect to the time coordinate. Letting
p_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, we write

H_{*}(p_{0},r)=\left\{(x,t)\,\middle|\,|x-x_{0}|^{2}+|t-t_{0}|^{2}<r^{2},\,t<t_{0}\right\}

for the open
lower half-ball
. Similarly, we write
H^{*}(p_{0},r)
for the open
upper half-ball
, defined analogously but with
t>t_{0}
. Note that these are standard balls cut in half, not heat balls.

The boundary
\partial Y
is classified into three types of points:
normal
,
semi-singular
(or
semi-normal
), and (
fully
)
singular points
. If
q\in\partial Y
, then
q
is said to be
normal
if either
q=\infty
is the point at infinity or if every lower half-ball
H_{*}(q,r)
centered at
q
intersects the complement of
Y
, viz.
H_{*}(q,r)\setminus Y\neq\emptyset
for all
r>0
, namely as
r\to 0^{+}
. For abnormal boundary points
q
, eventually there exists an
r_{0}>0
so that
H_{*}(q,r)\subseteq Y
. If there exists
r_{1}<r_{0}
such that
H^{*}(q,r_{1})\cap Y=\emptyset
, then
q
is a (fully) singular boundary point. (Note that this implies that any smaller upper half-ball
H^{*}(p,r_{2})
,
r_{2}<r_{1}
, will also be contained in the complement of
Y
.) Otherwise, for every
r<r_{0}
we have that
H^{*}(q,r_{1})\cap Y\neq\emptyset
and
q
is said to be
semi-singular
.

Returning to the example from earlier, we can see now how these criteria describe the different parts of the boundary. The initial condition set,
(a,b)\times\left\{0\right\}
, clearly contains only normal points since the lower half-balls
H_{*}(p,r)
are contained in the complement for any
r
, let alone merely intersecting the complement. On the edges of the rod, we also have that arbitrarily small lower half-balls always intersect the complement to the left or right, respectively. Thus this part of the boundary is also normal.

The top part of the boundary, however, is fully singular: the lower half-balls are strictly contained in the set
Y
and the upper half-balls are strictly contained in the complement. For these fully singular points, they can only be approached from within the set
Y
and only from past times, not future times. The conditions above amount to requiring that fully singular boundary points look locally like such points.

This example does not illustrate a semi-singular point, but for a simple example puncture the square
(a,b)\times(0,1)
at an interior point, say
(x_{0},t_{0})
. This new point in the boundary is then semi-singular. Imposing a boundary condition here amounts to instantaneously changing the temperature on the rod at
x_{0}
at the time
t_{0}
. In this case, one could expect a solution for times
t>t_{0}
which are compatible with the current values of the temperature at
t_{0}
for
x\neq x_{0}
and the new prescribed temperature at
x_{0}
. However, the solution in the larger set will not approach this arbitrarily changed value from times
t<t_{0}
.

So, for the Perron-Wiener-Brelot solutions to follow, we will impose boundary conditions only on the set of normal and semi-singular boundary points. For this reason, we say that the
essential boundary

\partial_{e}Y
of a set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is the union of the set of all
normal
boundary points, the
normal boundary

\partial_{n}Y
, and the set of all
semi-singular
boundary points, the
semi-singular boundary

\partial_{ss}Y
. If we denote the set of
fully singular
boundary points by
\partial_{s}Y
, called the
singular boundary
, then we have that
\partial_{e}Y=\partial Y\setminus\partial_{s}Y
. Note also that the normal boundary and the semi-singular boundary are disjoint by the definitions of normal and semi-singular boundary points, and thus
\partial_{e}Y=\partial_{n}Y\uplus\partial_{ss}Y
. Recall that
\partial Y
is considered with respect to the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and that
\infty\in\partial_{n}Y
is always a normal boundary point when
Y
is unbounded.

We now may define the sense in which boundary conditions will be satisfied. We will require convergence of the function to the boundary function at normal points to be for any sequence in
Y
converging to the boundary points. Meanwhile, for convergence to the value of the function at semi-singular boundary points, we will only impose convergence when the sequence approaches from future times, not past times.

Definition A.4: Regularity and Boundary Convergence

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
be an open set and let
\partial_{e}Y=\partial_{n}Y\uplus\partial_{ss}Y
be its essential boundary. Let
f
be a function on
\partial Y
and
u
a function on
Y
itself.

We say that
u

converges to

f
at a point
q\in\partial_{e}Y
if we have the following.

•

If
q\in\partial_{n}Y
, then we have that for any sequence
\left\{p_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset Y
with
p_{n}\to q
,
u(p_{n})\to f(q)
.

•

If
q=(y,s)\in\partial_{ss}Y
, then we have that for any sequence
\left\{p_{n}=(x_{n},t_{n})\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset Y
with
x_{n}\to y
and
t_{n}\to s^{+}
,
u(p_{n})\to f(q)
.

When
q\in\partial_{e}Y
is such that these limits exist,
q
is called a
regular boundary point
. We say that
u

converges to

f

on the regular (essential) boundary
if
u
converges to
f
at every regular point
q
in the essential boundary.

The last caveat is that given a set
Y
, it can have both
regular
and
irregular
boundary points in its essential boundary. (A boundary point is called
irregular
if it is not regular in the sense above.) So, the final task is to determine which points in the essential boundary are regular. In the case that the entire essential boundary is not regular, i.e. there exist irregular boundary points, then one must seek to understand which points are regular and which are irregular. We will not explore this issue further here, but refer the reader to the work of Evans and Gariepy
[
EG82
]
or to
[
Wat12
, Section 8.6]
for more information, including sufficient conditions to ensure that an entire boundary or a specific point in the boundary is regular.

A.4.
Perron-Wiener-Brelot Solutions

We now have the means to define a Perron-Wiener-Brelot solution to the heat equation on an arbitrary open set in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, identified with
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}
. Consider the following Dirichlet problem for the heat equation on an arbitrary open set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, given by

(A.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u-C\Delta u&=0&&\text{in }Y,\\
u&=f&&\text{on }\partial Y.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
C>0
is a positive constant. Note that
Y
is not necessarily a Cartesian product of an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the spatial domain, and an open set in
\mathbb{R}
, the time domain.

We consider now two collections of functions which we define based on our boundary function
f
. These are either hypertemperatures bounded from below by
f
or hypotemperatures bounded from above by
f
.

\displaystyle\text{Tem}_{+}

\displaystyle(Y;f):=

\displaystyle\left\{w\in\text{Tem}_{+}(Y)\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} \liminf_{(x,t)\to(y,s)}w(x,t)&\geq f(y,s)&(x,t)\in Y,\,(y,s)\in\partial_{n}Y\\
\liminf_{(x,t)\to(y,s^{+})}w(x,t)&\geq f(y,s)&(x,t)\in Y,\,(y,s)\in\partial_{ss}Y\end{aligned}\right\},

\displaystyle\text{Tem}_{-}

\displaystyle(Y;f):=

\displaystyle\left\{w\in\text{Tem}_{+}(Y)\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} \limsup_{(x,t)\to(y,s)}w(x,t)&\leq f(y,s)&(x,t)\in Y,\,(y,s)\in\partial_{n}Y\\
\limsup_{(x,t)\to(y,s^{+})}w(x,t)&\leq f(y,s)&(x,t)\in Y,\,(y,s)\in\partial_{ss}Y\end{aligned}\right\}.

These spaces of functions are each nonempty since
w\equiv\infty
is in the first and
w\equiv-\infty
is in the second.

We next define the upper and lower candidates for the solution to the heat equation.

(A.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}w_{Y,f}^{+}(p)&:=\inf\left\{w(p)\,\middle|\,w\in\text{Tem}_{+}(Y;f)\right\},\\
w_{Y,f}^{-}(p)&:=\sup\left\{w(p)\,\middle|\,w\in\text{Tem}_{-}(Y;f)\right\}.\end{split}

These define two functions
w_{Y,f}^{+}
and
w_{Y,f}^{-}
on
Y
. If they agree (and are sufficiently regular), then the function
f
is said to be
resolutive
.

Definition A.5: Resolutive Function

A function
f
on
\partial_{e}Y
, the essential boundary of
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, is said to be
resolutive
if the following hold. Firstly, the functions defined by (
A.4
) are the same, i.e.
w_{Y,f}^{+}\equiv w_{Y,f}^{-}
, and in this case, we define
w_{Y,f}:=w_{Y,f}^{+}=w_{Y,f}^{-}
. Secondly, we require that
w_{Y,f}\in C^{2,1}(Y)
, i.e.
w_{Y,f}
is twice continuously differentiable in its spatial coordinates and once continuously differentiable in its time coordinate, with
C^{2,1}(Y)
as defined in Section
A.2
.

When the boundary function
f
is resolutive, we obtain a
temperature
function
w_{Y,f}
, as it follows that since
w_{Y,f}\in C^{2,1}(Y)
,
\mathcal{H}w_{Y,f}=0
. From the definitions of the function spaces
\text{Tem}_{+}(Y,f)
and
\text{Tem}_{-}(Y,f)
, note that whenever the limit of
w_{Y,f}
as
p
approaches a point
q
in the essential boundary
\partial_{e}Y
exists, the value of the limit must necessarily equal
f(q)
since
w_{Y,f}\in\text{Tem}_{+}(Y,f)\cap\text{Tem}_{-}(Y,f)
. Therefore,
w_{Y,f}
converges to
f
on the regular essential boundary of
Y
. This is exactly what it means for
w_{Y,f}
to be a solution to a Dirichlet problem for the heat equation in the sense of Perron-Wiener-Brelot.

Proposition A.6: Perron-Wiener-Brelot (PWB) Solution

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and suppose that
f
is a
resolutive
function in the sense of Definition
A.4
. Then we say that
w_{Y,f}
is the
Perron-Wiener-Brelot solution
to Problem
A.3
and we have that
w_{Y,f}
converges to
f
on the regular essential boundary of
Y
in the sense of Definition
A.3
.

A.5.
Parabolic Measure and Resolutive Functions

The final ingredient in our study of PWB solutions is the last but most important remaining question. Given an open set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, when is a function
f
on
\partial_{e}Y
resolutive? Per Proposition
A.4
, this condition is exactly what is required for a PWB solution to exist with the Dirichlet boundary conditions specified by
f
.

Luckily, such functions are plentiful. In fact, any continuous function
f\in C^{0}(\partial_{e}Y)
on the essential boundary is resolutive; see for instance
[
Wat12
, Theorem 8.26]
. We can give a characterization of the class of resolutive functions in terms of an integrability condition relating to a family of measures called
parabolic measures
.

Given the open set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and a point
p\in Y
, it can be shown (see, for example,
[
Wat12
, Theorem 8.27]
) that there exists a unique nonnegative Borel probability measure
\omega_{Y,p}
on
\partial_{e}Y
such that for any continuous function
f\in C^{0}(\partial_{e}Y)
,

(A.5)

w_{Y,f}(p)=\int_{\partial_{e}Y}f\,d\omega_{Y,p}.

An integral of this form may be called a
Poisson integral
owing to their connection to the classical theory of Poisson kernels and integral representations (in the setting of harmonic functions which can be extended to the heat equation).

Definition A.7: Parabolic Measure and Parabolic Integrability

The completion of the measure defined by (
A.5
), which we shall also denote by
\omega_{Y,p}
, is called the
parabolic measure
relative to
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and
p\in Y
.

A function
f
on
\partial_{e}Y
is said to be
parabolically integrable
if it is
\omega_{Y,p}
-integrable for any
p\in Y
.

The set of resolutive functions
f
for a given set
Y
is exactly the class of
parabolically integrable
functions on
\partial_{e}(Y)
(see for instance Corollary 8.34 of
[
Wat12
]
). The integral representation given by (
A.5
) means that the PWB solution
w_{Y,f}
to Problem
A.3
at a point
p
is exactly the parabolic average of the boundary value
f
with respect to the associated parabolic measure
\omega_{Y,p}
.

Appendix B
Mellin Transforms

We first define Mellin transforms and their truncated counterparts, which are our main tool in solving scaling functional equations. The Mellin transform is an integral transform associated with the Haar measure of the positive real line with respect to multiplication in the same sense that the Fourier transform is associated with the Haar measure of the real line with respect to addition (which is simply the Lebesgue measure). The underlying space
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\cdot)
, the positive real line
\mathbb{R}^{+}=(0,\infty)
viewed as a group with respect to multiplication, can be thought of as the space of multiplicative scales. As fractals generally have scale invariance or approximate scale invariance, the Mellin transform associated with this space is well suited to study such shapes.

B.1.
Mellin Transform

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lebesgue integrable function. The standard
Mellin transform
of
f
is the integral

(B.1)

\mathcal{M}[f](s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s-1}f(x)\,dx,

defined for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
for which the Lebesgue integral converges. Strictly speaking, we identify the Mellin transform with its analytic continuation (which we shall restrict to be defined on a connected open subset of the complex plane). The factor
x^{s-1}
has an extra factor of
x^{-1}
since the Haar measure associated to the group
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\cdot)
is
dx/x
, where
dx
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Under a scaling transformation
S_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda x
, note that
d(\lambda x)/\lambda x=dx/x
is invariant.

Integrability of the function
f
and the existence of some polynomial growth conditions are sufficient for the transform to exist on a vertical strip in
\mathbb{C}
. However, we will often simply impose that
f
be continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
since the heat contents we consider in Section
4
are continuous.

B.2.
Truncated Mellin Transforms

A truncated, or equivalently a restricted, Mellin transform is simply an integral of the same integrand as (
B.1
), but over an interval (i.e. connected subset) of
(0,\infty)
.

Definition B.1: Truncated Mellin Transform

Let
f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
and fix
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, with
\alpha<\beta
. The truncated Mellin transform of
f
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
, is given by

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f](s):=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}t^{s-1}f(t)\,dt

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
for which the Lebesgue integral is convergent.

Typically, we will identify
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f](s)
with its analytic continuation to an open connected domain of
\mathbb{C}
. (In this work, we shall restrict ourselves to the case when these analytic continuations live on a connected open subset of the complex plane, rather than a more general
Riemann surface
.) If
\alpha=0
, we write
\mathcal{M}^{\beta}
for the truncated transform. If
\alpha=0
and we take
\beta=\infty
, then
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\infty}
is simply the standard Mellin transform. Note that we may equivalently define
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
to be the Mellin transform of
f
times the characteristic function of the interval
(\alpha,\beta)
, viz.

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]=\mathcal{M}[f\cdot\mathds{1}_{[\alpha,\beta]}].

This allows us to compare the convergence of the two directly, and it shows that the truncated transform inherits the properties of its standard counterpart, for example its linearity.

B.3.
Convergence of Mellin Transforms

The convergence properties of the truncated transform follow from standard results on the Mellin transform. In what follows, let

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}:=

\displaystyle\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,\sigma<\Re(s)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{a}^{b}:=

\displaystyle\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,a<\Re(s)<b\right\}

denote an
open right half-plane
and an
open vertical strip
in
\mathbb{C}
, respectively. If
\sigma=-\infty
, then
\mathbb{H}_{-\infty}=\mathbb{C}
and if
\sigma=+\infty
, then
\mathbb{H}_{+\infty}=\emptyset
. It is known (see, for example,
[
Gra10
, Chapter 6]
) that
\mathcal{M}[f](s)
is holomorphic in the vertical strip
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{-}}^{\sigma_{+}}
, where

(B.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma_{-}:=&\inf\left\{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)=O(x^{-\sigma})\text{ as }x\to 0^{+}\right\},\\
\sigma_{+}:=&\sup\left\{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)=O(x^{-\sigma})\text{ as }x\to+\infty\right\}.\end{split}

When
\alpha<\beta<\infty
,
f\cdot\mathds{1}_{[\alpha,\beta]}\equiv 0
as
x\to+\infty
, whence
\sigma_{+}=+\infty
. In this case, we say that
\sigma_{-}
is the
abscissa of absolute convergence
of
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
, denoted by
\sigma_{ac}
, and it follows that
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
is holomorphic in the open right half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{ac}}
. Similarly, if
0<\alpha<\beta
then
f\cdot\mathds{1}_{[\alpha,\beta]}\equiv 0
as
x\to 0^{+}
, whence
\sigma_{-}=-\infty
. So, if
0<\alpha<\beta<\infty
, then
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
is automatically entire, i.e. holomorphic in all of
\mathbb{C}
, in which case
\sigma_{ac}=-\infty
(formally). We collect these observations into the following lemma, which will be of use later.

Lemma B.2: Holomorphicity of Truncated Mellin Transforms

Let
f
be integrable on
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
and let
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
be its (truncated) Mellin transform.

•

Let
\beta<\infty
. Suppose that
f
is bounded away from
0
(i.e. on any interval of the form
[\varepsilon,\beta]
for
\varepsilon>0
) and that
f(x)=O(x^{-\sigma_{0}})
as
x\to 0^{+}
, for some
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f]
is absolutely convergent and holomorphic in the open right half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
.

•

Let
0<\alpha<\beta<\infty
. If
f
is bounded on
[\alpha,\beta]
, then
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
is entire.

Proof.

First, consider the case where
\beta<\infty
and
\alpha=0
. Since
f(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}})
as
t\to 0^{+}
and is bounded on any interval of the form
[\varepsilon,\delta]
, there exists
C>0
so that
|f(t)|\leq C\,t^{-\sigma_{0}}
for all
t\in(0,\delta]
. Thus, we may estimate the Mellin transform to find that

(B.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\beta}[f](s)|&\leq\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{\Re(s)-1}|f(t)|\,dt\leq C\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{\Re(s)-1-\sigma_{0}}\,dt=\frac{C}{\Re(s)-\sigma_{0}}\,\delta^{\Re(s)-\sigma_{0}},\end{split}

provided
\Re(s)>\sigma_{0}
. It follows that
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\beta}[f](s)
is convergent in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
. Further, this bound also implies that it is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
. This may be seen, for instance, by the application of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem to compute its derivative or alternatively by application of Morera’s theorem and the Fubini-Tonelli theorem.

Now let
0<\alpha<\beta<\infty
. Then the function
f(t)\mathds{1}_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
is integrable and in fact
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f](s)=\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f(t)\mathds{1}_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)](s)
for any
s
for which it is convergent, since
f(t)=f(t)\mathds{1}_{(\alpha,\beta)(t)}(t)
for all
t\in(\alpha,\beta)
. Note that
f(t)\mathds{1}_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)
is bounded on
[0,\beta]
and that for any
n>0
, we have that
f(t)=O(t^{n})
as
t\to 0^{+}
since
\mathds{1}_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)\equiv 0
whenever
t<\alpha
. By the first part of the proof, we have that
\mathcal{M}^{\delta}[f(t)\mathds{1}_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)]
is a convergent integral and is holomorphic in any half-plane of the form
\mathbb{H}_{-n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
converges for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
and is an entire function.
∎

Suppose now that
\zeta_{f}(s):=\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f](s)
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{ac}}
and that
\zeta_{f}
admits an analytic continuation to a half-plane of the form
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
, with
\sigma\leq\sigma_{ac}
.
In this case,
\zeta_{f}
is a
tamed Dirichlet-type integral
(DTI) in the sense of
[
LRŽ17
, Definition A.1.2]
. More generally,
\zeta_{f}
may admit holomorphic continuation to an open connected set
U
in
\mathbb{C}
containing
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{ac}}
. Typically, we will assume that
U
is a connected open neighborhood of the set
W\setminus\mathcal{D}_{f}(W)
, where
\mathcal{D}_{f}(W)
is a discrete subset of
W
which contains the possible singularities of
\zeta_{f}
. If
\zeta_{f}
is meromorphic in
U
, then its poles in
W
are contained in this set
\mathcal{D}_{f}(W)
. In this case, we say that
\zeta_{f}
admits a
meromorphic continuation
to a connected open neighborhood of
W
.

Next, we note that the estimates in (
B.3
) are
independent of the imaginary part
of the parameter. Thus, if the real part is bounded (as is the case in a horizontally bounded vertical strip), we may obtain uniform estimates for the Mellin transform. If
\alpha=0
, note that we must choose a vertical strip which lies in the half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{0}}
, where
f(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}})
, but the restriction is removed when
\alpha>0
since we may choose
\sigma_{0}
to be arbitrarily small.

Corollary B.3: Uniform Boundedness on Vertical Strips

Let
f
be integrable on
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
and let
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
be its (truncated) Mellin transform, with
0\leq\alpha<\beta<\infty
. Suppose that either of the two hypotheses of Lemma
B.3
hold. In the first case, let
a>\sigma_{0}
and in the second let
a\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary.

Then
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
is bounded in any vertical strip of the form

\mathbb{H}_{a}^{b}=\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,a<\Re(s)<b\right\}

where either
a>\sigma_{0}
in the first case (when
\alpha=0
and
f(t)=O(t^{-\sigma_{0}})
as
t\to 0^{+}
) or where
a
is arbitrary when
\alpha>0
. We may also choose any vertical line of the form
\Re(s)\equiv a
or the closed strip with
a\leq\Re(s)\leq b
.

Proof.

It is easiest to prove for closed vertical strips and deduce the other results for open strips and vertical lines as corollaries. Suppose that
\sigma_{0}<a\leq\Re(s)\leq b
. Then
|\Re(s)-\sigma_{0}|\leq a-\sigma_{0}>0
is bounded from below, whence its reciprocal is bounded. The function
\delta^{t}
is continuous on
[a,b]
, and thus bounded. It follows from (
B.3
) that
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f]
is bounded on this closed vertical strip. If
\alpha>0
, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
choose
\sigma_{0}<a
and apply the previous argument.

For vertical lines, take
\sigma_{0}<a=b
. For an open strip, note that it is contained in its closure where the function is bounded by the result for closed strips, and by assumption
\sigma_{0}<a
.
∎

Notably, the restricted transform may converge even if the full Mellin transform integral diverges. Indeed, this improvement to the convergence is the main reason to use this modification of the Mellin transform, as later we will see that truncation complicated the scaling property of the transform (viz. Lemma
B.4
) slightly. Perhaps the most important class of functions for which this occurs is that of polynomials. Without loss of generality, let
f(t)=t^{k}
be a monomial. If
\Re(s)>-k
, then
\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f](s)
converges; however,
\mathcal{M}[f](s)
is divergent for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus, for a general polynomial
p(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}t^{k}
, we have that

\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[p](s)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{k}}{s+k}\beta^{s+k},

which is valid when
\Re(s)>\max\left\{-k:a_{k}\neq 0\right\}
. Negative powers become admissible if the lower bound
\alpha
of the truncation is positive.

B.4.
Scaling Properties of Mellin Transforms

Owing to the role of the scale invariant Haar measure
dx/x
on
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\cdot)
defining the Mellin transform, it possesses a very simple formula for scaling. Let
\lambda>0
and define the scaling function
S_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda x
. Then for the standard Mellin transform,

(B.4)

\mathcal{M}[f\circ S_{\lambda}](s)=\lambda^{-s}\mathcal{M}[f](s).

This property can be seen as a consequence of the change of variables formula, the scale invariance of the Haar measure
dx/x
, and the scale invariance of the domain of integration,
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

For the truncated Mellin transforms, a property similar to (
B.4
) holds. However, the domain of integration,
(\alpha,\beta)
, is
not
scale invariant. Thus, the cutoffs of the truncated transform change. In what follows, we assume that the function is defined (and continuous, for simplicity) on all of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
so that the change of domain does not present any new issues.

Lemma B.4: Scaling Property of Truncated Mellin Transforms

Let
f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, let
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\infty)
with
\alpha<\beta
, let
\lambda>0
, and define
S_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda x
.

Then, provided that the transforms are convergent, we have that

(B.5)

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{\beta}[f\circ S_{\lambda}](s)=\lambda^{-s}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda\alpha}^{\lambda\beta}[f](s).

If
\alpha=0
, then (
B.5
) becomes

(B.6)

\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f\circ S_{\lambda}](s)=\lambda^{-s}\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f](s)+\lambda^{-s}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}^{\lambda\beta}[f](s).

The purpose of (
B.6
) is clear in our usage of this property. Essentially, it establishes a functional relation akin to the standard property of Mellin transforms (viz. (
B.4
)) up to the addition of an entire function,
\lambda^{-s}\mathcal{M}_{\beta}^{\lambda\beta}[f](s)
. Crucially, this means that
\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f\circ S_{\lambda}]
and
\lambda^{-s}\mathcal{M}^{\beta}[f]
have the exact same set of singularities.
