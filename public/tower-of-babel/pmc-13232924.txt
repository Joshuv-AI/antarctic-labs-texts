A case report on pulmonary aspiration of bilious gastric content in patients with previous one anastomosis – mini gastric bypass: a new risk factor to be incorporated in airway management guidelines?
Authors: Jonathan D Mathers, Gjermund Galleberg, Silje Nordrevoll, Marthe Håvardstun, Øyvind Sverre Svendsen, Ib Jammer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMini gastric-bypass or one-anastomosis mini gastric-bypass (OA-MGB) is an emerging surgical technique for weight loss surgery with a single anastomosis and similar weight loss results to a Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RY-GB). Reflux of bile secretions into the newly formed gastric pouch is a recognised consequence of the OA-MGB. We report a case of severe pulmonary aspiration of bile contents upon induction of anaesthesia for a planned laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a patient with a previous OA-MGB. Airway practice may need to be amended to reflect the specially increased risk of aspiration in patients who have had an OA-MGB.

Introduction

Surgical weight loss options include gastric banding, sleeve gastrectomy or a gastric bypass. Gastric bypass surgery is traditionally a Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RY-GB) whereby a gastric pouch is created. The smaller new stomach is connected to the small bowel, and the remaining stomach is anastomosed distally (Fig. 1a).1 This creates the classic ‘Y’ form in contrast studies that give it its name. The RY-GB can lead to significant weight loss and improvements in disease states such as Type 2 diabetes.2 Further advances in surgical technique led to the development of the one anastomosis mini gastric bypass (OA-MGB) in 1997 which simplified the surgery requiring only a single anastomosis with the first series of 1274 cases reported in 2001 (Fig. 1b).3 The OA-MGB is now growing in popularity with increasing acceptance of the procedure globally.4,5 However, there are concerns with the procedure as patients can develop biliary gastritis and oesophagitis with the resulting concern for increased risk of oesophageal and gastric cancer from reflux of bile.6–10 Reflux of bile into the oesophagus has also been described.11 It becomes clear from the anatomy after completion of the OA-MGB that normal peristalsis of the duodenum can drive bile secretions into the newly formed stomach (green arrows Fig. 1b) in a way that is unlikely with a classic RY-GB procedure due to the distal connection of the second anastomosis (Fig. 1a). There are no specific guidelines for the anaesthetic management of patients who have previously undergone a bariatric procedure. The latest guidelines for patients living with obesity do not mention a special risk of bile reflux or aspiration that can result from an OA-MGB.12 In the surgical literature, there is evidence that patients who have had an OA-MGB may be seven times more at risk of aspiration on induction of anaesthesia after their surgery.13

We report a case of significant aspiration of bile on induction of anaesthesia for planned surgery in a patient who had previously undergone an OA-MGB and had no symptoms of reflux. This case report has been prepared in accordance with CARE guidelines for case reports.

Case report

A healthy 37-year-old female patient presented for an elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy at our hospital. She took no regular medications and had not had any problems with previous general anaesthesia. She had had an OA-MGB five years before (but at the time of anaesthesia it was only known that she had had a gastric bypass, not specifically an OA-MGB). Her airway exam was unremarkable, and she described no symptoms of reflux or heartburn. Her previous anaesthetic charts were reviewed and identified no previous airway management problems. Her last general anaesthetic was for her OA-MGB, and she had lost 40 kg in weight since her operation. She was 1.69 m high and weighed 91 kg at the time of her laparoscopic cholecystectomy (BMI: 31.8). Her anaesthesia plan was for total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) with intubation under direct laryngoscopy without neuromuscular blockade. She received Paracetamol 1.5 g, Dexamethasone 16 mg and OxyContin 10 mg with a small amount of water 15 min prior to surgery as part of our Sip-til-Send liberal fasting regimen that is standard practice in our hospital (permitting a maximum of 170 ml of clear fluid per hour before surgery). She had had no food after midnight the day before surgery.

After routine preoxygenation with a facemask, induction was started with intravenous Fentanyl 200 mcg followed by Propofol and Remifentanil using Eleveld models set at effect site concentrations of 3.5 mcg/ml and 10 ng/ml respectively. Mask ventilation was initially easy but suddenly became difficult. Her TIVA doses were increased to 5.0 mcg/ml and 12 ng/ml but she remained difficult to mask ventilate so a bolus of 50 mg of i.v. Rocuronium was given and attempts at ventilation were paused for ninety seconds to give this time to work. There was no decrease in her peripheral oxygen saturations during the apnoeic pause. Direct laryngoscopy was performed whereupon large volumes of bilious fluid were found in the supraglottic area with suspicion of descent below the vocal cords. The fluid was suctioned with a 5.3 mm suction catheter before proceeding with intubation. Direct laryngoscopy was uncomplicated and with a Cormack-Lehane grade 1 view a size 7.0 cuffed endotracheal tube (ETT) was passed without difficulty. Endotracheal suctioning, performed twice prior to ventilation, yielded large volumes of bilious fluid, confirming pulmonary aspiration. At this point her oxygen saturations started to fall so recruitment manoeuvres were used multiple times but after each manoeuvre her peripheral oxygen saturations would swiftly drop to around 88% despite an FiO2 of 45%. A bronchoscopy was performed which was normal but because of the persistent problems with oxygenation a decision was made to abandon surgery. She was extubated without incident but required intermittent continuous positive airway pressure support (CPAP) at 5 cmH2O and an FiO2 of 40% with a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 33.4 kPa. She was transferred to the intensive care unit where she remained for the first night. An antero-posterior chest radiograph was taken which revealed bilateral consolidation consistent with aspiration (Fig. 2). She was started on intravenous Clindamycin and discharged 2 days later with continued oral Clindamycin for seven days with a diagnosis of aspiration pneumonia.

She returned to the hospital 3 weeks later for the laparoscopic cholecystectomy where a modified rapid sequence induction using TIVA, Rocuronium, no mask ventilation and no cricoid pressure was employed. The surgery and anaesthesia proceeded without incident, but a large volume of bilious fluid was aspirated from the nasogastric tube that was placed after induction. She was discharged home on the day of surgery.

Discussion

Aspiration at the moment of induction of anaesthesia is a serious but rare complication with the potential for significant patient harm.14 Our patient experienced sudden difficulty in ventilation soon after induction of anaesthesia. It is possible that this could be ‘wooden-chest’ syndrome as a result of administration of high-dose opioids.15 Studies suggest that administration of a hypnotic before a high dose opioid can reduce the incidence of wooden-chest syndrome.16 Our standard induction sequence involves simultaneous administration of propofol with remifentanil using the Eleveld models which gives a slow bolus of both propofol and remifentanil followed by a pause.17,18 This bolus may have been sufficient to induce wooden-chest followed by increased mask ventilation pressures and inadvertent insufflation of the stomach – increasing the risk of aspiration.

Conclusion

Currently, there are no airway guidelines that specify special management for patients after weight-loss surgery.12 Whilst there may be no clinical symptoms of reflux in patients who have undergone an OA-MGB, the unique nature of the postoperative anatomy lends itself to a risk for reflux of bile into the newly formed stomach.6,13 There are several reports of bile reflux into the gastric pouch in the surgical literature with an incidence as high as 55% in some studies.8 However, how this translates to the risk of pulmonary aspiration during anaesthesia is not fully defined. We present a patient without symptoms of reflux and with a history of previously uncomplicated general anaesthesia who experienced a significant aspiration of bilious gastric contents during induction of anaesthesia. We would therefore warn anaesthesia practitioners of the uniquely increased risk of aspiration in patients who have undergone an OA-MGB. Current guidelines on the airway management of patients who have undergone an OA-MGB may need to be amended to reflect the increased risk of pulmonary aspiration, and a rapid sequence induction may be indicated in this patient group as well as avoidance of the use of an unsecured airway such as an LMA.
