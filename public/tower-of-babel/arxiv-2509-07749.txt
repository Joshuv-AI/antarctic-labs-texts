A local generalisation of frame bundles
Jérémie Pierard de Maujouy,

Abstract
Frame bundles equipped with a principal connection have their local structure characterised by a 1 1 -form, called the Cartan connection 1 1 -form, which gathers the principal connection form and the soldering form. We introduce generalised frame bundles as a smooth manifold equipped with a coframe with value in a suitable Lie algebra and which furthermore satisfies a weakened version of the Maurer-Cartan equation. From this structure it is possible to construct a Lie algebra action on the manifold. We study the question of whether it is possible to construct a Lie group action and build a base manifold over which the initial manifold is a frame bundle. We find that generalised frame bundles can have singular underlying manifolds.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background and motivation

Principal bundles have a preponderant position in differential geometry.
They are a geometric realisation of an ambiguity often called
“gauge”
and parametrised by a Lie group.
The standard model of particle physics is famously a
gauge theory
, which relies crucially on the gauge principle.
Principal bundles are also omnipresent in pure differential geometry: the Hopf fibration is an example of a
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-principal fibre bundle, another example is the bundle of linear frames of a manifold, which embodies the non-unicity of local frames.

At least as important as principal bundles themselves is the notion of principal connection and its associated covariant derivations.
It is no overstatement to say that connections are the central object of gauge theories and that principal bundles are essentially viewed as support for them.
In Riemannian geometry as well, the Levi-Civita connection is sees way more use than the principal bundle that supports it, the bundle of orthonormal frames. It therefore makes sense to consider principal bundles equipped with principal connections as a single object.

Cartan geometry describes manifolds as a
“curved”
version of homogeneous spaces, much like Riemannian geometry can be understood as a curved version of Euclidean geometry. It uses principal bundles, which describe the different ways the manifold can be related to the model geometry, that are equipped with a different type of connection: a Cartan connection. The Cartan connection
“solders”
the manifold to the model geometry, by encoding how the model geometry can slide along a contact point with the manifold.

In this article, we look at a generalisation of Cartan geometry, in the specific case of frame bundles with affine connections.
The generalisation is very simple: we just formalise the local structure of frame bundles with affine connections.
A space with such a structure will be called
“generalised frame bundle”
(with connection – usually not-mentioned).
Since the structure of principal bundle is non-local, it cannot be captured by a purely local definition, and generalised frame bundle are not necessarily principal bundle: this is one of the matters we investigate in this article.

Why this generalisation?
It turns out that generalised frame bundles are characterised by a vector-valued
1
-form which satisfies a system of partial differential equations, along with a non-degeneracy condition. This allows for a very concise definition. Furthermore, this system of partial differential equations can be obtained as Euler-Lagrange equations. This is in fact the starting point for defining generalised frame bundles: they are naturally produced in a Lagrangian field theory which looks to produce the space-time of General Relativity by constructing its bundle of orthonormal frames, starting from a blank
10
-manifold
[
HV16
,
Pie22a
,
Pie24
]
.

A benefit of this generalisation is that by setting the focus on the total space of the principal bundle, it allows for base spaces which are more singular than smooth manifolds, such as orbifolds as we will discuss.

This article aims to investigate the following questions: when is a generalised frame bundle a standard frame bundle? If not, can it be related to a standard frame bundle? What would be the underlying base space?
These questions will involve the problem of integrating a Lie algebra action into a Lie group action. This problem was studied in detail by Palais
[
Pal57
]
and was revisited by Kamber and Michor
[
KM04
]
. The author will re-examine this matter in an upcoming paper; in the present article, we will only briefly describe the general results.

The structure of generalised frame bundles turns out to be a case of Alekseevsky and Michor’s definition of Cartan connections
[
AM95
]
. However, while they study characteristic classes and prolongations, we are interested in comparing the global geometry with the familiar structure of frame bundles.

Outline

In Section
1
, after general definitions, we will give an elementary illustration of a generalised frame bundle, with pictures.
In Section
2

G
-structures, we introduce the generalised frames bundles and standard constructions on them.
We describe a few examples in Section

An illustrative example of a generalised frame bundle

This article studies the structure of

In this paper we want to investigate such

Our study will generalise the case of the solid torus to a more general Lie group with a linear representation of any dimension. The local structure only defines the action of a Lie algebra, so we will have to attempt constructing an action of a Lie group starting from a Lie algebra action.

The structure of a connection

Figure 3:
The torus as a
“twisted”
frame bundle

The reader may have noticed that the trivialisation of the orthonormal frame bundle is not uniquely defined by the frame bundle structure. It is equivalent to the additional structure of a (metric and torsionless)
flat
affine connection. The Euclidean plane inherits one induced by its affine space structure. In particular, the frame field which equips
P
depends on this choice of connection. The structure we want to study on
P
is more properly called
“generalised frame bundle with connection”
but we will use
“generalised frame bundle”
as a shorthand.

Indeed a more convenient way to encode the parallelism
(e_{1},e_{2},\xi)
of
P
is by using the dual coframe, which is a family of three
1
-forms
(\varpi^{1},\varpi^{2},\varpi^{\xi})
. When
P
forms an actual frame bundle, the forms
\varpi^{1}
and
\varpi^{2}
correspond to the so-called
“solder form”
of the frame bundle and the form
\varpi^{\xi}
is a connection
1
-form. They are gathered in what is called a
Cartan connection (1-)form
. Using this
3

1
-form

is very convenient to formulate the infinitesimal equivariance equations (
2
) (details in Section
2.2
).

Because the frame bundle is the space supporting affine connections on the base manifold, this implies that covariant physical field theories can be formulated on the frame bundle. By extension, it is possible to formulate them on generalised frame bundles: they have all the required geometrical structure. This is of particular interest as a mean to formulate field theories on singular spaces. For example, in higher dimension, given the Cartan connection form
\varpi
it is very easy to construct the associated Riemann curvature tensor or the Einstein tensor, so that one can study Einstein’s field equations (see the example in Section
2.3.3
).

2.2

2.2.1
Definition

Let
P\to M
be an
G
-principal bundle equipped with a solder form
\alpha
and a connection form
\omega
.
Gathering the two forms in a
\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued

-form gives a

on

\varpi:=\omega\oplus\alpha
.
The coframe

is
G

As a coframe,
\varpi
can be used to find the fundamental vector fields of the action of
\mathfrak{g}
: if
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
is represented by
\bar{\xi}
, the following holds:

\bar{\xi}=\varpi^{-1}(\xi,0)

The idea of
generalised frame bundles with connection
is to start from the manifold
P
and the object
\varpi
and use it to define the action of
\mathfrak{g}
and the vertical and horizontal tangent spaces. The base manifold
M
is to be reconstructed as an orbit space. Of course,
\varpi
cannot be any arbitrary coframe: it has to be
G
-equivariant, and the vector fields
\bar{\xi}
have to form an action of
\mathfrak{g}
. As we will see, equivariance is already a stronger requirement than compatibility with the bracket. The coframe
\varpi
only defines an action of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
(and not a group action) so we will be looking at equivariance under the Lie algebra action.

Let us now derive an intrinsic characterisation of the
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariance of

Cartan
1
-forms

First we equip

with the Lie algebra structure

so that the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be handled in a uniform fashion as an adjoint action. We will use indices

for

for

and indices

for

Equivariance under

can be stated as:

\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\xi}}\varpi+\ad_{\xi}\varpi=0

(8)

The left hand term can be reformulated as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\xi}}\varpi+\ad_{\xi}\varpi=(i_{\bar{\xi}}{\operatorname{d}}+{\operatorname{d}}i_{\bar{\xi}})\varpi+[\xi,\varpi]=i_{\bar{\xi}}{\operatorname{d}}\varpi+{\operatorname{d}}\xi+[\varpi(\bar{\xi}),\varpi]=i_{\bar{\xi}}\left({\operatorname{d}}\varpi+\frac{1}{2}\left[\varpi\wedge\varpi\right]\right)

Thus Equation (

\forall\xi\in\mathfrak{g},\qquad i_{\bar{\xi}}\left({\operatorname{d}}\varpi+\frac{1}{2}\left[\varpi\wedge\varpi\right]\right)=0

Since the vectors

\displaystyle{\operatorname{d}}\alpha^{a}+\left[\omega\wedge\alpha\right]^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{a}_{bc}\alpha^{b}\wedge\alpha^{c}

\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathfrak{a}
such that
\Omega
is horizontal in the sense of having only components along the

directions. From this perspective, put forward in Wise’s works
[

Wis10

GW13
]
, the corresponding

can be understood as geometry with symmetry broken down from

to

We will call a manifold equipped with a

to obtain this structure from solutions to differential equations coming from a variational principle. We come back to this situation in Section

.
We have justified the following result:

Theorem 2.12
.

Let

be a generalised frame bundle with a
\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued Cartan
1

defines a free action of the Lie algebra

on

is an equivariant

We now extend the constructions used in the previous sections to the case of generalised frame bundles.

2.2.2
Basic fields on generalised frame Bundles

Let
P
be a generalised frame bundle with Cartan
1
-form
\varpi=\omega\oplus\alpha
. The coframe defines an
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant identification

TP\simeq P\times\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}

We define the horizontal and vertical distributions:

\displaystyle HP

\displaystyle:=\ker\omega=\varpi^{-1}(\mathfrak{g}\oplus 0)

(15)

\displaystyle VP

\displaystyle:=\ker\alpha=\varpi^{-1}(0\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n})

(16)

Vectors will be called
horizontal
if they belong to
HP
and vertical if they belong to
VP
. Conversely, differential forms will be called
horizontal
if they have vanishing contraction with vectors of
VP
and
vertical
if they have vanishing contraction with vectors of
HP
. The space of horizontal differential forms on
P
will be written
\Omega_{\text{hor}}^{\bullet}(P)
.

The structure equation

{\operatorname{d}}\alpha^{a}+\left[\varpi\wedge\alpha\right]^{a}=\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{a}_{bc}\alpha^{b}\wedge\alpha^{c}

implies that the ideal spanned by the forms
(\alpha^{a})
is a differential ideal and as a consequence the vertical distribution
VP
is involutive: it defines a foliation on
P
, which is regular.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
acts on
P
and the action naturally lifts to natural vector bundles such as
TP
or
\Lambda^{\bullet}TP
. The action on
T^{*}P
preserves the horizontal and vertical forms:

\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\xi}}\omega=-\ad_{\xi}\omega

is a vertical form and

\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\xi}}\alpha=-\ad_{\xi}\alpha

is a horizontal form, for all
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, since the action of
\mathfrak{g}
preserves the decomposition
\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

As a consequence,
\mathfrak{g}
also preserves the verticality or horizontality of vectors.

Definition 2.13

(Basic fields)
.

We define (local)

on

as local vector fields which are

and
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant
.
Note that a horizontal vector field is identified through

to a

-valued field. Since

is equivariant, it is equivalent to ask for the horizontal vector field to be
\mathfrak{g}
-invariant or for the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued field to be
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant.

Similarly we define
basic tensor fields
, respectively
basic differential forms
, as fields with value in a tensor product of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{*}
, resp.
horizontal
differential forms, which are
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant. Similarly to the situation of standard frame bundles, a horizontal differential form can be identified with a
\Lambda^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{*}
-valued field.

Finally, given a representation

of
\mathfrak{g}
, a

of

is a
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant

-valued field. They generalise the sections of associated bundles.
We denote as follows the space of basic

-valued differential forms:

\Omega^{\bullet}_{bas}(P,V):=\Omega^{\bullet}_{hor}(P,V)^{\mathfrak{g}}

(17)

Covariant derivation

(Covariant derivative)
.

Let

be a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
and

. Its

The

-form

The

2.2.4
Principal bundles

Let
P
be a generalised frame bundle with a Cartan form
\varpi
.
Given a Lie group
H
, we want to generalise the structure of
H
-principal bundles to the framework of generalised frame bundles. More precisely, we look to define a structure which will generalise the fibre product of a frame bundle and a
H
-principal bundle equipped with a connection. There are two approaches.

The first is to generalise
G
-equivariant
H
-principal bundles on
P
. Of course,
P
does not have a group action but a Lie algebra action of
\mathfrak{g}
so that we can only consider
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant bundles.

Definition 2.15

(
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant
H
-principal bundles)
.

An
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant
H
-principal bundle
on
P
is a fibre bundle
E\xrightarrow{\phi}P
with a lift of the action of
\mathfrak{g}
to
H
-invariant vector fields on
E
.

Let
E
be such a fibre bundle. We shall assume it is provided with an
\mathfrak{g}
-invariant
H
-principal connection
1
-form which we call
A
(which is always possible for a pullback principal bundle as described in Section
2.1.4
). The connection form
A
defines
on
E
horizontal and vertical distributions;
A
defines a
\mathfrak{h}
-valued coframe on the vertical distribution, while the Cartan form
\varpi
pulls back to a coframe
\phi^{*}\varpi
of the horizontal distribution of
E
. They can be gathered into a
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
-valued coframe on
E
:

A\oplus\phi^{*}\varpi\in\Omega^{1}(E,\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g})

which is both
H
-equivariant and
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant.

For
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
we define

\hat{\xi}:=\left(A\oplus\phi^{*}\varpi\right)^{-1}(0\oplus\xi)

which represents
\xi
by a horizontal vector field on
E
. The invariance of
A
under the fields
\hat{\xi}
for
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
is formulated as

0=\mathcal{L}_{\hat{\xi}}A=\left({\operatorname{d}}i_{\hat{\xi}}+i_{\hat{\xi}}{\operatorname{d}}\right)A={\operatorname{d}}0+i_{\hat{\xi}}{\operatorname{d}}A

Since
A(\hat{\xi})=0
, this is equivalent to

i_{\hat{\xi}}\left({\operatorname{d}}A+\frac{1}{2}\left[A\wedge A\right]\right)=0

We also define for

the vector fields
\hat{u}
by which
\mathfrak{h}
acts on
E
. Similarly to Equation (
9
), equivariance of
A
is equivalent to

\forall u\in\mathfrak{h},\ i_{\hat{u}}\left({\operatorname{d}}A+\frac{1}{2}\left[A\wedge A\right]\right)=0

The conclusion is that the equivariance of
A
under
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
is equivalent to the existence of coefficients
F^{I}_{ab}
, with
I
superscripts associated to the space
\mathfrak{h}
, such that

{\operatorname{d}}A^{I}+\frac{1}{2}\left[A\wedge A\right]^{I}=\frac{1}{2}F^{I}_{bc}\phi^{*}\left(\alpha^{b}\wedge\alpha^{c}\right)

(24)

The
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant
H
-principal connection thus satisfies an equation similar to (
13
), and the
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
-valued coframe
A\oplus\phi^{*}\varpi
is a Cartan
1
-form: using a superscript
B
for
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
, we have

{\operatorname{d}}(A\oplus\phi^{*}\varpi)^{B}+\frac{1}{2}\left[A\oplus\phi^{*}\varpi\wedge A\oplus\phi^{*}\varpi\right]^{B}=\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{B}_{bc}\phi^{*}\left(\alpha^{b}\wedge\alpha^{c}\right)

(25)

To sum up, the
\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant
H
-principal bundle
E
has the structure of a generalised frame bundle modelled on
\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This brings us to the second approach, which is to consider the
H
-principal fibration as directions in a generalised bundle taking the place of the bundle
E
above, instead of an actual
H
-principal bundle. This requires the generalised bundle to have the data of a
H
-principal connection which will be integrated into a coframe used to define the generalised frame bundle structure. A generalised frame bundle modelled on
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
is equipped with commuting actions of
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{g}
. Since
\mathfrak{h}
only acts trivially on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one can define basic sections of vector bundle associated to representations of
\mathfrak{g}
. In this sense, such a generalised frame bundle can support
“internal degrees of freedom”
with infinitesimal directions corresponding to
\mathfrak{h}
.

From this perspective, principal bundles are readily integrated in the formalism of generalised frame bundles: they correspond to a Lie-subalgebra direct factor
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
which acts trivially on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.3
Examples of generalised frame bundles

In this section we list a few examples in which the structure of generalised frame bundle with connection is relevant.
A first class of examples actually have a group action but with varying orbit types.

2.3.1
Conical singularity with restricted chirality

Consider the group
\Spin(4)
. It can be decomposed as a direct product of groups:

\Spin(4)\simeq\Spin(3)\times\Spin(3)\simeq\SpH(1)\times\SpH(1)

with
\SpH(1)
the group of unitary quaternions. The action of
\Spin(4)
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
by projection to
\SO(4)
can be represented using the quaternionic structure:
\SpH(1)\times\SpH(1)
acts on the quaternion space
\mathbb{H}
by:

\displaystyle\Spin(4)\times\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\simeq\SpH(1)\times\SpH(1)\times\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{H}

(26)

\displaystyle(g,z)

\displaystyle\equiv(p,q,z)

\displaystyle\mapsto\bar{p}zq

Indeed the Clifford algebra
\Cl(4)
is isomorphic to
\Mat_{2}(\mathbb{H})
and the subspace of vectors
\mathbb{R}^{4}
can be identified with a quaternionic line within
\Cl(4)
on which
\Spin(4)
has the described action.

Consider now the semi-direct product
\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
: it is the product manifold equipped with the following product structure:

(g_{1},x_{1})\cdot(g_{2},x_{2})=(g_{1}g_{2},\ g_{2}^{-1}\cdot x_{1}+x_{2})

It can be interpreted as the space of
“spinorial frames”
above the affine (coset) space

\displaystyle\left(\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)/\Spin(4)

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{R}^{4}

\displaystyle[g,x]

\displaystyle\mapsto g\cdot x

Let
\gamma^{5}
be a fixed chirality element of
\Spin(4)
:
it is the (ordered) product in the Clifford algebra
\Cl(4)
of vectors of an orthonormal direct basis (its sign depends on the chosen orientation). It squares to identity and takes the form

\displaystyle\Spin(4)

\displaystyle\simeq\SpH(1)\times\SpH(1)

\displaystyle\gamma^{5}

\displaystyle\equiv(1,-1)

The chirality element generates a central
{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}
subgroup of
\Spin(4)
:

\{(1,1),(1,-1)\}\subset\SpH(1)\times\SpH(1)\simeq\Spin(4)

Let us consider the quotient of
\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
under the corresponding left action of
{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}
:

P:=\left({\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}\right)\backslash\left(\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)

with the generator of
{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}
acting as

\gamma^{5}\cdot(g,x)=(\gamma^{5}g,x)

The quotient map

\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}\to\left({\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}\right)\backslash\left(\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)

is a
2
-fold covering map.
The action of
{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}
on the left on
\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
factors to the quotient
\mathbb{R}^{4}
on which it acts by parity, according to Equation (
26
):

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(g,x)\in\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\gamma^{5}g,x)\in\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(g\cdot x)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma^{4}\cdot g\cdot x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma^{5}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma^{5}}

As a subgroup of
\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
,
\{1,\gamma^{5}\}
is no longer central and is not even a normal subgroup so that
P
is not a quotient group but it still has a left action of
\Spin(4)
.

The Maurer-Cartan form
\varpi
on
\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
is invariant under the left action of
\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
hence factors to
P
. The manifold
P
is naturally fibred above
\left(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\right)\backslash\mathbb{R}^{4}
which has a conical singularity at the origin.

The perspective of generalised frame bundles suggests defining spinor fields on
\left(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\right)\backslash\mathbb{R}^{4}
as basic spinor fields on
\left({\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}\right)\backslash(\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4})
, according to Definition
2.13
.

Computing the isotropy groups, one finds that for
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\{0\}
,
[e,x]\in P
has orbits of type
\Spin(4)
but
(e,0)
has orbit type
\Spin(3)\times\SO(3)
. That is, the
\Spin(4)
-isotropy groups of the orbit of
[e,0]
correspond to the subgroup
1\times{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}\subset\Spin(3)\times\Spin(3)
. The decomposition
\Spin(4)\simeq\Spin(3)\times\Spin(3)
corresponds to the decomposition of Dirac spinors into left-handed and right-handed spinors. For a spinor, being invariant under
1\times{\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}
is equivalent to having a vanishing right-handed part.
As a consequence, basic spinor fields on
\left({\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}}\right)\backslash(\Spin(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4})
necessarily have a vanishing right-handed component above the origin of
({\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}})\backslash\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

In other words, the geometry of the generalised frame bundle
P
requires the spinor fields on
({\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}})\backslash\mathbb{R}^{4}
to have a right-handed part that vanishes at the origin.

2.3.2
Locally Klein geometries

This example generalises the previous one and gives a large family of
flat
frame bundles above singular spaces.

Let
G
be a Lie group acting on a vector space
V
. Consider the Lie group

: it has a (right) Maurer-Cartan form
\varpi
(Example
2.11
). The action of the group

on the left

The group

can be seen as a frame bundle as follows:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G\ltimes V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(G\ltimes V\right)/G}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}

is not a normal subgroup, the isomorphism

is not a group homomorphism. Furthermore the splitting gives a section of the frame bundle over

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G\ltimes V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G\times V}

The example presented in the introductory section

The torus is a depiction of the frame bundle

\displaystyle\{\pm 1\}\backslash\left(\SO(2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle[g,v]

2.3.3
Dynamical generalised frame bundle structures

Generalised frame bundles with connections are spaces with the same local structure as frame bundles with connection. Therefore they serve as a useful generalisation when one expects to produce a frame bundle structure, or, more generally, a
G
-structure, from local equations. In General Relativity the causal and gravitational structure of spacetime are encoded in a Lorentzian metric defined on a four-dimensional spacetime. Depending on the variant of the theory, the metric may be supplanted by a coframe field (a so-called
tetrad
) and the metric connection may have supplementary degrees of freedom. The point is that this geometry corresponds to a
G
-structure with connection over the spacetime. Generalised frame bundles with connections provide a new frame for dynamically defined
G
-structures, more precisely a generalisation thereof. In particular, the solder form is the frame bundle equivalent of the tetrad field.

Generalised frame bundles have sufficient structure to define curvature, torsion and matter fields which are all basic fields. This was already put to use by Ne’eman and Regge
[
NR78
]
for the specific case of
“group manifolds”
. For example, the Einstein tensor is defined as follows. We write
{\eta}^{ab}
for a
\mathfrak{g}
-invariant metric on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
\rho^{b}_{ia}
for the components of the representation (the
i
index corresponds to
\mathfrak{g}
)

\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{\mathfrak{so}}(\mathbb{R}^{4},{\eta})

Recall the curvature
2
-form;

\Omega={\operatorname{d}}\omega+\frac{1}{2}\left[\omega\wedge\omega\right]\in\Omega^{2}_{bas}(P,\mathfrak{g})

The curvature tensor is constructed as

\Riem=\Omega^{i}\rho_{i}\in\Omega^{2}_{bas}(P,\operatorname{\mathfrak{so}}(\mathbb{R}^{4},{\eta}))

The associated Ricci tensor is a basic
\mathbb{R}^{4*}
-valued
1
-form:

\Ric_{d}=\Omega^{i}_{bc}\rho^{b}_{id}\alpha^{c}\in\Omega^{1}_{bas}(P,\mathbb{R}^{4*})

and scalar curvature is a basic scalar:

\Scal=\Ric_{a}(\zeta^{a})\in\Omega^{0}_{bas}(P)

The (tetradic) Einstein tensor is defined as

\Ein_{a}=\Ric_{a}-\frac{1}{2}\Scal\eta_{ab}\alpha^{b}\in\Omega^{1}_{bas}(P,\mathbb{R}^{4*})

and with a matter field
\psi
on which depends an (adimensionalised) stress-energy tensor
T\in\Omega^{1}_{bas}(P,\mathbb{R}^{4*})
, Einstein’s field equation can be formulated as

\Ein=T\in\Omega^{1}_{bas}(P,\mathbb{R}^{4*})

(27)

Following an idea from Toller
[
Tol78
]
and revisited by Hélein and Vey as well as the author
[
HV16
,
Pie22
]
constructs a Lagrangian field theory on a
10
-dimensional manifold with a
\operatorname{\mathfrak{so}}(4)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
-valued coframe
\varpi
and a field
\psi
with value in a spinor representation of
\Spin(4)
as fields. The theory uses a kind of generalised Lagrange multipliers, and the equations of motion constrain
\varpi
to be a Cartan
1
-form and impose equations of Einstein-Cartan type and Dirac type on
\varpi
and
\psi
, which also involve the Lagrange multipliers. It is shown that in the case the generalised
\operatorname{\mathfrak{so}}(4)
-structure is a standard
\Spin(4)
-structure the field equations can be decoupled from the Lagrange multipliers and the usual Einstein-Cartan-Dirac field equations are recovered on the underlying spacetime (in Riemannian signature).

3
The structure of
G
-manifolds

The generalised frame bundles introduced in Section
2.2
have the action of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, but a priori they have no group action. On the other hand, frame bundles have the smooth action of a
structural group

G
, which is furthermore a principal action, so that the frame bundle does indeed form a
G
-principal bundle above a base manifold.

Are all generalised frame bundles actual frame bundles of smooth manifolds?
The example given in Section
2.3.1
shows that it need not be the case. This brings the question: what is the extent of this generalisation of the frame bundles?
In this section, we review conditions for a Lie algebra action to describe a principal fibre bundle.
In order to tackle this question, we focus on the three following points:

When does a Lie algebra action integrate to a Lie group action?

3.1
\mathfrak{g}
-manifolds

In this section we describe the different aspects of the problem of integrating the action of a Lie algebra to a Lie group action.
We do not got into much detail; the reader will find more detail in Palais’ thesis
[
Pal57
]
, a more recent article by Kamber and Michor
[
KM04
]
or the review in the author’s PhD thesis
[
Pie22a
]
.
We choose a connected Lie group integration
G
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

Let
P
be a
right

\mathfrak{g}
-manifold. To construct the action of an element

of
G
on a point
p\in P
, a natural idea is to choose a smooth path
\gamma

G
from

and to solve the differential equation

\begin{cases}c^{\prime}(t)=c(t)\cdot\gamma^{\prime}(t)\\
c(0)=p\end{cases}

(28)

The final endpoint is the candidate for
p\cdot g
.
In order for this procedure to succeed, the differential equation (
28

completeness
of the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
P
. According to
[
Pal57
]
(Theorem IV.III), in order for all such differential equations to admit complete solutions, it is sufficient that all fundamental vector fields are complete.

Let us assume that all the fundamental vector fields are complete.
For the above procedure to consistently define an action of
G
, the end of the path

must be independent from the choice of
\gamma
and only depend on the starting point
p
of
c
and the endpoint
g
of
\gamma
. This condition is called

[
Pal57
]
, and it is always verified (for a complete action) when

is simply connected. It is sufficient to consider loops
\gamma
about the identity element. One topological formulation of univalence is that the trivial bundle
P\times G\to P
equipped with the flat connection corresponding to the right action of
\mathfrak{g}
has trivial holonomy.

On a compact manifold, all vector fields are complete, so the following proposition holds:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a simply-connected Lie group and
P
a compact
\mathfrak{g}
-manifold.
Then the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
P
integrates to a Lie group action of
G
.

Let us now discuss the case when the action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
P
is incomplete. Then certain parts of the group are unable to act on
P
: only parts of the group may act on
P
, with this part of
G
depending on the point of
P
. This
“local action”
may be conveniently encoded into a Lie groupoid
[
Pie22a
]
, but we are looking for a group action. It is therefore necessary to

the manifold. There is a generic procedure, described in
[
Pal57
,
KM04
]
, which constructs, starting from
P
, a larger manifold on which
G
acts. This manifold is called the
G
-completion of

and is not always Hausdorff, since it is constructed as a leaf space. In fact, the
G
-completion of

is not always larger than
P
: when there is a defect of univalence,
P
is reduced to a suitable quotient so as to satisfy univalence.

3.2
Proper actions

In this section and the following sections, we consider a
Hausdorff
manifold

on which a Lie group
G
acts smoothly
on the right

P\to P/G
defines a
G
-principal bundle. First, we discuss

Definition 3.2

(Proper action)
.

The action of

\displaystyle P\times G

\displaystyle\to P\times P

\displaystyle(p,g)

\displaystyle\mapsto(p,p\cdot g)

Proposition 3.3

(
[
Mei03
]
)
.

If

is compact then the action on

is proper.

In fact, properness captures important topological properties of the continuous actions of compact groups.

Proposition 3.4
.

1.

If the action of
G
is proper then the isotropy groups of the elements of

are compact.

2.

If the action of

is proper then for every

is an embedding onto the orbit

.

3.

If the action of

is Haudorff.

Principal

-manifolds

In order for the quotient map

to define a

needs to act freely.

Theorem 3.5

(Quotient manifold theorem,
[

]
)
.

If the action of

has a structure of a (Hausdorff) quotient manifold and the quotient map

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\to P/G

defines a (locally trivial)

Naturally, when the action is not free but all isotropy groups are identical – let us call this subgroup

factors to a free action of the quotient Lie group

3.3
Orbit types and decomposition

In this section, we discuss the structure of the orbit space

and the quotient map when the action of

3.3.1
Orbit types

If

and

are two points of

belonging to the same orbit, their isotropy groups are conjugate.

Definition 3.6

(Type of an orbit)
.

The

(or isotropy type
4
4

4

Bredon
[

]
makes a difference between

and

. We are using here the isotropy type.
)
of an orbit

of

is the conjugacy class of its isotropy groups.
We write it with brackets

.

Note that orbits with different types may be diffeomorphic, for example different presentations of the circle group as quotients of the real line
\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, assuming properness, orbits with the same type are diffeomorphic (see Proposition
2
).

Orbit types form a pre-ordered set: we define for coset spaces

and

the following pre-order
5
5

5

A transitive and reflexive relation.
relation:

[O_{1}]\leqslant[O_{2}]

if and only if for any

, the isotropy group

is conjugated to a subgroup of the isotropy group

. Roughly speaking, an orbit is larger when its isotropy group is smaller.

This relation may or may not be antisymmetric. The following lemma justifies that it is under some assumptions we state below.

Lemma 3.7

(
[
Mes16
, Lemma 3.15]
)
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and
K
a closed subgroup which has a finite number of connected components (e.g. is compact).

For any

gKg^{-1}\subset K
implies that
gKg^{-1}=K
.

Proof.

Let

be an element of

such that
\Ad_{g}K\subset K
.

At the level of the Lie algebra, since
\Ad_{g}
is a linear automorphism, dimension considerations imply that
\Ad_{g}\mathfrak{k}\subset\mathfrak{k}\implies\Ad_{g}\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{k}
. The subgroups
\Ad_{g}K
and

thus have the same neutral component which we call

and which is preserved by
\Ad_{g}
. The action of

thus factors to the quotient group

, which is finite by assumption.

As an injection of the finite set

into the subset

\Ad_{h}(K/K_{0})=(\Ad_{h}K)/K_{0}\subset K/K_{0}

the map

necessarily defines a bijection of

. One concludes that
\Ad_{g}K
has a point in each connected component of

, thus is equal to
K
.

∎

As a consequence of Lemma
3.7
, when
G
is compact, so that all its closed subgroups are compact, the pre-order relation on orbit types is antisymmetric hence an order relation. More generally, when the action of

on

is proper, so that all isotropy groups are compact, the pre-order relation
on the orbit types of

is antisymmetric.

3.3.2
Decomposition of a proper
G
-manifold

When the
G
-manifold

has different orbit types, one cannot hope for a fibre bundle

since all orbits are not diffeomorphic as

If
[\mathcal{O}]

for the reunion of the orbits of type
[\mathcal{O}]
in

. We call

the

[\mathcal{O}]
of

. Not only orbits are embedded submanifolds (Proposition

) but the part of

of any given type is also an embedded submanifold:

Theorem 3.8

(
[

]
)
.

If

is a proper

[\mathcal{O}]
, the corresponding part

is an embedded submanifold of

.

The

3.3.3
Decomposition of the orbit space

Similarly to the decomposition of

, there is a decomposition of

according to orbit types.
Let us consider
P_{[\mathcal{O}]}/G\subset P/G
the part of the orbit space of type

.

Theorem 3.9

(
[
Mei03
]
)
.

Assume the action of

on

is proper. Let
\mathcal{O}
be an orbit type. Then

•

P_{[\mathcal{O}]}/G
has a quotient manifold structure,

•

The quotient map

defines a fibre bundle with each fibre a right

.

In other words, the quotient map

does not in general define a fibre bundle but can be decomposed into a collection of locally trivial fibrations.
The connected components of the subsets

form a so-called

]

Globally, the structure of the orbit space of a proper

), which are spaces locally isomorphic to the quotient of a Euclidean space by a compact group. In the case the action of

.

3.3.4
The principal orbit type

The quotient maps
P_{[\mathcal{O}]}\to P_{[\mathcal{O}]}/G
define fibre bundles but in the situation of generalised frame bundles, one could hope for the entirety of
P
to form a fibre bundle above its orbit space. Although it is not the case, it is true for a large part of

, as we explain here.

Theorem 3.10

(Principal orbit type,
[
Mei03
]
)
.

If
P
is a proper
G
-manifold, then it admits a maximal orbit type, which is unique.
The corresponding part of
P
is a dense open subset of
P
and its orbit space is connected, open and dense inside
P/G
.

The maximal orbit type is called the
principal orbit type
, the orbits are called
principal orbits
and the isotropy groups
principal isotropy groups
.

The quotient
P/G
is a manifold outside of a singular locus, bounds on its dimension can be found in Bredon’s monograph
[
Bre72
]
or in Meinrenken’s lecture notes
[
Mei03
]
.

The principal orbit can be characterised as follows:

Theorem 3.11

(
[
Bre72
]
)
.

Assume
G
acts properly on
P
.
Then an orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset P
is principal if an only if it admits a
G
-invariant neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}\supset\mathcal{O}
that is isomorphic as a
G
-manifold to
\mathcal{O}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some

, with
G
acting trivially on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

4.1
Integration of a Cartan 1-form

4.1.1
Cartan

We showed in Section

that

is equipped with a free action of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and that

If the vector fields are complete the Lie algebra action is readily integrated into a Lie group action of the simply connected Lie group integration

Let us introduce the following intermediate notion between Cartan

Definition 4.1

(Generalised Cartan connection)
.

Let

be a Lie algebra acting on

.
Let

be a
\mathfrak{g}
-manifold. A

is a nondegenerate

and satisfies the equivariance equation:

\displaystyle\varpi\left({\bar{\xi}}\right)=\xi

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{{\bar{\xi}}}\varpi+\ad_{\xi}\varpi=0

(31)

Remark
.

Note that our definition differs from the one in

]

(which requires the structure of a principal bundle).

Since

G
-manifolds.

Assuming that the problem of integrating the Lie algebra action to a group action was solved, a Cartan
1
-form becomes a generalised Cartan connection.
Let us now see to what extent generalised Cartan connections are related to Cartan connections on principal bundles.

4.1.2
Principal orbits and bundle fibration

In this section,
P
is a connected manifold with a Cartan form
\varpi

\mathfrak{g}
. We now assume that the infinitesimal action integrates to a
group
action of
G

. We want to construct from

We further require the action to be
proper
.
As a consequence, the isotropy groups are compact, and the action of
\mathfrak{g}
being free, they are discrete, hence finite:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and
P
a proper
G
-manifold.
If the infinitesimal action of
\mathfrak{g}
is free then the isotropy groups of

are finite.

We restrict our attention to

We need an effective group action on

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
P
be a connected
G

\mathfrak{g}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued generalised Cartan connection.
Assume that the action of
G

Assume furthermore that the isotropy groups are normal subgroups of

The manifold

has a free and effective action of

-bundle over the quotient

which has a quotient differentiable manifold structure.
The principal bundle is equipped with a solder form and a
(K\backslash G)
-principal connection.

Proof.

The action of
G
