Measure-Theoretic Aspects of Star-Free and Group Languages
Ryoma Sin’ya, Takao Yuyama

Abstract
A language L L is said to be 𝒞 {\cal C} -measurable, where 𝒞 {\cal C} is a class of languages, if there is an infinite sequence of languages in 𝒞 {\cal C} that “converges” to L L . We investigate the properties of 𝒞 {\cal C} -measurability in the cases where 𝒞 {\cal C} is 𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{SF} , the class of all star-free languages, and 𝖦 \mathsf{G} , the class of all group languages. It is shown that a language L L is 𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{SF} -measurable if and only if L L is 𝖦𝖣 \mathsf{GD} -measurable, where 𝖦𝖣 \mathsf{GD} is the class of all generalised definite languages (a more restricted subclass of star-free languages). This means that 𝖦𝖣 \mathsf{GD} and 𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{SF} have the same “measuring power”, whereas 𝖦𝖣 \mathsf{GD} is a very restricted proper subclass of 𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{SF} . Moreover, we give a purely algebraic characterisation of 𝖲𝖥 \mathsf{SF} -measurable regular languages, which is a natural extension of Schützenberger’s theorem stating the correspondence between star-free languages and aperiodic monoids. We also show the probabilistic independence of star-free and group languages, which is an important application of the former result. Finally, while the measuring power of star-free and generalised definite languages are equal, we show that the situation is rather opposite for subclasses of group languages as follows. For any two local subvarieties 𝒞 ⊊ 𝒟 {\cal C}\subsetneq{\cal D} of group languages, we have { L ∣ L ​ is ​ 𝒞 ​ -measurable } ⊊ { L ∣ L ​ is ​ 𝒟 ​ -measurable } \{L\mid L\text{ is }{\cal C}\text{-measurable}\}\subsetneq\{L\mid L\text{ is }{\cal D}\text{-measurable}\} .

Introduction

One of the fundamental results in semigroup theory is the Krohn-Rhodes decomposition theorem stating that every finite monoid can be decomposed in a suitable sense into a wreath product of finite aperiodic monoids and finite groups.
According to the Krohn-Rhodes decomposition theorem, the class of
star-free languages
(the language counterpart of aperiodic monoids) and
group languages
(languages recognised by finite groups) can be considered as two major “building blocks” of regular languages (see Chapter 7 of
[
5
]
for detailed introduction of the Krohn-Rhodes theorem).
While the theory of star-free languages is well-studied (
e.g.
, Schützenberger’s algebraic characterisation
[
23
]
, McNaughton-Papert’s logical characterisation
[
13
]
, the dot-depth hierarchy (described later) proposed by Cohen and Brzozowski
[
4
]
and recent progresses relating to this hierarchy (see
[
20
]
or the survey
[
15
]
), etc.), the theory of group languages seems not, albeit that there is a result on an analogue of the dot-depth hierarchy starting with group languages
[
12
]
and the decidability of the so-called separation problem for group languages
[
19
]
.
Although the definition of group languages is simple from an algebraic point of view, this class does not have a language theoretic nor logical different characterisation.
To borrow the phrase used by Place–Zeitoun
[
19
]
: “This makes it difficult to get an intuitive grasp about group languages, which may explain why this class remains poorly understood.”

This paper sheds new light on these two major building blocks — star-free and group languages — of regular languages, by using a measure-theoretic notion so-called
{\cal C}
-measurability
.
A language
L
is said to be
{\cal C}
-measurable if there exists an infinite sequence of pairs of languages
(K_{n},M_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
in
{\cal C}
such that
K_{n}\subseteq L\subseteq M_{n}
holds for all
n
and the
density
(described later) of the difference
M_{n}\setminus K_{n}
tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity.
The notion of
{\cal C}
-measurability was introduced in
[
25
]
and used for classifying non-regular languages by using regular languages.
The notion of
{\cal C}
-measurability can be considered as a non-commutative and high-dimensional extension of Buck’s measure theoretic notion for subsets of natural numbers so-called
measure density

[
3
]
, and the notion of
{\cal C}
-measurability can be defined by using a purely measure theoretic notion called
Carathéodory extension

[
26
]
.
So far, the decidability and alternative characterisation of
{\cal C}
-measurability is systematically studied for some subclasses of regular languages.
For example, simple and decidable characterisations of
\mathsf{PT}
-measurable and
\and
-measurable languages are given in
[
27
]
, where
\mathsf{PT}
is the class of all piecewise testable languages and
\and
is the class of all alphabet testable languages (
i.e.
, languages defined by the first-order logic with only one variable).
For the class
\mathsf{GD}
of
generalised definite
languages (
i.e.
, languages whose membership can be checked by finitely many prefix and suffix tests), an automata theoretic characterisation of the
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable regular languages is given in
[
28
]
and it is decidable in quadratic time with respect to the size of a given deterministic automaton
[
9
]
.
Some properties of the measuring power of star-free languages is investigated in
[
26
]
, but the decidability for regular languages was left open.
The decidability of
\mathsf{G}
-measurability, where
\mathsf{G}
is the class of all group languages, for regular languages is still unknown, while the measurability of two restricted subclasses

\mathsf{Gcom}
(languages recognised by
commutative
groups) and
\mathsf{MOD}
(languages defined by length-modulo condition) of group languages are shown to be decidable for regular languages
[
29
]
.

Our contribution

The main results of this paper (shown in Section
3
–
5
), relating to the measuring power of star-free and group languages, are three kinds as follows.

The first main result is about the measuring power of star-free languages (Section
3
).
It is shown that a language
L
is
\mathsf{SF}
-measurable if and only if
L
is
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable (Theorem
13
).
Since the
\mathsf{GD}
-measurability is known to be decidable for regular languages
[
28
]
, this characterisation also gives the decidability of the
\mathsf{SF}
-measurability for regular languages.
We also give an algebraic characterisation of the
\mathsf{SF}
-measurable regular languages (Theorem
17
), which can be considered as a natural extension of Schützenberger’s theorem.

The second main result (Section
4
), which is an application of the first main result, is the
probabilistic independence
of star-free and group languages (Theorem
18
) stating that

\delta_{A}(L\cap K)=\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\delta_{A}(K)
holds for any
star-free language
L
and any group language
K
where

\delta_{A}
is the density function defined as

\delta_{A}(X)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\#\!\left(X\cap A^{k}\right)/\#\!\left(A^{k}\right)
(where
\#\!\left(S\right)
denotes the cardinality of a set
S
).
Since the class of star-free languages corresponds to finite aperiodic monoids (monoids having no non-trivial subgroups) via Schützenberger’s theorem
[
23
]
, it is sometimes said that star-free languages and group languages are “orthogonal”.
For example, it is described in
[
17
]
that ‘This notion [aperiodic monoid] is in some sense “orthogonal” to the notion of groups’ and in
[
5
]
that ‘Aperiodic monoids are orthogonal to groups.’ Our second main result gives a rigorous statement how star-free and group languages are orthogonal in the probability theoretic sense.

Between
\mathsf{GD}
and
\mathsf{SF}
, there is a fine-grained infinite strict hierarchy called the
dot-depth hierarchy
introduced by Cohen and Brzozowski
[
4
]
in 1971.
For a family
{\cal C}
of languages, we denote by

{\mathscr{M}}{\cal C}=\{L_{1}\cdots L_{k}\mid k\geq 1,L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\in{\cal C}\}\cup\{\{\varepsilon\}\}
the monoid closure of
{\cal C}
,
and denote by
{\mathscr{B}}{\cal C}
the Boolean closure of
{\cal C}
.
The dot-depth hierarchy starts with the family
{\cal B}_{0}
of all finite
or co-finite languages, and continues as
{\cal B}_{i+1}={\mathscr{B}}{\mathscr{M}}{\cal B}_{i}
for each
i\geq 0
.
By definition, we have
\mathsf{SF}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}{\cal B}_{i}

and also
{\cal B}_{0}\subsetneq\mathsf{GD}\subsetneq{\cal B}_{1}
.
However, the first main result tells us that this infinite strict hierarchy
collapses
via taking all measurable sets in the sense that

\{L\mid L\text{ is }\mathsf{GD}\text{-measurable}\}=\{L\mid L\text{ is }\mathsf{SF}\text{-measurable}\}

holds.
The last main result (Section
5
) tells us that, for the measuring power of subclasses of group languages, the situation is rather opposite.
We show that, for any two local subvarieties
1
1

1

A
local variety
is a family of regular languages over
A
closed under Boolean operations and quotients.

{\cal C}\subsetneq{\cal D}\subseteq\mathsf{G}
of group languages, we have
\{L\mid L\text{ is }{\cal C}\text{-measurable}\}\subsetneq\{L\mid L\text{ is }{\cal D}\text{-measurable}\}
, and moreover,
\{L\mid L\text{ is }{\cal C}\text{-measurable}\}\cap\mathsf{G}={\cal C}
holds (Theorem
20
).

In Section
6
we summarise our main results and describe some open problems.

Preliminaries

This section provides the precise definitions of density and measurability.
We denote by
\mathrm{REG}_{A}
the family of all regular languages over an alphabet
A
. For a word
w\in A^{*}
and a letter
a\in A
, we write
|w|_{a}
for the number of occurrences of
a
in
w
. The complement of
L
over
A
is denoted by
L^{\mathrm{c}}=A^{*}\setminus L
.
We assume that the reader has a standard knowledge of algebraic language theory (
cf.
[
11
,
5
,
16
]
).

For a finite monoid
M
and an element
x\in M
, the unique
idempotent power
(
i.e.
, the power
x^{n}
satisfing
x^{n}x^{n}=x^{n}
) of
x
is denoted
x^{\omega}
.
A monoid
M
is called
aperiodic
if
x^{\omega+1}=x^{\omega}
holds for any
x\in M
.
Recall that Green’s
\mathrel{\cal H}
-relation on a monoid
M
is defined as
x\mathrel{\cal H}y\Leftrightarrow(xM=yM)\land(Mx=My)
, and

\mathrel{\cal J}
-relation is defined as
x\mathrel{\cal J}y\Leftrightarrow MxM=MyM
.
A monoid
M
is said to be
{\cal G}
-trivial if every
{\cal G}
-class is a singleton where
{\cal G}\in\{\mathrel{\cal H},\mathrel{\cal J}\}
.
It is well-known that a finite monoid
M
is aperiodic if and only if
M
is
\mathrel{\cal H}
-trivial.
The main targets of this paper are the following three subclasses of regular languages. The
star-free languages
, the
generalised definite languages

\mathsf{GD}
, and the
group languages

\mathsf{G}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{SF}_{A}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,L\subseteq A^{*}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}L\text{ is recognised by a finite $\mathrel{\cal H}$-trivial monoid}\,\right\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{GD}_{A}

\displaystyle={\mathscr{B}}\left\{\,wA^{*},A^{*}w\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}w\in A^{*}\,\right\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{A}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,L\subseteq A^{*}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}L\text{ is recognised by a finite group}\,\right\}.

Also, we denote by
\mathsf{Gcom}_{A}
the class of all languages recognised by finite commutative groups.
A monoid
M
divides a monoid
N
if
M
is a monoid homomorphic image of a submonoid of
N
.
It is well-known that a monoid
M
recognises a language
L
if and only if the syntactic monoid
M_{L}
of
L
divides
M
, hence

L
is in
\mathsf{SF}_{A}
(resp.
\mathsf{G}_{A}
) if and only if
M_{L}
is a finite
\mathrel{\cal H}
-trivial monoid (resp. a finite group).
It is easy to see that a language
L
is generalised definite if and only if
L=E\cup\bigcup_{(u,v)\in F}uA^{*}v
for some finite sets
E\subseteq A^{*}
and
F\subseteq A^{*}\times A^{*}
.

2.1
Density and measurability of formal language

For a set
X
, we denote by
\#\!\left(X\right)
the cardinality of
X
.
We denote by
{\rm Nature}
the set of natural numbers including
0
.

Definition 1

(
cf.
[
2
]
)
.

The
density

\delta_{A}(L)
of
L\subseteq A^{*}
is defined as

\delta_{A}(L)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\#\!\left(L\cap A^{k}\right)}{\#\!\left(A^{k}\right)}

if it exists, otherwise we write
\delta_{A}(L)=\bot
.
A language
L
is called
null
if
\delta_{A}(L)=0
,
and dually,
L
is called
co-null
if
\delta_{A}(L)=1
.

Example 2
.

It is known that every regular language has a rational density (cf.
[
2
]
) and the density is computable for a given automaton.
For each word
w
, the language
A^{*}wA^{*}
,
the set of all words that contain
w
as a factor,
is of density one (i.e., co-null). This fact follows from the so-called infinite monkey theorem:
take any word
w
. A random word of length
n
contains
w
as a factor with probability tending to one as
n
tends to infinity.

We list some basic properties of the density as follows.

Lemma 3

(
[
2
]
)
.

The function
\delta_{A}
is a finitely additive measure.
In particular, for any
K,L\subseteq A^{*}
with
\delta_{A}(K)=\alpha,\delta_{A}(L)=\beta
, we have:

1.

\delta_{A}(L\setminus K)=\beta-\alpha
if
K\subseteq L
.

2.

\delta_{A}(K\cup L)=\alpha+\beta
if
K\cap L=\varnothing
.

3.

\delta_{A}(uLv)=\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\#\!\left(A\right)^{-|uv|}
for each
u,v\in A^{*}
.

Lemma 4

(
[
2
, Example 13.4.9]
)
.

Let
\eta\colon A^{*}\rightarrow G
be a surjective morphism onto a finite group. For any
g\in G
, we have
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(g))=\#\!\left(G\right)^{-1}
.

A word
w
is said to be a
forbidden word
of a language
L
if
L\cap A^{*}wA^{*}=\emptyset
holds.
If a language
L
has a forbidden word, then
\delta_{A}(L)=0
holds by the infinite monkey theorem because its complement
\overline{L}\supseteq A^{*}wA^{*}
should be co-null.
The following lemma states that the converse of infinite monkey theorem is actually true for regular languages.
We notice that there is a context-free language with density zero but having no forbidden word (the set of all palindromes, for example).

Lemma 5
.

Let
L
be a regular language.
The density of
L
is zero if and only if
L
has a forbidden word.

There are several proof strategies of Lemma
5
.
One can prove this by using the asymptotic formula of a rational series (
cf.
Theorem 9.8 of
[
22
]
) with the fact that the growth rate of the language of the form
\overline{A^{*}wA^{*}}
is strictly smaller than
\#\!\left(A\right)

[
10
]
.
A more direct and automata-theoretic proof can be found in
[
24
]
.
The simplest proof is the following strategy using Myhill–Nerode theorem due to Tsuboi–Takeuchi
[
30
]
.

Proof.

The “if” part is immediate as described.
To prove “only if” part by contraposition, we assume that
L
does not have any forbidden word as described above.
In this case, we have the equation
A^{*}=\bigcup_{(u,v)\in A^{*}\times A^{*}}u^{-1}Lv^{-1}
since any word can be obtained by taking a quotient of some word in
L
.
By Myhill–Nerode theorem, there is a finite set of pairs of words

F\subseteq A^{*}\times A^{*}
such that

A^{*}=\bigcup_{(u,v)\in A^{*}\times A^{*}}u^{-1}Lv^{-1}=\bigcup_{(u,v)\in F}u^{-1}Lv^{-1}
.
If the language
u^{-1}Lv^{-1}
is null for any
(u,v)\in F
, we can deduce that its finite union
\bigcup_{(u,v)\in F}u^{-1}Lv^{-1}
must be null since
\delta_{A}
is a finitely additive measure.
But this contradict with
\delta_{A}(A^{*})=\delta_{A}\left(\bigcup_{(u,v)\in F}u^{-1}Lv^{-1}\right)=1
.
Hence
u^{-1}Lv^{-1}
should have a non-zero density for some
(u,v)\in F
, which implies that
L
should have a non-zero density, too.
∎

Remark 6
.

Let
L
be a regular language,
M
be a finite monoid and

\eta\colon A^{*}\rightarrow M
be a surjective morphism such that
L=\eta^{-1}(\eta(L))
.
The
kernel

K
of
M
is a unique minimal ideal of
M
(it always exists since
M
is finite), i.e., the minimal
\mathrel{\cal J}
-class of
M
.
For every
m
not in the kernel
K
of
M
,

\eta^{-1}(m)\cap A^{*}wA^{*}=\emptyset
holds for any
w\in\eta^{-1}(K)
hence
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(m))=0
by the infinite monkey theorem.
This implies that
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(K))=1
.
Moreover,
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(K))=1
implies that there exist at least one element
n\in K
such that
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(n))>0
since
K
is finite.
Because
K
is the kernel of
M
, for any
m\in K
there exists a pair of
element
x,y\in M
such that
xny=m
, hence
\eta^{-1}(m)
contains
u\eta^{-1}(n)v
where
u\in\eta^{-1}(x)
and
v\in\eta^{-1}(y)
so that

\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(m))\geq\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(n))\cdot\#\!\left(A\right)^{-(|u|+|v|)}
by Lemma
3
, i.e.,
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(m))>0
holds for each
m\in K
.

For more detailed properties of the density function
\delta_{A}
, see Chapter 13 of
[
2
]
.

The notion of “measurability” on formal languages is defined by a standard measure theoretic way as follows.

Definition 7

(
[
25
]
)
.

Let
{\cal C}_{A}
be a family of languages over
A
.
For a language
L\subseteq A^{*}
, we define its

{\cal C}_{A}
-inner-density

\underline{\mu}_{{\cal C}_{A}}(L)

and
{\cal C}_{A}
-outer-density

\overline{\mu}_{{\cal C}_{A}}(L)

over
A
as

\displaystyle\underline{\mu}_{{\cal C}_{A}}(L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sup

\displaystyle\left\{\,\delta_{A}(K)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}K\subseteq L,K\in{\cal C}_{A},\delta_{A}(K)\neq\bot\,\right\}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{{\cal C}_{A}}(L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf

\displaystyle\left\{\,\delta_{A}(K)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}L\subseteq K,K\in{\cal C}_{A},\delta_{A}(K)\neq\bot\,\right\}\text{, respectively.}

A language
L
is said to be

{\cal C}_{A}
-measurable
if

\underline{\mu}_{{\cal C}_{A}}(L)=\overline{\mu}_{{\cal C}_{A}}(L)
holds.
We say that an infinite sequence
(L_{n})_{n}
of languages over
A

converges to

from inner (resp. from outer)
if

L_{n}\subseteq L
(resp.
L_{n}\supseteq L
) for each

and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\delta_{A}(L_{n})=\delta_{A}(L)
.
A language
L
is
{\cal C}_{A}
-measurable if and only if
there exist two convergent sequences of languages in
{\cal C}_{A}
to
L
from inner and outer, respectively.

The following is an example of
\mathsf{Gcom}
-measurable non-regular languages.
For more detailed examples of
{\cal C}
-measurable/immeasurable languages for a subclass
{\cal C}
of regular languages, see
[

,
26
,
29
]
.

Example 8

(
[
25
]
)
.

The language
E=\{w\in A^{*}\mid|w|_{a}=|w|_{b}\}
over
A=\{a,b\}
is
\mathsf{Gcom}
-measurable.
For each

, the language
L_{k}=\{w\in A^{*}\mid|w|_{a}\equiv|w|_{b}\,\,(\!\!\!\mod k)\}
is in
\mathsf{Gcom}

\eta^{-1}(0)=L_{k}
holds for the morphism

Obviously,

2.2
Local varieties and profinite equations

For a family
{\cal C}_{A}
of languages over
A
, we denote by

\mathrm{Ext}({\cal C}_{A})
(resp.
\mathrm{RExt}({\cal C}_{A})
) the class of all
{\cal C}_{A}
-measurable languages (resp.
{\cal C}_{A}
-measurable
regular
languages) over
A
.
A family of regular languages over
A
is called a
local variety
(
cf.
[
1
]
) over
A
if it is closed under
Boolean operations and left-and-right quotients.
All classes
\mathsf{GD}_{A},\mathsf{SF}_{A},\mathsf{Gcom}_{A}
, and
\mathsf{G}_{A}
are local varieties.
The notion of
{\cal C}_{A}
-measurability is well-behaved on Boolean operations and quotients as the following lemma says.

Lemma 9

(
[
26
]
)
.

The operator
\mathrm{Ext}
is a closure,
i.e., it satisfies the following three properties for each
{\cal C}_{A}\subseteq{\cal D}_{A}\subseteq 2^{A^{*}}
:
(extensive)
{\cal C}_{A}\subseteq\mathrm{Ext}({\cal C}_{A})
,
(monotone)
\mathrm{Ext}({\cal C}_{A})\subseteq\mathrm{Ext}({\cal D}_{A})
and
(idempotent)
\mathrm{Ext}(\mathrm{Ext}({\cal C}_{A}))=\mathrm{Ext}({\cal C}_{A})
.
Moreover,
if
{\cal C}_{A}
is a local variety, then
\mathrm{Ext}({\cal C}_{A})
is also a local variety.

Let
\mathsf{FIN}_{A}
be the class of all finite and co-finite languages over
A
.
Hereafter, we only consider an alphabet
A
such that
\#\!\left(A\right)\geq 2
(because
\mathsf{SF}_{A}=\mathsf{GD}_{A}=\mathsf{FIN}_{A}
and
\mathsf{G}_{A}=\mathsf{Gcom}_{A}
holds if
\#\!\left(A\right)=1
) and sometimes omit the subscript
A
for denoting these classes of languages.

The notion of local varieties and local pseudovarieties can be captured by an appropriate notion of “equation” called
profinite equations
(
cf.
[
18
]
).
For a pair of words
u
and
v
, we can define a distance between them by

d(u,v)=2^{-r(u,v)}

where
r(u,v)=\min\{\lvert{\cal A}\rvert\mid{\cal A}\text{ is a deterministic automaton accepting one of }\{u,v\}\text{ but not the other}\}
.
Intuitively,
r(u,v)
represents the minimum number of states for separating
u
and
v
by using deterministic automata.
Then this function
d
is an ultrametric on
A^{*}
, and the completion of the metric space
(A^{*},d)
, denoted by
\widehat{A^{*}}
, is called the set of profinite words over
A
.
An element of
\widehat{A^{*}}
is a Cauchy sequence of (usual finite) words over
A
with respect to
d
.
For example, the profinite word
w^{\omega}
, associated with every word
w
, can be defined as a limit of the sequence of words
(w^{n!})_{n\geq 1}
.
For any finite monoid
M
and morphism
\eta\colon A^{*}\rightarrow M
, it is easy to see that
\eta(w^{n!})
is the idempotent power
\eta(w)^{\omega}
of
\eta(w)
for sufficiently large
n
.
Hence, for a finite monoid
M
, the equation
w^{\omega+1}=w^{\omega}
can be interpreted as
x^{\omega+1}=x^{\omega}
holds for any
x\in M
,
i.e.
,

M
is aperiodic (=
\mathrel{\cal H}
-trivial).
It is known that any local variety of languages can be captured by using profinite equations and vice versa.
We use the following theorem to prove Theorem
20
in Section
5
.

Theorem 10

(
[
7
, Proposition 7.4]
)
.

A set of regular languages over
A
is a Boolean quotienting algebra (i.e., local variety)
if and only if it can be defined by a set of semigroup equations of the form
u=v
, where
u,v\in\widehat{A^{*}}
.

For any finite monoid
M
and morphism
\eta\colon A^{*}\to M
,
we can associate its extension
\hat{\eta}
defined as
\hat{\eta}((w_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\eta(w_{n})
where
(w_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
A^{*}
.
It is known that this
\hat{\eta}
is a unique
uniformly continuous extension
of
\eta
(for details, see
e.g.
,
[
16
, Chapter X, Proposition 2.10]
).
A regular language
L\subseteq A^{*}

satisfies
an equation
u=v
, where
u,v\in\widehat{A^{*}}
,
if its syntactic morphism
\eta\colon A^{*}\to M_{L}
satisfies
\hat{\eta}(u)=\hat{\eta}(v)
.

2.3
Local varieties of finite nilpotent/solvable groups

Here we introduce two kinds of restricted subclasses of group languages called
nilpotent
and
solvable
group languages.
These two subclasses of groups are natural extensions of the class of commutative groups and well-inverstigated in classical group theory (
cf.
[
6
]
).

Definition 11
.

For a positive integer
n
, define

\displaystyle\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,L\subseteq A^{*}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{$L$ is recognised by a finite nilpotent group of class $\leq n$}\,\right\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,L\subseteq A^{*}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{$L$ is recognised by a finite solvable group of derived length $\leq n$}\,\right\}.

Recall that a group
G
is called a nilpotent group of class
\leq n
if and only if for any
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2},\dotsc,g_{n}\in G
,
the equation
[[\dotsm[[g_{0},g_{1}],g_{2}]\dotsm],g_{n}]=1_{G}
holds,
where
[g,h]=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}
is the commutator of
g
and
h
.
Hence, for example,
\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq 2}
is a local variety defined by the set of equations of the form

(uvu^{\omega-1}v^{\omega-1})w(uvu^{\omega-1}v^{\omega-1})^{\omega-1}w^{\omega-1}=\varepsilon
for
u,v,w\in A^{*}
.
Similarly, each
\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n}
is also a local variety.
It is also known that each
\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n}
is defined by a set of equations,
therefore
\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n}
is also a local variety.

We notice that
\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq 1}=\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq 1}=\mathsf{Gcom}_{A}
.
An important fact is that these two kinds of subclasses form infinite strict hierarchies of group languages stated as follows (see Appendix for the detailed proof).
This fact is a key to prove Corollary
21
in Section
5
.

Proposition 12
.

Suppose
\#\!\left(A\right)\geq 2
.
For every
n\geq 1
, we have
\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n}\subsetneq\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n+1}
and
\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n}\subsetneq\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n+1}
.

3
Measuring Power of Star-Free Languages

In this section, we give a proof of the first main theorem, which gives a decidable characterisation of
\mathsf{SF}
-measurability for regular languages.

Theorem 13

(S.-Y.)
.

The class of star-free languages and the class of generalised definite languages have the same measuring power, i.e.,
\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{SF})=\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{GD})
.

To prove Theorem
13
, we require some lemmata, where Lemma
16
is the key of our proof.

Lemma 14

(
[
28
]
)
.

Every regular language whose density is zero or one is
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable.

Lemma 15
.

Let
{\cal C}\subseteq\mathrm{REG}_{A}
be a class of regular languages closed under Boolean operations,
M
be a finite monoid,
\alpha\colon A^{*}\rightarrow M
be a monoid morphism, and
L\subseteq A^{*}
be a language such that
L=\alpha^{-1}(\alpha(L))
.
If there is a convergent sequence of languages in
{\cal C}
to
\alpha^{-1}(m)
from inner for every
m\in M
, then
L
is
{\cal C}
-measurable.

Proof.

For each
m\in M
, let
(L_{m,n})_{n}
be a convergent sequence of languages in
{\cal C}
to
\alpha^{-1}(m)
from inner.
Since
{\cal C}
is closed under finite union,
the sequence
(\bigcup_{m\in\alpha(L)}L_{m,n})_{n}
is a convergent sequence of languages in
{\cal C}
to
L
from inner.
Since
{\cal C}
is closed under finite intersection and complementation,
the sequence
(\bigcap_{m\not\in\alpha(L)}L_{m,n}^{\mathrm{c}})_{n}
is a convergent sequence of languages in
{\cal C}
to
L
from outer.
∎

Lemma 16
.

Let
L
be a regular language and
M=M_{L}
be its syntactic monoid.
If every
\mathrel{\cal H}
-class in the kernel of
M
is trivial, then

L
is
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable.

Proof.

Let
\eta\colon A^{*}\rightarrow M
be the syntactic morphism of
L
.
Let
K
be the kernel of
M
.
If
m\not\in K
, then the infinite monkey theorem implies

\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(m))=0
hence
\eta^{-1}(m)
is
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable by Lemma
14
.
Let
m\in M
be an element in
K
.
In this case, we have
\mathrel{\cal H}_{m}=mM\cap Mm
.
Since
K
is
\mathrel{\cal H}
-trivial by the assumption, this implies

\mathrel{\cal H}_{m}=mM\cap Mm=\{m\}
.
For the language
L_{m}=\eta^{-1}(m)
, we have

L_{m}=\eta^{-1}(mM\cap Mm)=\eta^{-1}(mM)\cap\eta^{-1}(Mm).

Let
w\in\eta^{-1}(K)
.
If a word
u\in A^{*}
contains
w
as a factor (
i.e.
,
u\in A^{*}wA^{*}
), then
\eta(u)\in K
and
\eta(uA^{*})=\eta(u)M
is a minimal right ideal.
Notice that the set of all minimal right ideals is a partition of the kernel

K=\bigsqcup_{n\in K/\mathrel{\cal R}}nM
where
\bigsqcup
denotes the disjoint union.
For each
\ell\geq 0
, we define a generalised definite language
L_{\ell}
as

L_{\ell}=\bigsqcup\left\{\,uA^{*}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}u\in A^{\ell},\eta(uA^{*})\subseteq mM\,\right\}.

The inclusion
L_{\ell}\subseteq\eta^{-1}(mM)
is obvious.
We can show that
L_{\ell}
converges to
\eta^{-1}(mM)
from inner as follows.

\displaystyle\,\,\delta_{A}\left(\eta^{-1}(mM)\setminus L_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\,\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(mM))-\delta_{A}\left(\bigsqcup\{uA^{*}\mid u\in A^{\ell},\eta(uA^{*})\subseteq mM\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,\sum_{n\in K/\mathrel{\cal R}}\left(\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(nM))-\delta_{A}\left(\bigsqcup\{uA^{*}\mid u\in A^{\ell},\eta(uA^{*})\subseteq nM\}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\,\sum_{n\in K/\mathrel{\cal R}}\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(nM))-\sum_{n\in K/\mathrel{\cal R}}\delta_{A}\left(\bigsqcup\{uA^{*}\mid u\in A^{\ell},\eta(uA^{*})\subseteq nM\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\,\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(K))-\delta_{A}\left(\bigsqcup\{uA^{*}\mid u\in A^{\ell},\eta(uA^{*})\subseteq nM\text{ for some }n\in K/\mathrel{\cal R}\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\,1-\delta_{A}\left(\bigsqcup\{uA^{*}\mid u\in A^{\ell}\cap A^{*}wA^{*}\}\right)

where the last term tends to zero as
\ell
tends to infinity, since

\delta_{A}\left(\bigsqcup\{uA^{*}\mid u\in A^{\ell}\cap A^{*}wA^{*}\}\right)=\frac{\#\!\left(A^{\ell}\cap A^{*}wA^{*}\right)}{\#\!\left(A^{\ell}\right)}

tends to one as
\ell
tends to infinity by the infinite monkey theorem.
By the same argument, there is a convergent sequence
(L^{\prime}_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
of generalised definite languages to
\eta^{-1}(Mm)
from inner.
This means that the sequence
(L_{\ell}\cap L^{\prime}_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}

of generalised definite languages converges to
\eta^{-1}(mM)\cap\eta^{-1}(Mm)=L_{m}
from inner.
We showed that, for every
m\in M
,
there is a convergent sequence of generalised definite languages to
L_{m}=\eta^{-1}(m)
from inner.
Hence
L
is
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable by Lemma
15
because
\mathsf{GD}
is closed under Boolean operations.
∎

Proof of Theorem
13
.

Since we have
\mathsf{GD}\subseteq\mathsf{SF}
, it suffices to show that

\mathsf{SF}\subseteq\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{GD})
, which implies

\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{GD})\subseteq\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{SF})\subseteq\mathrm{Ext}(\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{GD}))=\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{GD})
by Lemma
9
.
The syntactic monoid of any star-free language is
\mathrel{\cal H}
-trivial, hence
\mathsf{SF}\subseteq\mathrm{Ext}(\mathsf{GD})
holds by Lemma
16
.
∎

Actually, the converse direction of Lemma
16
holds. The following theorem gives an algebraic characterisation of
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable (
=

\mathsf{SF}
-measurable) regular languages.
This theorem can be considered as a natural extension of Schützenberger’s theorem stating that
L
is star-free if and only if its syntactic monoid is
\mathrel{\cal H}
-trivial.

Theorem 17

(S.)
.

Let
L
be a regular language and
M=M_{L}
be its syntactic monoid.
Then
L
is
\mathsf{GD}
-measurable if and only if the kernel of
M
is
\mathrel{\cal H}
-trivial, i.e., every
\mathrel{\cal H}
-class in the kernel of
M
is trivial.

Proof.

We show the “only if” part by contraposition.
Let
K
be the kernel of
M
.
Suppose that some
\mathrel{\cal H}
-class in the kernel of
M
is not trivial.
This implies that, there is some
k\geq 2
such that
\#\!\left(\mathrel{\cal H}_{m}\right)=k
for every
m\in K
since every
\mathrel{\cal H}
-class in
K
is a group and all these groups are isomorphic (
cf.
Chapter V, Section 4.1 of
[
16
]
).
If
\delta_{A}(L)=0
, then
K
should be trivial by Lemma
5
(the syntactic image of any forbidden word is
0
the zero element and hence
K=\{0\}
).
Hence we may assume that
\delta_{A}(L)>0
.
Let
\eta\colon A^{*}\rightarrow M
be the syntactic morphism of
L
and let
P=\eta(L)
.
Because
\delta_{A}(L)>0
and the infinite monkey theorem,
P
and
K
have a non-empty intersection and hence we can pick some element
m\in P\cap K
.
By the assumption there is some
n\in\mathrel{\cal H}_{m}
such that
m\neq n
.
Since
M
is the syntactic monoid of
L
, it is known that

P
is
disjunctive subset
of
M
(
cf.
Proposition 10.3.3 of
[
11
]
),
i.e.
,
for each
a,b\in M
, if

cad\in P\Leftrightarrow cbd\in P
for any pair
c,d\in M
, then
a=b
.
Hence
m\neq n
implies
m^{\prime}=cmd\in P
but
n^{\prime}=cnd\notin P
for some
c,d\in M
.
Now we show that
uA^{*}v\subseteq L
implies

uA^{*}v\cap\eta^{-1}(m^{\prime})=\emptyset
for any pair of words
u
and
v
.
This concludes that, every generalised definite subset
K
of
L
satisfies
\delta_{A}(K)\leq\delta_{A}(L)-\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(m^{\prime}))<\delta_{A}(L)
(notice that
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(m^{\prime}))>0
as stated in Remark
6
),
i.e.
,
L
is
\mathsf{GD}
-immeasurable.

Assume
uA^{*}v\cap\eta^{-1}(m^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
holds for some pair of words
u
and
v
.
Then
\eta(uwv)=m^{\prime}
for some
w\in A^{*}
.
Since
m^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathrel{\cal H}}n^{\prime}
, there is some
x\in M
such that
xm^{\prime}=n^{\prime}
.
Let
w_{x}\in\eta^{-1}(x)
.
Then the word
u(wvu)^{k-1}wvw_{x}uwv\in uA^{*}v
satisfies

\eta(u(wvu)^{k-1}wvw_{x}uwv)=\eta(uwv)^{k}\eta(w_{x})\eta(uwv)=(m^{\prime})^{k}xm^{\prime}=n^{\prime}.

Hence
uA^{*}v\cap\eta^{-1}(n^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
, which implies
uA^{*}v\not\subseteq L
since
\eta^{-1}(n^{\prime})\cap L=\emptyset
.
∎

4
Probabilistic Independence of Star-Free and Group Languages

In probability theory, two events
A
and
B
are said to be independent
if their joint probability equals the product of their probabilities:

P(A\cap B)=P(A)\cdot P(B)
.
One important application of Theorem
13
is the following probabilistic independence of star-free and group languages, which emphasises how these two language classes are orthogonal.

Theorem 18

(Y.-S.)
.

For any star-free language
L
and any group language
K
,
we have

\delta_{A}(L\cap K)=\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\delta_{A}(K).

The probabilistic independence of generalised definite languages and group languages is proved more easily; we use it as a lemma for proving Theorem
18
.

Lemma 19
.

For any generalised definite language
L
of the form
uA^{*}v
for some
u,v\in A^{*}
and any group language
K
,
we have

\delta_{A}(L\cap K)=\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\delta_{A}(K).

Proof.

Let
L=uA^{*}v
be a generalised definite language, and
K
be a group language such that
K=\eta^{-1}(S)
for some finite group
G
, where
S
is a subset of
G
and
\eta\colon A^{*}\rightarrow G
is a surjective morphism.
Observe that
L\cap K=u(u^{-1}Kv^{-1})v
holds.
Since we have
u^{-1}Kv^{-1}=u^{-1}\eta^{-1}(S)v^{-1}=\eta^{-1}(\eta(u)^{-1}S\eta(v)^{-1})
,
we can conclude that

\delta_{A}(L\cap K)=\delta_{A}(u(\eta^{-1}(\eta(u)^{-1}S\eta(v)^{-1}))v)=\#\!\left(A\right)^{-|uv|}\cdot\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(\eta(u)^{-1}S\eta(v)^{-1}))=\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\delta_{A}(K)

by Lemma
3
and Lemma
4
since

\#\!\left(S\right)=\#\!\left(\eta(u)^{-1}S\eta(v)^{-1}\right)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
18
.

Let
L
be a star-free language and
K
be a group language.
We only show that
\delta_{A}(L\cap K)\geq\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\delta_{A}(K)
holds;
the reverse inequality
\delta_{A}(L\cap K)\leq\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\delta_{A}(K)
is also proved in the same way.
By Theorem
13
, there is a sequence
M_{n}=(\bigcup_{(u,v)\in F_{n}}uA^{*}v)_{n\geq 0}
of generalised definite languages convergent to
L
from inner, where
F_{n}\subseteq A^{*}\times A^{*}
is finite for each
n
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
uA^{*}v\cap u^{\prime}A^{*}v^{\prime}=\emptyset
for each different pairs
(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in F
.
Then we have

\delta_{A}(L\cap K)\geq\delta_{A}\left(\bigcup_{(u,v)\in F_{n}}uA^{*}v\cap K\right)=\sum_{(u,v)\in F_{n}}\delta_{A}(uA^{*}v)\cdot\delta_{A}(K)=\delta_{A}(M_{n})\cdot\delta_{A}(K)

by Lemma
19
where
\delta_{A}(M_{n})
tends to
\delta_{A}(L)
as
n
tends to infinity.
Thus
\delta_{A}(L\cap K)\geq\delta_{A}(L)\cdot\delta_{A}(K)
.
∎

5
Hierarchies of Group Languages Are Not Collapsed by
\mathrm{RExt}

The main result of this section is Corollary
21
,
which is a consequence of Theorem
20
below and states that
the classes of regular languages approximable by nilpotent/solvable group languages form strict hierarchies.
This is completely different from the situation in Theorem
13
,
which shows that the dot-depth hierarchy is collapsed by
\mathrm{RExt}
.

Theorem 20

(Y.)
.

If
{\cal C}\subseteq\mathsf{G}_{A}
is a local variety of group languages, then
\mathrm{RExt}({\cal C})\cap\mathsf{G}_{A}={\cal C}
.
In particular, if
{\cal C}\subsetneq{\cal D}\subseteq\mathsf{G}_{A}
are local subvarieties,
then
\mathrm{RExt}({\cal C})\cap{\cal D}={\cal C}
,
{\cal D}\not\subseteq\mathrm{RExt}({\cal C})
, and
\mathrm{RExt}({\cal C})\subsetneq\mathrm{RExt}({\cal D})
.

Our previous result
[
29
, Theorem 1]
is the special case of Theorem
20
where
{\cal C}=\mathsf{Gcom}_{A}
.

Proof.

Let
L\in\mathsf{G}_{A}\setminus{\cal C}
.
It suffices to show that
L
is
{\cal C}
-immeasurable.
Since
{\cal C}
is a local variety and
L\notin{\cal C}
, Theorem
10
implies
that there exists an equation
u=v
(where
u,v\in\widehat{A^{*}}
) that every language in
{\cal C}
satisfies but
L
does not.
Let
G
be the syntactic monoid of
L
, which is a group, and
\eta\colon A^{*}\to G
be the syntactic morphism of
L
.

We claim that there exists
w\in\widehat{A^{*}}
such that the equation
w=\varepsilon
is equivalent to
u=v
on any
A
-generated finite group.
Indeed, if
(u_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
are Cauchy sequences in
A^{*}
converging to
u
and
v
in
\widehat{A^{*}}
respectively,
then the Cauchy sequence
(u_{n}v_{n}^{n!-1})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
satisfies the claim.

Hence, for every
L_{1}\in{\cal C}
and its syntactic morphism
\theta_{1}\colon A^{*}\to G_{1}
,
we have
\hat{\theta}_{1}(w)=\hat{\theta}_{1}(\varepsilon)=1_{G_{1}}
and
\hat{\eta}(w)\neq\hat{\eta}(\varepsilon)=1_{G}
.
Since
\hat{\eta}(w)\neq 1_{G}
and
G
is the syntactic monoid of
L
, there exist
x,y\in A^{*}

such that

\eta(x)\hat{\eta}(w)\eta(y)\in\eta(L)\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}\eta(xy)\in\eta(L).

(1)

Since the two languages
\eta^{-1}(\eta(x)),\eta^{-1}(\eta(y))\subseteq A^{*}
are group languages,
they are non-null by Lemma
4
.
In particular, there should be some word
w\in\eta^{-1}(\eta(x))
, and hence
\eta^{-1}(\eta(x))\eta^{-1}(\eta(y))\supseteq w\eta^{-1}(\eta(y))
should have a positive density by Lemma
3
.
Hence the concatenation
I=\eta^{-1}(\eta(x))\eta^{-1}(\eta(y))
is non-null.
To complete the proof, it suffices to show that
L_{1},L_{2}\in{\cal C}
and
L_{1}\subseteq L\subseteq L_{2}
implies
I\subseteq L_{2}\setminus L_{1}
.
Let
\theta_{1}\colon A^{*}\to G_{1}
and
\theta_{2}\colon A^{*}\to G_{2}
be the syntactic morphism of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, respectively.
Let
(w_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
be a Cauchy sequence in
A^{*}
converging to
w
in
\widehat{A^{*}}
.
Then we have
\eta(w_{k})=\hat{\eta}(w)
,
\theta_{1}(w_{k})=\hat{\theta}_{1}(w)
, and
\theta_{2}(w_{k})=\hat{\theta}_{2}(w)
for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm Nature}
.

We show
I\subseteq L_{2}\setminus L_{1}
.
Let
z\in I
.
Then there exist
\tilde{x}\in\eta^{-1}(\eta(x))
and
\tilde{y}\in\eta^{-1}(\eta(y))
such that
z=\tilde{x}\tilde{y}
.
Let
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Since
\theta_{i}(w_{k})=1_{G_{i}}
and
G_{i}
is the syntactic monoid of
L_{i}
,
we have
\theta_{i}(\tilde{x}w_{k}\tilde{y})\in\theta_{i}(L_{i})\iff\theta_{i}(\tilde{x}\tilde{y})\in\theta_{i}(L_{i})
,

i.e.
,

z=\tilde{x}\tilde{y}\in L_{i}\iff\tilde{x}w_{k}\tilde{y}\in L_{i}.

(2)

It follows from (
1
),
\eta(\tilde{x})=\eta(x)
,
\eta(\tilde{y})=\eta(y)
, and
\hat{\eta}(w)=\eta(w_{k})
that

z=\tilde{x}\tilde{y}\in L\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}\tilde{x}w_{k}\tilde{y}\in L.

(3)

We show
z\in L_{2}\setminus L_{1}
by considering two cases depending on whether
z\in L
or
z\notin L
.
First, suppose that
z\in L
.
Since
L\subseteq L_{2}
, we have
z\in L_{2}
.
On the other hand, by (
3
), we know that
\tilde{x}w_{k}\tilde{y}\notin L\supseteq L_{1}
, which implies
\tilde{x}w_{k}\tilde{y}\notin L_{1}
.
Then, by (
2
), it follows that
z\notin L_{1}
, and thus we obtain
z\in L_{2}\setminus L_{1}
.
The case where
z\notin L
can be proved in a similar manner.
∎

Corollary 21
.

Suppose
\#\!\left(A\right)\geq 2
.
For
n\geq 1
, we have

•

\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n})\subsetneq\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n+1})\subsetneq\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{G}_{A})
, and

•

\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n})\subsetneq\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n+1})\subsetneq\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{G}_{A})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
12
and Theorem
20
.
∎

6
Conclusion and Open Problems

Theorem
13
and our previous work
[
28
,
9
]
give a decidable characterisation of the
\mathsf{SF}
-measurable regular languages.
Its algebraic characterisation (Theorem
17
) can be considered as a natural extension of Schützenberger’s theorem for star-free languages.
The decidability of the
\mathsf{G}
-measurability for regular languages is still open.
Perhaps the profinite method can be useful to solve this problem.
The probabilistic independence of star-free and group languages describes how these two classes are orthogonal.

In 2023, the first author posed a question whether the equation

\mathrm{REG}={\mathscr{B}}(\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{SF})\cup\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{G}))
holds or not, at some domestic workshops in Japan.
If this kind of equation
were
true, it can be considered as a “measure-theoretic decomposition theorem” of regular languages.
Later, the second author claimed that a weaker equation

\mathrm{REG}=\mathrm{RExt}({\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{SF}\cup\mathsf{G}))
holds and
this result was announced at the international conferences Highlights 2024 by the first author.
However, after some discussion between these two authors, a flaw was found in the proof of
\mathrm{REG}=\mathrm{RExt}({\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{SF}\cup\mathsf{G}))
.
Actually, this equation does
not
hold as follows.

Proposition 22
.

Let
{\cal T}_{4}
be the full transformation monoid on the four elements set
\{1,2,3,4\}
whose identity element is the identity function
\mathrm{id}
.
Define
e,f\in{\cal T}_{4}
as

\displaystyle e\colon 1\mapsto 1,2\mapsto 2,3\mapsto 1,4\mapsto 2\quad\text{ and }\quad f\colon 1\mapsto 4,2\mapsto 3,3\mapsto 3,4\mapsto 4.

Let
M
be the submonoid of
{\cal T}_{4}
generated by
e
and
f
.
For an alphabet
A=\{a,b\}
, define a morphism
\eta\colon A^{*}\rightarrow M
as
\eta(a)=e
and
\eta(b)=f
.
Then
\eta^{-1}(e)
is
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{SF}_{A}\cup\mathsf{G}_{A})
-immeasurable.
In particular, we have
\mathrm{REG}_{A}\supsetneq\mathrm{RExt}_{A}({\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{SF}_{A}\cup\mathsf{G}_{A}))
.

Proof.

By simple calculation, one can easily observe that

e^{2}=e,f^{2}=f,efefe=e,fefef=f
hold so that

M=\{\mathrm{id},e,ef,efe,efef,f,fe,fef,fefe\}
.
The kernel of
M
is
M\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}
and

\mathrel{\cal H}_{e}=\{e,efe\}
and
\mathrel{\cal H}_{f}=\{f,fef\}
are maximal subgroups of
M
, respectively.

Thanks to Theorem
13
, we have

\mathrm{RExt}_{A}({\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{SF}_{A}\cup\mathsf{G}_{A}))=\mathrm{RExt}_{A}({\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{GD}_{A}\cup\mathsf{G}_{A}))
.
Notice again that
\delta_{A}(\eta^{-1}(e))>0
holds as stated in Remark
6
.
Let
L\in\mathsf{GD}_{A}
and
K\in\mathsf{G}_{A}
.
To show that
\eta^{-1}(e)
is
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{GD}_{A}\cup\mathsf{G}_{A})
-immeasurable, it is enough to show that
L\cap K\subseteq\eta^{-1}(e)
implies
\delta_{A}(L\cap K)=0
(because this condition implies there is no convergent sequence of languages in
{\mathscr{B}}(\mathsf{GD}_{A}\cup\mathsf{G}_{A})
to
\eta^{-1}(e)
from inner).
We actually show that
L\cap K\subseteq\eta^{-1}(e)
implies
L\cap K
is a finite set.

Assume
L\cap K\subseteq\eta^{-1}(e)
and assume that

L\cap K
is infinite.
Since
L\in\mathsf{GD}_{A}
, there is some constant
N
such that,
for any word
u,v\in A^{*}
with
|u|,|v|>N
,

uwv\in L\Leftrightarrow uw^{\prime}v\in L
for any pair of words

w
and
w^{\prime}
.
By the assumption,
L\cap K\subseteq\eta^{-1}(e)
is infinite hence there is a pair of words
u
and
v
such that
uv\in L\cap K
,
|u|,|v|>N
,
\eta(u)\in eM
and
\eta(v)\in Me
.
Since
K
is a group language, there is a finite group
G
and a morphism
\varphi\colon A^{*}\rightarrow G
such that

K=\varphi^{-1}(\varphi(K))
.
Since
\eta(u)\in eM=\{e,ef,efe,efef\}
and
\eta(v)\in Me=\{fefe,efe,fe,e\}
,
one can choose a word
w=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in A^{*}
so that
\eta(u)\eta(w)\eta(v)=efe
.
(In fact, we may assume
k\leq 2
.)
Let
n=\#\!\left(G\right)
and
\widetilde{w}=a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{n}\cdots a_{k}^{n}
.
Since
\eta(a)=e
and
\eta(b)=f
are both idempotents, we have

\eta(u\widetilde{w}v)=\eta(u)\eta(\widetilde{w})\eta(v)=\eta(u)\eta(w)\eta(v)=efe

thus
u\widetilde{w}v
is not in
\eta^{-1}(e)
.
On the other hand, we have
u\widetilde{w}v\in L
since
|u|,|v|>N
and
uv\in L
.
But
\varphi(u\widetilde{w}v)=\varphi(uv)
holds by the definition of
\widetilde{w}
which implies

u\widetilde{w}v\in\varphi^{-1}(\varphi(uv))\subseteq K
.
This means that
L\cap K
contains the word
u\widetilde{w}v
which is not contained in
\eta^{-1}(e)
, which contradicts with the assumption that
L\cap K\subseteq\eta^{-1}(e)
.
∎

The authors are still interested in whether there is a suitable notion
{\cal O}\colon 2^{A^{*}}\rightarrow 2^{A^{*}}
of “operation” on languages such that the equation like
\mathrm{REG}={\cal O}(\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{SF})\cup\mathrm{RExt}(\mathsf{G}))
or
\mathrm{REG}=\mathrm{RExt}({\cal O}(\mathsf{SF}\cup\mathsf{G}))
holds.
It must be richer than the Boolean closure operator
{\mathscr{B}}
, but might be simpler than the operation on languages corresponding to the wreath product operation on monoids.

∎

Lemma 24
.

For
n\geq 1
, there is a two-generated finite solvable group of derived length
n
.

To prove Lemma
24
, we use the following embedding theorem due to Neumann-Neumann,
and Lemmata
26
and
27
below.

Theorem 25

(
[
14
, Theorem 5.6]
)
.

Every finite solvable group
G
of derived length
n
can be embedded in
some two-generated finite solvable group
H
of derived length at most
n+2
.

Lemma 26

(
[
21
, 9.23 Corollary]
)
.

For each
n\geq 1
, there exists a finite solvable group of derived length
n
.

Lemma 27
.

If
G
is a solvable group of derived length
n+1
, then some factor group of
G
is solvable of derived length
n
.

Proof.

Let
G=G^{(0)}\rhd G^{(1)}\rhd\dotsb\rhd G^{(n)}\rhd G^{(n+1)}=\{1_{G}\}
be the derived series of
G
,
where
G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]
.
Since each
G^{(i+1)}
is a characteristic subgroup of
G^{(i)}
, the group
G^{(n)}
is also a characteristic subgroup of
G
,
hence in particular a normal subgroup of each
G^{(i)}
.
Let
\bar{G}=G/G^{(n)}
.
Then
G^{(0)}/G^{(n)}\rhd G^{(1)}/G^{(n)}\rhd\dotsb\rhd G^{(n)}/G^{(n)}=\{1\}
is the derived series of
\bar{G}
of length
n
,
since we have an isomorphism
[G^{(i)}/G^{(n)},G^{(i)}/G^{(n)}]\cong[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]/G^{(n)}=G^{(i+1)}/G^{(n)}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
24
.

By Lemma
26
, there exists a finite solvable group
G
of derived length
n
.
By Theorem
25
,

G
can be embedded in some two-generated finite group
H
of derived length at most
n+2
.
In particular, the derived length of
H
is one of
n
,
n+1
, or
n+2
.
Thus, by Lemma

,
we obtain a two-generated finite solvable group of derived length
n
as a factor group of

.
∎

Lemma
28
below is the last piece of the proof of Proposition

.

Lemma 28

(folklore,
e.g.
,
[
8
, 3.4.9 Proposition]
)
.

Let
G
be a group and
\eta\colon A^{*}\to G
be a surjective morphism.
Then the syntactic monoid of the language
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})\subseteq A^{*}
is isomorphic to
G
.

The language
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})
is known as the
word problem
for the group
G
(over
A
).

Proof of Proposition
12
.

By Lemma
23
, there exists a two-generated finite nilpotent group
G
of class
n+1
.
Since
G
is finite and generated by two elements as a group, it is generated by the two elements as a monoid.
Hence there exists a surjective morphism
\eta\colon A^{*}\to G
.
Since
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})
is clearly recognised by
G
, we have
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})\in\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n+1}
.
Contrary, suppose
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})\in\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n}
.
Then
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})
is recognised by some finite nilpotent group
H
of class
n
,
hence the syntactic monoid of
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})
, which is isomorphic to
G
by Lemma
28
, divides
H
.
In general, if
K
is a nilpotent group of class
c
,
then every subgroup of
K
and every factor group of
K
is nilpotent of class at most
c
.
Hence
G
is nilpotent of class at most
n
, contradiction.
(Note that every submonoid of a group is a subgroup.)
Thus
\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n}\subsetneq\mathsf{Gnil}_{A}^{\leq n+1}
.

By Lemma
24
, there exists a two-generated finite solvable group
G
of derived length
n+1
.
Then one can similarly show
\eta^{-1}(1_{G})\in\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n+1}\setminus\mathsf{Gsol}_{A}^{\leq n}
for a surjective morphism
\eta\colon A^{*}\to G
.
∎
