The Effectiveness of Educational Intervention in Promoting Preventive Behaviors of Dengue Fever in Southern Iran: Applying Health Belief Model (HBM)
Authors: Asiyeh Yari, Shokrollah Mohseni, Roghayeh Ezati Rad, Zahra Hosseini, Nahid Shahabi, Teamur Aghamolaei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDiseases transmittable by Aedes mosquito affect the health of millions of people every year, however, they can be effectively prevented. This study aimed to apply the Health Belief Model (HBM) in promoting preventive behaviors against dengue fever in southern Iran.MethodsIn this quasi‐experimental study, 320 people were randomly selected through multi‐stage cluster sampling in 2022 (161 in the intervention group and 159 in the control group). The data were collected using a researcher‐made questionnaire based on the HBM constructs before and 1 month after an educational intervention. Content validity index (CVI), content validity ratio (CVR), and Cronbach alpha coefficient were used for psychometric evaluation of the questionnaire. The educational intervention was made on a daily basis in a WhatsApp group in August and September 2022. Descriptive and inferential statistical tests were used for data analysis.ResultsAfter the educational intervention, the mean score increased in the constructs of behavior (38.42 ± 1.54), knowledge (10.77 ± 0.98), attitude (24.30 ± 1.59), perceived susceptibility (20.05 ± 1.42), perceived severity (21.31 ± 2.01), perceived benefits (28.00 ± 2.16), self‐efficacy (14.77 ± 0.51) of the intervention group. Moreover, after the intervention, the scores of perceived barriers (8.96 ± 1.31) decreased in the intervention group (p < 0.05). The analysis of covariance showed education had a statistically significant effect on HBM constructs.ConclusionsThe participants' knowledge about preventive behavior of dengue fever increased. They managed to remove the barriers to healthy behavior and began to show the desired preventive behavior with the self‐efficacy they gained; thus, the educational intervention based on the HBM can improve the behaviors to preventi of dengue fever, which is transmitted by the Aedes mosquito.

Introduction

The Aedes mosquito spreads arboviruses causing diseases like dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and yellow fever. This mosquito affects many people worldwide as it can expand to new areas [1, 2, 3]. In particular, arboviruses are mostly transmitted by female Aedes aegypti mosquitoes and sometimes by female Aedes albopictus mosquitoes [1, 3]. Dengue fever cases have increased significantly worldwide within the last two decades. A recent estimate indicates that as many as 390 million DENV infections occur each year and that many infected individuals are asymptomatic or subclinical [4]. With regard to dengue infection, 128 countries on a global scale are deemed to be in peril, wherein the Asian continent bears the brunt of the burden, accounting for 70% of the total global incidence. The incidence of dengue fever cases that were reported to the World Health Organization (WHO) exhibited a sharp escalation, surging from less than half a million in the year 2000 to over 3.34 million in 2016. This upsurge was marked by a global outbreak [5]. Millions of people show clinical symptoms of the disease, which include [6] high fever (39 to 40 Celsius degrees), headache, prostration, arthralgia, anorexia, asthenia, nausea, and so on. More severe cases of the disease can cause bleeding, shock and even death [5].

The female mosquito needs hematophagy for egg maturation. If the mosquito is infected, the virus is transmitted to humans during this process. The lifespan of a female mosquito is approximately 45 days [7]. Invasive mosquito species have adapted to the human environment by using artificial containers as larval habitats (such as tires, rainwater barrels, and fishing ponds) and have expanded their invasive range from tropical regions to other areas [8]. The increase in cargo transportation in ports and climate change contribute to the global spread of this mosquito [9, 10, 11].

Currently, there is no specific treatment for Aedes‐borne infections and only supportive care is available for symptoms such as headache, seizures, and management of fever and preservation of vital organs as coping strategies [12]. To control the spread of infections transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, common methods focus on human behavior as hosts and the interactions between humans and Aedes mosquitoes [13]. One way to fight Aedes mosquitoes is through the use of chemicals targeting immature stages (eggs, larvae, and pupae) as well as adults; however, these substances can adversely affect human health and the environment and may cause resistance in several vector species, such as carbamates and pyrethroids, which have shown resistance in Aedes aegypti [14, 15]. Another effective approach is to eliminate the aquatic habitats of Aedes mosquito larvae in urban environments. This includes targeting containers such as plastic bins, septic tanks, discarded tires, underground garbage bins, tree holes, and water barrels. Educating the public about proper waste disposal and water management can significantly aid in this effort [16, 17]. The use of models that identify and strengthen the effective factors in behavior can increase the effectiveness of education. One of these models is the health belief model (HBM). It is widely used for understanding health behaviors and can guide health promotion programs [18, 19]. HBM has been used to understand and influence Aedes mosquito prevention behaviors, particularly in the context of dengue fever [20, 21].

HBM considers behavior as a function of individuals' knowledge and attitude. It is formulated based on the idea that one's perceived health threat can lead to the adoption of healthy behaviors. One's knowledge along with perceived susceptibility and severity, and perceived benefits and barriers and cues for action altogether can lead to a change in preventive behaviors [22, 23].

HBM constructs include perceived susceptibility (the extent to which one is prone to being bitten), perceived severity (one's perception of the severity and complications of getting sick and being bitten), perceived benefits (one's assessment of the benefits of desired preventive behaviors in risk reduction), perceived barriers (one's assessment of the barriers to mosquito bite preventive measures), self‐efficacy (one's perception of the ability to perform mosquito bite preventive measures) as well as cues for action guidelines (having the incentive to perform mosquito bite preventive care) [24].

The presence of Aedes mosquito in some cities of Hormozgan province has been confirmed, including Bandar Abbas [25]. Studies have shown the importance of international exchanges in the spread of Aedes mosquitoes in commercial ports in Japan [9], Sudan [26] and Iran [27, 28].

Therefore, due to the fact that Bandar Abbas is the largest commercial port in Iran, this port is prone to the spread of the Aedes mosquito and concomitant diseases. Bandar Abbas has a tropical climate that provides suitable conditions for the spread of Aedes mosquito. Also, most Iranian studies were cross‐sectional studies that investigated mosquitoes with entomological monitoring [29, 30]; KAP (Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices) studies regarding mosquitoes and the diseases they transmit [31]; or examining the spread of these mosquitoes [32]. Our literature review found no studies in Iran that implemented interventions based on health education models. Considering the above issues and the importance of education in promoting preventive behaviors against dengue fever This study was application of the HBM to promoting preventive behaviors against dengue fever in Citizens of southern Iran. The present findings can provide solutions for policymakers and health system planners to prevent this disease.

Methods

Design of Study

The present quasi‐experimental study involved a pretest and a posttest for two groups, an intervention and a control. It was conducted on women and men visiting the health centers in Bandar Abbas in 2022.

Participants

The research population consisted of individuals aged 15 years and older. The study participants were selected from health service centers in Bandar Abbas. The study population was people who were covered by health service centers. The inclusion criteria were residence in Bandar Abbas, access to the Internet and WhatsApp, and informed consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were failure to receive educational content, and unwillingness to continue participation.

Since no study has been conducted regarding the impact of training on the structures of the HBM model and preventive behaviors of diseases transmitted by the Aedes mosquito in Iran, a pilot study was conducted with a sample size of 30 people in each group. The mean and standard deviation of the scores of the HBM model constructs were extracted. The health center personnel, who had previously received training, were selected as the intervention group, while the general public, who had not received any planned training, were selected as the control group. The scores of the HBM model structures were then extracted, and the sample size was calculated for each structure. The mean and standard deviation of the perceived susceptibility structure in the intervention group were 22 ± 8.79, while in the control group, they were 13 ± 10.31. Using a first‐type error of 0.05, a second‐type error of 0.2, and a significant difference of 2 points between the two groups, the sample size was calculated using the following formula: n=(z1−α2+z1−β)2(δ12+δ12)(μ1−μ2)2=(1.96+0.84)2+(10.31+8.79)232=162 the participants' presence was optional until the end of the study.

Sampling Method

The sampling method employed was a two‐stage cluster sampling approach. There are 20 comprehensive health centers in Bandar Abbas. Each health center in Bandar Abbas was considered as a cluster. Persian Gulf comprehensive health center was randomly selected as the intervention group and Seyed Mozaffar comprehensive health center as the control group. In each intervention and control centers, the people registered in the center, after meeting inclusion criteria included in the study available sampling. Finally, 161 participants remained in the intervention group and 159 in the control group.

Data Collection

The data were collected using a researcher‐made questionnaire comprised of two parts:

Part1: The demographic variables. It included six questions about age, sex, marital status, level of education, job and household size.

Part 2: Knowledge, attitude, and HBM constructs [33] (Table 1): It included 13 questions concerning knowledge. Any correct answer received one point and a wrong or I don't know answer got zero point. The overall score could range between 0 and 13. The attitude construct consisted of six questions rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score could range from 6 to 30, Perceived susceptibility included 5 questions on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score could range between 5 and 25, Perceived severity consisted of 5 questions on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score ranged between 5 and 25, Perceived benefits inlcuded six questions on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score ranged from 6 to 30, Perceived barriers comprised seven questions rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score ranged from 7 to 35, Cues for action included six questions about the knowledge of how to prevent Aedes mosquito bites. For each yes answer, one point is given and for each no answer, a zero score. The score could range from 0 to 6, The self‐efficacy construct consisted of three questions on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score ranged between 3 and 15, Behavior was assessed along eight questions ranged on a 1–5 Likert scale. An always answer received a rating of 5, most of the time a score of 4, sometimes a score of 3, rarely a score of 2, and never a score of 1. Thus, the range of scores could be 8 to 40.

To validate the questionnaire, the content validity was assessed. To this aim, the questionnaire was provided to 10 faculty members of the health education and entomology department. The suggested corrections were made and the validity was substantiated. To measure reliability, the questionnaire was completed by 20 subjects whose demographic characteristics were similar to those of the research population. The reliability test was done in SPSS 23 using Cronbach's alpha test. The Cronbach's alpha was 0.86 for the knowledge construct and 0.70 for the HBM constructs.

Procedure

The present research was done in three steps: (1) pre‐testing, (2) planning and implementing the interventions (for the intervention group), and analyzing the results, and (3) conducting the posttest 1 month after the educational intervention. The educational intervention was carried out by sending pictures, videos and educational clips, audio tracks and a doctor's speech about the Aedes mosquito and ways of prevention and self‐care, in the WhatsApp group from August to September 2022. In this way, daily educational messages were sent in the intervention WhatsApp group.

Educational Intervention

In the first week, for Knowledge & Attitude Constructs, training included an introduction and objectives, getting to know the transmitted diseases caused by the Aedes mosquito, and expressing the negative consequences of mosquito bites and their impact on behavioral beliefs. In the second week, for Perceived Susceptibility & Perceived Severity Constructs, training included presenting statistics and expressing the negative and serious consequences of being bitten by the Aedes mosquito and contracting dengue fever, Zika and other diseases transmitted by the Aedes mosquito. The training of the third week for Perceived Benefits & Perceived Barriers Constructs included expressing the benefits of using insecticide spray, installing nets on doors and windows, collecting stagnant water inside and outside the house, and wearing long and loose clothes. Removing misconceptions about the dangers of using insecticide sprays and assuring people that it costs little to make nets for doors and windows, and it takes little time to change planters and collect stagnant water in the fourth week for Self‐ Efficacy Constructs training included collecting stagnant water in the home environment step by step, teaching how to install a net in the form of an educational video prepared by a researcher, encouraging people to be effective in the actions they take and reducing people's stress by giving practical recommendations. The educational methods were Lectures, group discussions, questions and answers, posters and pamphlets, film and animation screenings and PowerPoint (Table 2). During the study, times were considered for sending feedback from the participants regarding the quality and their access to educational content.

One month after the educational intervention, a posttest was given to the intervention and control groups, and the effects of the educational intervention were measured on knowledge, attitude perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues for action, self‐efficacy, and preventive behaviors for Aedes mosquito bites. To comply with ethical considerations, an educational intervention was also conducted for the control group after the completion of the educational intervention.

Data Management and Analysis

Mean and standard deviation were used to describe interval variables, and frequency and relative frequency were used to describe noninterval variables. T‐test was used to compare the intervention group and control group at baseline and after intervention. A paired‐sample t‐test was used for comparing pre‐ and post‐intervention in the intervention group and control group. The Chi‐square test was used to compare the intervention group and control group in terms of demographic variables. The data were statistically analyzed in SPSS 23, and p‐value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

The present study was approved by the University Ethics Committee (IR.HUMS.REC.1401.088) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. An informed letter of consent was signed by all the participants. The participants were assured of the confidentiality of their information. Finally, the objectives of the study were explained to the participants. In the case of minors aged 15–18, we used an assent form written at an appropriate level by the minor, along with a separate, more detailed permission form from the parents or guardians.

Results

A total number of 320 men and women (161 individuals in the intervention and 159 individuals in the control groups) participated in this study. Discussion

We found that the educational intervention based on the HBM effectively promoted mosquito bite prevention behaviors through changing the precursors of the behavior. The HBM has been successfully applied in different cultures and contexts. This model contends that individuals are influenced by behaviors that protect their health through six belief constructs [34].

In the present study, the score of knowledge toward preventive behaviors in the intervention group increased after the educational intervention. When the knowledge increases, individuals will become adequately concerned about environmental issues [35, 36]. Educational measures to fight against Aedes aegypti help improve people's knowledge and change their behavior. They encourage people to manage mosquito habitat [37]. The results of Stefopoulou's study of people's knowledge, actual behavior, and attitude toward Aedes mosquito were in line with the present study [38]. This study showed that people's knowledge of Aedes mosquito and prevention measures could improve, which is consistent with Wong's study of dengue fever [20]. This level of knowledge in the intervention and control groups before the educational intervention was constructed by physicians and health staff (as a cue for action). Yet, after the educational intervention in the intervention group, most information was provided by social media, which shows people's tendency to use educational content. This study was conducted via WhatsApp. Therefore, it is suggested to hold more coherent educational programs through social media. In Clancy's study, among the HBM constructs, cues for action had the greatest impact on the desired behavior of preventing arbovirus transmission, while perceived benefits had the lowest impact [24].

In the present study, the score of attitudes toward preventive behaviors in the intervention group increased after the intervention. It proved the effect of the intervention on people's attitudes. Since the highest prevalence of diseases transmitted by Aedes mosquito in Iran is in Bandar Abbas, this issue has raised concerns and reinforced people's preventive beliefs. It has developed a negative attitude toward Aedes mosquito bites. According to the existing literature, knowledge alone is not enough to adopt preventive behaviors. How people think and perceive the disease matters too in their adoption of preventive behavior [39]. In Stefopoulou et al. (2021) used a door‐to‐door campaign to better affect the citizens' knowledge, attitude and performance in preventing Aedes mosquito bites [38]. In a body of research in many different countries, poor preventive measures followed the low level of health attitudes and beliefs and inappropriate living conditions [40, 41, 42, 43]. In another study by Linn et al. [44] in Myanmar, people showed a high attitude toward preventive measures against diseases transmitted by Aedes mosquito, and they corrected misconceptions about preventive measures such as covering water containers. They did a good job of using insect repellents and removing stagnant water. Arguably, promoting positive beliefs about preventive methods and developing a negative belief about the complications of diseases transmitted by the Aedes mosquito improved their attitude and performance in the present study.

In the present study, perceived susceptibility increased after the intervention in the intervention group. It proves the effect of the educational intervention on increasing the perceived susceptibility of the intervention group. It implies that most participants in the intervention group believed they may be exposed to diseases transmitted by Aedes mosquito after the educational intervention. In another study by Clancy et al. [24], to increase perceived susceptibility, messages were sent to emphasize the personal risk of infection if people did not take precautionary actions. These messages contained two textual posters: Did you know that the probability of infection with dengue fever inside your house is higher than outside? More than 90% of the breeding places are in houses, if you don't prevent it, you will be the first target of mosquito bites.). Also, posters with pictures of pregnant women and Aedes mosquitoes and different population groups were used, which were similar to the educational methods in the present study to improve perceived susceptibility. In another study by Liu et al [21], only one‐third of the participants enjoyed a perceived susceptibility of dengue fever and saw themselves at risk. They argued that Buddha's God would protect them. It seems in our study, increasing people's knowledge and sending educational videos and presenting the facts and figures led to the improvement of perceived susceptibility.

Our findings represented perceived severity of the intervention group improved after the intervention. This increase can be due to learning the educational content during the educational intervention. The knowledge of the severity of infection with the disease transmitted by the mosquito and attention to its complications and treatment costs accounted for the improvement of perceived severity discussed in the educational content. In another study by Wong et al. [45], participants with a high perceived severity of dengue fever and Zika virus were more likely to take control measures to prevent mosquito bites. In the control group, a slight increase in the mean score was observed in the perceived severity construct. Arguably, the knowledge they gained routinely in health centers was effective in increasing the severity of the complications of diseases transmitted by the Aedes mosquito.

A decrease in perceived barriers after the intervention shows that after the intervention, the participants became better aware of the barriers to preventive behaviors. Thus, they attempted to remove those barriers. In another study by Heydari et al. [46], the most important barrier to Aedes mosquito control was financial issues in families. Low‐income families in the study spent 10% of their savings on Aedes mosquito control. Therefore, due to the priority of the public health issue, low‐income families also participated. One suggested strategy was subsidies for the purchase of control products given to families. In the present study, after the intervention to promote preventive measure, mosquito nets and insect repellent sprays were provided for families, despite the high cost. These measures served to increase people's knowledge and susceptibility. Another barrier was the side effects of sprays and insect repellents on health and their adverse effect on lungs, which people had to consider. They tried to overcome this barrier by teaching the correct ways of using insect repellents. Several studies showed that perceived barriers are the most powerful aspect of expressing and predicting health protective behaviors. The results of similar studies were also in line with the present study [24, 47]. Considering that perceived barriers and perceived benefits are the strongest predictors of behavior [48], in the present study, these constructs were emphasized.

Perceived benefits increased after the intervention in the intervention group. The results of some other studies emphasize that one's understanding of benefits paves the way for adopting preventive behaviors. In Arham et al.'s study [49], perceived benefits were the only factor that influenced attitude. Similarly, Amin et al. [50], who studied the perceived effect, confirmed that perceived benefits were the main factors influencing attitude toward Aedes infection. The findings of many studies [51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56] were also in line with the present study and pointed to the importance of perceived benefits.

The present study also used the self‐efficacy theory. Self‐efficacy is a mediator between knowledge and behavior and belief in one's ability to show a behavior. Only knowing the reasons for showing the behavior is not enough. One also needs to prepare himself to show that specific behavior. The ability to know perceived self‐efficacy is considered a prerequisite for actual behavior. Thus, special attention should be paid to increasing self‐efficacy. In the present study, the mean self‐efficacy score before the intervention was 12.89, which increased to 14.77 after the intervention. In Rangama's study [57], self‐efficacy played an important role in adopting preventive behaviors against dengue fever, which is in line with the present study.

The findings of this research showed that the mean score of preventive behaviors of diseases transmitted by Aedes mosquito was 33.55 before the training, which increased to 38.42 after the intervention. Preventive behaviors in the present study included the mechanical elimination of mosquito breeding sites and public participation in repelling mosquitoes from homes. This was done by encouraging people to remove the breeding sites indoors and avoid stagnant water, which is consistent with Clancy's study [24]. Public participation in mosquito control through environmental management by covering water containers is one of the few effective strategies against Aedes mosquitoes [58]. The related literature has shown that interventions to eliminate mosquito breeding sites are effective in preventing arbovirus transmission [59, 60]. In the present study, preventive measures for personal protection included the use of insect killer spray or mosquito nets. It seems that providing these protective items and making them available by the government is especially useful in neighborhoods with low socioeconomic conditions. Considering that this mosquito prevails in Hormozgan province and several other provinces of Iran, the participation of local people can be truly effective.

Limitations of Study

Due to the restricted geographical distribution of this mosquito species across merely a select number of provinces, the corpus of pertinent scholarly articles available in Iran remains insufficient for a comprehensive comparison and discourse regarding the findings. Furthermore, individuals lacking access to social media or internet resources were excluded from the scope of this investigation, and this variable must be taken into account when evaluating the generalizability of the study's conclusions. Sufficient information was provided by the researcher and the staff of the health care centers, but the cooperation of the participants required a lot of follow‐ups. Also, completing the questionnaires by people was time‐consuming.

Conclusion

The present findings showed that the health education program designed based on the HBM managed to improve people's knowledge and attitude and also positively affected their perceived barriers and benefits, perceived susceptibility and severity. The intervention managed to increase self‐efficacy and significantly affected promoting preventive behaviors against dengue fever.

Suggestions

In the present study, men and women visitors to comprehensive health centers were included, and it is suggested to conduct similar research on other target populations in future.
