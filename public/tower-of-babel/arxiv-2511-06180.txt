A Dual Method for Minimax Quadratic Programming
Wenhui Ren and Liwei Zhang

Abstract
This paper investigates minimax quadratic programming problems with coupled inequality constraints. By leveraging a duality theorem, we develop a dual algorithm that extends the dual active set method to the minimax setting, transforming the original inequality constrained problem into a sequence of equality constrained subproblems. Under a suitable assumption, we prove that the associated S-pairs do not repeat and that the algorithm terminates in a finite number of iterations, guaranteed by the monotonic decrease of the objective function value. To ensure numerical stability and efficiency, the algorithm is implemented using Cholesky factorization and Givens rotations. Numerical experiments on both randomly generated minimax quadratic programs and illustrative applications demonstrate the accuracy, stability, and computational effectiveness of the proposed algorithm.



Keywords:
minimax optimization, coupled constraints,
quadratic programming, dual active set method.

Introduction

Consider the following minimax quadratic optimization problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\quad&f(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}x^{T}G_{11}x+x^{T}G_{12}y+\frac{1}{2}y^{T}G_{22}y+c_{x}^{T}x+c_{y}^{T}y,\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&s(x,y)=Ax+By+h\leq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
G_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
is a symmetric matrix,
G_{22}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times n_{y}}
is a symmetric negative definite matrix,
G_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{y}}
,
c_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
,
c_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{x}}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{y}}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Although the vectors of variables
x
and
y
may also be subject to equality constraints

\displaystyle l(x,y)=A^{l}x+B^{l}y+h^{l}=0,

we will ignore such constraints for the moment in order to simplify our presentation.

Minimax optimization problems arise in a broad range of fields, from modern machine learning such as generative adversarial networks, adversarial training, and multi-agent reinforcement learning to classical areas including saddle point problems, numerical partial differential equations, and game theory. Extensive studies have focused on unconstrained minimax problems and those without coupling constraints between the inner variable and the outer variable
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. More recently, several works have investigated minimax optimization problems with coupled equality constraints
[
5
,
6
]
. For general nonlinear programming, the sequential quadratic programming (SQP) method represents a cornerstone, providing an efficient and reliable approach for solving constrained problems
[
7
,
8
]
. We conjecture that extending the SQP method to minimax problems requires solving a quadratic subproblem at each iteration to obtain the search direction. Consequently, the development of an efficient algorithm for minimax quadratic programming with inequality constraints is of particular interest.

Quadratic programming (QP) has a long history and numerous applications, giving rise to a variety of algorithmic methods. Representative approaches include the primal methods of Beale
[
9
]
, Wolfe
[
10
]
, and Fletcher
[
11
]
; the dual methods of Lemke
[
12
]
, Van de Panne and Whinston
[
13
]
, and Goldfarb and Idnani
[
14
]
; the pivoting method of Keller
[
15
]
; and the iterative schemes of Hildreth
[
16
]
, Frank and Wolfe
[
17
]
, Pang
[
18
]
, Herman and Lent
[
19
]
, and Mangasarian
[
20
]
. Among these, the Goldfarb and Idnani method
[
14
]
is one of the most influential dual active set algorithms for strictly convex QPs. Powell
[
21
]
implemented this method and demonstrated its robustness and efficiency through extensive numerical experiments. Later, Boland
[
22
]
extended the approach to positive semi-definite problems. Motivated by these developments, we aim to propose a dual active set method to minimax quadratic programming with coupled inequality constraints, following the key ideas of the Goldfarb and Idnani method.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section

In Section

Finally, Section

Optimality conditions for constrained minimax problem

Considering the following problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\quad&f(x,y),\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&l(x,y)=0,\\
&s(x,y)\leq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

where
n_{x},n_{y},m_{l}
and
m_{s}
are positive integers and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
l:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}}
and
s:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m_{s}}
are twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of some feasible point
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
. Problem (
2
) can be rewritten as the following equivalent form,

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\max_{y\in Y(x)}f(x,y),

(3)

where

\displaystyle Y(x)=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}:l(x,y)=0,s(x,y)\leq 0\right\}.

(4)

For unconstrained continuous minimax optimization; namely the problem with
Y(x)=\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
, Jin et al.
[
24
]
proposed a proper definition of local optimality called local minimax. Dai and Zhang
[
23
]
extended this definition of local minimax point for constrained minimax problem including (
3
) as a special case. Now we give the definition of the local minimax point for Problem (
3
).

Definition 1
.

A point
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
is said to be a local minimax point of Problem (
3
) if there exist
\delta_{0}>0
and a function
\eta:\left(0,\delta_{0}\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying
\eta(\delta)\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
such that for any
\delta\in\left(0,\delta_{0}\right]
and any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(x^{\ast})\times\left[Y(x^{\ast})\cap\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(y^{\ast})\right]
, we have

\displaystyle f(x^{\ast},y)\leq f(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\leq\max_{\omega}\left\{f(x,\omega):\omega\in Y(x)\cap\mathbb{B}_{\eta(\delta)}(y^{\ast})\right\}.

(5)

We can understand Definition
1
in this way:
y^{\ast}
is the local maximal point of

, while
x^{\ast}
is the local minimal point of a surrogate objective function
\max_{y}\left\{f(x,y):\|y-y^{\ast}\|\leq\eta(\delta),l(x,y)=0,s(x,y)\leq 0\right\}

\max_{y}\left\{f(x,y):l(x,y)=0,s(x,y)\leq 0\right\}
.

For a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
around
x^{\ast}
, we use
(P_{x})
to denote the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \max_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\quad&f(x,\omega)\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&l(x,\omega)=0,\\
&s(x,\omega)\leq 0.\end{aligned}

The Lagrangian of Problem
(P_{x})
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x;\omega,\mu,\lambda)=f(x,\omega)+\mu^{T}l(x,\omega)+\lambda^{T}s(x,\omega).

In order to state the second order optimality conditions for Problem (
3
), we require the following conditions.

Definition 2
.

Let
(\mu^{\ast},\lambda^{\ast})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{s}}
be a point. We say that Jacobian uniqueness conditions of Problem
(P_{x^{\ast}})
are satisfied at
(y^{\ast},\mu^{\ast},\lambda^{\ast})
if

1.

The point
(y^{\ast},\mu^{\ast},\lambda^{\ast})
is a Karush-Kuhn-Tucker point of Problem
(P_{x^{\ast}})
; namely,

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}\mathcal{L}(x^{\ast};y^{\ast},\mu^{\ast},\lambda^{\ast})=0,

\displaystyle l(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=0,

\displaystyle 0\geq\lambda^{\ast}\perp s(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\leq 0.

2.

The linear independence constraint qualification holds at
y^{\ast}
; namely, the set of vectors

\displaystyle\left\{\nabla_{y}l_{1}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast}),\cdots,\nabla_{y}l_{m_{l}}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\right\}\cup\left\{\nabla_{y}s_{i}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast}):i\in I_{x^{\ast}}(y^{\ast})\right\}

are linearly independent, where
I(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=\left\{i\mid s_{i}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=0,i=1,2,\cdots,m_{s}\right\}
.

3.

The strict complementarity condition holds at

for
\lambda^{\ast}
; namely,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{\ast}+s_{i}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})<0,i=1,\cdots,m_{s}.

4.

The second-order sufficient optimality condition holds at

,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{yy}^{2}\mathcal{L}(x^{\ast};y^{\ast},\mu^{\ast},\lambda^{\ast})d_{y},d_{y}\right\rangle<0\quad\forall d_{y}\in\mathcal{C}_{x^{\ast}}(y^{\ast}),

where
\mathcal{C}_{x^{\ast}}(y^{\ast})
is the critical cone of Problem
(P_{x^{\ast}})
at
y^{\ast}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{x^{\ast}}(y^{\ast})=\left\{d_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}:\mathcal{J}_{y}l(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})d_{y}=0;\nabla_{y}s_{i}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})d_{y}\leq 0,i\in I_{x^{\ast}}(y^{\ast});\nabla_{y}f(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})d_{y}\leq 0\right\}.

Under the Jacobian uniqueness conditions, we can easily obtain the following result.

.

Let
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}

3
Basic method

The dual algorithm presented in the next section belongs to the class of
active set methods
.
An
active set
refers to a subset of the
m
constraints in Problem (
8
) that are satisfied as equalities at the current estimate
z
of the solution to Problem (
8
).
We denote by
[m]=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
the index set of all constraints, and by
\alpha\subseteq[m]
the index set corresponding to the currently active constraints.

Definition 3
.

Let
J\subseteq[m]
be a set of indices.
A problem is said to be a
subproblem

P(J)
of Problem (
8
) if it takes the following form:

\displaystyle P(J)\left\{\begin{aligned} \min_{x}\max_{y}\quad&f(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{T}Gz+c^{T}z,\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&s_{J}(z)=D_{J}z+h_{J}\leq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

Consider the problem
P([m])
.
Suppose that
P([m])
admits a local minimax point satisfying Corollary
1
, denoted by
z^{\ast}
.
We then define

\displaystyle\alpha^{\ast}=\left\{i\in[m]\mid D_{i}z^{\ast}+h_{i}=0\right\}.

Then
z^{\ast}
is a local minimax point of the following problem subject to equality constraints:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\quad&\frac{1}{2}z^{T}Gz+c^{T}z,\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&D_{\alpha^{\ast}}z+h_{\alpha^{\ast}}=0.\end{aligned}

(10)

and there exists
\lambda^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &Gz^{\ast}+c+\sum_{i\in\alpha^{\ast}}\lambda_{i}^{\ast}D_{i}^{T}=0,\\
&D_{\alpha^{\ast}}z^{\ast}+h_{\alpha^{\ast}}=0,\\
&\lambda_{i}^{\ast}\leq 0,i\in\alpha^{\ast},\\
&\lambda_{j}^{\ast}=0,i\in[m]\backslash\alpha^{\ast},\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

and

\displaystyle G_{11}-\begin{bmatrix}G_{12}&A_{\alpha^{\ast}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}G_{22}&B_{\alpha^{\ast}}^{T}\\
B_{\alpha^{\ast}}&0\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{12}^{T}\\
A_{\alpha^{\ast}}\end{bmatrix}\succ 0.

(12)

For convenient, we define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}=G_{11}-\begin{bmatrix}G_{12}&A_{\alpha}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}G_{22}&B_{\alpha}^{T}\\
B_{\alpha}&0\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{12}^{T}\\
A_{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

We have that

is a local minimax point of both Problem

By employing the active set idea, an inequality constrained problem can be transformed into an equality constrained one.
To characterize the pair

Definition 4

satisfying (

Each S-pair

Step 2

STOP-
z
is the optimal solution for Problem.

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=G^{-1}(I-N_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}^{\ast})=G^{-1}-G^{-1}N_{\alpha}(N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha})^{-1}N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}.

Using

Lemma 2
.

Consider the problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\quad&f(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}z^{T}Gz+c^{T}z,\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&s(z)=N_{\alpha}^{T}z+h_{\alpha}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

is a row full-rank matrix,

is negative definite, and

is positive definite.
If

where

.

From Lemma

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}g(\hat{z})=0.

Next, we consider the dual problem corresponding to Problem (
8
).
The Lagrangian function for Problem (

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(z,\mu)=f(z)+\mu^{T}s(z),

(17)

where

denotes the Lagrange multiplier.

Tsaknakis et al.
[
25

developed the duality theorem for minimax problems.
For Problem (

), if the objective function

is strongly concave in

\displaystyle\min_{x}\max_{y}\min_{\mu\leq 0}\mathcal{L}(x,y,\mu)=\min_{\mu\leq 0}\min_{x}\max_{y}\mathcal{L}(x,y,\mu).

(18)

This implies that if the dual problem is well-defined, the associated multipliers satisfy
\mu\leq 0
.

From the definitions of
N_{\alpha}^{\ast}
and

and Corollary

, we can derive

\displaystyle\mu=-N_{\alpha}^{\ast}g(\bar{z})

for Problem (

Accordingly, we define

\displaystyle u(\hat{z}):=-N_{\alpha}^{\ast}g(\hat{z})

to denote the dual variable of Problem (

).

Hence, the conditions

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}g(\hat{z})=0,\qquad u(\hat{z})\leq 0,

(19)

are necessary for

to be the optimal solution of Problem (

).

,

2.

(b)

r_{j}<0\end{subarray}}\left\{\frac{u_{j}^{+}}{r_{j}}\right\},\infty\right\},

&-\frac{s_{p}(z)}{d^{T}n^{+}},&\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

in Step 2, given an existing S-pair

and a violated constraint

Unlike in the standard quadratic minimization problem, for minimax problems one can easily construct examples whose feasible regions are nonempty but for which no local minimax point exists, even in the convex–concave case.
As we shall see shortly, the following assumption is useful.

Assumption 1
.

If Problem (
8
) admits a local minimax point, then every subproblem
P(J)
of Problem (
8
), where
J\subseteq K
, also admits a local minimax point.

It is evident that Assumption
1
is difficult to verify directly for Problem (
8
).
Therefore, we propose the following assumption, which is easier to check in practice.

Assumption 2
.

For Problem (
8
), the matrix
DG^{-1}D^{T}
is negative semi-definite.

If Problem (
8
) satisfies Assumption

1
.
To illustrate the relationship between an S-pair
(z,\alpha)
and a violated constraint
p
, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 5
.

A triple
(z,\alpha,p)
consisting of a point
z
and a set of indices
\alpha^{+}=\alpha\cup\{p\}
, where
p\in K\setminus\alpha
, is said to be a
V-triple
(violated triple) if the columns of
B_{\alpha}^{+}
are linearly independent and the following conditions hold:

1.

s_{i}(z)=0
for all
i\in\alpha
and
s_{p}(z)>0
;

2.

H_{\alpha_{+}}g(z)=0
;

3.

u_{\alpha_{+}}=-N_{\alpha_{+}}^{\ast}g(z)\leq 0
;

4.

\Gamma_{\alpha}\succ 0
.

For an S-pair
(z,\alpha)
, we have
H_{\alpha}g(z)=0
.
Using Proposition
1
, it follows that

\displaystyle H_{\alpha_{+}}g(z)=H_{\alpha_{+}}GH_{\alpha}g(z)=0.

The main difference between a V-triple and an S-pair lies in the value of
s_{p}(z)
.
The key question is how to move from a point
z
corresponding to a V-triple
(z,\alpha,p)
to a new point while maintaining all the other conditions except for
s_{p}(z)
.
We now establish a lemma to clarify this process.

Lemma 3
.

Let
(z,\alpha,p)
be a V-triple and consider points of the form

\displaystyle\bar{z}=z+td,

(20)

where

\displaystyle d=H_{\alpha}n^{+}.

(21)

Then

\displaystyle H_{\alpha_{+}}g(\bar{z})=0,

(22)

\displaystyle s_{i}(\bar{z})=0,\quad\text{for all }i\in\alpha,

(23)

\displaystyle s_{p}(\bar{z})=s_{p}(z)+td^{T}n^{+},

(24)

\displaystyle u_{\alpha_{+}}(\bar{z})=-N_{\alpha_{+}}^{\ast}g(\bar{z})=u_{\alpha_{+}}(z)-t\begin{bmatrix}r\\
1\end{bmatrix},

(25)

where

\displaystyle r=-N_{\alpha}^{\ast}n^{+},

(26)

and

\displaystyle f(\bar{z})=f(z)+td^{T}n^{+}\left(\frac{t}{2}-u_{p}(z)\right).

(27)

Proof.

According to Proposition
1
and Definition
5
, we have

\displaystyle g(\bar{z})=G\bar{z}+c=Gz+c+tGd=g(z)+tGd,

\displaystyle Gd=GH_{\alpha}n^{+}=(I-N_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}^{\ast})n^{+}=N_{\alpha_{+}}\begin{bmatrix}-N_{\alpha}^{\ast}n^{+}\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

Then

\displaystyle H_{\alpha_{+}}g(\bar{z})=H_{\alpha_{+}}g(z)+tH_{\alpha_{+}}Gd=0,

\displaystyle s_{i}(\bar{z})=s_{i}(z)+tn_{i}^{T}H_{\alpha}n^{+}=0,\quad\text{for all }i\in\alpha,

\displaystyle u_{\alpha_{+}}(\bar{z})=-N_{\alpha_{+}}^{\ast}g(\bar{z})=-N_{\alpha_{+}}^{\ast}g(z)-tN_{\alpha_{+}}^{\ast}Gd=u_{\alpha_{+}}(z)-t\begin{bmatrix}r\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

From
H_{\alpha_{+}}g(z)=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle g(z)=-N_{\alpha_{+}}u_{\alpha_{+}}(z).

Therefore,

\displaystyle f(\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bar{z}^{T}G\bar{z}+c^{T}\bar{z}

\displaystyle=f(z)+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}d^{T}Gd+td^{T}g(z)

\displaystyle=f(z)+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}d^{T}n^{+}-td^{T}N_{\alpha_{+}}u_{\alpha_{+}}(z)

\displaystyle=f(z)+td^{T}n^{+}\left(\frac{t}{2}-u_{p}(z)\right).

∎

For a V-triple
(z,\alpha,p)

, where

Lemma 4

Let
\Gamma_{\alpha}
be a positive definite matrix,
G_{22}
be a negative definite matrix, and

be a vector. Suppose that

is defined as in (

1.

If

\displaystyle n^{T}H_{\alpha}n\geq n^{T}H_{\bar{\alpha}}n\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\bar{\alpha}}\succeq\Gamma_{\alpha}\succ 0.

(28)

2.

If
n^{T}H_{\alpha}n<0

\Gamma_{\alpha_{+}}\succ 0

Proof.

For convenience, define

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}:=\begin{bmatrix}G_{12}^{T}\\
A_{\alpha}\end{bmatrix},\qquad K_{\alpha}:=\begin{bmatrix}G_{22}&B_{\alpha}^{T}\\
B_{\alpha}&0\end{bmatrix}.

By taking the Schur complement of
K_{\alpha}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}=\Gamma_{\alpha_{-}}+(B_{k}H_{\alpha_{-}}^{B}B_{k}^{T})^{-1}\left(A_{k}-\begin{bmatrix}B_{k}&0\end{bmatrix}K_{\alpha_{-}}^{-1}M_{\alpha_{-}}\right)^{T}\left(A_{k}-\begin{bmatrix}B_{k}&0\end{bmatrix}K_{\alpha_{-}}^{-1}M_{\alpha_{-}}\right),

where

and

\displaystyle H^{B}:=G_{22}^{-1}\left(I-B^{T}(BG_{22}^{-1}B^{T})^{-1}BG_{22}^{-1}\right).

Since

is negative definite,

is negative semi-definite, which implies

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}\preccurlyeq\Gamma_{\alpha_{-}}.

Hence, for

\Gamma_{\bar{\alpha}}\succeq\Gamma_{\alpha}
.

Next, we show that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}\succ 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}\prec 0,

under the assumptions that
\Gamma_{\emptyset}\succ 0
and
G_{22}\prec 0
.
By the Woodbury matrix identity
[

, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{\emptyset}+W_{\alpha}^{T}U_{\alpha}^{-1}W_{\alpha})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1}-\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1}W_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}+W_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1}W_{\alpha}^{T})^{-1}W_{\alpha}^{T}\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1}-\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1}W_{\alpha}(N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha})^{-1}W_{\alpha}^{T}\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1},

and

\displaystyle(N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(U_{\alpha}+W_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\emptyset}^{-1}W_{\alpha}^{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}^{-1}-U_{\alpha}^{-1}W_{\alpha}^{T}\Gamma_{\alpha}^{-1}W_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}^{-1},

where

and

U_{\alpha}:=B_{\alpha}G_{22}^{-1}B_{\alpha}^{T}
.
Since

, it follows that
\Gamma_{\alpha}\succ 0
if and only if

N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}\prec 0
.

Now consider the value
n^{T}H_{\bar{\alpha}}n-n^{T}H_{\alpha}n

\displaystyle n^{T}H_{\bar{\alpha}}n-n^{T}H_{\alpha}n

\displaystyle=-n^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{\ast}n+n^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}^{\ast}n

\displaystyle=n^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}\left(\begin{bmatrix}N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}&N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\beta}\\
N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}&N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}-\begin{bmatrix}(N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}})^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}n,

where
\beta=\alpha\setminus\bar{\alpha}
.
Since both
N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}
and

N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}
are negative definite, using Cholesky decomposition, we define
N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}=-L_{\bar{\alpha}}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}

can be written as

\displaystyle-N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}=-\begin{bmatrix}L_{\bar{\alpha}}&0\\
N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-T}&L_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}&L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\beta}\\
0&L_{\beta}^{T}\end{bmatrix},

where

. By taking the Schur complement of
\begin{bmatrix}L_{\bar{\alpha}}&0\\
N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-T}&L_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}L_{\bar{\alpha}}&0\\
N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-T}&L_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-1}&0\\
-L_{\beta}^{-1}N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-T}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-1}&L_{\beta}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}&N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\beta}\\
N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}&N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}-\begin{bmatrix}(N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-1}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}})^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}L^{T}L&-L^{T}L_{\beta}^{-1}\\
-L_{\beta}^{-T}L&L_{\beta}^{-T}L_{\beta}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}L^{T}\\
-L_{\beta}^{-T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}L&-L_{\beta}^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
L=L_{\beta}^{-1}N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-T}L_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-1}
. This shows that
\left(\begin{bmatrix}N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}&N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\beta}\\
N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}}&N_{\beta}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\beta}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}-\begin{bmatrix}(N_{\bar{\alpha}}^{-1}G^{-1}N_{\bar{\alpha}})^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)
is negative semi-definite, and thus
n^{T}H_{\bar{\alpha}}n\leq n^{T}H_{\alpha}n
.

Finally, we define
N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}=-L_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}^{T}
.
If
n^{T}H_{\alpha}n<0
, then

\displaystyle N_{\alpha_{+}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha_{+}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}&N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}n\\
n^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}&n^{T}G^{-1}n\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}L_{\alpha}&0\\
n^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}^{-T}&\sqrt{-n^{T}H_{\alpha}n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}L_{\alpha}^{T}&L_{\alpha}^{-1}N_{\alpha}^{T}G^{-1}n\\
0&\sqrt{-n^{T}H_{\alpha}n}\end{bmatrix},

which implies that
N_{\alpha_{+}}^{T}G^{-1}N_{\alpha_{+}}\prec 0
and, consequently,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha_{+}}\succ 0.

∎

Assume that
(z^{\ast},\alpha^{\ast})

Theorem 3

by (

&-\dfrac{s_{p}(z)}{d^{T}n^{+}},&\text{otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

Then the following statements hold:

By the definition of

\displaystyle H^{+}g(\bar{z})=0,\qquad s_{i}(\bar{z})=0~(\forall i\in\alpha),\qquad u^{+}(\bar{z})\leq 0.

\displaystyle N^{T}d

\displaystyle=G^{-1}(Nu-N^{+}u^{\ast}),

Lemma 5
.

Let
(z,\alpha)
be an S-pair.

5
Numerically stable implementation

There exist various approaches to implement the dual algorithm described in Section
4
in a numerically stable manner.
Our implementation is based on the Cholesky decomposition and Givens rotation.

Since
N^{T}G^{-1}N
is negative definite, we define its Cholesky factorization as

\displaystyle-N^{T}G^{-1}N=R^{T}R,

(36)

and introduce

\displaystyle M=R^{-T}N^{T}G^{-1}.

(37)

Then the matrices
H
and
N^{\ast}
can be written as

\displaystyle N^{\ast}

\displaystyle=(N^{T}G^{-1}N)^{-1}N^{T}G^{-1}=-R^{-1}M,

(38)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=G^{-1}(I-NN^{\ast})=G^{-1}+M^{T}M.

(39)

Hence, instead of explicitly storing
H
and
N^{\ast}
, it suffices to maintain
R
,
R^{-1}
, and
M
.

In the dual algorithm of Section
4
, the vectors
d
and
r
are required. They can be computed as

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=-N^{\ast}n^{+}=R^{-1}Mn^{+},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=Hn^{+}=(G^{-1}+M^{T}M)n^{+}.

In practice, we perform the following computations:

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=Mn^{+},

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=(G^{-1}+M^{T}M)n^{+},

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=n^{+T}(G^{-1}+M^{T}M)n^{+},

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=R^{-1}d_{1}.

Adding a constraint.

Let
N_{+}
denote the matrix obtained by adding one constraint column
n^{+}
to
N
, and define the Cholesky factorization

\displaystyle-N_{+}^{T}G^{-1}N_{+}=R_{+}^{T}R_{+},

that is,

\displaystyle R_{+}^{T}R_{+}=-\begin{bmatrix}N^{T}G^{-1}N&N^{T}G^{-1}n^{+}\\
n^{+T}G^{-1}N&n^{+T}G^{-1}n^{+}\end{bmatrix}.

Following the proof in Lemma
4
, one obtains

\displaystyle R_{+}=\begin{bmatrix}R&-d_{1}\\
0&\sqrt{-\delta}\end{bmatrix},\qquad R_{+}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R^{-1}&\frac{R^{-1}d_{1}}{\sqrt{-\delta}}\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\delta}}\end{bmatrix}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle M_{+}

\displaystyle=R_{+}^{-T}N_{+}^{T}G^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R^{-T}N^{T}G^{-1}\\
\frac{d_{1}^{T}R^{-T}N^{T}G^{-1}+n^{+T}G^{-1}}{\sqrt{-\delta}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}M\\
\frac{d_{2}^{T}}{\sqrt{-\delta}}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, after computing
d_{1}
,
d_{2}
,
\delta
, and
r
, the matrices
R
,
R^{-1}
, and
M
can be efficiently updated to
R_{+}
,
R_{+}^{-1}
, and
M_{+}
, respectively.

Dropping a constraint.

We first consider dropping the last constraint, that is, removing the last column of
N
.
Let
N=[\,N_{-}\;n\,]
and define
-N_{-}^{T}G^{-1}N_{-}=R_{-}^{T}R_{-}
.
Then

\displaystyle R=\begin{bmatrix}R_{-}&\ast\\
0&\ast\end{bmatrix},\quad R^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R_{-}^{-1}&\ast\\
0&\ast\end{bmatrix},\quad M=\begin{bmatrix}M_{-}\\
\ast\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, deleting the last row and column of
R
and
R^{-1}
yields
R_{-}
and
R_{-}^{-1}
, respectively, while deleting the last row of
M
gives
M_{-}
.

Next, consider dropping the
k
th constraint.
Let
N=\begin{bmatrix}N_{1}&N_{D}&N_{2}\end{bmatrix}
, where
N_{D}
is the column to be deleted, and define
N_{-}=\begin{bmatrix}N_{1}&N_{2}\end{bmatrix}
.
Let
R=\begin{bmatrix}R_{1}&R_{D}&R_{2}\end{bmatrix}
, where
R_{D}
corresponds to the
k
th column of
R
.
There exists a elementary matrix
P
such that

\displaystyle\hat{N}=NP=\begin{bmatrix}N_{1}&N_{2}&N_{D}\end{bmatrix},

which implies

\displaystyle-P^{T}N^{T}G^{-1}NP=P^{T}R^{T}RP,

and

\displaystyle RP=\begin{bmatrix}R_{1}&R_{2}&R_{D}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\begin{bmatrix}R_{1}&R_{2}\end{bmatrix}
is an upper-Hessenberg matrix, an upper triangular matrix

\displaystyle\hat{R}=\bar{Q}^{T}RP=\bar{Q}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}R_{1}&R_{2}&R_{D}\end{bmatrix}

can be obtained by applying at most
|\alpha|
Givens rotations, where

\bar{Q}=Q_{k-1,k}\cdots Q_{2,3}Q_{1,2}

is the product of the Givens matrices.
It follows that

\displaystyle\hat{R}^{-1}=(\bar{Q}^{T}RP)^{-1}=P^{T}R^{-1}\bar{Q},

and

\displaystyle\hat{M}

\displaystyle=\hat{R}^{-T}\hat{N}^{T}G^{-1}=\bar{Q}^{T}R^{-T}PP^{T}N^{T}G^{-1}=\bar{Q}^{T}R^{-T}N^{T}G^{-1}=\bar{Q}^{T}M.

Thus, using the elementary matrix
P
and the Givens rotations

, the
k
th constraint can be moved to the last column of
N

\hat{N}

Accordingly, deleting the last row and column of
\hat{R}
and
\hat{R}^{-1}
gives
R_{-}
and
R_{-}^{-1}
, while deleting the last row of
\hat{M}
gives
M_{-}
.

In Step 0(b) of the dual algorithm
2

for all

.
In practice, we first compute

The vectors

for

, then evaluate

\displaystyle d_{2}=M^{T}d_{1}+G^{-1}n_{p},

and

The step direction

After

6
Numerical results

This section presents numerical experiments to evaluate the proposed dual algorithm from both theoretical and computational perspectives. The experiments assess its numerical stability and efficiency across different problem settings. Three types of experiments are considered: the first assesses basic numerical properties on randomly generated problems, illustrating its stability and computational effectiveness; the second applies the algorithm to an adversarial attack on a mean–covariance portfolio model, demonstrating its practical effectiveness; and the third presents two illustrative examples under distinct assumptions, highlighting the detailed iterative behavior of Algorithm
2
.

6.2
Adversarial attack on a mean-covariance portfolio model

In this subsection we evaluate the proposed local minimax point and the dual algorithm
2
by constructing adversarial attacks against a mean-covariance portfolio model
[
28
,
29
]
. The attacker (adversary) chooses
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to degrade the investor’s performance, while the investor chooses
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to maximize return adjusted for risk. The adversarial problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad&f(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Hx+x^{T}Wy-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}\Sigma_{y}y+\mu^{T}y,\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&s_{i}(x,y)=x_{i}+y_{i}-(12-b)\leq 0,\quad i=1,\dots,n,\end{aligned}

(40)

where
n
denotes the number of assets. The objective function
f(x,y)
captures a simple mean-covariance trade-off for the investor
-\frac{1}{2}y^{T}\Sigma_{y}y+\mu^{T}y
, while the adversary implements an attack via the vector
x
. The adversary incurs a quadratic cost
\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Hx
and interacts with the investor via
x^{T}Wy
, which models how adversarial actions affect the investor’s decisions. The constraint
x_{i}+y_{i}\leq 12-b
models a limited liquidity budget: a larger
b
yields a tighter feasible set, i.e. a smaller value of
12-b
, which reflects deteriorated market liquidity. The parameter
b
is referred to as the liquidity intensity parameter.

We use the constituents of the NASDAQ-100 index for the year 2024 (252 trading days), hence
n=100
. For each asset
i
, we collect its daily closing prices
p_{t,i}
and daily trading volumes over the period from 2024-01-01 to 2024-12-31, where
p_{t,i}
denotes the closing price of asset
i
on trading day
t
. Based on the price series, we compute the daily arithmetic returns as
R_{t,i}=(p_{t,i}-p_{t-1,i})/p_{t-1,i}
and define

\displaystyle\mu:=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}R_{t},\qquad\Sigma_{y}:=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(R_{t}-\mu)(R_{t}-\mu)^{T},

with
T=252
. Let
\mathrm{ADV}_{i}
denote the average daily trading volume of asset
i
. The liquidity weighting matrix is

\displaystyle W:=\operatorname{diag}\big(1/\mathrm{ADV}_{1},\dots,1/\mathrm{ADV}_{n}\big).

The attacker matrix is chosen as
H=I_{n}
(the
n\times n
identity). To make the three matrices
H
,
W
and
\Sigma_{y}
comparable in magnitude we normalize them using
M_{\mu}:=\|\mu\|_{1}
. Specifically we apply the scalings

\displaystyle H\leftarrow\frac{0.1M_{\mu}}{\|H\|_{1}}\cdot H,\qquad W\leftarrow\frac{M_{\mu}}{\|W\|_{1}}\cdot W,\qquad\Sigma_{y}\leftarrow\frac{0.2M_{\mu}}{\|\Sigma_{y}\|_{1}}\cdot\Sigma_{y},

so that
\|H\|_{1}=0.1M_{\mu}
,
\|W\|_{1}=M_{\mu}
and
\|\Sigma_{y}\|_{1}=0.2M_{\mu}
.

We compare the proposed minimax attack (computed by the dual algorithm) with three baselines:

1.

Random attack.
An attack vector
x
is generated at random. The attack
x
is drawn from the uniform distribution
r(b-12,12-b)
where
r(a,b)
denotes a uniformly distributed random number between
a
and
b
.

2.

No-long (forbid long positions) attack.
Identify the 20 assets with the largest historical average daily return
\mu_{i}
. For each such asset set
x_{i}=12-b
, which forces
y_{i}\leq 0
by the constraint
x_{i}+y_{i}\leq 12-b
and thus prevents the investor
y
from taking long positions in these high-return assets. For all other assets set
x_{i}=0
.

3.

NI attack.
We reformulate the two-player zero-sum game using a Nikaido–Isoda function
[
30
]
, which is a widely used approach in the analysis of generalized Nash equilibrium problems. Let
z=(x,y)
,
\hat{z}=(\hat{x},\hat{y})
and
\gamma>0
, define Nikaido-Isoda function

\displaystyle\Psi_{\gamma}(z,\hat{z})=f(x,\hat{y})-f(\hat{x},y)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|z-\hat{z}\|^{2},

and the associated value function

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\gamma}(z)=\max_{s(\hat{z})\leq 0}\left\{\,\Psi(z,\hat{z})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|z-\hat{z}\|^{2}\right\}.

The NI attack is then obtained by minimizing
\hat{V}_{\gamma}(z)
with respect to
z
.

Let
x_{att}
denote the adversary’s decisions after an attack and
x_{be}=0
denote the no attack baseline. For a given attack vector
x
, we solve the maximization problem

\displaystyle q(x):=\max_{y\in Y(x)}f(x,y),\quad Y(x)=\left\{y\mid x+y\leq 12-b\right\},

which ensures the investor’s optimal response under the imposed attack. We measure the effectiveness of an attack by the relative reduction:

\displaystyle\rho:=\frac{q(x_{be})-q(x_{att})}{|q(x_{be})|}.

The larger values of
\rho
indicate a more effective attack relative to the no attack baseline. For cases in which the attack fails to reduce the objective,
\rho
is set to zero.

The random attack was executed over
2000
independent trials. The trial achieving the largest value of
\rho
was used for evaluation. The NI attack minimizes
\hat{V}_{\gamma}(z)
with respect to
z
using a Gauss–Newton method
[
31
]
, with the parameter set to
\gamma=0.01
. For all attack methods, the maximization problems
q(x)
were solved using the
quadprog
function in
MATLAB
. All computations were performed using
MATLAB
R2023a on a PC equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-12900K 3.20 GHz CPU and 128 GB RAM.

The experimental results are reported in Figure
3
. Compared with the three baseline methods, the proposed minimax attack demonstrates clear effectiveness. Extensive numerical experiments reveal that the random attack generally fails to produce noticeable impact. Both the random and no-long attacks are consistently ineffective, primarily because the adversary incurs a quadratic cost term
\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Hx
, which constrains the set of admissible strategies. The NI attack remains effective but exhibits inferior performance relative to the proposed minimax attack. When
0\leq b\leq\tilde{b}
, the solution
z^{\ast}
of the unconstrained problem (
40
) satisfies
s(z^{\ast})\leq 0
. In this regime, the minimax attack and NI attack exhibit similar levels of relative reduction
\rho
. When
\tilde{b}<b\leq 12
, both the attacker
x
and the investor
y
are influenced by the liquidity constraints. In this range, the relative reduction
\rho
of the minimax attack first decreases and then increases. This behavior is explained by Figure
3(b)
, which depicts the expected return
q
(i.e., the objective function value) before and after the attack. The non-monotonic trend in
\rho
arises because the rate of decline in
q
differs between the before and after attack, causing
\rho
to initially decrease and subsequently increase. As liquidity becomes nearly exhausted (
b
approaching
12
), the minimax attack exhibits pronounced effectiveness. Moreover, the experiments indicate that as market liquidity deteriorates corresponding to smaller values of
(12-b)
, a larger number of variables enter the active set, which is consistent with empirical observations in real markets.

.

Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate a minimax quadratic programming problem with coupled inequality constraints.
Motivated by the augmented Lagrangian method for equality constrained minimax optimization problems proposed by Dai and Zhang
[
6
]
,
we focus on extending dual active set methods to inequality constrained settings by leveraging the duality theorem introduced by Tsaknakis et al.
[
25
]
.
Under Assumption
1
, we establish that the S-pair does not repeat and that the proposed dual algorithm terminates in a finite number of iterations,
ensured by the monotonic decrease of the objective function value.
We further develop a numerically stable implementation of the algorithm using Cholesky factorization and Givens rotations,
and validate its performance through numerical experiments including randomly generated minimax quadratic programs and an adversarial attack on a mean-covariance portfolio model. Additionally, we provide illustrative examples that detail the dual algorithm’s iterative behavior.

Several directions for future research remain open.
First, although the proposed method ensures finite termination under Assumption
1
,
an effective strategy for selecting the violated constraint may substantially enhance computational efficiency and applicability to broader problem classes.
Second, it is of interest to extend this method to sequential minimax quadratic programming,
drawing inspiration from sequential quadratic programming (SQP) methods in nonlinear optimization.
