Response law and limit of triple bore pressure relief in coal seams under mining induced load
Authors: Zhang Lixin, Deng Zehui, Li Gang, Liu Guangchao, Lian Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the response characteristics and ultimate failure mechanisms of large-diameter triple-bore pressure-relief drill holes in coal seams under mining-induced incremental static loading. Combining theoretical analysis, laboratory experiments, and numerical simulations, we systematically examine borehole mechanical behavior, energy evolution, and structural stability. Theoretical results show incremental loading initially expands fracture and plastic zones, promoting stress relief. Continued loading induces borehole collapse where fractured coal expands, filling the cavity and restricting deformation space, ultimately attenuating pressure relief capacity. Laboratory tests reveal a 30.9% increase in coal sample peak strength when loading rates rise from 0.1 to 1.5 mm/min. Input elastic energy grows while dissipated energy declines, indicating reduced energy dissipation and increasingly brittle failure at higher loading rates. PFC simulations demonstrate tensile-dominated crack development under high loading increments. Force chain instability and abrupt contact force increases cause progressive deterioration of pressure relief effectiveness.FLAC3D simulations further confirm reduced plastic zone extent and impaired stress transfer capacity under high stress concentrations, forming localized high-energy zones that significantly weaken pressure relief. This work provides theoretical and engineering foundations for designing pressure relief boreholes in rockburst-prone coal seams.Keywords: Mining-induced incremental static loading, Large-diameter triangular borehole pattern, Energy evolution, Brittle failure, Rockburst preventionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Coal is a globally important energy source. With the development of coal resource mining to the deep, the primary challenge is high geostress. Under the action of high geostress, the rock body exhibits characteristics such as large deformation, significant rheological properties, and short dynamic response. Therefore, the prevention of rockburst is an important guarantee for the safe production of deep mines1–7. Drilled hole pressure relief has the advantages of small interference, convenient construction, strong applicability, and remarkable effect, and is the main prevention and control method used in most impact ground pressure mines 8–11.

Scholars have conducted a large number of studies on the stress distribution, displacement evolution, optimisation of pressure relief drilling parameters, and evaluation of the effect of drilling pressure relief in drilled coal-rock bodies10,12–16. Zhao14 established an intrinsic relationship between drill diameter, number of drill rows and energy evolution (energy dissipation and impact energy index), and concluded that the pressure unloading effect of triangularly arranged drill holes is better than that of conventional single-row drill holes. Zhang15 concluded that the higher the density of the drill holes, the more developed the fissures produced, the more energy released, and the better the pressure relief effect. Wu10 investigated the effect of hole shape on the mechanical properties and fracture characteristics of hole-bearing rocks under uniaxial loading, and found that circular holes had the best stability. Yao16 proposed a segmented large-diameter drilling technique to protect the support system while reducing the surrounding rock stress.

However, two critical gaps remain. First, most existing research focuses on the static relief effect of boreholes, while the dynamic evolution of abutment pressure during mining and its superposition with the quasi-stable state of pre-drilled boreholes has received little attention. Second, although triangular large-diameter boreholes have been increasingly applied in practice, their response to incremental static loads induced by face advancement and the limitations of their unloading capacity remain unclear. Addressing these gaps is essential for ensuring the reliability of borehole pressure relief under complex deep mining conditions17–21.

To fill this research gap, the present study systematically investigates the anti-impact characteristics of large-diameter, triangularly arranged boreholes in coal seams subjected to mining-induced incremental static loads. A combined methodology of theoretical analysis, laboratory experiments, and numerical simulation is employed. The findings are expected to reveal the response mechanisms and limitations of triangular boreholes, thereby providing new theoretical guidance for the design and application of pressure-relief drilling in deep mines.

Determination of the static load model for large-diameter pressure relief drilling holes

The large-diameter borehole pressure relief technique involves drilling large-diameter boreholes (typically ≥ 100 mm) in coal stress concentration zones. This process induces the ejection of substantial coal powder, thereby expanding the scope of the failure zone around the boreholes. Consequently, it effectively reduces the peak stress within the coal mass and shifts the peak stress location away from the roadway ribs (as illustrated in Fig. 1). After implementing pressure relief boreholes, the peak stress in the coal seam decreases from σmax to σ′max, while the distance from the peak stress location to the roadway rib increases from L1 to L2. This mechanism provides effective protection for the roof and surrounding coal of the roadway sides. With the continuous application of incremental static loading, the surrounding rock undergoes structural adjustments. Consequently, both the fractured zone and the plastic zone expand progressively with sustained incremental static loading. Furthermore, the circumferential stress within the plastic zone increases progressively with its expansion, reaching its peak value at the elastic–plastic interface.

Due to constraints on borehole diameter imposed during construction, the range of coal weakening achievable by the borehole is inherently limited. Consequently, the extent of the pressure-relief zone cannot increase indefinitely with static loading. Upon reaching a certain level of static loading, the borehole’s pressure-relief zone attains a critical limit state. Exceeding this limit results in stress recovery within the pressure-relief zone, rendering the large-diameter borehole ineffective for pressure relief. Therefore, under conditions of high static load concentration, assuming the coal within the fractured zone undergoes complete failure, the collapsed coal fills the entire borehole due to the dilatancy effect post-fracture. Under these circumstances, the extent of the fractured zone ceases to expand. Static loading test on drilled coal samples

As the working face advances, the distribution of advance support pressure continuously migrates, imposing a loading effect on the coal mass. Variations in mining speed lead to different magnitudes of loading and distinct modes of influence on the coal body. In essence, changes in mining speed correspond to adjustments in the loading rate per unit time applied to the coal mass. Accordingly, the investigation of borehole responses under incremental static loading essentially reflects how support pressure induced by different mining speeds imposes loading effects on the boreholes. On this basis, static loading experiments were conducted on prefabricated coal specimens containing unloading boreholes. By analysing the stress evolution, energy dissipation, and fracture morphology of the borehole-containing coal specimens under different loading rates, this study elucidates the effects of incremental static loading induced by mining disturbances on unloading boreholes.

The test coal specimens were collected from the main and auxiliary transport roadways of the 61,607 working face in the Longwang Gou Coal Mine. In accordance with sampling protocols, three intact coal specimens and fifteen borehole-containing coal specimens were prepared, yielding a total of eighteen samples. Owing to equipment limitations, a borehole diameter of 10 mm was adopted to simulate the unloading borehole, substituting for the large-diameter (150 mm) unloading boreholes typically encountered in situ.Preparation of borehole coal specimens: In the laboratory, a 10 mm borehole was first drilled using processing equipment. Subsequently, the coal sample was cut and processed into a cubic specimen with dimensions of 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm (Fig. 4a). The region surrounding the borehole was carefully ground and polished. The processed coal specimen was then further polished with sandpaper to ensure the smoothness and integrity of all end faces.

Higher loading rates concentrate mechanical loading within shorter timeframes, leading to significant early-stage energy accumulation during the compaction phase. At this stage, dissipated energy dominates due to the pre-cracking effect of boreholes: pre-existing microcracks undergo compaction, stable propagation, and rupture, converting input and stored elastic energy into dissipated energy. As loading progresses into the elastic–plastic phase, rising stress levels promote energy storage dominance, where input energy is preferentially converted to elastic energy. Upon reaching peak strength, a sudden release of stored elastic energy triggers sample failure, accompanied by a sharp surge in dissipated energy.

Under lower loading rates, dissipated energy exhibits higher fluctuation amplitude. Boreholes weaken the coal structure, allowing quasi-static energy dissipation through prolonged crack development, thereby reducing elastic energy release during failure and mitigating peak stress intensity. Conversely, high loading rates induce rapid energy accumulation but inhibit timely crack-mediated dissipation, resulting in flattened dissipation trends. This culminates in catastrophic instability characterized by instantaneous release of substantial elastic energy, markedly amplifying failure severity.

PFC simulation

Establishment of numerical model and calibration of microstructural parameters

The Discrete Element Method (DEM) is founded on discrete media mechanics theory. It characterizes the macroscopic mechanical behavior of materials through particle assemblies, overcoming the macroscopic continuity assumption limitations of continuum mechanics. This approach is particularly suitable for simulating nonlinear problems such as large rock deformations and fracturing.

The specific process of model establishment is as follows: Firstly, set the parameters of the linear contact model and its random factors, and generate the rigid walls on the top and bottom as the boundary for applying the load. The size of the area enclosed by the walls is 100 × 100 × 100 mm; then, randomly generate spherical particles within this area, and set the parameters such as the density and local damping of the particles; execute the “solve” command to calculate in a loop until the particle assembly reaches equilibrium, and generate a coal-rock model with the same size as the laboratory experiment (size of 100 × 100 × 100 mm); finally, change the contact model between particles to a parallel bonding model, and reset the particle displacements and the contact forces (torques) they receive, so as to obtain the three-dimensional numerical model required for the numerical simulation(Fig. 8).

PFC Model Diagram (particle count = 6334).

When using the discrete element numerical simulation software to construct a three-dimensional numerical model of composite coal-rock, the parallel bonding model was adopted in this paper. The parallel bonding model has the following five main characteristics:

The parallel bonding model degenerates into a linear contact model after bond failure. The parallel bonding part acts on the sample simultaneously under tension and compression conditions, but the elastic modulus under compression and tension is different, while the linear contact part only acts under compression;

The normal and tangential stiffness ratios control the Poisson’s ratio of the sample’s elastic deformation, and they are linearly correlated;

The effective modulus of the parallel bonding model controls the elastic modulus, and they are linearly correlated;

The ratio of normal bonding force to tangential bonding force plays a role in controlling the failure mode of the sample;

The parallel bonding friction angle has no significant effect on the deformation before failure, and the linear contact part only acts after the parallel bonding fails, so the modulus is generally the same as the effective modulus of the parallel bonding, and the stiffness ratio is also the same.

The PFC3D numerical simulation software adjusts the translational speed of the walls through the servo system to drive the constraint stress (the stress exerted by the walls on the sample) to maintain a certain value, achieving incremental loading of the numerical simulation sample of the drilled coal sample, that is, by writing a servo control function in the “Fish” language to control the speed of the walls, and then applying the load to the sample to ensure that the numerical simulation experiment has the same loading path as the laboratory experiment. Analysis of the destruction process of different loaded incremental coal samples

As shown in the Fig. 10, fracture initiation consistently occurs at the sample ends (contact zones with loading plates), where stress concentration first reaches critical thresholds. In borehole-containing coal samples, fractures preferentially propagate through borehole peripheries. Secondary cracks develop and coalesce within rock bridge zones. Borehole-navigated through-going fractures form demonstrating the path-guiding effect of boreholes on crack propagation. Fracture nucleation originates from structural plane slippage, causing displacement surges around cracks. At this stage, localized damage appears in borehole coal samples. Overall bearing capacity remains intact. With increasing loading increments, secondary cracks continuously develop around boreholes. Borehole collapse phenomena occur concurrently. During initial through-borehole fracture formation: Tensile cracks exceed shear cracks, tensile cracks increase dramatically, shear crack growth shows negligible change. Ultimately, samples lose bearing capacity via brittle tensile failure. As shown in Fig. 11, the failure mode displayed by the numerical simulation is in agreement with the actual experiment.

Macroscopic evolution characteristics of the force chain

Figure 12 shows the force chain characteristics under the 80% peak stress state after the peak in different incremental static loading processes.

Evolution characteristics of force chains failure in different loaded incremental coal samples.

At Low-loading-rate stage, strong and weak force chains are uniformly distributed within the rock mass, forming areticulated load-bearing network. Strong force chains predominantly bear axial loads, with stress transmitted through multi-level branched structures, exhibiting a tree like topological configuration. With Progressive damage development, as loading increases, force chain-sparse zones emerge along potential failure planes, gradually expanding into blank bands(low-force-chain-density regions).When these sparse zones fully interconnect, residual force chains on both sides and within the damage region experience abrupt force intensification .At peak stress, distinct strong-force-chain belts form, marking the onset of macroscopic instability. At High-loading-rate stage, force chains in damaged zones migrate and reorganize toward adjacent areas, causing localized force chain strengthening. Multiple force chain arch structures develop: Weak force chains concentrate along the arch interior to provide support. The arch structures maintain transient stability through load redistribution. Arch feet extend with failure surface propagation, eventually merging to form continuous strong-force-chain belts. High loading rates inducebond breakage and microcrack initiation at grain contacts. Reduced contact points force partial weak force chains to transform into strong ones, compensating for load-bearing loss and causing monotonic increase in maximum contact force. Despite declining total force chains, strong force chain intensity rises due to reorganization. Macroscopic failure occurs when the strong/weak force chain strength disparity reaches a critical threshold. Increased loading rate reduces force chain quantity while amplifying contact forces, indicating imminent force-chain-system collapse and loss of energy dissipation capacity. Under static loading, fractured coal gradually collapses, fills the borehole, and undergoes dilatancy-induced compaction. This reduces deformation compensation space, constrains plastic zone expansion, and diminishes pressure-relief capacity.

Numerical simulation of incremental static load responses of large-diameter pressure relief drilling holes

During the mining process of the working face, the spatial structure of the surrounding rock is constantly changing due to the influence of mining activities, and the advance support pressure continuously migrates forward, gradually loading the coal body. According to the distribution form of the support pressure, the affected area is mainly divided into the original rock stress zone, elastic zone, pressurization zone, and depressurization zone. In the coal seam with impact ground pressure, the relief drilling holes are generally constructed at a certain distance ahead of the working face to pre-relieve the coal seam, so the stress state of the relief drilling holes mainly undergoes the original rock stress zone, elastic zone, and pressurization zone. Therefore, the main form of the drilling hole being affected by the advance support pressure is static load progressive loading. This section conducts a numerical simulation study on the static load response law of large-diameter relief drilling holes.

Under static loading, pressure relief boreholes primarily undergo shear failure along paths between adjacent boreholes, reducing the surrounding rock mass bearing capacity. The interconnection of these failures generates plastic zones that facilitate stress transfer and release, thereby achieving pressure relief. As loading progresses, stress redistribution occurs through the continual adjustment of plastic deformation around the boreholes.

As shown in the Fig. 15 and Fig. 16, analysis of the evolving stress distribution reveals several key trends. Distinct low-stress zones (identified as red and orange) form near the borehole peripheries; however, increasing the loading rate significantly reduces the area of these low-stress zones by 18% to 30%, indicating a measurable decrease in pressure relief efficacy. Beyond the plastic zone boundary, horizontal stress recovers to the virgin in-situ stress level of 30.0 MPa. Concurrently, vertical stress in the near-borehole region undergoes a substantial increase, escalating from approximately 2.0 MPa to about 14.0 MPa under increasing load. Furthermore, localized zones of high stress concentration emerge between adjacent boreholes, particularly noticeable at elevated loading rates.

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that under elevated loading rates, stress transfer mechanisms become insufficient. This insufficiency progressively diminishes both the spatial scope (manifested in the reduced low-stress area) and the magnitude of the achievable pressure relief.

Conclusions

This study focuses on the mechanical response patterns, energy evolution characteristics, and failure limits of large-diameter triple-bore pressure-relief drill holes in coal seams under incremental static loads induced by mining activities during deep mining operations. Through a combination of theoretical analysis, laboratory experiments, and numerical simulations, the mechanical response patterns, energy evolution characteristics, and failure limits of these boreholes were systematically investigated. At high loading rates, stress concentration intensifies, plastic deformation capacity diminishes, and brittle failure characteristics become pronounced. Inputc elastic energy increases with loading rate, while dissipated energy exhibits a decreasing trend. As static loads increase, secondary cracks around the borehole continue to propagate. The coal mass in the fracture zone gradually collapses and fills the borehole, reducing the free deformation space and limiting the expansion of the plastic zone. Consequently, the pressure release capacity progressively diminishes. This provides theoretical basis and engineering reference for optimizing borehole parameters and designing anti-collapse measures under conditions of strong mining-induced stress and high stress. Therefore, relying solely on a single large-diameter borehole for pressure relief and impact prevention is limited. Multiple combined pressure-relief measures should be adopted, such as pre-cracking blasting in the roof, hydraulic fracturing, and blasting pressure relief within the coal seam, to enhance overall pressure reduction and impact resistance.
