Nonlinear association between body roundness index and all-cause mortality in myocardial infarction survivors
Authors: Shaoqiang Qin, Zhiqin Fang, Tianshu Gu, Sutao Hu, Jinhua Zhao, Xian Shao, Tong Liu, Yongjian Li, Kang-Yin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While anthropometric indices predict cardiovascular mortality, the novel body roundness index (BRI)‘s association with all-cause mortality in myocardial infarction (MI) survivors remains unknown. This cohort study analyzed 2099 National Health and Nutrition Examination participants (1999–2018) with prior MI. Mortality was ascertained via National Death Index linkage (median follow-up ~ 9 years). Using Cox regression with covariate adjustment and penalized splines, we identified an L-shaped BRI-mortality relationship with an inflection point at 3.99. Below this threshold, each 1-unit BRI increase significantly reduced mortality risk (hazard ratio = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.50–0.75, P < .0001). Above 3.99, BRI showed no association (hazard ratio = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.98–1.06, P = .4242). This threshold pattern persisted in subgroups. Our findings first demonstrate that lower BRI was associated with higher mortality in MI survivors, suggesting maintaining BRI above critical thresholds may confer survival benefits.

1. Introduction

All-cause mortality risk after a myocardial infarction (MI) remains substantial despite advances in cardiovascular therapies.[1] Survivors of MI are a high-risk group in whom secondary prevention and risk stratification are critically important.[2] Beyond traditional cardiac risk factors, overall nutritional and metabolic status: often proxied by body composition: may influence prognosis in these patients.[3] Numerous studies in populations with cardiovascular disease have reported an “obesity paradox” where in overweight or mildly obese patients experience lower mortality than their leaner counterparts. For example, Bucholz et al observed that MI patients with higher body mass index (BMI) had significantly lower 1-year mortality, with a nadir of risk in the overweight range and increased risk only at very extreme obesity.[4] This counter-intuitive finding has spurred interest in alternative measures of body fat and its distribution, since BMI alone cannot distinguish adiposity from lean mass or central vs peripheral fat storage.[5]

Conventional anthropometric indices each have limitations. BMI is a simple indicator of body size but fails to capture fat distribution or body shape.[6] Waist circumference (WC) is often used to gauge central obesity, which is more strongly linked to cardiometabolic risk.[7] In recent years, new composite indices have been developed to better reflect body shape and adiposity distribution. One such metric is the Body Roundness Index (BRI), introduced by Thomas et al as a geometrical index derived from height and waist circumference. BRI was designed to estimate percentage body fat and visceral adipose tissue volume from a simple anthropometric measurement.[8] Because BRI incorporates waist circumference, it more directly captures central obesity than BMI.[9]

Emerging evidence suggests that BRI is associated with various health outcomes. Higher BRI has been linked to greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular conditions.[9,10] In the general U.S. adult population, a recent study by Zhang et al found a U-shaped association relationship between BRI and mortality.[11] Similarly, in a cohort of osteoporotic older adults, Ding et al observed that higher BRI was independently associated with lower all-cause mortality; The relationship in that study was also described as L-shaped, with a threshold around BRI = 5 defining the point of risk-curve inflection.[12] These findings raise the question of whether BRI might similarly capture an “optimal” range of adiposity for survival in other high-risk groups, such as MI survivors, and whether very low BRI (indicating leanness) identifies individuals at excess risk.

To fill these knowledge gaps, our primary objective was to investigate the association between BRI and all-cause mortality in U.S. adults with a history of MI, hypothesizing a nonlinear relationship.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

We performed an observational cohort analysis using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination (NHANES 1999 to 2018). NHANES is a continuous series of cross-sectional surveys of the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized population, which includes detailed interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests.[13] Crucially, NHANES data can be linked to the National Death Index (NDI) to ascertain mortality outcomes for participants. For this study, we pooled 10 survey cycles (1999–2000 through 2017–2018) and identified participants aged ≥18 years who reported a history of MI. MI history was determined via a questionnaire item asking if a doctor or health professional ever told the participant that they had a heart attack (corresponding to MI). Baseline for each participant was the NHANES examination date.

We excluded participants with missing data on all-cause mortality. Additionally, participants lacking waist circumference and height measurements were also excluded, as these measurements were essential for calculating BRI (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of study participants. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

The NHANES study was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

2.2. Data availability statement

The data used in this study are publicly available from the NHANES database (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx) and the NDI (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm).

2.3. Exposure: BRI

The exposure of interest, BRI, is a continuous index derived from anthropometric measurements. At the NHANES physical exam, standing height (in m) and waist circumference (WC, in m) were measured by trained technicians using standardized protocols. We calculated each participant’s BRI using the formula proposed by Thomas et al[8] BRI = 364.2–365.5 × (1- [WC (m)/ 2π] 2/[0.5 × height (m)]2)½.

2.4. Outcome: all-cause mortality

The outcome was all-cause mortality during follow-up. Mortality status and time-to-event were determined by linkage of NHANES participants to the NDI records through December 31, 2019.[14] We calculated person-time from the NHANES exam date to the date of death or December 31, 2019, whichever came first. Participants not found to have died by that date were treated as censored alive. Follow-up time varied between participants based on survey cycle entry; the maximum follow-up was about 20 years (for those from 1999–2000 entering cohort) and the median follow-up was approximately 9 years.

2.5. Covariates

We included a comprehensive set of baseline covariates known or suspected to be associated with mortality and potentially with adiposity, to address confounding. Demographic variables included age (years), sex (male or female), ethnicity (categorized by NHANES as Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, or Other race/ethnicity), and marital status (married/cohabiting vs unmarried). Socioeconomic status was represented by the (poverty-income ratio, a ratio of household income to the federal poverty level) and education level (less than high school, high school graduate/GED, or some college/college graduate). Lifestyle factors included cigarette smoking status (never, former, or current smoker), alcohol use (classified as none, moderate, or heavy based on self-reported drinking frequency/quantity, with heavy drinking roughly corresponding to >14 drinks/week for men or >7 for women), and total physical activity level. Physical activity was quantified as metabolic equivalent task (MET) minutes per week (MET-min/week) from the NHANES physical activity questionnaire, summing across work, leisure, and transport activities; we treated this as a continuous variable (log-transformed in models due to skewness). Clinical comorbidities included hypertension (yes/no, defined by self-reported physician diagnosis or measured BP ≥ 140/90 mm Hg or use of antihypertensive medication) and diabetes mellitus (yes/no, defined by self-report of diagnosis, use of glucose-lowering medication, or fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%). These conditions are common in MI patients and are associated with both adiposity and mortality risk.

2.6. Statistical analysis

We first described the study sample characteristics by tertiles of BRI to understand how participants with different body roundness profiles differed in demographics, lifestyle, and comorbidities. We present means ± standard deviation or proportions for key variables across BRI tertiles.

Because our focus was on the shape of the BRI-mortality relationship, we used a generalized additive model (GAM) and a restricted cubic spline to model BRI and risk of death in our preliminary analysis to visualize its dose-response curve. A GAM and a smooth curve fitting were performed in order to address the association between the BRI and the BMI, as well as waist circumference.

To more formally test for a threshold effect, we employed a 2-piecewise Cox regression model.This approach, as described by Hu et al involved identifying a turning point in the exposure-outcome curve and estimating 2 separate hazard ratios (HRs) (slopes) on either side of that point.[15] This optimal cut-point was then confirmed by comparing the goodness-of-fit of the 2-piecewise model against a single-line (no threshold) model using a log-likelihood ratio test.

For additional insight, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in the subgroup of participants with hypertension (since hypertension is common in MI survivors and adiposity might have different implications in this group).

The 2-sided alpha level was set at 0.05. All the statistical analyses were performed using the EmpowerStats (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y solutions, Inc. Boston MA) and R software version 3.6.1 (http://www.r-project.org).

3.2. BRI and all-cause mortality: nonlinear association

The median follow-up duration was 6.75 (interquartile range, 3.5–10.92) years. During 15,932 personyears of follow-up, 956 all-cause deaths occurred.To address the nonlinearity of the relation between BRI and all-cause mortality, a Cox proportional hazards regression model with GAM and smooth curve fitting (penalized spline method) was conducted. The fully adjusted smooth curve fitting showed a nonlinear association, characterized by an “L”-shaped curve, between BRI and mortality risk (Fig. 2).

3.3. Threshold effect and two-piecewise cox model

Using the 2-piecewise Cox regression approach, we quantitatively confirmed a threshold effect at BRI = 3.99. This value (3.99) was identified as the inflection point that maximized model likelihood, indicating a change in slope of the BRI-mortality relationship. We compared a model with a single linear term for BRI vs a 2-segment model with a break at 3.99; the latter showed significantly better fit (likelihood ratio test P < .001), supporting the presence of a threshold (nonlinear) association.

In the piecewise model, for BRI < 3.99, the HR per 1-unit higher BRI was 0.62 (95% CI: 0.50–0.75, P < .0001). This indicates that in the lower BRI range (approximately corresponding to lean to mildly overweight individuals), each one-unit increase in BRI was associated with a 38% reduction in mortality hazard. For BRI ≥ 3.99, the HR per 1-unit increase was 1.02 (95% CI: 0.98–1.06, P = .4242), which is statistically null (Table 2).

3.4.2. Additional subgroup analyses

Additional subgroup analyses stratified by age(<60 vs ≥60 years), sex (male vs female), and diabetes status (yes vs no) revealed an inverse association between BRI and mortality at BRI levels below 3.99 across all subgroups, with mortality increasing as BRI decreased(Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2, Supplemental Digital Content and Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content 3, Supplemental Digital Content).

4. Discussion

This retrospective cohort study found BRI was associated with all-cause mortality in patients with MI in the NHANES database between 1999 and 2018. The major finding was that the association between the BRI and the risk of all-cause mortality was L-shaped, higher BRI was associated with lower mortality risk up to a threshold. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the association between the BRI and all-cause mortality in MI survivors.

To improve the clinical interpretability of our findings, we translated the BRI threshold into commonly used anthropometric indicators. The threshold value of 3.99 roughly equates to a BMI of 23.75 kg/m2 and a waist circumference of 90.07 cm, which are typically considered within the lower-normal range in general populations. Notably, 13.34% of the MI survivors in our cohort fell below this BRI threshold. This subgroup may represent individuals with insufficient adipose reserves or underlying frailty, potentially explaining their elevated mortality risk at lower BRI levels. Previous studies have similarly suggested that overly low adiposity may be detrimental in patients with chronic diseases, possibly due to decreased metabolic reserve or sarcopenia-related mechanisms.[16,17]

In our present study, we found that the smooth curve fitting showed a L-shaped association between BRI and all-cause mortality. Some previous studies also indicated L-shaped associations of BRI and mortality. Ding et al used data from 1596 osteoporotic individuals aged 50 and above in the NHANES and found that higher BRI was significantly associated with better long-term survival, showing an “L”-shaped nonlinear inverse relationship with mortality, with a threshold at BRI = 5.[12] Contrasting with the L-shaped association identified in our study, emerging evidence suggests a U-shaped relationship between BRI and mortality. Zhou et al demonstrated this pattern in a prospective cohort of 47,356 adults.[18] Zhang et al used data from 15,157 participants over 40 years old in NHANES and reported that both BRI and BMI also had a U-shaped association with the risk of all-cause mortality in frail individuals, and the 2 had comparable abilities to predict the risk of all-cause mortality.[19] Recently, a nonlinear association of BRI with all-cause mortality was reported in a large sample of a Chinese middle-aged and older population by Ding et al.[20] All of the above studies had shown that BRI was associated with mortality, and the relationship was non-linear.

The observed L-shaped association between BRI and all-cause mortality suggests that while excessive adiposity may not be beneficial, extremely low BRI values are also detrimental, particularly among MI survivors. A BRI below 3.99 reflects a combination of low BMI and low waist circumference, which may indicate a state of undernutrition, reduced lean body mass, or frailty. In post-MI patients, insufficient metabolic reserve and muscle mass can compromise recovery and increase vulnerability to adverse outcomes.[21] Several studies have shown that underweight status and low body fat are associated with increased mortality in patients with cardiovascular disease.[22,23] One explanation is the presence of sarcopenia or cachexia, which are linked to systemic inflammation, impaired immune response, and reduced cardiopulmonary function.[24] Additionally, a low waist circumference may reflect diminished visceral fat stores, which, while often considered harmful in excess, may play a protective metabolic and endocrine role when present at adequate levels.[25] Moreover, low BRI may serve as a surrogate marker for frailty, a multidimensional syndrome characterized by decreased strength, endurance, and physiological function. Frailty has been independently associated with higher mortality after MI.[26] Thus, maintaining a modest level of adiposity might be beneficial for survival in this high-risk group.

Given the established role of hypertension as a critical modifier of all-cause mortality,we conducted stratified threshold effect analyses to assess the robustness of the BRI-mortality association.[27] Notably, penalized spline models confirmed an L-shaped relationship in hypertensive individuals, paralleling the overall cohort,s trajectory.However, multiplicative interaction testing revealed no significant effect modification by hypertension status (P-interaction: .873),suggesting that the fundamental adiposity-mortality linkage operates independently of blood pressure dysregulation. Additional subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, and diabetes status demonstrated that the protective association of moderate BRI levels and the elevated mortality risk linked to very low BRI were consistently observed across diverse subpopulations of MI survivors.

A large number of studies have found that the BRI is associated with all-cause mortality, and the possible mechanism is as follows: Insulin resistance A higher BRI value often signals an increase in abdominal adipose tissue, which contributes to systemic metabolic disturbances. Specifically, abdominal fat cells secrete bioactive molecules such as free fatty acids and inflammatory factors, which disrupt insulin signaling pathways. This dysfunction may lead to insulin resistance, characterized by the body’s decreased ability to respond effectively to insulin.[28] Over time, if these metabolic dysfunctions remain unmanaged, they can pave the way for chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disorders and renal impairments, significantly increasing the risk of all-cause mortality.[29] Chronic inflammation Abdominal adipose tissue functions not only as an energy reservoir but also as an active endocrine organ that secretes inflammatory factors such as interleukin-6. These factors contribute to a chronic low-grade inflammatory state within the body, leading to tissue and organ damage.[28] Persistent inflammation can exacerbate the development of conditions such as atherosclerosis, insulin resistance, and tumors, thereby heightening the risk of all-cause mortality.[30] Research indicates that visceral fat is particularly potent in this regard, as it has been shown to release pro-inflammatory cytokines, which significantly impair insulin sensitivity and promote metabolic disorders.[31] Furthermore, the correlation between inflammatory markers and various diseases underscores the critical role of abdominal fat in health deterioration and longevity.[32]

Several limitations should be noted. First, causality cannot be established in an observational study. While we discuss mechanisms, one must be cautious not to infer that increasing one’s BRI would directly improve survival. Randomized trials of weight change would be needed to prove causation, and those are challenging. Second, BRI was measured only at baseline. We did not account for changes in weight or waist over time, which could be informative (some patients may lose weight due to advanced heart failure or other illness, which often portends worse outcomes). A single baseline measure may misclassify the exposure during follow-up, likely biasing results toward the null: yet we still saw a strong effect. Third, residual confounding is possible. We adjusted for many factors, but dietary quality, specific medications, or unmeasured disease severity (e.g., left ventricular ejection fraction, extent of coronary disease) could influence both weight and mortality. If, for instance, sicker MI survivors had lower BRI (due to cachexia) and higher mortality that could partly drive the association. We tried to mitigate this by adjusting for comorbidities, but we cannot fully rule it out. Fourth, our decision to not use NHANES sampling weights means our results are not strictly generalizable to the U.S. MI survivor population in terms of prevalence estimates. However, for exposure-outcome associations, unweighted analysis should yield unbiased estimates if the model is properly specified. Fifth, our population is heterogeneous (MI events could have occurred recently or decades before NHANES participation, and subsequent treatments were not known). We did not have data on time since MI or on cardiac rehabilitation, which might modify the relationship between adiposity and outcomes. Finally, we focused on all-cause mortality; it would be useful to examine cardiovascular-specific mortality and non-cardiovascular mortality separately to see if the obesity paradox is driven by one cause (some studies suggest obesity protects more against non-CV deaths like cancer or infection in cardiac patients). Our sample size and data limitations prevented a detailed cause-specific analysis. It is worth noting that while BRI incorporates both body shape and adiposity, our study was not intended to evaluate its predictive superiority over BMI or waist circumference. Future research using formal predictive modeling approaches is needed to directly compare these indices in terms of risk stratification.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study provided evidence that BRI showed an L-shaped association with all-cause mortality among U.S. adults with a history of MI. Lower BRI (leaner body shape) was associated with significantly higher mortality risk.
