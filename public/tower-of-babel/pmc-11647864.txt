Techno-economic study of gas turbines with hydrogen, ammonia, and their mixture fuels
Authors: Amir Mohammad Yazdani, Mohsen Salimi, Majid Amidpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ammonia is a versatile energy carrier without carbon emissions that can be used for power generation. In this article, a techno-economic analysis has been done to predict the levelized cost of electricity production using gas turbines with clean fuel in Iran. In the technical discussion, the analysis of different scenarios of ammonia and hydrogen fuel composition ratio was done, and by keeping the turbine inlet temperature to the same gas turbine as the SGT5-2000E turbine, the output power in different fuel ratios is around 192.8–229.0 MW was variable and reached the maximum value in some proportions. Also, in the economic discussion, the effects of fuel cost and interest rate parameters were investigated, sensitivity analysis was performed on different combined ratios of ammonia and hydrogen in fuel, and an economic analysis of the ideal ratio was conducted. The price of ammonia fuel was calculated from 222 $/ton to 2000 $/ton, and the levelized cost of electricity production changed from 91.7 $/MWh to 673.4 $/MWh. Additionally, an economic comparison was made between the utilization of ammonia-hydrogen and natural gas fuels. This alternative fuel can be a promising way to produce power without carbon emissions and suitable storage for renewables.

Introduction

The main factor in climate change is the emission of greenhouse gases caused by burning fossil fuels. Compared to before the industry, the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has increased by more than 40 % [1]. Initial costs for solar and wind energy have decreased in the last ten years [2], and these methods have also become more efficient and have led to lower energy prices. These matters make renewable energy attractive for energy production and energy storage in chemicals such as ammonia. The fact that the best locations for solar or wind energy production aren't always the locations with the greatest energy needs and that the necessary quantity may not always be accessible at the right moment is one of the critical problems with producing renewable energy. Mainly, too much renewable energy is produced when demand is low. The cost of connecting to the distribution network can be an essential obstacle for using such resources [3]. Therefore, by transforming electrical energy into chemicals, energy can be stored to be used at the time and place needed to convert energy into the desired form. Ammonia production industries can potentially play an essential role.

Expansion of the number of renewable resources in power generation is limited due to their intermittent and unpredictable nature. Without utilizing energy storage devices, increasing the proportion of renewable energy sources will not be viable when production and demand are balanced [4]. The basic issue for a storage device is to keep the stored energy ready until it is needed and release it at the required rate. For this reason, numerous studies have been conducted to develop hydrogen technology as a safe and dependable substitute for conventional fossil fuels with this unique property. There are numerous methods for producing hydrogen, which can be categorized into two broad groups, namely traditional and renewable technologies, depending on the raw materials employed [5]. The first group uses hydrocarbon reforming techniques and pyrolysis to treat fossil fuels [5]. Steam reforming, partial oxidation, and auto-thermal reforming are the chemical processes employed in hydrocarbon reforming [5]. The second group comprises processes that create hydrogen from renewable resources like water or biomass [6]. These processes, which use biomass as a raw material, fall into two broad categories: thermochemical and biological. Pyrolysis, gasification, combustion, and liquefaction are the key thermochemical processes [6]. In contrast, the main biological processes are direct and indirect bio-photolysis, dark fermentation, light fermentation, and sequential dark and light fermentation. The second group of renewable technologies relates to techniques that employ water as the sole input and can produce hydrogen using water decomposition procedures such as electrolysis, thermolysis, and photo electrolysis [7].

The most economical way to produce hydrogen is methane reforming, followed by gasification (one of the partial oxidation techniques) [7]. However, alternate methods must be developed to lessen reliance on fossil fuels. The most likely forms of large-scale competition in the near future are thermochemical pyrolysis and gasification, both economical methods [7]. The integration of membrane reactors and coupled cycles employing alternative energy sources, such as gas output from gas turbines and solar energy, appear to be the short-term trend in decreasing fossil fuels [8]. Governments must enact carbon taxes and conduct additional research before water-splitting technologies may compete [9]. The problems associated with hydrogen storage, transportation, and consumption should be considered in addition to research on hydrogen production.

The world's second most widely used chemical, ammonia, is used as a fertilizer for crops, an industrial material, a refrigerant, and an additive [10]. Due to its higher hydrogen storage density compared to hydrogen, low storage pressure, excellent stability for long-term storage, high auto-ignition temperature, and low compression pressure, ammonia has recently received a lot of interest as a potential energy carrier. Ammonia can be produced from various primary energy sources, such as biomass, coal, natural gas, solar, wind, geothermal, water, and nuclear sources, just like hydrogen. Thermochemical, electrochemical, photochemical, and plasma conversion processes can all be used to create ammonia [11]. However, the three primary conversion technologies in this work are Haber-Busch processes, electrochemical cycle processes, and thermochemical processes [11], considering technological viability and overall energy efficiency.

The method that Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch created nearly 100 years ago is essentially still used in the modern ammonia production process (Fig. 1) [12]. The Haber-Bosch process is the name given to this system as a result. This technique generates over 85 % of the world's ammonia production [13]. Even though electrochemical processing is less developed than the Haber-Bosch process, it is anticipated to produce more energy. This process uses roughly 20 % less energy than Haber-Bosch [2]. An alternate method using a thermochemical cycle has been created to produce ammonia. The reduction (nitrogen activation) and steam hydrolysis (ammonia production) reactions make up this system [2].Fig. 1Schematic diagram of Haber-Bosch process ammonia production.Fig. 1

Currently, ammonia is produced using fossil fuel sources (gray ammonia) near gas or coal mining areas or using imported fossil fuels. One hundred eighty million tons of ammonia were produced worldwide in 2019; this resulted in the release of 0.45 gigatons of carbon dioxide [14]. If the factories continue to operate at their current production capacity for the remainder of their useful lives (20–50 years), they will produce between 4.4 and 15.5 gigatons of carbon dioxide emissions. According to projections, the demand for ammonia by existing users will rise by 25 % by 2050, and the amount of energy needed to produce that ammonia will more than double [15]. The production of ammonia using both blue hydrogen (fossil fuels with carbon sequestration technology) and green hydrogen (electrolysis with renewable energy sources) can, for a time, increase to meet the predicted world demand. Also, replacing ammonia production with existing conditions can cause a significant reduction in greenhouse gas emissions [16]. The high price of fossil fuels caused by the war between Russia and Ukraine can accelerate replacing gray ammonia production with green ammonia production. Creating incentives for these investments requires an appropriate legal framework. Ammonia production includes two stages of: raw material production and ammonia synthesis. Carbon absorption in the ammonia production process requires a change of conventional (gray) technologies towards green technologies. For this to happen, alternative processes are needed to produce raw materials. In the air separation unit for the synthesis of nitrogen and ammonia, for instance, using renewable energies, depending on their location, can help make the process more environmentally friendly by employing the electrolysis process for hydrogen instead of the steam methane reforming (SMR) process. For every ton of ammonia produced, existing facilities using coal or natural gas release around 1.6 and 3 tons of carbon dioxide, respectively [14]. Of course, it is possible to reduce this ratio to 0.2 tons of carbon dioxide per ton of ammonia by the carbon absorption process; the ammonia produced in this way is known as blue ammonia [14]. The move to green ammonia depends on the hydrogen-generating process. Natural gas or hydrocarbons are used in the SMR process to oxidize coal to create hydrogen partially [14]. Hydrogen is electrolyzed from pure water using clean, renewable energy to produce green ammonia. Green ammonia is of interest as an energy carrier, and several international organizations have published materials in their reports [[17], [18], [19]]. With more focus on green ammonia in the industry, its production and use will accelerate due to the production of this material from different renewable energies. Recently, there have been numerous attempts to employ ammonia in gas turbines and internal combustion engines [20]. The California Public Service Commission has considered replacing diesel generators with ammonia-based generators with representatives from the industry [21]. This commission plans to convert 350 MW of used diesel generators to ammonia fuel generators [22]. Japan has started a significant initiative to use ammonia to produce electricity. Ammonia-based systems are anticipated to provide 1 % of Japan's electricity needs [23]. Therefore, it was planned and started to use gas turbines with ammonia fuel [20]. A Japanese shipping company has announced the start of the ammonia fuel ships project [24]. Most ammonia production is done in the fertilizer industry, about 180 million tons per year [17]. Due to new uses of ammonia, such as transportation [25] and hydrogen trade [26], ammonia production will increase. The primary goal is carbon absorption in fertilizer production and new industries [27]. Currently, the production of green ammonia is technically feasible in New Zealand [28], Saudi Arabia [26], Norway [25], Chile [29] and Australia [30].

In recent studies, Ebrahim Dincer et al. have conducted investigations about green ammonia production processes and the combustion of ammonia mixture with common fuels [1,21,31,32]. Zac Cesaro et al. have worked on the combustion of ammonia and hydrogen mixtures in a gas turbine [33]. Valera Medina et al. [34] have worked on the combustion of an ammonia-hydrogen mixture using a rotary burner in a gas turbine and using methane, ammonia, and hydrogen fuel mixture. The effectiveness and management of nitrogen oxides in the gas turbine combustion chamber have been studied by Park et al. [35]. Rahimi et al. have investigated the effect of adding hydrogen on the methane-air flame in a stratified rotary burner [36]. Combustion characteristics of ammonia/methane premixed fuels were studied by Xiao et al.

There are several instances of internal combustion engines being powered by ammonia [4]. The engine architecture, the combustion process, and the fuel's characteristics all affect how well ammonia performs as an ICE fuel [37]. Compared to gasoline or diesel, ammonia contains less energy, but because of its higher octane rating, it can tolerate greater compression ratios without producing engine knock [8]. The low ignition temperature of ammonia is a significant obstacle when utilizing it as an ICE fuel; however, this may be addressed by utilizing a preheating device or spark plug [8]. In comparison to 2008 levels, the International Maritime Organization (IMO) has set aggressive targets to cut greenhouse gas emissions from the shipping sector by at least 50 % by 2050 [38]. An ammonia-fueled engine performed similarly to a diesel engine and released less particulate matter and NOx, according to a research by Sun et al. [39].

A 40 MW gas turbine (H-25 series) that can directly burn 100 % ammonia was announced by Mitsubishi Power in 2020 [40]. In 2018, a 15 MW Turboden gas turbine powered by ammonia was erected in a cogeneration facility located in Germany [8]. It can run on either pure ammonia or a combination of ammonia and natural gas, with a 15 MW capacity. A 2 MW ammonia-fired gas turbine, built by Siemens, may run on either pure ammonia or a combination of ammonia and natural gas [41]. A 1 MW ammonia-fired gas turbine produced by Kawasaki Heavy Industries is presently undergoing testing in Japan [42]. The turbine is designed to operate on pure ammonia or a mixture of ammonia and natural gas.

In terms of the levelized cost of power, green ammonia provides several significant benefits over alternatives based on carbon absorption (carbon capture). If it is employed extensively in the shipping industry, green ammonia will likely be accessible at all ports, be geographically independent, and have minimal transportation costs. Underground storage close to carbon dioxide is necessary for carbon absorption, as is its transmission to appropriate underground storage locations. Ammonia can easily be used in gas turbines from a few megawatts to very high power, and its use will be a proper flexible method for the future decentralized network. However, nuclear energy and carbon absorption are often only considered in gigawatt scales. Frequent shutdown of carbon capture equipment may be another challenge. At the same time, ammonia can be used in the gas turbine cycle, which offers high flexibility. The initial capital costs for ammonia in the gas turbine combined cycle are significantly lower than carbon absorption. Electricity production from ammonia in a gas turbine combined cycle is affected by fuel costs, while electricity production based on carbon sequestration is affected by initial cost. Finally, the effectiveness of carbon capture systems in reducing greenhouse gas emissions is at best 90–95 % [7], unlike ammonia, which is almost carbon-free in a gas turbine combined cycle (although additional NOx emissions must be managed).

It is crucial to note that ammonia combustion has difficulties due to factors including a high ignition temperature, a sluggish flame speed, and a slow rate of chemical reaction. Ammonia is combined with common fuels, including gasoline, diesel, LPG, CNG, ethanol, methanol, hydrogen, etc., to solve the problem of ammonia ignition. The impact of adjusting the ratio of ammonia fuel components on output power is shown in Fig. 3a [7]. As the quantity of ammonia in the fuel mixture rises, the generator's output power falls. Additionally, ammonia-hydrogen has been found to have the maximum output power. CO2 and NOx emissions are caused when ammonia is combined with conventional fuels. In Fig. 3b and c, the impact of an ammonia fuel combination on CO2 and NOx emissions is depicted, respectively [7]. It is evident that reducing CO2 and NOx emissions greatly increases the amount of ammonia in the mixture. Ammonia oxidation can use a catalyst to get around this issue. Thus, it is possible to decrease NOx and unburned NH3. Carbon emissions can be eliminated entirely by combining hydrogen and ammonia. Additionally, increasing the combustion of ammonia can lower NOx emissions. The flame speed can be increased using ammonia mixture or preheated ammonia with a higher density. Due to its high ignition temperature and moderate flame speed, ammonia burns more slowly than other fuels when used as a fuel. Due to the slow rate of chemical reactions, some of it escapes the exhaust without burning. Adding a booster to the ammonia-air mixture is a typical method of speeding up the chemical reaction of ammonia combustion. Common fuels like hydrogen are typically employed as a booster in engines powered by ammonia. To speed up the chemical rate of ammonia combustion, catalysts including NaCl, BaCl2, and NaF are also employed [43].Fig. 3Output power (a) and CO2 emission (b) and NOx emission (c) diagram according to the ratio of ammonia to different fuels [21].Fig. 3

The majority of research on ammonia's usage as a fuel for gas turbines in the 2010s concentrated on ammonia blends with different fuels, including methane [44,45], propane [46,47], hydrogen [40,48,49], etc. Enhancing combustion efficiency and lowering NOx emissions are two advantages of utilizing a blend of ammonia and other fuels as a fuel in gas turbines. Ammonia can be used with other fuels to improve the fuel's total energy density because it has a lower heating value than traditional fuels like natural gas. In general, a more ecologically friendly and sustainable fuel substitute for conventional fossil fuels in gas turbines may be a blend of ammonia and other fuels. Furthermore, changes in combustion characteristics, such as adiabatic temperature and laminar flame speed, may result from combining ammonia with other fuels. Methane and hydrogen are the fuels that are mixed with ammonia the most frequently. The air-fuel equivalency ratio has the ability to regulate the adiabatic temperature [8]. At air-fuel equivalency ratio = 1, ammonia has a flame speed that is around 5 times slower than methane and 16 times slower than hydrogen [8]. Thus, one of the primary obstacles to the conversion of current gas turbines from natural gas to ammonia is the low flame speed. A gas turbine's combustion chamber gets bigger because ammonia has a low flame speed. One solution to this problem is to add hydrogen or other fuels.

The use of carbon-neutral fuels is the main emphasis of the shift to carbon-zero energy, with ammonia and hydrogen being highlighted because of their low cost and relatively widespread use. Kumar and Meyer demonstrated that the flame speed of ammonia-hydrogen blends may be the same as that of methane air combustion for a variety of mix ratios [50]. As a result, current gas turbines may employ ammonia-hydrogen mixtures without requiring major modifications to the combustion chambers. A research on fuel-rich ammonia-hydrogen injection for humidified gas turbines was presented by Bovzo et al. [51]. It is well knowledge that one of the most popular techniques for lowering NOx emission in gas turbines is steam injection. Additionally, gas turbine energy efficiency may be raised by the use of steam injection.

There are other obstacles to using hydrogen as fuel, but the following ones need to be noted: moving and storing - Since hydrogen compression and transportation need a substantial quantity of energy, the fuel supply infrastructure must be significantly modernized. H2 is pressured up to 600 bar in a gaseous condition [52,53]. It is feasible to identify specific strategies, such as on-board hydrogen generation devices, to raise the efficiency of ammonia combustion while taking these difficulties into consideration. Producing hydrogen before it is used in gas turbines is one approach to address the issues with the infrastructure for the delivery of hydrogen fuel. Ammonia is the most ecologically benign hydrogen carrier and has the largest hydrogen mass per unit mass of carrier among other highly efficient hydrogen carriers [[54], [55], [56]]. Ammonia decomposition reactions can be used to recover hydrogen from ammonia [57].

Ammonia undergoes an endothermic thermochemical change to produce a hydrogen-rich gas, however this process requires the supply of external heat sources. Ammonia combustion [58] or waste heat from gas turbines producing exhaust heat with temperatures as high as 500–600 °C [59] to supply external heat for the decomposition process are two examples of heat sources for ammonia thermochemical transformation.

This article studied ammonia as a clean fuel and a suitable alternative to fossil fuels. The methods of producing hydrogen and ammonia as an energy carrier and hydrogen carrier were explained. The limitations of using ammonia were investigated. With the advancement of technology, more appropriate and updated ways will be provided for better and more stable ammonia combustion. The thermodynamic modeling of the combined cycle power plant and the technical and economic analysis of the usage of ammonia and hydrogen fuel in combination in various ratios are covered in the following sections. It should be emphasized that ammonia fuel has not yet been widely commercialized in gas turbines. The technical sector has tried to investigate how crucial parameters are affected by the fuel composition ratio. The impact of the fuel mix ratio and other significant economic factors on the levelized cost of producing electricity have both been studied in the economic sector. From a technical and financial standpoint, this study aims to arrive at the correct and ideal fuel composition ratio.

Methodology

This article will discuss the simulation of the combined power generation cycle with the fuel of ammonia and hydrogen. ASPEN-HYSYS software has been used to conduct this simulation. The simulation's foundation is built on utilizing ammonia and hydrogen at a volume ratio of 70 to 30, which several businesses have already achieved [18]. Of course, the goal is to be able to use full ammonia fuel to reduce the costs related to splitting ammonia and then burning the resulting hydrogen. In addition, other mixing ratios were also simulated and are comparable. Finally, the combined cycle in different fuel ratios was analyzed economically. In this modeling, a gas turbine like Siemens SGT5-2000E is assumed to be used. The information and data are based on published commercial data on this turbine, which is fueled by natural gas [60]. A different combustion chamber is needed for combustion with hydrogen and ammonia fuel, and the methods explained in the previous section must be used for proper combustion. An essential element of the gas turbine process is the turbine inlet temperature (TIT). The performance of the gas turbine may be impacted by TIT [61]. Higher TIT can improve the efficiency and power of the net cycle output [61]. However, as TIT rises, a temperature limit is established for the turbine blades and combustion chamber parts, putting them under thermal stress. By developing the cooling system for the blades, using new materials for the thermal coating of the turbine blades, and increasing the alloy of the combustion chamber components, high TIT can be achieved [62]. In this model, the temperature of the inlet to the turbine part is tried to control by controlling the excess air to be within the standard range of this turbine model.

Fig. 4, Fig. 5 show the two parts of the gas turbine and steam turbine in the cycle simulated in Aspen-Hysys software. In this simulation, the gas turbine part is divided into three parts: compressor, combustion chamber, and turbine. Additionally, its steam cycle component, which has two turbines in separate low-pressure and high-pressure sections, generates the necessary heat using the gas turbine section's combustion exhaust gas. In the simulation, the Peng-Robinson equation was used for the gas turbine part [60], and the ASME Steam equation was used for the steam turbine part [8]. The economic results of the combined cycle

One of the most essential parts of examining a process is the related economic examination. In the simulated cycle, the life of the factory is considered to be 25 years [63]. According to Ref. [63], the initial investment cost of this power plant is 1084 $/kWh, and according to the same source, the maintenance cost is 3 % of the initial investment per year. Another cost that is effective in calculations the cost related to fuel consumption. In this simulation, the raw material of the fuel, ammonia, is considered, and due to the need for hydrogen to combine with ammonia for combustion, a splitter was used to decompose ammonia. According to the calculations in the source [33], the chart of these costs is consistent with the chart of initial costs using the methane reforming device. This reference assumes that 20 % of split hydrogen is burned to provide the necessary energy to split ammonia [33]. According to the forecast of the source [33], in 2040, the ammonia price is 380 dollars per ton to validate the economic analysis. The interest rate is also considered to be a 7.5%-dollar inflation rate. According to the output capacity of 225.8 MW, the initial capital cost of constructing a power plant on this scale is around 245 million dollars, and the necessary cost of the splitter is approximately 44.6 million dollars. This is true for the volume ratio of ammonia to hydrogen of 70/30. In total, the required initial capital cost will be 289.4 million dollars. Including maintenance and fuel costs in the 25 years of the factory's life and converting it to today's rate, the total cost will be 2.83 billion dollars. The amount of electricity produced in 25 years by applying the annual interest rate equals 19.8 million MWs. The obtained results show that the levelized cost of electricity production equals 143.4 $/MWh. Conclusion

Ammonia is a stable and acceptable fuel from a technological standpoint to replace natural gas for gas turbines and combined cycles power plants, which can give efficiency to the degree of other standard fuels. The levelized cost of its produced electricity is estimated from 210.2 $/MWh to 306.0 $/MWh depending on the combined ratio of ammonia and hydrogen. If natural gas plus carbon capture is used, this cost is estimated at 195$/MWh in 2020. Of course, with the increase in the price of gas in the world, this amount has also increased. The levelized cost of electricity using gas fuel with a 90 % carbon absorption system in Iran, according to calculations, is 36.9 $/MWh. This indicates that if policies such as the cost of carbon dioxide emissions and the increase in gas prices are not considered to support clean fuel, ammonia fuel will not be economically competitive. Of course, it should be considered that the initial investment cost of the power plant with ammonia-hydrogen fuel is lower, and the more economical factor of gas fuel is its price. The price of producing ammonia is now subject to a lot of uncertainty. The economic, political, and social variables will be the ones to bring about these uncertainties. The price of ammonia fuel was calculated from 222 $/ton, which is the minimum price of ammonia predicted in 2040, to 2000 $/ton. The levelized cost of electricity production from 91.7 $/MWh to 673.4 $/MWh changed and this shows the strong dependence of the cost price on the fuel price. As the fuel price stabilizes and reaches the predicted price, this fuel will be very competitive with standard fuels.

According to the analysis of the sensitivity of the levelized cost to the combined ratio of the fuel, the optimal ratio from the economic point of view is the ratio of 50 % ammonia. However, the 50 %–80 % ammonia ratio is ideal regarding combustion stability and output power [20]. In general, according to the closeness of the levelized cost in the ratios of 50 %–80 % of ammonia, it can be concluded that these ratios are the appropriate ratios for the combination of ammonia-hydrogen fuels. Of course, the additional costs for using the ammonia splitting device and the technical complexities of combining the two fuels ammonia and hydrogen make it challenging to achieve the technology of turbines that work with 100 % pure ammonia fuel. With increasing interest in using green ammonia to avoid carbon production in the process of fertilizer production and transportation, experiences related to the production of this energy carrier will increase, and such experiences will lead to cost reduction. In contrast to other low-carbon power generation choices like nuclear energy and carbon absorption in fossil fuel processes, power generation from green ammonia is, therefore receiving more attention as a clean fuel of the future at the scale of power plants.
