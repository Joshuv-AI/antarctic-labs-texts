Single versus double lung transplantation in patients with pneumoconiosis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chaojian Chen, Yifeng Huang, Zijie Li, Junhan Wu, Weifeng Zhong, Leyan Chen, Feng Liu, Guibin Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung transplantation (LT) is emerging as a critical final treatment for end-stage lung diseases including pneumoconiosis. Outcomes of single lung transplantation (SLT) and double lung transplantation (DLT) in pneumoconiosis remain understudied. This study evaluates clinical differences between the two procedures in pneumoconiosis patients to guide therapeutic decision-making for this occupational population.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 119 pneumoconiosis patients undergoing LT at The Affiliated Wuxi People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between Jan 2018 and Dec 2022. The choice of SLT versus DLT was primarily driven by clinical judgment of high surgical risk, primarily due to severe pleural adhesions or calcification deemed too risky for bilateral pneumonectomy. Data on demographics, intraoperative and postoperative characteristics and patient survival were collected to compare SLT and DLT outcomes using Mann-Whitney U test and Pearson’s chi-square test. The primary outcome was 1-year posttransplant survival. Posttransplant survival was assessed by Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOf all the transplant recipients, 61 (51.3%) underwent SLT and 58 (48.7%) underwent DLT. DLT recipients were younger (44 [33–51] vs. 49 [43–53], p = 0.012), had higher mean pulmonary pressure (25.0 [20.0–25.0] vs. 20.0 [15.0–25.0] mmHg, p = 0.005) and experienced longer ischemic time (16.1 [13.4–18.0] vs. 7.2 [6.2–8.5] hours, p < 0.001). The durations of intraoperative and postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) were significantly longer in DLT recipients (p < 0.001; p = 0.003). The perioperative mortality of recipients who underwent SLT was more than three times higher than that of those undergoing DLT (18.0% vs. 5.2%, p = 0.059). Those who received DLT had significantly improved survival compared to SLT, with a hazard ratio of 0.24 (95% CI: 0.07–0.81, p = 0.022).ConclusionOur preliminary data suggested that DLT might be associated with better survival in patients with pneumoconiosis compared to SLT. A randomized controlled trial or large-scale multicenter registry study is needed to confirm this survival benefit and refine patient selection criteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12995-026-00524-6.Keywords: Pneumoconiosis, Double lung transplantation, Single lung transplantation, Survival

Background

Pneumoconiosis is a group of interstitial lung disease caused by the inhalation of organic or inorganic airborne dust and fibers, which occurs among the most common occupational diseases in the world, particularly in developing countries [1–3]. Common types of pneumoconiosis include asbestosis, silicosis, coal workers’ pneumoconiosis (black lung disease) and berylliosis, which are caused by inhalation of asbestos, silica, coal dust and beryllium, respectively [4]. The accumulation of these dust particles in lungs can lead to pulmonary fibrosis or nodule formation, resulting in impaired lung function [5, 6]. Patients with pneumoconiosis usually have no symptoms or only slight symptoms at early stage. As the disease progresses, an increasing number of symptoms occur, primarily including respiratory symptoms like cough, expectoration, hemoptysis, chest pain and so on. At the end stage of disease, conventional medical therapy can only provide limited relief, making lung transplantation (LT) a potential life-saving therapeutic option [7].

LT is widely recognized as the final treatment for patients with advanced lung diseases including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), pulmonary hypertension and so on [7, 8]. Recently, LT has become increasingly common among patients with coal workers’ pneumoconiosis in the United States [9, 10]. Studies have reported the application of this technique in these diseases with single or double LT adopted [11–16]. Single lung transplantation (SLT) offers certain advantages, such as shorter operative times, preservation of donor lungs for other recipients and more societal benefits. In contrast, double lung transplantation (DLT) has been associated with better long-term outcomes, including improved forced expiratory volume in first second (FEV1) and survival, freedom from bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) and relative cost-effectiveness (individual perspective) [17].

Previous research has compared SLT with DLT in patients with COPD and IPF. For COPD recipients, a previous study demonstrated that there was no survival difference at 5 years in patients who underwent SLT and DLT [18]. However, long-term data revealed greater survival benefit and lower mortality risk in DLT recipients [19–21]. By contrast, posttransplantation outcomes in patients with IPF remained controversial. Schaffer et al. conducted a comparative study to compare outcomes in single- and double-lung transplant recipients with IPF and found that DLT was associated with better graft survival than SLT [18]. Nevertheless, evidence from research by Ranganath et al. and Chauhan et al. demonstrated that there was no statistically significant difference in actuarial graft survival between SLT and DLT cohort [22, 23]. Beyond COPD and IPF, there was a growing but still limited body of literature specifically addressing lung transplantation outcomes in patients with pneumoconiosis. Blackley et al. first drew attention to this issue by documenting a steady increase in the number of lung transplants performed for coal workers’ pneumoconiosis in the United States [9, 10]. This trend reflected both the persistent burden of occupational lung disease in certain populations and the expanding indications for lung transplantation as a viable therapeutic option for end-stage pneumoconiosis. More recently, Dotan et al. published a retrospective cohort study specifically comparing SLT versus DLT outcomes in 40 patients with artificial stone silicosis, a particularly aggressive and rapidly progressive form of silicosis that had emerged as a significant occupational health crisis in Israel and other regions [24]. They found no significant difference in the risk of death or re-transplantation in SLT versus DLT, and identified severe pleural adhesions and hilar calcification as the primary drivers of surgical complexity, which often precluded bilateral pneumonectomy and led to the selection of SLT. The pathophysiology of pneumoconiosis, which involves chronic inflammation of the lung and resulting fibrosis, differ in mechanisms from common obstructive and restrictive pulmonary diseases such as COPD and IPF [6, 25]. Whether previous findings on the superior type of transplantation procedure in other diseases work in pneumoconiosis requires further evidence.

Currently, there is lacking research focusing on the differences between SLT and DLT in patients with pneumoconiosis. Intraoperative complications and postoperative outcomes are not thoroughly investigated in these recipients. Therefore, we conducted this study to compare the clinical outcomes of SLT and DLT in pneumoconiosis patients, aiming to provide insights into transplantation strategies for this unique population who suffer from occupational lung diseases and hoping to improve clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.

Methods

Data sources and study design

We retrospectively reviewed a cohort of adult patients with pneumoconiosis who underwent LT at The Affiliated Wuxi People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between Jan 2018 and Dec 2022. The diagnosis of pneumoconiosis was established according to the Chinese National Diagnostic Criteria for Pneumoconiosis, which required: First, a confirmed occupational exposure history (e.g., mining, foundry work, asbestos handling); Second, characteristic high-resolution imaging findings (small opacities, progressive massive fibrosis, or pleural plaques); Third, exclusion of other interstitial lung diseases. The choice of SLT was primarily driven by severe pleural adhesions or obliteration, serious calcification of mediastinal lymph node and hilar structure that deemed too risky for bilateral pneumonectomy based on preoperative chest imaging, and by overall high surgical risk evaluated by multidisciplinary team. All donor lungs were procured exclusively from the China Organ Transplant Response System in strict compliance with the national legal framework. No organs were obtained from executed prisoners or detainees through illegal trafficking. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the World Health Organization Guiding Principles on Human Cell, Tissue and Organ Transplantation [26].

Totally, 779 LT procedures were performed during this 5-year period. Adult recipients with a primary diagnosis of pneumoconiosis before transplantation were selected for analysis. Patients who underwent re-transplantation or lobar LT were excluded, resulting in a total of 119 patients included in the study (Fig. 1). This cohort of pneumoconiosis patients were divided into two groups based on the type of transplantation, including those receiving either SLT or DLT. Demographic and clinical baseline characteristics of the recipients at the time of transplantation were identified. Postoperative short-term and long-term outcomes were compared between the two groups.

Data collection

The baseline data of the recipients contained age, sex, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), blood type, FEV1, FVC, DLCO, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, 6-minute walk distance, oxygen requirement, mean pulmonary pressure, serum creatinine level, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), ischemic time and the rate of preoperative advanced respiratory support including mechanical ventilation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Donor-related variables primarily included age, sex, weight, height, BMI, arterial oxygen partial pressure to fraction of inspired oxygen ratio (PaO2/FiO2) and smoking history. The PaO2/FiO2 ratio refers to the latest recorded value from arterial blood gas analysis after donor lung preservation. Intraoperative and postoperative parameters were collected, including intraoperative bleeding amount, intraoperative rate and duration of ECMO, postoperative rate and duration of mechanical ventilation and ECMO, postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stay, occurrence of primary graft failure (PGD) within 72 hours after transplantation, occurrence of postoperative infection and perioperative mortality. The primary outcome measure was patient survival. In terms of PGD, it was defined as pulmonary edema with diffuse alveolar damage occurred within the first 72 hours after transplantation which manifests clinically as progressive hypoxemia with radiographic pulmonary infiltrates [27]. Perioperative mortality was defined as death occurring within 30 days post-transplantation. All the clinical information was summarized according to the medical records and checked by at least three different clinical practitioners to avoid potential minor errors or biases.

Data analysis

Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages and continuous variables were described as medians with interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed data in this study. Demographic and clinical baseline characteristics of the two groups were compared using Mann-Whitney U test for nonparametric distributions of continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank test was performed and a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was applied to analyze posttransplant survival. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption for the Cox regression model was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals for each covariate included in the final multivariable model. The global test of the PH assumption was performed using the ‘cox.zph’ function of ‘survival’ package in R, which tested the correlation between Schoenfeld residuals and ranked survival time. A non-significant global p-value (> 0.05) indicated no violation of the PH assumption. To quantify the robustness of the findings against unmeasured confounding, we performed an E-value analysis for the adjusted hazard ratio of DLT versus SLT in the multivariable Cox model. The E-value represented the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the transplant type and the survival to fully explain away the observed association, beyond the measured covariates. Calculations were performed using the standard E-value formula for hazard ratios [28]. Missing data were minimal (3.4%) and occurred only in the 6-minute walk distance variable. Given the low proportion and random missingness, simple median imputation was applied for this single covariate. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) and the IBM SPSS 25 software.

Baseline characteristics

The annual distribution of transplant operations for pneumoconiosis was illustrated in Fig. 2. An upward trend was observed in the number of DLT performed until 2021, but more patients with pneumoconiosis received SLT in 2022. Of all the transplant recipients, 61 (51.3%) underwent SLT and 58 (48.7%) underwent DLT. Table 1 presented the baseline characteristics of SLT and DLT recipients. The majority of the recipients were male, except for one female recipient who underwent DLT. No significant differences were observed between the two cohorts in terms of recipient sex, BMI, ABO blood type, NYHA class, 6-min walk distance and creatinine level before transplantation. There were also no significant differences in the percentage of patients requiring advanced respiratory support before transplantation between the two groups.

However, compared with SLT recipients, those who underwent DLT were younger [44 (33–51) vs. 49 (43–53), p = 0.012], had higher mean pulmonary pressure [25.0 (20.0–25.0) vs. 20.0 (15.0–25.0) mmHg, p = 0.005] and experienced longer ischemic time [16.1 (13.4–18.0) vs. 7.2 (6.2–8.5) hours, p < 0.001]. Nevertheless, there were no significant differences between the two groups in donor sex, BMI, FVC, DLCO, CCI, PaO2/FiO2 ratio or smoking history.

Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes

The intraoperative and postoperative parameters of the transplant recipients in the SLT and DLT cohorts were shown in Table 2. The duration of intraoperative ECMO was significantly longer in patients undergoing DLT [7.8 (6.8–9.3) hours] compared with those undergoing SLT [5.5 (4.7–6.6) hours] (p < 0.001). Longer duration of postoperative ECMO was also observed in recipients receiving DLT [19.0 (16.8–21.0) vs. 18.2 (16.3, 20.5) hours, p = 0.003]. However, the amount of intraoperative bleeding, duration of postoperative mechanical ventilation, ICU stay and hospital stay, as well as the occurrence of PGD and infection after transplantation were similar between the two cohorts. Additionally, the perioperative mortality rate of recipients who underwent SLT was more than three times higher than that of those undergoing DLT (18.0% vs. 5.2%, p = 0.059). Among the 19 perioperative and 1-year deaths, causes mainly included infection or sepsis, severe PGD, multiple systemic organ failure (MSOF), hemorrhagic shock, airway anastomotic issues and other causes. Infection or sepsis accounted for the majority of deaths in both SLT (6/15, 40.0%) and DLT (2/4, 50.0%) group. MSOF was the second most common cause, occurring in 3/15 (20.0%) of SLT deaths and 1/4 (25.0%) of DLT deaths. Survival analysis and multivariable cox proportional hazards model

The survival status of recipients at one year after LT was collected based on the follow-up records. Figure 3 presented the Kaplan-Meier survival curves for overall, single and double lung transplant recipients. The overall 1-year survival for the study recipients was approximately 82.4% (Fig. 3a). Statistically significant differences were observed in posttransplant 1-year survival between single and double lung transplant (73.3% vs. 91.8%, p = 0.007) (Fig. 3b). To assess whether the temporal shift in transplant type proportions confounded our findings, we categorized the study period into “early era” (2018–2019) and “late era” (2020–2022) and included this as a covariate in the multivariable Cox model. After adjusting for recipient age, FEV1, mean pulmonary pressure, intraoperative bleeding, donor age and transplant era, DLT remained significantly associated with improved 1-year survival (adjusted HR: 0.24; 95% CI: 0.07–0.81; p = 0.022) (Table 3). The E-value for this adjusted HR was 7.81, indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both transplant type selection and survival by a risk ratio of 7.81 to completely negate the observed survival benefit. Using the more conservative approach of the upper CI bound (e.g., 0.81), the corresponding E-value was 1.76, suggesting that even under the most conservative scenario, residual confounding of a magnitude greater than most typical clinical variables would be required to fully explain away the association.

Discussion

In this cohort of pneumoconiosis patients who underwent LT, postoperative outcomes were compared between SLT and DLT groups. Our findings highlighted that DLT had marginally significant advantages over SLT in terms of perioperative mortality and significantly improved patient survival at 1-year post-transplantation.

A trend toward lower perioperative mortality was observed in DLT recipients, while SLT recipients exhibited a higher perioperative mortality rate. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, it suggested a potentially important trend in favor of DLT for improving perioperative survival. Moreover, the most striking finding of our study was the survival advantage conferred by DLT. Post-transplant survival at 1 year was significantly higher in patients receiving DLT, indicating a substantial reduction in the risk of death compared with those receiving SLT. Notably, the survival advantage of DLT persisted despite longer ischemic time and older donor lungs in this cohort, underscoring the robustness of bilateral transplantation in mitigating disease-specific complications like progressive pulmonary hypertension [29, 30].

No significant differences were found in most baseline characteristics between the SLT and DLT groups, such as BMI, blood type, FVC, DLCO, CCI, pre-transplant functional status and pre-transplant respiratory support. As directly affected by the type of transplantation, expected longer ischemic time was observed in DLT than SLT (16.1 vs. 7.2 h, p < 0.001). It was noted that recipients undergoing DLT had higher mean pulmonary pressure (25.0 vs. 20.0 mmHg, p = 0.005), suggesting that surgeons may prioritize DLT for patients with more advanced hemodynamic compromise. This mirrored trends in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), where DLT was associated with superior 5-year survival owing to reduced right ventricular strain [29, 30]. However, the longer intraoperative ECMO support (7.8 vs. 5.5 h, p < 0.001) and ischemic time in DLT highlighted the procedural complexity, which needed to be balanced against the survival benefit. It was worth mentioning that the multidisciplinary team identified patients with more complex lung disease and higher surgical risk, primarily those with severe unilateral pleural adhesions, hilar calcification, or hemodynamic instability, as candidates for SLT rather than DLT. This potential selection bias, which reflected real-world clinical decision-making rather than randomization, should be considered when interpreting the observed survival difference between the two groups.

Surgical complexity was particularly pronounced in patients with pneumoconiosis, who often presented with severe pleural adhesions and calcification of hilar lymph nodes. These pathological alterations rendered total pneumonectomy exceptionally challenging and proned to increasing the risk of intraoperative bleeding and tissue damage during surgery. Research had established a significant correlation between intraoperative bleeding and post-transplant prognosis [31]. Therefore, surgeons occasionally opted for SLT over DLT for patients with complex thoracic conditions to reduce intraoperative blood loss, shorten ischemic time and the duration of ECMO use, based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative exploration findings [32].

In our cohort, the baseline characteristics between SLT and DLT recipients showed no statistically significant differences except for age. The relatively advanced age of SLT recipients might contribute to higher postoperative risks, although it is important to note that at this age, age-related comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease or immune senescence would not typically be expected to be the dominant drivers of perioperative mortality. Given the limited sample size, we cannot robustly attribute the survival difference to age-related factors. For critically ill patients of advanced age, those with multiple comorbidities or intolerance to prolonged surgery, SLT could remain a clinically practical and potentially life-saving therapeutic option.

It was observed that there was a temporal shift in transplant type proportions in our cohort—with a higher proportion of SLT in 2018 and increasing DLT adoption from 2019 onward. In the initial year, our surgical team adopted a more conservative approach due to relatively limited surgical experience. As we gained experience, we gradually recognized that SLT in pneumoconiosis patients presented its own set of perioperative challenges, including ventilation-perfusion mismatch and native lung complications, which sometimes rendered postoperative management equally or more demanding than DLT. This cumulative experience, combined with the growing body of literature favoring DLT for long-term survival, led to a gradual shift toward DLT as the preferred approach for appropriate candidates. Importantly, the survival benefit of DLT remained significant after adjusting for transplant era, indicating that DLT might independently improve outcomes.

Annual reports from the International Society for Heart and LT (ISHLT) and United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) registries have shown that the annual ratio of DLT to SLT in all LT operations has been increasing over the last decade, and that DLT was associated with longer survival than SLT [33, 34]. The benefits of DLT remained debated, with uncertainty over whether its advantages arose primarily from improved functional reserve or from avoiding complications linked to retaining a diseased contralateral lung [35]. Proponents of DLT suggested a potential survival advantage, positing that replacing both lungs might reduce pulmonary function decline during early complications while enhancing long-term outcomes through superior respiratory mechanics and greater pulmonary capacity [36]. Unlike COPD, where hyperinflation of the native lung exacerbates ventilation-perfusion mismatch in SLT recipients [21], static fibrosis in pneumoconiosis could reduce postoperative complications in SLT, which might explain why SLT, despite its risks, remained a pragmatic option for older or comorbid patients [37].

Recently, Dotan et al. reported a single-center retrospective cohort study specifically comparing SLT versus DLT outcomes in patients with artificial stone silicosis, a particularly aggressive form of pneumoconiosis [24]. In their cohort of 40 patients, they observed no significant difference in survival in the SLT versus DLT group. Importantly, they identified severe pleural adhesions and hilar calcification as the primary drivers of surgical complexity, often precluding bilateral pneumonectomy and leading to the selection of SLT. These findings mirror our own experience and reinforce the notion that the survival disadvantage observed in SLT recipients with pneumoconiosis may be attributable less to the graft itself and more to the underlying surgical technical difficulty. This selection bias, inherent to all retrospective surgical studies, underscores the critical need for cautious interpretation of our findings and for future prospective risk-stratified studies.

Nevertheless, our data indicated that DLT’s functional and survival advantages outweigh these theoretical benefits, likely due to greater pulmonary reserve and reduced systemic inflammation from bilateral disease eradication [37, 38]. A previous study by Wu et al. investigated the outcomes of LT in patients with pulmonary hypertension and found that postoperative pulmonary function was better in recipients receiving DLT [30]. In our research, slightly higher preoperative pulmonary pressures were also observed in DLT recipients, which might suggest that bilateral replacement better addresses right ventricular afterload, a critical determinant of posttransplant outcomes, thus improving postoperative survival. On the other hand, by replacing both fibrotic lungs, DLT was able to eliminate residual potential native lung complications such as infection, pneumothorax and progressive fibrosis which might compromise SLT outcomes [37].

While the survival benefit of DLT was well documented in other patient populations, our research specifically highlighted the potential advantages of DLT in patients with pneumoconiosis, suggesting that DLT might be a preferable option in this cohort. Although we acknowledged the inherent selection bias in the retrospective study, the E-value analysis provided some reassurance regarding the robustness of our findings. An unmeasured confounder—such as the precise severity of pleural adhesions, intraoperative instability, or unrecorded comorbidities—would need to exert an exceptionally strong effect (HR > 7.81) on both the choice of transplant type and death to fully explain away the observed survival advantage of DLT. Given the measured covariates already included in our model, it was less likely that a single unmeasured variable of such magnitude existed in this clinical context. Nevertheless, residual confounding by multiple weaker unmeasured factors could not be entirely excluded, which was why we emphasized cautious interpretation of our results. Furthermore, progressive massive fibrosis (PMF), which was more frequently observed in silicosis than in coal workers’ pneumoconiosis, often presented with central fibrosis and calcification encasing the pulmonary vessels, substantially increasing the technical difficulty of bilateral pneumonectomy. Thus, it was possible that patients with silicosis-associated PMF were disproportionately represented in the SLT group due to these anatomic constraints. While we were unable to systematically stratify by pneumoconiosis subtype or PMF presence due to the limitations of our retrospective data, we acknowledged this as a potential source of systematic bias and recommend that future studies incorporate detailed radiologic and pathologic characterization to clarify the relationship between disease subtype, surgical complexity, and posttransplant outcomes.

There are several limitations in the study. Firstly, the retrospective observational nature of the study, combined with the lack of systematic pneumoconiosis subtype classification (although the majority of patients in our cohort were silicosis and coal workers’ pneumoconiosis based on regional exposure patterns), means that potential confounding factors and selection bias could not be fully excluded. Different dust etiologies might lead to varying degrees of pleural involvement and native lung disease progression, which could independently affect post-transplant outcomes. However, the stable distribution of subtypes across the study period makes systematic bias less likely. Efforts ought to be made to incorporate systematic etiological subtyping into future data collection protocols to enable more granular analyses. Secondly, since performed in a single institution with limited sample size, generalizability is restricted by regional practices and donor availability, particularly in China’s evolving organ allocation system. Future prospective multicenter studies are required to validate these findings across diverse populations and refine the risk stratification tools. Thirdly, the follow-up period was not sufficiently long. The 1-year survival focus precludes assessment of some long-term outcomes such as BOS that disproportionately affects SLT recipients in other populations [32]. Longer-term follow-up, if achievable, would provide more insights into the durability of the survival advantage of DLT. Furthermore, while our E-value analysis suggests that the observed association is robust against unmeasured confounding, this statistical approach cannot completely substitute for randomization or prospective, protocol-driven data collection.

Conclusion

Our preliminary data suggested that DLT might be associated with better survival in patients with pneumoconiosis compared to SLT. While SLT might retain a role in older individuals or those unable to tolerate bilateral LT, this study underscored the need to prioritize DLT where feasible. Policies must balance the optimization of donor lung utilization with survival benefits of DLT, especially in resource-limited settings. Future prospective multicenter studies are required to further investigate the long-term benefits of DLT and refine patient selection criteria to optimize outcomes for those with pneumoconiosis.
