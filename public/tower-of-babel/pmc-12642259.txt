Association between body-mass index change and lung cancer risk in Korea: nested case-control study
Authors: Ji-Woong Nam, Su-Eon Kim, Kyung-Min Lee, Ye-Won Jung, Jeong-Min Oh, Woo-Ri Lee, Young-Joo Won, Ki-Bong Yoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have demonstrated an association between body mass index (BMI) and lung cancer risk, while additional research has found that BMI change affects lung disease. This study aims to investigate the association between BMI changes with lung cancer risk in Korea.MethodsWe identified 18,941 patients diagnosed with lung cancer for the first time between 2018 and 2022 using the Korean National Health Insurance claims database. A nested case-control design was applied with 1:5 age, sex and year matching, resulting in 94,705 controls. BMI change variables included the magnitude of change in BMI, rates, and groups of rates. Odds ratios for cancer risk were estimated using conditional logistic regression, adjusted for covariates. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on obesity classification and smoking status.ResultsOf 113,646 individuals (87,912 [77.4%] male), underweight individuals had a higher risk (OR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.10–1.34) compared to normal-weight individuals, whereas overweight (OR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.88–0.95) and obese (OR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.80–0.86) individuals showed reduced risk. A decrease in BMI was associated with increased lung cancer risk (OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.96–0.98), particularly among normal-weight (OR, 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99) and obese (OR, 0.96, 95% CI, 0.94–0.99) individuals.ConclusionsThe impact of BMI decrease on lung cancer risk was most pronounced among current smokers, followed by ex-smokers and non-smokers. The association between BMI change and lung cancer risk was confirmed. As a predictive factor, BMI change could be assessed during health examinations as an indicator for cancer screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15122-8.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide [1]. While mortality rates from stroke and coronary heart disease have significantly declined, the incidence and mortality rates of cancer are rapidly increasing [2]. In South Korea, cancer remains the leading cause of death and its mortality rate continues to rise [3]. Lung cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer in Korea [4] and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the country [3].

Early detection is crucial for improving treatment outcomes and extending survival rates for chronic diseases with high mortality rates [5]. This necessity has driven numerous studies on cancer incidence and prediction [6–8]. In Korea, national cancer screening covers the five major types of cancer, including lung cancer. Korea offers lung cancer screening every 2 years to adults aged 54–74 years who have smoked over 30 pack-years [9].

Various risk factors have been identified for lung cancer. Conventional lung diseases are associated with the prevalence of lung cancer [10]. Among these, pneumonia, tuberculosis, and pulmonary fibrosis increase risk for lung cancer, regardless of smoking status. Asthma and chronic bronchitis in non-smokers and emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in smokers are risk factors for lung cancer as well [11, 12]. Health behaviors also play a critical role. Smoking is a well-established risk factor for lung diseases, including lung cancer [11, 13]. Alcohol consumption, which is often associated with smoking habits, is also linked to lung cancer risk [14]. Conversely, physical activity decreases the risk of lung cancer [15].

The association between body mass index (BMI) and cancer has been extensively researched, with studies indicating that changes in BMI can significantly impact health outcomes [16–18]. Abe et al. [19], Pati et al. [20], and Kim et al. [21] reported that BMI is associated with cancer risk, such as lung, breast, colorectal, endometrial, esophageal, pancreatic, renal, liver, stomach, gallbladder, ovarian, thyroid, and prostate cancers. Yu et al. identified BMI as an key predictor of overall cancer mortality risk [22].

Previous studies have established a link between BMI and lung diseases [23]. A lower BMI is associated with an increased risk of lung cancer [24], which is more common among patients who have experienced weight loss [22].

Numerous studies have demonstrated an association between various risk factors and cancer risk by analyzing the presence or absence of health risk factors [25–27]. Previous research has examined the relationship between BMI changes and risk of all cancers; however, few studies have examined this association in a significant detail, particularly in the categorization of individuals based on the rate of BMI change or stratifying analyses by obesity status. To address this gap, this study aimed to investigate the impact of changes in BMI on lung cancer risk in Korea, using claims data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS).

Methods

Data and study population

This study utilized data from National Health Insurance claims database established by the Korean NHIS, which provides medical coverage for approximately 97% of Korean citizens, with Medical Aid covering the remaining 3% [28]. The database contains comprehensive information on individuals’ health conditions, medical service utilization, and demographic characteristics including gender, birth date, death date, and residential area [29]. This study used data from 2012 to 2022. Among the 109,658 patients diagnosed with lung cancer (International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10): C33 or C34) and assigned a specific beneficiary code (V193 or V194) during this period, those with a diagnosis of another cancer before the diagnosis of lung cancer were excluded to minimize the potential for secondary cancer occurrence (N = 21,500). Additionally, participants diagnosed with lung cancer between 2012 and 2017 were excluded (N = 27,586). Participants with missing data for the variables—BMI, smoking status, drinking status, or physical activity—during the three consecutive health examination cycles preceding the index year were also excluded (N = 41,631). This requirement was necessary to assess BMI changes over both long-term and short-term periods. In Korea, despite a relatively high overall participation rate in biennial health examinations, the rate of consecutive participation over multiple cycles is low [30], leading to a substantial number of exclusions. After excluding 90,717 participants, 18,941 were included in the case group. A nested case-control design was conducted, with each case matched to five controls who were free of any cancer prior to the index year and had no diagnosis of any type of cancer within six years after the index year, based on sex (male, female), age group (aged 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, or 80 and over), and year (2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, or 2022) (Fig. 1).

Variables

The outcome variable was lung cancer incidence, and the independent variables were BMI category and BMI change. BMI data were derived from general health examination records provided at 2-year intervals for adults in Korea, as the examination, aimed at prevention and early management, is scheduled at 2-year intervals [31]. We categorized BMI into four groups according to the Asian-Pacific cut-off points; underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23–24.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 25 kg/m2) [32]. For BMI change, the amount of change, rate of change, and change rate groups were measured over two periods (Fig. 2): long term (from 5 to 6 years before the index year to 1–2 years before the index year) and short term (from 3 to 4 years before the index year to 1–2 years before the index year). All changes in BMI were calculated as annual average changes. The amount of change measures the absolute difference in BMI between two time periods, calculated in person-years. The rate of change measures the extent to which BMI changed relative to the baseline BMI. The rate of change was grouped into two classification schemes: a three-level scheme and seven-level one.

The covariates were insurance fees quintile, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and comorbidities. Smoking status was classified as non-smoker, ex-smoker, or smoker. Drinking status was categorized as non-drinker, light drinker (1 to 2 drinks per week), moderate drinker (3 to 4 drinks per week), or heavy drinker (5 or more drinks per week). Physical activity was classified as no activity, light activity (exercise at least once per week that was not heavy activity), or heavy activity (either high intensity for 3 h or more or medium intensity for 5 h or more per week). Comorbidities tracked included COPD (J43 and J44), pneumonia (J12–J18), asthma (J45–J46), respiratory tuberculosis (A15–A16), and interstitial lung disease (J84), as defined by the ICD-10. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated to assess comorbidity burden using ICD-10 diagnostic codes from the two years preceding the index date [33]. The CCI assigns weighted scores to 19 medical conditions, with higher scores indicating greater comorbidity burden. However, since CCI includes cancer diagnosis as a component (6 points for solid tumors), it was not included as a covariate in regression analyses due to perfect separation between cases and controls. CCI was used only for descriptive comparison of baseline comorbidity patterns between groups.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, percentages, and means were calculated to assess the participants’ characteristics, and t-tests and chi-square tests were conducted. Conditional logistic regression was adjusted for insurance fee quintile, smoking, drinking, physical activity, and other comorbidities. To assess the impact of BMI change, four different BMI change variables were analyzed separately within the same conditional logistic regression model. This analysis was repeated for each of the two distinct periods, allowing for a comparison of BMI change associations across time. We conducted two subgroup analyses to address potential confounding factors. The first model was based on BMI categories, and the second model was based on smoking status, as it is a known confounder that could influence lung cancer risk [34]. To address the potential for reverse causation—where preclinical lung cancer could cause unintentional weight loss—sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding controls diagnosed with any cancer within two years after the index year, within four years after the index year, and without excluding those who developed cancer after the index year. All data analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). We used a two-sided significance level of p < 0.05.

Ethics approval

The need for ethics approval was waived by the ethics committee of the Institutional Review Board of The Yonsei University Wonju (IRB No. 1041849-202308-SB-147-01), and the need for informed consent to participate for this study was also waived by the Yonsei University Wonju Institutional Review Board. The national health insurance claims database does not contain any identifying information [35] and is publicly available; thus, additional approval was not required. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

In a more granular analysis using seven levels of BMI change rate, the severe decrease group (long term: OR, 1.36, 95% CI, 1.20–1.54, short term: OR, 1.29, 95% CI, 1.21–1.37) and the moderate decrease group (long term: OR, 1.31, 95% CI, 1.20–1.42, short term: OR, 1.15, 95% CI, 1.08–1.23) were significantly more closely associated with an increased risk of lung cancer than the stable BMI group, while the slight decrease group, slight increase group, and moderate increase group showed no significant relationship lung cancer risk.

The first subgroup analyses were conducted according to baseline BMI categories (Table 3; Fig. 4). Normal-weight participants with an increasing BMI change rate showed significantly decreased odds of lung cancer (long term: OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99; short term: OR, 0.98, 95% CI, 0.97–0.99). Similarly, obese individuals exhibited a statistically significant decreased odds of lung cancer with increasing BMI change rates (long term: OR, 0.96, 95% CI, 0.94–0.99, short term: OR, 0.98, 95% CI, 0.97–0.99). No significant associations were observed for underweight or overweight categories. In the normal-weight group, those with decreasing BMI had higher odds of lung cancer risk than the stable group (long term: OR, 1.17, 95% CI, 1.07–1.29, short term: OR, 1.10, 95% CI, 1.01–1.20), while those with increasing BMI had lower odds (long term: OR, 0.91, 95% CI, 0.84–0.98).

Discussion

This study examined the association between BMI changes and lung cancer risk in the Korean population. Underweight individuals exhibited a higher risk of lung cancer than normal-weight individuals, whereas overweight and obese individuals had lower risk. Previous study similarly reported a reduced risk of lung cancer incident in overweight and obese groups compared to the normal-weight group [36]. A decreased BMI was associated with increased risk of lung cancer, which is consistent with previous researches showing a negative association between increased BMI and lung cancer [37]. This trend was particularly pronounced in the normal-weight and obese groups when analyzed according to BMI category [25].

Individuals with decreased BMI faced a higher risk of lung cancer than those with stable BMI. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that BMI loss was associated with a higher risk of lung cancer compared to those with stable BMI [38, 39]. Unlike previous studies, this research categorized participants based on their BMI status and examined the relationship between BMI change and lung cancer risk. Results indicated that this association was most evident in the normal-weight, overweight, and obese groups. While differences emerged in specific subgroups between the two time periods, the overall trend remained consistent.

As smoking is a key factor in the development of lung disease, including lung cancer [34], this study examined the association between changes in BMI and lung cancer risk with reference to smoking status. Among individuals with decreasing BMI compared to those with stable BMI, lung cancer risk increased incrementally from non-smokers to former smokers and current smokers, aligning with findings that show a stronger association between BMI decrease and lung cancer risk among smokers [39–41].

These findings highlight the complex relationship between changes in BMI and lung cancer risk, with significant implications for cancer screening, prevention, and personalized medicine. BMI change can arise from heterogeneous pathways, including behavioral changes, metabolic dysregulation, systemic inflammation, and preclinical disease processes. While BMI can be modified through lifestyle and other interventions, the magnitude of association observed in this study suggests that BMI change may, in part, reflect underlying disease processes rather than being solely a modifiable risk factor. Accordingly, BMI change may serve a dual role—both as a marker of preclinical disease and, to a lesser extent, as a target for behavioral interventions. Using BMI fluctuations as a predictive factor for lung cancer risk could enhance public health interventions, individualized risk assessment. From a predictive modeling perspective, decreased BMI could be a valuable indicator for risk assessment tools in lung cancer [42]. Digital health devices and personal health management tools could incorporate BMI tracking as key components in assessing an individual’s health risks [43]. By monitoring BMI changes over time, these devices could flag significant decreases as signals for further medical evaluation or increased screening frequency.

The relationship between decreased BMI and lung cancer risk is complex and significant with several contributing factors, such as metabolic changes, indicators of disease progression indicators, and prognostic values. Like other malignancies, lung cancer can induce significant metabolic alterations in the body, often driven by factors such as cancer-associated cachexia [44]. These changes often precede clinical diagnosis and may manifest as unintentional weight loss [45]. In chronic respiratory diseases, including lung cancer, decreased BMI often correlates with disease severity. Jee et al. found that BMI as a critical predictor of mortality in patients with COPD, even after adjusting for lung function [46]. Moreover, previous study have shown that BMI at diagnosis influences lung cancer prognosis, with both low and excessively high BMI being associated with poorer outcomes in advanced non-small-cell lung cancer [47]. BMI changes could therefore serve as a valuable indicator of disease advancement, potentially aiding risk assessment and prognosis for patients with lung cancer. Although BMI changes may partly arise from modifiable behaviors, the presence of preclinical disease processes limits the extent to which risk can be diminished solely by direct BMI modification. Thus, BMI change should be interpreted primarily as a risk indicator to guide clinical surveillance and personalized management strategies, rather than as a simple causal target.

This study had several limitations. First, weight loss, which can influence cancer development, could be understood as intentional or unintentional [48]. However, as this study relied on health insurance claims data, distinguishing between the two types was not possible. Nevertheless, we partially attempted to adjust for unintentional weight loss by adjusting risk factors associated with weight reduction like diseases and smoking. Second, although previous researches emphasized the importance of differentiating between the types and stages of lung cancer [24, 49], this study could not distinguish between non-small-cell and small-cell lung cancer or determine cancer stages. Third, this study has a limitation in that it used diagnosis codes from health insurance claim data, which may have low coding accuracy [50]. However, this study addressed this limitation by using specific beneficiary codes for registered cancer patients alongside diagnosis codes to identify lung cancer patients [51].

Conclusion

This study identified a significant association between BMI changes and lung cancer risk in the Korean population and found that the risk of lung cancer increases as BMI changes became more negative. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring BMI changes as a potential early indicator of lung cancer risk, particularly alongside other known risk factors such as smoking status. Therefore, by integrating BMI monitoring into existing risk assessment frameworks, we may enhance the effectiveness of lung cancer screening and prevention strategies.
