Redefining menstrual hygiene management with menstrual cups through project Thinkal; from self care to environmental resilience
Authors: Parvathy Jayasree, Krishna Sreelekha Hemachandran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Proper management of menstrual hygiene is a topic of interest among women and is a requisite to the well-being of women and adolescent girls worldwide. But lack of information about menstruation and menstrual hygiene practices creates a culture of taboos leading to a big barrier in the path of menstrual hygiene management. A menstrual cup (M-Cup) is considered as an ideal green substitute for the disposable sanitary products. Being economical than disposable ones, M-Cup has several benefits compared to other menstrual management products mainly long term use and environmental sustainability. However due to several factors, such as affordability, accessibility etc., the level of awareness among the public is less. A programme was launched to curb menstrual waste, to create awareness and encourage women to use menstrual cups, and to ensure proper menstrual hygiene by distributing M-cups free of cost. The programme have reached out to more than 5, 00,000 beneficiaries across India including the tribal population of Jharkhand, Kerala, Karnataka etc. and studies are being carried out to analyze the acceptability, adaptability and usage of such an insertion device like M-Cups. By promoting M-Cups and empowering menstruates with knowledge and resources for sustainable menstrual hygiene management, the programme aims to achieve women empowerment, substantial carbon footprint reduction, preserve natural resources and enhance public health.

Introduction

Menstrual Health and Hygiene encompasses the systemic factors that link menstruation with health, well-being, gender equality, education, equity, empowerment and rights. It is a determinant of the quality of life of menstruators which includes aspects like mobility, work participation, access to education, and freedom [1]. The World Health Organization recognizes and addresses menstrual health as a health and human rights issue, and not just a hygiene issue [2]. Achieving good menstrual health and hygiene is a crucial aspect of ensuring the equality, rights, and dignity of every person experiencing menstruation [3]. Menstrual hygiene management (MHM) is an integral part of the Swacch Bharat Mission (SBM), particularly the second phase (SBM-Grameen II) which focuses on solid and liquid waste management to sustain open defecation-free status. The use of menstrual cups aligns with the SBM in waste reduction, improved sanitation infrastructure and the empowerment of women and girls [4]. The M-cup study supports the core goals and strategic objectives articulated by the World Health Organization (WHO) in the domains of public health, gender equity, water sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) and sexual and reproductive Health. In order to ensure proper menstrual health, all menstruators must have access to effective and affordable materials to manage the menstrual blood, along with facilities for ensuring cleanliness and hygiene. Women and girls are crucial contributors, implementers and beneficiaries of sustainable development. Their empowerment is fundamental to the achievement of the 2030 Development Agenda. The stand-alone SDG on gender equality (Goal 5) should be celebrated, as it outlines targets for every country to make gender equality a reality [5]. Women and adolescent girls should also have access to proper information about menstruation and hygiene practices, to care for their bodies in ways that support their preferences, hygiene, comfort, privacy, and safety. Most adolescent females, particularly in developing countries, are unaware of the need for healthy Menstrual Hygiene Management [6]. Girls are forced to face fear, humiliation, and guilt during menstruation due to a lack of prior information about menstruation [7]. It is therefore imperative to address widespread misconceptions and myths about menstrual hygiene, and to ensure that adolescent girls receive the necessary support to practice sound menstrual-hygiene habits. Considering the global scenario, in the West Gonja Municipality of the Savannah Region in Ghana, about two-thirds of school girls are knowledgeable about menstrual hygiene. However, access to proper menstrual materials is limited: approximately half use commercial sanitary pads, while the others rely on cloth or cotton [8]. Schools have inadequate menstrual hygiene management facilities that could be a major setback to the health and educational attainment of young girls [9].

There are over 355 million menstruating persons in India, however millions of women experience severe challenges to a safe and dignified MHM experience [10]. These challenges include access to safe menstrual absorbents, water, sanitation and hygiene infrastructure, access to safe disposal of used menstrual absorbents, and adequate access to knowledge and information about menstruation. (The Ministry of Jal Shakti erstwhile Ministry of Drinking Water and Sanitation -MDWS). Access to water in shared sanitation facilities is essential to support menstruating women and girls throughout their lives, protect reproductive health, and encourage sustainable menstrual hygiene management that minimizes waste [11]. Lack of awareness, affordability, and availability of appropriate means to manage menstrual hygiene and lack of adequate facilities have been the major factors affecting effective MHM in India [12]. An exploration of the factors shaping disposal and washing behaviors reveals the connections between facilities, knowledge, and taboos. Incorporating these elements into infrastructure planning is vital to create accessible and suitable systems that effectively support MHM [13]. Dubey et.al delineates the factors beyond the individual level that influence menstrual health and hygiene among premenopausal people including transgender and non-binary (TNB) in urban India. However, there is a pressing need for initiatives that promote inclusivity and safety for TNB individuals in public spaces and facilities, ensuring that their identities and needs are recognized and respected. Challenging and deconstructing the cis gendered narrative surrounding menstruation is crucial for advancing gender equity [14].

The M cup is a relatively new entrant into menstrual hygiene management. It is a reusable and non-absorbent menstrual hygiene product that is inserted inside the vagina during menstruation to collect and hold menstrual blood. Made from medical-grade silicone, these are flexible, bell-shaped, and small, making them easier to carry. M cups have been accepted as safe menstrual hygiene options with no adverse health effects in pre-clinical and clinical testing and post marketing surveillances [15,16]. It is considered a more hygienic, comfortable, safe, and efficient alternative to the disposable menstrual hygiene products like sanitary napkins and tampons [17]. Studies have also shown the feasibility of M cup usage in lower and middle-income countries facing issues such as insufficient sanitation and hygiene facilities, limited access to water, lack of proper disposal facilities, and insufficient awareness [18]. Acceptance of M cups among school girls in the rural African settings, where they previously faced difficulties due to lack of adequate menstrual hygiene management options leading to school drop-outs and absenteeism, also points towards this [19]. The convenient usage, low cost, reusability, and the simple design of Menstrual cups help women manage their menstruation with ease [15]. Many women in India lack proper awareness regarding the features and advantages of M cups. There appears to be a gap between women's understanding of M cups and their willingness to use them [20]. In this context the provision of adequate information, training, and follow-up on proper usage is a necessity [21].

In The lancet Public Health, Anne maria van Ejik and colleagues [22] provides the systemic review and met analysis of 3319 participants and found that the adoption of the menstrual cup required a familiarization phase over several menstrual cycles and peer support improved uptake, and about 73 % of participants wished to continue use of the menstrual cup after completion of the study. The authors found that the awareness of M-cups was low. Moreover, there is immediate need for targeted interventions to provide information and address menstrual morbidities [5] and affordability challenges [6]. In view of the above it is clear that awareness creation is important for the success of M-cups since it is an alternative hygiene method which lacks knowledge among common people. Thinkal – a flagship project of HLL Lifecare Ltd., a Govt of India enterprise under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare implemented the awareness creation and distribution of M-cups by its social and educational initiative HLL Management Academy (HMA). The name Thinkal in Dravidian languages means ‘moon’ and evokes the relationship of moon's cycles with menstrual cycles. It is a pioneering initiative to educate and create awareness among women and girls on Menstrual Cups (M-Cups) as a healthy, safe, easy- to- use and affordable menstrual hygiene product by providing it free of cost to women of menstruating age.

The concept of introducing M-Cups originated from the relief camps set up in Alappuzha municipality during the 2018 Kerala floods. Water-logged areas, restricted women from access to safe menstrual hygiene products and the sanitary pads supplied by various agencies had clogged community toilets in relief camps, leading to depleting effects on health and environment. The second phase of the programme was implemented in Kochi Municipal Corporation covering 5000 beneficiaries. In the third phase 22,500 cups were distributed to the women of Ernakulam district in Kerala. Later on 15,200 menstrual cups were distributed in Thiruvananthapuram district. In the fourth phase, implementation of the programme outside Kerala in the selected districts (Chatra & Latehar) of Jharkhand state, 10,000 beneficiaries in various areas of Chennai, Tamil Nadu, 5000 beneficiaries in Raichur District, Karnataka & 8620 beneficiaries in Tripunithura Municipality and many more.

Materials and methods

The M Cup distributed through the programme is made up of medical grade non-toxic, non-allergic silicon rubber having a rim diameter of 45 mm and designed as a thin-walled reservoir, to collect and hold the menstrual fluid (20 ml). It was designed to minimize the problems associated with insertion and removal. They are shaped like a bell with a stem (knob). Once inserted, it opens to a bell shape. Removal is accomplished by holding the stem or knob. The bell-shaped menstrual cup seals against the vaginal wall just below the cervix. After each period, the cup requires sterilization in boiling water.

Both quantitative and qualitative methods were employed to study the impact of introducing menstrual M-Cups which involves various phases, including initiation, implementation, awareness creation by medical professionals, distribution of M-cup, introduction to digital information systems, support to beneficiaries, ongoing call center support by medical professional and project documentation. It aims to introduce and promote menstrual cups and ensure accessibility, awareness, and ongoing support for users. Through community outreach, digital platforms, and continuous evaluation, the project strives to address menstrual hygiene needs effectively and sustainably.

Desk research

In the initial phase of the research, secondary sources such as government reports and surveys and national and international journal articles discussing menstrual cups, menstrual health and hygiene, etc., were examined. These efforts collectively aided in comprehending the current scenario regarding menstrual cup usage among menstruators and identifying key aspects to be emphasized while exploring the experiences of M cup users.

Sample survey of M cup beneficiaries

The most important component of the study was a quantitative survey among a sample of beneficiaries.

Qualitative methods

In addition to the survey among beneficiaries, qualitative methods were also adopted. In-depth interviews were conducted with stakeholders such as Local Self Government Institutions (LSGI) representatives, medical professional, Child Development Project Officer (CDPO) from the Women & Child Department (WCD)etc. who were involved in the implementation of the programme. These interviews helped understand the challenges faced in project implementation. Three Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) were held among the beneficiaries and one FGD was held with the grass root level functionaries such as ASHAs and Anganwadi workers who were actively involved in project implementation. As the project has identified that M cups are particularly useful to women in waterlogged areas, an FGD among users and another among non-users were organized in the coastal belt. Another FGD among users of M cup was held in an urban area to understand the experience and perceptions of urban beneficiaries.

The survey among beneficiaries was conducted using a questionnaire. The questionnaire, originally prepared in English was translated to regional language. Checklists were used for FGDs and interview schedules were used for depth interviews. Female investigators with prior experience in survey and gender studies were engaged for field work. Prior to the fieldwork, two-day intensive training programme was conducted to sensitize the investigators about the programme and anticipated problems in data collection. The training programme was aimed at aiding the investigators in completing the schedules with precision and reliability. The training programme included a half-day field-testing of the research instrument by the investigators, in one of the locations where the M cups were distributed. The field trials helped in reducing the biases and minimizing the possibilities of errors in filling up the schedules. As mentioned earlier, neighborhoods were randomly selected from the list of neighborhoods/locations where the cup was distributed. For each location, a list of beneficiaries was randomly generated. A buffer list of beneficiaries was also generated for each location, depending on the number of beneficiaries, in order to substitute if there is rejection or missing in the primary list. Repeated visits were made to some sites to ensure coverage and telephonic interviews were resorted to, only after that. Quality control feedback was given at regular intervals to the investigators. Daily monitoring of the data collection was done using necessary formats.

To support these efforts, various communication materials such as handbooks, leaflets, and brochures will be developed and made available in the local language. These resources will provide clear, concise information about the M-Cup, including its benefits, usage instructions, and maintenance tips. The awareness creation and training sessions will be conducted by expert medical professionals, including doctors and public health specialists. Their expertise will lend credibility to the program, ensuring that the information shared is accurate and evidence based. These professionals will not only lead the sessions but will also be available to address any questions or concerns, providing personalized support and guidance to participants. By combining in-person training with diverse communication methods and professional expertise, the project aims to create a comprehensive understanding of the M-Cup and the female reproductive system. This multi-faceted approach will help to reduce stigma, promote healthy practices, and ultimately enhance the overall quality of life for menstruating individuals in the community.

Menstrual awareness sessions conducted involved an integral component that taught comprehensively on menstruation, body parts involved, their role and location, hygienic maintenance, introduction to M-Cup history, material, usage – insertion and removal, when to use and when not, the impact of age, past medical history on users, maintenance, storage, and replacement. The distribution and awareness sessions of M-cups were implemented by HMA through ASHA workers, local specific groups (SHGs, NGOs etc.) and facilitated by public health centres and local self-government bodies. Medical professionals provided guidance about the usage of M- Cups to the beneficiaries and also to Anganwadi (government-run rural child and maternal care centers in India), Ayalkootams and Kudumbasree workers (multi-tiered women's poverty eradication and empowerment program in Kerala) as they acted as peer educators for personal guidance at the community level. Theme-based awareness camps and training programmes were organized for the beneficiaries in sub areas. The training programmes focused on awareness creation and dissemination of information regarding the usage of menstrual cups, solving the issues faced by the beneficiaries. Counseling services were also offered to the beneficiaries to clarify their doubts on the usage. To explore more on the acceptance of M-Cup, a profiling of the users was done on various factors like; age, education, occupation and income status of the participants, currently used menstrual hygiene method etc.

Documentation and review

The documentation includes details of awareness sessions and beneficiary sheets. Feedback from beneficiaries will be actively captured through surveys and focus group discussions. This input will provide valuable insights into their experiences with the M-Cup and the effectiveness of the awareness sessions. The collected feedback will be analyzed to identify trends and areas for improvement. This analysis will help the project team make informed adjustments to enhance the initiative's effectiveness and address any challenges faced by beneficiaries. By prioritizing documentation and beneficiary feedback, the project aims to continuously strengthen its approach, ensuring it remains relevant and impactful for the community.

Impact assessment study

A comprehensive impact study is conducted to ensure an objective evaluation of the initiative's effectiveness. This independent assessment will provide valuable insights into the project's outcomes and overall impact on the community. By utilizing a mix of data collection techniques, the study will capture a holistic view of the project's impact on menstrual hygiene management and community awareness. Key indicators will be identified to measure the project's success, including changes in knowledge and attitudes regarding menstrual health, the adoption rate of M-Cups, and overall improvements in menstrual hygiene practices among beneficiaries. The study also assesses any barriers to access and usage, providing critical feedback for future interventions.

Findings and discussions

The impact assessment studies points to the characteristics of the M cup users. One of the findings of the quantitative study has been that women belonging to the age group of 20 to 35 years are more likely to adopt M cups. Statistically, it was found that the age category to which an individual belongs to was associated with their M cup usage. However, qualitative study also pointed to similar outcomes. It was observed that families of young women and young women themselves were less willing to use M cups due to cultural factors. Across castes and communities, menstrual bodies are considered impure and various rituals and practices are followed at home that mark menstruating bodies as dirty and impure. In patriarchal societies, family and community honor are implicated in women's bodies. Through processes of socialization, women in our society have an unhealthy and uncomfortable relationship with their bodies. Fear mediates this relationship, especially in the case of women who are young and are in their reproductive age. Inserting an object into the vagina is viewed with suspicion and fear. This was clear from both the studies conducted. The major reason for women to not use M cups was fear—fear of losing fertility, fear of causing vaginal wounds, fear of pain during insertion and removal of the cup. These fears were found to be stark among younger women or mothers of young women. Working women were more likely to use M cups than women who did not earn independent income. Working women found M cups to especially convenient as they found M cups to provide them with greater freedom to move. Users reported the M cups were more effective and convenient than sanitary pads as M cups have been able to remove unpleasant experiences caused by issues like leakage and did not cause them vaginal irritation and rashes from their everyday lives. Education did not seem to play any role either in one's perceptions about menstruation nor was it found to be an important factor that influenced M cup usage.

By focusing on the pioneering efforts of the programme, the assessment evaluates the short and mid-term impacts of menstrual cups on low and middle-income women. M - cups users also seem to have a reformulated relationship with their own bodies. The menstrual cup provided them with new experiences, configuring new points of reference that changed their daily lives, as well as convictions and concepts that mediated their relationships. M cups users no longer viewed their bodies with disgust. They feel more empowered to move freely and embrace their menstruating bodies. Through the qualitative study, we found that M cups may be particularly useful for women suffering from chronic diseases or who are differently abled as it is less hassle-free than sanitary pads. The participants, after discovering the exposed and non-manifest benefits of the menstrual cup, feel obliged to inform other women about the advantages acquired. For instance, many women reported that they got the confidence to use an M-cup because someone in their family or their friend circle had used it and found it very comfortable. Thus, it may be more effective to have some of these new M cup users become community anchors who can motivate, and build awareness within their neighborhoods and localities about MHM. Project Thinkal could identify such community anchors with the help of LSGs and Kudumbasree and provide them with training and leadership skills. These community anchors could follow up and provide women with practical knowledge and handholding support to women to use M cups. The cost-effectiveness of M also encouraged women to shift from pads to cups.

M cups not only transform the relationship that one has with their own body but also transform their relationship with their environment. All recent studies on the issue of solid waste management have pointed to the ill effects of sanitary pads on the environment. It was encouraging to find that a majority of M cup users were aware of the environmental degradation caused by sanitary napkins and began to use M cups because they are more environmentally friendly. By choosing to use M cups, women were also expressing their concerns over environmental pollution and were willing to adopt practices and technologies that do not hurt public well-being.

The programme has contributed to a positive transformation in menstrual hygiene management, improving health outcomes, financial savings, mobility, and comfort among rural women in Palkhed and Sakore villages of Maharashtra, the coastal belt of Ernakulam district such as Chellanam and Kumbalangi through innovative, sustainable healthcare solutions, particularly focusing MHM. The majority of sample beneficiaries come from households under middle-income families, followed by Below Poverty Line (BPL) and a very small percentage under Above Poverty Line (APL). When compared to non-users of menstrual cups, who continue to report regular monthly expenses across all cost brackets, the financial advantage of switching to M-cups becomes even more evident. This reinforces the economic value of the intervention, especially for low-income rural households where recurring menstrual product costs can strain household budgets. This economic vulnerability underlines the need for cost-effective menstrual hygiene solutions. The affordability and long-term cost savings of menstrual cups align well with this need, making the intervention particularly relevant in such settings.

The study also focused on the factors that affect the usage of M Cups. Majority of the users were having the fear that they may get a urinary infection or M Cup may get stuck in the vagina, but even after having this fear, they were using the M Cup because of the support provided by the counselors. In the case of non-users, the major fear that hindered them from using M Cup was that it may get stuck in the vagina, other reasons why the respondents were not comfortable using it in the public toilet, were even scared of getting some secondary infection and fear of leakage. Some participants were worried about the size, shape and texture of the menstrual cup and were unsure of how to insert the menstrual cup into their vagina. Moreover, the switch to M cups has a much larger impact on the community and environment as a whole due to the environment friendliness of the product and the adoption of this particular menstrual management practice. About 91.5 % beneficiaries shared their interest and acceptance on using M-Cups in the future after these awareness sessions during the study conducted at the Alappuzha district of the state of Kerala in India [ 23]. One-fifth of the M cup users identified environment friendliness as a major feature that led to their sustained use. Due to its reusability and ease in disposal of menstrual waste, that there is a significant reduction in the sanitary waste generated. This was also acknowledged by the interviewed community level stakeholders such as the elected representatives who highlighted that there has been a significant reduction in the sanitary waste found in the waste dumped in and collected from the community. All these narratives as well as user and administrative stakeholder perspectives focus mainly on the generation and management of sanitary waste, while a large scale switch to M cups has a much larger impact on the environment and reduction in the carbon footprint.

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a method adopted to assess the environmental impact of a product by quantifying the consumption of resources and the emissions to the environment for the entire life cycle of the product. It was seen earlier in the study that the average number of sanitary napkins used by a menstruator during a menstrual cycle was 13.5, i.e. around 162 pads in a year. As a cup can be used for a minimum of 5 years, this translates into a significant reduction of sanitary waste generated; a reduction of 810 sanitary pads per menstruator in 5 years, and double that when the cup is used for 10 years. It is largely agreed upon that individual product use depends on the flow and cycle of the user. Menstruators on an average have around 450 menstrual cycles during their life cycle and many study reports have assessed and estimated millions of tonnes of sanitary waste that is generated due to use sanitary napkins [8]. However, the estimation of menstrual waste generated and its impact on the environment has to be explored more on further studies.

Limitations and future research

Social stigma surrounding menstruation continues to hinder M-cup adoption even now in many communities. To counteract these barriers, interventions must move forward thereby fostering and integrating menstrual education into community programs by reshaping perceptions, reducing stigma and encouraging wide acceptance of M- cups. The scale of our study also imposes limitations. The quantitative component of some findings require further statistical validation. Nevertheless, we have sought to strengthen our analysis by triangulating these results with qualitative insights which confirm many of the patterns observed. There is a clear need for future longitudinal and mixed methods research to examine the short and long term effects of intervention in M-cup adoption. During the period in which multiple rounds of field work were conducted, India's non heteronormative communities gained greater visibility and voice. However, we were not able to interview transmen, who may not have undergone anatomical transitions through surgery and faces distinct and challenges related to menstruation, menstrual experiences of differently abled women as well as women living in care homes or prison remain unaddressed. Broadening this scope in future research would allow for more comprehensive understanding of M-cup usage across marginalized groups.

Conclusions

Therefore, it is exceptionally important to introduce M-Cup as a safe and healthy alternative which can directly improve the quality of menstrual health and hygiene thereby providing an option for women to have a vivid understanding on the importance of exercising their choice on menstrual health. It was inspiring to see the women who made their product choice after being provided with an option to choose by making them aware about menstruation, body parts involved, their role and location and hygienic maintenance. Such choices can create ripple effects and keep motivating people around them to switch over to healthier alternatives. A considerable shift from the usage of disposable plastic pads to M-Cups is observed when they were distributed with awareness and usage training-support. This shift could impact various features including reduction in carbon foot print by diversion of used sanitary napkins, increased freedom of movement and activities including sports, work life and mainly affordability due to reusability of M-Cups for up to five years. There is no doubt that some trans men who have not undergone anatomical transition through surgery may experience distinct and diverse challenges during menstruation. Similarly, women who are differently abled, women in institutions like hospitals, care homes, or prisons also may be suffering severely due to poor sanitation infrastructures that address their specific needs. Further measures should be investigated to effectively address these issues. Moreover, the widespread adoption of menstrual cups contributes to environmental sustainability by reducing waste and minimizing the carbon footprint associated with conventional menstrual products. Therefore, M-Cups can be a direct replacement for non-biodegradable plastic pads; not just on an individual level, but from the point of view of an entire country's waste management system, thereby eliminating hazardous pollutants to the environment and maintaining the ecosystem.
