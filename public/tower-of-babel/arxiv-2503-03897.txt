Endpoint-Explicit Differential Dynamic Programming via Exact Resolution
Maria Parilli, Sergi Martinez, Carlos Mastalli

Abstract
We introduce a novel method for handling endpoint constraints in constrained differential dynamic programming (DDP) . Unlike existing approaches, our method guarantees quadratic convergence and is exact, effectively managing rank deficiencies in both endpoint and stagewise equality constraints. It is applicable to both forward and inverse dynamics formulations, making it particularly well-suited for model predictive control (MPC) applications and for accelerating optimal control (OC) solvers. We demonstrate the efficacy of our approach across a broad range of robotics problems and provide a user-friendly open-source implementation within Crocoddyl .



I
Introduction

Efficient and exact handling of endpoint constraints is a critical component for the parallelization of algorithms in
optimal control (OC)
.
In recent years, various approaches have emerged to address this challenge through endpoint constraints.
One straightforward approach is leveraging algorithms for distributed optimization such as the
alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)

[
1
]
.
This approach was recently applied by Bishop et al.
[
2
]
.
However,
ADMM
is inherently inexact and achieves only linear convergence at best, due to its reliance on augmented Lagrangian techniques
(see
[
3
, Section II-C, p. 6]
)
.

A promising alternative for parallelizing computations is the use endpoint-explicit Riccati recursions, as proposed by Laine and Tomlin
[
4
]
.
These recursions offer the potential for quadratic convergence.
However, they manage endpoint constraints through pseudo-inverses
[
5
]
, a process that is both computationally expensive and numerically unstable.
Consequently, this approach is limited to
linear-quadratic regulator (LQR)
frameworks and struggles with more complex, nonlinear
OC
problems.

In many real-time robotics applications, strict terminal
constraints
must be met within tight computational time limits.
In this context,
differential dynamic programming (DDP)
has become the preferred approach, as it leverages the Markovian structure of the problem through Riccati recursions.
This results in significantly higher computational efficiency compared to traditional sparse linear solvers (e.g., MA27, MA57, MA97
[
6
]
) commonly used in
nonlinear programming (NLP)
.
However, recent advances in constrained DDP methods (e.g.,
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
) have focused mainly on stagewise constraints, often treating endpoint constraints inexactly or using penalty-based methods, which can compromise performance in tasks requiring precise endpoint
constraints
.

Contribution

In this work, we address the limitation of existing methods for handling endpoint constraints in
OC
.
Our approach is both exact and computationally efficient, offering quadratic convergence while managing rank-deficient endpoint constraints.
Furthermore, it accommodates nonlinearities in both forward and inverse dynamics formulations, making it highly suitable for
model predictive control (MPC)
applications.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method across various robotics applications (
Figure

1
). Our approach is adaptable to various stagewise constraints and readily accessible to practitioners through
Crocoddyl

[
11
]
.

II
Quadratic programs with endpoint constraints

Before diving into our algorithm, we first introduce a key abstraction that facilitates its development.
Specifically,
OC
problems subject to stagewise and endpoint constraints can be locally resolved by solving the following quadratic program:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}}\;\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\mathbf{a}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{b},

at each Newton step to find the nonlinear roots that define the
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT)
point
[
12
]
.

In this abstraction,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{w}}}
represents the primal and dual decision variables encountered in an
OC
problem without endpoint constraints (e.g., search direction for states, controls, and multipliers of the dynamics constraints).
The matrix
\mathbf{A}\in S^{{n_{w}}}_{++}
is a symmetric, positive definite, banded, and large, while
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{b}}\times{n_{w}}}
is the wide (
{n_{b}}\ll{n_{w}}
) Jacobian of the endpoint constraints, with
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{b}}}
as its bias term.

The Lagrangian of
Equation

1
is defined as:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{y}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{b}}}
represents the Lagrange multipliers for the endpoint constraints.
This allows us to establish the
KKT
conditions for
Equation

1
as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bigtriangledown_{\mathbf{w}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\text{(primal feasibility)}

(3)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\bigtriangledown_{\mathbf{y}}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\text{(dual feasibility)}

which leads to the large symmetric saddle point system:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{B}&\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}\\
\mathbf{b}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{w}}+{n_{b}}}.

(4)

Numerous methodologies have been proposed in the literature for factorizing such saddle point systems.
Here, we briefly overview two prominent techniques: the classical Schur-complement approach and nullspace factorization
[
13
]
.
These methods are crucial due to their exactness, which is essential for ensuring fast convergence when for handling endpoint constraints.
This stands in contrast to inexact approaches, such as Krylov subspace method or relaxed proximal formulations
[
14
]
.
The
latter
can be viewed as a generalized Augmented Lagrangian method
[
15
]
, where convergence depends on the accuracy of the estimated multiplier
\mathbf{y}_{e}
.
Moreover, solving
Equation

4
leverages second-order derivatives to achieve quadratic convergence.

II-A

Schur-complement resolution

Since
\mathbf{A}
is square and nonsingular, we can solve
Equation

4
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=-\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{a}),

(5a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y},

(5b)

where
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}
denotes the Schur complement, and its inversion can be efficiently computed via a sparse Cholesky decomposition.

From
Equation

5b
, we observe that the decision vector
\mathbf{w}
decomposes into two terms:

\mathbf{w}=\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-{\widecheck{\mathbf{W}}}\mathbf{y},

(6)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
is the endpoint-independent solution, and
{\widecheck{\mathbf{W}}}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{w}}\times{n_{b}}}
accounts for the effect of the endpoint multiplier
\mathbf{y}
, i.e., the endpoint-dependent solution.

Finally, the banded structure of
\mathbf{A}
, which arises in optimal control due to its Markovian nature, allows for the application of Riccati recursions when solving
Equation

5
. This results in a highly efficient algorithm for solving arbitrary constraints.

II-B

Nullspace resolution

If
\mathbf{B}
is rank deficient, the Schur complement of
Equation

5
becomes non-invertible, rendering the problem ill-posed.

A
straightforward solution is to parameterize the decision vector
\mathbf{w}
into its nullspace and range.
However, this strategy:

(i)

requires a costly nullspace decomposition due to the large and sparse nature of
\mathbf{B}
, and

(ii)

does not exploit the structure of
\mathbf{A}
, preventing efficient computation of
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
and
{\widecheck{\mathbf{W}}}\mathbf{y}
.

Instead, we propose performing a nullspace decomposition on the Lagrange multiplier
\mathbf{y}
.
Specifically, we parameterize
\mathbf{y}
as:

\mathbf{y}=\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{y}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}+\bar{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbf{y}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}},

(7)

where
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{b}}\times{n_{\bar{Z}}}}
represents the nullspace basis of
\mathbf{S}
and
\bar{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{b}}\times n_{\bar{Y}}}
denotes its orthonormal basis.
This reduces
Equation

4
to the following saddle point system:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\\
\mathbf{y}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}-\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\\
\mathbf{b}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\overset{\Delta}{=}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\overset{\Delta}{=}\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{b}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\overset{\Delta}{=}\bar{\mathbf{Z}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\overset{\Delta}{=}\bar{\mathbf{Y}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{b}
.
Additionally, since
\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}=\mathbf{0}
, the Lagrange multiplier is computed as:

\mathbf{y}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\mathbf{Y}}\mathbf{y}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}=}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\mathbf{Y}}}(\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}^{\intercal})^{-1}(\mathbf{b}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{y}}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}).

(9)

This formulation effectively handles rank
deficiency
in
\mathbf{B}
, allowing for efficient resolution of the system even when the Schur complement is
invertible
.

III
Optimal control with endpoint constraints

Nonlinear
OC
problems with endpoint constraints can be formulated using either forward or inverse dynamics.
In inverse dynamics formulation, the problem
involves
handling stagewise equality constraints as follows

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}_{s},\mathbf{u}_{s}}\;\ell_{N}(\mathbf{x}_{N})+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ell_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})

(10)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\ominus\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0},\quad

\displaystyle\text{(initial {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}constraint})}

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k})\ominus\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{0},\quad

\displaystyle\hskip-30.00005pt\text{(integrator / forward dyn.)}

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})=\mathbf{0},\quad

\displaystyle\text{(inverse dyn.)}

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x}_{N})=\mathbf{0}.\quad

\displaystyle\text{(endpoint {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}constraint})}

The state
\mathbf{x}\in{\cal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{{n_{x}}}
lies in a smooth manifold, with
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
specifying the system’s initial state. The control inputs
\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{u}}}
is optimized to minimize the objective, where:

(i)

\ell_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{{n_{x}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{n_{u}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{++}
represents the stagewise cost for each time step,

(ii)

\ell_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{{n_{x}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{++}
is the terminal cost,

(iii)

\mathbf{f}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{{n_{x}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{n_{u}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{{n_{x}}}
captures the system’s forward dynamics or integrator
as in
[
16
]
,

(iv)

\mathbf{h}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{{n_{x}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{n_{u}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{{n_{v}}+{n_{c}}}
incorporates inverse dynamics and contact constraints, with
{n_{v}}
representing the dimension of inverse dynamics and
{n_{c}}
the dimension of contact constraints,

(v)

\mathbf{r}:\mathbb{R}^{{n_{x}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{{n_{r}}}
defines the endpoint
constraint
.

Moreover,
\mathbf{u}
represents joint efforts in forward dynamics formulations
[
17
]
or generalized accelerations and contact forces in inverse dynamics formulations
[
16
]
.

To solve this large optimization problem, we find the
KKT
point using an iterative routine based on the Newton method.
This approach requires factorizing the following system of equations:

\begin{bmatrix}&-\mathbf{I}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\scalebox{0.7}{$\ddots$}\\
&&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{x}_{k}}}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{u}_{k}}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}^{\intercal}\\
&&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}{\mathbf{x}_{k}}}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}{\mathbf{u}_{k}}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}^{\intercal}\\
&&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}\\
&&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}&&\scalebox{0.7}{$\ddots$}\\
&&&&&&\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{x}_{N}}}&\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}^{\intercal}\\
&&&&&&\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}&\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\mathbf{+}}\\
\scalebox{0.7}{$\vdots$}\\
\delta\mathbf{x}_{k}\\
\delta\mathbf{u}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}^{\mathbf{+}}\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k+1}^{\mathbf{+}}\\
\scalebox{0.7}{$\vdots$}\\
\delta\mathbf{x}_{N}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{+}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\bar{f}}_{0}\\
\scalebox{0.7}{$\vdots$}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}\\
\mathbf{\bar{h}}_{k}\\
\mathbf{\bar{f}}_{k}\\
\scalebox{0.7}{$\vdots$}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}\\
\mathbf{\bar{r}}\end{bmatrix}

(11)

In
Equation

11
, the infeasibilities are defined as follows:
\mathbf{\bar{f}}_{0}\coloneqq\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\ominus\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
\mathbf{\bar{h}}_{k}\coloneqq\mathbf{h}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})
,
\mathbf{\bar{f}}_{k+1}\coloneqq\mathbf{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k})\ominus\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
,
\mathbf{\bar{r}}\coloneqq\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x}_{N})
, which corresponds to initial
constraint
, integrator, inverse dynamics,
and endpoint constraint,
respectively.
Moreover,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\mathbf{+}}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}^{\mathbf{+}}
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}^{\mathbf{+}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{+}
are their Lagrange multipliers, where the
+
notation stands for the next value, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathbf{+}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\xi}+\delta\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

Additionally,
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{p}}
,
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{p}}
represent the Jacobians of the dynamics and constraints,
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{p}}
denotes the cost gradient, where
\mathbf{p}
refers to either
\mathbf{x}
or
\mathbf{u}
.
The Lagrangian’s Hessian,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}}
, is introduced below.

Exploiting the structure of the problem

Equation

11
matches the form introduced in
Equation

3
.
Specifically, we can partition
Equation

11
as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}&-\mathbf{I}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\scalebox{0.7}{$\ddots$}\\
&&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{x}_{k}}}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{u}_{k}}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}^{\intercal}\\
&&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}{\mathbf{x}_{k}}}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}{\mathbf{u}_{k}}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}^{\intercal}\\
&&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}\\
&&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}&&\scalebox{0.7}{$\ddots$}\\
&&&&&&\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{x}_{N}}}\end{bmatrix},\,\,\mathbf{a}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\bar{f}}_{0}\\
\scalebox{0.7}{$\vdots$}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}_{k}}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}\\
\mathbf{\bar{h}}_{k}\\
\mathbf{\bar{f}}_{k}\\
\scalebox{0.7}{$\vdots$}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}\end{bmatrix},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\scalebox{0.7}{$\cdots$}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\scalebox{0.7}{$\cdots$}&-\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{b}=-\mathbf{\bar{r}},

where
\mathbf{A}
represents the
KKT
matrix associated to an optimal control problem without endpoint constraints, and
\mathbf{a}
denotes its
KKT
vector.

In
Section

II
, we initially outlined an abstract solution for this large saddle point system.
Now, we continue by detailing how to compute
Equation

5
, leveraging the temporal structure of the problem for computational efficiency.

III-B

Endpoint-independent search direction

To compute the term
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
, we can employ two factorization techniques: the Schur-complement and the nullspace approach.
Both methods enable the design of efficient Riccati recursions.
These Riccati recursions are carried out by factorizing the following system of equations backward in time:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}}\\
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}}&\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}}&&&-\mathbf{I}\\
&&&-\mathbf{I}&\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}\\
\delta\hat{\mathbf{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\gamma}}^{\mathbf{+}}\\
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\xi}}^{\mathbf{+}}\\
\delta\hat{\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathbf{{}^{\prime}}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{u}}\\
\mathbf{\bar{h}}\\
\mathbf{\bar{f}}\\
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix},

(13)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{x}_{N}}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}
correspond to
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{x}_{N}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}_{N}}
, respectively.
The notation
(\cdot)^{\prime}
refers to quantities at the next time step
k+1
, and
\hat{(\cdot)}
denotes the endpoint-independent variables.
In each Riccati sweep, we first condense
Equation

13
into the following system of equations

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}&\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}&\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}\\
\delta\hat{\mathbf{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\gamma}}^{\mathbf{+}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}}\\
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}\\
\mathbf{\bar{h}}\end{bmatrix},\;

(14)

where the
\mathbf{Q}
’s terms are defined as follows

\begin{matrix}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}},&\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{+},\\
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}},&\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{+},\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}},\end{matrix}

(15)

with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}\coloneqq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{+}\coloneqq\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}+\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\mathbf{\bar{f}}
.
Next, we compute
\delta\hat{\mathbf{u}}
as a function of
\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, transforming
Equation

14
into

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}&\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{u}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta\hat{\mathbf{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\widehat{\gamma}}^{\mathbf{+}}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}\\
\mathbf{\bar{h}}+\mathbf{h}_{\mathbf{x}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

By solving
Equation

16
we obtain the endpoint-independent local control policy:

\delta\hat{\mathbf{u}}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}},

(17)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
are the feed-forward and feedback terms in
OC
problems without endpoint constraints.
Finally, we update its local approximation of the value function needed for the next Riccati sweep as follows

\delta\hat{\mathcal{V}}=\frac{\Delta\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{1}+2\Delta\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{2}}{2}+\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}(\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}_{2}})+\frac{1}{2}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}

(18)

with terms computed as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{1}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}},

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{2}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}={\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}},

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}-2\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}+{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}.

(19)

For forward dynamics,
Section

III-B
simplifies, where
\Delta\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{1}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}
,
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}}{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
.
The expressions for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}
in
Equation

17
vary depending on whether a forward or inverse dynamics formulation is used.

III-B
1
Forward dynamics

In forward dynamics formulations, where stagewise constraints
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
are often not considered,
Equation

16
reduces to the classical
DDP

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{u}\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}_{u}\coloneqq\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}}.

(20)

III-B
2
Inverse dynamics

In inverse dynamics formulations, when factorizing
Equation

17
using the Schur complement, the feed-forward and feedback terms are computed as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{s}\coloneqq\mathbf{k}+(\mathbf{k}_{s}^{\intercal}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,{\boldsymbol{\Pi}}_{s}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}+(\mathbf{K}_{s}^{\intercal}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{\Psi}_{s}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}

(21)

V
Conclusion

We introduced an exact method for handling endpoint constraints in
differential dynamic programming
, designed to efficiently tackle rank deficiencies.
Our approach demonstrated high effectiveness across a wide range of optimal control problems, adeptly handling stagewise equality constraints like inverse dynamics and contact constraints.
By reusing the Riccati recursion from problems without endpoint constraints, our method achieved exceptional computational efficiency, making it an ideal solution for real-time
MPC
in robotics.
Looking ahead, we aim to extend this strategy to manage stagewise inequality constraints and unlock its full potential for parallelizing
DDP
algorithms.
