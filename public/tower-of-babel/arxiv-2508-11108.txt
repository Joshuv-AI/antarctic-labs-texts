Short mollifiers of the Riemann zeta-function
J. B. Conrey, D. W. Farmer, C.-H. Kwan, Y. Lin, C. L. Turnage-Butterbaugh

Abstract
. We apply the calculus of variations to construct a new sequence of linear combinations of derivatives of the Riemann ζ \zeta -function adapted to Levinson’s method, which yield a positive proportion of zeros of the ζ \zeta -function on the critical line, regardless of how short the mollifier is. Our construction extends readily to modular L L -functions. Even with Levinson’s original choice of mollifier, our method more than doubles the proportions of zeros on the critical line for modular L L -functions previously obtained by Bernard and Kühn–Robles–Zeindler, while relying on the same arithmetic inputs. This indicates that optimizing the linear combinations, an approach that has received relatively little attention, has a more pronounced effect than refining the mollifier when it is short. Curiously, our linear combinations provide non-trivial smooth approximations of Siegel’s 𝔣 \mathfrak{f} -function in the celebrated Riemann–Siegel formula.

1.
Introduction

There are two well-known methods for proving that a positive proportion of the zeros of the Riemann zeta-function
\zeta(s)
(
s=\sigma+it
) lie on the critical line
\sigma=1/2
: Selberg’s method
[
Sel42
]
and Levinson’s method
[
Lev74
]
. Both of these methods involve the use of
mollifiers
. In Selberg’s method, roughly speaking, one calculates a mollified second moment of
\zeta(s)

on
the critical line, where the mollifier itself is a fourth power of an approximation to
\zeta(s)^{1/2}
; see
[
IK04
, Chapter 24.2]
. In Levinson’s method, one calculates a second mollified moment of
\zeta(s)

off
the critical line, where the mollifier is an approximation to
\zeta(s)^{-1}
.

Selberg’s method has the advantage of working regardless of the length of the mollifier. By contrast, it has long been believed that Levinson’s method suffers from a major drawback: to obtain a positive proportion of zeros on the critical line, the mollifier must be sufficiently long. This viewpoint is supported Farmer
[
Far94
, p. 215]
, who provided the plot shown in Figure
1

Figure 1
.
Proportion of zeros on the critical line as a function of the length
\theta
via Levinson’s method; reprinted from
[
Far94
, p. 215]

and remarked, “It is interesting that Levinson’s method does not yield a result if
\theta
is too small.” Levinson’s method, however, tends to yield much stronger explicit bounds for the proportion, and in some cases, can also establish a positive proportion of simple zeros on the critical line. As described in
[
Iwa14
, Chapter 16]
,

Levinson’s approach seems to be a gamble, because it is not clear up front that at the end the numerical constants are good enough to yield a positive lower bound for [the number of critical zeros up to height
T
].

In this paper, we prove that Levinson’s method, as modified by Conrey
[
Con89
]
, will in fact give a positive proportion of zeros on the critical line no matter how short the mollifier is—contrary to the common belief.

1.1.
Levinson’s method

Levinson’s starting point is a theorem of Speiser
[
Spe35
]
, which states that the Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to the assertion that all non-real zeros of
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
are in the half-plane
\sigma\geq 1/2
. This result was refined by Levinson and Montgomery
[
LM74
]
, who proved a (essentially) one-to-one correspondence between the non-real zeros of
\zeta(s)
and those of
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
in the region
\sigma<1/2
(presumably both sets are empty).

Subsequently, Levinson
[
Lev74
]
proved that the number of zeros of
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
in the region
\sigma<1/2
,
0<t<T
is at most
(1/3)(T/2\pi)\log T
, i.e. at most
1/3
of the zeros are to the
left
of
\sigma=1/2
. The same result holds for
\zeta(s)
by the aforementioned theorem of
[
LM74
]
. The functional equation of
\zeta(s)
allows us to deduce that at most
1/3
of the zeros of
\zeta(s)
are to the
right
of
\sigma=1/2
. This leaves
at least

1/3
of the zeros of
\zeta(s)

on
the line
\sigma=1/2
.

Using the functional equation again, the zeros of
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
to the left of
\sigma=1/2
correspond to the zeros of
\zeta(s)+L^{-1}\zeta^{\prime}(s)
(
L:=\log(T/2\pi)
) on the
right
of
\sigma=1/2
. This formulation turns out to be more practical. The essential ingredient to Levinson’s argument is an asymptotic formula for the moment

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\,\int_{0}^{T}|(\zeta+L^{-1}\zeta^{\prime})(a+it)|^{2}|M(a+it)|^{2}~dt,

where

a
is a real number slightly less than
1/2
(see (
13
)), and
M
is a
mollifier
of the form

(2)

\displaystyle M(s)\,=\,\sum_{n\leq y}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s+1/2-a}}\frac{\log(y/n)}{\log y},

where
\mu(n)
is the Möbius
\mu
-function,
y=T^{\theta}
,
and
\theta>0
is the ‘length’ of the mollifier, the key parameter.

Levinson established (
1
) for all
\theta<1/2
. The admissible range for
\theta
was later extended by Conrey
[
Con89
]
to
\theta<4/7
. With refinements to be discussed below, he showed that at least
40\%
of the zeros of
\zeta(s)
are on the critical line
[
Con89
, Theorem 1]
. In
[
Far93
]
, Farmer proposed the ‘
\theta=\infty
’ conjecture, that is, the admissible value for
\theta
can be arbitrarily large. He showed that this would imply
100\%
of the zeros of
\zeta(s)
lie on
\sigma=1/2
. More recently, Bettin and Gonek
[
BG17
]
proved that ‘
\theta=\infty
’ in fact implies the Riemann Hypothesis.

Conrey
[
Con89
]
refined Levinson’s method in two crucial ways. First, he observed that the role of
\zeta(s)+L^{-1}\zeta^{\prime}(s)
in (
1
) can be replaced by any
linear combination
of derivatives of
\zeta(s)
of the form
Q(-\frac{1}{L}\frac{d}{ds})\zeta(s)
, where
Q
is a real polynomial that satisfies

(3)

\displaystyle Q(0)\,=\,1\hskip 15.0pt\text{and}\hskip 15.0ptQ^{\prime}(y)\,=\,Q^{\prime}(1-y).

The condition (
3
) stems from the functional equation of
\zeta(s)
. Second, he considered a more general class of mollifiers of the form

(4)

\displaystyle M(s,P):=\sum_{n\leq y}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s+1/2-a}}P\Big(\frac{\log(y/n)}{\log y}\Big),

where
P
is a real polynomial with

(5)

\displaystyle P(0)\,=\,0\hskip 15.0pt\text{and}\hskip 15.0ptP(1)\,=\,1.

He established an asymptotic formula for the corresponding mollified moment (see (
12
) and (
15
)) for any such
P
,
Q
and
\theta<4/7
, and determined the optimal choice of
P
.

1.2.
Results

We prove that no matter how small
\theta>0
is in (
2
), there exists an admissible linear combination
of derivatives of
\zeta(s)
that yields a positive proportion of zeros of
\zeta(s)
on the critical line via Levinson’s method. Moreover, this proportion can be made quantitative; see
Theorem
1
. In fact, our linear combination is optimal with respect to the variational problem we consider (see Problem
1
and equation (
22
)). Such an optimizer (for
Q
) is significantly more intricate than the one for the mollifier polynomial
P
in
[
Con89
, p. 9]
.

For small values of
\theta
, the improvement gained by choosing
Q
strategically is significant. For example, in Bernard’s work
[
Ber15
]
on critical zeros of modular (degree two)
L
-functions, he obtains a proportion of 2.97% of zeros on
\sigma=1/2
(using the optimal
P
and an empirical
Q
of degree
7
), whereas we get 6.32% (using
P(x)=x
and our newly constructed
Q
), both unconditionally and using the same shifted convolution estimate. We have not attempted to optimize the proportion here (a method for full optimization is described in Section
7
), but our result already comes close to the current record achieved in
[
AT21
]
(using the optimal
P
and an empirical
Q
of degree
27
!).

The broader goal is to initiate a systematic and
theoretical
study of how to select effective linear combinations of derivatives of the
\zeta
- (or
L
-)functions in analytic applications, in contrast to the predominantly
empirical
, computer-experimental approach used to date.

1.3.
Connections to Siegel’s method

Let
h(s):=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)
. Levinson’s method detects zeros of
\zeta(s)
on
\sigma=1/2
by identifying when
\mathrm{arg}\,(h\zeta^{\prime})(1/2+it)\equiv\pi/2\,(\bmod\,\pi)
occurs. By the argument principle, this task reduces to bounding the number of zeros of
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
on
\sigma<1/2
. In the introduction to
[
Lev74
]
, Levinson noted that the idea of detecting zeros of
\zeta(s)
on the critical line via changes in argument and moment estimates (of a suitable function) had been used in Siegel’s 1932 work
[
Sie32
]
.

In
[
Sie32
]
, instead of working with
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
as in
[
Lev74
]
, Siegel introduced an interesting entire function:

(6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(s)\,:=\,\int_{L}\frac{e^{i\pi w^{2}}}{e^{i\pi w}-e^{-i\pi w}}w^{-s}\,dw,

where the line of integration
L
has slope
1
, with
\mathrm{Im}\,w
decreasing, and intersects the real axis at a point between
0
and
1
. The function
\mathfrak{f}(s)
originates from the
Riemann–Siegel formula
, which is an integral representation of
\zeta(s)
given by

(7)

\displaystyle h(s)\zeta(s)\,=\,2\,\re h(s)\mathfrak{f}(s)\hskip 20.0pt(\re s\,=\,1/2).

This formula first appeared in Riemann’s 1859 memoir
[
Rie
]
but remained largely unnoticed in the literature until its rediscovery by Siegel.

Whenever
\mathrm{arg}\,(h\mathfrak{f})(1/2+it)\equiv\pi/2\,(\bmod\,\pi)
occurs, it follows from (
7
) that there is a zero of
\zeta(s)
on the critical line. In fact, Siegel proved that

(8)

\displaystyle N_{0}(T)\,>\,2\,\cdot\,\#\{s=\sigma+it:\,\mathfrak{f}(s)=0,\,\sigma<1/2,\,0<t<T\},

where
N_{0}(T)
is the number of zeros of
\zeta(s)
with
\sigma=1/2
with
0<t\leq T
. A
lower bound
to the right-hand side of (
8
) can be obtained by evaluating the moment

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{1}^{T}\,|\mathfrak{f}(\sigma+it)|^{2}\,dt,

which turns out to be
\sim C_{\sigma}\cdot T^{1/2-\sigma}
for some explicit
C_{\sigma}>0
, and for
\sigma<1/2
to be chosen. As a consequence, Siegel established, for
T\gg 1
, that

(10)

\displaystyle N_{0}(T)\,>\,\frac{3}{8\pi}e^{-3/2}T,

which improves upon the result of Hardy and Littlewood
[
HL21
]
that
N_{0}(T)\gg T
.

The function
\mathfrak{f}(s)
can be
formally
interpreted in Conrey–Levinson’s set-up. Consider

(11)

\displaystyle Q_{\infty}(y)\,:=\,\begin{cases}\ \ 1\hskip 15.0pt\text{if}\hskip 10.0pt0\,\leq\,y\,<\,1/2\\
\,1/2\hskip 10.0pt\text{if}\hskip 20.0pty\,=\,1/2\\
\ \ 0\hskip 15.0pt\text{if}\hskip 10.0pt1/2\,<\,y\,\leq\,1.\end{cases}

Let
s=1/2+iT
with
T\gg 1
, and
L:=\log(T/2\pi)
. By the saddle point method, Siegel deduced, up to a negligible error of
O(T^{-1/4})
, that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(s)\,

\displaystyle=\,\sum_{n\leq\sqrt{T/2\pi}}\,\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{-s}Q_{\infty}\Big(\frac{\log n}{L}\Big)\,=\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Q_{\infty}\Big(-\frac{1}{L}\frac{d}{ds}\Big)n^{-s}\,=\,Q_{\infty}\Big(-\frac{1}{L}\frac{d}{ds}\Big)\zeta(s).

The step function
Q_{\infty}(y)
satisfies the condition (
3
) apart from failing to be differentiable at
y=1/2
, and therefore does not qualify as an admissible function in Conrey–Levinson’s method. In fact, it was commented in
[
Lev74
, p. 384]
that

It appears to me that the function
f_{1}(s)
[i.e.,
\mathfrak{f}(s)
] is not amenable to improvement with a mollifier.

As Siegel noted at the end of his paper, his method itself
cannot
yield a bound
N_{0}(T)\gg T(\log\log T)^{1+\epsilon}
. However, we will show that

Proposition 1
.

For any
\theta>0
, let
Q_{\theta}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the function constructed in Theorem
1
, which is used to define linear combinations of derivatives of
\zeta(s)
. Then as
\theta\to 0+
, the pointwise limit of
Q_{\theta}(y)
converges to the step function
Q_{\infty}(y)
for any
y\in[0,1]
.

In other words, behind
Theorem
1
is the idea of mollifying linear combinations of derivatives of
\zeta(s)
that
approximate
Siegel’s function
\mathfrak{f}(s)
. Proposition
1
shows that we are actually using polynomials
Q
of
arbitrarily large
degree in showing Levinson’s method works for mollifiers of any length
\theta>0
. Unlike previous works on mollifiers and critical zeros, a key feature of our approach is that our linear combinations vary with the values of
\theta
.

2.
Preliminary work

2.1.
Set up

Let
P
be a polynomial satisfying (
5
),
Q
a polynomial satisfying (
3
), and let
R>0
be any constant. According to
[
Con89
, eq. (39)]
, if
\theta>0
is an admissible constant such that the asymptotic formula

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\,\Big|Q\Big(-\frac{1}{L}\frac{d}{ds}\Big)\zeta(a+it)M(a+it,P)\Big|^{2}\,dt\,\sim\,c(P,Q,R)

holds as
T\to\infty
, where

(13)

\displaystyle a\,=\,1/2-R/\log(T/2\pi),

and
c(P,Q,R)
is a constant depending on
P,Q,
and
R
, then the proportion of zeros of
\zeta(s)
on the critical line is
at least

(14)

\displaystyle\kappa\,:=\,1-\frac{\log c(P,Q,R)}{R}.

By
[
Con89
, Theorem 2]
, the constant
c(P,Q,R)
is given by

(15)

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{\theta}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\big(w(y)P^{\prime}(x)+\theta w^{\prime}(y)P(x)\big)^{2}~dx~dy,

where

(16)

w(y)\,:=\,e^{Ry}Q(y).

To simplify the exposition and illustrate the strength of our construction, we adopt Levinson’s original choice:

(17)

\displaystyle P(x)\,=\,x.

Also, observe that the class of admissible functions
Q
can be extended to include all continuously differentiable (
C^{1}
) functions
Q:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with

(18)

\displaystyle Q(0)=1\hskip 15.0pt\text{and}\hskip 15.0ptQ(y)+Q(1-y)\,=\,1.

Indeed, it follows from the Weierstrass approximation theorem that any such
Q
can be uniformly approximated by sequences of polynomials on
[0,1]
that satisfy (
18
).

We now state our main result.

Theorem 1
.

There exists
\theta_{0}>0
such that whenever
\theta\in(0,\theta_{0})
, there exists
Q=Q_{\theta}\in C^{1}[0,1]
satisfying (
18
) such that
\kappa
defined in (
14
) satisfies
\kappa>2\theta/3>0
.

The constant
\theta_{0}
is explicitly computable. In fact, our numerical evidence (see Figure
7(b)
) suggests the following explicit bound for any
0<\theta\leq 1/2
:

(19)

\displaystyle\kappa\,>\,(2/3)\theta.

Moreover, we will be able to make more general choices of
P
and
Q
in Section
7
.

2.2.
Simplification

Expanding the square of (
15
) and integrating the mixed term with the identity
\int_{0}^{1}\,f(x)f^{\prime}(x)\,dx\,=\,(f(1)^{2}-f(0)^{2})/2
, we have

(20)

\displaystyle c(P,Q,R)=\frac{1}{2}+J(Q),

where
J(Q)
is the functional

(21)

\displaystyle J(Q)=J_{R,\theta}(Q):=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}w(y)^{2}+\frac{\theta}{3}\,\,w^{\prime}(y)^{2}\right)~dy,

and
w(y)
is defined in (
16
). Given constants
R,\theta>0
, we aim to find the minimum of
J(Q)
subject to the condition (
18
).

The expressions can be simplified by some changes of variables. Letting

(22)

q(y):=Q(y+1/2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad S(t)=S_{R}(t):=q(t/2R)

in (
21
), we have

(23)

\displaystyle J(Q)\,=\,\frac{e^{R}}{2R}\int_{-R}^{R}\bigg(\frac{1}{\theta}e^{t}S(t)^{2}+\frac{\theta}{3}\,R^{2}e^{t}\big(S(t)+2S^{\prime}(t)\big)^{2}\bigg)~dt,

and the condition (
18
) becomes

(24)

\displaystyle S(-R)=1\hskip 15.0pt\text{and}\hskip 15.0ptS(t)+S(-t)\,=\,1.

Here,
S
is a real-valued,
C^{1}
-function defined on
[-R,R]
. It follows that
S(R)=0
, and, in particular, that

\displaystyle\int_{-R}^{R}e^{t}S(t)S^{\prime}(t)~dt\,=\,-\frac{e^{-R}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-R}^{R}e^{t}S(t)^{2}~dt.

Hence, we may write

(25)

\displaystyle J(Q)=-\frac{\theta}{3}\,R\,+\,\frac{e^{R}}{2R}\,K(S),

where

(26)

\displaystyle K(S)=K_{\theta,R}(S):=\int_{-R}^{R}e^{t}\left[c_{0}S(t)^{2}+c_{1}S^{\prime}(t)^{2})\right]~dt,

and the constants
c_{0},c_{1}
are given by

(27)

c_{0}=c_{0}(R,\theta):=\frac{1}{\theta}-\frac{\theta}{3}\,R^{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad c_{1}=c_{1}(R,\theta):=\frac{4\theta}{3}\,R^{2}.

By splitting the integral in (
26
) into the part from
-R
to
0
and the part from
0
to
R
, we may impose the constraint
S(t)+S(-t)=1
and further simplify
K(S)
to write

(28)

\displaystyle K(S)=\int_{0}^{R}\Big(e^{t}\left(c_{0}S(t)^{2}+c_{1}S^{\prime}(t)^{2}\right)+e^{-t}\left(c_{0}(1-S(t))^{2}+c_{1}S^{\prime}(t)^{2}\right)\Big)\,dt.

We have reduced the problem to finding the minimizer of
K(S)
subject to (
24
).

To keep our argument clean and reduce the number of variables in the course of proving Theorem
1
, we introduce the following assumption:

Assumption 1
.

Assume that
c_{0}=c_{1}
, where
c_{0},c_{1}
are defined in (
27
).

We emphasize, however, that the steps leading up to Section
3.1
do not depend on this assumption. A generalization is given in Section
7
. The computations following Section
3.1
can also be extended to the general case, though at the expense of increased computational complexity.

The assumption is equivalent to

(29)

\displaystyle\theta=R^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{3}{5}}.

In this case, we have
c_{0}=c_{1}=4/(5\theta)
. We note that
R
can be taken to be any positive constant. We insert (
29
) into (
28
) to write

(30)

\displaystyle K^{\ast}_{R}(S)\,:=\,\frac{4}{5\theta}\,\int_{0}^{R}e^{t}\left(S(t)^{2}+S^{\prime}(t)^{2}\right)+e^{-t}\left((1-S(t))^{2}+S^{\prime}(t)^{2}\right)~dt.

Here, ‘
\ast
’ in
K^{\ast}_{R}(S)
signifies the use of (
29
). Our main problem can now be stated as follows.

Problem 1
.

Given any
R>0
, find the minimum value of the functional
K_{R}^{\ast}(S)
and the minimizer
S=S_{R}(t)
subject to the condition (
24
).

In the next section, we solve this problem by using the calculus of variations.

3.
Calculus of variations

In the following, we write
K^{\ast}(S)=K^{\ast}_{R}(S)
. Suppose that
K^{\ast}(S)
attains a minimum at
S
, and let
\epsilon>0
be small and
\phi(t)
be an arbitrary smooth test function on
[0,R]
such that
\phi(0)=\phi(R)=0
. Then
K^{\ast}(S+\epsilon\phi)
, as a function of
\epsilon
, has a minimum at
\epsilon=0
. Since

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}K^{\ast}(S+\epsilon\phi)\Big|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{5\theta}\int_{0}^{R}\big(e^{t}(2S\phi+2S^{\prime}\phi^{\prime})+e^{-t}(2(S-1)\phi+2S^{\prime}\phi^{\prime})\big)~dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{16}{5\theta}\int_{0}^{R}\big(\cosh(t)S(t)-(S^{\prime}(t)\cosh t)^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}e^{-t}\big)\phi(t)~dt,

we must determine
S
for which the Euler–Lagrange equation

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(S^{\prime}\cosh t\right)=S\cosh t-\frac{1}{2}e^{-t}

is satisfied. Equation (
31
) can be rewritten as

(32)

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}(t)+(\tanh t)S^{\prime}(t)-\big(S(t)-\frac{1}{1+e^{2t}}\big)=0.

The boundary conditions for (
32
) are

(33)

\displaystyle S(0)=1/2\hskip 20.0pt\text{and}\hskip 20.0ptS(R)=0.

Before proceeding to solve for
S
, we incorporate the preceding steps to rewrite
c(P,Q,R)
as defined in (
20
). Assume that
S
satisfies the differential equation (
31
). Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}S^{\prime}(t)^{2}\cosh t~dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(t)S^{\prime}(t)\cosh t\bigg|_{0}^{R}-\int_{0}^{R}S(t)\frac{d}{dt}(S^{\prime}(t)\cosh t)~dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}S^{\prime}(0)-\int_{0}^{R}S(t)\big(S(t)\cosh t-\frac{1}{2}e^{-t}\big)~dt.

It follows from (
28
) that

\displaystyle K^{\ast}(S)

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{5\theta}\int_{0}^{R}\big(S(t)^{2}\cosh t+S^{\prime}(t)^{2}\cosh t-e^{-t}S(t)\big)\,dt\,+\,\frac{4}{5\theta}(1-e^{-R})

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{5\theta}\Big(1-e^{-R}-S^{\prime}(0)-\int_{0}^{R}e^{-t}S(t)~dt\Big).

Upon substituting (
29
) and (
3
) into (
25
), we find that (
20
) becomes

\displaystyle c(P,Q,R)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\theta}{3}R+\frac{e^{R}}{2R}\frac{4}{5\theta}\Big(1-e^{-R}-S^{\prime}(0)-\int_{0}^{R}e^{-t}S(t)~dt\Big)

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{15}}\,\Big(-1+2e^{R}\big(1-e^{-R}-S^{\prime}(0)-\int_{0}^{R}e^{-t}S(t)~dt\big)\Big).

Readers should keep in mind that (
3
) depends only on
R
because of (
29
) and (
17
).

3.1.
Solving the Euler–Lagrange equation

We first present the solution to the differential equation

(36)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}S^{\prime\prime}(t)+\tanh(t)S^{\prime}(t)-S(t)+(1+e^{2t})^{-1}=0,\\
\hskip 40.0ptS(0)=1/2,\hskip 10.0pt\text{and}\hskip 10.0ptS(R)=0.\end{cases}

It takes the form

(37)

\displaystyle S_{R}(t):=C_{1}(R)f(t)+g_{0}(t)+g_{1}(t)\int_{0}^{t}v_{1}(u)~du+g_{2}(t)\int_{0}^{t}v_{2}(u)~du,

where the functions
C_{1},f,g_{0},g_{1},g_{2},v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are all expressible in terms of the Gauss
{}_{2}F_{1}
-hypergeometric function

(38)

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;z)\,:=\,\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\,\frac{\Gamma(a+n)\Gamma(b+n)}{\Gamma(c+n)}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

The series (
38
) converges for
|z|<1
and
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
with
c\neq 0,-1,-2,\ldots
, and it admits an analytic continuation in the
z
-variable over

Our goal is to show that

(or equivalently,
\theta

in (

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)=-1.47277\dots,\hskip 15.0ptg_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0.602775\dots,\hskip 15.0ptg_{1}(0)=-1.07479\dots,

\displaystyle g_{2}(0)=0.759136\dots,\hskip 15.0ptv_{1}(0)=0.339496\dots,\hskip 15.0ptv_{2}(0)=0.480664\dots.

), (

7.
Generalizations and further questions

We sketch the general set-up. Let
P\in C^{1}[0,1]
be a
given
real-valued function such that
P(0)=0
and
P(1)=1
,
and we let

B:=\int_{0}^{1}P(x)^{2}~dx\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad C:=\int_{0}^{1}P^{\prime}(x)^{2}~dx.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
R>0
be constants at our disposal. By the same reasoning as in Section
3
, we are readily led to the problem of optimizing the functional

(59)

\displaystyle K(S)\,:=\,\int_{0}^{R}\Big(e^{t}(c_{0}S(t)^{2}+c_{1}S^{\prime}(t)^{2})+e^{-t}(c_{0}(\beta-S(t))^{2}+c_{1}S^{\prime}(t)^{2})\Big)\,dt,

with
S\in C^{1}[0,R]
and subject to boundary conditions

(60)

\displaystyle S(0)\,=\,\beta/2\hskip 20.0pt\text{and}\hskip 20.0ptS(R)\,=\,\beta-1,

where

\displaystyle c_{0}\,:=\,\frac{C}{\theta}-\theta BR^{2}\hskip 20.0pt\text{and}\hskip 20.0ptc_{1}\,:=\,4\theta BR^{2}.

For any
S
satisfying the constraints above and for any admissible value of
\theta>0
(cf. (
12
)), we have

(61)

\displaystyle\kappa\,=\,1-\frac{1}{R}\,\log\Big(\frac{1+e^{2R}(\beta-1)^{2}}{2}\,+\,c_{1}\,\frac{e^{R}}{2R}\frac{e^{R}(1-\beta)^{2}-e^{-R}}{2}\,+\,\frac{e^{R}}{2R}\,K(S)\Big).

Furthermore, for the optimal function
S
, we may simplify (
59
) as

(62)

\displaystyle K(S)=c_{0}\beta^{2}(1-e^{-R})-c_{1}\beta S^{\prime}(0)+2(\beta-1)c_{1}S^{\prime}(R)\cosh R-c_{0}\beta\int_{0}^{R}e^{-t}S(t)~dt.

Remark 3
.

The constant
\beta
arises from the constraint for (real-valued)
Q\in C^{1}[0,1]
:

(63)

\displaystyle Q(0)=1\hskip 15.0pt\text{and}\hskip 15.0ptQ(y)+Q(1-y)\,=\,\beta.

Again, we have
S(-t):=\beta-S(t)
for
t\geq 0
and
Q(y)=S(2R(y-1/2))
for
y\in[0,1]
.

We also let

\displaystyle c\,:=\,-\frac{c_{0}}{c_{1}}\,=\,\frac{1}{4}-\frac{C}{4B\theta^{2}R^{2}}.

With (
60
), the Euler–Lagrange equation for
K(S)
in this general case is given by

(64)

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}(t)\,+\,(\tanh t)S^{\prime}(t)\,+\,cS(t)\,=\,\frac{c\beta}{1+e^{2t}}.

Remark 4
.

The Euler–Lagrange equation above depends on
R>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
c<1/4
which further depends on
B,C,\theta,R
. When proving Theorem
1
, the only parameter that varies is
R
(or equivalently,
\theta
). In that case,
c=-1
,
\beta=1
,
B=1/3
, and
C=1
.

Let
\phi_{c}:=(1+\sqrt{1-4c})/2
. A set of fundamental solutions to the homogeneous equation of (
32
) is given by

\displaystyle g_{1}(t;c)\,

\displaystyle:=\,\exp(tc/\phi_{c})\,_{2}F_{1}\big(1/2,\,c/\phi_{c};\,1/2-c/\phi_{c};\,-e^{2t}\big),

(65)

\displaystyle g_{2}(t;c)\,

\displaystyle:=\,\exp(t\phi_{c})\,_{2}F_{1}\big(1/2,\,\phi_{c};\,1/2+\phi_{c};\,-e^{2t}\big).

Parallel to the notations in Section
3.1
, we set

\displaystyle\hskip 12.0ptF^{+}(t;c):={}_{2}F_{1}\big(1/2,\,\phi_{c},\,1/2+\phi_{c};\,-e^{2t}\big);\quad F^{-}(t;c):={}_{2}F_{1}\big(1/2,\,c/\phi_{c},\,1/2+c/\phi_{c};\,-e^{2t}\big);

\displaystyle F_{1}^{+}(u;c):={}_{2}F_{1}\big(1/2,\,1+\phi_{c},\,1/2+\phi_{c};\,-e^{2u}\big);\quad F_{1}^{-}(u;c):={}_{2}F_{1}\big(3/2,\,c/\phi_{c},\,1/2+c/\phi_{c};\,-e^{2u}\big).

The Wronskian for
\{g_{1}(t;c),\,g_{2}(t;c)\}
can be computed to be

(66)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(u;c)\,=\,\mathcal{W}(g_{1},g_{2})(u;c)\,=\,e^{u}(2\phi_{c}F^{-}(u;c)F_{1}^{+}(u;c)-F^{+}(u;c)F_{1}^{-}(u;c)).

Similar to before, we introduce:

(67)

\displaystyle f(t;c)=g_{1}(t;c)-\frac{g_{1}(0;c)}{g_{2}(0;c)}\,g_{2}(t;c),\hskip 15.0ptg_{0}(t;c)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{g_{2}(t;c)}{g_{2}(0;c)},

\displaystyle S_{R}(t;c,\beta)\,=\,C_{1}(R;c,\beta)f(t;c)+\beta g_{0}(t;c)\,-\,c\beta g_{1}(t;c)\int_{0}^{t}v_{1}(u;c)~du-c\beta g_{2}(t)\int_{0}^{t}v_{2}(u;c)~du,

where

(70)

\displaystyle C_{1}(R;c,\beta)\,=\,\frac{\beta-1-\beta g_{0}(R;c)+c\beta g_{1}(R;c)w_{1}(R;c)+c\beta g_{2}(R;c)w_{2}(R;c)}{f(R;c)}.
