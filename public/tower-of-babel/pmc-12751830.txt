RNA virome of ticks and fleas infesting Marmota himalayana from the Qinghai-Tibet plateau
Authors: Wentao Zhu, Ji Pu, Dong Jin, Shan Lu, Jing Yang, Jianguo Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTicks and fleas are important vectors and hosts for various emergent human pathogens, such as severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus, Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus, and tick-borne encephalitis virus. In the Qinghai-Tibet plateau of China, Marmota himalayana (marmots) serves as the primary reservoir for Yersinia pestis, primarily transmitted by fleas. Due to their wide distribution and large population, marmots serve as important host animals for ticks and fleas to survive and reproduce on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. However, the spectrum of RNA viruses found in ticks and fleas that feed on marmots is still not fully understood.MethodsTicks and fleas infesting marmots in the Qinghai-Tibet plateau, China, were collected in 2018. The RNA virome of pooled ticks and fleas were analyzed using a metatranscriptomics approach.ResultsA total of 30 RNA viruses were identified, with 11 viruses from ticks (Ixodes spp.) and 19 viruses from fleas (Oropsylla silantiewi), which were classified into 16 families, mainly Phasmaviridae (n = 4), Phenuiviridae (n = 4), and Iflaviridae (n = 3), Peribunyaviridae (n = 2). These viruses formed distinct clusters and shared less than 84% amino acid identity with their closest relatives, indicating that they belonged to novel members of their respective taxa and circulated among ticks and fleas for an extended period. Furthermore, six nearly identical viruses were shared between ticks and fleas, with 96.0 − 99.8% RNA-dependent RNA polymerase amino acid identity, but only 38.5 − 73.8% identity with known viruses, indicating potential interspecies transmission of viruses between ticks and fleas.ConclusionOverall, these findings highlight the diverse and abundant viruses that arthropods in the Qinghai-Tibet plateau can harbor, shedding light on the potential for cross-species transmission of viruses.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-025-00488-8.

Background

Arthropods, forming the most numerous phylum Arthropoda on Earth, account for over 80% of all known living animal species [1]. Fleas and ticks both parasitize Marmota himalayana. Fleas (order Siphonaptera) live by ingesting the blood of mammals and birds, and comprises greater than 2,500 species of small insects, belonging to the largest class (Insecta) of arthropods [2]. Fleas (Callopsylla dolabris) are vectors of Yersinia pestis with the main reservoir being Marmota himalayana [3]. Ticks are members of the Order Ixodida, including nearly 900 species (700 hard ticks and 200 soft ticks) [4–6]. Genus Ixodes is the richness group with about 250 species, some of which can release toxins causing paralysis. Ixodes crenulatus, as a vector of the spotted fever group rickettsiae, can parasitize numerous animals, such as Marmota himalayana, Ochotona curzoniae, and sparrow [7].

Ticks and fleas are important biological vectors responsible for transmitting various viruses. It is well-known that ticks and fleas are involved in the transmission of numerous known viral pathogens, such as tick-borne encephalitis virus [8, 9], Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus [10], Kyasanur Forest disease virus [11], Alkhumra hemorrhagic fever virus [12], and Omsk hemorrhagic fever virus [13]. Additionally, an increasing number of novel RNA viruses have been discovered in ticks and fleas both within and outside China [2, 14–20]. Although the diversity of RNA viruses has been studied in mammals and birds on the Qinghai-Tibet plateau [21–25], the RNA viromes of ticks and fleas in the high-altitude ecosystems remain largely unexplored.

Next-generation sequencing technology, particularly metatranscriptomics, has been widely proven as a powerful method in virology research, especially in viral discovery [26]. Using this powerful tool, our understanding of the RNA virosphere of arthropod species has greatly changed [6, 27]. Herein, we conducted the metatranscriptomic analysis of the ticks (Ixodes spp.) and fleas (Oropsylla silantiewi) parasitizing marmots to broaden our understanding of the virosphere structure in the Qinghai-Tibet plateau.

Materials and methods

Arthropod collection

During May to August 2018, marmots were captured for plague surveillance in the Qinghai-Tibet plateau (the Huangnan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture of Qinghai province; 3200 meters above sea level; N 35°49′, E 102°28′) of China. Ticks and fleas infecting marmots were collected, and placed into different aseptic tubes in liquid nitrogen for transportation, and stored at −80 °C freezer in our lab until further processing. The arthropod species were mainly identified by trained entomologists used a microscop, which were then confirmed by analyzing the host reads from the Illumina sequencing.

RNA extraction and library construction

Firstly, 1–5 ticks or 3–5 fleas were pooled into a tube for total RNA extraction. Pooled arthropods in each tube were washed thrice in 75% ethanol, twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and homogenized in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium using Tissue Lyser II (Qiagen, Hilden, Düsseldorf). These tissue homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The total RNA was extracted from supernatants by RNeasy Plus Universal Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Düsseldorf), and eluted in 60 µL of DNase- and RNase-free water. Finally, the extracted total RNA was further pooled in tick and flea pools in equal proportions from each tube based on the animal type.

The quality and concentration of RNA were measured by Qubit2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California) and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent). The host rRNA in each pool was removed using the Ribo-Zero Gold rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina, San Diego, California) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was randomly fragmented into 250 − 300 bp segments using NEB fragmentation buffer and reverse transcribed into cDNA with random primers. Libraries were constructed using TruSeq stranded total RNA library prep gold kit (Illumina, San Diego, California) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of libraries, including inset size, was determined using Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. Further, the Illumina HiSeq2000 platform with 2 × 150 bp paired reads was used for sequencing.

Virus discovery

The raw data were trimmed to remove the low-quality bases and adapter sequences using FastQC v0.12.1 (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC) and Trimmomatic v0.39 [28]. The obtained clean data were mapped to the corresponding host genome using Bowtie2 [29], and further de novo assembled into contigs using Trinity v2.8 [30], which were clustered using CD-HIT [31]. The assembled contigs were annotated using both the conserved domains database and the non-redundant protein database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) using Diamond with an e-value threshold of 1 × 10−5 [32]. The top blast hit was used to determine the taxonomic status of contigs, and those with all hits under the kingdom “Viruses” were retained and determined to be probable viral contigs for further analysis. Contigs belonging to the same virus that contained unassembled overlaps were reassembled using SeqMan method in the Lasergene v7 [33]. Reads were mapped back to viral contigs using Bowtie2 [29] and assembled again using SPAdes to confirm the assembled genome [34]. The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) region that shared < 90% amino acid identity with closely related known viruses was assigned as new virus species, which was verified by subsequent phylogenetic analysis. To explore the potential presence of the M fragment, polymerase chain reaction was conducted using the PrimeScript™ One Step RT-PCR Kit Ver.2 (Takara, Shimogyō-ku, Kyot).

Virus annotation and abundance

Putative open reading frames (ORFs) of viral contigs were searched using Open Reading Frame Finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/). The sequence homologs and functional analysis of proteins of identified viruses were annotated in the conserved domain database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) and the HMMER web server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/hmmer/). The amino acid identity between viral proteins was calculated using BioAider v1.314 [35].

To calculate the relative abundance, clean data were mapped to the SILVA database (http://www.arb-silva.de) using Bowtie2 as previously reported [36] to remove ribosomal RNA-associated reads. The genomes of each virus were mapped to the resulting unmapped reads using the “end-to-end’’ model [36]. The relative abundance of each virus was acquired by calculating the number of mapped reads per million total reads (RPM) in the corresponding library (viral reads/total non-rRNA reads × 1,000,000). To remove false positives resulting from index-hopping, a virus with an abundance of > 10 RPM was considered as a positive virus in the library and used for further analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequences of closely related individuals were obtained from NCBI using a BLASTX search. The RdRp sequence of the novel virus and corresponding relatives were aligned using Mafft v7 with the E-INS-i algorithm [37]. ModelFinder, which is included in IQ-TREE v.1.6, was used to estimate the best-fit substitution model [38]. Further, maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees of each alignment (all ambiguously aligned regions were removed) were constructed using PhyML 3.0 with Shimodaira–Hasegawa (SH)-like branch supports [39] and visualized in Tree of Life version 1.0 [40].

The total RNA of each sample was pooled according to arthropod species and used to perform metatranscriptomic sequencing. Novel +ssRNA viruses

Three iflaviruses, harboring non-segmented RNA genome, were discovered in ticks (tentatively named iflavirus qinghai P1 and iflavirus qinghai P2) and fleas (Iflaviridae sp. qinghaiense) in this study. The RdRp of iflavirus qinghai P1 and iflavirus qinghai P2 shared the highest amino acid identities with iflavirus IfronI (66.0%) and iflavirus IricIV-5 (83.5%), respectively, which were isolated from ticks (Ixodes ricinus and Ixodes frontalis, respectively). However, Iflaviridae sp. qinghaiense differed from its closest relative, sharing < 35.1% amino acid identity (Table S1). The relative abundance of the three iflaviruses was 7968.0 (95%CI: 7943.4–7985.1), 6092.9 (95%CI: 6074.2–6110.7), and 23041.1 (95%CI: 22941–23162), respectively. Phylogenetic analysis based on RdRp sequences revealed that the three iflaviruses identified in this study formed an independent clade distinct from previously known iflaviruses (Fig. 3). The threshold value of isolates belonging to the same species within the genus Iflavirus is > 90% identical to capsid proteins [41]. A luteo-like virus (Qinghai luteo-like virus P1), belonging to Luteoviridae, was detected in a tick sample. The RdRp sequence shared 82.5% amino acid identity with Ixodes scapularis-associated virus 2, and had a relative abundance of 925.6 (95%CI: 922.1–926.8) (Table S1). The phylogenetic tree based on the RdRp indicated that Qinghai luteo-like virus P1 was closely related to Ixodes scapularis associated virus 2, but belonged to a different clade (Fig. 3).

Two distinct viruses (Qinghai narnavirus F1 and Qinghai narnavirus F2) in the genus Narnavirus within the family Birnaviridae, which contain important pathogens for fish farms, were discovered from fleas. The two viruses had a relative abundance of 275.6 (Qinghai narnavirus F1, 95%CI: 273.1–279.8) and 241.3 (Qinghai narnavirus F2, 95%CI: 239.5–244.8) (Table S1), respectively. The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3) revealed that Qinghai narnavirus F1 belongs to the narnaviruses that are closely related to Phytomonas serpens narnavirus 1 (47.5%), whereas Qinghai narnavirus F1 belongs to another cluster and is basal to the related members that share the highest identity with Sanya narnavirus 10 (37.3%).

A nodavirus (Flea nodavirus F1) belonging to Nodaviridae, which could cause viral encephalopathy and retinopathy, was detected in a tick sample. The RdRp sequence shared 38.1% amino acid identity with Wenzhou bat nodavirus 6 (40.4%), with a relative abundance of 2179.3 (95%CI:2158.3–2193.6) (Table S1). The phylogenetic tree indicated that Flea nodavirus F1 was closely related to Wenzhou bat nodavirus 6 and Hubei noda-like virus 16 (Fig. 3).

Two sobemo-like viruses were identified from tick (Qinghai sobemo-like virus P1) and flea (Qinghai sobemo-like virus F1), respectively. All of them had the highest abundance [8524.4 (95%CI: 8496.1–8541.1) and 388427(95%CI: 38714–39009)] in the corresponding pool (Table S1). Based on the RdRp sequence (Fig. 3), phylogenetic analysis revealed that they formed a cluster within sobemo-like viruses, sharing the highest identity with Plasmopara viticola lesion associated sobemo-like 1 (57.6%).

A tombus-like virus (Qinghai tombus-like virus F1), belonging to Tombusviridae, was detected in the flea sample. The virus had a relative abundance of 12241.8 (95%CI: 12217–12270) (Table S1). The phylogenetic tree indicated that Qinghai tombus-like virus F1 belongs to tombus-like viruses, and shared the highest amino acid identity (57.1%) with the Cushing virus, which was identified from the goblin flea (Fig. 3).

A virga-like virus (Qinghai martelli-like virus F1) was identified in fleas, with a relative abundance of 6827.7 (95%CI: 6789.2–6887.7) (Table S1). The viral sequence shared 42.8% amino acid identity with Xiangshan martelli-like virus 2. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that Qinghai martelli-like virus F1 revealed that Qinghai martelli-like virus F1 was distinct from known virga-like viruses and belonged to a new species within the family Virgaviridae (Fig. 3).

Two viruses within the family Peribunyaviridae were discovered from tick (Qinghai tick virus P4) and flea (Qinghai flea virus F5) pools, respectively. They had a relative abundance of 128.3 (95%CI: 127.4–129.1) for Qinghai tick virus P4 and 203.0 (95%CI: 201.7–204.5) for Qinghai flea virus F5, respectively (Table S1). The phylogenetic tree revealed that Qinghai tick virus P4 and Qinghai flea virus F5 together formed a monophyletic group and relatively differed from the closest relative of the Haerbin tick virus 2 (73.8%) that was identified in Ixodes persulcatus from northeast China (Fig. 4).

Two chuvirus-like viruses were detected from the tick (Nigecruvirus qinghai) and flea (Qinghai chuvirus-like virus F1), which had a relative abundance of 324.2 (95%CI: 321.8–325.5) and 555.4 (95%CI: 547.4–561.1), respectively. Nigecruvirus Qinghai has a circular genome of about 14.5 kb in length, coding four putative ORFs. The phylogenetic analysis indicated that Nigecruvirus Qinghai shared 76.1% similarity with Taiga tick nigecruvirus obtained from Ixodes persulcatus. The Qinghai chuvirus-like virus F1 shared 72.5% of its sequence with the Chuviridae sp. 156_1620 identified in bat (Pipistrellus abramus) from China (Fig. 4).

Two viruses belonging to the genus Ganiavirus of family Lispiviridae were obtained from tick (Ganiavirus ixodes) and flea (Ganiavirus silantiewi) pools, which had a relative abundance of 903.5 (95%CI: 895.4–911.1) and 77.8 (95%CI: 77.1–78.4), respectively. The phylogenetic tree showed that they formed an independent cluster within members of Lispiviridae, all of which had 77.9% amino acid identity with closest relative (Mononegavirales sp. L1d), suggesting a novel species (Fig. 4).

A rhabdo-like virus species (Qinghai rhabdo-like virus F1) within the family Rhabdoviridae, which infected diverse hosts including vertebrates (especially mammals and fishes), plants, and insects, was newly identified from flea library, with a relative abundance of 598.5 (95%CI: 585.4–612.3) (Table S1). Based on the RdRp sequence, the phylogenetic tree indicated that Qinghai rhabdo-like virus F1 was related to members of the family Rhabdoviridae, however, it was relatively divergent with only 43.0% amino acid similarity to the closest relative of Sanxia Water Strider Virus 5 (Fig. 4).

Four phenui-like viruses belonging to the family Phenuiviridae, which includes well-known tick-borne viruses from around the world [42], were identified in the study, two from the tick library (Qinghai tick virus 1 and Qinghai tick virus P5) and two from the flea library (Qinghai flea virus F3 and Qinghai flea virus F4). They had a relative abundance of 476.4 (95%CI: 474.3–477.5) for Qinghai tick virus 1, 97.5 (95%CI: 96.7–98.2) for Qinghai tick virus P5, 163.9 (95%CI: 161.4–165.4) for Qinghai flea virus F3, and 2377.5 (95%CI: 2350.4–2398.3) for Qinghai flea virus F4, respectively. The phylogenetic analysis showed that Qinghai tick virus 1 and Qinghai flea virus F3 belonged to the genus Ixovirus, and both were closely related to blacklegged tick phlebovirus 3 (with 64.3% and 65.8% amino acid identity, respectively). In addition, the phylogenetic tree indicated that Qinghai tick virus P5 and Qinghai flea virus F4 formed a group distinct from the genera Ixovirus and Uukuvirus, and shared only 38.5% identity with Hangzhou phenuivirus 2, which may represent a candidate new genus (Fig. 4).

Four phasma-like viruses from the family Phasmaviridae that were associated with insect hosts were newly identified in this study, including two from the tick library (Qinghai tick virus 2 and Qinghai tick virus 3) and two from the flea library (Qinghai flea virus F1 and Qinghai flea virus F2). They had a relative abundance of 22.1 (95%CI: 21.7–22.4) for Qinghai tick virus 2, 48.3 (95%CI: 47.4–49.3) for Qinghai tick virus 3, 48389.6 (95%CI: 48210.5–48598.3) for Qinghai flea virus F1, and 545.5 (95%CI: 538.4–552.2) for Qinghai flea virus F2, respectively. The phylogenetic analysis revealed that these four viruses formed two distinct lineages and were distantly related to known viruses (Fig. 4). The Qinghai tick virus 3 and Qinghai flea virus F2 formed a group with closest related to Tibet bird virus 1 (only 46.8% amino acid identity). The Qinghai tick virus 2 and Qinghai flea virus F1 together formed another monophyletic group sharing 47.0% and 47.1% amino acid similarity with the closest relative of Tibet bird virus 1, respectively (Fig. 4).

Co-circulating viruses

Since the ticks and fleas were collected from marmots, we investigated whether there was any viral dispersal event between the two arthropod species. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that several viruses from ticks and fleas were clustered together with high support values in this study. The amino acid identity between these viruses was estimated based on RdRp sequences, which showed that six paired variants within the same clustered group sharing 96.0% to 99.8% similarity, indicating the same virus species (Fig. 6A). Thus, the tick library and flea library shared six virus species, belonging to Phasmaviridae (n = 2), Phenuiviridae (n = 1), Lispiviridae (n = 1), Solemoviridae (n = 1), and Peribunyaviridae (n = 1) (Fig. 6A).Fig. 6The same virus species circulating between ticks and fleas. Discussion

Ticks and fleas are the most important arthropods that can act as vectors of infectious pathogens, including numerous highly pathogenic viruses in humans, wildlife, and domestic animals [43]. However, the RNA virus diversity of animals, particularly arthropods, in the Qinghai-Tibet plateau is rarely studied owing to the biosafety concerns and difficulty in sampling. In this study, we conducted the first metagenomic analysis of the RNA viromes of ticks and fleas infecting marmots, revealing several novel arthropod-associated RNA viruses.

There was extensive diversity of viruses identified in the ticks and fleas infecting marmots. Overall, 30 novel arthropod-associated viruses belonging to 16 viral families were RNA viruses, of which 11 viruses were discovered in ticks and 19 viruses in fleas. The number of viruses belonging to order Bunyavirales (n = 10) was observed to be the highest, including Phasmaviridae (n = 4), Phenuiviridae (n = 4), and Peribunyaviridae (n = 2), which is consistent with a previous report [44], but significantly different from previous studies on virome diversity of ticks and fleas [2, 45]. Furthermore, the abundance and diversity of viruses observed in ticks differed from those in fleas. The viruses belonging to the Solemoviridae and Iflaviridae were observed in the greatest abundance in ticks, however, those belonging to the Solemoviridae, Partitiviridae, and Phasmaviridae were observed in the greatest abundance in fleas. Additionally, viruses identified in fleas belonged to 15 viral families whereas those in ticks belonged to only 8 vial families, which may be owing to the differences in tick and flea host ranges or/and differences in their ability to replicate in marmots.

These flea- and tick-associated viruses discovered in this study are most closely related to other flea- and tick-borne viruses, or viruses from the same genera. For example, Ollusvirus qinghaiense was discovered in fleas in this study, which forms a monophyletic group and was closely related to Oropsylla silantiewi mononega-like virus 2 detected from fleas (Oropsylla silantiewi) and Culverton virus discovered in fleas (Macropsylla hercules). However, there are exceptions. Although Qinghai rhabdo-like virus F1 was detected in fleas (order Siphonaptera) in this study, its related members were detected in the non-flea host, such as Hemiptera spp. (Water Strider Virus 5 and Shuangao Bedbug Virus 2), and Diptera spp. (Stang virus, Jingshan Fly Virus 2, and Drosophila busckii rhabdovirus). Further analysis will reveal whether this phenomenon indicates a host switch or is simply an occasional sampling bias [46].

The absence of a medium (M) RNA segment encoding the viral glycoprotein is a distinguishing feature of Qinghai tick virus 1 and Qinghai flea virus F3 (both belonging to the genus Ixovirus) identified in this study. These viruses share the greatest sequence identity with blacklegged tick phlebovirus 3 [47]. Despite the recovery of the complete large (L) and small (S) RNA segments (complete ORF), no sequences with similarity to the Bunyavirales M segment that were closely related to blacklegged tick phlebovirus 3 were identified in our library. Additionally, the consensus polymerase chain reaction with nested primers targeting to the Ixovirus M segment was performed, causing a negative outcome. The lack of the M segment in Ixovirus has been previously reported [44, 46, 48] and has become a common feature for the viruses within the genus Ixovirus of the family Phenuiviridae. These viruses lacking an M segment may represent a novel viral lineage that lacks the typical bunyaviral M segment in their genome, and enters the host cell through alternative routes [44]. Another possibility could be that the M segment of these viruses has significantly insufficient identity with that of known viruses could account for another explanation [49]. However, the mechanism is still unclear, if not ambiguous, and requires more research.

The presence of a diverse range of novel viruses shared by both ticks and fleas suggests that marmots may serve as central reservoir hosts. Interestingly, the 30 RNA viruses detected in this study differed from known viruses in having < 84% RdRp amino acid identity with only 5 viruses sharing > 70% identities. This indicates that the prominent pathogenic viruses, such as the Nairobi sheep disease virus [50], tick-borne encephalitis virus [9], and severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus [51], were not identified in our study. However, six same virus species were detected from both from fleas and ticks and differed from previously reported viruses, suggesting that they might have circulated within fleas and ticks for extended periods of time. The possible reason is that fleas and ticks obtained these viruses by co-infecting the same intermediate host (possibly marmots), indicating possible virus interspecies transmission between ticks and fleas (Fig. 5B). Additionally, these potential transmission events suggest that these viruses may be able to infect marmots, which are known to play key roles in viral transmission networks. However, high sequence similarity alone does not confirm direct transmission, and both vectors could have independently acquired the virus from the same host. Owing to lack of marmot samples, whether marmots are infected by these viruses remains unknown.

Conclusively, accumulating evidence indicates that arthropods, particularly ticks and fleas, harbors an extensive diversity of viruses belonging to different families. The results obtained here expand our understanding of the abundance, complexity, and diversity of the virosphere of ticks and fleas in the Qinghai-Tibet plateau and provide insight into the potential transmission of the virus between arthropods and marmots.
