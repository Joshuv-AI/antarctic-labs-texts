Real-time detection of hotspots in dry-type flexible cable terminals
Authors: Shunyu Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo accurately and quickly diagnose overheating defects in dry-type flexible cable terminals to reduce failure rates, hotspots must be detected in real time using aerial infrared images of the cable terminals. To address the challenges of the small target size, complex background, and real-time performance in the task, I defined four types of hotspots in dry-type flexible terminals and propose an improved model based on You Only Look Once model. This model embeds Diverse Branch Blocks to enhance the representation capabilities, introduces the Multi-Scale Convolutional Attention to better focus on key areas and detect small targets, and reduces the model’s complexity by introducing the FasterNet Block. The proposed model performs significantly better than the baseline model and other mainstream You Only Look Once series models, satisfying the real-time detection demand for hotspots in dry-type flexible cable terminals using aerial infrared images. Introduction

As the device to connect cables to other equipment, cable terminals have a significantly higher failure rate than the cable bodies and intermediate joints, which severely limits the operational reliability of cable lines1–5. For power transmission cables, cable terminals mainly include oil-filled terminals, dry-type flexible terminals, and GIS terminals. The former two can both be used outdoors to connect cables and overhead lines. Operational experience in a provincial capital of central China has shown that dry-type flexible terminals have significantly higher failure rates than oil-filled terminals. Dry-type flexible terminals have a simple structure, making it easy to install and avoiding the risk of oil leakage, so they had been widely used in 110 kV cable lines until 2018. Since 2018, the State Grid Corporation of China has required that 110 kV-and-above power cables should not use outdoor dry-type flexible terminals for the following reasons. In these terminals, relative displacement tends to occur between the terminal and the cable body during hoisting. Thus, the overlap position between the stress cone of the cable terminal and the insulation shield break of the cable body may not satisfy the design requirements and prevents the stress cone from controlling the electric field strength of the break. The displacement may also squeeze the insulating tape around the top of the cable’s metal sheath into the semi-conductive area of the stress cone and cause floating potential in the semi-conductive part of the stress cone. On the other, if these terminals are not properly fixed, the stress cone area may bend during operation, which causes an uneven interface stress between the stress cone and the insulation shield break. Both problems can ultimately cause cable terminal breakdown.

Currently, many regions in China retain a large number of 110 kV dry-type flexible cable terminals in operation because the terminal towers that support dry-type flexible cable terminals are significantly different from those for oil-filled terminals. Replacing them with oil-filled terminals will require replacing or modifying the terminal towers, which is costly and time-consuming. Therefore, dry-type flexible cable terminals cannot be completely replaced in a short time, and a risk assessment is necessary to formulate a replacement strategy. Terminals at risk of failure should first be replaced to avoid line outages. Abnormal heating is a common symptom of cable terminal failure. To reduce the failure rate, the timeliness and accuracy of diagnosing overheating defects in dry-type flexible cable terminals should be urgently improved.

In recent years, drones have been widely used in the power industry to collect infrared images of dry-type flexible cable terminals to manually diagnose overheating defects. This diagnosis method requires experienced personnel and often requires preprocessing by using the processing software that the drone manufacturer provides, so that the thermal characteristics are easier for manual observation. Thus, this method cannot diagnose on-site in real time and is highly likely to miss opportunities to detect early signs of faults. The rapid development of object detection technology provides a new approach to diagnose power equipment defects.

In recent years, object detection technology has been widely studied and applied in various industries. Reference6 designed a custom Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to recognize cucumber leaf diseases. Reference7 proposed a lightweight model for fall detection based on YOLOv8. Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) and Spatial Attention (SA) blocks were introduced in the Backbone separately for early-stage channel recalibration and later-stage regional focus, and Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) blocks were integrated in the Neck for refined feature fusion. Reference8 introduced multi-attention mechanisms and an optimized DP-Head structure into YOLOv10 to detect bridge surface defect under resource-constrained conditions. Reference9 designed a fire recognition method based on EfficientNetV2. Strip Pooling Coordinate Attention (SPCA) was introduced to enhance the long-distance spatial feature extraction of slender and inclined targets. A direction learning mechanism was used to strengthen the characteristics of the target form and extension direction. Besides, multi-scale feature fusion was optimized to enhance the target discrimination ability in complex backgrounds while maintaining lightweight, and improve the detection effect on irregular small targets.

In the power industry, YOLO-series algorithms have been widely used to diagnose defects in power equipment for their comprehensive advantages in efficiency and accuracy.

For insulators, the YOLO algorithm has been greatly improved to detect defects rapidly and precisely. Reference10 introduced a Contextual Transformer module to the Backbone for better contextual understanding and a Partial Convolution layer to lessen redundant computation in YOLOv8. Reference11 optimized YOLOv8 with the Coordinate Attention Mechanism, and reduced the two-dimensional pooling algorithm to one-dimensional computation to decrease parameters and complexity. Reference12 introduced a Coordinate Attention Mechanism and a Feature Channel Shuffle Operation into the Backbone network of the YOLOv8 model to improve the detection accuracy while reducing the number of parameters. Reference13 optimized the hyperparameters of the YOLOv5 model using a genetic algorithm to improve the detection results and visualized the regional attention in the detection results using the Eigen Class Activation Map (Eigen-CAM) technology, which improved the detection rate of surface contamination on power distribution insulators. Reference14 improved the YOLOv8 network by introducing Group Shuffle Convolution (GSConv) modules in its Backbone and Neck networks to reduce computation, used a Content-Aware Reassembly of Features (CARAFE) structure in the Neck network to enhance the feature fusion capabilities, and used four detection heads to enable the model to better identify small targets. Based on the YOLOv11 model, Reference15 used a self-supervised pre-training method to improve the small-sample generalization ability, added a small-target detection layer and introduced a Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD)16 loss function to help the model better detect small defects in insulators, and used the Knowledge Distillation Aided Group Channel Pruning (KD-GCP) method to compress the model, which significantly improved the accuracy and efficiency in power line insulator inspection.

The industry has also explored many improved methods based on the YOLO algorithm to specifically detect substation equipment. Reference17 added a Global-Local Fusion Module (GLFM) to the Backbone and Neck networks of YOLOv5 to enhance its feature extraction and fusion capabilities and used a Multi-Granularity Downsampler (MGD) in the Backbone network to better preserve small target features. A Re-parameterized Decoupling Head (RDHead) structure was used for the detection head, and an auxiliary detection head was used to improve the detection accuracy of the model. Reference18 introduced a lightweight MobileNetV3 structure and a Global Attention Mechanism (GAM) into the YOLO network, removed the large detection head, replaced the network’s loss function with the Shape IoU (SIOU) loss function, and improved the non-maximum suppression algorithm of the network based on Gaussian weighting. Thus it satisfied the lightweight and accuracy requirements of object detection tasks in substation drone inspection images. For the detection task of indoor substation equipment, Reference19 improved the YOLOv8 network by introducing the ConvNeXt network20 to replace the feature extraction network to speed up detection, used a Bidirectional Feature Pyramid Network (BiFPN)21 as the feature fusion network to enhance the feature fusion capabilities and reduce computational costs, added a tiny-object detection head to improve the small-object detection performance, and introduced the Wise-IoU loss function22 to reduce the impact of low-quality samples. Based on the YOLOv10 model, Reference23 introduced a dilated-wise re-parameterized residual bottleneck structure into the Cross-Stage Partial (CSP) network of the feature extraction network, used a Feature-Focusing and -Diffusing Block (FFDB) and a Dimension-Aware Selective Integration (DASI) module to improve the feature fusion network, and designed a Diverse Branch Detection Head (DBD Head), which improved the feature representation ability and multi-scale robustness of the model.

Hotspots

As shown in Fig. 2, a dry-type flexible cable terminal includes four hotspots: the connection plate, stress cone, grounding module, and grounding box. Based on their materials, these hotspots can be divided into two categories: metallic hotspots and non-metallic hotspots.

Four hotspots in a dry-type flexible cable terminal, of which Hotspot A is the connection plate, Hotspot B is the stress cone, Hotspot C is the grounding module, and Hotspot D is the grounding box.

Metallic hotspots

Connection plates, grounding modules, and grounding boxes are all metallic hotspots. Their abnormal heating mechanisms are described below.

Hotspot A in Fig. 2 represents the connection plate, which connects a cable terminal, a jumper, an arrester, and an insulator. Due to multiple bolted connections, if there are issues such as loose connections, corrosion, or ineffective copper-aluminum transitions, the connection plate will significantly overheat during operation, and these defects will be more pronounced during peak load periods. The overheat of Hotspot A may finally lead to fusing of metal connection and the heat it generates may cause damage to the insulation parts below the connection plate.

Hotspot C in Fig. 2 represents the grounding module, which connects the grounding cable from the cable’s metal sheath to a grounding box. Poor connections or incorrect grounding modes can also cause abnormal heating.

Hotspot D in Fig. 2 represents the grounding box. The grounding cable from the grounding module is grounded through the grounding box. The grounding box contains connection points between the three-phase grounding cables and the copper busbar as well as the connection point between the main grounding cable and the box body. All connection points can overheat due to poor connections. Incorrect grounding modes can also make the grounding box overheat.

Overheat in Hotspots C and D may also lead to serious results. Poor connections of the grounding module or inside the grounding box, and incorrect grounding modes may make metal sheath of the cable lose its only direct grounding point, causing a floating potential and discharge to the grounding body. Eventually, this may rapidly develop into a insulation damage or even breakdown.

Non-metallic hotspots

In Fig. 2, Hotspot B is the stress cone, which is the most critical part of a dry-type flexible cable terminal. It is integrally pre-molded with the terminal sleeve and made of silicone rubber. The stress cone is a non-metallic hotspot. It is installed at the cable insulation shield break to dissipate the excessively concentrated electric field of the break. The following defects frequently occur in a Hotspot B, causing poor control of the electric field over the insulation shield break of the cable body, and resulting in abnormal heating: The overlap position between the stress cone and the insulation shield break is beyond the scope permitted by the design, or insulation material is squeezed between the semiconducting part of the stress cone and the insulation shield break of the cable body, or there is insufficient local pressure between the stress cone and the insulation shield break. However, since the stress cone and terminal sleeve are pre-molded as a single unit, abnormal heat inside the stress cone is transmitted through the sleeve to the terminal surface, leading to relatively small temperature increase on the surface.

For hotspots such as connection plates, grounding modules, and stress cones, a defect is determined when the temperature difference among phases in the same hotspot exceeds a threshold. For grounding boxes, a defect is determined when the temperature difference between the box and the ambient temperature exceeds a certain threshold.

Methods

The YOLO series of algorithms all primarily consist of three parts: the Backbone, Neck, and Head, which perform feature extraction, feature fusion, and detection output, respectively. YOLOv11, a relatively new and stable version of YOLO algorithm, was launched by the Ultralytics team in September 2024. Compared to previous versions, it better balances speed and accuracy and has been widely applied in engineering. My proposed DTH-YOLO model makes the following improvements based on YOLOv11: First, a DBB structure is embedded in the feature extraction network to enhance the feature extraction capability of the Backbone network, so that the model can better recognize small objects. Second, a Multi-Scale Convolutional Attention (MSCA) mechanism is introduced into the feature fusion network to assign higher weights to key regions and feature channels, which enables the model to better distinguish the foreground and background, improving the detection accuracy. Third, a FasterNet Block is used to replace a CBS module in the feature fusion network to reduce the number of model parameters and increase the detection speed. Diverse branch block

Convolutional neural networks contain many convolutional layers, but the representational capacity of a single convolution is limited. To improve performance of one convolution, the Diverse Branch Block (DBB) replaces it with multiple branches of different scales and complexities during training. During inference, these branches are merged into one convolution without increasing the inference time cost.

To demonstrate that diversified branches can be equivalently converted into one convolution, reference26 proved that a K×K convolution was equivalent to six transformation forms: convolution-batch normalization (K×K-BN), summation of the output layers of two or more convolution with identical configurations (K×K + K×K), convolution sequence (1 × 1-K×K), concatenation along the channel dimension of output layers of two or more convolutions with identical configurations (Concat (K×K, K×K)), average pooling with a pooling kernel size of K×K and an equal stride to the K×K convolution (AVGK×K), and rectangular convolution with dimensions kh×kw (kh≤K, kw≤K).

Furthermore, reference26 concluded the following through experiments:

Combining a branch with weak representation capability and a branch with strong representation capability (e.g., a 1 × 1 convolution and a 3 × 3 convolution) is more effective than combining two branches with strong representation capabilities (e.g., two 3 × 3 convolutions).

Combining a branch with weak representation capability and a branch with strong representation capability (e.g., a 1 × 1 convolution and a 3 × 3 convolution) is more effective than combining two branches with strong representation capabilities (e.g., two 3 × 3 convolutions).

Adding Batch Normalization (BN) after each convolutional or average pooling layer can improve the training performance.

Adding Batch Normalization (BN) after each convolutional or average pooling layer can improve the training performance.

Thus, this work replaces two CBS modules in the Backbone network with the DBB structure in Fig. 4. During training, the three branches complement one another: the K×K-BN branch mainly extracts basic spatial information, preserving the feature extraction capability of the original convolutional layer, and ensuring that the effect of the DBB is no less than the original convolution; the 1 × 1-BN-K×K-BN sequential convolution branch extracts complex spatial features, where the 1 × 1 convolution mainly fuses the channel information, and the K×K convolution obtains spatial features with a larger receptive field based on the channel reorganization of the 1 × 1 convolution. Different from the detailed features that the first two branches extracted, the 1 × 1-BN-AVG-BN average pooling branch provides the mean features in the K×K sliding window, smoothing noise and improving the anti-interference ability of the model. During inference, the three-branch structure can be converted into a K×K convolution based on six equivalent transformations, which significantly improves the training effect without increasing the number of parameters in the deployed model.

Multi-scale convolutional attention

Traditional convolutional neural networks cannot effectively capture multi-scale features and treat all regions and channels equally, failing to adaptively increase their attention to key regions and channels. Although there are a lot of works focusing on the lightweight feature fusion mechanism, such as Concentric Mask Based Arbitrary-Shaped Text Detection30, Zoom Text Detector31, and Reinforcement Shrink-Mask for Text Detection32, which were mainly developed for text detection tasks, this work introduces MSCA, a channel-spatial mixed attention mechanism, to adaptively capture object features of drone-captured images with varying scales and complex backgrounds with better efficiency and representation ability under edge computing constraints.

Figure 5 shows the structure of MSCA, which has three main parts: a small-scale depth-wise convolution (DW Conv), multi-scale sequential strip convolutions, and a point-wise convolution (PW Conv). These three parts have clear divisions of labor and complement one another. This work uses the following MSCA structure. First, input feature map F undergoes a 3 × 3 small-scale depth-wise convolution, which extracts the local spatial information. Then, the output enters the module of multi-scale sequential strip convolutions. To extract spatial information with different receptive fields, this module uses a four-parallel-branch structure: branch 1 uses two cascaded 1 × 7 and 7 × 1 strip convolutions; branch 2 uses two cascaded 1 × 11 and 11 × 1 strip convolutions; branch 3 uses two cascaded 1 × 21 and 21 × 1 strip convolutions; an identity branch (branch 0) preserves the local spatial information from the small-scale depth-wise convolution. The outputs of the four branches are added element-wise to fuse local and global spatial information without involving the interaction among the channels. The subsequent point-wise convolution specifically fuses the channel information to generate a single-channel attention Attn, to avoid affecting the detection effectiveness causing by separated spatial and channel information. Regions in Attn with high weights correspond to the key areas of hotspots. Meanwhile, Attn is generated through point-wise convolution, which is equivalent to assigning implicit weights to channels of F. Finally, the input feature map is multiplied element-wise with Attn to obtain the output feature map F’, enhancing key areas and feature channels, while suppressing noise for the task. While providing higher detection accuracy, the modules of MSCA offer lightweight advantages. Compared to regular convolutions, small-scale depth-wise convolutions do not involve channel operations, whose minification in computation is equal to the number of channels. Sequential strip convolutions (e.g., 1 × 7–7 × 1 sequential convolutions) can achieve the effect of square convolutions (e.g., 7 × 7 convolutions), but the minification in computation compared to the latter is half the number of channels, and its use of depth-wise convolution improves the computational efficiency. Point-wise convolution only fuses channel information for a single pixel and does not require spatial traversal, so the computational cost is also relatively low.

This work introduces MSCA into the feature fusion network of the YOLOv11 model to automatically filter the features extracted from the Backbone network, reduce redundant features input to the Neck network, enhance the correlation among different spatial and channel dimensions, and help the model better detect objects of different scales.

Fasternet block

Currently, most lightweight improvements to convolutional neural networks focus on reducing the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs). However, the latency is not solely determined by FLOPs, but actually the ratio of FLOPs to floating-point operations per second (FLOPS). Many current lightweight algorithms that reduce FLOPs often have frequent memory access and consequently cannot effectively shorten the latency. Research shows that different channels may contain highly similar features, so convolution operations on all channels result in redundant computation29,33,34. FasterNet reduces FLOPs while increasing FLOPS by introducing partial convolution (PConv), effectively shortening the latency. This work uses the FasterNet Block to improve the detection speed.

As shown in Fig. 6, the FasterNet Block is a serial structure composed of one PConv and two point-wise convolutions (PWConv), and forms an inverted residual structure through a residual connection. The FasterNet Block performs batch normalization (BN) after the first point-wise convolution and subsequently uses a rectified linear unit (ReLU) for non-linear activation.

In the feature fusion network of YOLOv11, among all the modules that satisfy the input and output size requirements of the FasterNet Block, the layer with the highest number of parameters is selected and replaced with a FasterNet Block, which improves the model efficiency.

Experiment

Experimental setup

I independently constructed a dataset of aerial infrared images of dry-type flexible cable terminals. The dataset contains 3887 images from 82 dry-type flexible cable terminal towers in a region of central China. This study focuses on overheat defect diagnosis for dry-type cable terminals via UAV aerial inspection. To ensure flight safety, UAV inspections are not permitted in extreme weather such as strong wind, rain, or snow. In addition, the proposed algorithm will be deployed on the UAV remote-control system, and real-time detection is performed when the UAV is hovering stably under manual control. Therefore, all data in the dataset were collected under conventional weather conditions with the UAV in a stable hovering state. The ratio of the training, validation, and test sets was 4:1:1. The training set was used to train the model, the validation set was used to adjust the hyperparameters, and the test set was used to evaluate model performance. The LabelImage software was used to annotate four types of hotspots in the images. These hotspots are the connection plates, stress cones, grounding modules, and grounding boxes.

All models were trained on a 64-bit Ubuntu 20.04 operating system with two Tesla V100S-PCIE-32GB graphics cards, an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8260 CPU @ 2.40 GHz processor, and 125GB of RAM. Training was performed using the PyTorch framework. To reduce the model size, the YOLOv11n network model was used to obtain pre-trained model parameters for a single Backbone network. GPU acceleration was used during training.

An SGD optimizer with momentum was used in the training with a maximum of 1000 training epochs, including three warm-up epochs. The weight decay coefficient was set to 5e − 4, the SGD momentum was set to 0.937, and the learning rate was decayed using a cosine annealing strategy. The patience parameter was set to 100. Mosaic and Mixup data augmentation techniques were applied during training. The loss function of DTH-YOLO was consistent with YOLOv11. The batch size was set to 24.

To test the number of frames per second (FPS) of the model, a less powerful NVIDIA RTX 2050 GPU was used to simulate the real inference scenario with a test batch size of 1 and a floating-point precision of FP16. The RTX 2050 GPU employs the NVIDIA Ampere architecture with 3rd-generation Tensor Cores, consuming 30–45 W and featuring a compact form factor, matching the energy efficiency, size, and real-time demands of drone edge computing. Besides, the RTX 2050 shares the same architecture, fully compatible CUDA and TensorRT software stacks with the NVIDIA Jetson series of edge devices. Thus, it is reasonable and feasible to use it for simulating drone edge computing environments.

Evaluation metrics

For the experimental results, two types of metrics are used for evaluation: accuracy metrics and efficiency metrics.

This work uses three metrics to measure the accuracy of model: Precision (P), Recall (R), and mean Average Precision at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 (mAP@0.5)35. P is calculated according to (6), where TP is the number of positive samples correctly identified by the model, and FP is the number of negative samples incorrectly identified as positive samples by the model. R is calculated according to (7), where FN is the number of positive samples incorrectly identified as negative samples by the model. P and R often constrain each other. To comprehensively measure P and R, a P-R curve is constructed with R as the independent variable and P as the dependent variable. Average Precision (AP) is the area under the P-R curve and comprehensively characterizes the precision of the model at different recall rates. mAP@0.5 is the arithmetic mean of the AP values for all categories when the IoU threshold between predicted bounding box and ground truth bounding box is set to 0.5. In the context of this work, a lower P indicates that the model will more likely identify the background as a hotspot, and a lower R indicates that the model will more likely miss detecting hotspots. Considering actual field situations, even if the model incorrectly identifies the background as a hotspot, leading to false defect alarm, the consequences are not serious because the drone operator will manually verify the alarm information. However, if the model fails to identify a hotspot, a defect may be missed, which is likely to further develop into a line trip. Therefore, R is more important for this work compared to P.

Regarding the model efficiency, I use three metrics: floating-point operations (FLOPs), model size, and frames per second (FPS)36–38. FLOPs is the number of floating-point operations that the model needs to perform, which directly reflects the computational complexity. The model size is the amount of storage space that the model occupies, which also affects detection efficiency. FPS is the number of image frames that the model can process per second, for which a higher value indicates smoother real-time detection.

Although Model B has the best precision, the improvement in recall is not significant. Model C has the best mAP@0.5 but performs the worst in all the three efficiency metrics.

Models E, F, and G simultaneously incorporate two improvements. Model E introduces the DBB and FasterNet Block and achieves a precision of 91.7% (+ 1.7%), a recall of 89.1% (+ 1.4%), an mAP@0.5 of 92.2% (+ 1.1%), FLOPs of 6.2 G (− 0.1 G), a model size of 5.2 MB (− 0.3 MB), and an FPS of 109 frames/second (+ 7 frames/second). Model F introduces the DBB and MSCA, and achieves a precision of 93.4% (+ 3.4%), a recall of 88.4% (+ 0.7%), an mAP@0.5 of 93.3% (+ 2.2%), FLOPs of 6.4 G (+ 0.1 G), a model size of 5.7 MB (+ 0.2 MB), and an FPS of 96 frames/second (− 6 frames/second). Model G introduces the MSCA and FasterNet Block, and achieves a precision of 93.0% (+ 3.0%), a recall of 87.7% (identical to that of the baseline model), an mAP@0.5 of 92.9% (+ 1.8%), FLOPs of 6.3 G (identical to that in the baseline model), a model size of 5.4 MB (− 0.1 MB), and an FPS of 104 frames/second (+ 2 frames/second). Although models D and E perform the best on the three efficiency metrics, the improvements on the accuracy metrics are limited, particularly in recall.

Model H is the DTH-YOLO model that simultaneously incorporates three improvements. It achieves a precision of 92.7% (+ 2.7%), a recall of 90.5% (+ 2.8%), an mAP@0.5 of 92.9% (+ 1.8%), FLOPs of 6.3 G (identical to that of the baseline model), a model size of 5.4 MB (− 0.1 MB), and an FPS of 104 frames/second (+ 2 frames/second).

It is worth noting that Model H shows a 0.1% decrease in precision and a 1.3% decrease in mAP@0.5, while achieving a 0.2% improvement in recall compared with Model C. In addition, Model H also demonstrates advantages in efficiency metrics, including 0.1 G fewer FLOPs, a 0.4 MB smaller model size, and an 8 FPS higher inference speed. Whether the loss in precision (0.1%) and mAP@0.5 (1.3%) is worth the corresponding gains in recall, FLOPs, model size and FPS depends entirely on the application context and design priorities. On one hand, maximizing recall is essential to avoid missing potential hazards in the scenario of this work, and Model H has the best recall. On the other, the proposed model will be deployed on UAV edge devices, serving as part of an intelligent inspection and diagnosis system for overheating defects in cable dry-type flexible terminals. As a result, every FPS improvement is critical, and an 8 FPS speedup is of great significance. Meanwhile, the smaller model size reduces memory usage, which is particularly beneficial for edge devices with limited RAM. For UAV-based inspection tasks, even a moderate speedup can significantly improve overall throughput over time, enabling the system to process more frames during each flight mission. However, Model C performs the worst in all the three efficiency metrics, so it is not the best choice for this work.

Model H has the best critical recall as well as significant improvements on precision and map@0.5 compared with the baseline model. Its performance on the three efficiency metrics is only second to models D and E. Considering both accuracy and efficiency, Model H is the optimal model.

The main failure cases of Model H are mainly caused by low thermal contrast between the hotspots and the background. To alleviate this issue, it is suggested that UAV inspections should be carried out on cloudy days or after sunset on sunny days to obtain higher thermal contrast and improve detection reliability.

Conclusion

To satisfy the requirements for real-time detection of hotspots in dry-type flexible cable terminals from infrared aerial images, I define four types of hotspots in dry-type flexible cable terminals and develop the DTH-YOLO algorithm, which uses YOLOv11n as the baseline model. My algorithm incorporates several improvements. Firstly, the DBB structure optimizes the CBS modules in the feature extraction network, enhancing the feature extraction capabilities and improving the detection accuracy. Secondly, the MSCA attention mechanism enables the model to better distinguish the background and foreground and adjust to targets of different sizes. Finally, the FasterNet Block achieves a more lightweight model. Study shows that the DTH-YOLO model achieves a precision of 92.7%, a recall of 90.5%, an mAP@0.5 of 92.9%, FLOPs of 6.3 G, a model size of 5.4 MB, and an FPS of 104 frames/second. Compared to the baseline model, DTH-YOLO gets a 2.7% increase in precision, a 2.8% increase in recall, a 1.8% increase in mAP@0.5, the same FLOPs, a 0.1 MB decrease in model size, and a 2 frames/second increase in FPS. It performs better than RT-DETR and TY-RIST as well as mainstream YOLO series models, including YOLOv12n which was released after the baseline model, and achieves excellent detection results in actual detection tasks. Its sharp promotion in accuracy without compromised efficiency in the task with small targets and comprehensive background makes it a better choice for real-time detection of hotspots in dry flexible cable terminals from infrared aerial images than existing methods. The next step is to deploy this model on edge devices to diagnose overheating defects in dry flexible cable terminals in real time.

However, the DTH-YOLO algorithm proposed in this work has certain limitation. Although the internal structure and external features of dry flexible cable terminals in the industry are similar, terminals from different areas and manufacturers may have slight differences. To ascertain the model’s adaptability, future research should conduct tests across a broader range of geographical areas and various manufacturers.
