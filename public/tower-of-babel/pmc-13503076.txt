Proteomic Insights into the Maladaptive Biology of Steroid-Resistant Nephrotic Syndrome

Published: 2026

Idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (iNS) is a heterogenous manifestation of glomerular and podocyte injury. The level of glomerular dysfunction and therapeutic response varies widely and encompasses steroid-dependent and multidrug-resistant phenotypes. Resistance leads to a more complicated clinical scenario for treatment approaches as multidrug resistance occurs. The mechanisms of podocyte and glomerular dysfunction in these patient populations are not well defined and require innovative investigative approaches to more clearly define the sequence of events leading to dysfunction. Understanding and defining changes in the serum proteome in these difficult-to-assess patient populations could lead to a better clinical understanding and help elucidate mechanisms of glomerular and podocyte injury.

In the article by Spinelli and colleagues1, the authors employ label-free proteomics approaches combined with univariate and multivariate statistical analyses to identify quantitative changes in the serum proteome of steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome, steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome, multidrug-dependent nephrotic syndrome, and multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome in pediatric and young adult patient populations.1 Multidrug resistance is defined as the failure to achieve remission with 2 nonsteroid-based immunosuppressive medications. Multivariate and random forest classification techniques identified a focused 35-protein signature, distinguishing patients with multidrug-resistant, steroid-resistant, and steroid-dependent phenotypes. In this study, the 35-protein signature offers the potential to segregate patients with multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome from healthy patients but is unable to distinguish patients with multidrug-dependent nephrotic syndrome from those with steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome. This limitation suggests that more studies in large, well-defined patient cohorts are required to extend these findings toward clinical implementation.

Using this molecular signature, the authors focused on serum changes in TARBP2, CD44, and dystroglycan-1 (DAG1), which were high in multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome compared with steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome and multidrug-dependent nephrotic syndrome in active disease. DAG1 plays a role in the slit diaphragm by interacting with cell adhesion molecules under normal circumstances to facilitate podocyte contacts with the glomerular basement membrane. In this study the authors show that increased serum levels of DAG1 are derived from the cleavage of the extracellular domain portion that was identified by mass spectrometry. Interestingly, DAG1 levels have been shown to be elevated or maintained in focal segmental glomerulosclerosis but are low in minimal change disease.2 Alterations in DAG1 glycosylation can contribute to podocyte flattening and effacement from the glomerular basement membrane.3 However, genetic ablation of DAG in podocytes has been shown to be dispensable for normal podocyte development and function, as demonstrated in podocyte-specific knockout mouse models.4 Considering this context, DAG1 may be important in disease progression and the development of drug resistance as a biomarker of the pathology. The current finding that DAG1 levels appear to be most affected in patients who are multidrug-resistant, suggests a role in the pathological mechanisms of iNS rather than proteinuria, as other types of proteinuric syndromes do not necessarily lead to elevated DAG1. Additional findings in this study show that TARBP2 (a component of the RNA-induced silencing complex) levels are decreased in active disease compared with remission, in contrast to CD44, which is elevated in active disease.

The authors used gene ontology analysis of the serum proteomic data to determine functional pathway enrichment for further investigation in cultured podocytes and patient peripheral blood mononuclear cells. The predominant categories enriched in the serum proteome included immune-related proteins, proteases, complement, extracellular matrix, and cytoskeletal proteins. This distribution of pathways indicates alterations in inflammatory and structural components that are important features of iNS and could be further investigated in the context of multidrug resistance to determine mechanisms of injury in the podocyte as a function of therapeutic resistance. The authors first demonstrate the effects of multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome serumon cytoskeletal integrity and survival signaling in podocytes by measuring actin disruption and AKT inactivation. AKT pro-survival signaling was markedly reduced in multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome serum treated podocytes, and actin organization was disrupted, indicating impaired apoptotic signaling and cytoskeletal dynamics. Additionally, markers of autophagy were diminished in multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome exposed podocytes, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress markers (GRP78) were elevated. Direct effects of patient serum on podocytes provide an effective model of podocyte injury in vitro and can identify pathogenic pathways activated by serum factors that could contribute to drug resistance in vivo.

To determine survival signaling and proteostasis in vivo, the authors measured AKT activation and GRP78 levels in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome, multidrug-dependent nephrotic syndrome, and multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome during active disease and remission. Survival signaling was diminished (decreased AKT phosphorylation in multidrug–resistant nephrotic syndrome that was maintained during remission), whereas GRP78 levels were elevated in multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome patient cells and decreased during remission. The increased GRP78 levels indicate that the unfolded protein stress response is heightened in patients with multidrug-resistant nephrotic syndrome compared with other phenotypes and that sustained injury signals lead to decreased survival signals, as suggested by lower AKT phosphorylation. Moderate injury can activate ER stress without disrupting pro-survival signals to help maintain cell viability. However, sustained injury can lead to persistent ER stress and loss of protective survival signaling and activation of apoptosis.5 Elevation of ER-induced stress and altered AKT signaling are themes in nephrotic syndrome pathology that could indicate a maladaptive cellular state in multidrug resistance. The integration of proteomics analyses is a powerful approach for defining specific pathway features to better understand the mechanisms of multidrug resistance in iNS. Identification of molecular signatures for stratification of patients having iNS based on steroid resistance has been a major research focus over the last two decades.6

Extrapolating these functional analyses in cultured podocytes and patient-derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells provides a rationale for further investigating the molecular mechanisms of multidrug resistance in iNS. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of stratification and classification of patients having iNS to improve precision therapeutics and treatment approaches.
