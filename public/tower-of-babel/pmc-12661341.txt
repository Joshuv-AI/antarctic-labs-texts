A rheological study of human cranial cavity in shirodhara procedure using finite element method
Authors: K Sivaselvi, E Abhishek, M Abhijith, V Gautham, PV Anandaraman Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShirodhara is a classical Ayurvedic therapy involving the continuous pouring of medicated liquids on the forehead to treat stress, insomnia, migraines, and neurological disorders. However, its underlying biomechanical mechanisms remain poorly understood.ObjectiveTo simulate and analyze the biomechanical response of cranial tissues during Shirodhara using water, cow milk, and sesame oil, investigate the rheological behavior of the human cranial vault and assess their relative therapeutic efficacy.MethodsA forehead-skull model is developed in ANSYS Workbench to simulate fluid impact under controlled flow conditions. Key parameters such as velocity distribution, pressure gradient, total deformation, shear stress, and von Mises stress are evaluated for each fluid.ResultsSesame oil exhibits the highest velocity (1.13 m/s), pressure gradient (up to 587 Pa), and shear stress due to its greater viscosity and density. This resulted in more pronounced micro-stresses and tissue deformations compared to milk and water.ConclusionSesame oil demonstrated the most effective mechanical interaction with the cranial structure during Shirodhara, which correlates with its well-documented benefits in anxiety reduction, improved sleep, and nervous system regulation.Keywords: ANSYS, Viscoelasticity, Shirodhara, Biomechanics, Human head model

Introduction

With its extensive chronicles, Ayurveda, the ancient discipline of life, remains relevant due to its enduring fundamental principles. However, the core of every scientific discipline lies in the perpetual pursuit of fresh insights through diligent investigation, advancement, and the exploration of novel practical implementations. To transition to evidence-based Ayurveda, systematic documentation, suitable methodology, rigorous experimentation by best practices, and epistemologically sensitive approaches will continue to be essential [1]. Shirodhara, an ancient Ayurvedic practice, is widely prevalent in India, with doctors witnessing numerous clinical advantages in countless patients [2]. Shirodhara is an Ayurvedic treatment in which medicinal liquids are gently applied to the forehead. The liquid is maintained within a regulated temperature range of 38 °C–39 °C [3], aligning with standard therapeutic guidelines to reflect patient-specific variability. The patient rests quietly on a comfortable bed when the therapist slowly and steadily drips a medicated liquid, like buttermilk, coconut water, milk, water, and oil on the center of the forehead. In Shirodhara, medicated fluids are poured locally and left for 30–45 min before being absorbed by the scalp vein. Through emissary veins in the scalp, medications penetrate the systemic circulatory system [4].

Shirodhara may relieve mental stress, headaches, insomnia, and several kinds of mental disorders [5]. Shirodhara, an ancient yet highly proficient healing technique, effectively influences cerebral circulation, cerebral function, and the state of consciousness [6]. The fluid falls continuously at a certain height above the two eyebrows with constant flow and temperature. This produces constant pressure and vibrations which are enhanced by the frontal bone's hollow sinus. Subsequently, the vibration is transferred inside via the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fluid medium. This vibration in conjunction with low body temperature may stimulate the thalamus and basal forebrain, returning serotonin and catecholamine levels to normal [7]. The procedure will stimulate the central nervous system and the body's biological system by which the state of awareness is controlled [8]. This procedure results in micro-stresses and deformations in and between skin, tissue, and the skull which results in a relaxing effect. In patients with autoimmune encephalitis, CSF indices that show intrathecal antibody production and blood-brain barrier impairment seem to be promising indicators of the severity of the disease and the effectiveness of treatment [9].

It will bring about a calm state of consciousness, leading to a dynamic psycho-somatic equilibrium [10]. The clinical procedure for the Shirodhara is that medicated liquid is continuously poured into the forehead through the pot (dhara) at a constant speed, where a thread is tied over the eyebrows, which prevents spillage of liquid to the eyes.

Initially, it was almost impossible to learn the structure and functions of the brain due to its inaccessibility. Unlike most organs, it is unsafe to probe around the insides of the cranial cavity. However, the advent of mathematical tools like modeling using Finite element methods (FEM) has improved the accuracy of research [11]. Finite Element analysis provides a classified link between computers and biomechanics [12]. The rheological interaction of treatment liquids with cranial structures during Shirodhara has not previously been comprehensively investigated using Finite Element Analysis. Such an integrative study presents a novel way of insight into the mechanical significance of conventional therapeutic interventions and possibly informing the optimization of such treatments.

FEM helped in the study of how cranial parameters change when subjected to various external factors like impact, high-frequency loads, and other discrepancies. A linear elastic material model is applied to the human head's hard tissues, such as the tentorium, scalp, and skull. The type of impact loading is tiny, and the deformation expected is minimal [13,14]. The FEM has been used to significantly affect the modeling of the dynamic response of brain tissue under impact loadings [15,16].

Knowing the morphological effect on mechanical behavior is critical in medical use, particularly in low-strain-rate conditions like therapeutic procedures and neurological disorders [17]. From a mechanical point of view, the brain poses substantial difficulties: its tissue is very soft, threaded through with axon fibers and intricate fluid and blood network, and highly responsive to osmotic fluctuations, and all of these are encased in the rigid outer wall of the skull [18]. These features render direct experimental studies technically as well as ethically challenging. In such settings, finite element modeling provides an effective means to model internal biomechanical response. It allows investigation of stress, strain, and deformation patterns caused by interventions such as Shirodhara and provides useful insights interconnecting traditional therapy with contemporary biomedical analysis.

With a multidisciplinary effort, this work aims to elucidate the efficacy of the Shirodhara procedure using the FEM solver ANSYS Workbench. The CFD solver FLUENT imports the modal shapes of the fluid [19]. It observes and compares the micro stresses and micro deformations on the skin, tissue, and skull, and between these layers of the human cranial vault.

Three types of fluids, water, milk, and sesame oil, were most widely used in the Shirodhara procedure. Materials and methods

Modelling of materials

A forehead–skull model was developed in ANSYS Workbench to simulate the mechanical response of the head under controlled fluid flow, replicating conditions traditionally associated with Shirodhara therapy as shown in Fig. 2. The model geometry extended from the glabella to the vertex scalp and incorporated the relevant skin, tissue, and bone layers, with material properties assigned from established literature. The reference to conventional posture and relaxation state in this contest was not intended to describe an experimental procedure on human subjects, but to provide context for replicating the external boundary conditions of Shirodhara in the simulation environment. This background detail helped align the modelling assumptions with the real-world therapeutic scenario while ensuring that the study itself remained entirely computational, without the involvement of human participants.

A Shirodhara vessel with a centrally located outlet (approximately 6 mm in diameter) was suspended directly above the forehead, aligned with the anatomical location of the center of the forehead (Ajna Chakra). The height from which the fluid was administered was standardized between 4 and 6 inches (10–15 cm) from the forehead surface, based on classical Ayurvedic practice and supported by previous empirical studies. To maintain experimental consistency, a controlled flow mechanism ensured a continuous, laminar stream of liquid throughout the session.

All therapeutic liquids—water, cow milk, and sesame oil—were pre-heated and maintained at a steady temperature between 38 °C and 39 °C, simulating body-warm conditions to avoid thermal shock and to preserve therapeutic efficacy [20]. Each experimental session was conducted for a duration of 30–40 min, reflecting conventional Shirodhara therapeutic protocols [21]. During this period, the mechanical impact of the falling fluid on the forehead surface was monitored and modeled using finite element simulation to evaluate resultant biomechanical parameters such as stress distribution, pressure gradients, and deformation.

To generate the human head model in ANSYS–CFD analysis, give the mesh the proper boundary and interface conditions [22]. The skull-brain interface, which consists of the meninges and CSF, is critical. The morphology of the human brain and skull varies from person to person. The shape of an adult skull often plays a vital role in the biomechanics of biological structures [23]. To better understand the deformation mechanism of a human skull, find the change in ICP by developing and validating the finite element model [24].

In [25], Engin developed a theoretical model using a fluid-filled spherical shell subjected to a local radial impulsive load to represent the dynamic response of the human head. This simplified geometry allowed for tractable analytical solutions while capturing key aspects of skull-fluid interaction, particularly under localized loading conditions. The model included both membrane and bending effects via linear shell theory and offered insight into stress distribution and internal pressure propagation, which are relevant even in non-impulsive cases such as Shirodhara where fluid contact is continuous but localized.

In this study, the spherical approximation is considered primarily to evaluate the local micro-stresses and deformation patterns at the region of impact where the fluid stream contacts the cranial surface. At such localized points, the curvature of the skull approximates spherical geometry reasonably well, particularly around the frontal convexity, which is typically the target site in Shirodhara. The objective is not to generalize force transmission across the entire skull, but rather to examine micro-mechanical effects at the contact zone. Measuring the shear stress, the von Mises stress, and the intracranial pressure needs more tangled experiments. So, the shape of the whole head is considered to be spherical and it is analyzed only the top part of the human head and forehead as a hemispherical shape.

In this paper, the external radius of intracranial space is about 100 mm for modeling [10]. There are different layers like skin, tissue, and skull present in the head. Four different layers of tissue cover these are the connective tissue layer, galea aponeurotic, loose areolar connective tissue, and pericranium, which cover and support the structures beneath. These layers were considered as single layers because their physical properties are almost identical. The thickness of the facial skin on the forehead increases linearly with age. In various literature from direct experimental data, the forehead's skin thickness was taken as 1.7 mm [26]. In multiple studies, the skin is considered a viscoelastic material.

When stress is applied, viscous materials, such as water, resist shear flow and strain linearly with time. When exposed to relatively mild strains at room temperature, most metals display linearly elastic behavior [27]. Based on the several studies, the thickness of facial tissue is 4 mm, and the skull's innermost layer has a thickness of about 6.5 mm [28]. The skin and tissue function as a viscoelastic material, while the skull behaves as a rigid material, with unique properties for each.

One of the ways to determine the viscoelastic properties of skin and tissue is by Prony shear relaxation. Prony Series is a numerical method in the time domain that relates Viscoelastic material's relaxation and creep functions. Load versus time data for various sequences of load ratio adjusted to the convolution integrals of tested materials is one approach for evaluating the Prony Series coefficients of a viscoelastic relaxation modulus.

Relative moduli and relaxation times determine prony shear relaxation. Relative modulus is the measured Young's modulus divided by the Young's modulus of that specific material, and relaxation time is the characteristic time during which a system relaxes in response to changes in external variables.

Results and discussion

Velocity distribution and pressure gradient

The velocity distribution is calculated at the fluid outlet. It had a maximum value of 0.745 m/s for water, 0.845 m/s for milk, and 1.13 m/s for sesame oil.

The Shirodhara pot continuously pours the medicated liquid on the forehead, creating vibrations and applying constant pressure. The fluid flows slowly with constant speed at a height of 7.5 cm throughout the forehead by oscillation. At the point of contact, the applied fluid force is not only continuous but oscillatory, especially due to the wick motion.

The simulation results for velocity distribution in Fig. 3 for various fluids. Due to density and viscosity, sesame oil has a relatively high-velocity magnitude compared to water and cow milk. However, sesame oil is denser and has more viscosity, and we have neglected the effects of air; gravity will significantly impact it. Velocity distribution varies parabolically with a maximum at the center of the forehead. The constant impact of fluid flow influences the CSF which surrounds the brain and arrives at a tranquilizing impact in the brain cortex.

Velocity distribution at the outlet of (a) Water, (b) Cow milk and (c) Sesame oil.

Another parameter computed was the total pressure gradient at the outlet. The values ranged from 59.8 Pa to 285 Pa for water, 38.3 Pa–377 Pa for milk, and 7.12 Pa–587 Pa for sesame oil. The impact force of the continuous fall of liquid on the forehead from a height of 7.5 cm. So the pressure gradient is high and varies from a minimum pressure of 7.12 Pa–587 Pa for sesame oil than others.

A constant pressure is produced by pouring liquid over the forehead in a continuous stream. This continuous pressure generates and amplifies impulses through the hollow sinus, which are then transmitted within cerebrospinal fluid [29]. This will improve the function of the hypothalamus and autonomic nervous system.

The pressure gradient attained shows a higher flow rate for the sesame oil than others (Fig. 4). Even though the density of milk is more than of sesame oil, the pressure gradient is high for sesame oil.

Pressure gradient at the outlet of (a) Water, (b) Cow milk and (c) Sesame oil.

The highest velocity (1.13 m/s) and pressure gradient (up to 587 Pa) were recorded for sesame oil, indicating greater impulse generation. This sustained impulse force creates a quasi-harmonic mechanical stimulus at the forehead's glabellar region. According to classical Ayurvedic texts, the forehead's glabellar region is a critical marma point connected with the mind and sensory organs, believed to influence systemic equilibrium when stimulated. These factors amplify mechanical vibrations, which can interact with the hollow frontal sinus to produce resonant effects. This, in turn, may facilitate signal propagation through the CSF, influencing the thalamus and basal forebrain, structures responsible for regulating arousal and consciousness.

Total deformation

The maximum deformation is obtained in sesame oil, which is 4.7135ⅹ10-6 m, water shows the maximum deformation of 2.96ⅹ10-8 m, and the least maximum deformation in cow milk, which is 1.872ⅹ10-7 m. The deformation causes the skin, tissue, and skull of a human head. The simulation result in Fig. 5 shows that sesame oil has more deformation on the surface of the material. The material will deform for a higher viscosity as a Newtonian fluid. The forehead is considered as a fixed plate and the top plane is moving with liquid with constant speed in the wick.

Total deformation of (a) water, (b) milk and (c) sesame oil.

This higher deformation corresponds with greater energy transfer due to viscosity and fluid momentum.The transmission of these micro-stresses through the scalp and underlying tissues may influence mechanoreceptors, initiate neurovascular feedback, and stimulate thalamocortical oscillations. Literature indicates that such oscillations are implicated in mood regulation, stress relief, and sleep induction, aligning with clinical observations of Shirodhara in the treatment of anxiety, insomnia, and migraines.

Von-mises stress

The von Mises stress analysis gave results in the range 1.4508ⅹ10-8 Pa–0.3396 Pa while using water, 6.9317ⅹ10-9 Pa–1.0487 Pa while using milk, and 9,6326ⅹ10-7 Pa–25.694 Pa while using sesame oil. The maximum values were concentrated on the contact points between the fluid and the surface. Resulting the more yielding in skin, tissue, and skull when using the sesame oil which gives more impact forces is shown in Fig. 6. From a biomechanical perspective, the frontal and temporal lobes, which are situated where the soft tissues of the brain meet the hard bone of the skull, will accelerate and decelerate the pressures. The continuous fluid flow creates an impact force on the intracranial contents and induces tissue strains and stresses in the brain tissues. The von Mises stress influences both skull stresses and intracranial pressure.

Shear stress

Shear stress was found to increase with each layer since there were interactions between each layer when the liquid came in contact with the skin. The values for shear stress were found to be in the range of −0.10687 Pa–0.14668 Pa while using water, −0.29259 Pa–0.40675 Pa while using milk, and −11.727 Pa–4.1728 Pa while using sesame oil. The high shear stress was recorded on the contact area by sesame oil as shown in Fig. 7. The viscosity of sesame oil is higher, so it deforms when it falls on the forehead. Because of sesame oil's high shear stress, this will penetrate the sinus hollow of the frontal lobe.

Shear stress of (a) Water, (b) Cow milk and (c) Sesame oil.

The shear stress results indicate significant mechanical coupling across the skin–tissue–skull interface, especially with high-viscosity fluids like sesame oil. This mechanical interface, as shown in the von Mises stress distribution (up to 25.694 Pa for sesame oil), results in subtle but significant strain and deformation of superficial tissues.

Such mechanical interactions are known in mechanobiology to influence neurotransmitter release and neurovascular responses, possibly explaining the observed calming effects and improved mental clarity often reported in clinical Shirodhara sessions. The return of neurotransmitter levels to homeostasis thus aligns with both our mechanical findings and emerging literature on somatosensory-triggered central regulation.

From the study, it is observed that mechanical effects vary by fluid type, like Sesame oil, due to its higher density and viscosity, showed the highest values for total deformation, von Mises stress, and shear stress. This indicates a more significant mechanical stimulus, which is likely to elicit a greater neurological response through mechanosensory pathways. These stimuli may influence the limbic system, leading to subjective relaxation and mental calmness. Such effects support traditional claims about Shirodhara's role in reducing stress and promoting a tranquil mental state.

Conclusions

This research used finite element analysis to study the biomechanical efficacy of Shirodhara treatment using three different therapeutic fluids - water (Jaladhara), milk (Ksheeradhara), and sesame oil (Tailadhara). The simulation verified velocity distribution, pressure gradient, von Mises stress, total deformation, and shear stress to find out how each of the fluids mechanically interacts with the cranial surface and stimulate the neuralogical system. By linking biomechanical effects with potential neurophysiological outcomes, this study contributes to a mechanistic understanding of Shirodhara. This unique study provides an amalgamation of the traditional system of medicine and physical properties of the medicaments with a simulated model, which help in understanding the effect of shirodhara on the brain and the surrounding structures which has always been a challenging subject owing to the intricacies it poses.

Among the fluids tested, sesame oil was the most viscous and dense and therefore generated the greatest pressure gradients, shear stress, and total deformation. Such mechanical effects should enhance therapy by inducing greater tactile and vibrational stimulation of mechanoreceptors in the forehead, especially those innervating the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve. Such stimulation is suggested to transmit messages via the thalamus to the cerebral cortex and thereby modulate autonomic function and promote relaxation.

However, each of the liquids used in Shirodhara has certain ayurvedic clinical applicability. Water Shirodhara (Jaladhara) is traditionally used in pitta-imbalanced conditions to render a cooling and sedating action. Milk Shirodhara (Ksheeradhara) is used for rejuvenation and nourishing effects, particularly in insomnia, anxiety, and states of neurodegeneration. Sesame oil Shirodhara (Tailadhara) is used for its warming, stabilizing, and penetrating action, usually in vata-imbalanced states and neurological disorders.

Sesame oil has a slow flow rate because of its high density, whereas cow milk has a moderate flow rate compared to water. Due to the softest impact of viscosity (∼35–50 mPa s), the sesame oil gives a high and deep tissue penetration, which helps in the deep neuromuscular relaxation through transdermal absorption. The effect of sesame oil begins with tactile stimulation of the skin, which is innervated by the trigeminal nerve's first branch. Additionally, sesame oil's thermal retention and lipid-soluble antioxidants facilitate transdermal absorption, delivering neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects directly to cranial tissues.

Cow milk has higher viscosity (∼2–3 mPa s) than water and a slightly more laminar flow. Its flow velocity is moderate, allowing increased contact time and better tactile sensation compared to water. However, the velocity profile is still less controlled due to its lower viscosity and heterogeneous composition. Intermediate viscosity and velocity induce moderate shear stress and mild deformation of superficial tissues, improving sensory input and mild neurovascular stimulation.

Water's low viscosity (∼1 mPa s) allows it to flow rapidly and with a turbulent, less uniform velocity distribution. The fluid jet impacts the forehead with relatively high velocity, causing short contact times and low tactile stimulation. The rapid flow reduces heat transfer efficiency and does not provide minimal deformation of skin and underlying tissues, limiting mechanotransduction signals.

Microstresses and vibrations may stimulate the impulses to the thalamus, which forwards them to the cerebral cortex. Impulses from the main nucleus travel to the thalamus, which then sends them on to the cerebral cortex. Tactile stimulus of liquid on the forehead may influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, helping to regulate mood, sleep, and stress hormones.

The findings of this research validate the mechanical viability of differential effects observed under clinical conditions. While sesame oil was more mechanically efficient in deformation and also in stress transfer, by no means does it imply world superiority. Rather, the selection of therapeutic fluid is to be based on parametric imbalances at the patient level and clinical presentations as per the principles of Ayurveda. Incorporating traditionally used medicated liquids such as ghee and buttermilk will enhance the translational relevance of future work in the above mentioned directions. These substances, with their complex compositions and additional bioactive interactions, offer a promising direction for future computational and experimental investigations.

Future work shall further aim to incorporate anatomically accurate skull geometries through imaging-based modeling to enhance biomechanical fidelity. Follow-up research must integrate physiological recordings (e.g., EEG, heart rate variability, fMRI) for various therapeutic fluids with biomechanical models to connect computational predictions with neurophysiological response. Finally, internal validation through verification of such findings through clinical trials and extension of the model framework to include fluid-structure interaction and heat transfer will seal the gap between traditional knowledge and present scientific inquiry. This study is one such effort towards evidence-based standardization of Ayurvedic treatments using contemporary engineering approaches.
