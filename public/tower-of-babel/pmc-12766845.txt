Fibrotic liver injury in biliary atresia: long-term implications
Authors: Maria Hukkinen, Mikko P Pakarinen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNormalization of serum bilirubin after Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE) is a prerequisite for long-term native liver (NL) survival. Following a successful KPE, fibrotic liver injury progresses variably, although a significant proportion of patients show decreasing histological liver fibrosis during follow-up. Eventually, the great majority of patients develop cirrhosis and clinical manifestations of portal hypertension (PH), while liver malignancies and pulmonary complications of PH occur infrequently. Accurate prediction of liver fibrosis remains challenging. Development of reliable noninvasive biomarkers of liver fibrosis would improve patient management and benefit performance of interventional trials. Although successful KPE modifies liver gene expression profile, molecular signature of active fibrogenesis and ductular reaction (DR) persists. Successful KPE reduces the abundance of activated myofibroblasts and macrophages, whereas cholangiocytes tend to increase. Progression of long-term fibrotic liver injury is tightly connected with DR, which is associated with serum bile acids. While serum bile acids are predictive of portal fibrosis and NL survival, there are ongoing randomized clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of bile acids lowering therapies on KPE outcomes. A better understanding of disease mechanisms underpinning progression of liver injury and clinical complications following successful KPE is needed for the development of novel adjuvant therapies, improvement of follow-up, and optimizing outcomes.

Introduction

Biliary atresia (BA) is a rapidly progressing cholangiopathy characterized by progressive fibro-inflammatory obliteration of the biliary tree, manifesting during the first weeks of life.1 Timely surgical Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE) can restore bile flow in 50%–70% of patients; however, progression of intrahepatic cholangiopathy and ensuing biliary cirrhosis warrants liver transplantation (LT) in the majority before adulthood.2

After birth, the absence of bile flow induces fibrogenesis and inflammation, and liver biopsies taken at KPE invariably show moderate to severe fibrosis.3 4 Other characteristic histological features include portal inflammation and prominent ductular reaction (DR).3 Biliary components of DR can be visualized as cytokeratin 7 (K7)-positive expansion of biliary epithelium in portal areas and parenchymal intermediate hepatocytes, representing transdifferentiating hepatocytes or progenitor cells.5 After a successful KPE, defined as normalization of serum bilirubin levels within 3–6 months of surgery,2 the degree of liver fibrosis progresses variably, but eventually over years results almost invariably in cirrhosis and necessitates LT.6 However, fibrosis remains stable or even decreases in a subset of patients with good prognosis and prolonged long-term native liver (NL) survival.5 6 When KPE fails and cholestasis persists, cirrhosis and its complications develop within months, resulting in end-stage liver failure by the age of 2 years without a chance for long-term NL survival.3

Advancing fibrosis leads to clinical complications of chronic liver disease in BA. Portal hypertension (PH) develops very frequently after both failed and successful KPE, even before established cirrhosis, but is less common among patients with unprogressive liver fibrosis.7 Frequent postoperative cholangitis episodes threaten NL survival, and their risk has been reported to be associated with higher fibrosis stage at KPE.8 However, the progression of histological liver fibrosis after successful KPE was not associated with the occurrence of cholangitis episodes.6 Advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis predispose to hepatobiliary malignancies. Although hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has been diagnosed even before age 1 year, it is usually encountered among patients who have survived for years with their NL.9

Progression rate and severity of fibrosis largely dictate the need for LT.6 7 Accurate, non-invasive biomarkers to estimate liver fibrosis stage would improve management of BA patients. However, despite recent advances, results regarding available serum biomarkers or elastography have been inconsistent or require further validation in larger independent patient cohorts.71014 Interpretation of liver biopsies, on the other hand, is still commonly based on categorized manual scoring systems prone to intra- and interobserver variability while biopsies represent only a very small proportion of the liver.

In this review, we concentrate on liver fibrosis among long-term NL survivors following a successful KPE and discuss assessment of liver fibrosis, its evolution with underlying pathophysiology, and associated long-term clinical complications. Due to the study setting, no ethical committee approval was obtained.

Evolution and pathophysiology of long-term liver fibrosis

Aside from established cirrhosis, the degree of histological liver fibrosis at KPE is an inaccurate prognosticator for normalization of bilirubin or NL survival after KPE.5 15 16 However, patients with poor response to KPE and inferior NL survival display already at the time of KPE higher expressions of genes that functionally associate with liver fibrosis than patients with good outcomes.16 In line with this, liver fibrosis progresses more rapidly in patients who fail to normalize bilirubin after KPE, but the molecular signature of active fibrogenesis prevails also after a successful KPE normalizing serum bilirubin.3 17 18

As the liver gene expression profile is modified by a successful KPE, the underlying pathophysiology of fibrotic liver injury may differ from the one observed at presentation or at the time of KPE.18 Using bulk RNA sequencing, functional analysis of the liver gene signature at 1 year after a successful KPE discovered persistent upregulation of extracellular matrix production, whereas neutrophil-mediated inflammation declined. At the same time, relative transcriptional abundance of activated hepatic stellate cells (HSC) and macrophages reduced, CD8 T cells and natural killer cells remained unaltered, whereas cholangiocyte abundance showed an increasing trend (figure 1).18 Many of the genes that remain upregulated following a successful KPE are involved with fibrogenesis, myofibroblast activation, and cholangiocyte activation and injury modulated by TGF-β signaling including several collagen genes.1719

In Helsinki, protocol follow-up NL biopsies are obtained at 1-year and at 5-year intervals after KPE, providing a unique opportunity to investigate progression of post-KPE liver injury.3 20 Following a successful KPE, histological liver fibrosis progressed to cirrhosis in 53%, remained stable in 16%, and decreased in 22% by median age 4 years.6 Overall, 40% of patients showed a decrease in histological liver fibrosis by a median of 2 years after successful KPE.21 The presence of decreasing liver fibrosis in follow-up biopsies was strongly associated with efficient and sustained resolution of cholestasis and improved NL survival. An image-processing neural network analysis of histological liver sections showed that the biliary components of DR, K7 positive biliary epithelium, and parenchymal intermediate hepatocytes were strongly predictive of NL survival and correlated with the degree of liver fibrosis5 (figure 2). Following a successful KPE, parenchymal intermediate hepatocytes express matrix metalloproteinase 7 (MMP7) and GATA6 in addition to K7, and their expansion was more pronounced with a stronger connection to NL survival than expansion of biliary epithelium.3 5 19 22 Expanding K7 positive cholangiocytes displayed increased expression of MMP7 and secretin receptor, which were associated with the degree of liver fibrosis, providing further support for the central role of DR in mediating extracellular matrix accumulation and remodeling following KPE.19 23 In summary, liver fibrosis progresses variably after a successful KPE being closely linked with DR, especially transdifferentiating intermediate hepatocytes, in line with the above-described molecule genetic changes reflecting active fibrogenesis and cholangiocyte injury.1 Along with available experimental data, these findings suggest that DR is the functional niche driving liver fibrogenesis among long-term NL survivors after a successful KPE.

Although serum bilirubin levels normalize after a successful KPE, serum bile acids remain elevated and predict NL survival as well as development of PH and other complications of chronic liver disease.2426 The predictive ability of serum bile acids is present already at 1 month after KPE and lasts for years as serum bile acids remain mildly to moderately elevated in long-term NL survivors.25 26 Elevated serum bile acids correlate positively with DR expansion, particularly conjugated cholic acids with K7 positive parenchymal intermediate hepatocytes, introducing the theoretical possibility that the strong predictive ability of bile acids is explained by their involvement in promoting DR and subsequent fibrotic liver injury.5 25 While bile acids can induce hepatobiliary injury by a variety of different mechanisms, taurocholic acid has been reported to promote DR, hepatic myofibroblast activation, and fibrosis in human hepatocyte cultures.2729

Currently, there are no efficient medical therapies to tackle liver fibrosis in BA. Lowering of bile acids could be a promising therapeutic target for the management of the progressive long-term fibrotic liver injury following a successful KPE. At present, unpublished multicenter randomized trials have investigated the efficacy of ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT) inhibitors and farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonist obeticholic acid.30 IBAT inhibitors interrupt enterohepatic circulation of bile acids by preventing their active ileal uptake and thereby alleviate bile acid accumulation. Obeticholic is a synthetic primary bile acid and stronger FXR agonist than natural bile acids. FXR activation reduces hepatic bile acid load by lowering synthesis and promoting secretion of bile acids.31 Another potential therapeutic target could be fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19), which downregulates hepatic bile acid synthesis after being secreted by ileal enterocytes. An engineered, non-tumorigenic FGF19 analog, aldafermin, reduced fibrosis biomarkers in a randomized trial in primary sclerosing cholangitis sharing pathophysiological features with BA.32 In patients with BA, elevated serum FGF19 originates unphysiologically from the liver and correlates with DR and transplant-free survival, whereas aberrantly increased hepatocyte expression of FGF19 is associated with poor prognosis after KPE.25 33

Clinical phenotype and liver fibrosis

The severity of liver fibrosis varies according to clinical patient characteristics which also influence BA prognosis. BA splenic malformation (BASM) and cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection are known to associate with shorter NL survival,34 whereas cystic BA is often diagnosed at earlier age and relates to a better prognosis.35 While the frequency of cirrhosis at KPE is similar among BASM patients to isolated BA,34 fibrosis seemed to progress less rapidly after a successful KPE in syndromic BA patients with lower liver expression of α-smooth muscle actin and collagen 1.17 In addition, esophageal varices have been reported less frequently and aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI) lower among NL survivors with syndromic compared with isolated BA.36 compared with CMV-negative patients, those with CMV infection present later with more severe fibrosis.34 37 Fibrosis stage at KPE has been reported to be lower in cystic compared with non-cystic BA;38 however, others have not observed a difference between subgroups.35 Patients who are operated on before the age of 2 months have higher chances for clearance of jaundice and NL survival.34 Although KPE age correlated negatively with concomitant fibrosis stage,39 it was not predictive for progression of fibrosis.6

Assessment of liver fibrosis

Elastography

Elastography estimates the degree of liver fibrosis by measuring liver stiffness through shear wave propagation. Both in children with various liver diseases40 and in BA patients after KPE,7 11 41 liver stiffness predicts accurately cirrhosis or advanced fibrosis but is less reliable in detecting lower fibrosis stages when compared with biopsies staged according to conventional staging systems (table 1). The use of different elastography techniques complicates the comparison of results, as they are not always expressed as kilopascals, but shear wave elastography measurements may also be given as meters per second.32 Elastography results are influenced by multiple confounding factors, such as the extent of portal inflammation and cholestasis, patient size, fasting, sedation, and technical factors related to the measurement influence liver stiffness.32 40 Out of the studies among BA NL survivors, two have observed liver stiffness values of 24 kPa to predict cirrhosis,7 11 while a third study found a considerably lower cut-off of 2 m/s, corresponding to 12 kPa41 42 (table 1).

Serum markers

APRI has been extensively studied both as a surrogate of fibrosis and predictor of NLS among BA patients. Studies correlating the extent of histological fibrosis to APRI values during follow-up after KPE have reported poor to moderate accuracy for detection of cirrhosis with highly variable cut-off values7 11 12 43 (table 1). The ability of APRI to discriminate lower fibrosis stages appears even less reliable.11 12 43

HSCs secrete Mac-2 binding protein glycosylation isomer (M2BPGi) to activate Kupffer cells.44 Serum M2BPGi outperformed other noninvasive markers in discriminating METAVIR stages F2, F3, and F4 among adult hepatitis patients.45 In two small cohorts of BA NL survivors, serum M2BPGi predicted advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis with excellent accuracy and comparable cutoffs12 13 (table 1). On the other hand, a study among BA LT recipients reported considerably higher cutoffs for discrimination of F4 from F3.43 Accordingly, adult studies report variability in the accuracy and cutoffs of serum M2BPGi across different liver diseases.44 45 Further research in BA patients is warranted before applying serum M2BPGi into clinical practice.

Autotaxin is an enzyme generating lysophosphatidic acid, which activates profibrogenic HSCs. In adult hepatitis patients, serum autotaxin levels detected milder stages of fibrosis with excellent accuracy.46 Similar observations were made in a cohort of 35 BA NL survivors where the accuracy of serum autotaxin corresponded closely to M2BPGi and was excellent for detection of F3 while moderate for detection of F4 fibrosis47 (table 1). MicroRNA-214 is another regulator of HCSs, which liver and serum levels increase in the presence of fibrosis.13 Serum miRNA-214 predicted advanced fibrosis with moderate accuracy in a small cohort of NL survivors13 (table 1).

Liver biopsy

Conventional assessment of liver fibrosis relies on manual scoring of liver biopsy. The most used scoring systems include METAVIR, Scheuer, and Ishak scores, including four to six fibrosis categories (figure 3).15 48 Since manual scoring systems are based on pathologists’ subjective impression of fibrosis deposits, they are prone to intra- and interobserver variability. Digital quantification of hepatic collagen deposition, instead, provides a more reproducible method to assess cirrhosis.49 50 Computerized quantification of collagen has been shown to outperform manual scores in the prediction of clinical outcomes in BA as well as other cirrhotic liver diseases.50 51 Artificial intelligence-assisted histological assessment of liver fibrosis has the potential to greatly improve quality of information obtained from liver biopsies.

Liver biopsy is an invasive diagnostic method that carries a small risk for complications and requires general anesthesia in children. To overcome these problems and analytical limitations of liver biopsies, non-invasive biomarkers for fibrotic liver injury and prognosis have been searched extensively.14 However, none has proven sensitive or specific enough yet to replace liver biopsies in BA. Optimal cutoffs and accuracy differ according to timing of assessment, which complicates interpretation and clinical use of non-invasive surrogates of fibrosis in BA. In addition, most existing research has compared non-invasive methods to categorized histological fibrosis scores, which are not entirely accurate measures of liver fibrosis themselves. As liver biopsies are routinely obtained at KPE, most studies on non-invasive surrogates for fibrosis have concentrated on the time of KPE. However, reliable non-invasive fibrosis assessment would be necessary particularly during the follow-up of BA patients.

Long-term clinical complications

PH: definitions and pathophysiology

In children, the diagnosis of PH is almost invariably based on its clinical manifestations instead of direct or wedged portal pressure measurement.52 PH develops in BA patients parallel with progressing fibrosis, and non-invasive biomarkers of fibrosis such as APRI and liver stiffness have excellent accuracy in detecting PH.7 Elevated serum bile acid levels also correlate with the occurrence of PH.7 24 Despite the strong associations with progressive fibrosis, the pathophysiology of PH in BA remains incompletely understood. There is evidence demonstrating that excessive bile duct proliferation in BA patients directly compresses terminal portal vein branches and thereby mechanically promotes PH.53 Accordingly, the extent of histological DR, characterized by proliferating bile ducts, predicts PH development in BA patients.54 Both DR and obliterative portal venopathy are known to progress early even after a successful KPE,5 55 providing a possible explanation to PH susceptibility even in the absence of cirrhosis.

Many studies define PH as the occurrence of esophageal varices or the presence of both splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia,7 52 according to which over half of patients having undergone a successful KPE develop PH by 1 year of age and two thirds by 9 years.7 56 57 After a failed KPE, instead, both PH and cirrhosis typically develop before the age of 1 year.3 58 Although patients who survive with their NL until adulthood represent a very particular BA subgroup with benign disease course, the majority of them have developed PH by adult age.2

Esophageal varices

At the median age of 8.5 years, significant esophageal varices are detected in 60% of NL survivors undergoing endoscopic surveillance, demonstrating that the incidence of varices corresponds closely to that of PH.59 The most feared complication of PH is variceal bleeding with estimated incidence of 8% by 5 years after successful KPE and associated 6-week mortality approaching 9%.56 60

There is no consensus whether BA children should undergo routine endoscopic surveillance and primary endoscopic bleeding prophylaxis of varices. Primary prophylactic ligation or sclerotherapy is efficient in preventing variceal hemorrhage particularly among patients who normalize bilirubin levels after KPE.58 On the other hand, repeated endoscopies warrant hospitalizations and general anesthesia. The accuracy of noninvasive methods in identifying patients with significant varices is modest, but different combinations of platelet count, APRI, and liver stiffness values appear useful for their prediction.6163 Surprisingly, the accuracy of non-invasive fibrosis biomarkers, APRI and liver stiffness, has been reported to be even more accurate in predicting high-risk varices61 62 than presence of cirrhosis in BA.7 11 12 43

Pulmonary complications

PH may result in pulmonary complications, including hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS) and portopulmonary hypertension. HPS develops due to abnormal pulmonary vascular dilatations which result in intrapulmonary shunting, hypoxemia, and ventilation-perfusion mismatch.64 HPS has been reported to develop at a median age of 4 years in 5%–12% of BA NL survivors.57 65 The pathophysiology of HPS is not fully understood, and it remains unknown why HPS often develops in the presence of well-compensated liver disease to patients with low PELD scores.64 Portopulmonary hypertension, instead, is caused by pulmonary vasoconstriction and develops mainly to teenage girls with end-stage liver disease.65 66 Although portopulmonary hypertension has been estimated to affect only <1% of children with chronic liver disease,66 67 its prevalence among BA NL survivors aged 15 years has been reported as high as 19%.65 Both pulmonary complications of PH warrant evaluation for LT, which should be performed timely before the development of irreversible complications contraindicating transplantation.2

Malignancies

Advanced fibrosis increases the risk of HCC,68 69 which accounts for almost 75% of hepatobiliary malignancies among BA patients.9 69 Activated extracellular matrix cells are crucial in promoting HCC.70 Among published HCC cases in patients with BA, median age at cancer diagnosis has been 4 years, while elevated serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels have been detected only in around half of patients.9 69 As discrimination of AFP-negative HCC from other cirrhotic nodules may be impossible based on imaging findings only, biopsies should be taken with a low threshold when susceptible nodules are encountered.2 Disease-free survival can be achieved with timely HCC diagnosis and transplantation.9

One-third to half of HCC cases have been incidental findings in explanted livers,9 69 and only about half of all nodules detected in BA explants have been identifiable in pre-transplant imaging.71 As the sensitivity of MRI is superior to CT in detecting liver nodules and in discriminating between benign and malignant tumors,71 BA patients might benefit from regular follow-up MRI examinations. In addition, there are emerging new HCC markers used in adult patient populations that have not yet been tested in children.9 Current follow-up recommendation consisting of ultrasound and AFP monitoring every 6 months9 72 may be insufficient for timely HCC diagnosis.

Both cholangiocarcinoma and hepatoblastoma have been reported among NL survivors, the former associating with elevated serum Ca19-9 levels and the latter almost invariably with elevated AFP.9 While the prognosis of cholangiocarcinoma is dismal, hepatoblastoma carries a better outlook, likely due to its chemosensitivity.9 Elevated tumor markers may lead to early diagnosis.

Liver failure

When KPE fails and cholestasis persists, BA progresses to cirrhosis, end-stage liver disease, and eventually liver failure within 2 years.1 3 Liver failure manifests with ascites, coagulopathy, jaundice, and variceal hemorrhage.58 73 Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), affecting 12%–20% of BA patients, is characterized by acute decompensation of liver disease complicated by extrahepatic organ failure.74 75 ACLF associates with high morbidity, increased pre-transplant mortality, and is more common after failed than successful KPE.74 75 Patients with failed KPE have significantly higher bilirubin and PELD scores at listing and are also at high risk for malabsorption, malnutrition, and neurological impairment.2 Accordingly, waitlist mortality is highest among the youngest BA infants with poorest liver function.76 To minimize mortality, optimize transplantation outcomes, and ensure timely LT following KPE, children with BA should be followed up by multidisciplinary teams in expert transplant centers.73 77

After successful KPE, patients develop a chronic, variably progressing liver disease which nevertheless usually advances at a much slower rate than after failed KPE.3 Half of children undergoing a successful KPE have developed cirrhosis by the age of 8 years7 but may survive for years with compensated cirrhosis. Although indications and timing for LT may vary significantly between countries and centers, many patients having undergone a successful KPE are listed to LT before development of liver failure, with relatively low PELD scores, and mainly because of PH-related complications or refractory cholangitis.21 73 78 Timely listing for LT of older children with better graft availability associates with improved outcomes, and indeed, BA patients transplanted after successful KPE in Western countries have nonexistent waitlist mortality and short waitlist times.21 76

Treatment prospects

Patients who normalize bilirubin following KPE would greatly benefit from efficient medical therapies to decelerate the progression of liver fibrosis. As the liver gene expression profile is modified by a successful KPE, the underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms driving fibrotic liver injury may differ from the ones observed at presentation.18 A better understanding of these disease mechanisms, progression of liver injury, and clinical complications following successful KPE is needed for the development of novel adjuvant therapies, improvement of follow-up, and optimizing patient care. Future trials should continue to test whether bile acid lowering by pharmacological modulation of the FXR-FGF19 axis could counteract progression of fibrotic liver injury among longer-term NL survivors. Accurate noninvasive fibrosis biomarkers would greatly facilitate performance of such clinical trials and follow-up of BA patients.

Conclusion

Variable degrees of fibrosis together with prominent DR are observed in BA patients at the time of KPE. After successful KPE, altered liver gene expression favors extracellular matrix accumulation and remodeling, whereas fibrosis progression rate is variable. Progressive fibrosis is driven by DR, both of which associate with elevated serum bile acids and inferior NL survival. Reliable noninvasive predictors of fibrosis severity are lacking, although discrimination of cirrhosis from lower fibrosis stages is possible. The most common clinical complication after successful KPE is PH, which affects most long-term NL survivors even before development of cirrhosis. Development of anti-fibrotic medications could prolong NL survival rates but warrants more detailed understanding of the mechanisms driving liver injury in BA following KPE.
