Late-onset hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction in a childhood cancer survivor treated with cranial irradiation
Authors: Yuria Takamura, Reiko Inoue, Koji Takeuchi, Fukuo Kosokabe, Kota Ishizawa, Daisuke Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction is a well-recognized late complication in childhood cancer survivors (CCSs) treated with cranial irradiation. We report a 42-year-old male with a history of acute lymphoblastic leukemia diagnosed at 5 years of age and treated with chemotherapy and 26 Gray whole-brain irradiation. He presented with right upper extremity paresis due to bilateral chronic subdural hematomas. Following burr-hole drainage for the left chronic subdural hematoma, he developed anorexia, fatigue, and hyponatremia and was referred to the endocrinology department. Endocrine evaluation demonstrated hypothalamic adrenal insufficiency, severe adult growth hormone deficiency, mild gonadal dysfunction, and mixed hypothyroidism. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a suprasellar arachnoid cyst with compression of the pituitary gland. To our knowledge, this represents one of the longest reported intervals between childhood cancer treatment and the diagnosis of hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction, which became clinically apparent 37 years after treatment following a prolonged asymptomatic period. This case suggests that hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction may remain clinically silent and gradually progress for decades before becoming unmasked by the physiological stress, highlighting the need for prolonged, potentially lifelong, endocrine surveillance in CCS.

Introduction

Hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction is a well-recognized late complication in childhood cancer survivors (CCSs) treated with cranial irradiation and may develop years after treatment [1]. International guidelines recommend lifelong endocrine surveillance according to radiation exposure and risk [2]. However, follow-up may be discontinued during the transition to adult care. Herein, we report a CCS who developed clinically overt hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction 37 years after cranial irradiation for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).

Case presentation

A 42-year-old male presented to our hospital with right upper limb paresis. Brain computed tomography revealed left-dominant bilateral chronic subdural hemorrhage (CSDH) and enlarged sella turcica with a 34 × 27 × 18-mm cystic component. He reported repeated minor head trauma at work, which may have contributed to CSDH. He underwent burr-hole drainage for the left CSDH. At admission, routine laboratory testing was unremarkable, including serum sodium level (141 mEq/L [141 mmol/L], reference range [RR]: 138-145 mEq/L [138-145 mmol/L]). Serum sodium was 137 mEq/L (137 mmol/L) on postoperative day 1. Only extracellular fluid replacement solutions, without hypotonic intravenous fluids, were administered. Two days after surgery, he developed anorexia and fatigue with progressively decreased oral intake, despite recovery of the paresis without apparent neurosurgical complications. On postoperative day 6, serum sodium decreased to 119 mEq/L (119 mmol/L), prompting endocrinology referral.

He had been diagnosed with ALL at the age of 5 and treated with chemotherapy and 26 Gray (Gy) of whole-brain radiation therapy. He underwent at least annual biochemical and imaging assessments until age 19, although the timing of his last endocrine evaluation was unknown; no endocrine evaluation was documented thereafter until the current presentation. Annual medical checkups during adulthood revealed no apparent abnormalities. At presentation, his height was 162.3 cm, approximately 9 cm below his calculated target height of 171.5 cm. Detailed longitudinal data regarding childhood growth velocity and pubertal development were unavailable. He was unmarried, had no children, and had been smoking 20 cigarettes per day for 25 years.

Diagnostic assessment

At the time of severe hyponatremia, urinary sodium concentration was low at 10 mEq/L (10 mmol/L), with a plasma renin activity of 3.5 ng/mL/hour (3.5 μg/hour/L) (RR: 0.2-2.3 ng/mL/hour [0.2-2.3 μg/hour/L]) and plasma aldosterone concentration of 51.3 ng/dL (1423 pmol/L) (RR: 0.4-8.21 ng/dL [11-227 pmol/L]). Early-morning plasma cortisol was markedly low at 2.18 μg/dL (58.9 nmol/L) (RR: 7.1-19.6 μg/dL [195.9-540.9 nmol/L]), and 24-hour urinary free cortisol was undetectable. A rapid adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation test indicated hypoadrenalism, with basal and peak plasma cortisol levels of 5.56 μg/dL (153.4 nmol/L) and 10.8 μg/dL (298.1 nmol/L), respectively. Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate was markedly low at 16 μg/dL (0.4 μmol/L) (RR: 68-429 μg/dL [1.8-11.6 μmol/L]), further supporting the diagnosis of hypoadrenalism. Plasma ACTH level was 14.5 pg/mL (3.2 pmol/L) (RR: 7.2-63.3 pg/mL [1.6-13.9 pmol/L]), which were considered relatively low in the presence of low serum cortisol levels. ACTH responded to corticotropin-releasing hormone stimulation (Table 1) but not to insulin tolerance test (ITT) (peak ACTH: 5.1 pg/mL [1.1 pmol/L]; peak cortisol:0.99 μg/dL [27.3 nmol/L]; glucose nadir: 43 mg/dL [2.4 mmol/L]), consistent with hypothalamic adrenal insufficiency. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an enlarged sella turcica with a cystic component and a posteriorly displaced, atrophied pituitary gland without a solid mass (Fig. 1). His insulin-like growth factor-1 was extremely low for his age (11 ng/mL [1.4 nmol/L], RR:93-259 ng/mL [12.2-33.9 nmol/L]), and ITT confirmed severe adult growth hormone (GH) deficiency (peak GH: 0.11 ng/mL [0.11 µg/L], RR: >10 ng/mL [>10 µg/L]). Despite reduced serum free thyroxine (FT4) and triiodothyronine (FT3), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was slightly elevated (Table 1) with negative antithyroglobulin and antithyroid peroxidase antibodies. The TSH peak after thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) stimulation was delayed to 60 minutes, consistent with secretion of less biologically active TSH [3, 4]. Basal luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) were near the lower reference limits with mildly reduced total testosterone. Sexual function, LH/FSH responses to LH-releasing hormone stimulation, and bone mineral density (BMD) were preserved (total hip BMD T-score: −0.3), although testicular volume was slightly reduced (10-12 mL [RR: 13-16 mL]), suggesting early or partial hypogonadism. Thus, he was diagnosed with hypothalamic ACTH deficiency, severe adult GH deficiencies, mixed hypothyroidism, and mild gonadal dysfunction.

Treatment

Oral hydrocortisone replacement was initiated at 15 mg/day in 2 divided doses (10 mg morning and 5 mg evening), together with intravenous extracellular fluid replacement (500 mL/day for 2 days) for suspected volume depletion. Serum sodium level increased from 119 to 130 mEq/L within 24 hours and then to 132 and 134 mEq/L over the following 2 days, reaching 138 mEq/L by day 6 and 140 mEq/L by day 10. No neurological complications occurred during correction.

Outcome and follow-up

Appetite loss and fatigue resolved promptly, and he returned to work 1 month later. Levothyroxine was added and titrated to 75 µg daily (1.08 µg/kg/day), with normalization of FT3 and FT4. Six months later, he gained approximately 20 kg with worsening liver function and lipid profiles, and GH replacement therapy was initiated for severe adult GH deficiency.

Discussion

According to advances in childhood cancer treatment, the 5-year survival rate for childhood cancer now exceeds 80% [5]. It is well recognized that up to 50% of CCS will develop an endocrine disorder during their lifetime [5]. Radiation therapy involving the hypothalamus–pituitary region is an established risk factor for late-onset hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction. The susceptibility of pituitary hormones to radiation-induced damage differs among hormonal axes, with GH being the most radiosensitive, followed by LH/FSH, ACTH, and TSH [2]. Because of long-term adverse effects, including endocrine dysfunction and secondary brain tumors, prophylactic whole-brain irradiation has largely been abandoned in childhood ALL and replaced by contemporary chemotherapy-based central nervous system–directed strategies [6].

Empty sella and pituitary atrophy developing decades after cranial irradiation have also been reported [7, 8]. The suprasellar cystic lesion in our patient was considered most consistent with an arachnoid cyst, although Rathke cleft cyst and empty sella were included in the radiographic differential diagnosis. The lesion may have contributed to hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction through compression of the pituitary gland; however, the relative contributions of the cystic lesion and previous cranial irradiation could not be determined. To our knowledge, no clear association between suprasellar arachnoid cysts and childhood cranial irradiation has been reported. Because visual field testing was normal and the lesion was likely longstanding, urgent surgical intervention was not considered necessary, and improvement in pituitary function following decompression was considered unlikely.

Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) associated with CSDH or neurosurgical intervention was also considered as a potential contributor to the severe postoperative hyponatremia [9, 10]. However, the low urinary sodium concentration and elevated renin–aldosterone levels were less consistent with SIADH. Although adrenal insufficiency was considered the major contributor, the unusually severe hyponatremia may have been multifactorial, including reduced oral intake and a possible contribution of postoperative SIADH. Osmotic demyelination syndrome is an important concern during the correction of severe hyponatremia; however, the hyponatremia in our patient developed over a relatively short period, and no neurological findings suggestive of osmotic demyelination were observed during or after treatment.

The Endocrine Society guidelines recommend lifelong surveillance for GH deficiency in CCS exposed to irradiation doses of 18 Gy or more to the hypothalamus–pituitary region, whereas screening for ACTH deficiency in patients treated with 24 to 30 Gy is recommended primarily during the first 10 years after irradiation and thereafter when symptoms suggest adrenal insufficiency [5]. More recent International Late Effects of Childhood Cancer Guideline Harmonization Group recommendations suggest that routine laboratory surveillance for hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction may become optional after 15 years in survivors without previously detected endocrine abnormalities [2]. Nevertheless, radiation-induced hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction may progress over time [11]. In our patient, clinically overt hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction emerged 37 years after irradiation, representing one of the longest reported intervals to diagnosis [12, 13], possibly reflecting latent dysfunction unmasked by the physiological stress of CSDH and surgery. This case suggests that clinically significant hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction may emerge decades after cranial irradiation, beyond currently anticipated surveillance periods.

As for the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis, hypothyroidism accompanied by only mildly elevated TSH levels was observed in our patient. There was no evidence of chronic primary hypothyroidism, prior cervical irradiation, or excessive iodine intake. Some CCS treated with cranial or craniospinal radiation may develop central hypothyroidism accompanied by slightly elevated TSH levels, a condition referred to as mixed hypothyroidism [3]. Radiation-induced hypothalamic dysfunction may reduce TRH secretion, resulting in abnormal glycosylation of TSH that is biologically less active [4]. Attenuation or loss of the nocturnal TSH surge and delayed TSH peak responses during TRH stimulation test, as observed in our patient, are useful findings for distinguishing mixed hypothyroidism from primary hypothyroidism [14].
