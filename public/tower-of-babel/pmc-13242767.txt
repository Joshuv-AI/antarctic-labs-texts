Association of Scalp Nerve Block with Perioperative Outcomes and Postoperative Complications During Subthalamic Nucleus Deep Brain Stimulation: A Single-Center Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jianning Lan, Guoen Cai, Rifeng Jiang, Hongdan Guan, Xuru Zhang, Jiali Wang, Qinyong Ye, Yangfa Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of the scalp nerve block (SNB) on surgical outcomes and postoperative complications in the subthalamic nucleus (STN) deep brain stimulation (DBS) surgery.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed data from 121 adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent STN-DBS surgery at a tertiary teaching hospital in Fuzhou, China, between November 2, 2017, and April 4, 2024. Patients were divided chronologically into two groups: local anesthesia (LA) alone (2017–2020, n=50) and LA combined with SNB (2020–2024, n=71). The primary outcome was the incidence of pulmonary infection within 7 days post-surgery. Secondary outcomes included other complications, microelectrode recordings, and anesthesia and surgery time parameters. Data were analyzed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression.Main ResultsCompared with the LA group, the LA+SNB group had a significantly shorter intracranial electrode implantation duration (123.97 ± 39.76 min vs. 181.74 ± 53.57 min, p < 0.001) and a lower rate of postoperative pulmonary infection (11.3% vs. 32.0%, p = 0.005). In univariate analysis, SNB was associated with a lower risk of pulmonary infection (OR = 0.262, 95% CI: 0.102–0.675, p = 0.006). However, in multivariate analysis adjusting for anesthetic method, duration of DBS, and tracheal extubation time, the association between SNB and pulmonary infection was no longer statistically significant (OR = 0.426, 95% CI: 0.138–1.315, p = 0.138), whereas tracheal extubation time remained an independent predictor (OR = 1.026 per minute, 95% CI: 1.009–1.045, p = 0.004).ConclusionLA+SNB was associated with improved perioperative outcomes during STN-DBS surgery, including shorter operative time, reduced PACU stay, lower unadjusted pulmonary infection rates, and decreased intracranial air volume. However, only tracheal extubation time, rather than SNB itself, remained an independent predictor of pulmonary infection in multivariate analysis. Temporal confounding and selection bias are inherent limitations.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, deep brain stimulation, scalp nerve block, postoperative complications

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative condition that significantly impairs quality of life.1 For patients with advanced PD who respond poorly to medications, subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN-DBS) offers effective symptom control.2–7

Effective anesthesia management is crucial to the success of STN-DBS surgery, as it influences patient comfort, intraoperative cooperation, surgical efficiency, and postoperative recovery.8–10 The most commonly employed anesthesia techniques for DBS include local anesthesia (LA), conscious sedation, and general anesthesia (GA).11 While GA ensures patient comfort12 and has evolved with advances in imaging guidance such as intraoperative MRI,13 microelectrode recording-free protocols,14,15 and refined anesthetic agents, yielding comparable electrode accuracy and safety to LA in recent trials,11,12 for frail PD patients,16 prolonged exposure to GA still associates with higher risks of pulmonary issues,17 delayed recovery,18 and postoperative delirium,19 making LA a common choice in clinical practice.10 However, LA alone may be insufficient in managing the significant pain and discomfort that can arise during the implantation of intracranial electrodes. This can lead to decreased patient cooperation and prolonged operative time.10 Consequently, better pain control that improves patient cooperation is expected to enhance electrode implantation accuracy and reduce multiple insertion attempts and additional cranial punctures. Additionally, due to respiratory dysfunction and general frailty in this population, perioperative complications, especially pulmonary infections,20 remain a significant concern. These limitations have prompted clinicians to seek complementary anesthesia strategies to improve pain control and patient cooperation during surgery.21,22

Scalp nerve block (SNB) has emerged as a promising adjunct technique for managing pain in cranial surgeries, including those for conditions such as chronic headaches and trigeminal neuralgia.23–25 SNB has demonstrated positive effects in reducing postoperative pain and improving recovery, and a previous study has shown that SNB can provide perioperative analgesia and reduce pain-related complications.26 Moreover, meta-analyses confirm that SNB provides superior pain control compared to other methods and reduces opioid consumption.27 In PD patients undergoing STN-DBS, SNB’s opioid-sparing effect28 is particularly valuable as it lowers the risk of respiratory depression29 and postoperative cognitive dysfunction,30 enhancing postoperative recovery quality, reducing drug interactions, improving patient satisfaction, and lowering healthcare costs.31 Therefore, SNB-optimized pain management holds great clinical significance for elderly PD patients undergoing STN-DBS.

The integration of SNB with LA has been recommended by some surgeons to further improve patient comfort during DBS surgery, allowing for better cooperation with the procedure.10 However, despite the well-established role of intraoperative analgesia in enhancing patient comfort and cooperation, there is a paucity of studies addressing the broader impacts of SNB on the DBS surgical process, including its efficacy in improving surgical efficiency, the accuracy of electrode implantation, and the potential to mitigate postoperative complications. Consequently, the net benefits and drawbacks of incorporating SNB into DBS surgery remain to be elucidated. Although a recent single-center study suggests that SNB could reduce certain postoperative complications in DBS for PD patients,28 the evidence is limited and lacks comprehensive validation across diverse patient populations and healthcare settings. Furthermore, the spectrum of postoperative complications explored in the literature is incomplete, necessitating multicenter research to confirm these preliminary findings.

Based on prior research and our clinical experience, we hypothesized that integrating SNB with LA during DBS would improve surgical efficiency without compromising therapeutic outcomes, such as the accuracy of electrode implantation, and reduce the risk of postoperative complications, such as pulmonary infections, thereby contributing to better overall outcomes for DBS patients. To explore these possibilities, we conducted an observational cohort study to assess the impact of SNB on perioperative factors, including anesthesia management, surgical time indicators, and the effectiveness of electrode implantation, as well as postoperative complications in DBS patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study, approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (ID: 2024KY101, approval date: June 14, 2024), with a waiver of written informed consent. The study was also registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2400085670, registration date: June 14, 2024. This retrospective cohort study analyzed patient data collected between November 2, 2017, and April 4, 2024. URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/project/edit?pid=233651). This manuscript follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

This retrospective cohort study analyzed patient data collected between November 2, 2017, and April 4, 2024. The participants were adult PD patients (≥18 years) who underwent STN-DBS surgery. Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of idiopathic PD based on the United Kingdom Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank criteria; (2) presence of at least two cardinal motor symptoms (resting tremor, rigidity, or bradykinesia); (3) significant and sustained response to levodopa; (4) motor fluctuations or dyskinesias refractory to optimized medical therapy; and (5) ≥30% improvement in the MDS-UPDRS-III score in the medication-on state compared with the medication-off state.32,33 These criteria align with international guidelines for DBS candidacy.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) preoperative history of pulmonary infection or any chronic lung disease (eg., COPD, asthma, interstitial lung disease); (2) active infection at any other site within 2 weeks before surgery; (3) preoperative dysphagia (assessed by bedside swallow test or history); and (4) intraoperative severe complications that led to abortion of the procedure (eg., cardiac arrest, intractable epilepsy). These exclusion criteria were applied to minimize baseline pulmonary heterogeneity and confounding factors, thereby enabling a clearer assessment of the effect of anesthetic techniques on postoperative pulmonary outcomes. In addition, because the Implantable Pulse Generator (IPG) phase requires general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation, excluding patients with significant preoperative pulmonary disease also helped reduce perioperative respiratory risks in this retrospective cohort.

Preoperative Data Collection and Evaluation

We collected detailed clinical characteristics of the participants. These data included basic demographic information such as age, gender, height, and weight. Additionally, we recorded the participant’s medical history, with a focus on chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes. Hypertension was diagnosed based on three non-consecutive office blood pressure measurements ≥140/90 mmHg, or if the patient was receiving antihypertensive treatment. Diabetes was diagnosed if fasting blood glucose was ≥7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), random blood glucose was ≥11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL), or HbA1c was ≥6.5%.

For the evaluation of neurological status, we focused on the duration of Parkinson’s disease and the severity of motor dysfunction, assessed using the Hoehn and Yahr (H-Y) staging and MDS-UPDRS-III in both the medication-on (med-on) and medication-off (med-off) states. All data were collected from electronic medical records by two independent researchers, with cross-verification to ensure accuracy.

DBS Surgery

Anesthetic Techniques

Patients were retrospectively divided into two groups (LA alone vs. LA+SNB) according to the anesthetic technique documented in the anesthesia records. This grouping reflects real-world clinical practice evolution at our center rather than randomization: LA alone was the standard approach from 2017 to 2020; LA+SNB was gradually introduced and became the preferred technique from 2020 onwards based on preliminary observations of better patient tolerance. In the LA group, after sterilization and draping, surgeons administered scalp infiltration anesthesia (40 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine + 1% lidocaine). In the LA+SNB group, anesthesiologists performed bilateral nerve blocks at the supraorbital (0.5–1 mL), supratrochlear (0.5–1 mL), zygomaticotemporal (3–5 mL), and auriculotemporal (2–3 mL) nerves using 10 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine. After 10 minutes, sensory testing was performed to confirm the effectiveness of the block. If confirmed, surgery commenced, and additional scalp infiltration anesthesia (20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine + 1% lidocaine) was administered before the incision.

For the IPG phase, both groups received GA. At the end of the procedure, all anesthetic drugs were discontinued, and patients were transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for awakening. When the Aldrete score reached ≥9, patients were transferred to the surgical ward.

Electrode Implantation

During intracranial electrode implantation, microelectrode recordings (MERs) were used to determine the optimal placement of the electrodes. The microelectrodes were advanced towards the STN, and neuronal activity was recorded along the trajectory. The target was considered optimal if the recorded neuronal firing pattern was characteristic of the STN. If the firing pattern was atypical, the microelectrode was withdrawn, and the target was adjusted before re-inserting the microelectrode to achieve the best possible position. The puncture times of microelectrode insertions and MERs for both sides were recorded. Finally, the DBS electrode was implanted, and intraoperative microstimulation was applied to observe and confirm clinical improvement and side effects in the awake patient.10 The number of punctures was used as a procedural surrogate for intraoperative targeting effort.

MERs Recording and Quantitative Analysis

MERs were performed using the Stealth Station neuronavigation system (Medtronic Ltd., Minneapolis, MN, USA) and tungsten electrodes. The signals recorded were primarily local field potentials (LFP), using a bipolar configuration. Signals were acquired from contacts positioned 2 mm above the target STN border, starting from 10 mm above the target site and advancing in 1 mm steps until the dorsal STN, then 0.5 mm steps until the substantia nigra.34

The selected LFP signals were processed offline using MATLAB (version 2016b, MathWorks Corporation, Natick, MA, USA) and EEGLAB (v2020.0, https://sccn.ucsd.edu/eeglab). Initially, a band-pass filter (0.1–200 Hz) and a notch filter (to remove 50 Hz and its harmonics) were applied to eliminate interference, followed by down-sampling to 500 Hz and removing artifacts. The signals were then normalized using Z-scores, and beta oscillations (13–30 Hz) were extracted through further filtering. Power spectral density (PSD) of both the LFP signal and the filtered beta oscillations was calculated using the Welch method, employing a fast Fourier transform with a 1-second Hanning window and 0.5-second overlap to minimize edge effects. The ratio of beta oscillation PSD to LFP signal PSD denoted as PSDXb was then calculated.35

Postoperative Cranial and Chest CT Scans

Within 24 hours post-surgery, cranial and chest CT scans were performed. The CT examinations utilized a SOMATOM Definition DS CT scanner (Siemens Healthcare) or a Revolution CT scanner (GE Healthcare). For chest CT, the scan range extended from the clavicles to the lung base. The tube voltage alternated rapidly between 80 and 140 keV (0.25 ms per switch), with a tube current of 445 mA, a pitch of 0.992:1, a rotation speed of 0.5 seconds, a slice thickness of 0.625 mm, and a slice interval of 0.625 mm. For head CT, the Data were as follows: tube voltage of 120 kVp, tube current of 230 mA, the field of view (FOV) of 25.6 cm × 25.6 cm, matrix size of 512 × 512, slice thickness of 1.25 mm, and slice interval of 1.25 mm.

Pulmonary CT findings were interpreted together with clinical symptoms and laboratory evidence rather than used in isolation, because early postoperative imaging may also reflect atelectasis or other noninfectious changes. The volume of postoperative intracranial air was calculated by an experienced radiologist, who manually outlined and segmented the air volume on head CT images slice by slice to obtain the total air volume.36

Postoperative Outcomes

The primary outcome measured was the incidence of pulmonary infections within seven days post-surgery. Secondary outcomes included the occurrence of several key postoperative complications.

Postoperative complications: including infections, neurological complications, and electrolyte imbalances. Infections were diagnosed based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria (see https://www.cdc.gov/nhsn/psc/index.html) and included wound infections, pulmonary, bloodstream, urinary tract, or central nervous system infections. Specifically, pulmonary infections were diagnosed by the presence of radiological evidence (chest CT scan within 24 hours post-surgery) along with either clinical symptoms (fever >38°C, dyspnea, or purulent sputum) or laboratory evidence (leukocytosis >10×109/L).

Postoperative neurological complications (PONC): refer to neurological deficits arising from various causes after surgery, including but not limited to cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs), delirium, and postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD).

Local anesthesia complications: referring to local and systemic reactions caused by the administration or technique of local anesthesia, such as pain, swelling, and nerve injury at the injection site, as well as potential systemic toxic reactions, allergic reactions, or syncope.

Electrolyte imbalances: defined as disturbances in electrolyte homeostasis post-surgery due to fluid loss, surgical stress, intravenous fluids, blood transfusions, or underlying patient conditions. This included abnormal levels of sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, and magnesium. Overall complications were defined as the occurrence of at least one postoperative complication within 7 days after surgery; patients with multiple complications were counted once in the overall complication rate, whereas each complication type was recorded separately for component analyses. Postoperative complications were identified through a structured review of postoperative medical records, nursing notes, laboratory tests, imaging studies, microbiology results, and discharge summaries by two independent investigators. Additionally, the duration of hospitalization following DBS surgery was recorded. The 7-day observation window was chosen because the majority of anesthesia- and surgery-related pulmonary complications occur within this period; long-term outcomes were beyond the scope of this study.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were first assessed for distributional normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using independent samples Student’s t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed to explore factors associated with postoperative pulmonary infection, which was defined as the primary outcome. Variables demonstrating statistical significance in univariate analyses (P <0.05) were entered into the multivariate logistic regression model. Accordingly, these regression findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Potential confounding factors, including perioperative procedural variables, were considered through multivariable adjustment; however, unmeasured confounders and temporal bias related to the time-dependent adoption of SNB cannot be excluded. No formal stepwise variable selection procedure was used. Missing data were not imputed, and analyses were conducted using available-case data.

Sample size calculations were performed using PASS version 15.0 software (NCSS LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA). Based on our unpublished observations, the postoperative infection rate in the LA group was estimated to be approximately 30%, while in the LA+SNB group, it was around 10%. With a two-sided α error of 0.05, a total sample size of 118 patients would provide 80% power to detect a statistically significant difference between the two groups.

Because this study primarily focused on short-term perioperative outcomes, long-term postoperative efficacy and neurological outcomes were beyond the scope of the present analysis and should be evaluated in future prospective studies.

Logistic Regression for Postoperative Pulmonary Infection Risk

To further investigate the impact of different anesthesia methods on the risk of postoperative complications, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. In the univariate analysis, patients receiving SNB had a significantly lower risk of postoperative pulmonary infection (OR=0.262, 95% CI: 0.102–0.675, p=0.006). Additionally, longer duration of DBS (OR=1.011 per minute, 95% CI: 1.003–1.019, p=0.008) and longer duration of TE (OR=1.023 per minute, 95% CI: 1.006–1.039, p=0.007) were significantly associated with an increased risk of postoperative pulmonary infection. Variables with a p-value <0.05 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. In the multivariate analysis, after adjustment for surgery duration and TE duration, the association between SNB and postoperative pulmonary infection was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR=0.426, 95% CI: 0.138–1.315, p=0.138), whereas duration of TE remained an independent predictor of postoperative pulmonary infection (OR=1.026 per minute, 95% CI: 1.009–1.045, p=0.004) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study reveals that the integration of SNB with LA was associated with shorter STN-DBS surgery duration and a lower incidence of postoperative complications, particularly pulmonary infections and intracranial air accumulation. However, before interpreting these associations, a fundamental methodological limitation must be acknowledged: group allocation was entirely time-dependent, with the LA group treated between 2017 and 2020 and the LA+SNB group between 2020 and 2024. Improvements in surgical team experience, perioperative protocols, and infection control practices over this period may have independently contributed to the observed differences. Accordingly, the findings of this study reflect associations between variables, and causal inference cannot be drawn. Notably, in multivariate analysis, SNB was not an independent predictor of pulmonary infection, suggesting that its apparent univariate association may be mediated or confounded by other perioperative factors, particularly tracheal extubation time. This highlights the importance of optimizing mechanical ventilation management in reducing postoperative respiratory risk, and underscores that the relationship between SNB and pulmonary outcomes is likely indirect rather than direct.

The LA+SNB group exhibited a notably shorter average surgical duration, specifically a significantly shorter duration of intracranial electrode implantation (IEI duration: p < 0.001), compared to the LA group. This enhanced efficiency is likely attributable to improved pain control with SNB, which may minimize patient discomfort during electrode implantation, improve cooperation, and reduce the need for sedatives, thereby facilitating a smoother and more efficient procedure. Furthermore, SNB was associated with reduced duration in the PACU (p = 0.043), likely due to its effective analgesic effect in blocking nociceptive input and minimizing the physiological stress response.25 These effects, in combination with reduced reliance on opioids in the SNB group, may contribute to faster recovery and fewer opioid-related side effects, such as respiratory depression and sedation.37,38 Moreover, the LA+SNB group exhibited lower rates of postoperative pulmonary infections (11.3% vs. 32.0%, p = 0.005) and systemic inflammation, as evidenced by reduced CRP levels (15.5% vs. 34.0%, p = 0.017), which further supports a smoother and quicker recovery process.28,38 Finally, the shorter surgical time in the LA+SNB group decreased overall anesthesia exposure (although the duration of GA did not differ significantly between groups, the reduced total operative time likely lessens anesthesia-related stress), facilitating a faster return to baseline physiological status and earlier achievement of PACU discharge criteria.31,36 The shorter intracranial electrode implantation duration in the LA+SNB group is consistent with the well-established analgesic benefits of SNB in cranial surgery. Xie et al39 recently demonstrated that scalp block, compared with traditional local infiltration, was associated with shorter pre-acquisition time, longer stable recording periods, and a higher number of neurons recorded per task during awake DBS, reflecting improved patient cooperation and electrophysiological stability. Krauss et al40 similarly reported better hemodynamic stability and reduced need for antihypertensive medications with scalp block in DBS surgery. In addition, a recent network meta-analysis found that scalp nerve block with ropivacaine reduced postoperative pain scores and opioid consumption after craniotomy.27

Precision is crucial for successful therapeutic outcomes in STN-DBS surgery. Our study demonstrates that the use of SNB does not compromise the precision of electrode implantation. Although not statistically significant, a slight reduction in the number of electrode punctures with SNB was observed. This may be attributed to enhanced analgesia and better patient cooperation, as well as preserved quality of intraoperative MERs.36 The LA+SNB group also showed lower intracranial air volumes (p = 0.028). Intracranial air accumulation is a recognized factor that may affect DBS targeting accuracy and contribute to operative morbidity.32 It is plausible that shorter operative duration and better-controlled intraoperative conditions in the LA+SNB group contributed to reduced cerebrospinal fluid loss and air entry, thereby limiting intracranial air volume.29,32 However, since no direct metric of electrode placement accuracy was measured, inferences regarding improved surgical precision should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

SNB was also associated with a significant reduction in postoperative complications, particularly pulmonary infections. The incidence of postoperative pulmonary infection in the LA+SNB group was 11.3%, significantly lower than the 32.0% observed in the LA group (p = 0.005). Additionally, the LA+SNB group showed lower rates of elevated CRP (15.5% vs. 34.0%, p = 0.017). By reducing reliance on opioids, SNB may mitigate opioid-induced respiratory depression, a known contributor to pulmonary complications. The shorter surgery duration and reduced PACU stay may further improve postoperative immune function and lower respiratory risk.37,38 Moreover, the total amount of local anesthetics used in the LA+SNB group was approximately 50% less than in the LA group, which may help reduce the risk of systemic toxicity in elderly PD patients with multiple comorbidities.41–44

Early postoperative CT findings obtained within 24 hours of surgery may not exclusively represent true infectious infiltrates. Postoperative atelectasis is common following general anesthesia and can produce radiological opacities that are difficult to distinguish from early pneumonia; in severe cases, atelectasis may progress to or predispose patients to pneumonia.17 To reduce the risk of misclassification, we applied a composite diagnostic criterion requiring radiological evidence plus either clinical symptoms or laboratory abnormalities, rather than relying on imaging alone. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that even this approach cannot completely eliminate the possibility of diagnostic overlap in the early postoperative period, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the observed difference in pulmonary infection rates between groups. The lower incidence of CRP elevation in the LA+SNB group (15.5% vs. 34.0%, p = 0.017) provides additional supportive evidence of a genuine difference in inflammatory response, although CRP remains a nonspecific marker.

It is important to note that both groups received general anesthesia for IPG implantation. Therefore, outcomes such as tracheal extubation time and postoperative pulmonary complications are heavily influenced by GA management and perioperative respiratory care. A physiologically coherent interpretation is that SNB may improve conditions during the awake phase, indirectly contributing to smoother emergence and shorter extubation time.13 This aligns with the multivariate finding that in the multivariate logistic regression, only tracheal extubation time remained an independent predictor of postoperative pulmonary infection (OR = 1.026, 95% CI: 1.009–1.045, p = 0.004), while SNB and other variables did not reach statistical significance.

When compared with other anesthesia techniques, the LA+SNB strategy may offer perioperative advantages particularly relevant for elderly and frail PD patients. These observations are consistent with prior reports on scalp block in cranial procedures.29,41–43 SNB was not associated with significant differences in postoperative neurological complications or electrolyte imbalances. Neurological complications in this cohort, primarily hallucinations and behavioral abnormalities, are mainly related to peri-electrode brain edema.45 As SNB cannot influence the degree of brain edema, its impact on neurological complications is inherently limited.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective observational study, it is subject to selection and information biases. Additionally, the allocation to LA versus LA+SNB followed a time-dependent pattern (LA mainly 2017–2020, LA+SNB 2020–2024), introducing potential temporal confounding from improvements in surgical experience, perioperative care, and infection control practices. Second, the relatively small sample size may limit generalizability and statistical power for rare events. Third, the two groups differed in total local anesthetic dose and concentration, which may confound the specific contribution of SNB. Fourth, multivariate analysis included variables based on univariate significance, potentially excluding some clinically relevant confounders. Fifth, only short-term outcomes were evaluated. Finally, as an observational study, our findings demonstrate associations rather than causality and should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these associations.

In conclusion, LA+SNB was associated with improved intraoperative and immediate postoperative outcomes in STN-DBS surgery, including shorter operative time, reduced PACU stay, lower rates of pulmonary infection, and decreased intracranial air volume. However, in multivariate logistic regression, only tracheal extubation time (not SNB) remained an independent predictor of pulmonary infection. Given the above limitations, the findings of this study primarily suggest potential associations and await confirmation in prospective studies. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to confirm the role of SNB in DBS anesthesia management.
