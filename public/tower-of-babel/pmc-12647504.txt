The Impact of Intravesical Instillations on Quality of Life in Patients with Non-Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer: A Systematic Review
Authors: Ingolf Griebsch, Sunil Shrestha, Yair Lotan, Chi-Fai Ng, Andrew Bottomley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) is commonly treated with transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) followed by intravesical chemotherapy and/or Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) instillations. While these treatments aim to prevent recurrence and progression, the frequency of their administration and associated side effects can substantially impact patients’ quality of life (QoL).ObjectivesWe aimed to systematically review the impact of intravesical BCG and chemotherapy instillations on QoL in patients with NMIBC, with a specific focus on how instillation frequency influences treatment adherence and withdrawal rates.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Over 750 abstracts were independently screened by two independent reviewers. Studies published between 2005 and 2024 were included. Data were extracted on demographics, treatment protocols, QoL outcomes, and adherence rates. Risk of bias assessments were undertaken to assess the quality of the studies.ResultsA total of 17 studies were selected, comprising 6 qualitative, 8 quantitative, and 3 mixed-methods designs, totaling 20,045 patients. Frequent instillations were associated with increased urinary symptoms (e.g., urgency and dysuria), fatigue, and emotional distress. Withdrawal rates were high (up to 90%) in intensive schedules, particularly during the first year, due to treatment burden and side effects. Studies demonstrated that reduced instillation frequency could preserve treatment efficacy while improving QoL and adherence.ConclusionsFrequent intravesical instillations can significantly impair QoL, particularly among older patients. While emerging therapies and supportive strategies may help alleviate treatment burden, clinical decision-making must carefully balance QoL with oncologic outcomes. Future research should prioritize personalized approaches that integrate both clinical efficacy and patient-reported outcomes (PROs).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-025-00375-x.Keywords: Non-muscle invasive bladder cancer, Chemotherapy, BCG, Quality of life

Key Summary Points

Non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) treatments using Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) and chemotherapy instillations can significantly affect patients’ quality of life (QoL), particularly owing to their frequency and side effects.This systematic review examined studies from 2005 to 2024 to evaluate the QoL impact and treatment adherence linked to intravesical instillation schedules.Data from 17 studies involving > 20,000 patients revealed that frequent instillations often lead to urinary symptoms, fatigue, emotional distress, and high withdrawal rates.Evidence suggests that reducing instillation frequency may preserve treatment effectiveness while improving both QoL and adherence.Less intensive treatment regimens show promises for reducing patient burden and enhancing overall outcomes in NMIBC care.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the tenth most commonly diagnosed cancer globally and the thirteenth leading cause of the cancer-related mortality worldwide, with over 570,000 new cases and 210,000 deaths reported annually [1, 2]. Approximately 75% of patients present with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), which accounts for the majority of newly diagnosed bladder tumors [3, 4]. NMIBC is confined to the bladder mucosa and/or submucosa without invasion of the muscularis propria, allowing for localized treatment strategies that minimize systemic exposure and systemic toxicity [5, 6].

NMIBC is further subclassified on the basis of tumor stage [7], including papillary non-invasive tumors (Ta), tumors invading the lamina propria (T1), and flat high-grade lesions confined to the urothelium, known as carcinoma in situ (CIS). Although limited to the superficial bladder layers, NMIBC carries a substantial risk of recurrence, with reported 1-year recurrence rates ranging from 15% to 61% and 5-year rates from 31% to 78% [8]. These risks necessitate intensive intravesical therapy and long-term cystoscopic surveillance. The cornerstone of NMIBC management is the transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT), which is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Depending on risk stratification, TURBT is followed by intravesical therapy [9, 10]. High-risk NMIBC, as defined by the American Urological Association (AUA) and European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, includes high-grade Ta tumors > 3 cm, multifocal high-grade Ta, T1 tumors, CIS, prior intravesical therapy failure, and certain variant histologies. These patients are typically treated with intravesical Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG), which remains the most effective treatment for reducing both the risk of recurrence and progression. For low-risk cases (solitary LG Ta tumors < 3 cm), TURBT is followed by a single immediate instillation of intravesical chemotherapy, such as mitomycin C (MMC) or gemcitabine, to reduce early recurrence [11]. For intermediate-risk patients, which include larger, recurrent or multiple Low Grade (LG) Ta and solitary (< 3 cm) High Grade (HG) Ta, the typical recommendation is induction and maintenance intravesical chemotherapy. In contrast, for high-risk NMIBC, particularly with CIS that cannot be fully resected, induction and maintenance BCG therapy is the gold standard for reducing the risk of recurrence and progression [12].

BCG stimulates an immune response in the bladder, reducing the likelihood of recurrence and progression. Due to ongoing BCG shortages, chemotherapy agents such as gemcitabine and combination regimens (e.g., gemcitabine plus docetaxel) are being explored as alternatives [13, 14]. A number of novel therapies have also recently been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in patients with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC or are currently being investigated in phase 3 trials, both in BCG-naïve as well as BCG-unresponsive NMIBC [15]. Despite BCG’s efficacy, it is associated with significant toxicity, particularly during prolonged maintenance regimens [16]. Adverse effects include local urinary symptoms such as dysuria, frequency, hematuria, and urgency, as well as systemic symptoms such as malaise, fatigue, and flu-like illness. These symptoms often lead to treatment delays or discontinuation, especially in older patients and those with comorbidities [16–19]. The high frequency and long duration of BCG therapy place a considerable burden on patients and significantly impact quality of life (QoL), treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes [20]. To reduce treatment burden, several mitigation strategies have been explored, including reduced-dose BCG and antibiotic prophylaxis to lower toxicity and improve adherence [21–23]. Recent trials have also raised important considerations around adherence and de-escalation strategies. For example, prospective studies such as the recently published Crest trial have shown that treatment adherence may be higher in structured trial settings than in routine clinical practice [24].

It is also important to distinguish between BCG-naïve and BCG-unresponsive NMIBC. This review focuses on the BCG-naïve setting, where BCG remains the standard of care. In contrast, BCG-unresponsive patients with NMIBC may be treated with radical cystectomy [25–27]or, if unfit or unwilling, with alternative agents. Recently, the FDA has approved intravesical agents such as nadofaragene firadenovec and recombinant interleukin (IL)-15 super agonist N-803, as well as systemic therapies including pembrolizumab and valrubicin for this population. Additionally, the off-label use of chemotherapeutic agents is common in clinical practice as a bladder-sparing approach for these patients [27].

Overall, the intensity and duration of intravesical therapy must be weighed against its impact on QoL and adherence. This is particularly relevant for older or frail patients, for whom treatment side effects may outweigh clinical benefit. Increasing attention is now being paid to patient-reported outcomes (PROs), personalized therapy, and treatment strategies that balance efficacy with tolerability.

Objectives

Given the substantial burden associated with intravesical therapy, particularly BCG, this systematic review aims to assess the impact of treatment frequency and regimen intensity on QoL in patients with NMIBC. Specifically, it evaluates how different intravesical regimens affect PROs, treatment adherence, and withdrawal rates. By synthesizing evidence from both real-world studies and prospective trials, this review aims to support more patient-centered and individualized treatment strategies in NMIBC management.

Methods

Design

A systematic review was conducted following the principles outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [28]. It is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement (Appendix S1). The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (reg. no. CRD420251002227). This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

A comprehensive search was performed across multiple databases, including MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Scopus, CINHAL, APA PsycINFO, EMBASE, and Web of Science (WoS). The search included studies published between 2005 and 2024 that addressed QoL, PROs, and adherence related to BCG or chemotherapy instillations in patients with NMIBC. Studies included both BCG-naïve and BCG-exposed populations, encompassing patients who were intolerant, unresponsive, or had recurrent disease following prior BCG therapy.

Eligibility criteria were established to guide study selection. Inclusion criteria required studies to be published in English, involve patients with NMIBC receiving intravesical therapy, and report QoL outcomes, adherence data, or patient experiences. Studies focusing exclusively on muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) or lacking relevant QoL data were excluded.

The study selection process occurred in three stages. First, two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to exclude studies that were irrelevant. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Full-text articles were then reviewed for eligibility, with a consensus reached on the final inclusion of studies. The PRISMA flowchart (Appendix 1) provides a detailed overview of the study identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion process.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was conducted using a standardized data extraction form. The information collected from each study included the following key aspects:

Study demographics (authors, year of publication, and country of origin)

Study demographics (authors, year of publication, and country of origin)

Sample size and characteristics of the participants, including details on the intervention and control groups

Sample size and characteristics of the participants, including details on the intervention and control groups

Treatment protocols, which involve the type of intervention, frequency, and duration

Treatment protocols, which involve the type of intervention, frequency, and duration

Quality of life (QoL) measures, including but not limited to urinary symptoms, fatigue, and emotional distress

Quality of life (QoL) measures, including but not limited to urinary symptoms, fatigue, and emotional distress

The data extraction process was initially carried out independently by author S.S. for all the included studies. Following this, the extracted data underwent a second review and verification by author A.B. to ensure accuracy and consistency. In the event of discrepancies or disagreements in the extracted information, these were addressed and resolved through discussion among the authors to reach a consensus. Additionally, to ensure the robustness of the data extraction process, author I.G. was involved in reviewing and resolving any conflicts or remaining issues. This multi-step review process helped to maintain the integrity and reliability of the data extracted for the systematic review.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the studies included was evaluated by S.S. and A.B. To assess the risk of bias (ROB), we employed suitable tools for both qualitative and quantitative studies. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB 2.0) tool was used [29]. Mixed methods and qualitative studies were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist. For quantitative studies, including cross-sectional and cohort studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was applied [30].

Results

Identification and Selection of Studies

The PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) provides an overview of the literature search process. After removing duplicates using Covidence software, the search across various databases yielded 1317 articles. A manual search of relevant review articles and reference lists identified an additional article. The remaining articles underwent title and abstract screening, excluding 238 articles deemed irrelevant to the research questions. This left 493 articles for full-text review to assess eligibility. Of these, 238 articles were excluded for the reasons outlined in Fig. 1. Ultimately, 17 studies were included in the systematic review for qualitative synthesis.

Overview of Included Studies

Table 1 presents a comprehensive overview of the 17 included studies [17, 31–46], which represent a diverse geographical distribution, including Europe, Asia, and North America. The studies employed various designs, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, and mixed-method approaches. A total of 20,045 patients were recruited in the 17 included studies. Individual study sample sizes varied significantly, ranging from 6 participants in qualitative investigations [32] to 17,774 in large nationwide cohort studies [33]. The participants’ ages ranged widely, with mean values primarily between 63 and 74 years across studies. Most studies reported mean ages of around 70 years, with some indicating a median age of approximately 70 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 63–80). The age range spanned from 37 years to 94 years. A subset of studies categorized participants, showing that the majority were aged ≥ 70 years, while smaller proportions fell below this age threshold. The majority of participants across studies were male, with representation ranging from 55% in one study [37] to 90.1% in another study. Some studies reported a predominantly male cohort (e.g., ≥ 86% male) [35, 41], while others included more limited proportions of female participants, ranging from 9.9% to 24% [33, 41]. Although the lower end of this range reflects underrepresentation, proportions around 20–25% are consistent with the known epidemiology of NMIBC. In one study, all participants were male [39].

Treatment Modalities and Study Focus

The studies primarily focused on intravesical therapies, with the majority emphasizing BCG treatment. Although some included chemotherapy instillations, BCG remained the central therapy. For BCG, the treatment protocol commonly involved a 6-week induction phase followed by a maintenance phase. Maintenance therapy schedules and adherence were key areas of comparison, with limited studies [42, 43] focusing on optimizing treatment schedules to balance efficacy and tolerability. Maintenance schedules varied, ranging from tri-weekly to monthly instillations, typically lasting between 1 year and 3 years, depending on the protocol used. The protocols generally adhered to international guidelines such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), AUA, and EAU [27, 47, 48]. In addition to BCG, chemotherapy agents such as mitomycin C (MMC) and gemcitabine were also used in certain cases. A single instillation of MMC is commonly administered immediately after TURBT to reduce recurrence risk. Alternative intravesical chemotherapy regimens, including gemcitabine, mitomycin, or a combination of gemcitabine and docetaxel, are being investigated as potential replacements for BCG, particularly in response to BCG shortages or contraindications. Notably, the combination of gemcitabine and docetaxel is currently being studied in the BRIDGE trial (reg. no. ECOG-ACRIN EA8212), a phase 3 randomized trial evaluating its effectiveness compared with BCG in BCG-naïve patients with high-grade NMIBC [11].

Several studies also described reduced-dose regimens or alternative strategies during BCG shortages, which influenced treatment administration.

Frequency and Duration

The induction phase generally involved weekly BCG instillations for 6–8 weeks, aimed at eradicating cancerous cells and priming the bladder for maintenance. After induction, patients typically underwent maintenance therapy, which often consisted of three weekly instillations at 3, 6, and 12 months. This schedule could vary on the basis of protocol. For example, some involved 3-weekly treatments during the first year, and then every 6 months in subsequent years [46]. Limited studies also indicated that some patients experienced reduced maintenance frequency owing to toxicity or patient preference [34]. A reduced-frequency arm (e.g., 9 instillations instead of 15) was sometimes explored to assess the efficacy versus standard protocols [43].

Delivery Method

Treatment was predominantly delivered in outpatient clinic settings, often managed by nurses. Specialist Urology Nurses or Clinical Nurse Specialists typically oversaw instillation procedures and follow-up care [31]. During BCG instillations, patients were generally instructed to retain the bladder solution for 30 min to 2 h to ensure sufficient mucosal exposure. One study mentioned inpatient care in certain cases [44], particularly for initial cycles or patients with complex comorbidities.

Outcomes

Quality of Life and Patient-Centered Outcomes

QoL was assessed in multiple studies using validated tools such as the EORTC QLQ-C30 [35–37, 39, 43, 45], EORTC QLQ-NMIBC24 [37], and FACT-BL [39]. Key domains included physical functioning, emotional burden, treatment inconvenience, and symptoms scales. In studies, Gontero et al. and Koga et al. examined urinary symptoms, fatigue, and emotional distress, showing how these factors influenced patients’ patient-reported QoL outcomes [35, 36]. Frequent hospital visits, logistical challenges, and time commitment were also common complaints [40]. In one study, 60% of patients who discontinued treatment cited persistent mild discomfort affecting social life, and toxicity severity did not always correlate with treatment efficacy [41].

Adherence and Treatment Experiences

Observational studies such as Alcorn et al. [31] and qualitative investigations such as Alcorn et al. [32] and Vo et al. [44] explored patient experiences, adherence patterns, and treatment-related challenges. Alcorn et al. reported a 90% BCG withdrawal rate, with many patients discontinuing owing to toxicity. The most frequently reported adverse effects included urinary frequency (39%), pain (31%), and cystitis (28%), as well as catheterization difficulties, which collectively contributed to high discontinuation rates [31]. These findings underscore patients’ physical challenges, which may contribute to dissatisfaction and nonadherence. Alcorn et al. found that patients aged ≥ 70 years were significantly more likely to discontinue BCG early (log-rank 17.34, p = 0.001), with 46% withdrawing within 1 year. Side effects were also significantly associated with early withdrawal (log-rank 7.27, p = 0.007) [31]. These findings emphasize the need for age-specific support strategies to help older adults tolerate therapy. Factors such as travel, wait times, and healthcare access also influenced adherence [40]. Garg et al. noted that rural patients [34] reported long commutes and urologist shortages, leading to care delays and reduced treatment confidence. BCG shortages further complicated treatment delivery, with some patients receiving alternative agents or reduced dosages [34].

Survival and Recurrence Outcomes

Several studies assessed recurrence-free survival (RFS), progression-free survival (PFS), cancer-specific survival (CSS), radical cystectomy-free survival (RCFS), and overall survival (OS) [36, 42, 43]. Subiela et al. showed that BCG-unresponsive patients (inadequate response within 6 months of BCG therapy) had worse oncological outcomes: lower PFS (hazard ratio [HR] = 3.40, p < 0.001), lower CSS (HR = 4.42, p < 0.001), lower RCFS (HR = 3.52, p < 0.001), and no significant OS difference (p = 0.28) [42].

Koga et al. found improved RFS rate in the maintenance group (95.8% versus 74.1%), although not statistically significant (p = 0.078) [36]. The NIMBUS trial demonstrated that reduced-frequency BCG (with fewer instillations) was associated with significantly higher recurrence (42%) compared with standard-frequency arm (27%) at 1 year (HR = 1.95, p = 0.02) [43]. These results highlight potential risks in modifying standard regimens.

QoL Impact of Frequent Instillations

Urinary Symptoms

Urinary Symptoms

Urinary symptoms were commonly reported side effects across multiple studies. Gontero et al. found persistent urinary symptoms despite treatment. BCG instillations, in particular, were frequently associated with immune-mediated bladder wall irritation, leading to detrusor overactivity, cystitis, and symptoms such as urgency, frequency, and nocturia [33, 34, 39, 46]. Miyake et al. specifically noted that increased nocturia and urgency negatively affected sleep quality [39].

These symptoms significantly impacted patients’ QoL, often disrupting daily activities and emotional well-being. One study found that patients undergoing frequent or repeated intravesical instillations such as those receiving maintenance therapy or multiple TURBTs followed by further instillations experienced exacerbated urinary symptoms, leading to a fourfold increase in the use of bladder-relaxing medications and antibiotics [33].

Repeated treatments were associated with cumulative bladder irritation and chronic discomfort. One participant highlighted this burden: “If people heard my story, all they would hear is cancer, but what I went through—the chronic pain—was much worse.” Both BCG and intravesical chemotherapy (e.g., MMC) were linked to urinary frequency, urgency, and bladder discomfort, which patients described as distressing and disruptive to their routines [33, 44]. Additionally, some patients experienced technical and emotional challenges related to self-catheterization, which was sometimes required owing to urinary retention or complications following BCG treatment, not for BCG administration itself, which is typically performed by clinical staff [32]. One individual shared, “I had a problem just putting the catheter in. I have a problem with not relaxing, you know, and that’s why it doesn’t go into whatever… yes it makes you a little bit sore down there, but it just makes you a bit depressed. It’s hard to explain really” [32]. Common side effects reported by patients using self-catheterization included urinary frequency (39%), cystitis (28%), generalized pain (31%), and catheterization difficulties (20%) [31]. While not directly linked to the frequency of intravesical instillations, these experiences highlight the broader physical and psychological burdens that can accompany bladder cancer treatment, especially in those who develop complications requiring catheter use.

Fatigue

Fatigue

Severe fatigue was a prevalent side effect, affecting 52–55% of patients undergoing frequent BCG therapy [38, 46]. This fatigue was largely attributed to the combined physical strain of the treatment and its disruption of daily activities. Notably, patients undergoing maintenance therapy reported a further increase in fatigue complaints [46]. Across studies, fatigue has remained a consistent concern, with Gontero et al. documenting treatment-related fatigue [35], while Kuperus et al. identified it as a common side effect [38].

Emotional Distress

Emotional Distress

BCG instillations significantly affect the emotional well-being of patients with NMIBC, contributing to anxiety, depression, and emotional distress, especially during the maintenance phase. The repetitive nature of BCG therapy amplifies these psychological challenges [32, 34, 44, 46].

Alcorn et al. highlighted that the emotional strain of BCG treatments led to patients feeling disconnected from their bodies: “Mentally I thought, if this is what it does, I don’t think my body would take it.” This reflects the psychological toll of the treatment’s side effects [32]. Vo et al. further noted that many patients struggled with the emotional burden during the more frequent maintenance phases, with one participant saying, “It was like being stuck in a constant state of worry, unsure if the treatment would even work” [44]. Fear of death, tied to the treatment itself, was also evident. One participant expressed, “Oh dear, what if I should have them [BCG instillations], I might die from what happens from them; if I don’t have them, I might die…” [34]. This illustrates the heightened emotional distress caused by uncertainty regarding treatment outcomes.

Treatment Adherence and Withdrawal

Withdrawal Rates

Withdrawal Rates

One study found a high withdrawal rate of 90%, with only 10% of patients completing the full 3-year BCG treatment protocol [31]. Withdrawal was the most common during the induction phase, when 39% of patients stopped treatment within the first 42 days, a period marked by weekly instillations. Additionally, 46% of patients discontinued treatment within the first year [31].

The main reasons for withdrawal were treatment-related side effects, which were reported in 67% of those who discontinued. The most common side effects included urinary frequency (39%), cystitis (28%), and generalized pain (31%). Patients who experienced side effects were 5.44 times more likely to withdraw from treatment.

Older age was another significant factor, as patients aged 70 years or older were less likely to adhere to the treatment regimen compared with younger patients [31].

Influencing Factors

Influencing Factors

Patients who experienced severe side effects, such as persistent urinary symptoms, fatigue, and emotional distress, were more likely to discontinue intravesical therapy. Other contributing factors included fear of side effects, limited knowledge about their disease, lack of family or social support, and unrealistic expectations about treatment outcomes. Younger patients (ages 40–59 years) reported the highest levels of distress, often due to work-related challenges and caregiving responsibilities, although this was not directly linked to treatment discontinuation [46].

Emotional support and clear communication from healthcare providers were critical in promoting adherence. Some patients preferred written information over verbal explanations from nurses, which in certain cases was associated with earlier withdrawal from therapy. Patients frequently expressed concerns about the effectiveness of the treatment and its side effects. For example, one patient asked, “I could not retain the bladder instillation for 60 min; does this mean that the cancer will come back?” [46]. This emphasizes the importance of addressing patients’ fears and providing clear, reassuring communication throughout the treatment process.

Benefits of Reduced Frequency

Efficacy and QoL

Efficacy and QoL

Initial hypothesis suggested that reducing the frequency of BCG instillations could preserve efficacy while improving patients’ QoL by decreasing treatment burden and side effects [36]. However, the phase III NIMBUS trial, a key study in this area demonstrated that a reduced schedule of BCG instillations (standard dose but fewer administrations) resulted in significantly higher recurrence rates compared with the standard schedule [43]. The trial was halted early after interim analysis showed unacceptable inferiority in recurrence-free survival in the reduced-frequency arm [49].

Although reduced-frequency regimens were associated with fewer adverse events such as hematuria, dysuria, and fatigue, this did not consistently translate into improved overall QoL. In one study, patients reported lower symptom burden and emotional distress, with improved scores on specific QoL measures (e.g., EORTC QLQ-C30). However, other trials found no significant differences in mean QoL scores between treatment arms over time. For example, one study noted a lower incidence of urgency and general malaise in the reduced-frequency group at specific time points, but these findings did not result in a statistically significant improvement in overall QoL.

Patient preferences

Patient preferences

One study highlighted strong patient preferences for alternative intravesical therapy delivery, with 72% open to in-home therapy [40]. Many patients felt that it would be less disruptive (54%) and reduce anxiety (27%) [40]. However, there were also 11% of patients who expressed safety concerns, and 37% preferred the reassurance of clinic-based treatments [40].

Patients also emphasized the importance of clear communication regarding treatment expectations and potential side effects [44]. Peer support was also valued, especially for gender-specific needs, including open discussions on sexual function [44].

Results of the Quality Assessment of Included Studies

Qualitative Studies (CASP Analysis)

The four studies [32, 34, 37, 44] demonstrated strong methodology, with clear aims, appropriate qualitative approaches, and ethical rigor. They provided valuable insights into patient experiences and unmet needs in bladder cancer care [34, 44]. However, limitations included small, homogenous samples, lack of researcher reflexivity, and missing discussions on data saturation, affecting generalizability [32, 37].

Quantitative Studies

Randomized Control Trials (ROB 2.0)

The three RCT studies [35, 36, 43] had a low risk of bias overall, with robust methodologies. Koga et al. [36] and Straten et al. maintained low risk in most domains, although Straten et al. faced potential bias from a lack of blinding [43]. Gontero et al. had similar strengths but risked detection bias owing to unblinded outcome assessors [35].

Observational Studies (NOS Analysis)

Cross-sectional studies exhibited moderate to high bias, primarily due to small sample sizes and inadequate control of confounders. Scores ranged from 4/9 to 7/9, with only one study achieving high quality [31, 38–40, 46]. Cohort studies varied in quality [17, 33, 41, 42, 45]. Wang et al. scored 9/9, while others had moderate bias due to incomplete follow-up or confounding issues [45]. A supplementary file provides an overview of all the risk-of-bias scores assessed using various tools.

Discussion

This systematic review highlights the significant impact that the frequency and intensity of intravesical instillation, particularly BCG, have on the QoL of patients with NMIBC. While intensive BCG regimens are effective in reducing recurrence and progression, they often result in substantial side effects such as urinary symptoms, fatigue, and emotional distress, which were consistently reported across the included studies [36, 43]. These findings align with previous studies that emphasize the treatment burden associated with frequent and prolonged BCG administration [27, 32]. Currently, international guidelines continue to recommend intravesical BCG as the standard of care for high-risk NMIBC, primarily owing to its superior oncological efficacy compared with chemotherapy [7, 27, 47, 48, 50]. However, this benefit comes with significant toxicity and the challenge of prolonged maintenance regimens, both of which compromise QoL and treatment adherence [27, 48].

In BCG-naïve patients, standard regimens include induction followed by maintenance for up to 3 years [16]. In contrast, patients who are unresponsive to BCG, defined by persistent or recurrent disease following adequate BCG exposure, tend to experience poorer survival outcomes compared with patients who respond to BCG or those with lower-risk disease and are typically candidates for alternative strategies [42]. Although maintenance therapy prolongs recurrence-free survival, the long-term implications on QoL necessitates a careful balance between efficacy and tolerability [12, 36, 38]. Prolonged BCG maintenance is particularly challenging for many patients. Fatigue, urinary symptoms, and emotional distress are frequently reported, leading to treatment delays or discontinuation, especially among older patients or those with other responsibilities [32, 38, 43, 44]. Several studies have documented that high rates of treatment withdrawal over time, particularly among older patients and those with work and caregiving responsibilities, necessitate tailored interventions to enhance adherence [32, 46]. In the recently published CREST study (not included in this review owing to its 2025 publication date), which evaluated the addition of sasanlimab to BCG, adherence was significantly higher in the standard-frequency arm, with 74% of patients completing the full 3-year regimen [24]. In contrast, 1-year disease-free survival (DFS) was 71% in the standard arm compared with 59% in the reduced-frequency arm, underscoring the potential oncologic risks of de-escalation without sufficient evidence or patient stratification [24]. These findings align with those from the NIMBUS trial, which was halted early after interim analysis showed a significantly higher recurrence rate in the reduced-frequency arm (HR: 1.96, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.22–3.14, p = 0.0049) [43]. Together, these data reinforce that lowering BCG frequency without appropriate patient selection or additional therapeutic support may compromise treatment efficacy and should be approached with caution.

Several mitigation strategies have been investigated to reduce treatment burden without compromising oncologic outcomes. Reduced-dose BCG is one such approach. The EORTC 30962 trial found that one-third dose BCG preserved efficacy in many patients, particularly in intermediate-risk groups, and led to fewer adverse events [23]. Other strategies include antibiotic prophylaxis. Randomized trials using fluoroquinolones such as ofloxacin and prulifloxacin have shown significant reductions in BCG-associated toxicity and improved patient tolerance [21, 22]. These approaches are now being adopted in clinical practice and may offer practical pathways to improving adherence and QoL.

The psychological burden of intravesical therapy is another critical factor. Prolonged treatment can lead to emotional distress, anxiety, and diminished well-being. Integrating psychosocial support into routine care such as mental health referrals, counseling, or peer support can reduce these burdens and promote adherence [27, 32, 38, 43, 46–48].

Emerging therapies with less intensive schedules may further improve tolerability. Nadofaragene firadenovec and pembrolizumab have both been approved for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC [27]. In the KEYNOTE-057 trial, pembrolizumab demonstrated a 41% complete response rate at 3 months with durable responses in a subset of patients, leading to its FDA approval [51]. Similarly, the SWOG S1605 trial evaluated atezolizumab in a similar patient population and included detailed PRO data, providing insight into both tolerability and lived patient experience [52]. These trials reflect a broader shift toward patient-centered drug development. Initiatives such as the FDA’s Project Patient Voice and recent regulatory guidance on the inclusion of PROs in labeling demonstrate growing institutional support for integrating QoL measures into trial design and drug approval processes. These frameworks can support the development of treatments that improve not only survival but also daily functioning and emotional well-being [53, 54].

As NMIBC management evolves, future research must adopt study designs that prioritize QoL assessment alongside efficacy outcomes. This may include using co-primary or key secondary endpoints focused on patient experience, especially in maintenance trials or de-escalation studies. For older or frail patients, treatment strategies should be individualized and may include reduced-dose maintenance, use of emerging agents, or consideration of early cystectomy in selected high-risk cases.

Adaptive trial designs and biomarker-stratified approaches, such as those explored in CREST and other ongoing studies [24], offer promising avenues for matching treatment intensity to individual risk profiles. By integrating both clinical outcomes and patient-reported data, as seen in trials such as KEYNOTE-057 [51] and SWOG S1605 [52], NMIBC treatment can become more personalized, effective, and tolerable.

Implications

The findings of this study have significant implications for clinical practice and healthcare systems. Clinicians should incorporate QoL considerations into treatment decision-making, especially for patients with frailty, advanced age, or comorbidities. Where appropriate, reduced- dose frequency regimens, alternative schedules, or adjunctive strategies should be considered to preserve efficacy while minimizing harm. In addition, routine QoL assessments should be integrated into NMIBC management to identify patients at risk of poor adherence or intolerance.

From a policy perspective, investment in psychosocial services, patient navigation programs, and provider education can improve the delivery of intravesical therapies [55]. Health systems should also facilitate equitable access to innovation, including novel agents and modified BCG protocols, and support regulatory frameworks that prioritize patient-centered outcomes [56, 57]. By embedding these principles into NMIBC care can enhance both survival and QoL.

Strengths

A major strength of this systematic review is its comprehensive approach, combing clinical trial outcomes with patient-centered data to provide an in-depth evaluation. This dual perspective enables a comprehensive understanding of the balance between efficacy and tolerability. The review also rigorously evaluates the methodological quality of included studies, providing a balanced assessment of the evidence. By synthesizing PROs alongside clinical endpoints, this review encourages the development of more adaptive and individualized treatment strategies for NMIBC.

Limitations of This Study

Despite these strengths, this review has limitations. The included studies display heterogeneity in design, sample size, and QoL assessment tools, which may limit generalizability. Several quantitative studies lacked robust control groups or adequate adjustments for confounders, which affected the reliability of the results. Nonetheless, most RCTs demonstrated strong methodologies and low risk of bias [35, 36, 43]. Qualitative studies were generally rigorous but were limited by sample sizes and lack of diversity [32, 34]. Observational studies had moderate-to-high risk of bias, particularly in confounder control and sample representativeness.

Another key limitation is the geographical and socioeconomic bias present in the available literature. Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, restricting the applicability of findings to resource-limited settings. Additionally, the exclusion of non-English-language publications may introduce a language bias. Finally, while reduced-frequency and novel therapies show promise, long-term QoL data for these approaches remain limited. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies, incorporating diverse populations and standardized QoL assessment tools to improve generalizability and improve NMIBC care.

Future Research Directions

Several promising areas warrant further investigation into the treatment of NMIBC treatment. First, studies should focus on long-term, personalized treatment approaches that align intensity with patient-specific risk and tolerability.

Second, integrating psychosocial support into NMIBC care is essential. Studies exploring the impact of mental health interventions, structured peer support, and multidisciplinary management may help alleviate the psychological distress commonly reported during prolonged intravesical therapy [55]. Routine screening for emotional and supportive care needs could guide early interventions, enhancing both adherence and PROs. Finally, novel therapies, including gene-based intravesical treatments, immune modulators, and drug delivery innovations, hold promise for reducing treatment frequency and toxicity [56, 57]. These therapies may provide comparable oncological control while lowering procedural discomfort and emotional burden. Trials such as KEYNOTE-057 and SWOG S1605 have already demonstrated the feasibility of integrating PROs into therapeutic development [51, 52], and future studies should build on these frameworks to evaluate both clinical and QoL endpoints. Comparative effectiveness studies between traditional versus novel regimens will be critical to guiding future clinical guidelines. Prioritizing these research domains may lead to more and personalized, tolerable, and patient-centered NMIBC care.

Conclusions

Frequent intravesical instillations of BCG and chemotherapy substantially impact the QoL of patients with NMIBC, affecting both physical and emotional well-being. Although reducing treatment frequency may improve adherence and alleviate certain adverse effects, such modifications must be carefully evaluated to avoid compromising therapeutic efficacy. Evidence from recent trials, including CREST, suggests that reduced-frequency schedules may be associated with inferior efficacy if not implemented with appropriate patient selection or adjunctive support. Emerging therapies with less intensive treatment schedules represent a promising approach to minimizing treatment burden while maintaining disease control.

As treatment strategies continue to evolve, future research should prioritize study designs that integrate QoL outcomes, validate supportive interventions that enhance adherence, and explore novel therapies that balance oncologic effectiveness with patient tolerability. A more personalized and patient-centered approach will be essential for improving both survivorship and the overall experience of care for individuals with NMIBC.
