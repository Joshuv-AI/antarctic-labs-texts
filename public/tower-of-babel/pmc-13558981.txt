deduplicate.it: A Simple Open-Source Tool to Remove Duplicates From Literature Searches
Authors: David Purkarthofer, Sebastian Labenbacher, Helmar Bornemann-Cimenti, Giovanni Landoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Systematic reviews and meta-analyses aim to comprehensively identify, summarize and appraise literature. Record identification is performed within multiple databases with important overlap. Deduplication is therefore an important, time-consuming step often underreported or described only briefly. Unique identifiers like the digital object identifier (DOI®) provide a practical basis to identify and remove unambiguous duplicates, but require careful handling of missing, inconsistently formatted, or non-unique DOIs. We created a simple tool that removes duplicates based on normalized DOIs and titles, and make it available as a free-to-use web application hosted on deduplicate.it. It is designed for ease of use, while maintaining high transparency by utilizing a human-readable, simple algorithm and outputting exclusion files and a PRISMA-style flowchart alongside the deduplicated output. In a validation set of five reviews in medicine, education and social welfare, deduplicate.it was able to identify around 80% of duplicates and reduce the time dedicated to deduplication accordingly.

Introduction

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses strengthen evidence-informed decision-making by systematically identifying, appraising, and synthesizing research to answer a predefined question. Their credibility depends on rigor and transparency across the entire workflow, from searching and record management to screening, extraction, and synthesis (Page et al., 2021). To maximize coverage and reduce database-specific indexing bias, it is good practice to search multiple bibliographic databases, which inevitably results in overlapping records and duplicates (Ewald et al., 2022; Frandsen et al., 2025). Duplicate records can be removed before screening, as they can increase screening workload and error risk (Guimarães et al., 2022; Page et al., 2021). Deduplication is a non-trivial issue, as meta-data is not consistently reported between databases with differences found amongst author names, journal abbreviations or page numbers (Jiang et al., 2014).

In practice, deduplication is often performed manually or semi-manually using citation managers or screening platforms, which can be time-consuming (McKeown & Mir, 2021). While automated methods have been proposed, they require technical expertise, rely on algorithms not fully transparent to users, or are available through paid platforms (Askin & Okoli, 2025; Borissov et al., 2022; Forbes et al., 2024; Hair et al., 2023; McKeown & Mir, 2024; Wagner, 2024). While the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram records the number of duplicates removed, and the PRISMA-S extension asks authors to describe the deduplication process itself, including the software or method used, this level of detail is rarely reported in practice, limiting reproducibility and independent verification (Page et al., 2021; Rethlefsen et al., 2021). A recent review of searching and reporting in Campbell reviews found deduplication among the least consistently reported elements of the search, with less than one third of the included publications reporting some detail about the deduplication process (Young et al., 2024).

Digital object identifiers (DOI®) provide a standardized, globally unique identifier for many scholarly outputs and therefore offer a strong basis for automation (DOI Handbook, 2025). However, DOI-based matching is non-trivial in real-world exports: DOIs may be missing, expressed in different syntactic forms or shared across distinct records (e.g., conference abstracts published under a common DOI) (Cioffi et al., 2022; Hair, 2021). Automated removal therefore requires a conservative, auditable approach that preserves ambiguous records to avoid false exclusions.

In this article, we propose deduplicate.it, a free, easy-to-use, open-source tool that removes duplicates using DOI- and title-based matching and produces fully auditable outputs for integration into evidence synthesis workflows.

Methods

Study Design and Purpose

We developed and validated deduplicate.it, a tool for preliminary deduplication of literature-search exports, intended for use before manual (or manual-with-digital-assistance) deduplication. The tool is designed to remove duplicates using a combination of normalized DOI and normalized title, preserving ambiguous records for manual review.

Implementation and Availability

deduplicate.it is available in two forms that apply identical processing steps (Figure 1): a web application requiring no installation, registration or programming knowledge, and a command-line script for users who prefer to run the tool locally (implemented in PHP and Python, respectively). Every step follows fixed rules rather than probabilistic matching, so the same input files always produce the same output and any result can be repeated and checked. Input Formats and Unified Import

The tool accepts export files in five common bibliographic formats and derives format from file content: MEDLINE/PubMed NBIB, RIS (e.g., Embase/CINAHL/Scopus/Web of Science), Web of Science ISI-tagged, BibTeX, and CSV/TSV. Multiple uploaded files are merged into a unified internal representation while preserving source file identity and detected format for downstream auditing.

Definitions

Consistent with prior work, we distinguished data duplicates (same underlying data published in different outlets, e.g. conference abstract and subsequent full article, or guidelines published in multiple journals) from bibliographic duplicates (same publication appearing multiple times in a literature search result) (Askin & Okoli, 2025). Our algorithm only targets the latter.

We considered a DOI as valid if, after normalization, it had the structure defined by the DOI standard: the prefix “10.”, a registrant number, a forward slash, and a suffix. (DOI Handbook, 2025) Records without a valid DOI are retained.

DOI Normalization

Databases export the same DOI in different ways, for example in raw format as 10.1002/cl2.1433, with a prefix and capitalization as doi:10.1002/CL2.1433, or as a web address such as https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.1433. Before any comparison, deduplicate.it therefore rewrites every DOI into one common form: it removes prefixes and web addresses, restores characters that databases encode for use in links (for example %2F back to /), strips stray spaces and trailing punctuation, and converts the letters A–Z to lower case. Lower-casing is deliberately restricted to A–Z because the DOI standard does not define case conversion for other characters. Strings that do not then match the structure of a valid DOI are treated as absent. The individual steps are listed in Supplement A.

Title Normalization

Titles are normalized in a similar way, because databases differ in how they render accented letters, Greek characters, symbols and punctuation. The tool converts characters stored as web codes (for example & amp; to &), removes trademark and copyright symbols, rejoins words split by a hyphen at a line break, replaces accented letters with their unaccented equivalents (ä to a, ß to ss), spells out Greek letters (α to alpha), converts the text to lower case, and replaces remaining punctuation with spaces. “Effects of β-blockers on 30-day mortality” and “Effects of beta blockers on 30 day mortality” therefore reduce to the same string. The normalized title is used only for duplicate detection and does not appear in the deduplicated bibliographic exports.

DOI-collision Handling and Duplicate Detection

Two records are treated as duplicates only when both their simplified DOI and their simplified title are identical. We refer to this pair of values as the compound key: agreement on the DOI alone is not sufficient, and neither is agreement on the title alone.

Requiring both is deliberately stricter than matching on the DOI alone, because a single DOI is occasionally shared by several different papers. A common cause is conference supplements, where an entire abstract book is published under one journal-issue DOI, so many unrelated abstracts carry the same identifier; export errors that tag a record with another paper’s DOI produce the same pattern. Where one DOI carries two or more titles, termed a DOI collision, none of the affected records are merged. They are retained in the output, additionally exported to a separate collision file, and placed at the top of the deduplicated file so that reviewers encounter the most ambiguous records first in manual review.

Records passing these checks are grouped by their compound key, and any group of two or more forms a duplicate cluster. One record per cluster is kept, chosen by a fixed rule: a record with an abstract is preferred over one without; failing that, the record from the file uploaded earlier, which allows users to set source priority through upload order; failing that, the record with the longer abstract. Every removed record is written to the exclusion file together with the record it was matched to, so that each automated decision can be traced.

Export and Audit Outputs

The tool generates deduplicated bibliographic exports and audit files that document every automated decision. Deduplicated exports are provided as RIS, CSV, MEDLINE tagged text, and XML. Audit files include: excluded_duplicates.csv (one row per excluded duplicate paired to the retained record), doi_collisions.csv (all records involved in DOI collisions), and a prefilled flowchart created according to PRISMA standards (flowchart.html) (Page et al., 2021).

Evaluation Datasets

We evaluated deduplicate.it on real-world, multi-database search exports: Three datasets from the biomedical field generated for recent evidence syntheses (systematic or scoping reviews) (Eichlseder et al., 2025; Labenbacher et al., 2025, 2026), and two datasets from the education and social welfare field, where the searches of two recent Campbell systematic reviews were reconstructed from their published search strategies (Dorris et al., 2024; Muir et al., 2024). The reconstructed searches are based on, but not identical to, the originals: the original searches were conducted between 2020 and 2023 and we applied no date restriction, we searched only databases available to us (Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC, CENTRAL and DOAJ for the first dataset, and additionally MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL and PubMed for the second), and we matched on title, abstract and keywords. Detailed search strings and all deviations from the original strategies are given in Supplement B. Exports from ERIC via the public interface provide no dedicated DOI field; accordingly, deduplicate.it extracts the DOI from the link field where present.

For each dataset, we recorded total records, duplicates excluded for conducting the review (assisted manual removal with Rayyan [rayyan.ai]), proportion with valid DOIs, and the number of duplicates removed by deduplicate.it. An independent second deduplication of all five literature searches was performed to compare the time for deduplication. The time was measured from opening the webpage until the deduplication was finished. For manual deduplication we used Rayyan to flag potential duplicates, which were then manually reviewed. The deduplication with deduplicate.it consisted of preliminary deduplication with deduplicate.it, followed by removal of further potential duplicates similar to our manual group.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

For our analysis we used descriptive statistics including absolute and relative counts. No significance testing was performed.

Results

The free to use web service is available at https://deduplicate.it/. The source code is released under the Apache License, Version 2.0, and available at Zenodo and as a GitHub (https://github.com/dpurkarthofer/deduplicate.it) repository (Purkarthofer & Labenbacher, 2026).

Discussion

Key Findings

We have developed and tested deduplicate.it, a free, open-source tool for preliminary deduplication of literature searches. With a combination of DOI and title, the tool automatically removes duplicates. Across five real-world datasets from the biomedical, education and social welfare fields, deduplicate.it removed 76.6% to 89.6% of the duplicates identified during assisted manual deduplication and reduced deduplication time by 68.4% to 86.7%.

Relationship to Previous Evidence

Duplicate detection is supported by citation managers and screening platforms (e.g., Zotero, Mendeley, Rayyan), which typically flag potential duplicates but still require users to make decisions manually (Bramer et al., 2016; McKeown & Mir, 2021). In parallel, several automated deduplication tools aim for more comprehensive detection by incorporating multiple bibliographic fields and probabilistic matching (Borissov et al., 2022; Forbes et al., 2024; Hair et al., 2023; McKeown & Mir, 2024; Wagner, 2024). These approaches can improve recall in settings with missing DOIs or heterogeneous metadata, but they may not be as accessible to all potential users, as they may require greater technical expertise to deploy and interpret, and their decision logic may be less transparent to end users. deduplicate.it is intentionally positioned as a complement rather than a replacement for these tools: it provides a fast, conservative first pass, based on a simple algorithm transparent to investigators and reviewers, that removes only high-confidence duplicates and produces auditable logs that allow users to verify every automated action.

Significance of Study Findings

Deduplication is a necessary step in evidence synthesis workflows. By providing deterministic processing, and structured audit outputs, deduplicate.it supports transparent documentation and reproducibility of record management decisions. The tool is designed to integrate directly into downstream workflows by exporting in common bibliographic formats, allowing teams to archive deduplication outputs alongside review materials and to more clearly report record flow.

Our tool’s interface was deliberately designed to be accessible to end users with no prior experience, as prior research suggests that usability and learning curve are important barriers to adoption, and that most researchers typically learn such tools in an autodidactic manner (Scott et al., 2021).

Given the substantial costs associated with software used for evidence synthesis in systematic reviews (Askin & Okoli, 2025), this free tool may be particularly valuable for individual investigators and resource-constrained institutions and may help mitigate both local and global inequalities in access to conducting this type of research.

Limitations

First, deduplicate.it does not aim to detect all duplicates; records without valid DOIs are retained and require manual or alternative automated methods for deduplication. Yet, it reduces deduplication time and effort significantly.

Second, DOI metadata may be missing, inconsistently formatted, or erroneous in real-world exports, and DOI collisions can occur in certain publishing contexts, including conference abstract collections. To mitigate false exclusions, deduplicate.it requires agreement on DOI and title after normalization; however, this safeguard may reduce recall in collision-heavy datasets.

Third, our evaluation was conducted with a limited set of search exports and used assisted manual deduplication in Rayyan, which previously showed high accuracy, as a pragmatic comparator; (McKeown & Mir, 2021) while all automated exclusions were independently verified. Future work could evaluate performance across diverse disciplines, time periods, and database combinations, and could compare deduplicate.it to a bigger set of deduplication tools.

Future Perspectives

Further development could include optional interfaces for institutional deployment or integration pathways with screening platforms while preserving the tool’s conservative, auditable decision logic. Furthermore, as one of the few solutions released under a permissive open-source license, it can serve as the foundation for further refinement by any interested party. A direct comparison of deduplicate.it against other automated deduplication tools, run on identical datasets, would allow the trade-off between recall, precision and ease of use to be quantified, and we regard such a benchmarking study as a priority for future work.

Conclusion

deduplicate.it provides a simple, worldwide accessible approach to deduplication that removes duplicates using transparent DOI- and title-based matching, and produces auditable outputs suitable for integration into evidence synthesis workflows.
