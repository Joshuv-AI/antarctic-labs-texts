Carbon monoxide poisoning, gas embolism and fluid overload during resectoscopic myomectomy
Authors: Xue Li, Lei Zhang, Ying Liang, Zhen-Zhen Xu, Xian Su, Yu-Ting Li, Ye Lu, Ting Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResectoscopic myomectomy (RM) is a standard minimally invasive procedure but carries risks of distinct complications. We report a rare case of a 36-year-old woman who simultaneously developed a triad of life-threatening complications during HM: carbon monoxide poisoning, gas embolism, and fluid overload. The patient presented with precipitous drops in end-tidal carbon dioxide and bispectral index silence. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed severe metabolic acidosis, electrolyte disturbances, and a carboxyhemoglobin level of 27.1%. Definitive diagnosis via arterial blood gas analysis and subsequent multimodal management, bolstered by the patient’s excellent physiological reserve, led to a full recovery. This case highlights the critical need for strict fluid management during RM and CO-oximetry’s indispensable role in detecting concurrent carbon monoxide toxicity.Key words: resectoscopic myomectomy, carbon monoxide poisoning, gas embolism, fluid overload

Case presentation

A 36-year-old woman (G1P0) presented with severe menorrhagia that significantly impaired her daily quality of life and was diagnosed with submucosal leiomyomas (FIGO type 0). Transvaginal ultrasound revealed a fibroid measuring 44 × 47 × 43 mm. Her chronic, heavy bleeding had resulted in symptomatic anemia, with her lowest recorded hemoglobin level reaching 8.8 g/dL. During detailed preoperative counselling, the patient confirmed that her primary intent for treatment was to alleviate the debilitating bleeding symptoms, without current desires for fertility preservation or assisted conception. She received three cycles of gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRHa) therapy preoperatively, which successfully restored her preoperative hemoglobin to 136 g/L. She was scheduled for resectoscopic myomectomy (HM) under general anesthesia. Preoperative assessments, including electrocardiogram, complete blood count, renal function, and coagulation profile, were unremarkable, except for slightly elevated aminotransferase levels. Her baseline blood pressure on admission was 141/91 (108) mmHg.

General anesthesia was induced with propofol (100 mg), etomidate (6 mg), and sufentanil (5 µg), followed by the insertion of a laryngeal mask airway. Anesthesia was maintained with remifentanil (target effect-site concentration 2 ng/mL, Minto model), propofol infusion (200 mg/h), and 1% sevoflurane. The patient was ventilated with a tidal volume of 450 mL (approximately 6–7 mL/kg) and a respiratory rate of 12 breaths per minute. Surgery began at 14:43. The uterus was distended with normal saline using an automated pump (Shenyang Shenda Endoscope Co., Ltd., China) set at 110 mmHg. HM proceeded using the Olympus PLASMA System (Olympus Winter & IBE GmbH, Germany) at 80 W rather than mechanical simultaneous cutting and suction system.

At 15:09, approximately 26 minutes into the surgery, abrupt physiological changes were first noted. End-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) precipitated from 35 mmHg to 25 mmHg and subsequently fluctuated between 14 and 28 mmHg. Fifteen minutes later (15:24), the bispectral index (BIS) began to decline, falling below 20 at 15:25, then sharply to 0 at 15:38, persisting for 60 seconds. This was followed by three discrete episodes of BIS silence (value of 0) over the next 10 minutes, lasting 35 to 75 seconds each. Endotracheal intubation was performed immediately. During this crisis, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) reached a nadir of 91%, heart rate increased from 85 to 105 beats per minute (bpm), and non-invasive systolic blood pressure fluctuated between 78 and 100 mmHg. The anesthesiologist increased the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) to 75%, administered intermittent ephedrine, and adjusted the respiratory rate. At 15:43, the EtCO₂ waveform was transiently lost (reading of 0) for 70 seconds before returning to >20 mmHg. Suspecting gas embolism, the team initiated 100% oxygen ventilation, paused the procedure, and called for assistance.

An invasive arterial line was established. The initial arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis revealed severe acidemia (pH 6.92, base excess −21.5 mmol/L), hypercapnia (PaCO₂ 54 mmHg), hyperchloremia (119 mmol/L), hypokalemia (3.25 mmol/L), hypocalcemia (0.85 mmol/L), and hemodilutional anemia (hemoglobin 10.4 g/dl). These findings strongly indicated massive intravascular absorption of the distension medium. A distension medium deficit of 3000 mL was double-checked by the nurse and anesthesiologist. A fluid deficit of 3000 mL was noted, and lung ultrasound demonstrated diffuse B-lines with bilateral rales, confirming fluid overload. Notably, ABG analysis also revealed a carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) level of 27.1% (reference range: 0–1.5%), confirming concurrent acute carbon monoxide toxicity. Despite repositioning the arterial cannula and transducer, invasive blood pressure readings remained 20–30 mmHg lower than non-invasive measurements, displaying an overdamped waveform consistent with systemic emboli or vascular tone loss.

Management included 100% oxygen ventilation to displace carbon monoxide, application of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), administration of furosemide for diuresis, sodium bicarbonate for acidosis, and norepinephrine and dopamine infusions to support organ perfusion. By 16:05, vital signs stabilized, though tachycardia (95–115 bpm) persisted. Hemostasis was achieved by targeted bipolar electrocoagulation, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). A repeat ABG at 16:43 showed significant improvement (pH 7.27, base excess −8.4 mmol/L, COHb reduced).

Postoperatively, high-sensitivity Troponin I peaked at 2464.2 ng/L (reference range: 0–11.6 ng/L), and aminotransferase levels rose to three times the baseline, indicating transient organ injury. Under supportive care, COHb normalized to 0.6% within 9 hours, and metabolic acidosis resolved (Table). Fortunately, severe consequences of arterial embolism such as hemiparesis, blindness and coma were all averted. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1 with intact neurological function confirmed by neurologist through physical and radiologic examination. A Mini-Mental State Examination on day 3 scored 28/30, and cerebral MRI was unremarkable. She was discharged on postoperative day 6 without sequelae and came back for follow-up 3 months later.

Discussion

This case highlights rare and perilous complications during resectoscopic myomectomy: fluid overload, gas embolism, and carbon monoxide poisoning. A strength of this report is the high-resolution retrieval of monitoring data recorded at 5-second intervals (Figure), which allows for a meticulous reconstruction of the pathophysiological cascade. Here we discuss a framework for crisis diagnosis, immediate intervention, and future prevention, to aid clinicians in recognizing this complex scenario and treat similar patient in the future. Symptomatic gas embolism is exceptionally rare, with an estimated incidence of 0.002% to 0.01%. However, it is potentially catastrophic, with reported case fatality rates reaching up to 28%.1 The diagnosis of venous gas embolism was evident from the classic triad: abrupt EtCO₂ drop, severe hypotension, BIS decline and silence. Gas bubbles entering the venous system ordinarily lodge in the pulmonary circulation, resulting increased pulmonary dead space, EtCO₂ drop and potential hypoxia. The uniqueness of this case lies in the presence of carbon monoxide (CO) within the gas, which was generated by the incomplete combustion of organic tissue during electrosurgical resection. CO poisoning is an extremely rare complication limited to isolated case reports.2 Compared to monopolar devices, significant gas embolism and CO production was found with bipolar diathermy.3 In the circumstance of carbon monoxide poisoning, pulse oximetry monitoring is not reliable because it cannot distinguish between oxyhemoglobin and carboxyhemoglobin due to similar light absorption at 660 nm, leading to falsely normal SpO₂ readings despite profound hypoxia. When the amount of gas entrained exceeds the rate of pulmonary clearance, pulmonary artery pressure rises progressively. Eventually, cardiac output decreases in response to increases in right ventricular afterload and then hypotension occurs. To be noted, hypotension itself can facilitate further gas entry into the vascular system. An overdamped arterial waveform of this case manifested by the loss of the dicrotic notch and a decrease in systolic pressure is another documented sign of gas embolism.4 Additionally, the specific finding of BIS dropping to zero suggests severe burst suppression or transient cessation of cortical activity due to cerebral hypoperfusion or micro-emboli.

The overall incidence of fluid overload ranges from 1% to 5%. Although FIGO type 0 myomas generally carry a lower risk of fluid intravasation than types 1 or 2 due to minimal myometrial involvement, massive absorption can still occur, particularly with larger lesions. For example, Chung et al. recently reported life-threatening fluid overload during the resection of a 6.5 cm type 0 myoma.5

Immediate intervention for this intraoperative crisis included: 1) Halting the surgery to avoid gas and CO generation and absorption; 2) increasing the fraction of inspired oxygen to optimize oxygen supply. 3) vasoactive support to maintain organ perfusion; 4) administering diuretics to correct fluid overload and concurrently managing acid-base and electrolyte disturbance.

Prevention strategies learned from this case are listed as follows:(1) limiting intrauterine pressure (typically starting between 70 and 80 mmHg, and always below the patient’s MAP) and shorting surgical duration as much as possible; (2) regularly fluid deficit monitoring at a frequency of 10 to 15 minutes6; (3) establishing artery monitoring for those surgery with long anticipative time at the very beginning and regularly doing ABG analysis with CO-oximetry (specifically the fraction of oxyhemoglobin, FO2Hb) to assess true oxygenation capacity (4) considering transesophageal echocardiography in high-risk patients.7,8

Conclusion

In conclusion, while HM is often considered a minor procedure, it carries risks of lethal complications. The simultaneous occurrence of CO toxicity, gas embolism, and fluid overload is exceptionally rare but manageable with early detection. We advocate for standardized general anesthesia with capnography and EEG monitoring for complex HM cases. Crucially, in the event of unexplained instability, arterial blood gas analysis is indispensable for differentiating between metabolic, respiratory, and toxicological causes.
