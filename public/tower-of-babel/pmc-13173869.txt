Audit of antibiotic prescribing practices for pediatric pneumonia in the outpatient department: focus on amoxicillin and azithromycin
Authors: Waqar Ahmed, Iqrar Ali, Sheeraz Ahmed, Samina Naz, Bela Bashir, Zarrish Shabbir, Muhammad Kashif Khaskheli, Heera Nand, Habibullah Maitlo, Fatimah Unar, Fareeda Bhanbhro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibiotic overuse in childhood pneumonia contributes significantly to antimicrobial resistance, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This clinical audit evaluated outpatient antibiotic prescribing practices, implemented targeted educational interventions, and assessed improvements in the appropriateness of prescribing.MethodsA two-cycle clinical audit was conducted at the Children Hospital, Sukkur (SICHN), Pakistan. Children under 15 years diagnosed with pneumonia by treating clinicians in the outpatient department were included during pre-audit (n = 132) and post-audit (n = 153) phases. The audit focused on patients who received antibiotics, evaluating appropriateness in terms of choice, dose, and duration according to WHO AWaRe and IMNCI guidance. Educational sessions and real-time feedback were provided between audit cycles. Data on demographics, clinical features, antibiotic prescriptions, and follow-up were analyzed.ResultsOverall antibiotic prescribing remained high (116/132 [87.9%] pre-audit vs. 135/153 [88.2%] post-audit). However, prescribing quality improved substantially. Amoxicillin use increased from 26/132 (19.7%) to 119/153 (77.8%), with appropriate prescribing rising from 1/26 (3.8%) to 96/119 (80.7%). Azithromycin use decreased from 66/132 (50.0%) to 5/153 (3.3%), with appropriate use improving from 20/66 (30.3%) to 4/5 (80.0%). Documentation practices improved, although objective diagnostic criteria were inconsistently recorded. Follow-up rates remained low (~ 15%), but most returning patients showed clinical improvement.ConclusionStructured audit and feedback significantly improved the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing for children diagnosed with pneumonia in outpatient settings. While overall prescribing rates remained unchanged, a marked shift toward guideline-recommended antibiotic use highlights the effectiveness of stewardship-focused interventions in resource-limited, real-world clinical environments.Clinical trial registrationThis study was a clinical audit and not an interventional clinical trial. Introduction

Pneumonia is an acute infection of the lung parenchyma and remains the leading infectious cause of morbidity and mortality in children under five years of age worldwide, responsible for nearly one in six childhood deaths annually [1]. The burden is especially severe in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Pakistan, where limited access to healthcare and guideline-discordant antibiotic use contribute to poor outcomes [2]. In children, the World Health Organization (WHO) and Integrated Management of Neonatal and Childhood Illness (IMNCI) guidelines define pneumonia by cough and/or difficulty breathing with age-specific tachypnea, and in severe cases, chest indrawing or general danger signs [3, 4]. Within the context of these guidelines, the presence of tachypnea in a child with cough or difficulty breathing is considered diagnostic of non-severe pneumonia in the absence of chest indrawing or danger signs [3, 4]. Amoxicillin remains the recommended first-line antibiotic, supporting its judicious use in CAP, while azithromycin should be reserved for select situations. However, incorporation of these principles remains suboptimal in many LMIC outpatient departments [3–7]. Despite these recommendations, guideline-discordant antibiotic prescribing for childhood pneumonia remains common in outpatient settings, contributing to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and unnecessary healthcare expenditure. The WHO’s AWaRe classification (2022) emphasizes amoxicillin as an “Access” antibiotic, promoting its rational use while restricting macrolides such as azithromycin to targeted indications [6, 7]. Clinical audits are proven quality improvement tools that assess practice against standards, provide structured feedback, and monitor change.

Study objective

This audit aimed to evaluate outpatient antibiotic prescribing patterns for pediatric pneumonia, implement a targeted educational intervention, and assess post-audit improvements in guideline-based prescribing.

Hypothesis

We hypothesized that structured audit and feedback would increase the appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions—particularly amoxicillin—without significantly altering overall prescription rates in children presenting with non-severe pneumonia.

Methodology

This two-cycle clinical audit was conducted in the Pediatric Outpatient Department (OPD) of the Children Hospital, Sukkur (SICHN). All children aged under 15 years presenting with fever, cough, and/or difficulty breathing and diagnosed with pneumonia by the treating clinician were eligible for inclusion. The audit focused specifically on antibiotic prescribing practices; therefore, only patients who received an antibiotic prescription were included in the final analysis. Appropriateness of prescribing was assessed based on WHO AWaRe and IMNCI guidelines, considering the choice of antibiotic, dose, and duration. Amoxicillin was designated as the first-line therapy for non-severe pneumonia, administered at dose of 80– to 90 mg/kg/day in 2 to 3 divided doses for a duration of 5 days. Azithromycin was considered appropriate only in cases of documented beta-lactam allergy or atypical pneumonia, with a dose of 10 mg/kg on Day 1, followed by 5 mg/kg once daily for the remaining 4 days. A prescription was deemed fully appropriate only when all three criteria—choice, dose, and duration—were met.

Children with severe clinical features requiring urgent management, including those referred for oxygen therapy or hospitalization, were excluded from the outpatient audit and managed in the emergency department; these cases were recorded as presenting with shortness of breath. Objective clinical signs such as age-specific tachypnea and chest indrawing were documented when present but were not mandatory inclusion criteria, reflecting real-world outpatient decision-making in a high-volume clinical setting.

The pre-audit phase was conducted from 27 June to 26 July 2025, followed by an educational intervention and reinforcement phase from 27 July to 26 August 2025, and a post-audit phase from 27 August to 26 September 2025 (Fig. 1). The same clinicians participated across both cycles, ensuring continuity and comparability. The intervention included structured educational sessions conducted twice weekly for attending physicians, registrars, medical officers, and pharmacists, emphasizing rational antibiotic selection, appropriate dosing, treatment duration, documentation standards, and adherence to WHO AWaRe and IMNCI guidelines. Daily prescription monitoring through the Hospital Information Management System (HIMS) provided real-time feedback on non-guideline-compliant prescriptions, and weekly meetings reviewed trends and highlighted areas for improvement.

Data were anonymized prior to analysis, and variables collected included demographics, presenting clinical features, prescribed antibiotics with dose and duration, appropriateness of each prescription, and follow-up outcomes.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This audit was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Children Hospital, Sukkur (SICHN), under reference number SICHN/EX-006/2025. The audit involved retrospective review and analysis of anonymized outpatient medical records of children with non-severe pneumonia. As this was a non-interventional quality improvement audit using routinely collected data, the IRB waived the requirement for written informed consent.

All records were de-identified before analysis to ensure confidentiality and privacy. For minors whose data were included, parental or guardian consent was not applicable due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study. The audit adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and complied with the PLOS ONE Human Participants Research Policy for ethical standards in human subject research.

Results

A total of 285 children were included in the audit, with 132 in the pre-audit cycle and 153 in the post-audit cycle. Demographic characteristics were comparable between the two phases. Males comprised 81/132 (61.4%) in the pre-audit group and 93/153 (60.8%) in the post-audit group. The majority of patients were under five years of age, accounting for 252/285 (88.4%) of the total study population (Table 1).

Fever and cough were the most frequently documented presenting complaints in both cycles. Documentation of fever increased from 115/132 (87.1%) pre-audit to 149/153 (97.4%) post-audit (p < 0.01), while cough was recorded in 121/132 (91.7%) and 126/153 (82.4%) cases, respectively (p = 0.02). Shortness of breath was documented in a small proportion of patients (5/132 [3.8%] pre-audit vs. 13/153 [8.5%] post-audit; p = 0.05). Other symptoms, including vomiting, abdominal pain, and runny nose, were infrequently recorded (Table 1). Missing clinical details were considered as not documented.

On clinical examination, documentation improved notably following the intervention. Crepitations were recorded in 87/132 (65.9%) pre-audit compared with 141/153 (92.2%) post-audit, while wheezing was documented in 31/132 (23.5%) and 46/153 (30.1%) cases, respectively. Documentation of tonsillar hypertrophy and throat erythema decreased in the post-audit phase. Tachypnea, defined according to IMNCI criteria, was recorded in 11/132 (8.3%) pre-audit and 13/153 (8.5%) post-audit (Table 2). Children with severe clinical features requiring admission or oxygen therapy were referred to the emergency department and were not included in the outpatient audit, which likely contributed to the low proportion of tachypnea documented.

Overall antibiotic prescribing remained high and did not significantly change between the two cycles, with antibiotics prescribed in 116/132 (87.9%) patients in the pre-audit phase and 135/153 (88.2%) in the post-audit phase (p = 0.94). A marked shift in antibiotic choice was observed following the intervention. Amoxicillin prescribing increased significantly from 26/132 (19.7%) in the pre-audit cycle to 119/153 (77.8%) in the post-audit cycle (p < 0.001). Among these, appropriate amoxicillin prescribing improved substantially from 1/26 (3.8%) to 96/119 (80.7%). In contrast, azithromycin prescribing decreased sharply from 66/132 (50.0%) pre-audit to 5/153 (3.3%) post-audit (p < 0.001), with appropriate use increasing from 20/66 (30.3%) to 4/5 (80.0%) (Table 4).

Overall, appropriate antibiotic prescribing improved significantly, increasing from 21/116 (18.1%) in the pre-audit phase to 116/135 (85.9%) in the post-audit phase, reflecting a substantial improvement in adherence to guideline-based prescribing.

Regarding duration of therapy, documentation was incomplete in the pre-audit phase, with approximately half of prescriptions lacking a recorded duration in the medical record. Among those documented, the most common duration was five days. Following the intervention, duration of therapy was documented in nearly all cases and was predominantly five days, consistent with guideline recommendations.

Follow-up attendance remained low but showed a modest increase, from 15/132 (11.4%) pre-audit to 23/153 (15.0%) post-audit. Among patients who returned for follow-up, clinical improvement was observed in 11/15 (73.3%) in the pre-audit phase and 18/23 (78.3%) in the post-audit phase.

Among children with documented tachypnea, referral to higher-level care was common, occurring in 7/11 (63.6%) pre-audit and 9/12 (75.0%) post-audit, indicating consistent identification and appropriate escalation of more severe cases.

Overall, while the proportion of patients receiving antibiotics remained unchanged, the audit intervention resulted in a marked improvement in the appropriateness of antibiotic selection, dosing, and duration, along with improved clinical documentation in the outpatient setting.

Discussion

This audit demonstrated that structured educational feedback based on WHO AWaRe and IMNCI principles can significantly improve the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing for children diagnosed with pneumonia in outpatient settings. Following the intervention, appropriate amoxicillin prescribing increased from 1/26 (3.8% of prescriptions) pre-audit to 96/119 (80.7%) post-audit, while appropriate azithromycin use improved from 20/66 (30.3%) to 4/5 (80.0%). These findings reflect a substantial shift toward evidence-based prescribing practices and highlight the effectiveness of focused, low-cost educational interventions.

Our findings are consistent with international evidence demonstrating that amoxicillin remains the preferred first-line therapy for childhood community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in outpatient settings [3, 5, 8–11]. Similar audit-based interventions in India and Nepal have reported marked improvements in guideline adherence and reductions in unnecessary macrolide use following structured educational programs and adoption of AWaRe principles [12, 13]. These studies, together with our findings, emphasize that simple audit-and-feedback strategies can meaningfully influence prescribing behavior in resource-limited settings.

In Pakistan, irrational antibiotic prescribing remains a significant contributor to antimicrobial resistance. Previous studies have reported high rates of antibiotic use, often involving WHO “Watch” category agents, along with frequent prescribing errors [14, 15]. In our audit, although overall antibiotic prescribing remained high (116/132 [87.9%] pre-audit vs. 135/153 [88.2%] post-audit), there was a marked improvement in prescribing quality. Appropriate amoxicillin use increased substantially, while inappropriate macrolide prescribing declined sharply, indicating that targeted interventions can improve prescribing behavior even when overall prescribing rates are difficult to reduce.

Importantly, this audit was designed to evaluate prescribing practices rather than diagnostic accuracy. Patients were included based on clinician-diagnosed pneumonia in routine outpatient care, reflecting real-world clinical decision-making in a high-volume setting. The focus of the intervention was to ensure that when antibiotics were prescribed, they were aligned with guideline recommendations in terms of choice, dose, and duration. This approach is consistent with antimicrobial stewardship principles, particularly in LMIC settings where diagnostic uncertainty is common and treatment decisions are often guided by clinical judgment.

Evidence from randomized controlled trials in Pakistan supports the use of short-course oral amoxicillin for non-severe pneumonia. Bari et al. demonstrated no significant difference in outcomes between 3-day and 5-day regimens, reinforcing its effectiveness as a first-line therapy [16–19] In our setting, post-audit improvements in documentation and standardization of treatment duration (predominantly 5 days) reflect better alignment with guideline-based care.

Persistent high antibiotic use despite improved appropriateness mirrors patterns observed in other LMICs, where clinical uncertainty, high patient load, and caregiver expectations contribute to continued prescribing [15–17]. While our intervention successfully improved prescribing quality, reducing overall antibiotic use may require additional strategies, including caregiver education, delayed prescribing approaches, and integration of clinical decision-support tools.

Documentation practices improved following the intervention, likely reflecting increased clinician awareness and engagement. Clinical findings such as crepitations and indicators of disease severity were more consistently recorded in the post-audit phase. However, objective WHO-defined criteria such as tachypnea were still under-documented, highlighting ongoing challenges in busy outpatient environments.

Follow-up rates remained low, although modest improvements were observed post-audit. Among those who returned, most demonstrated clinical improvement, suggesting that appropriate antibiotic selection was associated with favorable short-term outcomes. Strengthening follow-up systems and caregiver counseling may further enhance patient monitoring and treatment outcomes.

Overall, this audit demonstrates that structured education, audit, and feedback can drive meaningful improvements in antibiotic prescribing practices in outpatient pediatric care. These findings highlight the feasibility and impact of low-cost antimicrobial stewardship interventions in high-burden, resource-constrained settings.

Limitations

This audit was conducted at a single tertiary-care center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. The relatively short duration of the post-audit phase may not fully capture the long-term sustainability of improvements in prescribing practices.

This study was designed as an audit of antibiotic prescribing practices rather than an evaluation of diagnostic accuracy. Patients were included based on a clinical diagnosis of pneumonia made by treating physicians in routine outpatient practice. As such, the audit did not aim to differentiate between viral and bacterial pneumonia, but rather to assess whether antibiotics—when prescribed—were appropriate in terms of choice, dose, and duration according to WHO AWaRe and IMNCI guidance.

As the audit relied on routinely documented data from the Hospital Information Management System (HIMS), variability and incompleteness in clinical documentation were observed. Objective WHO-defined diagnostic criteria, such as age-specific tachypnea and chest indrawing, were not consistently recorded. This reflects real-world constraints in a high-volume outpatient setting and may have influenced case classification.

Microbiological confirmation and radiological investigations were not routinely available in the outpatient setting, limiting diagnostic precision. Additionally, data on treatment adherence, caregiver understanding, and long-term clinical outcomes were not assessed.

Despite these limitations, the audit provides a realistic representation of prescribing behavior in a busy outpatient department and demonstrates that structured audit, feedback, and education can significantly improve the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing.

Conclusion

This audit demonstrates that targeted educational interventions, guided by WHO AWaRe and IMNCI principles, can substantially improve the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing in children diagnosed with pneumonia in outpatient settings. Significant improvements were observed in the choice, dose, and documentation of antibiotics, particularly with increased use of amoxicillin and reduced reliance on macrolides.

While overall antibiotic prescribing rates remained high, the shift toward guideline-concordant prescribing highlights the effectiveness of structured audit, feedback, and continuous clinician engagement in strengthening antimicrobial stewardship practices. Importantly, this audit focused on prescribing behavior within routine clinical practice rather than diagnostic validation, reflecting real-world decision-making in a high-volume outpatient setting.

Future multi-center studies across Sindh and similar LMIC settings are needed to validate these findings, assess long-term sustainability, and further integrate antimicrobial stewardship with improved diagnostics, clinician training, and caregiver education to combat antimicrobial resistance effectively.
