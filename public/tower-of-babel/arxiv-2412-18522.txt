SHARQ: Explainability Framework for Association Rules on Relational Data
Hadar Ben-Efraim, Susan B. Davidson, Amit Somech

Abstract
. Association rules are an important technique for gaining insights over large relational datasets consisting of tuples of elements (i.e. attribute-value pairs). However, it is difficult to explain the relative importance of data elements with respect to the rules in which they appear. This paper develops a measure of an element’s contribution to a set of association rules based on Shapley values, denoted SHARQ (ShApley Rules Quantification). As is the case with many Shapely-based computations, the cost of a naive calculation of the score is exponential in the number of elements. To that end, we present an efficient framework for computing the exact SHARQ value of a single element whose running time is practically linear in the number of rules. Going one step further, we develop an efficient multi-element SHARQ algorithm which amortizes the cost of the single element SHARQ calculation over a set of elements. Based on the definition of SHARQ for elements we describe two additional use-cases for association rules explainability: rule importance and attribute importance. Extensive experiments over a novel benchmark dataset containing 45 instances of mined rule sets show the effectiveness of our approach.

Unfortunately, as is the case with Shapley values in general, calculating the SHARQ score of an element can be extremely expensive. We show that, if done naively, the cost is exponential with respect to the size of the dataset (number of elements). However, by reasoning over a set of input rules we are able to develop an exact, optimized
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm that is practically linear with respect to the size of the rule set. This makes exact SHARQ score calculations feasible.
Going one step more, we show that we can further reduce the cost of calculating individual
SHARQ{}^{*}
scores for a set of elements using a multi-element
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm, which amortizes the costs over the set in comparison to calculating the
SHARQ{}^{*}
score of each element separately.

Since SHARQ scores can be effectively computed for all elements, we explore several explainability use-cases that demonstrate the benefits of our approach. The following example shows a basic use-case of
element importance
, where we analyze the impact of high and low-scoring elements.

Example 1.3.

The figure also includes some statistics for each element – its frequency within the data, and the number of rules it appears in, divided into three interestingness categories (IS): High, Medium, and Low.

We observe that the top five high-scoring elements are frequent and appear mainly in High IS rules. In contrast, the bottom five elements are less frequent and mostly appear in Low IS rules (see Section

for details).

We then show two additional explainability use-cases that are based on element SHARQ scores: rule importance and attribute importance.
As shown in Example
1.1
, despite their high IS score rules
r_{1}
and
r_{4}
are redundant of rules
r_{2}
and
r_{3}
, respectively. This could be seen easily since only the top-4 rules were being examined. However, in general, the number of rules above a given interestingness threshold is large, and visually detecting redundant rules becomes infeasible. We show how a notion of rule-level SHARQ scores can be used to narrow the user’s focus on a smaller set of important, non-redundant rules.
Similarly, the SHARQ scores of elements can be used to develop a measure of importance of attributes (features), providing users with a higher-level understanding of which attributes most significantly influence the rule generation process.

An extensive set of experiments show the effectiveness of our approach. In particular, we show that while a naive calculation of SHARQ is exponential in the size of the dataset and therefore infeasible, the optimized
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm grows only linearly in the size of the rules and number of attributes.
Concretely, computing the SHARQ score for an element using
SHARQ{}^{*}
takes 6.8 seconds on average, compared to hours and even more by the naive SHARQ calculation.
We also show that the multi-element
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm provides an improvement in running time that is proportional to the maximum rule length (12X, in our experiments) over a sequential application of
SHARQ{}^{*}
over a set of elements.
Finally, we show that while generic contribution measures are slightly faster than
SHARQ{}^{*}
, they provide substantially different ranks for the elements, thus cannot be used for approximating SHARQ. In contrast, we show that a direct approximation of
SHARQ{}^{*}
based on
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017b
)
is significantly better at preserving the element ranking while maintaining similar running times to
I_{TOP}
and
Influence

Contributions

The contributions of this paper include:

•

A novel measure of an element’s contribution to a set of rules based on Shapley values
(
Shapley 1953
)
called SHARQ, and theoretical analysis of a naive algorithm based on its definition showing exponential behavior in the size of the dataset.

•

An efficient algorithm,
SHARQ{}^{*}
, for calculating the SHARQ score of a single element, and theoretical analysis showing practically linear behavior in the size of the rule set.

•

An efficient multi-element

Use-cases for SHARQ. Building on the notion of element importance, we give a notion of

and

•

A novel evaluation benchmark containing 45 diverse rule sets mined from four different dataset. Each rules set instance contains a different number of rules, different rule lengths and different number of elements.

•

Extensive experiments show the effectiveness of our approach: the feasibility of our
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm, the effectiveness of our multi-element
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm, and the superiority of using a direct approximation of
SHARQ{}^{*}
rather than generic contribution measures such as

Interestingness of a rule and rules set

A rule score function
score(r)
quantifies the interestingness of a rule
r\in R(D)
.
In our implementation we focus on the IS score
(
Tan and Kumar 2000
)
, which combines two well-known measures:
support

(
Agrawal
et al. 1993
)
, which quantifies the frequency of the joint appearance of the rule’s elements, and
lift

(
Brin
et al. 1997a
)
, which measures the independence deviation of
E_{LHS}
and
E_{RHS}
:

support(r)=\frac{|\{t|t\in T\wedge{\mathcal{E}}({r})\subseteq\mathcal{E}(t)\}|}{|T|}

lift(r)=\frac{support(E_{LHS}\cup E_{RHS})}{support(E_{LHS})\cdot support(E_{RHS})}

The full IS score is then defined by:

IS(r)\coloneq\sqrt{support(r)\cdot lift(r)}

However, our framework can take as input any other measure for rule interestingness as suggested, e.g., in
(
Geng and Hamilton 2006
;
Freitas 1998
;
Chandola and
Kumar 2007
;
Lin and Brown 2006
;
Hilderman and
Hamilton 2013
)
.

We will also use a notion for the interestingness of a
set
of rules
R
, defined by

I(R)\coloneq\bigoplus\nolimits_{r\in R}\text{score}(r)

where
\bigoplus
is an aggregation operation over the individual scores of the rules
r\in R
. In our implementation we use
I(R)\coloneq max_{r\in R}~\text{score}(r)
, but our framework also supports other functions such as summation, average, top-k, etc. See Section
5.5
for an empirical comparison.

Measuring element contribution to a rules set

Given a set of dataset elements
E\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}({D})
and a set of rules
R\subseteq R(D)
, our goal is to assess the
contribution
of an element
e\in E
to
I(R)
the interestingness of
R
.
There are several possible ways to quantify an element’s contribution: For example, one could intuitively define the contribution of element
e
by the score of the most interesting rule that contains it, which we denote as
I_{TOP}
:

I_{TOP}(e)\coloneq max_{r\in\{r|r\in R\wedge e\in r\}}~\text{score}(r)

Another way is to use causality-based notions such as influence
(
Pearl
et al. 2009
;
Wu and Madden 2013
)
. In our context, influence can be defined as the difference in the aggregative interestingness of
R
when removing the rules that contain
e
. Formally:

Influence(e)\coloneq I(R)-I(R\setminus\{r|r\in R\wedge e\in r\})

However, as demonstrated in Example
1.2
, these relatively simple measures do not fully capture the contribution of elements.
We therefore devise a measure of element contribution based on the game theoretic notion of Shapley values
(
Shapley 1953
)
.
Our measure, SHApley Rules Quantification (SHARQ), formally defined in the next subsection,
captures the variability in interestingness across rules of different lengths when the element is excluded.
Note that, for Example
1.2
, SHARQ clearly differentiates between the different elements w.r.t. their contribution to the overall interestingness
as depicted in the rightmost column in Table
3
, whereas
I_{TOP}
and
Influence
do not.

2.2.
The SHARQ Formula

We assume a dataset
D
and set of rules
R_{D}
.
Given a set of elements
E\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}({D})
and a set of rules
R\subseteq R_{D}
,
our goal is to measure the
contribution
of an element
e\in E
to the interestingness of
R
. For this, we use the game-theoretic notion of the Shapely value
(
Shapley 1953
)
, which is widely used in XAI
(
Štrumbelj
and Kononenko 2014
;
Lundberg and Lee 2017a
)
as well as for data management and exploration tasks
(
Davidson et al. 2022b
;
Deutch
et al. 2020
;
Deutch
et al. 2021
)
(see Section
6
for a discussion).

The Shapley value measures a
player
’s
contribution
to the
utility
of all possible
player coalitions
.
In our adaptation of Shapley values, which we call SHApley Rules Quantification (SHARQ), the “players” are elements in
E
and “coalitions" are sets of elements with disjoint attributes.
That is, coalitions model rules and the contribution of an element is (roughly speaking) the difference in interestingness of rules in which they play a role and those in which they don’t play a role.

More precisely, given a set of elements
S\subseteq E
, we use
R_{S}
to denote the subset of rules of
R
that contain
exactly
the elements in
S
:

R_{S}\coloneq\{r|r\in R~\wedge~{\mathcal{E}}({r})=S\}

Note that a rule never contains two elements with the same attribute, and therefore
S
cannot contain two elements with the same attribute.
The
utility
of an element coalition
S
is then defined as
I(R_{S})
, i.e., the
aggregative
interestingness of
R_{S}
as defined previously. Naturally, if
R_{S}=\;\emptyset
then
I(R_{S})=0
.

Due to this notion of utility, we consider only
valid
coalitions with respect to an element, i.e. those that can possibly form a rule when the element is included.
Given an element
e
, and a set of elements
E\in{\mathcal{E}}({D})
, we define the set of
valid
coalitions as follows:

\mathcal{C}(e,E)\coloneq\{S|~S\subseteq E\;\wedge|attr(S)|=|S|\;\wedge\;attr(e)\notin attr(S)\}

Namely, all subsets of
E
that, together with element
e
, do not contain two elements of the same attribute.

The SHARQ score of an element
e
in the context of
E
and
R
can now be defined as follows:

SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{C}(e,E)}\frac{|S|!(|E|-|S|-1)!}{|E|}\cdot\left(I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)-I\big(R_{S}\big)\right)

Example 2.1.

Returning to Clarice’s analysis of the
Adults
dataset, the SHARQ scores were calculated as follows:
For element
e_{1}
the valid coalitions are any subset of
\{e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\}
that do not contain both
e_{4}
and
e_{6}
since they both have the same attribute.
For
S=\{e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
we have

R_{S}=\{r_{2}\}
and
R_{S\cup\{e_{1}\}}=\{r_{1}\}
.
As shown in Table
2
,
I(R_{S})=102
and
I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)=105
.

Therefore, for
S=\{e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
we get a score of
\frac{3!(6-3-1)!}{6!}\cdot\left(1.05-1.02\right)=0.05
. We sum the weighted value function difference for the rest of the valid coalitions, and calculate
SHARQ{}(e_{1})=-0.6
. This means that
e_{1}
has low contribution (relatively to the other elements) to the rules set formed by
e_{1}-e_{6}
since, when omitted from every valid coalition, there is always another rule with a similar IS score.

In contrast,
e_{5},e_{6}
obtain the highest SHARQ score of
4.6
.
For each of these elements, if we add them to a coalition that forms one of the rules
r_{2},r_{4}
the interestingness score difference will be negative. For example-
I(R_{\{e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}\cup\{e_{5}\}})-I(R_{\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}})=-102
. For element
e_{6}
and coalition
\{e_{5}\}
the calculation is
\frac{1!(6-1-1)!}{6!}\cdot\left(105-0\right)=3.5
.
∎

To find the valid coalitions for an element
e
,
The set
E
is divided into subsets of elements with the same attribute; elements with attribute
attr(e)
are omitted.
Coalitions are then formed by choosing either a single element or nothing from each attribute set.

Number of Coalitions

The cost of any application of Shapley values is determined by the number of
coalitions
, which is exponential in the number of
players
. For example, when computing Shapley values in the context of supervised ML explanations
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017b
;
Jethani et al. 2021
)
, the players are the data
attributes
. When computing Shapley values for database query results
(
Livshits et al. 2019
)
the players are the dataset
tuples
. In our context, the players are the dataset elements, which is often considerably larger than the number of attributes, and in many datasets can also surpass the number of tuples.

The number of valid coalitions for a dataset element
e
can be calculated as follows:
Let
E_{a}
be the subset of elements in
E
with attribute
a
, i.e.,
attr(e)=a

Each attribute
a
has
|E_{a}|+1
options for representation in a coalition - either the attribute does not appear or has one of the values in
\mathcal{E}_{a}
.
Therefore, the number of valid coalitions for
e
is
\prod_{a\in\{{attr(E)}\}-\{attr(e)\}}(|E_{a}|+1)
.
In the worst case, there are
|T|
unique elements in each column and
attr(E)=\mathcal{A}
, hence the cost of calculating the SHARQ score for a single element
e
is
O\left(|T|^{|\mathcal{A}|}\right)
.

As we show in Section
5.2
, calculating
SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)
becomes infeasible even for small rule sets. We therefore develop an improved formula in which a substantial number of irrelevant element coalitions that do not affect the SHARQ score are pruned, and use this to develop efficient algorithms for calculating SHARQ scores.

3.
Efficient SHARQ Algorithms

Since calculating the SHARQ score of an element
directly is often infeasible, in this section we devise a computational framework, denoted
SHARQ{}^{*}
, for facilitating the SHARQ computation while retaining the exact same output as SHARQ. First, in Section
3.1
, we give an improved formula
SHARQ^{*}_{(E,R)}(e)
in which element coalitions that do not affect the score are pruned. We further prove that the improved formula is equivalent to SHARQ, i.e., that
SHARQ^{*}_{(E,R)}(e)
=
SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)
. However, in contrast to the original formula in which the number of coalitions is
exponential
in the number of dataset
attributes
, the number of coalitions in the improved formula is several orders of magnitude smaller, bounded by the product of the number of rules and maximum rule size. This is essentially
linear
in the number of rules since the maximum rule size is typically small (e.g. less than 10)
1
1

1

This reduction is due to thresholds set on support and interestingness in rule mining algorithms
(
Han et al. 2000
;
Agrawal
et al. 1993
)
.

In Section
3.2
we then show an exact algorithm to calculate
SHARQ^{*}
for a single element
e\in E
.

Lastly, since many rule set explanation use cases require the calculation of SHARQ scores for
multiple
elements (see Section
4
), in Section
3.3
we describe a multi-element
SHARQ^{*}
algorithm. Given a
set
of elements
E
, the algorithm amortizes the cost of materializing the valid element coalitions for
SHARQ{}^{*}
by calculating it
once
, jointly for all elements in
E
.
We show that the multi-element
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm is superior to a sequential application of
SHARQ^{*}
over each element of
E
, reduces running time costs by an average of about 12X.

3.1.
Improved Formula:
SHARQ{}^{*}

Our improved formula,
SHARQ{}^{*}
, is based on the observation that an element coalition
S
has no contribution to the
SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)
calculation
if
R_{S}=\emptyset
and
R_{S}\cup\{e\}=\;\emptyset
.
In this case,
I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)=I\big(R_{S}\big)=\;0
.
Consequently, we can safely restrict the SHARQ calculation to coalitions formed from the elements in rules or those with one rule element removed:

(1)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{C}^{*}\coloneq\{S|\exists r\in R:(S\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}({r})~\wedge~|S|\geq|{\mathcal{E}}({r})|-1)\}}}

Given
e
and
E
, the
optimized valid
coalitions are thus:

(2)

\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)\coloneq\{S|S\in\mathcal{C}^{*}\;\wedge S\subseteq E\;\wedge\;attr(e)\notin attr(S)\}

The improved formula
SHARQ^{*}
then becomes:

(3)

SHARQ^{*}_{(E,R)}(e)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)}\frac{|S|!(|E|-|S|-1)!}{|E|}\cdot\left(I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)-I\big(R_{S}\big)\right)

We now show that SHARQ and SHARQ
∗
are equivalent.

Proposition 3.1.

\forall e\in E
,
SHARQ^{*}_{(E,R)}(e)=SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)

Proof.

Given an element
e
, we can divide
\mathcal{C}(e,E)
into two sets of coalitions: those that are contained in the elements of some rule, denoted
\mathcal{C}_{1}(e,E)
;
and those that are not contained in the elements of any rule, denoted
\mathcal{C}_{2}(e,E)
. We formally define theses subsets as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{C}_{1}(e,E)=\{S|&~S\subseteq E\;\wedge|attr(S)|=|S|\;\wedge\;attr(e)\notin attr(S)\ \wedge\\
&(\exists r\in R:S\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}({r}))\}\\
\mathcal{C}_{2}(e,E)=\{S|&~S\subseteq E\;\wedge|attr(S)|=|S|\;\wedge\;attr(e)\notin attr(S)\ \wedge\\
&(\forall r\in R:S\not\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}({r}))\}\end{array}

We can then rewrite the
SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)
expression:

(4)

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(e,E)}\frac{|S|!(|E|-|S|-1)!}{|E|}\cdot\left(I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)-I\big(R_{S}\big)\right)

+

(5)

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(e,E)}\frac{|S|!(|E|-|S|-1)!}{|E|}\cdot\left(I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)-I\big(R_{S}\big)\right)

Observe that for every
S\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(e,E)
,

R_{S}=R_{(S\cup\{e\})}=\emptyset
and therefore
I(R_{(S\cup\{e\})})=I(R_{S})=0
.
Hence we can simplify
SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)
to Equation
4
, which considers only the coalitions in
\mathcal{C}_{1}(e)
.

Since attributes are never repeated in rules,
\mathcal{C}_{1}(e,E)
can be simplified to:

\mathcal{C}_{1}(e,E)=\{S|~(\exists r\in R:S\subseteq({\mathcal{E}}({r})\cap E))\;\wedge\;attr(e)\notin attr(S)\}

Folding in the definition of
C^{*}
,
\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)
can be written as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)\coloneq\{S|&(\exists r\in R:(S\subseteq({\mathcal{E}}({r})\cap E))\;\wedge\;attr(e)\notin attr(S)\\
&~\wedge~|S|\geq|{\mathcal{E}}({r})|-1)\}\end{array}

Therefore
\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{1}(e,E)
.

Now suppose that
SHARQ^{*}_{(E,R)}(e)\neq SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)
for some element
e
.
Then there is a coalition
S
in
\mathcal{C}_{1}(e,E)

that is not in
\mathcal{C^{*}}(e,E)
for which
I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)-I\big(R_{S}\big)\neq 0
.

For this to be true, either
S
has to form some rule in
R
but not in
S\cup\{e\}
, or
S\cup\{e\}
has to form a rule in
R
but not in
S
, or both
S
and
S\cup\{e\}
form rules in
R
. In all cases,
S
must be in
\mathcal{C^{*}}(e,E)
by definition.
Therefore
\forall e,SHARQ^{*}_{(E,R)}(e)=SHARQ_{(E,R)}(e)
.
∎

Number of Coalitions

Since coalitions in
\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)
are formed using rules in
R
, each rule
r
creates either
|{\mathcal{E}}({r})|+1
coalitions (when
r
does not contain an element
e^{\prime}
with
attr(e)=attr(e^{\prime})
,
each subset
E\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}({r})
of size
\geq|{\mathcal{E}}({r})|
is considered
), or the single coalition (when
r
contains an element
e^{\prime}
with
attr(e)=attr(e^{\prime})

the only considered coalition is
{\mathcal{E}}({r})-\{e^{\prime}\}

). Assuming that
\gamma
percent of the rules do not contain an element with
attr(e)
and that the maximum rule size is
\tau
,
the size of
\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)
is at worst

\left(\gamma\cdot|R|\cdot\left(\tau+1\right)+\left((1-\gamma)\cdot|R|\right)\right)
, which is

O\left(|R|\cdot\gamma\cdot\tau\right)
.

In the worst case, in which
\gamma=1
and
\tau=|\mathcal{A}|
, the size of
\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)
is
O\left(|R|\cdot|\mathcal{A}|\right)
.
This is a significant improvement over the the number of coalitions in the non-optimized SHARQ score,
O\left(|T|^{|\mathcal{A}|}\right)
, which is
exponential
w.r.t. the number of rows in the dataset.

Note, however, that
|R|
may be larger than
|T|
for some datasets and rule mining settings (in the worst case, exponential in
|T|
). In our experimental evaluation, we examined
66
rule sets mined from four different datasets. We found that
|R|
was indeed larger than
|T|
in 11 cases, but with only a maximum ratio of
|R|\approx 64\cdot|T|
. Even in this extreme case, the number of coalitions used by the optimized
SHARQ{}^{*}
was 9.5K, compared to 16.7M used by the naive SHARQ computation.

This optimization allows us to compute SHARQ scores in a reasonable amount of time –
6.6
seconds on average, per element, compared to several hours (or more) by the naive SHARQ computation.
We next present our full
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm, which improves the calculation by considering two additional aspects: (1) the retrieval of the optimized valid coalition set
\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)
, and (2) the calculation of the utility function difference
I\big(R_{S\cup\{e\}}\big)-I\big(R_{S}\big)
.

The single element

Cost Analysis

In the first part of the algorithm,
we iterate through each
r\in R
and form coalitions. Forming a coalition takes time proportional to the size of
r
, which we assume is
\tau

\tau+1
coalitions per rule,

O\left(\tau^{2}\right)

O\left(|R|\cdot\tau^{2}\right)
for the first part.

In the second part, we iterate over
\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)
to compute the

\mathcal{R}
and calculating the utility scores
I(\cdot)
are both

\mathcal{C}^{*}(e,E)
.
The overall cost of the two parts is thus
O\left(|R|\cdot\tau\cdot(\tau+\gamma)\right)
.
Since
\gamma\leq 1
, this is essentially
O\left(|R|\cdot\tau^{2}\right)
.
Since rules are typically small (
\tau<<|\mathcal{A}|
), we call this “practically linear" in the number of rules.
However, in the worst case, where
\tau=|\mathcal{A}|

this becomes
O\left(|R|\cdot|\mathcal{A}|^{2}\right)
.

\widehat{SHARQ{}}_{(E,R)}(e)=\frac{R_{freq}(e)}{R_{SHARQ}(e)}

Intuitively, the
lower
the normalized SHARQ score the more
redundant
the element
e
is.

Using normalized SHARQ, we define the
rule-level
SHARQ:

R\text{-}SHARQ_{(E,R)}(r)=\min_{e\in\mathcal{E}(r)}\widehat{SHARQ}_{(E,R)}(e)

Namely, the SHARQ rule score is determined by its lowest-scoring element.

4.3.
Rules-driven Attribute Importance

We next show another common explainability use case, attribute (feature) importance, in which we use SHARQ to detect the most significant attributes for the given set of association rules.

To do so, we define an
attribute
-level SHARQ score:

A\text{-}SHARQ_{(E,R)}(a)=\frac{\sum_{e\in E_{a}\cap\mathcal{E}(R)}\widehat{SHARQ}_{(E,R)}(e)}{E_{a}}

where
E_{a}
is the subset of
E
whose elements have attribute
a
.

This is the mean
normalized
SHARQ score for the elements of attribute
a
that appear in at least one rule.

Figure

element

Notice that the
Income

’Not-in-Family’

Also note that the columns

Race
obtain the lowest A-SHARQ importance scores. This is because they all contain a single dominant element with a high frequency, yet low importance (see their corresponding element-level SHARQ scores in the lower part of Figure

).

The attributes importance scores can be used, e.g., to reduce the dimensionality of the data (by disregarding low-scoring attributes) when performing analytical tasks.

Summary & Discussion

In this section, we gave two additional explainability applications based on the element-level SHARQ scores: rule-level and attribute-level importance scores. As demonstrated in
(
Lundberg et al. 2020
)
, a similar analysis in the context of ML explainability is performed using the SHAP
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017b
)
explainability framework, where individual SHAP scores are aggregated over multiple predictions. Using such aggregations, one can explain global aspects of the ML model, including global feature importance scores and feature dependency analysis. In our context, additional interesting aggregations of the element-level SHARQ scores include feature interaction, which examines the change in importance of one feature (element) as a function of the values of a second feature; calculation of element
subsets
importance; and SHARQ correlations.

We conducted our experiments on a evaluation set consisting of

The evaluation setup is discussed in Section

Next, we analyze the performance of our multi-element

We then test the ability of alternative approaches such as

and

Finally, in Section

5.4.
Approximations
and Alternative Approaches

In Section
2.1
, we described two possible alternative methods for calculating the contribution of an element
e
to the interestingness of a rules set
R
,
I_{TOP(r)}(e)
and
Influence_{(R)}(e)
. While both methods can be computed more efficiently than SHARQ scores, we showed in Example
1.2
that they fail to adequately capture the difference in the contribution of elements.

In the next set of experiments, our goal is to validate this observation and examine whether
I_{TOP}
and
influence
can provide a good
approximation
for the SHARQ.
Since both alternative approaches use different calculation methods,
we expect their output to be different than SHARQ. We therefore calculate the following metrics, in order to determine how their ranking and top-scoring elements compare to those returned by the SHARQ calculation:

(1) p@10
, which compares the top-10 SHARQ elements to the top-10 w.r.t. the baseline score and then calculates
\frac{\text{\#top-10 matches}}{10}
, i.e., the number of matching elements in the two top-10 lists, divided by 10;
(2) ap@10
, defined as
\frac{1}{10}\sum_{k=1}^{10}{p@k}
, namely the average p@k score from 1 to 10 (p@k generalizes the p@10 measure defined above, for arbitrary values of
k
); and
(3) Spearman Rank Correlation
which measures the correlation between the
rank order
of the baseline scores compared to the SHARQ scores. We also report the running times of each baseline for computing the scores of all
\mathcal{E}(R)
elements.

We compare the alternative contribution calculation methods to five direct SHARQ approximations, previously suggested for Shapley-based calculations
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017b
;
Mitchell
et al. 2022
)
.
The first is (1)
Shapley kernel weighting
, based on
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017b
)
, in which
permutations
are sampled after assigned a weight of
\frac{|E|-1}{\binom{|E|}{|S|}|S|(|E|-|S|)}
.
The remaining four are permutation sampling techniques suggested in the context of Shapley approximations
(
Mitchell
et al. 2022
)
: (2)
Monte-Carlo
, in which the permutations are sampled i.i.d.; (3)
Antithetic Monte-Carlo
, where half of the permutations are sampled uniformly, and for each randomly-sampled permutation we also take its complement; (4)
Stratified sampling,
another common technique for reducing variance, where we first segment the permutations by size then take a uniform sample from each group; and (5)
Sobol-Sequence Sampling
, which provides a better coverage of the sampled domain by avoiding resampling similar points.

Table
6
first presents the approximation performance of the alternative contribution calculation approaches:
I_{TOP}
and Influence, and then gives the results for the direct SHARQ approximations.
Observe that all direct SHARQ approximation outperforms both alternative approaches across all approximation quality metrics. Notably, the Shapely kernel weighting method obtains the highest approximation quality, achieving scores of 0.92, 0.84, and 0.93 for p@10, ap@10, and rank correlation, with an overall average running time of 15.46s. In comparison, the alternative contribution methods are faster (10.14s and 10.48s) but have an inferior quality, not exceeding 0.73, 0.51 and 0.76 for p@10, ap@10, and rank correlation.
Note that the Sobol-sequence and Monte-carlo approaches obtains better results than
I_{TOP}
and
Influence
, with almost the same running times (13.38s and 12.85s) respectively.

These findings suggest that the alternative approaches are
not
good estimators for SHARQ scores, as the direct SHARQ approximations maintain higher accuracy and comparable running times.

In addition to these results which show the deviation in element rankings between SHARQ and alternative contribution scores, we have empirically observed (e.g. in Example
1.2
) the inadequacy of
I_{TOP}
and
influence
in capturing importance differences between elements. However, a more comprehensive analysis should be conducted using qualitative comparisons, such as case studies, user studies, and quantitative evaluations. This approach has recently been proposed for evaluating and comparing established XAI methods in the context of supervised learning models
(
Salih et al. 2024
)
and autonomous agents
(
Rosenfeld 2021
)
.

Since SHARQ is, to the best of our knowledge, the first framework for quantifying the importance of elements in an association rule set, we defer the development of such evaluation methods to future work.

6.
Related Work

While SHARQ is, to the best of our knowledge, the first framework for explaining association rules mined from relational data, there are several lines of related work that are relevant. We begin by describing existing techniques for analyzing a set of mined association rules, showing that explainability for rules is overlooked. We then present previous work on explaining the results of ML models and database queries, clarifying why such solutions do not help with our problem.

Analytical tools for analyzing and visualizing association rules.

A plethora of previous work recognizes the challenge of analyzing the results of rule mining tools, which often return thousands of different association rules. To address this, two prominent methods are suggested: (1)
ranking solutions
, which devise dedicated functions to sort the resulting rules by different interestingness criteria; and (2)
visualization solutions
, which provide graphical means to examine and browse through the resulting rules. None of these solutions, to the best of our knowledge, address the explainability problem of assessing the
contribution
of individual elements to the resulting rules.

(1)
Interestingness functions for ranking and pruning association rules.
Numerous techniques have been devised for
ranking
mined rules according to heuristic notions of
interestingness

(
Bayardo Jr and
Agrawal 1999
;
Brin
et al. 1997b
;
Zhang
et al. 2009
;
Freitas 1998
)
. These notions provide a numeric score for each mined rule based on factors such as accuracy, conciseness, reliability, peculiarity, and more (see
(
Geng and Hamilton 2006
;
Zhang
et al. 2009
)
for surveys). Our framework utilizes such notions of rule interestingness, but rather than measuring the rules themselves, it explains how each individual element
contributes
to these scores.

(2)
Visualization interfaces for rules.
While one can use the notions of interestingness to prune uninteresting rules, the number of remaining rules may still be too large for manual analysis. To address this, works such as
(
Huebner 2009
)
suggest keeping a
diverse
set of rules, covering different parts of the dataset;
(
Lent
et al. 1997
;
Jaroszewicz and
Simovici 2002
)
suggest methods for grouping similar rules together, providing a shorter list of more general patterns; and
(
Wong
et al. 1999
;
Hahsler and
Karpienko 2017
)
describe visual interfaces for analyzing the mined rules in a matrix-like display, allowing users to examine how items (elements) are connected across multiple rules.

Our explainability framework provides a different way of analyzing the rules, showing which
elements
make the rules interesting. This is achieved by measuring the contribution of individual elements to the interestingness scores of rules. Such information can be used, as discussed in Section
4
, to detect redundant rules (i.e. those containing non-influential elements), as well as to examine the importance of
attributes
to the overall interestingness of the mined rules.

Explainability solutions for machine learning models

As machine learning models become increasingly complex (e.g., ensemble models, neural networks), there is a growing need for
explaining
model decisions
(
Doshi-Velez and
Kim 2017
)
. A multitude of work suggest solutions for post hoc analysis of model predictions
(
Shrikumar
et al. 2017
;
Sundararajan
et al. 2017
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
;
Lundberg and Lee 2017a
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2018
)
(see
(
Linardatos et al. 2021
)
for a survey). The most prominent method for explaining ML model predictions is by providing an assessment of
feature importance

(
Saarela and
Jauhiainen 2021
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
;
Lundberg and Lee 2017a
)
, by calculating the contribution of each feature to the predictive performance of the model—either locally
(
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
;
Lundberg and Lee 2017b
)
for a single prediction or globally
(
Zien et al. 2009
;
Ibrahim
et al. 2019
)
for all model predictions.

In the context of explaining association rules, we follow
(
Lundberg and Lee 2017b
)
, a highly popular ML explainability framework, and calculate element contribution based on the notion of Shapley Values
(
SHAPLEY 1953
)
. However, using Shapley values in our context requires a novel adaptation of the concept as well as dedicated algorithms to speed up the computation, as detailed in Section
3
. Without our optimized algorithms, as shown in our experimental evaluation, the calculation of SHARQ is infeasible in most rule-mining settings.

Explainability solutions for database queries

Explainability has also been studied within the data management community, focusing primarily on explaining the results of database queries
(
Green
et al. 2007
;
Buneman
et al. 2001
;
Chapman and
Jagadish 2009
;
Amsterdamer
et al. 2011
)
. This is often done by utilizing data provenance and causality-based notions such as intervention and influence to identify tuples whose existence or absence affects the result of the inspected query.

In particular,
(
Livshits et al. 2019
;
Davidson et al. 2022a
)

7.
Conclusions

SHARQ is a novel explainability framework for a set of association rules which measures each element’s contribution to the interestingness of the set. The metric is based on the notion of Shapley values, and captures the frequency of the element in the dataset as well as the variability in interestingness across rules of different lengths when the rule is excluded. Since calculating the SHARQ score of an element can be extremely expensive, we give an efficient
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm that is linear in the size of the rule set, and further reduce the cost for a set of elements in a multi-element
SHARQ{}^{*}
algorithm. We also show two additional use-cases, rule importance and attribute importance.
Extensive experiments show the effectiveness of this approach.

In future work, we plan to extend our approach to decision and classification rules, resulting from predictive ML models. We also plan to look at the problem of updates to the data/rule set, and whether SHARQ scores can be incrementally updated.
