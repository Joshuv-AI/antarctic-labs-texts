Stability of oppositely-propagating pair of Hill’s spherical vortices
Young-Jin Sim

Abstract
. We establish the stability of a pair of Hill’s spherical vortices moving away from each other in 3D incompressible axisymmetric Euler equations without swirl. Each vortex in the pair propagates away from its odd-symmetric counterpart, while keeping its vortex profile close to Hill’s vortex. This is achieved by analyzing the evolution of the interaction energy of the pair and combining it with the compactness of energy-maximizing sequences in the variational problem concerning Hill’s vortex. The key strategy is to confirm that, if the interaction energy is initially small enough, the kinetic energy of each vortex in the pair remains so close to that of a single Hill’s vortex for all time that each vortex profile stays close to the energy maximizer: Hill’s vortex. An estimate of the propagating speed of each vortex in the pair is also obtained by tracking the center of mass of each vortex. The estimate can be understood as optimal in the sense that the power exponent of the ε \varepsilon —the small perturbation measured in the ( L 1 ∩ L 2 L^{1}\cap L^{2} +impulse) norm—appearing in the error bound cannot be improved.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are concerned with the homogeneous incompressible Euler equations in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(in vorticity form):

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\,\partial_{t}\bm{\omega}+({\bf u}\cdot\nabla)\bm{\omega}=(\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla){\bf u}\quad\mbox{in}\mbox{ }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&\,{\bf u}=\nabla\times(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}\omega,\\
&\,\bm{\omega}|_{t=0}=\bm{\omega}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

with a given initial data
\bm{\omega}_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
where the
velocity

{\bf u}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfies the incompressibility

\nabla\cdot{\bf u}=0,
and the
vorticity

\bm{\omega}:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is given as

\bm{\omega}=\nabla\times{\bf u}.
The expression
{\bf u}=\nabla\times(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})^{-1}\omega
denotes the 3D Biot-Savart law. Assuming that the velocity is axisymmetric (about
z-
axis) without swirl, the vorticity
\bm{\omega}
has its axisymmetric angular component
\omega^{\theta}
only, i.e.,

\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega}(r,\theta,z)=\omega^{\theta}(r,z)\cdot\left(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0\right)

in cylindrical coordinates
{\bf x}=(r,\theta,z)
. Then we can reduce (
1.1
) to the following active scalar equation

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\,\partial_{t}\xi+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla\xi=0\quad\mbox{in}\mbox{ }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&\,\xi|_{t=0}=\xi_{0}\end{aligned}\right.

for the
relative vorticity

\xi:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\xi:=\omega^{\theta}(r,z)/r.
The velocity
{\bf u}
is recovered from
\xi
by the axisymmetric Biot-Savart law
{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}[\xi]
(see Subsection
1.2
).

In this setting, the Hill’s spherical vortex
\xi_{H}
defined by

\xi_{H}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|{\bf x}|<1\}}

is a traveling wave solution to (
1.2
) moving along the vector
{\bf e}_{z}:=(0,0,1)
in that, for the traveling speed
W_{H}=2/15
, the function
\xi=\xi(t,{\bf x})
defined by

\xi(t,{\bf x}):=\xi_{H}\left({\bf x}-tW_{H}{\bf e}_{z}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }t\geq 0,\,{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

solves (
1.2
). We refer to

Main statement

Our main result consists of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. Theorem
1.1

\xi_{H}

Theorem 1.1
.

(Stability of odd-symmetric pair of Hill’s vortices)
For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
and
d_{0}=d_{0}(\varepsilon)>1
satisfying the following:

For any
d\geq d_{0}
and for any axisymmetric initial data
\xi_{0}\in\left(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\cap L^{1}_{w}\right)(\mathbb{R}^{3})

r\xi_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

1.2.
3D axisymmetric Euler equations without swirl

For the velocity
{\bf u}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
in the cylindrical form

{\bf u}={\bf u}(r,\theta,z)=u^{r}(r,\theta,z){\bf e}_{r}(\theta)+u^{\theta}(r,\theta,z){\bf e}_{\theta}(\theta)+u^{z}(r,\theta,z){\bf e}_{z}

with the cylindrical coordinate
(r,\theta,z)
where the unit vectors are defined by

{\bf e}_{r}(\theta):=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),\mbox{ }{\bf e}_{\theta}(\theta):=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0),\mbox{ }{\bf e}_{z}=(0,0,1),

we say the velocity is
axisymmetric (about
z
-axis)
when each component
u^{r},u^{\theta},u^{z}
does not depend on
\theta
. The
no-swirl
condition stands for
u^{\theta}\equiv 0
. In this setting, the velocity
{\bf u}
has the form

{\bf u}={\bf u}(r,\theta,z)=u^{r}(r,z){\bf e}_{r}(\theta)+u^{\theta}(r,z){\bf e}_{\theta}(\theta)+u^{z}(r,z){\bf e}_{z},

and the vorticity
\bm{\omega}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
has the form

\bm{\omega}=\nabla\times{\bf u}=\omega^{\theta}(r,z){\bf e}_{\theta}(\theta)\quad\mbox{where}\mbox{ }\omega^{\theta}:=\partial_{z}u^{r}-\partial_{r}u^{z}.

By defining the relative vorticity
\xi
by

\xi(r,z):=\frac{1}{r}\omega^{\theta}(r,z),

the velocity is recovered by the following axisymmetric Biot-Savart law:

{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}[\xi]:=\nabla\times\left(\frac{1}{r}\mathcal{G}[\xi]{\bf e}_{\theta}\right)

where the function
\mathcal{G}[\xi]
is defined as

\mathcal{G}[\xi]=\mathcal{G}[\xi](r,z):=\int_{\Pi}G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\xi(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})r^{\prime}dr^{\prime}dz^{\prime}

for the domain
\Pi:=\{(r,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,r>0)\}
with the Green’s function given as

(1.4)

G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{rr^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos\vartheta}{\sqrt{r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}}\textrm{d}\vartheta\mbox{ }>0.

In specific, we have

(1.5)

{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}[\xi]=\frac{-\partial_{z}\mathcal{G}[\xi]}{r}{\bf e}_{r}(\theta)+\frac{\partial_{r}\mathcal{G}[\xi]}{r}{\bf e}_{z}.

Then the 3D Euler equations (
1.1
) are reduced to (
1.2
):

\partial_{t}\xi+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla\xi=0\quad\mbox{in}\mbox{ }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\mbox{where }{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}[\xi]

with the initial data
\xi_{0}
. It is well-known that, given axisymmetric initial data
\xi_{0}\in\left(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\right)(\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfying
\xi_{0}\in L^{1}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
r\xi_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, there exists a unique axisymmetric weak solution
\xi\in L^{\infty}\left(0,\infty;\left(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\right)(\mathbb{R}^{3})\right)
of (
1.2
) (see
[
17
, Remark 3.5]
). The solution has several quantities that are conserved in time:

(i)

L^{p}
norms:

\|\xi(t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\quad\mbox{for each }p\in[1,\infty]

(ii)
Kinetic energy:

E[\xi(t)]:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\xi(t)\mathcal{G}[\xi(t)]\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

(iii)
Distribution:

\int_{\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:a<\xi(t)<b\}}\xi(t)\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }-\infty<a<b<\infty.

Moreover, if
\xi_{0}
has the odd symmetry with respect to the plane
\{x_{3}=0\}
:

\xi_{0}(r,z)=-\xi_{0}(r,-z)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }z\geq 0,

its solution
\xi(t)
also has the odd symmetry: for each
t\geq 0
, we have

\xi(t,(r,z))=-\xi(t,(r,-z))\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }r>0,\,z\geq 0.

1.3.
Key ideas

1.3.1.
Interaction between two vortices

For the governing equation (
1.2
), the kinetic energy
E[\xi]
is conserved in time
t\geq 0
, and has a bilinear form in
\xi
:

E[\xi]=\mathcal{E}[\xi,\xi]:=\pi\iint_{\Pi\times\Pi}G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\xi(t,(r,z))\xi(t,(r^{\prime},z^{\prime}))rr^{\prime}\,\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime},

for the Green’s function
G
with the domain
\Pi:=\{(r,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,r>0)\}
. Using this conservation,
[
17
]
obtained the stability of single Hill’s spherical vortex
\xi_{H}
:

Theorem 1.5
.

[
17
, Theorem 1.2]

For
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for any non-negative axisymmetric function
\xi_{0}
satisfying

\xi_{0},\,r\xi_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

and

\|\xi_{0}-\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|r^{2}(\xi_{0}-\xi_{H})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq\delta,

the corresponding solution
\xi(t)
of (
1.2
) satisfies

\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\|\xi(\cdot+\tau{\bf e}_{z})-\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\|r^{2}(\xi(\cdot+\tau{\bf e}_{z})-\xi_{H})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right\}\leq\varepsilon,\mbox{ }\forall t\geq 0.

The main idea was that, for any solution
\xi(t)
, if its kinetic energy
E[\xi(t)]\simeq E[\xi_{H}]
is conserved in time, then the global-in-time stability is obtained:

\xi\simeq\xi_{H}\mbox{ }\mbox{up to translation},

based on some energy-maximization problem concerning Hill’s vortex
\xi_{H}
(See Subsection
2.3
). In this paper, on the other hand, we assume that the solution has odd symmetry

\xi(t,(r,z))=-\xi(t,(r,-z))\geq 0\quad\mbox{for }r>0,z\geq 0,

we decompose the solution
\xi=\xi(t)
as

\xi=\xi^{+}-\xi^{-}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\xi^{+}=\xi\big|_{z>0}\mbox{ }\mbox{and}\mbox{ }\xi^{-}=-\xi\big|_{z<0},

to observe that

(1.6)

E[\xi(0)]=E[\xi(t)]=2\mathcal{E}\left[\xi^{+}(t),\xi^{+}(t)\right]-2\mathcal{E}\left[\xi^{+}(t),\xi^{-}(t)\right]=2\underbrace{E[\xi^{+}(t)]}_{>0}-2\underbrace{E_{inter}(t)}_{>0}

for the interaction energy

E_{inter}(t)=\mathcal{E}[\xi^{+}(t),\xi^{-}(t)]>0

(Its positivity stems from the odd symmetry of
\xi
). Here we can see that, despite the conservation of total kinetic energy

E[\xi]=E\left[\xi^{+}-\xi^{-}\right]
, the kinetic energy of the non-negative part
E[\xi^{+}]
and the interaction energy
E_{inter}
are not conserved in time.
The fact that
E[\xi^{+}]
is not conserved is a main obstacle in proving the stability of two oppositely signed Hill’s spherical vortices in the same way as
[
17
]
. We overcome this obstacle based on the strategy of
keeping
E_{inter}(t)
small enough for all time

and so maintaining the kinetic energy
E[\xi^{+}(t)]
close enough to the initial energy
E[\xi^{+}(0)]
, though it is not conserved. We obtain
E_{inter}(t)\ll 1
by following steps:

[Step 1] In the relation

0<E_{inter}(t)=E[\xi^{+}(t)]-E[\xi^{+}(0)]+E_{inter}(0),

we choose initial data
\xi^{+}(0)
close enough to
\xi_{H}
(up to translation) and obtain

E[\xi^{+}(0)]\simeq E[\xi_{H}].

[Step 2] We prove that

\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}}<\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(0)\|_{L^{1}}\quad\mbox{for }t\geq 0

(see Lemma
2.1
), which would turn out to imply

E[\xi^{+}(t)]<E[\xi_{H}]+\varepsilon\simeq E[\xi^{+}(0)]+\varepsilon

with some small error
\varepsilon\ll 1
. This is achieved by showing that the decrease of the impulse
\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}}
prevents the solution
\xi^{+}(t)
from possessing the kinetic energy much larger than the Hill’s vortex
\xi_{H}
: the energy maximizer under the fixed impulse.

[Step 3] Roughly speaking, we have obtained

0<E_{inter}(t)<E_{inter}(0)+\varepsilon.

We also get
E_{inter}(0)=\mathcal{E}\left[\xi^{+}(0),\xi^{-}(0)\right]\ll 1
by assuming that the supports of
\xi^{+}(0)
and
\xi^{-}(0)
are far away from one another. Hence, we get

E_{inter}(t)\ll 1.

These sorts of ideas can be found in
[
22
]
, which dealt with the odd-pair of the Chaplygin-Lamb dipole.

1.3.2.
Shift function estimate

As a function of time
t\geq 0
, the shift function
\tau:[0,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
in Theorem
1.1
refers to the
z
-translation of the Hill’s spherical vortex
\xi_{H}
to which
the positive part of the solution
\xi(t)
is close enough, i.e.,

\xi^{+}(t)=\xi(t)\big|_{z>0}\simeq\xi_{H}(\cdot-\tau(t){\bf e}_{z}).

Since
\xi_{H}
travels in time with the constant velocity
W_{H}{\bf e}_{z}
with
W_{H}=2/15
, we expect that
the shift function
\tau(t)
is close enough to
W_{H}t
in a finite time interval
(Theorem
1.2
), i.e.,

\tau(t)-\tau(0)\simeq W_{H}t.

Recalling that
\xi_{H}
has even symmetry:

\xi_{H}(r,z)=\xi_{H}(r,-z)\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }(r,z)\in\Pi

which implies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}x_{3}\xi_{H}\textrm{d}{\bf x}=0,
we note that the shift function
\tau(t)
is obtained by the relation

(1.7)

\tau(t)=\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}x_{3}\xi_{H}(\cdot-\tau(t){\bf e}_{z})\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}\,\simeq\,\|\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}x_{3}\xi^{+}(t)\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

implying that the
center of mass on
z
-axis of the solution
\xi^{+}(t)
is close to the translation
\tau(t)
. Similarly, the traveling speed
W_{H}
of the Hill’s vortex
\xi_{H}
is obtained by the relation

(1.8)

W_{H}t=\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}x_{3}\xi_{H}(\cdot-tW_{H}{\bf e}_{z})\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}.

Using the observations (
1.7
) and (
1.8
), we may obtain
\tau(t)-\tau(0)\simeq W_{H}t
by proving that

(1.9)

\|\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}x_{3}\xi^{+}(t)\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}-\mbox{ }\tau(0)\mbox{ }\simeq\mbox{ }\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}x_{3}\xi_{H}(\cdot-tW_{H}{\bf e}_{z})\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}.

Here, one may conceive a complication in that
\xi^{+}(t)
is the positive part of the actual solution
\xi(t)
of (
1.2
), while
\xi_{H}(\cdot-tW_{H}{\bf e}_{z})
denotes the genuine (traveling) solution of (
1.2
). However, we can expect the following:

As long as the positive part
\xi^{+}(t)
and negative part
\xi^{-}(t)
are at a long distance, i.e.,

\tau(t)\gg 1
, the dynamics of
\xi^{+}(t)
and
\xi_{H}(\cdot-tW_{H}{\bf e}_{z})
are somehow similar in the sense of (
1.9
).

Such an expectation is justified if one considers the time-derivative on both sides of (
1.9
) and the integration by parts in both integrals. Essentially, we come up with a bootstrap argument with the following steps:

[Step 1] At the initial time
t=0
, we assume that
\xi^{+}(0)
and
\xi^{-}(0)
are distant to each other, i.e.,

\tau(0)\gg 1,

so that (
1.9
) holds in a certain time interval
[0,T]
.

[Step 2] In the interval
[0,T]
, the relation (
1.9
) gives

\tau(T)>\tau(0)\gg 1,

so that we go back to [Step 1] and repeat the same process.

[Step 3] We obtain that
\tau(t)\gg 1
for all
t\geq 0
, and so we obtain (
1.9
) for all
t\geq 0
. It gives

\tau(t)-\tau(0)\simeq W_{H}t.

We refer to
[
23
]
for a similar idea to obtain the shift estimate in two-dimensional flow, concerning the stability of the Sadovskii vortex patches constructed in
[
21
]
by a variational approach. See also
[
20
]
for another way to estimate the translation of perturbed data near a single Hill’s vortex.

1.4.
Related topics

1.4.1.
Concerning Hill’s spherical vortex

Hill’s spherical vortex, initially introduced by M. Hill in
[
42
]
, is an exact traveling wave solution to the equations of inviscid, incompressible, axisymmetric fluid flow. In this model, a spherical region
\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,|{\bf x}|<1\}
contains a nonzero vorticity, while the surrounding fluid remains irrotational. Further analytical treatment was provided in Hydrodynamics
[
50
]
, formalizing the spherical vortex as a steady, axisymmetric solution to Euler’s equations. It is later extended to a one-parameter family of vortex rings, incorporating Hill’s vortex as a limiting case, together with some numerical descriptions (
[
55
,
56
]
). Shapes and properties of steady vortex rings with small cross-sections were explored in
[
31
]
, emphasizing their connection to Hill’s vortex.

On the other hand, several studies have focused on the properties of Hill’s vortex, including the uniqueness and stability/instability. In
[
5
]
, it was rigorously proved that Hill’s vortex is the unique (weak) solution to Hill’s problem of finding steady vortex rings under certain choices of vorticity function
\left(f(\cdot)=\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot>0\}}\right)
and the flux constant (
\gamma=0
). A nonlinear evolution of several axisymmetric perturbations to Hill’s spherical vortex was observed in
[
59
]
using numerical simulations, indicating its nonlinear instability. The linear stability of Hill’s vortex to axisymmetric perturbations is given in
[
60
]
, based on a spectral approach. In
[
65
]
, it was shown that the Hill’s vortex is a nondegenerate local maximum of the kinetic energy among vortex rings subject to a fixed impulse. Recently, the Lyapunov stability was obtained in
[
17
]
, showing that Hill’s vortex is the unique maximizer of the kinetic energy under fixed impulse and circulation constraints,
which is one of the main ingredients of the current paper.
Later, the linear-in-time filamentation near Hill’s vortex was shown in
[
20
]

1.4.3.
Multi-vortex problems

As the long-time dynamics of the Euler flow is a highly challenging problem in general, the multi-vortex problems are expected to play a role in understanding the evolution of the Euler flow as
t\to\infty
. It is to ask whether several coherent vortex structures–each of which would move coherently (translate, rotate, etc) if isolated–can be arranged so that they keep their shapes while their mutual interaction weakens over time, typically because they separate.

The key strategy is to construct a configuration of well‑separated vortex objects and prove that the residual interaction is too small to destroy their rigidity/coherency. In vortex dynamics, canonical building blocks might include translating dipoles (e.g., Chaplygin-Lamb dipole, Sadovskii vortex patches), rotating V-states (e.g., Rankine vortex, Kirchhoff ellipse), axisymmetric vortex rings including Hill’s vortex, and desingularized point vortex (or point-vortex ring). The question is whether copies of such structures can coexist, either moving apart or forming choreographed motions, without significant deformation. Several recent works have used the Chaplygin-Lamb dipole as a building block to construct and solve multi-vortex problems; see
[
22
]
for the case of the dipole pair moving away from each other, and see
[
2
]
for the stability of finite sums of the dipoles moving together in one direction, in which the faster dipoles are positioned to the right of the slower ones.

Several equilibria or explicit dynamics of point vortex system (e.g., various pairs, Thomson polygons, Von Kármán vortex streets, self-similar expanding triples, leapfrogging motions) provide a sketch of multi-vortex configurations in many cases (see
[
41

for the initial works of the point vortex system, followed by a vast amount of subsequent works; e.g., see

and references therein), the system has several equilibria consisting of multiple point vortices.

For example, a point-vortex quadruple with odd-odd symmetry can be viewed as a multi-vortex configuration comprised of an odd pair of traveling dipoles. The quadruple has an explicit trajectory (

1.5.
Open questions

We establish some open questions related to the current paper.

1.5.1.
Asymptotic behavior.

Compared to the case of the single Hill’s vortex, the variational approach for the odd-symmetric pair of Hill’s vortices seems to demonstrate the possibility of a time-asymptotic behavior of perturbed initial data under some proper constraints. Here, we give some observations.

Observation 1: The energy of a single vortex is convergent as

E[\xi^{+}(t)]\to\frac{1}{2}E[\xi(0)]\quad\mbox{as}\mbox{ }t\to\infty.

Theorem
1.2
implies that, for any perturbed initial data
\xi_{0}
, the interaction energy given by

E_{inter}(t)=\mathcal{E}\left[\xi^{+}(t),\xi^{-}(t)\right]=\pi\iint_{\Pi\times\Pi}G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\xi^{+}(t,(r,z))\xi^{-}(t,(r^{\prime},z^{\prime}))\mbox{ }\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}

vanishes as
t\to\infty
. Then, from the energy relation (
1.6
):

E[\xi(t)]=2E[\xi^{+}(t)]-2E_{inter}(t)

where the total energy is conserved as
E[\xi(t)]=E[\xi(0)]
for any
t\geq 0
, it holds that the energy of the single vortex,
E[\xi^{+}(t)]
, satisfies

E[\xi^{+}(t)]\to\frac{1}{2}E[\xi(0)]\quad\mbox{as}\mbox{ }t\to\infty.

Observation 2: The impulse of a single vortex is convergent in that, for some
\mu>0
, we have

\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}}\to\mu\quad\mbox{as}\mbox{ }t\to\infty.

It follows from the facts that
\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}}
decreases in
t\geq 0
(by Lemma
2.1
) and that
\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}}
is bounded from below (due to
\left|\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}}-\|r^{2}\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}\right|\ll 1
).

The above observations imply that both energy
E[\xi^{+}(t)]
and the impulse
\|r^{2}\xi^{+}(t)\|_{L^{1}}
are convergent. Suppose we can find a way to characterize the impulse limit
\mu>0
and find
\xi(0)
such that the energy limit
\frac{1}{2}E[\xi(0)]
is the maximal energy under the impulse constraint
\mu
. Then we may use the concentrated-compactness argument given in Theorem
2.8
to prove that the solution
\xi^{+}(t)
asymptotically approaches a maximizer set. After certain scaling, the Hill’s vortex is a unique maximizer (Theorem
2.5
), implying that the solution
\xi^{+}(t)
tends to the (scaled) Hill’s vortex, up to translations.

1.5.2.
Non-symmetric perturbed solutions

Theorem
1.1
concerns with initial data
\xi_{0}
satisfying the symmetry

\xi_{0}(r,z)=-\xi_{0}(r,-z)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }r,z\geq 0.

Such odd-symmetry easily implies that the impulse
\|r^{2}\xi(t)\|_{L^{1}}
decreases for all
t\geq 0
, which would guarantees that the interaction energy
E_{inter}(t)=\mathcal{E}[\xi^{+}(t),\xi^{-}(t)]
tends to
0
as
t\geq\infty
, so that each of
\xi^{+}(t),\xi^{-}(t)
stays close to the Hill’s vortex up to translations.

We now suppose that
\xi_{0}
only has the sign condition:

\xi_{0}(r,z),\,-\xi_{0}(r,-z)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }r,z\geq 0,

while
\xi_{0}
is close to the odd-symmetric pair of Hill’s vortex. In Lemma
2.1
, the decrease of impulse
\|r^{2}\xi(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}
relies heavily on such a sign condition, not on the symmetry itself, (at least) up to a finite time. We believe that one can prove the global-in-time stability after proper bootstrap arguments.

1.5.3.
Other types of multi-vortex

Even though the current paper concerns the pair of Hill’s vortex, it can be generalized to other types of vortices that are characterized as energy maximizers under certain constraints; e.g., see
[
22
, Remark 2.9]
for a similar discussion. One can also try to increase the number of vortices to more than two, provided that the interaction decreases under certain configurations. As far as the interaction energy vanishes, the vortices will nearly retain their initial shapes, implying their stability.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notations

•

We define the half-space
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
as

\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}:=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,x_{3}>0\right\}.

•

We define
\langle\cdot\rangle_{+}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\langle s\rangle_{+}:=\max\{s,0\}.

•

We define

\Pi:=\left\{(r,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,r>0\right\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q:=\left\{(r,z)\in\Pi:\,z>0\right\}.

•

We denote each norm of
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
as

\|f\|_{p}:=\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

•

We define the weighted space as

L^{1}_{w}(D):=\left\{f=(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{-1}g:\,g\in L^{1}(D)\right\}\quad\mbox{for given domain}\mbox{ }D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{1}_{w}(D)}:=\int_{D}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})|f({\bf x})|\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}.

For convenience, for the domain
D=\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we denote the norm of
L^{1}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as

\|r^{2}f\|_{1}:=\|f\|_{L^{1}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

which will be called as
impulse
.

2.2.
Anti-parallel flow

In subsection
1.2
, the 3D axisymmetric Euler equations with no swirl have the reduced form (
1.2
):

\partial_{t}\xi+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla\xi=0\quad\mbox{in}\mbox{ }[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\mbox{where }{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}[\xi]

with a given initial data
\xi_{0}
. Specifically, we deal with the solution that has odd symmetry:

\xi(t,(r,z))=-\xi(t,(r,-z))\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }r>0,\,z\geq 0.

In other words, we consider the anti-parallel flow that is non-negative in the upper half-space
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
. In this case, we identify the non-negative part
\langle\xi\rangle_{+}
of the solution
\xi
as the restriction of
\xi
to the half space
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
.
Then the following lemma shows that the impulse of such a solution decreases strictly in time (We do not claim any novelty of this result. For example, it can be found from
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
. Still, we give the proof for completeness).

Lemma 2.1
.

For any nontrivial initial data
\xi_{0}\in\left(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\cap L^{1}_{w}\right)(\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfying
r\xi_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with the odd symmetry

\xi_{0}(r,z)=-\xi_{0}(r,-z)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }r>0,\,z\geq 0,

the impulse of its unique solution
\xi\in L^{\infty}\left(0,\infty;\left(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\right)(\mathbb{R}^{3})\right)
of (
1.2
),

\|r^{2}\xi(t)\|_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})|\xi(t)|\,\textrm{d}{\bf x},

decreases strictly in
t\geq 0.

Proof.

We note that the solution
\xi(t)
also has odd symmetry with a sign condition as

\xi(t,(r,z))=-\xi(t,(r,-z))\geq 0\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }r>0,z\geq 0.

Then we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|r^{2}\xi\|_{1}=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{x_{3}>0}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\xi\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\,\int_{x_{3}>0}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\partial_{t}\xi\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\,-\int_{x_{3}>0}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})({\bf u}\cdot\nabla\xi)\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\,2\int_{x_{3}>0}((x_{1},x_{2},0)\cdot{\bf u})\xi\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\,4\pi\int_{Q}\left(r^{2}u^{r}\right)\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z

using integration by parts. We recall (
1.5
) , which gives

u^{r}(r,z)=-\frac{\partial_{z}\mathcal{G}[\xi](r,z)}{r}.

Here, considering the odd symmetry of
\xi
, we put

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}[\xi](r,z)

\displaystyle=\,\int_{\Pi}G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\xi(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\,\int_{Q}\left[G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})-G(r,z,r^{\prime},-z^{\prime})\right]\langle\xi(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle_{+}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}.

Then we observe that

G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{rr^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos\vartheta}{\sqrt{r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}}}\textrm{d}\vartheta=\mathscr{G}(r,r^{\prime},z-z^{\prime})

for the function
\mathscr{G}(r,r^{\prime},\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\mathscr{G}(r,r^{\prime},s):=\frac{rr^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\cos\vartheta}{\sqrt{r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+s^{2}}}\textrm{d}\vartheta

for fixed
r,r^{\prime}>0
. Then we rewrite (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|r^{2}\xi\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\,-4\pi\int_{Q\times Q}\left[\left(\partial_{s}\mathscr{G}\right)(r,r^{\prime},z-z^{\prime})-\left(\partial_{s}\mathscr{G}\right)(r,r^{\prime},z+z^{\prime})\right]\langle\xi(r,z)\rangle_{+}\langle\xi(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle_{+}rr^{\prime}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=:\,-4\pi\left[(I)-(II)\right].

We observe that the right-hand side is negative given that the following properties hold for fixed
r,r^{\prime}>0
:

(i)
\partial_{s}\mathscr{G}(r,r^{\prime},s)
is an odd function in
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii) It holds that
\partial_{s}\mathscr{G}(r,r^{\prime},s)<0
for each
s>0
.

Indeed, we observe that (i) implies

(I)=\int_{Q\times Q}\left(\partial_{s}\mathscr{G}\right)(r,r^{\prime},z-z^{\prime})\cdot\langle\xi(r,z)\rangle_{+}\langle\xi(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle_{+}rr^{\prime}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}=0

by changing the variable
z\leftrightarrow z^{\prime}
, and that (ii) implies

(II)=\int_{Q\times Q}\left(\partial_{s}\mathscr{G}\right)(r,r^{\prime},z+z^{\prime})\cdot\langle\xi(r,z)\rangle_{+}\langle\xi(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle_{+}rr^{\prime}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}<0.

Then we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|r^{2}\xi\|_{1}=-4\pi\left[(I)-(II)\right]<0

which completes the proof of Lemma
2.1
. It remains to show the properties (i) and (ii). We first observe that (i) follows directly from the observation that
\mathscr{G}(r,r^{\prime},s)
is an even function in
s\in\mathbb{R}
. For (ii), we observe that

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mathscr{G}(r,r^{\prime},s)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{rr^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{-s\cdot\cos\vartheta}{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+s^{2})^{3/2}}\textrm{d}\vartheta

\displaystyle=\,\frac{rr^{\prime}}{2\pi}\left[\int_{0}^{\pi/2}+\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{rr^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left[\frac{-s\cdot\cos\vartheta}{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+s^{2})^{3/2}}+\frac{s\cdot\cos\vartheta}{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}+2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+s^{2})^{3/2}}\right]\textrm{d}\vartheta

\displaystyle=\frac{rr^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\underbrace{(s\cdot\cos\vartheta)}_{>0}\underbrace{\left[\frac{-1}{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+s^{2})^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{(r^{2}+r^{\prime 2}+2rr^{\prime}\cos\vartheta+s^{2})^{3/2}}\right]}_{<0}\textrm{d}\vartheta<0

given that
s>0
. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

2.1
implies that the velocity
{\bf u}=\mathcal{K}[\xi]
has a uniform bound in space-time, given the odd symmetry of the solution:

\xi(t,(r,z))=-\xi(t,(r,-z))\geq 0\quad\mbox{for each }r>0,\,z\geq 0.

To see this, we refer to an inequality:

(2.2)

\|\mathcal{K}[\xi]\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim\|\xi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|\xi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\|\xi\|_{L^{1}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}

given in
[

]
. While
L^{1}
and

norms are conserved:

\|\xi(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\xi(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

Lemma
2.1
says that the quantity

\|\langle\xi(t)\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

decreases strictly in

if we additionally assume the odd symmetry:

\xi_{0}(r,z)=-\xi_{0}(r,-z)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\mbox{ }r>0,\,z\geq 0.

Then we obtain a uniform bound of velocity:

(2.3)

Remark 2.3

For a family of parameters

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\xi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):\,\xi=\lambda\mathbf{1}_{A}\mbox{ }\mbox{for some axisymmetric}\,A\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\,\frac{1}{2}\|r^{2}\xi\|_{1}=\mu,\,\|\xi\|_{1}\leq\nu\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\xi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):\,\xi:\mbox{axisymmetric,}\,0\leq\xi\leq\lambda,\,\frac{1}{2}\|r^{2}\xi\|_{1}\leq\mu,\,\|\xi\|_{1}\leq\nu\right\}

together with the maximal kinetic energies

\displaystyle=\|\xi_{H}^{\mu}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}=\|\xi_{H}^{\mu}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\nu_{\mu},

and

convergence

|\tau(t)-y_{3}|\leq 1.
On the other hand, by the assumption (
4.6
), we observe that the starting point
{\bf y}^{0,t}\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\langle\xi_{0}\rangle_{+}
of the point
{\bf y}^{t}\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,\langle\xi(t)\rangle_{+}
with the relation

{\bf y}^{t}=\phi(t,{\bf y}^{0,t})

satisfies

|\tau(0)-y_{3}^{0,t}|\leq 2.

Then we have

\displaystyle|\tau(t)-\tau(0)|

\displaystyle\leq\,|\tau(t)-y_{3}^{t}|+|\tau(0)-y_{3}^{0,t}|+|y_{3}^{t}-y_{3}^{0,t}|

\displaystyle\leq\,1+2+|\phi(t,{\bf y}^{0,t})-{\bf y}^{0,t}|

\displaystyle\leq\,3+t\cdot\sup_{s\geq 0}\|{\bf u}(s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

and by the same argument with (
4.10
), we get

|\tau(t)-\tau(0)|\lesssim 1+\mathcal{M}^{1/2}t.

We plug the estimate above into (
4.11
) and obtain the estimate (
4.1
):

|z_{c}(t)-\tau(t)|\lesssim\varepsilon\left(1+\mathcal{M}^{1/2}t\right).

[Step 2] It holds that

(4.12)

\left|\frac{d}{dt}z_{c}(t)-W_{H}\right|<C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\tau(t)-1}\right)\quad\mbox{if}\,\tau(t)\geq 2

for some uniform constant
C_{2}>0
.

We observe that

(4.13)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}z_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=\,\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}^{-1}\int_{x_{3}>0}x_{3}\partial_{t}\xi\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\,\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}^{-1}\int_{x_{3}>0}\xi u^{z}\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\,\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\left(\mathcal{K}[\xi]\cdot{\bf e}_{z}\right)\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}

Here, we define a bilinear operator
\mathcal{B}
as

\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\xi_{1}\left(\mathcal{K}[\xi_{2}]\cdot{\bf e}_{z}\right)\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}\quad\mbox{for axisymmetric}\mbox{ }\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\left(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\cap L^{1}_{w}\right)(\mathbb{R}^{3})

to obtain a simplified version of (
4.13
) as

(4.14)

\frac{d}{dt}z_{c}(t)=\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{B}\left[\langle\xi\rangle_{+},\xi\right].

For a moment, we observe several properties of the operator
\mathcal{B}
.

(i)
Bound of
\mathcal{B}
:
We observe that

|B[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]|\leq\|\xi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\|\mathcal{K}[\xi_{2}]\|_{L^{2}}

where

\|\mathcal{K}[\xi_{2}]\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=E[\xi_{2}]\lesssim\left(\|r^{2}\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}+\|\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}}\right)\|r^{2}\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}\|\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}^{1/2}.

Here, we used the energy bound (
2.5
) in Lemma
2.7
to obtain the last relation, and especially for the first equality, e.g., see
[
17
, Lemma 2.4]
.
Then we get the bound

(4.15)

|B[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]|\lesssim\|\xi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{\mbox{ }}\left(\|r^{2}\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}+\|\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\|r^{2}\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}^{1/4}\|\xi_{2}\|_{L^{1}}^{1/4}.

(ii)
Translation invariance:
By the relation

\mathcal{K}[\xi]\cdot{\bf e}_{z}=\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\mathcal{G}[\xi]

and the fact that
\partial_{r}G(r,r^{\prime},z,z^{\prime})
is a function of triple of variables
(r,r^{\prime},|z-z^{\prime}|)
, the bilinear operator
\mathcal{B}
has the translation invariance in
z
-axis in that, by denoting the shift operator as
f^{\tau}:=f(\cdot-\tau{\bf e}_{z})
for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]=\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}^{\tau},\xi_{2}^{\tau}]\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

(iii)
Traveling speed
W_{H}
of Hill’
s vortex
\xi_{H}
:
As the solution
\Xi
defined by

\Xi(t,{\bf x}):=\xi_{H}^{W_{H}t}
solves (
1.2
), we repeat the same process with (
4.13
) to get

(4.16)

\displaystyle W_{H}=\frac{d}{dt}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\|\Xi\|_{L^{1}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}x_{3}\Xi\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}\right)}_{=W_{H}t}\,=\,\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{B}\left[\Xi,\Xi\right]\,=\,\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{B}\left[\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)},\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}\right].

Then, using
(i)
-
(iii)
above, we will obtain (
4.12
). First, we combine (
4.14
) and (
4.16
) to observe that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}z_{c}(t)-W_{H}\right|

\displaystyle=\,\left|\frac{\mathcal{B}\left[\langle\xi\rangle_{+},\xi\right]}{\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}}-\frac{\mathcal{B}\left[\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)},\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}\right]}{\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\,\left|\frac{\mathcal{B}\left[\langle\xi\rangle_{+},\xi\right]}{\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}}-\frac{\mathcal{B}\left[\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)},\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}-\xi_{H}^{-\tau(t)}\right]}{\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}}\right|+\frac{\mathcal{B}\left[\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)},\xi_{H}^{-\tau(t)}\right]}{\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}}

\displaystyle=:\,(III)\mbox{ }+\mbox{ }(IV).

Our initial assumption (
4.3
), together with (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), gives

\displaystyle(III)

\displaystyle\leq\,\underbrace{\frac{|\,\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}-\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}|}{\|\langle\xi\rangle_{+}\|_{L^{1}}\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}}}_{\lesssim\varepsilon}\cdot\left|\mathcal{B}[\langle\xi\rangle_{+},\xi]\right|+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}}}_{\lesssim 1}\cdot\left|\mathcal{B}[\langle\xi\rangle_{+},\xi]-\mathcal{B}[\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)},\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}-\xi_{H}^{-\tau(t)}]\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\varepsilon\left|\mathcal{B}[\langle\xi\rangle_{+},\xi]\right|+\left|\mathcal{B}[\langle\xi\rangle_{+}-\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)},\,\xi]\right|+\left|\mathcal{B}[\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)},\,\xi-(\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}-\xi_{H}^{-\tau(t)})]\right|.

We now apply the initial assumption (
4.3
) and the bound (
4.15
) to obtain that

(III)\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon.

For
(IV)
, based on the observation that

|\partial_{r}G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{r^{\prime}}{[(r-r^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}]^{1/2}}+\frac{rr^{\prime}(r+r^{\prime})}{[(r-r^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2}]^{3/2}}\lesssim_{r,r^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}|}+\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}|^{3}}

which follows directly from the definition (
1.4
), we obtain that

\displaystyle|(IV)|

\displaystyle=\,\left|\frac{1}{\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{r}\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}\partial_{r}\mathcal{G}[\xi_{H}^{-\tau(t)}]\,\textrm{d}{\bf x}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\frac{1}{\|\xi_{H}\|_{L^{1}}}\int_{\Pi\times\Pi}\left|\partial_{r}G(r,z,r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\right|\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}(r,z)\xi_{H}^{-\tau(t)}(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})r^{\prime}\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\int_{\Pi\times\Pi}\left[\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}|}+\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}|^{3}}\right]\xi_{H}^{\tau(t)}(r,z)\xi_{H}^{-\tau(t)}(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\,\int_{\Pi\times\Pi}\left[\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}+2\tau(t)|}+\frac{1}{|z-z^{\prime}+2\tau(t)|^{3}}\right]\xi_{H}(r,z)\xi_{H}(r^{\prime},z^{\prime})\textrm{d}r^{\prime}\textrm{d}z^{\prime}\textrm{d}r\textrm{d}z

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\frac{1}{|\tau(t)-1|}+\frac{1}{|\tau(t)-1|^{3}}

which leads to

\left|(IV)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau(t)-1}

given that
\tau(t)\geq 2.
It proves (
4.12
):

\left|\frac{d}{dt}z_{c}(t)-W_{H}\right|\,\leq\,(III)+(IV)\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\tau(t)-1}\quad\mbox{given that}\mbox{ }\tau(t)\geq 2.

[Step 3] The estimate (
4.2
) holds:

|z_{c}(t)-z_{c}(0)-W_{H}t|<C_{3}\varepsilon t\quad\mbox{for each }t\geq 0

for some uniform constant
C_{3}>0
.

We observe that (
4.1
) implies

(4.17)

\tau(t)\geq z_{c}(t)-C_{0}\varepsilon(t+1)\quad\mbox{for }t\geq 0

for some constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(\mathcal{M})>0
. Since (
4.7
) and (
4.10
) give

\displaystyle z_{c}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\,z_{c}(0)-\sup_{s\geq 0}\|{\bf u}(s)\|_{L^{\infty}}t

\displaystyle>\,d-\varepsilon-C_{1}t\cdot\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/2}\|\xi_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}\|r^{2}\xi_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1/4}

\displaystyle\geq\,d-\varepsilon-C_{4}t\mathcal{M}^{1/2}t

for some uniform constant
C_{4}>0
, the inequality (
4.17
) leads to

\tau(t)\geq d-t\cdot\left(C_{4}\mathcal{M}^{1/2}+\varepsilon C_{0}\right)-\varepsilon(1+C_{0}).

We suppose that
\varepsilon>0
is small enough to satisfy

\varepsilon(1+C_{0})\leq\frac{1}{4}

and apply
d>\varepsilon^{-1}>1
to get

\tau(t)\geq\frac{3}{4}d-t\cdot\left(C_{4}M^{1/2}+\varepsilon C_{0}\right).

So there exists
T>0
such that

\tau(t)>\frac{d}{2}\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,T].

For each
t\in[0,T]
, by assuming
\varepsilon<1/4
, we get

\tau(t)>\frac{d}{2}\geq\frac{d_{0}(\varepsilon)}{2}>\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}>2,

and we can use (
4.12
) to obtain

(4.18)

\left|\frac{d}{dt}z_{c}(t)-W_{H}\right|<C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4}{d}\right)\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,T]

and so

(4.19)

|z_{c}(t)-z_{c}(0)-W_{H}t|<C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4}{d}\right)\cdot t\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,T].

It remains to show that the above inequality holds for all
t\geq 0
, since the relation
d\geq d_{0}(\varepsilon)>\varepsilon^{-1}
will be combined with the above relation to obtain (
4.2
). We assume the contrary, i.e., we suppose that (
4.19
)
ceases to hold for the first time at
t=\widetilde{T}\geq T
for some
\widetilde{T}<\infty
, i.e.,

\widetilde{T}:=\sup\left\{T\geq 0:\,|z_{c}(t)-z_{c}(0)-W_{H}t|<C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4}{d}\right)\cdot t\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,T]\right\}<\infty.

Then the continuity of
z_{c}(\cdot)
gives

(4.20)

|z_{c}(t)-z_{c}(0)-W_{H}t|<C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4}{d}\right)\cdot t\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,\widetilde{T})

and

(4.21)

|z(\widetilde{T})-z_{c}(0)-W_{H}\widetilde{T}|=C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4}{d}\right)\cdot\widetilde{T}.

Then (
4.20
) and (
4.7
) lead to

\displaystyle z_{c}(t)

\displaystyle>\,z_{c}(0)+\left(W_{H}-C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+4/d\right)\right)t

\displaystyle\geq\,d-\varepsilon+\left(W_{H}-5C_{2}\varepsilon\right)t\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,\widetilde{T})

due to
d\geq d_{0}(\varepsilon)>\varepsilon^{-1}
, and combining this result with (
4.17
) gives us

\displaystyle\tau(t)

\displaystyle\geq\,z_{c}(t)-C_{0}\varepsilon(t+1)

\displaystyle\geq\,d-\varepsilon+\left(W_{H}-5C_{2}\varepsilon\right)t-C_{0}\varepsilon(t+1)

\displaystyle=\,d-\underbrace{\varepsilon(1+C_{0})}_{(V)}+\underbrace{\left(W_{H}-(5C_{2}+C_{0})\varepsilon\right)}_{(VI)}t\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,\widetilde{T}).

Recall that we have assumed
\varepsilon>0
is small enough such that
(V)<\frac{1}{4}d.
So we get

\tau(t)>\frac{3}{4}d+(VI)\cdot t\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,\widetilde{T}).

We further assume
\varepsilon>0
is small enough to get
(VI)>0
(the smallness of
\varepsilon>0
depends only on
C_{2}>0
and
C_{0}=C_{0}(\mathcal{M})>0
, and so it depends only on
\mathcal{M}>0
). Then we get

\tau(t)>\frac{3}{4}d>2\quad\mbox{and so}\quad\tau(t)-1>\frac{1}{2}d\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\in[0,\widetilde{T})

(
\varepsilon>0
is already assumed to be small enough to guarantee
d\geq d_{0}(\varepsilon)>\varepsilon^{-1}>4
).
Applying it to (
4.12
) gives

|z_{c}(\widetilde{T})-z_{c}(0)-W_{H}\widetilde{T}|<C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{2}{d}\right)\cdot\widetilde{T}

after integration, which contradicts (
4.21
). Hence, we get
\widetilde{T}=\infty,
which means that the inequality
(
4.19
) holds for all
T>0
, i.e., we obtain (
4.2
):

|z_{c}(t)-z_{c}(0)-W_{H}t|<C_{2}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4}{d}\right)\cdot t<5C_{2}\varepsilon t\quad\mbox{for each}\mbox{ }t\geq 0

due to
d>\varepsilon^{-1}
. It completes the proof.
