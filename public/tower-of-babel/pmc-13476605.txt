Evaluating the National Hand Trauma Center Network from 2010 to 2022: Digit and Thumb Replant Activity and Trends
Authors: Joseph P Cavataio, Ivette Chen, Tara Reza, Jake Jacobs, Olivia C Lee, William F Sherman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study investigates the finger and thumb replant activity in the American Society for Surgery of the Hand/American College of Surgeons National Hand Trauma Center Network (NHTCN). Additionally, it evaluates trends in finger replantation and revision amputation by age, trends in replant success, and identifies demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with finger replantation.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the PearlDiver Mariner database, querying finger amputation diagnoses and subsequent replant and revision amputation surgeries with International Classification of Diseases, Ninth/Tenth and Current Procedural Terminology codes. Cases were stratified by age (0–17, 18−65, >65 years) and location, including states and metropolitan statistical areas (MSA) with NHTCN centers. Socioeconomic data were derived from US Census data. Replant success was defined as replantation without subsequent revision amputation.ResultsA total of 30,732 revision amputations and 1,202 replants of amputated digits were included. From 2010 to 2022, replants decreased (β: −0.50, P < .001) by 60.1% overall and by 57.1% in ages 0−17 (β: −0.94, P = .002). Nine centers averaged two or more finger replants per 100 amputations and 43 MSA averaged less than one replant per year. MSA replant rate correlated positively with NHTCN center count (r = 0.26; P = .040) yet state replant rate correlated negatively center count (r = −0.40; P = .018). Replant rate increased with mean family income (r = 0.39; P = .021). Replant success declined from 89.7% in 2010 to 75.0% in 2022 (β: −0.01, P = .004).ConclusionsDigit replantation continued to decline nationally, particularly in ages <65. There was a wide discrepancy between a few highly active and many under-active centers in the NHTCN. The replantation rate was lower in states with more centers, reflecting a decentralization of replantable fingers in the setting of higher amputation volumes. Greater age and lower mean family income are associated with lower replantation rates.Level of evidenceRetrospective cohort study, level II.Key words: Finger, Hand Trauma Center Network, Replant, Thumb, Trends

Traumatic finger amputations (TFA) are a common injury presentation in the emergency department, and the decision to revise or replant the finger is decided case by case.1, 2, 3 Thumbs and digits account for 91% of all traumatic amputations, occurring most often in males and in a bimodal age distribution with peaks in ages less than five and over 55 years old.4,5 However, young, working-class individuals also comprise a substantial portion of TFA, and this population may experience considerable disability and economic hardship as a result.6, 7, 8 Appropriate surgical management is crucial due to the risk of long-term functional loss and its associated economic impact.

Revision amputation involves shortening the digit and wound management, while replantation involves restoring finger length, sensation, and function by repairing arteries, veins, nerves, tendons, and bones.9,10 Indications for replantation are dependent on the digit type and amputation location. Patient selection is also critical, favoring motivated individuals with high functional demands.11,12 Over the past three decades, a national decline in finger replantation has been observed.13, 14, 15, 16 Multiple factors on all health system levels contribute to the decline, resulting in decentralization of replant patients from high- to low-volume hospitals and surgeons, inconsistent microsurgical services, and variable patient selection.16, 17, 18, 19 Additionally, replants have longer hospital stays, higher costs, lower reimbursement, and unclear cost-effectiveness.3,20,21

In 2007, the American Society for Surgery of the Hand (ASSH) and American College of Surgeons (ACS) began efforts to improve access to hand trauma care, laying the groundwork for the National Hand Trauma Center Network (NHTCN).1 The framework was formally codified in ACS guidance in 2011, which facilitated subsequent implementation of a hand trauma network with a pilot of 33 centers.1 Azoury et al. report the network’s participating centers expanded to 93 centers in 34 states by 2019 and increased from a smaller pilot.22 Despite the increase in NHTCN-listed centers, Eberlin et al23 found that certain aspects of emergency hand surgery care have changed little over the past decade, including the number of facilities offering 24/7 call and the share of hand surgeons paid for call. Despite ASSH efforts to regionalize care, access has not clearly improved, and the workforce needed to provide reliable microvascular coverage remains uncertain.

Given these clinical, economic, and access-related disparities, a comprehensive evaluation of national treatment patterns is warranted. The aims of this study are to examine treatment patterns for TFA by comparing rates of revision amputation and replantation, evaluate utilization of centers listed in the ASSH/ACS National Hand Trauma Center Network,24 assess the demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with rate of digit replantation, and evaluate the success rate of digital replantation over time. The investigators hypothesize that the rate of replantation has continued to decrease and mean family income is negatively correlated with rate of digit replantation.

Materials and Methods

Patient records were queried from the PearlDiver Mariner database (PearlDiver Inc), a commercially available administrative claims database containing deidentified patient data. The database contains the medical records of patients across the United States from January 1, 2010, through December 31, 2022, which are collected by an independent data aggregator. This study utilized the “M170Ortho” dataset within PearlDiver with 170 million patients. All health insurance payors are represented, including self-pay, commercial, private, and government plans. The investigators extracted data using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) and International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth revision (ICD-9/ICD-10) codes. An Institutional Review Board exemption was granted, as the provided data were deidentified and compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. No outside funding was received for this study.

Patients who sustained TFA of the digit or thumb were identified using ICD-9/10 diagnosis codes. Patients were then included if a subsequent revision amputation or replantation was recorded using CPT or ICD-9/10 procedure codes. Patients with amputations due to frostbite, gangrene, or crush injuries were excluded (Fig. 1). Table S1 (available on the Journal’s website at www.jhsgo.org) lists all codes used to query the database.

Cohort selection flow diagram for traumatic finger amputation (TFA).

Demographic and health data were collected, such as sex, age, and length of stay. Socioeconomic data provided by the database are derived from the United States (US) Census Bureau, estimated at the three-digit zip code tabulation area level and mapped to the five-digit zip codes. The PearlDiver database uses socioeconomic metric value based on the three-digit zip code corresponding to the patient’s residence at the time of the claim. Socioeconomic data collected in this study included mean family income, insurance type, and percentage of insured individuals.

Annual TFA incidence was calculated per 100,000 enrolled patients using PearlDiver’s year-specific enrollment denominator. Trends in revision amputation or replantation surgeries were estimated between 2010 and 2022 and stratified by age groups (0−17, 18−65, >65 years). Cases were also stratified by provider state and metropolitan statistical area (MSA) data if a state/MSA contains a center listed in the ASSH/ACS NHTCN.24 The NHTCN is a voluntary directory, and it may not capture all hospitals performing replantation. The NHTCN-listed centers represented 109 centers across 63 MSAs in 33 states (Table S2, available on the Journal’s website at www.jhsgo.org), accessed in December of 2025. This list continues to expand and is not comprehensive of all locations in the United States offering emergency microsurgical hand services. Each state and MSA replant rate was calculated per TFA in its respective year or location.

The success rate of replantation over time was analyzed overall and for each age group. A successful replantation was defined as a replantation without subsequent revision amputation within 6 months of replant, following prior studies.13,17 While the number of enrolled patients in the database increased continually throughout the study period, the number of TFA plateaued around 2019 and proceeded to decrease (Table S3, available on the Journal’s website at www.jhsgo.org).

The reporting and writing structure of this manuscript utilized the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology checklist. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square tests, and continuous variables were compared with Welch’s t test or Mann-Whitney U tests. Odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were also used to compare surgery utilization between cohorts. Trend metrics were calculated using linear regression with calendar year as the independent variable and the annual standardized rate (per TFA) as the dependent variable; the slope represents the annual change in rate, and no other variables were adjusted for in the model. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to estimate the correlation strength and direction between continuous metrics. State-level socioeconomic analysis was performed using weighted multivariable linear regression using mean age and mean family income as predictors, with replant rate per 100 traumatic finger amputations as the continuous outcome. States were weighted by their total traumatic finger amputation volume to account for differences in the precision of state-level replant rate estimates. A P-value of less than .05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 160,357 patients sustained TFA from 2010 to 2022. The mean age was 48.8 ± 21.6 years, and most were male (74.6%). Among all TFA, 30,732 patients (19.17%) underwent revision amputation, while 1,202 (0.75%) had replantation. Thumb replants accounted for 34.6% (416/1,202) of all replants.

There were 128,864 TFA patients without CPT or ICD-9/10 procedural codes for either revision amputation or replantation. The average age of patients in this group was 47.9 (± 22.1) years, with 72.9% being male, and 72.2% paying with commercial insurance. Most patients received outpatient treatment and were not billed for a procedure with 88.6% of patients treated in an office, clinic, or other outpatient setting.

Patients who had revision amputation were older (52.8 ± 18.7 years) than those who had replantation (38.7 ± 23.0 years, P < .001) (Table 1). Replant patients were 32% more likely to be female than those with revision amputation (P < .001).

National replant and revision amputation trend, and incidence by age

From 2010 to 2022, TFA declined by 14.9% (P < .001). Annual digit revision amputations were unchanged (P = .53), while digit replants decreased (β: −0.50, P < .001) by 60.1% (Fig. 2). Annual digit replants trended negatively in ages 0−17 (β: −0.94, P = .002) and ages 18−65 (β: −0.43, P < .001), decreasing by 57.1% and 65.8% over the study period, respectively (Table 2). Revision amputations had a positive trend in ages 0−17 (β: 2.16, P = .013) and remained unchanged (P = .57) or decreased (β: −5.02, P < .001) in ages 18−65 and >65, respectively. Trends in annual thumb replants were stable in ages 0−17 (P = .14) and 65+ (P = .21) yet trended negatively in ages 18−65 (β: −1.19, P = .005).

Replantation trends by state

Eight states demonstrated significant decreasing trend in digit replantation rate (FL, OH, LA, OR, NY, TX, PA, and TN; all P < .05). The remaining states included in the study demonstrated no significant trend in digit replantation (P > .05). With lower overall case volume, just two states (FL and VA) demonstrated negative trends in thumb replantation rate (P < .05).

Replantation activity and trends by MSA

The highest replantation rates (digit replants per 100 TFA, number of replant centers) were observed in Philadelphia-Camden-Wilmington:PA-NJ-DE-MD (6.32, 3), Rochester:MN (6.06, 1), Ann Arbor:MI (4.97, 2), Springfield:IL (4.35, 1), Columbus:OH (3.95, 4), and San Francisco-Oakland-Fremont:CA (3.41, 2) (Fig. 3).

A map of the United States displaying the replant rate per 100 traumatic finger amputations (TFA) for each state and each metropolitan statistical area (MSA) with an ASSH/ACS Hand Trauma Center.

Four (6.3%) MSA performed no replants over the whole study period and 43 (68.3%) MSA performed less than one digit replant per year on average over the 13-year study period (Table S4, available on the Journal’s website at www.jhsgo.org). Eleven (17.5%) MSA performed no thumb replants over the whole study period and 59 (93.7%) MSA performed less than one thumb replant per year on average.

Six MSAs demonstrated significant negative trends: Columbus:OH (rho: −0.74, P = .004), Dayton:OH (rho: −0.72, P = .006), New York-Northern New Jersey-Long Island:NY-NJ-PA (rho: −0.71, P = .006), Tampa-St. Petersburg-Clearwater:FL (rho: −0.62, P = .024), Orlando-Kissimmee:FL (rho: −0.59, P = .036), and Charleston-North Charleston:SC (rho: −0.57, P = .042). Thumb replantation rate had an increasing trend in Los Angeles-Long Beach-Santa Ana:CA (rho: 0.60, P = .029) and a decreasing trend in Springfield:IL (rho: −0.72, P = .008) and Atlanta-Sandy Springs-Marietta:GA (rho: −0.68, P = .010). The remaining MSA included in this study demonstrated no significant trend in all digits or thumb replantation.

There is a moderate and statistically significant negative correlation between replant rate per 100 TFA and the number of NHTCN centers in each state for all digits (rho: −0.40, P = .018) and for thumbs (rho: −0.54, P = .001). There are 15 MSAs with replant rates greater than the national replant rate, three MSAs with replant rates below the national replant rate, and the remaining MSAs have replant rates per 100 TFA not statistically different from the national replant rate.

Demographics and socioeconomic determinants

Using national-level socioeconomic data, the mean age of patients receiving a replant decreased significantly over the study period (β: −0.56, SE: 0.16, P = .004). Using socioeconomic data from each state’s TFA patient cohort, mean age was not significantly associated with replant rate (P = .24) after adjustment for mean family income. After adjustment for age, mean family income was also not significantly associated with replant rate (P = .06). The overall model was not statistically significant (R2 = 0.115; adjusted R2 = 0.060; P = .141).

Replant success rate

Replant procedures had an overall success rate of 82.5%, similar across all age groups (Table 3). From 2010 to 2022, the replant success rate decreased from 89.7% to 75.0% (β: −0.01, P = .004) and similarly declined in ages 0−17 (β: −0.02, P = .046) and ages 18−65 (β: −0.007, P < .001). Thumb replant procedures had an overall success rate of 80.9% (334/413). The replant success rate remained stable from 2010 to 2022 overall and within each age group (Table 3).

Discussion

Many MSAs with hand trauma centers had low to null rates of finger and thumb replants over this study period. More than 50% of MSAs performed ≤5 finger replants per 100 revision amputations, and 10 MSA performed ≤1 replant over the entire study period. Along with a continued national decline in replantation rates, replantation success rates have also decreased over time. Multiple factors at the provider, system, and patient levels have contributed to these declines. Case volume and outcomes depend on call coverage and surgeon experience. Without them, replant indications can be inconsistently applied, and complex cases can be declined.16,19,25,26 Hospital location also impacts volume and outcomes. High-volume urban centers are more likely to have experienced surgeons; yet uncoordinated and delayed patient transfers can result in patients presenting to facilities lacking microsurgical services, making it difficult for surgeons to maintain expertise.1,17,18,27 Declining procedural volume can limit training opportunities and may contribute to the gradual loss of technical skills. Concurrently, from 2010 to 2023, hand surgery fellowship positions increased by 38.8% while applicant volume remained stable, reducing the applicant-to-position ratio.28 In the setting of fewer replants, this expansion may further dilute trainee exposure and contribute to declining experience and outcomes. Low income, lack of private insurance, and socioeconomic disadvantages reduce patient access to replantation and decrease the likelihood of receiving the procedure even when presenting to a capable facility.1,6,13,25,26 These interrelated factors continue to limit access to and success of digit replantation demonstrated in this study. Additionally, replantations have longer hospital stays, higher costs, lower reimbursement, and unclear cost-effectiveness.3,20,21 These economic disincentives further discourage hospitals and surgeons from offering replantation, contributing to their ongoing decline in hand trauma centers meant to provide microsurgical services.

Overall state replantation rates decreased as the number of centers in each state increased for both finger (rho: −0.40) and thumb amputations (rho: −0.54). This opposing pattern may suggest that more centers per state could contribute to confusion in the current decentralized referral system in the setting of high TFA volume. Decentralization is caused by disorganized cross-regional referral patterns and variable on-call capability. Proximity to a high-volume hospital removes travel and time barriers to accessing care, yet O’Brien et al18 demonstrated that even when a closer high-volume center exists, 21.5% of patients still receive care at a low-volume hospital. Misra et al29 found in the US northeast (MA, CT, ME, NH, RI, VT) a median of four Level I trauma centers were bypassed when patients were transferred for urgent microsurgery. Hence, an increase in state replant centers may cause issues with the referral system and patient transfers, resulting in limited access to high-volume hospitals and surgeons.25,27,30,31 These administrative and organizational difficulties may be perpetuating the decentralization of replantation within United States into the 2020s.15,25,32 The solution of designating replant centers by region is supported by the ASSH and ACS to streamline patient transfers to high-volume surgeons and centers with the added benefit of higher success rates in their care.17,20 A program by these institutions has been initiated to designate centers that can provide 24/7/365 coverage of hand, microvascular, and replantation emergency services and attempt to financially incentivize these services. In countries such as Japan, Taiwan, and China, specialized replant centers are proposed to contribute to their higher success rates.27 This solution may address the incongruous correlation between state replant rate and the number of hand trauma centers.

In addition to an accelerated national decline, replantation in pediatric patients decreased by 57% over the study period. Despite replantation being a more technically demanding procedure in pediatric populations, an attempt to replant in these patients is highly recommended given the high likelihood of good outcomes. Evidence demonstrates improved long-term functional outcomes, superior recovery, and at least similar survival rates in pediatric patients compared to adults.11,33,34 With the decline in technical expertise,32 among other factors, the number of replants continued to decline in pediatric populations regardless of broad indications.

The selectivity in digit replantation is becoming increasingly restrictive, as the average age of replanted patients decreased from the beginning to end of the study period. As replantation becomes less common and patient selection narrows, factors such as age, employment, insurance, and income may eventually carry greater weight in patient selection.

Limitations

As a retrospective administrative-claims study, it lacks granular clinical and demographical context. Patient comorbidities and clinical factors influencing surgical decision-making, such as ischemia time, injury severity, or hand dominance, were not available and may have affected treatment selection. Approximately 80% of TFA encounters could not be linked to a revision amputation or replantation CPT/ICD-9/10 procedure code, raising concern for incomplete procedural capture and potential misclassification. A query of the missing patients’ demographics, insurance, and presenting location suggests they are similar in background to our main study cohort and were not treated in an inpatient setting. Coding errors are inherent in any analysis of administrative claims data; however, such instances are rare and account for only 0.7% of Medicare and Medicaid payments in 2021.35 The database excludes workers’ compensation claims, a relevant subgroup that may follow different referral pathways and reimbursement models, potentially influencing access to replantation. Since NHTCN participation expanded over time, this study used a contemporary NHTCN roster applied across 2010–2022, which may misclassify whether a given location was NHTCN-listed in earlier years. Socioeconomic variables were derived from US Census estimates based on patients’ three-digit zip codes, providing only area-level approximations of individual characteristics such as income, education, and employment status. This study evaluated replants by a standardized rate which accounts for the annual number of TFA; thus, estimates in this study were adjusted for this factor.
