GaussianPSL: A novel framework based on Gaussian Splatting for exploring the Pareto frontier in multi-criteria optimization
Phuong Mai Dinh, Van-Nam Huynh

Abstract
Multi-objective optimization (MOO) is essential for solving complex real-world problems involving multiple conflicting objectives. However, many practical applications - including engineering design, autonomous systems, and machine learning - often yield non-convex, degenerate, or discontinuous Pareto frontiers, which involve traditional scalarization and Pareto Set Learning (PSL) methods that struggle to approximate accurately. Existing PSL approaches perform well on convex fronts but tend to fail in capturing the diversity and structure of irregular Pareto sets commonly observed in real-world scenarios. In this paper, we propose Gaussian-PSL, a novel framework that integrates Gaussian Splatting into PSL to address the challenges posed by non-convex Pareto frontiers. Our method dynamically partitions the preference vector space, enabling simple MLP networks to learn localized features within each region, which are then integrated by an additional MLP aggregator. This partition-aware strategy enhances both exploration and convergence, reduces sensitivity to initialization, and improves robustness against local optima. We first provide the mathematical formulation for controllable Pareto set learning using Gaussian Splatting. Then, we introduce the Gaussian-PSL architecture and evaluate its performance on synthetic and real-world multi-objective benchmarks. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach outperforms standard PSL models in learning irregular Pareto fronts while maintaining computational efficiency and model simplicity. This work offers a new direction for effective and scalable MOO under challenging frontier geometries.



Introduction

Multi-objective optimization (MOO) plays a critical role in addressing complex decision-making problems where multiple conflicting objectives must be optimized simultaneously. Many real-world applications — from engineering design
(
Sinha and Suh 2018
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
and control systems to machine learning
(
Rai and Allada 2003
)
and autonomous decision-making
(
Mahapatra and Rajan 2020
;
Su et al. 2024
)
—involve non-convex Pareto frontiers, where optimal trade-offs form intricate, non-linear surfaces
(
Miettinen 1999
)
. In such settings, finding diverse and representative solutions becomes especially challenging, as traditional scalarization-based methods (e.g., weighted-sum approaches) often fail to capture the full spectrum of Pareto-optimal solutions, particularly in non-convex regions.

These challenges underscore the need for advanced MOO methods that can explore non-convex and discontinuous solution spaces
(
Huang, Nie, and Wang 2025
)
, maintain diversity across the Pareto front, and produce solutions aligned with real-world constraints and preferences. To address these needs, recent research has focused on surrogate modeling, decomposition-based strategies, and preference-guided approaches that can efficiently approximate non-convex fronts and guide decision-makers toward informed selections.

This paper investigates the integration of the Gaussian Splatting technique with the PSL model for tackling MOO problems with non-convex Pareto frontiers, with the aim of improving solution diversity and approximation accuracy while reducing computational cost.

Challenge.
Current Pareto Set Learning (PSL) models perform well on convex Pareto frontiers, as illustrated in the experimental results of multiple research
(
Ma, Du, and Matusik 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
)
. However, rarely does any work explicitly aim to target irregular Pareto frontiers (i.e., non-convex, degenerate, and discontinuous)
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, which are common in real-world problems
(
Tanabe and Ishibuchi 2020
)
. Pioneers in this field include Ye et al.
(
Ye et al. 2024
)
, who propose using Genetic Algorithms (GAs) for preference vector selection to enhance neural network performance by focusing on crucial regions. The intuition behind this idea is to force MLP models to concentrate on the strict Pareto frontier and ignore the non-strict part, thereby reducing noise in the weak-order region.

Approach.
In this paper, we present a novel framework for Pareto Set Learning that utilizes the concept of partitioning the preference vector. We apply the Gaussian Splatting technique
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
)
to dynamically manage these partitions. Simple multi-layer perceptron (MLP) networks are employed to learn the features of each subsection independently before merging them through another MLP network. This approach results in a more robust Pareto set, enhancing exploration while ensuring convergence to the optimal front.

We propose a new model for multi-objective optimization (MOO) called Gaussian-PSL, which builds upon an established framework. This model integrates Gaussian Splatting to improve the learning of irregular Pareto frontiers, including non-convex, degenerate, and discontinuous cases. Our approach tackles the challenge of local optima that frequently occur when exploring these frontiers, where the success of optimization often hinges on the quality of the initial conditions. Our key contributions are as follows:

•

We first summarize the mathematical formulation for learning a controllable Pareto set within the context of multi-objective optimization and Gaussian splatting.

•

Secondly, we introduce Gaussian-PSL, a novel framework for Pareto Set Learning that incorporates Gaussian Splatting and addresses irregular Pareto frontiers.

•

Third, we conduct extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world multi-objective optimization problems to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Gaussian-PSL method.

•

Finally, we perform an ablation study to examine the contribution of each component in our framework.

Preliminary

Pareto Set Learning

Multi-Objective Optimization

Given the feasible set
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of decision vectors
\mathbf{x}
, the vector-valued objective function is defined as:

\begin{split}f:\quad&\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
&\mathbf{x}\rightarrow f(\mathbf{x})=\left(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),f_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\cdots,f_{k}(\mathbf{x})\right)\end{split}

(1)

Without loss of generality, we assume all objective functions in Equation (
1
) are to be minimized. Then, the multi-objective optimization problem can be formulated as:

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}}f(\mathbf{x})=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}}(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),f_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_{k}(\mathbf{x}))

(2)

Here, any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}
is called a feasible solution or decision vector, and the set
\mathbf{Y}=\{\mathbf{y}=f(\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}\}
represents the objective space, i.e., the image of
\mathbf{X}
under the mapping
f
. Each
\mathbf{y}=f(\mathbf{x})
is referred to as an objective vector or outcome. The goal is to identify feasible solutions
\mathbf{x}
that minimize all the objectives simultaneously.

Pareto Optimization

In general, there is no feasible solution that minimizes all objective functions simultaneously. Therefore, attention is directed toward Pareto optimal solutions
(
Pareto 1906
)
, which are solutions that cannot be improved in any objective without degrading at least one of the others.

Definition 1
(Pareto Dominance)

Consider a vector-valued minimization problem. An objective vector
\mathbf{y}^{1}\in\mathbf{Y}
is said to Pareto dominate another vector
\mathbf{y}^{2}\in\mathbf{Y}
, denoted
\mathbf{y}^{1}\succ\mathbf{y}^{2}
, if and only if:

•

\forall\;i\in\{1\dots k\},\mathbf{y}^{1}_{i}\leq\mathbf{y}^{2}_{i}
and

•

\exists\;j\in\{1\dots k\},\mathbf{y}^{1}_{j}<\mathbf{y}^{2}_{j}

This means that
\mathbf{y}^{1}
is at least as good as
\mathbf{y}^{2}
in all objectives and strictly better in at least one. The relation
\succ
is called the Pareto order and defines a strict partial order over
\mathbf{Y}
.

If
\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{x}^{2}\in\mathbf{X}
are feasible solutions such that
f(\mathbf{x}^{1})=\mathbf{y}^{1}
and
f(\mathbf{x}^{2})=\mathbf{y}^{2}
, then we say
\mathbf{x}^{1}
Pareto dominates
\mathbf{x}^{2}
and denote this as
\mathbf{x}^{1}\succ_{f}\mathbf{x}^{2}
.

An objective vector
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\mathbf{Y}
is called Pareto optimal if there does not exist another objective vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{Y}
such that
\mathbf{y}\succ\mathbf{y}^{*}
. The set of all Pareto optimal outcomes, denoted
\mathbf{Y}^{*}
, is commonly referred to as the Pareto front, Pareto frontier, or Pareto boundary.

Definition 2
(Pareto Frontier and Optimal Solution Space)

An objective vector
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\mathbf{Y}^{*}\subset\mathbf{Y}
is said to lie on the Pareto frontier if and only if

\nexists\;\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{Y}
such that
\mathbf{y}\succ\mathbf{y}^{*}
. The corresponding set of feasible solutions
\mathbf{X}^{*}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}\mid f(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{Y}^{*}\}
is called the Pareto optimal solution space.

The Pareto frontier and its associated solution space are of great interest in multi-objective optimization because they provide a comprehensive view of trade-offs between conflicting objectives. Decision-makers can explore these trade-offs to select solutions aligned with their preferences.
However, the exact computation of the Pareto optimal solution space is often infeasible due to the high dimensionality and non-convexity of the objective and decision spaces. In practice, researchers attempt to approximate the Pareto front using a representative set of non-dominated points. Nonetheless, such approximations are of limited value without the corresponding mapping to decision variables, which can be prohibitively expensive to recover using brute-force methods like grid search.

Definition 3
(Strong and Weak Pareto Optimality)

Strong Pareto optimality implies that any change to the current objectives would make at least one objective worse off, while no other objectives get better. Weak Pareto optimality implies that a change might not make objectives worse off, but also wouldn’t make objectives better off.

In essence, strong Pareto optimality reflects a stricter notion of efficiency, where any deviation from the current solution would inevitably lead to a negative impact on at least one objective.

Pareto Set Learning

The advancement of neural networks (NNs) has enabled the approximation of highly complex functions in various domains. In conventional machine learning tasks, neural networks are typically employed to approximate the mapping from input features to output predictions. In the context of multi-objective optimization, however, the goal of Pareto Set Learning (PSL) is to train the neural network to approximate the function that maps a set of preference vectors
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to Pareto optimal solutions
\mathbf{X}^{*}
, given that the objective function
f
is known.

In PSL, preference vectors can be intuitively understood as direction vectors that guide the search toward specific regions of the Pareto frontier. Assume the Pareto frontier is being observed from a fixed point in the objective space(commonly the origin), a preference vector defines a directional ray originating from the observation point and pointing toward a target point on the Pareto frontier. So, depending on the observing points, the direction associated with a given point on the Pareto frontier might change accordingly. Different PSL algorithms interpret and utilize these preference vectors in various ways, associated with
different types of loss function scalarization.

PSL models are typically neural network-based and aim to approximate the Pareto optimal solution space by minimizing a loss function defined over the objective space. Since this loss function operates on objective values rather than directly on decision variables, it serves as a surrogate loss function, which can make the optimization process challenging and non-trivial. A Pareto set model can be formally defined as:

\begin{split}\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}&=\phi_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{\lambda})\\
\mathbf{y}_{\lambda}&=f(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda})\end{split}

(3)

where
{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the preference vector,
\phi_{\mathbf{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the Pareto set model, parameterized by
\mathbf{\theta}
,
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the vector-valued objective function,
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}
is the predicted decision vector, and
\mathbf{y}_{\lambda}
is the corresponding objective vector. During training, the model learns to map each preference vector
\mathbf{\lambda}
to a decision vector
\mathbf{x}_{\lambda}
such that the resulting objective vector
f(\mathbf{x}_{\lambda})
lies on or near the Pareto frontier. To achieve this, a scalarization function
s(\cdot)
is applied to the objective values, converting the multi-objective problem into a single-objective one. The optimization goal of PSL is to minimize the expected scalarized objective over a predefined distribution
\mathcal{D}
of preference vectors:

\begin{split}\theta^{*}&=\argmin_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\lambda}\sim\mathcal{D}}[s(f(\phi_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{\lambda}))]\\
&=\argmin_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\lambda}\sim\mathcal{D}}[s(f(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{\lambda}}))]\end{split}

(4)

After training, the learned Pareto set model can provide a solution for any preference of the decision-maker.

The choice of scalarization function determines how preference vectors intersect the approximated Pareto frontier and thus directly influences the learning dynamics of Pareto Set Learning (PSL) models. In this work, we examine several commonly used scalarization strategies:

1.

Linear scalarization (LS)
(
Navon et al. 2021
)

2.

COSMOS
(
Ruchte and Grabocka 2021
)

3.

Chebyshev scalarization (TCH)
(
Xi Lin 2022
;
Lin et al. 2022
)

4.

Modified Chebyshev scalarization (MTCH)
(
Wang et al. 2020
;
Ma et al. 2018
)

For neural network-based PSL models, strong Pareto optimality is generally more favorable. In such regions, the shape of the Pareto frontier induces well-behaved loss surfaces: a small deviation from an optimal solution typically results in a significant degradation in performance, which provides strong learning signals and helps the model converge effectively. However, when the Pareto frontier contains weakly optimal regions, the learning task becomes considerably more challenging. In such areas, an improvement in one objective may not significantly affect the scalarized loss due to differences in objective scales, and early stopping for other direction exploration is more beneficial at the current stage. This phenomenon makes it difficult for neural network-based PSL models to escape local optima induced by weak Pareto regions. Typically, Pareto frontiers comprise weak optimal regions, which often appear as discontinuous or irregular segments, posing significant challenges for PSL models.

Gaussian Splatting technique

Gaussian Splatting (GS) is a recent technique for 3D scene reconstruction from RGB images, first introduced by Bernhard Kerbl et al. at SIGGRAPH 2023
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
)
. Since its introduction, it has attracted significant attention from researchers worldwide due to its efficiency and high-quality visual results. Compared to other methods such as NeRF
(
Mildenhall et al. 2021
)
and Voxel
(
Sitzmann et al. 2019
)
, methods based on GS are faster, low-resource, and maintain good novel synthetic views.

The primary concept of the GS is to represent occupied 3D space using multiple ellipsoids with varying colors and transparency.
In 3D space, each ellipsoid is represented by a Gaussian function as follows:

\begin{split}G_{(\mu,\Sigma)}(p)&=\sigma(\alpha)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(p-\mu)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(p-\mu)}\\
\Sigma&=RSS^{T}R^{T}\\
R&=\begin{bmatrix}r_{xx}&r_{xy}&r_{xz}\\
r_{yx}&r_{yy}&r_{yz}\\
r_{zx}&r_{yz}&r_{zz}\end{bmatrix}\\
S&=\begin{bmatrix}s_{x}&0&0\\
0&s_{y}&0\\
0&0&s_{z}\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(5)

where
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix of the Gaussian distribution, presented by the 3×3 rotation matrix
R
analytically expressed with four quaternions and a diagonal scaling matrix
S
with three parameters for scale. Function
G_{(\mu,\Sigma)}(p)
computes the transparency or the effect of the ellipsoid represented by the Gaussian distribution
N(\mu,\Sigma)
on the point/pixel
p
. This function involves distance from
p
to the ellipsoid center, and the basic transparency of the ellipsoid
\sigma(\alpha)=\{0..1\}
. Fig.
1
illustrates an ellipsoid represented by Equation (
5
). The true color of the point/pixel
p
is the sum of the colors with transparency of all surrounding Gaussian ellipsoids.

Figure 1:
An ellipsoid represented by the Gaussian function and its influence on point
p

For a 3D representation, the covariance matrix
\Sigma
typically requires nine parameters. However, these parameters cannot be optimized directly, as a covariance matrix is physically meaningful only when it is positive semi-definite (PSD). Using gradient descent for optimization makes it difficult to impose such constraints directly on the matrix; therefore, it is typically factorized via eigen decomposition. Each ellipsoid effectively represents a localized region in space centered around its mean, as determined by its covariance. However, it has a theoretically infinite extent, since we can evaluate the influence of the ellipsoid on any predetermined points in 3D space based on their distance. This characteristic is advantageous during optimization, as it enables gradients to propagate from distant regions without vanishing.

Adaptive Density Control

We are particularly interested in the technique’s ability to manage an unknown number of ellipsoids in 3D space. Although constructing an ellipsoid using a Gaussian function ensures differentiability, it is important to note that gradient descent alone can only adjust existing points; it struggles to discover suitable parameters in regions that lack sufficient points or contain an excessive number of them. This is where adaptive densification becomes essential. Initially, distinctive points from the training image are identified using the Structure-from-Motion (SfM) technique
(
Schönberger and Frahm 2016
)
. These unique points are usually found at the corners of objects or at the intersections of parallel lines in 3D space. They are crucial for determining the camera pose and serve as the starting point for the number and position of the ellipsoids. If a point cloud is unavailable for any reason, a random initialization
(
Kheradmand et al. 2024
)
can be used as a substitute, although this may lead to a potential loss in the final reconstruction quality.

During the training process, these ellipsoids can move and rotate freely to adapt to the scene. However, after several epochs, the model may identify ellipsoids that fail to converge and are continually shifting within a localized area. This behavior typically arises when the occupied space does not conform to an ellipsoidal shape. To improve the fit, the model may clone or split the ellipsoid into multiple smaller ellipsoids, as illustrated in Fig.
2
. Transparent ellipsoids (i.e., those for which
\sigma(\alpha)\approx 0
) are discarded from the model.

Figure 2:
Top row (under-reconstruction): When small-scale geometry (black outline) is insufficiently covered, we clone the respective Gaussian. Bottom row (over-reconstruction): If small-scale geometry is represented by one large splat, we split it into two. Source:
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
)

This technique enables us to cover an unknown space using ellipsoids without prior knowledge of their exact number or position. It can also be regarded as the core principle underlying GS-based methods. Although the loss functions and ellipsoid parameterizations may vary across different GS method variants, the ideal and process of Adaptive Density Control remain the same.

Pareto Set Learning using Gaussian Splatting

Previous research has indicated that state-of-the-art (SOTA) models perform well with convex Pareto frontiers; however, they often struggle with non-convex cases, particularly those involving discontinuities. For discontinuous frontiers, the standard PSL model tends to become trapped in local optima due to weak Pareto dominance. When PSL explores these weak regions of the frontier, the impact on the loss functions is minimal, causing the model to prematurely abandon the exploration process. A similar issue arises in non-convex cases, where PSL tends to oversmooth the folds of the frontier, hindering further improvements in prediction performance.

To address these challenges, partitioning the frontier into multiple convex regions may be advantageous for the PSL model. Research by
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2024
)
has shown that most shapes in 2D and 3D can be effectively approximated using multiple ellipsoids at varying scales and orientations. This approach can also be generalized to higher-dimensional spaces. By dividing the preference vector space into multiple ellipsoidal regions, a lightweight multi-layer perceptron (MLP) can more effectively learn local features, which can then be integrated through an additional MLP to yield final predictions. However, in cases where the frontier’s shape is unknown, determining the appropriate number and placement of these regions becomes challenging. To overcome this, we propose using multiple ellipsoids to cover the preference vector space, in combination with an Adaptive Density Control technique to dynamically manage both their number and positioning.

The overall architecture of the proposed framework is illustrated in Fig.
3
. The preference vector space, which represents user-defined trade-offs, is covered by multiple ellipsoids defined by Gaussian functions, as shown in Equation (
5
). It is assumed that the closer the preference vector
\lambda
is to the centroid of the subspace, the greater the influence that subspace has on predicting
x_{\lambda}
. Each lightweight MLP extracts latent information from its respective subspace. The outputs from all subspaces are then combined, weighted according to the distance between the preference vector and the corresponding ellipsoid centers, and subsequently passed into a standard PSL model for final prediction. The number of Gaussian subspaces is regulated by Adaptive Density Control and optimized during the backpropagation process. To encourage the model to prioritize local information from the subspaces, we incorporate subspace entropy for each preference vector. The final loss function of the proposed framework is defined as:

\mathcal{L}={\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\lambda}\sim\mathcal{D}}}\left(s(f(\phi_{\mathbf{\theta}}(\mathbf{\lambda}))+\gamma H(W_{\lambda})\right)

(6)

Here,
H(W_{\lambda})
denotes the entropy computed based on the distance between the preference vector
\lambda
and the centers of the subspaces, and
\gamma
is a pre-defined hyperparameter. A higher value of
H(W_{\lambda})
indicates larger subspaces with fewer boundaries between them, which mitigates the risk of overparameterization due to an excessive number of subspaces. This structure can also facilitate improved prediction of folds or disconnected regions within the frontier by leveraging contextual information from neighboring subspaces.

Evaluation Metric

We use Log Hypervolume Difference (LHD) to evaluate the quality of a learned Pareto front, denoted as
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}
, compared to the true approximate Pareto front for the synthetic/real-world problems, represented as
\mathbf{Y^{*}}
. The calculation of LHD involves taking the logarithm of the difference in hypervolumes between
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}
and
\mathbf{Y^{*}}
as follows:

LHD(\hat{\mathbf{Y}},\mathbf{Y^{*}})=\log\left(HV(\mathbf{Y^{*}})+\epsilon-HV(\hat{\mathbf{Y}})\right)

(7)

with
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a small positive value. The smaller the log HV difference, the better the performance.

Main Results

As shown in Table
1
, we present the impact of our framework on the convergence behavior of various algorithms. The four scalarization techniques employ loss functions parameterized by preference vectors to update the PSL model. Our proposed method consistently outperforms all baselines across benchmarks and evaluation metrics. For the RE21 problem—where the Pareto front is continuous and convex in two-dimensional space—there is no significant difference between the baseline and our framework. However, in more complex cases, the scalarization techniques COSMOS, TCH, and MTCH demonstrate that GaussianPSL can escape most local minima and surpasses the vanilla PSL extension in both breadth and depth.
Furthermore, the GaussianPSL framework generally achieves lower standard deviations, indicating more stable and reliable performance. Although PSL with four layers typically performs better than with three layers, it still underperforms compared to the proposed method. EPS also lags behind in most settings.
The performance improvement is particularly notable in DTLZ7, ZDT3, DTLZ5, and RE37, where our framework exhibits a clear advantage across all three scalarization metrics - TCH, MTCH, and COSMOS.

Conclusion & Future Work

In conclusion, our proposed framework, which integrates the Gaussian Splatting technique with PSL models, offers a novel approach for exploring Pareto frontiers in real-world scenarios. Targeting non-convex frontiers, including discontinuous, non-convex, and degenerate cases, our GaussianPSL model outperforms state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines across most experimental datasets.

Despite these advancements, challenges remain in autonomously controlling the number of subspaces. In theory, a large Gaussian subspace can be decomposed into multiple smaller ones, which may lead to an excessive number of subspaces during the optimization process. Furthermore, the hypervolume (HV) indicator does not fully reflect frontier quality. For example, in the DTLZ7 dataset, our framework achieves the same hypervolume as the ground truth, yet lacks sufficient sparsity to ensure an even distribution across the frontier. We aim to address these limitations in future work.
