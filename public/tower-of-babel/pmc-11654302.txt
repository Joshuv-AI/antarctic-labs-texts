“Shrink back is not my intention”: a qualitative exploration of Chinese security guards’ experiences with bystander CPR
Authors: Mingzhu Yang, Junyang Song, Yuanyuan Jin, Youqing Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOut-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a major public health burden worldwide. Promoting bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (B-CPR) is a key element in improving the survival rate of OHCA. The security guard is a specific population in China that plays a significant role as bystanders in public settings. However, less is known about their experiences regarding B-CPR intention and performance. This study aimed to explore the experiences and perspectives of Chinese security guards regarding B-CPR and identify barriers to its performance.MethodsFifteen in-depth interviews were conducted with security guards in various public settings, including hospitals, residential living areas, office buildings, and public transportation stations. All interviews were audio recorded. Two researchers independently analyzed the transcripts through deductive and inductive content analysis. Informed by the “Intention-focused” model of B-CPR performance, deductive content analysis was initially performed to identify categories that align with the theoretical framework and thereby validate the theory. Subsequently, inductive content analysis was applied to code newly discovered content, therefore enriching the theory.ResultsAn “intention & performance-focused” model of B-CPR was developed. It was revealed that security personnel were more inclined to perform CPR without hesitation when the victim was a trusted individual. However, when faced with unfamiliar individuals, they tended to shrink back due to various barriers. Specifically, four layers of barriers to CPR performance were identified, encompassing security personnel factors, victim-related factors, organizational factors, and societal factors. Participants also shared their perspectives on the desired CPR training, including trainers, training contents, training formats, and training incentives.ConclusionSecurity guards should receive comprehensive CPR training to maximize their occupational value. In addition to strengthening CPR-related training, which includes theoretical knowledge and hands-on practice, psychological coping skills for managing overwhelming emotions and understanding relevant laws are also essential training elements that cannot be overlooked. When developing relevant intervention strategies, policies, and regulations, it’s critical to consider the country’s context and the cooperation of the organization where the security guard is employed. The model developed in this study can provide a reference for designing interventions and policy to improve the B-CPR intention and performance for other specific groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20888-w.Keywords: Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Qualitative research, Security guards

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a major public health burden worldwide. It requires immediate intervention to minimize the risk of mortality and morbidity [1, 2]. It refers to the cessation of functional cardiac mechanical activity, coupled with the absence of systemic circulation, occurring outside a hospital setting [3]. OHCA is time-critical, linked to substantial morbidity and mortality, and characterized by a high incidence but a low survival rate. According to the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) report, the emergency medical service (EMS) treated OHCA rate was 47.9 and 62.2 per 100,000 person-years in Norway and the United States [4], while in China, the number is 95.7 [5]. The survival rates at hospital discharge or 30 days in Norway and the United States are 15.7% and 12%, respectively, while it is concerning that the survival rate in China is only 1.2% [4, 5]. In cases of OHCA, the application of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is considered a potentially life-saving intervention, which is pivotal to the survival rate. Layperson bystanders assume a crucial role in the initiation of CPR.

CPR training for the lay public in China is offered by organizations like the Red Cross, EMS centers, hospitals, community health centers, medical schools, and commercial training institutions such as the American Heart Association (AHA), varying in duration. These programs follow Chinese Expert Consensus [6–8], which were adapted from international standards, primarily the AHA and ILCOR guidelines, while taking into account China’s specific context. Most people are willing to perform bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (B-CPR) for someone experiencing cardiac arrest [9]. However, a multicenter, population-based study on OHCA in China revealed a significantly low level of B-CPR at only 20.3% [5], which is at the limit level of the worldwide range (19.1−79.0%) [4]. A better understanding of the underlying causes of low B-CPR performance rates can provide valuable insights into potential solutions that could help improve not only the B-CPR performance rate but also the survival rates and long-term outcomes for OHCA patients in China.

According to the “Intention-focused” model of B-CPR performance [10], whether bystanders initiate B-CPR depends on various factors, including personal factors such as demographic characteristics, factors influencing intention, and factors affecting CPR performance, such as skills and environmental constraints, after forming the intention. The action can be ignited by enhancing intention through changing beliefs (i.e., attitude, perceived norms, and self-efficacy), enhancing skills, and attenuating environmental constraints [10]. Currently, many studies focus on enhancing the intention of CPR by improving the three factors of the Theory of Planned Behavior (i.e., attitude, perceived social norms, and perceived behavioral control/self-efficacy) [11–13]. However, few studies address the barriers that prevent the actual CPR performance, even when the intention is present. According to the novel concept of ‘Chainmail of Survival’, multiple joined links can strengthen the whole mission of resuscitative endeavors [14]. Thus, understanding the factors behind low CPR performance—one of the critical links following intention—is crucial for improving overall CPR outcomes. Additionally, increasing evidence shows a significant decrease in CPR intentions over time [13]. Therefore, it’s crucial to focus on both maintaining intention and promoting performance to enhance CPR success.

Additionally, previous research mostly focused on the general public and did not distinguish the special social characteristics of the bystanders. Maximizing the role of bystanders in the chain of survival is a goal that countries around the world strive to achieve, and this is especially important in China, where “chivalrous conduct” is a traditional virtue [15]. Chivalrous conduct emphasizes righteousness, bravery, and moral integrity. It is deeply rooted in Chinese historical philosophies and folklore, and its relevance endures in contemporary society, reflecting an evolving understanding of social responsibility. While OHCA incidents occur more frequently in homes (73.4%) than in public settings (16.3%) [16], it’s notable that a higher survival rate is observed in public areas (36.5%) compared to 22.9% in homes [17]. Therefore, it is imperative not to overlook the significant role of bystanders in public settings. In China, the security guard is a special group that is prevalent and crucial in society. It plays pivotal roles in critical scenarios within various settings, including hospitals, public transportation stations, shopping malls, and residential communities. Due to their professional requirements, security guards need to be on duty around the clock in public places, becoming the main witnesses of OHCA. Furthermore, security guards played an essential role in maintaining public health and societal safety, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, for example, fever screening and monitoring public health [18]. These emphasize the importance of studying the training and performance of B-CPR for security personnel, which is essential to improve the performance rate of B-CPR and then improve the survival rate of OHCA.

This study sought to explore the perspectives of security guards regarding B-CPR. It endeavors to delve into their sentiments and experiences, particularly those who have undergone CPR training and have encountered instances of collapses. Furthermore, the study aims to comprehensively explore the barriers that influence the intention and effective performance of B-CPR. The findings of this study can serve as a theoretical basis for developing intervention strategies to train and encourage the performance of B-CPR among security guards. Additionally, they can provide valuable insights for the training of other specific groups and the formulation of related policies.

Methods

Data collection

Individual in-depth interviews were conducted in person from June to August 2022. The researchers had no prior established relationships with the participants. Participants were encouraged to express their experiences and thoughts freely. The entire process was audio-recorded with agreement. The interview guide focused on personal perceptions of the occupation, personal beliefs bout B-CPR, and barriers to performing B-CPR, which was informed by the theoretical framework—the “Intention-focused” model of B-CPR performance [10]. Preliminary interviews with two qualified security guards were conducted to test the clarity of the questions, and issues identified during this process led to modifications in the interview guide. For example, initially, we asked the security guards if they had ever encountered someone collapsing, and some described situations where they dealt with people fainting due to low blood sugar. To more accurately gather experiences related to CPR, we revised the question to: “Have you ever encountered a situation where CPR was needed? If so, could you share your experience with us?” Details of the refined interview guide are provided in Table 1.

Sampling

A purposive sampling method was employed across various settings in Shanghai, including hospitals, residential living areas, office buildings, and public transportation stations. According to the theoretical framework and literature review, we presupposed inviting 10 participants to reach code saturation, which means that the code has been repeated in several interviews, and no new code appears or any other change is needed [19]. It was expected that three more participants would be invited to arrive at the stopping criterion that no more new information was needed, achieving the final meaning saturation [19, 20].

Inclusion criteria: ① Age range of ≥ 18 years old and ≤ 65 years old; ② Completion of CPR training; ③ Previous experience of encountering someone who collapsed, requiring the performance of CPR; and ④ Voluntary enrolment with informed consent obtained. Individuals with auditory impairments or any other impediments to oral communication were excluded.

Data analysis

NVivo 12.0 software was employed to organize data and facilitate the data analysis, with the following specific steps: Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within a 48-hour timeframe post-interview. Two approaches to content analysis were used [21]. Deductive content analysis was initially performed to identify categories that align with the “intention-focused” model of B-CPR performance theoretical framework and thereby validate the theory. Subsequently, inductive content analysis was applied to code newly discovered content, therefore enriching the theory [21, 22]. The research group comprised two master’s degree holders in nursing and education (one of whom has over 30 years of clinical experience in the field of CPR) and one PhD in nursing with extensive expertise in qualitative research. During the data analysis process, the researchers engaged in deep reflection, integrating personal experiences, existing literature, and the cultural context of China to gain a more profound understanding of the collected data. To enhance rigor and trustworthiness, two researchers independently coded the data and regularly reported the results to the research group. This approach was used to mitigate the potential influence of researchers’ personal presumptions on the analysis process and to enhance the credibility of the results. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist [23] was adhered to report this study (see Additional file 1).

Characteristics of participants

Self-perception of the occupation as security guards

Participants conveyed that becoming a security guard was often seen as a fallback option when no better alternatives were available. Security guard roles typically did not require a high level of education, and individuals were inclined to leave the job if presented with other employment opportunities. The high turnover rate in personnel posed challenges to CPR training. Furthermore, participants felt a lack of understanding and respect from society and feared potential repercussions such as being misunderstood, extorted for money, or accused if they performed CPR. Security guards in hospitals felt a heightened sense of responsibility but had fewer occasions to perform CPR. Conversely, security guards in public transportation systems, such as airports and subway stations, often had prescribed procedures to follow in emergencies.

The “intention & performance-focused” model of B-CPR

Key elements that influence the B-CPR intention and subsequent performance were identified. Two distinct scenarios were delineated, involving victims who are either familiar individuals with a trust relationship or strangers without a pre-existing trust relationship. It revealed that for security personnel when the victim was a trusted individual such as a family member, friend, or colleague, there was a tendency to perform CPR even in the absence of confidence, provided one possessed relevant CPR knowledge and skills. However, when faced with unfamiliar individuals without a trusting relationship, the intention of CPR hinged on personal characteristics (e.g., belief in kindness), past experiences, knowledge and skills, and self-efficacy. Despite harboring the intention to perform CPR, individuals would shrink back when faced with strangers due to multiple considerations. Action was only taken when all the barriers were overcome, facilitating CPR performance. Specifically, four categories of barriers to CPR performance were identified: security guard-related, victim-related, organizational, and societal factors. Security guard-related factors primarily involved uncertain operational skills in real-life situations. Factors related to the victim included scenarios where the victim is of the opposite sex or an older individual, uncertainty about the victim’s medical conditions, and an unsafe onsite environment. The onsite environmental safety entails the presence of video recording, cameras, or witnesses during CPR. Organizational barriers mainly stem from a lack of role identification for B-CPR performers, unreasonable workflow for handling OHCA, and non-standardized training. Social barriers predominantly include social norms, negative media reports, low awareness of relevant laws and regulations, and a lack of a reasonable training system. Notably, only by overcoming barriers at all the aforementioned layers (i.e., bystanders, victims, organizations, and society) can the intention to perform CPR be translated into action. Participants also shared their perspectives on the desired CPR training, including trainers, training contents, training formats, and training incentives. Regarding trainers, participants prefer to have medical professionals conduct the training. This preference stems from the professionals’ ability to share personal experiences with real cases, enhancing the training’s authenticity and effectiveness. Some participants expressed a desire to receive training every three months to reinforce memory and consolidate the related knowledge. For the training contents, there was an urgent demand for hands-on simulation exercises and psychological coping skills, in addition to the relevant theoretical knowledge. It was noteworthy that not every security guard had an equal opportunity to do hands-on practice——“In general, the company wouldn’t consider retraining unless it’s for promoting you to become a team leader…They rarely let us practice, with only a few selected individuals out of fifty or sixty getting the opportunity for hands-on experience.” Additionally, psychological coping encompassed not only strategies for overcoming nervousness and anxiety when confronting sudden cardiac arrest and rescue situations but also addressing or self-adjusting negative emotions following unsuccessful resuscitation attempts, such as potential feelings of guilt and flashbacks to the chaotic situations. Furthermore, certificates, rewards, and inclusion in the title evaluation system were all incentives to promote participation in training and enhance security guards’ self-confidence in performing CPR. Discussion

The findings of this study showed that the performance of CPR by Chinese security guards is not without its challenges. While security guards exhibit readiness to perform CPR without hesitation on individuals with whom they have established trust relationships, they may hesitate or “shrink back” when faced with strangers. The factors influencing their decision to perform CPR are multifaceted, encompassing considerations at the individual, organizational, and societal levels. Specifically, these factors include, for example, the security guard’s skills and self-efficacy in performing CPR, the victim’s medical conditions, the workflow of the organization for which the security guard works, and the broader societal attitudes. As such, promoting the performance of CPR for security guards needs to take into account multi-level factors that affect their decision-making.

As a vulnerable population with a relatively low socioeconomic status in China, security guards have concerns about potential job loss or facing legal complaints when performing CPR on strangers. Consequently, they tend to avoid performing CPR to mitigate potential complications. It is consistent with prior studies indicating that the fear of legal liability is one of the important reasons for reluctance to intervene [25, 26]. A 2017 online survey report [25] indicated that when administering CPR to strangers, 53.2% of participants expressed concerns about legal issues. The concern regarding legal liability highlights two primary social issues: the negative portrayal on social media and the insufficient dissemination of legal information, both of which have deterred security guards from performing CPR. Reports of negative incidents involving lawsuits due to failed rescue attempts make it more likely for security guards to anticipate being in a predetermined negative situation. However, the “chivalrous conduct” is always a traditional virtue in Chinese culture. Scholars argue that by bolstering positive publicity and providing scientific guidance, awareness of B-CPR can be heightened, thereby promoting public responsiveness to OHCA [15]. In the realm of legislation, while China initiated its laws relatively late compared to the enactment of the “Good Samaritan” law in the United States [27], it also introduced laws in 2021 to offer robust legal protection for bystanders who voluntarily provide reasonable emergency aid to the injured [15]. The reinforcement of relevant laws should be integrated into CPR training programs to encourage public responsiveness to OHCA. Furthermore, social media platforms should amplify the dissemination of relevant laws and positive examples to alleviate public fears and concerns.

It is worth mentioning that security guards do not have equal opportunities for hands-on practice and tend to defer to senior colleagues and leaders. It unveiled disparities in accessing life-saving training resources among security guards across hierarchical position levels. This adds to the previous finding that the likelihood of receiving CPR training was negatively related to income and education level [1]. However, to further enhance CPR competencies among security guards, collaborative training and educational sessions involving everyone in the chain of survival are necessary [28]. It must be emphasized that everyone should have the opportunity to be trained in CPR [29]. Security personnel who maintain a 24-hour vigil in public spaces not only require a fundamental understanding of first aid knowledge and skills but also necessitate organizational initiatives to enhance their role identification as primary laypersons in CPR performance for people with OHCA. Furthermore, organizations lacking formal CPR training rules, coupled with a deficiency in incentivization mechanisms, may potentially undermine the proactive engagement of security guards in training activities.

The whole training system is inseparable from the active support of social organizations. Specifically, the improvement of the CPR training system should be considered a crucial aspect of accelerating the development of the social emergency response system. The social emergency training system in Bao’an District, Shenzhen, known as the “Baoan Mode” [15, 30], emphasizes a government-led, expert-guided, technologically supported, and socially engaged approach to building the social emergency training system. Localities can adapt and refine the CPR training system, drawing inspiration from the “Baoan Mode”.

Another challenge affecting the CPR performance by security guards is psychological coping, consistent with the “psychological support” as critical link in the “chainmail of survival” [14]. Resuscitation of OHCA is emotionally overwhelming and is different from simulation, which typically occurs in safe and controlled environments [31]. Consistent with other studies, unidentified factors, such as the cause of the collapse, the presence of infectious diseases, the availability of witnesses, and environmental safety, pose additional challenges to the performance of CPR [32, 33]. Security guards in the study also expressed concerns about the potential harm or failure caused by the unqualified practice, which is a concern shared by laypersons universally [34]. This concern can be effectively addressed through action points in CPR education. For example, discussions can include the importance of talking with someone about the experience after the mission and providing opportunities for professional debriefing following participation in real-life CPR situations [35].

Implications

The study findings revealed barriers to CPR intention and performance at multiple levels, including the security guards, victims, organizations, and societal levels, by examining the experiences and perspectives of security guards. This “intention & performance-focused” model of B-CPR offers a theoretical foundation for developing a training model that involves close collaboration and active engagement from individuals, organizations, and society.

Limitations

Firstly, the participants shared their thoughts based on memories, which could have memory bias. Secondly, only two female security guards participated in the study, primarily due to the social demographics of the security guard profession, where females constitute a lower proportion than males, leading to their potential underrepresentation. Finally, this study was conducted with security guards in an urban setting; further research to explore the differences in rural regions is warranted. This will promote the formulation of more effective policies and strategies that cater to the unique needs of security guards in both urban and rural settings.

Conclusions

Security personnel, often the first witnesses to OHCA, should be recognized as a critical link in the “Chainmail of Survival”. This study highlighted that existing CPR training programs have not sufficiently prepared security personnel to initiate CPR. It also identified key factors leading security guards to hesitate or “shrink back” in performing CPR, spanning four layers: bystanders, victims, organizations, and society. Particularly, the reinforcement of relevant laws should be integrated into CPR training programs to encourage public responsiveness to OHCA. The “intention & performance-focused” model” proposed in this study can offer valuable insights and a theoretical basis for shaping future CPR training initiatives.
