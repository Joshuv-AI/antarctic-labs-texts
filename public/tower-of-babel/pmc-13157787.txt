Challenges and enablers in accessing paediatric inguinal hernia surgical care in Sierra Leone: a qualitative exploratory study
Authors: Johanna Mona, Johan G Ahlbäck, Aiah Lebbie, Thomas Ashley, Håkon A Bolkan, Alex J van Duinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess challenges and enablers in accessing paediatric inguinal hernia surgical care at governmental hospitals in Sierra Leone.MethodologyA qualitative exploratory interview study was conducted using semistructured interviews with 16 healthcare providers and 19 guardians of 17 children at four governmental secondary-level and tertiary-level hospitals in Sierra Leone. All interviews were analysed using thematic analysis within a constructivist paradigm, with subsequent framework-based interpretation using the WHO health system building blocks.ResultsThe study identified five interrelated themes describing barriers to accessing surgical care across household and health system levels: (1) access to equipment, consumables and medicines; (2) health-seeking behaviour; (3) financial considerations; (4) workforce; and (5) management and hospital structure. These themes illustrate how health system constraints and household-level barriers interact to delay or impede access to surgery. Key enablers included task-sharing within the surgical workforce, community trust and financial protection through the Free Healthcare Initiative or non-governmental organisations.ConclusionAccess to paediatric inguinal hernia surgery in Sierra Leone is hindered by economic barriers, limited awareness and shortages of staff, equipment and medicines. Targeted community outreach, expansion of the Free Healthcare Initiative beyond age 5 and securing dedicated paediatric surgical instrument sets emerged as feasible strategies to reduce delays and improve equity of care.

Introduction

Paediatric inguinal hernia (PIH) is a common surgical condition in children and carries a risk of serious complications if left untreated.13 PIHs are prone to incarceration, and surgical repair is therefore recommended for all affected children.47 Access to safe and affordable surgical care is a key component of universal health coverage (UHC), commonly conceptualised through dimensions of population coverage, service coverage and financial protection.811 Applying this framework enables a structured analysis of how barriers to paediatric surgical care operate at both household and health system levels.

Sierra Leone is a low-income country with a young population and a severe shortage of specialist surgical providers, including paediatric surgeons.1216 Previous studies in Sierra Leone and Uganda describe barriers to surgical care, including financial constraints, shortages of supplies and trained personnel and weak infrastructure.1720 Despite policy efforts to improve access—most notably the introduction of the Free Healthcare Initiative (FHCI) in 2010, which subsidises care for pregnant women and children under 5—substantial unmet need remains.2124 Population-based data indicate that thousands of children in Sierra Leone continue to live with untreated PIHs.25 26

Although governmental and non-governmental organisation (NGO) collaboration has contributed to improvements in surgical capacity, important structural and clinical challenges persist. According to a 2023 CapaCare survey, anaesthesia practices differed between hospitals, with ketamine sedation more common in district facilities and intubation with assisted ventilation more frequent in the capital. Although intubation is performed in some provincial hospitals, broader implementation would require substantial training and resources, making widespread expansion unrealistic in the short term (online supplemental material 1 and online supplemental material 2).

In Uganda, financial constraints and mistrust in the health system have been identified as critical obstacles to accessing paediatric surgery.19 27 While the magnitude of unmet need for PIH repair in Sierra Leone has been described, there is limited empirical evidence on how such care is accessed, perceived and navigated by guardians and healthcare providers within the public health system.24 25 This study therefore addresses this gap by exploring the challenges and enablers to accessing PIH surgical care at governmental hospitals in Sierra Leone. A qualitative design was chosen to capture context-specific experiences and perceptions of access that are not readily identifiable through routine quantitative data.

Methodology

Design

This qualitative study was based on semistructured interviews with guardians and healthcare staff at four government hospitals in Sierra Leone. The study explored challenges and enablers contributing to the unmet need for PIH repair, with the intention to inform future improvements in quality and volume.28 29

Setting and participants

The three regional government hospitals of Bo, Kenema and Makeni, and the national referral hospital in Freetown, Connaught Hospital granted permission to conduct the study. Participants were selected based on predefined inclusion criteria. Healthcare professionals were eligible if they had direct experience with PIH surgical care at the study hospitals. They were recruited through recommendations from paediatric ward and operating theatre (OT) staff, using purposive and snowball sampling. To ensure diversity of perspectives, staff from different professional disciplines (associate clinicians (ACs), medical doctors (MDs), nurses and anaesthesia providers) were included at each site, totalling 16 interviews. Guardians were eligible if their child had undergone or was awaiting PIH surgery at one of the study sites, and were recruited either during the patient’s hospital admission or shortly after discharge. An approximately equal number of interviews with healthcare professionals and guardians were targeted to facilitate a balanced comparison of perspectives. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to interviews.

Data collection

Semistructured interviews with open-ended questions were conducted to explore perceptions of access to PIH repairs. Interviews were focused on a clearly defined topic—access to PIH surgery—which contributed to their relatively concise duration. Interview guides and questions for guardians (online supplemental material 3) and healthcare staff (online supplemental material 4) were developed based on previous research171924 25 and relevant experience within the research team, and include open-ended questions covering care-seeking pathways, perceived barriers and enablers to PIH surgery. To ensure clarity and appropriate interpretation of questions, the interview guides were piloted, tested and revised based on six interviews with guardians (3) and healthcare providers (3) at Masanga Hospital, Tonkolili district, prior to the study start. With each study participant’s consent, all interviews were audio recorded. All staff interviews were conducted in English by a Norwegian master’s student in Global Health (JM), while most guardian interviews were translated between Krio and English by a local research assistant (RA), who is a native Krio speaker and fluent in English, with extensive experience with research data collection in Sierra Leone. JM transcribed the interviews, which were subsequently reviewed with the RA through transcript validation to ensure fidelity to the original recordings.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted within a constructivist paradigm, acknowledging that findings are coproduced through interaction between participants and researchers. Reflexivity was considered throughout the analytical process, as the dual roles of JM (a foreign researcher) and the RA (a local translator) may have influenced how questions were asked, translated and ultimately how participants chose to respond. Attention was given to potential translation-related influences, which were addressed through verification of translated transcripts. An audit trail of analytical decisions and memos was maintained to support transparency and dependability.

The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach to thematic analysis.30 This approach enabled the combination of inductive and deductive coding within a structured analytical process. Anonymised transcription files were assigned a non-traceable number. Initially, inductive codes were generated from recurrent patterns across the interviews. Deductive codes informed by relevant literature were subsequently incorporated to ensure alignment with existing evidence on access to surgical care in low-resource settings.17 18 27 31 32 Coding was conducted using NVivo (V.14). Codes were reviewed and refined through an iterative process, resulting in a final set of 44 codes. These codes were then grouped into broader themes based on similarity and relevance to the study aim (table 1). Throughout this process, regular peer debriefing discussions were held among JM, HAB and JGA to enhance analytical consistency.

To improve interpretive clarity and situate findings within a health system perspective, the WHO health system building blocks framework was applied at a later stage of the analysis.33 After themes had been developed, they were mapped to the six building blocks (service delivery, health workforce, health information systems, access to essential medicines, financing and leadership/governance). The framework was not used to guide data collection, initial coding or theme development, but was applied in a later stage as an interpretive lens to contextualise findings at the health system level and enhance policy relevance. Data saturation was assessed continuously during data collection and was judged to have been reached after approximately 10 guardian and 11 staff interviews, when little new information emerged. Nevertheless, data collection continued to the predefined target to ensure representation across study sites and professional groups.

Patient and public involvement

The study design was guided by input from staff, hospital management and guardians during an assessment visit to the four hospitals in January 2023. In addition, six pilot interviews (three with guardians and three with healthcare staff) were conducted to refine and adapt the interview guides prior to data collection. While patients and guardians were not formally involved in interpreting the results, their perspectives were incorporated during these preparatory stages to ensure clarity and relevance of the study tools.

Results

Description of participants

Between 10 October and 17 November 2023, a total of 33 interviews were conducted across the four hospitals, including 19 guardians of 17 children and 16 healthcare staff (table 2). Interviews lasted an average of 14 min. No ACs were interviewed at Connaught Hospital as they did not perform paediatric surgery. Instead, two anaesthesia providers with relevant experience were interviewed. Guardians comprised 11 mothers, 3 fathers, 4 grandparents and 1 uncle. Ages of paediatric patients ranged from 7 months to 17 years (median 4.5 years). 13 guardians received free surgery for their children, either through FHCI or NGO funding. Four guardians of children older than 5 years paid out-of-pocket (OOP).

Access to equipment, consumables and medicines

All 16 staff participants reported shortages of medications and equipment, reflecting one of the five themes identified in the analysis (figure 1), with 14 also noting a lack of consumables. Shortages often necessitated the use of adult-sized equipment and the reuse of single-use items, and were consistently associated with reduced surgical volume and concerns about quality of care.

Main results for each theme. OT, operating theatre; PIH, paediatric inguinal hernia.

[…] right now, we only have the adult-size laryngoscope. So, imagine we [are] using that for a ped [child]. You have to be very, very careful and skilled, you know. […] we only have about five paediatric size, different sized ET [endotracheal] tubes, which we have to constantly recycle. (Anaesthesia provider, 7)

Insufficient access to paediatric surgical instruments, monitoring devices and equipment such as autoclaves was reported to delay procedures and compromise surgical quality. Across the three provincial hospitals, dedicated paediatric instrument sets were largely unavailable; one site reported having none, and all emphasised the need for more. At one facility, one AC had personally purchased his own paediatric surgical instruments. Consequently, adult instruments were frequently used for PIH surgeries, which providers perceived to increase the risk of complications.

In fact, there’s a lot of issue with paediatric surgical instruments. […] the artery [forceps] we don’t have […] So even if the surgeon is very, very skilled… and the artery [forceps] are plumped, they cannot handle the tissue. There is a lot of damage to the tissue. The quality is dropped, and on the end of the day, there’s risk of what? Infection. (AC, 6)

In the capital, the availability of paediatric surgical instruments was considered sufficient, underscoring a marked urban–rural disparity in equipment access. Some staff emphasised that with the necessary equipment, they could increase surgical volume, as they already possessed the required surgical skills.

Health professionals considered the main prerequisite to increase surgical volumes and ensuring quality of care was to ensure consistent government supply of consumables, medicines and equipment, as well as strengthening hospital management and seeking additional support from NGOs. Shortages of medicines tended to delay surgeries or complicate the provision of anaesthesia.

[We need] un-interrupted supply of consumables, which is one of the problems that we have been encountering. And then, access to drugs. So that parents who come into the hospital with their kids do not go back because there are no supplies. (MD, 18)

Overall, shortages of equipment, consumables and medicines jointly constrained both access to and quality of paediatric surgical care, reflecting a systemic limitation rather than isolated stock-outs.

Health-seeking behaviour

Healthcare providers noted that many patients lived with their hernias for extended periods before seeking hospital care, often only presenting when severe complications arose. Delays in treatment were sometimes attributed to unprofessional advice or inadequate care received at private clinics. Narratives illustrate how misconceptions about hernia causation and treatment, shared by both community members and some healthcare providers, contributed to delayed care-seeking.

There are times we are only faced with the complications. […] there are times they wait until [the hernia is] obstructed before they send [them here]. […] And there are times they will even operate in their own small corners, which I think is inappropriate. (MD, 8)

One MD stated that when noticing abnormalities such as hernias on their children, guardians “associate it with either witchcraft, or […] traditional beliefs” (MD, 11) and would seek help from traditional healers or use traditional medicine to cure the condition. Most guardians reported not seeking other treatments before the current surgery, yet traditional medicine was often used to alleviate hernia pain and some asserted that it reduces the hernia size. Common remedies included plant-based preparations from papaya leaves or Gbangba (a local herb).

Misconceptions were not limited to community beliefs but were also reinforced through advice received within the health system, where hernias described as ‘too young’ for surgery led to treatment delays for years. Some guardians had pursued healthcare previously and were then advised to postpone surgery for years to avoid recurrence.

I brought him to the hospital when he was a toddler, but they told me that the hernia is young. So, they are not going to operate it, because if they did, it would come again in the future. So, I decided to wait until the child was [in early adolescence] before undergoing the operation. (Mother, 28)

Proper knowledge and trust in the health system enabled guardians to identify hernias and seek timely care. Typical clinical attributes that prompted contact with health services were general suspicion of illness or a visible lump in the groin. Several guardians had been informed that surgery was the only effective treatment for PIH, and many trusted the hospital treatment. To enable the early identification and referral of children with hernias, thereby reducing the risk of complications, healthcare professionals highlighted the need for increased public awareness and improved knowledge at peripheral healthcare facilities. Radio was considered an appropriate channel for disseminating information, and reports of successful surgeries were seen as crucial in motivating parents to seek hospital care.

We can do radio programs […] Because we have a lot of people performing surgery in the community […] and they are doing a lot of damage. So, if you sensitize the community, that these are the consequences, but there are people here [at the hospital] that are trained [and] have the resources to perform these [surgeries safely], […] those things will contribute towards patients coming to the hospital. (AC, 6)

Health-seeking behaviour was shaped by a combination of individual knowledge gaps and system-level misinformation, delaying timely referral and access to surgical care.

Financial considerations

Financial constraints emerged as a cross-cutting factor influencing access across multiple domains and are therefore addressed in detail in this section. Both staff and guardians identified financial constraints as the main barrier to PIH surgery. Poverty among guardians, underfunded hospitals and inadequate staff remuneration were described as interconnected challenges that delayed or prevented surgery.

So, we are asked to pay the amount of 300 leones (13 USD) [for an examination], but I did not have money at that time, so I decided to take the child back home and go to the pharmacy and buy some medications for her. (Mother, 27)

Across interviews, financial barriers were described as both a household-level constraint and a system-level challenge linked to inconsistent policy implementation. Unexpected costs for medications and lack of equipment were major concerns for guardians, compounded by inconsistent implementation of FHCI. To prevent patients from being discharged without care, staff occasionally contributed financially to cover associated costs. Across all four sites, guardians frequently paid for medications, and at one provincial hospital, even children under five were occasionally charged for surgery.

There are times they [children under five years] also pay for surgeries. Because I’ve scanned a child of recent […]. The child was just two years plus. He’s under five and was charged. […] [The mother] preferred taking the child home, because according to her, she cannot afford. So, this is the problem. (AC, 12)

Collaboration with hospital management was a facilitator in overcoming the financial constraints and guardians were in some cases able to negotiate the price of the treatment. One anaesthesia provider emphasised the priority given to paediatric surgeries, highlighting the efforts made by hospital staff to finance these procedures:

Sometimes they [parents] meet with the… the MS [Medical Superintendent, [or] they meet the matron. […] [The matron] does everything in her power to get those things for the procedure. Yea, because for children we don’t allow the surgery not to be done because of money, no, no. The matron will stand firm on that. (Anaesthesia provider, 10)

Among the 17 children, 13 had been able to get free surgery through an NGO or FHCI. Some guardians had to postpone surgery for several years due to lack of funds. This illustrates how external financial support was often decisive in enabling access to surgery.

Workforce

Across all sites and staff professions, limited access to post-graduate training in paediatric surgical management was described as a challenge to quality and timely care. Participants emphasised the need for specialised expertise in managing children, particularly in anaesthesia and perioperative care.

Having a specialist paediatric surgeon, like really labelled paediatric surgeon makes a lot of difference than just general surgeons trying to practise as paediatric surgeons. (Anaesthesia provider, 7)

Rather than framing workforce limitations solely in terms of staff shortages, participants emphasised the importance of skill mix and task-sharing as pragmatic strategies to sustain paediatric surgical services.

We need more paediatric surgeons. […] We need to specialize paediatric anaesthetic nurses or doctors, to be able to handle the kids well. (MD, 18)

At one hospital, community health officers (CHOs) were trained to manage postoperative care for paediatric patients, while at another, the implementation of the Emergency Triage Assessment and Treatment protocol enabled nurses to deliver emergency care when doctors were unavailable.

[…] we trained our CHOs on post management of cases in the ward. […] [Now] they are able to take care of minor problems that happen in the ward, and they can communicate them to us directly. (MD, 11)

Despite recognised gaps in formal training, both guardians and healthcare providers generally perceived paediatric surgical care to be of good quality. Participants described recent improvements driven by NGO support, task-shifting initiatives and strong collaboration within surgical teams.

Overall, workforce limitations reflected not only shortages of specialised staff but also uneven distribution of paediatric competencies, partly mitigated through task-sharing and local training initiatives.

Management and hospital structure

Participants consistently described hospital management and infrastructure as underlying determinants that shaped the feasibility of surgical care. Hospital infrastructure was identified as a major constraint on surgical capacity and quality. Ageing facilities, recurrent power outages and water shortages were reported to disrupt essential services such as oxygen access and OT functionality. Non-functioning air conditioning in the OT was perceived to compromise both working conditions and surgical safety, occasionally resulting in postponed surgeries.

Patients are sent home because the OT is not well functioning. […] Sometimes the parents, the relatives, they are just devastated, discouraged. (Ward nurse, 1)

The [water] pipe that is supplying the hospital sometimes is not running […] So, most times if we have surgeries, the relative have to find the water, and then bring it to the OT. (AC, 24)

Capacity limitations on wards, particularly in regional hospitals, further limited service delivery. The absence of dedicated paediatric surgical wards led to mixing of infectious and surgical cases, raising concerns about patient safety:

[…] There are infected cases, and we mix them with the surgical cases, which is not good. (Ward nurse, 9)

Beyond physical infrastructure, organisational and governance-related factors influenced access to care. Inconsistent implementation of FHCI and the absence of standard operating procedures impacted the quality and continuity of care. Delays were also attributed to late referrals within and between hospitals.

Leadership emerged as a critical enabler. Across all sites, supportive hospital management was reported to enhance staff motivation, foster collaboration and improve organisational culture. Participants highlighted recent improvements associated with leadership changes and NGO-supported renovations, which were perceived to strengthen both morale and surgical capacity.

Because of back bone good leadership, there is great improvement in the healthcare delivery […] There is no segregation, […] there is a feeling that everybody is considered important, ranging from the porter to the topmost clinicians. (MD, 21)

Despite expressing confidence in their technical skills, staff emphasised that adequate infrastructure and institutional support were prerequisites for expanding paediatric surgical services.

We have the manpower that can do the surgery. […] We need a building, we need the instruments, and then we increase the [surgical] capacity. If we have a paediatric building of excellence – you have a lot of cases [coming]. A lot! And once they come, our OT is set, our equipment is there – *whistles* – we are there. (MD, 21)

Taken together, management and infrastructure acted as structural determinants of paediatric surgical access, constraining service delivery when inadequate but enabling quality improvement where leadership and organisational support were strong.

Discussion

This study demonstrates how financial, informational and structural barriers intersect across household and health system levels to shape access to paediatric surgical care in Sierra Leone. These barriers often overlap, contributing to delays from symptom recognition to surgical treatment. These findings are consistent with studies from other low-resource settings, which describe similar interactions between poverty, health system capacity and service delivery constraints.31 32 At the hospital level, such barriers reflect broader system weaknesses, including shortages of equipment, medications, staffing and specialised expertise, as previously reported in Sierra Leone17 18 34 and other LMICs.19 27 35 36 Dedicated hospital leadership emerged as a critical and complex prerequisite for a functional healthcare system.37 38

At a structural level, under-resourced hospital facilities, unstable access to water and electricity, low wages and understaffing contributed to delays and interruptions in surgical care—constraints previously linked to inadequate healthcare financing in Sierra Leone.17 18 34 In this context, children were occasionally discharged without surgery due to inability to pay, increasing the likelihood that families would turn to unqualified providers or traditional healers, further delaying appropriate intervention. Similar financial barriers to accessing surgical care have been reported in studies from Sierra Leone and Uganda.171925 31 32 34 35

Misconceptions about PIH contributed to delays in receiving appropriate treatment, with some guardians only presenting after complications had occurred. This aligns with existing literature showing that cultural beliefs and traditional practices influence health-seeking behaviours in sub-Saharan Africa.171939 40 Although cultural and gender-related factors were not explicitly explored, they were implicitly reflected in accounts of traditional beliefs, misconceptions and family decision-making. Educational campaigns aimed at raising awareness about the signs and symptoms of PIH, as well as the importance of early surgical intervention, could result in more timely and appropriate treatment of paediatric hernias. Similar to prior studies, healthcare professionals emphasised community awareness initiatives as important enablers, with radio broadcasting viewed as effective.17 18 34 41 Emerging evidence suggests that mobile phone-based communication may represent an increasingly relevant platform for public health messaging, including in rural settings.4244

In addition to misconceptions among guardians, some participants reported having been advised by healthcare providers to postpone surgery because the hernia was considered ‘too young’, potentially reinforcing misinformation and delayed care. These findings suggest that delays were not solely driven by community beliefs but also by inconsistencies in health information within the formal health system. Despite acknowledged gaps in training, both guardians and providers expressed confidence in surgical quality, suggesting that experience, task-sharing and adaptive practices may foster functional resilience among healthcare workers in resource-constrained settings. Evidence from Sierra Leone shows that task-sharing and targeted training of ACs can increase surgical volume while maintaining quality.20 45 46 Consistent with this, participants viewed workforce expansion and paediatric-focused training as key enablers for improving paediatric surgical care, in line with previous research and the WHO health system building blocks related to workforce and service delivery.27

Limited access to appropriate surgical equipment and medications represents a key vulnerability, as reliance on adult instruments may compromise outcomes. While staff improvisation and commitment partially mitigated these constraints, such workarounds reveal systemic weaknesses that require structural solutions. Offering targeted incentives, such as rural allowances, may support the retention of skilled staff in underserved settings and complement investments in equipment and supplies.47 48 In parallel, strengthening government policies for standardised procurement and maintenance of medical equipment, supported by NGO collaboration, may reduce reliance on ad hoc improvisation and improve continuity of care.49

Previous research has shown increased utilisation of healthcare services for children under 5 following the introduction of FHCI.22 50 Consistent with this, most guardians in our study stated that financial support from FHCI or an NGO was necessary for the surgery to take place. However, inconsistent implementation of FHCI left some families facing treatment-related costs. For children developing hernias later in childhood or experiencing prolonged delays to surgery, expanding FHCI coverage beyond the age of 5 may therefore be critical. In addition, the introduction of a national health insurance scheme, initiated in Sierra Leone in 2018, represents a potential mechanism to reduce high OOP costs. Similar schemes have been associated with increased surgical activity in other LMICs.51 However, the scheme has yet to become operational, making it essential to address its implementation. These findings align with the WHO UHC cube, highlighting persistent gaps across all three dimensions: limited population coverage, constrained service availability due to shortages of equipment and trained staff and incomplete financial protection. Without addressing these gaps across all three dimensions, paediatric surgical care is unlikely to advance towards UHC in Sierra Leone.

Addressing barriers to PIH treatment in Sierra Leone requires coordinated action at both community and system levels. At the community level, targeted awareness initiatives, including engagement of primary care providers and guardians of patients with PIH, may help counter misconceptions and promote timely care-seeking. At the system level, improving access to paediatric surgical instruments and essential anaesthetic drugs, strengthening workforce capacity through task-shifting and extending effective financial protection mechanisms, such as FHCI, were identified as feasible strategies to enhance service coverage. If carefully designed and adapted, community-based health insurance and microfinance interventions may improve access among the economically vulnerable populations; however, evidence of their effectiveness has been mixed.5256 Finally, sustained investment in hospital leadership and infrastructure, supported through government and non-governmental collaboration, will be essential to reduce inequities and address the backlog of untreated children.

Strengths and limitations

This qualitative study provides valuable insights into health-seeking behaviours and access to surgical care through a structured approach guided by Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis. It prioritises the perspectives of healthcare workers and guardians of children with PIH, offering insights that could enhance patient-centred paediatric hernia care. The analysis gained credibility through the application of both inductive and deductive coding methods, and theoretical triangulation was achieved using the WHO Six Building Blocks framework. The inclusion of diverse professional roles and collaboration with the Ministry of Health and local partners further enriched the quality and breadth of the data.

The study faced several limitations. As only guardians whose children eventually accessed surgery were included, the perspectives of those who never reached treatment are absent. However, many children underwent surgery only after long delays, in some cases up to ten years, offering valuable insights into barriers that delayed timely access. Although interviews were relatively short in duration, their focused scope and the specificity of the research question supported the identification of consistent themes across participants. The targeted nature of the topic may have facilitated efficient data generation without requiring prolonged narrative interviews. The reliance on self-reported data introduces a risk of recall bias, especially since most guardian interviews occurred weeks after hospital admissions. The involvement of a foreign researcher and the use of translated interviews may have influenced how experiences were articulated, despite reflexive measures to mitigate this. Despite efforts to mitigate interruptions during interviews, these factors could have influenced the quality of the collected data.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that access to PIH surgery in Sierra Leone is shaped by interacting household-level and system-level barriers, including financial constraints, limited awareness and shortages of staff, equipment and medicines. Despite these challenges, task-sharing, supportive hospital leadership and community trust in surgical services function as important enablers. These findings suggest that targeted community awareness initiatives, improved availability of paediatric surgical instruments and essential anaesthetic drugs, and more consistent financial protection—particularly for children older than 5 years—are feasible strategies to reduce delays and improve equity of access to paediatric surgical care.
