Uniform set systems with small VC-dimension
Ting-Wei Chao, Zixiang Xu, Chi Hoi Yip, Shengtong Zhang

Abstract
We investigate the longstanding problem of determining the maximum size of a ( d + 1 ) (d+1) -uniform set system with VC-dimension at most d d . Since the seminal 1984 work of Frankl and Pach, which established the elegant upper bound ( n d ) \binom{n}{d} , this question has resisted significant progress. The best-known lower bound is ( n − 1 d ) + ( n − 4 d − 2 ) \binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2} , obtained by Ahlswede and Khachatrian, leaving a substantial gap of ( n − 1 d − 1 ) − ( n − 4 d − 2 ) \binom{n-1}{d-1}-\binom{n-4}{d-2} . Despite decades of effort, improvements to the Frankl–Pach bound have been incremental at best: Mubayi and Zhao introduced an Ω d ​ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega_{d}(\log{n}) improvement for prime powers d d , while Ge, Xu, Yip, Zhang, and Zhao achieved a gain of 1 for general d d . In this work, we provide a purely combinatorial approach that significantly sharpens the Frankl–Pach upper bound. Specifically, for large n n , we demonstrate that the Frankl–Pach bound can be improved to ( n d ) − ( n − 1 d − 1 ) + O d ​ ( n d − 1 − 1 4 ​ d − 2 ) = ( n − 1 d ) + O d ​ ( n d − 1 − 1 4 ​ d − 2 ) \binom{n}{d}-\binom{n-1}{d-1}+O_{d}(n^{d-1-\frac{1}{4d-2}})=\binom{n-1}{d}+O_{d}(n^{d-1-\frac{1}{4d-2}}) . This result completely removes the main term ( n − 1 d − 1 ) \binom{n-1}{d-1} from the previous gap between the known lower and upper bounds. It also offers fresh insights into the combinatorial structure of uniform set systems with small VC-dimension. In addition, the original Erdős–Frankl–Pach conjecture, which sought to generalize the EKR theorem in the 1980s, has been disproven. We propose a new refined conjecture that might establish a sturdier bridge between VC-dimension and the EKR theorem, and we verify several specific cases of this conjecture, which is of independent interest.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

Extremal set theory concerns the problem of determining the maximum or the minimum size of a set system with certain properties. This area of research originated in 1961 with the seminal work of Erdős, Ko, and Rado
[
8
]
, which states that for all
n\geq 2k
, the maximum size of a
k
-uniform set system on
[n]
in which any two members have non-empty intersection, is given by
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
; moreover, when
n\geq 2k+1
, all maximum intersecting families are trivially intersecting. This result laid the groundwork for a vibrant and extensive field of study. Over time, their theorem has been significantly generalized to encompass a variety of set systems, including those with subsets of varying sizes, families satisfying broader intersection conditions, and even analogues in algebraic and geometric contexts
[
6
,
16
,
19
]
.

In this paper, we focus on problems involving the
Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension
(
VC-dimension
), which is a classical complexity measure for set systems and hypothesis classes in machine learning. Formally, let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of subsets of a ground set
X
. The VC-dimension of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted by
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{F})
, is the largest integer
d
such that there exists a set
S=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\}\subseteq X
with the following property:

\text{For every subset }A\subseteq S,\text{ there exists }F\in\mathcal{F}\text{ such that }F\cap S=A.

In other words, the family
\mathcal{F}
shatters
S
if it can realize all
2^{d}
possible subsets of
S
. In particular, if no such
S
of size
d+1
exists, then
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{F})\leq d
.

The foundational Sauer-Shelah-Perles lemma was provided by three different groups of researchers
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, who completely determined the maximum size of a set system with a given VC-dimension.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{V}
be a set system with VC-dimension at most
d
, then

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d}\binom{|V|}{i}
.

Note that the Hamming ball of radius
d
has VC-dimension at most
d
, therefore the problem for non-uniform set systems has already been completely solved. However, the corresponding question for uniform set systems remains open.

Question 1.2
.

What is the maximum size of
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
with VC-dimension at most
d
?

When
n\geq 2d+1
, the star
\binom{[n-1]}{d}\cup\{n\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
has VC-dimension
d
, and it is a maximum intersecting family by the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem. To see the connection between Question
1.2
and the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem, observe that if
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{m}\}\subset\binom{[n]}{d+1}
, then the condition of VC
(\mathcal{F})\leq d
can be interpreted as follows: for each
1\leq i\leq m
, there exists a subset
B_{i}\subsetneq F_{i}
such that
F\cap F_{i}\neq B_{i}
for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Thus, if
\mathcal{F}
is an intersecting family, then
B_{i}
can be taken to be the empty set for all
i
.

In the 1980s, Erdős
[
7
]
and Frankl and Pach
[
13
]
conjectured that
\binom{n-1}{d}
is the correct answer to Question
1.2
when
n
is sufficiently large compared to
d
. If true, this conjecture would generalize the Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem. However, in 1997, Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
1
]
disproved this conjecture by constructing a
(d+1)
-uniform set system with VC-dimension
d
and size
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
for
n\geq 2(d+1)
. Later, Mubayi and Zhao
[
22
]
provided infinitely many non-isomorphic
(d+1)
-uniform set systems achieving size
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
, and conjectured that this is the correct answer to
Question

1.2
.

On the other hand, Frankl and Pach
[
13
]
provided an upper bound via an elegant algebraic proof.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
n,d
be positive integers with
n\geq d+1
. If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is a set system with VC-dimension at most
d
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n}{d}
.

Later, Mubayi and Zhao
[
22
]
showed that when
d
is a prime power and
n
is sufficiently large compared to
d
, the Frankl-Pach upper bound can be improved to
\binom{n}{d}-\Omega_{d}(\log{n})
. In a recent development
[
15
]
, Ge, Zhao, and three of the authors showed that the Frankl–Pach upper bound can be improved to
\binom{n}{d}-1
for all positive integers
n,d
with
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2d+2
. These results, while having elegant proofs, reveal how challenging it has been to make meaningful advances.

1.2
Improvement on the Frankl–Pach bound

The main term of the current gap between the upper and lower bounds is
\binom{n-1}{d-1}
, and the aforementioned improvements in
[
15
,
22
]
to the upper bound have been relatively modest. The main result of this paper is a significant improvement of the longstanding Frankl–Pach upper bound, eliminating the main term
\binom{n-1}{d-1}
from the gap between lower and upper bounds.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
be a positive integer and
n
be a sufficiently large integer compared to
d
. If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is a set system with VC-dimension at most
d
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d}+C_{d}\cdot n^{d-1-\frac{1}{4d-2}}
for some constant
C_{d}>0
.

Our result serves as additional evidence that the correct answer to
Question

1.2
is
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
; see
Section

3
and
Section

6
for related discussions. All previous upper bounds
[
13
,
15
,
22
]
for
Question

1.2
were derived using linear algebra methods. Surprisingly, our proof of
Theorem

1.4
employs a purely combinatorial approach instead. We present the entire proof in
Section

3
.

We still believe that linear algebra methods have significant potential for addressing this problem and may even provide a complete resolution. In
Section

4
, we refine the polynomial method used in
[
15
]
. This refinement leads to
Theorem

4.1
, which provides a polynomial improvement over
Theorem

1.3
by
\Omega_{d}(n^{d-4})
. While this result is weaker than
Theorem

1.4
, we believe that its proof is of independent interest.

1.3
A modified conjecture generalizing the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem

As mentioned earlier, one of the original motivations for studying
Question

1.2
, initiated by Erdős
[
7
]
, Frankl and Pach
[
13
]
in the 1980s, was to generalize the celebrated Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem
[
8
]
. However, this attempt was refuted by Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
1
]
.

Here we explore a different approach to generalizing the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem, this time in the context of VC-dimension theory. Specifically, we propose the following “uniform Erdős–Frankl–Pach” conjecture as a potential correct form.

Conjecture 1.5
.

Let
n\geq 2(d+1)
and let
0\leq s\leq d
. Assume
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is a set family such that for every set
F\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a subset
B_{F}\subseteq F
of size
s
such that
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F}
for all
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d}
.

In this paper, we verify this conjecture for several specific cases in
Section

5
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Conjecture

1.5
holds in the following cases:

•

s\in\{0,d\}
, or

•

s=1
, and
n
is sufficiently large compared to
d
.

Note that
Conjecture

1.5
recovers the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem as the special case
s=0
. In particular, if
\mathcal{F}
is a star, then for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
, we can take
B_{F}
to be an arbitrary subset of
F\setminus\{a\}
with size
s
; then clearly
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F}
for all
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
and the upper bound
\binom{n-1}{d}
on
\mathcal{F}
can be achieved. However, it appears that no other obvious family satisfies the assumption of the conjecture and achieves the same size. Thus, in the spirit of the Hilton-Milner theorem
[
17
]
, it is plausible to conjecture that all maximum families satisfying the assumption of
Conjecture

1.5
are stars provided that
n
is sufficiently large compared to
d
. Indeed, we confirm this when
s=d
in
Theorem

5.1
and
s=1
in
Theorem

5.2
. In particular,
Theorem

1.6
also indicates that
Conjecture

1.5
holds when
d=2
and
n
is sufficiently large.

2
Useful Tools

The Erdős-Rado sunflower lemma
[
9
]
is a classical result in combinatorics, which states that any sufficiently large set system must contain a sunflower. A
sunflower
(or delta-system) is a collection of
k
distinct sets
S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{k}
such that their pairwise intersections are identical, called the
core
. Their non-overlapping parts are the
petals
. The lemma gives an upper bound on the size of a set family that avoids a sunflower with
k
sets:
|\mathcal{F}|\leq(k-1)^{r}r!
, where
r
is the maximum set size. In a recent breakthrough, Alweiss, Lovett, Wu, and Zhang
[
2
]
improved the bound in the sunflower lemma significantly, and the current best-known bound was proven by Bell, Chueluecha, and Warnke
[
4
]
.

In recent years, significant progress has been made on several important combinatorial problems under the additional assumption of a bounded VC-dimension, such as the Erdős-Schur conjecture
[
11
]
, the Erdős-Hajnal conjecture
[
10
,
23
]
and the sunflower conjecture
[
3
,
12
]
. In particular, assuming bounded VC-dimension, Balogh, Bernshteyn, Delcourt, Ferber, and Pham
[
3
]
quantitatively strengthened the sunflower lemma. The iterated logarithm of
k
, denoted as
\log^{\ast}k
, is the number of times the logarithm function must be applied iteratively to
k
until the result is less than or equal to 1.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
n\geq k>d
and
r\geq 1
be positive integers. There exists a constant
C_{r,d}>0
such that any
k
-uniform set system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
with VC dimension at most
d
and
|\mathcal{F}|\geq(C_{r,d}\cdot\log^{\ast}k)^{k}
contains a sunflower with
r
petals.

For a set system
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
, and for an integer
1\leq s\leq d
, we denote the
s
-shadow
of
\mathcal{F}
as

\partial_{s}\mathcal{F}:=\{T\in\binom{[n]}{s}:T\subseteq F\text{ for some }F\in\mathcal{F}\}.

One of the earliest results in extremal set theory, the celebrated Kruskal-Katona theorem
[
18
,
20
]
, provides a concise and elegant description of the relationship between the cardinalities of
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
and its
d
-shadow. Here, we use the Lovász version from
[
21
, Exercise 13.31(b)]
, which reformulates the theorem to give a continuous relaxation of the combinatorial result.

Theorem 2.2

(Kruskal-Katona theorem)
.

For any
\alpha\geq d+1
, if
\mathcal{G}
is a
(d+1)
-uniform set family with
\lvert\mathcal{G}\rvert=\binom{\alpha}{d+1}
, then

\lvert\partial_{d}\mathcal{G}\rvert\geq\binom{\alpha}{d}=\lvert\mathcal{G}\rvert\cdot\frac{d+1}{\alpha-d}.

Füredi and Griggs
[
14
]
provided the following characterization of extremal families of shadows, which plays a key role in identifying certain extremal families.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
n>d\geq g
be positive integers. Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
be a set system such that its
g
-shadow is minimal. Then its
(g-1)
-shadow is also minimal.

We will also take advantage of a recent result in
[
5
]
on the partial shadows.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
n\geq d+1
and
k
be positive integers. Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{n}{d+1}
and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d}
be families such that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
, there exist
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\in\mathcal{G}
such that
G_{i}\subseteq F
for any
i\in[k]
. Then if
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{x}{k}
for some real number
x\geq k
, we have
|\mathcal{G}|\geq\binom{x}{k-1}
.

The only extremal family in the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem
[
8
]
is the
star
family, also referred to as a
trivially intersecting
family. Hilton and Milner
[
17
]
proved a stability result, demonstrating that the size of non-trivially intersecting families is considerably smaller than the maximum size given by the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem.

Theorem 2.5

(Hilton-Milner
[
17
]
)
.

Let
n,k
be positive integers with
n>2k
. If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
is an intersecting family and
\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}}F=\emptyset
, then we have

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{k-1}-\binom{n-k-1}{k-1}+1.

3
Proof of
Theorem

1.4

Throughout the section, let
d\geq 2
be a fixed integer and
n
be a sufficiently large integer compared to
d
. All the constants in the proof below are positive constants depending only on
d
, and we will not always state their explicit dependence on
d
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
be a set system with VC-dimension at most
d
. We can assume that
m\geq\binom{n-1}{d}
, otherwise we are already done. By the definition of VC-dimension, for each set
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
,
i\in[m]
, there must exist at least one subset
B_{i}\subsetneq F_{i}
such that
F\cap F_{i}\neq B_{i}
for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
. From this point onward, we always assume that when selecting
B_{i}
for each
i\in[m]
, we choose a set with the largest cardinality among all available options.

3.1
Overview of the proof

Let us first give an overview of our proof strategy. The proof consists of three steps. In the first step, we extend Mubayi and Zhao’s ingenious sunflower method to prove a preliminary but important estimate,
Claim

3.2
. In the second step, we use a double-counting argument on the link of
\mathcal{F}
to prove the key
Lemma

3.5
. This lemma constructs a small set
J\subset[n]
that intersects almost every set in
\mathcal{F}
. We note that such a
J
does not exist for the tight example
\binom{[n]}{\leq d}
of the non-uniform
Lemma

1.1
, so its existence in the uniform case sheds light on why the Frankl–Pach problem has a different answer. Finally, we split
\mathcal{F}
into
6
subfamilies
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}
based the set
J
constructed in the second step. With sophisticated structural analysis, we construct various near-injections (maps that are injective on most of the domain) to control the size of each part (
Claim

3.7
through
Claim

3.10
) and complete the proof.

We first prove a simple observation by the definition of
B_{i}
.

Claim 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a subfamily of
\mathcal{F}
, and let
\phi:\mathcal{G}\to 2^{[n]}
be a map that satisfies
\phi(G)\subseteq G
for any
G\in\mathcal{G}
. If
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{G}_{2}
, where each
F_{k}\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
satisfies
\phi(F_{k})=B_{k}
and
|B_{k}|=d
. Then
\phi
is injective if
\phi|_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}
is injective.

Proof of claim.

Suppose
\phi|_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}
is injective, but
\phi
is not. Then there exist
F_{k_{1}},F_{k_{2}}\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\phi(F_{k_{1}})=\phi(F_{k_{2}})
. Suppose that
F_{k_{1}}\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
, then we have
B_{k_{1}}=\phi(F_{k_{1}})=\phi(F_{k_{2}})\subseteq F_{k_{2}}
, which yields that
F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{2}}=B_{k_{1}}
, a contradiction. Therefore
F_{k_{1}}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, and by symmetry we also have
F_{k_{2}}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
. However, this is impossible since
\phi|_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}
is injective. This finishes the proof.
∎

3.2
Estimate via sunflower lemma

The selection criteria for
B_{i}
allows us to apply the sunflower lemma and derive an upper bound on the number of
F_{i}
’s sharing the same
B_{i}
. The proof is inspired by and generalizes
[
22
, Lemma 3]
in Mubayi-Zhao’s work.

Claim 3.2
.

For each subset
A\subseteq[n]
, the number of indices
i\in[m]
with
B_{i}=A
is at most
C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}
, where
C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}
is a constant arising from
Theorem

2.1
. Consequently, for each integer
0\leq s\leq d
, the number of indices
i\in[m]
with
|B_{i}|=s
is at most
C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}\cdot\binom{n}{s}
.

Proof of claim.

By
Theorem

2.1
, it suffices to show that for each subset
A\subseteq[n]
, the set system
\mathcal{F}_{A}:=\{F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}:B_{i}=A\}
does not contain a sunflower of size
d+3
.

Suppose there exists a sunflower with sets
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{d+3}\in\mathcal{F}_{A}
with
\bigcap_{j=1}^{d+3}G_{j}=C
. Obviously
|C|\leq d
since
\mathcal{F}_{A}
is
(d+1)
-uniform, which yields that
|G_{j}\setminus C|>0
for each
j\in[d+3]
. Moreover, by definition,
A
must be a proper subset of
C
. By the rule of selection of
B_{i}
’s, we can see for
G_{1}
, there must exist some
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that

F\cap G_{1}=A\cup(G_{1}\setminus C).

By definition of sunflower,
G_{2}\setminus C,G_{3}\setminus C,\ldots,G_{d+3}\setminus C
must be pairwise disjoint, therefore, there exists some
k\in\{2,3,\ldots,d+3\}
such that
F\cap(G_{k}\setminus C)=\emptyset
. However, this implies that the intersection
F\cap G_{k}=F\cap C=(F\cap G_{1})\cap C=A
, a contradiction. This finishes the proof.
∎

3.3
The link of the set system and a near-transversal set

Given
F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}
and their corresponding subsets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
, we now turn our attention to the
link
of
\mathcal{F}
. Specifically, for each element
v\in[n]
, define

\mathcal{X}_{v}:=\{F_{k}\setminus\{v\}:v\in B_{k},k\in[m]\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Y}_{v}:=\{F_{k}\setminus\{v\}:v\in F_{k}\setminus B_{k},k\in[m]\}.

Then we can view
\mathcal{X}_{v}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{v}
as
d
-uniform set systems on ground set
[n]\setminus\{v\}
and
\mathcal{X}_{v}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{v}
as the link of
\mathcal{F}
at
v
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{X}_{v}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{v}=\emptyset
. Moreover, there is a bijection
\mathcal{X}_{v}\sqcup\mathcal{Y}_{v}\to\{F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}:v\in F_{k}\}
defined by
S\to S\cup\{v\}
, so each set
F_{k}\setminus\{v\}
in
\mathcal{X}_{v}
or
\mathcal{Y}_{v}
corresponds uniquely to a set
F_{k}
in
\mathcal{F}
, and thus to a corresponding
B_{k}
.

Next, for a fixed
v\in[n]
, we proceed to estimate the sizes of
\mathcal{X}_{v}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{v}
respectively.

Claim 3.3
.

For each
v\in[n]
, the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{X}_{v}
is at most
d-1
. Consequently,
|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d-1}
.

Proof of claim.

For each
X_{k}=F_{k}\setminus\{v\}\in\mathcal{X}_{v}
, we have
X_{k}\cap X_{\ell}\neq B_{k}\setminus\{v\}
for any other set
X_{\ell}:=F_{\ell}\setminus\{v\}\in\mathcal{X}_{v}
, otherwise we have
F_{k}\cap F_{\ell}=B_{k}
, a contradiction. Therefore
\mathcal{X}_{v}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus\{v\}}{d}
has VC-dimension at most
d-1
, which yields
|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d-1}
by
Theorem

1.3
.
∎

Claim 3.4
.

For each
v\in[n]
, we have
|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|\leq C_{1}\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\right)^{\frac{d}{d-1}}+C_{2}n^{\frac{d(d-2)}{d-1}}
for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
which only depend on
d
.

Proof of claim.

For each
v\in[n]
, let

\mathcal{B}_{v}:=\{B_{k}\setminus\{v\}:v\in B_{k},|B_{k}|=d\}.

By definition of
X_{v}
, we can see

|\mathcal{B}_{v}|=|\mathcal{X}_{v}|-|\{k\in[m]:v\in B_{k},|B_{k}|\leq d-1\}|\geq|\mathcal{X}_{v}|-c_{1}n^{d-2},

for some constant
c_{1}>0
, where the last inequality holds by
Claim

3.2
.
On the other hand, by definition of
\mathcal{Y}_{v}
, any set
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{v}
cannot contain any set of
\mathcal{B}_{v}
. In other words, we have

\partial_{d-1}\mathcal{Y}_{v}\cap\mathcal{B}_{v}=\emptyset.

Then by
Theorem

2.2
, we have

|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|\leq c_{2}|\partial_{d-1}\mathcal{Y}_{v}|^{\frac{d}{d-1}}\leq c_{2}\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{B}_{v}|\right)^{\frac{d}{d-1}}\leq c_{2}\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{X}_{v}|+c_{1}n^{d-2}\right)^{\frac{d}{d-1}},

for some constant
c_{2}>0
. Moreover, since
(x+y)^{\frac{d}{d-1}}\leq 2(x^{\frac{d}{d-1}}+y^{\frac{d}{d-1}})
, we have

|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|\leq C_{1}\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\right)^{\frac{d}{d-1}}+C_{2}n^{\frac{d(d-2)}{d-1}}.

for some
C_{1},C_{2}
which only depend on
d
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We now present one of the critical components of our proof: we can always identify a transversal set with favorable properties.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and
n
be sufficiently large compared to
d
. If the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is at most
d
, then for any integer
1\leq s\leq n
, there exists a set
J
of size at most
c_{3}\cdot s
such that

\sum_{v\in J}|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|\geq|\mathcal{F}|-C_{3}\left(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-d-1}{d-1}}\right),

where
c_{3},C_{3}>0
are constants only depending on
d
.

Proof.

We first apply double counting based on
Claim

3.3
and
Claim

3.4
. By definitions, we first observe that

\sum_{v\in[n]}|\mathcal{X}_{v}|=\sum_{k\in[m]}|B_{k}|.

Notice that

\sum_{k\in[m]}|B_{k}|=\sum_{j=0}^{d}j\cdot|\{k\in[m]:|B_{k}|=j\}|\geq d\cdot|\mathcal{F}|-d\cdot|\{k\in[m]:|B_{k}|\leq d-1\}|.

By
Claim

3.2
and our assumption
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\geq\binom{n-1}{d}
, we have

\sum_{v\in[n]}|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\geq d\binom{n-1}{d}-c_{4}n^{d-1},

for some constant
c_{4}>0
, which further yields that

\sum_{v\in[n]}\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\right)\leq n\binom{n-1}{d-1}-d\binom{n-1}{d}+c_{5}n^{d-1}\leq c_{5}n^{d-1},

(1)

where
c_{5}=c_{4}\cdot d
. Note that by
Claim

3.3
, every term on the left-hand side is non-negative. On the other hand, since
\mathcal{F}
is
(d+1)
-uniform, we have

\sum\limits_{v\in[n]}(|\mathcal{X}_{v}|+|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|)=(d+1)\cdot|\mathcal{F}|.

This leads to

\sum\limits_{v\in[n]}|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|=(d+1)|\mathcal{F}|-\sum\limits_{v\in[n]}|\mathcal{X}_{v}|=\sum\limits_{k\in[m]}(|F_{k}|-|B_{k}|)\geq|\mathcal{F}|.

(2)

Now we take

J=\left\{v\in[n]:\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\geq\frac{n^{d-1}}{s}\right\}.

Then by inequality (
1
), we have
|J|\leq c_{3}s
for some constant
c_{3}>0
which only depends on
d
. On the other hand, for each
v\in[n]\setminus J
,
Claim

3.4
gives that

|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|\leq C_{1}\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\right)^{\frac{d}{d-1}}+C_{2}n^{\frac{d(d-2)}{d-1}}\leq C_{1}s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}\cdot n\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-|\mathcal{X}_{v}|\right)+C_{2}n^{\frac{d(d-2)}{d-1}}.

By inequality (
1
), we have

\sum\limits_{v\in[n]\setminus J}|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|\leq C_{1}^{\prime}s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d}+C_{2}^{\prime}n^{\frac{d^{2}-d-1}{d-1}},

for some constant
C_{1}^{\prime},C_{2}^{\prime}>0
which only depend on
d
. Finally, by inequality (
2
), we have

\sum\limits_{v\in J}|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|\geq|\mathcal{F}|-C_{3}\left(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-d-1}{d-1}}\right),

for some constant
C_{3}>0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

3.4
Controlling the partition via near-injections

Let
s\leq\frac{n}{4c_{3}}
be an integer, which we will set later, and
J\subseteq[n]
be the subset satisfying the conditions in
Lemma

3.5
. We partition
\mathcal{F}
into six parts based on the chosen
J
:
\mathcal{F}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{6}\mathcal{T}_{J}^{i},
where

•

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
F_{k}\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d+1}
and
B_{k}\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
B_{k}\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|F_{k}\cap J|=1
and
B_{k}\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|F_{k}\cap J|=1
and
B_{k}\in J\times\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|F_{k}\cap J|=2
(i.e.,
F_{k}\in\binom{J}{2}\times\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
) and
F_{k}\cap([n]\setminus J)\subseteq B_{k}
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}=\mathcal{F}\setminus(\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{5}\mathcal{T}_{J}^{i})
.

As an example and motivation for our subsequent arguments, we compute these families explicitly for Mubayi and Zhao’s construction in
Section

6
.

Now we define a
d
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{J}
with the vertex set
[n]\setminus J
and the edge set

E(\mathcal{G}_{J}):=\{F_{k}\setminus J:|F_{k}\cap J|=1,F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Assume that
|\mathcal{F}|\geq\binom{n-1}{d}
. By the selection of
J
and
Lemma

3.5
, we have
|J|\leq c_{3}\cdot s
and

\sum_{v\in J}|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|=|\mathcal{F}|-C_{3}\left(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-d-1}{d-1}}\right).

By definition we have

E(\mathcal{G}_{J})\supseteq\bigcup_{v\in J}\bigg(\mathcal{Y}_{v}\cap\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}\bigg).

Moreover, we define

\mathcal{Y}_{v}^{\prime}:=\{F_{k}\setminus\{v\}:v\in F_{k}\setminus B_{k},|B_{k}|=d\}.

Observe that

\mathcal{Y}_{v}=\mathcal{Y}_{v}^{\prime}\cup\{F_{k}\setminus\{v\}:v\in F_{k}\setminus B_{k},|B_{k}|\leq d-1\}.

By
Claim

3.2
, we have
|\mathcal{Y}_{v}^{\prime}|\geq|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|-C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{j}
. By the definition of
B_{k}
, the sets in
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{v}
are pairwise disjoint. Thus, we can obtain a lower bound for the number of edges in
\mathcal{G}_{J}
.

\displaystyle|E(\mathcal{G}_{J})|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{v\in J}\bigg|\mathcal{Y}_{v}^{\prime}\cap\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}\bigg|

(3)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{v\in J}\Bigg(|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|-\bigg(\binom{n}{d}-\binom{n-|J|}{d}\bigg)-C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{j}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{v\in J}\big(|\mathcal{Y}_{v}|-c_{6}|J|n^{d-1}\big)

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{F}|-C_{4}\big(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-d-1}{d-1}}+|J|^{2}n^{d-1}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\binom{n-|J|}{d}-C_{4}\big(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-d-1}{d-1}}+s^{2}n^{d-1}\big),

where
C_{4},c_{6}>0
are constants which only depend on
d
and the fourth inequality follows from
Lemma

3.5
. By inequality (
3
) and straightforward calculations, we obtain

|E(\mathcal{G}_{J})|\geq\binom{n-|J|}{d}-C_{4}\big(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-d-1}{d-1}}+s^{2}n^{d-1}\big)=\binom{n-|J|}{d}-Kn,

where

K:=K(s)=C_{4}\left(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d-1}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-2d}{d-1}}+s^{2}n^{d-2}\right).

We proceed to carefully analyze the size of each subfamily
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{i}
for
i\in[6]
and establish a series of claims. Before doing so, we present a simple fact, derived from the denseness of the hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{J}
, that will be repeatedly used in the following proofs.

Fact 3.6
.

For
|J|\leq\frac{n}{4}
(guaranteed by the assumption
s\leq\frac{n}{4c_{3}}
), let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
consist of
(d-1)
-subsets which are contained in at most
100d+|J|
edges of
\mathcal{G}_{J}
, then
|\mathcal{S}|\leq C_{5}K
for some constant
C_{5}>0
.

Claim 3.7
.

|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}|\leq C_{6}K
for some constant
C_{6}>0
.

Proof of claim.

Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F_{k}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d+1}
and
B_{k}\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
. Let
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}
with
B_{k}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
. If
\bm{e}\in E(\mathcal{G}_{J})\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}
is an edge containing
B_{k}
, then by definition, there exists some vertex
v\in J
such that
F_{\ell}=\bm{e}\cup\{v\}\in\mathcal{F}
. Since
F_{k}\cap F_{\ell}\neq B_{k}
, we have
\bm{e}\subseteq F_{k}
. Therefore, there are at most
d+1
edges in
\mathcal{G}_{J}
containing
B_{k}
.

Moreover, by
Claim

3.2
, each such
B_{k}
corresponds to at most
C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}
distinct sets in
\mathcal{F}
. Combining this with
Fact

3.6
, we obtain
|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}|\leq C_{6}K
, where
C_{6}=C_{5}\cdot C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Claim 3.8
.

|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}|\leq\binom{n-|J|}{d}+C_{7}(K+\sqrt{Kn^{d-1}}+n^{d-2})
for some constant
C_{7}>0
.

Proof of claim.

Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
B_{k}\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}
and
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|F_{k}\cap J|=1
and
B_{k}\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
. We define a map
\phi:\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}\rightarrow\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}
as

\phi(F_{k})=\begin{cases}B_{k},&\text{if }F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2},\\
F_{k}\setminus J,&\text{if }F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}.\end{cases}

By definition of
\phi
, we have

|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}|=\sum\limits_{B\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}}|\phi^{-1}(B)|\leq\binom{n-|J|}{d}+\sum\limits_{B\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d},|\phi^{-1}(B)|\geq 2}|\phi^{-1}(B)|.

Define

\mathcal{B}_{L}:=\{B\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}:|\phi^{-1}(B)|\geq 2\}.

For any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{L}
, if
\phi^{-1}(B)
contains a set
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}
, then by the definition of
B_{k}
, no other set in
\mathcal{F}
can contain
B_{k}
, implying
|\phi^{-1}(B)|=1
, which is a contradiction. Hence, for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{L}
,
\phi^{-1}(B)
can only contain sets from
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}
.

For each
B\in\mathcal{B}_{L}
, if
F_{k}\in\phi^{-1}(B)\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}
, then
B_{k}\subseteq B
. Consequently, the number of such
B_{k}
is at most
d
. Furthermore, by
Claim

3.2
, each such
B_{k}
corresponds to at most
C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}
distinct sets in
\mathcal{F}
. Thus, we have
|\phi^{-1}(B)|\leq c_{7}
, where
c_{7}=C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}d
. Combining these observations, we conclude that

|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}|\leq\binom{n-|J|}{d}+c_{7}\cdot|\mathcal{B}_{L}|.

Next, it remains to derive an upper bound for
|\mathcal{B}_{L}|
. Let
F_{k}=B\cup\{j\}
and
F_{\ell}=B\cup\{j^{\prime}\}
be two sets in
\phi^{-1}(B)
, where
j,j^{\prime}\in J
. We then consider the following two cases.

Case 1.

Suppose
B_{\ell}=B_{k}
. In this case,
B
is the only edge in the hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{J}
that contains
B_{k}=B_{\ell}
; otherwise, there would exist some
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\cap B=B_{k}=B_{\ell}
and
\lvert F\cap J\rvert=1
. Thus we have either
F\cap F_{k}=B_{k}
or
F\cap F_{\ell}=B_{\ell}
, which leads to a contradiction. Therefore, by
Fact

3.6
, the number of such
B_{k}
is at most
C_{5}K
.

Moreover, since
B
is an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{J}
by definition, it is uniquely determined by
B_{k}=B_{\ell}
. Thus, we conclude that the number of such
B\in\mathcal{B}_{L}
is also at most
C_{5}K
.

Case 2.

Suppose that
B_{\ell}\neq B_{k}
. Let
E=B_{k}\cap B_{\ell}
. Since
|B_{k}|=|B_{\ell}|=d-1
, it follows that
|E|=d-2
. We denote
B_{k}=E\cup\{v\}
and
B_{\ell}=E\cup\{w\}
for some elements
v,w\in[n]
. We refer to
E
as the
core
of
B
, and we say that the 4-tuple
(v,w,j,j^{\prime})

corresponds to
this
B
.

Observe that the set
B=E\cup\{v,w\}
is uniquely determined by the core
E
and the corresponding
4
-tuple. For a fixed
E\in\binom{[n]}{d-2}
, we define
N(E)
to be the number of
B
’s in this case with respect to the core
E
.

Now let
B^{\prime}
be any other set that falls into this case with respect to the same core
E
. First, we claim that
B^{\prime}\cap B=E
. Suppose not, then assume that
B^{\prime}=E\cup\{v,w^{\prime}\}
for some
w^{\prime}\neq w\in[n]
with the corresponding
4
-tuple being
(v,w^{\prime},h,h^{\prime})
for some
h,h^{\prime}\in J
. Then both of
F_{k}=E\cup\{v,w,j\}
and
F_{h}=E\cup\{v,w^{\prime},h\}
belong to
\mathcal{F}
, therefore we have

(E\cup\{v,w,j\})\cap(E\cup\{v,w^{\prime},h\})\neq B_{k}=E\cup\{v\},

which yields that
j=h
. Similarly, we can obtain that

(E\cup\{v,w,j\})\cap(E\cup\{v,w^{\prime},h^{\prime}\})\neq B_{k}=E\cup\{v\},

which leads to
j=h^{\prime}
, a contradiction.

We can then assume
B^{\prime}=E\cup\{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\}
, where
\{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\}\cap\{v,w\}=\emptyset
, with the corresponding 4-tuple
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime},h,h^{\prime})
. Our next goal is to show that there exists at least one pair
M\subseteq\{v,w\}\times\{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\}
such that
M\cup E\notin E(\mathcal{G}_{J})
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
j\neq h
. We claim that
\{v,v^{\prime}\}\cup E
is not an edge in the hypergraph
\mathcal{G}_{J}
. Suppose otherwise, then there exists some
F_{q}=\{v,v^{\prime},q\}\cup E\in\mathcal{F}
with
q\in J
. Since

(\{v,v^{\prime},q\}\cup E)\cap(\{v,w,j\}\cup E)\neq B_{k}=E\cup\{v\},

we deduce
q=j
. Similarly, we have

(E\cup\{v,v^{\prime},q\})\cap(E\cup\{v^{\prime},w^{\prime},h\})\neq E\cup\{v^{\prime}\},

which implies
q=h
, leading to a contradiction.

If
(v_{1},w_{1},*,*),\ldots,(v_{t},w_{t},*,*)
are the
4
-tuples corresponding to the
B
’s that fall into
Case 2
with respect to the core
E
, then
\{v_{1},w_{1}\},\ldots,\{v_{t},w_{t}\}
are pairwise disjoint. Moreover, for each pair
\{i,j\}\in\binom{[t]}{2}
, there is at least one pair
M\in\{v_{i},w_{i}\}\times\{v_{j},w_{j}\}
such that
M\cup E\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}\setminus E(\mathcal{G}_{J})
. Letting
N(E)=t
, we then have

\binom{N(E)}{2}\leq|\{A\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}\setminus E(\mathcal{G}_{J}):E\subseteq A\}|.

Summing over all possible
(d-2)
-subsets
E
, we then have

\sum\limits_{E\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-2}}\binom{N(E)}{2}\leq c_{8}Kn,

for some constant
c_{8}>0
which only depends on
d
. Therefore we have

\sum\limits_{E\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-2}}N(E)^{2}\leq\sum\limits_{E\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-2}}\left(4\binom{N(E)}{2}+1\right)\leq 4c_{8}Kn+n^{d-2},

where we take advantage of
x^{2}\leq 4\binom{x}{2}+1
. Then by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\sum\limits_{E\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-2}}N(E)\leq c_{9}\left(\sqrt{Kn^{d-1}}+n^{d-2}\right),

for some constant
c_{9}>0
which only depends on
d
. Therefore, the total number of distinct
B
’s falling into this case is at most
c_{9}\left(\sqrt{Kn^{d-1}}+n^{d-2}\right)
.

Combining these cases, we conclude that

|\mathcal{B}_{L}|\leq C_{7}\left(K+\sqrt{Kn^{d-1}}+n^{d-2}\right),

where
C_{7}=c_{7}\cdot\max\{c_{9},C_{5}\}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Claim 3.9
.

|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}|\leq(|J|-1)\binom{n-|J|}{d-1}+2C_{5}K
.

Proof of claim.

Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|F_{k}\cap J|=1
and
B_{k}\in J\times\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
. and
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}
consists of the sets
F_{k}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|F_{k}\cap J|=2
and
F_{k}\cap([n]\setminus J)\subseteq B_{k}
. For each
C\in\binom{[n]-J}{d-1}
, let

\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}:=\{F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}:C\subseteq B_{k}\},

and

\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}:=\{F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}:C\subseteq F_{k}\}.

By definition, each element of
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}
lies in
\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}
for exactly one
C
. So we have

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}\rvert=\sum_{C\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}}\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}\rvert.

For each
C\in\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d-1}
, define a map
\phi_{C}:\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}\to J
as follows.

•

For each set
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}
and each set
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}
with
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d
,
\phi_{C}
maps
F_{k}
to the unique element in
B_{k}\setminus C
.

•

It remains to map the sets
F_{k}
in
\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}
where
B_{k}=C
. We define an auxiliary graph
G^{5}_{C}
with vertex set
J
and edges given by
\{F_{k}\cap J:F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5},B_{k}=C\}
. In this graph, every pair of edges must intersect, meaning that the graph is either empty, a star, or a triangle. In each case, we can define
\phi_{C}(F_{k})
such that it maps
\{F_{k}:F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5},B_{k}=C\}
injectively to a non-isolated vertex in
G^{5}_{C}
, with
\phi_{C}(F_{k})\subseteq F_{k}
.

We apply
Claim

3.1
to the map
\phi_{C}^{\prime}(F)=\{\phi_{C}(F)\}\cup C
. Note that
\phi_{C}^{\prime}
is injective if
\phi_{C}
is injective on
\{F_{k}:F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5},B_{k}=C\}
, which holds by definition. Consequently, both
\phi_{C}^{\prime}
and
\phi_{C}
are injective. Thus, we obtain

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}\rvert\leq(\lvert J\rvert-1)\binom{n-\lvert J\rvert}{d-1}+|\{C:\phi_{C}\text{ is surjective}\}|.

If
G_{C}^{5}
is a star, then
\phi_{v}
must miss a non-isolated vertex, so it cannot be surjective. Therefore,
\phi_{v}
is surjective only if
G_{C}^{5}
is empty or a triangle. Next, we discuss these two cases separately.

Case 1.

Suppose
G_{C}^{5}
is empty. Then every set
F_{k}
in
\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}
satisfies
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d
, and for each
j\in J
, there exists some
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}
with
B_{k}=C\cup\{j\}
. Now suppose
\bm{e}
is any edge in
\mathcal{G}_{J}
containing
C
. Then there exists
j_{0}\in J
such that
\bm{e}\cup\{j_{0}\}\in\mathcal{F}
. Therefore, there exists
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}
with
B_{k}=C\cup\{j_{0}\}
, and this is possible only if
F_{k}=\bm{e}\cup\{j_{0}\}
.

Thus, any edge in
\mathcal{G}_{J}
containing
C
is of the form
F_{k}\cap([n]\setminus J)
for some
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J,C}^{5}
. In particular, there are at most
\lvert J\rvert
such edges. Then, by
Fact

3.6
, the total number of such
C
is at most
C_{5}K
.

Case 2.

Suppose
G_{C}^{5}
is a triangle with vertices
\{a,b,c\}
. Then
F_{k_{1}}=C\cup\{a,b\}
,
F_{k_{2}}=C\cup\{b,c\}
, and
F_{k_{3}}=C\cup\{a,c\}
are all sets in
\mathcal{F}
, where
B_{k_{i}}=C
holds for all
i\in[3]
. By the definition of
B_{k_{i}}
, there can be no other element of
\mathcal{F}
containing
C
. In particular, this implies that no edge in
\mathcal{G}_{J}
can contain
C
.

Thus, by
Fact

3.6
, the total number of such
C
is at most
C_{5}K
.

We conclude that

|\{C:\phi_{C}\text{ is surjective}\}|\leq 2C_{5}K,

which leads to

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}\rvert\leq(\lvert J\rvert-1)\binom{n-\lvert J\rvert}{d-1}+\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert\leq(\lvert J\rvert-1)\binom{n-\lvert J\rvert}{d-1}+2C_{5}K.

This completes the proof.

∎

Claim 3.10
.

|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}|\leq C_{8}|J|^{2}n^{d-2}
for some constant
C_{8}>0
which only depends on
d
.

Proof of claim.

We first claim that for each
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}
,
|B_{k}\cap([n]\setminus J)|\leq d-2
. Suppose there is some
F_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}
such that
|B_{k}\cap([n]\setminus J)|\geq d-1
. We consider the following cases.

Case 1.

If
|F_{k}\cap J|=0
, then
F_{k}
belongs to either
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}
or
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}
, a contradiction.

Case 2.

If
|F_{k}\cap J|=1
, then
F_{k}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}
,
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}
or
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}
, a contradiction.

Case 3.

If
|F_{k}\cap J|=2
, then
F_{k}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}
, a contradiction.

Case 4.

If
|F_{k}\cap J|\geq 3
, then since
B_{k}\subseteq F_{k}
, we can see
|B_{k}\cap(F_{k}\setminus J)|\leq d-2
, which yields that
|B_{k}\cap([n]\setminus J)|\leq d-2
, a contradiction to our assumption.

We observe that there are at most
\binom{|J|}{2}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\binom{n}{i}\leq c_{10}|J|^{2}n^{d-2}
distinct sets
B_{k}
such that
|B_{k}\cap([n]\setminus J)|\leq d-2
, where
c_{10}>0
only depends on
d
. By
Claim

3.2
, there are at most
C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}
sets
F\in\mathcal{F}
corresponding to each
B_{k}
. Therefore, it follows that

|\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}|\leq C_{8}|J|^{2}n^{d-2},

where
C_{8}=c_{10}\cdot C_{\eqref{thm:BVCSunflower}}
.
∎

Combining
Claim

3.7
,
Claim

3.8
,
Claim

3.9
, and
Claim

3.10
, we obtain the following bound on
|\mathcal{F}|
:

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-|J|}{d}+(|J|-1)\binom{n-|J|}{d-1}+C_{9}(K+\sqrt{Kn^{d-1}}+n^{d-2}+|J|^{2}n^{d-2}),

where
C_{9}=\max\{2C_{5}+C_{6}+C_{7},C_{8}\}
. Recall that

K:=K(s)=C_{4}\left(s^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}n^{d-1}+n^{\frac{d^{2}-2d}{d-1}}+s^{2}n^{d-2}\right),

and
|J|\leq c_{3}\cdot s
, we then get

\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{d}+C_{10}\left(s^{2}n^{d-2}+s^{-\frac{1}{2(d-1)}}n^{d-1}+n^{\frac{2d^{2}-4d+1}{2(d-1)}}+sn^{d-3/2}+n^{d-2}\right),

where
C_{10}>0
only depends on
d
.

Taking
s=\lfloor n^{\frac{d-1}{2d-1}}\rfloor
, we obtain the bound

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d}+C_{d}\cdot n^{d-1-\frac{1}{4d-2}}

for some constant
C_{d}>0
. This finishes the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

4
Polynomial improvement via linear algebra methods

We further refine the tools from linear algebra methods to show that the Frankl–Pach upper bound can be improved by
\Omega_{d}(n^{d-4})
. Although this result is weaker than our main finding, we believe it is of independent interest due to its potential applicability in providing the sharp result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 4
be a positive integer and
n
be sufficiently large compared to
d
. If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
is a set system with VC-dimension at most
d
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n}{d}-c_{d}n^{d-4}
for some constant
c_{d}>0
.

4.1
Overview of the proof of
Theorem

4.1

We first briefly recap preceding linear algebra methods. Frankl and Pach associated a vector (forming the so-called “higher-order inclusion matrix”)
\bm{v}_{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{d}}
to each
F\in\mathcal{F}
and proved that these vectors are linearly independent. By dimension counting, we have
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq\binom{n}{d}
. Mubayi and Zhao instead worked with
\mathbb{F}^{\binom{[n]}{d}}
for some finite field
\mathbb{F}
. They inserted
\Omega_{d}(\log n)
vectors into
\{\bm{v}_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F}\}
and proved that the resulting family is still linearly independent, thereby gaining
\Omega_{d}(\log n)
over the Frankl–Pach bound. Their use of a finite field imposes a number-theoretic condition on
d
. Ge, Xu, Yip, Zhang and Zhao
[
15
]
took a different approach, associating carefully designed degree
d
multilinear polynomials
f_{F},g_{G}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
G\in\mathcal{G}=\binom{[n]}{\leq d-1}
. The Frankl–Pach bound is recovered by showing that the family of polynomials
\{f_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F}\}\sqcup\{g_{G}:G\in\mathcal{G}\}
is linearly independent. By inserting a single extra polynomial into the family, the authors in
[
15
]
improved the Frankl–Pach bound by
1
.

In this section, we refine the approach in
[
15
]
by showing that it is possible to
\Omega_{d}(n^{d-4})
polynomials into the family
\{f_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F}\}\cup\{g_{G}:G\in\mathcal{G}\}
while preserving linear independence. Our main innovation is a novel sufficient condition for choosing these additional polynomials. Using combinatorial arguments, we then algorithmically construct a large family satisfying the desired condition.

4.2
An estimate

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}\}
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
be as in
Section

3
. In addition to
Claim

3.2
, we use a different double-counting approach to obtain a tight estimate for the number of indices
i
with
|B_{i}|=d
.

Claim 4.2
.

The number of indices
i\in[m]
with
|B_{i}|=d
is at most
\binom{n-1}{d}
. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
\{F_{i}:i\in[m],|B_{i}|=d\}
is a star with maximum size.

Proof of claim.

Let
\mathcal{Y}=\binom{[n]}{d+1}\setminus\mathcal{F}
. Let
P:=\big\{(i,Y):i\in[m],|B_{i}|=d,B_{i}\subseteq Y,Y\in\mathcal{Y}\big\}
. On one hand, since for each
i\in[m]
with
|B_{i}|=d
, and for each
x\in[n]\setminus F_{i}
, we have
B_{i}\cup\{x\}\notin\mathcal{F}
, which implies that
(i,B_{i})\in P
if
|B_{i}|=d
. Set
D:=|\{i:|B_{i}|=d\}|
, we have

|P|\geq(n-d-1)\cdot|D|.

On the other hand, for each
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
Y
has
d+1
subsets of size
d
. Moreover, by definition for each subset
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
of size
d
, there is at most one index
i\in[m]
such that
B_{i}=Y^{\prime}
. Therefore, we have

|P|\leq(d+1)\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-|\mathcal{F}|\right).

Observe that for each distinct
F_{i},F_{j}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|B_{i}|=|B_{j}|=d
,
B_{i}\neq B_{j}
, therefore
|D|\leq|\mathcal{F}|
.
Combining the above inequalities, we have

|D|\leq\frac{(d+1)\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-|\mathcal{F}|\right)}{n-d-1}\leq\frac{(d+1)\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-|D|\right)}{n-d-1},

(4)

which yields that

|D|\leq\frac{d+1}{n}\binom{n}{d+1}=\binom{n-1}{d}.

Furthermore, assume that
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert=\binom{n-1}{d}
and
|B_{i}|=d
for all
i\in[m]
. From the above proof, we deduce that for each
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
and each subset
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
of size
d
, there exists an index
i
such that
B_{i}=Y^{\prime}
. Therefore, we have

\partial_{d}\mathcal{Y}=\{B_{i}:1\leq i\leq\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\}.

Note that
\lvert\mathcal{Y}\rvert=\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n-1}{d}=\binom{n-1}{d+1}
, and
\lvert\partial_{d}\mathcal{Y}\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert=\binom{n-1}{d}
. Hence, the
d
-shadow of
\mathcal{Y}
is minimized by
Theorem

2.2
. Applying
Theorem

2.3
, we can conclude that the
k
-shadow of
\mathcal{Y}
is minimized for each
k\leq d
. In particular, we have
\lvert\partial_{1}\mathcal{Y}\rvert=n-1
. In other words,
\mathcal{F}
is a star centered at
[n]\setminus\partial_{1}\mathcal{Y}
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Upper bound via the polynomial method

We now develop a strategy that allows us to insert more polynomials into the family introduced in
[
15
]
.

Claim 4.3
.

If there exists a family
\mathcal{Y}:=\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{s}\}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}\setminus\mathcal{F}
, together with
Z_{i}\subseteq Y_{i}
of size
d
, such that the following three conditions hold:

(1)

For any
i\in[s]
and
j\in[m]
,
Z_{i}\neq B_{j}
.

(2)

For any distinct
i,j\in[s]
,
|Y_{i}\cap Y_{j}|\leq d-1
.

(3)

For any
i,j\in[s]
with
i<j
, there is no
k\in[m]
such that both of
F_{k}\cap Y_{i}=Z_{i}
and
F_{k}\cap Y_{j}=B_{k}
hold.

Then we have
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n}{d}-s
.

Proof of claim.

We consider the following polynomials, introduced in
[
15
]
.

1.

For each set
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
, let
f_{F_{i}}(\bm{x})=\prod\limits_{j\in B_{i}}x_{j}\cdot\prod\limits_{j\in F_{i}\setminus B_{i}}(x_{j}-1)-\prod\limits_{j\in F_{i}}x_{j}
.

2.

For each set
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
, let
y_{Y_{i}}(\bm{x})=-\prod\limits_{j\in Z_{i}}x_{j}
.

3.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\binom{[n]}{\leq d-1}
. For each set
H\in\mathcal{H}
, let
h_{H}(\bm{x})=\sum\limits_{j\notin H}x_{j}\cdot\prod\limits_{j\in H}x_{j}+(|H|-d-1)\prod\limits_{j\in H}x_{j}
.

It then suffices to show that
\{y_{Y_{i}}\}_{Y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}}
,
\{f_{F_{i}}\}_{F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}}
and
\{h_{H}\}_{H\in\mathcal{H}}
are linearly independent, which implies that
s+|\mathcal{F}|+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{i}\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d}\binom{n}{i}
, therefore,
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n}{d}-s
. Suppose there exist coefficients (that are not all zero)
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
,
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{m})
, and
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{w})
, where
w=|\mathcal{H}|=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{n}{i}
, such that

\sum\limits_{i\in[s]}\alpha_{i}y_{Y_{i}}+\sum\limits_{j\in[m]}\beta_{j}f_{F_{j}}+\sum\limits_{k\in[w]}\gamma_{k}h_{H_{k}}=0.

For each
A\subseteq[n]
, we use
v_{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to denote its indicator vector. We next substitute the indicator vector corresponding to each set in the families
\mathcal{Y}
,
\mathcal{F}
, and
\mathcal{H}
into the equation above.
By the properties (1)–(3) and some direct calculations, we can see

\left(\begin{matrix}-\bm{E}_{s}&\bm{T}^{\top}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{R}&-\bm{E}_{m}&\bm{0}\\
*&*&\bm{A}\end{matrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\alpha}\\
\bm{\beta}\\
\bm{\gamma}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\bm{0},

where
\bm{E}_{s}
and
\bm{E}_{m}
are the
s\times s
and
m\times m
identity matrices respectively, and the
*
’s denote matrices that we do not compute explicitly. The upper left block is
-\bm{E}_{s}
since
\lvert Y_{i}\cap Y_{j}\rvert\neq Z_{i}
for distinct
i
and
j
by condition (2). The matrices
\bm{T}
and
\bm{R}
are given by

\bm{T}_{k,i}=f_{F_{k}}(\bm{v}_{Y_{i}})=(-1)^{d+1-\lvert B_{k}\rvert}\cdot\bm{1}_{F_{k}\cap Y_{i}=B_{k}},

\bm{R}_{k,i}=y_{Y_{i}}(\bm{v}_{F_{k}})=(-1)^{d+1-\lvert Z_{i}\rvert}\cdot\bm{1}_{F_{k}\cap Y_{i}=Z_{i}}.

To obtain a contradiction, it suffices to show that the coefficient matrix

\left(\begin{matrix}-\bm{E}_{s}&\bm{T}^{\top}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{R}&-\bm{E}_{m}&\bm{0}\\
*&*&\bm{A}\end{matrix}\right)

is invertible. Using elementary row operations, we can transform the above matrix into

\bm{M}=\left(\begin{matrix}-\bm{E}_{s}+\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{R}&0&0\\
\bm{R}&-\bm{E}_{m}&0\\
*&*&\bm{A}\end{matrix}\right),

where the entries of
\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{R}
are given by

(\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{R})_{i,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert}\bm{T}_{k,i}\bm{R}_{k,j}=\sum_{k:F_{k}\cap Y_{i}=B_{k},F_{k}\cap Y_{j}=Z_{j}}(-1)^{\lvert B_{k}\rvert-\lvert Z_{j}\rvert}.

By conditions (1) and (3), the rightmost sum is empty when
i\geq j
. So
\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{R}
is upper triangular with zero diagonal. Also, note that
A
is a lower triangular matrix with nonzero diagonal entries by sorting the sets in
\mathcal{H}
by size in non-decreasing order. Thus each diagonal block of
\bm{M}
is invertible. The determinant of
\bm{M}
is the product of the determinants of
-\bm{E}_{s}+\bm{T}^{\top}\bm{R}
,
-\bm{E}_{m}
and
\bm{A}
, which are all non-zero. Therefore the transformed matrix
\bm{M}
is invertible. Thus the original coefficient matrix is also invertible, as desired.
∎

4.4
Finding the desired family

Our final objective is to identify a family
\mathcal{Y}
that satisfies the three properties outlined in
Claim

4.3
and is as large as possible. We show that it is always possible to construct such a family with size at least
\Omega_{d}(n^{d-4})
when
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert
is close to
\binom{n}{d}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}:=\{B_{i}:|B_{i}|=d\}
and
t:=\binom{n}{d}-|\mathcal{F}|
. Set
\Gamma_{d}
to be a large enough constant which only depends on
d
, we define

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:=\left\{B\in\binom{[n]}{d}:\text{there are at least }\Gamma_{d}\text{ indices }k\in[m]\text{ such that }B\subseteq F_{k}\text{ and }|B_{k}|=d-2\right\}.

For each set
S\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}
, we define

N(S)=\{T\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}:|T\cap S|\geq d\}.

Obviously for each
S\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}
, we have
|N(S)|=(n-d-1)(d+1)+1
.

We select the suitable sets
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{s}\in\binom{[n]}{d+1}
together with
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{s}\in\binom{[n]}{d}
via the following algorithm.

Step 1:

Initialize
\mathcal{Y}=\binom{[n]}{d+1}\setminus\mathcal{F}
.

Step 2:

For each
i\in[m]
with
\lvert B_{i}\rvert\leq d-3
, remove
N(F_{i})
from
\mathcal{Y}
.

Step 3:

Repeat the following steps until termination:

(a)

Termination Check:
If
\partial_{d}\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, terminate.

(b)

Find a Candidate:
Otherwise, there exists some
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
Z\subseteq Y
, such that
\lvert Z\rvert=d
and
Z\notin\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Select such a pair
(Y,Z)
and assign it to
(Y_{i},Z_{i})
.

(c)

Update
\mathcal{Y}
:
Remove
N(Y_{i})
from
\mathcal{Y}
.

(d)

Further Removal:
For each
k\in[m]
such that
F_{k}\cap Y_{i}=Z_{i}
, remove from
\mathcal{Y}
every
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
satisfying
Y^{\prime}\cap F_{k}=B_{k}
.

One can check that the sets
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{s}
and corresponding
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{s}
produced by the above algorithm satisfy all requirements of
Claim

4.3
. More precisely, condition (1) is guaranteed by step 3(b), condition (2) is guaranteed by step 3(c), and condition (3) is guaranteed by step 3(d). Applying
Claim

4.3
, we conclude that
s\leq t
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
denote the family
\mathcal{Y}
at the end of the algorithm. We now analyze the evolution of
\lvert\mathcal{Y}\rvert
throughout this algorithm and give a lower bound on
\lvert\mathcal{Y}^{*}\rvert
. Initially, we have

\lvert\mathcal{Y}\rvert=\binom{n}{d+1}-\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert.

As mentioned above,
\lvert N(F_{k})\rvert=(n-d-1)(d+1)+1=O_{d}(n)
. Furthermore, by
Claim

3.2
, we conclude that that in step 2, at most
C_{d}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d-3}\binom{n}{i}\cdot\big((n-d-1)(d+1)+1\big)=O_{d}(n^{d-2})
sets are removed from
\mathcal{Y}
.

Next, we focus on step 3. In each iteration of step 3:

•

Step 3(c)
We remove
(n-d-1)(d+1)+1=O_{d}(n)
sets from
\mathcal{Y}
.

•

Step 3(d)
Since
\lvert Z_{i}\rvert=d
, there are at most
n
possible values of
k
such that
F_{k}\cap Y_{i}=Z_{i}
. For each such
k
, step 2 ensures that
\lvert B_{k}\rvert\geq d-2
. If
\lvert B_{k}\rvert\geq d-1
, there are at most
n^{2}
sets
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
Y^{\prime}\cap F_{k}=B_{k}
; if
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d-2
, there are at most
n^{3}
such sets. However, since
Z_{i}\notin\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, by definition the number of
k
satisfying
F_{k}\cap Y_{i}=Z_{i}
and
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d-2
is at most
\Gamma_{d}
. Therefore, step 3(d) removes at most
n^{3}+\Gamma_{d}n^{3}=(\Gamma_{d}+1)n^{3}
sets.

Combining these, each iteration of step 3 removes at most
2\Gamma_{d}n^{3}
sets from
\mathcal{Y}
. When the algorithm terminates, since
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{n}{d}-t
and
t\geq s
, we conclude that

\lvert\mathcal{Y}^{*}\rvert\geq\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}-c_{1}n^{d-2}-2\Gamma_{d}n^{3}\cdot t,

where
s
denotes the number of iterations in step 3 and
c_{1}
is a constant which depend on
d
. When the algorithm terminates, we obtain a family
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{s}\}
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be the unique positive real number such that
\lvert\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rvert=\binom{\alpha}{d}
. When the algorithm terminates, we must have
\partial_{d}\mathcal{Y^{*}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Applying
Theorem

2.2
, we deduce that

\lvert\mathcal{Y}^{*}\rvert\leq\binom{\alpha}{d+1}.

This gives the inequality:

\binom{\alpha}{d+1}\geq\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}-c_{1}n^{d-2}-2\Gamma_{d}n^{3}\cdot t.

(5)

On the other hand, from inequality (
4
) in the proof of
Claim

4.2
, we have

\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert\leq\frac{(d+1)\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}+t\right)}{n-d-1}.

By definition of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
and
Claim

3.2
, we have
\lvert\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\frac{C_{d}(d+1)\binom{n}{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}.

Thus we obtain

\binom{\alpha}{d}\leq\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert+\lvert\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\rvert\leq\frac{(d+1)\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}+t\right)}{n-d-1}+\frac{C_{d}(d+1)\binom{n}{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}.

(6)

What remains is to perform an explicit computation by combining inequalities (
5
) and (
6
).

It follows from inequality (
6
) that

\binom{\alpha}{d+1}=\frac{\alpha-d}{d+1}\binom{\alpha}{d}\leq\frac{\alpha-d}{n-d-1}\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}+t\right)+\frac{(\alpha-d)\cdot C_{d}\binom{n}{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}.

(7)

By combining inequalities (
5
) and (
7
), we obtain the following inequality:

\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}-c_{1}n^{d-2}{-2\Gamma_{d}n^{3}t}\leq\frac{\alpha-d}{n-d-1}\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}+t\right)+\frac{(\alpha-d)\cdot C_{d}\binom{n}{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}.

Upon simplification, we arrive at:

c_{1}n^{d-2}+{3\Gamma_{d}n^{3}t+\frac{(\alpha-d)\cdot C_{d}\binom{n}{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}}\geq\frac{n-1-\alpha}{n-d-1}\left(\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}\right)\geq c_{3}(n-1-\alpha)n^{d},

(8)

where the term
\frac{\alpha-d}{n-d-1}\cdot t
is absorbed into
2\Gamma_{d}n^{3}\cdot t
, with
c_{2}^{\prime}
being a constant that depends only on
d
, and
c_{3}>0
is a constant depending on
d
.

On the other hand, since
\binom{\alpha}{d}
is a degree
d
polynomial in
\alpha
, when
\alpha\leq n-1
, we have

\binom{\alpha}{d}\geq\binom{n-1}{d}-c_{4}(n-1-\alpha)n^{d-1},

for some
c_{4}>0
which depends on
d
.
Therefore, it follows from inequality (
6
) that

\binom{n}{d+1}-\binom{n}{d}+t\geq\frac{n-d-1}{d+1}\binom{n-1}{d}-c_{5}(n-1-\alpha)n^{d}-\frac{C_{d}\binom{n}{d-2}(n-d-1)}{\Gamma_{d}},

for some
c_{5}>0
which depends on
d
. Since
\frac{n-d-1}{d+1}\binom{n-1}{d}=\binom{n-1}{d+1}
, we have

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\geq\binom{n-1}{d+1}+\binom{n}{d}-\binom{n}{d+1}-c_{5}(n-1-\alpha)n^{d}-\frac{C_{d}\binom{n}{d-2}(n-d-1)}{\Gamma_{d}}

(9)

\displaystyle=\binom{n-1}{d-1}-c_{5}(n-1-\alpha)n^{d}-\frac{C_{d}\binom{n}{d-2}(n-d-1)}{\Gamma_{d}}.

Taking a linear combination of inequalities (
8
) and (
9
), we can see that

\left({3\Gamma_{d}n^{3}}+\frac{c_{3}}{c_{5}}\right)t+c_{1}n^{d-2}\geq\frac{c_{3}}{c_{5}}\binom{n-1}{d-1}-\frac{(\alpha-d)\cdot C_{d}\binom{n}{d-2}}{\Gamma_{d}}-\frac{c_{3}\binom{n}{d-2}(n-d-1)}{c_{5}\cdot\Gamma_{d}}.

(10)

For sufficiently large
n
, we can take
\Gamma_{d}
to be a constant significantly larger than
c_{3},c_{5}
and
C_{d}
. Therefore, the inequality (
10
) implies that
t\geq c_{d}n^{d-4}
for some
c_{d}>0
, which depends only on
d
.

When
\alpha>n-1
, the inequality (
8
) holds trivially. Therefore, by setting
c_{4}=c_{5}=0
in the above argument, we deduce from (
9
) that
t\geq c_{d}n^{d-1}
.

In conclusion, by choosing
\Gamma_{d}
to be a sufficiently large constant depending only on
d
, we obtain
t\geq\Omega_{d}(n^{d-4})
. This completes the proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

5
Generalizations of the Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem: Proof of
Theorem

1.6

As mentioned earlier, the case
s=0
corresponds to the famous Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem. Next, we will prove the cases
s=d
and
s=1
separately.

5.1
The case
s=d

We re-state the result here for convenience.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
n\geq d+1\geq 3
be integers. If for every set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
, there exists some subset
B\subseteq F
of size
d
such that
F^{\prime}\cap F\neq B
for any
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d}
. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is a star of size
\binom{n-1}{d}
.

Indeed,
Theorem

5.1
follows immediately from
Claim

4.2
. Here we present another proof via
Theorem

2.4
, which might be of independent interest.

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
via
Theorem

2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{F}\mid F\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Note that we have
B_{F}\neq B_{F^{\prime}}
for any
F\neq F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. Thus,
\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert
.
Then we apply
Theorem

2.4
by setting
k=d
and

\mathcal{G}:=\{G\in\binom{[n]}{d}:G\subseteq F\ \textup{for\ some\ }F\in\mathcal{F},G\neq B_{F}\}.

Observe that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
, all of its
d
-shadows but
B_{F}
are in
\mathcal{G}
. Thus, the set families
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
satisfies the condition in
Theorem

2.4
. Moreover, we know that
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
since a
d
-shadow
A
of
F\in\mathcal{F}
which is not
B_{F}
can not be the same as
B_{F^{\prime}}
for any
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F^{\prime}}
.

Let
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{x}{d}
for some real number
x
. Ten we have

\binom{n}{d}\geq|\mathcal{G}|+|\mathcal{B}|\geq\binom{x}{d-1}+\binom{x}{d}=\binom{x+1}{d},

which yields
x\leq n-1
. This completes the proof.
∎

5.2
The case
s=1

In this subsection, we prove
Theorem

1.6
for the case
s=1
. Moreover, we establish a stronger stability result as follows:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer, and let
n
be a sufficiently large integer relative to
d
. Suppose
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
satisfies the condition that for every set
F\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a subset
B_{F}\subseteq F
of size
1
such that
F\cap F^{\prime}\neq B_{F}
for all
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is either a star with
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d}
, or
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d}-C_{d}n^{d-1}
for some constant
C_{d}>0
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

We start with the following strengthening of
Theorem

2.5
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2d
. Suppose
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d}
is a
d
-uniform intersecting family with
|\mathcal{H}|\geq 6n^{d-2}
. Then there exists an element
\ell\in[n]
and sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{d+1}\in\mathcal{H}
such that:

(1)

\ell\in A
for all
A\in\mathcal{H}
, and

(2)

A_{1}\setminus\{\ell\},A_{2}\setminus\{\ell\},\dots,A_{d+1}\setminus\{\ell\}
are pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

As
d(d-1)\leq 6(d-2)!
and
d^{2}<5(d-1)!
, we have

d(d-1)\binom{n-1}{d-2}<\frac{d(d-1)n^{d-2}}{(d-2)!}\leq 6n^{d-2}.

Applying the mean value theorem to the function
\binom{x}{d-1}
, we also have

\binom{n-1}{d-1}-\binom{n-1-d}{d-1}<\frac{d^{2}(n-1)^{d-2}}{(d-1)!}<5n^{d-2}.

Thus, it follows that

\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert>\max\left(\binom{n-1}{d-1}-\binom{n-1-d}{d-1}+1,d(d-1)\binom{n-1}{d-2}\right).

By
Theorem

2.5
, since
\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert>\binom{n-1}{d-1}-\binom{n-1-d}{d-1}+1
, we conclude that
I=\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{H}}A\neq\emptyset
.
If
\lvert I\rvert>1
, then we must have

\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert\leq\binom{n-\lvert I\rvert}{d-\lvert I\rvert}\leq\binom{n-2}{d-2},

which contradicts the assumption on
\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert
. Thus, we may assume
I=\{\ell\}
.
Next, let
r
be the largest integer such that there exist sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}\in\mathcal{H}
where
A_{1}\setminus\{\ell\},\dots,A_{r}\setminus\{\ell\}
are pairwise disjoint.
If
r\leq d
, then by the definition of
r
, every
A\in\mathcal{H}
contains
\ell
and intersects
\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\big(A_{i}\setminus\{\ell\}\big)
. It follows that

\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert\leq r(d-1)\binom{n-1}{d-2}\leq d(d-1)\binom{n-1}{d-2},

which again contradicts the assumption on
\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert
. Therefore, we must have
r\geq d+1
, as required.
∎

For each
a\in[n]
, define

\mathcal{F}_{a}:=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:B_{F}=\{a\}\}.

We say that
a
is
good
if
\lvert\mathcal{F}_{a}\rvert\geq 6n^{d-2}
; otherwise,
a
is called
bad
. Define

G:=\{a\in[n]\mid\text{$a$ is good}\}.

Now consider any
a\in G
. Observe that

\mathcal{X}_{a}=\{F\setminus\{a\}\mid F\in\mathcal{F}_{a}\}

is a
d
-uniform intersecting family of size at least
6n^{d-2}
. By applying
Lemma

5.3
to
\mathcal{X}_{a}
, there exists an element
\ell\in[n]
and sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{d+1}\in\mathcal{X}_{a}
such that:

(1)

\ell\in A
for all
A\in\mathcal{X}_{a}
, and

(2)

A_{1}\setminus\{\ell\},\dots,A_{d+1}\setminus\{\ell\}
are pairwise disjoint.

Finally, we set
f(a):=\ell
.

Claim 5.4
.

For any
a\in G
and
F\in\mathcal{F}
, if
F
contains
a
, then
F
must also contain
f(a)
.

Proof of claim.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that there exists
F\in\mathcal{F}
with
a\in F
and
f(a)\notin F
. Since
\lvert F\setminus\{a\}\rvert=d
, we know that there must exist an index
i\in[d+1]
such that
F\cap A_{i}=\emptyset
, where
A_{i}
is defined before the claim. However, this contradicts with the choice of
B_{A_{i}\cup\{a\}}=\{a\}
.
∎

Claim 5.5
.

If there exist some
a\in G
and positive integer
k
such that
a,f(a),f(f(a)),\dots,f^{(k)}(a)
are all good and
f^{(k)}(a)=a
, then
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{d}-c_{d}n^{d-1}
for some constant
c_{d}>0
,

Proof of claim.

We may assume, without loss of generality, that
k
is the smallest positive integer such that
a,f(a),\dots,f^{(k-1)}(a)
are all distinct. Since
f(a)\neq a
by definition, it follows that
k\geq 2
. To bound the number of sets
F\in\mathcal{F}
, we consider whether
F
intersects
\{a,f(a),\dots,f^{(k-1)}(a)\}
or not.
First, if
F
does not intersect
\{a,f(a),\dots,f^{(k-1)}(a)\}
, then by
Theorem

1.3
, the number of such sets is at most
\binom{n-k}{d}
, as any subfamily of
\mathcal{F}
has VC-dimension at most
d
. On the other hand, if
F
intersects
\{a,f(a),\dots,f^{(k-1)}(a)\}
, then by
Claim

5.4
,
F
must contain the entire set
\{a,f(a),\dots,f^{(k-1)}(a)\}
. The number of such sets is at most
\binom{n-k}{d+1-k}
.
Combining these bounds, if
k\geq 3
, we have

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-3}{d}+\binom{n-3}{d-2}<\binom{n-2}{d}=\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-2}{d-1}.

When
k=2
, applying
Theorem

1.4
, we obtain

|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-3}{d}+C_{d}n^{d-1-\frac{1}{4d-2}}+\binom{n-2}{d-1}=\binom{n-1}{d}-cn^{d-1},

for some constant
c>0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

By
Claim

5.5
, we may assume that for all good
a
, the sequence
a,f(a),f(f(a)),\dots
terminates at a bad element
f^{(k)}(a)
for some
k\geq 1
(otherwise, the proof is complete); we denote this bad element by
b(a)
. From the definition and
Claim

5.4
, if
F\in\mathcal{F}
contains
a\in G
, then
F
also contains
b(a)
. Let

B=\{b\in[n]:b=b(a)\text{ for some good }a\},

and define

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:F\text{ contains at least one good element}\}.

For any
F\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}
, the element in
B_{F}
lies in
[n]\setminus G
. By the definition of
G
, we have

\lvert\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}\rvert\leq\sum_{v\in[n]\setminus G}\lvert\mathcal{F}_{v}\rvert\leq 6(n-\lvert G\rvert)n^{d-2}.

We now prove the following key claim, which consists of three upper bounds on the size of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}
.

Claim 5.6
.

We have the following upper bounds on
|\mathcal{F}_{\textup{good}}|
.

(1)

We always have

\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}\rvert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{i}\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d-i}=\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d}.

(2)

If
\lvert B\rvert>1
, then we have

\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d}-\binom{\lvert G\rvert-1}{d-1}.

(3)

If
\lvert B\rvert=1
and there exists
F_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F_{0}\subseteq[n]\setminus(G\cup B)
, then we have

\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d}-\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d-1}.

Proof of claim.

For (1): We can count the number of sets
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}
based on the size of
F\cap G
. Suppose
\lvert F\cap G\rvert=i\geq 1
. There are at most
\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{i}
ways to select the
i
-element subset
F\cap G
. After fixing
A=F\cap G
, we then choose any element
a\in A
. We know that
b(a)\in F\setminus G
, and there are at most
\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d-i}
ways to pick the remaining elements of
F\setminus G
from
[n]\setminus(G\cup\{b(a)\})
.

Thus, the size of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}
is at most

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{i}\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d-i}=\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d},

by Vandermonde’s identity. This completes the proof of the first part.

For (2): Note that if
\lvert B\rvert>1
, and
A\subseteq G
is a subset of size
d
containing any pair
a,a^{\prime}\in A
such that
b(a)\neq b(a^{\prime})
, then it is impossible to have
F\cap G=A
for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
. This is because, in that case,
\{b(a),b(a^{\prime})\}\subseteq F\setminus G
, which implies
\lvert F\rvert\geq d+2
, a contradiction. Therefore, we conclude that

\lvert\{F:F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}},\lvert F\cap G\rvert=d\}\rvert\leq\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d}-\bigg|\left\{A\in\binom{G}{d}:\text{$A$ contains $a,a^{\prime}$ with $b(a)\neq b(a^{\prime})$}\right\}\bigg|.

Substituting this into the argument in part (1), we can improve the upper bound on
\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}\rvert
to

\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}\rvert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{i}\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d-i}-\bigg|\left\{A\in\binom{G}{d}:\text{$A$ contains $a,a^{\prime}$ with $b(a)\neq b(a^{\prime})$}\right\}\bigg|.

Fix any
b\in B
, and let
G_{b}=\{a\in G\mid b(a)=b\}
. Note that
1\leq\lvert G_{b}\rvert\leq\lvert G\rvert-1
since
\lvert B\rvert>1
. We now estimate

\bigg|\left\{A\in\binom{G}{d}\mid\text{$A$ contains $a,a^{\prime}$ with $b(a)\neq b(a^{\prime})$}\right\}\bigg|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\binom{\lvert G_{b}\rvert}{i}\binom{\lvert G\rvert-\lvert G_{b}\rvert}{d-i},

since we can choose
i
elements from
G_{b}
and
d-i
elements from
G\setminus G_{b}
to form
A
. By applying the identity for binomial coefficients, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\binom{\lvert G_{b}\rvert}{i}\binom{\lvert G\rvert-\lvert G_{b}\rvert}{d-i}=\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d}-\binom{\lvert G\rvert-\lvert G_{b}\rvert}{d}-\binom{\lvert G_{b}\rvert}{d}\geq\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d}-\binom{\lvert G\rvert-1}{d}=\binom{\lvert G\rvert-1}{d-1},

This completes the proof of part (2).

For (3): Assume
B=\{b\}
and
B_{F_{0}}=\{v\}
. For any
(d-1)
-set
A\subseteq G
, we know that
A\cup\{b,v\}
cannot lie in
\mathcal{F}
because
(A\cup\{b,v\})\cap F_{0}=\{v\}=B_{F_{0}}
. Therefore, we have

\big|\{F:F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}},\lvert F\cap G\rvert=d-1\}\big|\leq\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d-1}(n-1-\lvert G\rvert)-\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d-1}.

Substituting this into the argument from part (1), we obtain

\lvert\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}\rvert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{i}\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d-i}-\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d-1},

as desired. This finishes the proof.
∎

We are ready to complete the proof of
Theorem

5.2
. We proceed by casework on the sizes of
G
and
B
. Let
C^{*}_{d}\in(0,1)
be any constant such that
\frac{(C^{*}_{d})^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}>100(1-C^{*}_{d})
.

Case 1.

If
\lvert G\rvert\leq C^{*}_{d}\cdot n
, since
n
is sufficiently large, by
Claim

5.6
(1), we have

\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d}+6(n-\lvert G\rvert)n^{d-2}<\binom{n-1}{d}-C_{1}n^{d}

for some constant
C_{1}
which only depends on
d
.

Case 2.

If
\lvert G\rvert\geq C^{*}_{d}\cdot n
and
\lvert B\rvert>1
, then when
n
is sufficiently large, we have
\binom{\lvert G\rvert-1}{d-1}\geq\frac{\lvert G\rvert^{d-1}}{2(d-1)!}
. Thus
Claim

5.6
(2) implies that

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d}-\binom{\lvert G\rvert-1}{d-1}+6(n-\lvert G\rvert)n^{d-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{d}-\left(\frac{(C^{*}_{d})^{d-1}}{2(d-1)!}+6(C^{*}_{d}-1)\right)n^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{d}-6(1-C^{*}_{d})n^{d-1}.

Case 3.

If
\lvert G\rvert\geq C^{*}_{d}\cdot n
,
\lvert B\rvert=1
, and there exists
F_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F_{0}\subseteq[n]\setminus(G\cup B)
. When
n
is sufficiently large, we have
\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d-1}\geq\frac{\lvert G\rvert^{d-1}}{2(d-1)!}
. Thus,
Claim

5.6
(3) implies that

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{d}-\binom{n-1-\lvert G\rvert}{d}-\binom{\lvert G\rvert}{d-1}+6(n-\lvert G\rvert)n^{d-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{d}-\left(\frac{(C^{*}_{d})^{d-1}}{2(d-1)!}+6(C^{*}_{d}-1)\right)n^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{d}-6(1-C^{*}_{d})n^{d-1}.

Case 4.

Finally, suppose that
\lvert B\rvert=1
and there does not exist
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\subseteq[n]\setminus(G\cup B)
. We claim that every
F\in\mathcal{F}
contains
B
. Indeed, if
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{good}}
, then there is some
a\in G
such that
a\in F
, thus
B=\{b(a)\}\subseteq F
by
Claim

5.4
and the definition of
b(a)
. Otherwise, since
F\cap G=\emptyset
, we must have
B\subseteq F
. We conclude that
\mathcal{F}
is trivially intersecting and
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert\leq\binom{n-1}{d}
.

Based on the detailed analysis of the above four cases, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}
is either trivially intersecting, or
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{d}-C_{d}n^{d-1}
for some
C_{d}>0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

6
Concluding remarks

Although it still seems very hard to resolve
Question

1.2
, we believe the value
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
should be the correct answer and our proof provides some new insights. Here we discuss more about the connection between the best-known construction of Mubayi-Zhao in
[
22
]
(which generalizes the original construction of Ahlswede and Khachatrian
[
1
]
) and our proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

We first recall the construction of
\mathcal{F}
with
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
in
[
22
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\subseteq\binom{[n]\setminus\{1,2\}}{d}
satisfy the following three conditions:

\mathcal{G}_{1}\cup\mathcal{G}_{2}=\binom{[n]\setminus\{1,2\}}{d}
.

2.

\mathcal{G}_{1}\cap\mathcal{G}_{2}=\{F\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{1,2\}}{d}:\{3,4\}\subseteq F\}
.

3.

\mathcal{G}_{1}\supseteq\{F\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{1,2\}}{d}:3\in F\}
,
\mathcal{G}_{2}\supseteq\{F\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{1,2\}}{d}:4\in F\}
.

Note that

\binom{n-2}{d-1}+\lvert\mathcal{G}_{1}\rvert+\lvert\mathcal{G}_{2}\rvert=\binom{n-2}{d-1}+\binom{n-2}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}=\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}.

It was claimed in
[
22
]
that the number of such choices of
(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})
is
\frac{P(n-4,d)}{2}
, where
P(n-4,d)
is the number of non-isomorphic
d
-uniform hypergraphs on
n-4
vertices. After selecting the set systems
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, Mubayi and Zhao
[
22
]
constructed
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{d+1}
using four types of pairs
(F_{k},B_{k})
.

1.

Sets
F_{k}
in
\binom{[n]}{d+1}
containing

, with

and
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d-1
.

2.

Sets
F_{k}
in
\{1\}\cup(\mathcal{G}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{2})
with
B_{k}=F_{k}\setminus\{1\}
and
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d
.

3.

Sets
F_{k}
in
\{2\}\cup(\mathcal{G}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1})
with
B_{k}=F_{k}\setminus\{2\}
and
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d
.

4.

Sets
F_{k}
of the form
\{1\}\cup(\mathcal{G}_{1}\cap\mathcal{G}_{2})
and
\{2\}\cup(\mathcal{G}_{1}\cap\mathcal{G}_{2})
, with
B_{k}=F_{k}\setminus\{1,4\}
if
1\in F_{k}
and
B_{k}=F_{k}\setminus\{2,3\}
if
2\in F_{k}
. Note that for each set
F_{k}
of this type,
\lvert B_{k}\rvert=d-1
.

For this example, we have the small transversal set
J=\{1,2\}
. Then some calculations give

1.

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}=\emptyset
.

2.

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}
contains the sets of Type 2 and 3.

3.

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}
contains the sets of Type 4.

4.

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}=\emptyset
.

5.

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}
contains the sets of Type 1.

6.

\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}=\emptyset
.

Note that

\mathcal{G}_{J}=\mathcal{G}_{1}\cup\mathcal{G}_{2}=\binom{[n]\setminus J}{d}.

So we have
K=0
. We can compute that

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J}^{1}\rvert=0,

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J}^{2}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{3}\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{G}_{1}\rvert+\lvert\mathcal{G}_{2}\rvert=\binom{n-2}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}=\binom{n-\lvert J\rvert}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2},

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J}^{4}\cup\mathcal{T}_{J}^{5}\rvert=\binom{n-2}{d-1}=(\lvert J\rvert-1)\binom{n-\lvert J\rvert}{d-1},

\lvert\mathcal{T}_{J}^{6}\rvert=0.

Since
K=0
, this yields that
Claim

3.8
and
Claim

3.9
are sharp, which provides some evidence that the value
\binom{n-1}{d}+\binom{n-4}{d-2}
is the correct answer.

As for
Conjecture

1.5
, fully resolving it remains challenging. In
Theorem

1.6
, we verify the special cases where
s\in\{0,1,d\}
. In particular, for the case
s=d
, we present two distinct types of proofs: one based on a double counting argument and the other utilizing an advanced tool introduced in recent work
[
5
]
. Furthermore, we demonstrate that when
s=d
, the maximum size
\binom{n-1}{d}
can only be achieved if
\mathcal{F}
is a star of maximum size. A natural question arises as to whether a stronger stability result exists for
Theorem

5.1
, akin to the Hilton-Milner Theorem for the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem. Specifically, whether the size of
\mathcal{F}
would be significantly smaller than
\binom{n-1}{d}
if it is not a star. However, such a result does not hold. To illustrate this, consider
A=\{2,3,4,\ldots,d,d+1,d+2\}
and let
\mathcal{C}
consist of all
d
-subsets of
A
. Then, the
(d+1)
-uniform set system

\mathcal{F}:=\{A\}\bigcup\bigg\{G\cup\{1\}:G\in\binom{[n]\setminus\{1\}}{d}\setminus\mathcal{C}\bigg\}

satisfies the condition in
Theorem

5.1
, and
\mathcal{F}
is not a star. Nevertheless,
|\mathcal{F}|=\binom{n-1}{d}-d
, which is very close to
\binom{n-1}{d}
.

Fortunately, as we proved in
Theorem

5.2
, such a strong stability result does exist when
s=1
. From another perspective, attempting to extend the aforementioned construction indeed reduces
|\mathcal{F}|
by approximately
n^{d-1}
. Therefore, we conjecture that if
Conjecture

1.5
holds, there might also exist a similar stability result. Specifically, for general
s
, if
\mathcal{F}
is not a star, then its size would not exceed
\binom{n-1}{d}-Cn^{d-s}
for some positive constant
C
.

Nevertheless, we contend that a complete resolution of the entire
Conjecture

1.5
presents significant challenges and remains a highly nontrivial task. Even to completely settle the case
d=s-1
of this conjecture seems to require new ideas.
