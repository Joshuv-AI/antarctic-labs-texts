Systematic identification of pathogenic variants of non-small cell lung cancer in the promoters of DNA-damage repair genes
Authors: Mingxing An, Congcong Chen, Jun Xiang, Yang Li, Pinyu Qiu, Yiru Tang, Xinyue Liu, Yayun Gu, Na Qin, Yuanlin He, Meng Zhu, Yue Jiang, Juncheng Dai, Guangfu Jin, Hongxia Ma, Cheng Wang, Zhibin Hu, Hongbing Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDeficiency in DNA-damage repair (DDR) genes, often due to disruptive coding variants, is linked to higher cancer risk. Our previous study has revealed the association between rare loss-of-function variants in DDR genes and the risk of lung cancer. However, it is still challenging to study the predisposing role of rare regulatory variants of these genes.MethodsBased on whole-genome sequencing data from 2984 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and 3020 controls, we performed massively parallel reporter assays on 1818 rare variants located in the promoters of DDR genes. Pathway- or gene-level burden analyses were performed using Firth’s logistic regression or generalized linear model.FindingsWe identified 750 rare functional regulatory variants (frVars) that showed allelic differences in transcriptional activity within the promoter regions of DDR genes. Interestingly, the burden of frVars was significantly elevated in cases (odds ratio [OR] = 1.17, p = 0.026), whereas the burden of variants prioritized solely based on bioinformatics annotation was comparable between cases and controls (OR = 1.04, p = 0.549). Among the frVars, 297 were down-regulated transcriptional activity (dr-frVars) and 453 were up-regulated transcriptional activity (ur-frVars); especially, dr-frVars (OR = 1.30, p = 0.008) rather than ur-frVars (OR = 1.06, p = 0.495) were significantly associated with risk of NSCLC. Individuals with NSCLC carried more dr-frVars from Fanconi anemia, homologous recombination, and nucleotide excision repair pathways. In addition, we identified seven genes (i.e., BRCA2, GTF2H1, DDB2, BLM, ALKBH2, APEX1, and RAD51B) with promoter dr-frVars that were associated with lung cancer susceptibility.InterpretationOur findings indicate that functional promoter variants in DDR genes, in addition to protein-truncating variants, can be pathogenic and contribute to lung cancer susceptibility.Funding10.13039/501100001809National Natural Science Foundation of China, Youth Foundation of Jiangsu Province, Research Unit of Prospective Cohort of Cardiovascular Diseases and Cancer of Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and 10.13039/501100004608Natural Science Foundation of Jiangsu Province.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

DNA-damage repair (DDR) pathways play a crucial role in the maintenance of genome stability, which is strongly linked to an increased susceptibility to cancer. Previous studies have thoroughly examined genetic variants within the coding regions of DDR genes and their implications for cancer risk, including lung cancer. However, to the best of our knowledge, there has been a lack of systematic investigations into variants in the non-coding regions of the DDR pathway and their potential association with lung cancer.

Added value of this study

In this work, we performed massively parallel reporter assays to systematically evaluate rare variants in the promoter region of the DDR pathway. We identified 750 functional promoter variants within the DDR pathway and the burden of these variants was significantly elevated in cases. Additionally, it was noted that the 297 functional regulatory variants associated with downregulation of transcriptional activity, rather than the 453 functional regulatory variants associated with upregulation of transcriptional activity, were significantly linked to the risk of lung cancer. Patients with lung cancer demonstrated a higher prevalence of promoter variants with down-regulated transcriptional activity, which were associated with Fanconi anemia, homologous recombination, and nucleotide excision repair pathways. Furthermore, we identified seven genes (BRCA2, GTF2H1, DDB2, BLM, ALKBH2, APEX1, and RAD51B) whose promoters carried down-regulated transcriptional activity variants associated with the risk of lung cancer.

Implications of all the available evidence

The functional regulatory variants identified by massively parallel reporter assay contribute to our comprehension of lung cancer predisposition. Our study highlights the utility of massively parallel reporter assay as a high-throughput technique for discerning pathogenic variants in non-coding sequences.

Introduction

The genes responsible for DNA-damage repair (DDR) play a pivotal role in maintaining the stability of the human genome. Deficiency of DDR genes, typically resulting from disruptive mutations in their coding regions, is strongly linked to an increased risk of cancer.1 Extensive genomic sequencing has revealed many deleterious variants in DDR genes across multiple cancer types. For instance, the incidence of germline variants in genes mediating DNA-repair processes among men with metastatic prostate cancer was 11.8%,2 and nonpolyposis colorectal cancer was predominantly caused by germline variants in mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or by deletions in EPCAM.3 In the general population, it was estimated that BRCA1/2 variants were present in approximately 1 in 300–500 women, contributing to 5%–10% of breast cancer cases and 15% of ovarian cancer occurrences.4, 5, 6, 7 The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) has recommended the assessment of familial risk, genetic counseling, and genetic testing for BRCA1/2 variants in BRCA-related cancers, such as breast, ovarian, tubal, and peritoneal cancers.8

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with an estimated 2 million new cases and 1.76 million deaths per year.9,10 Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of all lung cancer incidences and poses a serious hazard to public health.9,11 Previous studies,12,13 including our own,14 have reported that individuals with lung cancer carry more truncating germline variants of DNA repair genes. These variants may represent promising candidates for consideration in genetic testing for lung cancer. Taking advantage of whole-genome sequencing (WGS), numerous variants in the non-coding regions, which encompass approximately 98.5% of the human genome, have been identified.15,16 Numerous research has documented various mechanisms through which variants in non-coding regions can lead to severe diseases. These mechanisms include impacts on splicing,17, 18, 19 transcription,20,21 translation,22 RNA processing and stability,23,24 and chromatin interactions.16 However, it is still challenging to efficiently and accurately distinguish pathogenic variants in non-coding sequences. The luciferase reporter assay has been traditionally employed to evaluate the function of a few non-coding variants, but it was inefficient when assessing thousands of variants.25 Bioinformatics annotations are commonly used to prioritize the function of a vast number of non-coding variants, yet they fail to indicate the direction of the variants’ effects, which greatly limits the ability to discern truly pathogenic variants.26,27 Massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs), in which barcoded plasmid reporters are introduced into cells, provide a viable solution for conducting high-throughput experimental analysis of the transcriptional regulatory capabilities of noncoding DNA elements and identify functional variants in the regulatory regions.28, 29, 30

Here, we performed MPRA experiments on rare variants in DDR pathway promoter regions based on WGS data from patients with NSCLC and cancer-free controls. After identifying functional regulatory variants, we investigated the association between these functional promoter variants and the risk of non-small cell lung cancer.

Methods

Whole-genome sequencing of NSCLC and cancer-free individuals

All samples included in the current study were obtained from our previously published study,14 including 6004 participants (2984 patients with NSCLC and 3020 controls) who underwent WGS (Supplementary Table S1a). Individuals with primary lung cancer were included if they met the following criteria: hospital-based, histopathologically confirmed as new patients with lung cancer by at least two pathologists, no prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy, of Chinese ancestry, aged 15 years or older at diagnosis, and had an electronic medical record indicating incident lung cancer. Controls without a history of cancer or a family history of cancer were recruited from local communities. Sex identification was self-reported by study participants. The present study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Medical University and all participants provided written informed consent for participating in the research.

Alignment, variant calling, and quality control (QC)

We utilized the FastQC package to evaluate the quality of sequencing reads (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). Mapping to the human reference genome (GRCh37) was performed using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM v0.7.15)31 with default parameters, followed by marking and removal of duplicates with SAMBLASTER (v0.1.24).32 Quality metrics, including coverage, median insert size, and chimeric read percentages, were assessed using Picard (v1.70) (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard), while contamination levels were estimated with VerifyBamID2 (v1.04) (https://github.com/Griffan/VerifyBamID). Subsequent local realignment and recalibration were conducted using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v3.8).33 Sample QC excluded individuals based on coverage, insert size, chimeric reads, contamination, relatedness, and sex mismatch, resulting in 2984 individuals with NSCLC and 3020 controls. Variant QC involved GATK variant quality score recalibration (VQSR) was used to filter variants, excluding variants based on sex chromosomes, VQSR criteria, inbreeding coefficients, genotype quality, sequencing depth, call rates, and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium deviations.14

MPRA variant selection and oligonucleotide library sequence design

We selected all rare variants (minor allele frequency < 0.1%) in −160 to +50-base pair (bp) windows around the transcription start site of all 172 DDR genes, comprising 110 genes from six classic DDR pathways and 72 genes from other DDR processes (Supplementary Table S1b),14 based on our large whole-genome sequencing data from 2984 patients with NSCLC and 3020 cancer-free controls (Supplementary Table S1a).14 The chosen promoter variants were derived from both case and control groups. Genetic variants present in the specified promoter region of all transcript isoforms of each gene were included, and any alternate alleles of a shared transcript isoform had a consistent reference allele. A total of 1818 variants within the DDR pathway were tested by MPRAs.

The oligonucleotide (oligo) library was designed mainly following published procedures with some modifications.28,34,35 To construct synthesis-ready sequences, 210-base sequences encompassing the variant with reference and alternative alleles were extracted from the human genome build GRCh37. Each fragment was flanked by binding sequences for PCR primers (240 bases oligo sequences: 5’-ACTGGCCGCTTGACG - [210 bp oligo] - CACTGCGGCTCCTGC-3’) and we purchased the Oligo pool from Synbio Technologies. Upon receipt, oligos were resuspended in water at 2 ng/μL, aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C.

Library construction

Unique 20 bp barcodes were attached to each oligo fragment by PCR. 28 PCR reactions were pooled together, each reaction containing 1.5 ng synthesized oligonucleotide library, 2.5 μL of 10 μM Barcode_F, 2.5 μL of 10 μM Barcode_R, 25 μL of NEBNext High-Fidelity 2 × PCR Master Mix (NEB), and water up to 50 μL.

To create our mpraΔorf library, the pMPRA1 (RRID: Addgene_49349) plasmid was digested with SfiI-HF (NEB) enzyme at 50 °C for 3 h and purified the DNA bands from agarose gels (NEB). Twelve assembly reactions were performed with individual mixtures containing: 100 ng of library, 332 ng of digested vector, 10 μL Gibson Assembly Master Mix (NEB), and water up to 20 μL. These reactions were incubated at 50 °C for 1 h. The ligated vector was expanded by electroporation into DH10B-Plus Electrocompetent Cell in six parallel transformations with BioRad Gene Pulser Xcell Eukaryotic System, following Maxiprep library extraction (Qiagen).

After constructing the mpraΔorf library, we performed sequencing to create a lookup table mapping barcode to oligos. Illumina libraries were prepared by performing 6 separate amplifications with 200 ng of plasmid in a 200 μL NEBNext High-Fidelity PCR reaction containing 10 μL of 10 μM pMPRA1Δorf_F and 10 μL of 10 μM pMPRA1Δorf_R primers. For indexing and sequencing of all libraries, multiplex adapters were added using the following PCR reaction: [50 μL NEBNext High-Fidelity 2 × PCR Master Mix, 10 μL of 10 μM illumia_P70X_F, 10 μL of 10 μM pMPRA1Δorf_R, 10 μL water, 30 μL sample]. The library was sequenced using 2 × 150 bp chemistry on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system.

To prepare our final mpra:luciferase library, 20 μg of mpraΔorf plasmid was linearized with 8 μL of PacI-HF (NEB), 40 μL of 10 × rCutsmart buffer (NEB), and 20 μL of rSAP (NEB) in a 400 μL volume for 2 h at 37 °C. The luciferase fragment was amplified from the pGL4.23 vector (Promega) using luciferase_F and luciferase_R primers. The fragment was then inserted by gibson assembly. The ligation product was electroporated as before except in ten parallel transformations followed by plasmid isolation. The final library was configured with the 210 bp oligo upstream of the luciferase and the 20 bp barcode located in the 3’ end of the luciferase transcript.

Massively parallel reporter assay

The human cell lines BEAS-2B (RRID: CVCL_0168) and NCI-H1299 (RRID: CVCL_0060) were purchased from the Fuheng Biotechnology (China). NCI-H1299 and BEAS-2B cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) or DMEM medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were plated across six 10 cm cell culture plates and grown to 60–70% cell density. On the day of transfection, media was replaced with 8 mL Opti-MEM followed by transfection with 45 μL of X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche) and 15 μg of DNA using the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were incubated with transfection reagents for 6 h, then washed with 3 mL of PBS followed by dissociation with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Life Technologies), centrifugation, PBS wash, and a final collection at 300×g prior to storage at −80 °C. All cell lines were recently validated with Short Tandem Repeat analysis, and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free by MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector Kit (Vazyme).

RNA extraction, normalization, and barcode sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Qiagen Mini RNeasy (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol including the on-column DNase digestion. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from purified RNA with HiScript II Enzyme Mix (Vazyme) and a gene-specific primer (RT_primer) using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

To minimize amplification bias during the creation of cDNA tag sequencing libraries, samples were amplified by qPCR to estimate relative concentrations of luciferase cDNA using 1 μL of sample in a 10 μL PCR reaction containing 5 μL ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme), 0.2 μL of 10 μM mpra:luciferase_F and pMPRA1Δorf_R primers. Samples were amplified with the following conditions: 95 °C for 20 s, 40 cycles (95 °C for 20 s, 65 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 30 s), 72 °C for 2 min. cDNA samples were diluted to match the replicate with the lowest concentration.

For sequencing of barcodes from plasmid or cDNA, the following PCR reaction was performed with each sample: [25 μL NEBNext High-Fidelity 2 × PCR Master Mix, 2.5 μL mpra:luciferase_F, 2.5 μL pMPRA1Δorf_R, 10 μL water, 10 μL sample] and cycled for [95 °C 2 min [95 °C 20 s, 60 °C 15 s, 72 °C 30 s] × 12, 72 °C 2 min]. Amplified material was purified with DNA clean beads using a 1.8 × bead/sample ratio and eluted with 30 μL of water. Individual sequencing barcodes were added to each sample by amplifying the entire 30 μL elution in a 100 μL NEBNext reaction: [50 μL NEBNext High-Fidelity 2 × PCR Master Mix, 10 μL of 10 μM illumia_P70X_F, 10 μL of 10 μM pMPRA1Δorf_R, 10 μL water, 30 μL sample] and cycled for [95 °C 2 min [95 °C 20 s, 60 °C 30 s, 72 °C 30 s] × 6, 72 °C 2 min]. Indexed libraries were DNA clean beads purified. Samples were sequenced using 2 × 150 bp Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

MPRA data analysis

We primarily rely on previous studies for the analysis of sequencing data from MPRA experiments.28 Essentially, we utilized FLASH to merge paired-end reads from NovaSeq data and subsequently employed STAR v2.7.1a to align the merged reads against a reference index created from the designed library sequences. Subsequently, reads that did not uniquely map to a designed sequence or had low-quality alignment scores were filtered out, and the resulting barcode-oligo pairs were extracted, with sequences detected on multiple oligos being removed. To quantify oligo-level counts from barcodes, Bartender v1.1 was utilized to identify barcode clusters for each sample individually and correct for sequencing errors. Oligo counts were computed by mapping each barcode to its corresponding parent oligo with an exact match and aggregating all barcode counts within each oligo after clustering. Oligos that could not provide activity measurements from at least five unique barcodes or had a mean raw count below 150 across all samples were excluded from the analysis. The final oligo count matrix included measurements for 1734 variants, encompassing a combination of two cell types (H1299 and BEAS-2B).

To address variants arising from sequencing depth and allelic ratios, a nested fixed model using DESeq236 was applied, as described for high-depth allele-specific expression analysis. Following model fitting, allele effects were evaluated through a linear contrast between DNA and RNA levels to test the null hypothesis of equal nested allelic coefficients. p from both sets of summary statistics were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. We required our final MPRA regulatory variant set to have significant allelic adjusted p (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.01) in either cell line. According to these criteria, a total of 750 variants were ultimately identified as functional regulatory variants (frVars) (Supplementary Table S2a). The variant was termed a functional regulatory variant that up-regulated gene transcription if its transcriptional activity exceeded that of the reference allele (subsequently referred to as “ur-frVar”); conversely, it was classified as a functional regulatory variant that down-regulated gene transcription if its transcriptional activity was lower than that of the reference allele (subsequently referred to as “dr-frVar”). In addition, we observed several variants involving changes in two adjacent nucleotides within the genomic DNA sequence of an individual, and we re-assessed these variants (1:45806128:CC > GG, 2:216974078:GC > AT, 6:43543906:CG > AA, 11:61100787:GA > CG, 14:68286493:GT > TC, and 22:42017202:CT > G) as dinucleotide variants (DNVs) using luciferase reporter assays (Supplementary Fig. S1). The effects on transcriptional activity of these six DNVs were consistent with that of their corresponding single-nucleotide variants, as quantified by MPRA (Supplementary Table S2b).

Bioinformatic analyses

Functional annotations and enrichment of frVars: We obtained narrowPeak files for lung-related DNase-seq and active promoter-related histone Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) (H3K4me3 and H3K9ac) marks from the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) project server (https://www.encodeproject.org/), specifically for the bronchial epithelial cell, lung fibroblast, lung tissues, and lung-related cell lines such as A549, AG04450, IMR90, PC9, and WI38. The enrichment odds ratio (OR) for functional annotations associated with frVars was calculated using Fisher’s exact test against a background set of all evaluated variants. We predicted the effect of transcription factors binding sites (TFBSs) using the motifbreakR package37 based on the JASPAR 201838 TF binding database. Only TFBSs with binding threshold p < 1 × 10−4 and “strong” predicted effects were included in the analyses. We used Fisher’s exact test to determine the enrichment of each motif in the dr-frVars sequence in comparison to all evaluated variants. TFBSs with an enrichment p of less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched in the dr-frVars set relative to the background set (Supplementary Table S2c).

All tested variants were assessed using the Lung Effect Neural Network (LungENN) algorithms, which we developed as an extension of DeepSEA,39 a convolutional neural network model that predicts the functional impact of variants. This model utilizes a convolutional neural network architecture and was trained using the PyTorch framework with the Selene library.40 The training and evaluation data for genome-wide features, such as histone marks, transcription factors, and DNase or ATAC accessibility, were derived from data sources including Cistrome,41 ENCODE,42 and Roadmap Epigenomics43 projects (Supplementary Table S3a). The LungENN model shared the same architecture as the HeartENN model,44 with the main difference being the regulatory features considered. Statistical analysis involved using the Wilcoxon test to compare predicted scores between the frVars and non-frVars groups, and assessing the correlation (Pearson’s rho) between allelic effect sizes and computational predicted scores in frVars.

The luciferase reporter assay

The promoter sequences containing either the reference or variant alleles were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (China). Constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. H1299 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium, containing 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected using X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche) with 0.5 μg of the promoter construct and 0.005 μg of pRL-TK (Promega), a control Renilla luciferase vector. 48 h later cells were lysed and luciferase activity was assayed with the Dual-Luciferase Reporter assay (Promega) in a 96-well format according to manufacturer instructions. Experiments were performed in triplicate to quadruplicate wells. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase.

CRISPR base editing

To introduce the promoter variants into lung cancer cells, we selected the four dr-frVars (11:18344003:A > G, 14:20923406:A > G, 11:47236417:C > T, and 11:47236479:G > A) that according to the following principles: (1) the dr-frVars meet the targeted installation of base editors; (2) the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence are NGG; and (3) the dr-frVars are located within the editing window (positions 4–8, counting the PAM as positions 21–23) of the base editor. We employed a system utilizing either the NG-ABE8e adenine base editor45 (RRID: Addgene_138491) or the pCMV_AncBE4max cytidine base editor46 (RRID: Addgene_112094), along with guide RNA plasmids. We purchased the guide RNA (gRNA) plasmids from Tsingke Biotechnology. H1299 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 3.5 × 105 cells per well, incubated overnight at 37 °C, and transfected with 3000 ng of NG-ABE8e or pCMV_AncBE4max plasmids, along with 1000 ng of sgRNA expression plasmids, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Following transfection, the cells underwent selection with 2.5 μg/mL puromycin for two days. Total RNA was then extracted from each replicate using the TRIZOL method. The cDNA was reversed transcribed using Hiscript Ⅲ Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme) with 1 μg of total RNA. We performed qPCR using the 2 × ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) and 200 nM qPCR primers. Relative transcript abundance was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method normalized to the geometric mean of the GAPDH reference genes. Three independent biological replicates were conducted. All primers and gRNA sequences in this study are shown in Supplementary Table S3b.

Statistical analysis

The MPRA data analysis has been extensively detailed previously. Enrichment analysis was predominantly conducted utilizing a Fisher’s exact test, unless otherwise specified. Evaluation of the discrepancy in algorithm prediction scores between frVars and non-frVars was achieved through Wilcoxon test. In reporter assay experiments, a two-tailed Student’s t test was employed, comparing the average value of the ref-allele against that of alt-allele conditions. Leveraging its capacity to reduce bias when dealing with imbalanced data, particularly for rare variants,47 we utilized Firth’s logistic regression or generalized linear model (GLM) for gene/pathway-based analyses of rare variants. Pathway-level or gene-level association analyses were performed by aggregating rare functional variants identified through MPRAs or luciferase reporter assays based on pathways or genes into a dose variable and applying a burden test for the association analyses,48,49 integrating findings from our previous study.14 The association between the number of variants in genes or pathways and the risk of NSCLC was evaluated with adjustment for age, sex, and pack-years. Multiple testing correction was performed, where necessary by using the FDR method. All reported p were two-sided. All the analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.1.3, R Project for Statistical Computing). Lollipop plots were created by the R package trackViewer.50

Results

Characterization of genes with promoter dr-frVars predisposing to lung cancer

Promoter variants with potential function, as filtered by our previously established functional prioritize score, BRCA2 has been associated with an increased risk of lung cancer.14 In the region evaluated in the current study, 17 rare variants of BRCA2 were detected by MPRA, of which 12 were regarded as potential functional variants based on the bioinformatics prioritize score from our previous study (Supplementary Table S6). Of these variants, only 7 were found to reduce transcriptional activity and were significantly associated with an elevated risk of lung cancer (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Table S6). The remaining 5 variants, including 3 that increased transcriptional activity and 2 that did not influence transcriptional activity, were not significantly associated with the risk of lung cancer (OR = 2.47, p = 0.185. Firth’s logistic regression). In addition, our previous study identified 23 loss-of-function (LoF) variants in the coding region of BRCA2, yet none of the carriers with dr-frVars had a LoF variant in BRCA2 (Supplementary Table S7).14 Similar to BRCA2, another HR gene RAD51B harbored an additional LoF variant in patients with lung cancer but did not contain any LoF variants in controls (Supplementary Table S7). After excluding BRCA2 and RAD51B, the dr-frVars burden of the HR pathway was comparable between cases and controls (OR = 1.17, p = 0.608. Firth’s logistic regression) (Supplementary Fig. S4a).

GTF2H1, which plays a vital role in the NER pathway, exhibited the second-highest prevalence of dr-frVars (Supplementary Table S5a). The burden of 29 variants in GTF2H1 determined by functional annotation was comparable between cases and controls (OR = 1.54, p = 0.202. Firth’s logistic regression); however, the 7 dr-frVars identified by MPRA were significantly associated with an increased susceptibility to lung cancer (Table 1; Fig. 5i). Among these 7 dr-frVars, 4 were predicted to disrupt TFBS, and 2 of them were predicted to disrupt the TFBS of ELF1, which was the transcription factor that was significantly enriched in the sequences of dr-frVars (Supplementary Fig. S5a and b; Supplementary Table S2c). ChIP-Seq from ENCODE project exhibited prominent confidence peaks of ELF1 that intersect with the two dr-frVars in the A549 cell line (Supplementary Fig. S5c). Another NER gene, DDB2, also carried a dr-frVar that was predicted to disrupt the TFBS of ELF1 (Supplementary Fig. S5d and e; Supplementary Table S2a). It is noteworthy that GTF2H1 did not harbor any LoF variant in the cases (Supplementary Table S7), a finding consistent with its high intolerance to LoF variants, as indicated by its probability of loss-of-function intolerance score of 1. In contrast, DDB2 harbored a LoF variant in the cases (Supplementary Table S7). After excluding GTF2H1 and DDB2, the dr-frVars burden of the NER pathway was not significantly associated with the risk of lung cancer (OR = 1.21, p = 0.437. Firth’s logistic regression) (Supplementary Fig. S4b).

Discussion

In this study, we provided a systematic evaluation of all rare variants in the promoter regions of DDR genes observed in a cohort of 2984 patients with NSCLC and 3020 controls, leading to the identification of 297 dr-frVars. The burdens of dr-frVars in the FA, HR, and NER pathways were significantly associated with the risk of NSCLC. Furthermore, we identified seven genes (i.e., BRCA2, GTF2H1, DDB2, BLM, ALKBH2, APEX1, and RAD51B) with promoter dr-frVars that were associated with lung cancer susceptibility and a total of 47 cases (1.58%) carried these non-coding functional variants.

Genetic screening for variants in cancer predisposing genes can identify individuals with elevated disease susceptibility, enabling them to potentially mitigate their risk through available preventive measures.53 The majority of cancer predisposing genes are involved in the DDR pathway. For example, the USPSTF recommends that primary care clinicians assess women who have an ancestry associated with BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants with an appropriate brief familial risk assessment.54 PALB2, CHEK2, ATM, NBN, and other DNA repair related genes have been included in genetic screening panels by many organizations due to the documented links between protein-truncating harmful variants in these genes and multiple cancers.55 In addition, prospective studies were currently underway to demonstrate the use of germline genetic markers (BRCA1/2) to identify men at higher risk of prostate cancer, which has the potential to enable better risk stratification to inform targeted screening.56, 57, 58 Germline variants in coding regions of 6 cancer predisposition genes (ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, CDKN2A, MLH1, and TP53) are associated with increased risks of pancreatic cancer.59 Compared with the general population, patients with renal cell carcinoma had a significantly increased frequency of germline CHEK2 variants.60 Similarly, our previous study also revealed that LoF variants of DDR genes predisposing were detected within the coding sequences of genes associated with NSCLC susceptibility.14 The majority of these studies have focused on variants in the coding sequences and splice junctions of the genes of interest, although accumulating evidence suggests that variants in non-coding sequences may also contribute to the pathogenesis of multiple diseases,44,61, 62, 63 including cancers.64 For instance, germline variants in the TERT promoter are associated with familial melanoma,64 while multiple single nucleotide polymorphisms in a gene desert on chromosome 8q24 upstream of MYC are related to increased risk for many cancer types.65,66 Our study systematically evaluated the function of promoter variants of all DDR genes and revealed their association with the risk of lung cancer. Our results indicated that the dr-frVars in seven predisposing genes were associated with increased risks of lung cancer. Additionally, as individuals rarely harbored both non-coding and coding functional variants in DDR genes concurrently, these non-coding variants yielded an additional 1.44% contribution to NSCLC incidence. Thus, our findings provided a number of potential candidates for consideration in future genetic testing for lung cancer risk assessment. It is critical that the need for further comprehensive characterization before these variants can be effectively utilized for high-risk screening in a clinical context.

MPRA has primarily been utilized to assess genetic variants within susceptibility loci identified by genome-wide association studies (GWASs),28,67, 68, 69 as a large proportion of GWASs’ hits are found to tag non-coding variants.70,71 Recently, a few studies have indicated that MPRA can also be applied to assess genetic variants with high penetrance in non-coding regions, although they primarily focused on congenital diseases.62,72 In this study, we adopted an integrative approach, combining MPRA with WGS data, to efficiently nominate functional variants and susceptibility genes for cancer, particularly focusing on rare variants within the promoter regions of DDR pathway genes. According to our data in the promoters, less than half of variants identified by bioinformatics prioritize score influenced transcriptional activity. The exclusion of these variants that did not affect transcriptional activity facilitated the identification of an association between DDR genes and lung cancer, suggesting that MPRA represents an improved strategy compared to bioinformatics annotation for high-throughput identification of rare pathogenic non-coding variants. More importantly, MPRA enabled us to distinguish up- and down-regulated transcriptional activity variants. Our findings revealed that rare dr-frVars, rather than ur-frVars, were associated with an elevated risk of NSCLC, which is consistent with the understanding that the loss of function of DDR genes contributes to cancer development.1,14,73 Although statistical methods have been developed for rare variants association analyses, allowing different variants to have different directions and magnitude of effects,74 the MPRA strategy directly enhanced the interpretability of the results of burden analyses, facilitating the identification of pathogenic non-coding variants and the evaluation of their prevalence.

Of the seven lung cancer susceptibility genes, BRCA2 and RAD51B (a paralogous homolog of RAD51) are involved in facilitating the nucleation of RAD51 onto single-stranded DNA, as well as the formation and extension of nascent RAD51 nucleoprotein filaments, promoting the repair of double-strand DNA breaks.75, 76, 77, 78 Abnormal expression or structure of the BRCA2 or RAD51B proteins may disrupt the repair of double-strand DNA breaks, leading to the accumulation of mutations and the occurrence of cancer within cells. The DDB2 and GTF2H1 in the NER pathway identified in this study are respectively one of the subunits of the NER pathway UV-damaged DNA binding factor and the multi-subunit transcription factor II H complex, involved in protecting against mutations caused indirectly by environmental carcinogens.79, 80, 81 Furthermore, we noticed that genes known to be intolerant to LoFs,82,83 such as GTF2H1, can harbor pathogenic dr-frVars in their promoter regions. Given that protein-truncating LoFs are the primary source of pathogenic variants in the current cancer genetic testing protocols, genes that lack such variants are often overlooked. Our data highlight the importance of these genes and suggest that the regulatory regions of these genes represent an under-explored source of pathogenic variants.

While our findings offer valuable insights into the genetic factors contributing to the onset of NSCLC, it is important to acknowledge and consider the limitations of our study when interpreting these results. Firstly, we did not report the association between rare variants in enhancers and lung cancer risk due to the challenges in identifying the enhancers of DDR genes. Several methods have been developed to pinpoint the precise locations of sequences bound by regulatory proteins within enhancers controlling specific promoters, such as Micro-Capture-C84 and next-generation Capture-C.85 These methods enable further investigation into the association between non-coding regions and the risk of lung cancer. Secondly, the current MPRA throughput remains inadequate for the comprehensive evaluation of all variants across the genome. Prediction model based on deep-learning algorithm may offer a complementary strategy for the identification of pathogenic non-coding variants in the future.86,87 Thirdly, the identified functional regulatory variants were identified in the Chinese Han population. Further studies are necessary to validate these findings in different ethnic groups to ensure broader applicability. Fourthly, the FDR q for BLM, DDB2, APEX1, ALKBH2, and RAD51B, did not reach the threshold of 0.1 for statistical significance in the current analysis. Further investigation in future studies is warranted to validate these findings. Fifthly, genetic variants identified in our study may interact with other factors, such as Body Mass Index, smoking or alcohol behavior, which could potentially influence lung cancer susceptibility, and such interaction and underlying genetic mechanisms are warranted to be evaluated in the prospective cohorts.

In conclusion, we provide a refined model for understanding the effects of rare variants in the promoter region of the DDR pathway. The burden of all dr-frVars across DDR genes showed a significant association with NSCLC risk, offering new insights into NSCLC predisposition. These findings provided a number of potential candidates for consideration in future genetic testing for lung cancer risk assessment.
