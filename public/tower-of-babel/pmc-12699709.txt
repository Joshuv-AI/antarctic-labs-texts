Barriers and facilitators to performing benign paroxysmal positional vertigo manoeuvres among primary care physicians in Barcelona: a qualitative study
Authors: Alicia Rullan-Rabassa, Daniel G Abiétar, Yolanda Rando-Matos, José Luis Ballvé-Moreno, Sheena Daryanani-Nawalrai, Patricia Hernández-Méndez, Malgorzata Anna Rozenek, Anna Santolalla-De Pedro, Raquel Torrecilla-Sánchez, Eva Peguero-Rodríguez

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo explore the barriers and facilitators in adherence to the guidelines in the management of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo from the perspective of primary care physicians.DesignQualitative study using focus groups.SettingL’Hospitalet del Llobregat (Barcelona), Spain.Study designQualitative study using focus groups. Structured 90 min focus groups were conducted until data saturation was reached. Each session included a moderator and an observer from the research team. Sessions were transcribed and thematically analysed by three independent researchers.ParticipantsPurposeful sampling was used to form four groups of 4–10 participants, selected by sex, age, years of experience and primary care team (PCT). Participants were recruited between January and February 2023.ResultsA total of 34 family physicians belonging to four PCTs participated in the study. The main barriers identified were a lack of time, negative initial experiences, a fear of harming patients (especially older adults), difficulty in nystagmus visualisation and challenges in managing patient expectations, as many preferred medication over physical manoeuvres. Introduction

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is the most frequent cause of peripheral vertigo, accounting for 17–42% of cases.1 Its diagnosis relies on careful history-taking and physical examination, including vestibular tests like the Dix-Hallpike test (DHT). Routine laboratory or imaging tests are generally unnecessary.2 Posterior canal BPPV (80–90% of BPPV cases) is treated using canalith repositioning manoeuvres, particularly the Epley manoeuvre (EM),3 which systematic reviews have confirmed as effective.4 5

Clinical practice guidelines recommend both the DHT and EM while discouraging other tests and medications,1 as drugs may relieve symptoms but hinder natural compensation and are actually contraindicated.4 Nevertheless, most BPPV patients are seen in primary care or emergency departments, where these manoeuvres are not routinely performed.6 7 This results in many patients being undiagnosed or mismanaged. For example, in a German study of patients with BPPV conducted through interviews, only 10% were treated with positioning manoeuvres, mainly Brandt-Daroff exercises.8

Inappropriate management leads to unnecessary expenses in tests, contraindicated medications, repeat consultations and prolonged sick leaves, often solvable within 24–48 hours.9

Two qualitative studies, one in Germany (primary care)10 and another in the USA (emergency care),11 explored healthcare professionals’ barriers and facilitators in managing BPPV. The German study10 explored aspects related to the diagnosis and treatment of vertigo or dizziness, not specifically BPPV, and the American study11 was conducted on emergency physicians, both through individual interviews. Barriers included poor previous experiences, uncertainty about manoeuvre performance and difficulty interpreting nystagmus. Key facilitators were accessible video-based training.10 11 For example, in his article, Kerber et al11 explain that participants preferred video-based resources and often used YouTube for instruction and teaching. They valued having multiple examples of nystagmus to compare and emphasised the importance of clear narration or annotation to highlight key features. To help solve this problem, our research team (the VERTAP group) has designed a tool (the NYSTAREC project) which uses a mobile phone to visualise, record and view eye movements during diagnostic manoeuvres. The aim is to analyse these movements automatically in future. The device will be accompanied by a mobile application to assist with the selection and performance of diagnostic and therapeutic manoeuvres. NYSTAREC (previously called NYSTAVERT) is a project that is still awaiting funding approval; however, information about it can be consulted via the following link: https://annualreport.idiapjgol.org/en/2023-annual-report-2023/research/vertap/.

To our knowledge, previous qualitative studies have explored the experiences of doctors in emergency contexts, or difficulties in managing vertigo/dizziness in general, but not in primary care for BPPV in particular. They also used individual interviews, which do not allow for the exploration of shared meanings and group dynamics. The research team aimed to explore the need for a more in-depth understanding of the factors contributing to the systematic/daily underuse of repositioning manoeuvres, but also discussing this ‘construction’ with researchers’ interpretative horizons (phenomenological and hermeneutical perspective). It is therefore essential to identify barriers and facilitators in BPPV management in primary care and to assess the potential of a supporting tool, which is the aim of the present qualitative study.

Methods

Study design and setting

A qualitative study was conducted using focus groups and a phenomenological and hermeneutical theoretical-methodological perspective to explore the everyday experiences of primary care professionals in the management of the diagnosis and treatment of BPPV. The phenomenological perspective seeks to understand participants’ lived experiences, while the hermeneutical perspective focuses on interpreting the meanings of those experiences, paying attention to the fact that interpretation entails a fusion of interpretative horizons between researchers and participants, so that the hermeneutic exercise first requires the most faithful possible collection of participants’ experiences within their broader social and cultural contexts.12 13 From this perspective, the VERTAP Primary Care Vertigo Research Group (online supplemental data 1) discussed the perceptions held by professionals when managing cases of BPPV.

The focus groups were convened during the last quarter of 2022. In January and February 2023, verbatim transcription of the collected data was carried out (AR-R, PH-M, MAR, AS-DP, RT-S, SD-N). Between February and August 2024, five researchers (YR-M, JLB, AR-R, DGA, EP-R) conducted the data analysis. From September 2024 to June 2025, this article was drafted.

In terms of the study setting, the neighbourhoods of La Florida and Pubilla Casas in L’Hospitalet de Llobregat have a high population density and a significant immigrant population, with small, ageing housing stock, leading to situations of overcrowding.14 In contrast, the Centre neighbourhood, the historical core of the city, is home to the main cultural and administrative facilities and has a more established urban infrastructure.15

Participants

The reference population comprised four primary care teams (PCT) in L’Hospitalet de Llobregat (a city within the metropolitan area of Barcelona). The selection criteria included family medicine professionals belonging to each of the PCTs who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study after signing informed consent. Other professional categories were not included because, in our area of care, it is only family doctors who see patients with vertigo/dizziness. Professionals not engaged in clinical care activities were excluded. The sociodemographic and professional characteristics collected from these participants were sex (male, female), age, resident tutor status (yes, no), years of professional practice and whether they had completed a vertigo-related training course within the past 5 years (yes, no). Patients were not involved in the design, conduct and reporting of the study.

Recruitment

Sampling was purposive and based on expert opinion. Recruitment was carried out through key informants (the management teams of the PCTs), who invited professionals to participate based on their availability and prior involvement in centre activities. Particular emphasis was placed on ensuring the participation of individuals both in favour of and opposed to the use of repositioning manoeuvres. The researchers (AR-R, PH-M, YR-M) sent email invitations to the management teams to facilitate recruitment.

Data collection

The focus groups were held in the libraries of the participating PCTs, with each session lasting between 60 and 90 min until data saturation was reached. Each session was led by a moderator (JLB) and a non-participant observer (AR-R), who was also responsible for audio recording and ensuring the smooth running of the meeting.

The sole homogeneity criterion for group composition was belonging to the same health centre. Heterogeneity was sought by considering the aforementioned sociodemographic characteristics to encourage variability in discourse.

A semistructured interview guide was developed in advance to address the research objectives (online supplemental table 1). The interview guide, which was developed based on previous literature, served as a flexible framework rather than a strict script. During the focus groups, the questions were not asked exactly as written. Instead, the guide was used to ensure that relevant topics were addressed if they did not emerge spontaneously in the discussion. While the sessions were predominantly based on open-ended questions and free conversation among participants, special attention was given to issues such as barriers to performing manoeuvres and potential facilitating factors, based on findings from previous studies.10 11 All sessions were audio recorded, field notes were taken and the recordings were subsequently transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

A reflexive thematic analysis was conducted based on Braun and Clarke,16 incorporating their most recent reflections on the variability of this method in the published literature,17 and adapted to the experience and knowledge of the research team. The analysis was carried out manually (using a shared text document), predominantly inductive in nature, with an initial descriptive coding process.17 A continuous process of comparison and constant review was undertaken to ensure rigour in the correlation between codes and the text corpus. A text corpus is a systematically assembled and delimited collection of textual materials used as the empirical basis for qualitative analysis. In this study, the corpus consists of the group interview transcripts, compiled and prepared following a consistent protocol to ensure the integrity of participants’ contributions. The procedure and its theoretical and practical assumptions are described in online supplemental data 2.

Triangulation in each stage (coding and themes interpretation) by DGA (one of the authors of this study) enabled the questioning and formulation/reformulation of interpretative and theoretical assumptions regarding the participants’ narratives.

Themes

Theme 1. "They believe in it": The professional’s inertia in hegemonic medical practice

V1 “Of course, because with all due respect, the otolaryngologist in our area does this [pharmacological exclusive management]… because it gets the patient off their hands and because they have a prescribing inertia — they’ve always done the same thing…” (Group 1)

Primary care professionals expressed throughout the discussion the clinical inertia they daily feel towards pharmacological prescription, as “it is the usual clinical practice even among local otolaryngologists”. This hegemonic practice refers here to the dominant biomedical and pharmacological approach that shapes both professional routines and patients’ expectations.

Most of them relate not performing manoeuvres to the lack of time in consultations, especially, they say, in urgent visits.

Subtheme 1.1. The patient’s accommodation to hegemonic practice

From the family physicians’ point of view, they perceive patients feel more comfortable with the option of pharmacological treatment, thus subscribing to this therapeutic approach, and professionals report difficulties in potentially deprescribing (v2).

V2 “For those who take betahistine — when you want to withdraw the medication, especially older people with cervical pain and so on — it’s impossible, they say things like ‘don’t take this away from me because I’ll get dizzy’. Patients say no.” (Group 1)

Furthermore, in older, multipathological patients or those with cervical osteoarthritis, performing the manoeuvres can be challenging due to added difficulty with mobilisation. Also, vertiginous symptoms may present in a more diffuse, complex way, making differential diagnosis more difficult.

Also, a previous negative patient experience with the manoeuvres adds greater discomfort for the physician when considering performing the repositioning manoeuvres, which synergies with patients’ ones, although professionals tend to conceptualise both fears separately (v3).

V3 “But it’s true that when they’ve had a bad experience, it’s very difficult to convince them to do it again. They even come straight away saying ‘No, no, no please, anything but that.” (Group 1)

In contrast, other facilitating arguments appear for performing the manoeuvres, such as greater willingness to do them in younger patients, and the opportunity for deprescribing after successful performance (v4).

V4 “I’ve had patients who had never experienced the manoeuvre before. When I performed it on them, I stopped giving them betahistine and they never experienced it again because they responded well to the manoeuvre. I tell them that if it happens again, they should come and see me just to have the manoeuvre done again.” (Group 1)

Subtheme 1.2. The insecurity of manual techniques, the safety of “This has always been done this way”

A subtheme that stood out in the focus groups was the insecurity professionals feel when performing BPPV manoeuvres, and throughout their discourse, various possible causes were alluded to. On the one hand, they referred to routine clinical practice and the previously mentioned care inertia, which also frees professionals from having to rethink certain diagnostic-therapeutic algorithms (heuristics) already integrated into their daily work (v5).

V5 “Depending on how the morning goes, you go directly, give some pharmacological treatment and say: look, let’s see how it goes, and if it continues, I’ll call them or do something else another day.…” (Group 1)

Furthermore, some professionals highlighted negative experiences when performing manoeuvres with adverse effects triggered in the patient, causing distress and making the physician question the appropriateness of the manoeuvre. This insecurity is especially relevant with elderly patients. As a result, this arouses an internal fear among professionals of harming the patient, prompting them in some cases to select a more specific patient profile as mentioned above, ensuring there is no uncertainty in the differential diagnosis (v6, v7).

V6 “Well, the two times I’ve done it, they ended up vomiting, it went terribly… I don’t know if something went wrong or… So then, of course, you hesitate too.” (Group 1)

V7 “The thing is, if it’s an older person and you’re not so sure it’s BPPV, I won’t take the risk… that they’ll have a bad time, that it won’t help, and that they’ll lose trust and won’t come back.” (Group 2)

Conversely, facilitators were also mentioned—for instance, having had a previous positive experience, even with ambivalent results, or simply trusting the effectiveness of the manoeuvres and being aware of their positive impact on the patient’s quality of life. Professionals also mentioned that having personally experienced BPPV as a patient themselves served as a natural empathy and a motivation to continue offering this intervention (v8, v9).

V8 “When I’ve tried it, sometimes it’s gone better, sometimes worse, but they don’t usually get terribly dizzy. I don’t have them vomiting in the office for ages.” (Group 1)

V9 “I remember having it once and feeling so awful I would’ve let anyone do anything. So when someone comes in doing horribly and leaves walking fine, you think “wow, this works”. ” (Group 4)

A lack of adequate and ongoing training also contributes to this professional insecurity and reluctance, due to the absence of habit in performing the manoeuvres, generating a spiral in which the decision becomes progressively harder to make. Family physicians advocate for a training approach focused both on the differential diagnosis of dizziness/vertigo and on the practical performance of manoeuvres (v10).

V10 “I think the key is training, being very well trained — knowing when it’s appropriate, not assuming everything is peripheral vertigo. If we’re well trained in the differential diagnosis and diagnosing it confidently, it gives us peace of mind and improves outcomes for these patients.” (Group 1)

All of the elements described in this section help us to further interpret the first theme of our analysis, as they all contribute to forming a hegemonic, widespread professional habit of avoidance among family physicians regarding diagnostic and therapeutic manoeuvres for vertigo, which is closely and jointly developed with that patients’ ‘faith’ in pharmacological treatment.

Theme 2. The demand for comprehensive institutional support

Family medicine professionals emphasised institutional shortcomings that hinder the proper management of patients with vertigo. An overloaded schedule, inadequate physical spaces and training programmes, and inefficient organisational resources were identified as obstacles to regular clinical practice.

Subtheme 2.1. Overloaded schedule as an institutional cause

Time pressure during consultations was related to both the workload, but it was also caused by the lack of experience in performing the manoeuvres, which require more time when performed by less experienced professionals. An overloaded schedule fosters care inertia, with professionals looking for faster or more automated mechanisms (the aforementioned heuristics). Delays in scheduled appointments also make it difficult rescheduling for follow-up. This ‘timing problem’ may be even more pronounced in emergency care settings (v11).

V11 “You need time. You have to book them in. You have to find a moment — and since we’re always as busy as we are… there’s never a good time.” (Group 3)

However, the possibility of follow-up appointments or monitoring patient progress was also mentioned as a facilitator (v12).

V12 “And follow-up consultations — because that’s what primary care is about. In the end, we’re the ones with longitudinal care, and we’ll see if someone’s not improving, right?” (Group 1)

Subtheme 2.2. Support tools—new technologies

The proposal of devices to assist in situ with nystagmus interpretation for guided manoeuvres was considered, but it raised as a potential obstacle due to the time required when the device is shared among the entire team, which would interrupt the workflow and pace of the consultation (v13).

V13 “I still say that the main problem is the lack of time. So, the tool is great, but if we don’t improve the time issue, it’s going to sit there unused and no one will pick it up.” (Group 1)

However, some professionals did recognise the value of having such a device in refractory cases when supervised by an expert or when time could be saved by the institution. It could also be used to help reduce betahistine prescriptions or assist with nystagmus interpretation (v14).

V14 “Sure — maybe not for every vertigo patient, because there are some cases in which you say, ‘I’ve got this handled, I don’t need it,’ but for the patient who’s not improving, maybe then I’d go and fetch it.” (Group 1)

There was also mention of new technologies like tutorial videos as a potential solution to support professionals with manoeuvre techniques by providing accessible visual resources (v15).

V15 “You don’t remember, but sometimes I gave him a medical appointment and put the YouTube video in. I remembered it in a moment, because otherwise you don’t know where to start.” (Group 3)

Beyond the need for technical resources and training, some participants that usually perform the manoeuvres mentioned institutional factors that, though less frequently cited, were seen as limiting BPPV management. These included inadequate physical space for manoeuvres and mixed views on the ‘Vertigo Care Lead’ role—valued by some as support, but questioned by others for its unclear scope. While not themes, these reflections reveal underlying organisational weaknesses that may hinder routine use of repositioning techniques.

Discussion

This study identified two key themes that could explain the limited use of diagnostic and therapeutic manoeuvres for vertigo in primary care. The first theme highlights how clinical routines and patient expectations around pharmacological treatment reinforce a pattern of avoidance towards manual techniques. Professionals express uncertainty, lack of training and fear of adverse effects, especially in older patients. The second theme points to structural barriers—such as time pressure, inadequate physical space and insufficient training—that optimistically could be a window of opportunity for improvement in manoeuvres use, but currently reinforces hegemonic pharmacological assistance.

Time constraints in emergency care are often cited as a barrier to performing early manoeuvres, despite evidence showing they reduce relapse and reconsultation.18 Primary care—whether emergency or scheduled—remains the ideal setting for managing these cases,10 11 19 though time issues persist in Spain. In our study, reluctance to use manoeuvres was reinforced by the perceived fact that “even the experts don’t bother” (otolaryngologists) rarely performed them, and that leads primary care physicians to normalise their avoidance. However, this should be understood as a subjective perception arising from a qualitative study and does not necessarily reflect the usual practice of otolaryngologists in general. The attitude of “we’ve always done it this way” reflects resistance to change based on habit rather than evidence. Despite that, while pharmacological treatment may seem time-saving, it only offers short-term relief, and time limitations affect all areas of primary care.20

Time pressures related to the management of vertigo were closely linked to overall workload, as observed in our study and also reported by Stephan et al,10 who interviewed 12 primary care physicians in Germany. The German authors suggested that time constraints might actually mask motivational barriers—an interpretation we have also considered in our analysis.

Interestingly, previous literature has not highlighted the institution’s role in time management. Both early recall appointments—that need institutional excellent organisation and resources—even when there are multiple reasons for consultation, and adequate spaces to perform manoeuvres are institutional concerns, also raised in Stephan et al10 and Bradshaw et al.19

Vestibular sedatives are often prescribed out of convenience and habit, despite weak evidence.21 Doctors feel patients trust and request them and fear resistance to deprescribing. As patients readily accept this treatment, it reinforces doctors’ reluctance to change. This cycle leads us to insist on initial non-prescription as a strategy to prevent dependency and encourage more appropriate, evidence-based care through constant training and manoeuvre implementation.

Family physicians tended to be more reluctant to perform manoeuvres in older or clinically complex patients,19 whereas positive prior experiences encouraged their use. Providing reassurance regarding the very limited contraindications,22 promoting slow execution in older adults and encouraging routine practice19 may help to improve clinicians’ confidence, patient outcomes and professional satisfaction.10 11

Previous literature also indicates that adverse effects of manoeuvres can create aversion among physicians19 or influence future clinical behaviour in response to patient complaints.11 As one of our participants stated, informing patients in advance about possible side effects could help reduce professionals’ anxiety and activate patients’ coping mechanisms.

Furthermore, Bradshaw et al19 reported that many doctors lacked sufficient skills in BPPV management, often relying on physiotherapists who demonstrated greater confidence. While some clinicians regarded this as beneficial, others expressed concern that such reliance might discourage doctors from acquiring the necessary skills themselves. From our perspective, the presence of a reference figure can provide valuable support for less confident clinicians in the initial stages. However, in centres with stronger training provision, this role was perceived as less necessary, as familiarity with manoeuvres reduced dependence on external support.

In addition, Bradshaw et al19 emphasised that BPPV management requires complex cognitive processes, including memory, attention and decision-making, and that without simple, rapid-access guides, maintaining good clinical practice is challenging. This observation is consistent with Stephan et al,10 who found that German physicians preferred concise, single-page algorithms, brief summaries and remote access to expert support for complex cases (a clinical lead, as highlighted in our findings). In line with this, our participants suggested that explanatory videos and vertigo-related applications could also serve as valuable practical tools, as previously described by.11

Strengths and limitations

Each participant was identified only by their PCT and an identification number. This approach ensured anonymity but also meant that it was not possible to examine how discursive positions might have been influenced by factors such as gender, age or prior professional experience. Due to the absence of prior literature and consolidated hypotheses within the research team, it has not been previously considered that these variables could influence discursive variability to the extent of requiring independent analyses. Accordingly, group heterogeneity was prioritised in order to foster a group dialogue as rich as possible in perspectives on the phenomenon, relying on the facilitation process to support this aim.

Although probing techniques were employed during moderation,23 they might have been applied more extensively to elicit richer and more nuanced responses, and that could partially explain the scarce number of themes. Furthermore, the familiarity of participants with the research team and its stance on the phenomenon under study—given the team’s recognised expertise in the field—may have influenced the diversity and depth of their contributions, potentially leading to a tendency to align with perceived expectations. These factors could have limited thematic variability. While the interview guide was based on previous literature and designed to encourage in-depth reflections, future research should consider additional strategies to foster more elaborate responses. In addition, some participants, being aware of the aforementioned research team’s views on the management of BPPV, may have felt hesitant to disclose openly their habitual use of vestibular sedatives. Despite this potential limitation, the attitude of the research team during data collection remained consistently open, respectful and genuinely inquisitive, with the aim of encouraging participants to share their perceptions as freely and authentically as possible.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study using focus groups to explore barriers and facilitators to BPPV manoeuvre use among primary care physicians—the first contact for most vertigo patients. Barriers were grounded in real-world practice, while most facilitators reflected solutions or expectations, suggesting an aspiration towards improved care.

Resources should support training via refreshers and access to clinical leads. Improvements in space and time—where feasible—should also be considered. Proper manoeuvre use may ultimately save time by reducing repeat visits. Easy-to-use apps and video tutorials could help clarify diagnostic uncertainty, especially regarding nystagmus.

Future research should identify the most effective strategies to align vertigo management in primary care with clinical guidelines, including the perspective of primary care managers through qualitative studies. This could greatly enhance patient quality of life. The recent integration of physiotherapists into primary care in our context offers a promising opportunity to promote and evaluate their role in this clinical shift.
