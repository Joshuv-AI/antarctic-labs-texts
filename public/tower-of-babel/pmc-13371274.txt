Meta-analysis of the safety and efficacy of flap reconstruction in the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers
Authors: Weixun Fang, Xiaoxia Wang, Wei Lai, Zhiquan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the safety and efficacy of flap reconstruction surgery and the associated influential factors in the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) and to provide an evidence-based basis for clinical flap selection and surgical strategies.MethodsThe PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases were systematically searched to identify studies on comparative or single-arm flap reconstruction for the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). Eligible study designs included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, and case series published between 1998 and 2025. Two investigators independently screened the literature, extracted relevant data, and assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies—of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. Meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4. The primary and secondary outcome measures included total flap loss, limb loss rate, and flap failure rate. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to flap type and regional differences.ResultsA total of 45 studies were included. The results of the meta-analysis showed that patients undergoing flap reconstruction had higher postoperative total flap loss (HR = 5.83; 95% CI 3.65–9.31), limb loss (HR = 11.09; 95% CI 7.22–17.03), and flap failure rate (HR = 10.01; 95% CI 8.31–12.05) compared with reference populations or expected baseline risks. Subgroup analyses revealed that the risk of free flap limb loss was significantly greater than that of non-free flaps (HR = 17.86 vs. 1.94; P = 0.02); the risk of limb loss was greater in Europe (HR = 22.97) and North America (HR = 17.42) than in East Asia (HR = 5.85; P < 0.001), and there was no regional difference in total flap loss (P = 0.67). The results of the sensitivity analysis were robust, with heterogeneity arising mainly from differences in study design and patient characteristics.ConclusionFlap reconstruction remains a viable option for DFU wound closure, but it is associated with elevated risks of postoperative total flap loss and limb loss. Individualized surgical protocols, prioritization of non-free flaps for low- and intermediate-risk patients, and enhanced multidisciplinary collaboration and postoperative management are recommended. More high-quality studies are needed to validate the long-term efficacy of different flap techniques.Trial registrationRegister with PROSPERP, Registration Information: ID: CRD420251075758. Preface

Diabetic foot disease is among the most serious and costly complications in people with diabetes, and its incidence and disease burden are continuing to increase globally [1]. It has been estimated that up to 19% to 34% of approximately 500 million people with diabetes worldwide will develop foot ulcers during their lifetime [2, 3]. Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) not only significantly increases the risk of pain, disability and death but also imposes a heavy economic burden on the healthcare system, with the associated medical costs accounting for 20% to 40% of the total diabetes healthcare expenditure [4, 5].

The main pathological basis of prolonged DFUs includes peripheral neuropathy, ischemia, and infection, of which ischemia is the key factor leading to tissue necrosis and amputation [6, 7]. Although recent advances in vascular interventional techniques and means of revascularization have enabled many patients with severe lower limb ischemia to avoid immediate amputation, the problem of wound closure after revascularization remains prominent [8, 9]. Studies have shown that even if revascularization is successfully achieved, approximately 18% of patients still accept amputation because of the failure of wound healing [10], which suggests that revascularization is not equivalent to the endpoint of tissue repair, and how to achieve lasting wound closure and functional recovery has become a major clinical challenge.

In this context, flap reconstruction surgery has been increasingly used in clinical practice as an important means of repairing complex foot wounds. The flap technique includes local flaps, regional tipped flaps and free tissue flap grafts, which can provide a well-blooded soft tissue cover for the defective area, and is especially suitable for bone and tendon exposure or deep ulcers that have not healed for a long time [11, 12]. Rational flap selection and design can significantly reduce the amputation rate, promote functional recovery, and improve patient quality of life [13, 14].

However, the current evidence on the use of flap reconstruction in diabetic feet still has many controversies and limitations. First, the literature is dominated by single-center, small-sample case series and lacks high-level supporting evidence; second, there is a lack of systematic comparisons of indications, long-term patency rates, and risk of complications among different flap types; in addition, the patient population is highly heterogeneous, and factors such as ethnicity, regional medical care, and ulcer characteristics may influence prognosis, but there is a lack of subgroup analysis based on large-sample data [15, 16]. These uncertainties limit the standardized application and promotion of flap reconstruction techniques in the comprehensive treatment of diabetic foot.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the effects of flap reconstruction surgery on primary outcomes (e.g., total flap loss and amputation rate) and secondary outcomes (e.g., flap failure rate, ulcer recurrence rate, and quality of life) in patients with diabetic foot ulcers through systematic review and meta-analysis and to explore the effects of different flap types and regional variations on therapeutic efficacy through subgroup analysis in an attempt to provide an evidence-based rationale for clinical practice and to optimize therapeutic strategies. The aim is to provide an evidence-based foundation for clinical practice, optimize treatment strategies, and ultimately improve patient prognosis. Although several systematic reviews have examined flap reconstruction for diabetic foot ulcers, they often lack comprehensive regional comparisons, up—to-date evidence, and detailed assessment of differences between flap types. This study aims to address these gaps by providing an updated meta-analysis that includes studies published up to 2025, compares free versus non-free flaps, and examines variations in outcomes across geographic regions.

Literature search strategy

Relevant comparative trials were searched by a computer with no restriction on the year of publication using the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library databases. Randomized controlled trials were accessed that aimed at understanding the prognostic outcomes of diabetic foot after various flap reconstructions, and the access was limited to the period from the establishment of the above databases until June 2025. In performing the literature search, we used different combinations of the following terms or phrases to maximize the scope of the search: “Diabetic Foot”, “Ulcer”, “Lower Extremity”, “Free Tissue Flap”, etc. The search was limited to the full text, and no language or geographical restrictions were imposed on the search.

Inclusion criteria

The study population included patients with foot ulcers due to diabetes mellitus of any age. The exclusion criteria included ulcers not located on the foot (i.e., ankle or above), acute trauma, and ulcers primarily caused by other diseases. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasirandomized studies, cohort studies, case‒control studies, case series studies, and published abstracts were included. Given the complexity of DFU reconstruction and the associated steep learning curve, studies with fewer than 10 cases were excluded, as were animal studies, cadaveric studies, systematic reviews, background articles, and expert opinion articles. Articles not written in English were translated, and any reconstructive procedure using a local, regional (tipped), or free flap was included in the study. Other interventions, such as debridement or vascular interventions that did not involve flap reconstruction, skin grafts, or dermal substitutes, were excluded. Patients requiring bone debridement (or minor amputation) with flap reconstruction to preserve the foot were included in the study; all major amputations (ankle or above) were excluded. Studies comparing flap reconstruction with any other intervention were included, as were single-arm, uncontrolled studies of flap reconstruction for diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). The primary outcome measures were total flap loss, defined as a transplanted or transferred flap suffers complete blood flow interruption, thrombosis, severe infection, or other causes, resulting in 100% tissue necrosis and failure to survive, and eventually sloughs off entirely or requires surgical excision, and limb loss (amputation), defined as failure to preserve the foot, necessitating amputation at the ankle level or higher. Secondary outcomes included ulcer recurrence, flap reconstruction failure (defined as flap surgery failure), adverse events as reported, and health-related quality of life (HRQL).Investigators were contacted for additional information in cases where unclear inclusion criteria existed or when outcome data were not reported separately for patients undergoing flap reconstruction. If data were not available, these studies were excluded.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two reviewers independently reviewed the full text of the relevant articles and then screened the titles and abstracts of the literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, further read the full text of the literature that met the criteria, and again excluded the literature that did not meet the requirements in strict accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria; at the same time, the number of excluded studies and the reasons for exclusion were recorded. They then performed data extraction, including participant data, inclusion and exclusion criteria, intervention details and outcome measures. The results from the two reviewers were compared, and any discrepancies or differences were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer who evaluated the same data. Study authors were contacted for additional information to determine the final literature to be included, if necessary. Information was extracted from the literature screened above, including the first author, year of publication, region and country, study design, number of patients, age of participants, duration of follow-up, method of reconstruction, location of ulcers, and primary and secondary outcome indicators.

Literature quality assessment

This study was independently evaluated by two researchers who strictly followed the methodological standards of evidence-based medicine and separately used an internationally recognized risk of bias assessment tool to evaluate the included literature: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were evaluated by assessing the methodological quality of the included literature according to the Cochrane Assessment Tool. This tool consists of 7 aspects (including randomization, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other risks of bias). The quality of the literature is grade A if each study is at a low risk of bias across all 7 aspects; the quality of the literature is grade B if it is partly at a low risk of bias and partly at an uncertain risk; the quality of the literature is grade B if one or more of the evaluated indicators are at a high risk; and the quality of the literature is grade C if one or more of the evaluation indicators are at a high risk of bias. Publication bias and small sample bias were assessed by visual measures of funnel plots. For observational studies (e.g., cohort studies and case‒control studies), the risk of bias assessment tool for nonrandomized interventional studies (ROBINS-I) scale was used to comprehensively assess seven dimensions, including confounders, selection of study participants, and classification of interventions, and for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the risk of bias assessment tool recommended by the Cochrane Handbook, version 5.1.0, was used to assess the risk of bias around randomization, sequence generation, allocation concealment, and blinding. The systematic evaluation was carried out in six core areas, including sequence generation, allocation hiding, blind implementation, data integrity and selective reporting. A double-blind cross-validation mechanism was implemented in the evaluation process, and after the researchers independently completed the initial evaluation, differences were resolved through structured discussions, and a third methodology expert was introduced for arbitration when necessary.

Statistical analysis

It is important to note that many of the included studies were single arm cohorts, which inherently lack a concurrent comparison group. For these studies, the derived HRs represent a comparison against an assumed baseline risk (e.g., expected total flap loss in a comparable diabetic population without flap surgery), rather than a direct within-study treatment effect. This approach introduces additional uncertainty, and the resulting estimates should be interpreted with caution. Sensitivity analyses comparing fixed and random effects models showed consistent results, suggesting that the derivation method did not materially bias the pooled estimates.

Literature search results

A total of 734 studies, including 224 from PubMed, 96 from the Cochrane Library, and 414 from Embase, were retrieved in our initial search, and we identified 420 records after removing duplicates and grey papers. Subject irrelevance was the reason for excluding 354 articles on the basis of screening of titles and abstracts, and the full texts of 66 articles were subsequently reviewed. A total of 45 [17–61] studies that met the inclusion criteria were eligible for data extraction and quantitative analysis. The specific process is shown in Fig. 1.

Results of the characteristics of the included studies

A total of 45 [17–61] clinical studies on reconstructive foot ulcer surgery (1998—2025) were included in this study, and their basic characteristics are summarized as follows: the sample sizes varied significantly, with the number of patients in a single cohort ranging from 10 (Pallua 2000, Moucharafieh 2003) to 386 (Chiu 2011 control group), and they included 4 comparative studies (1 RCT, 3 comparative cohorts) and 41 noncomparative cohort studies. Patient age spanned a wide range, with a median mean age of 60 years (range 34–90 years) for studies for which data could be extracted, and approximately 35.6% (16) did not report age in full (labeled “CD” or “NR”). The location of the ulcers was uneven: the forefoot (22), heel (18), and plantar/dorsal foot (15) were the main areas of involvement, while the location was not specified in 11 studies. Reconstruction techniques were dominated by free flaps (34, 75.6%), with the anterolateral thigh flap (ALT) being the most commonly used procedure (9) and local/regional flaps (e.g., medial plantar artery flap, V–Y advancement flap) accounting for 11; the technique of the perforator flap has increased significantly in recent years (up to 38% of cases after 2016). The follow-up time spanned a great deal of time (1–150 months), with a median time of approximately 24 months, but 48.9% (22 items) had missing follow-up data. The geographic distribution was concentrated in East Asia (21 items, including China, Korea, Taiwan, and Japan), North America (12 items), and Europe (10 items).Only 8.9% (4 items) reported the specific prevalence of PAD (labeled “YES”), ranging from 5.0% to 50.0%.None of the 45 studies (labeled “NO”)reported the prevalence of CLTI (according to the WIII classification or modern definition). The remaining 41 studies completely lacked quantitative data on PAD or CLTI(labeled “NO”) (Table 1).

Results of the literature quality assessment

Only one randomized controlled trial (Li 2011) was included in this study, and according to the evaluation criteria, the study had a low risk of bias, and the quality of the literature was evaluated as a grade A evaluation result, with no unclear or high risk of bias. The results of the quality evaluation of the ROBINS-I scale for nonrandomized controlled trials revealed that among the 44 included studies, confounding bias was manifested as 10 low-risk, 28 medium-risk, and 6 high-risk (e.g., Oishi 1993, Sunar 2004). The selection bias of the study subjects was predominantly low risk (35 items), but there were 5 medium risk (e.g., Karp 1994, Kim 2007 et al.) and 4 high risk (e.g., Drazan 2001, Altindas 2013 et al.). Bias in intervention categorization and bias in deviation from established interventions were extremely well controlled in all studies, with all 44 being low risk. Bias in missing data was overwhelmingly low risk (38), with only 6 being medium risk (e.g., Karp 1994, Yang 2018 et al.). Bias for outcome measures was predominantly low risk (36 items), and 8 were intermediate risk (e.g., Colen 1988, Chen 2005 et al.). Bias for selective reporting of outcomes was 28 low risk, 10 intermediate risk (e.g., Kaji 2002, Lee 2014 et al.), and 6 high risk (e.g., Oishi 1993, Kallio 2015 et al.). The overall risk of bias distribution was as follows: 12 studies were at “low” risk (e.g., Chiu 2011, Pallua 2000, Li 2022), with high reliability of the results; 26 were at “medium” risk (more than half of them, e.g., Gentile 1998, Cohen 1999), with high reliability of the results, which need to be interpreted with caution; and 6 “high" risks (e.g., Oishi 1993, Drazan 2001, Sung 2018, etc.), whose conclusions need to be treated with a high degree of caution (Table 2).

Meta-analysis results

After reviewing these 45 publications, we extracted relevant data for comparison. Meta-analysis was conducted using total flap loss and limb loss rates as the primary outcomes and flap necrosis rates as the secondary outcome. Subgroup analysis was employed to identify potential determinants of the results. On the basis of the study characteristics, two subgroups were identified: the flap reconstruction technique subgroup and the patient region subgroup. Given the limited prevalence of local flap and perforator flap techniques, we categorized studies on the basis of whether free flap techniques predominated: free flap-dominant and non-free flap groups. We also stratified the North American, European, and Asian populations by ethnicity. Owing to the uneven distribution of ulcer locations across the included studies and inconsistent anatomical region classifications without detailed summarization, relevant subgroup analyses were not feasible.

Primary Outcomes

Total flap loss

Eleven included studies are shown in Fig. 2. Analysis revealed low heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 0%, P = 0.99), indicating the suitability of a fixed-effects model. The pooled overall hazard ratio (HR) was 5.83 (95% CI [3.65, 9.31]), indicating that postoperative total flap loss was higher in patients who underwent flap reconstruction than in the comparison groups (Z = 7.39, P < 0.00001).Among individual studies, all except Karp (1994), Nguyen (2019), and Wheble (2019) reported higher total flap loss risks, with 95% CIs spanning 1 (not statistically significant). Verhelie (2004) (HR = 9.46) and Kallio (2015) (HR = 6.89) reported the highest risks.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the stability of the meta-analysis results, a sensitivity test was conducted in this study by eliminating sources of heterogeneity. To address the heterogeneous differences in the outcome indicators in the included studies, the data were analyzed using the fixed-effects model and the random-effects model, and the robustness of the conclusions was verified by comparing the combined effect of the risk factors and the range of the confidence intervals under the two models. The results revealed the following.

Consistency of effect sizes: The combined effect sizes of the risk factors in the fixed-effects model and the random-effects model were close to each other, and the effect sizes obtained in the fixed-effects model were within the confidence intervals of the random-effects model, which indicated that there were no significant differences between the different models.

Stability assessment: The above results suggest that the conclusions of the meta-analysis are less sensitive to model selection, the data stability is good, and the impact of heterogeneity on the overall conclusions is controllable. In summary, the sensitivity analysis further supported the robustness of this study, and no conclusion bias due to model switching was found, suggesting that the current combined results are highly reliable (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively assessed the recovery of patients with diabetic foot ulcers after they underwent flap reconstruction surgery through a systematic review and meta-analysis. Unlike previous meta-analyses, our study incorporates recent evidence, compares outcomes between free and non-free flaps, and explores geographic variations in results. We included a total of 45 relevant studies that spanned from 1998 to 2025 and covered different types of flap surgeries and clinical data from multiple regions. Although flap surgery contributes to trauma repair, patients have significantly higher rates of postoperative flap failure, limb loss, and total flap loss, suggesting that the procedure is still highly risky in terms of overall prognosis [10].

The observed increase in total flap loss (HR = 5.83) should be interpreted with caution [11]. This elevation is unlikely to reflect a direct causal effect of the surgical procedure itself; rather, it more plausibly represents the underlying disease burden of the patient population. Patients undergoing flap reconstruction for diabetic foot ulcers typically present with advanced peripheral artery disease, neuropathy, and multiple comorbidities, including cardiovascular and renal impairment, all factors independently associated with higher rates of total flap loss. In this context, flap surgery may serve more as a marker of disease severity than as a direct contributor to flap failure. The absence of regional variation in total flap loss (P = 0.67) further supports this interpretation, highlighting that while surgical techniques and perioperative care differ across regions, the systemic burden of diabetes remains a global constant.

Further analysis revealed that patients with free flaps had a significantly greater risk of limb loss than those who received non-free flaps did (e.g., local or regional flaps), suggesting that the type of flap is an important factor affecting surgical outcomes [9]. In addition, there were differences in outcomes between regions, with patients in Europe and North America having a higher risk of limb loss than those in East Asia do, which may be related to differences in basic patient conditions, medical care, or surgical techniques between regions [15]. In terms of secondary outcomes, the risk ratio for failure of free flaps (HR = 10.01) was greater, reflecting the strong dependence of this procedure on the technical requirements and local conditions of patients [12].

Subgroup analysis revealed that postoperative total flap loss was significantly greater among patients from North America, Europe and Asia, but the difference between regions was not significant (P = 0.67) [3]. This implies that a high total flap loss risk is a common global challenge, which may be related to diabetic patients being often associated with multiple comorbidities and poor systemic conditions coupled with the physical burden of the surgery itself [62]. Therefore, careful evaluation and enhanced preoperative preparation and postoperative management are necessary when a flap reconstruction procedure is chosen [63].In contrast, regional differences in limb loss rates were significant (P < 0.001). The risk was much greater in Europe (HR = 22.97) and North America (HR = 17.42) than in East Asia (HR = 5.85) [8]. The reasons for this difference may include the older average age of patients in Europe and the United States, the severity of vascular disease changes, and the fact that ulcers tend to occur at more difficult-to-heal sites, such as the heel or hindfoot [64]; In contrast, East Asia (e.g., South Korea and Taiwan, China) commonly adopts a MDT approach that integrates vascular surgeons, plastic surgeons, endocrinologists, infectious disease specialists, and podiatrists. This model promotes earlier revascularization, optimized glycemic control, aggressive infection management, and structured postoperative off-loading and surveillance, factors that are essential for limb preservation [2]. The MDT approach may also facilitate more timely intervention before ulcers progress to extensive tissue loss, thereby reducing the need for free flaps and improving outcomes even when such flaps are required. Furthermore, East Asia has accumulated substantial expertise in perforator flap techniques, which enable precise, tailored reconstruction with minimal donor-site morbidity and may contribute to lower rates of flap failure and amputation [4].

We also reported that the type of flap technique significantly affected the rate of limb loss. The risk was significantly greater in the free flap group (HR = 17.86) than in the non-free flap group (HR = 1.94) [5]. This is mainly because free flaps are mostly used for more severe and complex trauma (e.g., large defects in the weight-bearing area of the heel), and the risk of amputation is inherently greater in these cases [14]. Moreover, free flap surgery is complex, time-consuming, and technically demanding for surgeons, and once the flap is necrotic, it can easily lead to limb preservation failure [65]. In contrast, localized flap surgery is simpler, the blood supply is more stable, and it is mostly used for small and medium-sized defects under better conditions [66].

In the heterogeneity analysis, the two outcomes of total flap loss and flap failure rates differed slightly between the studies (I2 ≤ 24%), indicating a relatively consistent result [67]. In contrast, there was moderate heterogeneity in the limb loss rate (I2 = 55%), which may have stemmed from differences in design, patient characteristics, follow-up time, and surgical details among the studies [68]. Our subgroup analysis revealed that region and flap type were important factors contributing to this variation.

This study has several limitations. First, only one of the included studies was a randomized controlled trial, while the remaining 44 were observational (primarily single‑arm cohort studies). The inclusion of such a high proportion of non‑comparative evidence substantially limits the certainty of our findings. According to GRADE guidance, evidence from observational studies starts as low certainty and can be further downgraded due to confounding, selection bias, or imprecision. In our analysis, the predominance of single‑arm designs precluded direct risk estimation against a concurrent control group, and the calculated hazard ratios should be interpreted as measures of association rather than causal effects. The findings therefore provide hypothesis‑generating insights rather than definitive conclusions about the comparative effectiveness of flap reconstruction [69]. Second, the length of follow-up varied across studies, and some data reporting was incomplete (e.g., missing information such as age and ulcer location), limiting further in-depth analysis [70]. A key source of clinical heterogeneity not captured in our quantitative synthesis is the anatomical location of the ulcer. Plantar ulcers, particularly those in weight-bearing areas, are biomechanically distinct from dorsal ulcers and may carry a higher risk of recurrence and flap failure. However, the majority of included studies either did not specify ulcer location or used non-standardized descriptions, precluding subgroup analysis. This limitation underscores the need for future research to adopt uniform classification systems (e.g., the University of Texas Wound Classification) and to report outcomes separately for different foot regions.In addition, the limited number of studies on non-free flaps (n = 2 for the limb loss analysis) substantially restricts the statistical power and generalizability of comparisons between free and non-free flaps. Although our subgroup analysis suggested a lower risk of limb loss with non-free flaps (HR = 1.94 vs. 17.86), this finding is based on sparse evidence and should be interpreted with caution. It may reflect not only true differences in risk but also confounding by indication, as non-free flaps are typically employed for smaller and less severe defects. Therefore, the recommendation to prioritize non-free flaps in low- to moderate-risk patients should be considered preliminary and warrants validation in larger, well-designed comparative studies [71].Finally, the observed funnel plot asymmetry suggests the presence of potential publication bias for the limb loss outcome, indicating that studies reporting favorable results (e.g., lower amputation rates) may be disproportionately represented in the literature. Such bias may lead to an underestimation of the true risk of limb loss associated with flap reconstruction, as studies with unfavorable outcomes are less likely to be published or may experience delays in publication. Consequently, the pooled estimate (HR = 11.09) reported in this study may be conservative, and the actual risk could be higher. These findings should be interpreted with caution, and future research should emphasize prospective study registration and comprehensive reporting, irrespective of outcome direction [72].

Furthermore, a major limitation of the current evidence base is the lack of standardized reporting of baseline ischemic severity. Upon re-examination of all 45 included studies, we found that only 4 studies reported the proportion of patients with peripheral artery disease (PAD), with proportions ranging from 5.0% to 50.0%. The remaining 41studies provided no quantifiable PAD data. More importantly, none of the 45 studies reported the proportion of patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) using contemporary classification systems (e.g., the WIfI score). This substantial missingness prevents any meaningful subgroup analysis or meta-regression based on ischemic severity. Consequently, the pooled hazard ratios for limb loss and flap failure (HR = 11.09 and 10.01, respectively) may be confounded by unmeasured variation in baseline arterial disease across studies. Readers should interpret these estimates with caution, as they reflect associations in a heterogeneously reported population rather than adjusted causal effects.

This study has a clear complementary relationship and unique perspective with previously published reviews (including Bhat et al. [PMID: 37500000] [73], Reed et al. [PMID: 38334716] [74], Young Sing et al. [PMID: 40776704] [75], Xiong Lingyun et al. [PMID: 26731718] [76], Abu-Baker et al. [PMID: 41375784] [77], and Tan et al. [PMID: 36870103] [78], and together they provide a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of flap reconstruction for diabetic foot ulcers.

First, there are differences and continuity in the included studies and timeliness. The reviews by Bhat et al. (2023) and Reed et al. (2024) included 67 and 45 studies up to 2022, respectively, providing a broad overview of evidence. The latest review by Tan et al. (2023) focused specifically on diabetic wounds of the lower extremity, distinguished outcomes between free flaps and local flaps, and highlighted the impact of revascularization on the risk of free flap failure (pooled failure rate 18.2%), which provides direct support for the risk factors concerned in this study. Young Sing et al. (2025) further focused on the comparative analysis of different free flaps. While including similar studies (45 studies, 1998–2022), this study particularly integrates the high-risk factors (such as combined revascularization) revealed by Tan et al. (2023) into the background analysis, and shifts the focus to the assessment of "relative risk" and exploration of heterogeneity sources that were not fully emphasized in previous reviews.

Most previous reviews (e.g., PMID: 40,776,704, 37,500,000) mainly reported pooled event rates (e.g., flap survival rate, limb salvage rate), with conclusions focusing on verifying technical feasibility.

The study by Xiong Lingyun et al. [PMID: 26731718] was a meta-analysis of free flap repair for soft tissue defects of various etiologies in the lower extremity, including trauma, tumor, diabetic foot and other indications. It concluded that the procedure was generally safe and reliable, and no significant increase in surgical risk was found in diabetic foot patients, focusing on the application of free flaps in different indications. In contrast, this study focuses on the application of flap reconstruction in diabetic foot patients, including both free and non-free flaps, with emphasis on analyzing the safety and efficacy of different outcome indicators. The two studies differ in analytical focus.

The study by Abu-Baker et al. [PMID: 41375784] only focused on the anterolateral thigh (ALT) free flap for diabetic foot, which was a single-arm meta-analysis with only 6 studies and 162 patients. It concluded that ALT flap had a low failure rate and satisfactory functional recovery, without comparison with other flaps or regional differences.

The core difference of this study lies in methodology and in-depth analysis. Effect size indicator: We used hazard ratio (HR) instead of simple event rate for meta-analysis, which directly quantifies the relative association strength between "receiving flap reconstruction" and adverse outcomes (total flap loss, amputation), providing data of a different dimension for clinical risk assessment. Depth of subgroup analysis: We thoroughly analyzed the effects of flap type and regional differences on primary outcomes. The study found that the risk of limb loss with free flaps (HR = 17.86) was significantly higher than that with non-free flaps (HR = 1.94), and this quantification provides a key basis for surgical selection. More importantly, we systematically revealed significant regional differences in limb loss risk for the first time in a meta-analysis (Europe HR = 22.97, North America HR = 17.42, East Asia HR = 5.85), which strongly suggests that healthcare systems, multidisciplinary collaboration models or baseline patient risks may have a decisive impact on outcomes, beyond the discussion of technical aspects alone.

The differences in methodology and analytical focus directly lead to complementary conclusions. Previous reviews (including Tan et al., 2023) were generally positive, emphasizing that flap reconstruction (especially for suitable patients) is a safe and effective limb salvage method. Based on HR analysis, this study draws a crucial supplementary and cautionary conclusion: in the real-world complex and high-risk population of diabetic foot ulcers, the postoperative total flap loss rate (HR = 5.83), limb loss rate (HR = 11.09) and free flap failure risk (HR = 10.01) are all significantly increased after flap reconstruction. This does not negate the value of flap technology, but advances the discussion from "whether the technology is feasible" to "for whom, under what conditions, and how to weigh risks and benefits". Our results corroborate the findings of Tan et al. regarding "revascularization increases risk", and jointly emphasize that neuroischemic wounds with severe vascular lesions and other complex conditions are extremely difficult to treat and risks cannot be ignored.

In summary, this study is not a simple repetition of previous high-quality systematic reviews. Previous studies (including the latest review by Tan et al.) have established the technical feasibility of flap reconstruction and favorable outcomes under optimized conditions. The unique contribution of this study is: through hazard ratio analysis and in-depth subgroup exploration, it focuses on revealing the significant relative risks associated with the application of this technology in a wide range of high-risk patient groups, and clarifies the distribution of risks among flap types and different regions worldwide. This requires clinical decision-making thinking to shift from "whether surgery is feasible" to "how to select the optimal strategy for individual patients to maximize net benefit". Our conclusions further strengthen the extreme importance of individualized treatment based on risk stratification, enhanced perioperative management and multidisciplinary collaboration, providing key evidence for building a more refined clinical decision-making pathway.

Conclusions

Diabetic foot flap reconstruction surgery provides an important means of trauma repair, but it is associated with considerable risks of postoperative flap failure, limb loss, and total flap loss. Therefore,surgical plans should be carefully formulated in clinical practice with comprehensive consideration of individual patient differences, choice of flap type, and regional medical resources. Future studies should adopt standardized reporting of ulcer anatomical location and other key clinical variables to reduce heterogeneity and enable more meaningful subgroup analyses. High‑quality, multicenter, long‑term follow‑up studies are needed to further validate the efficacy and safety of various flap techniques.
