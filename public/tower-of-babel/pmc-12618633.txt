Prognostic value of the advanced lung cancer inflammation index for 28 day mortality in sepsis associated acute kidney injury
Authors: Mengfei Li, Runbing Xu, Yu Wu, Jiajun Pan, Xinyu Zhang, Miao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is highly heterogeneous in critically ill patients. Reliable tools that integrate multiple indicators to predict outcomes remain limited. The advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), a composite marker reflecting both inflammation and nutritional status, has demonstrated prognostic value in oncology and chronic diseases. However, evidence for its predictive performance and clinical utility in SA-AKI is scarce and requires further validation. This study analyzed 5,565 SA-AKI patients from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database. Patients were divided into quartiles based on the ALI. The primary outcomes were 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and restricted cubic spline models were used to explore associations between the ALI and patient outcomes. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to test consistency and robustness. ROC analysis was used to compare the discriminatory performance of ALI with other indices and assess its incremental value in existing models. Patients in the highest ALI quartile had significantly lower risks of 28-day (HR = 0.72, p < 0.0001) and 90-day (HR = 0.74, p < 0.0001) mortality than did those in the lowest quartile. Survival curves indicated a strong positive association between the ALI and cumulative survival (log-rank p < 0.0001), and RCS analysis suggested a nonlinear trend. Subgroup analyses revealed no substantial heterogeneity, and sensitivity analyses supported the stability of these associations. ROC analysis demonstrated ALI had superior discrimination for 28-day mortality and provided incremental prognostic value when integrated into the SOFA score. ALI is independently associated with 28-day and 90-day mortality in SA-AKI. Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated systemic inflammatory response to infection that frequently involves multiple organs, particularly the kidneys1. Globally, there are approximately 19 million sepsis cases annually, among which nearly two-thirds of these patients develop acute kidney injury (AKI), with approximately 40% progressing to severe AKI. These complications make clinical care more difficult2–5. Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is closely linked to high in-hospital mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and increased long-term risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD)4,6–9. The 28th ADQI consensus group10 defines SA-AKI as AKI diagnosed within seven days of sepsis onset, following Sepsis-3 definitions1 and KDIGO guidelines11.

The pathogenesis of SA-AKI is highly heterogeneous and involves complex interactions among microcirculatory dysfunction, immune-metabolic reprogramming, and mitochondrial impairment10. Recent studies indicate a close interplay between systemic inflammation and nutritional status, forming an “inflammation–nutrition axis” that may profoundly influence both the progression and recovery of SA-AKI12–15. Although increasing attention has been given to this axis, no dedicated models currently exist to predict the incidence and prognosis of SA-AKI effectively. Commonly used clinical scores, such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)16 and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II)17, or AKI-specific risk models, such as the PICARD18 and Demirjian19, focus primarily on global functional impairment while failing to integrate multidimensional parameters, including inflammation and nutrition. This limitation reduces the predictive accuracy and hinders broader clinical applicability.

The advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), which is originally developed as a composite prognostic marker in oncology, is calculated via body mass index (BMI), serum ALB, and the neutrophil‒lymphocyte ratio (NLR)20,21. As the ALI reflects both the inflammatory status and nutritional reserve, it has been increasingly applied to risk stratification in various nonmalignant conditions, including cardiovascular, respiratory, and metabolic diseases22–26. Recent research has also suggested its potential utility in critical care, with associations between ALI and clinical outcomes reported in patients with sepsis, heart failure, and stroke27–31. However, the prognostic significance of the ALI in specific high-risk populations of SA-AKI patients, particularly in terms of its ability to predict short-term mortality, remains inadequately studied.

This study aimed to explore the association between ALI and all-cause mortality in patients with SA-AKI from the perspective of the inflammation–nutrition axis and examine its potential value as a composite indicator reflecting inflammation and nutritional status, in order to provide more information for prognostic evaluation in this population.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study adopted a retrospective cohort design. Data were sourced from the publicly available MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1). This database contains detailed clinical information on critically ill patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in the United States between 2008 and 2022. The data include demographic information, real-time physiological measurements, laboratory test results, medication records, ICD codes, and clinical procedures32. All patient identifiers in the database were deidentified. Therefore, this study was exempt from formal ethical review and did not require informed consent. All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki and the MIMIC-IV data use agreement. The first author, Mengfei Li, completed the CITI training program and obtained formal authorization to extract data (Record ID: 69379007).

The study focused on patients with SA-AKI, identified according to the criteria recommended by the 28th ADQI Consensus10. Sepsis is diagnosed on the basis of the Sepsis-3 criteria1 and is characterized by suspected or confirmed infection along with a SOFA score increase of 2 points or more. AKI is defined by the KDIGO criteria11, which are based on changes in serum creatinine (SCr) levels and urine output. Baseline SCr was defined as the lowest measurement within the 7 days preceding ICU admission. If no prior measurement was available, the first value obtained after ICU admission was used.

To minimize observational bias, the following exclusion criteria were applied: first, patients with ICU stays shorter than 48 h were excluded to ensure adequate observation of SA-AKI progression and to reduce selection bias; second, repeated ICU admissions were limited to the first admission record; third, patients lacking key baseline indicators within 24 h of ICU admission—such as BMI, serum albumin, neutrophil count, or lymphocyte count—were excluded; and finally, patients with extreme or abnormal ALI values were removed. In accordance with these criteria, a total of 5,565 patients meeting the criteria for SA-AKI were included in the final cohort and categorized into quartiles on the basis of ALI values. Acquisition of data

Data extraction and integration were performed via structured query language (SQL). The collected variables included demographic information (age, sex, race, and BMI). Vital signs were recorded at ICU admission, such as HR, DBP, SBP, RR, body temperature, and SpO₂. Several disease severity scores were also collected, including the SOFA, APS III, SAPS II, OASIS, GCS, and CCI scores. Laboratory parameters, comorbidities, and clinical outcomes were also collected. Specifically, the use of key interventions within the first 2 days of ICU admission—including mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy (RRT), vasoactive drugs, diuretics, and glucocorticoids—was documented. The detailed medication types were provided in Supplementary Table S1. All laboratory parameters were obtained from the initial assessment after ICU admission.

The admission ALI is calculated via the following formula: ALI = BMI × Alb/NLR, where NLR denotes the ratio of the absolute neutrophil count to the absolute lymphocyte count, albumin (Alb) is measured in g/dL, and BMI is calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m)²21. To reduce bias from missing data, variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded. For variables with less than 20% missing values, multiple imputation was performed via the “mice” package in R, which is based on a random forest algorithm33, to improve data completeness and statistical efficiency. The missing data for each variable were summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Clinical outcomes

The 28-day all-cause mortality rate was the primary outcome, whereas the 90-day all-cause mortality rate was the secondary outcome. For patients discharged from the hospital, survival data were verified by linking them with the U.S. Social Security Administration’s death master file.

Statistical analysis

The ALI was divided into quartiles for clinical interpretation. Categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages). Group comparisons were performed with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Skewed continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared via the Mann–Whitney U test.

Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate the cumulative incidence of mortality. Differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. To systematically evaluate the associations between the ALI and clinical outcomes, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed with the ALI modeled as both continuous and categorical variables. Covariate selection was guided by the Boruta machine learning algorithm34, a random forest–based feature selection method that provides an objective ranking of predictor importance and minimizes subjective bias (Fig. 2). The final covariates were determined by integrating the results from univariate analyses, variable importance rankings, expert clinical judgments, and prior literature. Three nested models were constructed: Model 1 included ALI alone; Model 2 adjusted for demographic variables (age, sex, race); and Model 3 further incorporated SOFA score, CCI score, comorbidities (cancer, liver disease, diabetes, CKD, CHD), laboratory variables (SCr, BUN, PLT, potassium, bicarbonate, lactate, TB, ALP, PT, PTT), and treatment interventions (vasoactive drugs, glucocorticoids, diuretics, mechanical ventilation, and RRT) to comprehensively account for potential confounding factors.

Restricted cubic spline analysis was applied to explore potential nonlinear relationships between ALI and mortality risk. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the consistency of the prognostic value of the ALI across clinical dimensions, including age, sex, SOFA score, CKD, CHF, liver disease, diabetes, cancer, AKI stage, and treatment strategies. Several sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings, including the exclusion of patients who died within 72 h, patients with CHF/diabetes, patients with cancer, and those receiving RRT.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the discriminative ability of ALI and other indices for 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with SA-AKI, with comparisons made against PNI, SII, AISI, albumin, lymphocyte count, and BMI. To further assess its incremental prognostic value, ALI was incorporated into the SOFA score, and model performance was compared using the C-statistic, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Analyses were performed in R 4.5.0, with two-tailed p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 5,565 patients with SA-AKI were included in this study. The majority of the cohort was White (60.66%) and male (59.46%), with a mean age of 64.73 ± 15.85 years. The overall 28-day all-cause mortality was 29.99%. Patients were divided into four quartiles according to the ALI: Q1 (0.04–4.46), Q2 (4.46–8.48), Q3 (8.48–15.07), and Q4 (15.07–37.47). Analysis revealed that patients in higher ALI quartiles were generally younger and presented a higher incidence of hypertension, diabetes, and stroke, but a lower incidence of cancer and CKD. As the ALI increased, several physiological and organ function indicators improved, including higher BMI, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, lymphocyte counts, hemoglobin levels, and electrolyte balance. In contrast, the levels of inflammatory and stress-related markers, such as the NLR, WBC count, and lactate level, were lower. Disease severity scores (SOFA score and APS-III score) were also lower in the higher ALI groups.

In terms of treatment strategies, patients with a greater ALI were less likely to receive vasoactive drugs, glucocorticoids, or RRT during the early ICU period (p < 0.01), but they received diuretics more frequently (p < 0.05). These findings suggested that patients with a greater ALI presented with greater clinical stability and less reliance on intensive interventions. Crucially, both the 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality decreased significantly across increasing ALI quartiles (p < 0.01), indicating a strong association between increased ALI and improved survival outcomes.

Further comparisons of survivors and non-survivors (Supplementary Table S3) revealed that non-survivors were older and presented higher rates of cardiovascular disease, liver disease, and cancer. These patients are also associated with more severe kidney injury, higher baseline severity scores, and more frequent use of vasoactive drugs, mechanical ventilation, and RRT. The laboratory data revealed increased inflammatory markers, worsened hepatic and renal dysfunction, and increased coagulation abnormalities. In contrast, oxygenation, acid–base balance, and nutritional markers such as albumin decreased significantly. Moreover, non-survivors had a more extended ICU stay (7.13 vs. 6.57 days, p < 0.05) and a markedly lower median ALI value (7.01 vs. 8.95, p < 0.001). These findings further support the clinical utility of the ALI as a prognostic marker for risk stratification in patients with SA-AKI.

Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier curves revealed that patients in the lowest ALI quartile (Q1) had significantly poorer overall survival than those in higher quartiles (Q2–Q4). Both the 28-day and 90-day cumulative survival rates were markedly reduced in Q1, and this survival disadvantage persisted over time (Fig. 3). With increasing ALI, the survival probability improved in a stepwise manner, indicating an apparent effect of the ALI on the short-term and medium-term prognosis of SA-AKI patients. Log-rank tests confirmed significant between-group differences (p < 0.0001). These findings consistently support the ALI as a reliable marker for survival risk stratification in SA-AKI patients.

Cox proportional hazards model of the ALI and mortality

Univariate Cox regression identified several variables associated with 28-day all-cause mortality, including comorbidities, severity scores, and early treatment strategies (Table 2). To further evaluate the independent prognostic value of the ALI, a multivariate Cox model has been constructed.

Restricted cubic spline analysis

To explore potential nonlinearity, Cox models were fitted with restricted cubic splines. The results showed significant nonlinear associations between ALI and both 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality (28-day: P overall < 0.001, P non-linear = 0.0078; 90-day: P overall < 0.001, P non-linear = 0.0011) (Fig. 4). A two-piecewise regression model further identified inflection points: ALI = 10.52 for 28-day mortality and ALI = 9.77 for 90-day mortality. Overall, when ALI was below these thresholds, higher ALI was markedly associated with lower mortality risk, whereas above the thresholds, its protective effect was weakened and became nonsignificant. These findings suggest that the prognostic value of ALI is mainly observed at lower levels, with limited predictive ability in patients with high levels.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to test the consistency of the ALI–mortality relationship. There have been no significant interactions were detected across age, sex, comorbidities (CKD, liver disease, diabetes, cancer, CHF), AKI stages, or treatment strategies (p for interaction > 0.05) (Figs. 5 and 6). These findings indicate that the prognostic value of the ALI is stable across different patient populations, supporting its broad applicability in clinical practice.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the negative association between the ALI and 28-day or 90-day mortality remained significant after patients who died within 72 h, who received RRT, who had CHF/diabetes, or who had cancer were excluded (Supplementary Table S4-S5). The consistency of these results suggests that extreme cases or specific subgroups did not account for the association. This stability further supported the reliability of the main conclusions.

Discriminative ability and incremental prognostic value of ALI

When the assessment has been implemented independently, ALI showed limited discriminative ability for 28-day all-cause mortality (AUC = 0.561, 95% CI 0.544–0.578). However, further ROC analyses demonstrated that the AUC of ALI exceeded those of PNI (0.427), SII (0.526), AISI (0.529), BMI (0.471), and single indicators such as albumin (0.428), lymphocyte count (0.461), neutrophil count (0.535), and monocyte count (0.521) (Table 4). These results suggest that although ALI is not a strong independent predictor, it provides superior integrative prognostic performance compared with other nutritional or inflammatory indices.

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic evaluation of ALI as a prognostic marker in SA-AKI. The findings showed that ALI was independently and inversely associated with 28- and 90-day mortality, and this association remained robust across clinical subgroups and sensitivity analyses. Furthermore, ALI provides its greatest value as a complementary indicator to established severity scores, rather than functioning independently. In particular, low ALI levels identifies high-risk patients and supports early risk stratification. Nevertheless, its application has certain limitations and should be interpreted in conjunction with the clinical context and other assessment instruments.

Prognostic value of the ALI and potential mechanisms

The pathogenesis of SA-AKI is complex and cannot be explained solely by prerenal hypoperfusion. It involves multiple mechanisms, including systemic and renal inflammation, microcirculatory dysfunction, endothelial injury, and mitochondrial impairment10,35–37. In addition, sepsis often coexists with malnutrition, metabolic disturbances, and inflammation-induced immune dysfunction. These conditions exacerbate organ damage and increase mortality risk. In this context, a single biomarker is insufficient to capture the complexity of a disease fully. Consequently, research has shifted toward composite indicators that enable earlier identification of SA-AKI, more precise risk stratification, and individualized prognostic evaluation10.

Several markers related to inflammation and nutrition have been developed for sepsis and SA-AKI. The NLR independently predicts short-term mortality38. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) is associated with malnutrition-related poor outcomes39. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) incorporates platelet and immune cell data and improves the prediction of 28-day mortality40. The aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) also shows promise. It remains closely linked to mortality risk at multiple time points14. However, these indices focus on a single physiological domain. They assess either inflammation or nutrition, but rarely assess both.

The ALI, which integrates body mass index (BMI), serum ALB, and the NLR, offers a more comprehensive evaluation of nutritional status and systemic inflammation. Previous studies have demonstrated its prognostic relevance in various critical illnesses, including cancer, sepsis, heart failure, and intracerebral hemorrhage22–31. In our study, ALI is also independently associated with all-cause mortality in SA-AKI patients. The robustness and generalizability of these findings were supported by consistent results in subgroup and sensitivity analyses. These results provide new evidence to support its clinical applicability.

Serum ALB is not only a classical nutritional marker but also a negative acute-phase protein. It plays a key role in maintaining endothelial integrity, regulating oncotic pressure, and preserving microcirculatory perfusion41. Hypoalbuminemia in sepsis is associated with impaired hepatic synthesis, increased capillary leakage, and uncontrolled systemic inflammation and is strongly associated with multiorgan failure and mortality42–45.

The “obesity paradox” has been frequently reported in sepsis. In patients with sepsis and SA-AKI, being moderately overweight (reflected by higher BMI) may provide survival benefits by offering greater energy reserves and metabolic adaptability46–50. Furthermore, adipose tissue exerts immunomodulatory effects by secreting cytokines such as leptin, which has been found at higher levels in sepsis survivors than in nonsurvivors51,52. Thus, a higher BMI may partially mitigate the adverse effects of acute metabolic derangements, conferring a survival advantage in critically ill patients53,54.

Inflammation and immune dysregulation play central roles in the prognosis of SA-AKI patients. Sepsis progression is often characterized by neutrophilia and lymphopenia, although these changes may not occur simultaneously55,56. The NLR integrates these dynamic shifts, offering a more accurate reflection of the balance between neutrophil activation and lymphocyte suppression. An elevated NLR has been consistently associated with poor outcomes and increased mortality in patients with SA-AKI57.

Clinical implications and utility

This study, conducted in a large cohort of SA-AKI patients, confirms a significant association between ALI and short-term mortality. RCS analysis reveals a nonlinear relationship between ALI and short-term mortality in SA-AKI patients. These findings highlight the potential value of ALI in risk assessment: in patients with low ALI levels, decreases in ALI may reflect the combined effects of malnutrition and systemic inflammation, thereby identifying a high-risk subgroup. In contrast, at high ALI levels, its association with mortality diminishes, suggesting that prognosis is primarily driven by other pathological processes, such as organ dysfunction.

Compared with single nutritional or inflammatory markers and other composite indices (PNI, SII, AISI), ALI demonstrates modestly better discriminative ability for short-term mortality, underscoring the value of multidimensional composite indices in risk assessment. Nevertheless, its independent predictive performance (AUC = 0.561) remains lower than that of established scoring systems, such as SOFA (AUC = 0.614). Although the inclusion of ALI into the SOFA score significantly increases the C-statistic, the absolute increment (from 0.415 to 0.431) is modest. These findings indicate that ALI is more suitable as a complementary tool rather than a replacement for existing scoring systems. This observation aligns with common patterns in risk prediction research: novel markers rarely replace established scores, but they remain valuable when they provide incremental statistical or clinical benefit58,59.

From a clinical utility perspective, a low ALI score can serve as a trigger for initiating a “nutrition–inflammation assessment protocol” rather than as an absolute indication for treatment. For instance, when ALI falls below a defined threshold, a consultation with a registered dietitian may be prompted, more detailed evaluations of protein and caloric intake may be initiated, or further investigations into inflammatory sources beyond the primary diagnosis may be conducted. Although direct evidence demonstrating that such interventions improve outcomes in SA-AKI patients is currently lacking, these findings provide a theoretical basis for future clinical trials designed to validate these strategies. Within this framework, ALI is transformed from a passive prognostic marker into an active tool for identifying patients who may benefit from enhanced supportive interventions, thereby informing individualized patient management.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, due to the retrospective design, this study identifies associations rather than establishing causal relationships. Second, although multiple potential confounders have been adjusted for, residual confounding may still exist. Third, as a composite marker of inflammation and nutrition, ALI may vary dynamically during ICU stay due to fluid resuscitation, infection progression, or nutritional interventions. This study relies solely on baseline measurements at ICU admission, which may miss prognostic information associated with temporal changes. Finally, this study was based on retrospective data from a single medical center (BIDMC), where clinical practices, nutritional strategies, and patient characteristics may differ from those in other settings, limiting the generalizability of the conclusions. Future studies should employ multicenter, prospective cohorts with dynamic follow-up assessments to further validate the prognostic value of ALI and its potential clinical applications.

Conclusions

This study, based on the MIMIC-IV database, confirms that ALI is significantly and independently associated with 28-day and 90-day mortality in patients with SA-AKI. Although its independent predictive ability is relatively limited, ALI provides incremental value to existing scoring systems in risk indication and stratification, particularly in identifying high-risk patients with low ALI. Nevertheless, because of the single-center retrospective design and reliance on baseline measurements, these findings require confirmation in multicenter prospective studies to establish the clinical utility and potential benefits of ALI.
