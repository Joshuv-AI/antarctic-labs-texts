TAMMs: Temporal-Aware Multimodal Model for Satellite Image Change Understanding and Forecasting
Zhongbin Guo, Yuhao Wang, Ping Jian, Xinyue Chen, Wei Peng, Ertai E

Abstract
. Satellite image time-series analysis demands fine-grained spatial-temporal reasoning, which remains a challenge for existing multimodal large language models (MLLMs). In this work, we study the capabilities of MLLMs on a novel task that jointly targets temporal change understanding and future scene generation, aiming to assess their potential for modeling complex multimodal dynamics over time. We propose TAMMs , a Temporal-Aware Multimodal Model for satellite image change understanding and forecasting, which enhances frozen MLLMs with lightweight temporal modules for structured sequence encoding and contextual prompting. To guide future image generation, TAMMs introduces a Semantic-Fused Control Injection (SFCI) mechanism that adaptively combines high-level semantic reasoning and structural priors within an enhanced ControlNet. This dual-path conditioning enables temporally consistent and semantically grounded image synthesis. Experiments demonstrate that TAMMs outperforms strong MLLM baselines in both temporal change understanding and future image forecasting tasks, highlighting how carefully designed temporal reasoning and semantic fusion can unlock the full potential of MLLMs for spatio-temporal understanding.

1.
Introduction

Satellite image time-series analysis is fundamental to perceiving environmental transformations, monitoring urban evolution, and managing disaster impacts
(
Fu et al. 2024
;
Miller et al. 2024
;
Lara-Alvarez et al. 2024
)
. The advent of powerful multimodal large language models (MLLMs)
(
Wu et al. 2024a
;
Bai et al. 2025
;
OpenAI 2025
;
Anthropic 2025
)
offers unprecedented potential for interpreting complex satellite scenes
(
Luo et al. 2024
;
Muhtar et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
. However, unleashing their full capabilities in dynamic remote sensing applications requires addressing two core challenges related to temporal change perception and generation.
(1) Deficient temporal perception
: Current MLLMs excel in static image-text tasks such as captioning
(
Bazi et al. 2024
;
Silva et al. 2024
)
and detection
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Yu and Ji 2022
;
Tang et al. 2025
)
, yet lack inherent mechanisms to model evolving sequences or comprehend long-range change narratives embedded in satellite image time-series.
(2) Limited change-aware generation
: Though state-of-the-art diffusion models generate high-quality images from prompts
(
Sastry et al. 2024
;
Khanna et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
, they typically fail to account for spatio-temporal dependencies or extrapolate change dynamics grounded in historical observations.

While time-series modeling has benefited from large-scale models in other domains
(
Wen et al. 2023
;
Jin et al. 2024b
;
Jin et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2024
)
, adapting MLLMs to satellite imagery presents unique challenges. Satellite image sequences are sparse, long-term, and often capture abstract change processes that evolve gradually, unlike the short-term motion patterns in conventional video data. Video-centric MLLMs
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
are limited in their capacity to model temporally sparse visual events. Numerical adaptations like TimeLLM
(
Jin et al. 2024a
)
lack the visual-semantic fusion needed for imagery-based reasoning.

Existing remote sensing image models partially address these issues: SITSCC
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
introduces change captioning but is limited to short input sequences and lacks the abstraction capacity to describe cause-effect relations behind visual shifts. DiffusionSat
(
Khanna et al. 2024
)
produces realistic images from metadata but fails to explicitly model semantic drivers of change or ensure consistency over time. Moreover, these methods often rely on handcrafted temporal designs or task-specific heuristics, limiting generalizability to diverse geographic patterns and long-range evolution. These limitations highlight the need for a unified approach capable of perceiving long-term dynamics and generating semantically faithful future states.

To bridge these gaps and stimulate temporal reasoning within MLLMs, we propose
TAMMs
, a Temporal-Aware Multimodal Model for satellite image change understanding and forecasting. TAMMs is a unified framework that equips frozen MLLMs with the ability to interpret change over time through integrated image-text-time fusion. To address deficient temporal modeling, we introduce two complementary modules: a
Lightweight Temporal Adaptation Module (LTAM)
that infuses timestamp awareness through learnable temporal token injection, and
Contextual Temporal Prompting (CTP)
, which implicitly encodes historical progression via adaptive prompt shaping. These modules collaboratively activate the latent temporal capacity of frozen MLLMs, enabling them to perceive evolving patterns and track semantic change over time.

TAMMs connects this enriched temporal understanding to change-aware image generation through dual-path conditioning applied to a frozen diffusion U-Net. The MLLM first generates textual change descriptions, providing high-level semantic control via a CLIP-based text encoder. Simultaneously, we introduce a novel
Enhanced Control Module
that fuses hierarchical structural representations from frozen 3D Control Blocks with temporally grounded semantic features from the MLLM. These features are adaptively integrated via
trainable semantic processors
,
gated fusion units
, and optional
Temporal Transformers
to form spatio-temporal control signals. Injected via a
Semantic-Fused Control Injection (SFCI)
strategy, these signals guide the diffusion decoder toward generating plausible, temporally consistent future scenes that faithfully reflect the anticipated changes.

By selectively training only adapter layers (e.g., LTAM, CTP, semantic processors), TAMMs achieves parameter efficiency while endowing foundation models with the capacity to perceive, reason about, and generate complex temporal dynamics in remote sensing. Our framework is theoretically grounded in decoupling temporal encoding and generative control, which jointly activate the MLLM’s latent spatio-temporal abstraction abilities. Through prompt-driven alignment and modular control injection, TAMMs effectively bridges perception and generation under sparse temporal supervision and visual-semantic gaps.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose
TAMMs
, a unified framework that empowers frozen MLLMs with temporal reasoning abilities for satellite image change understanding and future scene generation.

•

We introduce
LTAM
and
CTP
to stimulate perception of long-range temporal dynamics and improve change narrative comprehension in MLLMs.

•

We design a novel
SFCI
mechanism, combining Enhanced ControlNet and gated semantic fusion, to enable fine-grained change-aware generation conditioned on both structural context and MLLM-derived semantics.

•

Experiments show TAMMs consistently outperforms baselines in both change description and temporally coherent image generation, validating the impact of our targeted enhancements to MLLM temporal reasoning and control.

Related Works

2.1.
Time-Series Analysis

Analyzing temporal dynamics is crucial in many fields. Traditional time-series methods like ARIMA
(
Box et al. 2015
)
and LSTMs
(
Graves and Graves 2012
)
, along with modern Transformers
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
, primarily handle structured numerical sequences. While adaptations like Time-LLM
(
Jin et al. 2024a
)
show promise in leveraging LLMs for forecasting, these approaches struggle with the complex spatio-temporal dependencies, multimodality, and irregular sampling inherent in satellite image time-series
(
Khanna et al. 2024
)
.

2.2.
Multimodal Large Language Models

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) excel at integrating vision and language
(
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Wu et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
, with some variants like Qwen2.5-VL
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
incorporating basic video timestamp understanding. However, these are typically optimized for densely sampled videos with minimal inter-frame variation, making their temporal reasoning mechanisms less effective for the long intervals and significant environmental changes characteristic of satellite image data
(
Khanna et al. 2024
)
. Existing satellite-specific methods, such as SITSCC
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
and Time-VLM
(
Zhong et al. 2025
)
for change captioning, often use handcrafted temporal encoding and lack generative capabilities or deep MLLM reasoning.

2.3.
Diffusion Models and Controllable Generation

In generative modeling, Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) like Stable Diffusion
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
achieve high-fidelity synthesis, and ControlNet
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
enables spatial control. DiffusionSat
(
Khanna et al. 2024
)
adapted LDMs for satellite imagery using metadata conditioning. However, these generative approaches generally lack mechanisms for deep temporal reasoning or conditioning on derived semantic understanding of
changes
occurring within a sequence. They typically cannot extrapolate observed trends or generate futures conditioned on a nuanced understanding of historical evolution. Our work, TAMMs, addresses these limitations by proposing a unified framework that enhances MLLMs with specialized temporal perception modules (LTAM, CTP) and introduces a novel semantically-informed control mechanism (SFCI via an Enhanced ControlNet) for diffusion models, enabling integrated change understanding and temporally consistent future prediction specific to satellite image time-series.

3.
Methodology

This section details the core architectural components and mechanisms of TAMMs’ framework, previously overviewed in Figure
1
.

3.1.
Lightweight Temporal Adaptation Module (LTAM) via Temporal Reprogramming

To imbue frozen MLLMs with temporal awareness without fine-tuning their core parameters, we introduce a Lightweight Temporal Adaptation Module (LTAM) based on the principle of temporal reprogramming. Instead of modifying the MLLM architecture, LTAM subtly alters the input feature space to make temporal dynamics explicitly recognizable by the pre-trained model.

Learnable Temporal Token Injection.

Central to LTAM is the introduction of a novel, learnable embedding representing temporal passage, denoted as the
[TIME_DIFF]
token. We hypothesize that a dedicated token, whose embedding is optimized during training, can effectively capture the complex, non-linear relationships between varying time intervals (
\Delta t_{i}
) in satellite image sequences. The embedding for a specific interval,
\mathbf{t}_{i}
, is computed by adding a time-difference-aware offset, learned via a small Multi-Layer Perceptron (
\text{MLP}_{\text{time}}
), to the base
[TIME_DIFF]
token embedding
\mathbf{e}_{\text{time}}
:

(1)

\mathbf{t}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{\text{[TIME\_DIFF]}}+\text{MLP}_{\text{time}}(\phi(\Delta t_{i}))

where
\phi(\cdot)
is an optional non-linear transformation or encoding of the raw time difference
\Delta t_{i}
(e.g., sinusoidal positional encoding applied to
\Delta t_{i}
, or simply the identity). This
\mathbf{t}_{i}
represents a temporally contextualized marker.

LTAM then performs temporal feature injection by strategically inserting these learned temporal tokens
\mathbf{t}_{i}
into the sequence of multimodal features
\mathbf{E}_{\text{vis-text}}
(derived from image patches
\mathbf{V}_{i}
and associated text). The injection occurs immediately preceding the features corresponding to each image
i
(except the first), effectively reprogramming the input sequence:

(2)

\mathbf{E}_{\text{fusion}}=[\dots,\text{Text}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{V}_{i},\text{Text}_{i+1},\mathbf{t}_{i+1},\mathbf{V}_{i+1},\dots]

This precisely positioned temporal signal allows the frozen MLLM’s attention mechanisms to directly associate subsequent visual information (
\mathbf{V}_{i}
) with the preceding time interval represented by
\mathbf{t}_{i}
. The parameters of
\mathbf{e}_{\text{[TIME\_DIFF]}}
and
\text{MLP}_{\text{time}}
constitute the entirety of LTAM’s trainable components, ensuring minimal overhead.

3.2.
Contextual Temporal Prompting (CTP) via Guided Language Generation

To enhance the model’s focus on relevant changes and provide high-level semantic guidance, we employ Contextual Temporal Prompting (CTP). Unlike methods that inject separate historical prompts, CTP leverages the inherent generative capabilities of the MLLM, guided by a structured input prompt.

Specifically, the input sequence provided to the MLLM is carefully constructed. It includes not only the sequence of satellite images (represented as <image> placeholders) but also a tailored textual instruction incorporating historical context and the task requirement. The prompt structure resembles:

<image>...<image> Scene: [Scene Description]. Describe specific changes between these time-series remote sensing images in a single paragraph. Focus on concrete changes to structures, landscape, or development with precise location details.

This instructional prompt acts as the CTP mechanism. It explicitly directs the frozen MLLM’s language model to perform temporal reasoning on the provided visual sequence (whose features are enhanced by LTAM’s injected
[TIME_DIFF]
tokens). The "[Scene Description]" part provides overall context, while the explicit request to "Describe specific changes…" focuses the MLLM’s attention on temporal differences.

The MLLM processes the combined visual features (interleaved with temporal tokens from LTAM) and the instructional prompt to generate the change description text. This process implicitly performs temporal reasoning and contextual grounding, as the language model must correlate features across different <image> segments, guided by the temporal markers and the explicit prompt instructions. The generated text thus encapsulates the model’s understanding of the temporal evolution, serving as a crucial output for downstream tasks like future prediction conditioning. Intermediate features extracted during this prompted generation process (
\mathbf{M}_{t}
) capture the fused spatio-temporal context informed by both LTAM and CTP.

Training Objective.

To effectively train the model, particularly the lightweight LTAM modules (
\mathbf{e}_{\text{[TIME\_DIFF]}}
and
\text{MLP}_{\text{time}}
), we formulate a composite loss function. The primary objective is to ensure accurate text generation aligned with the ground truth change descriptions, which is achieved using a standard text cross-entropy loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}
. Critically, to explicitly enforce temporal consistency and encourage meaningful representations for the injected temporal tokens
\mathbf{t}_{i}
, we introduce a dedicated temporal loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{temp}}
. This temporal loss regularizes the learned temporal embeddings, for instance, by minimizing the difference between feature representations associated with adjacent temporal markers (
\mathbf{t}_{i}
,
\mathbf{t}_{i+1}
) within the sequence
\mathbf{E}_{\text{fusion}}
. The final training loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}
is a weighted sum of these two components:

(3)

\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\text{text}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}+\lambda_{\text{temp}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{temp}}

where
\lambda_{\text{text}}
and
\lambda_{\text{temporal}}
are hyperparameters balancing the contribution of text generation accuracy and temporal feature regularization. Minimizing this combined loss guides the optimization process to learn effective temporal representations via LTAM while maintaining high-quality text generation as guided by CTP.

3.3.
Temporal-Conditioned Diffusion Generation via Enhanced Control Module

To generate future satellite images (
\mathbf{I}_{t+1}
) conditioned on historical sequences (
\{\mathbf{I}_{t-n},...,\mathbf{I}_{t}\}
) and guided by semantically rich temporal context (
\mathbf{M}_{t}
) derived from LTAM and CTP, we propose a novel diffusion-based generation architecture. Our approach enhances a pre-trained latent diffusion model (LDM) by incorporating an Enhanced Control Module (ECM). Unlike traditional ControlNets that typically process external conditioning signals
(
Sastry et al. 2024
)
(like Canny edges or depth maps), our ECM synergistically integrates multi-scale spatio-temporal structure derived directly from the diffusion U-Net’s intermediate features with high-level semantic guidance from the MLLM.

Architectural Overview.

The core generation process relies on a standard LDM U-Net
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
responsible for denoising a latent variable
z_{t}
at diffusion timestep
\tau
. The key innovation lies in the ECM, which operates in parallel to the U-Net’s encoder and injects refined control signals into the U-Net’s decoder stages. As illustrated in Figure
3
(referencing your main figure), the ECM processes features extracted from multiple resolution levels of the U-Net encoder.

Enhanced Control Module (ECM).

The ECM is designed to capture and fuse both structural evolution and semantic change information. For each feature map
\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(enc)}
extracted from the
l
-th level of the U-Net encoder (including input convolution and subsequent down-sampling blocks), ECM performs the following operations sequence (visualized in the yellow box of Figure
3
):

(1)

Structural Feature Extraction (3D Control Block):
The U-Net encoder features, representing a sequence of latent states over time, are first reshaped into a spatio-temporal tensor
\mathbf{h}_{l,ST}^{(enc)}
. This tensor is then processed by a level-specific 3D Control Block
(
Khanna et al. 2024
)

\text{CtrlBlock}_{3D}^{(l)}
. These blocks typically employ 3D convolutions or equivalent operations designed to capture local spatio-temporal patterns and dynamics within the evolving latent representations.

(4)

\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(ctrl)}=\text{CtrlBlock}_{3D}^{(l)}(\mathbf{h}_{l,ST}^{(enc)})\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C^{\prime}_{l}\times T\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}

These blocks extract features encoding the structural layout and its temporal evolution at resolution
l
.

(2)

Semantic Feature Projection (Semantic Processor):
Concurrently, the temporally-enriched semantic feature vector
\mathbf{M}_{t}
is processed by a level-specific Semantic Processor
\text{SemProc}^{(l)}
, usually an MLP, to project the high-level semantic information into a space compatible with the structural features at this level.

(5)

\mathbf{s}_{l}^{(proj)}=\text{SemProc}^{(l)}(\mathbf{M}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times C^{\prime}_{l}}

This projected semantic feature
\mathbf{s}_{l}^{(proj)}
is then spatially broadcasted to match the dimensions of
\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(ctrl)}
, resulting in
\mathbf{s}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C^{\prime}_{l}\times T\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}
.

(3)

Adaptive Gated Fusion:
To dynamically balance the influence of structural and semantic guidance, the outputs from the previous steps are fused using a learned gating mechanism
\text{Gate}^{(l)}
. The gate value
\mathbf{g}_{l}
, computed from the projected semantic features, determines the weighting between the structural control signal and the semantic contribution.

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{l}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\text{Gate}^{(l)}(\mathbf{s}_{l}^{(proj)}))\in[0,1]^{B\times T\times C^{\prime}_{l}}

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{l}

\displaystyle=\text{expand}(\mathbf{g}_{l})\odot\mathbf{s}_{l}+(1-\text{expand}(\mathbf{g}_{l}))\odot\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(ctrl)}

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function and
\text{expand}(\cdot)
spatially broadcasts the gate values. This fusion yields a feature tensor
\mathbf{f}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C^{\prime}_{l}\times T\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}
that integrates both types of information adaptively.

(4)

Temporal Refinement (Temporal Transformer):
The fused features
\mathbf{f}_{l}
undergo further temporal modeling using a level-specific Temporal Transformer block
\text{TempTrans}^{(l)}
, potentially incorporating self-attention across the time dimension
(
Bertasius et al. 2021
;
Arnab et al. 2021
)
. This step explicitly models longer-range temporal dependencies and refines the temporal consistency of the control signal. A learnable mixing parameter
\alpha_{l}
might control the contribution of the temporal refinement via a residual connection:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{l}^{(temp)}

\displaystyle=\text{TempTrans}^{(l)}(\text{reshape}(\mathbf{f}_{l}))

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{(B\times T)\times C^{\prime}_{l}\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{l}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{l}\cdot\text{reshape}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}_{l}^{(temp)})+(1-\alpha_{l})\cdot\mathbf{f}_{l}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C^{\prime}_{l}\times T\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}

where
\alpha_{l}\in[0,1]
is learned or fixed, and
reshape
operations adapt the tensor for the transformer input/output.

Integration with U-Net Decoder.

The final output
\mathbf{z}_{l}
from the ECM at each level
l
represents a refined, spatio-temporally aware, and semantically guided control signal. Before injection into the U-Net decoder, this signal is typically aggregated along the time dimension, resulting in
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C^{\prime}_{l}\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}
. This aggregated control signal is then added to the corresponding skip connection feature map
\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(skip)}
from the U-Net encoder before it is passed to the
l
-th level of the U-Net decoder.

(10)

\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(dec\_in)}=\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(skip)}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{l}

This mechanism allows the ECM to modulate the generation process at multiple spatial scales, ensuring that the synthesized future image aligns with both the fine-grained structural evolution and the high-level semantic changes described by
\mathbf{M}_{t}
.

Two-Stage Training Strategy.

Training the enhanced generation model involves a carefully designed two-stage procedure to first establish robust structural control and then integrate semantic guidance without disrupting the learned spatial priors. The parameters of the Enhanced Control Module (ECM) are partitioned into structural components
\theta_{struct}
and semantic/temporal components
\theta_{sem-temp}=\{\text{SemProc}^{(l)},\text{Gate}^{(l)},\text{TempTrans}^{(l)},\alpha_{l}\}
.

Stage 1: Structural Control Learning.
In the first stage, we focus on enabling the model to capture spatio-temporal structural consistency based solely on the evolving latent representations within the U-Net. We freeze the pre-trained LDM U-Net and the MLLM (LTAM & CTP modules are inactive). Only the structural parameters
\theta_{struct}
(the 3D Control blocks) are trained. The Semantic Processors, Gates, and Temporal Transformers are bypassed or their outputs zeroed out, meaning the control signal
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{l}
injected into the U-Net decoder is derived purely from the structural path output
\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(ctrl)}
. Training minimizes the standard LDM diffusion loss, updating only
\theta_{struct}
:

(11)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Stage 1}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0},\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),\tau}\left[\left\|\epsilon-\hat{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(stage1)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

where

\hat{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(stage1)}=\epsilon_{\theta}(z_{\tau},\tau,\emptyset,\{\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(enc)}\}_{l=1}^{L};\ \theta_{U\text{-}Net}^{\text{frozen}},\ \theta_{struct}^{\text{trainable}})

This stage teaches the 3D Control Blocks to extract meaningful temporal structure from the U-Net’s internal states.

Stage 2: Semantic and Temporal Refinement.
In the second stage, we introduce the semantic guidance
\mathbf{M}_{t}
from the now-active (but frozen) LTAM & CTP modules and enable the full ECM pathway. We freeze the LDM U-Net and the previously trained structural parameters
\theta_{struct}
. Only the semantic and temporal parameters
\theta_{sem-temp}
(Semantic Processors, Gates, Temporal Transformers, and mixing parameters
\alpha_{l}
) are trained. The model now utilizes the full ECM computation as described (Eqs.
7
,
9
), where the gated fusion adaptively combines the frozen structural features
\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(ctrl)}
with the newly processed semantic features
\mathbf{s}_{l}
. The Temporal Transformers further refine the fused signal. Training again minimizes the standard LDM diffusion loss, but gradients only update
\theta_{sem-temp}
:

(12)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Stage 2}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{M}_{t},\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),\tau}\left[\left\|\epsilon-\hat{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(\text{stage2})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

where

(13)

\hat{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(\text{stage2})}=\epsilon_{\theta}\left(\begin{aligned} &z_{\tau},\ \tau,\ \mathbf{M}_{t},\ \{\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(\text{enc})}\}_{l=1}^{L};\ \\
&\theta_{U\text{-}Net}^{\text{frozen}},\quad\theta_{struct}^{\text{frozen}},\quad\theta_{sem\text{-}temp}^{\text{trainable}}\end{aligned}\right)

This staged approach, particularly the freezing strategy and the adaptive gating mechanism in Stage 2, allows the model to integrate high-level semantic conditioning and sophisticated temporal modeling without catastrophic forgetting of the foundational structural control learned in Stage 1.

Datasets

Our primary training dataset was constructed from the Functional Map of the World (fMoW) dataset
(
Christie et al. 2018
)
. We meticulously curated 37,003 high-quality sequences, ensuring each contained at least four distinct temporal observations of the same location. All images were resized to 512
\times
512 resolution. We employed the Qwen2.5-VL
Bai et al. 2025
model to automatically generate textual annotations: fine-grained scene descriptions for each image, and concise change descriptions (limited to
\approx
50 words) summarizing the evolution within a sequence. For change description tasks, the model uses the first three images as input; for future image prediction, the sequence’s final image serves as the ground truth target. Our independent test set comprises 150 sequences (4 images each, 512
\times
512 resolution) curated from Google Earth, spanning 5-10 years and featuring diverse scenes (e.g., grasslands, residential areas). Crucially, this test set was annotated by human experts who provided change descriptions focused on summarizing key anthropogenic activities observed across the temporal sequence. Further details regarding prompts and data distributions can be found in the Appendix.

4.1.2.
Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate our model’s performance on its two primary outputs: the generated temporal change descriptions and the predicted future satellite images.

For assessing the textual descriptions summarizing temporal changes, we utilize standard natural language generation metrics: BLEU-4
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, METEOR
(
Lavie and Agarwal 2007
)
, ROUGE-L
(
Lin 2004
)
, and CIDEr-D
(
Vedantam et al. 2015
)
. These metrics collectively measure aspects like n-gram precision, word alignment considering synonyms, longest common subsequence overlap, and consensus among reference descriptions.

For the predicted future images, standard image quality metrics including Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR)
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)
(
Wang et al. 2004
)
, and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
are employed to evaluate pixel-level fidelity, structural similarity, and perceptual realism, respectively.

Temporal Consistency Score (TCS):

Conventional evaluation metrics frequently demonstrate inadequacy in capturing the intricate spatial coherence inherent in remote sensing time-series data. Empirical evidence from prior investigations
(
You et al. 2020
;
Jiang et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2024
;
Prakasam 2010
)
has established that abrupt, unjustified spatial displacements or magnitude variations between sequential temporal intervals typically manifest as implausible phenomena. To quantitatively assess temporal plausibility through characterization of spatial change dynamics that are commonly obscured by feature averaging methodologies, we introduce a novel Temporal Consistency Score (TCS). This metric systematically compares binary change masks
CM_{hist}
(derived from
I_{2}\to I_{3}
) and
CM_{pred}
(derived from
I_{3}\to Gen(I_{4})
), generated through the P2V-CD framework
(
Lin et al. 2023
)
, concentrating on two fundamental spatial dimensions:

•

Spatial Proximity Score (SPS):
Quantifies locational concordance utilizing the normalized Euclidean distance (
d_{norm}
) between the centroids of
CM_{hist}
and
CM_{pred}
, formulated as:

(14)

SPS=e^{-d_{norm}/\sigma}

where
\sigma=0.2
serves as the normalization parameter.

•

Area Consistency Score (ACS):
Evaluates the congruence in change magnitude through the relative differential between the areal extents (pixel enumerations) of the masks, expressed as:

(15)

ACS=e^{-\beta\cdot|Area_{pred}-Area_{hist}|/(\max(Area_{pred},Area_{hist})+\epsilon)}

where
\beta=1.0
functions as the scaling coefficient.

The composite score is computed as
TCS=SPS\times ACS
, incorporating specialized handling for scenarios where significant alterations are absent in either or both intervals (assigning unity value for consistent non-change instances, and implementing an area-proportional penalty otherwise). By anchoring the evaluation in the spatial distribution and magnitude of detected transformations, TCS provides a methodologically robust and discriminative measurement of temporal consistency.

Wu et al. 2024a
)

4.3.
Limitations and Future Work

Despite promising results, this work has several limitations warranting future research. First, TAMMs’ predictive accuracy may deteriorate over extended temporal horizons (>2 years) due to error accumulation in sequential forecasting. Second, predicting rare or abrupt events remains challenging, as these typically lack discernible precursors in historical observations. Third, the computational demands of our dual-stream architecture, which leverages large foundation models, presents deployment challenges in resource-constrained environments. Fourth, TAMMs provides deterministic predictions without explicit uncertainty quantification. This is an important consideration for real-world deployment, though our focus was on establishing the foundational framework for unified temporal understanding and forecasting, where uncertainty modeling would add significant complexity to an already novel architecture. However, our comprehensive evaluation across multiple metrics (e.g., BLEU-4, TCS) provides indirect validation of prediction reliability, and the consistent performance gains across all metrics suggest robust model behavior. Fifth, while our paper does not explicitly model atmospheric conditions, the fMoW training dataset contains real-world images with varying visibility and cloud cover. Our SFCI module leverages DiffusionSat’s pretraining, which already accounts for real-world variations including cloud cover, GSD, and geolocation, providing inherent robustness. Our modular design allows adaptation to different atmospheric conditions, though comprehensive evaluation under extreme weather is beyond this work’s scope.

Future work will aim to mitigate these limitations. We plan to explore hierarchical temporal modeling approaches to better capture long-range dependencies and improve multi-year forecasting robustness. Investigating the integration of external data sources, such as climate data or socio-economic indicators, could enhance the prediction of less frequent events. Furthermore, research into model compression and knowledge distillation techniques is warranted to develop more computationally efficient versions of TAMMs, facilitating broader applicability. Incorporating uncertainty estimation through techniques like ensemble methods or Bayesian approaches represents a valuable direction for future enhancement, enabling more reliable deployment in critical applications where prediction confidence is essential.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced TAMMs, a novel framework designed to jointly tackle temporal-aware satellite image understanding and future prediction. By synergistically integrating sequential reasoning capabilities within a diffusion-based generative model, TAMMs effectively captures spatio-temporal dynamics from remote sensing image sequences. Key contributions include LTAM, a lightweight temporal adaptation module enhancing frozen MLLMs with temporal awareness, and CTP, a contextual prompting method leveraging historical context. Critically, our dual-stream conditioning mechanism, utilizing an enhanced 3D ControlNet, injects both sequence structure and MLLM-derived semantic guidance into the diffusion process, enabling the generation of temporally coherent and plausible future satellite imagery.

Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that TAMMs achieves state-of-the-art performance, significantly outperforming existing methods in both describing temporal changes and predicting future satellite scenes. Our findings highlight the efficacy of structured temporal conditioning in generative models for Earth observation time-series and offer a new perspective on adapting multimodal foundation models for specialized remote sensing applications. This work opens promising directions for future research in time-series-aware generative modeling and its application to understanding and forecasting complex environmental changes.

C.2.
Multi-Stage Training Strategy

The training process for the image generation component of TAMMs follows a carefully designed multi-stage strategy to effectively integrate semantic guidance while preserving the structural control learned by the base ControlNet. The MLLM components (LTAM/CTP adapters, LM Head for text generation) are typically trained separately or concurrently based on their respective objectives (e.g., cross-entropy loss for text). The stages below focus specifically on optimizing the diffusion loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau},\tau,\text{cond})\|^{2}_{2}\right]
) for the Enhanced ControlNet Module and ensuring stable integration:

•

Stage 1: Structural Control Pre-training.
In this initial stage, we train only the original 3D Control Block weights within the Enhanced ControlNet module, keeping the U-Net and MLLM frozen. This stage establishes the foundational spatial-temporal structural guidance based on the input image sequence. A constant learning rate of
2e-5
is used with the AdamW optimizer.

•

Stage 2: Initial Semantic Fusion Training.
The pre-trained 3D Control Blocks (Structural Path) are frozen. We now introduce and train the new components: the Semantic Processors and the Gated Fusion modules. These learn to process MLLM semantic features and fuse them with the structural guidance. This stage also uses a constant learning rate (e.g.,
2e-5
or potentially a slightly lower value, specify if different from Stage 1) with AdamW.

•

Stage 3: Fine-tuning Semantic Fusion with Cosine Decay.
Both the Semantic Processors and Gated Fusion modules continue training, potentially unfreezing the 3D Control Blocks slightly if needed (optional fine-tuning). The key difference is the learning rate schedule, which is switched to a Cosine Annealing scheduler, decaying from the initial rate (e.g.,
2e-5
) towards a minimum value over the remaining training steps. This allows for finer convergence and stabilization of the semantic fusion mechanism.

This staged approach ensures that basic structural control is learned first, followed by the gradual and stable integration of semantic guidance from the MLLM, preventing catastrophic forgetting and optimizing the fusion process.
