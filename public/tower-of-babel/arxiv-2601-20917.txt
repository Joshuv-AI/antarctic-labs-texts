FIPS 204-Compatible Threshold ML-DSA via Masked Lagrange Reconstruction


Abstract
We present masked Lagrange reconstruction , a technique that enables threshold ML-DSA (FIPS 204) with arbitrary thresholds T T while producing standard 3.3 KB signatures verifiable by unmodified FIPS 204 implementations. Concurrent approaches have limitations: Bienstock et al. (ePrint 2025/1163) achieve arbitrary T T but require honest-majority and 37–136 rounds; Celi et al. (ePrint 2026/013) achieve dishonest-majority but are limited to T ≤ 6 T\leq 6 . Our technique addresses the barrier that Lagrange coefficients grow as Θ ⁡ ( q ) \Theta(q) for moderate T T , making individual contributions too large for ML-DSA’s rejection sampling. Unlike ECDSA threshold schemes where pairwise masks suffice for correctness, ML-DSA requires solving three additional challenges absent in prior work: (1) rejection sampling on ‖ 𝐳 ‖ ∞ \|\mathbf{z}\|_{\infty} must still pass after masking, (2) the r0-check exposes c ​ 𝐬 2 c\mathbf{s}_{2} enabling key recovery if unprotected, and (3) the resulting Irwin-Hall nonce distribution must preserve EUF-CMA security. We solve all three. We instantiate this technique in three deployment profiles with full security proofs. Profile P1 (TEE-assisted) achieves 3-round signing with a trusted coordinator, with EUF-CMA security under Module-SIS (Theorem 4.2 ). Profile P2 (fully distributed) eliminates hardware trust via MPC in 8 rounds, achieving UC security against malicious adversaries corrupting up to n − 1 n-1 parties (Theorem 6.2 ). Profile P3 (2PC-assisted) uses lightweight 2PC for the r0-check in 3–5 rounds, achieving UC security under a 1-of-2 CP honest assumption (Theorem 6.5 ) with the best empirical performance (249ms). Our scheme requires | S | ≥ T + 1 |S|\geq T+1 signers and achieves success rates of 23–32%, matching single-signer ML-DSA.

1
Introduction

The imminent arrival of cryptographically relevant quantum computers poses an existential threat to currently deployed public-key cryptography. In response, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has completed a multi-year standardization process, culminating in the release of FIPS 204
[
32
]
, which specifies ML-DSA (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm) as the primary post-quantum signature standard. As organizations worldwide begin transitioning to ML-DSA, there is an urgent need for threshold variants that enable distributed signing while maintaining compatibility with the standard.

1.1
Motivation: The Threshold Gap

Threshold signatures allow a group of
N
parties to collectively sign messages such that any subset of
T
or more parties can produce a valid signature, while any coalition of fewer than
T
parties learns nothing about the secret key. This primitive is essential for protecting keys by distributing signing authority across multiple servers, enforcing multi-party authorization in financial systems, and building robust PKI infrastructure through distributed certificate authorities.

For classical ECDSA and Schnorr signatures, efficient threshold protocols have been extensively studied and deployed
[
23
,
9
,
28
,
26
]
. However, the lattice-based structure of ML-DSA presents new challenges. The core difficulty lies in the
Fiat-Shamir with Aborts
paradigm: signatures are only valid when the response vector
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}+c\mathbf{s}_{1}
satisfies
\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\infty}<\gamma_{1}-\beta
, where
\mathbf{y}
is a masking vector,
c
is a sparse challenge, and
\mathbf{s}_{1}
is the secret key. This rejection sampling step complicates the threshold setting.

The State of Affairs.

Current approaches to threshold ML-DSA can be categorized as follows:

1.

Short-coefficient secret sharing

[
17
]
: Uses carefully constructed linear secret sharing schemes (LSSS) where reconstruction coefficients remain small. This approach produces standard-size signatures and has been demonstrated for
T\leq 8
. The Ball-Çakan-Malkin bound
[
3
]
shows that
\{0,1\}
-LSSS for threshold
T
requires share size
\Omega(T\log T)
, which imposes practical limits on scaling this approach.

2.

Noise flooding

[
6
]
: Masks the large Lagrange coefficients with overwhelming statistical noise. This works for arbitrary thresholds but produces signatures approximately
5\times
larger than standard ML-DSA, breaking FIPS 204 compatibility.

3.

Specialized constructions

[
8
]
: Ringtail and similar schemes modify the underlying signature algorithm to be threshold-friendly, achieving good efficiency but producing signatures incompatible with the FIPS 204 standard.

This leaves a gap: for
T\in[9,32]
, there is no known scheme that simultaneously achieves standard signature size, practical success rates, and FIPS 204 compatibility. This range is precisely where many real-world applications fall—organizations typically require 16-of-32 or similar thresholds for robust multi-party control.

1.2
Our Contribution

We present
Masked Threshold ML-DSA
, a threshold signature scheme for ML-DSA that supports arbitrary thresholds
T\leq N
while producing standard-size signatures (
\approx 3.3
KB for ML-DSA-65) identical to single-signer ML-DSA. Our scheme requires a
two-honest requirement
: signing sets must have size
|S|\geq T+1
to ensure at least two honest parties for mask privacy. This addresses the threshold gap for
T\in[9,32]
while remaining practical—configurations like
(3,5)
,
(11,15)
, and
(16,31)
naturally satisfy
N\geq T+1
.

The scheme achieves practical success rates comparable to single-signer ML-DSA (theoretical baseline
\approx 20\%
, observed
23
–
32\%
due to the more concentrated Irwin-Hall nonce distribution) and is fully verifier-compatible with FIPS 204: produced signatures are accepted by unmodified FIPS 204 verifiers. While our signer-side protocol is interactive and induces an Irwin-Hall nonce distribution rather than uniform, this affects only the signing process, not verification.

Prior approaches attempt to ensure each party’s contribution is individually small, but this hits a barrier: Lagrange coefficients
\lambda_{i}\approx 2^{T}
grow exponentially, reaching
\Theta(q)
for
T\geq 23
in ML-DSA. If party
i
naively computes
\lambda_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{i}
where
\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{\infty}=O(\gamma_{1})
, the result exceeds the rejection threshold for any
\lambda_{i}>1
.

Our approach instead ensures that
only the sum needs to be small, not individual contributions
. While pairwise-canceling masks exist for ECDSA
[
9
]
, directly applying them to lattice signatures does not work because: (1) ML-DSA requires rejection sampling on
\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\infty}
, not just correctness; (2) the r0-check leaks
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
which enables key recovery; (3) the Irwin-Hall nonce distribution must preserve EUF-CMA security. Our masked Lagrange reconstruction addresses these challenges:

1.

Each pair of parties
(i,j)
establishes a shared seed
\mathsf{seed}_{i,j}
(e.g., via ML-KEM).

2.

During signing, party
i
computes a mask:

\mathbf{m}_{i}=\sum_{j>i}\mathsf{PRF}(\mathsf{seed}_{i,j},\mathsf{nonce})-\sum_{j<i}\mathsf{PRF}(\mathsf{seed}_{j,i},\mathsf{nonce})

3.

By construction,
\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{m}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
for any signing set
S
—the masks pairwise cancel.

4.

Party
i
sends
\mathbf{U}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i}+\lambda_{i}\cdot c\cdot\mathbf{s}_{1,i}+\mathbf{m}_{i}
instead of the unmasked value.

While each
\mathbf{U}_{i}
may have arbitrary norm in
[0,q-1]
, their sum is:

\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{U}_{i}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}+c\cdot\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{s}_{1,i}+\sum_{i}\mathbf{m}_{i}=\mathbf{y}+c\cdot\mathbf{s}_{1}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{z}

which correctly reconstructs the signature vector
\mathbf{z}
and passes rejection sampling with probability matching single-signer ML-DSA (theoretical
\approx 20\%
, observed
23
–
32\%
for ML-DSA-65). We note that while the aggregated nonce
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}
follows an Irwin-Hall distribution rather than uniform (see Definition
O.5
), this does not affect EUF-CMA security and only introduces a quantifiable statistical difference that we analyze in Section
3
.

Performance.

Our implementation achieves practical signing times: approximately 1 second average for
(3,5)
-threshold and 4 seconds for
(16,31)
-threshold on commodity hardware (including retries due to rejection sampling), using NTT-accelerated polynomial arithmetic. The communication cost is approximately 12.3 KB per party per signing attempt.

Security.

We prove security in the random oracle model. Unforgeability reduces to the Module-SIS problem, matching the security of single-signer ML-DSA, while privacy against coalitions of up to
T-1
corrupted parties reduces to Module-LWE and PRF security.

Deployment Profiles.

We define three deployment profiles with different trust assumptions, all with complete security proofs:

Profile P1 (TEE-Assisted):

A TEE/HSM coordinator performs the r0-check and hint generation inside a trusted enclave, achieving optimal 3-round signing. We prove EUF-CMA security under Module-SIS and privacy under Module-LWE (Theorem
4.2
).

Profile P2 (Fully Distributed):

A fully untrusted coordinator where the r0-check is computed via an MPC subprotocol using SPDZ
[
15
]
and edaBits
[
20
]
. We prove UC security against malicious adversaries corrupting up to
n-1
parties in 8 online rounds (Theorem
6.2
).

Profile P3 (2PC-Assisted):

A practical middle ground where two designated Computation Parties (CPs) jointly evaluate the r0-check via lightweight 2PC. We prove UC security under the assumption that at least one CP is honest (Theorem
6.5
)—weaker than TEE trust, more efficient than full MPC. Achieves the best empirical performance with 3–5 rounds and 249ms signing time.

1.3
Technical Overview

Protocol Overview.

A trusted dealer creates Shamir shares of
\mathsf{sk}=(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})
and establishes pairwise seeds via ML-KEM. The 3-round signing protocol has parties (1) commit to nonces
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in[-\gamma_{1}/|S|,\gamma_{1}/|S|]
, (2) exchange masked commitments to derive challenge
c
, and (3) send masked responses
\mathbf{U}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i}+\lambda_{i}\cdot c\cdot\mathbf{s}_{1,i}+\mathbf{m}_{i}
. The combiner aggregates
\mathbf{z}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{U}_{i}=\mathbf{y}+c\mathbf{s}_{1}
(masks cancel) and outputs
\sigma=(\tilde{c},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{h})
if
\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\infty}<\gamma_{1}-\beta
.

2.1
Notation

We use bold lowercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{s}
) for vectors and bold uppercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{A}
) for matrices. For an integer
q
, we write
\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
for the ring of integers modulo
q
. We define the polynomial ring
R_{q}=\mathbb{Z}_{q}[X]/(X^{n}+1)
where
n
is a power of 2.

For a polynomial
f\in R_{q}
, we write
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}|f_{i}|
for its infinity norm, where coefficients are represented in
(-q/2,q/2]
. For a vector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in R_{q}^{k}
, we define
\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}\left\|v_{i}\right\|_{\infty}
.

We write
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}S
to denote sampling
a
uniformly at random from a set
S
. We write
\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
for a negligible function in the security parameter
\kappa
.

2.2
Lattice Problems

Our security relies on the hardness of the Module-SIS and Module-LWE problems
[
35
,
29
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Module-SIS)
.

The
\mathsf{M\text{-}SIS}_{n,k,\ell,q,\beta}
problem is: given a uniformly random matrix
\mathbf{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}R_{q}^{k\times\ell}
, find a non-zero vector
\mathbf{z}\in R_{q}^{\ell}
such that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}\mod q
and
\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta
.

Definition 2.2

(Module-LWE)
.

The
\mathsf{M\text{-}LWE}_{n,k,\ell,q,\chi}
problem is to distinguish between:

•

(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e})
where
\mathbf{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}R_{q}^{k\times\ell}
,
\mathbf{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\chi^{\ell}
,
\mathbf{e}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\chi^{k}

•

(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{u})
where
\mathbf{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}R_{q}^{k}

where
\chi
is a distribution over
R_{q}
with small coefficients.

For ML-DSA-65 parameters (
n=256
,
q=8380417
,
k=6
,
\ell=5
), both problems are believed to be hard at the 192-bit security level.

2.3
ML-DSA (FIPS 204)

ML-DSA
[
32
]
, based on the CRYSTALS-Dilithium design
[
18
]
, is a lattice-based digital signature scheme using the Fiat-Shamir with Aborts paradigm
[
30
,
34
]
. Security in the quantum random oracle model follows from
[
25
]
. We summarize the key algorithms.

Parameters.

For ML-DSA-65 (NIST Security Level 3):

•

n=256
: polynomial degree

•

q=8380417=2^{23}-2^{13}+1
: modulus

•

k=6
,
\ell=5
: matrix dimensions

•

\eta=4
: secret key coefficient bound

•

\gamma_{1}=2^{19}
: masking range

•

\gamma_{2}=(q-1)/32
: rounding parameter

•

\tau=49
: challenge weight (number of
\pm 1
entries)

•

\beta=\tau\cdot\eta=196
: signature bound

Key Generation.

1.

Sample seed
\rho\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\{0,1\}^{256}
and expand to matrix
\mathbf{A}\in R_{q}^{k\times\ell}

2.

Sample short secrets
\mathbf{s}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\chi_{\eta}^{\ell}
and
\mathbf{s}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\chi_{\eta}^{k}
with coefficients in
[-\eta,\eta]

3.

Compute
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{1}+\mathbf{s}_{2}
and decompose
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d}+\mathbf{t}_{0}

4.

Output
\mathsf{pk}=(\rho,\mathbf{t}_{1})
and
\mathsf{sk}=(\rho,\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\mathbf{t}_{0})

Signing.

Sample nonce
\mathbf{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\{-\gamma_{1}+1,\ldots,\gamma_{1}\}^{n\ell}
, compute
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathsf{HighBits}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y},2\gamma_{2})
, derive challenge
c=H(\mu\|\mathbf{w}_{1})
, and set
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}+c\mathbf{s}_{1}
. If
\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\gamma_{1}-\beta
, restart (rejection sampling). Compute hint
\mathbf{h}
and output
\sigma=(\tilde{c},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{h})
.

Verification.

Expand
c
from
\tilde{c}
, compute
\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathsf{UseHint}(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}-c\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d})
, and verify
\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}<\gamma_{1}-\beta
and
\tilde{c}=H(\mu\|\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\prime})
.

The z-bound check and r0-check together yield
\approx 20
–
25\%
per-attempt success (4–5 expected attempts). This is inherent to Fiat-Shamir with Aborts
[
30
]
, not threshold-specific.

2.4
Shamir Secret Sharing

Shamir’s
(T,N)
-threshold secret sharing
[
36
]
allows a secret
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
to be distributed among
N
parties such that any
T
parties can reconstruct
s
, but any
T-1
parties learn nothing.

Sharing.

To share secret
s
:

1.

Sample random polynomial
f(X)=s+r_{1}X+\cdots+r_{T-1}X^{T-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}[X]

2.

Give share
s_{i}=f(i)
to party
i
for
i\in[N]

Reconstruction.

Given shares from set
S
with
|S|\geq T
:

s=f(0)=\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_{i}\cdot s_{i}\mod q

where the
Lagrange coefficients
are:

\lambda_{i}=\prod_{j\in S,j\neq i}\frac{j}{j-i}\mod q

Remark 2.3

(Lagrange Coefficient Magnitude)
.

Lagrange coefficients
\lambda_{i}=(-1)^{i-1}\binom{T}{i}
grow as
\approx 2^{T}/\sqrt{T}
. For
T\leq 8
, coefficients remain small (
\binom{8}{4}=70
), enabling short-coefficient schemes
[
17
]
. For
T\geq 24
, coefficients exceed
q\approx 2^{23}
, aligning with the Ball-Çakan-Malkin bound
[
3
]
. The obstacle is that
\lambda_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{i}
has norm
O(q\cdot\gamma_{1})\gg\gamma_{1}-\beta
, causing rejection.

2.5
Security Definitions for Threshold Signatures

Definition 2.4

(Threshold Signature Scheme)
.

A
(T,N)
-threshold signature scheme consists of algorithms:

•

\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\kappa},N,T)\to(\mathsf{pk},\{\mathsf{sk}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]})
: Generates public key and secret key shares

•

\mathsf{Sign}(\{\mathsf{sk}_{i}\}_{i\in S},\mu)\to\sigma
: Interactive protocol among parties in
S
(where
|S|\geq T+1
; see Section
4
for the two-honest requirement) to sign message
\mu

•

\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{pk},\mu,\sigma)\to\{0,1\}
: Deterministic verification

Remark 2.5

(
T+1
Signing Requirement)
.

Our protocol requires
|S|\geq T+1
signers (not
T
) to ensure at least two honest parties for mask privacy. This is standard for pairwise-mask schemes
[
9
,
13
]
; see Remark
4.1
.

Definition 2.6

(Unforgeability (EUF-CMA))
.

A threshold signature scheme is EUF-CMA secure if for any PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
corrupting up to
T-1
parties:

\Pr[\mathsf{EUF\text{-}CMA}_{\Pi}^{\mathcal{A}}(\kappa)=1]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)

where in the security game:

1.

(\mathsf{pk},\{\mathsf{sk}_{i}\})\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\kappa},N,T)

2.

\mathcal{A}
receives
\mathsf{pk}
and
\{\mathsf{sk}_{i}\}_{i\in C}
for corrupted set
C
with
|C|<T

3.

\mathcal{A}
can request signatures on messages of its choice (interacting with honest parties)

4.

\mathcal{A}
outputs
(m^{*},\sigma^{*})

5.

\mathcal{A}
wins if
\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{pk},m^{*},\sigma^{*})=1
and
m^{*}
was never queried

Definition 2.7

(Privacy)
.

A threshold signature scheme provides privacy if the view of any coalition
C
with
|C|<T
can be efficiently simulated given only
\mathsf{pk}
and the signatures produced.

2.6
Pseudorandom Functions

Definition 2.8

(PRF Security)
.

A function family
F:\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a secure PRF if for any PPT distinguisher
\mathcal{D}
:

\left|\Pr[\mathcal{D}^{F_{k}(\cdot)}=1]-\Pr[\mathcal{D}^{R(\cdot)}=1]\right|\leq\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)

where
k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\mathcal{K}
and
R:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a truly random function.

In our construction, we instantiate the PRF with SHAKE-256, modeled as a random oracle in the security analysis.

3
Masked Threshold ML-DSA Protocol

We now present our masked threshold ML-DSA protocol in full detail. The protocol consists of a setup phase (key generation) and a signing phase (three-round interactive protocol).

3.1
Overview

The core challenge in threshold ML-DSA is handling the large Lagrange coefficients that arise in Shamir secret sharing over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. As noted in Remark
2.3
, the Lagrange coefficients grow exponentially as
\approx 2^{T}
, exceeding
q
for
T\geq\log_{2}q\approx 23
. Even for moderate
T
(e.g.,
T=16
), the coefficients
\binom{16}{8}=12870
can cause the naive approach to inflate norms beyond the rejection threshold.

Our solution is based on two key observations:

1.

Masking the coefficients
: We add pairwise-canceling masks
\mathbf{m}_{i}
to each party’s contribution. While individual contributions
\mathbf{U}_{i}
may have arbitrary magnitude, the masks cancel in the sum, leaving the correct short result.

2.

Separating nonce and secret
: The nonce
\mathbf{y}_{i}
and secret share
\mathbf{s}_{1,i}
must be treated differently. Nonces are summed directly (
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}
), while secret shares are reconstructed via Lagrange interpolation (
\mathbf{s}_{1}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{s}_{1,i}
).

(CPs) to jointly evaluate the r0-check via lightweight 2PC. Each CP receives additive shares of
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
; neither learns the complete value unless they collude. This achieves the best empirical performance (see Table
5
) while requiring only that at least one of the two CPs is honest—a weaker assumption than TEE trust and more efficient than full MPC. The 2PC uses standard garbled circuits
[
37
]
or oblivious transfer-based protocols for the infinity norm comparison. Profile P3 is ideal for deployments where two semi-trusted servers can be provisioned (e.g., multi-cloud setups), offering a practical middle ground between P1’s hardware trust and P2’s computational overhead.

3.4
Pairwise-Canceling Masks

The key innovation of our protocol is the use of pairwise-canceling masks. For any signing set

\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{m}_{i}=\mathbf{0}

Definition 3.1

For signing set

\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\mathsf{dom})}=\sum_{j\in S:j>i}\mathsf{PRF}(\mathsf{seed}_{i,j},\mathsf{dom})-\sum_{j\in S:j<i}\mathsf{PRF}(\mathsf{seed}_{j,i},\mathsf{dom})

We use domain separators
\mathsf{dom}=\mathsf{nonce}\|\texttt{"resp"}
for response masks

and
\mathsf{dom}=\mathsf{nonce}\|\texttt{"comm"}
for commitment masks

.

Lemma 3.2

(Mask Cancellation)
.

For any signing set
S
and nonce,
\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{m}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proof 3.3
.

Each pair

i<j

•

from party

from party

(since

These cancel exactly, so
\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{m}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
.

3.9
The r0-Check Subprotocol

A critical component of ML-DSA signing that requires careful treatment in the threshold setting is the
r0-check
: verifying that
\left\|\mathsf{LowBits}(\mathbf{w}-c\mathbf{s}_{2},2\gamma_{2})\right\|_{\infty}<\gamma_{2}-\beta
. This check ensures that the hint
\mathbf{h}
correctly recovers
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathsf{HighBits}(\mathbf{w})
during verification.

The Challenge.

In single-signer ML-DSA, the signer has direct access to
\mathbf{s}_{2}
and can compute
\mathbf{w}-c\mathbf{s}_{2}
directly. In our threshold setting,
\mathbf{s}_{2}
is secret-shared among the parties, and no single party (including the combiner) knows the complete
\mathbf{s}_{2}
.

Our Approach: Masked Reconstruction of
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
.

We extend the pairwise-canceling mask technique to reconstruct
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
at the combiner while preserving privacy of individual shares:

1.

Each party
i
computes
\mathbf{V}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\cdot c\cdot\mathbf{s}_{2,i}+\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(s2)}
, where
\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(s2)}\in R_{q}^{k}
is a pairwise-canceling mask using domain separator
\mathsf{nonce}\|\texttt{"s2"}
.

2.

The combiner aggregates:
\sum_{i\in S}\mathbf{V}_{i}=c\cdot\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{s}_{2,i}+\sum_{i}\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(s2)}=c\mathbf{s}_{2}

3.

The combiner computes
\mathbf{w}-c\mathbf{s}_{2}
and performs the r0-check locally.

Computing
c\mathbf{t}_{0}
for Hints.

The hint computation requires
c\mathbf{t}_{0}
where
\mathbf{t}_{0}=\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d}
. Since
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{1}+\mathbf{s}_{2}
, we have:

c\mathbf{t}_{0}=c\mathbf{t}-c\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d}=c(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{1}+\mathbf{s}_{2})-c\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d}

The combiner can compute this as follows:

•

c\mathbf{s}_{2}
is obtained from the masked reconstruction above

•

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y}+c\mathbf{s}_{1})=\mathbf{w}+c\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{1}
, so
c\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_{1}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}

•

Thus
c\mathbf{t}_{0}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w})+c\mathbf{s}_{2}-c\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d}

Security Consideration.

The combiner learns
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
during r0-check. Since virtually all ML-DSA challenges are invertible in
R_{q}
(Monte Carlo: 100% of
10^{4}
samples), an adversary with
(c,c\mathbf{s}_{2})
can recover
\mathbf{s}_{2}=c^{-1}(c\mathbf{s}_{2})
. Thus
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
must be protected.

Resolution.

We address this via three deployment profiles (Table
2
):

•

P1 (TEE)
:
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
computed inside enclave, never exposed

•

P2 (MPC)
: Parties jointly evaluate r0-check without reconstructing
c\mathbf{s}_{2}

•

P3 (2PC)
: Two CPs each hold additive share; neither learns complete value

See Section
6
for P2/P3 details.

4
Security Analysis

We prove that our masked threshold ML-DSA protocol achieves EUF-CMA security and privacy. This section contains complete proofs; we do not defer arguments to supplementary material.

4.1
Security Model

We consider a static adversary
\mathcal{A}
that corrupts a set
C\subset[N]
with
|C|<T
before execution. The adversary controls corrupted parties, observes all broadcasts, and adaptively chooses messages to sign.

Trust Assumptions.

Profile P1 (Primary):
The coordinator runs in a TEE/HSM. The adversary cannot observe values computed inside the enclave (specifically
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
); only the final signature
\sigma=(\tilde{c},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{h})
exits the enclave. We model the enclave as ideal; compromise of the TEE is out of scope. Our theorems target this profile.

Remark 4.1

(Why
|S|\geq T+1
: Two-Honest Requirement)
.

Our protocol requires signing sets of size at least
T+1
rather than
T
. This ensures that even when the adversary corrupts the maximum
|C|=T-1
parties, every signing set contains at least
two
honest parties—necessary for mask privacy (see Lemma
4.6
).

4.2
Unforgeability

Theorem 4.2

(Unforgeability)
.

If Module-SIS is hard and
H
is a random oracle, then the masked threshold ML-DSA scheme is EUF-CMA secure:

\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{EUF\text{-}CMA}}_{\Pi}(\mathcal{A})\leq O(q_{H})\cdot\epsilon_{\mathsf{M\text{-}SIS}}+\frac{(q_{H}+q_{s})^{2}}{2^{256}}

where

are random oracle and signing queries. The security loss matches single-signer ML-DSA
[
1
,
18
]

Proof 4.3
.

We reduce to Module-SIS via a sequence of game hops. Let
\mathsf{Game}_{i}
denote the
i
-th game and
\mathsf{Win}_{i}
the event that
\mathcal{A}
produces a valid forgery in
\mathsf{Game}_{i}
.

\mathsf{Game}_{0}
(Real).

The real EUF-CMA game. Challenger runs
\mathsf{KeyGen}
honestly, gives
\mathcal{A}
the public key
\mathsf{pk}
and corrupted shares
\{\mathsf{sk}_{i}\}_{i\in C}
.

\mathsf{Game}_{1}
(Random Oracle).

Replace
H
with a lazily-sampled random function. By the random oracle model:
|\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{1}]-\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{0}]|=0
.

\mathsf{Game}_{2}
(Programmed Challenges).

During signing, sample challenge
c\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}\mathcal{C}
uniformly
before

\mathbf{w}_{1}
, then program

. This fails only on collision. By birthday bound:
|\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{2}]-\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{1}]|\leq(q_{H}+q_{s})^{2}/2^{256}
.

\mathsf{Game}_{3}
(Simulated Shares).

Replace honest parties’ shares with uniformly random values, setting
\mathbf{s}_{1,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\$}}{{\leftarrow}}R_{q}^{\ell}
for

. By

-Shamir security (any

\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{3}]=\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{2}]
.

\mathsf{Game}_{4}
(Simulated Signatures).

Simulate signing without knowing

. The masked values

|\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{4}]-\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{3}]|\leq\binom{|S|}{2}\cdot\epsilon_{\mathsf{PRF}}
.

Reduction to M-SIS.

In

\mathcal{B}
. Given M-SIS instance
\mathbf{A}

sets up the public key with
\mathbf{t}_{1}
computed from
\mathbf{A}
and runs
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathsf{Game}_{4}
. If
\mathcal{A}
outputs forgery

, apply the forking lemma
[
34
,
5
]
: rewind and obtain two forgeries

and
(\mathbf{z}_{2},c_{2},\mathbf{h}_{2})
with

on the same

. By verification:

\mathsf{HighBits}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{i}-c_{i}\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d},2\gamma_{2})=\mathbf{w}_{1}\text{for}i\in\{1,2\}

Define
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}
and

(non-zero since

). Then
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\Delta c\cdot\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d}+\boldsymbol{\delta}
where

. Rearranging:

[\mathbf{A}|-\mathbf{t}_{1}\cdot 2^{d}]\cdot\bmatrix\mathbf{v}\\
\Delta c=\boldsymbol{\delta}

This yields a short vector for the augmented M-SIS instance

\Pr[\mathsf{Win}_{4}]\leq O(q_{H})\cdot\epsilon_{\mathsf{M\text{-}SIS}}

Conclusion.

Combining all bounds:

\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{EUF\text{-}CMA}}_{\Pi}(\mathcal{A})\leq O(q_{H})\cdot\epsilon_{\mathsf{M\text{-}SIS}}+\frac{(q_{H}+q_{s})^{2}}{2^{256}}+\binom{N}{2}\cdot\epsilon_{\mathsf{PRF}}

For SHAKE-256 as PRF,
\epsilon_{\mathsf{PRF}}
is negligible, yielding the stated bound.

Theorem 4.4

(Privacy)
.

If PRF is secure, then the view of any coalition

Proof 4.5
.

We construct simulator

Simulator
\mathcal{S}

Given

Hybrid
\mathsf{H}_{0}

Real protocol execution with honest parties using true secrets.

\mathsf{H}_{1}

Replace PRF outputs for honest-honest pairs with truly random: for

Conclusion.

Lemma 4.6

(Mask Hiding)
.

An honest party’s masked response
\mathbf{U}_{j}=\mathbf{y}_{j}+\lambda_{j}c\mathbf{s}_{1,j}+\mathbf{m}_{j}
is computationally indistinguishable from uniform in

.

Since

and

for some honest

\mathbf{U}_{j}=(\text{fixed})+(\text{uniform})\equiv\text{uniform}
.

All three deployment profiles (P1, P2, P3) achieve identical EUF-CMA security bounds, as the final signature structure

We model the TEE as an ideal functionality that correctly executes the r0-check and hint computation.

Proof 4.11
.

The proof is immediate by the ideal functionality abstraction. The simulator
\mathcal{S}

2.

Remark 4.12

(TEE Trust Assumption)
.

Theorem
4.10
assumes the TEE is
ideal
—it correctly executes the specified computation and reveals nothing beyond the output. This is a hardware trust assumption, not a cryptographic one. In practice, TEE security depends on the specific implementation (Intel SGX, ARM TrustZone, etc.) and is subject to side-channel attacks. Profiles P2 and P3 eliminate this assumption via cryptographic protocols.

4.7.2
Summary of UC Security

•

Profile P1 (TEE)
: UC security under ideal TEE assumption (Theorem
4.10
). EUF-CMA and privacy follow from Theorems
4.2
and
4.4
.

•

Profile P2 (MPC)
: UC security against dishonest majority via MPC (Theorem
6.2
). This provides the strongest cryptographic guarantees with no hardware trust.

•

Profile P3 (2PC)
: UC security under 1-of-2 CP honest assumption (Theorem
6.5
). Combines cryptographic security with best performance.

The choice of profile affects trust assumptions and performance, but not the fundamental cryptographic security of the resulting signatures.

Implementation and Benchmarks

We provide a reference implementation of our masked threshold ML-DSA protocol and present benchmarks demonstrating its practical efficiency.

5.1
Implementation Overview

Our implementation targets ML-DSA-65 (NIST Security Level 3) with the following components:

Core Modules.

•

Polynomial Arithmetic
: Operations over
R_{q}=\mathbb{Z}_{q}[X]/(X^{256}+1)
with
q=8380417
, including addition, subtraction, multiplication, and NTT-based fast multiplication.

•

Shamir Secret Sharing
: Polynomial evaluation and Lagrange interpolation over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. Lagrange coefficients
\lambda_{i}=\prod_{j\in S,j\neq i}\frac{j}{j-i}\mod q
depend on the signing set
S
and are computed per-session in
O(T^{2})
time. For fixed-
S
deployments (e.g., always using the same
T
parties), coefficients can be precomputed and cached.

•

Mask Generation
: Pairwise-canceling mask computation using SHAKE-256 as the PRF, with efficient seed management.

•

Protocol Logic
: Three-round signing protocol with commitment verification, challenge derivation, and signature aggregation.

Optimizations.

1.

NTT Acceleration
: Polynomial multiplication uses the Number Theoretic Transform with
\omega=1753
as a primitive
256
-th root of unity modulo
q
. This reduces multiplication complexity from
O(n^{2})
to
O(n\log n)
, providing a
7\text{--}10\times
speedup.

2.

Montgomery Reduction
: Modular arithmetic uses Montgomery form for efficient reduction without division.

3.

Vectorized Operations
: Polynomial and vector operations leverage NumPy’s SIMD-optimized routines.

4.

Lazy Reduction
: Intermediate polynomial coefficients are reduced modulo
q
only when necessary to prevent overflow.

6.2
Optimistic Blame Protocol

Our protocol uses an
optimistic
approach: the normal signing path has no verification overhead. Only when failures exceed statistical expectation do we trigger blame.

Triggering Conditions.

•

Excessive aborts
: More than
K=30
consecutive failures (honest probability
(0.8)^{30}<0.13\%
)

•

Commitment mismatch
: Verifiable inconsistency in hash values (immediate blame)

Blame Protocol (Profile P1).

Under TEE coordinator:

1.

TEE requests each signer to reveal nonce seeds and mask inputs (sent only to TEE)

2.

TEE recomputes
\mathbf{y}_{i}
,
\mathbf{w}_{i}
, masks, and verifies consistency with submitted values

3.

Inconsistent parties are identified; TEE outputs only the cheater’s identity

4.

Malicious party is excluded from future sessions

This preserves privacy: all sensitive values stay inside the enclave; only cheater identities are output.

6.3
Profile P2: Fully Distributed Signing

For deployments where TEE trust is unacceptable, we provide a fully distributed protocol using secure multi-party computation. The core challenge is evaluating the r0-check predicate without reconstructing
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
at any single point—since virtually all ML-DSA challenges
c
are invertible in
R_{q}
, leaking
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
would compromise the secret key.

The r0-Check Problem.

The predicate
\left\|\mathsf{LowBits}(\mathbf{w}-c\mathbf{s}_{2},2\gamma_{2})\right\|_{\infty}<\gamma_{2}-\beta
requires:

1.

Computing
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=\mathbf{w}-c\mathbf{s}_{2}
(parties hold shares of
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
)

2.

Extracting
\mathsf{LowBits}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime},2\gamma_{2})
— a non-linear operation

3.

Comparing the infinity norm against
\gamma_{2}-\beta

Naive reconstruction of
c\mathbf{s}_{2}
would leak the secret key (since virtually all challenges
c
are invertible in
R_{q}
; see Section
3.9
).

Approach: edaBits-Based Arithmetic-to-Binary Conversion.

We leverage
edaBits

[
20
]
, which provide correlated randomness
(\langle r\rangle_{q},\langle r\rangle_{2})
— the same random value shared in both arithmetic (mod
q
) and binary form. This enables efficient domain conversion:

1.

Masked Opening
: Parties compute
\langle\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r}\rangle_{q}
and open this masked value. The mask
\mathbf{r}
hides
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
.

2.

Binary Subtraction
: Using
\langle\mathbf{r}\rangle_{2}
, parties compute
\langle\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\rangle_{2}=(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r})\boxminus\langle\mathbf{r}\rangle_{2}
via binary circuits, where
\boxminus
denotes binary subtraction with borrow propagation.

3.

Comparison
: With
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
in binary form, the range check
|\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{j}|<\gamma_{2}-\beta
becomes a binary comparison circuit, evaluable in constant rounds.

4.

AND Aggregation
: Results for all
nk
coefficients are combined via an AND-tree to produce a single pass/fail bit.

Protocol Structure.

Our protocol achieves
8 online rounds
:

Rounds

Operation

1–2

3–4

5

A2B conversion via edaBits

6–7

8

Comparison with Prior MPC Approaches.

•

Bienstock et al.

[
7
]
: 37–136 online rounds, honest majority

•

Celi et al.

[
14
]
: 6 online rounds, but restricted to
T\leq 6

•

: 8 online rounds, dishonest majority, arbitrary
T

Profile P2 thus achieves dishonest-majority security with arbitrary thresholds in constant rounds.

Communication Complexity.

Per-party online communication is
O(nk\cdot\log q)
bits for the A2B conversion and comparison circuits. Preprocessing (edaBits generation) requires
O(nk)
correlated randomness per signing attempt.

Conclusion

We have presented Masked Threshold ML-DSA, a threshold signature scheme for FIPS 204 ML-DSA that supports arbitrary thresholds while maintaining standard signature sizes and verification compatibility with existing FIPS 204 verifiers.

7.1
Positioning Among Concurrent Work

Concurrent work includes Bienstock et al.
[
7
]
(arbitrary
T
, UC security, but honest-majority and 37–136 rounds), Celi et al.
[
14
]
(dishonest-majority, 6 rounds, but

[

(UC-secure, but 2-party only). Our Profile P2 targets a different point in the design space: arbitrary

7.2
Summary of Contributions

Our main technical contribution is
masked Lagrange reconstruction
, which adapts pairwise-canceling masks
[
9
]
to the lattice setting. This approach addresses three challenges absent in ECDSA: rejection sampling bounds, r0-check key leakage, and Irwin-Hall nonce security. The key insight is ensuring only the sum needs to be small, not individual contributions.

We instantiate this technique in three deployment profiles. Profile P1 (TEE-Assisted) achieves 3-round signing with EUF-CMA security under Module-SIS (Theorem
4.2
). Profile P2 (Fully Distributed) uses MPC to achieve 8-round signing with dishonest-majority and UC security (Theorem
6.2
). Profile P3 (2PC-Assisted) offers the best performance (249 ms, 3–5 rounds) with UC security under a 1-of-2 CP honest assumption (Theorem
6.5
).

Our unoptimized Python prototype demonstrates practical efficiency: approximately 4 seconds per signature for
(16,31)
-threshold, with success rates of
23\%
–
32\%
matching single-signer ML-DSA and overhead of 2.5–7.1
\times
depending on configuration. This represents a
4.8\times 10^{10}
improvement over naive threshold approaches for
T=16
. All signatures are syntactically identical to single-signer ML-DSA and pass standard FIPS 204 verification without modification.

7.3
Limitations and Future Work

Current Limitations.

Each deployment profile involves different trust assumptions: Profile P1 requires TEE/HSM trust, Profile P2 eliminates hardware trust at the cost of 8 rounds, and Profile P3 requires 1-of-2 CP honesty with 3–5 rounds. Our protocol requires
|S|\geq T+1
signers to ensure at least two honest parties for mask privacy, which is inherent to pairwise-mask schemes
[
9
]
. The aggregated nonce follows an Irwin-Hall rather than uniform distribution; while the security loss is negligible (
R_{2}^{\epsilon}\leq 1.003
; see Appendix
D
), this is a deviation from single-signer ML-DSA. Finally, we assume a synchronous model where all parties are online simultaneously during signing.

Future Directions.

Distribution-preserving variants with uniform nonces; two-round protocols; efficient malicious security via lightweight ZK proofs; asynchronous variants; Rust/C+AVX-512 production implementation; formal verification in EasyCrypt.

7.4
Impact

As organizations transition to post-quantum cryptography, our work enables ML-DSA adoption in cryptocurrency custody, distributed certificate authorities, enterprise key management, and government applications—all while maintaining threshold security and FIPS 204 compatibility for smooth integration with existing infrastructure.

Lemma O.20

(Challenge Invertibility in

)
.

Proof O.21
.

Monte Carlo verification with

(Security Implication)
.

.
