Class-adaptive oracle-free metamorphic test case prioritization framework for vision-based deep neural networks
Authors: Junhan Li, Radziah Mohamad, Johanna Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metamorphic testing (MT) provides an effective oracle-free strategy for evaluating deep neural networks under semantics-preserving perturbations. However, existing MT prioritization methods typically rely on static global heuristics and do not adequately capture class-dependent variation in model behavior or the complementary roles of predictive uncertainty and interpretability drift. This limitation is especially important in safety-critical vision applications. We propose a class-adaptive uncertainty–interpretability metamorphic test case prioritization framework (CUI-MTP) for vision-based deep learning systems. Rather than ranking individual samples, the framework prioritizes executable metamorphic test cases and formulates prioritization as a class-conditioned optimization problem that jointly models probabilistic instability and saliency-level behavioral change. Predictive uncertainty and interpretability drift are integrated through multi-objective Bayesian optimization with expected hypervolume improvement. Experiments on CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, and ISIC2019 with ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and ConvNeXt-Base show that the proposed method consistently outperforms representative oracle-free baselines across datasets, architectures, and Top-N budgets, with statistically significant improvements (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.01$$\end{document}). Qualitative Grad-CAM case studies further show that the framework captures not only obvious prediction failures but also high-risk cases with substantial saliency drift. Overall, the results demonstrate a practical and scalable strategy for robust, safety-aware validation of vision-based deep learning systems.

Introduction

Background

Deep neural networks (DNNs) are now widely used in intelligent systems for data-intensive and safety-critical applications. Despite their impressive predictive performance, prior studies have shown that DNNs remain vulnerable to slight perturbations, distribution shifts, and semantic-preserving input variations, which can lead to erroneous yet unnoticed behaviours in real-world deployment1,2. This issue is particularly concerning in high-stakes scenarios, where unreliable predictions may have serious downstream consequences. Traditional software testing relies on explicit test oracles, but for learning-based systems the correct output is often unavailable or prohibitively expensive to obtain3,4. Metamorphic testing (MT) helps address this challenge by examining whether model behaviour remains consistent under semantic-preserving transformations, referred to as metamorphic relations (MRs)3. Rather than requiring exact expected outputs, MT assesses behavioural stability under controlled perturbations and has shown promise in image classification and autonomous perception tasks5,6.

Related progress and research gap

Beyond MT, a broad range of testing approaches has been developed for DNNs. Coverage-guided testing adapts adequacy criteria through neuron-level and contribution-aware coverage metrics5, while DeepCon extends this idea by targeting previously untested neuron–weight interactions7. Search-based testing generates failure-inducing inputs through optimization, as illustrated by DeepAtash8. Related efforts have also emphasized optimization-oriented and standards-aligned validation frameworks for trustworthy AI systems9. Adaptive Random Testing for Deep Learning (ARTDL) improves testing efficiency by incorporating diversity and feature-space distance information6. Mutation-based fuzzing2 and GAN-based test generation10 have also been explored for robustness assessment and automated test generation. Together, these studies form an important foundation for modern DNN testing research11.

Despite this progress, one practical question remains insufficiently addressed: under limited testing budgets, which metamorphic test cases should be executed first in an oracle-free setting? Existing prioritization strategies mainly rely on global heuristics, such as uncertainty, gradient sensitivity, diversity, or coverage-related proxies6,7. However, these approaches have several limitations. First, they typically use globally fixed prioritization rules and do not explicitly account for class-level behavioural heterogeneity. Second, output-level uncertainty alone may fail to capture internal representation drift, even when transformed inputs cause substantial changes in model attention or feature alignment7. Third, many existing methods are essentially static and are therefore not well suited to iterative and budget-constrained MT workflows2,8. Recent reviews likewise suggest that many optimization-based testing techniques remain insufficiently adaptive for dynamic testing scenarios11.

Motivation

Recent advances in interpretability provide an opportunity to strengthen oracle-free prioritization. In particular, Grad-CAM offers a practical means of inspecting the class-discriminative evidence used by DNNs5. Compared with output-only criteria, interpretability drift can reveal representation misalignment between original and transformed inputs, including cases in which the predicted class remains unchanged. This is especially relevant in MT, where semantic-preserving transformations may leave the final prediction unchanged while still altering the model’s internal reasoning pattern3,5. Nevertheless, interpretability cues are still used mainly for post hoc analysis rather than as operational signals for prioritization2,7. As a result, a unified and practically usable framework that jointly exploits uncertainty and interpretability for adaptive, oracle-free MT prioritization is still lacking.

Overview of the proposed framework

To address this gap, we propose CUI-MTP, a class-adaptive uncertainty–interpretability driven framework for metamorphic test case prioritization, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The method constructs class-specific MR candidate pools and prioritizes test cases through a class-conditioned fusion of predictive uncertainty and Grad-CAM-based interpretability drift. In this way, it captures both output instability and representation-level discrepancy. Because the relative value of these signals may vary across classes and MR types, we formulate prioritization as a class-adaptive optimization problem over fusion parameters and MR arms. The optimization stage is oracle-free, as it relies only on behavioural signals derived from model outputs and interpretability responses, without using ground-truth labels. Ground-truth labels are used only in the final evaluation stage to assess prioritization effectiveness.

We further formulate the prioritization objective as a multi-objective problem. In practice, an effective prioritization strategy should not only improve error-detection effectiveness, but also remain robust across different testing budgets and heterogeneous MR behaviours. We therefore adopt multi-objective Bayesian optimization with Gaussian-process surrogate modelling and Expected Hypervolume Improvement (EHVI) to iteratively learn Pareto-optimal and budget-aware prioritization policies.

The proposed approach is evaluated on CIFAR-1012, Fashion-MNIST13, and ISIC201914 using ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and ConvNeXt-Base, and is compared with representative oracle-free baselines, including DeepGini15, EX216, QUOTE17, DistXplore18, SeedPrior19, and random selection.

Contributions

The contributions of this work are threefold. (1) We present, to the best of our knowledge, the first class-adaptive uncertainty–interpretability fusion approach for oracle-free metamorphic test case prioritization, explicitly accounting for class-level and MR-level behavioural diversity beyond globally fixed heuristics. (2) We formulate MT prioritization as an oracle-free, class-adaptive multi-objective optimization problem and address it using Gaussian-process-based Bayesian optimization with EHVI, enabling Pareto-aware and budget-aware prioritization under dynamic testing conditions. (3) Through extensive experiments on natural-image and medical-image benchmarks, we show that the proposed framework improves prioritization effectiveness over representative oracle-free baselines while offering a more auditable and risk-oriented workflow for trustworthy AI validation.

MR diversity and composition of prioritized test suites

To further characterize how the proposed framework distributes its prioritized budget across metamorphic relations, we analyzed the diversity and composition of the selected test suites. Rather than treating diversity as an objective that should be maximized unconditionally, we focus here on how the distribution of selected MR types changes with budget and how this distribution differs across methods and domains. In this analysis, two complementary views are considered: the normalized entropy of the MR distribution within the prioritized test suite, and the explicit MR composition of the earliest selected samples.

Fig. 6Normalized entropy of the MR distribution within prioritized test suites across Top-N budgets. Higher values indicate that selected samples are distributed more evenly across MR types, whereas lower values indicate stronger concentration on a smaller subset of transformations.

Normalized entropy of the MR distribution within prioritized test suites across Top-N budgets. Higher values indicate that selected samples are distributed more evenly across MR types, whereas lower values indicate stronger concentration on a smaller subset of transformations.

Figure 6 shows that the diversity profile of selected metamorphic relations varies substantially across datasets and methods. Across all four configurations, the proposed framework does not simply maximize MR entropy. Instead, it learns a dataset-dependent allocation pattern, remaining more concentrated in some settings and more distributed in others. This suggests that strong prioritization performance is not achieved by uniformly spreading budget across all transformations, but by balancing concentration and diversity appropriately.

On CIFAR-10 and Fashion-MNIST, CUI-MTP maintains a moderate entropy profile, avoiding both excessive collapse onto a few MR types and near-uniform allocation across all transformations. In both benchmark settings, the strongest performance therefore appears to arise from an intermediate regime rather than from either extreme concentration or maximal dispersion.

A more distinctive pattern emerges on ISIC2019. Under both ConvNeXt-Base and ResNet-50, CUI-MTP begins from a relatively lower-entropy regime and broadens progressively as Top-N increases. This indicates that, in the medical-imaging setting, the strongest prioritization behavior is associated with more selective early-budget MR allocation, followed by gradual diversification as additional budget becomes available. Figure 6 shows that MR diversity should not be treated as a monotonic objective, but as a budget-aware and dataset-dependent balance between concentration and spread.

Figure 7 makes the entropy patterns more concrete by showing which MR types dominate the earliest prioritized budget. On CIFAR-10, CUI-MTP is mainly driven by noise_gauss and rot_small-brightness, whereas QUOTE and Random are more concentrated on a single dominant component and EX2 and Seed Selection are more broadly distributed. This indicates that the proposed framework favors a selective but non-collapsed low-budget allocation.

On Fashion-MNIST, CUI-MTP is mainly allocated to hflip-brightness and rot_small-brightness, again reflecting a moderate but task-specific MR mixture. On ISIC2019, under both ConvNeXt-Base and ResNet-50, the Top-50 composition is dominated by hflip-brightness and rot_small-brightness, with relatively limited use of brightness and noise_gauss. This selective early-budget composition is consistent with the lower entropy regime observed in the medical setting. Figures 6 and 7 show that the proposed framework learns a domain-dependent balance between diversity and concentration rather than maximizing MR diversity in isolation.

Qualitative analysis of Grad-CAM case studies

To further illustrate the behavioral patterns captured by the proposed prioritization strategy, we examined representative Grad-CAM case studies from both CIFAR-10 and ISIC2019. In particular, we focused on two types of samples that are especially relevant to metamorphic testing: high-drift but no-flip cases, in which the final prediction remains unchanged despite substantial attention relocation, and error-plus-drift cases, in which predictive failure is accompanied by pronounced saliency drift. Together, these examples provide an intuitive view of why uncertainty alone is insufficient to characterize high-value test samples, and why interpretability drift offers a complementary signal for identifying semantically fragile behaviors.

As shown in Fig. 8, substantial interpretability drift can arise even when the model output is preserved. The selected CIFAR-10 and ISIC2019 examples show the same qualitative pattern: the prediction remains unchanged, yet the attended regions shift noticeably after transformation. In the natural-image case, attention is redistributed across different parts of the object and its surrounding context, whereas in the dermoscopic case the saliency focus moves within or around the lesion region. These examples highlight a form of latent instability that would be overlooked by output-based criteria alone. Prediction consistency under metamorphic transformation should therefore not be treated as sufficient evidence of stable internal reasoning.

Comprehensive ablation analysis across datasets and architectures

To examine the respective contributions of uncertainty, interpretability, class adaptiveness, and arm-level refinement, we conducted a structured ablation study across four representative dataset–backbone configurations: CIFAR-10 with ResNet-50, Fashion-MNIST with ResNet-18, ISIC2019 with ConvNeXt-Base, and ISIC2019 with ResNet-50. The ablation variants include uncertainty-only prioritization (U), interpretability-only prioritization (I), the arm-level variant, the full class-level formulation (CUI-MTP), and two reduced class-level variants in which either class adaptiveness or interpretability drift is removed. As in the main experiments, a higher error rate indicates better prioritization effectiveness, because it means that a larger proportion of oracle-based errors is concentrated within the same Top-N testing budget.

Figure 10 shows that the contribution of each component is dataset-dependent, although several overall patterns remain stable. Across all four configurations, the full class-level formulation is either the strongest or among the strongest variants, indicating that the joint use of class adaptiveness, uncertainty, and interpretability provides the most reliable overall design. In contrast, the uncertainty-only and interpretability-only variants are consistently weaker than the strongest adaptive formulations, showing that neither signal alone is sufficient.

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-50, the arm-level and full class-level variants remain the two leading curves across the full Top-N range, indicating that both hierarchical adaptation and joint uncertainty–interpretability modeling are beneficial in a natural-image setting with substantial inter-class diversity. On Fashion-MNIST with ResNet-18, the uncertainty-only, arm-level, and full class-level variants are nearly indistinguishable, whereas the interpretability-only variant is clearly weaker, suggesting an uncertainty-dominated regime. On ISIC2019, the full adaptive design becomes more important: under both ConvNeXt-Base and ResNet-50, the full class-level formulation remains consistently strong, while single-signal strategies are clearly inferior.

Above all, the ablation results show that the effectiveness of the framework arises from the interaction of uncertainty, interpretability, and adaptive policy learning rather than from any single component in isolation.

As shown in Table 1, runtime is highly consistent across datasets, sampling ratios, backbones, and class-level variants. In all settings, execution is dominated by the MOBO stage, which accounts for over 99% of total runtime, while UI computation contributes only marginal overhead. The effect of sampling ratio is modest, and the three class-level variants exhibit very similar runtime profiles within each setting. Overall, these results indicate that the practical computational bottleneck lies in the BO-based search procedure rather than in uncertainty- or interpretability-driven scoring. To support repeated large-batch runs, our implementation also reuses cached UI signals and deterministic alignments, which reduces redundant recomputation but does not alter the prioritization mechanism.

Ablation results across datasets and architectures

The ablation results show that the relative importance of uncertainty and interpretability is strongly domain-dependent. On CIFAR-10, the class-level framework performs best, indicating the value of class-conditioned prioritization in visually diverse natural images. On Fashion-MNIST, class-level, arm-level and U-only strategies are nearly indistinguishable, suggesting an uncertainty-dominated regime. On ISIC2019, both class-level and arm-level adaptive variants clearly outperform single-signal baselines, showing that dermoscopic imaging requires balanced, class-aware fusion.

These results further support the adaptive nature of the proposed framework across domains and architectures.

Qualitative analysis of Grad-CAM case studies

To further illustrate the behavioural patterns captured by the proposed prioritization strategy, we examined representative Grad-CAM case studies from both CIFAR-10 and ISIC2019. We focused on two sample types that are especially relevant to metamorphic testing: high-drift but no-flip cases, in which the final prediction remains unchanged despite substantial attention relocation, and error-plus-drift cases, in which predictive failure is accompanied by pronounced saliency drift.

As shown in Fig. 8, substantial interpretability drift can arise even when the model output is preserved. This pattern appears in both CIFAR-10 and ISIC2019, indicating that output consistency does not necessarily imply stability of the model’s internal decision basis.

Figure 9 shows a stronger failure mode, in which metamorphic transformation is associated with both predictive error and pronounced saliency relocation. The same pattern is observed in both datasets, suggesting that the model no longer relies on the same discriminative cues after mutation.

The case studies in Figs. 8 and 9 provide qualitative support for the central rationale of the proposed method: high-value metamorphic test cases include not only obvious prediction failures, but also samples whose outputs appear stable while their internal saliency structure changes markedly after transformation. By jointly considering uncertainty and interpretability drift, the proposed prioritization strategy is therefore better positioned to identify both overt failures and subtler forms of decision instability.

Discussion

The results show that oracle-free metamorphic test case prioritization is more effective when formulated as an adaptive, class-conditioned optimization problem rather than as a static global scoring rule. Across CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, and ISIC2019, the proposed framework maintains strong performance while allowing the dominant fusion behavior to vary with data structure, task ambiguity, and backbone representation. The consistency of CUI-MTP-Class should therefore be interpreted not as rigid uniformity, but as stable adaptive behavior under heterogeneous testing conditions.

Scope and modality considerations

Although the framework is formulated in general terms for deep neural networks, the present implementation and evaluation focus on vision models. In particular, the interpretability component is instantiated through Grad-CAM, which is naturally suited to convolutional architectures in visual domains. The present study therefore does not claim direct, unchanged applicability to non-visual modalities such as natural language processing or time-series analysis.

This focus is deliberate. Vision-based deep learning systems, especially those used in safety-sensitive contexts such as medical imaging, represent a high-stakes setting in which oracle-free evaluation is immediately relevant. At the same time, the underlying design principle is not tied to Grad-CAM itself. For other modalities, the same logic could be instantiated with modality-appropriate attribution mechanisms, such as attention-based saliency for Transformer models or gradient-based relevance measures for time-series networks.

Computational overhead and practical trade-offs

Compared with lightweight baselines such as random sampling or static uncertainty-based ranking, the proposed framework incurs additional computational overhead because it relies on Grad-CAM extraction and Bayesian optimization during prioritization. This overhead is an inherent trade-off of adaptive, information-rich prioritization.

However, the framework is intended for testing-time decision making rather than deployment-time inference, and therefore operates outside the critical prediction path. In practical testing workflows, especially in safety-critical and regulated settings, the cost of prioritization is typically small relative to the cost of executing, inspecting, and validating large test suites or acquiring expert annotations. By surfacing high-risk samples earlier, the framework can reduce the number of executions required to uncover meaningful failures, thereby improving efficiency at the system level. Its budget-aware design through Top-N prioritization and MR-Lite configurations further supports deployment in centralized or offline testing pipelines.

Practical significance and budget-dependent scalability

The practical value of the proposed method extends beyond statistical dominance. Effect-size analysis shows that the gains of CUI-MTP-Class over competing baselines are not only statistically significant but also operationally meaningful. Across datasets and backbones, Cohen’s d values against DeepGini, EX2, QUOTE, and Random remain well above the conventional threshold for a large effect, and even relative to DistXplore the observed effects remain substantial. Similar links between test-suite optimization, data efficiency, and fault exposure have been reported in recent work on machine-learning-based test optimization and fuzzing-driven data augmentation11,20, while generative test case synthesis has also been shown to improve efficiency by targeting hard-to-reach failure regions10.

The budget-dependent analysis against DistXplore further shows that the proposed framework scales well as testing budgets increase. On CIFAR-10, CUI-MTP-Class already provides a clear advantage in the low-budget regime, and this advantage does not collapse at larger Top-N values, suggesting resistance to early saturation. Comparable saturation behavior has been noted in search-based and combinatorial testing of DNNs21,22. On Fashion-MNIST and ISIC2019, the relative gain becomes even more pronounced at medium and high budgets, indicating that adaptive UI fusion remains sensitive to subtler failure modes deeper in the prioritized pool, unlike purely diversity-driven approaches such as DistXplore23,24.

Clinical safety and deployment relevance

The ISIC2019 results point to a broader implication of oracle-free prioritization for safety-critical imaging workflows, including dermatology triage and computer-aided diagnosis. Recent robustness-enhancement frameworks have shown that actively targeting high-uncertainty samples during testing and retraining can yield substantial robustness gains in realistic deployment settings25. In parallel, semantics-aware fuzzing and interpretability-oriented test generation, such as NSGen, demonstrate that neuron-semantic criteria can trigger more faults while also providing human-readable rationales for failure26. These directions align with regulatory expectations that emphasize not only aggregate accuracy, but also transparent failure analysis and systematic risk mitigation.

Recent distribution-aware testing methods further suggest that regulatory-grade assurance requires more than dense sampling around nominal operating points. Hierarchical distribution-aware testing, low-density risk-targeted generation, and context-aware fuzzing all show that clinically meaningful failures often reside in subtle, hard-to-cover regions of the input space22–24. Evidence from other safety-critical domains also suggests that strong offline test suites cannot replace ongoing scenario-specific testing and monitoring27. In this context, the proposed framework can serve as a modular component for robustness-enhancement pipelines, distribution-aware test generation, and repeated regression validation22–26.

Backbone effects and implications for oracle-free testing

Across the evaluated datasets, similar tier structures are observed under ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and ConvNeXt-Base. Although deeper backbones tend to compress rank gaps among stronger baselines, they do not overturn the dominance of CUI-MTP-Class. On ISIC2019, deeper architectures even slightly amplify the relative gains of adaptive prioritization, suggesting that richer internal representations may strengthen the interaction between uncertainty and interpretability signals23,28. This architectural stability is important for deployment because practical testing pipelines evolve quickly and model families are frequently replaced.

More broadly, the findings reinforce an important conceptual point: metamorphic testing is fundamentally a problem of behavioral consistency rather than one of uncertainty estimation alone. Output-layer uncertainty cannot fully characterize MT violations, especially when confidence remains stable despite pronounced internal feature drift27. In medical imaging in particular, saliency relocation may reflect clinically meaningful deviations in lesion-focused attention even when predictive confidence remains high29, whereas JSD captures probabilistic instability under subtle perturbations30. The proposed UI fusion framework treats these two signals as complementary and learnable rather than fixed, consistent with recent work on neuron-semantic test generation and context-aware fuzzing24,26.

Threats to validity

Internal validity

Internal validity may be affected by the selection and parametrization of metamorphic relations. To ensure controllable and executable experiments, we adopted an MR-Lite setting with visually moderate, semantics-preserving transformations. Although this supports fair comparison, it may introduce MR selection bias because the chosen transformations do not cover the full range of real-world perturbations. We mitigated this threat by using a consistent candidate pool and evaluating multiple datasets, backbones, and Top-N budgets.

External validity

External validity is limited by the current experimental scope. Although the framework was evaluated on multiple datasets and architectures, the study remains confined to vision-based classification. The interpretability component is based on Grad-CAM, which is well suited to visual models but does not directly transfer to non-visual modalities. Further validation on broader architectures, tasks, and data modalities is therefore needed.

Construct validity

Construct validity concerns whether the adopted signals and metrics reflect real defect detection capability. Predictive uncertainty and Grad-CAM drift are used as oracle-free surrogate signals, while effectiveness is evaluated by oracle-based Top-N error rates. These proxies are practical but imperfect, since high uncertainty or drift does not always imply misclassification, and some true errors may occur without strong interpretability change.

Concluding perspective

This work presents a class-level adaptive UI fusion framework for oracle-free metamorphic test case prioritization and demonstrates its statistical and practical superiority across diverse visual datasets and modern backbone architectures. The findings suggest that future MT systems should move beyond globally fixed scoring rules toward adaptive, multi-signal fusion mechanisms that explicitly account for class-level and transformation-wise behavioral diversity. As deep learning models continue to scale and enter increasingly high-stakes domains, such adaptive oracle-free testing strategies are likely to become an important component of trustworthy AI validation pipelines.

Metamorphic relations and test case construction

To construct executable metamorphic test cases, we applied a set of semantics-preserving metamorphic relations (MRs) that simulate mild yet realistic distribution shifts. Each transformed instance, together with its oracle-free consistency check, forms a unique executable test case. In this study, a test case is defined at the level of an original input–MR chain pair, rather than as an isolated transformed sample. This formulation is consistent with the practical objective of metamorphic testing, namely, to prioritize executable verification units that combine a transformation rule with a behavior-based consistency check.

Conventional MR-based and MR-Lite test cases

In conventional metamorphic testing, each transformation defines a separate MR arm, and each (x, MR) pair constitutes a standalone test case that preserves semantic class identity while enabling oracle-free consistency checking. Such formulations are commonly used to explore a broad transformation space for general robustness assessment. Representative examples include geometric transformations, photometric perturbations, and mild noise injection.

By contrast, the proposed MR-Lite suite is designed for large-scale test case prioritization under limited computational budgets. Rather than exhaustively expanding the transformation space, MR-Lite restricts the candidate pool to a small set of visually moderate, label-preserving perturbations. This design makes it possible to evaluate prioritization quality without conflating it with the effects of aggressive or highly diverse test generation. As a result, MR-Lite provides a lightweight yet practically meaningful candidate space for oracle-free test case ranking.

In the main experiments, each MR-Lite configuration generates a family of executable test cases by applying one or two bounded perturbations. The resulting construction space is summarized in Table 3. All selected configurations are intended to preserve semantic class identity while remaining strong enough to induce prediction-level or feature-level behavioral drift.

Proposed framework overview

The proposed CUI-MTP framework is designed for metamorphic testing in settings where ground-truth labels are unavailable, delayed, or too costly to obtain during prioritization. This situation commonly arises in large-scale regression testing, post-deployment monitoring, and safety-critical applications, where manual annotation may require substantial effort or domain expertise. The framework therefore performs prioritization strictly in an oracle-free manner and uses only model-derived signals computed from original inputs and their metamorphic variants.

All prioritization decisions rely on prediction outputs and uncertainty–interpretability (UI) drift metrics derived from metamorphic relations, without using true labels at any stage of optimization. Ground-truth labels are used only after prioritization to evaluate the effectiveness of the selected test cases. This separation avoids information leakage and matches realistic deployment settings in which test ranking must be determined before human verification is available.

The overall workflow comprises three stages: deterministic data pairing, oracle-free extraction of uncertainty and interpretability signals, and class-adaptive prioritization through multi-objective optimization. Original and transformed inputs are first deterministically paired and organized into class-wise and class–arm-wise pools. Uncertainty and interpretability signals are then extracted and normalized at the class level. Finally, class-adaptive multi-objective optimization is performed across multiple Top-N budgets to derive budget-aware prioritization policies.

Each transformed sample was deterministically matched to its corresponding original input using filename-based identifiers. This deterministic pairing step ensures that every executable test case is consistently represented across ranking, export, and evaluation stages, and that comparisons across methods are performed over the same candidate set.

Interpretability drift is quantified using Grad-CAM maps extracted from the original input and its transformed counterpart. For each pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x,x')$$\end{document}, interpretability is defined with respect to the predicted classes of the two inputs, namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(x)=\arg \max f(x)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(x')=\arg \max f(x')$$\end{document}. This formulation preserves the oracle-free setting while allowing the method to detect representation misalignment even when the final predicted label does not change.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D_c(x, x') = \frac{1}{|\Omega |} \sum _{u \in \Omega } \left| G_c^{(u)}(x) - G_c^{(u)}(x') \right| , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_c(x)$$\end{document} denotes the Grad-CAM map associated with class c and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document} is the spatial domain of the attention map. The final interpretability drift score is defined as3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} I(x, x') = \frac{1}{2} \left[ D_{c(x)}(x, x') + D_{c(x')}(x, x') \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This bidirectional definition captures attention shifts with respect to both the original prediction and the transformed prediction.

Class-level normalization

After computing uncertainty and interpretability signals, each test case is assigned normalized scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{U}_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{I}_i$$\end{document}. In our implementation, normalization is performed at the class level so that all MR arms within the same class share a common normalization reference. This improves comparability across arms and supports class-adaptive ranking. Class-wise normalization and class-conditioned ranking also reduce the dominance of high-volume classes and allow the prioritization policy to adapt to class-specific sensitivity to different metamorphic relations.

Class-adaptive scoring and dynamic fusion

For a fixed global fusion rule, the fused score for test case \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$tc_i$$\end{document} can be written as4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_i = \alpha \tilde{U}_i + (1-\alpha )\tilde{I}_i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in [0,1]$$\end{document} controls the relative emphasis on uncertainty and interpretability.

In the proposed class-adaptive setting, however, the fusion weight is both class-specific and budget-dependent. For a test case \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$tc_i$$\end{document} belonging to class c, the fused score under budget N is defined as5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_i^{(c)}(N;\theta ) = w_U^{(c)}(N;\theta )\,\tilde{U}_i + \big (1-w_U^{(c)}(N;\theta )\big )\,\tilde{I}_i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_U^{(c)}(N;\theta )\in [0,1]$$\end{document} denotes the uncertainty weight induced by the class-specific parameterization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}.

For a given class c and budget N, the prioritized set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{Top}}-N_c(\theta )$$\end{document} is obtained by ranking candidate test cases in descending order of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i^{(c)}(N;\theta )$$\end{document} and selecting the first N cases. The corresponding oracle-free prioritization utility is then defined as6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_N^{(c)}(\theta ) = \frac{1}{N}\sum _{i \in {\textrm{Top}}-N_c(\theta )} S_i^{(c)}(N;\theta ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This definition makes the optimization target explicit while preserving the oracle-free setting, since neither ground-truth labels nor post hoc error information are used in computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_N^{(c)}(\theta )$$\end{document}.

Multi-objective Bayesian optimization

Given a testing budget N, prioritization is defined as selecting the Top-N test cases with the highest fused UI scores. Since ground-truth error labels are unavailable during prioritization, the fused score serves as an oracle-free surrogate for ranking.

To model behaviour across multiple testing budgets, we parameterize the class-specific fusion rule by a dynamic pair\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =(\alpha _s,\alpha _l),$$\end{document}which represents the uncertainty emphasis at smaller and larger budgets, respectively. For each budget \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N \in \mathscr {B}$$\end{document}, a budget-specific uncertainty weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_U^{(c)}(N;\theta )$$\end{document} is obtained by decoding this parameterization into a dynamic fusion curve.

In the implementation, the optimizer first produces a raw parameter vector, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _{\textrm{raw}}$$\end{document}. This vector may then be regularized or shrunk toward a reference configuration to obtain an effective parameter vector, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _{\textrm{eff}}$$\end{document}. The final budget-specific uncertainty weight is then decoded from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _{\textrm{eff}}$$\end{document} to produce the dynamic curve\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_U^{(c)}(N;\theta _{\textrm{eff}}), \qquad N\in \mathscr {B}.$$\end{document}Using the oracle-free utility \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_N^{(c)}(\theta )$$\end{document} defined above, we consider two objectives for each class:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_1^{(c)}(\theta )&= \frac{1}{|\mathscr {B}|}\sum _{N \in \mathscr {B}} F_N^{(c)}(\theta ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_2^{(c)}(\theta )&= -\textrm{Var}_{N \in \mathscr {B}}\big (F_N^{(c)}(\theta )\big ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The first objective rewards strong average prioritization quality across budgets, whereas the second favours stability across budgets. This yields a two-objective Pareto problem, which is more appropriate than single-objective tuning because a useful prioritization policy should be both effective and robust under varying testing resources.

We solve this problem using multi-objective Bayesian optimisation with Expected Hypervolume Improvement (EHVI)31. At each iteration, a candidate parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _t$$\end{document} is proposed, evaluated through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\big (f_1^{(c)}(\theta _t), f_2^{(c)}(\theta _t)\big )$$\end{document}, and appended to the observed objective set. The final class-specific parameter is selected from the resulting Pareto set and used to derive the dynamic uncertainty-weight curve for ranking. Ground-truth labels and oracle-based error rates are used only after prioritization for evaluation and are never used during this optimization procedure.

Top-N prioritization outputs

Once the fusion parameters have been determined, all metamorphic test cases are ranked according to their fused UI scores. For a given budget N, the prioritized test suite consists of the Top-N ranked test cases. In the class-adaptive setting, grouping is based on the predicted class of the original input, rather than the ground-truth class, so that the procedure remains fully oracle-free during prioritization.

To support reproducibility and downstream analysis, the framework exports ranked test sets, class-level summaries, MR-level summaries, class-wise and arm-wise dynamic uncertainty-weight curves, optimization traces, hypervolume summaries, and runtime profiles. These artifacts are used both for quantitative evaluation and for case-based analysis of representative metamorphic failures.

Evaluation protocol and statistical analysis

All quantitative comparisons were conducted after oracle-free prioritization had been completed. Ground-truth labels were used only at this evaluation stage. For each dataset–backbone configuration, prioritized test cases were evaluated using oracle-based error rates at Top-N budgets of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N \in \{50,100,150,200,300,400\}$$\end{document}. Results were aggregated across independent runs, and the reported values correspond to mean error rates across seeds.

Global differences among methods were assessed using the non-parametric Friedman test. When the null hypothesis was rejected at the 0.05 significance level, post hoc Nemenyi tests were performed. Average ranks and critical-difference values were then used to construct the ranking diagrams reported in the Results section.

To quantify practical significance, pairwise effect sizes were computed between CUI-MTP-Class and the comparison methods. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _1,\sigma _1$$\end{document} denote the mean and standard deviation of the oracle-based error rates for CUI-MTP-Class, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _2,\sigma _2$$\end{document} denote the corresponding values for a baseline. The pooled standard deviation and Cohen’s d were computed as9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_p = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma _1^2 + \sigma _2^2}{2}}, \quad d = \frac{\mu _1 - \mu _2}{s_p}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Following standard conventions, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d \ge 0.8$$\end{document} was interpreted as a large effect. In addition, to quantify improvement over a representative baseline (DistXplore), absolute and relative gains at each Top-N level were computed as10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta _N = R_N^{\text {CUI-MTP}} - R_N^{\text {Base}}, \quad \delta _N = \frac{R_N^{\text {CUI-MTP}} - R_N^{\text {Base}}}{R_N^{\text {Base}}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_N$$\end{document} denotes the oracle-based error rate at budget N.

To reflect different testing-resource constraints, results were also analyzed by budget tier: low budget (Top-50 to Top-100), medium budget (Top-150 to Top-200), and high budget (Top-300 to Top-400). This budget-wise analysis allows early prioritization effectiveness, mid-range stability, and large-budget robustness to be examined separately.
