Control of Intestinal Type 3 Immunity by the Unfolded Protein Response Sensor Inositol-Requiring Enzyme 1α in Antigen-Presenting Cells
Authors: Dominique Fernández, Antonia Geisse, Pamela González-Maldonado, Ailiñ Tapia, Francisca Gutiérrez-Ballesteros, Valentina Ugalde, Felipe Tapia, Felipe Flores-Santibañez, Takao Iwawaki, Karina Pino-Lagos, Louis Boon, María Rosa Bono, Carolina Prado, Rodrigo Pacheco, Fabiola Osorio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThe unfolded protein response is an essential cellular program maintaining intestinal homeostasis by acting on several cell types. However, whether the unfolded protein response sensor inositol-requiring enzyme 1α acts on dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells for shaping intestinal immunity remains poorly defined.MethodsHere, we dissect the role of the endoribonuclease domain of the unfolded protein response sensor inositol-requiring enzyme 1α in intestinal CD11c+ antigen-presenting cells, using inositol-requiring enzyme 1α endoribonuclease reporter mice and conditional loss-of-function models for inositol-requiring enzyme 1α and its downstream transcription factor, X-box binding protein 1 spliced.ResultsWe identify robust inositol-requiring enzyme 1α endoribonuclease activity in intestinal conventional dendritic cell subsets, dendritic cells associated with crypto patches and isolated lymphoid follicles, and macrophages, independently of microbial signals. Targeted ablation of inositol-requiring enzyme 1α endoribonuclease in the intestinal CD11c+ compartment provoked spontaneous features of type 3 immunity in the small intestine, characterized by increased antimicrobial peptides and neutrophil recruitment, as well as elevated production of interleukin 17 and interleukin 22 by T helper 17 cells and group 3 innate lymphoid cells. This dysregulated activation required inositol-requiring enzyme 1α endoribonuclease but occurred independently of X-box binding protein 1 spliced, implicating regulated inositol-requiring enzyme 1α–dependent decay rather than canonical X-box binding protein 1 spliced signaling. Mechanistically, distinct inositol-requiring enzyme 1α endoribonuclease–deficient intestinal antigen-presenting cell subtypes such as conventional dendritic cells exhibited increased Il6, Il1b, and Ccl22 transcript levels, whereas conventional dendritic cells and dendritic cells associated with crypto patches and isolated lymphoid follicles showed marked reductions in interleukin 22BP expression, a profile known to increase interleukin 22 bioavailability and enhance T helper 17 cell–group 3 innate lymphoid cell responses.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings uncover inositol-requiring enzyme 1α endoribonuclease in intestinal CD11c+ antigen-presenting cell subtypes as a critical checkpoint restraining type 3 immunity and maintaining intestinal immunity in steady state, highlighting its potential as therapeutic target for intestinal inflammation.Keywords: APCs, Intestine, IRE1, Type 3 Immunity

Summary

This study identifies IRE1 RNase activity in intestinal antigen-presenting cells as a critical regulator of type 3 immunity. Through a mechanism independent of XBP1s, IRE1 restrains inflammatory responses and maintains intestinal homeostasis by modulating cytokine expression and interleukin-22 availability.

What You Need to Know

Background

Intestinal immunity depends on balanced antigen-presenting cell function to prevent inflammation. How the stress-response sensor inositol-requiring enzyme 1α controls these cells to maintain immune equilibrium remains insufficiently understood.

Impact

This work identifies inositol-requiring enzyme 1α endoribonuclease as a key regulator of intestinal type 3 immunity, positioning antigen-presenting cell–intrinsic unfolded protein response pathways as promising therapeutic entry points for further studying intestinal inflammatory disorders.

Future Directions

Future studies should evaluate the pharmacologic modulation of inositol-requiring enzyme 1 endoribonuclease activity, define actionable targets of inositol-requiring enzyme 1α activity in intestinal antigen-presenting cell subtypes, and assess the translational potential in patient-derived tissues and preclinical models of intestinal inflammation.

The intestine is a dynamic organ crucial for maintaining health by balancing nutritional absorption with the detection of harmful agents. Central to this dual function are antigen-presenting cells (APCs), including dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, which are strategically distributed across the intestine. These cells sample environmental antigens for initiation of adaptive immunity or contribute to local responses through crosstalk with resident immune cells, such as innate lymphoid cells (ILCs).1, 2, 3, 4 The intestinal lamina propria harbors several APC subtypes, including conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1, defined as CD103+CD11b−XCR1+ cells) and conventional type 2 DCs (cDC2, identified by CD103−CD11b+ and CD103+CD11b+ profiles),1,5, 6, 7, 8, 9 which orchestrate T-cell tolerance or immunity, depending on the nature of the encountered threat. In addition, intestinal macrophages also support local T-cell responses to commensals and infectious pathogens,10 whereas more recently identified APC subsets such as APCs expressing the transcription factor retinoid-related orphan receptor γt (RORγt), or DCs residing within crypto patches and isolated lymphoid follicles (termed ‘CIA-DCs’) promote tolerance to food antigens or control ILC function, respectively.4,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19

A relevant mechanism safeguarding immune responses at mucosal sites is the so-called type 3 immunity, a program commanded by CD4+ T helper 17 (Th17) cells and type 3 ILCs (ILC3s), which protect the intestinal barrier through production of the effector cytokines interleukin (IL)17 and IL22.1,20,21 This program promotes neutrophil infiltration, expression of antimicrobial peptides and epithelial cell turnover, playing a protective role against infections with extracellular pathogens.21, 22, 23 However, beyond its protective role in infection contexts, exacerbation of type 3 immunity can cause detrimental inflammation and immunopathology.24 In this context, the identification of the APC subtype responsible for regulating intestinal type 3 immunity is a matter of intense research. cDC2s, macrophages, and certain subsets of RORγt+ APCs have been shown to instruct intestinal Th17 responses to distinct antigens, highlighting functional redundancy.7,25, 26, 27 Furthermore, the function of ILC3s can also be regulated by DCs, as ILC3 expansion is regulated by CIA-DCs within local lymphoid follicles via production of IL22BP, a soluble IL22 receptor.4 On mechanistic level, innate signaling pathways linked to Toll-like receptor 5 signaling and regulators of nuclear factor-κB signaling (such as TNFAIP3 and NIK) in DCs have been identified as potential drivers of intestinal Th17 differentiation.8,28, 29, 30, 31 These data indicate that multiple APCs are involved in type 3 immune induction in the gut and that the mechanisms regulating Th17 and ILC3 cell maintenance by APCs remain to be fully understood.

The unfolded protein response (UPR) is a signaling pathway that contributes to intestinal homeostasis by protecting cells from the detrimental effects of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress.32 This cytoprotective response is orchestrated by 3 ER-resident sensors: inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1), activating transcription factor 6, and PKR-like eIF2α kinase, which play vital roles in cell survival, proliferation, and inflammation.33, 34, 35, 36 IRE1, the most conserved UPR sensor with demonstrated roles in immunity,33,37,38 possesses serine and threonine kinase domain–engaging proinflammatory signaling pathways,39, 40, 41, 42, 43 and an endoribonuclease (RNase) domain. The RNase domain of IRE1 processes the transcription factor XBP1 spliced (XBP1s), the master regulator of ER biogenesis.44, 45, 46 IRE1 RNase also cleaves additional messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and pre–micro RNAs (miRNAs) bearing a consensus sequence through a process termed ‘regulated IRE1-dependent decay’ or RIDD.47,48 Interestingly, XBP1s and RIDD play relevant roles in immunity, metabolism, inflammation, and cell survival.49, 50, 51, 52, 53

The IRE1–XBP1s axis is a recognized regulator of intestinal homeostasis through roles that extend beyond proteostatic control.54, 55, 56, 57 Mice bearing the selective loss of XBP1s in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) exhibit Paneth cell impairment, spontaneous enteritis, and Crohn’s disease–like ileitis.58,59 IRE1 activation in IECs also contributes to intestinal tumorigenesis, IgA responses, and Th17 activity via purine metabolism.54, 55, 56 The IRE1–XBP1s axis has also emerged as a regulator of intestinal ILC3 function, influencing mucosal responses.60 Notably, the relevance of the IRE1–XBP1s axis in intestinal human health is underscored by genetic studies identifying XBP1 as a susceptibility gene for Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis.61

Despite these findings, the relevance of IRE1–XBP1s activation in fine tuning the function of intestinal APCs remains to be defined. Nevertheless, previous reports have shown that cDC1s associated with intestinal tissue exhibit elevated IRE1 RNase activation compared with their counterparts from extraintestinal sites.62, 63, 64 Furthermore, in the small intestine lamina propria (SI-LP), IRE1 RNase activation confers survival to cDC1s undergoing ER stress through mechanisms dependent on RIDD activation and inhibition of protein synthesis.64 Despite these findings, it remains unclear whether IRE1 RNase activity in intestinal APC subtypes can shape long-lasting mucosal immune responses.62, 63, 64

Here, we demonstrate that selective ablation of IRE1 RNase in the CD11c-expressing compartment results in spontaneous activation of type 3 immunity in the SI-LP, characterized by increased IL17 and IL22 production, elevated frequencies of Th17 cells, heightened neutrophil infiltration, and enhanced barrier integrity without signs of exacerbated inflammation. Furthermore, IRE1-dependent control of type 3 immunity by CD11c+ cells does not require XBP1s, highlighting a role for RIDD in the regulation of intestinal homeostasis. On the functional level, IRE1 RNase regulates the expression of type 3 polarizing factors by diverse APC subtypes; whereas IL6, C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL-2), and IL1β transcription is increased in cDCs deficient for IRE1 RNase, and the expression of IL22BP is markedly decreased in IRE1-deficient cDC and CIA-DC subtypes. This phenotype is associated with reduced lipid transporter expression and decreased lipid uptake by epithelial cells, recapitulating observations reported in IL22BP-deficient mice. Altogether, these findings highlight the critical role of IRE1 RNase in regulating intestinal type 3 immunity and its potential role as a regulator of lipid metabolic homeostasis in the gut.

Results

Intestinal Antigen-Presenting Cells Exhibit Signs of Inositol-Requiring Enzyme 1α Endoribonuclease Activation

Intestinal cDC1s exhibit heightened IRE1 RNase activity relative to their extraintestinal counterparts.64 Also, gut resident cells such as goblet cells, IECs, and ILC3s show signs of IRE1–XBP1s activation.60,61 Thus, we hypothesized that the intestinal milieu promotes IRE1 RNase activation across intestinal immune cells. To this end, we quantified IRE1 RNase activation utilizing the ERAI transgenic mouse strain65 that reports IRE1 RNase activity through expression of a partial human XBP1 sequence containing the IRE1 splicing sites, fused to Venus fluorescent protein (Venus FP), and validated in multiple reports63,64,66 (Figure 1A). We employed a universal gating strategy of multiparametric flow cytometry (reported in67) (Figure 2A) to compare the VenusFP signal in a range of innate/adaptive immune cell populations and epithelial cells from the SI-LP. Among SI-LP immune cells, cDC1s expressed high levels of VenusFP, in line with reported findings.64 CD103+ and CD103 cDC2s and intestinal macrophages also showed significant expression of VenusFP (Figure 1B and C), feature also observed in natural killer (NK) T cells, whereas IECs exhibited background fluorescence (Figure 1B and C). Additional intestinal immune cells, including monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, NK cells, and B and T lymphocytes, did not express VenusFP signal (Figure 1B and C). Unsupervised t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding analysis performed on CD45+ cells (Figure 1D and E, Figure 2D), revealed that VenusFP expression was mainly restricted to myeloid cell subsets (Figure 1E). ILC3s, which are recently reported to activate the IRE1–XBP1s axis,60 also expressed noticeable VenusFP fluorescence, albeit at lower levels than cDC1s (Figure 1F, Figure 2B and C). Altogether, these data indicate that, in the SI-LP, IRE1 RNase is predominantly active among DC subsets and macrophages, followed by NK, NKT, and ILC3 populations.

In the gut, the IRE1–XBP1s axis is proposed to be induced by multiple triggers, including the microbiota.68 To determine whether IRE1 RNase activation observed in intestinal DCs is microbiota dependent, we measured VenusFP signal in intestinal cDC1s from ERAI mice treated for 10 days with an antibiotic cocktail or a vehicle control. As shown in Figure 1G, VenusFP expression by cDC1s from the SI-LP, mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) and spleen remained unaltered upon antibiotic treatment, suggesting that IRE1 RNase activation in DCs does not rely on bacterial colonization.

Spontaneous Activation of Type 3 Immunity in Duodenum of IRE1TruncDC Mice

We sought to investigate the functional relevance of IRE1 RNase activation in APCs for intestinal immune homeostasis. To this end, we crossed the Itgax-Cre (CD11c-Cre) mice line69 with Ern1 flox/flox mice,70 which express loxP sites flanking exons 19 and 20 of Ern1 gene (coding IRE1). In the intestine, CD11c is expressed by multiple APC subsets including cDCs, macrophages, CIA-DCs, and a fraction of RORγt+ APCs.4,7,13,14,19,71,72 Accordingly, our CD11c–Cre strategy interrogates the CD11c+ compartment in aggregate. The generated mice line, named ‘IRE1TruncDC’ mice, expresses a truncated version of IRE1 lacking the RNase domain in the CD11c+ compartment.63 Experimental controls correspond to littermates Ern1 flox/flox lacking Cre recombinase (‘IRE1WT’). In agreement with previous work,64 IRE1TruncDC mice showed normal composition of CD103+ and CD103− cDC2s (Figures 3A and 5A–C), although some experimental variability was observed. Also, as reported, IRE1TruncDC mice exhibited decreased frequencies of cDC1s in the SI-LP and in the migratory compartment of the MLNs (Figures 3A and 5A–C), consistent with the reported role of IRE1 in controlling cDC1 homeostasis. However, cDC1s and CD103+ and CD103− cDC2 from IRE1TruncDC mice exhibited functional abrogation of IRE1 RNase activity in the SI-LP, evidenced by reduced levels of Xbp1s in these cells compared with their control counterparts (Figure 5D). Macrophages, monocytes, plasmacytoid DCs, B cells, and CD4+ T cells displayed similar frequencies between IRE1TruncDC and control mice, although a mild reduction in CD8+ T cells was noticed in conditional deficient animals (Figure 3A, Figure 5E). Furthermore, comparable total cellularity was found in SI-LP preparations between conditional knockout mice and controls (Figure 5I).

Next, we collected RNA from the small intestine of IRE1TruncDC and IRE1WT control littermates and assessed a range of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Duodenal tissues from IRE1TruncDC mice express normal levels of the proinflammatory factors interferon (IFN)-γ, IL6, IL1β, IL23, and the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL10 and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β compared with their control counterparts (Figure 3B). In contrast, a marked accumulation of IL17 and IL22 transcripts were found in the duodenum from IRE1TruncDC mice relative to controls (Figure 3B). Considering that IL17 and IL22 are hallmark cytokines of type 3 immunity, we investigated whether the small intestine of IRE1TruncDC mice exhibited tissue signs of this immune program. Flow cytometry analysis revealed increased neutrophil accumulation in the SI-LP from IRE1TruncDC mice (Figure 3C), accompanied by elevated transcription of the neutrophil chemoattractants C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (Figure 3D). Also, the small intestine of IRE1TruncDC mice was consistently larger than those of the IRE1WT control littermates (Figure 3E) and showed signs of epithelial hypertrophy (Figure 3F). These features were accompanied by a significant increase in goblet cell numbers (Figure 3G) and increased levels of transcripts encoding antimicrobial proteins Reg3g and Reg3b, which are known barrier-associated epithelial programs engaged upon type 3 immune activation (Figure 3H). Altogether, these data indicate that IRE1 RNase loss in the CD11c+ compartment leads to spontaneous intestinal type 3 immune activation.

Next, we sought to investigate whether the phenotypes observed in IRE1TruncDC mice are attributed to variations in the microbiota. Quantification of 16s ribosomal RNA levels of predominant bacterial genera revealed no significant differences between transgenic and control mice, and similar levels of the Th17 inducer segmented filamentous bacteria were found between transgenic and control mice (Figure 3I–K). These data suggest that the immunologic variations observed between IRE1-deficient and -sufficient animals are unlikely to be driven by microbiota changes.

IRE1TruncDC Mice Exhibit Accumulation of T Helper 17 Cells and Type 3 Innate Lymphoid Cells in the Small Intestine Lamina Propria

To further understand the immune phenotype of IRE1TruncDC mice, cytokine measurements were carried out in duodenum, jejunum, and ileum explants of IRE1TruncDC and control mice. Analysis revealed that IRE1TruncDC mice showed a significant increase of secreted IL17 in the duodenum and jejunum, highlighting that regulation of IRE1 RNase is restricted to the upper-mid small intestine (Figure 4A). Notably, differences in cytokine secretion were only observed for IL17, as other effector cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL4, IL10, and IL2, remained unaltered between IRE1TruncDC and IRE1WT control mice, confirming selective type 3 immune activation without influencing type 1 or type 2 immune programs (Figure 4A).

Next, we sought to investigate whether the induction of type 3 immunity observed in IRE1TruncDC mice is coordinated by Th17 cells and/or ILC3s. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that IRE1TruncDC mice display marked accumulation of IL17-producing CD4+ cells in the SI-LP (Figure 4B). These Th17 cells were primarily IL17 single producers, although some IL17 and IFN-γ double producers were detected (Figure 4B). In the gut, Th17 cells can be subdivided into signaling lymphocytic activation molecule family member 6 (SLAMF6)- and CXC chemokine receptor (CXCR)6-positive populations, which bear stem-like homeostatic properties or effector–pathogenic roles, respectively.73 Whereas Th17 cells from control mice exhibit a balanced proportion of CXCR6+ SLAMF6+ and CXCR6+ SLAMF6− cells, counterparts from IRE1TruncDC mice exhibit a bias towards the CXCR6+SLAMF6− population (Figure 4C). Furthermore, frequencies of IL22-producing CD4+ T cells were also increased in the SI-LP of IRE1TruncDC mice without influencing Foxp3+ regulatory T cell frequencies (Figure 4D and E).

Next, we sought to investigate whether Th17 expansion also occurred in tissues outside the small intestine. Whereas Th17 frequencies in spleen were comparable between IRE1TruncDC and IRE1WT control mice, Th17 accumulation was also noticed in MLNs from IRE1TruncDC mice, albeit to a lower extent than in the SI-LP (Figure 5F and G). These data indicate that IRE1 RNase in CD11c+ APCs selectively regulates Th17 accumulation in the intestinal tissue and associated draining lymph nodes.

Given their central role in mucosal type 3 immunity, we assessed the ILC3 compartment in the SI-LP of IRE1TruncDC mice. Mirroring the phenotype observed in Th17 cells, the SI-LP of IRE1TruncDC mice exhibited significant increase of IL17-producing ILC3s, whereas the total frequencies of ILC3 and IL22 production on a per-cell basis remained unchanged (Figure 4F). By contrast, IL17 production by intestinal γδ T cells was comparable between IRE1TruncDC and control mice (Figure 4G), indicating that not all IL17-producing populations are impacted by IRE1 deficiency in CD11c+ cells. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that loss of IRE1 RNase in CD11c+ cells selectively expands intestinal Th17 and ILC3s, resulting in elevated accumulation of IL17 and IL22 in the SI-LP of IRE1TruncDC mice.

Inositol-Requiring Enzyme 1α Endoribonuclease in CD11c+ Cells Regulates Intestinal Type 3 Immunity Independently of X-Box Binding Protein 1 Spliced Function

Next, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the initiation of type 3 immunity in IRE1TruncDC mice. IRE1 RNase can exert its functions via the XBP1s transcription factor or by RNA decay through the RIDD pathway. To determine whether XBP1s or RIDD accounts for the increased IL17 and IL22 responses, we crossed the Itgax Cre mice line with Xbp1fl/fl animals, which lack the exon 2 of Xbp1s gene, resulting in loss of the XBP1s in CD11c+ cells.74 We referred to these animals as ‘XBP1ΔDC’.63,64,66 Experimental controls correspond to littermates Xbp1 flox/flox lacking Cre recombinase (‘XBP1WT’). XBP1ΔDC mice display a normal composition of cDC subsets in the SI-LP, as reported64 (Figure 6A). XBP1s deletion in cDC1s induces compensatory activation of the RIDD pathway63,64; thereby, to validate effective XBP1s deletion, we assessed the surface expression of CD11c, a surrogate RIDD marker.62 CD11c forms a heterodimer with CD18 (Itgb2), a bona-fide IRE1 substrate, which, upon RIDD mediated degradation, prevents CD11c from reaching the cell surface, a parameter that can be assessed by flow cytometry63,64 (Figure 7A and B). SI-LP cDC1s from XBP1ΔDC mice exhibited a significant reduction in CD11c surface levels, confirming effective XBP1s ablation (Figure 7B).

Next, we functionally evaluated whether XBP1ΔDC mice also exhibit activation of intestinal type 3 immunity. Data depicted in Figure 6B and C revealed that the SI-LP of XBP1ΔDC mice expresses comparable mRNA levels of IL22 and IL17 relative to control littermates without neutrophil accumulation (Figure 6A and B). In contrast to the results observed in IRE1TruncDC mice, XBP1ΔDC mice exhibit reduced Th17 frequencies in the SI-LP relative to XBP1WT controls (Figure 6C) and histologic hematoxylin and eosin analysis of the duodenum of XBP1ΔDC mice revealed no signs of epithelial cell hypertrophy (Figure 6D). Collectively, these data indicate that IRE1 RNase and XBP1s play divergent roles in the induction of intestinal Th17 cells and suggest that the spontaneous activation of intestinal type 3 immunity is attributed to IRE1 RNase-dependent, XBP1s-independent mechanisms operating in gut-associated CD11c+ cells.

We aimed to identify the APC driving type 3 immunity in IRE1TruncDC mice. We first investigated whether IRE1 RNase deficiency alters phenotype and function of intestinal RORγt+ APCs, given their strong association with mucosal immune regulation.11, 12, 13, 14, 15,17 An adapted flow cytometry strategy from15,19 revealed that the frequencies of SI-LP ILC3–major histocompatibility complex class II+ and RORγt+ DCs were comparable between IRE1TruncDC and control mice (Figure 8A). These subsets were also similarly represented in other tissues such as the MLNs and the spleen from control and conditional knockout mice (Figure 8B and C), indicating that IRE1 RNase activity does not regulate ILC3–major histocompatibility complex class II+ and RORγt+ DC frequencies.

Functionally, RORγt+ APCs selectively control the homeostasis and function of intestinal RORγt+ forkhead box P3 (FOXP3)+ double-positive cells, which are involved in promoting tolerance to food and microbiota antigens.11, 12, 13, 14, 15,17 Analysis of RORγt and FOXP3 expression within the CD4+ T cell compartment revealed significant expansion of RORγt+FOXP3− but not RORγt+FOXP3+ or RORγt−FOXP3+ T cells in the SI-LP of IRE1TruncDC mice (Figure 8D). Thus, we conclude that IRE1 deletion does not regulate canonical functions of RORγt+ APCs. These findings align with the phenotype recently reported for the conditional deletion of IRE1 RNase in the Rorc+ compartment, which decreases IL17 production by ILC3s,60 contrasting with the phenotype observed for the IRE1TruncDC mice displayed here. Collectively, these findings suggest that IRE1 RNase has distinct roles in Rorc+ vs CD11c+ compartments.

Inositol-Requiring Enzyme 1α Endoribonuclease Loss Increases Expression of T Helper 17 Polarizing Factors by Conventional Dendritic Cell Subtypes

cDC subtypes can prime intestinal Th17 immunity.7,25,28,29 We evaluated whether IRE1 RNase loss predisposes cDCs for type 3 immune activation. We assessed expression of surface immunoregulatory molecules programmed death-ligand 1, CD80, and CD86, which were comparable between cDC1s (Figure 9A) and cDC2s (Figure 9B) from IRE1TruncDC and control littermates. We next interrogated the antigen presentation capacity of IRE1TruncDC mice upon transfer of CD4+ T cells from the transgenic OT-II mice, (which express a TCR recognizing a peptide derived from ovalbumin [OVA]), followed by OVA administration through an intragastrical route (Figure 9C). Whereas OT-II T cells exhibit similar proliferative capacity in the MLNs and Peyer Patches of IRE1TruncDC and control mice, a mild but significant proliferative reduction was noticed in the SI-LP of conditional knockout animals (Figure 9C). These data suggest that IRE1TruncDC mice display unaltered initial antigen recognition by CD4+ T cells in lymph nodes but show moderate impairment in intestinal tissue accumulation, expansion, or survival.

Next, we evaluated whether IRE1 RNase deficiency regulates the expression of Th17 polarizing factors by cDCs. To this end, we sorted cDC1a and CD103+ and CD103− cDC2s from the SI-LP of IRE1TruncDC and control mice and quantified cytokines involved in type 3 immune induction, including IL6, TGF-β, IL23, IL1β and CCL22.75,76 Analysis revealed that cDC1s and CD103+cDC2s from IRE1TruncDC mice displayed elevated levels of IL6 mRNA (Figure 9D). In addition, TGF-β mRNA remained unaltered between IRE1TruncDC and IRE1WT control mice, whereas IL23(p19) transcript showed a trend toward increase in cDC1s from IRE1TruncDC mice (Figure 9D). Notably, additional factors boosting Th17 cell development/recruitment such as IL1β mRNA and CCL22, were also upregulated in the CD103− cDC2 subset from IRE1TruncDC mice (Figure 9D). In addition, the expression of retinaldehyde dehydrogenase mRNA, a key enzyme involved in retinoic acid metabolism for regulatory T cell generation,77 was significantly reduced in cDC1s from IRE1TruncDC mice (Figure 9E). These data indicate that IRE1-deficient intestinal cDCs are reprogrammed to induce the expression of factors promoting Th17 cell induction and recruitment to the gut.

Interleukin22 Binding Protein Expression Is Regulated by Inositol-Requiring Enzyme 1α Endoribonuclease in Intestinal Dendritic Cells

We next focused on studying the mechanisms accounting for the expansion of ILC3 in IRE1TruncDC mice. A circuit regulating ILC3 homeostasis is controlled by CIA-DCs, a specific subset residing in crypto patches and isolated lymphoid follicles.4 CIA-DCs express CD11c and regulate ILC3 homeostasis through production of IL22BP (also known as IL22RA2), a soluble decoy receptor that blocks bioactive IL22, regulating ILC3 hubs.4,78,79 IL22BP is also produced by cDCs, emerging as a relevant control of IL22-mediated responses.77 Expression analysis revealed a significant reduction in Il22ra2 levels in duodenal tissue from IRE1TruncDC mice relative to controls (Figure 10A). Furthermore, IRE1-deficient cDC1 and CD103+ and CD103− cDC2 subsets also exhibited reduced expression of Il22ra2 compared with their control counterparts, with statistical significance noticed in the CD103− cDC2 subset (Figure 10B).

We next sought to assess CIA-DC function. These cells are contained within the CD103- cDC2 gate and can be identified by placenta expressed transcript 1 expression.4,80 We interrogated whether CIA-DCs induce IRE1 RNase activity. Using ERAI mice, we observed that CIA-DCs express high levels of VenusFP, comparable to CD103+ and CD103− cDC2s (Figure 10C). In addition, CIA-DCs frequencies were similarly represented between IRE1TruncDC and control mice (Figure 10D), indicating that IRE1 does not control CIA-DC differentiation and homing into the gut. Next, we analyzed expression of IL22BP on protein levels using flow cytometry (Figure 10E). Notably, analysis revealed that CIA-DCs, alongside cDC1s and CD103+ and CD103− cDC2s from IRE1TruncDC mice expressed significantly decreased levels of IL22BP relative to controls (Figure 10E). Decreased IL22BP expression is expected to increase IL22 bioavailability, resulting in the induction of IL22 target genes associated with the type 3 immune program.81 Indeed, in addition to the increased expression of Reg3g and Reg3b, which are IL22-dependent genes (Figure 3H), we also observed an increased expression of Saa1/2 and Muc2 (which depend on IL22 signaling82, 83, 84, 85), in the duodenal tissue of IRE1TruncDC mice. Altogether, these results demonstrate that IRE1 RNase deletion results in the broad reduction of IL22BP production by multiple APC subsets with a concomitant increase in the expression of IL22-dependent genes in the SI-LP.

IRE1TruncDC Mice Exhibit Altered Lipid Uptake and Reduced Expression of CD36

Activation of intestinal type 3 immunity can impact physiological responses on the organ level. It is reported that mice models of unrestrained ILC3 expansion, induced by the deletion of adaptive immunity or upon IL22BP deficiency, exhibit impaired lipid metabolism and decreased intestinal lipid transporter expression.4,86 Thus, considering our findings, we investigated whether IRE1TruncDC mice exhibit alterations in intestinal lipid uptake. We observed that Cd36 mRNA expression levels were consistently reduced in the duodenum and jejunum of IRE1TruncDC mice compared with their control littermates, aligning with observations in IL22BP knockout mice4 (Figure 11A). Furthermore, lipid uptake experiments in intestinal epithelial cells using fluorescently labeled bodipy revealed decreased lipid absorption by epithelial cells derived from IRE1TruncDC mice relative to controls (Figure 11B and C). Interestingly, the differences were more prominently noticed in epithelial cells derived from duodenal and jejunal areas, in line with the duodenal accumulation of IL17 noticed in IRE1TruncDC mice (Figure 11A). These results suggest that IRE1 RNase in the CD11c-compartment controls lipid absorption by IECs, recapitulating lipid uptake features associated with unrestrained ILC3 responses and IL22BP loss in the gut.4,86

Finally, ER stress in IECs also exacerbates Th17 differentiation in the gut, by mechanisms dependent on reactive oxygen species production and purine metabolites generated by the DUOX2–DUOXA2 system.57 Interestingly, we observed that expression of transcripts coding DUOX–DUOXA2 enzymes were significantly elevated in IECs from IRE1TruncDC mice relative to control cells, highlighting tight reprogramming of the intestinal milieu IRE1TruncDC mice (Figure 11D). Notably, these findings are not due to IECs being directly targeted by the CD11c-driven Cre recombinase, as these cells do not delete the exon 19 and 20 of the Ern1 gene (data not shown). These findings indicate that IRE1 RNase deletion in CD11c+ potentiates intestinal type 3 immunity and organ function via direct reprogramming of intestinal APC subtypes, and in-trans mechanisms operating on IECs.

Discussion

The IRE1–XBP1s axis of the UPR is increasingly recognized as a central regulator of intestinal homeostasis, by acting on multiple cell types including IECs, Paneth cells, and ILC3s.60,61,87,88 Here, we uncovered an unappreciated role of IRE1 RNase in CD11c+ APCs as a key determinant of intestinal immune balance. Our findings build upon previous findings demonstrating that IRE1 RNase is markedly active in intestinal cDC1s and partially active in cDC2s,64 but extends these observations to additional APCs such as CIA-DCs and intestinal macrophages. Notably, IRE1 activation occurs even in antibiotic presence, suggesting that nonbacterial, endogenous, or nutritional cues may sustain its activity under homeostatic conditions.

A novel aspect derived from this work is the functional consequence of IRE1 activity in CD11c-APCs, leading to spontaneous activation of intestinal type 3 immunity, and accompanied by Th17 expansion, elevated IL17 and IL22 production, neutrophil recruitment, antimicrobial peptides, and epithelial reprogramming. These findings demonstrate that, in basal conditions, the RNase domain of IRE1 in APCs actively controls Th17 and ILC3 function to maintain intestinal immune balance. The selective accumulation of Th17 cells in the small intestine and MLNs, with no corresponding increases in the spleen, suggests that IRE1 RNase in CD11c+ cells regulates the local maintenance and expansion of IL17+ CD4+ T cells within the intestinal milieu. However, it is relevant to note that the epithelial program of IRE1TruncDC mice was associated with barrier protection without overt inflammation. Furthermore, the activation of type 3 immunity observed in the small intestine of IRE1TruncDC mice appears to be selective rather than associated with a global expansion of immune cells, as total immune cell numbers did not differ between control and conditional-deficient animals. A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size in the analysis of ILC3 (n = 10 per group), which was lower than the number estimated a priori to achieve 80% power. Nevertheless, the observed large effect size and statistically significant between-group difference in IL17 production support the findings, whereas validation in a larger cohort is warranted. On the other hand, expression of DUOX–DUOX2 transcripts in IECs suggests tight epithelial reprogramming as a consequence of IRE1 deletion in APCs, with implications in Th17 induction.57 Future work should directly assess intestinal permeability and clarify whether pre-existing type 3 immunity poises the tissue towards inflammatory and autoimmune features upon challenge.

In addition, this study reveals that the IRE1 control over type 3 immunity via APCs does not require the transcription factor XBP1s, as XBP1ΔDC mice do not replicate the phenotype observed in IRE1TruncDC mice, suggesting that IRE1 RNase in APCs may restrain type 3 immunity through selective miRNA and mRNA degradation via RIDD. Along these lines, pre-miRNA preservation upon RIDD inhibition has been previously observed in hepatocytes.89 Future studies should identify specific miRNA and mRNA substrates of IRE1 competent to fine-tune intestinal immunity via APCs. Along these lines, our findings provide a relevant functional insight that decouples IRE1 RNase functions from the well-known XBP1s-associated mechanisms reported for other intestinal cells. For instance, intestinal ILC3s require an intact IRE1 RNase–XBP1s pathway for optimal production of IL22,60 and XBP1s deficiency in IECs enhances Th17 responses through ROS generation and increased purine metabolite synthesis.57 Our study shows that IRE1 RNase loss in CD11c+ cells similarly augments type 3 immune responses, albeit independent of XBP1s presence, highlighting the multifaceted and cell-type–specific roles of the IRE1–XBP1s pathway and reinforcing its role as a key regulator of type 3 immunity.

Furthermore, we describe that IRE1 deficiency reprograms the profile of intestinal DCs toward a pro-type 3 profile. IRE1-deficient SI-LP cDCs increase IL6, IL1β, and CCL22 transcription, all of which are known to be involved in the priming and recruitment of Th17 cells.7,25,29 In addition, IRE1 RNase deficiency also led to a pronounced reduction of IL22BP expression by cDC subtypes and CIA-DCs. IL22BP is a well-described negative regulator of IL22 bioavailability and ILC3 functions,81 reported to be produced by various subtypes of intestinal APCs.4,79,90,91,94Therefore, decreased IL22BP levels are expected to increase type 3 immune response in IRE1TruncDC mice. In line with these findings, IRE1TruncDC mice phenocopy several features of IL22BP knockout mice, such as the reduced expression of CD36 and the impaired lipid uptake.4,92 Future studies manipulating the IL22–IL22BP in IRE1TruncDC mice are required to establish the necessity or sufficiency for the lipid uptake defects.

Overall, our findings suggest that the tissue responses observed in IRE1TruncDC mice arise from reprogramming of multiple APC subtypes, particularly cDCs and CIA-DCs. This is relevant considering that the identity of the APC driving gut Th17 induction has remained inconclusive. Intestinal Th17 cells persist upon deletion of cDC1s, cDC2s, or both, whereas CSF1R-dependent macrophages regulate intestinal Th17 numbers.10 It is important to note that IRE1TruncDC mice show decreased numbers of SI-LP and migratory cDC1s in the MLNs.64 However, the effects observed here are unlikely to be driven only by decreased cDC1 frequencies as cDC1 loss impairs intestinal Th1 but not Th17 responses.93 Furthermore, the unaltered Foxp3+/RORγt+ compartment in IRE1TruncDC mice also indicates that RORγt+ APCs are not main IRE1 targets under steady state conditions, underscoring a selective regulatory function of IRE1 RNase in intestinal APC subsets. However, as the CD11c–Cre mice line targets several APCs and small populations of other cell types such as NK cells,94 future work using cDCs, CIA-DCs, and macrophage- and monocyte-restricted Cre lines will be required to ascribe cell-intrinsic causality.

IL17 and IL22 are elevated in samples of patients with ulcerative colitis, with ER stress emerging as a common denominator of intestinal inflammation.95,96 However, the extent of IRE1 RNase activation, considering RIDD and XBP1s outputs, remain to be defined. Importantly, IL17A blockade with a monoclonal antibody (secukinumab) has not benefited patients with inflammatory bowel disease in clinical trials and showed unfavorable responses,97 underscoring the complexity of type 3 immunity in intestinal pathology. Given that IL22 is reported to protect mice from inflammatory bowel disease,98 precise modulation of IRE1 RNase activity in gut DCs may represent an interesting therapeutic avenue to enhance protective IL22 and IL17 responses in absence of pathologic inflammation, warranting further investigations.

To sum up, our study identifies IRE1 in CD11c+ APCs as a central regulator of intestinal immune balance. These findings not only redefine the complex roles of the UPR in intestinal immunity, but also open new therapeutic avenues aimed at fine-tuning IRE1 signaling in APCs to control barrier protection and restore mucosal equilibrium.

Material and Methods

Study Approval

All animal procedures were approved by bioethical committees and performed in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal care of the Fundación Ciencia y Vida and Universidad San Sebastian and the Faculty of Medicine, University of Chile.

Mice

ERAI,65 XBP1WT (XBP1fl/fl74), XBP1ΔDC (XBP1fl/fl x CD11c-Cre69), IRE1WT (IRE1fl/fl70), IRE1TruncDC (IRE1fl/fl x CD11c-Cre), and OT-II mice were bred at Universidad de Chile and Fundación Ciencia y Vida in specific pathogen-free conditions. All mice were kept on a C57BL/6 background. Litters with mice of both sexes at 8 to 14 weeks of age were used for experiments.

Antibiotic Treatment

WT and ERAI mice were administered an antibiotic cocktail for 10 consecutive days via oral gavage. Each treatment consisted of 200 μL of autoclaved water, either alone or supplemented with the following antibiotics: ampicillin (1 mg/mL), kanamycin (1 mg/mL), gentamicin (1 mg/mL), metronidazole (1 mg/mL), neomycin (1 mg/mL), and vancomycin (0.5 mg/mL). Then, mice were sacrificed, and samples from organs were collected for further analysis.

Preparation of Cell Suspensions

Spleen and MLNs were minced and digested with collagenase D (1 mg/mL, Roche) and DNAse I (50 μg/mL, Roche) for 45 minutes at 37 °C in a water bath. Digested tissue was then passed through a 70-mm cell strainer, and single cells were kept on ice. For spleens, red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (Biolegend).

Intestines were cleaned with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS), and fat and Peyer’s patches were removed. Epithelial cells and intraepithelial lymphocytes were removed by incubating the tissues for 20 minutes at 37 °C with constant stirring at 100 rpm in serum-free medium (RPMI 1640 medium with 25 mM HEPES and 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with 0.94 mM dithiothreitol and 5 mM EDTA. The buffer was discarded, and the tissues were minced and incubated in serum-free medium supplemented with liberase TL (0.1 mg/mL, Roche) and DNAse I (0.5 μg/mL, Roche) for 30 minutes at 37 °C with constant stirring at 100 rpm. Digested tissue was then passed through a 100-μm cell strainer. Leukocytes were enriched by density gradient centrifugation with 40/75 Percoll (Cytiva) for 20 minutes at 600 × g.

Flow Cytometry and Cell Sorting

For surface staining, cells were incubated with anti-Fc receptor antibody and then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] + 1% fetal bovine serum [FBS] + 2mM EDTA) for 30 minutes at 4 °C. Viability was assessed by staining with fixable viability Zombie (BioLegend) or LIVE/DEAD fixable (Invitrogen). Flow cytometry was performed on BD Fortessa LSR, BD FACS Verse, Cytek Northern Lights, or Cytek Aurora instruments. Analysis of flow cytometry data was done using FlowJo or Omiq software. Cell sorting was performed using a BD FACS Aria II.

Transcription Factors Intracellular Staining

After surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Foxp3 transcription factor staining set (eBioscience) followed by intracellular staining of transcription factors (Foxp3 and RORγt) as indicated by the manufacturer protocol.

T Cell Stimulation and Intracellular Cytokine Staining

SI-LP, MLN, and spleen cell suspensions were stimulated ex vivo prior to staining with 0.25 μM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma) and 1 μg/mL ionomycin (Sigma) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 3.5 hours in the presence of 1 μL/mL Brefeldin A (BD GolgiPlug). After stimulation, cells were surface stained as mentioned above. Then, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm fixation and permeabilization kit (BD Biosciences) followed by the intracellular staining of cytokines (IFN-γ, IL17, and IL22) as indicated by the manufacturer protocol.

Adoptive T Cell Transfer

CD4+ T cells were purified from the spleens of OT-II mice. The spleen was perfused with RPMI + 10% FBS, and red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (Biolegend). CD4+ T cells were isolated using the CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified CD4+ T cells were stained with CellTrace Violet (Invitrogen) at 5 μM. Labeled OT-II CD4+ T cells were resuspended in PBS, and 2 × 106 CD4+ T cells were intravenously transferred into WT and IRE1TruncDC mice.

Oral Ovalbumin Administration

OVA (A5503; Sigma-Aldrich) was administered orally using plastic gavage needles. Fifty milligrams in 200 μL PBS OVA was administered on days 1 and 2 after OT-II CD4+ T cell transfer, and 25 mg in 100 μL PBS OVA on days 3 and 4 until the experiment end point on day 5.

Ex Vivo Assay for Lipid Absorption by Intestinal Epithelial Cells by Flow Cytometry

Lipid absorption by intestinal epithelial cells was performed as previously described.4 Briefly, the small intestine was collected, and tissue was cleaned from feces and fat. The small intestine was sectioned into the proximal third (duodenum), middle third (jejunum), and distal third (ileum). The epithelial layer was dissociated by incubating the tissue for 40 minutes in 10 mL HBSS supplemented with 5 mM EDTA and 10 mM HEPES at 37 °C with constant stirring at 100 rpm. Following this, mechanical isolation of the epithelial fraction was obtained by vigorous vortexing of the tissue for 40 seconds. The tissue was discarded, and the entire epithelial fraction was collected and washed twice with 10 mL cold PBS. The washed epithelial cells were then filtered using a 100-μm cell strainer. The epithelial cells were stained with CD45, CD306 (EPCAM), and LIVE/DEAD fixable (Invitrogen) for 20 minutes at 4 °C. Following staining, the cells were washed with PBS + 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and resuspended in 150 μL of warm HBSS + 0.1% fatty acid–free BSA + Bodipy FL C12 2 μM (D3822, Invitrogen) and incubated in the water bath at 37 °C for 30 minutes. The lipid absorption assay was stopped using ice-cold PBS + 0.2% BSA. The cells were analyzed immediately.

RNA Isolation and Complementary DNA Generation

Total RNA was extracted from sorted cDCs from SI-LP with the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen) and from murine intestinal tissue with Trizol reagent (Life Technologies) after the homogenization of the tissue using 3-mm zirconium beads and the BeadBug homogenizer. RNA from the duodenum was reverse-transcribed with a mixture of Random Hexamer Primer (Thermofisher) and with the M-MLV reverse transcriptase Kit (Invitrogen). Complementary DNA from sorted cells was synthesized and amplified using the Ovation PicoSL WTA System V2 (Nugen).

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

qPCR was performed with a SYBR Green PCR Master Mix kit (Applied Biosystems). Quantification of the PCR signals of each sample was performed by comparing the cycle threshold (Ct) values, in duplicate, of the gene of interest with the Ct values of the L27 housekeeping gene. See Supplementary Table 2 for primers used for qPCR.

Cytokine Secretion Measurement

Tissue sections 1 cm in length of the different intestinal segments were placed into RPMI medium (Corning) containing 2% FBS and incubated for 24 hours at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The supernatants were collected and stored before analysis at −80 °C. The supernatants were analyzed using the CBA Kit Mouse Th1/Th2/Th17 (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histology

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections of the small intestine were stained with hematoxylin and eosin or Periodic acid-Schiff. The number of goblet cells (Periodic acid-Schiff+ cells along the villus length) was determined under light microscopy; 10 villi from at least 2 sections per animal were evaluated.

16S Ribosomal RNA Gene Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction Analysis

Fecal pellets were collected from IRE1TruncDC and control littermates and frozen at −80 °C. Fecal material was subjected to the QIAmp Fast DNA Stool isolation kit (Qiagen) following the provider’s instructions. Total DNA was quantified using Nanodrop One C (Thermo Fisher), and 10 ng/mL was used to set up the qPCR reaction. The primers for the bacterial genes are indicated in.99 For analysis, relative expression was determined using the ΔΔCt method, using 16S as a housekeeping control. To calculate the relative abundance, we considered the sum of all bacteria genera tested as total microbiota or 100% and then obtained the relative presence for each one. Segmented filamentous bacteria primers were also included in this analysis.100

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (v9.1.2). Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. To ensure a normal distribution, data were subjected to the Shapiro–Wilk test. Two groups were compared using the 2-tailed t test with Welch’s correction (if the 2 populations do not have the same standard deviation) or using a nonparametric 2-tailed Mann–Whitney test as indicated in figure legends. Multiple groups were compared using 1-way analysis of variance with Šídák's post-test or using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test as indicated in figure legends. A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.
