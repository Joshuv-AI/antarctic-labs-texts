Regulation and functional analysis of BvMBF1c in response to Cercospora leaf spot (CLS) stresses in sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.)
Authors: Hongyong Lou, Guangzhou Ding, Chunlei Zhao, Yanli Li, Changmei Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Multiprotein Bridging Factor 1 (MBF1) gene family, a conserved transcriptional coactivator central to plant stress adaptation, has been systematically characterized in this study across diverse species, including the economically critical crop sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.). Phylogenetic analysis delineated two evolutionarily distinct clades: Group I (MBF1a/b) with conserved exon-intron structures and Group II (MBF1c) exhibiting intron-poor architectures, a feature linked to rapid transcriptional activation under stress. In sugar beet, BvMBF1c demonstrated nuclear localization and pathogen-responsive expression dynamics, peaking within 48 h post-Cercospora beticola (C. beticola) inoculation. Yeast two-hybrid assays confirmed a novel interaction between BvMBF1c and trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS5), implicating its role in osmotic regulation through trehalose biosynthesis—a mechanism critical for mitigating pathogen-induced cellular stress. Synteny and collinearity analyses revealed lineage-specific duplication events, underscoring the role of genomic plasticity in stress adaptation. Notably, MBF1c homologs displayed conserved microsynteny in woody perennials (Populus trichocarpa, Citrus sinensis) but diverged in monocots, reflecting adaptive evolution to species-specific environmental pressures. This study integrates evolutionary, structural, and functional insights to position MBF1c as a molecular hub bridging transcriptional regulation with metabolic homeostasis, offering actionable targets for breeding climate-resilient crops. Our findings highlight BvMBF1c as a candidate for enhancing disease resistance in sugar beet, with broader implications for improving stress tolerance in agronomically vital species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07567-7.Keywords: Sugar beet, MBF1, Cercospora beticola sacc, Disease resistance

Introduction

Sugar beet (B. vulgaris L.), a biennial herbaceous plant within the Amaranthaceae family, originates from coastal regions of Europe and North Africa [1]. As a cornerstone of global sugar production, it contributes approximately 30% of the world’s sucrose supply [2, 3]. Recent taxonomic revisions delineate four cultivated varietal groups based on integrated phenotypic and phylogenomic evidence: leaf beet (B. vulgaris subsp. vulgaris var. cicla), garden beet (var. conditiva), fodder beet (var. crassa), and sugar beet (var. altissima) [4–6]. China, as a major global producer, cultivates sugar beet across 229,300 hectares, where it supplies 30–40% of the nation’s sugar–second only to sugarcane in domestic production [3, 7].

Despite advances in breeding and agronomy that enhance yield and sucrose content, sugar beet remains vulnerable to multifaceted stressors. Among these, Cercospora beticola-induced Cercospora leaf spot (CLS) poses a pervasive biotic threat in warm-humid regions like the U.S. Upper Midwest and Central Europe [8–11]. CLS reduces root sucrose by over 42%, impairs juice purity, elevates processing costs [12–14], and can cause extractable sucrose losses exceeding 40–100% during severe outbreaks [15, 16]. Consequently, deciphering the sugar beet-C. beticola interaction is critical for developing novel disease management strategies.

The Multiprotein Bridging Factor 1 (MBF1) family, a class of evolutionarily conserved transcriptional coactivators, plays a pivotal role in plant stress adaptation by integrating environmental signals into transcriptional reprogramming. Structurally, MBF1 proteins exhibit a bipartite architecture: a dynamic N-terminal domain that binds transcription factors (TFs) and a conserved C-terminal domain. This C-terminal domain, identified in the Pfam database as the aMBF1 (archaeal MBF1) domain (PF08523), adopts a helix-turn-helix (HTH) structure that facilitates dimerization and DNA interaction [17–19]. This modular design enables MBF1 to act as a molecular bridge, it achieves this by serving as a molecular bridge that connects the TATA box - binding protein (TBP) with specific transcription factors [20], thereby amplifying the expression of defense genes under abiotic and biotic stresses [17]. The MBF1 family comprises three subfamilies—MBF1a, MBF1b, and MBF1c [21]—these coactivators show broad spatial expression patterns across roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, and seeds [17, 22–24], reflecting their dual roles in development and stress responses. Recent studies have shown that overexpression of MBF1 can increase the resistance and function of Arabidopsis [25, 26], tomato [27], chrysanthemum [28] and Chinese pepper [29], this aligns with findings in rice, where OsMBF1c expression surged sevenfold in shoots under cadmium (Cd) stress [30], correlating with enhanced detoxification pathways. Meanwhile, in potato (Solanum tuberosum), MBF1c mediates resistance to bacterial wilt through its interaction with trehalose-phosphate synthase 5 [31]. MBF1a/b regulate developmental processes, such as flowering time modulation and seed yield enhancement [32]. Despite these advancements, researchers have also demonstrated the potential of using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to edit the CLS resistance genes (SP1 and SP2 encode acid chitinase, SE1 encodes chitinase, SE2 encodes exochitinase, qcr1 and qcr4 are quantitative resistance loci, and the insecticidal protein genes Cry1Ab and Cry1C) [33–36]. However, the role of MBF1 in the resistance of sugar beets (B. vulgaris) to Cercospora beticola, a destructive fungal pathogen, remains unclear.

To address this knowledge gap, we systematically identified MBF1 homologs across five representative plants-Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), rice (Oryza sativa), poplar (Populus trichocarpa), sweet orange (Citrus sinensis), and sugar beet (B. vulgaris)—and conducted comprehensive bioinformatic analyses of the gene family. We employed a multi-faceted experimental approach to elucidate the function of BvMBF1. First, we measured phytohormone levels in sugar beet upon pathogen challenge. The expression dynamics of BvMBF1 were then validated through a combination of transcriptome data and qPCR analysis. Furthermore, subcellular localization experiments provided crucial spatial information regarding the protein’s function within the cell. Finally, yeast two-hybrid assays were conducted to identify proteins that interact with BvMBF1, thereby uncovering potential partners in the disease resistance signaling pathway. This integrated strategy allowed us to gain a comprehensive understanding of the protein’s mechanisms and functions, provides a fundamental insight into the MBF1-mediated defense mechanism in beet, and lays a theoretical framework for future disease resistance breeding.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification of MBF1 genes

Genomic data for Arabidopsis thaliana (TAIR11 assembly) [37], Oryza sativa (IRGSP v7.0) [38], and Populus trichocarpa (v4.1) [39] were retrieved from the Joint Genome Institute’s Plant Portal (https://genome.jgi.doe.gov/portal/pages), while Citrus sinensis (v2.0 HBvU) [40] and Beta vulgaris [41] genomes were sourced from the Citrus Genome Database (https://www.citrusgenomedb.org) and the Sugar Beet Genome Resource (https://bvseq.boku.ac.at/), respectively. MBF1 homologs were systematically identified across these species using iTAK v1.6a for transcription factor prediction [42], followed by domain validation via Pfam [43] (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org/). Coding sequences (CDS) and genomic DNA were extracted from corresponding GFF3 files using SeqKit [44], with subsequent protein characterization—including molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY)—performed via the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam) [45]. The deduced amino acid sequence of BvMBF1c and its homologs from other species were aligned using ClustalX [46], and the resulting alignment was visualized with DNAMAN software. This integrated pipeline ensured consistent identification and annotation of MBF1 family members, leveraging both sequence homology and structural domain conservation.

Evolutionary relationship of MBF1 protein

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of the MBF1 protein family, 55 MBF1 homologs from 25 representative plant species were retrieved from the Phytozome v13 database [47]. All sequences exhibited conserved MBF1 and helix-turn-helix (HTH) domains, as validated by Pfam (PF 08523) and InterPro (IPR001387) annotations. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA11 with incorporation 1,000 bootstrap replicates [48].

Gene structure and subcellular localization analysis

We analyzed gene structures across five species—Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Populus trichocarpa, Citrus sinensis, and Beta vulgaris—using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS; https://gsds.gao-lab.org/) to map exon-intron organization and untranslated regions (UTRs) [49]. Conserved protein motifs were identified via MEME Suite v5.4.1 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) with default parameters, revealing lineage-specific motif distributions, particularly in the N-terminal regulatory region [50, 51]. Structural domains were annotated through the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) [52] and visualized using TBtools [53], confirming the universal presence of the helix-turn-helix (HTH) domain (cd00093) and MBF1_C domain (pfam08523) across all homologs. Subcellular localization predictions performed via deeploc-2.1. (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.1/) [54] and SignalP-3.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/) [55].

The pHK-35 S-GLosGFP vector is a plant subcellular localization vector containing the strong promoter of the Cauliflower Mosaic Virus (CaMV) 35 S (Boyuan Biotech. china). The fusion vectors pHK-35 S-GLosGFP-BvMBF1c and pHK-35 S-GLosGFP-BvMBF1b are used to detect the subcellular localization of BvMBF1c and BvMBF1b, respectively. The sequences of BvMBF1c and BvMBF1b were amplified by PCR, with the upstream homologous arms of the vector added to the 5 ‘end of the insert fragment’s upstream primer, and the downstream homologous arms of the vector inserted into the 5 ‘end of the insert fragment’s downstream primer BvMBF1c-F and BvMBF1c-R. The corresponding fragments were then recovered and homologously recombined to generate pHK-35S-GLosGFP-BvMBF1c and pHK-35S-GLosGFP-BvMBF1b. Both pHK-35S-GLosGFP-BvMBF1c, pHK-35S-GLosGFP-BvMBF1b, and the 35S::GFP plasmid were transiently transformed into tobacco leaves via Agrobacterium tumefaciens injection. Fluorescence signals were detected using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM780, Zeiss, Germany).

Chromosomal location, gene replication and synteny

Chromosomal localization and synteny analyses were conducted across five species (A. thaliana, O. sativa, P. trichocarpa, C. sinensis, and B. vulgaris). Chromosomal coordinates of MBF1 homologs were mapped using TBtools v2.210 [53] with genome annotation files, revealing species-specific distribution patterns. Collinearity analysis via MCScanX (E-value ≤ 1e − 10, minimum aligned = 5) identified tandem duplication events [56].

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements and three-dimensional structure of proteins

We extracted 2000 bp upstream sequences of MBF1 genes using SeqKit2 [57] and analyzed cis-regulatory elements via PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/) [58], All cis-regulatory elements related to abiotic stress and plant hormones were identified and displayed by TBtools software [53]. Parallelly, the tertiary structure of BvMBF1 was modeled using SWISS-MODEL [59].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

We selected the highly resistant variety (81GM241) and the low-resistant variety (KWS6661) for this study. The beet seeds were sterilized and then placed on sterilized filter paper in petri dishes to germinate. The soil used in the experiment was nutrient soil purchased from a specialized company, sterilized with γ-rays, and stored sealed. Each pot contained 0.5 kg of soil. The pathogenic fungus used in this experiment was C. beticola, isolated and purified from beet leaves. A 10^6 spores/ml suspension was prepared using sterile water. The two beet varieties, 81GM241 and KWS6661, were divided into the diseased group and the control group. After 3 days of germination, healthy seedlings were transplanted into pots filled with sterile soil. Each treatment group consisted of 6 pots, totaling 48 pots, with 2–3 beet plants per pot. The plants were watered every 3 days and placed in an inoculation room with a 16/8 hours light cycle, a temperature difference of 28 °C and 19 °C between day and night, and a relative humidity of 65% to 90%. Six weeks after the beet plants had grown, C. beticola was sprayed onto the plants, with 10 ml of the C. beticola spore suspension applied to each pot. The control group received 10 ml of sterile water per pot. Leaves were collected from different parts of the beet plants on 0,10,20, and 30 days post-inoculation (dpi), frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at-80 °C for the determination of gene expression and hormone levels. Each treatment group was replicated three times.

Total RNA was isolated from 100 mg of leaf tissue using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara Bio, Japan), incorporating on-column DNase I treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination [60]. RNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio > 1.9) were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with 1 µg total RNA using PrimeScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Takara Bio) and oligo(dT) primers, following manufacturer protocols. Gene-specific primers for BvMBF1 were designed via Primer3Plus [61], and the length of the amplified product was 100–300 bp. Quantitative RT-PCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara Bio) under optimized conditions. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method [62], with Bvactin as the reference gene and 0 h samples as calibrators. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) was determined by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test in SPSS v26 (IBM), while data visualization utilized GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (121) for enhanced reproducibility [63].

Validation of yeast two-hybrid interaction in the Y2HGold-GAL4 system

To verify protein interactions, the prey and bait vectors containing specific genes were co-transferred into Y2HGold chemically competent cells (Beijing Coolaber Technology Co., Ltd., China). Positive yeast clones expressing bait-prey interaction proteins were selected from the SD/-Trp-Leu medium. The 2–3 mm diameter single colonies were resuspended in 1 ml of 0.9% sodium chloride solution, serially diluted, and re-inoculated onto SD/-Trp-Leu and SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade media (Coolaber, China) to test the bait-prey interaction.

Quantification of jasmonic acid and Salicylic acid by HPLC-MS/MS

Phytohormone quantification was performed on sugar beet (B. vulgaris) leaf samples collected from potted plants, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Hormone extraction was performed by weighing 0.2 g of tissue into glass tubes, adding 5.0 mL of acetonitrile and 30 µL of internal standard solution, followed by overnight extraction at 4 °C. After centrifugation (7,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C; H-1850R High-Speed Centrifuge, Hunan Kecheng Instrument, China), supernatants were collected, and residues were re-extracted with 3.0 mL acetonitrile. Combined supernatants were purified with 200 mg CNW C18 QuEChERS sorbent (ANPEL, China) via 30 s vortexing and centrifugation (7,000 × g, 5 min). Purified extracts were concentrated to dryness using a CentriVap600 Vacuum Concentrator (Jiaimu, China), reconstituted in 150 µL methanol, filtered through 0.22 μm organic membranes, and immediately analyzed by HPLC-MS/MS (QTRAP 5500, SCIEX, USA) with chromatographic separation employing methanol/water/formic acid (Merck, Germany) mobile phases.

Results

Identification of the MBF1 Gene Family

This study comprehensively characterized the MBF1 gene family across five phylogenetically diverse species: the eudicot models A. thaliana (Brassicaceae), the monocot model Oryza sativa (Poaceae), the woody perennial Populus trichocarpa (Salicaceae), the citrus model Citrus sinensis (Rutaceae), and the economically critical crop Beta vulgaris (Amaranthaceae) (Supplementary material 1: Table S1). Genome-wide analysis revealed conserved MBF1 subfamily distributions, with three paralogs (MBF1a, MBF1b, MBF1c) identified in A. thaliana, O. sativa, and P. trichocarpa, while C. sinensis uniquely harbored five MBF1 homologs (CsMBF1b.1–4, CsMBF1c), likely due to lineage-specific tandem duplication events. In sugar beet, two MBF1 genes (BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c) were annotated, with genomic DNA lengths ranging from 222 bp to 3,230 bp, and coding sequences (CDS) spanning 222–468 bp. Notably, MBF1c subfamily members exhibited longer CDS lengths compared to MBF1a/b across all species except C. sinensis, where CsMBF1c displayed atypical truncations. Protein characterization via ExPASy ProtParam revealed conserved physicochemical properties: molecular weights (14.5–17.8 kDa), isoelectric points (pI 4.8–5.6), and hydrophilic profiles (GRAVY: −0.94 to −0.40) (Fig. 1A). Pfam domain analysis (PF01381) confirmed universal retention of the helix-turn-helix (HTH) domain across all 16 MBF1 proteins (Fig. 1B), while MBF1c uniquely harbored an extended N-terminal region enriched in serine phosphorylation sites, suggesting species-specific regulatory adaptations. In conclusion, these molecular features lay the foundation for elucidating the molecular functions of the BvMBF1 family.

Evolution and phylogenetic analysis of the MBF1 family

To resolve the evolutionary relationships of the MBF1 family, the 55 MBF1 protein sequences from 25 plant species were systematically retrieved from public genomic databases (Phytozome v13). a neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was reconstructed, including B. vulgaris, A. thaliana, O. sativa, P. trichocarpa, and C. sinensis and so on (Fig. 2A, Supplementary material 2: Table S2). The topology delineated three major clades: Cluster A (MBF1a subfamily), Cluster B (MBF1b subfamily), and Cluster C (MBF1c subfamily), consistent with conserved subfamily classifications reported [17, 21]. MBF1a and MBF1b formed sister clades with 85% bootstrap support, suggesting functional redundancy or cooperative roles in basal transcriptional regulation, as evidenced by their co-expression in Arabidopsis stress responses [21]. In contrast, MBF1c appears as a unique lineage, which may indicate that plants have undergone new functionalization in the process of stress adaptation.

We analyzed 25 phylogenetically diverse plant species. The copy number of MBF1 genes ranged from 1 to 5 per genome, with most species harboring 2–3 copies (Fig. 2B). Representative examples include: Arabidopsis thaliana, Brassica rapa, Oryza sativa, and Populus trichocarpa (3) Beta vulgaris, Vitis vinifera, Cornus florida, and Eutrema salsugineum (2). Notably, lineage-specific expansions were observed in the MBF1a/b subfamilies of select species: Citrus sinensis uniquely retained 5 MBF1 homologs (CsMBF1b.1–4, CsMBF1c), Actinidia eriantha, Mangifera indica, and Populus trichocarpa exhibited tandem duplications of MBF1a/b paralogs. Theses asymmetric expansion of MBF1a/b suggests subfunctionalization pressures driving stress adaptation. Specifically, the retention of multiple MBF1a/b copies may underpin enhanced abiotic stress resilience in plants [22].

Gene structure and subcellular localization analysis

Gene structure analysis of the MBF1 family across five species (A. thaliana, O. sativa, P. trichocarpa, C. sinensis, and B. vulgaris) revealed conserved organizational patterns, with MBF1a and MBF1b subfamilies clustered into a single group characterized by 2–3 introns and 3–4 exons. While exon lengths were conserved across MBF1a and MBF1b homologs, substantial variation in intron length was observed among species. Notably, significantly elongated introns were found in certain lineages, such as O. sativa and some eudicots, which may contain regulatory elements conferring stress-responsive splicing [28, 29, 31]. Notably, BvMBF1b retained a canonical four-exon structure, whereas BvMBF1c and its orthologs in citrus (CsMBF1c), rice (OsMBF1c), and poplar (PtMBFF1c) were intronless—a feature often associated with rapid stress-induced transcription [26]. In contrast, the Arabidopsis MBF1c homolog (AtMBF1c) contained introns (Fig. 3A). MEME motif analysis revealed seven conserved motifs (Supplementary material 3: Table S3), indicating strong functional conservation despite the varying genomic architectures. Among these, Motif 2 aligned with the MBF1 domain, a defining feature of the aMBF1 superfamily (Fig. 3B, Supplementary material 4: Table S4). We used deeploc-2.1 to predict subcellular localization and found that all MBF1 proteins were enriched in the nucleus and cytoplasm except OsMBF1c from Predicted localizations (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Material 5: Table S5). Combined with Sorting Signal Importance, we found that all MBF1 were enriched in the nucleus (Supplementary Material 6: Figure S1). Signal peptide absence in BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c confirmed their non-secretory nature, aligning with roles as transcriptional coactivators (Fig. 3D, E). These findings support the hypothesis that MBF1 nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling, regulated by developmental cues and stress-specific phosphorylation, enables dynamic coordination of defense gene networks.

In order to determine the spatial position of BvMBF1c and BvMBF1b, we engineered C-terminal GFP fusion constructs (pHK-35 S-GLosGFP-BvMBF1c/b) under the control of the constitutive CaMV 35 S promoter. Primers containing homologous arms were designed to amplify the target product for homologous recombination (Supplementary material 7: Table S7). These constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana epidermal cells via agroinfiltration. At 48 h post - infiltration, confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed that the cell nuclei and membranes of BvMBF1c and BvMBF1b showed GFP fluorescence (Fig. 3F), this is consistent with previous studies that CsMBF1c plays an important role in regulating the heat tolerance of cucumber [25].

Chromosomal location and collinearity analysis

Chromosomal localization analysis revealed that MBF1 genes exhibit positional conservation linked to functional relatedness, with two BvMBF1 homologs (BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c) mapped to distinct chromosomes (BvChr4 scaffold and BvChr8, respectively; Fig. 4A). We conducted a collinearity analysis using B. vulgaris genomic data through the MCScanX method. The results showed significant collinearity in the genomic profile of B. vulgaris, but no collinearity was observed between BvMBF1c and BvMBF1b (Fig. 4B). In order to further analyze the evolutionary relationship of the MBF1 gene in plants, a comparative syntenic maps was constructed between B. vulgaris and other species. The results showed that abundant correlation of collinearity was detected between B. vulgaris and A. thaliana, but it doesn’t exist in the MBF1 family of genes (Fig. 4C), the same results were seen in P. trichocarpa and C. sinensis (Fig. 4E and F). Notably, a unique orthologous relationship was identified between BvMBF1c and rice OsMBF1c (Fig. 4D). Although sugar beets (eudicots) and rice (monocots) are distantly related, their MBF1c genes originated from a common ancestral gene. This phenomenon demonstrates that a gene’s evolutionary history (phylogeny) does not always align with its species’ evolutionary history (systematics). In conclusion, the evolutionary history of the beet MBF1 gene family is very complex and independent.

Analysis of cis-elements and tertiary structure of MBF1

In order to further determine the molecular function and expression pattern of the MBF1 family, we isolated a 2000 bp promoter region upstream of the transcription start site from the genomic data of five species (Supplementary material 8: Table S8). A series of cis-acting elements related to biotic stress were identified. In addition, most promoters respond to light, anaerobic, and MeJA conditions (Fig. 5A). A conserved AT-rich binding motif, recognized by AT-rich DNA-binding proteins, was identified in the MBF1 promoter across all five species. In BvMBF1b, promoter analysis revealed 7 light-responsive and 4 MeJA-responsive cis-elements. Simultaneously, 22 cis-elements—primarily associated with light responsiveness and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) signaling—were characterized in the BvMBF1c promoter. Notably, several cis-elements associated with stress-related phytohormone pathways were detected within these promoter regions. at least one cis-element involved in light-responsive was identified in all species examined, although no elements linked to gibberellic acid (GA) response were found. Numerous cis-elements were associated with responses to methyl jasmonate (MeJA), salicylic acid (SA), and abscisic acid (ABA). These results suggest that MeJA, SA, and ABA signaling pathways may play significant roles in both biotic and abiotic stress resistance in sugar beet.

Furthermore, the tertiary structure of BvMBF1 amino acid sequence was studied by SWISS-MODEL server (Fig. 5B-E). The MBF1 domain and helix-turn-helix domain were detected in each protein. Similar 3D structures were observed in BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c, suggesting that these genes may perform shared functions. However, BvMBF1c exhibits a relatively different tertiary structure, especially in the helix-turn-helix domain.

Validation of yeast two-hybrid interaction in the Y2HGold-GAL4 system

The yeast two-hybrid assay confirmed a specific interaction between BvMBF1c and TPS5 in the GAL4-based Y2HGold system. the positive control (pGBKT7-53 + pGADT7-T) exhibited robust growth on SD/-Trp-Leu selection plates and developed intense on SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade/x-α-gal media, confirming proper system functionality. In contrast, the negative control (pGBKT7-Lam + pGADT7-T) showed no growth under identical conditions, ruling out autoactivation or nonspecific interactions (Fig. 6).

Y2HGold[pGBKT7-53 + pGADT7-T] served as the positive control, Y2HGold[pGBKT7-Lam + pGADT7-T] as the negative control, Y2HGold[pGBKT7-TPS5 + pGADT7-BvMBF1c] as the experimental group, Y2HGold[pGBKT7-TPS5 + pGADT7] as control group 1, and Y2HGold[pGBKT7 + pGADT7-BvMBF1c] as control group 2

Co-transformants of pGBKT7-TPS5 and pGADT7-BvMBF1c displayed growth patterns and β-galactosidase activity comparable to the positive control. Serial dilutions (10×, 100×, 1000×) of yeast cultures (OD600 = 0.2) demonstrated dose-dependent colony formation on both selection and interaction media. Notably, colonies retained viability at the highest dilution (OD600 = 0.0002), indicating strong interaction strength. In contrast, co-transformation of pGBKT7-TPS5 with the empty pGADT7 vector yielded no growth on interaction plates, confirming the specificity of the BvMBF1c-TPS5 interaction. This robust interaction profile positions BvMBF1c as a critical regulator of TPS5 activity in sugar beet stress responses.

Expression profile of BvMBF1 family

To delineate the stress-responsive roles of the BvMBF1 family, we analyzed the expression dynamics of BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c under C. beticola treatments using qPCR. Gene-specific primers for BvMBF1 were designed using Primer3Plus, as detailed in Supplementary material 9: Table S9. Discussion and conclusion

Multiprotein bridging factor 1 (MBF1), an ancient transcriptional coactivator superfamily in plants characterized by a conserved helix-turn-helix (HTH) structure [17], was first identified in silkworms as a non-DNA-binding regulatory partner [64]. Phylogenetic analysis across diverse species—including the eudicot model Arabidopsis (A. thaliana), Populus (P. trichocarpa), sweet orange (C. sinensis), sugar beet (B. vulgaris) and monocot rice (O. sativa)—revealed conserved MBF1 subfamily distributions (Fig. 2A). While Arabidopsis, rice, and poplar harbor three orthologs (MBF1a/b/c), sweet orange uniquely retains five (CsMBF1b.1–4, CsMBF1c), likely due to lineage-specific tandem duplications [25]. In sugar beet, two MBF1 genes (BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c) were annotated, the genome DNA lengths were 3746 bp and 1813 bp, respectively, and the coding sequences (CDS) were 429 and 438, respectively. The consistent results were that MBF1 family could be divided into two groups, and the structure of Group I was four exons and three introns. The structure of group II contained zero, one, or two introns [17, 21]. Thus, MBF1a and MBF1b could be deemed to belong to the Group I subfamily, and MBF1c could be deemed to belong to the group II subfamily in the current study. This might be the reason why the MBF1a and MBF1b subfamilies were alternately clustered into a large group in the present study (Fig. 2A). At the same time, there are few or no introns in the MBF1c cluster gene (Fig. 3A), which may enable MBF1c to respond quickly to stress. During subcellular localization analysis, laser confocal microscopy revealed that GFP-tagged BvMBF1c was localized to both the nucleus and the cell membrane (Fig. 3F), which contrasted with in silico predictions that indicated exclusively nuclear localization. To explore this discrepancy, we performed transmembrane domain prediction using TMHMM and other bioinformatic tools. The results confirmed that neither BvMBF1b nor BvMBF1c contains transmembrane domains (Supplementary Material 6: Fig. S2), indicating that the membrane association is not due to direct integration into the lipid bilayer. Instead, the observed membrane localization may be mediated through transient protein–protein interactions with membrane-associated partners. This is consistent with its role as a transcriptional cofactor and aligns with previous studies highlighting the importance of MBF1 proteins, such as CsMBF1c, in regulating stress responses like heat tolerance [21, 25, 65]. We propose that under oxidative stress, BvMBF1c may shuttle between membrane-associated ribonucleoprotein complexes and nuclear transcription hubs [66, 67], facilitating a rapid response to environmental cues.

Previous studies suggest that tandem duplications may confer conserved genetic functions and expression patterns [24, 68], yet the specific function of BvMBF1 genes remain to be fully elucidated. Gene family expansion in plants often arises from duplication events, including tandem and segmental duplications. While such events are well-documented in resistance gene families [24, 68], no evidence currently links them to MBF1 evolution. In sugar beet (B. vulgaris), the two BvMBF1 paralogs (BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c) reside on separate chromosomes without tandem duplication or collinearity (Fig. 4B), implying distinct evolutionary trajectories. Notably, a lineage-specific whole-genome duplication (WGD) event in Beta vulgaris may have facilitated the retention of these paralogs [69, 70]. Cross-species synteny analysis revealed that BvMBF1c shares collinearity exclusively with OsMBF1c in rice (Fig. 4D), mirroring conserved syntenic patterns observed in WRKY (A. comosus, A. thaliana, V. vinifera, M. acuminata, Z. mays) and NAC (Z. jujuba, A. thaliana, M. domestica, V. vin, 0. sativa) gene families [71, 72].

Despite the deep divergence between Beta vulgaris (Amaranthaceae, eudicot) and Oryza sativa (Poaceae, monocot) approximately ~ 120 million years ago (mya) [73, 74], syntenic analysis revealed an unexpected retention of MBF1c orthologs between these species. Notably, this collinearity is absent between Beta vulgaris and the eudicot model Arabidopsis thaliana (Brassicaceae), even though both underwent post-core-eudicot divergence ~ 64 mya [75]. The MBF1c gene may have played a crucial function in the ancestors of monocots and certain dicots. This function, which has strict requirements for the genomic environment of genes, has been highly conserved through evolution, even as species diverged far apart [69, 76]. This evolutionary trajectory shows that the subfunctionalization of MBF1c was selectively retained in rice of the Poaceae family and beet of the Lablab family to cope with convergent environmental pressures. This lineage-specific co-linear pattern highlights the dynamic interaction between the redundancy mechanism driven by whole genome replication and ecological adaptability, which jointly shape the evolutionary trajectory of gene families [19, 22, 28, 65].

Transcription factors regulate gene expression by binding to cis-acting elements within promoter regions, playing pivotal roles in plant development and stress adaptation [77]. Analysis of MBF1-associated promoters across five species (A. thaliana, P. trichocarpa, O. sativa, C. aurantium, and B. vulgaris) identified 401 cis-elements linked to drought, light, hypoxia, and phytohormone responses (ABA, MeJA, SA). Eudicots (P. trichocarpa and (A) thaliana) exhibited the highest cis-element diversity, correlating with their multifaceted stress resilience (Fig. 5A). Notably, the MBF1 promoter region in sweet orange (C. sinensis) retains nearly all detected cis-regulatory elements, exhibiting conservation patterns analogous to those observed in Arabidopsis thaliana but distinct from the lineage-specific losses seen in species like sugar beet (B. vulgaris). This extensive retention of regulatory elements likely reflects evolutionarily maintained pleiotropic regulatory capacity in sweet orange, conferring transcriptional flexibility for adaptation to complex environmental stresses. Although gibberellin (GA)- and auxin (IAA)-responsive motifs are present in MBF1 promoters across most species, sugar beet uniquely lacks GA-responsive elements and core IAA-binding sites. This absence suggests lineage-specific rewiring of stress signaling pathways in (B) vulgaris. The deficiency of GA motifs in BvMBF1 promoters further implies that its stress responses may depend on methyl jasmonate (MeJA)/salicylic acid (SA) pathways or novel interactors.

Our analysis of phytohormone dynamics in Cercospora-resistant (81GM241) and susceptible (KWS6661) sugar beet lines revealed that endogenous JA and SA levels exhibited rapid accumulation, reaching significance at 6 hpi (p < 0.01) and peaking at 18 hpi (Fig. 8C, D). Curiously, after infection by C. beticola, both resistant (81GM241) and susceptible (KWS6661) varieties showed similar increases in JA and SA levels. However, the sustained induction effect of BvMBF1c was observed only in the resistant genotype (Fig. 7). This suggests that while elevated JA/SA levels are necessary, they alone are insufficient to achieve complete resistance. We propose that the key determinant may lie in genotype-specific mechanisms that convert these hormone signals into sustained defense responses mediated by BvMBF1c. This specific mechanism has also been demonstrated in Arabidopsis thaliana [78]. We observed a methodological discrepancy in the expression profile of BvMBF1c at the R4 time point: RNA-seq data indicated a downregulation trend, whereas qRT-PCR confirmed a significant upregulation (Fig. 7). Such inconsistencies between high-throughput sequencing and targeted qPCR assays, while not uncommon, warrant careful consideration. They may stem from several factors: (1) Wherein RNA-seq normalization methods (FPKM) can skew the quantification of genes with extreme GC content or low abundance, while qRT-PCR efficacy is highly dependent on primer specificity and reverse transcription efficiency [79, 80]; (2) Exon transcriptome markers can reduce the reverse transcription efficiency in the preparation of RNA-seq libraries, alter the affinity of primers in qPCR detection, and regulate RNA stability differently across platforms [81]; and (3) RNA-seq captures a snapshot of total transcriptomic complexity, while qRT-PCR, optimized with gene-specific primers and a stable reference gene (BvACTIN), often provides more sensitive and accurate quantification for individual genes. Previous studies have mentioned that qRT-PCR is widely regarded as the gold standard for verifying changes in gene expression, we consider the upregulation pattern of BvMBF1c at R4 to be reliable [82, 83]. This sustained induction further reinforces its role as a persistent determinant of resistance, potentially acting downstream of initial hormone signaling. In sugar beet, BvMBF1c’s robust response suggests its pivotal role in Cercospora leaf spot resistance, potentially through interactions with trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS5) genes to mitigate oxidative damage [31, 84]. However, functional divergence persists: while MBF1 broadly enhances biotic stress resilience [28, 32, 85], its efficacy against pathogens varies across species. Given C. beticola’s escalating threat to global sugar production [86], elucidating BvMBF1’s regulatory networks—particularly its interplay with anaerobic signaling and antioxidant pathways—could unlock breeding strategies for high-yield, disease-resistant cultivars. Future studies should employ exogenous hormone assays, CRISPR knockout mutants, and multi-omics integration to dissect these mechanisms.

The study systematically characterized MBF1 genes across five representative species, elucidating their gene structures, phylogenetic relationships, motif compositions, chromosomal distributions, cis-regulatory elements, duplication events, and syntenic conservation. All species harbored at least two distinct MBF1 homologs, with MBF1c emerging as an essential member in most lineages. In B. vulgaris, the absence of duplication between BvMBF1b and BvMBF1c—coupled with BvMBF1c’s exclusive synteny to O. sativa—highlights lineage-specific evolutionary trajectories. Promoter analysis identified 401 cis-elements associated with biotic stress and phytohormone pathways (ABA, MeJA, SA), reflecting conserved yet species-tailored regulatory strategies. Notably, MeJA-responsive elements dominated BvMBF1 promoters, aligning with BvMBF1c’s strong induction under methyl jasmonate and salicylic acid treatments. Tissue-specific profiling revealed BvMBF1c’s pivotal role across multiple organs, suggesting its involvement in developmental phase transitions from vegetative to reproductive growth. These findings lay the foundation for functional genomics of the stress response MBF1 network in sugar beet, and we can use MBF1 as a candidate gene for disease resistance breeding in sugar beet in the future.
