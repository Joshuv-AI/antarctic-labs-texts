Unmasking Fitness in Healthy Adults Versus Hypertensives Through Cardiorespiratory Responses to Treadmill Exercise: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Ruchi Kothari, Suryadev A. Vrindavanam, Alekhya Emandi, Rashmita Vakamullu, Mohana Kanthamneni, Sai S. Vemparala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Hypertension is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, and its impact on cardiorespiratory fitness is significant. This study aims to compare the cardiorespiratory responses to treadmill exercise between healthy adults and hypertensive individuals to evaluate the fitness differences between these two groups.Methodology:This case-control study included 100 participants, 50 hypertensive patients, and 50 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. All participants underwent a standardized treadmill exercise test, during which heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen consumption (VO2 max) were measured. The primary outcomes were the differences in heart rate response, systolic blood pressure, and VO2 max between the two groups.Results:The hypertensive group exhibited significantly higher resting heart rates and systolic blood pressure than the healthy controls (P < 0.01). During exercise, hypertensives showed a blunted heart rate response and lower VO2 max, indicating reduced cardiorespiratory fitness. Recovery time post-exercise was also prolonged in the hypertensive group (P < 0.05).Conclusion:The study highlights significant differences in cardiorespiratory fitness between healthy adults and hypertensive individuals. Hypertensives demonstrate reduced exercise capacity, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to improve cardiovascular health in this population.

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension, often termed the “silent killer,” is a chronic condition that significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and stroke. Although pharmacological management is essential in controlling blood pressure, the role of physical fitness, particularly cardiorespiratory fitness, cannot be overlooked. Cardiorespiratory fitness, measured through parameters such as VO2 max, is a critical indicator of overall cardiovascular health.[12] This study aims to explore the differences in cardiorespiratory responses to treadmill exercise between healthy adults and hypertensive individuals, shedding light on the fitness disparities between these populations.

METHODOLOGY

Study design

This case-control study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital over six months. In total, 100 participants were enrolled, comprising 50 hypertensive patients and 50 healthy controls. Hypertensive participants were recruited from the outpatient department, while controls were age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers with no history of hypertension or other cardiovascular diseases.

Exercise testing

Participants underwent a standardized treadmill exercise test based on the Bruce protocol. The test involved progressive increases in treadmill speed and incline until the participant reached volitional fatigue. Heart rate, blood pressure, and VO2 max were recorded at rest, during peak exercise, and in the recovery phase.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software. Continuous variables were compared using independent t-tests, and categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square tests. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographics

The mean age of the participants was 45 years, with a nearly equal distribution of males and females in both groups. The BMI was comparable between hypertensives and controls.

Cardiorespiratory responses

The hypertensive group exhibited significantly higher resting heart rates (mean 82 bpm) compared to the controls (mean 72 bpm, P < 0.01) [Table 1 and 2]. Resting systolic blood pressure was also elevated in the hypertensive group (mean 140 mmHg vs. 120 mmHg, P < 0.01).

Exercise test results

Exercise outcomes

During the treadmill test, the hypertensive group had a blunted heart rate response, with a peak heart rate of 145 bpm, significantly lower than the 160 bpm observed in controls (P < 0.01). VO2 max was markedly reduced in hypertensives (32.5 mL/kg/min) compared to controls (40.2 mL/kg/min, P < 0.01), indicating poorer cardiorespiratory fitness. Additionally, the hypertensive group exhibited prolonged recovery times, with heart rate and blood pressure returning to baseline values more slowly than in the control group.

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study highlight significant differences in cardiorespiratory fitness between healthy adults and individuals with hypertension. The hypertensive group exhibited a higher resting heart rate and systolic blood pressure, both of which are well-documented risk factors for adverse cardiovascular events. The blunted heart rate response and reduced VO2 max observed during exercise testing further underscore the compromised cardiovascular fitness in hypertensive individuals.[345]

These results align with existing literature, which suggests that hypertension negatively impacts the heart’s ability to respond to physical stress, leading to reduced exercise capacity. The prolonged recovery times observed in the hypertensive group indicate delayed autonomic recovery, which could increase the risk of post-exercise hypotension or other adverse events.[45]

The study’s findings have important clinical implications. Given the reduced cardiorespiratory fitness observed in hypertensives, there is a clear need for targeted exercise interventions to improve cardiovascular health in this population. Regular physical activity, tailored to individual fitness levels, can help mitigate the cardiovascular risks associated with hypertension and improve overall quality of life.

CONCLUSION

This case-control study reveals significant disparities in cardiorespiratory fitness between healthy adults and hypertensive individuals, as evidenced by differences in heart rate response, VO2 max, and recovery times during treadmill exercise. The findings underscore the need for proactive measures, including structured exercise programs, to enhance cardiovascular health in hypertensives. Early intervention and regular monitoring of fitness levels can play a crucial role in reducing the long-term cardiovascular risks associated with hypertension.
