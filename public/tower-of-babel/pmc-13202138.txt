Psychological self-care, burnout and quality of life among university students in health-related fields across three regions of Thailand
Authors: Katekaew Seangpraw, Supakan Kantow, Wannita Sakulwattana, Nisarat Auttama, Karnchanok Sukhabot, Sasivimol Bootsikeaw, Boonruang Khaonuan, Parichat Ong-Artborirak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesPublic health students face academic and training pressures as well as challenges arising from patient and community expectations, which may adversely affect their quality of life (QoL) and contribute to burnout and student dropout. This study aimed to examine psychological self-care, burnout and QoL as well as the factors associated with these outcomes among university students.DesignA cross-sectional study conducted between January and March 2024.SettingUniversities in northern, southern and northeastern Thailand.ParticipantsA total of 1426 health-related students aged 18–25 years were recruited using non-probability sampling.Primary and secondary outcome measuresData were collected using a self-administered questionnaire, which included the Thai version of the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF instrument (WHOQOL-BREF-THAI), the Maslach Burnout Inventory to assess emotional exhaustion (EE) and cynicism (CY) and a psychological self-care assessment.ResultsMost participants (79.9%) reported a moderate level of QoL. The prevalence of burnout was 18.9%. Pearson’s correlation analysis indicated statistically significant relationships between psychological self-care, EE, CY and QoL (all p values<0.001). Multilevel linear regression further highlighted that psychological self-care was significantly associated with sex, academic performance, presence of chronic conditions, smoking and sleep duration. In terms of burnout, EE was significantly linked to chronic conditions, alcohol consumption and cannabis use. CY showed significant associations with academic performance, chronic conditions, alcohol consumption and cannabis use. Additionally, QoL was significantly associated with sex, academic performance, income adequacy, chronic conditions, smoking and cannabis use.ConclusionsGood mental healthcare behaviours and lower levels of burnout are associated with higher QoL among public health students. Universities and programme administrators should implement mental health support strategies, including routine screening, counselling services and self-care promotion, to reduce burnout and improve students’ overall well-being.Keywords: MENTAL HEALTH, Burnout, Self Care

Introduction

Students commonly encounter mental health challenges during their university years, a critical period for psychological development. Young people aged 15–24 years experience a substantial burden of mental disorders, with depressive and anxiety disorders among the leading contributors.1 2 The prevalence of mental disorders among individuals aged 15–19 and 20–24 years has been reported at 15.4% and 15.8%, respectively.1 A similar burden has been reported in Thailand.2 Among university students, several factors affect mental health, including academic difficulties, family relationships, interactions with peers and seniors and adjustment to changing environments.3 4 In addition, unhealthy lifestyle behaviours—such as poor diet, physical inactivity, alcohol and drug use, smoking and insufficient sleep—are associated with mental health problems.5,7

Healthcare education is widely recognised as academically and practically demanding. During their studies, healthcare students may experience high levels of stress, leading to anxiety, depression and burnout, which can adversely affect quality of life (QoL) and increase the risk of suicidal ideation.8,10 These outcomes are associated with a range of academic and social stressors, including demanding training programmes characterised by intensive study loads, long hours of training and increasing responsibilities.8,10 Students in health-related fields, such as public health students enrolled in different academic programmes, face substantial academic challenges and future professional expectations related to health service provision and have been reported to experience higher levels of psychological distress.11 12 As future healthcare workers, students are expected to provide services while dealing with complex challenges within healthcare systems.13 Therefore, engaging in self-care practices, including psychological self-care, is essential for preventing burnout and maintaining overall well-being.

Psychological self-care is the intentional, self-directed practice of maintaining mental health and continuous behaviours aimed at nurturing and promoting psychological well-being for normal development.14 It is a guideline for self-care practices aimed at promoting good health, reducing stress and improving overall well-being,15 and encompasses behaviours toward holistic healthcare.11 Self-care practices can predict psychological well-being. Additionally, professional self-care practices strengthen the inverse relationship between perceived stress and psychological well-being.11 16 Psychological self-care can mitigate the negative impacts of perceived stress, anxiety and fatigue on well-being and QoL. Engaging in effective mental health self-care behaviours helps individuals manage everyday challenges and enhance overall life quality.17

Burnout, as defined by the WHO, is a syndrome caused by chronic, unmanaged workplace stress.18 It is characterised by emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalisation (DP) and reduced personal accomplishment.19 Burnout arises from chronic stress due to work demands, leading to physical and mental exhaustion, especially among those who frequently interact with others.20 21 Prolonged academic pressures also diminish enthusiasm for university work, leading to apathy, detachment from classmates and reduced diligence in completing assignments.22 A systematic review in Spain reported a 35.3% prevalence of burnout syndrome among health sciences students.23 Significant levels of burnout have also been reported among undergraduate medical and health sciences students (eg, medicine, nursing, dentistry and pharmacy) in the UAE.24 In Thailand, high levels of burnout, particularly EE and DP, have been reported among medical students.25 Burnout and reduced QoL, especially in the psychological domain, have emerged as major concerns among medical students.26 In addition, low QoL has also been reported among nursing students.27 Therefore, implementing self-care guidelines, effective programmes and long-term monitoring may help reduce burnout and enhance well-being among healthcare students.

However, many studies have focused on burnout and QoL among health sciences students, such as medical and nursing students, whereas public health students have received less research attention. Public health and complementary medicine programmes are expanding across Thailand, with universities tailoring their teaching methods to specific contexts. The curricula combine theoretical coursework with practical and field-based training, and students may face heavy academic workloads and high professional expectations, which may expose them to psychological stress and burnout and negatively affect their well-being. Understanding these elements is crucial for developing strategies to promote and support students’ mental health.

Objective

This study aimed to evaluate psychological self-care, burnout and QoL, as well as to identify factors associated with these variables among public health students in Thai universities. As part of a larger project, surveys on mental health were conducted at universities across three regions of Thailand. The findings may help inform institutional policies and practices to monitor the risk of mental health problems and support the overall well-being of undergraduate students in health-related fields.

Methods

Data source, study design, study participants and sample size

The cross-sectional study was conducted between January and March 2024. The research area was selected using non-probability sampling, specifically purposive sampling based on the following criteria: (1) universities accredited by government agencies offering undergraduate programmes in public health and complementary medicine, (2) universities with more than 60 students per programme and (3) cooperation and willingness from university administrators to participate in the study. Therefore, the study areas comprised three regions: the northern region with the School of Public Health, University of Phayao; the northeastern region with the College of Medicine and Public Health, Ubon Ratchathani University; and the southern region with the Faculty of Science, Health and Sports, Thaksin University, Phatthalung Campus. Convenience sampling was employed to select the study participants. Criteria for selecting the sample group included the following: (1) individuals aged between 18 and 25 years; (2) currently studying in their first to fifth academic year; (3) enrolled in public health and related programmes, including Community Health, Health Promotion, Environmental Health, Occupational Health and Safety, Traditional Thai Medicine and Chinese Medicine; and (4) voluntary participation in the study.

A sample size was determined using G*Power for multiple linear regression, based on a small effect size (0.02), an alpha level of 0.05, 80% power and a number of predictors set at 25. To account for potential incomplete responses, 25% was added, resulting in a final target sample size of 1453 participants. In total, 1426 undergraduate students provided complete data, while 27 did not respond or returned incomplete questionnaires. The final sample included 533 students from Thaksin University, 349 from Ubon Ratchathani University and 544 from the University of Phayao.

Measurements

The self-administered questionnaire (see online supplemental material) was developed based on a conceptual framework to analyse issues aligned with the following areas. The first section comprised personal information including gender, age, year of study, grade point average (GPA), perceived income sufficiency, perceived body mass index (BMI), medical condition, peer relationships, parental care, smoking habits, marijuana use, alcohol consumption, sleep patterns, phone usage and hours worked as assigned. The second part consisted of a modified psychological self-care questionnaire adapted from Auttama et al.11 The original 46-item questionnaire was shortened to 10 items by selecting items from relevant domains. The items covered exercise, self-management and dietary intake. Responses were scored on a 4-point scale as follows: 0 (never practiced), 1 (practiced occasionally; at least once per week), 2 (practiced sometimes; two to three times per week) and 3 (practiced regularly; more than four times per week). Total scores ranged from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better self-care. The third part was the Thai version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) General Survey for Students, which assesses the frequency of feelings experienced in the context of being a student.20 The evaluation consists of 16 items across three dimensions, with response options ranging from 0 (not true at all) to 6 (true nearly all the time): (1) EE, 5 items; (2) cynicism (CY), 5 items—possible score range 0–30, where higher scores indicate greater burnout; and (3) professional efficacy, 6 items—possible score range 0–36, where higher scores indicate lower burnout. Each dimension was categorised into tertiles (low, average, high). The presence of burnout was classified using a two-dimensional model—high scores on both the EE and CY dimensions as these are considered the key indicators of academic burnout among Thai university students.28 Finally, the WHOQOL-BREF, developed by the WHO29 and translated into Thai as the Thai version of the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF instrument (WHOQOL-BREF-THAI,30 consists of 26 items divided into four domains: physical health, 7 items (DOM1); psychological health, 6 items (DOM2); social relationships, 3 items (DOM3); and environmental health, 8 items (DOM4). Each item was rated on a scale from 1 to 5. Scores were categorised into three levels: 26–60 indicates poor QoL; 61–95, moderate QoL; and 96–130, good QoL. The questionnaire content was validated by three experts in mental health, adolescence and public health. The index of item–objective congruence values for each item were greater than 0.67. Following revisions based on expert feedback, a pilot study was conducted with 30 nursing students. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding values of 0.772 for psychological self-care, 0.735 for the MBI (all dimensions >0.7) and 0.787 for the WHOQOL-BREF-THAI.

Data collection was conducted by announcing recruitment on bulletin boards in each department of the faculty, allowing students to decide independently to participate in the study. No consent form was required as no personal data were collected. Participants were informed about the study objectives, methodology and potential benefits. Each participant spent approximately 15–20 min completing the questionnaire during convenient times, 12:00–13:00 and 16:00–18:00, to avoid disrupting study schedules and ensure privacy. Participants received a pen as a token of appreciation for their voluntary participation in the research project.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Descriptive statistics, including mean, SD, minimum and maximum values, as well as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), were used to describe personal characteristics and study outcomes. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to assess correlations among the outcomes, including psychological self-care, burnout and QoL. The independent t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to examine differences in continuous outcome variables (scores of psychological self-care, EE, CY and QoL) between two groups (eg, male vs female) and among multiple groups (eg, grade levels), respectively. The χ2 test was used to identify factors associated with burnout.

To account for the random effects of universities, univariable models using multilevel linear regression were applied to assess the relationships between each factor and the continuous outcomes, and multilevel logistic regression was used for burnout. All variables significantly associated with outcomes at the 0.05 level in the univariable models were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Non-significant variables were manually eliminated one by one until only those with p values<0.05 remained in the final models. The unstandardised coefficients (B) or ORs, along with their 95% CIs, were reported for each model.

Results

Table 1 presents data on psychological self-care, burnout and QoL among university students. The mean psychological self-care score was 19.72 (SD=2.23), and the mean QoL score was 78.48 (SD=12.88). The mean burnout scores were 13.83 (SD=2.46) for EE and 12.06 (SD=3.06) for CY. Overall, most participants reported a moderate level of QoL (79.9%), followed by good (11.4%) and poor (8.7%) levels. The prevalence of burnout was 18.9% (95% CI 16.8% to 20.9%). Significant correlations were found among psychological self-care, burnout and QoL. A positive correlation was observed between psychological self-care and QoL (r=0.550, p<0.001), and EE and CY (r=0.350, p<0.001). In contrast, negative correlations were found between EE and QoL (r=−0.423, p<0.001), psychological self-care and EE (r=−0.304, p<0.001), CY and QoL (r=−0.282, p<0.001) and psychological self-care and CY (r=−0.243, p<0.001).

Table 2 presents psychological self-care, burnout and QoL across different personal characteristics of university students. Most participants were female (83.9%) with a mean age of 20.12 years (SD=1.08), and the largest proportion were second-year students (38.1%). Regarding academic performance, 43.0% of students had a GPA between 3.01 and 3.50. Approximately one-third of the sample (31.8%) reported experiencing financial difficulties. Chronic conditions were reported by 9.7% of students, with the most common being allergies (5.6%). About half of the participants (50.6%) reported a normal perceived BMI. Most reported having a moderate relationship with peers (60.7%) and a democratic caring pattern (66.3%). In terms of substance use, 54.6% reported alcohol consumption, 11.2% smoked and 7.7% used cannabis. Nearly half of the students (48.0%) reported using their phones for 6–10 hours per day. In addition, 51.8% reported spending ≥5 hours per week on homework, while 75.4% reported sleeping less than 8 hours per night.

T-test and one-way ANOVA analyses revealed significant differences in the mean scores of all study outcomes across six key factors: grade, chronic condition, alcohol consumption, smoking, cannabis use and phone usage (all p values<0.05). Additionally, significant differences in psychological self-care scores were found based on sex, income and sleep duration (all p values<0.05). For burnout subscales, significant differences in EE scores were associated with peer relationships, homework hours and sleep duration; and CY scores differed significantly by income, peer relationship and caring pattern (all p values<0.05). QoL scores also showed significant differences based on sex, income, peer relationship, caring pattern and sleep duration (all p values<0.05).

Table 3 presents the unstandardised coefficients and OR from the univariable multilevel analysis examining factors associated with psychological self-care, burnout and QoL. Sex and income were significantly associated with psychological self-care and QoL. A higher GPA was associated with greater psychological self-care and QoL, and with lower levels of burnout. Students with chronic conditions and those engaging in any type of substance use showed lower psychological self-care and QoL, and higher levels of burnout. Students reporting poor peer relationships experienced the highest levels of burnout and the lowest QoL. Caring patterns were significantly associated with all study outcomes. Phone usage and sleep duration were significantly associated with psychological self-care, EE and QoL. No significant associations were found between age, year of study, perceived BMI or homework hours and any of the study outcomes.

Table 4 presents the final model from the multilevel linear regression analysis examining factors associated with the study outcomes. Psychological self-care was significantly associated with being female (B=0.50); having a GPA of 3.01–3.50 compared with 2.50 (B=0.51); having a chronic condition (B=−2.08); smoking (B=−0.65); and sleeping ≥8 hours (B=0.25). In terms of burnout, EE was significantly associated with having a chronic condition (B=2.22), alcohol consumption (B=0.27) and cannabis use (B=0.84). CY was significantly associated with having a GPA of 2.51–3.00 and 3.01–3.50 compared with 2.50 (B=−0.63 and −0.56, respectively); having a chronic condition (B=2.21); alcohol consumption (B=0.35); and cannabis use (B=1.14). For the final multilevel logistic regression model, burnout was significantly associated with having a chronic condition (OR=7.26; 95% CI 4.83 to 10.92) and smoking (OR=1.98; 95% CI 1.33 to 2.96).

Discussion

The average score for psychological self-care and QoL are at a moderate level. For the MBI variables, increases in EE and CY, resulting in burnout (19%), may be linked to the deterioration of QoL. This may be related to the characteristics of the sample group, which consisted of undergraduate students studying health sciences and public health professions, with a mean age of 20 years. These students face challenges related to an intensive curriculum, adapting to their studies and family expectations.31 These findings may be comparable with a study in Thailand that reported high levels of EE (32.4%) and DP (21%) among medical students.25 Additionally, teaching methods may vary according to the local context, so differences in demands may lead to stress and fatigue, resulting in burnout and a decrease in QoL.10 31 Similarly, medical students have been reported to experience burnout in relation to their studies and various contributing factors.32 33 Both psychological self-care and burnout may be related to the QoL of university students across the three campuses, suggesting that self-care behaviour can be defined as engaging in behaviours that maintain and promote physical and emotional well-being. This may include factors such as sleep, exercise, social support and effective emotional regulation strategies,14 which can negatively impact burnout and help improve student QoL.9 34 This aligns with studies indicating that scores on the MBI are associated with physical health, mental health, social relationships and environmental domains. Additionally, the relationship between the EE subscale and physical health is most significant (p=0.001).34 It was also observed that poor mental health is linked to lower QoL among medical students.10

When analysing the factors related to psychological self-care, it was found that sex, GPA, smoking, pre-existing health conditions and sleep were significantly associated. Behaviours related to psychological self-care and participating in activities, clubs or volunteering for social work or recreational activities scored low. This suggests that many health-risk behaviours often occur during adolescence and early adulthood, which is a critical time for establishing health-related behaviours. This is especially true for declining mental healthcare, such as lack of exercise, alcohol consumption and smoking.35 36 This aligns with the concept of self-care, which explains that an individual’s background factors affect their ability to care for themselves.17 37 This is also consistent with previous studies showing that good personal hygiene practices are associated with better QoL and improved sleep, which in turn is linked to lower stress levels.38 Declining academic performance may be related to mental healthcare behaviours and a higher likelihood of burnout.35 Additionally, a lack of mental healthcare may be associated with the expression of burnout.32 As in previous studies, a significant relationship was observed between higher psychological self-care and being female among Thai university students.11 Additionally, health-impairing behaviours are considered a possible link between burnout and negative health outcomes.36

Regarding burnout, existence of a chronic condition and smoking were associated with an increased risk, with students having a chronic condition being more than seven times more likely to experience burnout, and smokers nearly two times as likely. When analysing the factors associated with each dimension of the MBI, it was found that pre-existing health conditions, alcohol consumption and cannabis use were significantly correlated with high levels of EE on the MBI. This is consistent with study findings that explain how emotional exhaustion can lead to a loss of identity, negative outlook and lower personal achievement.19 33 Similar to systematic reviews, EE reflects reduced achievement and increased levels of stress.39 Reduced work motivation, emotional frustration and health issues may occur in relation to burnout.33 39 Additionally, factors associated with burnout include insufficient sleep, smoking, alcohol consumption and drug use, all of which have been significantly correlated with burnout.36,41 Multivariable analyses have revealed that students with pre-existing health conditions and insufficient income have a significant correlation with EE.10 33 Therefore, focusing on self-awareness, emotion management, personal achievement and enhancing individuality is crucial to reduce key symptoms of burnout.21 39

When analysing another aspect of the MBI, it was found that pre-existing health conditions, alcohol consumption and marijuana use are significantly associated with high levels of CY. Additionally, higher GPAs are significantly associated with reduced CY. In line with the concept of work-related burnout, the initial stage involves EE, which is an emotional response to work stress. When EE occurs, individuals may cope by distancing themselves from others, leading to a decrease in personal identity, negative feelings toward others and dissatisfaction with their responsibilities.19 Health issues or physical illnesses are associated with changes in emotional states, and when EE occurs, it is also related to other aspects in the progression of work-related burnout.3 19 Similarly, prior reviews indicate that risky behaviour leads to feelings of indifference and a detached attitude toward both studies and the environment, both inside and outside the university.3 33 Similar to systematic reviews, meta-regression analyses show that health-risk behaviours have a significant relationship with mental health.3 Likewise, previous studies have reported relationships between depersonalisation and psychological health, suggesting that students with feelings of cynicism and detachment tend to report lower self-esteem, more negative attitudes and reduced confidence in the psychological health domain.10 42 Reduced exercise, increased alcohol consumption, smoking and drug use have also been correlated with burnout.36 39 40 43

Being female, having a higher GPA, and sufficient income were positively associated with QoL, whereas pre-existing health conditions, smoking and marijuana use were significantly associated with lower QoL. The concept of QoL is a subjective measure of well-being, influenced by factors such as cultural background, personal values and stressful conditions.30 Universities can be such stressful environments, especially in medical and public health programmes where teaching methods may not vary according to regional contexts.11 17 34 Adapting to the learning environment, peers and society may lead to burnout and a loss of personal identity, which are dimensions of burnout that can decrease student’s QoL related to their studies.19 34

Decreased physical health is associated with EE and life success.17 34 Therefore, QoL is directly linked to healthcare. Although public health students learn methods to promote healthcare for others, they sometimes neglect their own healthcare.11 34 Consistent with previous studies, it was found that QoL is related to dimensions of burnout, explaining 22% and 21% of the variance in physical and mental well-being, respectively.34 Additionally, average QoL scores are higher for women than for men, and women experience significantly higher levels of exhaustion compared with men (p=0.001).44 45 Financial problems and access to healthcare services are also significantly related to QoL,44 and factors such as the environment, basic sanitation, education, healthcare and work conditions are statistically significant correlates of QoL.44 45 Therefore, these findings suggest that burnout has a significant negative relationship with QoL.44,46

Limitations

A limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design using a convenience sample. It only describes the relationships and potential causations between psychological self-care, burnout, QoL and the demographic characteristics of students within the study area. Generalisability may be limited due to the nature of the study population. Future studies should consider using other methodological designs (such as longitudinal studies and probabilistic sampling) to monitor outcomes effectively and implement efficient monitoring measures. Social desirability bias may have occurred in participant responses, particularly regarding health-risk behaviours such as cannabis use or smoking. Therefore, careful planning and preparation of the research team are crucial, especially data collection objectives and confidentiality measures, to ensure that participants understand and answer the questionnaire truthfully. Moreover, multiple factors that may influence the outcomes such as chronic stress, social support and mental health problems which were not considered in this study, should be investigated in future research.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the relationships between psychological self-care, burnout and QoL among public health students. Mental healthcare behaviours are associated with burnout and particularly high levels of EE and CY among students. Therefore, to improve QoL early in students’ academic careers, it is necessary to develop strategies and activities to help manage stress within the learning environment, especially for students with low academic performance, chronic conditions or health-risk behaviours. This includes mental health screening, counselling services or preventive stress management services to reduce burnout and improve QoL. Additionally, promoting self-esteem and implementing short- and long-term support plans to monitor, assess and enhance students’ academic performance should be emphasised. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of mental health interventions to promote psychological self-care, reduce burnout and improve quality of life among university students.
