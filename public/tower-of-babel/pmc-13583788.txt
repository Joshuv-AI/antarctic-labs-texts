Design and psychometric properties of an instrument to evaluate nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by nurse educators: a protocol for a mixed-method study
Authors: Mokhtar Yaghobi, Fazlollah Ahmadi, Sima Mohammad Khan Kermanshahi, Anoshirvan Kazemnejad, Mojtaba Vaismoradi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionPerceived discrimination by nurse educators is a harmful phenomenon in nursing education that influences nursing students in both classroom and clinical settings. It is associated with psychological distress, reduced learning outcomes, feelings of hopelessness and higher rates of attrition. There is a need to identify the dimensions of discrimination for developing strategies to promote educational justice. This research aims to develop and test the psychometric properties of an instrument to evaluate nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of discrimination by nurse educators.Methods and analysisA sequential exploratory mixed-methods design (QUAL → QUAN) will be used in this research. Phase 1 involves a qualitative descriptive study using semistructured interviews with nursing students chosen through purposive sampling with the consideration of maximum variation. Data will be analysed using conventional qualitative content analysis and trustworthiness will be addressed according to Lincoln and Guba’s criteria. In phase 2, the qualitative findings will guide item generation and preliminary scale development based on Waltz et al’s framework. In phase 3, psychometric properties of the scale will be evaluated in terms of content, face, construct validity and reliability. This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the validity, reliability and cultural appropriateness of an instrument for systematic assessment of perceived discrimination among nursing students.Ethics and disseminationThis protocol describes a mixed-method study conducted as part of a doctoral thesis that explores nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by nurse educators. The study has received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committees of Tarbiat Modares University, Iran (decree code: IR.MODARES.REC.1404.068). The findings will provide educational experts and policymakers with evidence-based insights to inform the development of interventions aimed at reducing discrimination and promoting justice and equity in nursing education.Keywords: Discrimination, Nursing Student, Nurse Educator, Faculty, Psychometric Properties

Introduction

Discrimination is defined as unfair treatment of individuals and groups within a social group.1 Perceived discrimination happens when persons believe that they have been treated unfairly by other individuals or social institutions.2 The main sources of this discrimination in nursing students are nurse educators,3 clinical nurses and patients.4 The ethical principle of justice emphasises fair, equitable and appropriate treatment of individuals.5 Discriminatory behaviour is fundamentally incompatible with the principles of justice. Therefore, identifying the dimensions of discrimination is essential for its prevention and elimination.

In a Canadian mixed-methods study by Dhari et al,6 95 nursing students (79.2%) experienced discrimination, and 37 students (30.8%) reported being discriminated against in clinical settings by nurse educators.6 Discrimination refers to race-based prejudice related to skin colour,7 unfair evaluation of ethnicity,8 stigma due to mental and physical disability, gender identity3 and appearance.9 The highest level of discrimination is for questioning the student’s competence and abilities,10 exclusionary behaviour, race and ethnicity.11 Nursing students specifically face discrimination based on their race, sexual orientation, gender identity, ability and mental health in educational institutions.12 Gender bias has also been reported as discrimination for performing activities in favour of female students, being singled out leading to isolation from the peer group, being forced to perform heavy physical work as so-called masculine and restricted mobilities in clinical settings.13 Moreover, discrimination in nursing students has been shown due to high authority and power of physicians over nurses and controlling the constant physical presence in clinical settings.10 Other forms of discrimination in clinical practice include racial microaggressions, unfair treatment and stringent evaluations.14 Discrimination has significant negative impacts on nursing students such as increased student dropout and reduced entry into the nursing profession.15 Accordingly, the feeling of worthlessness, isolation and enhanced stress lead to poorer learning outcomes,16 depression and substance use,17 reduced educational achievement and motivation,18 social isolation, feelings of lack of belonging,9 decreased self-confidence10 and dropout from nursing education.19 Nursing students’ dropout rates vary internationally. For instance in the United States and Finland it has been reported to be 18% and 9%, respectively.20 Increased student attrition resulting from stigma and discrimination contributes to the nursing workforce shortage, posing significant challenges to the delivery of healthcare services.21

Nursing students often refrain from voicing concerns due to a fear that it may influence their evaluations and grades by nurse educators, leaving them with no choice but to remain silent and tolerate discrimination.22 Nursing students often hesitate to voice their concerns because the decision to pass or fail the course remains in educators’ hands.14 Fear of retaliation by educators deters students from protesting discriminatory behaviours.23 In clinical nursing education, educators have the power to approve or reject students’ internship hours and even punish students.15 24 The root of this fear lies in the absence of protective rules in educational institutions. Specifically, within the Iranian nursing education system, clinical assessment is predominantly performed by individual nurse educators and frequently relies on subjective judgments rather than standardised performance indicators. They also possess substantial authority over students’ grades, clinical progression, internship completion and graduation. This structural power imbalance can discourage students from reporting discriminatory behaviours due to concerns about retaliation by educators.10 Additionally, clinical evaluations are often established on educators’ personal and professional perspectives influenced by students’ appearance, gender and ethnicity.23 Subjective evaluations and lack of transparency in assessment criteria serve as tools for asserting power and silencing criticism.25 Therefore, nursing students strive to align themselves with educators’ preferences.14 Such a power imbalance can influence career advancement and quality of education, leading to reduced patient care15 and endangered patient safety.26 27

There are instruments to assess nurses’ perceptions and experiences of discrimination, such as the Nurses’ Perception of Discrimination Scale (NPDS), which evaluates discrimination within hospital settings and patient care.28 Also, the Everyday Discrimination Scale has been designed and validated for clinical settings.29 The Racism Perception Scale measures the experience of racism perceived by African American adults.30 However, none of these instruments are suitable for the assessment of nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by nurse educators. For instance, unfair evaluation, student non-participation in courses and the internship period and derogatory remarks by educators, gender, ethnicity, social category and educator–student relationship have not been covered by these instruments. Studies on discrimination in England and Canada3 6 7 have examined the aspects of discrimination that are more applicable to nurse–patient relationships in hospital settings. Existing instruments have been adapted to the cultural contexts of the countries in which they have been developed and cannot be used in other contexts. Instruments developed in western educational contexts identify discrimination for race and ethnicity in Black or Hispanic students.14 The feeling of inferiority that is common in Iranian nursing students is less frequently recognised as the indicator of discrimination in Western instruments.10 Available instruments often focus on inequality in career advancement,31 but unfair evaluation in the Iranian educational context is an unethical behaviour by educators32 influenced by personal biases and perspectives rather than race.24 In addition, nursing students are subject to unique forms of scrutiny related to minor behaviours, reflecting differences from other healthcare disciplines.10 In Canada, ‘safe reporting’ and ‘curriculum reform’ have been proposed to support students against discrimination,11 but supportive organisations for Iranian nursing students do not involve themselves in educational matters.10 While western instruments focus on racial minorities, discrimination in the Iranian educational context roots from educator–student relationships.24 Therefore, the development of a valid instrument to evaluate nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by educators, tailored to the Iranian educational system and other countries with similar educational cultural context is required. The distributive justice framework will be used for developing an instrument on this topic. This framework is established on Rawls’s theoretical foundations with a focus on the fairness of outcomes consisting of equality, equity, fair grading, academic feedback and equal clinical opportunities.33 In addition, this framework is connected to procedural justice, based on Leventhal’s criteria that focus on the fairness of processes in terms of transparency and consistency in educational regulations.34 35 Moreover, this framework relates to interactional justice based on Bies and Moag’s theoretical foundations emphasising interpersonal behaviours such as respect and dignity as respectful communication free from blame and humiliation.36–38 Structural justice based on Rawls’s theoretical foundations supports this framework in terms of systemic and infrastructural fairness consisting of intersectionality and critical theory as equitable access to the learning environment.39 40

This instrument can enable the assessment of the extent, dimensions and underlying causes of discrimination within nursing education. The findings can inform the identification of discriminatory behaviours and help design targeted improvement strategies such as training programmes and the ongoing evaluation of their effectiveness. At a broader level, it can serve as a foundation for evidence-based decision-making to promote educational justice and foster sustainable improvements in the nursing education system. Therefore, this study aims to develop and test the psychometrical properties of an instrument to evaluate nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of discrimination by nurse educators. Materials and methods

Study design

This research will use a sequential mixed-methods design (QUAL → QUAN). The lived experiences of nursing students will be explored to provide an explicit operational definition of perceived educational discrimination by nurse educators. Item generation will be performed based on the codes, categories, themes, participants’ own words and relevant theories of justice.

Integration of qualitative and quantitative findings will occur at design, methods and interpretation levels. In-depth investigation of the experiences of nursing students in the qualitative phase provides context-specific constructs required for developing the instrument. Construct-to-item matrix matches each theme, category, subtheme and subcategory to its items, response scales and scores. Before going through the quantitative phase, the research team will develop a mapping matrix consisting of constructs and how they are converted to items. The final goal is to develop an instrument evaluating perceived discrimination by nursing students. Also, the conceptual and operational definitions of perceived discrimination through iterative comparison of interview data, justice-related theoretical frameworks and expert consultation will be created. It aims to ensure that the instrument items measure the construct of discrimination rather than dissatisfaction, incivility or poor educational quality.

Rationale for choosing the design

A mixed-methods research ensures gaining a more holistic understanding of the construct using both qualitative and quantitative research approaches. It also helps with enhancing the depth of interpretations and strengthening methodological rigour. Sequential exploratory mixed design will be used in this research because existing instruments cannot sufficiently capture the research concept and there is a need for contextual adjustments.41 42 Accordingly, qualitative interviews will be used as the first step to gather data on nursing students’ experiences and perspectives of discrimination affected by power imbalance, fear of punishment by nurse educators and the context of unfair treatment in nursing educational institutions. The instrument will undergo the evaluation of its psychometric properties using a sample of nursing students.41 43

Research context and setting

Data collection will be conducted at a nursing and midwifery school in an urban area of Iran, which is suitable for the initial development of a context-based instrument. Participants will be nursing students selected using purposive sampling, with the consideration of maximum variation in terms of academic level (bachelor’s, master’s and doctoral degree), educational semester, gender, age group and cultural background.

Study phases

It consists of three phases as follows: (1) exploration of nursing students’ perspectives and experiences to define the concept and identify dimensions of perceived discrimination using qualitative research; (2) transformation of qualitative data into items of an instrument through the mapping process; (3) assessment of psychometric properties of the instrument using quantitative research.

Phase 1: Qualitative: Semistructured interviews with nursing students will be transcribed verbatim and coded through conventional qualitative content analysis to uncover meanings, dimensions, contextual factors of discrimination experienced and perceived by nursing students.

Phase 2: Instrument development: the first draft of the instrument will be developed based on the qualitative findings, where items will be produced from codes, themes, categories, subthemes, subcategories and participants’ own accounts.

Phase 3: Psychometric testing: preliminary instrument will be assessed quantitatively in terms of face, content and construct validity, internal consistency, test–retest reliability, score distribution and floor-ceiling effect (figure 1). Phase 1: qualitative

A qualitative descriptive approach through a conventional qualitative content analysis will be used. Reasons for choosing this research approach are limited existing knowledge about the study phenomenon, lack of appropriate instruments for evaluating students’ perceived discrimination in Iranian educational institutions and the need to explore and conceptually define this phenomenon from the perspective of nursing students.

Data analysis procedure

Data analysis will follow the method suggested by Graneheim et al,44 as a data-driven approach in which themes and categories are developed from participants’ narratives.44 Nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of discrimination by nurse educators will be explored through semistructured interviews. The data analysis products are the identification of the boundaries and dimensions of discrimination that will be used for item generation.

Sampling procedure

Nursing students will be chosen using purposeful sampling method. After obtaining permissions from educational authorities, information about the research will be disseminated through several recruitment strategies including posters displayed on student notice boards in the nursing school, announcements made during theoretical classes and information sheets distributed in clinical training wards. In addition, the study invitation will be shared through official student social media groups (eg, Telegram or WhatsApp groups administered by student representatives) and through the students’ academic email addresses. The invitation letter will include the research aim and objectives, eligibility criteria, voluntary nature of participation, researcher’s contact information and procedure for expressing interest to participate. To alleviate the fear among probable participants, they will be assured that nurse educators will not be informed of their participation in this research.

Inclusion criteria

Completion of at least two academic semesters, willingness to share perspectives of discrimination by nurse educators, experience of discrimination either themselves or its observation among other students and provision of informed consent will be the criteria for recruiting the participants. Maximum variation in terms of academic levels (bachelor’s, master’s and doctoral degree), education semester, gender, age group and cultural background will be used to capture a broad range of perspectives and experiences.

Data collection procedure

Data will be collected through in-depth, semistructured, face-to-face interviews in a private room within the nursing school to minimise bias in responses and ensure the confidentiality of collected data. The initial interview guide will be pilot-tested with a couple of nursing students before its application (online supplemental file 1). The interviews will begin with broad, open-ended questions to allow participants to freely share their experiences without concern. Participants will be encouraged to elaborate on their responses through probing questions such as ‘Can you give an example?’, ‘Can you explain more?’ and ‘How did you feel and react?’ Interviews will continue until participants indicate that they have shared all relevant perspectives and experiences. Follow-up sessions will be scheduled if participants inform the researcher of recalling additional information. All interviews will be audio-recorded, and non-verbal interactions such as facial expressions, tone of voice and emotions will be documented to enrich the interpretation and depth of the collected data.

Content analysis method

Inductive qualitative content analysis as outlined by Graneheim et al.44 Graneheim et al will be used to analyse the interview data. The audio-recorded interviews will be transcribed verbatim, and Microsoft Word and Excel will be used to manage, organise and track the transcriptions. The text will be read to gain an overall understanding of data and the unit of analysis and meaning units will be identified for detailed interpretation. Line-by-line coding will be carried out, and codes will be extracted inductively from participants’ expressions. To enhance analytical rigour and consistency, 10% of the transcribed texts will be independently double coded by two qualitative researchers, and any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion and seeking the perspectives of the third member of the research team. Themes, categories, subthemes and subcategories will be developed, and semantic structures, dimensions, characteristics and interrelationships of the data analysis products will be examined to extract overarching themes reflecting students’ perceptions of discrimination. All analytical steps and procedures will be documented and reported to ensure methodological rigour and reproducibility.44

Validity of the findings

The trustworthiness of the research will be ensured according to Lincoln and Guba’s four criteria: credibility, confirmability, dependability and transferability.45 For credibility, as internal validity, efforts will be made to ensure that the research findings accurately represent the participants’ experiences and correspond to reality.45 46 Sufficient time will be devoted to data collection, and the researcher will undertake prolonged engagement with the data and document reflective field notes. Maximum variation in sampling helps capture all aspects of discrimination and achieve a deeper understanding of nursing students’ perspectives and experiences. For confirmability, findings will be examined and corroborated by other qualitative researchers.47 Two independent qualitative researchers will compare extracted codes, categories and themes with the interview transcripts. Disagreement will be resolved through discussion, and contentious issues will be referred to a third researcher for reaching consensus. Transcripts will be shared with participants for member checking and approval of the findings. All analytical steps will be thoroughly documented to provide a clear audit trail. Transferability as the extent to which research findings can be applied to other contexts or populations48 will be ensured by selecting participants with maximum variations. Additionally, concrete examples and direct participant quotes in the final report will allow readers to evaluate the transferability of the findings to other contexts.

Phase 3: psychometric testing

The validity and reliability of the instrument will be examined.

Validity

Validity refers to the extent to which a measurement instrument accurately and precisely captures the key characteristics of intended constructs. An instrument is considered valid if it effectively measures the variable of interest with high accuracy and completeness.50 The instrument’s validity will be assessed using face and content validity as well as construct validity through factor analysis.

face validity

Face validity of the instrument will be assessed using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. For qualitative face validity, 10 nursing students will review the items for clarity, simplicity and comprehensibility, and their feedback will be considered to revise the wording and structure of the items. For quantitative face validity, the same participants will rate the importance of each item on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (‘not important’) to 5 (‘very important’). Content validity

Content validity refers to the extent to which an instrument adequately represents the construct it is intended to measure.51 It will be assessed using an expert panel consisting of 5–7 members52 Therefore, a panel of 10 nursing faculty members and instrument development experts will be consulted. They will evaluate the instrument in terms of item relevance, wording, grammatical structure, clarity, logical sequencing and scoring procedure, with revisions made based on their feedback.

For quantitative content validity, the content validity ratio (CVR) and the Content Validity Index (CVI) will be used in conjunction. They measure different aspects of content validity and compensate for the other’s limitations.

CVR evaluates whether each item is considered essential by subject experts, thereby supporting the necessity of retaining individual items. It will be calculated using Lawshe’s method as the index makes use of critical value tables, which are calculated based on the number of experts, and allow for determining whether the agreement on the item exceeds random levels. Experts will rate each item as ‘essential’, ‘useful but not essential’ or ‘not essential’. Items with CVR values exceeding 0.62 will be retained, based on Lawshe’s critical values for a 10-member panel.

CVI provides detailed evidence regarding the relevance, clarity and simplicity of each item and is widely recommended in instrument development studies. It will assess both the item (I-CVI) and scale (S-CVI) levels as standard indices. The panel of experts will rate item relevance on a four-point scale. Items with I-CVI values ≥0.79 will be considered acceptable, those between 0.70 and 0.79 will be revised, and the instrument will be deemed satisfactory if the S-CVI exceeds 0.90.53 To compensate for one of the major drawbacks of CVI, as the lack of correction for chance agreement, the modified kappa will be additionally calculated. It takes into account the likelihood of agreement due to chance and hence provides a more strict measure of the quality of expert consensus.43 Item analysis using the loop method will be conducted following the administration of the finalised 50-item instrument. One of the iterative, systematic approaches to item analysis is the loop method. As a gradual deletion technique, it allows the iterative refinement of the questionnaire while preserving factor stability, unlike the single-step item deletion procedure. Compared with traditional one-time analysis, it offers greater precision in identifying problematic items because the simultaneous deletion of multiple items can inadvertently affect the instrument’s factor structure and reliability. Items with item–total correlations below 0.30 or negative correlations, or those whose removal improves overall reliability of the instrument, will be excluded from the scale.

Construct validity

Factor analysis as a widely accepted method for identifying the underlying structure of measurement instruments and evaluating relationships among items will be used.54 55 It enables the identification of latent constructs by grouping items with strong inter-correlations and provides evidence for convergent and discriminant validity, which are the essential components of construct validity.54 Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) will be conducted to assess the factor structure of the instrument measuring nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by nurse educators. Sampling adequacy will be evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test, with values above 0.60 as acceptable, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity, with p<0.05 indicating suitability for factor analysis.51 Factors will be extracted based on the correlation matrix, with highly correlated items forming common factors. A sample size of 10 participants per item will be used for EFA to ensure stable multivariate results, in accordance with recommended ratios.56 For confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), larger samples of 10–20 participants per item or a minimum of 200 participants will be targeted to ensure adequate model fit and reliable estimation.57 In CFA, construct validity will be further evaluated using standard goodness-of-fit indices. Reliability

The reliability of the instrument will be assessed using internal consistency and stability methods described as follows.

Internal consistency

It will be evaluated using the calculation of the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which reflects the degree of correlation among items and assesses whether items consistently measure a single construct. Generally, a Cronbach’s alpha value above 0.70 is considered acceptable, above 0.80 is desirable, and above 0.90 indicates excellent internal consistency.58 59 The same sample used for EFA will be used to assess internal consistency. Items exhibiting low or negative correlations with the total scale score will be considered for revision or deletion to improve the instrument reliability.51 56

Instrument stability

The test–retest method consists of administering the instrument twice to a sample of students with a 2-week interval between administrations to determine the stability of scores over time.60 The stability of the instrument will be assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to examine the consistency of scores. An ICC value greater than 0.74 indicates that the instrument is stable.

The interpretability of the questionnaire

The assessment of floor and ceiling effects, calculation of the minimum significant change, and examination of the response format based on Likert-scale scoring will be carried out.61 Floor and ceiling effects will be assessed by calculating the proportion of respondents achieving the lowest or highest possible scores on each scale, with effects considered present if more than 15%–20% of participants select extreme response options, potentially limiting the instrument’s discriminatory capacity.51 Item-level non-response rates will also be examined as an indicator of instrument quality. A non-response rate of 10%–20% will be considered acceptable. The relative frequency of missing responses will be calculated for each item to evaluate item clarity and usability. Following item finalisation, scoring rules will be established based on the instrument structure. Responses will be recorded using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree,’ developed in consultation with nursing and psychometric experts. For standardised interpretation, raw scores will be linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale, where higher scores indicate greater perceived discrimination, facilitating comparison and classification of perception levels across respondents. To calculate the final score, each Likert response will be coded from 1 to 5, and the total raw scores for each participant will be converted to the 0–100 range using the following formula:

(Raw score obtained − lowest possible score) / (highest possible score − lowest possible score) × 100

The final score represents a comprehensive index of the severity of discrimination perceived by nursing students. This scoring system, developed in accordance with psychometric standards and designed to minimise ambiguity in data interpretation, will serve as the final framework of the instrument after confirmation of CVR and CVI, and reliability in the preceding stages. Before using the linear transformation procedure, the normality of the score distribution will be evaluated to see whether the transformation may hide any skewness in the data. In case of the non-normality of the score distribution, other scoring procedures based on the decision of statistician will be used.

Data analysis procedure

Descriptive statistics, EFA, the testing of the internal consistency and test–retest reliability will be used.62 They help interpret data and give the possibility to understand the instrument structure and relationships between items. CFA will be carried out using the AMOS V.24 to assess whether the instrument structure coincides with qualitative categories and themes, and whether the fit indices give a good model fit. Before performing CFA and other related procedures, the normality of overall scores and item scores will be assessed by drawing histogram and Q-Q plot, and assessing skewness and kurtosis. Skewness and kurtosis with values from −2 to 2 show approximate normality and enable performing CFA using maximum likelihood estimation. However, in case when data do not have normal distribution, the robust statistical procedures will be applied. Accordingly, the Satorra–Bentler scaled χ² statistic will be used for CFA, while principal axis factoring or rank-based least-squares methods will be applied for EFA. Besides, the adequacy of the correlation matrix will be examined using Bartlett’s test of sphericity and KMO measure of sample adequacy.

Discussion

This research protocol uses a three-stage sequential exploratory mixed-methods design to investigate nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by nurse educators, addressing a critical gap in nursing education. The proposed instrument will be grounded in the lived experiences of students in classroom and clinical settings. A key strength of this study is its mixed-methods approach in which qualitative findings provide an in-depth understanding of the dimensions of perceived discrimination and guide item generation, instrument development, and subsequent evaluation of preliminary psychometric properties. This study aims to design and evaluate a culturally and contextually appropriate instrument to measure nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by nurse educators, addressing a complex cultural, social, behavioural and educational issue within the nursing academic environment.

It is believed that effective, professional and academically appropriate communication between nurse educators and nursing students is a fundamental principle in nursing education.63 Discrimination is a significant barrier to effective communication between educators and students, manifesting as overt disrespect, display of superiority and favouritism based on appearance, ethnicity or academic performance.64 Students’ perceptions of injustice and disrespect undermine trust in the educational system and increase the feelings of disillusionment with the nursing profession.65 Instruments have been developed to measure perceptions of discrimination, including the Nursing Student Gender Attitudes Scale, which evaluates gender role attitudes, professional stereotypes and experiences of gender-based discrimination.66 The Bullying in Nursing Education Scale, focuses on bullying as a specific form of discrimination within the academic and clinical environment.67 The Perception of Gender Discrimination in the Workplace Scale, has been designed to measure female nurses’ perceptions of gender-based discrimination in professional settings.68 The NPDS, has been developed by ZareKhafri et al31 in Iran, and the EDS scale has been validated by Ulusoy et al29 in Germany, focusing primarily on the clinical work environment and interactions with patients or colleagues. These instruments do not capture discrimination experienced by nursing students in educational institutions. They do not cover theoretical and clinical learning, evaluation process, daily interactions with nurse educators, and are not appropriate for the Iranian cultural context.

The instrument that will be developed in this research can help assess perceived discrimination in nursing education and identify its potential behavioural, structural, grading-related and learning opportunity-related dimensions in educational settings. It can enable the assessment of the extent, dimensions and underlying causes of discrimination within nursing education. It can inform the identification of discriminatory behaviours, and help design targeted improvement strategies such as training programmes and the ongoing evaluation of their effectiveness. At a broader level, it can serve as a foundation for evidence-based decision-making to promote educational justice and foster sustainable improvements in the nursing education system. However, its broader use requires further validation in multicentre and cross-cultural studies.

This instrument can be used to accurately assess the status of discrimination in nursing education and to identify its various dimensions including behavioural, structural, grading and learning opportunity in both classroom and clinical settings. It can be applied in several ways: (1) educational institutions can periodically assess nursing students’ perceptions of discrimination by educators to implement preventive interventions; (2) documenting and analysing students’ experiences of perceived discrimination to help identify systemic weaknesses and unfair practices, informing curriculum revisions, course evaluations and educational policy improvements and (3) confronting discrimination to foster a fairer and more supportive educational environment, enhance student self-confidence and engagement, prevent dropout, improve mental health and overall learning conditions. It has also the potential to enhance educational environments, promote equity and reduce stress, cultivate fair-minded nursing professionals and contribute to enhanced quality of patient care.

This research protocol focuses on developing an instrument to assess nursing students’ perception of discrimination by nurse educators. A central and critical challenge is to ensure a clear definition of the construct of ‘discrimination’ prior to its quantification. To address this issue, before item generation and psychometric testing, this research will concentrate on the establishment of an operational definition, development of a dimensional map and construction of a construct–item relational matrix. This approach aims to ensure that the resulting scale measures discrimination rather than general dissatisfaction.

Due to the evaluative and supervisory role of nurse educators in monitoring students’ academic and clinical performance, nursing students often avoid reporting discrimination and remain silent, because complaints may influence their future career prospects. Therefore, in this study, discrimination is not limited to unfair behaviour, but rather encompasses unequal access to feedback, clinical learning opportunities, respectful communication and transparent evaluation processes. This fear is intensified in contexts where instructors have direct authority over grading, clinical evaluation and internship approval, creating a structural power imbalance in the educational system.

A limitation of this study is that it will be conducted in a single school of nursing within a provincial centre, which may limit the generalisability and transferability of the findings. Additionally, purposive sampling will be used to recruit nursing students who report experiences of perceived discrimination, which may result in an overrepresentation of negative experiences. However, participation from students with subtle, ambiguous or low-intensity experiences of discrimination is welcomed during the recruitment process. In both qualitative and quantitative phases, data collection relies on self-report, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitivity of discrimination in educational settings.

The integration of qualitative and quantitative findings during the psychometric testing phase is challenging due to changes in context between the two stages. Additionally, qualitative findings may not fully align with the quantitative factor structure, so that some categories and themes may be merged, separated or excluded during EFA and CFA. These inconsistencies will be addressed analytically, and the final interpretation will consider both the qualitative conceptual understanding and the quantitative model fit.

The resulting instrument should be regarded as a context-bound, psychometrically evolving tool. Prior to its use in large-scale studies, it requires further validation through multicentre studies, cultural adaptation and testing across diverse nursing education contexts to ensure its cultural relevance and applicability.

Ethical considerations

This study has received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committees of Tarbiat Modares University, Iran (Decree code: IR.MODARES.REC.1404.068), and will be conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants will sign the written informed consent form after receiving full explanation of the study objectives, procedures and measures to ensure anonymity and confidentiality. Interviews will be scheduled at times convenient for the participants. Participants will be assured that all information provided will be kept strictly confidential, participation will be entirely voluntary and they may withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason and without any adverse consequences. Nurse educators will not be informed of the students’ participation in this research, and students’ participation or non-participation will have no impact on their academic evaluation and status. Additionally, participants will be asked to provide permission for audio recording and note-taking during the interviews. Once peer-reviewed and published, the findings will inform experts and policymakers and provide evidence-based guidance for designing interventions and policies to reduce discrimination and strengthen justice and equity in nursing education.
