Eco-friendly fabrication of ZnO–Co₃O₄/C S-scheme photocatalyst from bimetallic ZIF for sunlight-powered H₂ generation and CO₂-to-fuel conversion
Authors: Walle Tilahun, Nisha Angeline M, S K Manikandan, M Vijayakumar, G Vijayakumar, V Arun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe efficiency of photocatalytic fuel generation continues to be constrained by swift electron-hole recombination and inadequate harvesting of visible-range photons in monocomponent semiconductors. In this study, we describe an environmentally benign approach to prepare a ZnO–Co₃O₄/C S-scheme photocatalyst, starting from a Zn/Co bimetallic ZIF that undergoes regulated pyrolysis at 600 °C in an inert N₂ environment. The bimetallic ZIF was synthesized through a water–ethanol solvothermal process with a low environmental impact score (E-factor: 3.2), completely eliminating hazardous organic solvents from the procedure. Powder XRD, EM imaging, and XPS collectively verified the development of an intimately integrated ZnO–Co₃O₄/C junction while preserving the porous architecture inherited from the parent ZIF. A suite of characterization tools including Mott–Schottky plots, attenuated PL emission, reduced EIS arc radii, and ESR spin-trapping signatures all point toward operation via an S-scheme charge migration pathway. Under optimized conditions, the composite photocatalyst delivered H₂ at 2340 ± 45 µmol per gram per hour (AQY reaching 8.2% under 450 nm illumination) while concurrently converting CO₂ to CO at 58.7 ± 2.1 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹, maintaining 95.5% of its initial catalytic activity across five consecutive reuse cycles. Our findings illustrate that sustainable, scalable synthesis of MOF-based S-scheme photocatalysts is achievable and can simultaneously drive two distinct solar-to-fuel conversion processes.Keywords: ZnO–Co₃O₄/C heterojunction, S-scheme photocatalysis, Bimetallic ZIF-derived photocatalyst, Visible-light hydrogen evolution, Photocatalytic CO₂ reduction, green solvothermal synthesis, charge separation, Solar fuelSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The impending exhaustion of fossil fuel reserves and the accelerating threats associated with climate change have led to a growing interest in global sustainable approaches for clean energy production and carbon management. Photocatalytic fuel synthesis driven by solar radiation offers a compelling route to tackling both issues, as it enables simultaneous water-to-hydrogen splitting and CO₂ conversion into useful fuel products under freely available sunlight1–3. However, despite many years of research, low visible-light absorption, fast electron–hole recombination and lack of redox potential in active sites pose fundamental limitations for practical application of photocatalytic systems3–4.

Traditional mono-phase semiconductor photocatalysts (e.g., TiO₂ and ZnO) are quite stable but they have a wide band gap, the poor absorption ability of solar-light, and small quantum efficiency5, in contrast surface modification with narrow-band-gap materials can cause severe photo-corrosion and rapid recombination on carrier2. To tackle these issues, heterojunction engineering recently has been extensively developed as a useful route to facilitate spatial charge separation and boost light absorption6–7. Among different hetero junction models, the S-scheme photocatalyst has attracted considerable interest in recent years because it possesses high charge separation efficiency and its solution upholds redox ability strongly, unlike the type II one that low potential band in both oxidation and reduction process8,9.

In an S-type heterojunction, electrons with low reduction ability and holes with weak oxidation ability within the semiconductor deposit at the surface interface, while energetic charge carriers remain on the band edges to power surface redox reactions8,10. It has been shown that this mechanism can be quite useful for challenging transformations, like water splitting and CO₂ reduction, which demand high thermodynamic driving force and efficient suppression of charge recombination4,11. The deliberate fabrication of S-scheme junctions featuring robust interfacial bonding, however, continues to present significant synthetic difficulties. Established preparation techniques such as solid-state grinding and coprecipitation typically yield disordered phase boundaries with poorly regulated defect concentrations16. Porous coordination polymers, commonly known as MOFs, present an attractive templating approach to circumventing this limitation. Owing to their adjustable chemical makeup, well-defined porous networks, and uniformly distributed metal centers, MOFs can serve as sacrificial precursors that transform under regulated heating into closely integrated semiconductor heterostructures5,13–15,17,18. Recent studies have demonstrated that MOF-derived oxides and carbon composites can inherit the parent framework’s morphology while generating coherent heterointerfaces conducive to efficient charge transfer18,19,20.

Nevertheless, a considerable portion of previously reported MOF-based photocatalytic systems depend on harmful solvents such as DMF, demand substantial energy inputs during fabrication, or require expensive noble-metal promoters, all of which undermine their prospects for large-scale implementation and raise environmental sustainability questions5,23. There is thus a pressing need for environmentally responsible fabrication protocols utilizing non-toxic solvents, moderate processing temperatures, and naturally abundant constituent elements19. As far as we are aware, no prior literature has reported the integration of environmentally friendly MOF preparation with subsequent regulated pyrolysis to generate an S-scheme photocatalyst capable of concurrently driving hydrogen evolution and carbon dioxide photoreduction.

A Zn/Co bimetallic ZIF precursor is first obtained through a water–ethanol solvothermal route, followed by controlled thermal conversion under nitrogen to generate the final heterostructured composite with intimate interfacial contact between ZnO and Co₃O₄ domains. In this work, we present a ZnO–Co₃O₄/C S-scheme heterojunction photocatalyst derived from a Zn/Co bimetallic ZIF precursor synthesized via an eco-friendly water–ethanol solvothermal route. Upon controlled calcination at 600 °C under nitrogen, the bimetallic ZIF transforms into a porous ZnO–Co₃O₄/C composite with a well-coupled heterointerface. The structural, optical, and electrochemical properties are systematically characterized, and the S-scheme charge transfer pathway is supported by Mott–Schottky analysis, photoluminescence, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, and electron spin resonance measurements. The optimized photocatalyst demonstrates substantially enhanced visible-light-driven H₂ evolution and CO₂-to-CO conversion compared with its pristine counterparts, along with good cycling stability.

Green synthesis of bimetallic ZIF precursor

To avoid harmful organic solvents, the bimetallic Zn/Co-ZIF precursor was synthesized utilizing an eco-friendly solvothermal approach with a water–ethanol mixed solvent system (1:1 v/v)19. Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O (1.0 mmol) and Co(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O (1.0 mmol) were dissolved in 30 mL of water-ethanol mixture at room temperature for 15 min under magnetic stirring (Solution A). 2-methylimidazole (8.0 mmol) was dissolved in 30 mL of Solution B. Under continuous stirring, Solution B was added dropwise to Solution A. In a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, the mixture was heated at 120 °C for 12 h. After cooling naturally to room temperature, the precipitate was centrifuged (8000 rpm, 10 min), washed three times with ethanol and deionized water, and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The calculated E-factor for our protocol stands at 3.2, representing roughly a four-fold improvement over the 12.8 value typical of conventional DMF-based ZIF syntheses. Table 3 provides a side-by-side green chemistry assessment, clearly illustrating the lowered solvent toxicity, decreased waste output, and diminished overall environmental footprint. The proposed synthesis demonstrates a 3–4× reduction in environmental burden compared to conventional MOF synthesis routes when considering combined waste, toxicity, and energy factors. The suggested synthesis approach was compared to traditional DMF-based solvothermal methods using green metrics to quantify its environmental effect. The E-factor (waste mass per product mass) for this approach was 3.2, substantially lower than the normal DMF-assisted ZIF synthesis value of ~ 12.8. The solvent system utilized in this study (water–ethanol combination) is “recommended” by CHEM21 solvent selection criteria, but DMF is a chemical of concern owing to its toxicity and disposal issues. The solvothermal synthesis at 120 °C for 12 h and calcination at 600 °C for 2 h used less energy than usual MOF-derived photocatalyst synthesis procedures (typically above 700 °C and longer durations). Without high-boiling hazardous solvents, downstream purification energy is reduced. These features demonstrate that the suggested synthesis technique is chemically effective, ecologically friendly, and industrially scalable.

Thermal conversion to ZnO–Co₃O₄/C heterostructure

Pyrolysis of the dried bimetallic ZIF was conducted in a horizontal tube furnace wherein the sample, placed in an alumina boat, was subjected to a continuous flow of ultrapure N₂ at 50 mL per minute. A furnace was heated from ambient temperature to 600 °C at a rate of 2 °C min⁻¹ and maintained for 2 h. The modest ramp rate allowed partial carbonization of the organic linker while keeping the porous framework design. A desiccator was used to collect the black powder after natural cooling to ambient temperature. ZnO–Co₃O₄/C is the name given to the product in this publication. The same thermal treatment was applied to monometallic Zn-ZIF and Co-ZIF precursors to create ZnO and Co₃O₄/C for comparison.

Structural and physicochemical characterization

Crystal phases of the as-prepared materials were characterized by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to observe the surface morphology, and microstructural features were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to observe heterointerfaces and lattice fringes. The optical absorption properties were studied by UV–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–Vis DRS) and band gap energies were evaluated from the Kubelka–Munk function. Charge recombination was investigated by photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. The charge-transfer resistance and interfacial conductivity of the ZnO–Co₃O₄/C heterostructure were characterized by EIS measurements with a classic three-electrode arrangement23,24.

Photocatalytic hydrogen evolution experiments

The complete experimental sequence, encompassing photocatalyst preparation through photocatalytic testing, is schematically depicted in Fig. 2. It summarizes the precursor synthesis, thermal conversion step, reactor configuration, gas purging conditions, and irradiation arrangement used for the photocatalytic measurements. Photocatalytic hydrogen generation experiments were performed in a gastight quartz reactor (100 mL capacity, 50 mL liquid fill) equipped with a gas recirculation loop. In a typical run, 50 milligrams of the photocatalyst was dispersed in 50 mL of aqueous medium. The reaction medium was supplemented with 0.35 M sodium sulfide and 0.25 M sodium sulfite to serve as sacrificial hole acceptors. Prior to irradiation, the catalyst suspension underwent ultrasonic dispersion for ten minutes, followed by N₂ bubbling for half an hour to purge dissolved molecular oxygen. Illumination was provided by a 300 W xenon arc lamp filtered to transmit only wavelengths above 420 nm, delivering an irradiance of 100 milliwatts per square centimeter (standard AM 1.5G solar simulation) measured at the exterior reactor wall, with the light source positioned 15 centimeters away. Hydrogen gas produced during the reaction was periodically withdrawn at one-hour intervals and analyzed by GC-TCD (Shimadzu GC-2014 system equipped with a molecular sieve 5 A separation column and nitrogen as the carrier gas). The apparent quantum yield (AQY) was determined using monochromatic band-pass filters (420, 450, 500, 550, and 600 nm). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Photocatalytic CO₂ reduction experiments

Carbon dioxide photoreduction tests utilized an identical reactor configuration. Fifty milligrams of photocatalyst was suspended in 50 mL of CO₂-purged aqueous medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) triethanolamine as an electron donor scavenger. The reaction vessel was flushed with research-grade CO₂ flowing at 40 mL per minute for thirty minutes before light exposure. Gaseous reaction products were subsequently quantified: CO by GC-TCD and methane by GC-FID. Isotopic labeling experiments using ¹³CO₂ were performed to confirm the carbon origin of the products by GC–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Blank experiments were performed under three negative control conditions, absence of illumination, absence of catalyst, and replacement of CO₂ with N₂ atmosphere, to confirm that observed product formation was genuinely photocatalytic in origin4,11,16.

Active species trapping and stability test

To study photocatalytic mechanism, active-species trapping experiments were performed using selective scavengers to capture photo-generated hole-electron pairs and/or reactive radical species18. The stability and recyclability of the photocatalyst were investigated through recycling photocatalytic experiments, in which after each run, the catalyst was collected by centrifugation, washed with water and dried; then it was used again under similar conditions. The XRD measurements were used to evaluate structural stability after cycling.

UV–visible diffuse reflectance spectra measurements were used to understand the optical absorption behavior of the photocatalysts. More importantly, a great improvement in visible-light absorption was realized for the MOF-derived S-scheme cross-linked heterostructured photocatalysts than that of pristine constituents, and could be extended to a longer wavelength range. This enhancement is ascribed to the synergistic coupling and defective structure from thermal transformation. The optical band gaps were calculated based on Kubelka–Munk plots; they reported a reduced energy gap in the heterostructured photocatalyst when compared with its individual units3,6. The band engineering of this porous structure enables the visible-light excitation with sufficient redox potential. This crystallite size derived from x-ray diffraction agrees with the above optical findings, and nanodimensions are definitely giving rise to enhanced light harvesting. The recombination dynamics of photo injected charge carriers were investigated by photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. The heterostructured photocatalyst showed much lower PL intensity than the pristine material, suggesting that the recombination of electrons and holes was being suppressed effectively. This result further shows that the S-scheme heterojunction mainly contributes to the extension of carrier lifetime via interfacial separation with AQY of 8.2% at 450 nm23,24.

Electrochemical charge transfer behavior

Nyquist plots from electrochemical impedance measurements featured a compressed semicircular arc for the heterostructured photocatalyst compared to the individual phases, signifying diminished interfacial charge transfer resistance. Following the analytical framework established by Tilahun, W et al.28 for evaluating interfacial charge dynamics in plasma-treated systems, we correlated the reduced charge transfer resistance with enhanced separation of photogenerated carriers. Specifically, their work demonstrated that lower EIS arc radii correspond to higher concentrations of reactive radicals, a relationship we confirmed here using radical scavenger experiments. Addition of AgNO₃ (electron scavenger) suppressed H₂ evolution by 80.5% and CO production by 79.4%, while EDTA (hole scavenger) caused approximately 52% inhibition in both reactions. These results mirror the scavenger-based validation reported in28, where photogenerated electrons and holes were identified as the dominant active species in interfacial redox processes.The flat-band potentials for ZnO and Co₃O₄/C were − 0.52 V (vs. NHE, pH 7) and + 1.68 V, respectively.

Band structure and charge transfer pathway

A schematic energy band diagram for the ZnO–Co₃O₄/C composite was assembled by combining flat-band potentials obtained from Mott–Schottky plots with bandgap energies derived from optical absorption data. ZnO, an n-type semiconductor, exhibits a conduction band edge at − 0.52 V vs. NHE, while Co₃O₄/C, a p-type semiconductor, shows a valence band edge at + 1.68 V vs. NHE. Using the experimentally determined bandgap of 2.48 eV for the heterocomposite, the respective valence and conduction band edge positions were calculated. When the two semiconductors are brought into contact, electrons spontaneously flow from ZnO to Co₃O₄/C until equilibrium Fermi level alignment is established, resulting in the build-up of a built-in interfacial electric field. Under visible-light illumination, both semiconductor components become excited; the photogenerated electrons occupying the conduction band of Co₃O₄/C subsequently recombine with holes residing in the valence band of ZnO. Meanwhile, the more energetic electrons retained in ZnO’s conduction band and the deeply seated holes remaining in Co₃O₄/C’s valence band are conserved via this selective recombination process, enabling them to participate productively in surface redox chemistry.

The observed band energetics indicate that charge transfer proceeds through an S-scheme rather than a conventional type-II pathway, a distinction that ensures preservation of strong reduction and oxidation capability while suppressing detrimental bulk recombination.

S-Scheme charge transfer mechanism

Figure 8 depicts the suggested S-scheme charge transfer mechanism for ZnO–Co₃O₄/C heterostructure. Drawing on Mott–Schottky analysis, spontaneous electron migration occurs from the higher Fermi level of n-ZnO toward the lower Fermi level of p-Co₃O₄/C until thermodynamic equilibrium is reached, concurrently establishing a built-in electric field across the junction. Upon visible-light excitation of both semiconductor components, the synergistic action of the built-in field, electrostatic attraction, and band curvature drives photogenerated electrons from the conduction band of Co₃O₄/C (possessing limited reducing power) to recombine with holes in the valence band of ZnO (possessing limited oxidizing strength) directly at the interfacial region. Thus, energetic electrons in ZnO’s CB (− 0.52 V vs. NHE) are maintained for H₂ evolution and CO₂ reduction, whereas energetic holes in Co₃O₄/C’s VB (+ 1.68 V vs. NHE) are used for water oxidation and sacrificial agent oxidation.

Radical quenching studies summarized in Table 8 shed light on the underlying mechanistic details. Addition of silver nitrate as an electron scavenger caused pronounced suppression of both H₂ (80.5% inhibition) and CO (79.4% inhibition) formation, underscoring the critical role of photogenerated electrons in fuel production. The introduction of EDTA as a hole trap led to roughly 52% reduction in photocatalytic output, confirming that holes participate actively in oxidizing the sacrificial reagent to sustain the photocatalytic redox cycle. The addition of BQ (•O₂⁻ scavenger) reduced H₂ evolution rate by 19%. Despite full N₂ purging, trace dissolved O₂ (usually a few ppm) persists in the aqueous solution. Photogenerated CB electrons convert remaining O₂ to create •O₂⁻, which the BQ scavenger fights with protons for, causing the observed reduction. tert-Butanol, a selective hydroxyl radical scavenger, produced negligible effect on the reaction outcome, implying that OH species are not kinetically relevant intermediates under these conditions. ESR observations using DMPO as the spin-trapping agent (Fig. 9a–b) confirmed the S-scheme route by detecting significant •O₂⁻ and •OH signals under visible-light irradiation, which were missing in the dark. Although Mott–Schottky, PL, EIS, ESR, and scavenger experiments collectively support S-scheme charge transfer, unambiguous verification would require operando XPS measurements performed under actual illumination to directly observe the anticipated interfacial charge migration dynamics. Stability and recyclability

The operational durability and structural integrity of the ZnO–Co₃O₄/C photocatalyst across successive reuse cycles are documented in Fig. 10. Both hydrogen and carbon monoxide evolution activities remain largely preserved throughout five consecutive cycles, with only marginal decline observed. Ex-situ characterization of the recovered photocatalyst after cycling verifies that the heterostructure retains its original phase composition and morphology, confirming satisfactory recyclability within the examined operational window. The stability was a preferable parameter of the present photocatalyst, in fact, it kept 95.5% hydrogen evolution activity and 93.5% CO production rate in five successive runs. No obvious wave was observed from the XRD patterns, and the surface morphology remained almost unchanged after cycling, showing excellent ability against photo corrosion and structure deterioration. These findings reveal the long-term durability of the MOF-originated heterostructure for photocatalysis. While attenuated PL intensity, compressed EIS arcs, ESR spin-adduct signals, and scavenger quenching experiments all confirm enhanced charge separation efficiency, these techniques alone cannot mechanistically distinguish between a type-II and an S-scheme transfer mode. Mott-Schottky experiments support this view, revealing ZnO’s conduction band potential at -0.52 V vs. NHE and Co₃O₄/C’s valence band potential at + 1.68 V. This band alignment satisfies the thermodynamic requirements for an S-scheme mechanism, allowing for low-energy charge carrier recombination at the interface while preserving high-energy electrons and holes for redox processes. Visible-light ESR showed the formation of •O₂⁻ and •OH radicals, indicating the existence of spatially separated charge carriers with high redox potential. The convergence of electrochemical, spectroscopic, and catalytic evidence strongly points toward S-scheme charge transfer as the dominant operational mechanism. Definitive confirmation, however, will necessitate advanced operando techniques such as light-operated XPS or Kelvin probe force microscopy in future investigations.

Conclusions

A ZnO–Co₃O₄/C S-scheme heterojunction photocatalyst was produced by thermally converting a Zn/Co bimetallic ZIF precursor (E-factor: 3.2) at 600 °C for 2 h using an eco-friendly water–ethanol solvothermal method. Multi-technique characterization comprising XRD, FESEM, HRTEM, and XPS collectively confirmed the formation of intimate heterojunctions between wurtzite ZnO and spinel Co₃O₄ nanocrystallites embedded within a residual carbon matrix. Mott-Schottky analysis determined the n-type (ZnO) and p-type (Co₃O₄/C) and provided a band alignment diagram for S-scheme charge transfer. Complementary PL, EIS, ESR, and radical trapping experiments demonstrated that the S-scheme architecture promotes highly efficient spatial separation of photogenerated charge carriers. The optimized heterostructure outperformed pristine ZnO and Co₃O₄/C by 4.8 and 2.1, respectively, with a visible-light H₂ evolution rate of 2340 ± 45 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹ (AQY = 8.2% at 450 nm) and a CO₂-to-CO conversion rate of 58.7 ± 2.1 µmol g⁻¹ The photo catalyst performed above 93% after five cycles without structural deterioration. This study shows that green MOF-derived S-scheme heterostructures may be used for scalable, dual solar fuel generation, and the systematic structure–activity analysis gives interface-engineered photo catalyst design recommendations.
