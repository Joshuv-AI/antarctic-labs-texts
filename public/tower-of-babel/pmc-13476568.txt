Examining the nexus between household livelihood vulnerability and ecosystem service dependence in southern Shaanxi
Authors: Wei Liu, Liyang Guo, Jie Xu, Marcus W Feldman, Cong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUrban resettlement is a key mechanism for promoting ecological conservation and improving livelihoods. This study investigates how urban resettlement influences the relationship between household livelihood vulnerability and the index of dependence on ecosystem services (IDES). Using survey data from 657 households in Ankang prefecture, Shaanxi province, China, we analyze this relationship in the context of resettlement. Using the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s (IPCC) “exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity” framework for assessing livelihood vulnerability, and measuring IDES across provisioning, regulating, and cultural services, households are classified into four “vulnerability-dependence” categories via the four-quadrant method. The findings indicate that (1) relocated households exhibit significantly higher livelihood vulnerability and lower overall IDES than non-relocated households; (2) new-stage relocation and higher subsidies increase the likelihood of households being in the “high vulnerability-low dependence” and “low vulnerability-low dependence” categories; and (3) long-distance relocation significantly reduces the probability of households falling into the “low vulnerability-high dependence” category. To address ongoing challenges, policymakers should optimize resource management, facilitate livelihood transformation, and thereby enhancing both livelihood and ecological security.Keywords: social-ecological systems, livelihood vulnerability, dependence on ecosystem services, urban resettlement policy, sustainable livelihood

Introduction

Global climate change is increasingly evident through the rising frequency and intensity of extreme weather events. This poses unprecedented challenges to livelihood stability and long-term development for populations in vulnerable and impoverished regions.1 In response, resettlement has emerged as a key strategy to address climate-induced disasters, mitigate ecological degradation, and enhance human well-being.2 While existing research often focuses on development-induced poverty alleviation and disaster migration,3,4,5 this study specifically focuses on urban resettlement, defined here as the planned relocation of populations from rural or peri-urban areas to urban centers. To evaluate the outcomes of resettlement from a livelihood perspective, this study centers on two interconnected concepts: livelihood vulnerability and dependence on ecosystem services. Livelihood vulnerability refers to the extent to which a livelihood system is subjected to external pressures and is susceptible to damage.6,7 Greater vulnerability entails a lower capacity to withstand shocks.6 Dependence on ecosystem services reflects the degree to which households rely on these services.8,9 Livelihood vulnerability and dependence on ecosystem services are intimately linked with livelihoods, respectively regarded within the sustainable livelihoods framework. External conditions may precipitate adverse livelihood states that increase vulnerability, while the natural resource base is essential for maintaining livelihood stability.10,11

Numerous studies have explored the socio-cultural and economic impacts of urban resettlement.12,13 However, fewer studies have addressed livelihood sustainability in this context, and there is a paucity of relevant empirical evidence. Livelihood, as a vital means of survival and a core component of household sustainable development, offers a crucial lens through which to examine resettlement outcomes.14 Livelihood capital constitutes the resource base for households facing external shocks and includes natural, financial, physical, human, and social dimensions.15 This capital shapes the range of livelihood strategies—from pure agriculture to diversified activities—that households adopt. During urban resettlement, diversified strategies help households reduce risk and enhance their resilience.16 Evidence from existing research indicates that while urban resettlement has significant socio-economic impacts17 and environmental benefits,18 it may also exacerbate the vulnerability of displaced, land-losing households, potentially undermining livelihood security for disadvantaged groups and therefore impeding sustainable urban development.19,20 Households’ dependence on ecosystem services often undergoes profound changes during the resettlement process, and there may be inherent tensions between such dependence and livelihood vulnerability. However, few studies have empirically examined the two within a unified framework. This paper examines the impact of urban resettlement on the relationship between household livelihood vulnerability and dependence on ecosystem services at the household level.

The concept of vulnerability originated in disaster-risk research21 and has since been widely adopted across disciplines, including anthropology, ecology, sociology, and sustainability science.22 The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) defines vulnerability as the degree to which a system is susceptible to adverse effects and lacks the capacity to cope. This is expressed as a function of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.23 This framework has been widely applied in sustainable livelihood research to develop region-specific indices, such as the climate vulnerability and adaptive capacity index for Himalayan communities.24 It has also been used to assess vulnerability in regions like the Gangetic Plain7 and the Indian Himalayas.25 As the impacts of global climate change on rural households deepen, the vulnerability-assessment framework proposed by the IPCC has been increasingly applied in the field of sustainable livelihoods, with livelihood vulnerability becoming a key focus of research.22 The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment defines ecosystem services as the direct or indirect benefits—both tangible and intangible—that ecosystems provide for human well-being.26 As human dependence on ecological systems intensifies, reliance on ecosystem services has emerged as a key area for the study of ecological protection and sustainable development. The index of dependence on ecosystem services (IDES), proposed by Yang et al.,8 provides a crucial tool for quantifying this dependence and has been applied in multiple empirical studies.9,27,28 Research on livelihood vulnerability and dependence on ecosystem services has recently expanded, and both domains present opportunities for cross-disciplinary integration. Studies on livelihood vulnerability have primarily concentrated on its drivers—climate change, natural disasters, economic transitions, and socio-cultural influences—and use diverse assessment frameworks.29,30,31,32 Only a limited number of studies have assessed the impact of policy implementation on reducing livelihood vulnerability at the household level. Research on dependence on ecosystem services has predominantly addressed domains such as rural livelihoods9 and household well-being.27 Key topics include evaluating the dependence on ecosystem services33 and the harmonization of human livelihoods with ecosystem services.9,34

Although livelihood vulnerability and IDES are closely linked and jointly shape the dynamic relationship between households and the environment, existing analyses have largely been unidimensional18,20,23,35 and lack a comprehensive examination of the complex interactions between the two. Specifically, these studies have neither thoroughly elucidated the trade-offs between ecosystem services and human activities nor explored the potential contribution of households at different vulnerability levels to ecosystem services. As a pivotal ecological and development-oriented initiative, the extent to which urban resettlement can realize its original policy objective of attaining the dual sustainability goals of livelihood development and ecological conservation depends not only on evaluating the policy’s ecological benefits from the perspective of rural households but also on examining the sustainable livelihood transformation of relocated households, with particular emphasis on revealing the trade-offs between human activities and ecosystem services across policy impacts. Although existing studies have examined the relationship between ecosystem services and human well-being,27,36,37 the broad conceptual scope of “human well-being” hampers precise capture of the specific interaction mechanisms between livelihood activities and IDES at the micro-household level. Here, we study the inherent interaction between IDES and livelihood vulnerability at the micro-household level, thereby enabling a more comprehensive assessment of the effects of urban resettlement and providing policy grounds for promoting synergistic and sustainable development of ecology and livelihoods. The findings are expected to yield practical insights into balancing ecological conservation with livelihood security during resettlement, a central challenge in achieving sustainable development goals.

This study focuses on rural households in southern Shaanxi, China, as its empirical subjects (Figures 1 and 2). Utilizing questionnaire survey data, it assesses their livelihood vulnerability (Figure 3) and IDES (Figure 4, Table 1) and classifies them into “vulnerability-dependence” categories (Figures 5 and 6). A multinomial logistic regression is employed to examine the impact of urban resettlement on household livelihood vulnerability and IDES (Table 2). We address two primary research questions: (1) what are the degrees of livelihood vulnerability and IDES for households that have been resettled in urban areas of southern Shaanxi, China? and (2)in what ways do the characteristics of urban resettlement affect the “vulnerability-dependence” categories of households? The study aims to elucidate the role of urbanization resettlement policies in reducing farming households’ livelihood vulnerability and decreasing their IDES, thereby offering policy implications for the synergistic governance of ecological protection and sustainable livelihoods within resettlement programs. Furthermore, to systematically address these research questions, this paper proposes the following research hypotheses.

Comparison of diverse livelihood strategies across categories

Table 3 illustrates the various livelihood strategies employed by households based on distinct categories. In general, households pursuing non-agricultural strategies represented the largest share, followed by those engaging in livelihood diversification and subsidy-dependent approaches. In contrast, households that rely solely on agricultural activities comprised the smallest fraction (10.807%). Among all categories, non-agricultural households dominate in number. Within the HH and LH categories, households diversifying their livelihoods are the next most frequent. HL and LH differ in the share of pure farming households. Both the HL and LL categories have the lowest frequency of pure farming households. These data suggest that households are increasingly adopting non-farming or diversified livelihood strategies rather than depending on conventional farming activities. Additionally, a notable relationship exists between the various categories and the strategies employed. Specifically, those in the HL and LL categories show a decreased likelihood of opting for pure farming, whereas LH households exhibit a lower dependence on government aid.

Comparison of livelihood vulnerability and its sub-dimensions

Table 5 shows how various characteristics of relocation affect the livelihood vulnerability of households and their three sub-dimensions using the t test. Households that moved exhibit greater livelihood vulnerability than their counterparts that did not move. In particular, compared to non-migrant families, those that moved face significantly greater exposure levels, whereas their sensitivity and adaptive capacity are markedly lower. Additionally, although the livelihood vulnerability of households in the new stage of relocation is less than that of those in the early stage, the difference is not statistically significant. Regarding relocation subsidies, there is no notable difference in livelihood vulnerability between households that receive high subsidies and those that receive low subsidies. However, in terms of sensitivity, households with higher relocation subsidies demonstrate significantly lower sensitivity than those with lower subsidies. Furthermore, households that have relocated over long distances show a significantly lower adaptive capacity than those that have relocated over short distances.

Analysis of the relationship between livelihood vulnerability and IDES

Figure 7 illustrates the IDES related to various levels of livelihood vulnerability for both non-relocated and relocated households. It shows that in the case of non-relocated households, the box positions are generally elevated compared to those of relocated households, suggesting that non-relocated households typically exhibit a greater reliance on ecosystem services than their relocated counterparts. Impact of relocation factors on “vulnerability-dependence” categories

Table 7 uses regression analysis to see how factors associated with relocation influence the “vulnerability-dependence” categories. In model 1, the effects of engaging in relocation on the 4 categories are analyzed. models 2 to 4 include variables such as relocation timing, relocation subsidy, and relocation distance to further investigate how these impact the categories. In the regression models, the HH category is designated as the reference category. In comparison to the HH category, relocation shows a significant negative association (5% level) with the LH category, indicating that relocation coincides with greater livelihood vulnerability and lower reliance on ecosystem services. In contrast, relative to the HH category, both new-stage relocation and high subsidy relocation show a significant positive association (1% level) with the HL and LL categories, pointing to a pattern in which these relocation types tend to coincide with a higher likelihood of being in those outcome categories; thus, hypothesis H1a is supported. Additionally, compared to the HH category, long-distance relocation is significantly negatively associated (5% level) with the LH category, suggesting that long-distance relocation tends to co-occur with a lower probability of falling into the LH category, thereby supporting hypothesis H1b. These results collectively demonstrate that urban resettlement exerts heterogeneous effects on different “vulnerability-dependence” categories of rural households, thereby supporting hypothesis H1.

Discussion

Urban resettlement is regarded as a key strategy to alleviate livelihood challenges among vulnerable groups in ecologically fragile regions.38 It can improve households’ dependence on ecosystem services and reduce their livelihood vulnerability.39 This study connects livelihood vulnerability and IDES to assess urban resettlement’s impact on “vulnerability-dependence” categories in southern Shaanxi’s households. Specifically, for households classified within the LL category, the strategy of engaging in non-agricultural work represents the greatest fraction, while livelihood diversification follows, and reliance on subsidies represents the smallest fraction. This suggests that non-agricultural activities may help households achieve the LL category. This aligns with the findings of Zhang and Zhao,40 who pointed out that non-agricultural activities can effectively reduce livelihood vulnerability through income diversification and capital accumulation. However, the present study uncovers a dual mechanism behind this phenomenon: on the one hand, non-agricultural employment directly strengthens livelihood adaptive capacity by enhancing households’ accumulation of financial and human capital, thereby lowering their exposure and sensitivity to external shocks, and alleviating livelihood vulnerability, but on the other, it drives a shift in household livelihood strategies from “land-dependent agricultural production” to “labor-dependent non-agricultural operations.” This transition reduces households’ reliance on the provisioning and regulating services of ecosystems, ultimately moving them toward the ideal LL state. However, this positive effect is not absolute. If resettlement sites are located in urban fringe areas with scarce employment opportunities, inadequate public services, or degraded ecosystem services, rural households, despite moving away from agriculture, may face unstable informal employment and struggle to access the ecosystem services provided by cities.41,42 This “spatial mismatch” mechanism explains the phenomenon observed by Yang and Qian43 in concentrated resettlement communities in Hangzhou, where households relying primarily on non-agricultural activities appeared to exhibit higher vulnerability. Therefore, whether the non-agricultural pathway can successfully guide households toward the LL category depends on whether the resettlement has achieved an effective spatial transition from a “rural ecosystem-dependent system” to a “functionally sound urban social, economic, ecological support system.”

Our research shows that involvement in urban resettlement initiatives is significantly associated with a lower probability of households being in the LH category, in contrast to the HH category. Specifically, urban resettlement is associated with greater household livelihood vulnerability. Moreover, compared with non-relocated households, relocated households experience significantly higher livelihood vulnerability and lower overall IDES. These findings engage in a deep dialog with earlier research on resettlement in southern Shaanxi by Li et al.36 and Liu et al.44 Li et al.27 demonstrated that resettlement substantially reduced households’ dependence on ecosystem services, but did not address the accompanying increase in vulnerability. Our study goes further by critically uncovering the core mechanism of “vulnerability transfer” behind this pattern: while urban resettlement helps households avoid natural risks—such as exposure to natural disasters and ecological degradation in their places of origin—and reduces their direct reliance on the ecosystem, it simultaneously exposes them to new socio-economic risks. These include market, employment, and social integration risks arising from land loss, the breakdown of pre-existing social networks, and the forced restructuring of livelihood strategies. This finding aligns with studies by Zhang and Song45 and Aboye,46 both of which pointed out that some resettled households face the risk of increased livelihood vulnerability due to the loss of land, social networks, and original livelihood practices, while new livelihoods have yet to stabilize. However, the underlying mechanism extends beyond mere resource loss; it centers on the failure to restructure livelihood capital during spatial displacement. When rural households migrate from rural ecological spaces to urban social spaces, the dominant risks to which their livelihoods are exposed also undergo a fundamental shift: from nature-related ecological risks to socio-economic risks dominated by market conditions and social networks. Huang et al.19 and Yang and Qian43 further suggest that insufficient supplementation and transformation of livelihood capital in this process will lead to inadequate household sensitivity and adaptive capacity, thereby trapping them in a “high vulnerability” predicament. This echoes a common phenomenon identified in global climate migration research, namely that relocation often shifts populations from one type of environmental risk to another type of socio-economic risk, resulting in “vulnerability transfer” rather than elimination.35,47 Moreover, urban resettlement may exert a dampening effect on the growth of rural households’ dependence on ecosystem services,9,27 which agrees with Cheng’s48 assertion that relocation directly reduces the intensity of human activities in the original habitats, thereby easing direct pressures on ecosystem structure and function from activities such as agricultural production and fuelwood gathering.49 Furthermore, centralized urban infrastructure and public services can effectively substitute for the provisioning and regulating services previously sourced directly from nature.27,36 However, this dampening effect can be superficial. If resettlement sites lack adequate ecosystem services or if urban systems fail to provide stable, equitable alternatives, households risk shifting from ecological dependence to urban ecological poverty or heightened socio-economic vulnerability.41,50 Therefore, a systemic resilience perspective is essential to evaluate resettlement outcomes, ensuring vulnerability is not merely displaced but genuinely mitigated.

Different relocation factors have varying impacts on the “vulnerability-dependence” categories. Our findings suggest that compared with the HH category, households undergoing new-stage relocation and those receiving high relocation subsidies are more likely to be in the HL and LL categories. This reflects the positive buffering effect of initial resource injection and facility improvement.43 The new-stage relocation usually entails more complete community infrastructure and public service provisions, while high subsidies provide a critical buffer for households to achieve livelihood initiation or human capital investment at the relocation destination.43 Both types of relocation factors help alleviate the vulnerability of rural households during the initial transition period and support them to break away more rapidly from their livelihood dependence on the original ecosystem. However, the sustainability of this effect faces challenges. If resettlement communities are located in areas where urban ecological health spatial inequalities are intensifying,51 short-term material improvements may be offset by long-term environmental degradation, lack of public services, and social exclusion. Conversely, households that have relocated over long distances show a reduced likelihood of attaining the LH category. This highlights the role of spatial distance as a key driver of vulnerability. Long-distance migration not only leads to physical separation from their original livelihood capital and social networks51 but also means that migrants may face multiple challenges, such as cross-cultural adaptation, institutional barriers, and marginalization in the urban labor market.47,52 This “spatial uprooting” effect systematically elevates the baseline of livelihood vulnerability in spatial terms, making it difficult for relocated households to rebuild effective livelihood buffers in the short term, thus increasing their likelihood of falling into a state of high vulnerability.35

Conclusion

Urban resettlement is a major development initiative in China designed to fundamentally transform household livelihoods. This study evaluates how urban resettlement influences households, particularly in terms of their livelihood vulnerability and dependence on ecosystem services. Relocated households are shown to exhibit significantly greater vulnerability than their original settlement counterparts, whereas their overall IDES is considerably lower. This decline in ecosystem service dependence appears to result from reduced dependence on provisioning, regulating, and cultural services. Further analysis reveals that new-stage relocation and high subsidy relocation are positively associated with the likelihood of belonging to the HL and LL categories, while long-distance relocation shows a negative association with the LH category.

Households continue to face challenges during urban resettlement, including how to reduce livelihood vulnerability while easing dependence on ecosystem services, and how to encourage a shift toward the LL category. Our findings offer practical guidance for those working on urban resettlement and sustainable livelihoods. For policymakers, several directions are suggested: establishing differentiated subsidy schemes and dependable social safety nets that prioritize long-distance relocatees and households receiving low subsidies; improving the overall design of resettlement policies so that industrial development and livelihood support are planned in tandem throughout the entire process; setting up a dynamic monitoring system to track how household vulnerability and dependence on ecosystems evolve; and strengthening market-based mechanisms that realize the value of ecological products, thereby aligning ecological protection with sustained household income growth. For local officials, community workers, and non-governmental organizations, the study highlights the importance of providing skills training that matches the specific industrial features of each resettlement site rather than offering generic programs; building employment support platforms that help relocated households secure stable non-agricultural income; and improving community-level mechanisms for coordinating resource management in ways that combine ecological protection with livelihood support. These strategies are consistent with China’s national priorities of ecological civilization and rural revitalization, and help to connect local implementation with macro-level governance. China’s experience has not only relieved pressure on ecosystem service provision and reduced livelihood vulnerability among resettled populations but also offers a replicable model for other regions and developing countries that face similar trade-offs between ecological conservation and livelihoods during resettlement. It is broadly relevant for advancing the UN Sustainable Development Goals and fostering harmony between people and nature. Future research could explore how different types of households in different regions adapt their livelihoods, providing more systematic theoretical backing for the joint enhancement of human well-being and ecological security.

Limitations of the study

This study has certain limitations. The focus was on Ankang prefecture, located in southern Shaanxi province, which possesses a distinct geographical profile regarding the types of ecosystem services and patterns of livelihood. There exist considerable variances in urban resettlement policies, ecological conditions, and the adaptive capacities of households across various regions, suggesting that the findings may not be universally applicable in different geographical contexts. Secondly, there is a degree of subjectivity in variable selection. Although the selection of indicators for IDES and livelihood vulnerability is based on existing research frameworks, the weighting of some indicators relies on subjective judgment, and the coverage of key dimensions of the “ecology-livelihood” interaction is insufficient. Furthermore, this study is based on cross-sectional data, lacking long-term tracking of the dynamic evolution process of households’ IDES and livelihood vulnerability, which limits the understanding of the long-term trends and patterns of change. To study the evolution of rural household types, longitudinal data are necessary for long-term follow-up analysis. Future research could further expand the geographical scope and enhance the universality of conclusions by collecting survey data across different periods and regions.

Method details

Study area

The region examined in this study is Ankang Prefecture, situated in the southern part of Shaanxi Province (Figure 1). Nestled between the Qinling and Daba Mountains, Ankang Prefecture serves as a crucial area within the upper Han River basin, characterized by its distinctive natural landscape and abundant ecological resources. As a core component of the Qinling-Daba Mountains Biodiversity Ecological Function Zone and the key water source area for the Middle Route of China’s South-to-North Water Diversion Project, Ankang provides a compelling example where ecological conservation, disaster risk governance, and urban-oriented resettlement are pursued simultaneously—a policy mix increasingly common in ecologically fragile mountain regions across China and other developing countries. From a socio-economic perspective, Ankang Prefecture has long faced notable development challenges. Historically, the rural household poverty rate remained relatively high, with agricultural production constrained by fragile ecological conditions and frequent natural disasters. The regional economy has been predominantly agrarian, with limited non-agricultural employment opportunities and per capita disposable income below the provincial average.36 Demographically, the rural population constitutes a large share of the total residents, and the labor-force structure is characterized by a high proportion of agricultural workers and a degree of rural–urban labor mobility.

After the Shaanxi provincial government initiated the Disaster Resettlement Project in 2011,44 Ankang Prefecture became a key implementation area, with a large rural population relocated to urban resettlement areas. After relocation, the housing conditions of the residents improved significantly, living infrastructure became more complete, accessibility to public services greatly increased, household economic sources diversified, poverty incidence markedly decreased, livelihood vulnerability weakened, adaptive capacity strengthened, and human well-being improved considerably. This resettlement initiative has now been in place for over a decade, allowing us to examine how resettled households’ livelihood vulnerability and dependence on ecosystem services evolve beyond the initial transition period. Prior research in this region3 focused primarily on short-term outcomes during the early implementation phase. Studies of single-objective resettlement programs elsewhere in China, including poverty alleviation-oriented relocation in Guizhou53 and ecological migration in Gansu,54 have also tended to examine narrower policy targets. In this context, Ankang’s multi-objective resettlement approach offers a distinctive empirical setting for understanding the interplay between livelihood systems and ecosystem services under compound policy pressures. These features make Ankang Prefecture a particularly suitable setting for research on rural household livelihoods.

Data sources and sampling design

The data utilized in this study were derived from a livelihood survey conducted by the research team among farmers in Ankang, Shaanxi Province (Figure 2). We used a multistage stratified random sampling approach to ensure sample representativeness and analytical credibility, with explicit delineation of stratification criteria, sampling frames, sample-size determination, and nonresponse handling throughout the process. In the first stage, the sampling frame encompassed all counties in Ankang Prefecture, stratified by two principal criteria—local GDP and resettlement intensity—and three counties (Hanbin, Ziyang, and Ningshan) were purposively selected to balance economic heterogeneity and resettlement characteristics, thereby ensuring the sample reflects Ankang’s overall conditions. In the second stage, administrative-village units were sampled from these counties, specifically including four townships and eight administrative villages in Ningshan County and Hanbin District, as well as three representative resettlement communities in Ziyang County. Each village unit was further categorized into three types: villages with only non-relocated local households, resettlement villages entirely composed of relocated households, and resettlement villages with a mix of relocated and local households. In the third stage, the list of villager groups provided by the village committee of each selected administrative village served as the sampling frame, and two villager groups were randomly selected from each village using simple random sampling to ensure randomness within strata. In the fourth stage, the sampling frame consisted of all permanent households within the selected villager groups, and a full census of these households was carried out to maximize the sample size. For nonresponse, the village committee was consulted to obtain relatives’ contact information for telephone interviews; when telephone interviews were not feasible, nonresponding households were substituted with adjacent households of the same type within the same villager group to preserve balance across strata. Finally, we conducted face-to-face household interviews with all permanent residents in the selected groups during the survey period. Using a semi-structured interview format, we systematically collected information on household basic circumstances, daily livelihood activities, economic income sources, and relocation-related details.

Survey quality control

To ensure the reliability and validity of the research data, rigorous quality-control measures were implemented across the entire process of questionnaire design and data collection.

Questionnaire validity and scale optimization

The questionnaire was designed to match the study’s core theoretical constructs. Content validity was ensured through expert review and iterative revisions. A pretest was carried out in three typical resettlement communities in Ankang, yielding 52 valid responses that were used to refine the questionnaire. Based on the pretest results, three targeted revisions were made to the questionnaire: (1) To address potential dialect-related misunderstandings, academic terms like “livelihood diversification” and “ecosystem services” were replaced with locally understood expressions such as “the number of household income sources” and “the proportion of income from relying on mountain resources”; (2) The skip logic for household income questions was improved to prevent duplicate or missing answers; (3) The number of sensitive questions on household assets and loans was reduced from 12 to 8 to lessen respondents’ reluctance.

Data collection process control

All enumerators received centralized training before the survey. The survey team comprised 22 master’s and doctoral students in human geography and applied economics, all with prior rural survey experience. A three-day intensive training was held before the survey, covering consistent interpretation of questions, standardized interview techniques, familiarization with the local dialect, on-site detection of logical inconsistencies, and research ethics. Following the training, 18 enumerators who passed a simulated interview and a written test were selected and formed six teams. Each team included at least one member fluent in the local dialect and a local village cadre as a guide. Key responses were verified with respondents on site, and enumerators signed each completed questionnaire to ensure accountability. Supervisors performed random spot checks and unannounced visits. Each day, teams peer-reviewed questionnaires for completeness and logical consistency, correcting discrepancies immediately. A double-review system was adopted: after fieldwork each day, every team cross-checked all questionnaires, and those with logical contradictions were re-verified through same-day telephone call-backs. In total, 87 questionnaires underwent telephone verification, and 132 data errors were corrected.

Data entry and cleaning

To ensure data quality, we focused on rigorous data cleaning during processing. We used double data entry: two researchers entered each questionnaire independently, and entries were compared in Stata 17, resulting in a 99.7% agreement rate. Cleaning comprised range and logic checks to verify response standardization, data entry accuracy, and internal consistency. All data were then verified a second time. By combining process controls and outcome checks, we maximized the authenticity and accuracy of the dataset.

Of the 670 questionnaires distributed, 657 complete responses were obtained, reflecting a 98.06% return. Of these, 459 responses came from households that had been relocated, while 198 originated from those that had not.

The questionnaire employed in this study was designed to closely align with the core research framework. It comprises four principal sections: (1) Household Basic Information: This section covers fundamental socio-economic characteristics, including household composition, the highest educational attainment of members, dependency ratios, per capita annual income, and the proportion of non-agricultural employment. Additionally, it provides contextual details such as regional location and relocation status. (2) Livelihood Vulnerability Assessment: The questions were designed using the ‘Exposure-Sensitivity-Adaptive Capacity’ analytical framework to evaluate the external risks faced by farming households, their sensitivity to these risks, and the status of their livelihood capital in coping with such risks. (3) Ecosystem Service Dependency Survey: This survey focuses on examining the interdependence between household livelihoods and ecosystems, detailing the various benefits derived from ecosystem services, including provisioning, regulating, and cultural services. (4) Resettlement-Related Information: This section primarily collects key characteristics such as household participation in resettlement, the timing of resettlement, the amount of resettlement subsidy received, and the distance of relocation.

Theoretical framework

Based on the UK’s Department for International Development’s55 ‘Sustainable Livelihoods Framework’ and insights from Liu et al. (2020a, 2026)44,56 and Li et al. (2021),27 the study develops an analytical framework that focuses on the ‘vulnerability-dependence’ categories as core livelihood outcomes. The framework not only analyses the complex dynamic linkage mechanisms between livelihood capital stocks, livelihood strategy choices, and livelihood outcomes in the ‘vulnerability-dependence’ category but also highlights the interactions of households with the environment and social systems in the livelihood decision-making process, providing a systematic analytical perspective for understanding the dynamic evolution of livelihood systems.

Under urban resettlement, households face challenges of ecological vulnerability and livelihood risks. On the one hand, the ecological conditions of their original habitats are often unfavorable. Frequent natural disasters, ongoing ecological degradation, and resource scarcity encountered during environmental protection collectively constitute the main manifestations of their ecological vulnerability.51 On the other hand, as these households previously resided mostly in remote mountainous areas, they also bear numerous livelihood risks.5 These intertwined factors have profound negative effects on household livelihood assets, constraining their sustainable development during the urban resettlement process.

Figure 5 shows the analytical framework for household ‘vulnerability-dependence’ categories under urban resettlement, revealing how households, in response to external environmental disturbances, adjust livelihood capital to balance resource dependence relationships. This forms a multidimensional poverty reduction mechanism and helps achieve coordinated and sustainable development between their household systems and the external environment. To empirically operationalize the proposed framework, the study is structured as follows. First, we assess households’ vulnerability under urban resettlement in southern Shaanxi using the IPCC framework of Exposure-Sensitivity-Adaptive Capacity. Second, we measure households’ IDES across three dimensions: provisioning, regulating, and cultural services. Next, we classify livelihood vulnerability and IDES into four types and conduct a comparative analysis of household livelihood strategies and resettlement characteristics within these categories. Finally, we employ multinomial logistic regression to examine how urban resettlement and various relocation attributes influence the ‘vulnerability-dependence’ aspect of households.

Construction of indicators for livelihood vulnerability

Based on existing literature and the empirical reality of the survey area, this study selected the ‘Exposure-Sensitivity-Adaptive Capacity’ analytical framework proposed by the IPCC and constructed a livelihood vulnerability indicator system applicable to the research area (Figure 3). ‘Exposure’ indicates the risk that households may face under various types of external environmental shocks. Accordingly, four indicators—agricultural loss, property loss, livestock loss, and labor force shock—were chosen to directly capture households’ actual exposure to external risks such as natural disasters and market fluctuations.23,35 ‘Sensitivity’ is a degree of sensitivity shown by households when facing external risk shocks, usually reflecting a change in some aspect of the household after being hit by a shock or the formation of a certain trend.57 Sensitivity was measured through loan accessibility, the proportion of medical expenditure, drinking water safety, and perception of resettlement loss, covering financial resilience, basic living security, and welfare loss perception.21,57 ‘Adaptive capacity’ denotes the resilience of households when facing risk disturbances and their potential for post-risk restoration.58 This study examined the adaptive capacity of households from the perspective of livelihood capital, which includes natural capital, financial capital, physical capital, human capital, and social capital.57,59 Drawing on the Sustainable Livelihoods Framework, 14 sub-indicators across these five capital dimensions were selected to comprehensively assess adaptive capacity.10,15 Typically, a household’s capacity to manage external risks and shocks increases in relation to the amount of livelihood capital it has, resulting in a decreased level of livelihood vulnerability.

Construction of indicators for ecosystem service dependence

IDES can serve as an indicator representing local ecological conservation status. As household input-output costs affect their dependence on the ecosystem, the study uses IDES to measure each household’s dependence on the ecosystem, facilitating comparison among different households.27 This study adopts the definition and index system construction for IDES from Yang et al. (2013).8 The index system comprises a total IDES index along with three sub-indices. The total IDES index represents the proportion of net benefits that households gain from the ecosystem relative to the absolute value of the aggregate net benefits obtained from both the ecosystem and various socio-economic activities.27 The three sub-indices are categorized as provisioning services, regulating services, and cultural services (Figure 4). The net benefits associated with provisioning services predominantly arise from income generated through crops, livestock, and other agricultural operations. Regulating services encompass ecological compensation income, which includes subsidies from initiatives like Grain for Green, forest conservation, and agriculture subsidies. Cultural services include income from ecotourism projects conducted in rural areas, such as rural tourism, rural homestays, and picking gardens.

The numerals ‘0’ and ‘1’ following the service type indicators (P, R, and C) denote direct and indirect benefits derived from ESs, respectively, with the core classification criteria being the correlation path between benefits and ecosystems, as well as the degree of households’ direct dependence on ecosystem supply.8,26 Direct benefits denote goods or services derived directly from ecosystems that households obtain through direct extraction, harvest, or utilization of ecosystem material products and regulatory functions, whose value can typically be quantified through market prices, imputed market values, or replacement costs. Such benefits have a direct productive correlation with ecosystems, as their generation completely relies on the direct supply of ecosystems, representing immediate, material contributions to household income or consumption. Typical examples include crop harvests, livestock products, fuelwood collection (all P0), and direct cash subsidies from ecological compensation programs (R0). Indirect benefits refer to advantages obtained indirectly through the provisioning, regulating, or cultural services provided by ecosystems via derived socio-economic activities that take ecosystem service functions as the core carrier and realize value transformation through households’ secondary business activities. These benefits do not usually manifest as tangible goods for direct trading but rather support livelihoods by maintaining productive environments or delivering non-material services. Typical examples include value-added income from non-timber forest product processing (P1), operation and labor income related to ecotourism and rural homestays (C1), and regulating services such as water purification and climate regulation that maintain basic conditions for agricultural production and household well-being. Distinguishing direct from indirect benefits is essential for capturing the full scale of household dependence on ecosystem services, given that indirect contributions are often substantial yet frequently overlooked.

For households, income is typically categorized into operating income, non-farm business income, wage income, property income, transfer income, other income, and avoided costs. This study links each income subcategory to the corresponding ecosystem services. Agricultural operating income—including crop income, non-timber forest products (NTFPs) income, other forestry income, and livestock income—is classified under provisioning services. When household socio-economic activities are related to ecotourism, such income is classified as cultural services; otherwise, it is considered unrelated to ecosystem services. Local ecosystem service payment schemes primarily target regulating services, thus grain-for-green program subsidies, ecological forest subsidies, and agricultural subsidies are categorized as revenues from regulating services.

Four ‘vulnerability-dependence’ categories

Referring to Li et al. (2021),27 LVI is on the horizontal axis, and the IDES is on the vertical axis, employing a four-quadrant classification method to categorize the ‘vulnerability-dependence’ relationship of households. The ‘vulnerability-dependence’ relationship falls into the following four categories (Figure 6): I: high vulnerability-high dependence (H-H); II: high vulnerability-low dependence (H-L); III: low vulnerability-high dependence (L-H); IV: low vulnerability-low dependence (L-L). Within this classification, the L-L represents the most favourable scenario, while the H-L and L-H demonstrate intermediate outcomes, and H-H reflects the worst condition.

Using a sample of 459 relocated households, we conduct a quantitative analysis.

Drawing upon existing research frameworks and adapting to the specific circumstances of this survey, we employed the following four classification criteria: (1) By resettlement type: ecological restoration households; project-induced relocation households; poverty alleviation households; disaster-related relocation households; and households relocated for other reasons. (2) By relocation area: local communities; local townships; neighboring townships; and households relocated to other regions. (3) By relocation timing: early-stage resettled households and households resettled in a new phase. (4) By relocation subsidy: high-subsidy households and low-subsidy households.

Following the completion of this overall stratified description, we further defined three key relocation characteristics in a more operational manner to serve the second part of the aforementioned regression analysis and conducted sample statistics: First, regarding the timing of relocation, the implementation of disaster resettlement in southern Shaanxi Province in 2011 serves as a pivotal reference point. Prior to this implementation, most households relocated sporadically in the early phase, characterized by a relatively limited scale of relocation, lower subsidy levels, and minimal policy support. The households that participated in relocations thereafter constituted the new-phase resettlement cohort, comprising 314 households (68.41%). Second, concerning relocation subsidies, this study categorized households into high-subsidy and low-subsidy groups based on the median subsidy amount received. Among these, 167 households (36.38%) received high relocation subsidies. Finally, regarding relocation distance, we primarily classified relocations as either short-distance or long-distance based on administrative levels and the extent of changes in livelihood activities. Short-distance relocation refers to resettlement within the same county (e.g., within the same village, township, or across townships within the county), where the migrants’ livelihood patterns are unlikely to undergo fundamental changes. Conversely, long-distance relocation refers to resettlement across counties or cities, where migrants often encounter greater challenges in transforming their livelihoods. According to this classification, 198 households (43.14%) fell into the short-distance relocation category.
