Abstract
. This article studies the temporal approximation of hyperbolic semilinear stochastic evolution equations with multiplicative Gaussian noise by Milstein-type schemes. We take the term hyperbolic to mean that the leading operator generates a contractive, not necessarily analytic C 0 C_{0} -semigroup. Optimal convergence rates are derived for the pathwise uniform strong error E h ∞ ≔ ( 𝔼 ​ max 1 ≤ j ≤ M ​ ‖ U t j − u j ‖ X p ) 1 / p E_{h}^{\infty}\coloneqq\Big(\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\|U_{t_{j}}-u_{j}\|_{X}^{p}\Big)^{1/p} on a Hilbert space X X for p ∈ [ 2 , ∞ ) p\in[2,\infty) . Here, U U is the mild solution and u j u_{j} its Milstein approximation at time t j = j ​ h t_{j}=jh with step size h > 0 h>0 and final time T = M ​ h > 0 T=Mh>0 . For sufficiently regular nonlinearity and noise, we establish strong convergence of order one, with the error satisfying E h ∞ ≲ h ​ log ⁡ ( T / h ) E_{h}^{\infty}\lesssim h\sqrt{\log(T/h)} for rational Milstein schemes and E h ∞ ≲ h E_{h}^{\infty}\lesssim h for exponential Milstein schemes. This extends previous results from parabolic to hyperbolic SPDEs and from exponential to rational Milstein schemes. Root-mean-square error estimates are strengthened to pathwise uniform estimates. Numerical experiments validate the convergence rates for the stochastic Schrödinger equation. Further applications to Maxwell’s and transport equations are included.

Introduction

In this article, we study the temporal approximation of semilinear stochastic evolution equations of the form

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}U+AU\,\mathrm{d}t&=F(U)\,\mathrm{d}t+G(U)\,\mathrm{d}W\quad\text{on }[0,T],\\
U_{0}&=\xi\in L^{p}(\Omega;X).\end{cases}

Here,
-A
is the generator of a contractive but in general not analytic
C_{0}
-semigroup on a Hilbert space
X
, the nonlinearity
F
and the multiplicative noise
G
are globally Lipschitz,
W
is a cylindrical Brownian motion,
\xi
is the initial data, and
p\in[2,\infty)
.

The aim of this paper is to obtain strong convergence rates exceeding
1/2
for a class of time discretization schemes, so-called
Milstein schemes
, in the
hyperbolic setting. Hyperbolic SPDEs such as Schrödinger or Maxwell’s equations have have attracted considerable attention in recent years (see
[
AC18
,
BLM21
,
BC23
,
CCHS20
,
CL22
,
CLS13
,
CQS16
,
Cui25
,
HHS22
,
KLP20
]
and references therein). Strong convergence rates have been studied in this setting for the exponential Euler method for several non-parabolic equations
[
AC18
,
CCHS20
]
, which was generalised to rational schemes with a unifying approach by one of the authors in
[
KV24a
]
. However, it is known that rate
1/2
up to a logarithmic correction factor is optimal among all schemes solely using the Wiener increments as information from the Brownian motion. Indeed, already for SDEs, the error of such schemes has to grow at least like
\sqrt{\log(T/h)}h^{1/2}
for step size
h\to 0

[
MG02
]
.

The Milstein scheme avoids this shortcoming by incorporating more information from the Brownian motion. Originally developed for SDEs
[
Mil75
]
(see also
[
KP99
, Sec. 10.3]
) via an Itô–Taylor expansion of first order, Jentzen and Röckner extended it to SPDEs in their seminal paper
[
JR15
]
.

The central idea behind the Milstein scheme is as follows. Assume for simplicity that the mild solution of (
1.1
) is even a strong one, i.e. it can be written as an Itô process
U_{h}=\xi+\int_{0}^{h}F(U_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{h}G(U_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
, where
h>0
is the time step. To construct a first-order approximation, it suffices to approximate the temporal integrand
F(U_{s})\approx F(U_{0})
to order
0
. In contrast, since the stochastic integral is only of order
1/2
, the stochastic integrand should be approximated to order
1/2
rather than
0
. From Itô’s formula, we deduce
G(U_{s})\approx G(U_{0})+\int_{0}^{s}G^{\prime}(U_{r})\,\mathrm{d}U_{r}
and thus, using (
1.1
) and omitting all terms of order greater than
1/2
,

\displaystyle G(U_{s})

\displaystyle\approx G(U_{0})+\int_{0}^{s}G^{\prime}(U_{r})F(U_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{s}G^{\prime}(U_{r})G(U_{r})\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}

\displaystyle\approx G(U_{0})+\int_{0}^{s}G^{\prime}(U_{0})G(U_{0})\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}.

Inserting these approximations in the solution formula results in the expansion

\displaystyle U_{h}\approx\xi+hF(U_{0})+\int_{0}^{h}G(U_{0})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{s}G^{\prime}(U_{0})G(U_{0})\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}

with an iterated stochastic integral. An analogous reasoning applies to mild (rather than strong) solutions via mild Itô formulas, cf.
[
DPJR19
, Sec. 3.3]
, and leads to additional semigroups in the integrands. Motivated by this observation, we define the
rational Milstein scheme
by
u_{0}\coloneqq\xi
and for
1\leq j\leq M

\displaystyle u_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq R_{h}^{j}\xi+h\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}R_{h}^{j-i}F(u_{i})

(1.2)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}R_{h}^{j-i}\Big\lparen\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G(u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G^{\prime}(u_{i})\Big[\int_{t_{i}}^{s}G(u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\Big\rparen.

Here,
t_{j}=jh
,
0\leq j\leq M
, is an equidistant time grid and
R_{h}\approx S(h)
a time discretization scheme approximating the semigroup. If
R_{h}=S(h)
, (
1
) is called the
(exponential) Milstein scheme
.

The Milstein scheme has been studied extensively for parabolic SPDEs. Convergence of the root-mean-square error at rate
1
was shown in
[
JR15
]
under a commutativity condition on the noise, which has subsequently been lifted in
[
vHR20
]
. Further developments for the Milstein scheme include its analysis for SPDEs driven by non-continuous martingale noise
[
BL12
]
, its

- and almost sure convergence for advection-diffusion equations
[
BL13
]
, and the space-time-discretisation by a Milstein–Galerkin scheme
[
Kru14a
]
. Rational Milstein schemes have been formulated as mild Itô processes in
[
DPJR19
]
and a software package for the numerical approximation of the iterated stochastic integrals in (
1
) is available
[
KR23
]
. Finally, derivative-free Milstein-type schemes have been proposed
[
WG13
,
vHR23
]
, offering an easier implementation while preserving high convergence orders.

However, all of these higher-order convergence rates pertain to the parabolic case, where regularisation phenomena of the underlying analytic semigroup can be used to improve convergence rates. These are not applicable to (
1.1
) if the associated semigroup is merely contractive. Establishing higher-order convergence of the Milstein scheme for hyperbolic stochastic evolution equations has already been raised as an open research direction over a decade ago in
[
JR15
, p. 324]
, together with the generalisation from exponential to rational Milstein schemes. Numerical simulations suggest convergence at rate
1
of the Milstein scheme for stochastic Schrödinger equations
[
LL20
]
. Recently, convergence rates of up to

(\tilde{\alpha},\beta)
-scheme related to the Milstein scheme
[
FPP24
]
. Further recent developments for hyperbolic SPDEs include
[

]
, where rate

[
Cui25
]
with multiplicative noise.

Often in the literature, the error considered is the
pointwise strong error

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq j\leq M}\mathbb{E}\|U_{t_{j}}-u_{j}\|_{X}^{p},

where

is the mild solution to (

(u_{j})_{j=0,\ldots,M}
its temporal discretisation. However, (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\lparen\sup_{0\leq j\leq M}\|U_{t_{j}}-u_{j}\|^{p}\bigg\rparen

Method of proof

We extend Kato’s framework
[
Kat75
]
, which was first used for SPDEs in
[
KV24a
]
, to systematically treat hyperbolic problems beyond convergence rate
1/2
. This entails working with a pair of Hilbert spaces
Y\hookrightarrow X
such that
F
and
G
in (
1.1
) are, as in Theorem
1.1
, Lipschitz continuous on
X
and of linear growth on
Y
. Lipschitz continuity on higher order Sobolev spaces typically fails for Nemytskii operators and is thus not assumed. Although some parabolic equations are covered by the Kato setting, alternative approaches based on regularisation phenomena yield better results. Consequently, we focus on hyperbolic problems.

The key ingredient to obtain higher orders of convergence are suitable Taylor expansions, which require Fréchet differentiability. Care must be taken in specifying between which spaces this differentiability assumption is imposed. Indeed, assuming Fréchet differentiability of
F\colon X\to X
for
F
a Nemytskii operator and
X=L^{2}
immediately limits the setting to affine linear ’nonlinearities’
F
.

Proposition 1.2

(Proposition 2.8 in
[
AP95
]
)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
bounded, and
F\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),F(u)\coloneqq\phi\circ u
the Nemytskii operator associated to a Lipschitz continuous
C^{1}
-function
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. If
F\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
is Fréchet differentiable at some
\tilde{u}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
then
F
is affine linear, i.e.
F(u)=au+b
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
for all
u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
.

This was recently pointed out as a prevalent problem in the numerical analysis literature
[
DGK24
, Rem. 1.3]
. We avoid this pitfall by merely assuming Gâteaux differentiability of
F
as a map on
X
. This is satisfied for any
\phi
as in Proposition
1.2
and, together with Lipschitz continuity on
X
, ensures uniform boundedness of the Gâteaux derivatives
F^{\prime}\colon X\to\mathscr{L}(X)
. In order to have Taylor expansions at hand, we assume Fréchet differentiability of
F\colon Y\to X
. As this only requires controlling the norm of the derivative of
F
uniformly over a unit ball in
Y
rather than
X
, genuinely nonlinear Nemytskii operators are not excluded provided that
Y
is sufficiently regular. For instance, in one dimension,
Y=H^{s}
for
s>\frac{1}{2}
suffices due to the embedding into
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
. One then has the Taylor expansion

(1.6)

\displaystyle F(u)

\displaystyle=F(v)+F^{\prime}(v)[u-v]+\int_{0}^{1}\big(F^{\prime}(v+\zeta(u-v))-F^{\prime}(v)\big)[u-v]\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

for all
u,v\in Y
. Together with Lipschitz continuity of
F^{\prime}\colon Y\to\mathscr{L}(Y,X)
, which implies Fréchet differentiability of
F\colon Y\to X
, this suffices to prove convergence at rate
1
. Contrary to
[
JR15
]
, we do not assume twice Fréchet differentiable
F
and
G
.

To consider pathwise uniform errors in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for general
p\in[2,\infty)
instead of mean square errors for
p=2
in
[
JR15
]
, a stochastic Fubini argument is employed in one of the terms arising from (
1.6
) and maximal inequalities for stochastic convolutions are used. A crucial ingredient that enables the extension from exponential to rational Milstein schemes is a logarithmic square function estimate (
[
KV24a
, Prop. 2.3]
and
[
CvW24
, Thm. 3.1]
) of the form

(1.7)

\mathbb{E}\sup_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\sup_{t\geq 0}\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{i}(s)\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\Big\|_{X}^{p}\lesssim\sqrt{\log(n)^{p}}\|(\Phi_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\|^{p}

for a suitable square function norm of
\Phi
(see Proposition
2.4
below).

Furthermore, regularity estimates are needed. It is only possible to show
1/2
-Hölder continuity of the
p
-th moment of the mild solution
U
to (
1.1
) in
X
but not in
Y
. A different splitting of the error permits us to circumvent this: Rather than
(\mathbb{E}\|U_{t}-U_{s}\|_{X}^{p})^{1/p}
for
s\leq t
, we estimate differences with the semigroup
(\mathbb{E}\|U_{t}-S(t-s)U_{s}\|_{X}^{p})^{1/p}
in terms of
(t-s)^{1/2}
. Since some terms then vanish, the remaining terms can be estimated via linear growth and thus also in
Y
, as illustrated in Lemma
5.1
. On
X
, this decay can be improved to
t-s
by additionally considering the difference with the stochastic integral term
\int_{s}^{t}G(S(t-s)U_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}
(cf. Lemma
5.2
). All in all, we show an estimate of the error in terms of itself, from which we deduce the final bound (
1.5
) by a discrete Grönwall inequality.

to

where

{\mathscr{L}_{2}^{(2)}(H,X)}
of bilinear Hilbert–Schmidt operators consists of all bilinear bounded operators

It is easily checked that

(2.1)

\phi\gamma=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\gamma_{n}\phi h_{n},\quad\Phi(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\gamma_{m}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\Phi(h_{m},h_{n}),

where the convergence is in

for every

Given a linear, bounded, positive self-adjoint operator

A central estimate for stochastic integrals is the following maximal inequality for stochastic convolutions with a quasi-contractive semigroup.

Proof.

2.2.
Gâteaux and Fréchet differentiability

The analysis of the Milstein scheme relies on Gâteaux and Fréchet derivatives, whose definition we recall.

Definition 2.5
.

For Banach spaces
Z_{1},Z_{2}
and an open subset
O\subseteq Z_{1}
, an operator
B\colon O\to Z_{2}
is said to be
Gâteaux differentiable at
x\in O
if there exists a bounded linear operator
R\in\mathscr{L}(Z_{1},Z_{2})
such that for all
z\in Z_{1}
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\lVert B(x+\varepsilon z)-B(x)-\varepsilon Rz\rVert_{Z_{2}}}{\varepsilon}=0,

where
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, and
Gâteaux differentiable on
O
if the above holds for all
x\in O
. The operator
R
is then called the
Gâteaux derivative of
B
at
x
and denoted by
B^{\prime}\colon O\to\mathscr{L}(Z_{1},Z_{2}),x\mapsto B^{\prime}(x)
with
B^{\prime}(x)\colon y\mapsto B^{\prime}(x)[y]
.
We say that
B
is
Fréchet differentiable at
x\in O
if there exists a bounded linear operator
R\in\mathscr{L}(Z_{1},Z_{2})
such that

\lim_{\|y\|_{Z_{1}}\to 0}\frac{\lVert B(x+y)-B(x)-Ry\rVert_{Z_{2}}}{\|y\|_{Z_{1}}}=0.

If the above holds for all
x\in O
, we say that
B
is Fréchet differentiable on
O
and write
B\in C^{1}(O,Z_{2})
.

Both Gâteaux and Fréchet derivatives are unique whenever they exist by linearity and uniqueness of limits in
Z_{2}
. We point out that the limit in the definition of Gâteaux differentiability only involves the norm on the codomain
Z_{2}
, whereas for the Fréchet derivative, also the norm on the domain
Z_{1}
appears. By setting
y=\varepsilon z
in the Fréchet derivative, one immediately sees that every Fréchet differentiable operator is Gâteaux differentiable and the derivatives agree. The converse is false in general, since only scalar multiples of directions
z\in Z_{1}
are considered for Gâteaux differentiability and no uniform norm control is required. Under a continuity assumption on the Gâteaux derivatives, Fréchet differentiability is assured.

Proposition 2.6

(Theorem 1.9 in
[
AP95
]
)
.

Suppose that
B\colon O\subseteq Z_{1}\to Z_{2}
is Gâteaux differentiable on
O
and
B^{\prime}\colon O\to\mathscr{L}(Z_{1},Z_{2})
is continuous in some
x\in O
. Then
B\colon O\subseteq Z_{1}\to Z_{2}
is Fréchet differentiable in
x
and the Fréchet derivative at
x
and the Gâteaux derivative
B^{\prime}(x)
agree.

Fréchet differentiability is not required to obtain uniform boundedness of the Gâteaux derivatives. Indeed, Lipschitz continuity is a sufficient condition.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
be Banach spaces and
O\subseteq Z_{1}
a nonempty subset.
Suppose that
B\colon Z_{1}\to Z_{2}
is Lipschitz continuous and Gâteaux differentiable on
O
.
Then the Gâteaux derivative
B^{\prime}\colon O\to\mathscr{L}(Z_{1},Z_{2})
is uniformly bounded by the Lipschitz constant of
B
.

Proof.

Denote the Lipschitz constant of
B
by
L>0
and let
x\in O
. The Gâteaux derivative in
x\in O
can be bounded independently of
x
by

\displaystyle\lVert B^{\prime}(x)\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(Z_{1},Z_{2})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert y\rVert_{Z_{1}}=1}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{\lVert B(x+\varepsilon y)-B(x)\rVert_{Z_{2}}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\lVert y\rVert_{Z_{1}}=1}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\frac{L\lVert\varepsilon y\rVert_{Z_{1}}}{\varepsilon}=L.\qed

The case of Nemytskii operators
B
on
Z_{1}=Z_{2}=L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})
for a bounded domain
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, illustrates that Fréchet differentiability on all of
L^{2}
is a strong assumption.

Proposition 2.8

(Theorem 2.7 and Proposition 2.8 in
[
AP95
]
)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
a bounded domain, and
\phi\colon\mathcal{O}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
\phi(x,\cdot)
is continuously differentiable for almost all
x\in\mathcal{O}
with uniformly bounded partial derivatives
|\partial_{s}\phi(x,s)|\leq C
for all
x\in\mathcal{O},s\in\mathbb{R}
and assume that
\phi(\cdot,s)
and
\partial_{s}\phi(\cdot,s)
are measurable. Further, let
B\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}),(B(u))(x)\coloneqq\phi(x,u(x))
for
x\in\mathcal{O}
be the corresponding Nemytskii operator. Then
B\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})
is Gâteaux differentiable. Moreover, if
B\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})
is Fréchet differentiable at some
\tilde{u}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})
then it is affine linear. That is, there are measurable functions
a,b\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
(B(u))(x)=a(x)u(x)+b(x)
for all
u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})
and
x\in\mathcal{O}
.

In case
\phi(x,s)\equiv\phi(s)
, the conditions on
\phi
in the proposition above reduce to
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
being Lipschitz continuous. Moreover, the affine linearity in the above only follows in the case
Z_{1}=Z_{2}=L^{2}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R})
stated above. Changing
Z_{1}
, Fréchet differentiability no longer implies that
B
is affine linear.

2.3.
Approximation of semigroups

A key step in solving a stochastic evolution equation numerically is to approximate the associated semigroup, which describes the behaviour of the linear part, by some scheme. The next definition introduces such schemes and quantifies how well they approximate the semigroup.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
X,Y
be Hilbert spaces with
Y\hookrightarrow X
continuously and let
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a
C_{0}
-semigroup on
X
. An
\mathscr{L}(X)
-valued scheme
is a function
R\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathscr{L}(X),~h\mapsto R_{h}\coloneqq R(h)
. It is said to
approximate
S
to order
\alpha>0
on
Y
if for all
T>0
there is a constant
C_{\alpha}\geq 0
such that

\|(S(t_{j})-R_{h}^{j})u\|_{X}\leq C_{\alpha}h^{\alpha}\|u\|_{Y}

for all
u\in Y
,
h>0
, and
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t_{j}=jh\in[0,T]
. Equivalently, we say that
R
converges of order
\alpha
on
Y
.
An
\mathscr{L}(Z)
-valued scheme
R
is called
contractive on
Z
for
Z\hookrightarrow X
continuously if
\|R_{h}\|_{\mathscr{L}(Z)}\leq 1
for all
h\geq 0
.

Common choices of schemes with their respective approximation orders of the semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
generated by
-A
include:

•

exponential Euler (EXE):
R_{h}=S(h)
, any order
\alpha>0
on
X
;

•

implicit Euler (IE):
R_{h}=(1+hA)^{-1}
, order
\alpha\in(0,1]
on
\dom(A^{2\alpha})
;

•

Crank–Nicolson (CN):
R_{h}=(2-hA)(2+hA)^{-1}
, order
\alpha\in(0,2]
on
\dom(A^{3\alpha/2})
provided that
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is contractive.

An essential assumption in our hyperbolic setting consists of contractivity of the semigroup and the approximating scheme. For EXE, contractivity of the scheme clearly is equivalent to that of the semigroup.
The latter two examples IE and CN are instances of
rational schemes
, which are schemes
R
that are induced by a rational function
r\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
via
R_{h}=r(h\cdot(-A))
for all sufficiently small

. This definition uses the bounded
H^{\infty}
-calculus of
A
as the negative generator of a
C_{0}
-contraction semigroup to define

. Under additional assumptions on

on the open left half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{-}
, contractivity of the scheme is recovered as a consequence of
[
HvNVW17
, Thm. 10.2.24]
, which is essential in our hyperbolic setting.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space and
-A
generate a
C_{0}
-contraction semigroup on

. For a holomorphic function

satisfying

for all

, let

for

. Then

is contractive.

This proposition ensures the contractivity of IE, CN, but also several higher-order implicit Runge-Kutta methods including Radau methods, BDF2, Lobatto IIA, IIB, and IIC as well as some DIRK schemes. More generally, all A-stable schemes
[
Dah63
]
and all A-acceptable schemes
[
BT79
]
satisfy the condition above on

, where the latter require it to hold on
\overline{\mathbb{C}_{-}}
and an additional consistency condition. As is the case for these examples, a common choice for the spaces

on which the semigroup

is approximated to some rate depending on
\alpha>0
consists of domains
\dom(A^{\alpha})
of fractional powers of

. It is useful to know that the embedding

\dom(A^{\alpha})\hookrightarrow\dom_{A}(\alpha,\infty)\coloneqq(X,\dom(A))_{\alpha,\infty}

into the real interpolation space with parameter

holds. For details on interpolation spaces, we refer the interested reader to
[

Tri95
]
.

We consider contractive approximations of the semigroup associated with the stochastic evolution equation, and recall Definition

Assumption A3

(Discretization Scheme)
.

Let

be a time discretization scheme and

as in Assumption

Admissible choices for

of (

Definition 3.5

(Rational Milstein scheme)
.

Let

. The

on an equidistant time grid
\{t_{j}=jh:j=0,\dots,M\}
with step size

is defined as the time-discrete stochastic process

and, for a scheme

(3.3)

u_{j}\coloneqq R_{h}^{j}\xi+h\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}R_{h}^{j-i}F(t_{i},u_{i})+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}R_{h}^{j-i}[G(t_{i},u_{i})\Delta W_{i+1}+(G^{\prime}G)(t_{i},u_{i})\Delta_{2}W_{i+1}]

\Delta W_{i+1}\coloneqq W(t_{i+1})-W(t_{i})
denotes the usual Wiener increments,
\Delta_{2}W_{i+1}\coloneqq\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\int_{t_{i}}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
the iterated stochastic integrals, and

is as defined in Notation

Definition 3.6

(Exponential Milstein scheme)
.

The

, or simply

Recalling Notation

and Lemma

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}R_{h}^{j-i}\Big\lparen\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G(t_{i},u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G^{\prime}(t_{i},u_{i})\Big[\int_{t_{i}}^{s}G(t_{i},u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\Big\rparen

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}R_{h}^{j-i}\Big\lparen\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G(t_{i},u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\int_{t_{i}}^{s}(G^{\prime}G)(t_{i},u_{i})\,\mathrm{d}W_{r}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\Big\rparen.

We focus on the case

.
Otherwise, the exponential Euler scheme or rational schemes, which are both using only the Wiener increments of the noise, are easier to implement and allow for the same pathwise uniform convergence rate

4.

Definition 4.1

If

is the exponential Milstein scheme, the statement holds with

, i.e. the convergence rate

is attained without logarithmic correction factor.

Proof.

We verify that Assumptions

and

Since

follow immediately via

\displaystyle\lVert F(\omega,t,x)-F(\omega,t,y)\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle=\lVert F_{0}(\omega,t)(x-y)\rVert_{X}\leq L_{F_{0},X}\lVert x-y\rVert_{X},

\displaystyle\lVert F(\omega,t,x)-f(\omega,t)\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle=\lVert F_{0}(\omega,t)x\rVert_{Y}\leq L_{F_{0},Y}\lVert x\rVert_{Y}.

To allow for

\displaystyle\bigg\lVert

6.3.
Stochastic Maxwell’s equations

The stochastic Maxwell’s equations

(ME)

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\begin{split}\mathrm{d}U&=[AU+F(U)]\,\mathrm{d}t+G(U)\,\mathrm{d}W_{Q}\quad\text{on }[0,T],\\

For a bounded, simply connected domain

\varepsilon,\mu\geq\kappa>0
for some constant

with vanishing tangential trace
\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{H}|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}
, where

\mathbf{H}_{V}\end{pmatrix}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})^{6},~~x\in\mathcal{O}

for

5.11

As noise

Assumption A8

and uniform Lipschitz continuity of

)

up to a logarithmic correction factor.

u\mapsto\phi\circ u
and
G\colon L^{2}\to\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},L^{2})
,
G(u)=[h\mapsto M_{\psi\circ u}Q^{1/2}h]
.

To show convergence, we need to assume sufficient regularity of
\phi
and
\psi
and the covariance operator.
We make this precise in the next theorem. Before, we recall useful estimates of composition and multiplication operators in the one-dimensional case that have been used in the previous subsections.

Lemma 6.5
.

Denote by
M_{f}
the multiplication operator associated with
f\in L^{2}
.

(i)

Let
s\in[0,1]
and
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
f(0)=0
and
\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\rvert\leq C
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and some
C>0
.
Then
\lVert f\circ u\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}}
for all
u\in H^{s}
.

(ii)

Let
s>\frac{1}{2}
and
r\in\{0,s\}
.
Then
\lVert M_{f}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{s},H^{r})}\lesssim\lVert f\rVert_{H^{r}}
.

Proof.

Part i) for
s\in(0,1)
is proved as
[
Tay00
, Prop. II.4.1]
.
For
s=0
and
s=1
this follows by direct calculation, in the latter case using
[
Tay00
, Prop. II.6.1]
in

\displaystyle\lVert f\circ u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert f\circ u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert(f\circ u)^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C^{2}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C^{2}\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=C^{2}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Part ii) for
r=0
is a direct consequence of Hölder’s inequality and the Sobolev embedding
H^{s}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
, since
\lVert fg\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert g\rVert_{H^{s}}
.
For
r=s
, instead of Hölder’s inequality we can use the Banach algebra property of
H^{s}
to estimate
\|fg\|_{H^{s}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{s}}\|g\|_{H^{s}}
. Taking the supremum over
g\in H^{s}
of unit norm yields the claim for
r\in\{0,s\}
.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
and
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
.
Assume that
\xi\in L_{\mathscr{F}_{0}}^{2\alpha p}(\Omega;H^{\alpha})
and
Q^{1/2}\in\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})
.
Suppose that
\phi,\psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are differentiable with bounded and
(2\alpha-1)
-Hölder continuous derivatives such that
\psi^{\prime}\psi
is Lipschitz continuous.

Then the exponential Milstein scheme
(u_{j})_{j=0,\ldots,M}
converges at rate
\alpha
as
h\to 0
to the mild solution
U
of (
TE
).
In particular, there is a constant
C\geq 0
such that

\bigg\lVert\max_{0\leq j\leq M}\lVert U_{t_{j}}-u_{j}\rVert_{L^{2}}\bigg\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{\alpha}.

Proof.

Note that by assumption there exists a real number
K\geq 0
such that
\varphi\in\{\phi,\psi\}
is Lipschitz with constant
K
and for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lvert\varphi^{\prime}(x)\rvert\leq K,~~\lvert\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(y)\rvert\leq K\lvert x-y\rvert^{2\alpha-1},~~\lvert\psi^{\prime}(x)\psi(x)-\psi^{\prime}(y)\psi(y)\rvert\leq K\lvert x-y\rvert.

We now verify Assumptions
A1
to
A4
for
X=L^{2}
and
Y=H^{\alpha}
in order to apply Theorem
5.7
. As discussed above, Assumption
A1
is fulfilled and thus also Assumption
A3
for the exponential Euler method. The Lipschitz continuity of
\phi
is easily seen to imply the same of
F
.
Indeed, for
u,v\in L^{2}
we see

\displaystyle\lVert F(u)-F(v)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\lvert\phi(u(x))-\phi(v(x))\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq K^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\lvert u(x)-v(x)\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=K^{2}\lVert u-v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Lemma
6.5
(i)
with
f(x)=\phi(x)-\phi(0)
implies linear growth of
F-F(0)
on
Y
.
Lipschitz continuity of
u\mapsto G(u)=M_{\psi(u)}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
can be shown similarly. Using Lemma
6.5
(ii)
with
r=0
and Lipschitz continuity of
\psi
, we deduce

\displaystyle\lVert

\displaystyle G(u)-G(v)\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},L^{2})}\leq\lVert M_{\psi\circ u-\psi\circ v}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{\alpha},L^{2})}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\psi\circ u-\psi\circ v\rVert_{L^{2}}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}\leq K\lVert u-v\rVert_{L^{2}}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}.

Analogously, linear growth on
Y
can be shown using Lemma
6.5
(ii)
with
r=s
.

Gâteaux differentiability of
F
follows from Proposition
2.8
because
\phi
is continuously differentiable with bounded derivative.
The Gâteaux derivative is given by
F^{\prime}(u)[v]=\big[x\mapsto\phi^{\prime}(u(x))\cdot v(x)\big]
for
u,v\in L^{2}
. Next, we check that the Gâteaux derivative is Hölder continuous from
H^{\alpha}
to
\mathscr{L}(H^{\alpha},L^{2})
.
We use Hölder’s inequality and the embedding
H^{\alpha}\hookrightarrow L^{4\alpha}
for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
to show that for
u,\tilde{u}\in H^{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\lVert

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(u)-F^{\prime}(\tilde{u})\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{\alpha},L^{2})}=\sup_{\|v\|_{H^{\alpha}}=1}\lVert(\phi^{\prime}\circ u-\phi^{\prime}\circ\tilde{u})\cdot v\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\phi^{\prime}\circ u-\phi^{\prime}\circ\tilde{u}\rVert_{L^{\frac{4\alpha}{2\alpha-1}}}\sup_{\|v\|_{H^{\alpha}}=1}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{4\alpha}}\lesssim K\lVert u-\tilde{u}\rVert_{L^{4\alpha}}^{2\alpha-1}\lesssim K\lVert u-\tilde{u}\rVert_{H^{\alpha}}^{2\alpha-1}.

We pass to Gâteaux differentiability of
G
on
L^{2}
. We claim that
G\colon L^{2}\to\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},L^{2})
is Gâteaux differentiable with derivative
G^{\prime}(u)[v]=M_{\psi^{\prime}(u)\cdot v}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
for
u,v\in L^{2}
. Indeed, by Lemma
6.5
(ii)
with
r=0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\lVert G(u+\tau v)-G(u)-G^{\prime}(u)[\tau v]\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},L^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\lVert M_{\psi(u+\tau v)-\psi(u)-\psi^{\prime}(u)\cdot\tau v}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{\alpha},L^{2})}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\lVert\psi\circ(u+\tau v)-\psi\circ u-(\psi^{\prime}\circ u)\cdot\tau v\rVert_{L^{2}},

and the limit vanishes by Proposition
2.8
for
\psi
. As for
F^{\prime}
, we check that this Gâteaux derivative is Hölder continuous on
H^{\alpha}
. For
u,\tilde{u}\in H^{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(u)-G^{\prime}(\tilde{u})\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{\alpha},\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},L^{2}))}\leq\sup_{v}\lVert M_{\psi^{\prime}(u)\cdot v-\psi^{\prime}(\tilde{u})\cdot v}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{\alpha},L^{2})}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{v}\lVert\lparen\psi^{\prime}\circ u-\psi^{\prime}\circ\tilde{u}\rparen\cdot v\rVert_{L^{2}}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}\lesssim K\lVert u-\tilde{u}\rVert_{H^{\alpha}}^{2\alpha-1}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})},

where the supremum is taken over all
v\in H^{\alpha}
of unit norm and in the last inequality, we have repeated the estimates for
F^{\prime}
from above.

Finally, we check the assumptions on
G^{\prime}G
given by
(G^{\prime}G)(u)=M_{\psi^{\prime}(u)\cdot\psi(u)}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
, again using the notation
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
from (
6.2
). The Lipschitz continuity of
G^{\prime}G
is essentially implied by the assumed Lipschitz continuity of
\psi^{\prime}\psi
noting that

\displaystyle\lVert(G^{\prime}G)(u)-(G^{\prime}G)(v)\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}^{(2)}(L^{2},L^{2})}\leq\lVert M_{(\psi^{\prime}\psi)(u)-(\psi^{\prime}\psi)(v)}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{\alpha},L^{2})}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{\alpha})}^{2}

for
u,v\in L^{2}
and continuing as in the Lipschitz estimate of
G
. Likewise, linear growth of
G^{\prime}G-\tilde{g}
follows from Lemma
6.5
(i)
with
f(x)=\psi^{\prime}(x)\psi(x)-\psi^{\prime}(0)\psi(0)
.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

The proof also gives convergence for rational Milstein schemes, albeit with a lower rate. Since the composition estimate of Lemma
6.5
(i)
is only available for
s\in[0,1]
, the Assumptions
A2
and
A4
can only be verified on
Y=H^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\leq 1
. Recall that IE and CN approximate the shift semigroup to orders
\frac{\alpha}{2}
and
\frac{2\alpha}{3}
, respectively. This limits the convergence rates of the IE-Milstein scheme and the CN-Milstein scheme for the nonlinear transport equation (
TE
) to at most
\frac{1}{2}
and
\frac{2}{3}
, respectively. This limitation does not apply in the linear case.

7.
Numerical Simulations of the Stochastic Schrödinger Equation

To complement the theoretical findings presented above, we include simulations of three variants of the stochastic Schrödinger equation. First, the linear case is considered with a potential and multiplicative trace-class noise in Subsection
7.1
. Second, a nonlocal nonlinearity as considered in Subsection
6.2
is simulated in Subsection
7.2
. Lastly, we include simulations with a nonlinearity not covered by our results as an outlook in Subsection
7.3
.

All three are considered on
L^{2}\coloneqq L^{2}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})
with the orthonormal Fourier basis functions
e_{\ell}(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\ell x}
for
x\in\mathbb{T}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
and up to the final time
T=\frac{1}{2}
.
We abbreviate
H^{s}\coloneqq H^{s}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})
for
s\in[0,\infty)
.

For the spatial and the noise discretization we used a spectral Galerkin approach utilizing the first
2^{10}
Fourier coefficients, i.e. we only considered the components with
\ell\in\{-2^{9}+1,\dots,2^{9}\}
.
As initial value, we chose
\xi=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu_{\ell}e_{\ell}
with
\mu_{\ell}=(1+\lvert\ell\rvert^{6})^{-1}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, being the Fourier coefficients of
\xi
.
Using the Fourier characterisation
\|\xi\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\simeq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+|\ell|^{2})^{s}|\mu_{\ell}|^{2}
of the
H^{s}
-norm due to
\lVert e_{\ell}\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}\lesssim(1+\lvert\ell\rvert^{2})^{s}
, we see that
\xi\in H^{s}
for all
s\in[0,\frac{11}{2})
, i.e.
\xi
is sufficiently smooth.

Similarly, we define the covariance operator
Q=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{\ell}\langle\cdot,e_{\ell}\rangle e_{\ell}
with
\lambda_{\ell}=(1+\lvert\ell\rvert^{5.1})^{-1}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The exponent
5.1
determines the regularity of the covariance operator and thus of the driving noise.
We have
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{s})
for all
s\in[0,\frac{5.1-1}{2})=[0,2.05)
, in particular,
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{2})
.
Indeed, reasoning as for
\xi
, we can bound

\displaystyle\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\rVert_{\mathscr{L}_{2}(L^{2},H^{s})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{\ell}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{\ell}\lVert e_{\ell}\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{1+\lvert\ell\rvert^{5.1-2s}},

which is finite in the given range of
s
.

All simulations were performed using the linear, multiplicative noise coefficient
G(u)=-{\mathrm{i}}M_{u}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
This implies that
(G^{\prime}G)(u)
is symmetric as a bilinear Hilbert–Schmidt operator for all
u\in L^{2}
(also referred to as the
commutativity condition
).
Thus, we can make use of the identity (cf.
[
JR15
, Eq. (80)]
)

\displaystyle(G^{\prime}G)(u_{i})\Delta_{2}W_{i+1}^{K}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime}(u_{i})\big[G(u_{i})\Delta W_{i+1}^{K}\big]\Delta W_{i+1}^{K}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}h\sum_{\ell=-K/2+1}^{K/2}\lambda_{\ell}G^{\prime}(u_{i})\big[G(u_{i})e_{\ell}\big]e_{\ell}

to simulate the iterated integral terms required to implement Milstein schemes.
Here, the superscript
K
denotes the truncation of the Wiener process to the first
K
Fourier coefficients.
In general, i.e. if the operator does not fulfil the commutativity condition, it is currently not known how to simulate the iterated integrals exactly.
Therefore, an approximation of the iterated integrals becomes necessary.
We refer to
[
KR23
]
for a recent survey of suitable approximation algorithms in the finite-dimensional case, as well as
[
LR18
]
for the infinite-dimensional case.

We denote by EXE, CN, and IE the exponential Euler, the Crank–Nicolson, and the implicit Euler method, respectively, and by EX-Milstein, CN-Milstein, and IE-Milstein (or simply EXM, CNM, IEM) the corresponding rational Milstein schemes. Approximations computed using EXM, CNM, and IEM are compared with the rational schemes EXE, CN, and IE simulated in
[
KV24a
, Sec. 6.5]
for five different step sizes
h\in\{2^{-5},2^{-6},\dots,2^{-9}\}
.
To estimate the pathwise uniform error for
p=2
, we computed the root-mean-square error over
100
samples.
As no closed form of the analytical solution is available for the equations considered, the errors are computed w.r.t. a reference solution, which was generated using the exponential Euler scheme with the significantly smaller step size
h=2^{-14}
.

In Subsections
7.1
and
7.2
, we make use of a centred bump function
\chi\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
on the torus defined by

(7.1)

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}Ce^{\frac{1}{x^{2}-c^{2}}},&\lvert x\rvert<c,\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\chi(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(x),&x\in[0,\pi),\\
f(x-2\pi),&x\in[\pi,2\pi)\end{cases}

for
c\in(0,\pi)
.
Here,
C=\exp(1/c^{2})
is a normalization constant such that
\chi(0)=f(0)=1
, and we chose
c=\frac{\pi}{2}
for the simulations.

Finally, we mention that the simulations were performed using Julia v1.11.7
[
BEKS17
]
on a standard laptop.
