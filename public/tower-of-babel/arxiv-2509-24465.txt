Interfaces between materials or phases – whether in condensed matter systems, biological membranes, or catalytic surfaces – are rich with phenomena that are fundamentally distinct from those present in the bulk
[
1
]
. In some cases, interfaces host quasiparticles, such as surface plasmons or Majorana fermions, which can significantly influence the material properties. Here, we investigate a unique 1D interface formed between two monolayer 2D semiconductors, MoSe
2
and WSe
2
, and unambiguously observe a new type of excitonic quasiparticle that resides exclusively at the boundary. Our optical measurements reveal several remarkable properties of this interfacial exciton. First, it possesses an in-plane dipole moment with exceptionally large dipole lengths of
\approx 2\,
nm. The emission spectrum reveals narrow discrete states, indicating strong quantum confinement of the center-of-mass motion across the interface. Power-dependent measurements show a sublinear saturation behavior, reflecting interactions between dipolar excitons in this low-dimensional system. Moreover, the exciton displays striking sensitivity to charge carriers due to its dipolar character. Our results introduce a new platform for exploring interacting 1D dipolar bosonic systems with unique quantum properties and open pathways for novel optoelectronic devices, including applications in nonlinear quantum photonics.

Dimensionality plays a fundamental role in shaping the properties of many-body systems. Since most bulk materials are three-dimensional, interfaces between them typically exhibit two-dimensional surface effects such as Shockley and Tamm surface states in silicon, 2D electron gases (2DEGs) at GaAs/AlGaAs heterojunctions, topological surface states, and surface plasmon polaritons at metal-dielectric interfaces
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. Truly one-dimensional interfaces are rare - for instance quantum hall edge states in 2DEGs - as they require the underlying bulk materials or phases to be inherently two-dimensional. Realizing such interfaces with tunable material platforms could enable exploration of new phenomena and open the door to novel applications.

The advent of atomically thin van der Waals materials, especially transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), has paved the way for investigating such interface physics in semiconductor materials. To date, most research on van der Waals materials has focused on vertically stacked heterostructures, which give rise to a range of phenomena from interlayer excitons to strongly correlated electrons in moiré superlattices
[
6
,
7
]
. However, recent advances in Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) have enabled the growth of unique lateral TMD heterostructures, where two direct bandgap monolayer semiconductors, such as MoSe
2
and WSe
2
, are seamlessly joined laterally by an atomically sharp interface. The one-dimensional interface between two-dimensional materials can be just a few nanometers wide while extending over tens of microns
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. An example micrograph of a CVD-grown monolayer lateral heterostructure (LHS) is shown in Fig.
1

a
. Here, the inner triangular region of monolayer MoSe
2
is surrounded by a triangular monolayer WSe
2
region. In the inset, we show a High Angle Angular Dark-Field Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (HAADF-STEM) scan of a typical atomically sharp interface. Details on the CVD growth and interface properties are given in the Supplementary Information.

These lateral heterostructures feature a type-II band alignment
[
12
]
, with the conduction band minimum in MoSe
2
and the valence band maximum in WSe
2
, as illustrated in Fig.
1

b
. The optical properties of monolayer MoSe
2
and WSe
2
are well understood, with both materials supporting strongly bound direct excitons with large oscillator strengths
[
13
]
. The central question of this work is: what is the optical response at the 1D interface where these two semiconductors meet? Does the interface simply reflect a sum of the optical responses of the individual materials, or do new states emerge?

This problem has been examined theoretically in several previous studies
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. To gain an intuitive understanding, we consider the potential energy landscape experienced by an electron (or hole) across the interface
[
15
,
17
]
(see Fig.
1

b
). The potential energy consists of two main contributions: the electron-hole Coulomb interaction potential (
V_{\mathrm{C}}
) responsible for strong exciton binding energy; and the band-offset potential (
V_{\mathrm{BO}}
) between the band edges of the two monolayers. The band-offset effectively acts as a steep repulsive potential barrier for both electrons and holes, keeping them apart, and thus counteracting the Coulomb attraction. The optical excitations in this region are therefore determined by the interplay between these opposing energy scales. When the band offset
V_{\mathrm{BO}}
is much smaller than
V_{\mathrm{C}}
, the excitonic properties should resemble that of the 2D exciton. However, when
V_{\mathrm{BO}}
becomes comparable to or larger than the Coulomb attraction (characterized by the direct exciton binding energy,
E_{\mathrm{B}}\sim 200\,
meV
[
17
]
), a new stable excitonic state
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
is expected to form at the Lateral Interface (LI). This is a charge-transfer type exciton consisting of a Coulomb-bound electron and hole, each residing in separate regions; the electron in MoSe
2
and the hole in WSe
2
. The band-offset compensates for the reduced Coulomb binding (due to spatial separation), making
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
the lowest energy optical excitation in the system. The precise conditions for the occurrence of interface excitons have been detailed in Refs
[
15
,
17
]
.

An important consequence of spatial separation of charges (distance,
d_{e-h}
) is that the interface excitons possess a permanent in-plane dipole moment perpendicular to the interface,
\vec{p}_{x}=e\,\vec{d}_{e-h}
. The resulting reduced exciton wavefunction is illustrated in Fig.
1

c
, with the electron and hole wavefunctions occupying separate regions, and hence having vanishing overlap. Although this scenario may appear analogous to interlayer excitons in vertical TMD heterostructures
[
19
,
20
,
6
]
, there are several fundamental differences. First and foremost, LI excitons are trapped along a 1D interface, leading to dimensional effects such as
1D density of states and quantum confinement, and resulting relation between interaction and kinetic energies
[
21
]
. Furthermore, in interlayer excitons, the localization of charges in different layers gives rise to out-of-plane dipole moment with fixed size determined solely by the layer spacing. In contrast, for interface excitons, both the electron and hole are itinerant within their respective 2D regions. This means the dipole length is not fixed, and may depend on various factors such as the dielectric environment, interface width and band-offset potential. Theoretical studies have predicted dipoles as large as
\sim 5
nm
[
17
]
. These properties may have significant implications for opto-electronic applications.

Lateral semiconductor heterostructures have been investigated in several previous experimental works
[
22
,
23
,
17
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. Although preliminary evidence for interfacial excitons has been reported in Refs.
[
17
,
24
]
, their definitive observation and demonstration of their key properties has so far remained elusive. This is mainly due to the stringent conditions required for their realization, which include high quality lateral heterostructure devices with interface widths on the order of the direct exciton Bohr radius (
w\lesssim 1\,
nm, see Supplementary Information for the effect of interface sharpness) and sufficiently large band offsets of the order of the exciton binding energy (
V_{\mathrm{BO}}\gtrsim 200\,
meV). In this work, we meet these conditions, enabling us to unambiguously observe the interfacial exciton state in atomically sharp, high-quality LHS and identify its key properties.

1D confinement and dipole moment

A closer examination of the lineshape of the interface exciton state in Fig.
2

c
suggests a fine structure consisting of multiple states. To resolve these features, we measure PL spectra at significantly reduced excitation powers (100 nW) to minimize power-broadening effects. In Fig.
3

a
, the low-power PL spectra measured at the interface show that the broad
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
peak consists of a ladder of narrow, discrete peaks, with linewidths ranging from
\Gamma\sim 1-3\,
meV. While the absolute energies of these states vary slightly with position, the overall ladder structure and energy splittings are consistently observed across the interface. This reproducibility rules out the possibility that the observed states arise from random disorder, such as defects.

The emergence of such discrete ladder of states is a clear signature of quantized exciton motion, as demonstrated in previous works
[
30
,
31
]
. The energy of the states as a function of state index (with the lowest state
n=1
) is shown in Fig.
3

b
. Each data point represents the peak energy averaged over seven different positions along the interface, with error bars corresponding to the standard deviation of the mean. We find a clear linear dependence (
E=E_{0}+\hbar\omega n
) of the energy on state index, with a harmonic level splitting of
\hbar\omega=7.8\pm 0.5\,\text{meV}
. This corresponds to a harmonic oscillator length
\ell_{x}=\sqrt{\hbar/m_{\mathrm{X}}\omega}=2.7\pm 0.1\,\text{nm}
, where
m_{\mathrm{X}}=1.3\,m_{0}
is the exciton mass and
m_{0}
is the free electron mass (SI). The nanoscopic confinement length combined with the extension of the state along the interface results in an extraordinary aspect ratio which underscores unique 1D nature of the system.

The quantum confinement mechanism of
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
at the interface is intuitive. Because the electron and hole are bound across the sharp interface, the center-of-mass (COM) degree of freedom is strongly localized perpendicular to the interface, while remaining extended along it. Any motion of the interface exciton in this direction incurs an energy cost, as the electron and hole must overcome the steep band-offset potential. This results in confinement of the COM motion. In Fig.
3

d
, we illustrate the reduced COM wavefunction to visualize the quantization of the LI exciton at the interface.

Next, we probe the internal structure of the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
state. As explained earlier, the spatial separation of charges is expected to result in a permanent in-plane electric dipole moment perpendicular to the interface. To confirm this, we study the response of the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
states to in-plane electric field applied via the split-gates (SG
1
and SG
2
). The large distance between the split gates (
D_{\mathrm{SG}}=2.7\,\mu
m) relative to the thickness of the top hBN (30 nm) ensures that the electric field at the interface is purely in-plane, and given by

F_{\mathrm{x}}=({V_{\mathrm{SG1}}-V_{\mathrm{SG2}}})/{D_{\mathrm{SG}}}
,
where
\text{V}_{\text{SG1}}
and
\text{V}_{\text{SG2}}
are the split-gate voltages. We note that for dipoles with fixed orientation, a positive
F_{\mathrm{x}}
(anti-aligned with dipole) induces a blue shift, while a negative
F_{\mathrm{x}}
(aligned with dipole) induces a red shift
[
32
]
. Details of the measurement are provided in SI.

In Fig.
3

c
, we present PL spectra as a function of in-plane electric field
F_{x}
, which shows clear field-dependent energy shift of the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
states. For small values of
F_{\mathrm{x}}\lesssim 4\,\mathrm{V}/\mu
m, the observed Stark shift is smaller than the predicted LI exciton binding energy (
E_{\mathrm{B}}^{\text{LI}}\simeq 30\,\text{meV}

[
15
,
17
]
) and is therefore captured by first-order perturbation theory. A linear fit of the Stark dependence
E_{n}=E_{0}+e\,d_{e-h}(n)F_{\mathrm{x}}
yields a permanent dipole length
d_{e-h}(n)
for each center-of-mass (COM) state. The linear fits are represented by dashed lines in Fig.
3

c
, and the error bands represent the Voigt fit errors to the peak energies of
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
states (SI). Spectral linecuts for three electric field values are shown in Fig.
3

f
. Notably, we find a dipole size of approximately 2.2 nm for
n=1
, which decreases to about 1.6 nm for
n=5
, indicating that higher COM modes have progressively shorter dipole lengths. This trend is illustrated in Fig.
3

.

Notably, this power-law scaling is in the intermediate regime between 2D and 0D, where the power dependence is slower than the linear scaling of 2D excitons but faster than the perfect saturation behavior typically seen in 0D quantum dots or defects

. This further corroborates the 1D nature of the system. Further studies on nonlinear effects could uncover collective dipolar phenomena in this 1D system.

These measurements highlight the uniqueness of the 1D interface. The presence of an excitonic in-plane dipole of length
\sim 2\,
nm with substantial brightness is particularly intriguing, as similar large dipoles in vertical heterostructures would not emit brightly due to reduced interlayer electron or hole tunneling. Additionally, the variation in dipole length across COM confined states indicates strong coupling between the COM and relative coordinates of the exciton
[
37
]
. The COM state governs how the exciton interacts with the interface; for higher COM states, the exciton wavefunction extends beyond the steepest part of the interface potential, reducing the effective band offset. In this regime, the Coulomb interaction pulls the electron and hole closer, so that the lower energy confined state exhibits the larger dipoles.

Doping induced behavior

Having described the properties of interfacial excitons, we now examine their response to gate-induced doping. The overall charge density in the heterostructure is tunable using the bottom gate. The charge configuration in each region can be discerned by the onset of doping in MoSe
2
and WSe
2
which is accompanied by the appearance of the trion states (
X_{\mathrm{Mo}}^{\pm}
,
X_{\mathrm{W}}^{\pm}
). In Fig.
4

a
, we show PL spectra as a function of
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}
. At
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}=0\,
V, we observe the
\text{X}_{\text{LI}}
ladder of states as previously discussed.

Upon doping, we observe a rich spectral response from the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
states. On the n-doping side (
V_{BG}>2.5\,
V), the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
state experiences a dramatic blue shift of
\sim 70\,
meV within a voltage range of just 1 V (over 30 nm of bottom hBN thickness). At higher voltages (
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}\gtrsim 3\,
V), the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
state flattens, approaches the trion state of MoSe
2
(
X^{-}_{\mathrm{Mo}}
). For larger
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}>6V
, global charge accumulation screens the interface band offset, eventually leading to the disappearance of the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
state, leaving the trion as the lowest optically active state. On the p-doping side (
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}<-3\,
V), we also observe a similarly large blue shift, but with a more gradual slope than in the n-doping regime. Additionally, we detect an enhancement in brightness at more negative voltages. This asymmetry can be attributed to differences in the graphene electrical contact quality on n- and p-doping in the MoSe
2
and WSe
2
regions, respectively, which leads to n-doping of MoSe
2
being more efficient than p-doping in WSe
2
[
38
]
.

While the precise mechanism behind the spectral shift requires further theoretical investigation, we provide a heuristic explanation. Due to the type-II band alignment, n-doping occurs first in the MoSe
2
region, while p-doping occurs first in WSe
2
as
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}
is varied. This generates a strong in-plane electric field across the interface, always oriented from WSe
2
towards MoSe
2
regardless of the doping configuration, as illustrated in Fig.
4

c
. The left panel of Fig.
4

d
shows the spatially dependent charge density, and the right panel shows the in-plane field distribution, both as functions of
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}
, obtained from finite element electrostatic simulations (see SI). These simulations show the accumulation of charge density at the interface at the onset of doping. Since the in-plane field is always anti-aligned with the dipole orientation, it leads to a DC Stark shift of the
X_{\mathrm{LI}}
state. In Fig.
4

e
, we estimate the Stark shift as a function of
V_{\mathrm{{BG}}}
using the simulated inhomogeneous electric fields, and find it reasonably consistent with experimental observations. Some discrepancies between our calculations and measurements are expected, as our model is perturbative and does not account for changes in the exciton wavefunction under large fields. Additionally, residual doping, hysteresis, and imperfect contacts in the sample could contribute to further discrepancies
[
38
]
. Nevertheless, this model offers a preliminary understanding of the mechanism behind the rapid blue shifts, which are otherwise not observed in TMD excitons.

Discussion

In this work, we have explored a unique 1D excitonic system formed at the lateral interface between two monolayer transition metal dichalcogenides, MoSe
2
and WSe
2
. Our key finding is the unambiguous observation of a quantum confined in-plane dipolar exciton localized exclusively at the 1D interface, with a permanent in-plane dipole moment of
e\times 2
nm. Measurements across several devices (see Supplementary Information) indicate that the emergence of this interfacial excitonic state with such remarkable properties relies critically on the atomic sharpness of the interface and the high quality of the TMD materials. Experiments on more diffuse interfaces produce a broader and much weaker spectral feature, consistent with previous reports
[
17
]
.

Our results raise several important questions about the mechanisms governing formation and recombination of interfacial excitons. These could be addressed by investigating the exciton lifetime, valley properties via magneto-optical experiments
[
18
]
, transport and diffusion effects
[
39
]
, and nonlinear effects due to dipolar interactions.

The existence of a stable, spatially extended, and potentially long-lived interface excitonic state opens exciting opportunities for both fundamental studies and device applications. One intriguing direction is the realization of in-plane 1D excitonic insulating states
[
40
,
41
]
along the interface by engineering the band-offset potential. If the effective band-gap across the interface is reduced below the binding energy using gates, a new equilibrium ground state of 1D wire of bound electron-hole pairs could emerge. Furthermore, the interfacial and dipole character could also be leveraged for optical sensing of topological edge states of electrons in moiré superlattices. From a theoretical perspective, investigating many-body phenomena
[
21
]
, such as Tonks-Girardeau physics in dipolar systems
[
42
]
, could reveal new quantum phases and enhance our understanding of exciton-exciton interactions in low-dimensional systems.

On the device front, the reduced Coulomb binding of interfacial excitonic states could lead to new approaches for photodetectors. The reduced dimensionality of these in-plane excitons also makes them interesting for lasing applications. Furthermore, their potentially strong nonlinearities make them promising for nonlinear quantum photonics.
