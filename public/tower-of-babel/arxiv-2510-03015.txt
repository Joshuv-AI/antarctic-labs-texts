Lagrange-Mesh Method in Momentum Space: an Alternative Formulation
Cyrille Chevalier, Joachim Viseur

Abstract
This work presents a new methodology for computing potential matrix elements within the Lagrange-mesh method in momentum space. The proposed approach extends the range of treatable potentials to include previously inaccessible cases, such as Coulomb and linear interactions. The method is validated across a variety of systems. A particular attention is given to the representation of both momentum and position probability densities.



I
Introduction

In three dimensions, time-independent Schrödinger-like equations are analytically solvable only for a few combinations of kinetic and potential energy operators. This difficulty is overcome by resorting to numerical approximate methods. For two-body problems, the Lagrange-mesh method (LMM) is a good candidate, capable of efficiently and accurately handling various potentials and kinematics
Baye 2015
;
Semay et al. 2001
;
Lacroix et al. 2012
. The method is based on an expansion in trial states which are chosen so that they vanish at all points of a particular mesh but one. In this way, using appropriate quadratures to evaluate the integrals, the numerical computation of matrix elements is drastically simplified. Nevertheless, since quadratures are only approximate, this prevents the method from being variational.

The LMM has abundantly been studied in configuration space and gives very good results with high precision for many two-body systems. However, in many applications, a momentum space description of the system becomes more relevant. This is especially true for systems with exotic kinematics or momentum-dependent interactions
Szczepaniak et al. 1996
;
Llanes-Estrada and Cotanch 2000
;
Aguilar et al. 2011
;
Godfrey and Isgur 1985
. Momentum space also fits better with descriptions based on quantum field theories, the latter being naturally written using momentum eigenstates
Chevalier and Mathieu 2025
. These arguments motivate the development of a LMM directly written in momentum space. A first attend was made by G. Lacroix et al. in Reference
Lacroix and Semay 2011
;
Lacroix et al. 2012
. For systems interacting via Gaussian and Yukawa potentials, they obtained results similar to those obtained in configuration space. However, it turns out that their methodology is not numerically appropriate to manage physically important potentials, such as the Coulomb or the linear ones. These are widely used, for instance, in hadronic physics
Fulcher 1994
;
Mathieu et al. 2008
;
Godfrey and Isgur 1985
;
Capstick and Isgur 1986
. The present paper develops an alternative methodology to handle interactions for the LMM in momentum space. It is inspired by the way exotic kinematics are handled in the original LMM based on configuration space
Semay et al. 2001
.

Section
II
is devoted to the theoretical description of the LMM. Some results from References
Semay et al. 2001
and
Lacroix et al. 2012
are reviewed to keep the article self-contained. The essential contribution of this work to LMM is contained in Section
II.1.2
which introduces the aforementioned alternative methodology to compute potential matrix elements. Accuracy tests on a Coulomb system are also proposed in Section
II.2
. In Section
III
, the wave functions in position representation are obtained from those found in momentum space. Finally, Section
IV
illustrates the method on a more physical system, used in Reference
Fulcher 1994
to describe quark-antiquark interactions. Let us also mention the
Appendix A
which illustrates that the methodology from Reference
Lacroix et al. 2012
is not appropriate to handle Coulomb and linear potentials. This work uses natural units (
\hbar=c=1
).

II
The Lagrange Mesh Method

Let us consider a time-independent Schrödinger-like equation for a system of two bodies in its centre-of-mass frame,

\left[T(p^{2})+V(r^{2})\right]\ket{\psi}=E\ket{\psi},

(1)

Above,
p=|\boldsymbol{p}|
and
r=|\boldsymbol{r}|
stand for the modulus of the relative momentum and the separation of the two particles, respectively. The functions
T(p^{2})
and
V(r^{2})
represent a generic kinetic and potential contribution to the system’s energy. The latter is assumed to depend only on the relative distance between the two particles
1
1

1

The present developments remain valid for any equation analogous to (
1
) in which variables
r
and
p
have a different interpretation. The true requirement for
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
is to be conjugate variables.
.
To solve equation (
1
), many resolution methods rely on variational computations. Namely, the eigenstates
\ket{\psi}
are approximated by normalised linear combinations of a finite number of trial states
\ket{f_{\alpha}}
,

|\psi\rangle\approx\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}C_{\alpha}\ket{f_{\alpha}}\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{\alpha}|C_{\alpha}|^{2}=1.

(2)

For orthonormal trial states, MacDonald’s theorem ensures that the above combinations provide good approximations of the true eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors provided that the
C_{\alpha}
parameters are solutions of the following eigenvalue equation
MacDonald 1933
,

\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\bra{f_{\alpha}}\left[T(p^{2})+V(r^{2})\right]\ket{f_{\beta}}C_{\beta}=E\,C_{\alpha}.

(3)

If the above matrix elements are computed exactly, MacDonald’s theorem even demonstrates that the approximate eigenenergies are upper bounds of the exact ones. The LMM suggests a definite set of trials states whose properties are tuned to facilitate the evaluation of
T(p^{2})
and
V(r^{2})
matrix elements by mean of Gauss-Laguerre quadratures.

Gaussian quadratures consist of approximating an integral by a weighted sum of its integrand evaluated in the zeros of definite orthogonal polynomials
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
. Precisely, the
N
-points Gauss-Laguerre quadrature resorts to Laguerre polynomials of degree
N
, denoted
L_{N}(x)
. Using this quadrature, the integral of an arbitrary function of a single positive-definite variable
g(x)
is approximated as

\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)dx\approx\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}g(x_{k}),

(4)

where
x_{k}
are all the solutions of
L_{N}(x)=0
. In the following, these roots are referred to as the Lagrange mesh. The weight coefficients
\lambda_{k}
are chosen so that the above approximation is exact for
g(x)
being any polynomial of order
2N-1
multiplied by a decreasing exponential
2
2

2

It is worth mentioning that in a lot of references (see
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
, for instance) the decreasing exponential is even made explicit at the level of equation (
4
),

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}g(x)dx\approx\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}g(x_{k}).

One will easily convince that this results in a simple rescaling of the weight coefficients,
w_{k}=e^{-x_{k}}\lambda_{k}
. The present convention is chosen to simplify further expressions.
,
e^{-x}
. As a result, weights
\lambda_{k}
are defined by the following formula
Lacroix et al. 2012
,

\ln\lambda_{k}=x_{k}-\ln x_{k}+2\ln\Gamma(N+1)-\sum_{j\neq i=1}^{N}2\ln|x_{k}-x_{j}|.

(5)

To define the trial set considered by the LMM, let us fix a representation for the states. In the current work, calculations are driven in momentum representations (the LMM in position representation has been abundantly developed in previous works
Baye 2015
;
Semay et al. 2001
). The following momentum wave functions are chosen for the trial states,

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|f_{i};lm}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{i}(p/h)}{p\sqrt{h}}\tilde{Y}_{lm}(\theta,\phi)

(6)

with
i\in\{1,...,N\}
,
N
being the number of mesh points, and with
\braket{\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{p}}=\delta(p^{\prime}-p)\delta(\cos\!\theta^{\prime}-\cos\!\theta)\delta(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)/p^{2}
as the orthonormalisation convention for the momentum eigenstates. Above,
\theta
and
\phi
denote the polar and azimuth angles of the relative momentum
\boldsymbol{p}
, respectively. Angular momentum quantum numbers are provided to the trial states using modified spherical harmonic
\tilde{Y}_{lm}

Khersonskii et al. 1988
. In this work, a solution with a given angular momentum is always expected, but the procedure can easily be generalised to coupled channels. In definition (
6
), the parameter
h
is an energy scale whose job will be to adjust the mesh to the size of the system under consideration. The way this parameter is fixed is illustrated in an example in Section
II.2
. Finally,
f_{j}(x)
are regularized Lagrange functions defined as follows
Lacroix et al. 2012
,

f_{i}(x)=(-1)^{i}x_{i}^{-1/2}x(x-x_{i})^{-1}L_{N}(x)e^{-x/2}.

(7)

Note that this function vanishes at the origin, as expected for a physical wave function in the present conventions. In addition, each Lagrange function cancels at all points of the Lagrange mesh, except at a single one,

f_{i}(x_{j})=\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}\delta_{ij},

(8)

This property is the keystone for constructing the LMM. As mentioned before, trial states (
6
) are expected to be orthonormalised. With the chosen orthonormalisation conventions for momentum eigenstates, their overlap writes as follows
Lacroix et al. 2012
,

\braket{f_{i};lm|f_{j};lm}=\int p^{2}\mathrm{d}p\,\frac{f_{i}(p/h)}{p\sqrt{h}}\,\frac{f_{j}(p/h)}{p\sqrt{h}}\int\mathrm{d}\!\cos\!\theta\mathrm{d}\phi\,\tilde{Y}_{lm}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\tilde{Y}_{lm}(\theta,\phi).

(9)

The orthogonality of spherical harmonics
Khersonskii et al. 1988
simplifies the angular integral, leaving only the radial one,

\braket{f_{i};lm|f_{j};lm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,f_{i}(u)f_{j}(u).

(10)

where
u=p/h
. If one evaluates this integral with a
N
-points Gauss-Laguerre quadrature (
4
) and resorts to property (
8
), the expected Kronecker delta is reproduced,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,f_{i}(u)f_{j}(u)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}f_{i}(x_{k})f_{j}(x_{k})=\delta_{ij}.

(11)

The above calculation illustrates that the LMM trial states are orthogonal only at the Gauss approximation (represented here with an
\approx
symbol). This observation is a first hint that the LMM is not a genuine variational method, as it evaluates its matrix elements approximately, resorting to Gauss-Laguerre quadratures. For instance, this makes the interpretation of its energies as upper bounds of the true eigenenergies impossible. Nevertheless, in spite of all these approximations, the method can achieve impressive accuracy levels
Dohet-Eraly 2017
.

II.1
Matrix Element Computation in Momentum Space

To apply equation (
3
), kinetic and potential energy matrix elements are now to be computed on trial states (
6
). The next sections are devoted to these evaluations. It is shown that the strategy in reference
Semay et al. 2001
also holds in momentum space, provided that the treatment of kinetic and potential energy are inverted.

Kinetic Energy Matrix Elements

The kinetic energy matrix elements
\bra{f_{i};lm}T(p^{2})\ket{f_{j};lm}
are comparatively easier to determine. This operator being scalar, the angular part of the integral factorises and the normalisation of spherical harmonic can be used. Only the radial part of the integral remains,

\bra{f_{i};lm}T(p^{2})\ket{f_{j};lm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,T(h^{2}u^{2})f_{i}(u)f_{j}(u)

(12)

where
u=p/h
, again. As with the evaluation of the orthonormalisation of the trial states, a
N
-points Gauss-Laguerre quadrature (
4
) can be used to evaluated the residual integral. Resorting again to property (
8
), one gets

\bra{f_{i};lm}T(p^{2})\ket{f_{j};lm}\approx T(h^{2}x_{i}^{2})\delta_{ij},

(13)

With this formula, the evaluations of the kinetic energy
T
at the mesh points are the only ones needed. This is a standard formula within the LMM in momentum representation
Lacroix et al. 2012
.

Potential energy matrix elements

Evaluating potential matrix elements prove more difficult. In Reference
Lacroix et al. 2012
, a procedure based on the calculation of the potential’s Fourier transform is suggested. Position-dependant potentials with Gaussian and Yukawa shapes are turned into their non-local momentum-dependant equivalents. Gauss-Laguerre quadratures are then used to obtain closed formulas to evaluate potential matrix elements. However, the strategy from
Lacroix et al. 2012
does not solve Coulomb or linear potentials, as illustrated in
Appendix A
. For that reason, the current work suggests an alternative methodology for computing potential energy matrix elements which is compatible with the aforementioned potential shapes
3
3

3

Let us mention that similar calculations are presented in
Lacroix et al. 2012
to evaluate position-dependent observables. Nevertheless, such calculations have never been used to solve the Schrödinger-like equation (
1
) itself.
.

Basically, the potential energy matrix elements
\bra{f_{i};lm}V(r^{2})\ket{f_{j};lm}
are calculated using the same four-step procedure as that used in reference
Semay et al. 2001
to compute kinetic energy matrix elements in the configuration space. The resulting methodology is described below.

•

First, the matrix elements of the operator
r^{2}
are computed on the trial set,
R_{ij}^{2}=\bra{f_{i};lm}r^{2}\ket{f_{j};lm}
. It is well-known that the expression of
r^{2}
as a differential operator in momentum representation is the same as that of
p^{2}
in position representation. Therefore, evaluating
r^{2}
on momentum-dependant Lagrange functions necessarily uses the same formulas as evaluating
p^{2}
on position-dependant Lagrange functions. The latter being computed in reference
Semay et al. 2001
, one simply has to mimic their calculation steps to show that

R_{ij}^{2}\approx\frac{1}{h^{2}}\left(r_{ij}^{2}+\frac{l(l+1)}{x_{i}}\delta_{ij}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad r_{ij}^{2}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{i-j}(x_{i}x_{j})^{-1/2}(x_{i}+x_{j})(x_{i}-x_{j})^{-2}&\text{for }i\neq j,\\
(12x_{i}^{2})^{-1}\big(4+(4N+2)x_{i}-x_{i}^{2}\big)&\text{for }i=j.\end{cases}

(14)

An approximate equality symbol is used because
N
-point Gauss-Laguerre quadratures are used to obtain the above expression.

•

The finite
R^{2}
matrix is then diagonalised. The diagonal matrix is denoted
D^{2}
, while the corresponding transition matrix is denoted
S
,

R^{2}=SD^{2}S^{-1}.

(15)

•

The potential energy matrix
V_{D^{2}}
is then computed on the basis of the
r^{2}
eigenstates. This simply requires acting on each element of the diagonal matrix
D^{2}
with the function
V
.

•

Finally, the potential matrix in the initial basis
V_{R^{2}}
is obtained by returning to the original basis with the transformation law (
15
),

V_{R^{2}}=SV_{D^{2}}S^{-1}.

(16)

As discussed in Reference
Semay et al. 2001
, the potential matrix elements obtained in this way are approximate for two reasons. First, formula (
14
) using Gauss-Laguerre quadratures, the
R^{2}
matrix elements are only evaluated approximately. But even if these matrix elements were computed exactly, as this methodology uses a finite number of trial states to picture
r^{2}
, the obtained eigenstates would only be approximations of the real ones. However, for both these approximations, increasing the number of mesh-points should result in a more accurate description. Note that, although the method was originally based on the variational theorems, the variational character of the solution is lost because all the matrix elements have been computed approximately.

Resulting eigenvalues and eigenstates

Once the
N\times N
potential and kinetic matrix elements are computed thanks to the above procedure and formula (
13
), one can construct the matrix associated to
T(p^{2})+V(r^{2})
and diagonalise it, as suggested by equation (
3
). The resulting eigenvalues and eigenvectors provide approximations for the spectrum of the system. As only states with a given angular momentum
l
have been used, the obtained approximations share this angular momentum.

Concerning the eigenvectors, the
C_{i}
coefficients are to be inserted into the equation (
2
) to infer the approximate eigenstate. In the next section, these will be used to compute approximate probability densities
\mathcal{P}\,\mathrm{d}p
associated with the modulus of the relative momentum
p
,

\mathcal{P}(p)\mathrm{d}p=p^{2}\mathrm{d}p\int\mathrm{d}\!\cos\!\theta\mathrm{d}\phi\,\left|\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|\psi}\right|^{2}\quad\text{where}\quad\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{i}\ket{f_{i};lm}

(17)

Using definition (
6
), one finds

\left|\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|\psi}\right|^{2}\approx\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}C_{i}^{*}C_{j}\frac{f_{i}(p/h)f_{j}(p/h)}{(p\sqrt{h})^{2}}\left|\tilde{Y}_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\right|^{2}.

(18)

Since all matrix elements are real, the coefficients
C_{i}
can likewise be chosen real. Using the orthonormality of the spherical harmonics
Khersonskii et al. 1988
, an expression for the approximate density is obtained,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(p)\mathrm{d}p

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{h}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}C_{i}C_{j}f_{i}(p/h)f_{j}(p/h)\right)\mathrm{d}p=\frac{1}{h}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{i}f_{i}(p/h)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}p.

(19)

The above formula can be used to represent the corresponding eigenstate or to compute momentum-dependent observables, for instance.

II.2
Validation with analytical results

Now that the method is presented, it can be illustrated by comparison with analytical results. For this purpose, let us consider a system of two non-relativistic particles that interact with a pure Coulomb potential,

\displaystyle T(p^{2})=\frac{p^{2}}{2\mu}\text{ with }\mu=\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},

\displaystyle V(r^{2})=-\frac{a}{\sqrt{r^{2}}}.

(20)

For simplicity, particles are considered identical and have unit mass, namely
m_{1}=m_{2}=1
. The dimensionless parameter
a
is also fixed at
1
. Arbitrary units (a.u.) are used. The Schrödinger equation to solve becomes

\left[p^{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right]\ket{\psi}=E\ket{\psi}.

(21)

This equation can be solved exactly, its energy spectrum being below
Flügge 1994
;
Yàñez et al. 1994
,

E_{n}=-\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\quad\text{with }n=n_{r}+l+1.

(22)

Above,
n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
is the radial quantum number and
l\in\{0,...,n_{r}-1\}
. In the following, tests are performed on the three lowest states, denoted using spectroscopic notation
1S
,
2S
, and
1P
, respectively. The eigenstates of equation (
21
) can also be obtained analytically, even in momentum representation
Flügge 1994
;
Yàñez et al. 1994
. For the three lowest levels, the exact probability densities
\mathcal{P}\,\mathrm{d}p
associated with the modulus of the relative momentum
p
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1S}(p)\,\mathrm{d}p=\left(\frac{8}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{2p}{(1+4p^{2})^{2}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}p,

(23a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2S}(p)\,\mathrm{d}p=\left(2^{5}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{2p(1-16p^{2})}{(1+16p^{2})^{3}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}p,

(23b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1P}(p)\,\mathrm{d}p=\left(2^{7}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3\pi}}\frac{4p^{2}}{(1+16p^{2})^{3}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}p.

(23c)

These exact results can be compared to those obtained within the present LMM. First, the dependency in
h
is investigated in Figure
1
. The ground-state energy is plotted versus
h
for different mesh sizes. One can immediately see that the LMM is not variational as no clear minimum is observed. Instead, each curve shows a large plateau along which the energy is stable. The size of this plateau increases with
N
. This illustrates that the parameter
h
enables to adapt the mesh to the typical scale of the system, but that an accurate value is not required as long as
N
is chosen large enough.

Eigenenergies obtained with various mesh sizes
N
and
h=0.1
are displayed in Table

and compared with the exact ones. With only
50
points, the LMM already reproduces at least three significant digits for all the investigated states. With
300
points, the accuracy increases and more than five significant digits are systematically reproduced. Concerning eigenstates, Figure

compares the probability density
\mathcal{P}(p)
obtained using expression (
19
) to the exact ones from (

). Disparities decrease with increasing mesh size, with the two curves becoming indistinguishable for
N=135
. As an order of magnitude, our implementation on a regular laptop using Python 3 took only a few seconds to produces all the results from Table

III
Representation of the state in configuration space

The previous section closed with an illustration that the LMM in momentum space provides precise approximations for the momentum probability densities. However, in many applications, the position probability density is also required. This suggests developing formulas to compute the Fourier transform of the LMM approximation,

\braket{\boldsymbol{r}|f_{i};lm}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}p\,\frac{f_{i}(p/h)}{p\sqrt{h}}\tilde{Y}_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\,e^{i\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}

(24)

where the convention
\braket{\boldsymbol{r}|\boldsymbol{p}}=e^{i\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}/(2\pi)^{3/2}
is used. Solving the angular dependence of the above integral results in

\braket{\boldsymbol{r}|f_{i};lm}=i^{l}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\int p^{2}\mathrm{d}p\,\frac{f_{i}(p/h)}{p\sqrt{h}}\,j_{l}(pr)\right)\tilde{Y}_{lm}(\theta_{r},\phi_{r})

(25)

where
r
,
\theta_{r}
and
\phi_{r}
refer to as the modulus, orbital angle and azimuthal angle of the relative position between the particles, respectively. The
j_{l}
function is a spherical Bessel function
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
. The remaining integral can be evaluated approximately by using again a
N
-point Gauss-Laguerre quadrature,

\int p^{2}\mathrm{d}p\,\frac{f_{i}(p/h)}{p\sqrt{h}}\,j_{l}(pr)\approx\sqrt{h^{3}\lambda_{i}}\,x_{i}\,j_{l}(hx_{i}r).

(26)

As a result, one finds
4
4

4

Similar developments were carried out in Reference
Lacroix and Semay 2011
to transition from the position to the momentum representation. The inverse operation is addressed in the current work.

\braket{\boldsymbol{r}|f_{i};lm}=i^{l}\sqrt{\frac{2h^{3}\lambda_{i}}{\pi}}\,x_{i}\,j_{l}(hx_{i}r)\tilde{Y}_{lm}(\theta_{r},\phi_{r}).

(27)

Considering a given linear combination of trial states
\ket{f_{i};lm}
and following developments similar to those in Section
II.1.3
, the radial probability density of the system,
\mathcal{R}(r)\mathrm{d}r
, is given by

\mathcal{R}(r)\mathrm{d}r=\frac{2h^{3}}{\pi}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\,x_{i}r\,j_{l}(hx_{i}r)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}r.

(28)

28
) provides an accurate representation of the state in configuration space, one can also test the formula on the Coulomb system used in Section

IV
Application for a physical example

In this last section, the LMM in momentum representation is used to study a system of two semi-relativistic particles interacting with Cornell interactions. This model has been used to describe light mesons (quark-antiquark systems) in Reference
Fulcher 1994
. Consider two particles in their centre-of-mass frame with mass
m_{1}
and
m_{2}

V
Conclusion

This paper introduces an alternative way to compute potential matrix elements within a LMM written in momentum space. It mimics the strategy used to handle semi-relativistic kinematics in configuration space
Semay et al. 2001
. This method allows the resolution of two-body Schrödinger-like equations with any kind of kinematics and potentials. This was not the case with the LMM in momentum space developed in Reference
Lacroix et al. 2012
, which is not appropriate to handle Coulomb and linear potentials, for instance.

The efficiency of the method has been validated by comparison with analytical results for the Coulomb potential and is also compared to that from Reference
Lacroix et al. 2012
in
Appendix B
. The method has also been tested on a phenomenological meson model. The corresponding results were compared with Lagrange-mesh calculations in position representation
Semay et al. 2001
. Special attention has been devoted to representing the position and momentum densities associated to the states.

Appendix Appendix A
Direct calculation of the LMM matrix elements in momentum representation

This appendix intends to illustrate the claim that the LMM developed in Reference
Lacroix et al. 2012
cannot naively be used to solve potentials such as the Coulomb or the linear one. Developments are driven using the Coulomb potential, but similar results can be obtained for a linear one.

The methodology from reference
Lacroix et al. 2012
suggests to evaluate via a direct calculation the potential energy ME,

V_{ij}=\bra{f_{i};lm}V(r)\ket{f_{j};lm}.

(A.33)

To this end, it aims at evaluating the potential Fourier transform
V_{\text{FT}}(|\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}}|)
and decomposes it in partial waves denoted
V_{l}(p,p^{\prime})
,

V_{\text{FT}}(k=|\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}}|)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}k}\int_{0}^{\infty}V(r)\sin(kr)r\mathrm{d}r\quad\text{and}\quad V_{l}(p,p^{\prime})=2\pi\int_{-1}^{+1}P_{l}(t)V_{\text{FT}}\left(\sqrt{p^{2}+p^{\prime\,2}-2pp^{\prime}t}\right)\mathrm{d}t

(A.34)

where
P_{l}
is a Legendre polynomial
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
. Matrix elements (
A.33
) are then easily computed using
N
-points Gauss-Laguerre quadratures,

V_{ij}\approx h^{3}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}x_{i}x_{j}V_{l}(hx_{i},hx_{j}).

(A.35)

Altough these formulas prove very convenient to use for short-range potentials, such as the Gaussian or Yukawa ones, it fails when long-range interactions, such as the Coulomb one, are used. In the Coulomb case,
V(r)=-1/r
, one shows that
(
Lacroix et al. 2012
, formula (37) with
b=0
)
Hersbach 1993

V_{l}(p,p^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{\pi pp^{\prime}}Q_{l}\left(\frac{\bar{p}^{2}+p^{2}}{2\bar{p}p}\right).

(A.36)

where
Q_{l}
is a second kind Legendre function
Abramowitz and Stegun 1964
. As a result, formula (
A.35
) becomes

V_{ij}\approx-\frac{h}{\pi}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}\,Q_{l}\left(\frac{x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}}{2x_{i}x_{j}}\right).

(A.37)

However, considering the case
i=j
, the argument of the second kind Legendre function becomes identically equal to
1
, a value for which
Q_{l}
diverges. This makes impossible to use formula (
A.35
) in presence of a Coulomb potential.

The situation for a linear potential is even trickier as, in that case, the potential’s Fourier transform necessarily takes a distributional sense. The problem is solved in References
Hersbach 1993
;
Norbury et al. 1992
, which allows to write the matrix element (
A.33
) in an integral form. However, this integral employs the derivative of a second kind Legendre function, and, as before, the matrix element proves divergent for
i=j
.
