The association between family support and maternal parenting stress: the potential mediating role of postpartum depressive symptoms
Authors: Jiaqi Shi, Jianing Xu, Yuyin Xiao, Chenshu Shi, Feifei Li, Yujie Cui, Chenhao Yu, Fan Jiang, Guohong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs a critical factor in early childhood development (ECD), parenting stress has been demonstrated to adversely affect maternal well-being and child developmental outcomes. This study examined the association between family support and maternal parenting stress and assessed whether postpartum depressive and anxiety symptoms account for this association.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,099 mothers of children aged 0–2 years in Shanghai, China. The Simplified Chinese version of the Parenting Stress Index-Short Form (PSI-SF), the Family Adaptability, Partnership, Growth, Affection, and Resolve (APGAR) Scale and the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21) were used to assess maternal parenting stress, family support and postpartum depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively. Regression models were used to examine the study hypotheses and evaluate whether depressive and anxiety symptoms statistically explained the association.ResultsThe mean parenting stress score was 26.37 ± 9.05. Overall, 24.3% of mothers reported low family support, 8.6% exhibited postpartum depressive symptoms, and 12.2% showed postpartum anxiety symptoms. Family support was negatively associated with maternal parenting stress (β=−5.415, p < 0.001). After depressive and anxiety symptoms were entered into the model as candidate statistical mediators, depressive symptoms were significantly associated with maternal parenting stress (β = 5.847, p < 0.001), whereas the association between family support and maternal parenting stress became non-significant (β=−1.924, p > 0.05). There was no evidence of statistical mediation by anxiety symptoms.ConclusionsLower family support was associated with higher maternal parenting stress, and depressive symptoms accounted for much of the association in the specified cross-sectional model. While these findings cannot establish temporality or causality, they support the prospective evaluation of family-centered care practices within the primary health care system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05220-y.Keywords: Postpartum, Parenting stress, Family support, Depressive symptoms

Introduction

Parenting stress, defined as the psychological strain or emotional discomfort resulting from a perceived mismatch between parenting needs and available support [1], is increasingly recognized as a critical factor in early childhood development (ECD) [2]. Research has consistently shown that parenting stress exerts a negative effect on the caregiver’s quality of life and parent-child interactions, and can increase the risk of child maltreatment [3–5]. Additionally, high parenting stress are associated with adverse child outcomes, including behavioral problems, emotional difficulties and impaired cognitive development [6, 7]. Globally, the prevalence of parenting stress varies according to cultural and socioeconomic factors, with significant increases seen in conditions associated with disease or impaired function [8, 9]. It is estimated that 36%-50% of parents have experienced a certain degree of concern regarding their roles as parents, and the behavior or the development of their children [10]. Parenting stress is a salient issue in China as well. A study conducted between May and October 2019, comprising 14,667 samples across 13 cities in China, revealed that over 70% of parents of children aged 0–3 years reported moderate to severe levels of parenting stress, with those residing in rural areas or low-income households being particularly vulnerable [11].

Family support constitutes a significant component of social support, which encompasses the provision of emotional care, praise, information guidance and material satisfaction [12, 13]. Current evidence suggests that family support may serve as a protective factor against parenting stress [14, 15]. A multicenter study in Europe revealed that support from partners, family, and peers can significantly reduce parenting stress and role strain among first-time mothers [16]. A recent study conducted among Chinese parents of children with special needs also demonstrated a significant negative correlation between family support and parenting stress [17]. Additionally, research shows that psychological factors such as depression and anxiety are also significantly associated with parenting stress. For instance, the study by Sakkalou and colleagues has reported significant positive correlations between both depression/anxiety and parenting stress in mothers with visually impaired infants [18]. A recent system review revealed that maternal depression constitute a significant risk factor for parenting stress, while the relationship between anxiety and parenting stress has yielded inconsistent findings across studies [14].

The Vulnerability-Stress Model (VSM) posits that stressful events not only directly elevate stress levels but also indirectly exacerbate stress responses by activating vulnerability pathways - namely, by heightening an individual’s susceptibility to stress [19]. Within the system comprising family support, maternal psychological factors, and parenting stress, low family support functions as a stressor that elevates parenting stress, while maternal psychological factors (e.g., depressive or anxiety symptoms) serve as latent vulnerability pathways. Several research has demonstrated that low family support is an important risk factor for mental health issues such as depression and anxiety [20–22]. In turn, mental disorders such as depression and anxiety can further exacerbate parenting stress. For instance, a study conducted in mothers of children with an intellectual disability in England demonstrated a strong negative correlation between family support and parenting stress, with the association potentially mediated by parental locus of control—reflecting confidence in influencing a child’s behavior or feelings of helplessness due to a lack thereof [23]. However, given the cultural heterogeneity and distinctiveness of family patterns observed in Eastern societies, there is an urgent need for more extensive research to be conducted within Asian maternal populations [24, 25].

In consideration of the aforementioned observations, there appears to be a consistent association among high parenting stress, low family support, and symptoms of depression and anxiety. However, no study has yet confirmed this conclusion, particularly among the specific population of postpartum mothers. These findings highlight several limitations in prior research: (1) nearly all studies on parenting stress have been conducted in disadvantaged families or families with problematic children, with limited focus on general populations [6, 18, 23, 26]; (2) family support is often measured as part of broader social support rather than as a distinct construct, potentially introducing bias [27]; and (3) to our knowledge, no study has examined the interrelationships and potential statistical indirect associations among these three factors within a unified framework.

In this study, we aim to investigate the relationship among parenting stress, family support, depressive and anxiety symptoms in postpartum women. We investigated these associations in a rigorously randomized sample of Shanghai postpartum women, ensuring the findings are generalizable to the broader population. Additionally, our study measured family support as an independent construct to enhance measurement precision. This study specified a theoretically informed statistical mediation model to examine the putative pathways linking family support to parenting stress in the study population, with maternal depressive and anxiety symptoms hypothesized as candidate statistical mediators in this association. The findings are expected to identify ways to improve maternal mental health and reduce parenting stress, thereby contributing to fertility-supportive environments and enhancing women’s well-being.

Methods

Study design and ethics

A cross-sectional survey was conducted from September 2022 to March 2023 among mothers of children aged 0–2 years in child health care clinic of community health service centers (CHSCs) in Shanghai. This research adhered to the standards outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [28]. Ethical approval was granted by the Joint Research Ethics Board of the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Public Health and Nursing, under the protocol code SJUPN-202115. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed information about the study purposes and procedures. All data were collected via our self-designed WeChat Program with a matching online web platform. Data collection and storage strictly adhered to Personal Information Protection Law and Cybersecurity Law. Privacy was ensured through measures like data encryption, access control, and permission management.

Study participants and randomization

Our study is part of a cross-sectional survey assessing the prevalence of depression and anxiety among parents of children aged 0–2 years in Shanghai. The detailed steps for selecting the study participants and randomization are provided in another published study [29]. Briefly, the research employed a stratified multi-stage cluster random sampling method to select 46 CHSCs from six districts of Shanghai (Huangpu, Xuhui, Putuo, Yangpu, Fengxian, and Pudong, which represent varying geographical conditions and different levels of primary health care resources). The inclusion criteria for participants are: (1) children aged 0–2 years, (2) mothers over 18 years old who were fluent in reading and writing Chinese, (3) mothers responsible for bringing the child to health check-ups and (4) willing to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria comprised: (1) incomplete responses to key questions, (2) apparent logical inconsistencies, and (3) patterned responses or abnormally rapid completion times.

Procedure

According to the requirements of Shanghai’s primary public health care service program, parents should bring their children to CHSCs for free physical examinations at 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months of age. All mothers of children aged 0–2 years who visited sampled CHSCs during the survey period were invited to participate in this study. Prior to the initiation of the survey, the sampled CHSCs’ pediatricians and nurses underwent training as investigators. Their responsibilities included identifying eligible participants, distributing the QR code for the survey to mothers, and guiding them in completing the online survey. On the survey platform, the project team provided two questionnaires: the mandatory questionnaire-A on demographic information, maternal mental health, and family functioning, and the optional questionnaire-B on maternal parenting stress. Below the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 5355 Questionnaire-A responses were received, of which 375 were excluded due to incomplete basic information or obvious logical errors. This resulted in 4980 valid responses, with a completion rate of 93.0%. In addition, 1149 Questionnaire-B responses were received, with 50 excluded for similar reasons, yielding 1099 valid responses and a completion rate of 95.6%. Ultimately, the final sample size included in this study consisted of 1099 mothers who completed both questionnaires effectively. To ensure the representativeness of the sample data, we compared the demographic characteristics of the study sample (n = 1099) with those of the total sample (n = 4980) and found no significant differences. Measures

Parenting stress

The Parenting Stress Index (PSI) instrument is a scale designed to measure parents’ perceptions of stress within their parent-child system. The scale was developed by Abidin in 1995 [1], and its Chinese simplified version (Parenting Stress Index-Short Form, PSI-SF) was adapted by Luo and Wang in 2021 [30]. The assessment contains three sections: parental distress (PD), parent-child dysfunctional interaction (PCDI) and difficult child (DC). Each section comprises five items, with responses evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total additive score was calculated, and higher scores indicate greater levels of parenting stress. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for maternal parenting stress was 0.937, and the Cronbach’s α for PD, PCDI, DC subscales were respectively 0.874, 0.914, and 0.850. These values compare favorably with the original scale’s reliability coefficients (PD Cronbach’s α = 0.71, PCDI Cronbach’s α = 0.82, DC Cronbach’s α = 0.79, total scale Cronbach’s α = 0.87) reported in the development study [30].

Family support

The Family Adaptability, Partnership, Growth, Affection, and Resolve (APGAR) Scale is a self-report instrument designed to assess respondents’ perceptions of family functioning [31]. This scale comprises five items, each representing one of the five sections. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 (hardly ever) to 2 (almost always), with cumulative scores ranging from 0 to 10. Higher total scores indicate better family functioning, with scores ≥ 7 representing good functioning and scores ≤ 6 indicating problematic functioning based on standard scoring criteria. The Chinese version of the APGAR has been rigorously validated and exhibits robust psychometric properties, demonstrating good reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.905) comparable to prior Chinese validation results (Cronbach’s α = 0.91) [32].

Depression and anxiety

The symptoms of depression and anxiety were measured using the depression (DASS-D) and anxiety (DASS-A) subscales of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) [33]. The scale was originally developed by Lovibond and Lovibond in 1995, and is now widely used in mental health screening across all age groups including postpartum women [34, 35]. Each subscale consists of seven items and is scored on a scale from 0 (never) to 3 (most of the time), reflecting the frequency of the state in the week preceding the survey. The total score of each subscale is obtained by summing the individual item scores and multiplying by 2, resulting in a score range of 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating more severe symptomatology. The level of mental health among mothers was defined based on their results of the DASS-D and DASS-A scores, using the standard cutoff scores (DASS-D > 9 for depressive symptoms and DASS-A > 7 for anxiety symptoms). In this study, we used the validated Chinese version, demonstrating good reliability (DASS-D: Cronbach’s α = 0.820; DASS-A: Cronbach’s α = 0.806) that approaches the original scale’s established reliability (DASS-D: Cronbach’s α = 0.91; DASS-A: Cronbach’s α = 0.81) [36].

Other variables

Building on extant literature, additional variables were incorporated, including demographic and psychosocial factors, as covariates to account for their potential confounding relationships. The characteristics including age, area, yearly income, education, occupation, newborn gender, expectation of the child’ gender, delivery history and delivery forms.

Data analysis

All statistics were stored in Microsoft Excel version 2010 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), Amos version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and RStudio version 2022.12.0-353 (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Continuous data were characterized by mean (standard error) or median (interquartile range, IQR), whereas categorical data were described by frequency (percentage). Bivariate analyses to test the overall differences on all indicators between the two groups were calculated by the Kruskal-Wallis tests. The internal consistency of the subscales was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Spearman correlation coefficients were estimated for observed variables, including the scores of PSI-SF, the scores of APGAR, and the scores of DASS-D and DASS-A. Additionally, we constructed several linear regression models to examine the hypothesized associations among family support and maternal parenting stress, and the potential statistical mediating role of postpartum depressive and anxiety symptoms [37]. We selected linear regression because it (1) separately estimates direct and indirect effects, consistent with the the proposed VSM mechanism; (2) coefficients quantify association estimates in original scale units; and (3) permit flexible covariate adjustment. Discussion

The postpartum period represents a critical juncture for both mothers and children, underscoring the imperative to prioritize maternal parenting stress and mental health during the initial two years postpartum. In this study, the mean score of parenting stress was 26.37 ± 9.05, and 24.3% of the participants reported experiencing low levels of family support. Additionally, the study revealed postpartum depressive status in 8.6% of participants and postpartum anxiety symptoms in 12.2%, which is consistent with the 9.13% and 12.28% reported by Wang et al. (2025) in their prospective study of Chinese postpartum women [38]. We also found a statistically significant relationship between family support and parenting stress in postpartum women, and depressive symptoms statistically accounted for this association in the mediation analysis.

This study found that lower family support was associated with higher parenting stress, consistent with the initial hypothesis. One possible explanation is that greater involvement of family members, especially partners, in co-parenting has been associated with fewer parenting challenges and lower parenting stress among mothers [39]. However, in the context of traditional Chinese culture, mothers are typically positioned as primary caregivers in parenting practices. This culturally entrenched expectation may be associated with greater reliance on familial support among modern mothers, but when this need is not met, conflict between traditional parenting norms and modern parenting needs may be associated with greater parenting stress [40]. Another possible explanation is that greater family support is associated with higher life satisfaction and self-efficacy among mothers [41]. Of these, life satisfaction is thought to be associated with low parenting stress [42], while greater maternal self-efficacy is associated with lower mothering stress, parenting stress, and perceived stress during the transition to motherhood [41, 43]. Studies have also shown that new mothers’ parenting knowledge and practices are still largely shaped by intergenerational transmission [44], and grandparents’ participation in intergenerational co-parenting may enhance maternal parenting confidence and ability while reducing parenting stress [45–47].

After depressive symptoms were included in the model, family support was no longer directly associated with parenting stress; depressive symptoms statistically accounted for the observed association. Specifically, low family support was associated with a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms. Associations between family support and depression have also been reported in other populations (e.g., adolescents, older adults, and sexual minorities) [21, 48]. Severe maternal depression has been associated with lower confidence and competence in providing a stable environment for children and with higher parenting stress [45, 49]. Depressive symptoms, but not anxiety symptoms, showed a statistical mediation pattern in the association between family support and parenting stress. This pattern may reflect differences in the symptom profiles of the two conditions. Tamir analyzed depressive symptoms from the perspective of emotion regulation, noting that depression is characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, and diminished motivation, which are associated with difficulties in regulating stress [50]. When faced with routine caregiving tasks, individuals with depressive symptoms may require more effort to complete these tasks than those without depressive symptoms [51, 52]. Conversely, anxiety is typified by feelings of unease and apprehension and may be less closely associated than depression with withdrawal from parenting responsibilities and diminished parent-child interactions [53]. Despite some existing studies reporting potential associations between anxiety and the mother-child relationship [18, 54, 55], a recent large-scale systematic review of parenting stress noted that the evidence was inconsistent for an association between maternal anxiety and maternal parenting stress [14]. Future longitudinal research should examine the temporal ordering and potential causal relationships among depression, anxiety, and maternal parenting experiences during the early years of a child’s life.

In the subscale mediation analyses, the coefficient patterns were consistent with a complete statistical mediation pattern by postpartum depressive symptoms for the associations of family support with PD and DC, and with a partial statistical mediation pattern for the association with PCDI. A prior study reported distinct patterns of association between postpartum depressive symptoms and various dimensions of parenting stress, which may partially explain our findings [56]. One possible explanation is that the observed associations of family support with PD and DC may reflect maternal emotional state and perceptions of child behavior [30]. In contrast, PCDI may be shaped not only by maternal depressive symptoms but also by maternal parenting skills and other personality traits. Accordingly, the observed PCDI pattern may reflect both depressive symptoms and practical parenting assistance from family members, although the cross-sectional data cannot establish these pathways. In addition, subscale analyses showed that mothers of daughters had significantly higher PD scores than mothers of sons. This association may reflect the cultural roots of son preference in traditional Chinese society [57, 58]. In this cultural context, mothers of daughters may report greater familial pressure or lower family support. However, because most existing studies have reported no significant association between child sex and parenting stress [59–62], and because evidence from the Chinese cultural context remains limited, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and examined further in future studies.

Research shows that inadequate family support during the first two years postpartum is associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms and parenting stress. This can have potential adverse effects on both women and children. Therefore, it is essential to focus on the family support for women during this critical period. Family-Centered Care (FCC) is a health management approach that involves family members in healthcare and is implemented at the family level [63], its application has been demonstrated in the domains of child health care and chronic disease management. The core components of FCC, including self-care education, shared decision-making, needs-based emotional support, and family participation strategies, have demonstrated measurable benefits in patient satisfaction, health behaviors, psychological wellbeing, and family functioning [64]. Based on Chinese highly comprehensive maternal and child healthcare system and the evident decline in fertility, the present moment appears to be an opportune time to integrate FCC practice into China’s primary health care, with the objective of providing postpartum support to women. Several key considerations emerge for successful integration into China’s primary healthcare system. First, workforce capacity building through specialized training programs for community health workers will be essential. Second, systematic integration of FCC principles into existing postnatal care protocols could provide a feasible entry point. Third, policy alignment with national health priorities, including mental health promotion and fertility support initiatives, may facilitate institutional adoption. The implementation of such practices holds the potential to facilitate the establishment of family network relationships and promote functional support, which may in turn help create positive postpartum care environments and enhance women’s well-being.

Several limitations should be noted. First, because all variables were measured at a single time point, the temporal ordering of family support, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and parenting stress cannot be established; reverse causation and bidirectional associations remain possible. The mediation analysis should therefore be interpreted as a statistical decomposition of contemporaneous associations rather than evidence of a causal mechanism. Second, due to practical constraints, data on prenatal and other neonatal factors were not collected. Third, the reliance on self-report questionnaires introduces the potential for recall and reporting biases. Additionally, while parenting stress was the primary outcome, other potential stressors (e.g., stressful life events) were not controlled for, which may compromise the internal validity of our findings. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to confirm temporal sequence and test the proposed pathways.

Conclusion

Lower family support was significantly associated with higher parenting stress in postpartum women. Depressive symptoms statistically explained this association within the cross-sectional model, although causality and temporal ordering cannot be established. The findings support the prospective evaluation of FCC practices in primary healthcare, including approaches that promote family participation and supportive environments.
