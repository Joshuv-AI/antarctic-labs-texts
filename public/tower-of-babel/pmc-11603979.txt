Prevalence and factors associated with tramadol use among university students in Ghana: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Joseph Lasong, Yula Salifu, Jonas Assani wa Mwenda Kakungu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are progressively alarming rates of non-medical use, addiction and possible dependence on tramadol in low-middle-income countries. Tramadol is known to heighten negative consequences on social interactions, physical and cognitive abilities among adolescents, students and youth, particularly those with polysubstance use. However, literature on the use of tramadol in low-middle-income countries, especially among undergraduate university students in Ghana remains inadequate. Thus, this study sought to determine the prevalence and factors associated with tramadol use among undergraduate students in Ghana.MethodsAn institutional-based cross-sectional study and a quota sampling technique were employed to gather data from January to March 2023 on 600 undergraduate students from the University for Development Studies, Tamale, Ghana and analyzed with SPSS (version 26.0). Multiple logistic regression model was used to identify factors associated with the use of tramadol (p-value ≤ 0.05).ResultsThe lifetime prevalence of tramadol use was 17.8%. Among those who used tramadol in their lifetime, 14.95% used tramadol alone whereas 85.05% used at least one substance alongside tramadol. The study recorded more females (67.8%) than males. Students aged 20–24 years (20.5%), those single/never married (18.7%), those renting/living alone (19.7%) and first year students (17.4%) accounted for the majority of groups at high risk of lifetime tramadol use. To improve academic performance was the primary reason for initiating tramadol use. Male sex (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 2.673; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.590—4.493; p < 0.0001) and those with lifetime cannabis use (AOR 2.137; 95%CI 1.267—3.604; p = 0.004) were significantly associated with lifetime tramadol use.ConclusionsThis study revealed that lifetime tramadol use was high. Male sex and those with lifetime cannabis use were significantly related with lifetime tramadol use. The use of tramadol has become a public health threat and is important to limit its incidence and continuous use through extensive school and community health campaigns and strengthening of governmental policies against tramadol and other substance use since they increase the propensity of unwarranted cognitive, physical and social outcomes.Keywords: Tramadol, Use, Lifetime, University, Students, Youth, Ghana

Introduction

According to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC), the number of individuals with opioid use rose by approximately 97% over a ten-year period from 2010 to 2020 [1]. Additionally, 1.2% of people aged 15 to 64 engaged in non-medical use of opioids, including tramadol. In 2020, opioid use led to the loss of around 10 million disability-adjusted life years and was responsible for about 70% of fatalities associated with substance use [1]. The consumption of substances in ways that deviate from medical advice, including varying dosages, frequencies, or reasons other than intended by healthcare professionals is referred to as non-medical use (NMU) of substances [2]. Tramadol is a centrally acting pain reliever that functions as a mild μ-opioid agonist and inhibits the reabsorption of serotonin and norepinephrine [3]. It is commonly used to treat moderate-to-severe pain as well as chronic pain [3]. Tramadol is suggested to prevent the reabsorption of monoamines and uses an M1 metabolite with strong opioid characteristics to deliver its analgesic effects [3]. Research demonstrates that tramadol strengthens the reward system, supporting its tendency for abuse [4, 5]. Further, tramadol inhibits the serotonin transporter (SERT), which can affect ejaculation delay in men who experience premature ejaculation [6], nonetheless, hyperprolactinemia and poor sperm quality may result from prolonged use [7]. Tramadol has been assessed multiple times from 1992 and 2014 by an Expert Committee on Drug Dependence of the World Health Organization (WHO) [8]. The committee emphasized at its 41st meeting in 2019 that the data at hand prevented it from adding tramadol to the list of drugs that are globally monitored, regardless of worries being voiced about the increasing number of cases of tramadol use in different regions, especially in low- and middle-income states [8]. The primary cause did not stem from the absence of evidence but rather the inaccessibility of substitute analgesics in a number of nations, where tramadol is frequently the only treatment for acute to chronic pain and the only opioid analgesic available that is listed as a national essential drug in many of these nations; therefore that scheduling has not been advised to prevent access to this prescription from being limited, and an increase in subpar and fake tramadol prescriptions has further led to heightened unlawful use and unreported deaths [9]. Furthermore, experimental studies suggest that tramadol may be used for non-medical purposes, but far less so than with other opioids [10, 11] indicating the underrated risk of tramadol use. It is sometimes dubbed the "cocaine of the poor" [12] due to its affordability and accessibility, which contribute to its prominence among youthful populations in Africa [9, 12–14]. Analogously, Africa reported significant tramadol seizures, which in 2016 alone accounted for more than 85% of all prescription opioid seizures worldwide [15]. Furthermore, in West Africa, 17 tons of illicit tramadol were intercepted in 2014, 121 tons in 2015, and 170 tons in 2017 [9]. Moreover, 50–100 mg every 4–6 h is the suggested daily dose of immediate-release tramadol. Due to the higher likelihood of adverse reactions associated with greater amounts, the manufacturer recommends a limit of 400 mg during 24 h [16]. Nevertheless, tramadol's NMU is extremely dangerous because the non-recommended dosages, which are typically sold in Ghanaian markets, are two to five times greater than the typical dosages (100–250 mg versus 50–100 mg), which heightens the drug's dependence potential [15]. Studies on tramadol use have produced a range of prevalence rates for different research populations and geographical areas. For instance, the estimated rate of prescription opioid use was 10.7/10,000 people in the UK and between 0.6 and 12.0/10,000 people in Germany, Italy, and Spain [17]. Tramadol use rose from 2,476 to 124,592 people, according to a Korean survey conducted between 2003 and 2013 [18]. Furthermore, in an Iranian survey, the rates of tramadol use over the course of a lifetime were found to be approximately 16%, 1%, 8%, and 2% among adult males and females as well as male and female university students [19]. Moreover, studies by Saapiire et al. [20] and Osman et al. [21] found that 36.2% of youth in Ghana used tramadol for non-medical purposes, while Sudanese university students reported regular drug use at a rate of 31% and lifetime opioid use at 2.4%, respectively. Additionally, a study conducted among students in Benin found that 9.6% of them had lifetime NMU of tramadol [22]. Religion, sex, age, educational status, socioeconomic status, culture, other substance use, drug control laws, and socioenvironmental factors may influence tramadol use, addiction and dependence [13, 20, 21, 23, 24]. Tramadol usage is known to cause paranoia and disorientation among those who use it [25]. Other negative consequences associated with tramadol use are physical health challenges, social problems (e.g., thievery) [21] and depression [11]. Curiosity, peer influence, pain relief, sexual enhancement among other factors contribute to the initiation of substance use (e.g., tramadol) [20, 21, 23, 24]. According to investigations, students often face a double threat of substance use than their non-student colleagues [26]. Sadly, given its widespread occurrence, students rarely express a need to seek therapy [26]. It is a real issue in need of a concerted effort to curtail its menace especially in countries where there are heavy strains of NMU, addiction and dependence. Education and health policy-makers may find it useful to understand the prevalence of tramadol use challenges among students (such as low enthusiasm, learning challenges, psychological and physical diseases, and high-risk sexual behaviors) [21, 27] for needed interventions. There is scant information on the trends of tramadol use among individuals in low-middle-income countries and student population groups, and a postulated high prevalence of NMU of tramadol among higher education students. Thus, this study sought to determine the prevalence and associated factors of tramadol use among undergraduate students of the University for Development Studies, Tamale-Ghana.

Materials and methods

Study population, inclusion and exclusion criteria

Individuals pursuing undergraduate studies at the University for Development Studies (UDS) were surveyed. Those present at the time of the study and accepted to participate in the survey were included. Students who disagreed to participate and severely ill persons were exempted from the study.

Study design and area

An institutional-based cross-sectional design was used in this study to determine the prevalence and associated factors of the lifetime use of tramadol among undergraduate students of the University for Development Studies, Tamale-Ghana, from January to March 2023. Since cross-sectional designs are useful in investigating the magnitude of phenomena [28], it helped in defining the existing lifetime prevalence of tramadol use as well as its associated factors.

The University for Development Studies (UDS), Tamale Campus was the study area. It can be found in the Northern Region of Ghana along the Tamale-Kumasi Road, and serves as the first public University in Northern Ghana. It was founded by a seed grant given to the then President, Jerry John Rawlings under the PNDC Law 279 and gazette on May 15, 1992. There are more than 10,000 people enrolled undergraduate students at the university.

The majority of the population of Tamale, is Dagomba, however there are also small populations of the Gonja, Kusaal, Gruni, Konkomba, Akan, and Dagaaba tribes. Although Christianity and African Traditional Religion are also widely practiced, Islam is the dominant religion in the region. The Dagomba people use a patrilineal system of inheritance, and the majority of the men are polygamous. The University has health-based and non-health-based schools, faculties and departments. The University also runs several modules for students to access higher educational success at ease.

Sample and sampling technique

An average sample size was estimated for the study relying on Cochrane [29] formula; N ≥ Z2pq/d2, where N = sample size, Z = z-score at 95% confidence interval (1.96), p = assumed prevalence of lifetime tramadol use among undergraduate university students of (50%) was used due to paucity of the prevalence study in this population in Ghana at the time of the study, q = (100%—p) and d = precision error averaged at 5%. Considering a non-response rate of 10%, the minimal sample size of 425 was eligible for the study. The sample population finally gathered for the analysis was 600.

Particular to the sensitive nature of drug use, especially tramadol, in the Ghanaian setting, the study was subjected to a quota sampling method proportional to population size (size of individual schools, departmental size, faculty size, class size, and sex distribution) in gathering sample. Study participants were gathered from lecture rooms. Participants were informed on the purpose and nature of the study through an organized orientation session. Participants were requested to participate in the study to meet the generated quota from each cohort. Orientations were conducted in an enclosed environment for purposes of providing a safety-net for participants to freely ask questions regarding the survey and appropriate answers were duly given. Finally, a simple random sampling technique was employed to select study participants based on a sampling frame from registration/admission records of students. This was done till all sample were gathered. Contact details of participants were requested for follow-up purposes and strict anonymity measures were instituted. An electronic link to the questionnaires with a consent form both for guardians/parents and participants were shared with the students via their social media handles (What’s app, email). Participants were given 14 days to respond to the survey. Reminders were sent to participants every two days from the orientation day through text messages, phone calls and e-mails. Those < 18 years were requested to submit a complete survey form with both the participant and guardian/parental consent form to be considered viable for inclusion. Those ≥ 18 years were requested to submit the complete form with a consent form to be included in the survey. Responses from those who submitted a complete form with appropriate documentation within the stated time frame were used in the final analysis.

Data collection and procedure

The World Health Organizations’ student drug-usage survey model [30] was adopted in gathering responses. In-depth review of literature [13, 20–24, 31] and responses from well-versed researchers in the subject area were further used to modify questionnaires.

The questionnaires were structured, coded and anonymously self-administered. The questionnaires contained socio-demographic characteristics of participants, general lifetime drug use by participants and questions on lifetime tramadol use and its associated factors. General lifetime drug use by participants were limited to tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, heroin, cocaine/crack, intake of “wuole”, petrol/turpentine sniffing, sniffing of snuff, sniffing of glue and diazepam consumption.

Data on reasons for initiation of tramadol use was also gathered from participants. “Wuole” was termed as mixture of cooked “wee” (cannabis) and akpeteshie/local jin or any alcoholic beverage and with/without ginger [20, 32]. Tramadol use and other substance use in this study was highlighted as ever using tramadol and other substances at least once in an individual’s lifetime respectively. Polysubstance/multiple substance use was defined as ever using tramadol in addition to one or more other substance(s) at least once in an individual’s lifetime.

Quality assurance

The questionnaires were pretested among 30 students of a nearby third cycle institution. Questionnaires were adjusted for correctness and completeness of filled questionnaires were evaluated on a day-to-day basis. The pretesting also aided in the discovery of local terminologies for different drugs. Computed information on participants were put under lock, with only the principal researcher having access to it.

Statistical analysis

Data was extracted and cleaned in excel spread sheet and transported to IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences Version 26 for data analysis. Descriptive statistics like mean, standard deviation, frequencies and percentages were generated for categorical and continuous variables. Chi-squared/Fishers’ exact test for bivariate analysis was used to determine the relationship with the dependent variable (lifetime tramadol use) and independent variables. Variables significant at the bivariate level were included in a logistic regression model with odds ratio representation to identify independent predictors of lifetime tramadol use after confounding factors were adjusted for (p-value was ≤ 0.05).

Results

Out of the invited 720 students, 600 followed necessary documentation and agreed to participate in the study, accounting for an 83.33% response rate.

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

Overall, 600 undergraduate students met the study’s inclusion and were recruited for the study. The mean ± SD age (years) of the respondents was 21.81 ± 3.48 years. Participants were between the ages of 15–29 years with most (56.0%) within the 20–24-year age group. Most (49.8%) of the participants were Christians with a greater percentage (67.8%) of them being females (Table 1). In this study, individuals within the ages of 20–24 years used tramadol the most in their lifetime (20.5%). Males (32.12%), never married/single individuals (18.7%) and first year students (17.4%) also comprised majority of the clusters that suffered from a heavy burden of lifetime tramadol use. About 54.5% of the participants highlighted that, their parents/guardians used a strict supervision method (Table 1).

General lifetime drug use by participants

The prevalence of lifetime tramadol use among students was 17.8% (Table 2). Among those who used tramadol in their lifetime, 16/107 (14.95%) used tramadol alone whereas 91/107 (85.05%) used at least one substance alongside tramadol. Lifetime prevalence of other reported drug use are as follows: Alcohol (17.7%), Cannabis (16.7%), Intake of “wuole” (5.2%) and sniffing of glue (4.5%). Alcohol, tobacco and cannabis were the top three drugs used independent of tramadol (Table 2).

Discussion

Tramadol use has been classified as a public health risk and is known to affect people's health in unexpected ways, especially young people [21, 23]. This study identified lifetime tramadol use to be 17.8%. The following studies recorded lower prevalences of lifetime tramadol use than the current study: The prevalence of life-time use of tramadol among high school students was 7.8% among males and 1.8% among females [33]. Also, a Beninois study among students revealed a lifetime prevalence of NMU of tramadol of 9.6% [22]. In an Iranian survey, the lifetime rates of tramadol use among male adults and male college students were approximately 16% and 8%, respectively. Moreover, roughly 1% and 2% of female adults and female college students, respectively, reported having taken tramadol at some point in their lives. [19]. Another Iranian study reported frequency of lifetime tramadol use to be 12.5% among university students [31].

Current data suggests that the African continent is seeing a rise in the emergence and demand for tramadol, much like high-income nations [34, 35]. Also, studies indicate that tramadol increases the excitatory system, thereby corroborating its abuse tendency [4, 5], which might explain the current study result. Further, most Sub-Saharan African states have not listed tramadol as a controlled drug closely monitored by regulatory bodies, increasing its ready availability in pharmacies and black markets [13, 14], ease of purchase and hiding [9], thus could explain the high lifetime usage in the current study. Moreover, higher academic environments serve as an avenue for social networking and possible adoption of normative values contrary to entrenched societal norms, thus could expose persons to high-risk taking behaviors such as substance use (e.g., tramadol). This could possibly explain the findings of the study.

The Food and Drugs Commission of Ghana revealed tramadol addiction and usage as highly prevalent among young, commercial drivers and student populations [15]. Further, life-time use of tramadol among youthful populations was 26.9% [36] which is higher than the recorded prevalence in this study. Moreover, reports documented that the prevalence of NMU of tramadol ranged between 2%-50% among students [37–41]. The NMU of tramadol is banned in Ghana, making this topic sensitive, especially among university students. It is likely that tramadol cannot be advertised because it is prohibited. Tramadol's purchase options are further constrained due to its acquisition process (physician prescription). Again, Ghana has made an effort to educate its people about the dangers of tramadol use, which may have contributed to the lower prevalence found in this study than in others. Further, the sample technique may have produced comparable findings to other studies.

This sampling technique has been used by several research to produce conclusive results [21, 41]. Similar sample sizes to the one employed in this study have previously been reported in other investigations [21, 38, 39]. In juxtaposition to this study, studies in Greater Accra [23] and Kpandai District of Ghana's Northern Region [42] have found escalating lifetime prevalences of tramadol use among drivers and their “mates” and the youth respectively, which may have a lasting impact on the nation's most productive and efficient population base. It is possible that tramadol use is rapidly increasing in these research locations, leading to an uncontrollably larger prevalence or the difference in population groups, study setting, and design chosen may produce divergent results. It may be challenging to implement drug control measures, particularly in locations where the drug epidemic could be very severe.

In this study, exploring the connection between tramadol and other substance use was crucial due to their hypothesized shared impact on magnitude, damage, and willingness to seek therapy. Additionally, a majority of students who used tramadol had also used other substances at some point in their lives (at least one substance in addition to tramadol). Those who used tramadol alone in the current study could use it for medical reasons such as pain or premature ejaculation among others and those who use it with other substances could have substance-use related disorders [43]. Due to its inhibition of SERT [6], analgesic activity derived from an M1 metabolite with potent opioid properties, and inhibition of monoamine reabsorption [3], tramadol has a significant effect on the ejaculatory delay in men with premature ejaculation. As a result, it is a highly effective drug for managing premature ejaculations, enhancing sexual performance, and relieving pain. According to a study, young people used tramadol to treat a wide range of symptoms, including psychological issues like depression and sleep disorders as well as physical challenges like pain and early ejaculation [44]. Again, due to its heightened excitation of the reward system [5], it may be widely used to complement other substances for greater psychological effects and its risk of abuse and dependence [45]. Approximately 95% of patients using tramadol had used at least one illegal drug at some point in their lives, according to a study tracking tramadol use in the US [46]. It was found that there was a reciprocal connection between excessive smoking and dependence on tramadol among Egyptian patients who had been hospitalized for detoxification. The authors also concluded that quitting smoking might assist people who were dependent on tramadol in cutting back on their intake [47]. Additionally, a study discovered a correlation between smoking and NMU of drugs and tramadol use among Iranian university students [48]. Tramadol usage in young people was linked to an increased risk of consuming drugs including alcohol and cannabis [33], and the majority of young people who used tramadol started after smoking [49]. Tramadol use is seen as an additional step toward moving from purely inadvertent or exploratory drug use to regularized use [43]. This research highlights the significant risks that tramadol may have on young people. It also shows how students view tramadol as a pure substance of usage rather than just a prescription medicine that must be taken under stringent guidelines. Studies [21, 50] indicate that, students who use substances other than medical purposes run the risk of experiencing legal implications, theft, reduced productivity, and impaired academic achievement. Increased availability to and/or accessibility of cannabis could have an impact on how much it is used in a certain environment [21]. Students may use multiple drugs to increase their effects because of their academic load and the need to decompress, which could trigger them to choose cannabis as the best drug to use in combination with tramadol. In addition, the gateway drug theory views cannabis, alcohol, and tobacco as "gateway" substances´; it postulates that consuming these substances serves as a behavioral path that results in the intake of other substances, like tramadol [51]. Tramadol dependence has been observed in people with a history of using the drug or in people who use many psychoactive substances at once [10]. Cannabis use alone is linked with the emergence of psychotic disorders, anxiety, and melancholy [39]. Also, lung illness, coronary artery disease, depressive disorders, and other ailments have been linked with the use of tramadol or dependence in those who use cannabis [52–54]. In the current study, the likelihood of ever using tramadol was about 2.14 times higher among those with lifetime cannabis use than their counterparts. Tramadol, although primarily a prescription drug, was associated with more traditional proscribed drugs, including cannabis [45]. Moreover, Mbanga et al. [55] reported tramadol and cannabis as the most used pleasure substances among Cameroonian students. In a Swedish study, a large proportion of people who tested positive for opioids in a hair analysis also tested positive for other substances, including cannabis [56]. Furthermore, a Ghanaian study reported findings comparable to this study (e.g., those who use cannabis had a four-to-one propensity to use tramadol for non-medical purposes) [23]. People who use multiple substances seldom cut back on their usage over time [57], and people who smoke, for example, cannabis, have a worse prognosis for quitting other substances, such as tramadol [58]. The goal of multiple substance usage is to experience the redoubling of psychoactive effects [45]. These effects include pleasure, drowsiness, relaxation, and a slight psychedelic effect. Therefore, focusing on smoking and other substance use among students who use tramadol can help them become less satisfied with the drug and improve their treatment preparedness [43, 47]. In this study, the likelihood of lifetime tramadol use was roughly 2.7 times higher in men than in women. Underlined in a Sudanese study [21], male students had nearly 5.6 times the probabilities of using tramadol than woman peers, supporting this study. Male students and those who used multiple substances, particularly cannabis and tramadol, were more likely to view tramadol as a legitimate substance [43], while those with smoking habits were less likely to seek therapy. Once again, men are inherently more likely than women to use drugs, and the stress of contemporary schooling is believed to serve as a threshold for the emergence of addiction in male students [59]. Moreover, males frequently rationalize their substance-related behaviours even when they are abstaining [60], and they may take tramadol for its "heightened" impact [44] or as a treatment for early ejaculation [61]. Additionally, female use of psychoactive substances may be completely proscribed in some cultures, while tolerable among males despite its ramifications. Nevertheless, concurrent subjection to tramadol, may result in its potential non-medical use among males.

Research [62, 63] showed that, numerous persons choose to use drugs after a thoughtful evaluation process versus an impulsive response to the availability of drugs. This study suggested that students started using tramadol primarily to enhance their academic performance. The university setting is intended for advanced learning and superior academic performance. Students may face intense academic pressure to perform well and earn good grades in order to grow professionally. This could make it more likely for students to start using tramadol. In line with this study, [64] discovered that academic achievement and ambitions have an enormous impact on the commencement of substance use. Peer pressure, curiosity, libido enhancement, pain alleviation, premature ejaculation, and enjoyment have been identified as the primary motivators of tramadol use [13, 20, 21, 23], which is in contrast to the findings of this study. Despite tramadol's additional advantages, one must also take into account its underappreciated dependence, non-medical use, and heightened adverse effects on cognitive and motor function.

Limitations of the study

To our knowledge, this is the first study in Ghana to be conducted among tertiary students to determine the prevalence of lifetime tramadol use and its associated factors. This study will go a long way to influence policy decisions on tramadol and other drug use and add to literature on tramadol use among the youth and student population for targeted interventions. The study also included participants from all the schools and faculties of the university.

Notwithstanding the strengths of this study, some bottle necks were experienced. The cross-sectional design used may not allow for generalizability of findings. Other sampling styles may have provided better insights into the underlying issues of tramadol use. With that notwithstanding, comparable outcomes using the quota sampling technique were reported by other studies [21, 53].

In addition to other significant characteristics like knowledge, perception, and intent towards the use of tramadol, dependence on tramadol was not also observed in this study. Since individuals might have underreported tramadol and other substance usage, there was a chance for underreporting, social desirability, response and recollection biases, among others. Again, tramadol usage was measured using lifetime experience, which could affect the real issue on ground, as past year, month, week and current use were not evaluated as done in other studies [20, 65]. Enumerators were in the same age range as the participants, which boosted trust and confidentiality and helped to reduce the tendency for response bias.

This finding cannot be extended to the full Ghanaian undergraduate student population because other samples from other higher institutions may have provided a far broader picture of the underlying tramadol issue. Other students who were omitted from the study could have increased the sample size for a more understandable picture of the problem.

Conclusions

The study revealed a high prevalence of lifetime tramadol use among students, who make up a sizable portion of the socially engaged population. The study highlighted the emergence of other dangerous drug use (like "wuole") and high use of multiple substances. Male sex and those with lifetime cannabis use were significantly related to lifetime tramadol use. To improve academic performance was the main reason for tramadol initiation. Higher academic environments predispose persons to high-risk taking activities such as substance use due to increased interpersonal and social contacts. The need to decompress from academic pressure could push persons into substance use. Since cannabis use serves as a gate substance for other substance use (like tramadol, “wuole”, etc.) it is important to advance the knowledge among youthful populations on its use and other substances and their potential hazards. More efforts should be made by governmental and non-governmental bodies to support males in identifying the trends and deleterious nature of tramadol use because they are more predisposed to its use because of societal norms, exposure to strenuous working environments and their continuous need for enhanced sexual performance. To fully understand the devastation caused by tramadol use, advanced technology is required to measure addiction, tolerance, psychological/mental, physical, and social consequences, among others. Multi-center studies and trends analysis of the NMU of tramadol could enhance knowledge on tramadol use and tailor interventions towards its management.

Recommendations for policy actions and future studies

Tramadol use can progress to addiction, which is a public health concern with severe social and health implications such as legal suits, reduced academic achievement, respiratory illnesses and depressive disorders [21, 50, 52–54].

The authorities of schools need to keep a closer eye on their students. To lessen the burden of non-medical substance use generally, suitable counseling and coaching sessions are required. Peer education efforts should be stepped up across institutions and behavior modification interventions should be improved. For a more wholesome population, drug control laws should also be upheld in institutions and across the country. University campuses should carryout medical screening using toxicological methods to detect and manage substance use disorders of newly admitted undergraduate students.

To reduce students' possible tramadol addiction and dependence, it is also important to educate them about the negative effects of non-medical tramadol use on society as a whole. Other forms of engagement, such as service projects and community involvement, sports, debates, and other positive environmental forces, should also be promoted. To care for students who require such services, institutions should have designated areas for psychological and mental health counseling as well as safety corners.

To prevent or lessen the negative effects of tramadol use on children's social and physical well-being, parents and guardians must be educated on current events, the identification of non-medical tramadol uses and addictive/dependence behaviors, risks, and possible solutions. Further methodologies (e.g., exploratory mixed methods) could be employed to reveal other determinants of lifetime tramadol use and reasons behind tramadol’s continuous use and possible dependence. This could guide health promotion interventions tailored to curb/limit non-medical tramadol use prevalence and its deleterious health and social outcomes. Although the differences in predictor variables prevent the conclusions of this study from being generalized to the entire population of undergraduate students in Ghana and around the world, it is crucial to continue research into it using more reliable longitudinal and epidemiological methods to comprehend its pattern and implications for resolute control approaches.
