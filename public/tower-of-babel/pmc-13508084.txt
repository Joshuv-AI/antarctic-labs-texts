Organocatalytic Asymmetric Formal Aza-[3 + 2] Cycloaddition via Domino Michael/Acetalization: Stereoselective Access to 3,4-cis-Disubstituted Pyrrolidines and Pot-Economical Total Syntheses of (−)-Kainic Acid and (−)-Domoic Acid
Authors: Yutaro Hatano, Hinata Odaira, Toshiki Tachibana, Yujiro Hayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An asymmetric catalytic formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition (domino Michael/acetalization reaction) between aldehydes and benzyl N-(3-nitro-2-propen-1-yl)­carbamate catalyzed by a diphenylprolinol silyl ether has been developed, affording 3,4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidines with excellent diastereo- and enantioselectivity. The transformation proceeds via a domino sequence comprising a Michael addition followed by hemiacetalization. This reaction exhibits broad substrate scope and high functional-group tolerance, enabling concise access to densely functionalized pyrrolidine scaffolds. The method was further applied to the asymmetric total syntheses of (−)-kainic acid and (−)-domoic acid, neuroexcitatory marine natural products bearing highly substituted pyrrolidine cores. These syntheses highlight the versatility of the formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition adduct as a chiral building block, featuring efficient one-pot transformations, Nef oxidation, and olefination to assemble complex pyrrolidines. Overall, this work establishes a unified asymmetric strategy for the rapid and stereocontrolled construction of biologically active pyrrolidine natural products.

Introduction

The pyrrolidine scaffold is one of the most ubiquitous heterocyclic motifs found in biologically active molecules, occurring widely in both natural products and pharmaceuticals. Several notable bioactive compounds, including (−)-kainic acid (1), (+)-allokainic acid, (−)-domoic acid (2), and acromelic acid (Figure ), feature a 3,4-disubstituted pyrrolidine core. Accordingly, the development of efficient, practical, and environmentally benign methods for the construction of this structural motif remains a significant objective in synthetic chemistry.

(−)-Kainic acid, (+)-allokainic acid, (−)-domoic acid, and acromelic acid.

(−)-Kainic acid (1) and (−)-domoic acid (2) belong to the kainoid familystructurally related excitatory amino acids exhibiting potent neuroactivity. (−)-Kainic acid, first isolated from the red alga Digenea simplex in 1953, displays strong anthelmintic and excitatory activities and serves as a valuable neuropharmacological probe for studies of excitotoxicity and neurodegenerative disorders such as epilepsy and Alzheimer’s disease. (−)-Domoic acid, isolated from Chondria armata by Takemoto and Daigo, is the causative agent of amnesic shellfish poisoning (ASP) and exhibits even greater neurotoxicity through stimulation of kainate receptors, as well as α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors. Owing to their potent biological activities and limited natural abundance, the development of efficient and scalable synthetic routes to kainoid natural products remains an important objective in synthetic chemistry.

Numerous syntheses of (−)-kainic acid have been reported, whereas only limited progress has been made toward the domoic acid family. , To date, only two total syntheses of (−)-domoic acid itself have been disclosed, , along with merely four reports on structurally related derivatives, underscoring the considerable synthetic challenges associated with this class of compounds. These natural products share a highly functionalized C3,C4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidine core. Most syntheses of (−)-kainic and (−)-domoic acids rely on diastereoselective transformations from chiral precursors. Diastereoselective aza-[3 + 2] cycloadditions have been employed in the total syntheses of (−)-kainic acid by Fukuyama and Aggarwal (Scheme , eqs 1 and 2). A single example of a catalytic asymmetric approach, involving an ene reaction reported by Ganem, exhibited only moderate selectivity and required a catalyst loading of 200 mol % (Scheme , eq 3). Although the aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition represents a powerful and convergent strategy for constructing pyrrolidine frameworks, to the best of our knowledge, no catalytic asymmetric variant of this transformation has yet achieved the synthesis of 3,4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidines with high enantioselectivity (≥90% ee). Moreover, despite the close structural relationship between (−)-kainic acid and (−)-domoic acid, no unified synthetic strategy has been developed to access both molecules using the same methodology.

Syntheses of 3,4-Disubstituted Pyrrolidine

One-pot transformations have emerged as powerful approaches for rapidly increasing molecular complexity through multiple bond-forming events in a single operational sequence. Such processes streamline synthesis by avoiding intermediate purifications and minimizing waste generation.

Several metrics can be considered in a synthesis, such as overall yield, average yield per step, number of operating steps, selectivity (chemo-, regio-, diastereo-, and enantioselectivity), cost of starting materials and reagents, solvent cost, labor cost, E-factor, scalability, environmental considerations, as well as atom economy, step economy, and redox economy.

Building upon these considerations, we have previously advanced the concept of “pot economy” as a guiding principle for efficient total synthesis. Based on this concept, we have been engaged in the pot-economical synthesis of biologically active molecules.

Herein, we describe an organocatalyst-mediated asymmetric formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition (domino Michael/acetalization reaction) that enables the efficient construction of 3,4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidines with excellent enantioselectivity. This methodology was successfully applied to concise and pot-economical total syntheses of (−)-kainic acid and (−)-domoic acid.

Results and Discussion

Asymmetric Formal Aza-[3 + 2] Cycloaddition Reaction

We previously reported a syn-selective, asymmetric catalytic Michael reaction between aldehydes and nitroalkenes catalyzed by a diphenylprolinol silyl ether, a catalyst independently developed by our group and by Jørgensen’s group. Building on this precedent, we envisioned a domino sequence comprising the Michael addition of amino-bearing nitroalkene 3 to aldehyde 4, followed by intramolecular cyclization to furnish the 3,4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidinol 5 (eq 4). As a model substrate, 3-phenylpropanal (4a) was selected, and the reaction was examined in toluene. However, the reaction proceeded slowly, providing the desired product 5a in only 34% yield, along with 10% of byproduct 6, in which the catalyst was trapped (Table , entry 1). We independently prepared 6 from equimolar amounts of 3, 4, and the catalyst, and confirmed its structure. Compound 6 is presumed to arise from intramolecular nucleophilic attack on the iminium ion intermediate 7 (red arrow)formed from the enamine and nitroalkene 3prior to hydrolysis (blue arrow) (Scheme ).

To convert 6 into 5a, we examined the effects of acid and water additives (Table ). Among the acids tested, 4-nitrobenzoic acid (10 mol %) proved optimal, and its combination with water (3 equiv) afforded 5a in excellent yield (entry 5) as a mixture of C2 diastereomers (dr = 1.4:1). Subsequent treatment of this diastereomeric mixture of 5a with Et3SiH and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) provided the corresponding pyrrolidine 8a in excellent yield and with outstanding cis-selectivity (>20:1, eq 5). Notably, 5a was obtained in nearly enantiomerically pure form (>99% ee).

The generality of this pyrrolidine synthesis was explored (Table ). The sequence, consisting of a formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition (Reaction I: 3 + 4 → 5) followed by reduction (Reaction II: 5 → 8), proceeded smoothly across a wide range of aldehydes. Linear, branched, and aromatic aldehydesincluding propanal, butanal, isovaleraldehyde, and 3-phenylpropanalas well as functionalized aldehydes bearing alkynyl, α-benzyloxy, or γ-siloxy substituents, all afforded 3,4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidines in high yields with uniformly nearly perfect enantioselectivities. Aldehydes with γ-methoxycarbonyl or γ-tert-butoxycarbonyl groups provided highly substituted pyrrolidines 8h and ent-8i, respectively; the latter required the opposite enantiomer of the catalyst, generating 2-hydroxy pyrrolidine ent-5i as an intermediate, which serves as a key precursor in the total syntheses of (−)-kainic acid and (−)-domoic acid (vide infra). The relative and absolute configurations were confirmed by conversion of ent-5i into these natural products. This two-pot organocatalyzed formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition and reduction sequence thus provides rapid access to 3,4-cis-pyrrolidines with excellent stereocontrol across a broad range of substrates.

Total Synthesis of (−)-Kainic Acid

The retrosynthetic analysis of (−)-kainic acid is shown in Scheme . The target molecule was envisioned to arise from the 2-cyano derivative 9 through hydrolysis of the nitrile group followed by global deprotection. 9 would be obtained from ketone 10 via olefination, and 10 in turn would derive from nitroalkane 11 through a Nef reaction. The key 3,4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidine intermediate ent-5i was prepared by the asymmetric formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition of 3 with aldehyde 4i. The major challenges were the introduction of the methyl group at the α-position of the nitro group of ent-5i and the suppression of facile epimerization at C4 of 10.

Retrosynthetic Analysis of (−)-Kainic Acid

The synthesis commenced with a formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition (Scheme ). Nitroalkene 3 was readily prepared from commercially available N-Cbz-glycinal via a one-pot sequence consisting of a Henry reaction followed by dehydration, affording the product 3 in 77% yield. The reaction of 3 and commercially available 4i proceeded smoothly in the presence of (R)-diphenylprolinol silyl ether to furnish ent-5i in excellent yield and enantioselectivity when the reaction was quenched at this stage (Table , entry for ent-5i). Next, cyanation at the C2 position was investigated. Fukuyama and coworkers reported that the direct introduction of a cyano group using TMSOTf and TMSCN results in concurrent deprotection of tert-butyl ester; therefore, they adopted a two-step sequence in which the 2-hydroxy group was first converted into a 2-methoxy group and subsequently into the corresponding 2-cyano derivative. We modified this two-step protocol into a one-pot procedure: treatment of ent-5i with PPTS in MeOH furnished methyl ether 12, which, after solvent exchange, was treated with TMSCN and BF3·OEt2 at −40 °C to afford nitrile 13. Notably, by connecting the preceding formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition with this one-pot sequence, we accomplished four consecutive transformations (3 + 4i → ent-5i → 12 → 13) in a single pot with good overall yield.

Pot-Economical Total Synthesis of (−)-Kainic Acid

Methylation at the α-position of the nitro moiety (13 → 11) proved challenging because direct C-alkylation of the corresponding nitronate was not feasible due to predominant O-alkylation. We therefore conceived an alternative strategy involving the conversion of 13 to 14 using Eschenmoser’s salt, followed by transformation to nitroalkene 15, and subsequent 1,4-reduction to furnish 11.

Initially, each transformation was optimized individually. When LHMDS was employed as the base in the first step, treatment of 13 with Eschenmoser’s salt afforded 14 in good yield. Subsequent treatment of isolated 14 with mCPBA promoted Cope elimination efficiently, delivering nitroalkene 15 in high yield. Furthermore, the reduction of isolated 15 with NaBH­(OAc)3 smoothly provided 11.

However, complications arose when these steps were conducted in a one-pot sequence. In the first step, the use of LHMDS led to the formation of HMDS after the reaction, and this residual HMDS acted as a nucleophile toward the highly reactive nitroalkene 15 in the subsequent step, thereby interfering with the desired transformation. This issue was successfully addressed by replacing LHMDS with t-BuOK.

In the second step, although mCPBA was effective under stepwise conditions, the residual oxidant adversely affected the efficiency of the subsequent reduction (15 → 11) in the one-pot system. Accordingly, mCPBA was replaced with MeI, which induced the elimination of trimethylamine to generate nitroalkene 15 in good yield.

In the final step, NaBH­(OAc)3, while effective for the reduction of isolated 15, exhibited insufficient reactivity in the presence of residual reagents under one-pot conditions. In contrast, the more reactive NaBH4 proved compatible with the reaction system and enabled efficient 1,4-reduction, affording 11 in good yield.

Thus, by carefully selecting appropriate bases and reagents for each step, a one-pot transformation from 13 to 11 was successfully realized.

The subsequent Nef reaction was a critical step. Because the resulting ketone 10 is prone to epimerization at C4 and the following transformations were to be performed in one pot, the reaction conditions had to be carefully optimized. We found that an oxidative Nef reaction using DMDO and t-BuOK was optimal, and it was important to use slightly less than one equivalent of the base (0.95 equiv). This protocol generates only acetone and KNO2 as byproducts, which do not interfere with the subsequent steps and thus allows for a one-pot sequence. By using 0.95 equiv of t-BuOK, the nitronate is fully generated without leaving residual base in the reaction mixture, resulting in near-neutral conditions. Under these conditions, ketone 10 does not undergo unwanted epimerization, even though it could otherwise readily epimerize to the trans isomer under basic conditions.

Without isolation of 10, the addition of Ando’s sulfonate reagent in the same vessel effected olefination to yield alkene 9 in 65% yield over two steps, with only minor epimerization at C4. In contrast, the Takai olefination ,,,,,,, gave lower yield, and the Wittig reaction (Ph3PMeBr/t-BuOK) caused complete isomerization to the 3,4-trans isomer. , Finally, hydrolysis of 9 under refluxing aqueous NaOH, following the protocol of Fukuyama, effected nitrile hydrolysis, C2 epimerization, and global deprotection in a single pot to deliver (−)-kainic acid (1) after recrystallization. In total, (−)-kainic acid (1) was synthesized in only five pots with an overall yield of 12%.

Total Synthesis of (−)-Domoic Acid

The structural distinction between (−)-domoic acid and (−)-kainic acid resides in the C4 side chain, which is considerably more elaborate in the former. We envisioned that the installation of a C4 nitromethyl substituent on the formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloadduct would provide a versatile synthetic handle for constructing this fragment. The target molecule was planned to arise from α,β-unsaturated aldehyde 16 via an olefination reaction (Scheme ). Aldehyde 16 was designed to originate from ester 17 through a Z-selective Michael addition followed by reduction, whereas 17 would be obtained from alkyne 18, prepared by a Seyferth–Gilbert homologation of aldehyde 19. Aldehyde 19 was expected to be accessible by a route analogous to that developed for the synthesis of (−)-kainic acid.

Retrosynthetic Analysis of (−)-Domoic Acid

Because selective reduction of the ester was to be performed at a late stage, the introduction of the C2 cyano group was scheduled after this reduction step. In contrast to ketone 10 in the (−)-kainic acid, aldehyde 19 is more prone to isomerization; therefore, suppression of this undesired process represented one of the key challenges. With respect to the protection of the pyrrolidine nitrogen, the kainic acid synthesis employed a Cbz group, whose deprotection required harsh conditions (NaOH, reflux). To enable the removal of the protecting group under significantly milder conditions at the final stage of the synthesis, we selected a Boc group.

The synthesis commenced with the formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition of commercially available Boc-protected nitroalkene 21 and aldehyde 4i (Scheme ). It was found that not only Cbz protected alkene 3 but also Boc protected alkene 21 worked well to afford cycloadduct 20. After evaporation, MeOH and catalytic amount of PPTS were added to furnish 2-methoxy pyrrolidine 22α and 22β in 46% and 46% yield, respectively, with excellent 3,4-cis selectivity in a single reaction vessel. 22α and 22β were separable, and both were advanced independently.

Total Synthesis of (−)-Domoic Acid

An oxidative Nef reaction using DMDO, followed by in situ treatment with the Ohira–Bestmann reagent, provided alkynes 18α and 18β in good overall yields over the two steps in a one-pot operation, with full retention of stereochemical integrity at C3 and C4. Under the traditional Ohira–Bestmann conditions (K2CO3, 0 °C), isomerization at C4 was observed. In contrast, activation of the Ohira–Bestmann reagent with NaOMe at −78 °C generates the anionic form of the Seyferth–Gilbert reagent. Slow addition of the aldehyde solution to this anion at −78 °C, followed by gradual warming to ambient temperature, resulted in significant isomerization at C4. Conversely, when the reaction mixture was warmed to room temperature immediately after the addition, only minimal isomerization occurred, and alkynes 18α and 18β were obtained in good yields.

We attribute the isomerization to the rapid yet reversible 1,2-addition of the Seyferth–Gilbert anion to the aldehyde at low temperature; the subsequent nucleophilic attack of the resulting alkoxide on phosphorus is comparatively slow. Regeneration of the aldehyde under these conditions enables epimerization at C4. Furthermore, when the anionprepared separately at −78 °Cwas added to a solution of aldehyde 19 and the mixture was warmed directly to room temperature, 18α and 18β were again formed in good yields. This protocol also enabled a concise two-step, one-pot conversion from 22.

Subsequent reaction of alkynes 18α and 18β with n-BuLi and ClCO2Me, followed by the addition of Me2CuLi in THF, afforded the corresponding (Z)-alkenes 23α and 23β with excellent Z-selectivity. This excellent Z-selectivity is noteworthy, and the use of THF was crucial for achieving this selectivity. Notably, the two transformations(i) C–C bond formation between the alkynyl anion and ClCO2Me and (ii) the Michael reaction of Me2CuLican be conducted in a single reaction vessel with excellent overall selectivity.

A difference in reactivity between the α- and β-isomers was observed in the lithium diisobutyl-t-butoxyaluminum hydride (LDBBA) reduction of 23. In both cases, the major products were allyl alcohols 24. Whereas selective reduction of the methyl ester in the presence of the tert-butyl ester was achieved from 23α, partial reduction of the tert-butyl ester occurred from 23β. This outcome is attributed to the more accessible environment around the tert-butyl ester in 23β relative to 23α, rendering it susceptible to reduction.

Allylic oxidation of 24α and 24β with MnO2 furnished the corresponding α,β-unsaturated aldehydes 16α and 16β in good yields. The modified Julia–Kocienski olefination developed by Kan and coworkers proceeded smoothly from 16α and 16β, delivering the corresponding E,Z-dienes 25α and 25β with high stereoselectivity.

Treatment of 25α and 25β with Pd­(PPh3)4 and TolSO2Na furnished carboxylic acids 26α and 26β, which were subsequently transformed into 2-cyano pyrrolidine 27 through reaction with TMSCN and BF3·OEt2 in a one-pot sequence. Compound 27 was obtained as a mixture of isomers at the C2 position (α:β = 1:2), and the same diastereomeric ratio was observed regardless of whether 25α or 25β was used as the starting material. These isomers 27α and 27β were separable by chromatography.

Isomer 27α was isomerized to 27β upon treatment with 120 mol % t-BuOK in THF/t-BuOH, wherein an equimolar amount of base served to generate the potassium carboxylate from the corresponding carboxylic acid (eq 7). The resulting 27β was then transformed into imidate 28β by reaction with NaOMe, followed by acid treatment (aq. HCl) to afford methyl ester 29β in 67% yield over two steps in a one-pot procedure. Finally, global deprotection of the Boc and tert-butyl esters under acidic conditions, followed by hydrolysis of the methyl ester, provided (−)-domoic acid (2) quantitatively over two steps in a one-pot operation.

In total, (−)-domoic acid (2) was synthesized with excellent diastereo- and enantioselectivity in 10.8% overall yield over 10 pots, including the isomerization step (27α → 27α + 27β). To the best of our knowledge, this represents the highest overall yield reported to date.

Conclusion

We have developed a general and efficient asymmetric organocatalytic formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition (domino Michael/acetalization) between aldehydes and benzyl N-(3-nitro-2-propen-1-yl)­carbamate (3), providing direct access to 3,4-cis-disubstituted pyrrolidines with excellent diastereo- and enantioselectivities. The reaction proceeds through a domino Michael addition–cyclization sequence and exhibits broad substrate scope and high functional-group tolerance.

The synthetic value of this transformation was demonstrated through concise, stereoselective asymmetric total syntheses of (−)-kainic acid and (−)-domoic acid. These syntheses rely on unified strategies that capitalize on the high stereochemical fidelity and modularity of the formal aza-[3 + 2] cycloaddition products.

A formal C-alkylation at the α-position relative to the nitro group, an epimerization-free Nef reaction, and an efficient methylenation sequence were key to achieving a pot-economic synthesis of (−)-kainic acid. In contrast, the synthesis of (−)-domoic acid highlights an epimerization-free Ohira–Bestmann homologation and a stereoselective assembly of the C4 side-chain E,Z-diene moiety. (−)-Kainic acid and (−)-domoic acid were synthesized in five pots and ten pots, respectively.

Overall, this work establishes a versatile and highly stereocontrolled asymmetric catalytic platform for constructing biologically relevant pyrrolidine architectures and sets the stage for future applications in the synthesis of complex natural products and pharmaceutical agents.
