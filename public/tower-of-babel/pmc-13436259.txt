Clinical analysis of tracheobronchial foreign body aspiration in children aged six years and older
Authors: Xiaoxu Zhang, Li Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveForeign body aspiration (FBA) occurs across all age groups but predominantly affects children under three years of age. Clinical evidence suggests that tracheobronchial foreign bodies in school-age children (> 6 years) present distinct characteristics in terms of foreign body types, clinical progression, and management strategies compared to younger children. This study aimed to analyze the clinical features and outcomes of school-age children who underwent rigid bronchoscopy for suspected FBA and to propose a clinically applicable management algorithm.Materials and methodsA retrospective single-center study was conducted involving 58 patients over six years of age who underwent rigid bronchoscopy for suspected foreign body aspiration between January 2019 and December 2023. All patients underwent imaging evaluation, including low-dose computed tomography (CT) following the ALARA principle when clinically indicated.ResultsThe cohort included 51 males (87.9%) and 7 females (12.1%), with a mean age of 9.14 years (range: 6.17–13.83 years). The most common clinical manifestations were cough (94.8%) and wheezing (89.7%). Pen caps (43.1%) and watermelon seeds (17.2%) were the most frequently aspirated foreign bodies. Most patients (62.1%) presented within 24 h. Bronchoscopic removal was successful within 30 min in 56.9% of cases. Two patients (3.4%) required thoracotomy following unsuccessful bronchoscopic attempts.ConclusionFBA in school-age children demonstrates distinct clinical characteristics, with non-food foreign bodies such as pen caps being predominant. Low-dose CT plays an important diagnostic role, while rigid bronchoscopy remains the primary treatment for central airway foreign bodies, and flexible bronchoscopy is useful for distal airway involvement. A structured algorithmic approach may facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate intervention.

Introduction

Foreign body aspiration (FBA) is a potentially life-threatening condition that can occur across all age groups but is most prevalent in children under three years of age. It remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in pediatric populations [1].

Most existing studies have focused on infants and toddlers, in whom aspirated foreign bodies are typically food-related, such as nuts and seeds. However, school-age children (> 6 years) represent a distinct subgroup with different behavioral patterns and risk factors. Previous studies have demonstrated that the type of aspirated foreign bodies varies across age groups and regions [2].In addition, anatomical characteristics of the pediatric airway, including narrower lumens and structural differences in bronchial orientation, may influence both the location and clinical presentation of foreign body aspiration [3]. Compared with younger children, school-age children are more likely to aspirate non-food objects, such as pen caps, due to activities such as studying and playing. Despite these differences, there is a lack of dedicated clinical studies focusing specifically on school-age children. Furthermore, the diagnostic and management strategies in this population remain incompletely defined. Therefore, the present study aimed to analyze the clinical characteristics and outcomes of school-age children with suspected FBA and to propose a practical, algorithm-based management approach.

Materials and methods

A retrospective single-center study was conducted on 58 consecutive patients over six years of age who underwent rigid bronchoscopy for suspected foreign body aspiration between January 2019 and December 2023.All patients underwent initial clinical evaluation, including history taking and physical examination. Imaging studies were performed based on clinical suspicion. Chest radiography was used as an initial screening tool in patients without a clear history of choking and with no significant abnormal findings on lung auscultation.; however, due to its limited sensitivity, especially for radiolucent foreign bodies, low-dose computed tomography (CT) was performed when necessary. All CT examinations were conducted following the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle to minimize radiation exposure in pediatric patients. Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 6 years old and those diagnosed with broncholiths, bronchomalacia, or bronchial tumors. All rigid bronchoscopy procedures were performed under general anesthesia in an operating room setting. Flexible bronchoscopy was selectively used in cases involving distal airway foreign bodies or residual fragments.Data collected included demographic characteristics, history of choking, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, location and type of foreign body, and details of the removal procedures.This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval number is B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/428.

Results

A total of 58 school-age children with suspected foreign body aspiration were included in this study. The cohort consisted of 51 males (87.9%) and 7 females (12.1%), with a mean age of 9.14 ± 1.96 years (range: 6.17–13.83 years).

The most common presenting symptoms were cough (94.8%) and wheezing (89.7%), followed by dyspnea and decreased breath sounds. A definite history of choking was reported in the majority of patients, although a small proportion presented without a clear history but with suggestive clinical findings.

Regarding the type of foreign bodies, pen caps (Fig. 1) were the most frequently encountered (43.1%), followed by watermelon seeds (17.2%). Other foreign bodies included small plastic objects and food particles. In terms of anatomical distribution, most foreign bodies were located in the bronchial tree, with a predominance in the right bronchus.

The time from aspiration to presentation varied, with 62.1% of patients presenting within 24 h. Delayed presentation was associated with less specific symptoms and occasionally required more complex management.

Bronchoscopic removal was successfully performed in the majority of cases. The duration of the procedure was less than 30 min in 56.9% of patients. Flexible bronchoscopy was utilized in selected cases involving distal airway foreign bodies or residual fragments following rigid bronchoscopy. Two patients (3.4%) required thoracotomy due to failure of endoscopic removal, both of whom had tightly impacted foreign bodies. No major perioperative complications were observed (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Discussion

This study highlights the distinct clinical characteristics of foreign body aspiration (FBA) in school-age children, a subgroup that has been relatively underrepresented in the literature. FBA remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in pediatric populations and is largely preventable [1, 4]. Compared with younger children, who predominantly aspirate food items such as nuts and seeds, school-age children are more likely to aspirate non-food objects. This difference is likely related to behavioral factors and is consistent with previous reports indicating geographic and age-related variations in foreign body types [2].

In our cohort, pen caps were the most frequently encountered foreign bodies. From an anatomical perspective, the predominance of foreign bodies in the right bronchus observed in this study is consistent with the structural characteristics of the airway, where the right main bronchus is wider and more vertically oriented, facilitating foreign body entry [3].Delayed presentation remains a critical issue in FBA. Although most patients in our study presented within 24 h, a subset of patients had delayed diagnosis, often due to the absence of a clear choking history. Previous studies have shown that delayed presentation is associated with nonspecific symptoms and increased procedural difficulty [5, 6], and may lead to inflammatory complications such as granuloma formation [7].Imaging plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of FBA. Chest radiography is often used as an initial screening tool due to its accessibility and low radiation exposure; however, its sensitivity is limited, especially for radiolucent foreign bodies. In contrast, computed tomography (CT) provides superior sensitivity and specificity, allowing for accurate localization of the foreign body and identification of secondary changes such as air trapping or atelectasis [8, 9]. In this study, low-dose CT was widely utilized in accordance with the ALARA principle. Therefore, in patients without a clear history of choking but with persistent symptoms or abnormal physical findings, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion. In our diagnostic algorithm, if the initial chest radiography is negative, further evaluation with low-dose CT is recommended when symptoms persist or recur after a short course of medical therapy. This stepwise approach may help reduce missed diagnoses and avoid delays in management.

Bronchoscopy remains the gold standard for both diagnosis and treatment of FBA. Rigid bronchoscopy is generally preferred for foreign bodies located in the central airways due to its superior airway control and ability to remove large or impacted objects. Flexible bronchoscopy is particularly useful for foreign bodies located in distal airways and for retrieving residual fragments [10]. The complementary use of rigid and flexible bronchoscopy has been shown to improve the overall success rate of foreign body removal [11].Certain types of foreign bodies pose unique challenges. Organic materials may swell or fragment, increasing the difficulty of removal. In cases of tightly impacted foreign bodies, endoscopic removal may fail, necessitating surgical intervention.Based on our findings and previous literature, we propose an algorithmic approach for the management of suspected FBA in school-age children (Fig. 2). This approach integrates clinical history, imaging findings, and the anatomical location of the foreign body to guide decision-making. Prevention remains a critical component in reducing the incidence of FBA. Educational efforts targeting parents, caregivers, and school-age children are essential [4].

Despite these findings, this study has several limitations. It is a retrospective, single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Previous studies have also indicated that early intervention is associated with improved outcomes [12]. Future multicenter studies are needed to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

Foreign body aspiration in school-age children presents distinct clinical characteristics compared with younger populations, with a predominance of non-food foreign bodies such as pen caps. Low-dose CT plays an important role in improving diagnostic accuracy, particularly in cases with inconclusive clinical findings. Rigid bronchoscopy remains the first-line treatment for foreign bodies in central airways, while flexible bronchoscopy serves as a valuable adjunct for distal airway involvement and residual fragments. An algorithmic, location-based management strategy may facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate intervention, ultimately improving clinical outcomes in this patient population.
