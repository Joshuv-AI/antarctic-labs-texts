Hypo-fractionated radiotherapy combined with PD-1 inhibitor for relapsed/refractory B-cell lymphoma after failure to CD19 CAR-T therapy
Authors: Min Li, Fang Bao, Ran Peng, Hongmei Jing, Hao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
B-cell lymphoma relapsed after chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy lacks effective standard treatment options and generally has a poor prognosis, largely due to mechanisms such as immune escape and tumor heterogeneity. Radiotherapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) may exert synergistic effects, including local tumor control through radiation-induced DNA damage and systemic immune activation via modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. This case report describes a patient with relapsed/refractory (R/R) B-cell lymphoma who experienced multiple relapses and treatment failures, including CD19 CAR-T therapy. Almost 7 years after CAR-T treatment, the patient developed disease relapse and was diagnosed with follicular lymphoma (FL). The patient received a personalized treatment strategy combining hypo-fractionated radiotherapy (30 Gy/5 fractions) and the Programmed Death-1 (PD-1) inhibitor camrelizumab, achieving complete remission (CR) and remaining disease-free for over 43 months. This case highlights the potential efficacy of this combination strategy in R/R lymphoma, particularly in patients with limited treatment options.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) accounts for approximately 30% of all non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) cases and represents the most common aggressive subtype of NHL. Over 60% of patients can achieve a cure with R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) immunochemotherapy. 1 However, 20%–40% of patients develop relapsed/refractory (R/R) B-cell lymphomas after R-CHOP treatment. These patients typically have a poor prognosis.2–4

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy is an established standardofcare for R/R B-cell lymphomas, demonstrating high tumor remission rates. Despite this, over 50% of patients experience relapse within six months after treatment.5,6 For those whose disease progresses following CAR-T therapy, there is no consensus on a standard salvage treatment, and the median overall survival is reported to be only 5.2 months. 6

Hypo-fractionated radiotherapy combined with a programmed death-1 (PD-1) inhibitor has demonstrated efficacy in achieving local tumor control and systemic immune activation, with this immunomodulatory effect being observed in several solid tumors.7–9 This case report describes a patient with R/R B-cell lymphoma who developed a tumor relapse almost 7 years after CAR-T therapy. The patient was treated with a combination of Hypo-fractionated Radiotherapy and a PD-1 inhibitor, achieving complete remission (CR). This case highlights the potential of Hypo-fractionated radiotherapy combined with a PD-1 inhibitor as an effective therapeutic strategy for R/R B-cell lymphomas following CAR-T failure.

1. Initial diagnosis and treatment

In June 2011, a 37-year-old female presented with a mandibular mass. Positron Emission Tomography-Computed Tomography (PET-CT) revealed extensive lymphadenopathy, with additional involvement of the left parotid gland, liver, spleen, and left adrenal gland. Biopsy of the left supraclavicular lymph node confirmed DLBCL of germinal center origin, with a Ki-67 positivity of 40%-50%. Bone marrow biopsy showed no lymphoma involvement.

The patient was diagnosed with DLBCL, NOS (WHO classification), Ann Arbor stage IVA, with an International Prognostic Index (IPI) score of 2. Following eight cycles of first-line R-CHOP chemotherapy, follow-up PET-CT revealed minimal residual retroperitoneal lymph nodes with low standardized uptake values (SUV; exact values not available). The patient then received four cycles of rituximab maintenance therapy.

2. First relapse and treatment challenges

In March 2013, the patient developed lymph node swelling behind the left ear and in front of the right ear. Biopsy confirmed recurrent DLBCL. The patient was enrolled in the phase Ib GERSHWIN study (NCT01680991) without achieving effective tumor control. Treatments with DICE, GEMOX, E-POCH, and ESHAP regimens were also unsuccessful. The patient was diagnosed with R/R DLBCL and received multiple immunochemotherapy regimens without achieving tumor control. At the time, options included novel targeted therapies, immunotherapy agents, or participation in clinical trials for emerging treatments. Ultimately, the patient enrolled in a clinical trial for CD19 CAR-T-cell therapy.

In January 2015, the patient received CD19 CAR-T cell therapy following conditioning with the FC regimen (fludarabine and cyclophosphamide). A total of 1.27×10^8/L cells were infused. On day 7 post-CAR-T, the patient developed grade 1 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) with fever, which resolved within a week with NSAIDs. Transient immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) occurred on day 21 and resolved spontaneously. Four months after CAR-T therapy, PET-CT showed CR (Figure 2(B)).

3. Second relapse and diagnosis of follicular lymphoma

Almost 7 years post-CAR-T therapy, an abdominal ultrasound revealed a mass below the right kidney in the retroperitoneal region. PET-CT showed multiple nodules and masses in the right psoas major and abdominal cavity, with the largest measuring approximately 3.4 × 2.3 cm and an SUVmax of 9.8 with increased metabolic activity (Figure 3(A)). Biopsy of an abdominal lymph node revealed follicular lymphoma (FL), grade 1-2. Given that the Ki-67 index did not exceed 50% at either the initial diagnosis or the first relapse, we consulted pathology experts and considered the possibility that the DLBCL had arisen through transformation of an underlying FL. However, as only a core needle biopsy was initially performed, the possibility that FL tissue was missed cannot be excluded. Previous studies have shown that CREBBP and KMT2D are shared mutations in FL and transformed FL (tFL), whereas TP53 is a tFL-specific target.11,12 The presence of these mutations in our patient, therefore provides additional support for this hypothesis. Furthermore, the patient achieved CR and remained disease-free for almost 7 years following CAR T-cell therapy. Although the overall incidence of B-cell NHL as a second primary malignancy is relatively low, 13 the discordant histological subtypes at the initial diagnosis and at relapse suggest that a second primary malignancy should be carefully considered. Unfortunately, the limited diagnostic material at initial diagnosis and the prolonged archival period meant that we could not obtain sufficient high-quality DNA for reliable molecular analysis, preventing pathological confirmation of this hypothesis.

The presence of a TP53 mutation is associated with chemotherapy resistance and poor prognosis, 14 along with a rapid increase in SUVmax, which strongly suggested disease progression. Therefore, continued observation is not recommended. For patients with stage I or localized stage II disease, involved-site radiotherapy (ISRT) alone remains a viable treatment option, with evidence indicating that first-line radiotherapy achieves superior outcomes compared to first-line systemic therapy. 15 The patient declined further chemotherapy. Bispecific antibodies were unavailable in China at that time, and lenalidomide and BTK inhibitors were unaffordable. Given these constraints and the localized abdominal lesion, we adopted a highly individualized treatment strategy. Hypo-fractionated radiotherapy was combined with a PD-1 inhibitor, aiming to achieve both local tumor control and systemic immune activation. This approach is supported by emerging evidence of the synergistic antitumor effects of radiotherapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including PD-1 inhibitors, particularly in hypo-fractionated radiotherapy.7–9 Additionally, ICIs have shown favorable efficacy in the treatment of FL. 16

In January 2022, the patient underwent hypo-fractionated radiotherapy (30Gy/5f) for the abdominal lesion, combined with immunotherapy using camrelizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets PD-1. Following 2 cycles of camrelizumab, the patient developed “pearl-like” Reactive Cutaneous Capillary Endothelial Proliferation (RCCEP), a known immune-related adverse event associated with favorable prognosis. 17 After 4 cycles, the RCCEP progressed to Grade 2 (cutaneous/mucosal nodules >10 mm with local ulceration/bleeding), leading to drug discontinuation. The lesions gradually resolved after stopping the medication. The patient achieved near CR within three months of radiotherapy. One year later, PET-CT confirmed a CR (SUVmax 2.1, Deauville score 3) (Figure 3(B)). The patient remained disease-free for 43 months from the initiation of radio-immunotherapy (last follow-up in August 2025).

Discussion

Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone for achieving local tumor control, with its primary mechanism involving radiation-induced deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage that triggers tumor cell death through apoptosis, senescence, and autophagy.18–21 Radiotherapy can also remodel the tumor microenvironment, induce the release of cytokines and chemokines, promote leukocyte infiltration, and increase tumor cell susceptibility to immunologic attack.22,23 Studies have shown that local radiotherapy may elicit a reduction in distant, non-irradiated lesions—a phenomenon referred to as the abscopal effect.24–27 This effect is believed to be a systemic antitumor immune response. However, the abscopal effect is rarely observed with radiotherapy alone, likely due to insufficient immune activation to overcome tumor immune evasion mechanisms.

The combination of radiotherapy with ICIs, such as PD-1 inhibitors, has demonstrated synergistic antitumor effects.28–30 Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying the abscopal effect remain incompletely understood. One proposed mechanism is that radiation can facilitate the release of tumor-associated antigens and induce immunogenic cell death (ICD). 31 These newly exposed antigens are taken up by antigen-presenting cells, which migrate to lymph nodes and present the antigens to T cells via the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) and other costimulatory signals (e.g., CD80 and CD28). T cells—especially CD8+ T cells—are subsequently activated and proliferate. Activated effector T cells leave the lymph nodes and home to tumor sites, both irradiated and non-irradiated, to eliminate tumor cells. Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) competes with CD80/86 for binding and inhibits T-cell activation, while PD-1 on the T-cell surface primarily binds programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and suppresses immune responses. ICIs targeting CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1 can therefore enhance the antitumor immune response induced by radiation. 32

Treatment options for patients with R/R B-cell lymphoma are limited, and clinical outcomes remain poor, underscoring a substantial unmet medical need for the development of novel therapeutic regimens in this setting. Augmenting the antitumor immune response through ICIs would be a promising approach in the treatment of R/R B-cell lymphoma. Monotherapy with ICIs shows limited efficacy, 33 whereas combining ICIs with conventional chemotherapy or targeted agents enhances efficacy and is being extensively investigated.34–36 Against this background, the patient received an individualized regimen combining PD-1 inhibitor therapy with radiotherapy and achieved a favorable therapeutic outcome.

When combining radiotherapy with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, neither the optimal radiation dose nor the fractionation schedule has been standardized, especially in the setting of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Most currently available evidence is extrapolated from data on solid tumors. Preclinical studies suggest that in comparison with conventional fractionation (2 Gy × 5 fractions), hypo-fractionation (5 Gy × 3 fractions) better promotes antigen-presenting cell proliferation and activation. Such a regimen may cause less lymphocyte depletion than conventional fractionation, thus having a smaller negative impact on immunotherapy 7 9. When the fraction size exceeds 5 Gy, radiotherapy may indirectly enhance ICD, 37 but higher fraction sizes do not necessarily translate into superior clinical efficacy. Some studies indicate that a single high-dose (15 Gy × 1 fraction) may increase Treg infiltration in the tumor microenvironment, thereby offsetting the local tumor control benefits of radiation, whereas hypo-fractionation (7.5–10 Gy × 2–3 fractions) may maintain lower Treg levels and effectively activate immune responses. 24 In the PEMBRO-RT study, 8 Gy × 3 fractions combined with PD-1 inhibitors for advanced metastatic non-small cell lung cancer showed favorable clinical efficacy, 28 and such dosing did not increase additional immune-related adverse events.

It also remains unclear which sequencing approach—radiotherapy before or concurrent with immunotherapy—optimally enhances the antitumor effect. Although there is no consensus, clinical and preclinical evidence demonstrating abscopal effects suggests that beginning immunotherapy prior to or concurrently with radiotherapy may be preferable.25–27

In this case, the patient had received multiple prior lines of chemotherapy as well as cell-based therapy that led to a long period of remission before eventual progression. At the time of local therapy choice, systemic control was also a concern, and further progression at other sites had to be taken into consideration. Therefore, radiotherapy was combined with immunotherapy. The treatment strategy initiated PD-1 inhibitors prior to radiotherapy, employing a hypo-fractionated dose schedule to achieve local tumor control. No new lesions have since been detected, and due to intolerance of immune checkpoint inhibitor–related toxicities, the patient did not continue immunotherapy long-term.

Conclusion

This case underscores the potential of combining hypo-fractionated radiotherapy with PD-1 inhibitors as a salvage therapy for patients with R/R lymphoma, particularly those with high-risk genetic mutations or intolerance to conventional therapies. As cell-based therapies become more commonplace, the number of such patients may steadily rise. The successful experience of this patient provides valuable insights into the exploration of future treatment paradigms for similar patient populations and merits further clinical investigation.
