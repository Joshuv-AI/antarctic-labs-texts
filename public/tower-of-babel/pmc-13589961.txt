Breaking the Feedback Loop: A Model of Vicious Cycle for CIPN and Acupuncture-Medication Combination
Authors: Jiajing Wang, Yifei Guo, Jiao Gou, Xuan Liu, Xiaorui Wang, Chenguang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a prevalent and often debilitating complication of cancer therapy, whose chronicity and refractoriness cannot be adequately explained by traditional linear etiological models. This review proposes and organizes a “vicious cycle” model as a hypothesis: initial neuronal injury caused by chemotherapeutic agents initiates a self-sustaining positive feedback loop in which mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress reciprocally amplify each other, thereby activating neuroinflammation and ion channel remodeling. These processes collectively drive neuronal hyperexcitability, which further exacerbates mitochondrial damage and inflammatory responses, ultimately establishing a pathological circuit composed of multiple interconnected nodes. Building on this hypothesized framework, the article systematically evaluates current therapeutic strategies. Many conventional pharmacological interventions predominantly focus on specific targets. While some exhibit pleiotropic effects, the difficulty in disrupting the integrated dynamics of the entire cycle may partially explain their limited disease-modifying capabilities. In contrast, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM)—through the synergistic multi-target effects of acupuncture, bioactive herbal constituents, and compound formulations—shows potential to concurrently modulate several critical nodes within the cycle, suggesting promising advantages in holistic regulation, despite the preliminary and heterogeneous nature of current supporting evidence. On this basis, we propose an integrative “acupuncture combined with herbal medicine” strategy: acupuncture may enable rapid modulation of the neuro-immune network, whereas herbal medicine may support sustained repair of molecular pathways, together potentially contributing to disruption of the pathological cycle from microscopic mechanisms to macroscopic phenotypes. This approach advocates a paradigm shift in CIPN management—from symptom control toward breaking the pathological cycle—and offers a novel theoretical framework and integrated therapeutic pathway for further investigation of this persistent clinical challenge.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a common and disabling adverse effect associated with antineoplastic agents such as taxanes, vinca alkaloids, and platinum-based compounds.1–4 Characterized by “glove-and-stocking” paresthesia, neuropathic pain, and motor deficits, CIPN significantly impairs patients’ quality of life and may necessitate dose reduction or discontinuation of chemotherapy, thereby compromising oncological outcomes and long-term survival.5–9 Notably, CIPN can exhibit delayed onset or progressive worsening even after cessation of chemotherapy—a phenomenon known as the “coasting phenomenon”.1,2,10,11 Although many studies conceptualize CIPN as a linear pathological cascade,12–15 and a substantial body of literature has elegantly illuminated the critical roles of glial and neuroimmune signaling in this process, the dynamic integration of these intercellular crosstalk pathways into a comprehensive, self-sustaining “vicious cycle” remains underexplored.16–18 Despite mechanistic heterogeneity across chemotherapeutic classes, diverse agents converge on a similar chronic neuropathic state, yet the unifying pathological circuitry underlying this convergence remains poorly defined.13,19 To date, no pharmacological agent has demonstrated consistent efficacy for the primary prevention of CIPN.8,14,20

This review aims to synthesize current evidence on CIPN pathogenesis and construct a dynamic “vicious cycle” model that spans from initial injury to chronic maintenance, integrating fragmented pathological observations into a coherent, interactive circuit. Based on this model, we systematically analyze the mechanisms and limitations of existing interventions from both modern Western medicine and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), ultimately proposing a multi-target synergistic strategy designed to disrupt the self-perpetuating loop central to CIPN progression.

For this narrative review, we searched PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang Data for articles published from database inception up to March 2026. Search terms included combinations of “chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy,” “CIPN,” “vicious cycle,” “mitochondrial dysfunction,” “oxidative stress,” “ion channel,” “neuroinflammation,” “acupuncture,” “traditional Chinese medicine,” and “herbal medicine.” We also manually screened reference lists of retrieved articles and relevant reviews to identify additional studies. We included peer-reviewed original research, clinical trials, and systematic reviews that addressed the mechanisms, prevention, or treatment of CIPN, with a focus on literature published in English or Chinese. We excluded conference abstracts, case reports, and studies not directly related to CIPN pathophysiology or intervention.

Construction of the “Vicious Cycle” Model for the Pathogenesis of CIPN

Convergent Amplifier: Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Energy Crisis

The initiating mechanisms of CIPN differ across chemotherapeutic classes. Taxanes primarily disrupt microtubule dynamics and axonal transport, whereas platinum-based agents induce DNA adduct formation in dorsal root ganglion neurons. Despite this mechanistic heterogeneity, accumulating evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction may represent a convergent downstream event that contributes to the amplification and maintenance of neuropathic pathology across multiple forms of CIPN. Multiple first-line chemotherapeutic agents accumulate preferentially in peripheral nerve tissues, where they disrupt mitochondrial morphology and bioenergetic capacity.21 For instance, cisplatin and carboplatin induce elevated mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in Schwann cells, reduce mitochondrial membrane potential, and impair ATP synthesis, culminating in demyelination characterized by downregulation of myelin protein zero (MPZ) and upregulation of p75NTR.22 Beyond bioenergetic failure, imbalance in mitochondrial dynamics constitutes another hallmark feature: chemotherapeutics perturb the equilibrium between fusion and fission. Evidence from copper overload models indicates that neurotoxic insults suppress fusion by downregulating Opa1, Mfn1, and Mfn2, while promoting fission via Drp1 upregulation, leading to pathological mitochondrial fragmentation.23

PGC-1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, plays a pivotal role in preserving mitochondrial integrity, protecting peripheral nerves, and mitigating CIPN.24 Additionally, therapy-induced senescence (TIS) has emerged as a systemic mechanism contributing to CIPN; the TIS phenotype observed in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) closely parallels mitochondrial dysfunction, suggesting that TIS-associated mitochondrial decline may serve as a persistent driver of neural injury following chemotherapy.25 Thus, mitochondrial impairment may function not only as a bioenergetic disturbance but also as an important amplifier of downstream pathological cascades in CIPN.

Amplification Stage I: The Cascade of Oxidative Stress and DNA Damage

Mitochondrial dysfunction is closely associated with excessive ROS generation due to electron transport chain leakage, triggering profound oxidative stress.26 This not only perpetuates mitochondrial injury—establishing a positive feedback loop—but also inflicts widespread damage on cellular DNA, proteins, and lipids. In cisplatin-induced models, activation of p38 MAPK has been identified as a key upstream event driving both oxidative stress and mitochondrial impairment; pharmacological inhibition of p38 MAPK markedly attenuates ROS production and confers neuroprotection.27

ROS can induce single- or double-strand DNA breaks, activating DNA damage response pathways that, if unresolved, culminate in apoptosis. Concurrently, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress—frequently co-occurring with oxidative stress—is increasingly recognized as a contributor to CIPN pathogenesis. Sustained activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) promotes neuronal apoptosis, whereas phytochemicals exert neuroprotective effects partly by alleviating ER stress, underscoring a tight interplay between oxidative and ER stress.28 Flavonoids, for example, ameliorate CIPN largely through their potent antioxidant activity, which inhibits ROS-mediated neuronal injury and cell death.29 Collectively, these findings indicate that oxidative stress functions not only as a consequence of mitochondrial damage but also as an independent amplifier of DNA damage and apoptosis, thereby constituting the first positive feedback loop in the vicious cycle.

Amplification Stage II: Ion Channel Remodeling and Abnormal Neuronal Excitability

As oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage intensify, sensory neurons undergo ion channel remodeling, leading to abnormal neuronal excitability—the electrophysiological substrate of CIPN symptoms. Chemotherapeutic agents alter the kinetics of voltage-gated sodium, calcium, and potassium channels, resulting in increased ectopic discharges and spontaneous activity.30 Compromised ATP supply from dysfunctional mitochondria impairs Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, lowering the threshold for membrane depolarization and promoting a hyperexcitable state. Flavonoids alleviate thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia by suppressing ion channel activation and ectopic firing.29 Moreover, topical capsaicin nanocrystal gel modulates TRPV1 channel activity, significantly improving mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity while reducing neuroinflammatory markers, highlighting ion channel modulation as a strategic node for interrupting pain signaling.31 Dysregulated ion channels also disrupt calcium homeostasis; mitochondrial calcium overload can trigger opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, exacerbating cellular injury.32 In neurological disorders, calcium-induced collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential is a core pathological mechanism, and this process similarly operates in CIPN: ion channel–mediated calcium influx further burdens mitochondria, creating a second self-reinforcing feedback loop.33

Amplification Stage III: Neuroinflammation and Immune Cell Activation

Neuroinflammation serves as a critical bridge linking peripheral nerve injury to central sensitization within the CIPN “vicious cycle”. Chemotherapeutic agents not only directly damage neurons but also activate resident immune and glial cells in both the peripheral and central nervous systems. These activated cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines—including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6—creating a neurotoxic microenvironment that further injures neurons.30 Glatiramer acetate, for example, significantly attenuates paclitaxel-induced neuropathy by suppressing TNF-α and NF-κB while elevating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of inflammation modulation.34 Similarly, a composite material derived from turmeric rhizome and nano-hydroxyapatite (TRE@NHA) exerts anti-CIPN effects partly by inhibiting cytokine production and reducing oxidative stress, illustrating the synergy between anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions.35 Mesenchymal stem cell (MSCs) therapy also demonstrates promise by reducing neuroinflammation and apoptosis via paracrine signaling, thereby promoting axonal regeneration and highlighting the regenerative potential of immune modulation.36 Importantly, inflammatory mediators not only harm neurons but also reciprocally induce mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, forming a third positive feedback loop.

Core Mechanism: Amplification Effect of Intercellular Signal Interaction on Vicious Cycle

The CIPN “vicious cycle” does not operate in neuronal isolation but emerges from a complex network of intercellular signaling interactions that amplify damage signals. Neurons injured by chemotherapy release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate glial cells; in turn, these glia produce ROS and cytokines that feed back onto neurons, exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction and ion channel remodeling.29,37

Furthermore, the gut microbiota–brain axis plays a non-negligible role. Chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis may reduce microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), thereby diminishing their inhibitory influence on neuroinflammation and worsening neuropathy.38 Disruption of metabolic coupling between Schwann cells and axons—driven by mitochondrial failure—also accelerates axonal degeneration.22 These cross-cellular communication pathways form an expansive feedback network that stabilizes the vicious cycle, rendering it resistant to conventional interventions. However, it is important to explicitly note that the incorporation of the gut microbiota-brain axis into this CIPN vicious cycle is currently speculative. Because direct evidence linking short-chain fatty acids and microbiota dysbiosis specifically to CIPN remains sparse, this node is largely an extrapolation drawn from other neurodegenerative contexts (such as Parkinson’s disease). Therefore, its exact structural role within the CIPN cycle should be viewed as an emerging hypothesis requiring further direct experimental validation in chemotherapy-specific models.

Interactions Between Various Links and the Formation of a Vicious Cycle

The pathogenesis of CIPN converges into a tightly interconnected “vicious cycle”: diverse initial insults converge to induce mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to reduced ATP synthesis and a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS); excessive ROS triggers oxidative stress, causing DNA damage and lipid peroxidation while concurrently activating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress; energy depletion and oxidative injury drive ion channel remodeling, resulting in neuronal hyperexcitability and ectopic discharges; damaged neurons release signaling molecules that activate neuroinflammation, and activated glial and immune cells secrete additional ROS and inflammatory mediators, which further aggravate mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress. This self-amplifying process is reinforced through intercellular crosstalk, ultimately culminating in irreversible axonal degeneration and chronic neuropathic pain.37,39 Within this proposed framework, these interacting processes may be conceptually summarized as: mitochondrial dysfunction - oxidative stress and DNA damage - neuronal hyperexcitability - neuroinflammation (as shown in Figure 1). The “vicious cycle” model not only accounts for the “coasting phenomenon” but also provides a mechanistic rationale for developing multi-target, disease-modifying therapies. Effective disruption of this self-sustaining loop is essential to transition from symptomatic palliation to true disease modification. The three amplification loops described above—oxidative stress, ion channel remodeling, and neuroinflammation—do not occur in a strictly sequential order. Rather, they operate in parallel and mutually reinforce one another in a temporally dynamic manner. Although oxidative stress may be the earliest amplifier to emerge following mitochondrial injury, ion channel remodeling and neuroinflammation can develop concurrently or successively and may sustain the cycle even after the chemotherapeutic agent has been withdrawn.

The proposed model also offers a potential mechanistic explanation for the coasting phenomenon—the observation that CIPN can progress or emerge after chemotherapy has been discontinued. Once the initial chemotherapeutic insult has triggered the self-sustaining feedback loops (eg, mitochondrial dysfunction promoting oxidative stress, which further damages mitochondria; neuroinflammation generating cytokines that reinforce neuronal hyperexcitability, which in turn sustains glial activation), these loops may become independent of the original trigger. Consequently, the cycle can persist and even amplify after the chemotherapeutic agent has been cleared from the body. This interpretation, however, remains hypothetical and requires direct experimental testing.

Mechanism and Limitations of Modern Western Medicine Treatment Based on the “Vicious Cycle” Model

Drugs Targeting Mitochondrial Protection and Antioxidant Stress

PFT-μ specifically inhibits p53 translocation to mitochondria, thereby preserving mitochondrial membrane potential.40 PGC-1α, as a central regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, significantly ameliorates oxidative stress and sustains mitochondrial function upon activation.24 Vinpocetine enhances mitochondrial biogenesis via the PGC-1α–NRF1–TFAM pathway, reducing mitochondrial ROS generation.41 Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) exhibits robust antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, while acetyl-L-carnitine (LAC) shows notable efficacy in patients receiving platinum-based regimens.42 Antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine, lipoic acid, and vitamin E suppress ROS production and lipid peroxidation in the spinal cord.43 Ozone therapy and specific plant extracts (eg, ethanol extract from immature citrus peel) indirectly modulate oxidative stress by enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses or inhibiting enzymes like NOX2.44–46

Ion Channel Modulators and Their Efficacy Analysis

Meclizine and metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR) agonists—particularly mGluR8 and Group II mGluR agonists—alleviate hyperexcitability in DRG neurons by modulating calcium homeostasis.47 The 8% capsaicin patch exerts analgesic effects through TRPV1 receptor desensitization and demonstrates potential disease-modifying capabilities. A single application significantly reduces both spontaneous and evoked pain in CIPN patients, and skin biopsy reveals regeneration and restoration of epidermal nerve fiber density, suggesting promotion of sensory nerve repair.48 Mitragynine, by activating α2-adrenergic receptors and modulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, offers insights into the cross-regulation between ion channels and neurotransmitter systems.49

Targeted Effects of Anti-Inflammatory and Immunomodulatory Drugs

Duloxetine functions not only as a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor but also exerts anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects via inhibition of the p38 MAPK/NF-κB pathway, which may underlie its clinical efficacy in CIPN.50,51 Monoclonal antibodies targeting MMP9 or its endogenous inhibitor TIMP1 reduce levels of IL-6 and TNF-α and prevent intraepidermal nerve fiber loss.52 Additional strategies include targeting specific inflammatory mediators such as MMP9 and IRE1α, as well as supplementing with omega-3 fatty acids, all aimed at precisely inhibiting glial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production (eg, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β).52–54 Activation of the Sonic hedgehog signaling pathway confers neuroprotection through mechanisms including anti-senescence effects.55

Applications and Mechanisms of Neurotrophic Factors

The p75NTR modulator LM11A-31 and the novel compound CN016 show significant efficacy in preclinical models: the former normalizes neurotrophic signaling, while the latter suppresses macrophage-mediated neuroinflammation.56 The humanized NGF monoclonal antibody DS002, by blocking NGF binding to TrkA, validates neurotrophic factor modulation as a viable therapeutic approach for CIPN.57 Meanwhile, studies on mitragynine indicate that neurotrophic factors such as BDNF can exert dual effects, emphasizing the need for precise regulatory control.49

Limitations of Existing Western Drug Treatments and the “Off-Target” Phenomenon

Despite substantial advances in mechanistic understanding and preclinical validation, translating these findings into effective clinical therapies remains challenging. A major challenge in current pharmacological management is disrupting the integrated “vicious cycle” of CIPN (as shown in Figure 2). It is important to note that not all conventional therapies are strictly single-target; for instance, agents like duloxetine and mesenchymal stem cells exhibit pleiotropic effects that modulate multiple pathways simultaneously. However, many standard small-molecule drugs predominantly act on specific nodes. For example, antioxidants such as ALA or LAC may alleviate oxidative stress, but without concurrent modulation of ion channel remodeling or neuroinflammation, symptom relief is often partial. Clinical trials have yielded inconsistent results, potentially due to variations in administration routes or chemotherapy regimens.42 Furthermore, many agents lack disease-modifying capabilities or exhibit “off-target” effects. Gabapentin, pregabalin, and tricyclic antidepressants primarily provide symptomatic relief without halting neurodegeneration.39 Although capsaicin patches show some neuroregenerative potential, most small-molecule drugs fail to achieve structural repair.48 The differential efficacy of LAC—effective in platinum-treated but not paclitaxel-treated patients—suggests distinct underlying mechanisms across chemotherapeutic classes, implying that a universal single-target strategy is unlikely to succeed.42 Timing also presents a critical barrier: interventions targeting the eIF4E complex may be effective early, but delayed treatment risks irreversible neurodegeneration,58 multi-target combination strategies grounded in the “vicious cycle” model are urgently needed to enable a shift from symptom control to disease modification. Analysis of the Mechanisms of Multi-Target Intervention in CIPN by Traditional Chinese Medicine

Regulation and Prevention of CIPN via Multiple Targets Through Acupuncture Therapy

Acupuncture is the most widely employed non-pharmacological intervention in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) for CIPN and demonstrates clear advantages in multi-target modulation. A multicenter randomized controlled trial involving 168 CIPN patients found that acupuncture significantly improved FACT-Tax scores, particularly for symptoms such as hand numbness, tingling, and local discomfort.59 Multiple studies confirm that acupuncture-related modalities—including manual needling, electroacupuncture, and transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation—effectively alleviate clinical symptoms, enhance nerve conduction velocity, and improve quality of life in CIPN patients, with favorable safety profiles and no serious adverse events.60–76

Mechanistic investigations reveal that electroacupuncture exerts neuroprotective effects by inhibiting the thalamic paraventricular nucleus TRPC6/PKC pathway, reducing calcium influx, suppressing aberrant glutamatergic neuron activation, and reversing loss of intraepidermal nerve fibers and Merkel cells.77 This mechanism directly targets the ion channel remodeling and neuronal hyperexcitability components of the CIPN vicious cycle. Additionally, acupuncture alleviates pain through modulation of the endogenous opioid system, enhances sensory nerve function via BDNF-mediated neural plasticity, and regulates both systemic and local inflammatory responses.61

Acupoint selection patterns: Data mining analyses identify Zusanli (ST36), Quchi (LI11), Hegu (LI4), Taichong (LR3), and Sanyinjiao (SP6) as core acupoints for CIPN treatment, supplemented by Bafeng (EX-LE10) and Baxie (EX-UE9) for distal symptoms. This reflects foundational TCM principles of “treating flaccidity by focusing on the Yangming channels” and “selecting acupoints locally”.66,78,79

Preventive application: Electroacupuncture initiated one day before chemotherapy and maintained throughout the treatment course demonstrates prophylactic potential. In a randomized controlled trial, the incidence of CIPN at 24 weeks was only 17.4% in the electroacupuncture group versus 72% in the conventional care group, with no cases of high-grade CIPN in the intervention arm,80 However, these dramatic preventive effects must be interpreted with explicit caution. This finding derives from a relatively small trial lacking a sham-acupuncture control and rigorous blinding, which carries a high risk of performance and detection bias. Further large-scale, sham-controlled trials are required to confirm whether acupuncture truly mitigates initial neuronal injury to this extent.

Molecular Mechanisms and Multi-Target Effects of Active Components in Traditional Chinese Medicine

The active ingredients in traditional Chinese medicine hold potential for combating oxidative damage, reducing inflammation, and providing neuroprotection, which aligns with the multi-mechanism pathogenesis of CIPN.81–83 Numerous clinical studies support the use of blood-activating and collateral-dredging herbs such as Salvia miltiorrhiza and Ligusticum chuanxiong for CIPN prevention and treatment. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that such TCM formulations significantly reduce the incidence of both all-grade and high-grade CIPN (total effective rate: 77.19% vs 45.79% in controls) and markedly improve sensory and motor nerve conduction velocities.84

Topical interventions such as herbal fumigation and external application significantly improve clinical symptoms, increase Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scores, reduce pain Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) scores, and alleviate TCM syndrome patterns. They also enhance nerve conduction velocity in the common peroneal and median nerves. With low publication bias and robust evidence, these approaches represent safe and effective therapeutic options.85 Wenluotong (WLT), a topical TCM formulation used clinically for over a decade, demonstrates clear efficacy in CIPN management. Metabolomic studies suggest that WLT alleviates mechanical hypersensitivity in paclitaxel-induced CIPN rats by modulating linoleic acid and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways. Key active components—including hydroxysafflor yellow A, icariin, epimedin B, and dihydroxybenzoic acid—are predicted by molecular docking to have favorable binding affinities to core proteins in these pathways, thereby reversing CIPN-related metabolic dysregulation.86

Nevertheless, these efficacy figures should not be viewed uncritically. The “total effective rate” is a notoriously soft composite endpoint. Furthermore, many of the included primary studies are small-scale trials with recognized methodological flaws, including inadequate blinding and the absence of placebo controls. Consequently, these results carry a significant risk of bias and merely suggest potential efficacy rather than definitive proof.

The Regulatory Effect of Traditional Chinese Medicine Compounds on the Overall Microenvironment

Classic TCM compound prescriptions adhere to the monarch-minister-assistant-envoy compatibility theory. Through synergistic interactions among multiple constituents, they holistically regulate the patient’s pathological state, improve the local microenvironment at sites of nerve injury, and disrupt the CIPN vicious cycle at a systemic level. Recent advances in network pharmacology and experimental research have predicted potential targets for the molecular mechanisms of these formulas in CIPN treatment.

Huangqi Guizhi Wuwu Tang (Astragalus and Cinnamon Twig Five-Substance Decoction) is a classical TCM formula for blood impediment syndrome and is now widely applied in CIPN management. Network pharmacology predicts 63 active components in this decoction that interact with 41 core CIPN-related targets, primarily through modulation of inflammatory responses and nerve repair. The study also identified two novel potential targets—CDK7 and GSTM2—through computational prediction, highlighting the multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway nature of TCM compound prescriptions.87 Subsequent in vitro experiments confirmed that medicated serum from Huangqi Guizhi Wuwu Tang significantly enhances the survival of cisplatin-injured PC12 cells. Transcriptomic profiling further revealed upregulation of the axon guidance pathway, indicating promotion of axonal repair during the early phase of neuronal injury in CIPN.88

Taohong Siwu Decoction, a classic blood-activating and stasis-resolving formula, also demonstrates clinical efficacy in CIPN. Network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses predict 69 active components acting on 105 shared CIPN targets, including MYC, TNF, MAPK14, AKT1, ESR1, RELA, TP53, and HSP90AA1. Enriched pathways involve PI3K-Akt, NF-κB, and HIF-1 signaling—processes closely linked to inflammation and neuroprotection. Molecular docking confirms strong binding affinity between selected components and core targets, suggesting the molecular basis for its blood-activating and meridian-unblocking effects in CIPN treatment.89

However, it is crucial to emphasize that network pharmacology and molecular docking only provide static computational predictions. The actual in vivo bioavailability of these complex multi-component formulas, and their precise pharmacokinetic profiles within the peripheral nervous system, remain to be rigorously validated by future pharmacological studies.

Analysis of Key Intervention Points of Traditional Chinese Medicine in the “Vicious Cycle”

Mapping TCM interventions onto the “vicious cycle” model reveals precise targeting of critical nodes: at the stage of initial neuronal injury and mitochondrial dysfunction, TCM compound formulas exert protective effects that mitigate early damage.87,88 Electroacupuncture can even prevent CIPN onset, substantially reducing incidence.80 The antioxidant properties of TCM active components and formulas directly counteract the oxidative stress–DNA damage amplification cascade.80–82 Acupuncture effectively modulates ion channel remodeling and neuronal hyperexcitability77 and further reduces excitability via the endogenous opioid system to alleviate pain.61 Both acupuncture and TCM also target neuroinflammation and immune activation: acupuncture modulates systemic and local inflammatory responses, lowering pro-inflammatory cytokines,61 while TCM formulas inhibit inflammatory pathways to limit ongoing neuronal injury.87,89 Moreover, intercellular signaling amplification—central to cycle maintenance—is addressed by TCM: blood-activating and collateral-dredging formulas enhance local perfusion and nutrient delivery, improving the neural microenvironment;84,85 acupuncture promotes recovery of nerve conduction, thereby dampening damage signal propagation.90 Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) employs synergistic multi-component, multi-method, and multi-target strategies that simultaneously engage multiple critical nodes of the vicious cycle. This integrative capacity constitutes the core therapeutic advantage of TCM in CIPN management71,72,91,92 (as shown in Figure 3). However, it is essential to explicitly clarify that the claim of “simultaneous” multi-node modulation is currently a composite inference synthesized from separate studies evaluating isolated endpoints across different models. At the same time, it is important to contextualize the current evidence within major oncology guidelines. For instance, the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines currently recommend duloxetine as the only agent with sufficient evidence for treating established CIPN, while rating the evidence for acupuncture and botanical therapies as inconclusive. Although this article cites numerous studies that reported positive results, these findings have not led to any changes in the guidelines, primarily because these trials suffered from methodological flaws such as small sample sizes, the absence of sham acupuncture controls, and a lack of independent replication of the findings.

Furthermore, a balanced perspective must acknowledge that the multi-target nature of TCM is not without significant limitations. While acting on multiple nodes provides holistic regulation, it inherently implies lower pharmacological specificity, complex pharmacokinetic interactions, and greater uncertainty in identifying the exact mechanisms of action. The difficulty in standardizing active dosages within compound formulas remains a major hurdle. Future research should prioritize large-sample, multicenter, rigorously designed randomized controlled trials to establish robust efficacy data and optimize intervention protocols for broader clinical implementation.63,72,84,91

When advocating for the concurrent use of TCM during active cancer treatment, the potential for negative herb-drug interactions must be critically addressed. A paramount scientific concern is whether the potent antioxidant and anti-apoptotic properties of certain herbal medicines might inadvertently blunt the pro-oxidant, cytotoxic mechanisms that chemotherapeutic agents rely on for tumor eradication. Additionally, complex herbal formulas may interfere with the pharmacokinetics of chemotherapy drugs via cytochrome P450 enzyme induction or inhibition, potentially altering drug efficacy or exacerbating systemic toxicity. Although several short-term trials cited in this review report that TCM interventions do not compromise chemotherapy efficacy, these studies are rarely adequately powered to assess long-term oncological outcomes. In addition, no cited study has incorporated long-term oncological outcomes such as cancer recurrence or overall survival. Short-term safety and symptom relief do not equate to confirmation of non-interference with anti-tumor mechanisms. Therefore, future research must rigorously monitor tumor response rates, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) to definitively confirm that neuroprotective TCM strategies do not simultaneously protect the tumor.

The Synergistic Strategy of “Acupuncture Combined with Medication” to Break the Vicious Cycle

This paper proposes “acupuncture combined with traditional Chinese medicine” as a theoretical framework and develops corresponding synergistic strategies in the hope of breaking this closed loop; it must be clearly stated that this integrated therapy is currently still a hypothesis based on deduction rather than a clinically validated strategy, and that this approach embodies a deep integration of the holistic perspective of traditional Chinese medicine with modern systems biology.

Theoretical Basis of Acupuncture and Medicine Synergy

On the neurophysiological dimension, acupuncture rapidly suppresses ectopic discharges by modulating neuronal excitability (eg, via the TRPC6/PKC pathway)77 and activates the endogenous opioid system for immediate analgesia;61 meanwhile, herbal medicine persistently repairs mitochondrial function, stabilizes ion channel expression, and provides neurotrophic support (eg, Huangqi Guizhi Wuwu Tang promotes axonal regeneration).88 Thus, acupuncture addresses acute symptoms while herbal medicine consolidates structural recovery, embodying the TCM principle of “treating symptoms in acute stages and addressing the root cause in chronic stages”.

On the immunological dimension, the combination regulates both systemic and local inflammation by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (eg, TNF-α, IL-1β);61 acupuncture rapidly modulates immune cell trafficking and inhibits inflammatory cascades such as NF-κB and PI3K-Akt, while herbal formulas like Taohong Siwu Decoction suppress glial activation.88 Together, they establish a sustained anti-inflammatory microenvironment—acupuncture interrupts inflammatory feedback acutely, whereas TCM maintains long-term immune homeostasis to prevent relapse.

At the metabolic level, the strategy enhances local microcirculation and neural perfusion, potentially via autonomic nervous regulation; herbal medicine (eg, Wenluotong) corrects CIPN-associated metabolic disturbances by modulating linoleic acid and glycerophospholipid pathways,85 while acupuncture improves the metabolic milieu. This dual action synergistically restores metabolic homeostasis and mitigates oxidative stress.

These three complementary mechanisms converge on a unified objective: disrupting the positive feedback amplification nodes that sustain the vicious cycle.

To comprehensively break the CIPN cycle, the acupuncture-medication combination achieves precise complementation through a “spatiotemporal synergy” mechanism.

Temporally, acupuncture acts as an immediate interventional trigger. By rapidly modulating transient receptor potential (TRP) channels and local neuro-immune cross-talk, acupuncture provides swift suppression of neuronal hyperexcitability and acute pain signals. In contrast, herbal medicine (such as Huangqi Guizhi Wuwu Decoction) acts as a sustained modifier. It gradually accumulates active compounds to activate long-term survival and repair pathways, such as the PI3K/Akt signaling cascade, promoting continuous mitochondrial restoration and axonal regeneration.

Spatially, acupuncture effectively targets specific regional reflex arcs and systemic neuro-endocrine axes (eg, the gut-brain axis). Meanwhile, oral herbal formulations exert systemic pharmacological effects, improving systemic microcirculation and delivering active multi-target molecules directly to the damaged dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and peripheral nerve microenvironment. This precise temporal (rapid vs sustained) and spatial (network-axis vs systemic-molecular) complementation creates a robust synergistic blockade against the vicious cycle.

Indirect Experimental Evidence of Acupuncture and Medication Synergy

Acupuncture combined with oral herbal medicine represents a classical TCM paradigm grounded in the principle of “simultaneous regulation of qi and blood and unblocking of meridians”. In a rat model of cerebral infarction, this combination significantly increased intestinal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) while reducing serum IL-17 levels, with synergistic effects surpassing monotherapy. Correlation analysis revealed a strong negative association between SCFAs and IL-17, suggesting that acupuncture–herb synergy may powerfully suppress neuroinflammation via the gut–brain axis or related systemic immune pathways.93

The integration of acupuncture with modern Western medicine is a growing trend in CIPN management. Multiple randomized controlled trials confirm its efficacy: in multiple myeloma patients with CIPN, acupuncture plus mecobalamin significantly outperformed mecobalamin alone in pain relief (VAS score), quality of life (FACT/GOG-Ntx score), and nerve conduction velocity (NCV) improvement.94 Another trial similarly showed that the combination markedly reduced NCI-CTCAE toxicity grades, improved EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 scores, and enhanced nerve conduction function, with an excellent safety profile.95

However, it must be explicitly acknowledged that direct experimental or clinical evidence proving that acupuncture combined specifically with herbal medicine breaks the CIPN cycle is currently lacking. The studies cited above—utilizing a cerebral infarction model and integrating acupuncture with a Western agent—serve only as indirect, mechanistic proof-of-concept extrapolations. While they provide a proof-of-concept for the benefits of combination therapy, they do not constitute direct evidence for the acupuncture-herb synergy proposed in our model. This highlights a critical gap in current research.

Advantages of Acupuncture-Pharmacotherapy Synergy in Targeting Each Node of the Malignant Cycle

Acupuncture alone cannot directly restore mitochondrial structure, and herbal medicine alone acts slowly. However, their combination synergizes: acupuncture enhances local perfusion and energy metabolism, while herbal formulas (eg, Huangqi Guizhi Wuwu Tang) boost mitochondrial biogenesis, accelerating energy recovery. Antioxidants like ALA offer limited ROS clearance; in contrast, acupuncture upregulates endogenous antioxidant enzymes, and herbal medicine (eg, Wenluotong) scavenges free radicals and corrects metabolic imbalances, establishing a dual antioxidant defense. Ion channel modulators such as gabapentin alleviate symptoms without nerve repair; acupuncture rapidly suppresses ectopic firing, while herbal medicine stabilizes channel expression and promotes axonal regeneration, achieving “rapid symptom control + long-term repair”. Anti-inflammatory agents like duloxetine may interfere with chemotherapy; acupuncture provides localized anti-inflammatory effects without systemic interference, and herbal formulas (eg, Taohong Siwu Tang) inhibit the NF-κB pathway, jointly reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines without compromising anti-tumor immunity. Critically, in the context of cross-amplifying feedback loops, single-target approaches cannot simultaneously block multiple pathways. Conclusion and Outlook

This review systematically integrates the complex pathophysiology of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) and constructs a proposed “positive feedback vicious cycle” model as a testable hypothesis. Based on this model, we critically evaluate current interventions. While many conventional pharmacological treatments offer crucial symptomatic relief and some possess multi-pathway effects, fully dismantling the interdependent feedback nodes remains a significant clinical hurdle. In contrast, traditional Chinese medicine—through the synergistic actions of acupuncture, bioactive herbal constituents, and compound formulas—has the potential to simultaneously engage multiple critical links in the cycle. We further propose an integrative “acupuncture combined with herbal medicine” strategy as a research agendarequiring future investigation, theoretically leveraging acupuncture’s rapid regulation of the neuro-immune network alongside herbal medicine’s enduring repair of molecular pathways to achieve disruption from microscopic mechanisms to macroscopic phenotypes. This paradigm offers a novel theoretical foundation for the integrated management of CIPN, but it requires rigorous experimental and clinical validation before it can be considered a proven therapeutic approach.

Future Research Directions and Clinical Translation Pathways

Several Concrete Research Priorities and Testable Predictions Emerge From This Framework:

First, direct experimental evidence is urgently needed to test the hypothesis that acupuncture combined with herbal medicine can simultaneously modulate multiple nodes of the proposed cycle in CIPN-specific models. Existing synergy data are derived from stroke models and from trials pairing acupuncture with mecobalamin rather than herbal medicine; thus, dedicated CIPN studies are required to establish direct causality.

Second, we propose testable predictions regarding the quantitative “threshold” and “dose-response” relationships between key nodes. For instance, identifying the specific concentration of inflammatory cytokines required to significantly alter ion channel expression, or the extent of oxidative stress needed to trigger mitochondrial permeability transition, would transform the current qualitative model into a more precise, predictive framework.

Third, the existence of a potential “main switch” node—whose modulation may produce downstream effects surpassing those of other individual nodes—warrants systematic investigation. If such a master regulatory node is identified, it could serve as the most efficient therapeutic entry point for intervention.

Fourth, regarding study design, future clinical trials must strictly adopt multi-center, sham-controlled (for acupuncture), and placebo-controlled (for herbal medicine) designs. Standardized and validated endpoints, alongside adequate sample sizes, are essential to address the methodological shortcomings of the current evidence base. Furthermore, longitudinal oncological outcomes, including cancer recurrence and overall survival, must be incorporated to definitively rule out any interference with anti-tumor efficacy.

In conclusion, the vicious cycle model provides an integrative framework that reframes CIPN as a dynamic, self-sustaining system rather than a linear cascade. This reconceptualization directs future research toward multi-target, disease-modifying strategies. Ultimately, the acupuncture-herbal combination presents a conceptually promising but strictly hypothesis-driven strategy that awaits rigorous empirical validation.
