Association Between Motor Pathway Damage and Motor Deficit in Upper and Lower Limb in People With MS
Authors: Mathilde Liffran, Malo Gaubert, Alice Dufey, Elise Bannier, Audrey Rico, Jean‐Christophe Ferré, Raphaël Chouteau, Laure Michel, Emmanuelle Le Page, Virginie Callot, Bertrand Audoin, Sarah Demortière, Benoît Combès, Anne Kerbrat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCorticospinal tract damage is common in people with MS, but the degree of clinical symptoms varies. We hypothesize that corticospinal tract lesions are more extensive and severe in people with MS with motor impairments in both upper and lower limbs. We compared three groups of patients: isolated hyperreflexia (A), motor deficits limited to the lower limbs (B), or in both upper and lower limbs (C).MethodsWe included 100 people with MS in a prospective cross‐sectional study. Corticospinal tract lesions were segmented on 3D FLAIR (brain) and axial T2* and T2‐weighted images (cervical and thoracic spinal cord). MP2RAGE quantitative T1 was used to assess microstructural damage severity in the brain and cervical spinal cord corticospinal tract.ResultsNinety‐two people with MS were included in the analysis (A = 38, B = 31, C = 23). Lesion volume was higher in groups B and C than in group A in the brainstem and cervical corticospinal tract (all p < 0.001), and quantitative T1 was higher only in the cervical corticospinal tract (all p < 0.05). In the multivariate analysis, age, disease duration, lesion volume in the brainstem, and quantitative T1 in the cervical spinal cord distinguished group A from groups B and C, but no differentiating factors were identified between groups B and C.InterpretationOur study suggests that motor deficit in pwMS was associated with brainstem and cervical spinal cord corticospinal tract damage. Differences between pwMS with lower limb deficits and those with both lower and upper limb deficits could not be established.

Introduction

Motor impairments are prevalent in people with multiple sclerosis (pwMS), significantly contributing to disability in day‐to‐day activities. However, the severity of motor symptoms varies among patients. From a clinical point of view, some exhibit a pyramidal reflex syndrome without severe functional consequences, others present motor deficits limited to the lower limbs, and some are affected in both upper and lower limbs. Notably, progressive forms of MS are most often characterized by a worsening pyramidal syndrome involving the lower limbs, and to a lesser extent, the upper limbs. The anatomical basis for this variation—why some patients develop motor deficits in both upper and lower limbs while others do not—is not yet well understood. It would benefit from a comprehensive exploration of the entire corticospinal tract (CST), the main motor pathway.

To date, imaging of the entire CST from the motor cortex to the bottom of the spinal cord in pwMS has been limited. This is mainly due to the extensive and thus time‐consuming acquisition required to cover the entire central nervous system, the spinal cord size, and its susceptibility to MRI artifacts [1]. Consequently, previous studies have primarily focused on the brain portion of the CST [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8], while the spinal cord portion—an important prognostic factor [9]—has been less studied [10, 11, 12, 13]. However, recent advancements in spinal cord MRI have made it possible to reconstruct the motor pathways from the primary motor cortex to the lower spinal cord, allowing for improved quantification of lesions and microstructural damage.

In the present study, we hypothesize that the extent of CST lesions and microstructural damages is more pronounced in pwMS experiencing motor impairments in both the upper and lower limbs than in those with impairments limited to the lower limbs, which in turn is more pronounced than in pwMS with isolated pyramidal reflex syndrome. To test this hypothesis, we compared three groups of patients with clinical evidence of motor tract damage: those with a pyramidal syndrome but without motor deficits (group A), those with motor deficits limited to the lower limbs (group B), and those with deficits in both the upper and lower limbs (group C).

Methods

Study Design

We performed a retrospective analysis including patients prospectively enrolled in the MAPMS and MSTRACTS cohorts (NCT04220814 and NCT04918225). The study design is summarized in Figure 1 and details are provided in the following sections.

Study design. 3D FLAIR = three‐dimensional fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, 9HPT = nine‐hole peg test, ASIA = American Spinal Injury Association, C2 = second cervical vertebra level, C3 = third cervical vertebra level, CSA = cross sectional area, CST = corticospinal tract, EDSS = expanded disability status scale, MNI = Montreal Neurological Institute, MP2RAGE = magnetization‐prepared 2 rapid gradient‐echo, PAM = Polytechnique, Aix‐Marseille University and Montreal Neurological Institute, PPMS = primary progressive multiple sclerosis, pyramidal FSS = pyramidal functional system score, qT1 = quantitative T1, RRMS = remitting–relapsing multiple sclerosis, SPMS = secondary progressive multiple sclerosis, T1w = T1 weighted, T25‐FW = timed 25‐ft walk, TSE = turbo spin echo.

Participants

The study included 100 pwMS from the MS‐TRACTS cohort (43 from Rennes center and 17 from Marseille center) and MAP‐MS cohort (40 from Rennes center), approved by the Institutional Review Board (NCT04220814 and NCT04918225). The same imaging protocol and clinical follow‐up were implemented in both cohorts. The main inclusion criteria were (i) aged over 18, (ii) at least one clinical sign of pyramidal involvement (hyperreflexia and/or Babinski sign and/or upper and/or lower limb motor deficit), (iii) no relapse or corticosteroids in the month before inclusion, and (iv) no history of another neurological or systemic disease. The patients had to be diagnosed with remitting–relapsing MS (RRMS) (MS‐TRACTS cohort), primary progressive MS (PPMS) or secondary progressive MS (SPMS) (MAP‐MS cohort) as defined by the McDonald criteria revised in 2017 [14]. A sex‐ and age‐matched control group was also included, comprising 40 healthy participants (HCs) aged over 18 years (31 from Rennes University Hospital and 9 from Marseille University Hospital). HCs were excluded if they had a personal history of central nervous system‐related diseases or a family history of MS. All participants gave written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study. Of the 100 patients and 40 HC initially included in the study, 92 pwMS (37 PMS and 55 RRMS) and 36 HCs were included in the analyzes after MRI acquisition quality control and preprocessing.

Clinical Evaluation

Clinical assessment included EDSS, pyramidal functional system score (FSS)—which refers to EDSS motor‐sub‐score [15], timed 25‐ft walk (T25‐FW) [16], nine‐hole peg test (9HPT) [17] and the ASIA motor score for each limb [18]. The ASIA motor score is based on a manual strength test according to the Medical Research Council scale for five muscles in the upper limb and five muscles in the lower limb. It is rated up to 25 for each limb. A lower score indicates higher motor deficiency of the limb. All clinical assessments were conducted on the same day as the MRI acquisitions for each patient.

MRI Acquisitions

Both centers were equipped with a 3 T MRI scanner (Prisma in Rennes, Vida in Marseille) and a 64‐channel head/neck coil from the same manufacturer (Magnetom Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The same MR sequences and acquisition parameters were applied in both centers. Details of the acquisition parameters are given in Table A.1. In summary, brain sequences included a 3D fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and a 3D T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo (MPRAGE). Spinal cord sequences included two axial T2*‐weighted sequences (covering the cervical levels from C1 to C3 and from C4 to C7), two axial T2‐weighted sequences (covering both thoracic and lumbar levels from C7 to T8 and from T8 to L1), two sagittal T2 turbo spin echoes (TSEs) covering the whole spine, and a sagittal T2 short TI inversion recovery (STIR). Finally, a 3D magnetization‐prepared 2 rapid gradient‐echo (MP2RAGE) sequence [19] and a 2D magnetization‐prepared turbo FLASH B1+ map, optimized to cover both the brain and the cervical spinal cord from C1 to C7 were acquired [20, 21]. For quantitative T1 (qT1) map processing, the scanner automatically generated the MP2RAGE UNI composite volume (from the GRE 3D volume base at both inversion times) and the B1+ map (flip angle map). The latter was used to post‐correct the T1 map from B1+ inhomogeneities [22].

MRI Processing

To determine the volume and severity of lesions on the motor pathways in pwMS, lesions in the brain and spinal cord were segmented using conventional MRI sequences. Microstructural damage in the brain and cervical spinal cord was approximated using mean quantitative T1 (qT1) intensity values along the CSTs computed from the MP2RAGE sequence. MRI processing of brain sequences was performed using the software anima v4.2 (https://anima.irisa.fr, anima.readthedocs.io, RRID:SCR_017072), the Computational Anatomy Toolbox for Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM CAT 12.8.2) [23], and the FMRIB Software Library (FSL) v6.0.5 [24]. We used the Spinal Cord Toolbox (SCT) v6.1 [25] and FSL to process spinal cord sequences. For visualization, the following analysis software was used: FSLeyes v1.12.6 [26] and ITK‐SNAP3 v3.8.0 [27, 28].

Lesion Volume Fraction in the CST

Lesion Segmentation

A preliminary automatic segmentation of brain lesions in the 3D FLAIR images was conducted using an in‐house automatic detection tool called LongiSeg4ms. The segmentation was then checked and eventually manually corrected using the ITK‐SNAP software. Spinal cord lesions were manually segmented using ITK‐SNAP by ML. Lesions segmentation masks have been reviewed if necessary by AK (in the axial T2*‐weighted images for the cervical spinal cord and in the axial T2‐weighted images for the thoracic spinal cord, using MP2RAGE, sagittal T2, and T2 STIR sequences to facilitate lesion identification and segmentation).

Computing Lesions Volume in the CST of the Brain

T1 sequences were normalized from the patient's space to the template MNI152 [29]. To achieve this, different brain masks were extracted from 3D FLAIR and T1‐weighted (T1w) images using the animaAtlasBasedBrainExtraction command. Linear registration followed by non‐linear registration (animaPyramidalBMRegistration then animaDenseSVFBMRegistration) from T1w to the MNI152 template was computed. FLAIR sequences were rigidly registered to T1w space. To compute the transformations between FLAIR space and MNI152 space, all registrations were merged (animaTransformSerieXmlGenerator).

White matter probability maps were computed with FSL FAST on T1w images, thresholded to 0.5 (animaThrImage) and then filled with lesion segmentations (animaImageArithmetic). Lesion masks from FLAIR and white matter masks from T1w were registered to MNI152 (animaApplyTransformSerie). The CST ROIs were defined in the brain based on the left and right M1 cortex in the MNI152 template space, including both brain [30] and brainstem parts [31], as previously described [13].

Computing Lesion Volume in the CST of the Spinal Cord

On the sagittal T2 and axial T2* sequences, the spinal cord was segmented (sct_deepseg_sc). Manual correction was applied if necessary using FSLeyes. The spinal cord was then semi‐automatically labeled (sct_label_vertebra). The sequences and their segmentations were registered to the PAM50 template [32] for normalization (sct_register_to_template [33] for sagittal T2 and sct_register_multimodal for axial T2*/T2 based on sagittal T2 transformation). The CST ROIs were defined based on the left and right lateral CST defined in the PAM50 template [32].

Lesion Volume Fraction in the CST

Atlases have been transferred from the template spaces (MNI/PAM) to the native space. Lesion volumes in both CSTs and CST volumes were then extracted in native space using fslstats and the lesion volume fractions calculated as follows: CSTlesion volume fraction=Lesion volume inCSTCSTvolume×100

These measures were extracted in the brain, the brainstem, and the cervical SC (C1–C7) and thoracic SC (T1 to T10).

qT1 Values in the CST

Computation of Quantitative Maps

To compute the qT1 maps, Bloch's equations and a look‐up table accounting for B1+ variations [22] were taken into account in the qT1 maps (brain and spinal cord) to minimize the risk of inaccurate estimation of qT1 values (due to B1+ bias).

Brain and Spinal Cord Segmentation and Normalization

In the same way as for the image processing of the T1w sequences, brains from MP2RAGE UNI sequences were extracted and registered (rigid and then non‐linear registration) to MNI space.

The cervical spinal cord was segmented on MP2RAGE UNI sequences using sct_deepseg. Manual correction was applied if necessary using FSLeyes. Using the function sct_register_multimodal, the MP2RAGE sequence and segmentation were then registered to the PAM50 template. The normalization parameters computed using MP2RAGE UNI sequences were then applied to qT1 maps.

Extraction and Harmonization of qT1 Values

Atlases have been transferred from the template spaces (MNI/PAM50) to the native space. Mean qT1 values were then extracted in the CST for the brain, the brainstem, and the cervical SC (C1–C7) from the native space. Mean qT1 values have been harmonized between the two centers using the NeuroCombat package [34, 35] as of 22 October 2024 in R. In addition, age, belonging to the pwMS or HCs group, and gender were the co‐variables taken into account for data harmonization.

Computing of Others Metrics

Cortical Thickness and Brain Volume Measurements

3DT1w acquisitions were used to obtain the vertex‐based cortical thickness volume of the whole brain using SPM CAT12 and the normalized cerebral volume using the Sienax package [36] from the FSL software.

CSA Measurement

The CSA was measured on brain 3D T1w sequences at the C2–C3 levels. The spinal cord was segmented (with sct_deepseg_sc). Manual correction was applied if necessary using FSLeyes. The spinal cord was then labeled (with sct_label_vertebra) using SCT. This enabled us to obtain spinal cord masks for each patient. From these masks, we calculated the mean value of the CSA for slices between C2 and C3 using sct_process_segmentation from SCT [32, 37].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyzes were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and R Studio (version 2025.5.0.496) except the analysis of the comparisons of voxel‐based cortical thickness which were computed with SPM CAT12.8.2.

Group Formation

Patients were divided into three groups: pyramidal syndrome with no motor deficit (group A), motor deficit of the lower limbs solely (group B), and motor deficit of both the lower and upper limbs (group C). None of the patients in the cohort had motor deficit of the upper limbs only. Patients in group A were required to have an ASIA motor score of 25 for each limb. Patients in group B had to have ASIA motor scores of 25 for each upper limb and a score below 25 for at least one of the two lower limbs. Finally, patients in group C had to have an ASIA motor score lower than 25 in at least one of the upper and at least one of the lower limbs.

Metrics Studied

At the end of the image processing, we obtained for each patient the following metrics: structural measures (cortical thickness and cerebral volume for the brain, CSA for the SC), lesion volume fractions in the brain, brainstem, and whole SC CST, and the mean qT1 intensity's values in the brain, brainstem, and cSC CST. Lesion frequencies in each slice of the CST were computed for each group.

Statistical Analysis

Evidence for group differences was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for disease duration, EDSS, ASIA score, LVF, qT1 mean intensity, CSA, and normalized cerebral volume. If the test revealed a significant difference between the groups, a Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test (with Bonferroni correction on each test) was used to compare groups two by two (with significance level set at alpha = 0.05). Comparison of the distribution of MS phenotypes and sex between the groups was performed using a χ2 test. ANOVA was used for the comparisons of voxel‐based cortical thickness with age, sex, and disease duration as covariables.

Associations between LVF and qT1 values for each portion of the CST were characterized using Spearman correlations with sex, age, and disease duration as covariables. A Spearman's correlation was also performed to assess whether LVF and qT1 values were correlated between CST‐specific metrics and whole‐region measures.

Finally, we created LASSO logistic regression models. First, we sought to determine the variables of interest in determining the probabilities of belonging to group A compared to the two other groups (with lambda.min = 0.03). Subsequently, we sought to determine the variables of interest in determining the probabilities of belonging to group B compared to group C (with lambda.min = 0.15). The following variables of interest were explored: disease duration, age, LVF (brain, brainstem, cervical and thoracic spinal cord), and the average qT1 values (brain, brainstem, cervical spinal cord).

Using the Kruskal–Wallis test, we established differences between the patients' groups in terms of age, disease duration, phenotypes, overall EDSS score and pyramidal functional subscore, as well as ASIA score for the lower limbs, T25‐FW (all p < 0.001), 9HPT and Multiple Sclerosis Severity Score (MSSS) (p < 0.01). Post hoc tests (Wilcoxon‐Mann–Whitney test with Bonferroni correction) then revealed that patients in group A were significantly younger than those in groups B and C (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively) and had a shorter disease duration (p < 0.05 and p < 0.001) (Table 1, Figure 2A). Conversely, no significant difference was found between group B and group C for either disease duration (p = 0.4) or age (p = 0.1). The distribution of disease phenotype differed significantly between the three groups (p < 0.001). Most RRMS patients were found in group A; a mix of RRMS, PPMS and SPMS patients in group B; and most SPMS patients in group C. Although patients in groups B and C had lower‐limb motor deficits, the lower‐limb ASIA scores of patients in group C (mean = 33.43, SD = 8.84) were significantly lower than those in group B (mean = 42.42, SD = 8.04). These results indicate a more severe motor deficit in lower limbs when both upper and lower limbs are involved (p < 0.001).

With regard to microstructural damage to the CST, approximated by quantitative T1 values, no significant differences between groups were found in the brain and brainstem. A group difference was observed in the cervical spinal cord (p < 0.01) and was confirmed by post hoc testing: groups B and C had significantly higher qT1 values than group A in the cervical spinal cord. As for lesion load, no significant difference was found between groups B and C at any of the three levels considered (Table 2 and Figure 4E–G).

Lesion volume fraction and qT1 values are highly correlated, particularly in the CST of the brain (r = 0.57, p < 0.001) and cervical spinal cord (r = 0.81, p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant association was observed between LVF and qT1 values in the brainstem CST (r = 0.10, p = 0.37).

Strong correlations exist between LVF values in the CST and total lesion volume within each anatomical region. Similarly, qT1 values showed strong correlations between CST‐specific measures and whole‐region metrics (Table A.2).

When reproducing our analyzes using whole‐region measures instead of CST‐specific metrics, the results were very similar but less significant: some group differences were no longer significant when considering global measures (e.g., qT1 in the whole cervical spinal cord between groups A and B, lesion volume in the whole cervical spinal cord between groups A and C, and lesion volume in the whole brain–brainstem–spinal cord region between groups A and B) (Table A.3).

Concerning brain and spinal cord atrophy, no significant differences were found in primary motor cortex cortical thickness or in CSA between groups. However, normalized brain volume was significantly different between groups (p < 0.001): it was higher in group A than in groups B (p < 0.01) and C (p < 0.001) but with no difference between groups B and C (p = 1) (Table A.4 and Figure A.1).

Multivariable Analysis

Multivariable logistic regression (LASSO model) identified variables associated with the probability of belonging to group A rather than B or C. The pwMS in group A were more likely to be younger, to have a shorter disease duration, to have a lower LVF in the CST of the brainstem, and a lower qT1 in the cervical spinal cord than those in groups B and C (Table A.5). No differentiating factors were identified between groups B and C.

Discussion

In this study, we found a higher lesion load in the brainstem and cervical portions of the CST in patients with a motor deficit compared to patients with isolated pyramidal syndrome, as well as increased microstructural damage at the cervical level, independently of age and disease duration. Conversely, no significant differences were found in the structural integrity of the CST between pwMS with motor deficit limited to the lower limbs compared to pwMS with both lower and upper limb deficits.

Lesions and Microstructural Damage Along the Motor Tract and Association With Motor Deficit

First, we highlighted an uneven distribution of lesions and microstructural damage along the CST. Lesions were more frequently observed at the cervical level, regardless of the group considered. Microstructural damage, as measured by qT1, was also more severe in this region. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a greater susceptibility of the cervical spinal cord CST to MS‐related lesions, compared to the brain portion or the thoracic portion [13, 38, 39, 40]. This uneven distribution along the spinal cord is confirmed, albeit to a lesser extent, by anatomopathological studies [41]. Second, both brainstem and cervical CST lesions were more frequent in patients with motor deficits than in those presenting with isolated pyramidal signs. This observation confirmed the predominant involvement of these lesions in the development of motor disability [9, 13, 42]. Third, lesions were not more frequent in the cerebral portion of the CST in patients with isolated hyperreflexia and/or Babinski sign compared to those with motor deficits, unlike the cervical spinal cord portion. This observation suggests that the distribution of lesions along the CST also differs between patient groups, suggesting a more “spinal cord” phenotype of the disease associated with the development of motor deficits. In clinical practice, there is considerable heterogeneity in the distribution of lesions. While lesions are almost always present in the brain, only 67 to 88% of patients—depending on the study—have spinal cord lesions [43, 44]. The literature also shows a lack of correlation between brain and spinal cord lesion loads [45]. It can be therefore hypothesized that patients may exhibit either a “spinal cord” or “cerebral” profile potentially driven by distinct underlying mechanisms.

Interestingly, even patients with isolated pyramidal signs exhibit lesions within the CST, associated with significant microstructural damage measurable in each portion of the CST when compared to healthy controls. Several factors may account for the absence of motor deficits despite the presence of lesions. First, these patients were younger and had a shorter disease duration. Our results are supported by the literature, which has consistently demonstrated the effect of age and disease duration on the appearance and severity of motor disorders [46, 47, 48, 49]. In addition, both lesion volume and severity of microstructural alterations were lower in this group than in patients with motor deficits, suggesting a role for the extent/severity of CST lesions. Demyelinated lesions can indeed undergo a repair process and the motor consequences may differ depending on whether the lesions remain demyelinated or have been remyelinated [50]. Furthermore, we were unable to take into account compensatory systems such as individual functional reserve [51, 52]. Thus, patients in group A may have a functional reserve that supports the reorganization of motor networks. This could allow them to better compensate for lesions—up to a certain threshold, beyond which motor impairments begin to manifest in the limbs.

Motor Disorders Limited to Lower Limbs Compared to Both Upper and Lower Limbs

In this study, we did not find differences in CST lesion load between groups B and C. Group B even tended to have a higher spinal cord lesion load than group C at the cervical level, whereas only the lower limbs showed motor deficits in this group. It is also interesting to note that the motor deficit was more severe in the lower limbs in group C than in group B. These results suggest a continuity of the different phenotypes, that is, when a motor deficit in the lower limbs appears and continues to worsen, a deficit in the upper limbs begins to appear. This observation has already been reported in the literature [53] and a length‐dependent axonopathy hypothesis has been proposed to explain the delayed onset of the motor deficit in the upper limbs compared to the lower limbs. Thus, shorter axons tend to accumulate fewer lesions and therefore exhaust their reserves and repair capacity less. As the axons controlling the upper limbs are shorter than those controlling the lower limbs, this would explain the late onset of motor disorders in the upper limbs. However, this concept remains largely theoretical, as pathophysiological evidence is still limited [54, 55]. Our results also suggest that once a certain degree of CST damage is reached, lower limb deficits appear, followed by upper limb motor deficit, with no evidence of a higher accumulation of focal lesions along the CST. These results are consistent with the literature, which has shown that even when acute inflammatory activity is largely suppressed by highly effective treatments, there is no clear impact on the risk of EDSS score increase in patients with non‐active progressive MS (i.e., in the majority of cases with progressive worsening of motor deficits) [56, 57].

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of this study are its prospective design, the inclusion of a cohort of 100 pwMS with various degrees of motor symptoms at clinical examination (from isolated hyperreflexia to motor deficit affecting upper and lower limbs), the association of conventional and quantitative MRI acquisitions to approximate lesion load and microstructural damage, and the evaluation of motor function using detailed clinical examination. These features provide a unique dataset to study the consequences of CST damage on motor function. In this study, we chose to examine LVF and qT1 values specifically within the CST rather than across the entire brain, brainstem and spinal cord. Although analyzes using whole‐region measures showed similar trends, they were less statistically significant than those obtained with CST‐specific metrics. These findings suggest that CST‐specific measures provide better performance than global spinal cord or brain metrics.

The main limitations are the size of the groups and therefore the statistical power of our tests. As a result, we may have missed relevant findings, particularly regarding the differentiation between group B and group C. We performed a sample size estimation based on the measured effect sizes and variances and estimated that 206 patients in each of groups B and C would be required to detect differences in LVF in the brainstem and cervical spinal cord.

Another limitation concerns the reliability and accuracy of the clinical tests applied to determine group affiliation. The ASIA score is a value estimated by the neurologist or physiotherapist. This value is therefore subjective and depends on the observer. Moreover, motor performance may vary in the same patient, depending on the time or condition of the patient. Consequently, the ASIA test, though regularly used, may have certain limitations in estimating motor disorders [58]. Similarly, the presence of hyperreflexia and/or a Babinski sign on clinical examination—which constituted inclusion criteria for this cohort—is subject to intra‐ and inter‐observer variability [59]. Additional measures could have provided a more precise assessment of patients' motor deficits. Firstly, dynamometric measurements would have allowed a more quantitative evaluation of motor strength. Secondly, spasticity has been shown to be associated with corticospinal tract involvement and lesion location [60]. Its quantification using a standardized scale would have allowed a more accurate assessment of patients' motor impairment. Thirdly, electrophysiological measurements, such as central motor conduction time (CMCT), could have enabled a deeper investigation into the relationship between CST damage and patients' level of disability.

Finally, challenges related to the acquisition of high‐resolution, high‐contrast, artifact‐free MRI of the entire spinal cord—and, consequently, challenges related to the accurate identification and segmentation of spinal cord lesions—contribute to the limitations of our study.

Conclusion

In our study, the motor deficit in pwMS was associated with brainstem and cervical spinal cord CST lesion load and with severity of the microstructural damage at the cervical spinal cord level. Conversely, we were unable to establish a difference in CST damage between pwMS with lower limb deficits compared to pwMS with both lower and upper limb deficits. Transposed to clinical practice, these results highlight the importance of preventing any accumulation of lesions on the cervical and brainstem portions of the CST by using effective therapies early in the disease before the occurrence of any measurable motor deficit.
