Abstract
We introduce a novel projection depth for data lying in a general Hilbert space, called the regularized projection depth, with a focus on functional data. By regularizing projection directions, the proposed depth does not suffer from the degeneracy issue that may arise when the classical projection depth is naively defined on an infinite-dimensional space. Compared to existing functional depth notions, the regularized projection depth has several advantages: (i) it requires no moment assumptions on the underlying distribution, (ii) it satisfies many desirable depth properties including invariance, monotonicity, and vanishing at infinity, (iii) its sample version uniformly converges under mild conditions, and (iv) it generates a highly robust median. Furthermore, the proposed depth is statistically useful as it (v) does not produce ties in the induced ranks and (vi) effectively detects shape outlying functions. This paper focuses mainly on the theoretical properties of the regularized projection depth.

Introduction

Many tools in both theoretical and applied statistics require ordering data points according to their “typicality” or “centrality” within a dataset.
In the univariate setting, this task is naturally solved by sorting the observed values.
The problem becomes more involved
as data structure exhibits more complexity.
For instance, for multivariate, functional, manifold, or object data,
it is not straightforward to define canonical ordering.
To address this challenge, the concept of statistical depth was introduced.
A statistical depth function assigns a non-negative number to each point in the underlying space where the observed random elements lie.
The corresponding depth value quantifies its centrality with respect to (w.r.t.) a given distribution or data cloud.
Points with high depth are considered central, while those with low depth lie near the boundary and may be considered potential outliers.
Among the many applications of depth functions are robust estimation, outlier detection, hypothesis testing, and data visualization; see
20
;
38
;
25
and the references therein.

Numerous multivariate depth functions have been introduced in the literature.
The earliest and the most thoroughly studied is the halfspace (or Tukey) depth
(
36
)
.
It has inspired a wide range of other depth notions such as the simplicial depth
(
21
)
, the spatial depth
(
5
)
, and the zonoid depth
(
26
)
.
A prominent notion is the
projection depth

(
39
)
.
It is based on the concept of projection pursuit
(
16
)
and the Stahel-Donoho outlyingness
(
35
;
6
)
.

To describe the multivariate projection depth,
let
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space with the usual inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and the associated Euclidean norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
.
For a point
x\in\mathbb{H}
and a unit vector
v\in\mathbb{S}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{H}\,\colon\,\left\lVert y\right\rVert=1\right\}
, the outlyingness of
x
w.r.t. an
\mathbb{H}
-valued random variable
X\sim P_{X}
in direction

\displaystyle O_{v}(x;P_{X})=\frac{\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\mathsf{med}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]}\right|}{\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]}.

(1)

Here,
\mathsf{med}[U]
and
\mathsf{MAD}[U]
stand for the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) of the real-valued random variable
U
.
1
1

1

We define the median of a real-valued random variable
U
, denoted by
\mathsf{med}[U]

satisfying
\min\left\{\mathbb{P}(U\leq m),\mathbb{P}(U\geq m)\right\}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}

The median absolute deviation

is then defined as
\mathsf{med}[\left|{U-\mathsf{med}[U]}\right|]

The projection depth at

w.r.t.

is then defined as

\displaystyle D(x;P_{X})=\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left(1+O_{v}(x;P_{X})\right)^{-1}=\left(1+\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})\right)^{-1},

(2)

that is, we take the largest outlyingness over all directions and transform it so that points that are less outlying receive higher depth values.
The projection depth (

\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies a range of desirable properties related to invariance, symmetry, and consistency.
25
provide an overview of the relevant properties of the projection depth in comparison with other depth concepts.
22

Beyond multivariate data,
modern statistical applications often involve datasets in which each observation is a function such as a curve or a surface.
As a result, the framework of functional data analysis has become increasingly relevant

As in the multivariate setting, it is important to assess how central or outlying a given function is within a sample.
This need has led to the development of
functional depth
, which has been an active research topic for over two decades.
Beginning with the work of
12
, several notions of functional depth have been proposed and studied,
including those by
23
;
27
;
4
;
28
;
29
;

;

We now denote by
\mathbb{H}
a (typically infinite-dimensional) Hilbert space equipped with the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and the associated norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
.
A natural approach is to generalize a depth function on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
—such as halfspace depth or projection depth (
2
)—to this more general functional setting by simply replacing
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in (
2
) by a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
and by considering the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}=\{y\in\mathbb{H}\colon\left\lVert y\right\rVert=1\}
in
\mathbb{H}
.
However, it is well-known that a naive extension of multivariate depth functions to functional data may suffer from the so-called
degeneracy
, meaning that the depth is zero almost everywhere. This was shown by
8
and
19
for halfspace depth, and by

for both halfspace and projection depths.
2
2

2

4
show that the usual projection depth degenerates in the space of continuous functions on
[0,1]
under a Gaussian underlying distribution.
Our Theorem
1
shows that this is true in any infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.

As we show in the following result, the naive extension of the projection depth degenerates for any Gaussian distribution in an infinite-dimensional
\mathbb{H}
.
The proof of Theorem
1
is in the Appendix.

Theorem 1

(Degeneracy of projection depth)
.

Suppose
\dim(\mathbb{H})=\infty
and
X\sim P_{X}
is a Gaussian random element on
\mathbb{H}
with positive definite covariance operator. Then, for an independent copy
X_{0}
of
X
, we have
D(X_{0};P_{X})=0
almost surely.

As a consequence, the naive extension of projection depth to a functional setup does not provide any sensible ranking of data, already in the canonical case of a Gaussian random variable.
The core issue in infinite-dimensional spaces
\mathbb{H}
is that the set of directions
\mathbb{S}
is too large.
Due to the infinite dimensionality of
\mathbb{H}
and the tightness of the distribution of
X\sim P_{X}
living in
\mathbb{H}
, it is possible to find a direction
v\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]
is arbitrarily small.
Consequently, the outlyingness (
1
) in such a direction becomes arbitrarily large, which in turn causes the depth to degenerate to zero.

To overcome the degeneracy identified in Theorem
1
,
this paper introduces the
regularized projection depth
(
RPD
), a novel statistical depth for random elements that take values in a (typically infinite-dimensional) Hilbert space, referred to as
Hilbertian elements

(
17
)
.
The key idea is to regularize the MADs of projections in order
to prevent them from taking extremely small values.
In effect, we exclude the directions along which projected data are highly concentrated around their medians.
This restriction prevents the outlyingness defined in (
1
) from diverging to infinity and ensures that the corresponding projection depth (
2
) remains non-degenerate, as shown in Theorem
2
below.
We establish several desirable theoretical properties of the
RPD
, including invariance, maximality at center, monotonicity, and continuity, under appropriate modification to the postulates by
38
;
30
;
13
. We also show that the sample version of
RPD
can be consistently estimated, and that the induced median is robust.

RPD
is obtained using a regularization approach, that is, by restricting the set of directions
\mathbb{S}
in (
2
). This is motivated by the recently introduced regularized halfspace depth
(
37
)
, which applies a related idea to construct a halfspace depth that is suitable for functional data. Although both these depths use regularization, the two approaches are fundamentally different.
The definition of regularized halfspace depth depends on the covariance operator and involves the reproducing kernel Hilbert space norm. As a result, that depth inherits technical limitations and requires strong assumptions. In contrast,
RPD
does not require the existence of any moment of the underlying distribution, which makes
RPD
suitable even for heavy-tailed data.
Moreover,
RPD
exhibits greater robustness as it is defined in a fully nonparametric manner. Since
RPD
does not rely on the covariance operator, its sample version also avoids cumbersome dependencies on principal components and the associated choice of truncation parameters as encountered in
37
.
In summary,
RPD
is conceptually simpler and more broadly applicable than the functional depth from
37
; additional advantages of
RPD
will appear in its performance in practical tasks.

2
Regularized projection depth

Inspired by the approach of
37

we aim to incorporate regularization into the definition of the classical projection depth

1

To do so, we restrict the set of directions considered in its definition in (

Definition
.

For
\beta\geq 0

\mathcal{V}_{\beta}=\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(P_{X})=\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\,\colon\,\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]\geq\beta\right\}.

(3)

The

)

is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}}\left(1+O_{v}(x;P_{X})\right)^{-1},

(4)

where the projection outlyingness

is given in (
1

In the regularization step, we consider only those

that satisfy

onto which projections of

are sufficiently dispersed.
For

, this ensures that

In the extreme case, the direction set

Therefore, throughout most of the paper, we assume

Reasonable choices of

In practice (see Section
4
), we set

to be the

,

is independent of

Here

The following theorem shows that

4

Let

An important family of well-behaved functional distributions is the

(
3

Recall that a random element

This simplified form mirrors the structure of the direction set

3.1
Basic properties of regularized projection depth

The

We begin by analyzing key properties of this set, which directly influence the behavior of the depth.

(Properties of the direction set)

Let

(V1)

\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
is antipodally symmetric; that is,

(V2)

If

3

3

(V3)

Suppose that

e\in\mathbb{H}
is a fixed vector, then

D_{\beta}(\mathcal{T}x;P_{\mathcal{T}X})=D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{H}.

(N3)

(
Vanishing at infinity
) Let
\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}
be a sequence such that
\left\lVert x_{k}\right\rVert\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, and assume that

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}}\left\langle\frac{x_{k}}{\left\lVert x_{k}\right\rVert},v\right\rangle>0.

(9)

Then
D_{\beta}(x_{k};P_{X})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. In particular, if
\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\right)
and
\dim\operatorname{span}\left(\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\right)<\infty
, then condition (
9
) is satisfied.

(N4)

(
Effective domain reduction
) Denote by
\widehat{x}
is the orthogonal projection of
x
onto
\operatorname{cl}\left({\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\right)}\right)
. Then it holds that
D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})=D_{\beta}(\widehat{x};P_{X})
.

With
\beta>0
, the
RPD
is
1/\beta
-Lipschitz continuous
(N1)
, which ensures its regular behavior in the argument
x
;
the larger the regularization parameter
\beta
, the more stable the depth function becomes.

Invariance under translations and unitary transformations
(N2)
is another key property of the
RPD
,
capturing essential symmetries such as invariance under coordinate permutations.
In contrast, affine invariance—which is standardly expected from multivariate depths—has no relevance in the functional data setting
(
13
, Section 2.1)
.

The
RPD
satisfies the vanishing at infinity property
(N3)
under an additional geometric condition (
9
).
Specifically, increasing
\beta
reduces the direction set
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
,
which, according to
(N3)
, limits the depth’s ability to detect extreme deviations perpendicular to these directions.
Consequently, the decay of depth for distant observations is not guaranteed in full generality, but still holds within the constrained geometry of
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
.
Our goal is thus to select the regularization parameters to keep
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
sufficiently large.
Importantly, the failure to satisfy the vanishing at infinity property is common among functional depths;
see
13
for a summary.
Therefore, property
(N3)
provides a valuable guarantee.

Finally, the
RPD
value of a point
x\in\mathbb{H}
depends solely on its projection onto the closed linear span of the direction set
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}

(N4)
.
This effective domain reduction implies that the depth is determined entirely by its behavior on this subspace of the ambient space.
Furthermore, this property emphasizes that regularization not only stabilizes the depth functional,
but also induces an implicit dimension reduction aligned with the most informative directions in the data. In other words,
\operatorname{cl}\left({\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\right)}\right)
carries all the information in
D_{\beta}(\cdot;P_{X})
,
since replacing any point by its projection onto this set leaves the depth value unchanged.

For this reason, it is therefore natural to define the
RPD
median as

\theta(P_{X})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\left\{D_{\beta}(\theta;P_{X})\,\colon\,\theta\in\operatorname{cl}\left({\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\right)}\right)\right\}.

(10)

The
RPD
median always exists, as shown in the following theorem. Note, however, that the maximizer of
D_{\beta}(\cdot;P_{X})
on
\operatorname{cl}\left({\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\right)}\right)
may not be unique; in such cases, the whole set of maximizers

M(P_{X})=\left\{x\in\operatorname{cl}\left({\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\right)}\right)\colon D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{H}}D_{\beta}(y;P_{X})\right\}\subset\mathbb{H}

is regarded as the set of the
RPD
medians. Note that the set
M(P_{X})
is convex, but may fail to be compact in
\mathbb{H}
. Consequently, the usual approach of selecting a single representative as the barycenter of
M(P_{X})
does not apply in the functional setting (because the uniform distribution on a non-compact set
M(P_{X})\subset\mathbb{H}
, necessary to define a barycenter of
M_{X}
, is not necessarily well defined).

Theorem 6

(
RPD
median existence)
.

Let
X\sim P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
and assume that
\beta\geq 0
is chosen so that
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\neq\emptyset
. Then the
RPD
median set
M(P_{X})
is non-empty and convex in
\mathbb{H}
.

3.2
Sample
RPD
and its consistency

Let
X\sim P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
and
\beta>0
. Consider a random sample
\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
of size
n
with
X_{i}\overset{d}{=}X
, and let
\widehat{P}_{n}
denote the corresponding empirical distribution
\widehat{P}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
, where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure at
x\in\mathbb{H}
.
For
v\in\mathbb{S}
,
we write

\widehat{\mathsf{med}}\left[\left\{\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]
and

\widehat{\mathsf{MAD}}\left[\left\{\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]
for indicating the sample median and sample MAD based on projections
\{\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
respectively.
6
6

6

For a sample
\left\{Z_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
with order statistics
Z_{(1)}<\cdots<Z_{(n)}
, we define the sample median as
\widehat{\mathsf{med}}\left[\left\{Z_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]=Z_{((n+1)/2)}
if
n
is odd and
\widehat{\mathsf{med}}\left[\left\{Z_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]=(Z_{(n/2)}+Z_{(n/2+1)})/2
otherwise. Then, the sample MAD is defined by
\widehat{\mathsf{MAD}}\left[\left\{Z_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]=\widehat{\mathsf{med}}\left[\left\{\left|{Z_{i}-\widehat{\mathsf{med}}\left[\left\{Z_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}\right]}\right|\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]
.

We then define the sample
RPD
of
x\in\mathbb{H}
as

D_{\beta}(x;\widehat{P}_{n})=\inf_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}\left(1+O_{v}(x;\widehat{P}_{n})\right)^{-1}=\left(1+\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}\frac{\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\widehat{\mathsf{med}}\left[\left\{\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]}\right|}{\widehat{\mathsf{MAD}}\left[\left\{\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]}\right)^{-1},

(11)

where
the sample direction set
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}
is defined by

\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}=\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(\widehat{P}_{n})=\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\,\colon\,\widehat{\mathsf{MAD}}\left[\left\{\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right]\geq\beta\right\}.

The following theorem establishes the strong consistency of the sample
RPD
. It is interesting to note that the proof technique used to derive this result appears to be new in the functional depth literature, and the derived uniform consistency results do not require compactness of the set of functions over which convergence is established.
To state our result, we introduce the maximal outlyingness function
\Gamma_{x}
at
x\in\mathbb{H}
over
\mathcal{V}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{x}(t)=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}O_{v}(x;P_{X}),\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(12)

Theorem 7

(Consistency of
RPD
)
.

Suppose that
X\sim P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
has contiguous support and
\beta>0
is chosen so that
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\neq\emptyset
.
Furthermore, assume that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{H}
is a bounded set such that the family
\left\{\Gamma_{x}:x\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
of maximal outlyingness functions (
12
) is equicontinuous at
\beta
, i.e.,

\lim_{t\to\beta}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}\left|{\Gamma_{x}(t)-\Gamma_{x}(\beta)}\right|=0.

(13)

Then, the sample
RPD
(
11
) is uniformly consistent for the population
RPD
(
4
) over
\mathcal{F}
as

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}\left|{D_{\beta}(x;\widehat{P}_{n})-D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})}\right|\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{\text{a.s.}}0.

The key condition (
13
) reduces the problem of establishing finite sample consistency of
RPD
to the task of verifying continuity of the deterministic map
\Gamma_{x}
. The following theorem shows that under fairly general conditions, one obtains pointwise consistency of the sample
RPD
.

Theorem 8
.

Suppose that
X\sim P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
has contiguous support and
\beta>0
is chosen so that
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\neq\emptyset
,
and let
\mathcal{F}=\{x\}
for some fixed
x\in\mathbb{H}
.
Furthermore, assume that
the maximal outlyingness at
x\in\mathbb{H}
is achieved over
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}^{+}
, i.e.,

\Gamma_{x}(\beta)=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}^{+}}O_{v}(x;P_{X}),

(14)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}^{+}=\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\,\colon\,\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]>\beta\right\}
.
Then, the condition (
13
) is satisfied,
implying that

\left|{D_{\beta}(x;\widehat{P}_{n})-D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})}\right|\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{\text{a.s.}}0.

Formula (
14
) essentially states that the supremum defining
\Gamma_{x}(\beta)
is not attained solely along directions with
\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]=\beta
, but can instead be approximated by directions with
\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]>\beta
. In other words, the boundary directions do not contribute to the supremum. As we show next, this condition is fairly mild.

For elliptically symmetric distributions with characteristic function in (
5
), we have (after a suitable reparametrization)
\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]=\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert
; see (
6
).
Fix
0<\beta<\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert
.
It is easy to see that the closure of the set
\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\colon\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert>\beta\right\}
(with respect to the relative topology on
\mathbb{S}
) coincides with
\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\colon\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert\geq\beta\right\}
.
Together with the continuity of the mapping
v\mapsto O_{v}(x;P_{X})
(see part
(V2)
of Theorem
3
), this immediately implies that the condition (
14
) holds.
Moreover, under ellipticity, one also obtains uniform consistency on bounded sets, as established in the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Let
X\sim P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
be elliptically symmetric with location
\mu\in\mathbb{H}
and scatter operator
\Sigma
, defined via the characteristic function given in Equation (
5
), parametrized such that
\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]=\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert
(see (
6
) for details). Here,
\Sigma
is a self-adjoint, positive definite, and compact operator on
\mathbb{H}
.
Assume that
0<\beta<\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}
, where
\sigma_{1}
is the maximal eigenvalue of
\Sigma
.
Then, for any
M>0
, the condition (
13
) is satisfied with

\mathcal{F}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{H}\,\colon\,\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq M\right\}
,
and therefore,
the sample
RPD
(
11
) is uniformly consistent for the population
RPD
(
4
) over
\mathcal{F}
as

\sup_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq M}\left|{D_{\beta}(x;\widehat{P}_{n})-D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})}\right|\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{\text{a.s.}}0.

3.3
Robustness of induced median

Robustness analysis is a cornerstone in the study of depth-based functionals.
The median derived from a depth function is considered robust if small contaminations in the data distribution do not cause large changes in its value.
In our analysis,
we measure robustness using a notion of the breakdown point, which quantifies
the largest proportion of contamination that can be introduced before the estimator becomes arbitrarily distorted.
Although breakdown points have been extensively studied in Euclidean spaces
(
6
;
39
, e.g.,)
,
their investigation for functional depth medians has been comparatively limited.

To explain the details, let
X\sim P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
and
\beta>0
be such that

\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(P_{X})=\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\,\colon\,\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]\geq\beta\right\}\neq\emptyset
.
For
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
Q\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
,
we define the
\varepsilon
-contaminated distribution

P_{(Q,\varepsilon)}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
by
Q
as

P_{(Q,\varepsilon)}(A)=((1-\varepsilon)P_{X}+\varepsilon Q)(A)=(1-\varepsilon)P_{X}(A)+\varepsilon\,Q(A),\quad\text{for all Borel sets }A\subseteq\mathbb{H}.

The corresponding contaminated direction set
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(Q,\varepsilon)
is defined by

\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(Q,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(P_{(Q,\varepsilon)})=\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\,\colon\,\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X^{\prime},v\right\rangle]\geq\beta\right\},\quad\text{where }X^{\prime}\sim P_{(Q,\varepsilon)}.

To ensure that the contaminated
RPD

D_{\beta}(\cdot;P_{(Q,\varepsilon)})
and the subsequent median
\theta(P_{(Q,\varepsilon)})
remains well-defined,
we only consider the set
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
of contaminating distributions that yield a non-empty direction set defined by

\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)=\left\{Q\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})\,\colon\,\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(Q,\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

This is a natural condition, which is assumed throughout our paper.
We then introduce the following definition
of the breakdown point of the
RPD
median
\theta=\theta(P_{X})
from (
10
) tailored to our infinite-dimensional setting:

\varepsilon^{*}(\theta;P_{X})=\inf\left\{\varepsilon\in[0,1]\,\colon\,\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(Q,\varepsilon)}\left|{\left\langle\theta(P_{(Q,\varepsilon)})-\theta(P_{X}),v\right\rangle}\right|=\infty\right\}.

(15)

The next theorem shows that the breakdown point
\varepsilon^{*}(\theta;P_{X})
in (
15
) of the
RPD
median
\theta(P_{X})
in (
10
) is
1/2
,
which is the highest possible value among reasonable estimators.
This indicates that the
RPD
median possesses good robustness properties, which is consistent with the high breakdown point of the classical multivariate projection depth
(
39
, Theorem 3.4)
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
and
\beta>0
be such that
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(P_{X})\neq\emptyset
. Then
\varepsilon^{*}(\theta;P_{X})=1/2
.

While the previous theorem guarantees exceptional robustness properties for the
RPD
median, it is important to note that
\varepsilon^{*}(\theta;P_{X})
is a weaker notion than the usual breakdown point, in which the estimator
breaks down when the difference
\left\lVert\theta(P_{(Q,\varepsilon)})-\theta(P_{X})\right\rVert
in norm can be made arbitrarily large
(
15
, Section 1.4)
.
In infinite-dimensional spaces, establishing the classical norm-based breakdown point can be technically challenging,
and this issue is rarely addressed directly in the literature.
Our formulation (
15
) of breakdown point captures the idea that the estimator breaks down once it can be pushed
arbitrarily far in some direction
v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(Q,\varepsilon)
.
This is consistent with the weak topology in the space
\operatorname{cl}\left({\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(Q,\varepsilon)\right)}\right)
that we consider throughout the paper.
Further, in Theorem
10
, we do not consider the situation in which the median breaks down in the sense that

\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X^{\prime},v\right\rangle]<\beta
for all
v\in\mathbb{S}
, that is, when the set
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}(Q,\varepsilon)

becomes empty. While one could, in principle, extend the breakdown analysis to include such degeneracies, we do not address that here due to space constraints.

4
An application: Outlier detection

The goal of the present paper is to introduce the projection depth for functional data and develop its theoretical foundations. The more practical issues like the computation of
RPD
, the choice of the tuning parameter
\beta
, and a comprehensive set of applications of
RPD
to statistical inference, will be part of a separate article. Here, we limit ourselves to a few remarks about the computation of
RPD
and a single selected application to an outlier detection problem, which allows us to compare
RPD
with the regularized halfspace depth from
37
and other classical functional depths, and showcase some of the strengths of the new proposal.

4.1
Practical implementation and tuning

The
RPD
in (
4
) is naturally a conditional version of the classical projection depth
(
39
)
from (
2
) applied in a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. For that reason, some of the elaborate computational procedures for the projection depth are also applicable in the setting of
\mathbb{H}
-valued data, see
22
,
34
, and the references therein.

In our implementation, we followed a simple random approximation of the
RPD
proposed by
9
, where the infimum in (
4
) over
v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
is replaced by a minimum over a (large number)
M\geq 1
of directions
v_{1},\dots,v_{M}\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
sampled independently from a distribution
Q_{V}
supported on the set
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
. We obtain the random
RPD

D_{\beta}^{(M)}(x;P_{X})=\min_{m=1,\dots,M}\left(1+O_{v_{m}}(x;P_{X})\right)^{-1}.

(16)

A straightforward adaptation of
9
then gives that, as long as
Q_{V}
does not vanish on
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
, we have a strong convergence result
\lim_{M\to\infty}D_{\beta}^{(M)}(x;P_{X})=D_{\beta}(x;P_{X})
a.s., for any
x
and
P_{X}
in
\mathbb{H}
. Due to the space constraints, the complete proof of this result and the technical conditions on
Q_{V}
will be detailed in our follow-up study of the practical properties of
RPD
.

We have implemented the approximated depth (
16
) efficiently in
C++
, with an interface in the software environment
R
via package
RcppArmadillo

(
10
)
. The resulting package
RPD
is available online.
8
8

8

https://github.com/NagyStanislav/RPD

Numerical experiments confirm that for data of low-to-intermediate effective dimension (that is, the dimension of the “signal” in the functional data, as opposed to the typically infinite-dimensional “noise”), our approximate procedure gives stable and reliable results (we use
M=10\,000
in (
16
) by default).

Recall that the positiveness of
\beta>0
ensures the non-degeneracy of
RPD
, and thus establishes theoretical guarantees on the performance of
RPD
.
In our implementation, we set
\beta
to be the
u
-quantile of
\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,V\right\rangle]
,
where
V
is independent of
X
and follows a distribution supported in
\mathbb{S}
.
9
9

9

In our numerical study in Section
4.2
, we discretize all functional data onto a grid of
101
points in their domain; the random variable
V
is then taken uniform on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{101}
.

This ensures that
\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
is non-empty.
The practical choice of parameter

follows the general strategies outlined by
37
: the
RPD
with smaller quantile level

guarantees sensitivity to various functional outliers and is expected to generally perform well. Overall, we recommend a relatively small (but positive) value

4.2
Proof of concept: Detection of shape outliers

The performance of the

. Still, however, the gap in performance compared with

.

5
Conclusion

The novel
RPD
introduced here offers several key advantages over existing functional depth notions.
Thanks to its grounding in Hilbert space and its definition based on projections,
the
RPD
is sensitive to shape-related features in functional data.
This makes it particularly effective for tasks like functional outlier detection; see Section
4
.
In contrast, depth functions developed in Banach spaces, such as the integrated
(
12
;
28
)
or infimal
(
27
;
29
)
depth, often struggle with identifying shape outliers.
The
RPD
also overcomes some drawbacks of the recently introduced regularized halfspace depth
(
37
)
, such as sample depth discreteness and zero values outside the convex hull of the data, which hinder the latter depth in outlier detection by assigning tied values to multiple outliers
(see
37
, Section 3)
.
In addition, unlike the Mahalanobis
(
2
)
,
L^{2}

(
32
)
, or regularized halfspace
(
37
)
depths, the
RPD
does not require any moment assumptions on the data,
leading to its natural and strong robustness.
The
RPD
also satisfies several desirable theoretical properties not shared by other depths; see Section
3
.
Finally, well-known advantages of projection depth in finite dimensions
(
39
;
40
, e.g.,)
naturally carry over to the infinite-dimensional
RPD
setting.

Proof of Theorem

Let
\Sigma
be the positive definite covariance operator of

are Gaussian, we have for every

where

3/4

6
). The projection depth (
2
) thus reduces to

D(x;P_{X})=\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left(1+b^{-1}\left|{g_{v}(x)}\right|\right)^{-1},\quad\text{where }g_{v}(x)=\left\langle x,v\right\rangle/\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert.

Let

D(X_{0};P_{X})\leq\inf_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\left(1+b^{-1}\left|{\xi_{i}}\right|\right)^{-1}=\left(1+b^{-1}\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\left|{\xi_{i}}\right|\right)^{-1}=0\quad\text{a.s.}

It remains to prove (
17
). For any
M>0
and
J\in\mathbb{N}

the distribution function of a standard Gaussian random variable. Hence,

Lemma 11
.

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
and a non-empty antipodally symmetric set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{S}
(that is,
\mathcal{U}=-\mathcal{U}
) with

. Then, for any sequence
\left\{u_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{U}\right)\cap\mathbb{S}

\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\left\langle u_{k},v\right\rangle>0

Proof.

Let
K=\dim(\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{U}\right))<\infty
, and let
\left\{v_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{K}\subset\mathbb{S}
be an orthonormal basis of
\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{U}\right)
(not necessarily
\left\{v_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
). For any
v\in\mathcal{U}
, write
v=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\delta_{\ell}(v)\,v_{\ell}
with
\delta(v)=(\delta_{1}(v),\dots,\delta_{K}(v))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Then
\left\langle\delta(v),\delta(w)\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
, in particular
\left\lVert\delta(v)\right\rVert_{2}=1
. Here,
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
is the usual Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Since
\mathcal{U}
spans
\operatorname{span}\left(\mathcal{U}\right)
, the set
\{\delta(v):v\in\mathcal{U}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Thus, there exist
w_{1},\dots,w_{K}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\{\delta(w_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{K}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. For each
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, let
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
be the
j
th canonical basis vector, and consider its expansion
e_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\alpha_{j,\ell}\delta(w_{\ell})
, where
\alpha_{j,\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since
\mathcal{U}
is symmetric, we have
\sup_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\left\langle u_{k},v\right\rangle\geq 0
for all
k
. Assume for contradiction that
\inf_{k}\sup_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\left\langle u_{k},v\right\rangle=0
. Choose
\varepsilon>0
such that

\varepsilon^{2}<\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\alpha_{j,\ell}\right)^{2}\right)^{-1}

(19)

and find
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sup_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\left\langle u_{k},v\right\rangle\leq\varepsilon
. In particular, we have
\left\langle u_{k},w_{\ell}\right\rangle=\left\langle\delta(u_{k}),\delta(w_{\ell})\right\rangle\leq\varepsilon
. Thus, we obtain

1=\left\lVert\delta(u_{k})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left\langle\delta(u_{k}),e_{j}\right\rangle^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\alpha_{j,\ell}\left\langle\delta(u_{k}),\delta(w_{\ell})\right\rangle\right)^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\alpha_{j,\ell}\right)^{2},

which contradicts (
19

\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{s}(v)-s(v)}\right|\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{\text{a.s.}}0
.

Proof.

We first prove that the real mappings

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\colon v\mapsto m(v),\quad\psi_{2}\colon v\mapsto\widehat{m}(v),\quad\psi_{3}\colon v\mapsto s(v),\quad\psi_{4}\colon v\mapsto\widehat{s}(v),

defined on
\mathbb{H}
are weakly sequentially continuous.
Indeed, let
v_{k}\xrightharpoonup[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}v
,
meaning that
\left\langle u,v_{k}\right\rangle\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}\left\langle u,v\right\rangle
for all
u\in\mathbb{H}
.
In particular,
\left\langle X(\omega),v_{k}\right\rangle\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}\left\langle X(\omega),v\right\rangle
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Consequently,
\left\langle X,v_{k}\right\rangle\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{d}\left\langle X,v\right\rangle
.
By
15
,
the median is continuous under weak convergence provided that the limit distribution has connected support,
which holds for
\left\langle X,v\right\rangle
by our assumption on
P_{X}
.
Hence,
m(v_{k})\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}m(v)
.
It follows that
\left|{\left\langle X,v_{k}\right\rangle-m(v_{k})}\right|\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{d}\left|{\left\langle X,v\right\rangle-m(v)}\right|
, and the same argument implies
s(v_{k})\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}s(v)
.
Thus,
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{3}
are weakly sequentially continuous.

Next, because
v_{k}\xrightharpoonup[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}v
,
we also have
\left\langle X_{i},v_{k}\right\rangle\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Therefore, all order statistics of
\{\left\langle X_{i},v_{k}\right\rangle\}_{i=1}^{n}
converge to the corresponding order statistics of
\{\left\langle X_{i},v\right\rangle\}_{i=1}^{n}
as
k\to\infty
.
This yields
\widehat{m}(v_{k})\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}\widehat{m}(v)
and
\widehat{s}(v_{k})\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}\widehat{s}(v)
,
showing that
\psi_{2}
and
\psi_{4}
are also weakly sequentially continuous.

Note that since we assume a separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
, it follows that the relative weak topology on the unit ball
\mathbb{B}=\{v\in\mathbb{H}\colon\left\lVert v\right\rVert\leq 1\}
is metrizable
(
33
, Theorem 3.16)
, since
\mathbb{B}
is weakly compact. Consequently, because
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{4}
are weakly sequentially continuous, the restrictions of
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{4}
to the unit ball
\mathbb{B}
are also weakly continuous
(
33
, p. 395)
.

Denote by
d_{P}
the Prohorov distance on
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})

(
7
, Section 11.3)
. Since we assume that
\mathbb{H}
is separable, it follows that
d_{P}
metrizes the weak topology of probability measures on
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
.
By
1
, we have

\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}d_{P}(\widehat{P}_{n}(v),P_{X}(v))\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{\text{a.s.}}0.

(22)

Restrict ourselves to the set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Omega
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})=1
on which this convergence holds.

We prove the convergence result for median;
the case of
\mathsf{MAD}
is analogous.
The following argument follows
1
.
Define
a_{n}=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{B}}\left|{\widehat{m}(v)-m(v)}\right|
, and we aim to show that
L=\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=0
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that
L>0
.

Since
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are weakly continuous on
\mathbb{B}
,
the mapping
v\mapsto\left|{\widehat{m}(v)-m(v)}\right|
is weakly continuous on
\mathbb{B}
.
Because
\mathbb{B}
is weakly compact,
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
u_{n}\in\mathbb{B}
such that
a_{n}=\left|{\widehat{m}(u_{n})-m(u_{n})}\right|
. Assume that
a_{n}\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}L
; otherwise, choose a subsequence satisfying this.
Similarly, assume
u_{n}\xrightharpoonup[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}u\in\mathbb{B}
;
if not, pass to a weakly convergent subsequence,
which exists due to the weak compactness of
\mathbb{B}
.
From (
22
),
d_{P}(\widehat{P}_{n}(u_{n}),P_{X}(u_{n}))\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}0
.
Since
u_{n}\xrightharpoonup[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}u
, we also have that
d_{P}(P_{X}(u_{n}),P_{X}(u))\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}0
.
By the triangle inequality,
d_{P}(\widehat{P}_{n}(u_{n}),P_{X}(u))\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}0
. As before, because
P_{X}(u)
has connected support,

15
implies that
\widehat{m}(u_{n})\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}m(u)
. Hence
a_{n}\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{}0
, contradicting
L>0
.
This shows that
L=0
. Since
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{B}
, this completes the proof.
∎

The following lemma addresses the first term
U_{n}(x)
of (

Lemma 13
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
7
, we have
\sup_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq M}U_{n}(x)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, for any
M>0
.

Proof.

Note that, by the assumption (
13
), there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathcal{V}_{\beta+\varepsilon}\neq\emptyset
. Therefore, by Lemma
12
, for sufficiently large
n
, it holds a.s. that
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}\neq\emptyset

Furthermore, by Lemma

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Omega

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})=1
, on which

\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{m}(v)-m(v)}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,\quad\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{s}(v)-s(v)}\right|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(23)

Consider
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that
\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{s}(v)-s(v)}\right|\leq\beta/2
. Then, for any
v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}

s(v)\geq\widehat{s}(v)-\beta/2\geq\beta/2

for any

Using these bounds, for any

and all sufficiently large

, we obtain

\displaystyle U_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left|{\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}O_{v}(x;\widehat{P}_{n})-\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})}\right|\leq\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}\left|{O_{v}(x;\widehat{P}_{n})-O_{v}(x;P_{X})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}\left|{\frac{\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\widehat{m}(v)}\right|}{\widehat{s}(v)}-\frac{\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-m(v)}\right|}{s(v)}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}\frac{1}{\widehat{s}(v)\,s(v)}\Big|s(v)\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\widehat{m}(v)}\right|-\widehat{s}(v)\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-m(v)}\right|\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}\frac{1}{\widehat{s}(v)\,s(v)}\Big((\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle}\right|+\left|{m(v)}\right|)\left|{s(v)-\widehat{s}(v)}\right|+s(v)\left|{m(v)-\widehat{m}(v)}\right|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\beta^{2}}\Big((M+\mathsf{med}[\left\lVert X\right\rVert])\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{s}(v)-s(v)}\right|+2\,\mathsf{med}[\left\lVert X\right\rVert]\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{m}(v)-m(v)}\right|\Big).

By (
23
), the right-hand side converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Since this holds for every
\omega\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})=1
, the claim follows.
∎

Next, we address the second term

of (

Recall that
\Gamma_{x}(t)=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
X\sim P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{H}})
have contiguous support. Consider
\beta>0
and
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{H}
. Assume that
\left\{\Gamma_{x}\,\colon\,x\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
is equicontinuous at
t=\beta
, i.e.,

\lim_{t\to\beta}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}\left|{\Gamma_{x}(t)-\Gamma_{x}(\beta)}\right|=0.

Then
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}W_{n}(x)\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{\text{a.s.}}0
.

Proof.

Denote
\delta_{n}=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{s}(v)-s(v)}\right|
. Note that for all
v\in\mathbb{S}
we have

s(v)\geq\beta+\delta_{n}\;\implies\;\widehat{s}(v)\geq\beta\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{s}(v)\geq\beta\;\implies\;s(v)\geq\beta-\delta_{n}.

Consequently,

\mathcal{V}_{\beta+\delta_{n}}=\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\,\colon\,s(v)\geq\beta+\delta_{n}\right\}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}\subseteq\left\{v\in\mathbb{S}\,\colon\,s(v)\geq\beta-\delta_{n}\right\}=\mathcal{V}_{\beta-\delta_{n}}.

It follows that

\Gamma_{x}(\beta+\delta_{n})=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta+\delta_{n}}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})\leq\sup_{v\in\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{\beta,n}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})\leq\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta-\delta_{n}}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})=\Gamma_{x}(\beta-\delta_{n}).

Hence, we can bound

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}W_{n}(x)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}\left|{\Gamma_{x}(\beta-\delta_{n})-\Gamma_{x}(\beta+\delta_{n})}\right|.

Since
\delta_{n}=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{S}}\left|{\widehat{s}(v)-s(v)}\right|\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$n\to\infty$}]{\text{a.s.}}0
, this converges almost surely to
0
by the assumption.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

First, let
\beta_{k}\xrightarrow[\scalebox{0.7}{$k\to\infty$}]{}\beta
from the left, that is,

.
Then

\Gamma_{x}(\beta)=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})\leq\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta_{k}}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})=\Gamma_{x}(\beta_{k})

(25)

Since the unit ball

Proof of Theorem
9
.

First, note that the mapping
v\mapsto O_{v}(x;P_{X})
is Lipschitz continuous, uniformly over
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathcal{V}_{\beta/2}
. Indeed, observe that
v\mapsto\mathsf{MAD}[\left\langle X,v\right\rangle]=\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert
is
\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert
-Lipschitz, where
\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert
is the operator norm of
\Sigma^{1/2}
. Consequently, for any
v,w\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta/2}
we obtain

\displaystyle\left|{O_{v}(x;P_{X})-O_{w}(x;P_{X})}\right|=\left|{\frac{\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu,v\right\rangle}\right|}{\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert}-\frac{\left|{\left\langle x,w\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu,w\right\rangle}\right|}{\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}w\right\rVert}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\frac{\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}w\right\rVert\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu,v\right\rangle}\right|-\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert\left|{\left\langle x,w\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu,w\right\rangle}\right|}{\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}w\right\rVert}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\beta^{2}}\left(\left|{\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}w\right\rVert-\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert}\right|\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu,v\right\rangle}\right|+\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert\bigl(\left|{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle-\left\langle x,w\right\rangle}\right|+\left|{\left\langle\mu,v\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu,w\right\rangle}\right|\bigr)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\beta^{2}}\left(\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert\left\lVert v-w\right\rVert(M+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert)+\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert\left\lVert v-w\right\rVert(M+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert)\right)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert v-w\right\rVert\,\frac{8}{\beta^{2}}\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert(M+\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert)=C\left\lVert v-w\right\rVert,

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
v
and
w
.

Next, consider the Hausdorff distance

d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta})=\inf\left\{\varepsilon>0\,\colon\,\mathcal{V}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}^{\varepsilon}\wedge\mathcal{V}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\beta}^{\varepsilon}\right\},

where
\mathcal{V}_{s}^{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{V}_{s}}\left\{y\in\mathbb{H}\,\colon\,\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\leq\varepsilon\right\}
. Note that for an arbitrary fixed
x\in\mathcal{F}
and any
t
with
\beta/2\leq t<\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}
, we have

\left|{\Gamma_{x}(t)-\Gamma_{x}(\beta)}\right|=\left|{\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})-\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}}O_{v}(x;P_{X})}\right|\leq C\,d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta}).

(27)

Indeed, for any
v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
there exists

such that
\left\lVert v-w\right\rVert\leq d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta})
. Therefore,

O_{v}(x;P_{X})\leq O_{w}(x;P_{X})+C\,d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta})\leq\Gamma_{x}(t)+C\,d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta}),

and by taking the supremum over
v\in\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
, we obtain

\Gamma_{x}(\beta)-\Gamma_{x}(t)\leq C\,d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta})
. Similarly, one obtains
\Gamma_{x}(t)-\Gamma_{x}(\beta)\leq C\,d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta})
, which proves (
27
). Since the upper bound in (

) is independent of

, we also obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}\left|{\Gamma_{x}(t)-\Gamma_{x}(\beta)}\right|\leq C\,d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta}).

(28)

We claim that

\displaystyle d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta})\leq 2\max\left\{1-\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{1}-t^{2}}{\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2}}},1-\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2}}{\sigma_{1}-t^{2}}}\right\}.

(29)

This implies
d_{H}(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{V}_{\beta})\to 0
as

,
which along with the inequality in (

It now suffices to derive the upper bound in (

).
Recall that we have
s(v)=\left\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}v\right\rVert=\sqrt{\left\langle v,\Sigma v\right\rangle}
for any

.
Take
\beta<t<\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}
. Note that
\mathcal{V}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\beta}
. Take any

, i.e.,
\beta\leq s(v)<t
. Consider the eigenvector
\phi_{1}\in\mathbb{S}
of
\Sigma

\sigma_{1}
. This means that
\Sigma\phi_{1}=\sigma_{1}\phi_{1}
, and also

. Since these identities are valid for both
\phi_{1}
and
-\phi_{1}
, we may assume without loss of generality that

\theta=\arccos\left(\left\langle v,\phi_{1}\right\rangle\right)\in(0,\pi/2]

between the directions

\phi_{1}

\displaystyle s(\gamma(r))^{2}=\left\langle\gamma(r),\Sigma\gamma(r)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\alpha_{1}(r,\theta)v+\alpha_{2}(r,\theta)\phi_{1},\Sigma\left(\alpha_{1}(r,\theta)v+\alpha_{2}(r,\theta)\phi_{1}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}^{2}(r,\theta)\left\langle v,\Sigma v\right\rangle+\alpha_{2}^{2}(r,\theta)\left\langle\phi_{1},\Sigma\phi_{1}\right\rangle+2\,\alpha_{1}(r,\theta)\alpha_{2}(r,\theta)\left\langle v,\Sigma\phi_{1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{1}^{2}(r,\theta)\beta^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}(r,\theta)\sigma_{1}+2\alpha_{1}(r,\theta)\alpha_{2}(r,\theta)\sigma_{1}\cos(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}-(\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2})\left(\frac{\sin((1-r)\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}\right)^{2}=g_{\theta}(r).

, with

. As a function of

is non-increasing in

, and thus

g_{\theta}(r)\geq g_{\pi/2}(r)=\sigma_{1}-(\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2})\left(\sin((1-r)\pi/2)\right)^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }\theta\in(0,\pi/2]\mbox{ and }r\in[0,1].

Set

\tau_{0}=\tau_{0}(t)=g_{\pi/2}^{-1}(t^{2})=1-\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{1}-t^{2}}{\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2}}}\right),

(30)

where
g_{\pi/2}^{-1}
is the inverse function of
g_{\pi/2}
.
Then we have
t^{2}=g_{\pi/2}(\tau_{0})\leq g_{\theta}(\tau_{0})
for all
\theta\in(0,\pi/2]
, meaning that also
s(\gamma(\tau_{0}))^{2}\geq g_{\theta}(\tau_{0})\geq t^{2}
, hence
\gamma(\tau_{0})\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
. As for the distance of
v
and
\gamma(\tau_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert v-\gamma(\tau_{0})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle v-\gamma(\tau_{0}),v-\gamma(\tau_{0})\right\rangle=2-2\left\langle v,\gamma(\tau_{0})\right\rangle=2-2\left\langle v,\alpha_{1}(\tau_{0},\theta)v+\alpha_{2}(\tau_{0},\theta)\phi_{1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2-2\left(\alpha_{1}(\tau_{0},\theta)+\alpha_{2}(\tau_{0},\theta)\cos(\theta)\right)=2-2\cos(\tau_{0}\,\theta),

which is an increasing function of
\theta\in(0,\pi/2]
. We obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert v-\gamma(\tau_{0})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1-\cos(\tau_{0}\pi/2)\right)=2\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{1}-t^{2}}{\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2}}}\right),

where the last equality follows from (
30
)

\cos\left(\tau_{0}\pi/2\right)=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{1}-t^{2}}{\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2}}}\right)\right)=\sin\left(\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{1}-t^{2}}{\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2}}}\right)\right)=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{1}-t^{2}}{\sigma_{1}-\beta^{2}}}.

We have established that for any

there exists

Analogously, we could prove that if

This derives (

) and concludes the proof.
