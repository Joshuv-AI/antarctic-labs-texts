Patient-specific virtual surgical simulation: the preliminary exploration of surgical evaluation for difficult pediatric liver tumors
Authors: Wenli Xiu, Jie Liu, Jiatong Chen, Jingli Zhang, Chenyu Yang, Kai Wang, Rongkun Zhu, Lun Yu, Xianjun Zhou, Feifei Wang, Leonardo Borgioli, Alvaro Ducas, Shiyu Jia, Qian Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe surgical evaluation of pediatric liver tumors is challenging, especially in difficult cases that require precise surgery. Three-dimensional visualization (3D) based on two-dimensional CT (2D) has been widely used. However, virtual surgical simulation (VS), which can provide more procedural details for specific patients, is worthy of further exploration.MethodsSix pediatric liver tumor patients with the surgical difficulty increasing sequentially were selected. Recruited pediatric surgeons (15 junior and 15 senior) guided professional technicians in performing surgical evaluation using 2D, 3D, and VS sequentially. The patient-specific VS based on 3D can be constructed within the clinically permissible time window to meet clinical needs.ResultsFor objective analysis, the scores in 3D Group and VS Group were significantly higher than those in 2D Group (P < 0.0001), and there was also a statistically significant difference between 3D Group and VS Group (P < 0.0001). As the difficulty increased, the 3D Group and VS Group consistently maintained higher scores. The scores of Junior Group using 2D were significantly lower than those of Senior Group (P < 0.0001), but there was no significant statistical difference in the scores of using 3D and VS. For subjective assessment, the scores of 3D Group and VS Group were significantly higher than those of 2D Group (P < 0.0001). VS was more aligned with clinical reality compared to 3D.Conclusion3D and VS offer significant advantages in surgical evaluation compared with 2D, particularly for difficult cases and junior surgeons. The novel perspective and realistic experience provided by VS have attracted attention, and future research will further validate its potential clinical value in preoperative rehearsals and advanced training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03380-z.

Background

Pediatric liver tumors are common abdominal solid tumors in children, with malignant liver tumors ranking third among all pediatric malignant solid tumors [1]. Pediatric liver tumors often have an insidious onset and rapid growth, and by the time they are detected, the tumors are usually large and frequently involve the hepatic hilum. Even after a full course of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, some children still have tumors that involve major hepatic vessels, making treatment planning a challenging clinical issue [2, 3]. Surgical intervention is the cornerstone for the definitive diagnosis and radical treatment of these tumors. However, due to the complex anatomy and numerous variations in the liver, the evaluation of liver tumors using two-dimensional CT (2D) is limited. Additionally, the delicate organ structures and small abdominal cavity in children pose significant physiological and anatomical challenges, making surgical resection highly demanding. This is especially true for difficult pediatric liver tumors, which require precise preoperative planning and meticulous surgical operation [4, 5].

With the integration of medical and engineering disciplines and the advancement of precision surgery, three-dimensional visualization (3D) technology has been widely applied in individualized precision assessment and surgical decision-making for pediatric liver tumors [6–8]. However, the reconstructed 3D models are rigid and fixed, and therefore are unable to simulate the viscoelastic characteristics of human soft tissues or force feedback during operations. This limitation results in a lack of realism and technical training in preoperative planning, leading to inconsistencies with actual intraoperative procedures and restricting its clinical utility [8].

In recent years, virtual surgical simulation (VS) has emerged as a promising technology in the field of precision surgery. It enables comprehensive simulation of surgical procedures and rehearsal of key steps, providing realistic and real-time visual and haptic feedback. Simulations of procedures such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy and gastrointestinal endoscopy have been shown to be beneficial for developing fundamental surgical skills in medical education and surgical training [9, 10]. Furthermore, simulations of more complex procedures, such as laparoscopic/robotic hepatectomy and colectomy, are increasingly recognized for their advantages in mastering sophisticated surgical steps, enhancing advanced surgical skills, and promoting patient-specific clinical decision-making. However, the realism and real-time performance of current VS systems worldwide still require further improvement. The evolution from generic to patient-specific simulation models remains in its early stages, limiting their translation from training settings into clinical practice [11, 12].

Building on 3D models reconstructed from our wide clinical application of the Computer-Assisted Surgery System (Hisense CAS), our team has independently developed patient-specific VS models and successfully simulated tissue deformation and cutting effects during liver tumor resection through advanced software engineering [13–15]. This study aimed to compare the surgical evaluation of six pediatric patients with difficult liver tumors using 2D, 3D, and VS sequentially to further explore the potential clinical value of VS in the future.

Methods

Patient-specific cases

Six pediatric patients with difficult liver tumors who underwent surgical resection in the Department of Pediatric Surgery at the our hospital were selected, with cases arranged in order of surgical difficulty. Difficult pediatric liver tumors were defined using the following criteria: ① large volumes compressing or displacing the major liver vessels (MVs); ② tumors located in complex regions such as the central liver or multiple lobes; ③ anatomical variations in the MVs; and ④ tumors remaining close proximity to the MVs even after a full course of neoadjuvant chemotherapy [3, 16]. The surgical difficulty was assessed based on the above criteria, considering the cumulative effect of factors such as the type and extent of tumor involvement of the MVs, the presence of variations in the MVs, tumor location in complex regions, and tumor size. Detailed assessment considerations are provided in the “Surgical risk points” and “Surgical key points” sections of Table 1, and the ascending order of surgical difficulty for these six patients was determined after careful review by the chief surgeon. Table 1 also presents the general information, imaging findings, preoperative planning, as well as intraoperative and postoperative details of the six patients. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the children.

Limitations and clarification of key terms

For precise surgical evaluation, the MVs included the following: ① major portal veins (PVs): main portal vein and its bifurcation, right portal vein, and left portal vein; ② major hepatic veins (HVs): right hepatic vein, middle hepatic vein, and left hepatic vein; ③ inferior vena cava; and ④ major hepatic arteries (HAs): right hepatic artery and left hepatic artery. The normal anatomy of the MVs is defined based on the standard branching pattern: ① the main portal vein enters the first hepatic hilum, then branches into the right portal vein and left portal vein, with the right portal vein further dividing into the right anterior branch and right posterior branch; ② the right hepatic vein, middle hepatic vein, and left hepatic vein separately converge into the inferior vena cava at the second hepatic hilum; and ③ the proper hepatic artery branches into the right hepatic artery and left hepatic artery at the first hepatic hilum. Tumor proximity to the MVs is classified as follows: ① tumor within 1 cm of the vessel (degree = 0); ② tumor touching the vessel (degree = 1); ③ tumor distorting, displacing, or encasing the vessel (degree = 2); and ④ tumor thrombus within the vessel or cavernous transformation of the main portal vein (degree = 3) [4, 17].

Study design and grouping

The imaging data of six pediatric patients with difficult liver tumors were processed by a multidisciplinary team of experts with expertise in radiology, surgery, and engineering. Professional technicians operated 2D, 3D, and VS equipment. 30 pediatric surgeons, including 15 junior surgeons with less than five years of experience and 15 senior surgeons with five or more years of experience in hepatic resection, were recruited from multiple centers as participants. After filling out the “Participants’ Baseline Survey Questionnaire”, they sequentially conducted objective analysis and subjective assessment on surgical evaluation for these six patients. These methods can be divided into 2D Group, 3D Group, and VS Group (Fig. 1). The surgeons were categorized into Junior Group and Senior Group based on their experience levels.

2D Group surgical evaluation

Prior to contrast-enhanced CT, patients were required to fast for 4 to 8 h, and an indwelling needle was placed. The routine scanning range extended from the diaphragm dome to the lower edge of the pancreas. Arterial phase scanning was performed 20 s after the injection of the nonionic contrast agent, portal venous phase scanning was performed at 50 s, and equilibrium phase scanning was performed at 90 s. The scan thickness ranged from 0.625 to 2.000 mm, with a spacing of 5 mm. Under the guidance of the chief surgeon, professional technicians adjusted the three-phase images to optimal window width and level for participants to perform the 2D Group surgical evaluation. The image data were stored in Dicom format.

3D Group surgical evaluation

The 2D image data in Dicom format were imported into the Hisense CAS. The 3D model could be constructed in approximately 15 min through the following steps: ① Liver and tumor segmentation and reconstruction: ① Liver and tumor segmentation and reconstruction: Using artificial intelligence and traditional image segmentation algorithms, the liver and tumor boundaries were precisely segmented, and a 1:1 three-dimensional model was reconstructed. The liver and tumor volumes were automatically calculated based on imaging scan parameters (pixel spacing, slice spacing, etc.) and displayed in the lower left corner. ② Vessel extraction: Major hepatic vessels were automatically reconstructed by selecting seed points and applying vessel tracking algorithms. ③ 3D registration and display: The above 3D data were integrated and registered in three dimensions, allowing for arbitrary rotation and zooming. Any organ or vessel could be rendered transparent or semitransparent, allowing for 3D spatial distance analysis to evaluate the positional relationship between intrahepatic vessels and the tumor, as well as vascular involvement.

Under the guidance of the chief surgeon, professional technicians performed further 3D preoperative planning. Using 3D vascular basin analysis and 3D cutting tools, anatomical liver lobe/segment resections, tumor dissections, or 3D non-anatomical resections were simulated. The virtual resections provided residual liver volume information to assist in planning the surgical approach [6, 13]. Based on the above 3D visualization results, participants conducted the 3D surgical evaluation, and the 3D reconstructed models were exported in Stl format.

VS Group surgical evaluation

The VS program was independently developed using C++ (implemented on a Dell Precision 5820 with Windows 10 64-bit, programmed in the Microsoft Visual Studio 2015 integrated development environment, and utilizing OpenGL for graphics rendering). The 3D models in Stl format were imported into this VS program, and the VS models could be constructed in approximately 15 min through the following steps: ① Construction of the VS model: The surface models were discretized into voxels for volumetric representation. Adjacent voxels were connected by links that could be cut to model surgical cutting. Fine surface meshes for realistic visual feedback and collision processing were constructed in the voxel grid, while coarse octree meshes for deformation were constructed on top of the voxel grid. ② Construction of perihepatic ligaments: Ligaments are thin structures and are difficult to voxelize. Therefore, virtual ligament envelopes that encase the ligaments and are thicker than the ligaments were constructed using 3DMax software. The virtual ligament envelopes were used for deformation but were not rendered, whereas the real ligaments were embedded in and deformed alongside with them. Fixed boundary conditions on the outer edges of the ligaments were applied to restrict the movement of the model during deformation. ③Application of elastic deformation parameters: The biomechanical properties, such as Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and density of different soft tissues (i.e., liver and tumor), were set for the octree elements. A corotational linear finite element algorithm was used for deformation. Both collision and deformation were performed on the GPU.

The VS was performed by professional technicians using two Phantom Desktop haptic devices to control the movement of the virtual surgical instruments. Under the guidance of the chief surgeon, the entire surgical procedure was simulated on the virtual model according to the surgical path planned using the 3D visualization model. Adjustments to the viewing angle and transparency were made to obtain detailed information about the surgical process [14, 15, 18, 19]. Based on the above virtual surgical simulation process, participants conducted the VS surgical evaluation.

Participant management and data statistics

When evaluating each patient in a group, each participant was required to complete a 20-item “Surgical Objective Analysis Questionnaire”. This questionnaire objectively assessed tumor location (lobes, segments and hepatic hilum), tumor size (diameter and volume), vascular involvement (type and extent), and preoperative planning, with each item scored either 0 or 5 points. After completing each group, each participant filled out a 20-item “Surgical Subjective Assessment Questionnaire” to subjectively evaluate the satisfaction and usefulness of the three methods using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). All participants were given unlimited time to complete both questionnaires. The final scores of both questionnaires were recorded on a percentage scale, and the participants were also asked open-ended questions about their evaluation experience. The questionnaires related to this article can be found in the supplemental material.

Statistical methods

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and R software. Normally distributed measurement data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (Height="17"/>± s), whereas nonnormally distributed measurement data are expressed as the median and interquartile range M (Q1, Q3). Count data are expressed as numbers (%). For comparisons among 2D Group, 3D Group and VS Group (non-normally distributed, paired data), the Friedman test was performed, and effect size was measured using Kendall’s W concordance of coefficient. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni corrections. Comparisons among each challenging patient (non-normally distributed, paired data) were performed using the same test and correction methods. Comparisons between Junior Group and Senior Group (non-normally distributed) were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and effect sizes were reported as Rosenthal’s r. Independent samples t-test was used to compare normally distributed continuous data and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare count data between the two groups. For comparisons of subjective assessment results among the three groups (normally distributed, paired data), a repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted, and effect size was reported as partial eta-squared (ƞ2p). Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests with Bonferroni corrections. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analysis of each challenging patient (Fig. 3B) revealed significant differences among the three groups (P < 0.0001 for each patient). Post-hoc analysis showed that the scores for correct answers in both 3D Group and VS Group were significantly higher than those in 2D Group (P < 0.0001 for each patient). Between the 3D and VS Groups, statistically significant differences were observed for Patients 1, 4, 5, and 6 (P = 0.025, 0.0084, 0.0075, and 0.0106, respectively), whereas no significant differences were found for Patients 2 (P = 0.059) and 3 (P = 0.1107). The learning effect and the increasing difficulty of the patients may have contributed to these results. Furthermore, as the difficulty of surgical evaluation for challenging patients increased, the scores obtained via 2D gradually decreased (P < 0.0001). In contrast, participants using 3D and VS consistently maintained high scores (3D Group, P = 0.2165; VS Group, P = 0.0989). This indicated that 3D Group and VS Group maintained more stable evaluation results.

These findings objectively indicate that 3D and VS surgical evaluations offer greater clinical efficiency than 2D, particularly providing greater advantages for junior surgeons when evaluating difficult surgical cases.

Discussion

The liver, as a parenchymal organ, contains major vessels, such as the inferior vena cava, hepatic veins, portal vein, and hepatic artery, which exhibit complex paths and frequent anatomical variations among individuals [20]. For patients with difficult liver tumors, it is essential to rely on individualized imaging data to delineate the internal structures. This ensures complete tumor resection, protection of critical vessels, and preservation of sufficient liver volume, ultimately guaranteeing surgical safety and improving patient outcomes [21]. Pediatric cases often draw on research and experience from adult cases. However, children are not simply smaller versions of adults. Pediatric liver tumors are often large in volume, frequently involve major hepatic vessels, and leave minimal residual normal liver tissue. Additionally, the delicate and fine structures of children’s organs, combined with their poor surgical tolerance, make liver surgery in this population particularly challenging [22]. This study selected six clinically challenging pediatric liver tumor patients with progressively increasing surgical difficulties to provide representative and meaningful research materials.

In clinical practice, the surgical evaluation of liver tumors often relies on traditional 2D imaging data. However, the intricate branching and path of internal vessels typically require surgeons to spend considerable time analyzing and confirming details on solid anatomical knowledge and extensive imaging experience, resulting in subjective interpretations [23]. In recent years, precision liver surgery, which is centered on 3D visualization technology, has gained widespread adoption and has been proven to provide significant clinical value in individualized precision assessment and surgical decision-making. Advances in AI-based medical image segmentation have improved the efficiency and accuracy of this process [24]. This study involved 30 surgeons who analyzed 2D and 3D imaging data from the six difficult patients mentioned previously. The results demonstrated that 3D significantly outperformed 2D imaging, in terms of overall scores, scores for each individual patient, and regardless of whether the surgeons were junior or senior. The 3D approach resulted in approximately 90% accuracy in the objective analysis results. This leap from 2D to 3D facilitates the acquisition of objective clinical data and the prediction of surgical outcomes, enhancing the efficiency and experience of surgical decision-making. Notably, it is particularly beneficial for junior surgeons, offering them a more user-friendly and effective tool.

However, during actual surgical procedures, the liver and its internal vascular structures inevitably deform and undergo positional and morphological changes, such as grasping, pressing and cutting, due to surgical manipulation. At this point, the preoperative 3D visualization planning path, which lacks the ability to simulate elastic deformation, may not fully align with the actual intraoperative path. This discrepancy can lead to difficulties in precise intraoperative judgment, potentially resulting in incomplete tumor resection or insufficient residual liver volume, thereby adversely affecting postoperative recovery and treatment outcomes in pediatric patients [25]. On the other hand, VS leverages the advantages of simulating the elasticity of human organs and tissues and replicating the entire surgical process in a realistic and real-time manner. VS allows surgeons to practice repeatedly without harm to the patient and at low economic cost. Numerous studies have confirmed that VS can shorten the learning curve for surgical skills [26, 27]. However, constructing virtual simulation systems typically requires months or even years, and most internationally available surgical simulators (such as LapSim, LAP Mentor III, and LaparoS) are based on generic anatomical models, significantly limiting their clinical applicability [9, 26, 28]. This study provided a preliminary exploration of the application of VS in pediatric hepatobiliary surgical evaluation, suggesting that its application in training programs and safety assessment merits further investigation.

Currently, patient-specific 3D printing and extended reality (XR) technologies are gradually being applied in clinical practice and precision surgery. 3D printing technology transforms virtual models into real-world objects, providing a more realistic sense of spatial relationships and anatomical detail. But its application is limited by high time and production costs [29]. Virtual reality (VR) technology, which places 3D models in an immersive and interactive virtual environment to assist surgery, has been proven to be an effective tool for liver surgery education, training, and preoperative planning [30, 31]. Augmented reality (AR) technology overlays virtual information onto the real environment to provide an additional perceptual layer, while mixed reality (MR) technology further allows interaction between virtual elements and the real world [29, 32]. These technologies have attracted growing attention for their potential to enhance the safety and comfort of complex abdominal and liver surgeries and to reduce operative time. The integration of these advanced technologies with laparoscopic and robotic surgical imaging may offer even greater advantages for real-time intraoperative navigation and remote surgical guidance in the future. VS enables for simulated surgery on virtual models, and the operative practice and procedural information it provides also demonstrate significant clinical potential. This study, built on years of interdisciplinary collaboration, successfully achieved the transformation from individualized 3D models to virtual simulation models, enabling preoperative VS for specific patients in a very short time. Preliminary clinical evaluations have confirmed that VS surgical evaluation performs as well as 3D in clinical applications. Its unique immersive experience and the detailed procedural information it provides are valuable for enhancing surgical proficiency and accumulating surgical experience. Additionally, our results revealed an encouraging level of enthusiasm and interest among surgeons for VS, with many expressing a strong desire for hands-on experience and further exploration of its practical applications.

In fact, the development of VS has largely stalled at the technical level of achieving realism and real-time performance. For example, Yukio Oshiro et al.33 developed the Liver-sim system, which successfully performed preoperative simulations. However, Liver-sim is limited to creating cutting planes at specified positions and orientations and lacks the ability to perform freeform cutting. Its “real-time” performance of the simulation model is delayed by 1 ~ 2 s after the cutting operations, with a frame rate of only 0.5 ~ 1.0 Hz, which is far below the 30 Hz required for visual continuity. Additionally, constructing the simulation model takes 1 ~ 2 h, incurring significant labor and time costs [33]. Similarly, Yuichiro Uchida et al. [25] developed the Resection Process Map system, and other related studies face comparable limitations. In contrast, this study has achieved a streamlined process from 2D CT to 3D visualization and the construction of a virtual simulation model in just 30 min. The virtual models can deform under external interactions, allowing for realistic surgical operations such as grasping, pressing, and cutting at any position and in any direction. The surfaces and cut surfaces of the models are rendered with detailed textures and lighting effects, showing realistic visual feedback. The simulation frame rate reaches an average of 150 Hz, demonstrating highly efficient real-time performance. This indicates that our technology has successfully operated within the clinically permissible time window to meet the clinical needs of preoperative surgical simulation. The next step can be to further conduct related preoperative rehearsals or simulated training studies for surgeons.

However, since the VS program is in the preliminary stage of clinical exploration, this study has several limitations. First, although representative patients of difficult pediatric liver tumors were selected, the sample size was small. Second, as all participants sequentially evaluated the same patients using 2D, 3D, and VS methods, potential learning or order effects may have introduced bias into the results. Although this effect may have been mitigated by allowing participants unlimited time for evaluation and the opportunity to correct previous answers, it could not be completely excluded. In future research, we plan to expand the number of cases and conduct randomized controlled trials in a prospective interventional setting to further explore the clinical utility of VS in preoperative planning and intraoperative navigation. For example, it may provide early warnings for major vascular structures and resection margins during surgery and help evaluate its impact on operative time and postoperative short- and long-term outcomes. In addition, as the system was operated by professional technicians under the guidance of the chief surgeon, this may have limited participants’ degree of autonomous operation, thereby reducing their hands-on experience of identifying real surgical risks and key steps. Future studies allowing surgeons to directly operate the VS program may better elucidate its potential benefits in skill development and experience accumulation.

Conclusion

This study recruited 30 surgeons to compare 2D, 3D, and VS surgical evaluation of six difficult pediatric liver tumor patients. The results confirmed patient-specific 3D and VS offer significant objective analysis advantages and improved subjective assessment experience, particularly for difficult cases and junior surgeons. As the difficulty of surgical evaluation increased, 3D and VS performed better and more stable compared to 2D. In particular, VS provided a novel perspective on surgical evaluation and a realistic experience of the surgical process. Future prospective multicenter studies will further validate its potential clinical value in preoperative rehearsals for patient-specific cases and advanced training for difficult cases.
