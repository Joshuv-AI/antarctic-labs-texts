Douglas-Rachford algorithm for nonmonotone multioperator inclusion problems
Jan Harold Alcantara, Akiko Takeda

Abstract
The Douglas-Rachford algorithm is a classic splitting method for finding a zero of the sum of two maximal monotone operators. It has also been applied to settings that involve one weakly and one strongly monotone operator. In this work, we extend the Douglas-Rachford algorithm to address multioperator inclusion problems involving m m ( m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 ) weakly and strongly monotone operators, reformulated as a two-operator inclusion in a product space. By selecting appropriate parameters, we establish the convergence of the algorithm to a fixed point, from which solutions can be extracted. Furthermore, we illustrate its applicability to sum-of- m m -functions minimization problems characterized by weakly convex and strongly convex functions. For general nonconvex problems in finite-dimensional spaces, comprising Lipschitz continuously differentiable functions and a proper closed function, we provide global subsequential convergence guarantees. Keywords. Douglas-Rachford algorithm; product space reformulation; nonmonotone inclusion; generalized monotone operator

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the problem

\text{Find~}x\in\mathcal{H}~\text{such that }0\in A_{1}(x)+A_{2}(x)+\cdots+A_{m}(x)

(1.1)

where
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{m}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
are set-valued operators on a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. We assume that each operator is accessible through its resolvent, and therefore we focus on so-called
backward algorithms
for solving (
1.1
).

A popular backward algorithm for solving (
1.1
) when
m=2
is the classical
Douglas-Rachford
(DR) algorithm, which was initially proposed in 1956 by Douglas and Rachford
[
11
]
as a numerical method for solving linear systems related to heat conduction. Later, Lions and Mercier (1979) extended its scope, making it applicable to finding zeros of the sum of two maximal monotone operators
[
16
]
. In particular, it can be used to minimize the sum of two convex functions, as this task is equivalent to finding the zeros of the sum of the subdifferential operators of the functions.

Extensions to non-maximal monotone cases have been explored in subsequent works. For the specific case of a two-term optimization problem involving a weakly convex and a strongly convex function in
\mathcal{H}={\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
,
[
13
]
established that the “shadow sequence” of the DR algorithm, with a sufficiently small step size, is globally convergent to the optimal solution when the sum of the functions is strongly convex. The subdifferential operators of these functions belong to the class of
generalized monotone operators
, which was the central focus of
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
. These works specifically extended the analysis of the DR algorithm to accommodate this broader class of operators in real Hilbert spaces (not necessarily finite dimensional), providing convergence guarantees under generalized monotonicity conditions. Specifically, when
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are maximal
\sigma_{1}
-monotone and maximal
\sigma_{2}
-monotone operators (see
Definition

2.1
) with
\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}>0
, the shadow sequence of the DR algorithm is guaranteed to globally converge to a zero of
A_{1}+A_{2}
, provided the step size is sufficiently small.

On the other hand, for the
m
-operator inclusion problem (
1.1
), a traditional strategy is to first reformulate it as a two-operator problem via
Pierra’s product space reformulation

[
20
,
21
]
:

\text{Find}~\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{m}~\text{such that }0\in\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}),

(1.2)

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathcal{H}^{m}
,
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq A_{1}(x_{1})\times\cdots\times A_{m}(x_{m})
and
\mathbf{G}\coloneqq N_{\mathbf{D}_{m}}
, the normal cone operator to
\mathbf{D}_{m}\coloneqq\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathcal{H}^{m}:x_{1}=\cdots=x_{m}\}
. The defined operators retain key properties:
\mathbf{F}
is maximal monotone when each
A_{i}
is maximal monotone, while
\mathbf{G}
is maximal monotone due to the convexity of
\mathbf{D}_{m}

[
3
, Proposition 26.4]
. Consequently, the shadow sequence of the standard DR algorithm applied to (
1.2
) is globally convergent to a zero of
\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{G}
, which corresponds to a solution of (
1.1
). However, one major drawback of the reformulation (
1.2
) is its incompatibility with the theory for sum of two generalized monotone operators. Specifically, if
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}
are maximal
\sigma_{\mathbf{F}}
- and
\sigma_{\mathbf{G}}
- monotone with
\sigma_{\mathbf{F}}+\sigma_{\mathbf{G}}>0
, then one must have
\sigma_{\mathbf{F}}>0
since
\sigma_{\mathbf{G}}=0
. On the other hand,
\sigma_{\mathbf{F}}=\min\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}\}
if
A_{i}
is maximal
\sigma_{i}
-monotone (see
Proposition

4.1
). Hence,
\sigma_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,m
, making it impossible for the reformulation (
1.2
) to handle cases where at least one
\sigma_{i}<0
.

Contributions of this work

In this work, our primary goal is to extend the existing convergence theory for the two-operator inclusion problem involving generalized maximal monotone operators to the case of the

m
-operator inclusion problem (
1.1
). The main contributions are as follows:

1.

We establish the convergence theory for the DR algorithm applied to a certain two-operator reformulation of (
1.1
), distinct from Pierra’s product space reformulation (
1.2
). Specifically,
Theorem

4.14
shows that when the operators
A_{i}
are maximal
\sigma_{i}
-monotone such that
\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{m}>0
, the derived DR algorithm with an appropriate step size achieves global convergence to a fixed point, which corresponds to a solution of (
1.1
). These results cannot be recovered by directly applying
[
10
,
12
]
to our reformulation. By contrast, our refined analysis provides stronger guarantees: it relaxes the requirements on the
\sigma_{i}
and permits larger step-size ranges, whereas a direct application of
[
10
,
12
]
would require significantly stricter conditions and yield smaller step sizes (see
Remark

4.15
).

2.

A secondary contribution of this work is the introduction of a flexible product space reformulation for (
1.1
) that does not require generalized maximal monotonicity assumptions. Building on Campoy’s product space reformulation
[
8
]
, which originates from
[
14
]
, the proposed formulation is valid for arbitrary
m
-inclusion problems. Unlike previous approaches, it is independent of (generalized) monotonicity conditions but reduces to Campoy’s formulation when generalized monotone operators are present.

3.

We apply our results to sum-of-
m
-functions unconstrained optimization problems (see (
5.1
)) involving weakly and strongly convex functions. For general nonconvex problems in finite-dimensional spaces, we prove global subsequential convergence under the condition that all but one function have Lipschitz continuous gradients, with the remaining function being any proper closed function.

Organization of the paper

In
Section

2
, we review some background materials on set-valued operators, generalized monotonicity and extended real-valued functions. We recall Campoy’s product space reformulation in
Section

3.1
, and present our flexible reformulation in
Section

3.2
. Based on this, the proposed Douglas-Rachford algorithm is presented in
Section

3.3
. Our convergence analysis and main results for the inclusion problem are presented in
Section

4
, and the applications to nonconvex optimization are discussed in
Section

5
. Concluding remarks are given in
Section

6
.

Preliminaries

Throughout this paper,
\mathcal{H}
denotes a real Hilbert space endowed with the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and induced norm
\|\cdot\|
. For any real numbers
\alpha,\beta\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
and any
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
, we recall the following identity:

{\left\|{\alpha x+\beta y}\right\|}^{2}=\alpha(\alpha+\beta){\left\|{x}\right\|}^{2}+\beta(\alpha+\beta){\left\|{y}\right\|}^{2}-\alpha\beta{\left\|{x-y}\right\|}^{2}.

(2.1)

When
\alpha+\beta\neq 0
, (
2.1
) is equivalent to

\textstyle\alpha{\left\|{x}\right\|}^{2}+\beta{\left\|{y}\right\|}^{2}=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}{\left\|{x-y}\right\|}^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}{\left\|{\alpha x+\beta y}\right\|}^{2}.

(2.2)

A sequence
\{x^{k}\}
is said to be
Fejér monotone
with respect to a nonempty subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{H}
if

\forall z\in S,~\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad{\left\|{x^{k+1}-z}\right\|}\leq{\left\|{x^{k}-z}\right\|}.

We use
\to
and
\rightharpoonup
to denote strong and weak convergence, respectively.

2.1
Set-valued operators

A set-valued operator
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
maps each point
x\in\mathcal{H}
to a subset
A(x)
of
\mathcal{H}
, which is not necessarily nonempty. The image of a subset
D\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is given by
A(D)\coloneqq\bigcup_{x\in D}A(x)
. The
domain
and
range
of
A
are given respectively by

\displaystyle\dom(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{H}:A(x)\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\ran(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{y\in\mathcal{H}:y\in A(x)~\text{for some }x\in\mathcal{H}\}.

The
graph
of
A
is the subset of
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
given by

\gra(A)\coloneqq\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}:y\in A(x)\}.

The
inverse
of
A
, denoted by
A^{-1}
, is the set-valued operator whose graph is given by

\gra(A^{-1})=\{(y,x)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}:(x,y)\in\gra(A)\}.

The
zeros
and
fixed points
of
A
are given respectively by

\displaystyle\zer(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq A^{-1}(0)=\{x:0\in A(x)\},

\displaystyle\Fix(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{H}:x\in A(x)\}.

The
resolvent
of
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
with parameter
\gamma>0
, denoted by
J_{\gamma A}:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
, is defined by

J_{\gamma A}\coloneqq(\Id+\gamma A)^{-1},

where
\Id:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is the identity operator
\Id(x)=x
. The
reflected resolvent
of
A
with parameter
\gamma>0
is given by

R_{\gamma A}\coloneqq 2J_{\gamma A}-\Id.

2.2
Generalized monotone operators

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
and let
\sigma\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
. We say that
A
is
\sigma
-monotone
if

\left\langle x-y,u-v\right\rangle\geq\sigma{\left\|{x-y}\right\|}^{2}\quad\forall(x,u),(y,v)\in\gra(A).

A
is
monotone
when
\sigma=0
,
strongly monotone
if
\sigma>0
and
weakly monotone
if
\sigma<0
. Moreover,
A
is
maximal
\sigma
-monotone
if
A
is
\sigma
-monotone and there is no
\sigma
-monotone operator whose graph properly contains
\gra(A)
.

A
is
maximal monotone
when
\sigma=0
,
maximal strongly monotone
if
\sigma>0
and
maximal weakly monotone
if
\sigma<0
.

We summarize some facts about maximal monotone operators.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A,B:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
be maximal monotone operators. Then the following holds

1.

A(x)
is convex for any
x\in\mathcal{H}
.

2.

If
\interior(\dom(A))\cap\dom(B)\neq\emptyset
, then
A+B
is maximal monotone.

Proof.

Part (i) holds by
[
3
, Proposition 20.36]
).
Part (ii) follows from
[
23
, Theorems 1 and 2]
.
∎

We also recall an important characterization of maximal
\sigma
-monotone operators.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
and let
\sigma\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
.
Then
A
is maximal
\sigma
-monotone if and only if
A-\sigma\Id
is maximal monotone.

Proof.

See
[
4
, Lemma 2.8]
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
be
\sigma
-monotone, and let
\gamma>0
such that
1+\gamma\sigma>0
. Then
\dom(J_{\gamma A})=\mathcal{H}
if and only if
A
is maximal
\sigma
-monotone.

Proof.

See
[
10
, Proposition 3.4(ii)]

∎

2.3
Extended real-valued functions

Let
f:\mathcal{H}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be an extended real-valued function. The
domain
of
f
is given by the set
\dom(f)=\{x\in\mathcal{H}:f(x)<\infty\}
. We say that
f
is a
proper
function if
\dom(f)\neq\emptyset
, and that
f
is
closed
if it is lower semicontinuous.
f
is said to be a
\sigma_{{}_{f}}
-convex
function if

f-\frac{\sigma_{{}_{f}}}{2}{\left\|{\cdot}\right\|}^{2}
is convex for some

\sigma_{{}_{f}}\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
. If
\sigma_{{}_{f}}>0
, then
f
is

\sigma_{{}_{f}}
-strongly convex
. If
\sigma_{{}_{f}}\leq 0
, we denote

\rho_{f}\coloneqq-\sigma_{{}_{f}}
and call
f
a

\rho_{f}
-weakly convex function
. In other words,
f
is

\rho_{f}
-weakly convex for
\rho_{f}\geq 0
if
f+\frac{\rho_{f}}{2}{\left\|{\cdot}\right\|}^{2}
is convex.

The
subdifferential
of
f
is the set-valued operator
\partial f:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
given by

\displaystyle\partial f(x)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{z\in\mathcal{H}:\exists\{(x^{k},z^{k})\}\text{ s.t. }x^{k}\xrightarrow{f}x,~z^{k}\in\hat{\partial}f(x^{k}),~\text{and}~z^{k}\to z\}&\text{if~}x\in\dom(f),\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.3)

where
x^{k}\xrightarrow{f}x
means
x^{k}\to x
and
f(x^{k})\to f(x)
, and

\textstyle\hat{\partial}f(x)\coloneqq\left\{z\in\mathcal{H}:\liminf_{\bar{x}\to x,\bar{x}\neq x}\frac{f(\bar{x})-f(x)-\left\langle z,\bar{x}-x\right\rangle}{{\left\|{\bar{x}-x}\right\|}}\geq 0\right\}.

When
f
is convex,
Eq.

2.3
coincides with the classical subdifferential in convex analysis:

\partial f(x)=\{z\in\mathcal{H}:f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle z,y-x\right\rangle,~\forall y\in\mathcal{H}\}.

The
indicator function
of a set
D\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is the function
\delta_{D}:\mathcal{H}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
, such that
\delta_{D}(x)=0
if
x\in D
and
\delta_{D}(x)=+\infty
if
x\notin D
. If
D
is closed and convex, then
\delta_{D}
is a convex function whose subdifferential coincides with the
normal cone
to
C
, denoted by
N_{D}
:

\textstyle\partial\delta_{D}(x)=N_{D}(x)=\begin{cases}\{z\in\mathcal{H}:\left\langle z,y-x\right\rangle\leq 0,~\forall y\in D\}&\text{if~}x\in D,\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
f:\mathcal{H}\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
is continuously differentiable, the subdifferential reduces to
\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}
for any
x\in\mathcal{H}
. We say that
f
is
L_{{}_{f}}
-smooth
if its gradient satisfies

{\left\|{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}\right\|}\leq L_{{}_{f}}{\left\|{x-y}\right\|},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

If
f
is
L_{{}_{f}}
-smooth, we have from
[
3
, Lemma 2.64(i)]
the following inequality

\textstyle\left|f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\right|\leq\frac{L_{{}_{f}}}{2}{\left\|{y-x}\right\|}^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H},

(2.4)

which is also known as the
descent lemma
. If
f
is
L_{{}_{f}}
-smooth and convex, then (
2.4
) is equivalent to (see
[
3
, Theorem 18.15]
)

\textstyle f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2L_{{}_{f}}}{\left\|{\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)}\right\|}^{2}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H},

(2.5)

and

\textstyle\left\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{L_{f}}{\left\|{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}\right\|}^{2}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

(2.6)

For a proper, closed function
f:\mathcal{H}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
, the
proximal mapping
of
f
is given by

\textstyle{\rm prox}_{\gamma f}(x)\coloneqq\argmin_{w\in\mathcal{H}}\,f(w)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|w-x\|^{2},\quad\gamma>0.

(2.7)

From the optimality condition of (
2.7
),
we have that if
y\in{\rm prox}_{\gamma f}(x)
, then
x-y\in\gamma\partial f(y)
.
That is,

{\rm prox}_{\gamma f}(x)\subseteq J_{\gamma\partial f}(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{H}.

(2.8)

Note that equality in (
2.8
) holds whenever
f
is convex. By contrast, strict inclusion can occur for nonconvex
f
. For instance, if
f(t)=-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}
, then
{\rm prox}_{\gamma f}(t)=\varnothing
for every
\gamma>1
, whereas
J_{\gamma\partial f}(t)=\{\,t/(1-\gamma)\,\}
for every
\gamma\neq 1
.

3
A general product space reformulation and the Douglas-Rachford algorithm

In
Section

3.1
, we recall the product space reformulation by
[
8
]
(inspired by
[
14
]
) that relies on maximal monotonicity of the operators. In the absence of this assumption, we present an alternative product space reformulation in
Section

3.2
. Some fundamental formulas for resolvents of operators defining the reformulation are established in
Section

3.3
.

3.1
Campoy’s product space reformulation

Denote

\mathcal{H}^{m-1}=\mathcal{H}\times\overset{(m-1)}{\cdots}\times\mathcal{H}
, which is a Hilbert space with inner product

\left\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left\langle x_{i},y_{i}\right\rangle\quad\forall\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1}),~\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m-1}),

and define

\mathbf{D}_{m-1}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m-1})\in\mathcal{H}^{m-1}:x_{1}=\cdots=x_{m-1}\}.

We also denote by
\bm{\Delta}_{m-1}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{m-1}
the embedding operator
x\mapsto(x,\cdots,x)
.
The following result is from
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
be maximal monotone operators.
Define the set-valued operators
\mathbf{F},\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}:\mathcal{H}^{m-1}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}^{m-1}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq A_{1}(x_{1})\times\cdots\times A_{m-1}(x_{m-1}),

(3.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x})+N_{\mathbf{D}_{m-1}}(\mathbf{x}),

(3.2)

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\frac{1}{m-1}A_{m}(x_{1})\times\cdots\frac{1}{m-1}A_{m}(x_{m-1}).

(3.3)

Then
\mathbf{F}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
are maximal monotone. Moreover,

\zer(\mathbf{F}+\widetilde{\mathbf{G}})=\bm{\Delta}_{m-1}\left(\zer\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}\right)\right).

(3.4)

By (
3.4
), the
m
-operator inclusion problem (
1.1
) can be equivalently recast as a two-operator problem

\text{Find }\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{m-1}~\text{such that }\mathbf{0}\in\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})+\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x}).

(3.5)

On the other hand, the Pierra’s product space reformulation (
1.2
) is a two-operator inclusion problem defined on the space
\mathcal{H}^{m}
. Consequently, the ambient space of Campoy’s reformulation (
3.5
) has dimension reduced by
\dim(\mathcal{H})
, which is more desirable in practice
[
17
]
. Note that the reformulation (
3.5
) has also been used in
[
14
, Theorem 2]
.

We remark that it is straightforward to verify that “
\supseteq
” in (
3.4
) holds without the maximal monotonicity assumption. To motivate the product space reformulation in
Section

3.2
, we briefly recall the proof of the inclusion “
\subseteq
”, highlighting the role of maximal monotonicity. For any
\mathbf{x}\in\zer(\mathbf{F}+\widetilde{\mathbf{G}})
, we have that
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})+\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x})+N_{\mathbf{D}_{m-1}}(\mathbf{x})
. Then
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{D}_{m-1}
so that
\mathbf{x}=(x,\cdots,x)
and there exist
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{v}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{w}\in N_{\mathbf{D}_{m-1}}(\mathbf{x})
such that
\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}
. By the definition of
\mathbf{F}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}
, it follows that
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m-1})
where
u_{i}\in A_{i}(x)
, and
\mathbf{v}=\frac{1}{m-1}(v_{1},\dots,v_{m-1})
where
v_{i}\in A_{m}(x)
for
i=1,\dots,m-1
.
Noting that
\mathbf{w}\in N_{\mathbf{D}_{m-1}}(\mathbf{x})
, the normal cone to
\mathbf{D}_{m-1}
is given by
[
3
, Proposition 26.4]

N_{\mathbf{D}_{m-1}}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{D}_{m-1}^{\perp}=\{\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m-1}):\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}w_{i}=0\}&\text{if }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{D}_{m-1},\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and
-\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}
, we obtain

0=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}-w_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}u_{i}+\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}v_{i}.

It is clear that the first term on the rightmost side belongs to
A_{1}(x)+\cdots+A_{m-1}(x)
. On the other hand, we have from
Lemma

2.2
(i) that
A_{m}(x)
is a convex set by the maximal monotonicity of
A_{m}
. Consequently, we see that
\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}v_{i}\in A_{m}(x)
since
v_{i}\in A_{m}(x)
. Putting these together, we see that
0\in A_{1}(x)+\cdots+A_{m}(x)
,
i.e.,

x\in\zer\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}\right)
. Thus, we have shown that “
\subseteq
” holds in (
3.4
).

Observe that the convexity of
A_{m}(x)
, which is a consequence of the maximal monotonicity of
A_{m}
, plays a crucial role to guarantee that (
3.4
) holds. In the absence of this assumption, the set on the left-hand side of (
3.4
) may properly contain the right-hand side.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}={\rm I}\!{\rm R}
,
A_{1}\equiv 0
,
A_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x-1
and
A_{3}(x)=0
if
x<1
,
A_{3}(x)=1
if
x>1
and
A_{3}(1)=\{0,1\}
.
Observe that
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
are monotone functions and
\zer(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3})=\emptyset
. On the other hand, we have
\mathbf{F}(1,1)=(0,-1/2)
and

(0,1/2)\in\widetilde{K}(1,1)
, so that
(1,1)\in\zer(\mathbf{F}+\widetilde{\mathbf{G}})
. Hence, (
3.4
) does not hold. Note that in this case,
A_{3}
is not maximal monotone. In particular,
A_{3}(1)=\{0,1\}
is not a convex set, which precludes 1 from being an element of
\zer(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3})
.

3.2
A product space reformulation without convex-valuedness

The disadvantage of the reformulation (
3.5

3.3
Douglas-Rachford Algorithm

We now consider the Douglas-Rachford (DR) algorithm to the two-operator reformulation (

J_{\gamma\mathbf{F}}
and

The resolvent
J_{\gamma\mathbf{F}}

\mathbf{F}
.
On the other hand,

is not straightforward due to the presence of arbitrary weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m-1}

warped resolvent
introduced in
[
7

Definition 3.4
.

Let
A:\mathcal{H}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}
and
\Lambda:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be an invertible linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
. The
\Lambda
-warped resolvent
of
A
with parameter
\lambda>0
is defined by
J_{\lambda A}^{\Lambda}\coloneqq(\Id+\lambda\Lambda^{-1}\circ A)^{-1}.

We now show that for
\bm{\Lambda}
given in (
3.6
), we can calculate the
\bm{\Lambda}
-warped resolvents of
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathbf{F}:\mathcal{H}^{m-1}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{H}^{m-1}
be given by (
3.1
) and let
\bm{\Lambda}
be defined by (
3.6
) for some
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m-1}\in(0,+\infty)

\lambda>0

\displaystyle J_{\lambda\mathbf{F}}^{\bm{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=J_{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{1}}A_{1}}(x_{1})\times\cdots\times J_{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{m-1}}A_{m-1}}(x_{m-1}),

(3.12)

for any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})\in\mathcal{H}^{m-1}

Proof.

For
\mathbf{F}
given by (

(\bfId+\lambda\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\circ\mathbf{F})(\mathbf{x})=\left(\Id+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{1}}A_{1}(x_{1})\right)\times\cdots\times\left(\Id+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{m-1}}A_{m-1}(x_{m-1})\right).

Noting the separability of the above operator, it is not difficult to prove that the formula given in (

The warped resolvent of

Proposition 3.6
.

Let

Let

\mathbf{y}\in\hat{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{x})+N_{\mathbf{D}_{m-1}}(\mathbf{x})

\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m-1})\in A_{m}(x)\times\cdots\times A_{m}(x)
such that

.
Let

where the first equality holds since

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x})

Remark 4.8
.

As indicated in its proof,
Proposition

4.9
is a direct application of
[
10
, Theorem 4.5(ii)]
, which provides the convergence of the Douglas-Rachford algorithm (with equal weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m-1}
) for finding the zeros of the sum of two operators. It is worth noting that
Proposition

4.9
(A) also covers the situation where one operator among
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m-1}
is only
\sigma_{i}
–weakly monotone. In that case the condition enforces
\sigma_{m}>-(m-1)\sigma_{i},

i.e., the strong monotonicity modulus required of
A_{m}
grows linearly with
m
. For large
m
, this demands an impractically large modulus.

Our contribution strengthens
Proposition

4.9
in three directions (see also
Remark

4.15
):
(i) we relax the moduli requirement from

\min_{i=1,\dots,m-1}\sigma_{i}+\tfrac{\sigma_{m}}{m-1}>0

to the significantly weaker condition
\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{m}>0;

(ii) we obtain a strictly larger admissible stepsize window ensuring convergence; and
(iii) we establish convergence for arbitrary weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m-1}
(not just the uniform choice).
Our analysis adapts and extends the techniques of
[
10
, Thms. 4.2 and 4.5(ii)]
, relying on Fejér monotonicity, a standard tool in convergence proofs for algorithms with monotone operators.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

be an

-smooth function for all

-convex for some

2.

For any

Proof.

Part (i) directly follows from (
2.4
), from where we also see that

is maximal
(-L_{i})
-monotone, and so part (ii) follows by
Lemma

5.1
(ii).
The inclusion in (iii) is a consequence of

, and the last equality holds by
[

, Exercise 8.8(c)]
.
∎

From

5.4
(iii), solving (
5.2
) provides candidate solutions to (
5.1
). Since

may not be
\sigma_{m}
-convex,
{\rm prox}_{\gamma f_{m}}

. While

may not equal

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f_{i}:\mathcal{H}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be an

-smooth function for all

, and let

be a proper closed function. If

We argue as in the proof of

\displaystyle\min\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(x)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(f_{i}(\bar{x})+\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(\bar{x}),x-\bar{x}\right\rangle\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}L_{i}{\left\|{x-\bar{x}}\right\|}^{2}+f_{m}(x),

∎

Theorem 5.6

Let

Thus,

\displaystyle{\rm prox}_{\lambda f_{m}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{i}(2z_{i}^{k}-x_{i}^{k})\right)

\displaystyle={\rm prox}_{\lambda f_{m}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{i}\left(z_{i}^{k}-\gamma_{i}\nabla f_{i}(z_{i}^{k})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}{\left\|{z-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{i}\left(z_{i}^{k}-\gamma_{i}\nabla f_{i}(z_{i}^{k})\right)}\right\|}^{2}+f_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}{\left\|{z}\right\|}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(\left\langle z,\nabla f_{i}(z_{i}^{k})\right\rangle-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda}\left\langle z,z_{i}^{k}\right\rangle\right)+f_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(f_{i}(z_{i}^{k})+\left\langle\nabla f_{i}(z_{i}^{k}),z-z_{i}^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}{\left\|{z-z_{i}^{k}}\right\|}^{2}\right)+f_{m}(z),

6
Conclusion

This paper studied the global convergence of a weighted Douglas-Rachford algorithm for the multioperator inclusion problem involving generalized monotone operators.
We proved that if the sum of the operators’ monotonicity moduli is strictly positive, the shadow sequence of the proposed DR algorithm with an appropriate step size converges to the inclusion problem’s solution. This generalizes prior work on two-operator inclusion problems with generalized monotone operators. Applications to unconstrained sum-of-
m
-functions optimization involving strongly and weakly convex functions are presented. Lastly, we established global subsequential convergence in finite dimensions, assuming all but one function has Lipschitz continuous gradients, with the remaining function being proper and closed. Preliminary experiments indicate that both the ordering of the functions and the choice of weights affect empirical performance. A key practical question is how to select these in a principled manner (e.g., whether the strongly convex block should systematically be placed last) so as to balance accuracy and speed.
