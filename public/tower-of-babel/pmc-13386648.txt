The principle of double effect in palliative care: a systematic review of ethical, legal, and clinical perspectives
Authors: Reem Ashraf Makarem, Sharvari Patil, Oqab Baniowdeh, Ahmad Arar, Yusuf Muslih, Lela Shengelia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Principle of Double Effect (PDE) has historically been used to justify medical interventions intended to relieve suffering, even when such treatments may carry a foreseeable risk of hastening death. In palliative care, the doctrine is particularly relevant to opioid administration and palliative sedation. However, its relevance and practical importance remain debated in contemporary clinical and ethical discourse.ObjectiveThis systematic review aims to examine how the Principle of Double Effect is interpreted and applied in palliative care literature, with a focus on ethical, legal, and clinical perspectives.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. Studies addressing the Principle of Double Effect in the context of palliative or end-of-life care were included. Titles and abstracts were screened, followed by full-text assessment based on predefined inclusion criteria. Data were extracted using a standardized form and synthesized using a thematic narrative approach. The review followed PRISMA guidelines.ResultsThirty studies met the inclusion criteria. The included literature comprised ethical analyses, clinical reviews, empirical studies, and clinical guidelines, primarily originating from Europe and North America. The Principle of Double Effect was most frequently discussed in relation to opioid administration for pain and dyspnea, palliative sedation for refractory symptoms, and the ethical and legal distinction between symptom relief and life-ending practices. Evidence suggests that appropriately titrated opioids rarely shorten life, reducing reliance on the doctrine in this context. In contrast, palliative sedation remains more ethically complex, and the principle continues to play a more prominent role in its justification. While some authors support the doctrine as an important ethical safeguard, others question its practical applicability in contemporary clinical practice.ConclusionThe Principle of Double Effect remains a central concept in ethical discussions of end-of-life care, particularly in distinguishing symptom relief from intentional life-ending practices. However, its practical relevance is context-dependent and evolving. Further empirical research examining how clinicians interpret and apply the principle in practice is needed to clarify its role in modern palliative care.

Introduction

Palliative care aims to alleviate suffering and improve the quality of life of patients with serious or life-limiting illness. As patients approach the end of life, clinicians frequently encounter complex situations requiring interventions to relieve severe pain, dyspnea, and other distressing symptoms. Some of these treatments—particularly high-dose opioids, sedatives, and palliative sedation—have historically raised concerns that they may unintentionally shorten life. Consequently, such decisions involve challenging ethical and legal considerations related to physician intention, proportionality, and the permissibility of actions that may produce both beneficial and potentially harmful outcomes [1–3]. The ethical application of these interventions has therefore been widely debated in palliative care, particularly in relation to balancing symptom relief with concerns about life-shortening effects [4].

The Principle of Double Effect (PDE) has long been invoked as an ethical framework to address such dilemmas. Originating from the moral philosophy of Thomas Aquinas, the doctrine proposes that an action producing both a good and a harmful effect may be morally permissible under specific conditions: the act itself must be morally good or neutral; the harmful effect must not be intended, even if foreseen; the good effect must not be achieved through the harmful effect; and there must be a proportionately serious reason for permitting the negative outcome [5, 6]. Within medical ethics, this framework has been widely used to justify interventions aimed at relieving suffering when death may be a foreseeable but unintended consequence.

In palliative medicine, the Principle of Double Effect is most commonly discussed in relation to opioid administration for severe pain or dyspnea and the use of palliative sedation for refractory symptoms [1, 4]. Historically, clinicians expressed concern that escalating doses of opioids could lead to respiratory depression and thereby hasten death. The doctrine therefore served as an important ethical justification for aggressive symptom management, distinguishing symptom relief from intentional life-ending actions such as euthanasia or physician-assisted dying [3]. However, accumulating clinical evidence suggests that appropriately titrated opioids rarely shorten life in patients with advanced illness, and that mortality is primarily attributable to disease progression rather than pharmacological effects. This evolving evidence has led some scholars to question whether reliance on the Principle of Double Effect remains clinically necessary in the context of opioid-based symptom control [2, 4].

In addition, the application of the Principle of Double Effect to palliative sedation remains more complex. Sedation for refractory symptoms requires careful assessment of proportionality, prognosis, and patient suffering, and continues to raise important ethical and legal questions regarding intention and acceptable clinical practice. As a result, the relevance of the principle may differ across clinical contexts, with ongoing debate regarding its applicability in contemporary palliative care.

In recent years, the Principle of Double Effect has been increasingly debated within both bioethical and clinical literature. Some scholars argue that the distinction between intended and foreseen outcomes is difficult to establish in practice and may provide limited guidance for clinicians facing complex end-of-life decisions [5]. Others suggest that advances in palliative care, improved symptom management, and the development of clinical guidelines may reduce reliance on the doctrine as a moral justification for treatment decisions [7]. Nevertheless, proponents maintain that the principle remains ethically important, as it clarifies the moral distinction between relieving suffering and intentionally causing death, particularly within diverse legal and regulatory contexts [8, 9].

Despite extensive philosophical discussion, the literature on the Principle of Double Effect in palliative care remains fragmented across ethical, legal, and clinical domains, with limited systematic synthesis examining how the principle is interpreted and applied in practice. Furthermore, evolving legal frameworks and changing societal attitudes toward end-of-life care continue to influence how the doctrine is understood within contemporary clinical settings [10].

Therefore, this systematic review aims to synthesize the existing literature on the Principle of Double Effect in palliative care from ethical, legal, and clinical perspectives. Specifically, the review seeks to examine how the principle is defined and interpreted, evaluate the ethical and legal arguments surrounding its use, and assess its relevance for clinical decision-making in end-of-life care.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a systematic review examining the ethical, legal, and clinical perspectives on the Principle of Double Effect in palliative care. The review followed the reporting guidelines outlined in the PRISMA 2020 Statement.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. The search aimed to identify publications discussing the Principle of Double Effect within the context of palliative care and end-of-life decision-making. No date restrictions were applied in order to capture both historical and contemporary discussions of the principle.

The search strategy included combinations of keywords and controlled vocabulary terms related to the Principle of Double Effect, palliative care, and end-of-life care. In addition, manual searches of reference lists of relevant articles were performed to identify further eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they discussed the Principle of Double Effect in the context of palliative or end-of-life care, addressed ethical, legal, or clinical aspects of the principle, were published in peer-reviewed journals, were written in English, and provided substantive analysis of the topic.

Studies were excluded if they did not address palliative or end-of-life care, mentioned the principle only briefly without meaningful discussion, were conference abstracts or editorials without full text, or focused exclusively on non-clinical philosophical debates unrelated to medical practice.

Study selection

All records identified through database searches were imported into reference management software, and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened to assess potential eligibility. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were then retrieved and assessed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Any disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion and consensus among the reviewers.

Data synthesis

Given the conceptual and qualitative nature of the included literature, a narrative synthesis approach was adopted. The included studies were categorized into thematic domains to facilitate analysis and interpretation. These domains encompassed ethical foundations of the Principle of Double Effect, its clinical applications in palliative care, legal interpretations and medico-legal considerations, and critiques and limitations of the principle. This approach enabled a comprehensive synthesis of ethical, legal, and clinical perspectives across literature.

Results

Study selection

The literature search identified a total of 340 records across the selected electronic databases, with an additional 20 records identified through manual searches. After removal of duplicates, 260 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 190 articles were excluded as they were not directly related to the Principle of Double Effect or did not address palliative or end-of-life care.

Seventy full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 40 were excluded due to insufficient discussion of the doctrine in clinical contexts or because they focused exclusively on philosophical debates unrelated to palliative care practice. Ultimately, 30 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 30 studies were included in the final analysis, comprising a heterogeneous body of literature that included ethical analyses, clinical reviews, empirical studies, systematic reviews, and clinical guidelines. The included studies originated primarily from North America and Europe and reflected the multidisciplinary nature of research on the Principle of Double Effect in palliative care [3–5, 7–9, 11–24].

The literature addressed a range of clinical contexts, most commonly opioid administration for pain and dyspnea, palliative sedation for refractory symptoms, and ethical and legal distinctions between symptom relief and practices such as euthanasia and physician-assisted dying [1, 2, 4, 7, 11–13]. Across studies, the Principle of Double Effect was variably interpreted, with some authors supporting its continued ethical relevance, while others raised concerns regarding its practical applicability in contemporary clinical settings [3, 5, 7, 9].

Ethical foundations of the principle of double effect

The ethical foundations of the Principle of Double Effect were discussed extensively across the included literature. Several studies emphasized the classical formulation of the doctrine, highlighting its four core conditions: the moral permissibility of the act, the intention to achieve a good effect, the independence of the good effect from the harmful effect, and the requirement of proportionality [1, 5, 8].

Supporters of the principle argued that it provides an important ethical distinction between intended and merely foreseen consequences, thereby allowing clinicians to justify interventions aimed at alleviating suffering without endorsing actions intended to cause death [5, 8]. However, a substantial body of literature raised concerns about the practical application of this distinction. Critics argued that intention is often difficult to determine in clinical practice and that reliance on the doctrine may obscure the complexity of decision-making at the end of life [3, 7, 9].

Over time, interpretations of the Principle of Double Effect have shifted from a strictly philosophical framework toward more applied clinical and ethical discussions, reflecting the increasing complexity of modern medical decision-making.

Clinical perspectives in palliative care

Opioid use for pain and dyspnea

Opioids are a cornerstone of symptom management in palliative care and are widely used to relieve severe pain and dyspnea in patients with advanced illness. Historically, concerns were raised that high-dose opioid administration might lead to respiratory depression and unintentionally hasten death. Within this context, the Principle of Double Effect was frequently invoked to justify opioid use, emphasizing that the clinician’s primary intention is the relief of suffering rather than the production of harm [3, 18].

However, evidence from palliative care research does not support the concern that opioid use hastens death when administered appropriately. Patients receiving regular opioid therapy develop tolerance to the respiratory depressant effects of opioids, and studies in end-of-life care have consistently demonstrated that there is no causal relationship between appropriately titrated opioid use and shortened survival. Instead, mortality in these patients is driven by the progression of underlying disease rather than pharmacological intervention [4, 18, 26].

This shift in clinical understanding has important implications for the ethical relevance of the Principle of Double Effect. Earlier reliance on the doctrine reflected uncertainty regarding opioid safety, whereas current evidence-based practice has reduced the need to invoke the principle as a primary ethical justification. In contemporary palliative care, opioid use is widely regarded as a standard, evidence-based intervention supported by clinical guidelines and grounded in the principles of beneficence and proportionality [2, 5].

Furthermore, the ongoing debate surrounding the Principle of Double Effect reflects broader questions about the role of intention in clinical decision-making. While the doctrine may retain conceptual value, its practical importance in the context of opioid administration appears diminished in light of advances in clinical knowledge and practice.

Palliative sedation for refractory symptoms

Palliative sedation emerged as a distinct and more complex clinical context in the included studies. The literature emphasizes that sedation is typically reserved for refractory symptoms that cannot be adequately controlled through other interventions and requires careful assessment of proportionality, patient preferences, and clinical context [11, 12, 17].

Unlike opioid administration, palliative sedation involves more profound and sustained alterations in consciousness, raising additional ethical concerns regarding intention, proportionality, and potential effects on survival. While empirical evidence suggests that palliative sedation does not significantly shorten life when applied in accordance with established guidelines [12, 13], the strength of this evidence remains limited. Randomized studies are rare, and outcomes are influenced by multiple clinical factors, including disease trajectory, symptom burden, and patient condition, making interpretation challenging.

This ongoing uncertainty contributes to continued ethical and legal debate, particularly regarding the distinction between palliative sedation and life-ending practices such as euthanasia or physician-assisted dying [14, 16]. In this context, the Principle of Double Effect continues to play a more prominent role, as clinicians must justify interventions that may have foreseeable but unintended consequences [7, 9].

Clinical relevance and evolution over time

The clinical relevance of the Principle of Double Effect has evolved alongside advances in palliative care. Earlier reliance on the doctrine was largely driven by uncertainty regarding the safety of opioid use and concerns about unintended life-shortening effects. However, as evidence has demonstrated that appropriately titrated opioids do not hasten death, the need to invoke the principle in this context has diminished [4, 18, 26]. In contrast, palliative sedation remains an area of ongoing ethical complexity, where uncertainty and variability in clinical practice sustain the relevance of the principle. This shift reflects broader changes in scientific understanding and highlights the context-dependent role of the Principle of Double Effect in contemporary clinical care.

Critique of the principle of double effect

Despite its historical importance, the Principle of Double Effect has been subject to significant critique. A central concern is the difficulty of distinguishing between intended and foreseen outcomes in clinical practice, as intention is often complex and context-dependent [7, 9]. Some authors argue that reliance on the doctrine may oversimplify decision-making and obscure other important ethical considerations, such as patient autonomy, transparency, and shared decision-making. These critiques have contributed to ongoing debate regarding the practical applicability of the principle in modern palliative care.

Legal perspective

Legal considerations were addressed in a substantial proportion of the included studies, particularly in relation to distinguishing ethically permissible symptom relief from legally prohibited practices such as euthanasia and physician-assisted dying [14, 16]. The Principle of Double Effect has been used as a conceptual framework to support this distinction, emphasizing that interventions intended to relieve suffering may be considered legally acceptable even when they carry a foreseeable but unintended risk of hastening death [8, 16].

At the same time, the application of the Principle of Double Effect within legal contexts is not uniform. Differences in national legislation, regulatory frameworks, and professional guidelines influence how end-of-life practices are interpreted and governed. Regardless of whether assisted dying is legally permitted, clinicians are required to navigate complex ethical and professional responsibilities, including maintaining patient trust, clinical transparency, and adherence to standards of care.

Over time, the legal relevance of the Principle of Double Effect has evolved alongside broader changes in societal attitudes and medical practice. While the doctrine continues to inform legal reasoning in some contexts, its application is increasingly shaped by advances in clinical knowledge, the development of professional guidelines, and a growing emphasis on patient-centered decision-making [9, 22]. These developments highlight that the role of the Principle of Double Effect in legal frameworks remains dynamic and context-dependent.

Critiques and contemporary relevance

Across the included studies, a recurring theme was the critique of the Principle of Double Effect in contemporary clinical practice. Critics argued that the distinction between intended and foreseen outcomes is often ambiguous and may not provide sufficient guidance for complex end-of-life decisions [3, 7, 9].

In addition, advances in palliative care, improved symptom management, and the development of clinical guidelines have contributed to a shift in how the principle is applied. While some authors maintain that the doctrine remains ethically important, others suggest that its role has diminished, particularly in contexts where clinical evidence clearly supports the safety and effectiveness of interventions such as opioid therapy [2, 4, 7].

Discussion

This systematic review provides a structured synthesis of the ethical, clinical, and legal literature on the Principle of Double Effect in palliative care. The findings demonstrate that, while the doctrine continues to hold conceptual importance, its practical relevance varies across clinical contexts and has evolved over time.

A key finding of this review is that the clinical application of the Principle of Double Effect is not uniform. In the context of opioid administration for pain and dyspnea, the doctrine historically served as an important ethical justification in response to concerns about respiratory depression and the potential to hasten death [3, 4]. However, accumulating clinical evidence suggests that appropriately titrated opioids rarely shorten life and that mortality is primarily driven by the underlying disease process rather than pharmacological effects [4, 26]. As a result, several authors have questioned whether reliance on the Principle of Double Effect remains necessary in this context, arguing that contemporary clinical practice, supported by evidence-based guidelines, provides sufficient justification for opioid use without recourse to the doctrine [2, 5].

In contrast, the application of the Principle of Double Effect to palliative sedation remains more complex and contested. Sedation for refractory symptoms involves deeper and more sustained reductions in consciousness and requires careful consideration of proportionality, patient preferences, and clinical prognosis [11, 12, 17]. Although empirical studies indicate that palliative sedation does not significantly shorten survival when appropriately applied [12, 13], the ethical distinction between sedation and life-ending practices continues to generate debate [14, 16]. In this setting, the Principle of Double Effect remains more frequently invoked, reflecting the greater uncertainty and ethical sensitivity surrounding these interventions.

Another important finding is the ongoing debate regarding the role of intention in ethical decision-making. While proponents of the Principle of Double Effect emphasize the moral distinction between intended and foreseen outcomes, critics argue that this distinction is difficult to apply in practice and may offer limited guidance in complex clinical situations [3, 7, 9]. The determination of intention is inherently subjective and may not fully capture the nuances of real-world decision-making at the end of life. Consequently, some authors have suggested that ethical frameworks based on proportionality, patient-centered care, and transparency may be more relevant to contemporary practice [7, 9].

The review also highlights the influence of legal and regulatory frameworks on the interpretation and application of the Principle of Double Effect. In many jurisdictions, the doctrine continues to serve as a conceptual tool that supports the legal distinction between permissible symptom relief and prohibited life-ending interventions [8, 14, 16]. However, variations in legal approaches and evolving societal attitudes toward euthanasia and physician-assisted dying complicate its application and may limit its consistency as a guiding principle across settings [9, 22].

Importantly, the findings of this review suggest a broader historical shift in the role of the Principle of Double Effect in palliative care. Earlier literature relied heavily on the doctrine to justify opioid use in the face of clinical uncertainty. In contrast, more recent studies reflect increased confidence in symptom management and a reduced reliance on the doctrine in certain contexts. At the same time, debates surrounding palliative sedation and end-of-life decision-making continue to sustain its relevance, particularly where ethical and legal boundaries remain contested.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. It provides a comprehensive synthesis of multidisciplinary literature spanning ethical, clinical, and legal domains, thereby offering a more integrated understanding of the Principle of Double Effect in palliative care. The use of a systematic search strategy and structured thematic analysis enhances the transparency and rigor of the review.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the included studies were heterogeneous in design, encompassing ethical analyses, narrative reviews, empirical studies, and clinical guidelines, which limited the possibility of quantitative synthesis. Second, the interpretation of ethical concepts such as intention and proportionality is inherently context-dependent, which may affect the generalizability of findings. Third, legal perspectives vary significantly across jurisdictions, and the conclusions drawn in this review may not fully reflect local regulatory frameworks. Finally, as the review relied on published literature, it may not fully capture how clinicians interpret and apply the Principle of Double Effect in everyday practice.

Implications for practice and research

The findings of this review have important implications for both clinical practice and future research. Clinicians should recognize that the relevance of the Principle of Double Effect varies depending on the clinical context and that ethical decision-making in palliative care requires careful consideration of intention, proportionality, and patient-centered values. In particular, the diminished reliance on the doctrine in the context of opioid use reflects broader advances in clinical knowledge and practice.

Future research should move beyond purely conceptual debate and focus on how ethical principles, including the Principle of Double Effect, are applied in real-world clinical settings. Empirical studies examining clinician decision-making, patient perspectives, and the impact of legal frameworks would provide valuable insights into the practical relevance of the doctrine in contemporary palliative care.

Conclusion

The Principle of Double Effect remains an important ethical concept in palliative care, but its practical relevance is context-dependent and evolving. While its role appears diminished in relation to opioid-based symptom management, it continues to play a more prominent role in discussions of palliative sedation and end-of-life decision-making. A nuanced, context-sensitive approach is therefore essential for applying ethical principles in clinical practice and ensuring that patient care remains both effective and ethically sound.
