Predictors and risk factors of typhoid fever in urban informal settlements of Nairobi, Kenya
Authors: Susan Mutile Kavai, Cecilia Mbae, Michael Mugo, Mary Kariuki, Diana Imoli, Maureen Kawira, Peter Muturi, Collins Kebenei, Kelvin Kering, Amos Njuguna, Samuel Kariuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA clear understanding of the risk factors and key predictors of typhoid fever in endemic, low-resource settings is essential for informing effective prevention strategies and guiding empiric patient management. This study aimed to identify the predictors and risk factors linked to culture-confirmed typhoid fever in Mukuru informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya. Between November 2020 and July 2025, patients presenting with a fever of ≥ 38 °C, and/or with diarrhea were enrolled at seven health facilities in the Mukuru settlements. Structured questionnaires and case report forms were used to collect socio-demographic, environmental, and clinical data. Blood and stool cultures were performed to confirm the presence of Salmonella Typhi (S. Typhi). The associations between potential risk factors and typhoid positivity were analysed using chi-square tests and multivariable logistic regression to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Of the 8,950 patients enrolled, 131 (1.46%, 95% CI:1.23–1.73%) had S. Typhi positive blood culture. In the adjusted analysis, patients aged 5–16 years (aOR 2.50, 95% CI 1.55–4.04) and those over 16 years (aOR 2.16, 95% CI 1.41–3.31) had significantly higher odds of typhoid infection compared to children under five. Continuous fever (fever ≥ 38 °C for the last 3 days) (aOR 3.48, 95% CI 2.11–5.73), vomiting (aOR 1.89, 95% CI 1.29–2.75), moderate dehydration (aOR 2.95, 95% CI 1.25–6.95), and headache (aOR 1.68, 95% CI 1.12–2.53) were independently associated with typhoid infection. Households with two or more children under five years had increased odds of infection (aOR 1.98, 95% CI 1.28–3.07). Usage of a shared flush toilet was significantly associated with increased odds of typhoid infection (aOR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.69–3.47, p < 0.001). Our findings demonstrate that typhoid fever in Mukuru informal settlements is driven by factors such as age, unsafe water practices, and shared sanitation. Interventions, such as strengthening water, sanitation, and hygiene (WaSH) infrastructure and practices are vital to reducing typhoid fever risk in these endemic settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14428-7.Keywords: Typhoid fever, Salmonella Typhi, Predictors, Risk factors, Urban informal settlements, Mukuru

Introduction

Typhoid fever, caused by Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi (S. Typhi) exists exclusively in humans and can be fatal in severe cases, resulting in systemic febrile illness [1, 2]. A report estimated a pooled incidence of 206 cases per 100,000 person-years from population-based studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). In Kenya, an estimated 126,098 typhoid fever cases, corresponding to an incidence of 251 cases per 100,000 population, were reported, resulting in 1,568 typhoid-related deaths [3].

Typhoid fever is endemic in low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs) characterized by inadequate sanitation and poor hygiene practices [1, 4]. The disease transmission is via the fecal-oral route. Urban informal settlements typified by high population density, inadequate sanitation, unreliable potable water supply, open drainage, and poor waste management create environments favorable for the spread of S. Typhi [5, 6]. In Kenya, persistent outbreaks of salmonellosis, including typhoid fever, have been reported in Mukuru and Kibera informal settlements in Nairobi County [7, 8].

To formulate effective interventions in such typhoid endemic settings, there is need for a comprehensive, multi-dimensional analysis that examines clinical predictors, environmental, and socio-demographic drivers of typhoid across all age groups. This data will inform targeted public health interventions and guide urban health policies in Kenya and other typhoid endemic settings, especially in LMICs.

Methods

Study design and population

We recruited participants from seven health facilities within Mukuru informal settlements in Nairobi County using a cross-sectional study design from November 2020 to July 2025. Patients aged ≤ 65 years that resided in Mukuru informal settlements and sought medical care at the outpatient departments were eligible for enrollment. The health facilities included: Kayole two - level four hospital (K2H), Our Lady of Nazareth - Njenga (LN), Mukuru Maendeleo-Reuben (NMS), Medical Missionaries of Mary (MMM), Municipal County Council (MCC), Mukuru Reuben (MR) and Mama Lucy Kibaki Hospital (MLK) (Referral facility for patients from Mukuru). Mukuru Informal settlements, one of the largest informal settlements (population ca. 250,000) in Nairobi, is characterized by poor water, sanitation and hygiene (WaSH) infrastructure, over-crowding and predominance of street foods. While Mukuru is divided into eight villages, this study was conducted in two largest villages: Mukuru Kwa Njenga and Mukuru Kwa Reuben.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were recruited if they presented to the health facilities with an auxiliary temperature of ≥ 38 °C or a history of fever for the past three days, and with or without having experienced three or more loose watery stools in the preceding 24 h.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals were excluded from the study during screening if they reported having taken antibiotics to treat the typhoid-related symptoms. Additionally, participants unable to provide assent/consent to the study were also excluded.

Risk factors data collection

The patient’s clinical presentation was assessed by a clinician and documented in a standardized case report form (CRF) (supplementary file 1) after informed consent was obtained. Upon recruitment, patients were subjected to an interview using a structured questionnaire (supplementary file 2) capturing environmental, social, and demographic characteristics. Data were collected electronically through EpiCollect5, with all study variables programmed as mandatory fields to ensure data completeness and prevent submission of records without missing information.

Sample collection

Blood and stool/rectal swabs samples were obtained from participants using routine techniques. From the stool samples collected, swabs were prepared, and placed in Cary Blair transport medium inside zip lock bags. Samples were transported to the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) laboratories in cooler boxes with ice packs within 4 h of collection. Blood samples were aseptically collected using venipuncture and inoculated into blood culture bottles. The blood samples were transported at room temperature to the laboratory for processing.

Laboratory processing

Isolation and identification of S. Typhi

Stool samples were enriched in selenite fecal (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) for 18–24 h at 37 °C. Blood cultures were placed in Bactec FX 40 Blood Culture System (BD, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). Initial culture for both blood and stool samples was done in MacConkey, Salmonella Shigella and Xyline Lysine Deoxycholate agars (all from Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK). Suspected non-duplicate colonies were selected and sub-cultured on Tryptone Soy agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) for purification.

Further identification of bacterial colonies was done using analytical profile indexing (API 20E) biochemical tests (Montalieu Vercieu, France). S. Typhi confirmation was done using serology testing; polyvalent O and monovalent (9, d, and vi) (Remel Europe Ltd). Typhoid fever was defined as the detection of S. Typhi in blood samples.

Statistical data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing STATA version 15.1 software. Descriptive statistics were summarized as counts and percentages. A Chi-square test was performed to preliminarily assess the statistical significance of the relationship between S. Typhi positivity and each independent variable. Crude odds ratios (ORs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

To identify factors that were independently linked to S. Typhi positivity while controlling for potential confounders, all variables from the bivariate analysis exhibiting a P value of < 0.2 were included, in addition to the participant’s age and sex. A binary logistic regression model was constructed through a stepwise selection process, employing both forward and backward conditional methods, with an inclusion criterion established at P < 0.2. Critical factors, such as age and sex, were retained in the model irrespective of their statistical significance.

Two distinct multivariable logistic regression models were developed, one focused on demographic characteristics, household water, sanitation, and hygiene (WaSH) factors, household behavioral factors, and socioeconomic status, interpreted through parsimonious modeling; and a separate parsimonious model focusing on the clinical characteristics. This approach was implemented to address potential adjustment challenges, including both insufficient adjustment and over adjustment of confounders, which could obscure the true effect of interest. By separating these analyses, a clearer understanding of the unique influences exerted by non-clinical determinants on Typhi infection was achieved. Additionally, the overall goodness-of-fit of each multivariable model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The results indicated adequate fit for both the household-level factors model (χ²(8) = 7.39, p = 0.495) and the clinical characteristics model (χ²(8) = 5.99, p = 0.648). The strength of the associations was expressed as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Chi-square analysis demonstrated that age was significantly associated with S. Typhi positivity (p = 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1). The highest proportion was observed among children aged 5–16 years (2.07%), followed by individuals older than 16 years (1.78%), while children under five years had the lowest (0.96%). A statistically significant association was also observed between S. Typhi positivity and the number of children under five years in the household (p = 0.042). Households with two or more children under five years had a higher proportion (2.08%) compared to those with one child or none (1.35%), Supplementary Table 1.

In contrast, no significant associations were identified between S. Typhi positivity and gender (p = 0.385) or the education level of the household head (p = 0.445). Similarly, household structural and composition factors, including household size (p = 0.821), number of children above five years (p = 0.25), type of household construction (p = 0.895), and number of rooms used (p = 0.932), were not significantly associated with infection. Socio-economic indicators such as main fuel used for cooking (p = 0.396), access to electricity (p = 0.991), and ownership of household assets including a sofa set (p = 0.246), sewing machine (p = 0.099), and refrigerator (p = 0.185), as well as household expenditure levels (p = 0.771), also showed no statistically significant relationship with S. Typhi positivity. Additionally, duration of residence at the current location was not associated with infection risk (p = 0.939), Supplementary Table 1.

Behavioral and WaSH characteristics

After adjusting for potential confounders; the multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several factors independently associated with S. Typhi positivity. Age remained a strong predictor of infection, with children aged 5–16 years having significantly higher odds of S. Typhi positivity compared to those under five years (aOR = 2.50, 95% CI: 1.55–4.04, p < 0.001). The number of children under five years in a household was also independently associated with infection. Individuals from households with two or more young children had nearly twice the odds of S. Typhi positivity compared to those with one child or none (aOR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.28–3.07, p = 0.002). Sanitation-related factors were notably important. Use of a shared flush toilet was significantly associated with increased odds of infection (aOR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.69–3.47, p < 0.001) Table 2.

Other variables, including gender, possession of a sewing machine (as a proxy for socio-economic status), water treatment practices, and washing vegetables before cooking, were not statistically significant, although some showed trends toward increased risk. Males had slightly higher odds compared to females (aOR = 1.22, p = 0.274), but this was not significant. Not treating drinking water was associated with higher odds of infection (aOR = 1.37, p = 0.089), Table 2.

Clinical characteristics of participants

Participants presenting with continuous fever (fever lasting 3 days) had significantly higher odds of S. Typhi infection (aOR 3.48, 95% CI 2.11–5.73; p < 0.001). On the other hand, non-continuous fever was not significantly associated with infection (aOR 1.22, 95% CI 0.64–2.31; p = 0.542). Vomiting was independently associated with S. Typhi positivity (aOR 1.89, 95% CI 1.29–2.75; p = 0.001). Seemingly, moderate dehydration was significantly associated with infection (aOR 2.95, 95% CI 1.25–6.95; p = 0.014), while mild dehydration was not (p = 0.127).

Absence of bloody diarrhea was associated with higher odds of infection compared to those reporting bloody diarrhea (aOR 2.92, 95% CI 1.27–6.71; p = 0.011). Headache was independently associated with infection (aOR 1.68, 95% CI 1.12–2.53; p = 0.012). Absence of dysuria showed increased odds of infection compared to those reporting dysuria; however, this association was not statistically significant (aOR 5.83, 95% CI 0.81–42.15; p = 0.081), Table 3.

Discussion

Typhoid fever remains a major public health challenge in low- and-middle-income countries, particularly in densely populated urban informal settlements where sanitation and water infrastructure are inadequate. In this study, blood and stool culture confirmed S. Typhi infection was observed at a relatively low prevalence (1.46%) among febrile participants. Our findings highlight the multifactorial nature of typhoid transmission in informal settlements, where demographic factors, household crowding, unsafe water practices, and inadequate sanitation collectively contribute to increased risk of typhoid infection. These associations align with existing evidence on typhoid risk factors in endemic settings particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [1, 7, 9]. While the identified predictors largely confirm established determinants of typhoid fever rather than revealing novel risk factors, they provide important context-specific evidence from Nairobi’s informal settlements, reinforcing the persistent role of overcrowding and inadequate WaSH conditions in sustaining typhoid transmission amid rapid urbanization.

Our study reported that age was significantly associated with S. Typhi infection; school-aged children (5–16 years) and adults (> 16 years) had significantly higher odds of infection compared to children under five. A similar study in Kenya reported higher proportions of infection with S. Typhi observed among children aged 5–6 years and 7–8 years whereas lower proportions were observed among children aged 0–2 years [7]. This is consistent with other studies [10, 11, 12, 13, 14] showing that typhoid risk varies with age, with school-age children often bearing a substantial disease burden in endemic regions of Africa and Asia, though the precise age distribution can differ by context and seasonality [12, 13]. Our findings highlight the possibility of sustained community transmission, schools as important transmission settings, and the need for broader vaccination strategies that also target adults.

Households with two or more children under five had significantly higher odds of typhoid fever infection, suggesting that crowded living conditions could contribute to the risk of spreading the disease. A previous study [11] reported high population density as a contributor to increased risk of typhoid fever transmission in low-resource settings. Another study in Pakistan reported that the most powerful predictors of typhoid disease were socioeconomic with the majority of cases (67.1%) originating from low-income households [10]. These findings highlight the importance of overcrowding and low socioeconomic status in typhoid transmission dynamics.

Our analysis showed multiple WaSH-related factors within the household associated with increased odds of typhoid fever infection. The use of shared flush toilets doubled the odds of S. Typhi positivity. Although the flush toilets are considered improved sanitation, they are still shared by multiple individuals which increases the risk of typhoid transmission. Previous studies [15, 16] in Indonesia reported that poor hygiene and housing are among other household factors suggested to increase the risk of typhoid fever. A study in Uzbekistan [17] reported that endemic typhoid fever is transmitted by contaminated water. Another study in Pakistan [11] reported that children in households that reported use of a safe drinking-water source were less likely to have typhoid fever. These findings underscore the importance of improving sanitation and hygiene in the prevention and control of Typhoid in these endemic settings.

Clinical features that were strongly associated with S. Typhi infection included continuous fever, vomiting, headache, and moderate dehydration. These findings mirror clinical observations reported in Asia [12, 18], where prolonged fever is a prominent feature of typhoid fever and is often more predictive of S. Typhi infection compared to other febrile illnesses. The absence of bloody diarrhea being associated with infection is also consistent with typhoid’s characteristic non-bloody systemic presentation, distinguishing it from other enteric pathogens that commonly cause grossly bloody stools. These clinical features could be used as predictors of typhoid disease in low resource health settings where laboratory diagnostic capacity may be limited.

Given the multiple risk factors associated with typhoid fever in this study setting, different control and prevention strategies including use of vaccines and strengthening of WaSH infrastructure are of paramount importance. During the time period of this study, there were national plans to introduce the Typhoid Conjugate Vaccine (TCV) in Kenya. The study was in the conclusion phase when the nationwide rollout of TCV commenced, hence not factored in. The TCV campaign was conducted in Kenya from July, 2025 targeting children aged 9 months to 15 years. It was reported that a nationwide vaccination coverage rate of 84% was achieved. This campaign highlights the critical role of vaccines in typhoid endemic settings. Future studies should focus on measuring the impact of this vaccination in relation to the burden of typhoid disease in these endemic settings.

Our study acknowledges a number of limitations. As a facility-based study, the findings may not be fully representative of the prevalence of typhoid fever in the wider community. Although analyses adjusted for available covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Temporal changes during the 2020–2025 study period, including shifts in healthcare-seeking behavior, disease epidemiology, public health interventions, and environmental conditions, may have influenced the observed associations. In addition, facility-level differences across participating health centers, including variations in patient case-mix, clinical practices, and healthcare delivery, may have introduced heterogeneity that was not fully accounted for in the analyses. Important factors such as typhoid vaccination status and socioeconomic determinants, including occupation and other indicators of deprivation, were also not comprehensively captured and may have contributed to unmeasured confounding. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of temporal relationships and limits causal inference. Finally, the use of stepwise variable selection in multivariable analyses may have introduced model-selection uncertainty and increased the risk of overfitting, potentially excluding relevant predictors and affecting the stability of estimated associations. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution, future studies employing alternative modeling approaches are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

These findings of predictors and critical risk factors of typhoid fever in this endemic setting underscore the importance of integrated typhoid control strategies in informal settlements. Notably, the use of shared flush toilets was among the strongest independent risk factors, underscoring inadequate sanitation as a critical target for intervention. Accordingly, typhoid control efforts should prioritize improved sanitation infrastructure, particularly access to safe, private sanitation facilities, alongside enhanced household water treatment and hygiene practices. These interventions, complemented by targeted typhoid vaccination in high-risk populations, could substantially reduce the burden of typhoid fever in informal settlements.
