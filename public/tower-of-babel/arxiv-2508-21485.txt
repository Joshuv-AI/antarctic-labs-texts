Interpolation for Converse PDL
Johannes Kloibhofer, Valentina Trucco Dalmas, Yde Venema

Abstract
Converse 𝖯𝖣𝖫 \mathsf{PDL} is the extension of propositional dynamic logic with a converse operation on programs. Our main result states that Converse 𝖯𝖣𝖫 \mathsf{PDL} enjoys the (local) Craig Interpolation Property, with respect to both atomic programs and propositional variables. As a corollary we establish the Beth Definability Property for the logic. Our interpolation proof is based on an adaptation of Maehara’s proof-theoretic method. For this purpose we introduce a sound and complete cyclic sequent system for this logic. This calculus features an analytic cut rule and uses a focus mechanism for recognising successful cycles.



1
Introduction

Propositional Dynamic Logic (abbreviated:
\mathsf{PDL}
) was introduced by Fischer & Ladner
[
8
]
in 1979 as a propositional formalism to reason about the behaviour of programs.
The language of
\mathsf{PDL}
features an infinite collection of modalities, the intended interpretation of
\langle\alpha\rangle\varphi
being that ‘after some execution of the program
\alpha
, the formula
\varphi
holds’.
The inductive structure of programs is reflected by the syntax of
\mathsf{PDL}
, where complex programs are constructed from atomic ones and formula tests, by means of program constructors for sequential composition, nondeterministic choice and iteration.

Converse
\mathsf{PDL}
or
\mathsf{CPDL}
, also defined in
[
8
]
, extends
\mathsf{PDL}
with a converse operator on programs, which facilitates backwards reasoning about programs.

\mathsf{PDL}
and
\mathsf{CPDL}
also have applications in for instance knowledge representation
[
2
]
, where the program expressions represent
roles
between objects, and the program constructions correspond to natural operations on such roles; in particular, the converse operator corresponds to
inverse roles
.

\mathsf{PDL}
and
\mathsf{CPDL}
both have the small-model property and an
Exptime
-complete satisfiability problem, as established by Fischer & Ladner
[
8
]
and Pratt
[
18
]
).
A natural axiomatisation was given by Segerberg
[
20
]
and proved to be complete by Parikh
[
17
]
and others.
Generally,
\mathsf{PDL}
and related formalisms have been recognized as important modal logics for quite some time now, see for instance Troquard & Balbiani
[
22
]
for a recent survey.

Here we study interpolation properties for
\mathsf{CPDL}
.
A logic has the
Craig Interpolation Property
(CIP) if any pair
\varphi,\psi
of formulas such that
\varphi\models\psi
has an
interpolant
, that is, a formula
\theta
in the common vocabulary of
\varphi
and
\psi
such that
\varphi\models\theta
and
\theta\models\psi
.
(Here
\models
denotes the local consequence relation, that is: we have
\varphi\models\psi
iff the implication
\varphi\to\psi
is valid.)
A related property is the Beth Definability Property, which informally states that any concept which can implicitly be defined in the logic, in fact also has an explicit definition.
Interpolation and definability are generally considered to be attractive properties of a formalism; applications in computer science include the modularisation and optimisation of reasoning
[
15
,
19
,
6
,
11
]
.
Both properties have been studied extensively in the literature on modal logic, see Gabbay & Maksimova
[
9
]
for a survey.

Our main result states that
\mathsf{CPDL}
has the Craig Interpolation Property indeed, with respect to both atomic programs and propositional variables.
As a direct corollary we obtain the Beth Definability Property for
\mathsf{CPDL}
.

We prove Craig interpolation following Maehara’s proof-theoretic method, adapted to our logic.
That is, we first introduce a sound and complete cyclic proof system for this logic.
This calculus features an analytic cut rule and uses a focus mechanism for recognising successful cycles.
To prove interpolation we consider a version of the proof system that operates on
split sequents
, that is, pairs of finite sets of formulas.
Given a pair
\varphi,\psi
of formulas such that
\varphi\models\psi
, we fix a derivation
\pi
of the split sequent
\overline{\psi}\!\mid\!\varphi
.
This derivation induces a system of equations, whose solution would immediately yield an interpolant of
\varphi
and
\psi
.
As in the case for standard
\mathsf{PDL}
, a straightforward approach would result in a system of equations that cannot be solved
inside

\mathsf{CPDL}
itself.
To solve this problem we employ an idea from Borzechowski
[
4
]
:
based on an
auxiliary structure
rather than on
\pi
itself, we set up
an alternative system of equations, which
does
have a solution in
\mathsf{CPDL}
,
thus providing us with the desired interpolant.

Related work

Maehara’s method has been extended to cyclic proof systems by Shamkanov
[
21
]
, Afshari & Leigh
[
1
]
and Marti & Venema
[
14
]
, in order to prove interpolation properties for, respectively, Gödel-Löb logic, the modal
\mu
-calculus
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}
and its alternation-free fragment.
Closely related to our work is the paper by Borzechowski et al.
[
5
]
, who use ideas from Borzechowski
[
4
]
to establish interpolation for
\mathsf{PDL}
.
Our work extends
[
5
]
to include converse modalities: notable differences are that our proof system features a cut rule and a more involved modal rule, while on the other hand our rules for the program constructors are simpler than those in
[
5
]
.
Kloibhofer & Venema establish Craig interpolation for the two-way
\mu
-calculus
[
12
]
.
Our cyclic proof system is much simpler, since it avoids the use of trace atoms and can restrict to a simple focus annotation system; the construction of our interpolants is rather more intricate, however.

Here a

Note that we do not require

\Delta

Given a split sequent

We prove the soundness and completeness of

8
Conclusions

We presented a sound and complete cyclic proof system for
\mathsf{CPDL}
and used it to show that the logic enjoys the Craig Interpolation Property. As a corollary we established that
\mathsf{CPDL}
also has the Beth Definability Property.

With some minor modifications we can make our approach work to prove interpolation for
\mathsf{PDL}
itself as well.
First, we simplify the rule
\mathsf{{\langle a\rangle}}
to the standard rule for (one-way) modal logic:

\varphi^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}},\Sigma
\langle a\rangle\varphi^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f}},[a]\Sigma,\Gamma

We then verify that, with minor adaptations (that are in fact simplifications), the completeness proof still holds.
Finally, we observe that the current definition of the interpolant will not involve the use of the converse modality, and check that the correctness proofs for the interpolant can be adapted to the system with the new modal rule.
We hope to supply the details of this approach in an expanded version of this paper.

Finally, it would be interesting to see whether one can find a proof system for
\mathsf{CPDL}
, such that the proof of correctness of the interpolant can be proven inside the system without the need of adding unrestricted cut. Achieving this seems to require adding admissible rules to
\mathsf{CPDL}_{f}
.
Another open question is whether our method can be extended to other variants of
\mathsf{PDL}
such as
\mathsf{PDL}
with intersection
[
13

5

The

For a list

It is obvious that
\varphi

\varphi
itself.

We only consider the case where
\varphi
is a diamond fixpoint formula, say,
\varphi=\langle\alpha^{*}\rangle\psi

.

\alpha
.

\psi_{n}\in\Lambda^{d}(\rho_{n})^{-}
. Then
\psi_{n}^{o}\in\mathsf{last}(\rho_{n})^{d}

Lemma 20

be a local path containing
\Psi
. Let

\psi_{n}^{f}\in\mathsf{last}(\rho_{n})^{d}

Proof

We prove the claim by induction on

. The base case holds by assumption. For the induction step let
\psi_{n}^{f}\in\Lambda^{d}(\rho_{n})
. We need to show that
\psi_{n+1}^{f}\in\Lambda^{d}(\rho_{n+1})
. We proceed by a case distinction on the shape of
\psi_{n}
. If
\psi_{n}
is not of the form
\langle a\rangle\chi
for some action

\rho_{n+1}=\rho_{n}
.

The cases

. In the case

to guarantee that the choice of

The case where

Lastly assume that

.

Lemma 21

Let
\psi_{0}\in\Sigma
and let
\rho_{0}
be a local path containing
\Sigma
. Then the strategy
\underline{f}
is winning for
\exists
in
\mathcal{E}@(\psi_{0},\rho_{0})
.

Proof

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an
\underline{f}
-guided
\mathcal{E}@(\psi_{0},\rho_{0})
-match. If
\mathcal{M}
is finite it is straightforward to check that it is winning for
\exists
. Thus we consider the case where
\mathcal{M}=(\psi_{n},\rho_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is infinite and assume that it is winning for
\forall
. Then there is
N\in\omega
such that
\psi_{n}
is a diamond formula for all
n\geq N
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
N
is big enough such that for all formulas
\psi_{n}
of the form
\langle\varphi?\rangle\chi
(with

) it holds that
\psi_{n+1}=\chi
.

Let
M\geq N
be such that
\psi_{M}\in\Lambda^{l}(\rho_{M})^{-}
, or if such an
M
does not exist (meaning that
\psi_{n}\notin\Lambda^{l}(\rho_{n})
for all
n\geq N
), let
M\mathrel{:=}N
. In the first case let
d\mathrel{:=}l
and in the second let
d\mathrel{:=}r
. In both cases it holds that
\psi_{n}\in\Lambda(\rho_{n})^{-}
for all
n\in\omega
by Lemma
19
.

We first assume that for infinitely many
n
the formula
\psi_{n}
is of the form
\langle a_{n}\rangle\chi
for some (atomic) program
a_{n}
. Let
K\geq M
such that
\psi_{K}
is of the form
\langle a\rangle\chi
, then
\psi_{K+1}=\chi
and
\chi^{f}\in\Lambda^{d}(\rho_{K+1})
.
By Lemma
20
we have
\psi_{n}^{f}\in\Lambda^{d}(\rho_{n})
for all
n>K
. Additionally, for every
n>K
there is some
m\geq n
such that
\psi_{m}=\langle a_{m}\rangle\chi
and
\psi_{m}^{f}\in\mathsf{last}(\rho_{n})^{d}
. But if the last sequent on
\rho_{n}
has a formula in focus, then all sequents in
\rho_{n}
have a formula in focus for all

, as no
\mathsf{f}
rule is applied on local paths. Between the local paths
\rho_{n}
and
\rho_{n+1}
with
\rho_{n}\neq\rho_{n+1}
no
\mathsf{f}
rule is applied either, as by the definition of
\underline{f}
applications of
\mathsf{f}
are minimized. Thus there is an infinite path in
\mathcal{T}
where cofinitely many sequents have a formula in focus and infinitely many rule instances with principal formula in focus are applied. This contradicts the assumption that Builder plays a winning strategy.

K\geq M
there is no

such that the formula
\psi_{n}
is of the form
\langle a\rangle\chi
. As a consequence
\rho_{n}=\rho_{N}
for all

.

\mathsf{last}(\rho_{K})=\Pi\!\mid\!\Xi
, we show how to obtain an infinite successful path in
\mathcal{T}
starting at
\Pi\!\mid\!\Xi
contradicting the assumption that Builder plays a winning strategy.
Assume that
d=l
, the case where

is analogous.
At
\Pi\!\mid\!\Xi
no rule instance of type 1 – 5 is applicable, thus in
\mathcal{T}
Prover may put
\psi_{K}
in focus to obtain a node
\psi_{K}^{f},\Pi^{u}\!\mid\!\Xi^{u}
. We will show that there is an infinite path
\Sigma_{0}i_{0}\Sigma_{1}i_{1}...
in
\mathcal{T}
, where for all
n\in\omega
it holds
\Sigma_{n}=\psi_{K+n}^{f},\Pi^{u}\!\mid\!\Xi^{u}
and

is a rule instance of type 5.

Inductively assume that the position
\psi_{K+n}^{f},\Pi^{u}\!\mid\!\Xi^{u}
is in
\mathcal{T}
. Following her strategy in
\mathcal{T}
Prover may pick a rule instance of type 5 with principal formula

. We proceed by a case distinction on the shape of

Theorem 0.C.0
(Completeness)

If

\mathsf{SCPDL}_{f}
proof of

Proof
