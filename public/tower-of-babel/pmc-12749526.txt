Exploiting fuzzy weights in CNN model-based taxonomic classification of 500-bp sequence bacterial dataset
Authors: Abeer D Algarni, Fathi E Abd El-Samie, Naglaa F Soliman, Heba M Emara, Fatimah Algarni, Samia M Abd-Alhalem, Hanaa Salem Marie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaxonomic classification plays a crucial role in understanding the diversity and evolutionary relationships among bacteria. Accurately classifying bacterial DNA sequences based on a limited 500-bp segment remains challenging. This paper presents an improved Fuzzy-weighted Convolutional Neural Network (F-CNN) for taxonomic classification of bacterial DNA sequences, specifically focusing on the 500-bp segments. The proposed model aims to overcome the limitations of traditional classification methods by leveraging the power of deep learning and fuzzy logic processing. The improved fuzzy deep learning model is proposed to handle the problem of classifying samples with similar probabilities in the classification layer. It incorporates a feature selection stage using various techniques and a fuzzy weighting system to handle the uncertainty associated with similar classes in the classification layer and optimize parameters using fuzzy weights. The experimental results on the Ribosomal Database Project Release 11 (RDP 11) sequences dataset show the superiority of the proposed model, especially at the 500-bp region. Experimental results on the RDP 11 dataset, which includes over 1.4 million bacterial gene sequences, demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed model, achieving a classification accuracy up to 84.03% at the genus level for 500-bp segments and demonstrating high generalization when applied to longer sequences. This paper has significant implications for various fields, including microbiology, epidemiology, and environmental science, where accurate classification of bacteria is crucial for understanding their roles in different ecosystems and disease outbreaks.Keywords: Bacterial taxonomic classification, 16 S rRNA, Fuzzy convolutional neural network, Feature selection, Improved binary genetic algorithms, Deep learning, Short-read DNA sequences, Chaos game representationSubject terms: Biological techniques, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Without the need to cultivate specific strains, metagenomics has emerged as a popular technique for studying microbial communities. Furthermore, the 16 S ribosomal RNA (16 S rRNA) gene is a useful tool for identifying species and evaluating the makeup of bacteria, because it has regions that are both hypervariable and well-conserved across a range of species. As a result, the conserved areas can be used as primers for amplicon sequencing targeting particular hypervariable regions as illustrated in Petrosino et al. 1. In addition, hypervariable regions facilitate the identification of bacterial taxonomy by leveraging sequence similarities among different species. Analyzing sequenced metagenomes from diverse environmental samples remains a challenging task in translational bioinformatics. Although multiple studies have shown that 16 S rRNA can be used to classify bacteria at the genus level, it remains challenging to accurately determine the composition of metagenomic data using RNA-seq short-read data.

Gwas, H. J. et al. discussed a data-driven modeling approach for species-level taxonomic assignment using 16 S rRNA in human microbiomes. This approach uses homologous species groups and consensus sequence models for each hypervariable region in 16 S rRNA to differentiate highly similar species and determine species-level taxonomy. This approach was evaluated using simulated, mock, and real microbiome samples 2. Taxonomic profiling, gene prediction, and comparative metagenomics have been extensively researched and developed in recent years. Two types of approaches, namely alignment and assembly, have been developed, but they have computational limitations. New approaches based on the identification of 16 S small subunit ribosomal RNA genes in related data have been proposed. Deep learning techniques are state-of-the-art pattern classification tools. Pattern Discovery Framework (PDF) provides classification based on deep learning models, Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) classifiers, and some existing deep learning techniques 3.

Recently, CNN model-based taxonomic classification has emerged as an alternative for identifying bacteria according to taxonomic rank using 16 S rRNA gene segments. A basic CNN is a model used in classification tasks, where the model training is completed by learning the weights or parameters in the model. The classification process, comprising the categorization of bacteria into taxonomic ranks, is elementary for comprehending the biological family tree and the functions of microbes for numerous fundamental research and practical applications in biotechnology, including drug development and disease diagnostics. The traditional classification of bacteria according to their taxonomic rank relies exclusively on specific phenotypic characteristics and a few 16 S rRNA gene sequencing methods with potential taxonomic inaccuracies for identifying bacteria with closely-sequenced 16 S rRNA genes. In addition, these methods perform at a slow pace and have strict requirements.

Bosco et al. 4 examined two deep learning algorithms to automatically classify bacterium species without using prior feature extraction techniques. The authors proposed convolutional and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) by applying the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) paradigm to the RNNs. To evaluate their approach, they fed a deep learning architecture with a dataset encoded using the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) nucleotide representation. Soliman et al. 5 introduced a modified CNN model aimed at building an integrated system for classifying DNA. The presented system depends on downsampling and CNNs for feature reduction. The study explores the impact of altering various hyperparameters, including the learning rate, mismatch size, and number of epochs, on the performance of CNNs. An evaluation was conducted using 16 S rRNA bacterial sequences. The simulation results demonstrate that employing a CNN with wavelet subsampling provides an optimal balance between processing time and accuracy. Additionally, the authors delved into the potential of deep learning in bioinformatics, particularly for the classification and regression of DNA sequences. Soliman, N. et al. 6 classified bacteria into taxonomic levels by developing a hybrid model that combines a CNN with an RNN. The model depends on a wavelet-based pooling method, and it was compared to a CNN with random projection and an RNN with histogram-oriented gradient features. The simulation results indicate that the hybrid model achieves the highest classification accuracy. The key contribution of this work is the application of deep neural networks to improve existing DNA sequence analysis for the whole-genome sequence categorization of bacteria. Garrity, G. M. et al. 7 proved that an enhanced identification method could aid in the transition from 16 S rRNA taxonomy to a whole-genome-based bacterium taxonomy.

Despite extensive research on taxonomic classification, a significant research gap exists because traditional methods become computationally expensive as the data volume increases. In contrast, deep learning models, which are data-intensive, benefit from larger datasets. Therefore, we propose an improved F-CNN for taxonomic classification of bacterial DNA sequences, specifically focusing on the 500-bp segment. Understanding the CNN approach relative strengths and weaknesses in comparison to previous methodologies can help confirm its effectiveness and identify areas, where it excels. The effectiveness of the proposed model was investigated for sequences longer than 500 bp. This is particularly relevant given that bacterial sequences can vary greatly in length. Generally, understanding the performance of a model on longer sequences can provide insights into its scalability and applicability to real-world datasets.

Literature survey

Classical machine learning techniques for taxonomic classification of bacterial datasets

Recent studies have explored the use of classical Machine-Learning (ML) techniques for taxonomic classification of bacterial datasets. Kotwal et al. 8 used classical ML techniques like Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) classifiers, for taxonomic classification of bacteria, achieving high accuracies up to 97.24% for bacterial species and general classification. Slabbinck et al. 9 proposed a framework for bacterial species classification that integrates 16 S rRNA gene data into Fatty Acid Methyl Ester (FAME) based classification, and achieved better results for hard-to-distinguish species. Slabbinck et al. 10 applied ML techniques to FAME profiling, achieving perfect identification at the genus level and good species identification for specific genera. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the potential of classical ML techniques for taxonomic classification in bacterial datasets. Courtney et al. 11 proposed a method that uses raw 16 S rRNA gene sequence data from human stool samples they downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA). These samples come from 490 subjects and are categorized as either normal or having Screen-Relevant Neoplasia (SRN) according to the metadata. This method accelerates the classification of new samples by eliminating the need for model retraining. Fuhl et al. 12 used k-mer distributions from DNA sequences with ML techniques for taxonomic classification to improve classification performance, while reducing runtime and energy consumption. This study highlights the use of k-mer distributions and ML techniques for taxonomy classification. The results demonstrate that the presented approach performs better at the genus level and produces similar results at the superkingdom and phylum levels to those obtained with advanced algorithms like BERTax and MMseqs2.

Deep learning taxonomic classification techniques on bacterial datasets

Helaly et al. 13,14 achieved high-accuracy taxonomy classification of bacterial 16 S rRNA genes by combining CNN architectures with sequence representations. They leveraged 16 S rRNA sequences to accurately categorize bacterial species into taxonomic ranks, while evaluating several sequence representations and CNN architectures. Results show that representations that retain sequential information perform better, with a deep CNN achieving 91.7% accuracy for more representative sequences; and a wide, shallow network reaching 90.6% accuracy for less representative sequences. Wonohadidjojo et al.15 proposed a method to classify bacterial images using a deep CNN with Transfer Learning (TL), specifically implementing the ResNet-50 architecture, and their method demonstrated successful classification.

Raman et al. 16 applied Deep Belief Networks (DBNs) and CNNs for taxonomic classification using metagenomic data, achieving over 90% accuracy. Nonetheless, these models require substantial training resources and show reduced adaptability to new environments. Refahi et al. 17 presented a lightweight CNN architecture combined with dynamic feature recalibration layers designed specifically for resource-constrained environments. Despite its minimal parameters, the model maintained over 90% accuracy on full-length 16 S rRNA sequences, showcasing its generalizability. Kumar et al. 18 presented the fuzzy representation as a newly-proposed method in the field of bioinformatics and taxonomic classification. The representation with fuzzy weights is obtained by applying a Gaussian fuzzy inference system on the similarity scores of the sequences when compared with a reference sequence database.

Jiang et al. 19 introduced the Bacterial Sequence Classification with Taxonomic Tree Embedding (BSC-TTE) technique, which offers improved classification accuracy compared to other advanced techniques. Current deep learning techniques for bacterial sequence classification typically depend on a discriminator, which requires numerous model parameters. However, these techniques often lack generalizability and have limited applicability. In contrast, the BSC-TTE technique is simpler and faster. It exhibits better adaptability than that of a conventional deep learning discriminator. Recent work related to this field is presented in Table 1.

F-CNN model for bacterial sequence classification

The proposed F-CNN model classifies bacteria DNA sequences, accurately, even when only the 500-bp part of all sequences is available. It predicts the uncertainty and imprecision in the input data, which are common in 500-bp sequences, and performs label assignment. The F-CNN model was inspired by the architecture proposed by Soliman et al. 5, which is effective in classifying bacteria. As shown in Fig. 1, the F-CNN model comprises three sequential phases, namely preprocessing, feature selection, and classification.

Structure of the proposed model.

This study depends on the RDP 11 dataset, a publicly available and widely-recognized repository of bacterial 16 S rRNA sequences. This dataset includes over 1,423,984 annotated sequences covering multiple taxonomic ranks from phylum to genus. For experimental evaluation, we extracted two types of subsets, namely short-read sequences (500-bp) and full-length sequences. Short-read sequences are represented with a random sample of 50,000 sequences trimmed to 500 base pairs to simulate real-world limitations in read length. On the other hand, full-length sequences are represented with another sample of 50,000 untrimmed sequences to test generalization on longer reads. Each taxonomic level was sampled to ensure class balance: 3 classes (Phylum), 5 classes (Class), 19 classes (Order), 65 classes (Family), and 100 classes (Genus).

Preprocessing phase

This phase plays a crucial role in accurately analysing input data, particularly in the case of DNA sequences. The challenge associated with DNA sequences lies in reading and determining the order of nucleotides on the DNA strand. These sequences must be transformed into vectors using methods that can interpret them. In addition, data imbalance often leads to overfitting in the training of classifiers. Improper data preprocessing can directly cause numerical bias and signal loss. In this study, the input DNA sequences were preprocessed using the Frequency Chaos Game Representation (FCGR) algorithm with k = 6, which converts these sequences to image representations. Image representation techniques generate much more information than text representation 23. The FCGR is used to analyze and visualize DNA sequences 24. In this representation, the DNA sequence is divided into non-overlapping k-mer, which are subsequences of length k. Each k-mer is then represented as a point in a multidimensional space, with each dimension corresponding to one of the four nucleotides (A, T, C, and G). The FCGR takes this step further by using the Chaos Game Representation (CGR) to map the frequency of each k-mer onto a two-dimensional plane. The FCGR-45 matrix is used to construct a grayscale image that can be considered a sequence signature, as shown in Fig. 2. The dimensions of this image are functions of the dimension k of the k-mer. Feature selection phase

The input image data is converted to feature maps extracted from the trained multilayer CNNs. These features are sent to the feature selection phase to select the most important subset of characteristics using different methods. The IBGA 26 can reveal important characteristics of an image, namely its fitness function. IBGA depends on both F-score and BGA methods to efficiently select relevant features by utilizing multiple classifiers for fitness evaluation, ultimately aiming to improve the classification performance across various models and enhance the performance of BGA, which typically has a long execution time. The primary goal of IBGA is to speed up feature selection by first using the F-score to remove irrelevant features from the dataset. This initial step attempts to reduce the execution time of BGA. Subsequently, BGA is used with an improved fitness function to evaluate each chromosome in the population 27–29.

To reduce computational complexity and enhance feature relevance, an initial filtering phase is performed using the F-score metric. This technique quickly ranks all CNN-extracted features based on their discriminative power concerned with class labels. Irrelevant or weakly-contributing features (those with low F-score) are discarded. Only the top-ranked features are passed as input to the IBGA, which further performs fine-grained feature selection by optimizing the classification performance. This two-stage process, comprising F-score based filtering followed by IBGA, substantially reduces dimensionality and accelerates convergence, as shown in Fig. 3.

The IBGA feature selection.

The IBGA depends on a fitness function to calculate the average accuracy across many classification models trained on the same dataset. The proposed IBGA feature selection is illustrated in Fig. 3. This approach ensures a comprehensive evaluation of chromosomes in the population. By considering several classifiers rather than relying on a single classifier, IBGA selects a subset of features that have a significant impact across various classification methods. Following the implementation of the F-score filtering, the IBGA initializes a population comprising chromosomes representing binary features. Each chromosome corresponds to a number of features in the dataset, where 1 indicates feature selection and 0 indicates feature removal at specific positions.

Once a population of chromosomes is generated, each chromosome fitness value is estimated. The fitness value is the average accuracy of multiple classifiers (Nc) to ensure that the selected feature subset improves the performance of every classifier.

The fitness value for the ath chromosome is represented by fit(cha), the number of classifiers used to evaluate the selected features within each chromosome is indicated by Nc, and the accuracy value of the jth classifier on the selected features of the ath chromosome is indicated by Accuracyj (cha).

Dataset description and results

The dataset used in this study was downloaded from the RDP repository 30, which contains data on 1,423,984 bacterial gene sequences. For each bacterium, we obtain information about its taxonomic categories, in addition to its genetic sequence.

The training set was 80% of the data, and the testing set was the other 20%. Additionally, 20% of the training data was internally used for validation during training via the validation_split parameter in Keras. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001 and the categorical cross-entropy loss function that is appropriate for multi-class classification. The reported processing time reduction of approximately 215 s was obtained by averaging the total training times of the proposed F-CNN + IBGA model and comparing them with those of the F-CNN + GA, FSDOC, OLS, and other models over three independent trials for each model using identical hardware and batch configurations. The timing was recorded using Python time module from the start of model training to the final evaluation. All training experiments were conducted using the same settings. Optimizer is Adam optimizer, learning rate is 0.0001 (constant across all experiments), batch size is 64, number of epochs is 20, loss function is categorical cross-entropy, validation split depends on 20% of the training data, and early stopping is not applied (fixed epochs are used for fairness across methods).

These settings were held constant across all models (GA, FSDOC, OLS, and the proposed model based on IBGA), ensuring fairness and reproducibility.

Quantitative validation: K-fold cross-validation

A 5-fold cross-validation strategy was employed to assess the model performance stability. The dataset was randomly partitioned into five equal subsets. In each iteration, four subsets were used for training and one for testing. The average results across all folds were as follows: Accuracy is 83.98%, Precision is 82.56%, Recall is 84.10%, and F-score is 83.31%. The low standard deviation across folds (< 1.5%) indicates strong generalizability and stability of the proposed model.

Comparative validation

The proposed F-CNN with IBGA was compared against multiple baseline models, including CNN + GA, CNN + FSDOC, and CNN + OLS. State-of-the-art models include Rizzo et al.22, Lo Bosco et al.4, and Soliman et al.5.

As summarized in Table 7 and Fig. 9, the F-CNN + IBGA model outperformed all other ones for 500-bp sequences, achieving the highest accuracy (84.03%) and a superior F-score. Additionally, the model demonstrated 215 s reduction in runtime, validating its efficiency alongside accuracy. This combination of statistical validation and model benchmarking confirms the applicability, novelty, and robustness of the proposed model.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced a novel Fuzzy-weighted Convolutional Neural Network (F-CNN) model enhanced with an Improved Binary Genetic Algorithm (IBGA) method for the taxonomic classification of bacterial DNA sequences, particularly targeting the classification of short 500-bp segments. The proposed model effectively combines the strengths of deep learning and fuzzy logic processing to address challenges such as overlapping class boundaries and classification uncertainty—issues that are often underrepresented in traditional classification frameworks.

The F-CNN model leverages a robust feature extraction process through CNN layers and refines the input features using IBGA to select the most informative subset. Experimental results on the RDP 11 dataset, which includes over 1.4 million bacterial sequences, demonstrate the superiority of our model. The model achieved a classification accuracy of 84.03% at the genus level for 500-bp sequences and showed competitive performance on full-length sequences as well. Furthermore, the approach significantly reduced the training time by approximately 215 s compared to other methods.

The novelty of this work is highlighted by the integration of fuzzy weights into the CNN classification layer, which improves robustness to ambiguous or closely-related classes. Additionally, the IBGA-driven feature selection process ensures efficiency and enhances overall model performance.

For future research, we plan to extend the model to support whole-genome bacterial classification, explore the use of the model in real-time diagnostic and metagenomic tools, and investigate the incorporation of explainable AI (XAI) methods to improve the transparency and interpretability of classification decisions, particularly in sensitive applications such as healthcare and bio-surveillance.

This study provided a scalable and interpretable framework that advances bacterial taxonomic classification and can be adapted to various biological and environmental analysis domains.
