AI Guided Parenteral Nutrition Therapy After Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation
Authors: Abdel Badih el Ariss, Thanaphong Phongpreecha, Marc Ghanem, Mehrdad Kimari, Tomin James, Elise Freche, John A. Kerner, Brice Gaudillière, Martin S. Angst, Shabnam Gaskari, David K. Stevenson, Melody Smith, Nima Aghaeepour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adults undergoing hematopoietic cell transplantation often develop serious complications that cause rapid nutritional decline. We developed and evaluated an AI approach to standardize intravenous nutrition (total parenteral nutrition, TPN) during this vulnerable period. Using real-world records from Stanford Health Care (6402 transplants, 2008–2025), we analyzed 1473 adults who received TPN, totaling 27,447 patient-days, linking each day’s clinical state to the next day’s prescription. We created a library of 30 standardized TPN regimens and trained a model to recommend next-day dose adjustments based on laboratory data and the existing prescription. (Pearson r ≈ 0.71). We then assessed an AI policy learned from past care and found that the Reinforcement learning agent selected dose adjustments with a higher composite score than the existing clinical policy. These results show that AI-guided TPN is feasible and may enhance bedside decision-making for adult transplant care, warranting prospective evaluation.

Introduction

Hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) is a high-intensity therapy used to treat a range of malignant and non-malignant hematologic disorders1. While potentially curative, it is frequently complicated by profound nutritional decline driven by conditioning regimens and acute graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), resulting in rapid weight loss, muscle wasting, and protein–energy malnutrition2–4. This makes HCT a representative clinical context in which to evaluate AI-driven strategies for optimizing total parenteral nutrition (TPN).

Total parenteral nutrition (TPN)—the intravenous delivery of complete macro- and micronutrient formulations—remains an essential adjunct for HCT recipients who cannot meet their nutritional requirements orally or enterally because of severe gastrointestinal toxicity (e.g., mucositis, nausea, and vomiting)5. Although contemporary practice guidelines prioritize enteral feeding whenever it is tolerable6, TPN is frequently indispensable during the peri-transplant interval to avert or correct the rapid lean-body-mass depletion that accompanies conditioning and early complications. Tailoring TPN for these patients is notoriously complex because clinicians must navigate risks associated with both the composition of the infusion and the duration of therapy. The formulation must be precise: an incorrect balance can trigger acute complications such as hyperglycemia, electrolyte imbalances, or refeeding syndrome7,8. At the same time, prolonged reliance on TPN carries its own set of hazards. Long-term administration frequently leads to hepatic dysfunction, including cholestasis, and increases the risk of catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), potentially compounding transplant-associated injuries9,10. This dual challenge requires satisfying exceptionally high nutritional demands while meticulously avoiding these immediate and long-term dangers.

Operationally, implementing TPN therapy is resource-intensive, presenting significant logistical and financial constraints. The estimated average cost of TPN exceeds $239 per patient per day, significantly contributing to the already high expenses associated with HCT procedures11. These costs derive from nutritional components, specialized compounding processes, pharmacy staffing, infusion equipment, and rigorous laboratory monitoring. Compounding personalized TPN formulations under sterile conditions is complex, requiring substantial pharmacy infrastructure and skilled personnel. Additionally, intensive multidisciplinary oversight, including daily laboratory monitoring of electrolytes, glucose levels, liver, and renal function tests, further increases operational demands and staffing requirements12.

HCT patients display considerable variability in nutritional requirements based on baseline nutrition status, organ function, infection status, and metabolic needs. However, TPN formulations typically follow standardized protocols, often insufficiently personalized beyond basic adjustments for weight or general nutritional targets13. There are currently no validated, standardized clinical decision-support tools that integrate patient-specific data to systematically guide macronutrient and micronutrient adjustments in response to evolving clinical status14,15.

Building on our prior work in neonatal intensive care16, this study applies and extends a multi-stage computational approach to guide TPN formulations for adult HCT recipients. First, we develop predictive models that learn to compress each patient’s complex clinical picture into a concise representation of their nutritional state. Within this learned space, patients with similar metabolic profiles naturally group together, forming a continuous map of nutritional needs. We leveraged this property to organize the space into 30 clinically representative TPN dosing profiles. Using temporal sequences of these representations alongside prior prescriptions, we then trained sequential models to forecast each patient’s next-day TPN composition. Finally, to move beyond replicating existing practice and toward optimizing it, we introduce a novel prescriptive framework built around an adaptive policy. Formulated within the reinforcement learning paradigm, this policy serves as a data-driven decision function that maps a patient’s clinical state to a treatment action, explicitly balancing immediate metabolic needs with longer-term homeostatic goals. Pre-trained on a decade of clinical data reflecting standard practices aligned with American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN) guidelines, it proposes the most appropriate standardized TPN formulation for each 24-hour interval. By establishing this robust, data-driven system for TPN management, our approach aims to optimize the selection of standardized formulations, improve therapeutic consistency, and ultimately enhance recovery for this vulnerable patient population.

Results

We analyzed longitudinal records from 1473 adult autologous and allogeneic HCT recipients who received TPN at Stanford between 2008 and 2025 (Table 1). Among TPN recipients with malignant disorders, the primary indication for bone marrow transplant was acute myeloid leukemia (Fig. S1a), while aplastic anemia was the most common indication for non-malignant disorders (Fig. S1b). The median treatment duration was 11 days (IQR 5–24), with the 95th percentile extending to 53 days (Fig. S2). Temporal trajectories of macronutrients, electrolytes, and additives differed markedly across the cohort: several components rose monotonically, while others peaked in the second week before declining (Fig. S3). These heterogeneous patterns reflect the interplay of individual patient physiology, variation in provider prescribing practices, and the evolution of conditioning regimens and supportive care over the 17-year study period. By encoding this temporal and practice-level variability directly into the training data, our computational framework learns from, rather than is confounded by, these real-world sources of heterogeneity. By distilling these heterogeneous prescribing patterns into a structured, data-driven framework, we established the foundation for a standardized TPN formulary. Next, we used a machine learning model to analyze this complex snapshot. To predict a patient’s TPN requirements, the model first mapped high-dimensional raw clinical concept codes into a 16-dimensional latent feature vector representing the patient’s clinical state. By condensing these longitudinal records, the model effectively captured the unique and dynamic physiological demands of the hematopoietic cell transplant recovery period, including the effects of concomitant medications and the specific metabolic requirements for engraftment. This holistic, low-dimensional representation was subsequently integrated with the patient’s immediate pre-administration clinical measurements to serve as the foundational input for our downstream tasks, enabling the accurate and individualized prediction of TPN dosages.

The model accurately predicted prescribed TPN doses, especially for ingredients that are governed by standardized protocols. We saw exceptionally high agreement for selenium (R = 0.971), dextrose (R = 0.904), and insulin (R = 0.913), and strong agreement for most electrolytes (R > 0.85; Fig. 1b). In contrast, ingredients where dosing is more tailored to the individual patient, such as lipids (R = 0.600) and standalone vitamins administered outside the standard multivitamin (MVI) package, like phytonadione (R = 0.605), showed more moderate agreement. This difference reflects the real-world variability in clinical decision-making for these components, rather than a limitation of our model. Ultimately, the model’s 16-feature summary proved effective at capturing the critical information needed to guide TPN dosing.

Optimizing the TPN formulary: balancing accuracy and clinical practicality

An optimal library size of 30 TPN formulas (K = 30) was selected to provide the best balance between reconstruction accuracy and practical complexity. Our analysis revealed a sharp increase in performance up to K = 30, which captured approximately 93% of the maximum achievable accuracy (measured by relative Pearson’s R). Beyond this inflection point, increasing the library size yielded negligible performance gains at the cost of substantially greater complexity, demonstrating diminishing returns. This approach ensures a high-fidelity yet manageable set of base formulas for clinical application (Fig. S4).

Guided clustering establishes 30 representative TPN dosing profiles with preserved accuracy

Using a guided clustering approach (see “Methods”), we organized patients into 30 clinically representative TPN dosing profiles. We began with 100 data-driven candidate groups and iteratively merged them while monitoring performance on a held-out validation set. Compared to standard K-means clustering, our approach maintained or enhanced dose-prediction accuracy across 15 of the 24 TPN components. Notably, this performance extended to several infrequently prescribed components, resulting in more robust recommendations for complex TPN regimens.

Our clustering methodology preserved accuracy for common TPN constituents while dramatically sharpening predictions for underrepresented additives. The largest performance jumps were seen in rarely prescribed micronutrients, such as Levocarnitine (Δ = +1.02) and Folic Acid (Δ = +0.58), with modest gains for core nutrients like Potassium (Δ = +0.03). This reallocation of the model’s focus involved a slight trade-off, causing small performance decreases for a few components like copper (Δ = −0.04) and lipids (Δ = −0.05). This outcome validates the strategy as an effective post-hoc calibration for handling imbalanced clinical data (Fig. 1c).

Meeting clinical needs with targeted TPN adjustments

Analysis of the 30 formulations reveals three primary TPN archetypes, each designed for a distinct clinical purpose. These can be broadly classified as: amino-acid/electrolyte–dense bags for acute repletion, dextrose-heavy bags for maintenance, and micronutrient- or lipid-enriched bags for specialized nutritional requirements (Fig. 1a). This functional categorization demonstrates that TPN prescriptions are highly structured and tailored to meet specific, targeted therapeutic goals.

A patient’s physiological state improved in direct response to targeted changes in their TPN formulation. Initially, the patient presented with significant electrolyte imbalances, including low sodium and magnesium (Fig. 2, cluster C6). This prompted a switch to a corrective, electrolyte-rich TPN, which successfully stabilized their levels and transitioned them to an improved physiological state (cluster C18). Once stabilized, the prescription was appropriately de-escalated to a maintenance formula to meet their ongoing needs (cluster C1). The LSTM model served as a strong baseline, particularly excelling in predicting core nutrients. It achieved high correlation scores for electrolytes and trace elements—such as calcium, sodium, and selenium (R > 0.90)—demonstrating its strength in capturing predictable temporal patterns. Learning from clinical practice: a framework for offline evaluation of AI dosing policies

To assess the safety and potential utility of our learned policies prior to any consideration of clinical deployment, we conducted a comprehensive offline policy evaluation (OPE). This evaluation tested whether the policies produced statistically significant improvements in expected clinical outcomes, defined as achieving laboratory measurements within the normal range following TPN administration, and examined the novel decision-making patterns learned by the model in comparison with clinician practice.

Offline reinforcement-learning policies produced statistically significant gains in simulated patient outcomes compared with the historical clinician policy. On held-out historical data, the expected reward increased by 8.9% with implicit Q-learning (IQL) and 8.1% with conservative Q-learning (CQL) over a baseline of 1.27 ± 0.02. Proactive vs. reactive: contrasting AI and clinician dosing strategies

The dosing recommendations of a reinforcement learning (RL) agent are contrasted with historical prescriptions from clinicians (Fig. 4b), where both are plotted against the laboratory data that informed the decisions. Because the agent was trained offline, the plotted outcomes reflect the actual decisions made by clinicians. In contrast, the agent’s recommended doses represent a hypothetical alternative that was systematically learned from data-driven estimations of these same clinical scenarios.

The analysis revealed a consistent pattern of the RL agent employing a proactive, finely tuned dosing strategy, whereas clinicians favored a more conservative, reactive approach. This was most evident in glucose management, where the agent recommended immediate, large insulin adjustments via TPN for hyperglycemia (levels >180 mg/dL), while clinicians waited for repeated high readings, indicating reliance on bedside boluses. Similarly, the agent anticipated downward trends in sodium and made preemptive adjustments, unlike the expert policy of waiting for significant drops before making larger, infrequent changes. The agent also demonstrated superior calibration for magnesium, avoiding the overshoots caused by the clinicians’ abrupt dose changes. For potassium and calcium, the agent used frequent, carefully titrated supplements and preemptive boluses to maintain stability, contrasting with the experts’ more passive and less granular interventions. It is also noteworthy that components like trace elements were excluded, as there is no routine lab monitoring available to inform our dynamic reward function.

While clinicians typically exhibit conservative and reactive patterns when prescribing continuous infusions such as TPN, the RL agent’s learned policy was consistently more proactive and granular.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive computational framework designed to confront the enduring difficulties of total parenteral nutrition (TPN) management in the high-risk hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) population2,3. Traditional TPN management, a cornerstone of supportive care, is notoriously complex, expensive, and logistically demanding, often failing to provide sufficiently personalized nutrition8. Our work addressed these gaps by demonstrating how a multi-stage machine learning pipeline created a more standardized, efficient, and intelligent system for nutritional support.

Our findings show that the apparent complexity of individual nutritional needs can be systematically organized into a manageable, standardized formulary16. Patients with similar metabolic states naturally cluster together within a continuous ‘nutritional manifold’ learned by the model, and from this structure, we extracted a library of 30 prototype formulas that covers 93% of the observed nutritional variance across the entire cohort. Notably, our task-aware compression substantially improved the representation of clinically vital but rarely used additives, ensuring the resulting formulary captures the full spectrum of nutritional requirements rather than defaulting to common prescribing patterns.

The operational and clinical ramifications of such a formulary are substantial. From an operational standpoint, it directly counters the immense logistical and financial burdens of daily, customized prescription compounding. Pharmacies can pre-batch these 30 formulas, slashing preparation times, reducing staffing needs, and minimizing waste. Clinically, it provides a robust, data-backed starting point for any patient, ensuring a baseline level of evidence-based care while still allowing for clinician oversight and adjustment.

A standardized formulary defines what goes into the TPN bag; our framework goes further, steering both when adjustments should be made and how they are implemented over the course of therapy. We first demonstrated that the temporal logic of clinician prescribing can be learned. Both LSTM and, more effectively, the sequence-to-one Transformer model, were able to predict the next TPN bag with high fidelity for most core nutrients such as dextrose, insulin, and key electrolytes. This enables improved planning of nutritional requirements in advance.

The primary contribution of this research is the application of advantage-weighted imitation learning to identify and replicate the subset of historical clinical decisions that correlated with better immediate biochemical outcomes, as measured by our proxy reward function. The administration of TPN is a complex, dynamic task where clinicians must continually adjust nutrient compositions based on a patient’s evolving condition. We model TPN prescriptions as independent contextual decisions, where a policy network learns a policy to map patient states (e.g., lab values, vital signs, and demographics) to optimal actions (TPN dose vectors) that maximize immediate biochemical improvements17. By leveraging a large historical dataset of electronic health records, our RL agent can learn this policy entirely offline. This approach is critical in medicine, as it avoids the significant safety risks and ethical concerns associated with the trial-and-error learning required by online RL methods18. Our risk management framework prioritizes clinical safety through comprehensive offline validation and transparent recommendation logic. By thoroughly characterizing system behavior during development, we provide clinicians with well-understood, predictable decision support that integrates seamlessly with existing clinical workflows19–21.

In this domain, a common baseline is behavioral cloning (BC), which uses supervised learning to directly copy the actions taken by clinicians in the dataset22. However, BC is fundamentally limited because it learns to replicate the average recorded behavior, failing to distinguish between optimal and suboptimal decisions. Our work instead employs IQL, a state-of-the-art offline RL algorithm designed to overcome this limitation23. IQL works by learning an implicit Q-function that preferentially up-weights state-action pairs that led to high-reward outcomes. In doing so, it learns to selectively imitate the most effective decisions from historical data, effectively extracting a policy superior to the average behavior. Furthermore, IQL is an inherently conservative algorithm that constrains the learned policy to actions within the support of the training data. This property is crucial for clinical safety, as it prevents the agent from recommending novel or untested dosages that lie outside the scope of established clinical practice24.

Our rigorous offline evaluations, conducted on a held-out test set, confirm the value of Implicit Q-Learning. The policy derived from the IQL agent achieved a statistically significant 8.9% increase in cumulative reward compared to the historical policies enacted by physicians. This result indicates that the agent successfully identified and synthesized sequences of superior dosing strategies from the data. This success lays the groundwork for a clinical decision support system at the bedside, where an agent could analyze real-time patient data from the electronic health record and recommend TPN adjustments to a supervising clinician. While these promising offline results must be validated in prospective randomized controlled trials to prove clinical efficacy and safety, they represent a critical first step. This work demonstrates a clear potential to establish a more consistent, optimized, and data-driven standard of care for TPN administration, echoing successes of offline RL in other areas of critical care, such as mechanical ventilation and sepsis management20,25. Moreover, this approach provides value beyond direct policy optimization. The trained model itself serves as a powerful tool for scientific inquiry, allowing us to generate new, data-driven hypotheses about nutritional therapy.

Limitations and a roadmap for clinical translation

This study’s primary limitations are its retrospective, single-center design and, most importantly, its reliance on in silico evaluation of the reinforcement learning policy. Although robust offline policy evaluation was used, these statistical findings are predictive estimates and not a substitute for the causal evidence of improved patient outcomes that can only be established through a prospective trial. Our offline-only approach was necessitated by the significant safety and regulatory challenges inherent in deploying a live learning system. Therefore, while our results highlight the promise of using offline RL to derive optimized policies, they also underscore the critical need for future research into frameworks that enable the safe, prospective validation and adaptation of these policies in a real-world clinical environment.

Confounding variables in observational data may influence results, and the observed outcomes are tied to the clinician’s policy, not the agents. Secondly, the learned formulary and RL policy are products of our institution’s specific patient population, transplant methodology, and current prescribing patterns. Consequently, their generalizability to centers employing different conditioning and GVHD prophylaxis regimens must be formally validated, and the RL agent will require proactive updating should standard clinical guidelines evolve over time. Future work could also explore transplant-type stratified analyses to characterize any differential model performance across autologous and allogeneic subgroups.

In addition, the current implementation relies on structured data formatted according to the OMOP common data model, with daily inputs consisting of diagnoses, medications, procedures, and laboratory measurements encoded into vector representations prior to inference. Although this ensures standardization, it limits immediate portability across heterogeneous EMR systems; broader deployment will require a preprocessing layer capable of mapping local EMR data into OMOP format to allow seamless integration without modification of the downstream pipeline.

These limitations define a clear roadmap for future work. The immediate next step is a prospective clinical pilot to validate the 30-prototype formulary, measuring its real-world impact on pharmacy efficiency, costs, and key clinical metrics like the incidence of hyperglycemia, electrolyte imbalances, and catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs). Following this, the RL agent will be prepared for clinical translation. This involves refining the reward signal to incorporate a broader set of outcomes (e.g., inflammatory markers and organ function scores) and ultimately testing the agent in a randomized controlled trial, likely as a real-time advisory tool whose recommendations clinicians can accept or reject.

Methods

Experimental design

This investigation was a retrospective, observational analysis of the bone-marrow-transplantation registry at Stanford Health Care, a quaternary academic center in California. All Stanford Electronic health records (EHR) data were mapped to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) version 5.3.1. The dataset included patient observations (61,925,860), procedures (2,136,805), laboratory measurements (24,029,190), medication administrations (1,048,575), and diagnosed conditions (1,048,575). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Stanford University. The registry spans from 25 April 2008 until 29 January 2025 and documents 6402 autologous or allogeneic transplants. Among these recipients, 1473 adults (age ≥ 18 years) received at least one course of total parenteral nutrition (TPN) (Table S1), yielding 27,447 patient-days of paired “clinical state → TPN prescription” examples. Because the focus of this study is the optimization of TPN administration, both autologous and allogeneic HCT recipients were included. The model encodes each patient’s full clinical context, including diagnoses, medications, procedures, and transplant type, alongside real-time laboratory measurements, enabling it to learn appropriate TPN dosing across the full spectrum of transplant-related clinical scenarios.

For every prescription, we retrieved the complete macro- and micronutrient profile, infusion parameters, and a temporally aligned snapshot of the patient’s physiology: laboratory tests, medication administrations, procedures, diagnostic codes, and clinical observations recorded. We leveraged MOTOR26, a self-supervised foundation model pretrained for time-to-event (TTE) prediction on the STARR-OMOP repository, to transform raw clinical data into meaningful patient representations. The model compresses the daily multivariate signal from a suite of structured tables (condition_occurrence, measurement, drug_exposure, procedure_occurrence, person, death, and observation) into a unified 896-dimension embedding (768-d from MOTOR representing condition, drug, procedure, and 128-d representing prior measurement before TPN onset). Each resulting vector serves as a comprehensive representation of the patient’s clinical state on a given day before TPN was initiated.

Variational autoencoder with iterative clustering

A variational autoencoder (VAE) provides a probabilistic framework that simultaneously learns (i) a low-dimensional representation z of the high-dimensional clinical context and (ii) a generative decoder that maps z to a full 25-component TPN formulation. By forcing z to follow a spherical Gaussian prior through a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty, the encoder is encouraged to organize similar clinical states near one another, creating a continuous “nutritional manifold” on which interpolation is meaningful27.

After removing entries with corrupted timestamps all remaining columns were cast to numeric types. We reserved 80% of patient-days for training and 20% for final testing, preserving the empirical distribution of TPN volumes and caloric densities through stratified sampling. Mean imputation of missing covariates was restricted to training rows; missing target nutrient values, which represent components deliberately omitted from a prescription, were set to zero. Predictors and targets were standardized with μ = 0, σ² = 1 computed on the training split and applied unchanged to validation and test splits. For this predictive task, the model inputs (x) were defined as the 768-dimensional clinical embedding from the MOTOR foundation model combined with a vector of prior clinical measurements. The prediction target (y) was the subsequent day’s complete TPN dose. This vector of measurements was comprehensive, encompassing four main categories: Demographics (e.g., age, gender, and detailed race categories including combinations such as “White | Asian” or “Black or African American | Declines to State”); Anthropometrics & Vital Signs (including body weight, height, BMI, BSA, temperature, heart rate, pulse, systolic/diastolic/mean blood pressure, respiratory rate); a wide array of Laboratory Panels, spanning metabolic markers (e.g., glucose [blood, serum], electrolytes including sodium, potassium, calcium [total and ionized], chloride, bicarbonate, anion gap), liver function (e.g., AST, ALT, ALP, bilirubin [direct, indirect, total], albumin), renal indicators (e.g., creatinine, BUN, eGFR [CKD-EPI, MDRD], uric acid, phosphate, magnesium), coagulation (e.g., INR, PT, aPTT, thrombin time, fibrinogen, D-dimer, D-dimer FEU), lipids and vitamins (e.g., cholesterol [total, HDL], triglycerides, 25-hydroxyvitamin D2/D3, vitamin B12), and enzymes (e.g., amylase, LDH, CK, lactate); and Other Clinical Measurements, including fluid input/output balance, venous oxygen saturation, expired volume, pCO₂ (venous/adjusted), GCS, and left ventricular ejection fraction.

Sequential models for TPN forecasting

To model the inherently sequential nature of TPN prescription—where clinical decisions depend on patient trajectories and prior treatments—we evaluated two distinct deep learning architectures designed for time-series data: a long short-term memory (LSTM) network and a Transformer-based sequence-to-one model.

Long short-term memory (LSTM) network

Our first approach utilized an LSTM, a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) specifically designed to capture temporal dependencies by maintaining a memory of previous inputs over extended periods. For this model, the input at each time step was a 41-dimensional feature vector, created by concatenating a 16-D latent embedding from the VAE (summarizing the patient’s contemporaneous state) with the 25 components of the preceding TPN formulation. Patient histories were structured into uniform 10-step sequences, using padding and zero-masking to handle variable-length episodes.

A single-layer LSTM containing 2048 hidden units processed these sequences to forecast the subsequent TPN prescription. To rigorously prevent data leakage, the dataset was split at the patient level. The model was trained to minimize mean squared error using the AdamW optimizer (learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁵, weight decay = 0.1), with an early stopping criterion on the validation loss to ensure efficient convergence28.

Transformer-based sequence-to-one model

To capture more complex, long-range, and non-local patterns within the clinical trajectories, we next implemented a more advanced sequence-to-one (seq2one) Transformer architecture. The encoder for this model processed sequences of five consecutive latent VAE vectors. These vectors were projected into a 128-dimensional space and augmented with sinusoidal positional encodings to preserve temporal context. Three stacked encoder blocks, leveraging multi-head self-attention, then generated a rich representation of the patient’s state over time.

The decoder, in turn, processed historical sequences of TPN prescriptions (right-shifted by one time step) to autoregressively generate the prediction for the next TPN bag. To enhance robustness against outliers in the data, this model was trained using a Huber loss function (δ = 1). The training protocol also included an adaptive learning-rate decay strategy and an early stopping criterion with a patience of 50 epochs. The final selected model, chosen based on minimum validation loss, contained approximately 1.5 million trainable parameters29.

For our TPN dataset, the 0.7 expectile struck a pragmatic compromise—optimistic enough to allow the agent to deviate from median practice when the data give strong evidence of benefit, yet conservative enough to prevent large excursions in rarely explored regions of the 25-dimensional dosing space. Because the actor and critic share a 512-256 dense encoder initialized from the same random seed and are updated synchronously (soft target rate τ = 0.002), representation drift is minimized, and training remains numerically stable over the full 150-epoch schedule.

Empirically, IQL’s selective policy update translated into the narrowest interquartile range of action errors across most macro- and micronutrients, while achieving a mean FQE value slightly above the historical clinician policy and the BC baseline. These gains arise without the explicit conservative regularizer of CQL, illustrating that advantage-weighted imitation alone can extract clinically meaningful improvements from retrospective data when reward shaping is informative and the behavioral support is dense—as is the case for our 27,447 TPN orders. Because the historical TPN logs rarely capture more than a single laboratory draw after each bag because of possible external interventions from the recorded TPN prescription, the reward signal effectively ends at the next measurement; there is little, if any, information linking a prescription to longer-term biochemical evolution32, thus we focused on the pre-dose value and the very first lab result after each daily TPN order because, in routine practice, that single follow-up measurement is what clinicians use to judge whether the prescription requires adjustments. To mirror this real-world decision cycle and honor our data constraint, we set the discount factor to γ = 0, turning each dose into a self-contained, one-step problem. With γ at zero, the value function collapses to the expected immediate reward, eliminating the need to propagate credit through unobserved future states and removing bias that could arise from extrapolating over missing trajectory segments.

Qualitative trajectory analysis

To visualize behavioral differences at the bedside, we randomly selected a held-out patient and reconstructed their full TPN course. For each calendar day, we plotted the measured laboratory trajectory against both the expert dose and the IQL agent’s recommendation, highlighting normal laboratory ranges. These plots, together with distributional histograms and scatter matrices of action differences, provide an interpretable audit of where and how the agent departs from historical practice, complementing the aggregate metrics above.

Collectively, this offline RL pipeline transforms the latent clinical manifold into a control policy that can be quantitatively benchmarked against expert prescribing patterns and qualitatively inspected at the patient level, laying the groundwork for prospective evaluation in a decision-support setting.

Computational environment

All experiments were conducted using Python 3.12.2 on a high-performance computing (HPC) node running Ubuntu 24.04 LTS. The server was equipped with four NVIDIA L40S GPUs (46 GB VRAM each, CUDA 12.6), powered by two Intel Xeon Gold 6548Y + CPUs (128 logical threads) and 1 TB of RAM.

The software stack relied on NumPy 1.26.4, SciPy 1.15.1, and pandas 2.2.3 for numerical operations, with classical machine learning models implemented using scikit-learn 1.5.2. All deep learning and reinforcement learning models were built in PyTorch 2.5.1, and offline RL experiments leveraged d3rlpy 2.8.1. Visualizations were generated using Matplotlib 3.10.0 and Seaborn 0.13.2, while parallel computations were managed with joblib. For reproducibility, all relevant random seeds were fixed to a constant value, and deterministic CUDA kernels were enabled.
