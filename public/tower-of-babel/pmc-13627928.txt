TaNRT1.1B-mediated nitrogen acquisition contributes to salt adaptation and yield-trait maintenance in wheat
Authors: Shuxuan Quan, Yitian Li, Cheng Peng, Qian Zhang, Zhen Wang, Jie Zang, Guiluan Wang, Xian Sheng Zhang, Yifeng Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil salinity is a major environmental stress that limits global wheat production, in part by impairing nitrogen acquisition, which integrates essential nutrient supply with stress-adaptation signaling. Although nitrogen fertilization can alleviate salt stress, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unknown. Here, we show that salt stress suppresses nitrate accumulation in wheat seedlings, as well as reducing root and shoot growth. Comparative transcriptome profiling reveals that nitrate-associated genes and transcriptional regulators are significantly enriched among salt-responsive genes. To explore the functional relevance of this enrichment, we focused on TaNRT1.1B, a prominently downregulated nitrate transporter gene, demonstrating its role as a key regulator linking nitrate acquisition and salt-stress responses. Disruption of TaNRT1.1B reduced nitrate uptake, impaired ABA responses, compromised salt tolerance, and significantly reduced the values of yield-related traits under salt stress. Supporting the functional relevance of TaNRT1.1B, salt-tolerant wheat accessions had significantly higher TaNRT1.1B expression and nitrate levels under saline conditions than salt-sensitive lines. Collectively, our results demonstrate that TaNRT1.1B links nitrate uptake with salt-stress tolerance to coordinate growth and stress resilience in wheat, providing a potential genetic target for breeding of varieties with improved salt tolerance, nitrogen use efficiency, and grain yield.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum) is a staple crop cultivated worldwide, whose stable production is fundamental to the global food security of a growing population [[1], [2], [3]]. However, wheat growth and productivity are constrained by multiple environmental factors. Soil salinity is one of the most detrimental, severely limiting plant growth and threatening wheat production [4,5]. The situation is exacerbated by the continued expansion of saline soils worldwide, driven by climate change, poor irrigation practices, and industrial pollution. This poses a particular challenge for wheat, which—despite being cultivated across a wider geographic range than any other crop—is relatively sensitive to salinity [1,2,4]. Dissecting the molecular mechanisms that underlie salt tolerance in wheat is therefore essential for understanding plant adaptation and sustaining grain production in an era of expanding saline soils.

From a plant physiological perspective, the detrimental effects of salinity manifest as hyperosmotic stress, ion toxicity, and oxidative damage triggered by excessive ROS accumulation [6]. Salt tolerance in wheat is a complex quantitative trait governed by a network of genes involved in ion homeostasis, ROS scavenging, osmotic adjustment, and signal transduction. These functional classes of genes work in concert to maintain ionic balance, reduce ROS levels, transduce stress signals, and modulate cellular metabolism. Characterizing the expression patterns and functional interactions among these genes provides a foundation for improving salt tolerance in wheat through molecular breeding and genetic engineering [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]].

High concentrations of sodium ions (Na+) impair the uptake of water and essential nutrients such as potassium (K+), nitrogen, phosphorus, and calcium, leading to ionic imbalance, oxidative damage, and ultimately reduced grain yield and quality [13]. Nitrogen in particular has emerged as a key player in stress adaptation owing to its dual role as an essential macronutrient and a signaling molecule [14]. Nitrogen supplementation has been shown to enhance salt tolerance by regulating osmotic potential, mitigating oxidative damage, maintaining ion homeostasis, stabilizing membrane integrity, and optimizing photosynthetic performance [13,[15], [16], [17]]. Moreover, an increased content of amino acids, direct products of nitrogen assimilation, can enhance both grain yield and salt tolerance in rice [18]. However, less is known about how nitrogen metabolism interacts with salt-stress responses in wheat.

Plants acquire nitrogen primarily in the form of nitrate (NO3−), whose uptake and assimilation pathways have been extensively characterized in model species. In Arabidopsis, the nitrate transporter Nitrate Transporter 1.1 (NRT1.1) functions as both a transporter and a sensor, regulating nitrate-responsive gene expression [[19], [20], [21]]. Upon nitrate perception, NRT1.1 triggers a signaling cascade that leads to the nuclear accumulation of Nodule Inception (NIN)-like protein 7 (NLP7), a master transcription factor that activates nitrate-responsive genes [14,22,23]. In rice, OsNRT1.1B plays a central role in nitrate assimilation and plant growth and also functions as an abscisic acid (ABA) receptor, integrating nitrate and stress signaling pathways [24]. Despite these findings in model species, the potential role of TaNRT1.1 in linking nitrogen metabolism to salt-stress responses in wheat remains to be characterized.

In this study, we sought to characterize the molecular mechanisms by which salt stress affects nitrogen metabolism in wheat. We performed a time-series transcriptome analysis of roots and shoots under salt treatment, integrating morphological and transcriptomic data to identify key regulators that link the nitrogen and salt response pathways. This approach led to the identification of TaNRT1.1B as a candidate for the integration of these processes. By combining genetic, physiological, and molecular analyses, we demonstrated that TaNRT1.1B functions in coordinating nitrate uptake, ABA responses, stress tolerance, and plant growth in response to salt and nitrogen cues. These findings reveal a functional coupling between nitrate acquisition and salt tolerance mediated by TaNRT1.1B, highlighting a molecular entry point for coordination of stress resilience and nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) in wheat.

Results

Salt stress inhibits wheat seedling growth and reduces nitrate content

To investigate the effects of salt stress on wheat seedlings, we treated plants at the two-leaf- one-heart stage with 100 mM NaCl for various durations (0, 0.5, 3, 12, 24, 48, 72, or 96 h). No significant morphological changes were observed within the first 48 h of treatment. However, after 96 h of salt exposure, both root length and root biomass were significantly reduced compared with those of control plants (Fig. 1A–C). Similarly, shoot height and shoot biomass decreased under prolonged salt treatment (Fig. S1A–C). Given the essential role of nitrogen in plant growth and stress tolerance [13,24], we examined changes in nitrate content under salt stress to explore the underlying causes of this growth inhibition. Nitrate levels began to decline significantly after 12 h of treatment and continued to decrease with increasing treatment duration (Fig. 1D), demonstrating that salt stress impairs nitrate accumulation in wheat. Because nitrogen is a critical macronutrient for plant growth, this reduction in nitrate content likely contributes to the observed inhibition of root and shoot development.

In addition to nutritional imbalances, salt stress also triggers oxidative damage, necessitating the activation of antioxidant defenses. Accordingly, the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD), increased markedly after 12 h of salt treatment (Fig. 1E), and the transcript levels of multiple genes encoding antioxidant enzymes, including TaSOD-2A, TaSOD-4A, TaGR-4D, and TaCAT-4D, were progressively upregulated (Fig. 1F). Taken together, these results demonstrate that salt stress suppresses nitrate accumulation, limiting nitrogen availability for plant growth while simultaneously activating the antioxidant defense system.

Salt stress induces rapid transcriptional reprogramming in wheat seedlings

To examine the molecular basis for the physiological responses observed above, we performed a transcriptome analysis of roots and shoots from wheat seedlings treated with 100 mM NaCl for 0, 0.5, 3, 12, 24, or 48 h. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation of transcriptome profiles among treatment groups. In roots, PC1 and PC2 explained 23.59% and 16.02% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 2A). Control samples were separated from salt-treated samples, indicating extensive transcriptional reprogramming in response to salinity. Samples from 12, 24, and 48 h clustered more closely, suggesting relatively minor transcriptional changes after prolonged treatment. In shoots, PC1 and PC2 accounted for 20.8% and 9.24% of the variance, respectively (Fig. S2A). Samples from 0 to 0.5 h clustered together, as did those from 24 to 48 h, indicating that major transcriptional shifts occurred between 3 and 24 h of salt treatment. We next examined overall patterns of gene expression using boxplots based on FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) values. Having examined global transcriptional activity, we next analyzed pairwise differences in gene expression between salt-treated and control samples at each time point. Genes with fold change ≥ 2 and adjusted P ≤ 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). We detected 9230, 9996, 8367, 7920, and 8713 DEGs in roots and 3527, 3076, 4926, 4204, and 3726 DEGs in shoots at 0.5, 3, 12, 24, and 48 h (Table S1–S2), respectively, relative to 0 h (Fig. 2C, Fig. S2C). A Venn diagram revealed shared and unique DEGs among the different treatment durations, including 1417 genes in roots and 259 genes in shoots that were differentially expressed in response to salt stress at all time points (Fig. 2D; Fig. S2D). Volcano plots were also constructed to visualize differential gene expression: points above the x axis represent upregulated genes, and those below represent downregulated genes (Fig. 2E; Fig. S2E).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that salt stress induces rapid and extensive transcriptional changes in both roots and shoots, with roots exhibiting an earlier and more pronounced response. The identified DEGs provide a foundation for further dissection of the molecular mechanisms that underlie salt-stress responses in wheat.

Transcriptional dynamics of salt-responsive genes reveal suppression of nitrate-related pathways

To further explore gene-expression dynamics in roots and shoots in response to salt treatment, we performed fuzzy c-means clustering of DEGs using the Mfuzz R package. We identified six expression clusters in both tissues, each of which exhibited unique temporal patterns following salt exposure (Fig. 3A and B; Fig. S3A–B). In roots, genes in clusters 1 and 4 showed rapid induction, with peak expression at 0.5 and 3 h, respectively. Genes in clusters 6 and 2 exhibited relatively stable expression during early treatment but were prominently upregulated at 12 and 24 h. Transcript levels of genes in cluster 3 continuously decreased throughout the time course, whereas those in cluster 5 showed a gradual increase (Fig. 3A). In shoots, genes in clusters 6, 1, 3, and 2 were upregulated in response to salt, reaching peak transcript levels at 0.5, 3, 12, and 48 h, respectively. Conversely, genes in clusters 4 and 5 were downregulated. We next performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on each cluster to gain insight into the biological processes associated with these gene-expression patterns. In roots, cluster 1 was enriched in genes related to root development, cell wall organization, and defense response, suggesting that these processes are rapidly regulated upon salt exposure (Fig. 3C). Other upregulated clusters (clusters 2, 4, 5, and 6) were predominantly associated with defense response and transmembrane transport (Fig. 3E; Fig. S4). Upregulated genes in shoots were enriched in comparable GO terms, suggesting that similar salt response mechanisms occur in roots and shoots (Fig. S3).

Among the downregulated gene clusters, cluster 4 in shoots was enriched in genes related to photosynthesis and light response, suggesting that these processes are suppressed by salt treatment, consistent with the observed changes in shoot morphology (Fig. S3F). Most notably, cluster 3 in roots and cluster 5 in shoots were significantly enriched in terms associated with nitrate assimilation, nitrate response, and nitrate transmembrane transport (Fig. 3D; Fig. S3G). This transcriptional suppression of nitrate-related genes is consistent with the reduced nitrate accumulation observed under salt stress (Fig. 1D) and likely contributes to impaired nitrogen metabolism and subsequent inhibition of seedling growth. Together, these results demonstrate that salt stress suppresses the expression of genes involved in nitrate acquisition and assimilation, providing a molecular basis for the reduced nitrate content and impaired plant development observed under saline conditions.

Key nitrate transporter and assimilation genes are downregulated under salt stress

In plants, nitrate is taken up and transported by NPF and NRT2 family transporters, then reduced to nitrite by NR and further to ammonium by nitrite reductase (NiR). Ammonium is subsequently assimilated into amino acids through the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase (GS/GOGAT) cycle [25]. Prompted by enrichment of nitrate-related terms among the downregulated gene clusters, we investigated how salt stress disrupts nitrogen metabolism in wheat seedlings by analyzing the expression of key nitrate transporter and assimilation genes using our RNA-seq datasets.

We first examined the expression patterns of NPF and NRT2 family members, which encode the major nitrate transporters in plants. Heatmap visualization revealed distinct expression patterns of these genes under salt treatment (Fig. S5). TaNRT1.1B exhibited the highest basal expression and was markedly downregulated in response to salt stress. This suppression preceded or coincided with the decline in nitrate content, positioning TaNRT1.1B expression as an early molecular event in the salt-induced disruption of nitrogen metabolism and suggesting that it may play an important role in mediating nitrate uptake under saline conditions.

We next examined genes involved in nitrate assimilation. Transcript levels of TaNIA (encoding nitrate reductase), TaNiR (encoding nitrite reductase), and TaGS2 (encoding glutamine synthetase) were significantly reduced under salt stress (Fig. S6A). These results indicate that salt treatment broadly suppresses the expression of genes involved in nitrate uptake and assimilation, impairing the efficient acquisition and metabolic utilization of nitrate in wheat seedlings.

To confirm the reliability of our RNA-seq data, we examined the expression of selected marker genes (NRT1.1B, NIA1, NiR, and GS2) by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR). The expression patterns derived from RT–qPCR were highly consistent with those derived from the RNA-seq data across all time points (Fig. S6B), confirming the accuracy and reproducibility of our transcriptomic analyses. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that salt stress suppresses the transcription of genes central to nitrate transport and assimilation, providing a molecular explanation for the reduced nitrate accumulation and impaired nitrogen metabolism observed in salt-treated wheat seedlings (Fig. 1D).

Salt stress influences nitrate uptake by modulating TaNRT1.1B expression

Given the pronounced downregulation of TaNRT1.1B under salt stress (Fig. S6), we next investigated its role in mediating nitrate uptake and salt tolerance in wheat. Previous studies in Arabidopsis and rice have established NRT1.1 as a key regulator of nitrate signaling and transport. To characterize the function of TaNRT1.1B in wheat, we generated knockout mutants using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing in the Fielder background.

Two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed to target conserved regions in the exon of the three TaNRT1.1B homeologs (TaNRT1.1B-A1, -B1, and -D1) (Fig. 4A). After Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of immature Fielder embryos, we obtained two independent mutant lines, tanrt1.1b-9 and tanrt1.1b-18. Sequencing of the target regions revealed distinct mutations in each homeolog (Fig. 4B–D). The tanrt1.1b-18 line carried a 265-bp deletion in TaNRT1.1B-A1, a 1-bp insertion in TaNRT1.1B-B1, and a 249-bp deletion in TaNRT1.1B-D1. We next examined the response of tanrt1.1b mutants to salt stress. Upon salt treatment, germination and survival rates were significantly lower in the tanrt1.1b mutants than in the wild-type (Fig. 4E, F and Fig. S7). Salt stress caused greater reductions in shoot height, shoot biomass, and shoot dry weight in the mutants than in wild-type plants (Fig. 4G–I), indicating that disruption of TaNRT1.1B compromises plant growth under saline conditions.

To explore the physiological basis of this heightened sensitivity, we measured Na+ and K+ contents in wild-type plants and tanrt1.1b mutants. The Na+/K+ ratio was significantly higher in the mutants under salt stress (Fig. 4J), consistent with their more severe phenotypic changes, and nitrate content was significantly lower in tanrt1.1b mutants than in wild-type plants under both control and salt-treated conditions (Fig. 4K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that TaNRT1.1B positively regulates salt tolerance in wheat by sustaining nitrate uptake and contributing to ionic homeostasis under saline conditions.

Histochemical staining with nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) revealed substantially stronger signals of O2− and H2O2 in the tanrt1.1b mutants under salt stress (Fig. 5A and B). We next assessed the expression of genes related to ROS detoxification. The tanrt1.1b mutants exhibited significant downregulation of TaSOD-4A, TaCAT-4D, TaSOD-2A, TaGPX-4B, TaGR-4D, and TaAPX-2B, which may have caused excessive ROS accumulation and ultimately impaired salt tolerance (Fig. 5G–J and Fig. S8A and B). These results are in agreement with the enzyme activity assays. However, given that TaNRT1.1B functions in nitrate acquisition rather than directly in ROS detoxification or transcriptional regulation, these transcriptional changes likely reflect secondary consequences of altered nitrogen status and associated metabolic or hormonal reprogramming.

To gain further insight into why ROS scavenging–related genes were downregulated in the tanrt1.1b mutants, we analyzed the expression of reported salt-tolerance regulatory genes in wheat. Positive regulators of salt tolerance such as TaSnRK2.4, TaGPAT6, TaSK5, TaFER5D-1, and TaPRX-2A were significantly downregulated in the tanrt1.1b mutants, whereas negative regulators such as TaGBF1 and TaPLATZ2 were markedly upregulated (Fig. 5K–N and Fig. S8C–J).

To investigate the molecular basis for the elevated Na+/K+ ratio in the tanrt1.1b mutants, we examined the expression of genes related to Na+ and K+ transport in wheat. Genes associated with Na+ efflux (TaHKT1;5), Na+ extrusion (TaSOS1), and vacuolar sequestration (TaTVP1) were down-regulated in the tanrt1.1b mutants, as were genes related to K+ uptake (e.g. TaHAK7 and TaHAK9); collectively, these changes likely contributed to the increased Na+/K+ ratio (Fig. 5O–R and Fig. S8K–P). These concerted changes in the transcription of genes associated with salt-tolerance regulation, ROS scavenging, and Na+/K+ transport likely underlie the reduced ROS detoxification capacity and disrupted ionic homeostasis observed in the tanrt1.1b mutants, ultimately accounting for their salt-sensitive phenotype.

TaNRT1.1B contributes to ABA sensitivity in wheat

In the absence of exogenous ABA, there were no significant differences in seedling or root growth between the tanrt1.1b mutants and wild-type plants. Loss of TaNRT1.1B function significantly reduced wheat yield traits under salt stress

Having established that TaNRT1.1B positively regulates vegetative growth under salt stress (Fig. 4), we next asked whether it also influences reproductive development and yield traits. To this end, we grew wild-type and tanrt1.1b plants with or without 75 mM NaCl starting at the one-leaf-one-heart stage, then performed phenotypic analyses at maturity. Under control conditions, the tanrt1.1b mutants initiated heading earlier than wild-type plants. Both genotypes reached heading under salt stress, but the mutants completed heading earlier than the wild-type (Fig. 7A). We next examined yield-related traits in mature plants. The spike length of tanrt1.1b mutants was significantly reduced compared with that of wild-type plants under both control and salt-stressed conditions (Fig. 7B). The grain number per spike decreased by 17% in the WT and by 22% and 24%, in the mutants under salt stress (Fig. 7C–F). Consistent with these observations, grain weight per spike and grain weight per plant were significantly lower in the mutants under both control and saline conditions (Fig. 7G and H). The reductions in yield-related traits observed in tanrt1.1b mutants under salt stress are consistent with the impaired vegetative growth and reduced nitrate accumulation observed at the seedling stage, suggesting that the function of TaNRT1.1B is essential for maintaining productivity under saline conditions throughout development.

Discussion

Soil salinity is a major environmental constraint that severely inhibits plant growth and reduces crop yields worldwide [5,30]. High salinity leads to hyperosmotic stress and ion toxicity, thereby suppressing water and nutrient uptake [5,24]. Although the physiological consequences of salt stress are well documented, the molecular mechanisms by which salt stress affects nitrogen metabolism and plant development remain poorly understood in wheat. In this study, we demonstrated that salt stress inhibits wheat seedling growth, reduces nitrate accumulation, and suppresses the expression of genes involved in nitrate transport and assimilation (Fig. 1, Fig. 3 and Fig. S6). Through combined transcriptomic, genetic, and molecular analyses, we identified TaNRT1.1B as a key regulator that links nitrate acquisition and salt-tolerance pathways, coordinating nutrient status and stress responses to modulate wheat growth and yield.

Salt stress disrupts nitrogen metabolism at the physiological and transcriptional levels

Soil salinity and nutrient bioavailability are critical environmental factors that jointly influence plant growth and development. Nitrogen fertilization has been shown to enhance plant salt tolerance on saline soils [31,32]. However, the molecular basis for this beneficial effect has not been fully clarified in wheat. Here, we observed that nitrate content declines significantly under prolonged salt treatment (Fig. 1D). Transcriptome profiling revealed that genes associated with nitrate transport (NPF and NRT2 families) and assimilation (NIA, NiR, and GS) are progressively downregulated under salt stress (Fig. 3, Fig. S5 and S6), providing a molecular basis for the observed reduction in nitrate accumulation. Enrichment of nitrate-related terms among downregulated genes in both roots and shoots indicates that the suppression of nitrogen metabolism in response to salinity occurs across tissues. This nitrogen deficit likely contributes to the observed growth inhibition, as nitrogen is essential for the biosynthesis of amino acids, proteins, and other nitrogen-containing compounds [14,25].

TaNRT1.1B is associated with altered ROS accumulation under salt stress

The NRT1.1 family has been studied extensively in Arabidopsis and rice. In these species, NRT1.1 functions as both a nitrate transporter and a sensor, modulating nitrate signaling, plant growth, and stress responses [20,24,33]. In wheat, we observed that TaNRT1.1B is strongly downregulated under salt stress (Fig. S6) and that its disruption reduces nitrate content, increases the Na+/K+ ratio, and enhances salt sensitivity (Fig. 4). These phenotypes are consistent with the notion that TaNRT1.1B-mediated nitrate uptake contributes to ionic homeostasis under saline conditions.

Salt stress induces the production of various ROS in plants, including the superoxide anion (O2 ⁠−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), singlet oxygen (1O2), and the hydroxyl radical (·OH). In addition to causing oxidative damage, such ROS can also function as signaling effectors in plant stress-adaptation mechanisms [[34], [35], [36]]. Numerous protein kinases, transcription factors, and key enzymes play pivotal roles in the regulation of ROS accumulation and scavenging [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. Our results showed that loss of TaNRT1.1B function led to downregulation of multiple genes encoding ROS scavenging enzymes and salt-tolerance regulators, and this was accompanied by reduced antioxidant enzyme activities (Fig. 5). Given that TaNRT1.1B primarily functions in nitrate acquisition, these transcriptional changes likely reflect secondary consequences of altered nitrogen status rather than direct regulation by TaNRT1.1B.

Like its rice ortholog, wheat TaNRT1.1B is involved in ABA responses

Rice OsNRT1.1B has been shown to function as an ABA receptor, playing an essential role in modulating ABA signaling [24]. Our findings reveal that this function is conserved in wheat, as tanrt1.1b mutants exhibited pronounced ABA insensitivity in root growth assays and failed to fully induce ABA-responsive genes such as TaRAB18, TaRAD29B, TaABI5, and TaCOR47 upon ABA treatment (Fig. 6). This discovery not only establishes TaNRT1.1B as a dual-function protein involved in both nitrate transport and ABA perception but also suggests that the ABA signaling module may serve as an additional point of crosstalk through which nitrogen status modulates stress adaptation. Given that ABA is a central regulator of salt and drought responses, the impaired ABA sensitivity of the tanrt1.1b mutants likely exacerbates their salt-sensitive phenotype, linking the nitrate transport function of TaNRT1.1B to broader hormonal stress networks.

TaNRT1.1B links nitrate uptake with developmental and yield responses under salt stress

The improvement of crop NUE is critical for tackling salinity-related challenges and increasing sustainable crop production. Soil nitrogen deficiency limits primary metabolic activities in plants, reducing rates of both transpiration and photosynthesis and thus compromising plant growth. Notably, flowering is accelerated under low-nitrogen conditions. Nitrogen deficiency causes the most significant yield losses when it occurs at the tillering stage [[41], [42], [43]]. Consistent with these observations, our results demonstrated that salt stress induces early heading in wheat and that this effect is exacerbated by TaNRT1.1B mutation. Moreover, this mutation significantly reduced spike length, grain number per spike, and grain weight per plant in wheat (Fig. 7). These findings suggest that salt stress may suppress the expression of TaNRT1.1B, thereby compromising NUE and ultimately reducing wheat yield.

Implications for breeding of salt-tolerant wheat varieties

The identification of TaNRT1.1B as a key determinant of salt tolerance and nitrogen uptake suggests that it may be a valuable genetic target for wheat improvement. The positive association between TaNRT1.1B expression and salt tolerance in diverse wheat accessions (Fig. 8) suggests that natural or engineered variation in this gene could be exploited to enhance stress resilience. However, several questions remain to be addressed. First, our finding that tanrt1.1b mutants are insensitive to ABA suggests that TaNRT1.1B functions in both nitrate transport and ABA signaling. However, the underlying mechanism by which TaNRT1.1B integrates these two functions under saline conditions requires further investigation. Second, the downstream targets and interaction partners of TaNRT1.1B remain to be identified, as do natural variations and superior haplotypes of TaNRT1.1B in diverse germplasm. Follow-up studies will expand the screening of natural variations to further evaluate their potential for molecular breeding. Lastly, the effect of TaNRT1.1B manipulation on wheat performance under field conditions and its effect on yield under combined stresses (e.g., salinity and nitrogen deficiency) remain to be evaluated.

In summary, this study identifies TaNRT1.1B as a key determinant of both salt tolerance and nitrogen acquisition. By coupling nitrate uptake with salt tolerance, TaNRT1.1B modulates wheat growth, stress tolerance, and yield-related traits (Fig. 9). Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

A panel of 13 hexaploid wheat (Triticum aestivum) varieties was used in this study: Jimai 60, Fielder, Qingmai 6, Cangmai 16, Cangmai 6005, Neixiang 188, Hengguan 35, Chinese Spring, Norin 67, Funo, Bainong 64, Jing 411, and Luyuan 502. All wheat varieties were grown in growth chambers under long-day conditions (16-h light/8-h dark) at 23 °C for phenotypic analyses and transformation experiments.

Plasmid construction and wheat transformation

To generate CRISPR/Cas9 constructs targeting TaNRT1.1B, two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed to target the coding sequence of each homeolog and were inserted into the pBUE413 vector as described previously [44].

Immature Fielder embryos were collected at 14 days post anthesis for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using the EHA105 strain harboring the TaNRT1.1B-pBUE413 construct as described previously [45].

To identify mutations in the target genes, gene-specific primers flanking the target sites were used to amplify genomic DNA fragments, and the PCR products were sequenced to confirm the mutations. Primers used for genotyping are listed in Table S4.

Salt treatment and phenotypic analysis of wheat seedlings

For hydroponic experiments, wheat seeds were germinated and grown in half-strength Hoagland solution. At the two-leaf-one-heart stage, uniformly sized seedlings were selected and exposed to 100 mM NaCl for various durations (0, 0.5, 3, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h). Tissue nitrate content was measured as described previously [46].

In soil-culture experiments, wheat seedlings were grown to the one-leaf-one-heart stage and then treated with different concentrations of NaCl. The salt tolerance of Fielder and tanrt1.1b mutant seedlings was assessed after treatment with 150 mM NaCl for 14 days. Yield traits of Fielder and tanrt1.1b mutants were observed after treatment with 75 mM NaCl from the one-leaf-one-heart stage until the final harvest. The salt tolerance of different wheat genotypes was assessed after treatment with 100 mM NaCl for 21 days. For each cultivar, ten wheat seeds were sown per 1-gallon pot, and three pots were used as biological replicates. Plant height was recorded for each individual, and three plants with similar growth were selected and pooled for fresh weight measurement. The ratio of fresh weight under stressed versus control conditions was used as an indicator of salt tolerance.

Measurement of ROS-related physiological parameters

Histochemical staining with NBT and DAB was used to visualize levels of O2− and H2O2, respectively. Antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD and POD) and ROS contents (H2O2 and O2−) were quantified using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer's protocols (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

ABA treatment and phenotypic analysis of wheat seedlings

To investigate the effect of ABA on wheat growth, seeds were surface sterilized with 2% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min, washed five times with water, and then grown in germination pouches supplemented with 0 or 200 nM ABA for 4 days, after which root length and shoot height were recorded. Wheat seedlings grown hydroponically to the two-leaf-one-heart stage in half-strength Hoagland nutrient solution were treated with 0 or 200 nM ABA for 2 h, and the roots were harvested to examine the expression of ABA-responsive genes.

RNA-sequencing and data analysis

For bulk RNA-seq analysis, root and shoot tissues were collected from Jimai 60 seedlings treated with 100 mM NaCl for 0, 0.5, 3, 12, 24, or 48 h. Three biological replicates were used, each consisting of pooled tissues from six individual plants. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and RNA-seq was performed by Berry Genomics (Beijing, China). Raw reads were aligned to the wheat reference genome IWGSC RefSeq v2.0. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.16.1). Genes with a fold change ≥ 2 and an adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 were considered DEGs. GO enrichment analysis was performed using the GO.db R package (v3.20.0).

Clustering analyses

DEGs across time points were selected for clustering analysis using the Mfuzz R package (v2.66.0) [47]. Expression data were standardized using the standardize function to ensure a zero mean and unit variance for each gene. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the c selection function with the minimum centroid distance method, and a final clustering was performed with c = 6. Clusters with different temporal expression patterns were visualized using the mfuzz.plot2 function.

RNA extraction and RT–qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen root and shoot tissues using the Ultrapure RNA Kit (CWBIO, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg of total RNA using All-In-One 5X RT MasterMix with gDNA Removal (Applied Biological Materials, Vancouver, Canada). RT–qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Gene-specific primers used for RT–qPCR are listed in Table S4. Relative expression levels were normalized to that of TaACTIN as an internal control and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Three biological replicates were performed for each sample, with three technical replicates per biological replicate.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.1). Data are presented as means ± SD (n ≥ 3). Statistically significant differences were determined by Student's t-test or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple comparisons (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗P < 0.05; ns, not significant).
