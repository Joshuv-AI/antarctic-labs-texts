Sex-specific microbial and tryptophan signatures of depression implicate archaeal methanogens and indole-3-acetic acid only in women
Authors: Anna Motger-Albertí, Laura Gallardo-Nuell, Marisel Rosell-Díaz, Mihaela Stefoglo, Javier Pons, Josep Garre-Olmo, Vicente Pérez-Brocal, Andrés Moya, Josep Puig, Rafael Ramos, Jordi Mayneris-Perxachs, José Manuel Fernández-Real
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sex differences in mental health are often overlooked, yet gut microbiota–host metabolite interactions may contribute to sexual dimorphism in depression. In a population-based cohort, we investigated sex-specific links among plasma tryptophan metabolites, depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), and the gut microbiome, controlling for smoking, diet, alcohol, and physical activity. Women (N = 419) exhibited higher plasma indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and picolinic acid (PA) concentrations, but lower trigonelline (TRIG) than men (N = 383). Machine learning models with SHAP explanations revealed that IAA and TRIG were positively associated, whereas PA was negatively associated with depression severity in women, whereas only kynurenic acid (KA) was inversely associated in men. In women, depression severity strongly correlated with methanogenic archaea, including Methanobrevibacter smithii, and microbial methane-production pathways. Methanobrevibacter and specifically M. smithii were negatively linked to genes for tryptophan, PA, and KA biosynthesis, but positively to genes for IAA and nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism. Most microbial species associated with depression severity in women were predicted to produce IAA. These findings reveal pronounced sex-specific microbiome–metabolite interactions, highlighting potentially distinct microbial mechanisms shaping depression in men and women.

Introduction

Research in psychology and medicine has often neglected sex as a fundamental biological variable—the recognition that disease processes can differ between females and males1. Many aspects of human biology and behaviour exhibit sexual dimorphism2, and depression is no exception. Depression is one of the most prevalent mental health disorders and could arise from a complex interplay of genetic, biological, and social factors. It also represents one of the main causes of disability worldwide3,4. Recent evidence suggests that the gut microbiota may also play a crucial role in the pathophysiology of depression5–7. Despite the development of multiple pharmacological treatments over the recent decades, its prevalence remains far from decreasing8,9. Although some studies have examined sex differences in depression, the gender perspective in evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment remains underrepresented in much of the research10. Fortunately, studies on gender and sex differences in mental health have expanded over the past decade11–13.

Clinical presentations of depression also differ between men and women. Men are more likely to be undiagnosed10 because they usually have a predominance of externalizing symptoms (e.g. aggression, impulsivity, substance abuse), whereas women are more likely to experience internalizing symptoms (e.g. hopelessness, sadness, loneliness.)11,12. Sex differences in antidepressant treatment response remain inconsistent and incompletely understood14–16. Some studies suggest that women respond better to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), while men respond better to tricyclic antidepressants (TCA) treatment11,12. These differences can be explained by multiple factors, including biological, psychological, and social determinants. One potential mechanism that could help clarify these differences involves the tryptophan metabolism.

L-tryptophan is an essential amino acid and the sole precursor of serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT)17 in both the brain and the gut, and it has been implicated in multiple psychiatric and neurological conditions18,19, among others20. Several physiological functions in the human body are controlled by tryptophan and its catabolites, which are produced through three main routes: the kynurenine pathway, the serotonin pathway, and the microbial indole pathway. Additionally, a sexual dimorphism has been described in tryptophan metabolism21. Alterations in tryptophan metabolism linked to depressive phenotypes may be driven, at least in part, by the gut microbiome22,23. Consistent with this, previous studies linked gut microbial features to brain-related phenotypes such as food addiction and attention in association with alterations in tryptophan metabolism and related neurotransmitter pathways24,25.

However, the sex-specific relationship between tryptophan catabolites and depression has not yet been elucidated. To the best of our knowledge, no previous research has examined how tryptophan metabolism, gut microbiota, and depression severity jointly relate within each sex. Here, we investigated these associations separately in men and women to clarify potential sex-specific biological pathways linking the gut microbiome, tryptophan metabolism, and depression severity.

Sex-specific differences in plasma tryptophan catabolites are shown in Fig. 1 (Supplementary Table 1). No significant differences were observed in tryptophan (TRP) (p = 0.54), kynurenine (KYN) (p = 0.32), serotonin (5-HT) (p = 0.51), tryptamine (TA) (p = 0.89), kynurenic acid (KA) (p = 0.90), anthranilic acid (AA) (p = 0.79), indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) (p = 0.21), 3-hydroxykynurenine (3-HK) (p = 0.089), 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3-HAA) (p = 0.94), 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) (p = 0.082), 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) (p = 0.83), quinolinic acid (QA) (p = 0.32), or xanthurenic acid (XA) (p = 0.57). In women, Methanobacteriaceae archaeon (Euryarchaeota phylum) was strongly positively associated with PHQ-9 scores (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 4). These associations between depression severity and archaeal methanogens were further supported when PHQ-9 was analyzed as a categorical variable. Comparing the no depression with mild depression severity groups, Methanocateria sp. and Methanobacteriaceae sp. were positively associated with PHQ-9 scores (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 5). Similarly, when comparing the no depression vs moderate/severe depression severity groups, Euryarchaeota sp., Methanobrevibacter sp., and Methanobacteriaceae archaeon sp., were positively associated with PHQ-9 scores (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 6). Finally, the comparison between mild and moderate/severe depression also highlighted Euryarchaeota sp. and Methanobrevibacter smithii among the species most strongly positively species associated with PHQ-9 scores (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 7). Consistently, functional analyses based on KEGG orthologs, identified several bacterial genes positively associated with depression severity in women (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 8) and a pathway overrepresentation analysis of these genes revealed a significant enrichment of the Methane metabolism (Fig. 2h, i, k).

Finally, we also predicted tryptophan metabolites production from species positively associated with PHQ-9 (both continuous and categorical analyses) using TrpNet and found that most of them were predicted to produce IAA, which was also strongly associated with depression severity (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

Our findings indicate sex-specific differences in plasma tryptophan catabolites, gut microbiome, and their associations with depression symptom severity, suggesting distinct host–microbe metabolic interactions in women and men. Women exhibited significantly higher plasma concentrations of IAA and PA, but lower concentrations of TRIG compared to men after adjusting for key confounding factors, consistent with previous reports of sex-based variation in the plasma metabolome21. Notably, these three tryptophan-related metabolites that differed between men and women were among those most strongly associated with depression severity in women. Thus, IAA and TRIG were positively associated with depressive symptom severity, whereas PA had a negative association with depression scores in women. In men, only KA was negatively associated with depression severity.

The KA finding in men is consistent with multiple human studies reporting reduced KA in major depression and related phenotypes26–29. In line with this, men have shown higher KA levels, while women show lower KA concentrations and sex-specific differences in serotonin turnover30. Given the neuroactive and potentially neuroprotective properties of KA, including modulation of glutamatergic signalling31,32, this microbial–metabolite axis may be relevant to the lower depressive symptom severity33,34 observed in men. However, results are not uniform across studies and may depend on hormonal milieu and other modifiers. Importantly, although KA does not readily cross the blood–brain barrier, peripheral KA levels may reflect broader kynurenine pathway activity and capture immune-metabolic states relevant to depression pathophysiology.35

In contrast to our findings, administration of IAA, a gut microbiota–derived indole metabolite, has demonstrated antidepressant-like effects in preclinical models36. However, in humans, IAA correlated positively with anxiety (R = 0.52, p = 0.005) and depression (R = 0.39 p < 0.05) in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD)37. At the same time, emerging human data indicate that IAA can increase in stress-linked states and correlate with gastrointestinal dysfunction. Thus, higher faecal IAA concentrations were reported in individuals with mental distress and IAA was linked to stress-responsive gut epithelial change38.

Emerging evidence indicates that gut microbiota composition differs between women and men, including in depressive disorders39–43. In line with this and consistent with sex specificity observed in metabolites, we also identified sex-specific microbial associations with depressive symptoms. In women, several taxa within the Euryarchaeota phylum, specifically archaeal methanogens, including M. smithii, were positively associated with PHQ-9 scores. Consistently, functional analyses revealed an enrichment of methane metabolism among genes correlated with depressive symptom severity. Methanogens are key hydrogen consumers and form tight metabolic networks with bacterial fermenters; changes in methanogenesis can shift community-level fermentation outputs44. Notably, experimental work suggests that introducing methanogenesis can reduce butyrate formation in gut microbial communities, providing a plausible link between archaeal enrichment and depletion of potentially beneficial bacterial metabolic outputs45. Moreover, M. smithii has been associated with inflammatory diseases in prior studies46,47.

The integration of both metabolic and metagenomics functional results in women supports the role of archaeal methanogens on depression severity through coordinated shifts in microbial tryptophan metabolism and NAD-related metabolism. Both M. smithii and Methanobrevibacter_uc were positively associated with depression severity and with microbial functions related to nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, a pathway that intersects the kynurenine arm of tryptophan catabolism through quinolinic acid, an established neurotoxic metabolite repeatedly connected to depressive symptoms, neuroinflammation, and suicidality, particularly under inflammatory conditions48–51. Consistently, TRIG, a metabolite participating in the NAD cycle, was positively associated with depression severity, potentially reflecting microbiome-driven modulation of NAD precursor availability52. In contrast, K03392 and K00825, microbial genes involved in the production of picolinic acid and kynurenic acid, both generally considered neuroprotective, were negatively associated with these archaeal species, suggesting a shift away from protective branches of tryptophan metabolism. Notably, three microbial genes implicated in IAA biosynthesis (K00128, K01426, K01501), were also positively linked to M. smithii and Methanobrevibacter_uc, aligning with the positive association between plasma IAA and depression symptom severity. Finally, all microbial genes involved in de novo biosynthesis of tryptophan from chorismic acid, were negatively associated with both archaeal methanogens, consistent with a microbial functional profile favouring tryptophan catabolism and downstream neuroactive derivatives over tryptophan biosynthesis.

In contrast, in men depressive symptom severity was not associated with archaeal species, but other microbiome-metabolite interactions centered on KA and several Firmicutes species, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia hominis, and Blautia wexlerae, often linked to short-chain fatty acid production.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which sex-specific microbial community structure intersects with host tryptophan metabolism to shape systemic metabolite profiles associated with depressive symptoms and may contribute to the greater incidence of depression in women53. Although the observed sex differences in metabolite concentrations were characterized by small effect sizes, such differences may still be biologically meaningful given the cumulative and network-level nature of metabolic and microbiome–host interactions. While causal mechanisms cannot be inferred from the present study, the results highlight the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in microbiota–tryptophan research54 and underscore the need for future longitudinal and multi-omics studies to clarify the functional and causal relationships underlying these associations.

Our research has several limitations. First, as an observational study, the results represent associations rather than causal relationships; longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings and hypotheses. Secondly, the PHQ-9 test collected mostly internalizing symptoms, which may be less sensitive in men. Thirdly, biological factors such as the menstrual cycle and sex hormones, which could potentially help explain these associations, were not assessed in our sample. Finally, our findings focused on the depression symptoms in a general population cohort and cannot be generalized to individuals with a diagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD). Consequently, additional studies with MDD patients are needed to verify and confirm these findings.

Methods

Participants

Participants from the multicentric observational Health Imageomics cohort (Health Imageomics Study) in the Girona province of north-eastern Catalonia (Spain) were included (N = 802). It covers a sample of people from Girona (419 women and 383 men) between the ages of 16 and 52 who were chosen through recruitment at public primary care facilities. Institutionalisation, terminal illness, intellectual disability, dementia diagnosis, and language barriers in Catalan or Spanish were among the exclusion criteria. The IDIBGI Horizontal Aging Program and the IDIBGI Biobank from the Healthy Imageomics Study provided samples and data from participants included in this study. A detailed description of the cohort can be found in Castells-Nobau et al.24. The Dr. Josep Trueta University Hospital’s ethics committee approved the protocol of the study (Project Code GO03-001848) and all participants gave informed consent. The demographic and clinical variables from Table 1 were collected in one visit according to Puig et al.55.

Patient health questionnaire

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)56 was used as an assessment of depressive symptoms, a self-administered questionnaire containing nine items of depression symptomatology from the previous two weeks, with Likert-type responses with values ranging from 0 to 3 (0–27). We created three main groups according to depression severity: a) none, PHQ-9 = 0–4 (women N = 281 and men N = 278); b) mild, PHQ-9 = 5–9 (women N = 108 and men N = 88); c) moderate/severe, PHQ-9 ≥ 10 (women N = 28 and men N = 13). We also recorded antidepressant treatments as a confounding variable.

Mediterranean diet adherence, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption

Adherence to the Mediterranean diet was assessed using the validated 14-item MD-Adherence-Screener (MEDAS) from the PREDIMED study. Physical activity was assessed using the REGICOR Short Physical Activity Questionnaire (Minnesota-REGICOR), which collects information on leisure-time physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and occupational physical activity. Participants reported the monthly frequency and average daily duration of six common leisure activities (walking, brisk walking, gardening, walking trails/hiking, stair climbing, and sports/exercise). Leisure-time energy expenditure was estimated using the validated REGICOR algorithm by multiplying activity-specific MET values by reported frequency and duration and summing across activities (expressed as MET-min/week), with additional derivation of intensity-specific expenditure (light, moderate, vigorous) according to standard MET cut-offs. Sedentary behaviour was estimated from reported screen time on a usual weekday and weekend day, and occupational physical activity was recorded using a single categorical item. Alcohol consumption and smoking status were also collected.

Fecal genomic DNA extraction and whole-genome shotgun sequencing

Using the PowerSoil DNA extraction kit (MO BIO Laboratories) and adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions, DNA was recovered from stool samples. The Illumina DNA Prep kit (Illumina) was used to prepare libraries for Illumina sequencing using 400–500 ng of total DNA. All libraries were evaluated with an Agilent Technologies TapeStation High Sensitivity DNA kit, and their quantities were determined by Qubit (Invitrogen). Equimolar amounts of validated libraries were pooled, and a 150-cycle paired-end run on an Illumina NextSeq2000 was performed. Using a custom Python script, raw reads were filtered for quality value (QV > 30).

FASTQ output files were initially pre-processed using fastp57, a FASTQ data pre-processing tool for quality control, trimming of adapters, and quality filtering, to analyse the taxonomic and functional diversity of the microbiota present in the samples of all cohorts. Clean reads were mapped using Bowtie258 against the Homo sapiens genome database (GRCh38.p13) to filter out reads of human origin. Unmapped reads were analysed using SqueezeMeta v1.3.159 using the co-assembly mode to pool all samples into a single assembly. Megahit60 was used for contig assembly, and Bowtie 2 was used for mapping reads into contigs. Prodigal61, Diamon62, and the KEGG63 database were utilised for ORF prediction; ORF search and alignment to the GenBank nr database for taxonomy assignment; and functional annotation, respectively.

Metabolomics analyses

UPLC-MS/MS-based targeted analysis of tryptophan metabolism (Health Imageomics). Analyses were performed as previously described64. One-hundred μL of plasma samples, 10 μL of internal standard (0.2 mg/L nicotinic acid-d4, 1 mg/L serotonin-d4, 0.2 mg/L kynurenic acid-d5, 100 mg/L tryptophan-d5, 0.2 mg/L xanthurenic acid-d4, 0.2 mg/L, 0.5 mg/L of 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid 10 mg/L of 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetic Acid-d2 and 1 mg/L of Kynurenine, 1 mg/L of 3-Indolepropionic-d2, 1 mg/L of Indole-2,4,5,6,7-d5-3-acetic-d2, and 1 mg/L of 2-Picolinic acid-d4), and 500 μl of 10 mM ammonium formate in methanol were mixed in a centrifuge tube. Samples were vortexed and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were then purified using Phree phospholipid removal plates from Phree (Phenomenex, California, United States). The supernatants were then evaporated, resuspended in 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid in methanol:water (2:8), and transferred to glass vials for analysis. Gradient technology was used to carry out the chromatographic separation. Mobile phases were A (0.1% formic acid) and B (0.1% formic acid in 90% methanol). The injection volume was 1 μL, and the column temperature was set at 50 °C.

For all the analyses, the identification of metabolites was done through direct comparison with industry standards. The method was validated through routine addition to a pool of plasma samples. Throughout the course of the investigation, other samples from the same pool were tested as quality control (QC). Repeatability was evaluated using intraday (the same day) and interday (five distinct days) deviations. Several internal standards (Table S16) were added to the extractions for the mass spectrometry in order to normalise the signalling from different samples across all analyses to determine the absolute quantification of each metabolite.

Statistical analysis

R (version 4.3.1) was used for visualization and statistical analysis of data. First, the normality was visually assessed and tested using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test.

Metabolomics statistical analysis

Differences in tryptophan metabolites between men and women were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test on residualized metabolite values adjusted for age, BMI, smoking status, diet, and physical activity.

We applied a machine learning approach based on random forest (Boruta algorithm)65 to identify relevant metabolites associated with the PHQ-9 scores. According to current research, it is one of the two most effective RF-based variable selection techniques for high-dimensional omics datasets66. The Boruta method is a wrapper algorithm that chooses features based on how well the model learns65. Three steps are involved in the selection of variables: a) Randomization: This involves making a duplicate copy of the original features and letting it randomly permutates across the observations; b) Model building: This involves using RF with the extended data set to compute the normalized permutation variable importance (VIM) scores; c) Statistical testing: This involves using a Bonferroni corrected two-tailed binomial test to identify the relevant features whose VIM is higher than the best randomly permutated variable. d) Iteration: This process continues until the status of every feature is determined. We execute the Boruta algorithm using 100 iterations, 50.000 trees to develop the forest (ntree), a confidence level cut-off of 0.005 for the Bonferroni adjusted p values, and several features randomly sampled at each split determined by the rounded down number of features/3.

To improve interpretability of the machine learning models, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to quantify the contribution of each metabolite to the model predictions at the individual level67. SHAP values, grounded in game theory, estimate the impact of each feature by comparing model predictions with and without that feature, accounting for interactions with other variables. SHAP analyses were performed using the R package treeshap.

Microbiome analysis

To find the associations between gut microbiota, depression scores, and tryptophan metabolites in women and men, we employed the ANCOM-BC methodology68, adjusting the models for age, BMI, smoking status, diet, alcohol intake and physical activity. It considers the bias due to differential sampling fractions across samples by adding a sample-specific offset to a linear regression model that is estimated from the observed data. The linear regression model in log scale is analogous to log-ratio transformation to consider the compositional nature of metagenomics datasets, while the offset term serves as the bias correction. Microbial functionality was also assessed using the ANCOM-BC methodology on KEGG orthologs (KO). Significantly enriched microbial pathways were then identified using an over-representation analysis using the Clusterprofiler R package (q-value < 0.1). P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Sequential Goodness-of-Fit procedure69, implemented in the SGoF R package. In contrast to false discovery rate (FDR) approaches, whose statistical power decreases as the number of tests increases, SGoF methods gain power with a growing number of tests. This approach has been shown to outperform FDR-based corrections particularly in settings with a large number of tests and limited sample size, as commonly encountered in omics studies.

Microbiome-metabolomics interaction analysis

TrpNet prediction analysis70 was applied to explore associations between microbial species identified as significant in the microbiome analysis and tryptophan metabolites.
