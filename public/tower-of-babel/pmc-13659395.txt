Rethinking Postmigration HIV Vulnerability Through Migrant Networks and Whiteness: Race‐Conscious Conversations With Newly Arrived Asian‐Born Gay Migrants in Australia
Authors: Satrio Nindyo Istiko, Allyson Mutch, Judith A. Dean, Lisa Fitzgerald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMigrants do not only acquire HIV in their home countries; a significant proportion acquire HIV postmigration. Newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants in Australia have been identified as having low HIV risk but still being vulnerable to HIV. In this paper, we qualitatively examine their postmigration HIV vulnerability embedded within migrant networks, drawing from race‐conscious conversations with 11 newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants in Queensland. The formation, composition and power dynamic in their migrant networks reveal the centrality of Whiteness, as a dominant cultural capital rooted in Western European colonisation, in shaping their postmigration HIV vulnerability. Whiteness creates situations where sexual violence and HIV transmission are likely to occur. But it also reduces HIV risk by making access to and understanding of local sexual health information and services desirable. We call for Australia's sexual health sector to dismantle Whiteness as a hegemony through several multifaceted strategies.

Introduction

Since the first identified case of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) in the early 1980s, migration and HIV have been deeply intertwined, with some Western nations describing HIV as a national threat requiring strict migration regulations (Yue 2008). Significant migration flows from countries with higher HIV prevalence to countries with lower HIV prevalence have led to a common assumption that migrants only acquire HIV in their home countries and spread the virus postmigration (Yue 2008; Fakoya et al. 2015). This persistent racist discourse of migrants as ‘disease carriers’ fails to acknowledge postmigration HIV acquisition. As Mann et al. (2024) found, one in three migrants across 11 European countries (N = 47,182) acquired HIV postmigration between 1996 and 2022. In Australia, where HIV rates remain low (J. King et al. 2023), 49% of migrants diagnosed with HIV between 2016 and 2020 (N = 1909) were estimated to have acquired the virus postmigration, compared to one in three premigration (J. M. King et al. 2023).

Across both European and Australian contexts, Asian‐born gay migrants have been increasingly affected by HIV (Mann et al. 2024; J. King et al. 2023). Those who are from Southeast and Northeast Asia in Australia experienced a 59% increase in HIV notifications between 2013 and 2019, in contrast to a 36% decline among Australian‐born men who had sex with other men during the same period (J. King et al. 2023). Evidence on sexual behaviours among Asian‐born gay migrants continues to accumulate, with a focus on identifying and modifying individual‐level risk factors (Tieosapjaroen et al. 2024; Murray et al. 2020; Wong et al. 2022). Yet survey findings on this population group indicate that regular sexual relationships are more common than casual sexual relationships (Wong et al. 2022), with the vast majority testing regularly for HIV, including those who use drugs during sexual activities (Murray et al. 2020). Asian‐born gay migrants who are also identified as being at higher risk (e.g., had condomless anal sex in the last 6 months) have been reported to engage in key prevention practices, including being more likely to use pre‐exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)—a form of biomedical prevention (Tieosapjaroen et al. 2024; Ung et al. 2023).

Asian‐born gay migrants who are newly arrived (i.e., have lived in the country for 4 years or less) are recognised as ‘an emergent risk group’ in Australia (Blackshaw et al. 2019, 424). Yet, data from sexual health clinics show similar patterns to those observed in the wider Asian‐born gay migrant population: Those who are newly arrived have fewer sexual partners, higher rates of self‐reported condom use and shorter intervals between HIV tests compared to Australian‐born gay men (Blackshaw et al. 2019). Overall, the sexual and prevention behaviours of newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants largely demonstrate low HIV risk, a pattern that can be traced back to a sexual behaviour survey in 1993 (Hood et al. 1994); so how did they acquire the virus and become a key affected population group in Australia in the last decade (Australian Federations of AIDS Organisations 2021)?

Behavioural science, which has largely relied on psychological and individualist understandings of sexuality, has limited scope to address the above question. In this paper, we bring postmigration HIV vulnerability, embedded within migrant networks, and notions of race and Whiteness into the foreground to deepen our understanding.

Postmigration HIV Vulnerability, Migrant Networks and Whiteness

HIV vulnerability is understood as the social, economic and cultural drivers that pattern HIV transmission (Stephenson and Kippax 2016). The concept of vulnerability is often thought to be just another synonym for risk; however, sociologists have long differentiated between ‘a population at risk’ due to prolonged/excessive exposure to risk factors and ‘a vulnerable population’, disproportionately affected by diseases because of their social location (Frohlich and Potvin 2008). In simple terms, risk is only potentially modifiable—genetic predisposition, for instance, cannot be altered, whereas vulnerability is modifiable but dependent on social context and political will (Frohlich and Potvin 2008).

Relational, contextual and situational factors are integral for examining postmigration HIV vulnerability (Hirsch 2014; Mellini et al. 2024; Zhou 2017). Existing studies indicate that sexual networks and practices are products of living and working conditions, shaped by social (Mellini et al. 2024), economic (Hirsch 2014) and cultural (Zhou 2017) contexts of migration. Examination of postmigration HIV vulnerability must move beyond sexual and drug‐using networks towards what migration scholars call migrant networks, which operate within and across nation‐states (Phillimore et al. 2014; Ryan and Mulholland 2015).

Massey defined migrant networks as: ‘sets of interpersonal ties that link migrants, former migrants, and non‐migrants in origin and destination areas through the bonds of kinship, friendship and shared community origin’ (Massey 1988, 396). This definition suggests diverse, transnational and intersecting forms of relations in migrants' social worlds (Phillimore et al. 2014). Some studies with migrants have attempted to provide an intersectional understanding, stepping beyond sexual and drug‐using networks to identify HIV‐related informational, practical and emotional support within diaspora communities (Barrington et al. 2021; Chan et al. 2025). However, these studies do not consider the association between migrant networks and aspirations to migrate (Aslany et al. 2021), which is critical to understand who and what drives decisions to move.

Migrants move across international borders for multiple reasons, from improving their economic conditions to seeking safety from oppression. These multiple reasons, arising from differences between current and aspired quality of life, have been conceptualised as migration aspirations (Carling and Collins 2018). Social networks between current and potential migrants distribute information and ideas that foster migration aspirations (Aslany et al. 2021). Ideas related to sexuality, for example, have long been acknowledged as a determinant driving and influenced by migration (Carrillo and Fontdevila 2014). HIV prevention practices are subsequently changing as one's sexuality shifts across migration trajectories, causing compositional and power dynamic changes within sexual and intimate relationships across time and space (Carrillo and Fontdevila 2014; Zhou 2017).

To fulfil one's migration aspirations, support is essential (Carling and Collins 2018), but economic, social and psychological support are unequally distributed and recognised within one's networks (Anthias 2007; Ryan et al. 2015). Anthias (2007) argues that once a person has successfully migrated, support from their diaspora communities may not translate to social capital within their new location because they are less valued economically and/or culturally in the host societies. For example, in a Canadian study with Chinese migrants, Zhou (2017) found most considered HIV a taboo topic, which was culturally perceived as an effective approach to avoid risky sexual behaviours in China; however, gay participants in her study noted the same approach did not work in the Canadian context, where they opted to trust local White gay men's judgement on what was safe and pleasurable. Indeed, the distribution and value of support follow existing societal hierarchies based on race, gender, sexuality, class, disability and other social locations (Ryan et al. 2015).

Inequities in access to and value of support are also structurally embedded in migration‐related policies and institutions, which influence postmigration HIV vulnerability (Desgrees‐du‐Lou et al. 2016; Mellini et al. 2024). In Australia, access to PrEP is stratified based on citizen/permanent and temporary migration categories (e.g., international students) (Sudarto et al. 2022). Asian‐born gay migrants who are categorised as temporary residents, with weekly income below AU$ 1500 and no access to Medicare (Australia's national medical insurance), are less likely to use PrEP (Wong et al. 2022). This limitation in PrEP access is of particular importance because their sexual networks are changing postmigration, with White men being the most commonly reported sexual and intimate partners (Wong et al. 2022). In these White‐dominated sexual networks, power dynamics are determined by sexual desirability based on skin colour and other racialised characteristics (Caluya 2006; Jordens and Griffiths 2022). Many Asian‐born gay migrants are further racially stereotyped as subordinated female subjects (Jordens and Griffiths 2022), putting them into a vulnerable situation, shaped by racial and gender oppressions, which can impede their capabilities to access, appraise, apply and/or prioritise sexual health information to prevent HIV.

The literature in the above paragraph suggests the potentiality of Whiteness in shaping postmigration HIV vulnerability embedded in migrant networks. The notion of Whiteness, as developed by Hage (Hage 2000), acknowledges ways of thinking, being and acting that perceive Western European colonisation and cultures, including its views on gender and sexuality, as superior to other cultures. As a form of embodied cultural capital, the hegemony of Whiteness has long shaped the collective desire among migrants to assimilate into Australian society (Thai et al. 2020; Hage 2000), including configuring racial hierarchies within gay communities (Caluya 2006; Jordens and Griffiths 2022). Structurally, Whiteness shapes institutional practices within Australia's sexual health sector that privilege White gay men's worldviews (Istiko and Sudarto 2025) and ambivalently include or exclude others (Norman et al. 2025).

We wrote this paper to present our examination of postmigration HIV vulnerability within migrant networks of newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants in Queensland, Australia. Our examination reveals the role Whiteness plays transnationally, by shaping migrant networks, and locally, by facilitating situations where sexual violence and HIV transmission can occur, while also reducing HIV risk by making access to and understanding of local sexual health information and services desirable. The contribution of our examination to the scholarship of migration, HIV and race is twofold. Methodologically, in the next section, we describe an innovative interviewing style called race‐conscious conversations to help researchers co‐construct deeper understandings of subtle, structural and intersectional forms of racism with research participants. This is followed with three thematic sections representing our empirical contribution: a sociologically plausible mechanism that links Whiteness to the formation of migrant networks and postmigration HIV vulnerability. The final section outlines the implications of our findings for decolonising Australia's sexual health sector.

Methodology

Race‐Conscious Conversations

We developed race‐conscious conversations as a decolonial approach to interviews, defined as ‘an interviewing style that situates interviewer and interviewee as knowledge producing partners, and is sensitive towards the complex manifestations of race and racism, as well as their intersections with other structures of oppressions’ (Istiko 2025, 5). Details of race‐conscious conversations are explained elsewhere (Istiko 2025), but five principles underpin their design and implementation: (1) acknowledge the multipositionalities of the researcher and participants; (2) use multiple, active interviews to enable respectful debates and deeper interrogation of participants' responses; (3) use creative methods during data collection to enable shared critical consciousness; (4) use theories of race and antiracism as a critical lens for data analysis; and (5) work with feminist ethics of care in mind to ensure the safety and well‐being of both participants and researcher when discussing racial oppression.

Participants

Aligned with recent HIV epidemiological evidence (Blackshaw et al. 2019), we purposively recruited participants between September 2021 and March 2022, using the following criteria: (1) aged 18 and above; (2) born in a Southeast or Northeast Asian country; (3) self‐identified as gay, bisexual or men who have sex with men; (4) lived in Queensland; (5) arrived in Australia no more than 4 years prior; and (6) able to communicate in English. The inclusion of HIV‐positive participants was essential not only for a comprehensive understanding of sexual health literacy and migrant networks in this group but also for their lived experience and knowledge that could inform HIV prevention.

The first author's local networks were crucial for recruitment. Within Indonesian diaspora communities, he recruited four participants through convenience and snowball sampling. Local community‐based sexual health providers helped recruit a further five participants from South Korea, China, Hong Kong and the Philippines by distributing flyers in venues frequented by gay men and through their networks of volunteers and colleagues. Two participants from Taiwan and Cambodia were recruited through Facebook using paid advertisements and unpaid posts in closed groups for gay men.

Eleven participants, aged 23–49, were included in the study. All were university‐educated, with professional careers premigration, and had high international mobility. Seven were international students, three were graduate visa holders, and one was an asylum seeker 1 . Participants had lived in Australia between 8 months and 4 years. Nine lived in Brisbane, and two lived in regional Queensland. Eight were HIV‐negative, and three were HIV‐positive (two received diagnoses in their home countries, and one acquired HIV in his home country but was diagnosed in Australia). Their relatively affluent backgrounds were consistent with the characteristics of Asian‐born respondents in Australia's sexual behaviour surveys over the last 30 years (Wong et al. 2022; Hood et al. 1994).

Data Generation

Participants were interviewed three times over 6 months. The first round of interviews explored migration aspirations, settlement experiences, migrant networks, support systems and sexual health knowledge. Explicit and implicit conversations on race provided foundations for deeper discussions in the second round of interviews conducted 3 months later. The final round of interviews involved mapping participants' migrant networks using a sociogram—a two‐dimensional pie chart with three layers (inner, middle and outer), each representing the degree of emotional closeness between participants and their networks (Tubaro et al. 2016). As a creative method, sociograms are useful for generating a visual representation of intersectional migrant networks (Tubaro et al. 2016) and identifying sources of sexual health support (Hollingdrake 2020). We drew on data from the first and second rounds of interviews to identify 11 relationship categories (see Figure 1) and help participants systematically map their social lives.

An empty sociogram.

To be sensitive to changes in migrant networks over time, participants wrote the year of arrival in Australia and the year they met each person in their migrant network on their sociograms. Participants used coloured pens to answer three questions: (1) who are the most important people in your life? (black); (2) who are the important people in your migration journey to Australia? (blue); (3) who are the people who give you support for your sexual health? (red). A few participants created new categories, others added a new layer, and some redefined the meanings of the categories we provided, highlighting critical reflections on their networks and social practices.

Analytical Procedure

We used Braun and Clarke’s (2024) reflexive thematic analysis by, first, rereading the transcripts, professionally transcribed, for familiarity. Then, we coded the transcripts using the following codes, which emerged from our rereading to construct our understanding of postmigration HIV vulnerability: ‘sexuality’, ‘sexual pleasure’, ‘sexual health services’, ‘PrEP’, ‘stigma’, ‘HIV diagnosis’, ‘HIV informational support’, ‘HIV emotional support’ and ‘sexual violence’.

To analyse the sociograms, we went back and forth between textual and visual data to pay attention to: the network density; the strength of relationships within a network; the locations (local or transnational) of people in a sociogram; emotional bonds between participants and their networks; forms and distribution of support; and reciprocity of relationships. The result was a final set of five categories of relationships in their migrant networks: (1) family; (2) Asian diaspora; (3) workplace‐based networks; (4) sexual health services; and (5) local gay networks.

While rereading the coded data under each of the above categories, we noticed racial discourse underpinning the manner in which participants tacitly and explicitly discussed Australian society (e.g., using the term ‘white’ to refer to Australian‐born gay men) in comparison with their home countries and diaspora communities (e.g., using the term ‘backwards’ when describing home countries); hence, we created ‘race’ as a new code for some of the data. To make sense of the relationships between ‘race’ and the previous codes identified as relevant to postmigration HIV vulnerability, we drew on the concept of Whiteness, aligning with the principle of engaging with theories of race in race‐conscious conversations (Istiko 2025).

A workshop involving a few participants and three local community‐based sexual health providers was held to discuss preliminary findings. During the workshop, we received strong opposition from some providers for linking race and HIV, reflecting Australia's wider culture of silence and silencing when it comes to race (Bargallie et al. 2023). For example, a White staff member representing a multicultural organisation said they did not like the word ‘racism’ because they perceived it as a term that did not come from a ‘strength‐based point view’ or ‘positive things’. The first author had also encountered similar rejections for discussing race and HIV from other stakeholders within Australia's sexual health sector (Istiko 2024), though never from the participants themselves. We decided to forge forward with our analysis.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study came from the University of Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (2021/HE000574). Throughout the research, we were pragmatic yet critical in our engagement with ethnic and racial categories. We used ‘Asian‐born’ in the hope of supporting knowledge translation, as the term is widely used within Australia's sexual health sector. At the same time, we believed that by using this epidemiological label to refer to people from Southeast and Northeast Asia (and excluding other Asian regions), we were reproducing a racial grouping common in the Australian context. This tension denotes the messiness of decolonial research in the context of Western funding, institutions and systems of classification.

The interviews also included stories of sexual violence. Aligned with feminist ethics of care in race‐conscious conversations (Istiko 2025), well‐being checks during the conversations and follow‐ups were conducted, though the relevant participants declined referral to services when offered. Affected by these stories, the first author and interviewer had sought professional psychological support along with support from his partner, close friends and family members.

Findings

Most participants saw migration to Australia as an opportunity to improve career outcomes through further education. As they settled in Australia, their career aspirations became entwined with their desire to stay in the country as out and proud gay men, forming what we have termed ‘career‐sexuality aspirations’. As Amir said: ‘first, [I looked for] a better job; second, a better life which was the gay LGBT 2 ‐friendly countries, and then Australia is one of the best ones.’ Their aspirations were influenced by a perception that Australia was a ‘progressive’ society (Hamzah), offering much better employment opportunities in conjunction with societal acceptance of their sexuality. Such a perception produced two types of social practices relevant to the formation of migrant networks. The first one was ‘selective’ practices (Earvin), enacted to maintain some distance from their family and diaspora communities, deemed ‘conservative’ in direct opposition to Australian society (Gaspard). The other was embedding practices, deployed to assimilate into Australian ‘progressive’ society, in particular, White‐dominated gay communities. These binary worldviews and practices were shaped by Whiteness.

The first two of the following three themes describe the context in which Whiteness emerges and shapes migrant networks, whereas the final theme explains the process through which Whiteness influences postmigration HIV vulnerability. In this final theme, participants' narratives suggest that Whiteness contributes to situations where sexual violence and HIV transmission are likely to occur; at the same time, Whiteness reduces HIV risk by making access to and understanding of local sexual health information and services desirable.

Aspiring to Be Progressive = Aspiring to Be White

According to Hamzah, objectified and institutionalised forms of cultural capital in his home country (i.e., books and formal education, respectively) cemented the idea of Western countries as ‘progressive’, with dominant power to set the global indicators for societal progress. These indicators included the degree to which LGBT people are accepted and the level of national economic development. In this sense, ‘progressive’ became a coded term to think of Western countries as the most socially just, economically powerful and culturally civilised countries in the world. Informed by this ‘progressive’ lens, Hamzah and Youngjoon explicitly referred to Australia as a ‘white country’. The term ‘Australians’ was used as a stand‐in for White people: ‘I'm the only Asian and the only international student who is working in that job, all of them [the others] are Australians, like they are all white’ (Earvin). Consequently, most perceived White gay men as a symbol of a progressive society, providing an opportunity for self‐discovery and self‐affirmation through sexual and dating relationships: ‘choosing white people over Asian people is more opportunity to discover more about myself’ (Kheang).

The idea of ‘conservative’ Asia, on the other hand, was rooted in the power religions had over LGBT people in many Asian countries and career‐related factors. Regarding religions, Eric said: ‘It's quite difficult, to be honest, because in Christianity, being gay is like a sin. I have to accept myself first. Now I have the perspective that religion is different with the relationship with God.’ This separation between religions and God was one of the few approaches that contributed to diverse and ever‐changing understandings of sexualities across participants. Based on his extensive travels, Gaspard further considered Thailand and Taiwan as ‘advanced’ in the context of LGBT rights, while classifying other Asian countries as conservative due to the hegemony of religions. Other ‘conservative’ markers for Asian societies were career‐related factors, such as working conditions, work–life balance, salary and professional recognition. Joseph, for example, described his move to Australia as a strategy to move away from ‘toxic’ working conditions that involved: ‘a lot of pressure, a lot of workload, but not compensated very well.’ Both notions of ‘conservative’ Asia and ‘progressive’ Australia among the participants were narrow, binary worldviews shaped by Whiteness, which subsequently produced selective and embedding practices.

Binary Worldviews and Migrant Networks: Selective and Embedding Practices

Participants deployed selective practices to distance themselves from ‘conservative’ Asia, while maintaining access to support from trusted members of family and diaspora communities. In these practices, they carefully chose who to trust, what support to seek and what personal information was safe to share. Joseph's sociogram (Figure 2) is useful to understand selective practices. The grey circle with the title ‘Sister’ refers to the most trusted person within his Catholic family: ‘I came out officially to her and we're like practically best friends. So, no secrets whatever, no information withheld.’ Joseph sought her emotional support after he was potentially exposed to a sexually transmitted infection (STI), even though she lived in his home country. He did so while knowing that she (and his other family members) was not knowledgeable about sexual health or gay cultures: ‘when it comes to technical information, I think they don't have any information about that.’

Joseph's sociogram.

Selective practices were also used when engaging with their diaspora community. These practices were a response to a culture of jealousy and gossiping that often targeted those who tried to be successful. Earvin said: ‘… that's one thing that my dad advises to me, if you are going to go to Australia, make sure you become selective when it comes to your own [country] fellowmen, because they are the ones who are going to put you down if you are going to succeed.’ Despite such concerns, participants like Joseph found acceptance of their sexuality, and even other gay friends, among carefully selected members of their diaspora community, as shown by ‘Friend 3–4’ in Figure 2 and Joseph's testimony: ‘I have never felt discriminated [in my community] about me being gay whatsoever.’ He and Hamzah also sought emotional support from their diaspora community after he was potentially exposed to HIV: ‘… they are being supportive to me, checking in with me all the time, every day, “Have you had dinner?” “We'll send you food” just because I was so frustrated with the situation.’ In sum, amid a sense of distrust, selective practices provided strong emotional bonds and sexual acceptance, which helped to fulfil career‐sexuality aspirations postmigration and to access emotional support during HIV/STI testing.

Meanwhile, embedding social practices (Ryan and Mulholland 2015) was used to assimilate into Australian society via local gay networks, in particular, alongside sexual health services and workplace‐based networks. The first two were most relevant to postmigration HIV vulnerability.

Attempting to embed themselves into local gay networks, most participants tried to form sexual and intimate relationships with White gay men. David said his preferences for White men only began to emerge in Australia: ‘I would say 90% of my fuck buddy [here] are white.’ However, racism was a major challenge: ‘It's difficult to make a relationship with them because I'm Asian’ (Eric). Indeed, embedding in this case often resulted in fleeting social and sexual relationships due to subtle racism, as Youngjoon also articulated: ‘people don't really explicitly expose or express their opinion about racism or things like that.’ His online interactions with White gay men were often imbued with racialised sexual compliments: ‘… they don't really see me as a person, but it's more like, “I have some Asian fetish, and I really love Asian men … Blah, blah, blah. I love Asian ass”’.

A few participants attempted to embed themselves into gay social groups. Gaspard discussed social groups based on the intersections of physical appearance and subcultures (e.g., a ‘bear’ club) or hobbies: ‘There are many, many groups. A simple internet search would lead you to them. I think the main tasks to just to screen what is the right fit for you, rather than finding them.’ Brian tried a few groups before finding some success with a gay men's sports group (green box in Figure 3 below). Yet during a conversation about his dating life, Brian revealed that most young White gay men in the group avoided him. His communication with other group members tended to be restricted to older White gay men. These ‘cliques’ within the sports group became a window for him to understand how local gay cultures operated. An important part of internalising local gay cultures through embedding practices was consuming products and services used by White gay men, particularly sexual health services, which included both sexual health clinics and community‐based sexual health service providers. Youngjoon said: ‘I was so looking forward to [my first visit to the sexual health clinic] because it's kind of like a new place, and it seems like it's really normal and common to Australian gay people. So, I feel like I'm just finally joined the club.’ For Brian, whose sociogram does not show any actors within the ‘Sexual health services’ category (Figure 3), he still received information about where to access HIV/STI prevention from other (mostly White) members of the gay men’s sports groups. Overall, compared to selective practices with family and diaspora communities, relationships with White gay men and sexual health services were central for accumulating Whiteness and being seen as ‘progressive’ gay individuals. These complex relationships had dual impacts on postmigration HIV vulnerability.

Dual Impacts of Accumulating Whiteness on Postmigration HIV Vulnerability

As the existence of White gay men became normalised within participants' sexual lives, most experienced racial stereotypes which made them vulnerable to HIV and sexual violence in certain situations. Most reported being stereotyped through the texture of their skin: ‘So I normally got you know just got some comments about my skin as well. Like it's just smooth, no hair, and then just like–yeah that's normally what I got you know when I got comment about my skin’ (Youngjoon). Such seemingly positive, harmless compliments were accompanied by stereotypes of gay men from Asian countries as bottoms: ‘… if I see an Asian and a white [gay] couple I would just automatically think that Oh God, he's the bottom, he's the top’ (Joseph). Both ‘smooth skin’ and ‘bottom’ stereotypes were deployed in impersonal ways, making them difficult for some to discern as a form of sexual objectification that pressured them to be submissive. In such a vulnerable position, some reported being introduced to a culture of using drugs while having sex by White gay men. ‘All of my drug experience begin here,’ said Hamzah, who used ecstasy and crystal meth given to him for free by White gay men. ‘Back home, I don't have access to [drugs]. Maybe I'm not aware of it, but also it was normatively bad, but here [it is part of] embracing all my identity,’ Hamzah further explained, as he also only began embracing ‘bottoming’ in Australia. Critically, half of the HIV‐negative participants who reported drug use while having sex were not on PrEP, including Hamzah, suggesting that racial stereotypes, drug use and HIV are entangled in complex ways within contemporary Australian gay cultures.

A couple of participants went further to accommodate a wider range of White gay men's sexual preferences, with limited capacity for negotiation. Joseph once agreed to a White gay man's request for a threesome with an Indian man. Joseph called the third man ‘curry’ (a racial slur) and, at one point during the sexual encounter, he wished for the man to stop penetrating him, though he did not feel confident enough to verbalise it: ‘… [there] was no intimacy or no connection between the three of us … when the Indian guy was fucking me I was like okay, can you stop now, can it be his turn [the white guy]? I didn't say that to them but that's what I was thinking.’ The threesome continued despite it no longer being consensual, indicating that even attempts to maintain safety could be nullified in situations where racial power imbalances became fixed. During an encounter with a White gay man, Kheang, who was positive with an undetectable 3 viral load, chose not to share his HIV status and asked the man, whom he met for the first time, to put on a condom. Kheang said: ‘I could hear him take off–it sounded like he was putting it–it sounded like he was stretching the band but he was not putting it on.’ Post‐coital, the man proudly declared he did not use the condom, a practice known as stealthing, as he assumed Kheang was HIV‐negative. These incidents of racial sexual violence were situations where Whiteness prevailed, and HIV transmission could have occurred.

On the other hand, Whiteness also strengthened the desire to access sexual health information and services, especially PrEP. Brian's relationship with his White‐dominated gay men's sports groups involved the distribution of information related to HIV testing, PrEP and overcoming shame after an STI diagnosis. He might not yet apply this information in his sexual life, but he internalised the cultural value of such information to the point of being able to recognise PrEP as an important part of local gay cultures: ‘I got that exposure of PrEP in Australia knowing that [other gay men said] “I'm using PrEP,” and will ask me, “Do you use PrEP?”’ Even among participants who reported other prevention strategies (e.g., condoms and nonpenetrative sexual activities), PrEP was acknowledged as essential to strengthen connections with local gay men. As Gaspard articulated: ‘I was very happy to have sex with condoms, and in Australia, PrEP is the norm, PrEP is the standard.’ In a discursive way, he also perceived PrEP through the binary lens of ‘progressive’ Australia and ‘conservative’ Asia: ‘PrEP being the norm and not making gay people feel guilty, is extremely important and I think Asia has a huge, huge, huge gap to fill because they're very retarded when it comes to prevention of STIs and in particular HIV.’ His use of the term ‘retarded’, which many may find offensive, indicates that biomedical HIV prevention such as PrEP is a perceived marker that separates Western civilisations and Asian societies.

Government subsidies to purchase PrEP were not available to temporary residents, but some HIV‐negative participants purchased it through out‐of‐pocket expenses, which was reflective of their relative affluence. David said: ‘When I start doing [PrEP] because I know I'm going to see the GP a lot, I know I'm going to do everything a lot, so I would say I want a good insurance, I don't want a cheap insurance, so I will go to the GP all the time.’ At the same time, participants with PrEP tended to show stigmatising attitudes towards people living with HIV. Gaspard, for instance, used the term ‘contaminated’ when referring to HIV acquisition. Amir, who acquired the virus in his home country but was diagnosed in Australia, reported an experience of HIV discrimination from a PrEP‐using White gay man during their second sexual encounter. The man asked if he was also on PrEP, and Amir told him the truth. The man then spent the night scrolling on his phone, ignoring him while reading information about HIV transmission. ‘He didn't know that I was crying during the sleep,’ said Amir, who had an undetectable viral load. These examples demonstrate that the desire to use PrEP among HIV‐negative participants was not necessarily accompanied by the desire to reduce HIV stigma.

Overall, our conversations with the participants demonstrated that their relationships with White gay men, forged through embedding practices, contributed to an embodied desire to access sexual health services and PrEP, though they had to navigate transience, racialisation and sexual violence that haunted these relationships and encounters.

Discussion and Conclusion

Race‐conscious conversations with 11 newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants revealed a network of complex sociocultural drivers that influenced their postmigration HIV vulnerability. Attracted to the idea of Western ‘progressive’ societies, many moved to Australia, with the aspiration to belong to Australian, White‐dominated society. Assimilation occurred through embedding practices within their migrant networks, which were oriented towards local gay communities dominated by White gay men. Through embedding practices, most accumulated Whiteness, which had both positive and negative implications for postmigration HIV vulnerability.

Whiteness provides a sociologically plausible mechanism to explain the contradiction of low HIV risk yet significant HIV vulnerability among newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants in Australia. A recent online survey with Asian‐born gay migrants in Sydney highlights that most had good engagement with local LGBT communities, which was associated with low HIV risk (Chan et al. 2023). Our analysis further specifies that such engagement is oriented towards White gay men as primary actors of interest (see, as well, Wong et al. (2022) on White men as the most commonly reported sexual and intimate partners among Asian‐born gay migrants). As outlined in the Introduction section, many Asian‐born gay migrants prefer to use PrEP, especially those who are newly arrived (Ung et al. 2023) and/or have high HIV risk (Tieosapjaroen et al. 2024). Our analysis suggests that such preference is not only based on risk reduction considerations but also on a racialised desire to embody local gay cultures dominated by White gay men.

We notice, however, that some HIV‐negative participants who preferred PrEP were able to materialise their preference because of their relatively affluent backgrounds. This is important in the context of stratified access to healthcare between citizens/permanent residents and temporary migrants (Sudarto et al. 2022). Our analysis also indicates a difference between those who desire Whiteness and those who are able to accumulate it; the latter requires financial resources that not all newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants have. Those who do not have the financial means to purchase PrEP can face significant challenges in accessing it (Sudarto et al. 2022), further affirming the false logic of PrEP as an invention by and for Western societies, as some participants tacitly indicated.

The dark side of Whiteness for postmigration HIV vulnerability is the occurrence of situations where threats of sexual violence and HIV transmission are ever present. Fear of stealthing, a form of sexual violence, was also featured in the main storyline of Hot Peach Tea, a participatory digital storytelling intervention that was effective in increasing HIV‐related awareness and PrEP knowledge among newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants (Tieosapjaroen et al. 2025). Other forms of entanglement between HIV and sexual violence (e.g., forced transactional sexual relationships) were also documented in studies on HIV vulnerability among sub‐Saharan African migrants in France (Desgrees‐du‐Lou et al. 2016) and Switzerland (Mellini et al. 2024). Our analysis further affirms the entanglement between gender and racial oppressions in the context of postmigration HIV vulnerability.

Situated within this entanglement is the use of drugs for sex by some of our participants (widely referred to as ‘chemsex’), who were all introduced to it by White gay men. We acknowledge that there is a varying degree of HIV risk in chemsex depending on sexual relations and settings (Bourne et al. 2015; Drysdale et al. 2020). However, considering the potential for racial power imbalance, those who do chemsex without PrEP, as in the case of a couple of our participants, may find themselves in a position of vulnerability to HIV. What is more concerning is that there were no reports of engagement with harm reduction support despite multiple conversations with participants. Moving forwards, further theoretical and empirical knowledge is needed to understand drug use, including chemsex, across migration trajectories and in the context of racial power imbalance.

Within Australia's HIV response, Whiteness is also the norm for developing and implementing HIV prevention; Budi Sudarto, an Indonesian‐born trans activist with more than two decades of experience in Australia's HIV response, recently said: ‘there is still a view that HIV response for Asian‐born gay men are to duplicate the Anglo Australians HIV response’ (Istiko and Sudarto 2025, 9). Whiteness further makes research and interventions on migration, HIV and race less desirable, as indicated during the workshop discussing our study's preliminary findings. However, changes have recently begun to emerge from scholars working to improve the quality of life (Norman et al. 2025) and care provision for Asian‐born gay migrants with HIV (Sombrea et al. 2026).

We advocate for the dismantling of Whiteness as a hegemony across Australia's sexual health sector using the following strategies. First, invest in epistemological and methodological innovation to ensure subtle, structural and intersectional forms of race are consistently and rigorously studied across different groups of migrants. We have contributed by developing race‐conscious conversations as a decolonial approach to interviews, one of the most widely used qualitative methods (Istiko 2025). The same method can be used to co‐construct a deeper understanding of race among HIV health and social care professionals and researchers. An intersectional understanding of migrants' relationships is equally important (Tubaro et al. 2016); thus, we recommend social network analysis with sociograms for more robust public health evidence. A mixed‐methods approach may be required if the group is more heterogeneous than newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants.

Second, improve migrant representation by considering their intersectional experiences with race, HIV (Istiko and Sudarto 2025) and drug use. This is not just about increasing the number of migrant representatives in HIV prevention and harm reduction programmes; the focus is more on their capabilities to develop racial literacy in the Australian context and to be cognisant of the narratives they produce about their diverse communities and Australian society. In this context, digital storytelling, with one of its main features being co‐constructed and linked narratives from different members of the same community, can present more nuanced and pedagogical narratives on migration, HIV and race (TIeosapjaroen et al. 2025). Such an intervention can further prevent infantilisation of migrants, which can occur through simplified translation of clinical information from English into other languages (Gray et al. 2024). New ways of thinking and intervening in the intersections of migration, race and drug use, especially as informed by lived experience, are also urgently needed.

Third, subsidise PrEP for temporary residents and other migrants who are ineligible for Medicare (sudarto et al. 2022). This policy intervention would ease the gap between Australian‐born gay men and gay migrants and between migrants who are affluent and those who are not, further dismantling the status quo of PrEP as a ‘progressive’ Western invention that only benefits White gay men and those who embody their sexual and dating cultures.

Most urgently, we call for a shift away from the narrow focus on risk in HIV prevention targeting newly arrived Asian‐born gay migrants. An intersectoral approach to address postmigration HIV vulnerability, as shaped by migration‐related policies, institutions and networks, is no longer negotiable. Australia's HIV policies and agenda have spent enough resources focusing on changing the behaviours of a group of people with low HIV risk yet high HIV vulnerability; it is time to consider what can be modified in the policies, institutions and practices of powerful actors shaping their lives.
