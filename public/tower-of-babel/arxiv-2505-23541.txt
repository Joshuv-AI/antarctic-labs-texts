Upper and lower bounds for local Lipschitz stability of Bayesian posteriors
Nada Cvetković, Han Cheng Lie

Abstract
The work of Sprungk (Inverse Problems, 2020) established the local Lipschitz continuity of the misfit-to-posterior and prior-to-posterior maps with respect to the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the total variation, Hellinger, and 1-Wasserstein metrics, by proving certain upper bounds. The upper bounds were also used to show that if a posterior measure is more concentrated, then it can be more sensitive to perturbations in the misfit or prior. We prove upper bounds and lower bounds that emphasise the importance of the evidence. The lower bounds show that the sensitivity of posteriors to perturbations in the misfit or the prior not only can increase, but in general will increase as the posterior measure becomes more concentrated, i.e. as the evidence decreases to zero. Using the explicit dependence of our bounds on the evidence, we identify sufficient conditions for the misfit-to-posterior and prior-to-posterior maps to be locally bi-Lipschitz continuous.



Keywords:
Bayesian inference, posterior perturbation analysis, noninvertibility, total variation metric, Hellinger metric, Wasserstein metric, Kullback–Leibler divergence

1
Introduction

In the theory of inverse problems, one is given an observed data vector
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, an unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, and a function
G:\Theta\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
y=G(\theta)
, and the task is to infer
\theta
given
y
.
In the frequentist Bayesian approach to inverse problems, one assumes that the observation is a realisation of a
\mathcal{Y}
-valued random variable
Y=G(\theta^{\dagger})+\varepsilon
, where
\theta^{\dagger}
is fixed and
\varepsilon
is observation noise, and one models the unknown
\theta^{\dagger}
using a
\Theta
-valued random variable with prior law
\mu
. The solution to the Bayesian inverse problem is given by the posterior law
\mu_{\Phi}
of
\theta
given the observed quantity
y
, where
\mu_{\Phi}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the prior
\mu
and uniquely defined by the normalised likelihood
\ell_{\Phi,\mu}
:

\ell_{\Phi,\mu}(\theta;y)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\Phi}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}(\theta)=\frac{\exp(-\Phi(\theta;y))}{Z_{\Phi,\mu}(y)},\quad Z_{\Phi,\mu}(y)\coloneqq\int\exp(-\Phi(\theta^{\prime};y))\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta^{\prime}).

(1.1)

We shall refer to the negative logarithm
\Phi
of the unnormalised likelihood and the normalisation constant
Z_{\Phi,\mu}
as the ‘misfit’ and the ‘evidence’ respectively.
The misfit is determined by the ‘forward model’
G
that maps unknowns to observed quantities and the distribution of the observation noise
\varepsilon
.
For example, if
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon
is Gaussian with mean 0 and positive-definite covariance
C
, then
\Phi(\theta;y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|C^{-1/2}(G(\theta)-y)\right\|^{2}
.

An important theme in the theory of Bayesian inverse problems is the stability of the posterior with respect to perturbations in one of the objects that define it.
Let
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}
be some chosen metric or divergence on the space of probability measures on a fixed measurable space
(\Theta,\Sigma)
.
For a fixed prior
\mu
, the stability of the posterior
\mu_{\Phi}
with respect to the misfit
\Phi
can be described by the local Lipschitz continuity of the misfit-to-posterior mapping
L^{p}_{\mu}\ni\Phi\mapsto\mu_{\Phi}
, i.e. by an upper bound of the form

\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}})\leq C(\mu,\Phi_{1})\left\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\right\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}}.

(1.2)

Among the best-known instances of stability results in Bayesian inverse problems of the form (
1.2
) are those from
[
16
]
.
For example, the Hellinger metric was used to measure the distance between a reference posterior measure and an approximate posterior measure associated to a perturbed misfit in
[
16
, Theorem 4.6]
.
In the case of Gaussian observation noise, this stability result can then be used to show stability of the posterior with respect to the data.
An important consequence of the stability results in
[
16
]
is that many Bayesian inverse problems of interest are well-posed in the sense of Hadamard, i.e. the posterior depends continuously on the data
y
.

An important reason for the importance of stability with respect to the misfit is that it implies stability with respect to the forward model, under certain assumptions on the observational noise.
Stability with respect to the forward model is important in the context of numerical methods for solving Bayesian inverse problems, and more generally when accounting for the consequences of using an incorrect forward model in inference tasks.
This is because such stability results ensure that more accurate approximations of the true forward model lead to more accurate approximations of the true posterior, when all other factors are held fixed.
Stability with respect to the forward model establishes a link between numerical analysis and approximation theory on one hand, and robust statistical inference on the other hand, in the sense that one can use error bounds for a given numerical method or approximation in order to bound the error in the output of a statistical procedure based on that method or approximation.
Although the constants in these bounds are often difficult or costly to evaluate in practice, the bounds nevertheless provide a more precise characterisation of the continuous dependence of the inference outcome on numerical or modelling approximations.

An important milestone in the stability analysis of Bayesian inverse problems is given in the work
[
15
]
, which presents stability results of the posterior with respect to misfit perturbations and prior perturbations. Perturbations in the posterior are measured in the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the Hellinger, total variation, and 1-Wasserstein metrics.
Stability with respect to the prior is important because the choice of prior determines the maximal support of the resulting posterior, and thus the set of statistically admissible parameter values.
Furthermore, it is well-known from the mathematical-statistical theory of nonparametric Bayesian inference that the prior plays a key role in posterior consistency and contraction; see e.g.
[
7
, Chapter 6]
.

1.1
Contents of this work

The goal of this work is to further investigate the bounds and ideas relating to local Lipschitz stability results for the misfit-to-posterior and prior-to-posterior maps that were presented in
[
15
]
.
To this end, we prove upper and lower bounds on Kullback–Leibler divergences and Hellinger, total variation, and 1-Wasserstein metrics between posterior measures associated to different misfits and priors.

Recalling that
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}
denotes a chosen metric or divergence on the space of probability measures on
\Theta
, the upper bounds on posteriors that we state below are of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}})\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}\mathcal{U}_{M}(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2},Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu},Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu})

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi},(\mu_{2})_{\Phi})\leq

\displaystyle C_{2}\mathcal{U}_{P}(\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}},Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}),

(1.3b)

where
C_{1}=C_{1}(\mu,\Phi_{1})
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(\mu_{1},\Phi)
are positive scalars.
In (
1.3a
), we consider posteriors resulting from perturbed misfits and the same prior
\mu
, while in (
1.3b
), we consider posteriors resulting from different priors
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
and the same misfit
\Phi
.
The nonnegative functions
\mathcal{U}_{M}
and
\mathcal{U}_{P}
depend on the choice of
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}
and converge to zero as
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
converges to zero in an appropriate norm and as
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
converges to zero respectively.

If
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}
satisfies the triangle inequality, then the upper bounds above can be combined to obtain an upper bound on
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi_{1}},(\mu_{2})_{\Phi_{2}})
, i.e. on the measure of discrepancy when both the prior and misfit are perturbed.

While some of our upper bounds appear implicitly in the proofs of results in
[
15
]
, others do not.
We seek upper bounds with explicit dependence on the evidence, because the evidence plays a crucial role in determining the behaviour of the posterior, by Bayes’ formula (
1.1
).

The second main class of results that we state below are lower bounds on the above-mentioned metrics or divergences between posteriors, where perturbed misfits and perturbed priors are considered separately.
That is, we investigate inequalities of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}})\geq

\displaystyle C_{3}\mathcal{L}_{M}(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2},Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu},Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}),

(1.4a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi},(\mu_{2})_{\Phi})\geq

\displaystyle C_{4}\mathcal{L}_{P}(\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}},Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}),

(1.4b)

where
C_{3}=C_{3}(\mu,\Phi_{1})
and
C_{4}=C_{4}(\mu_{1},\Phi)
are positive.
The functions
\mathcal{L}_{M}
and
\mathcal{L}_{P}
are nonnegative functions whose values converge to zero as
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
converges to zero in an appropriate norm and as
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
converges to zero respectively.
Lower bounds of the type shown in (
1.4
) do not appear in
[
15
]
.

The first motivation for considering lower bounds is to shed light on whether the upper bounds shown in (
1.3
) are sharp up to local Lipschitz continuity prefactors, i.e. whether
\mathcal{L}_{M}=\mathcal{U}_{M}
for misfit perturbations and
\mathcal{L}_{P}=\mathcal{U}_{P}
for prior perturbations.
For example, when
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}
is the total variation metric and one considers misfit perturbations, then
Proposition

3.2
below implies that this is indeed the case, with

\mathcal{U}_{M}(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2},Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu},Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu})=\left\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}+\log\tfrac{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}}{Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}}\right\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}=\mathcal{L}_{M}(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2},Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu},Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}).

On the other hand, when
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}
is the total variation metric and one considers prior perturbations, then
Proposition

3.7
below shows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{P}(d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}},Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}})=

\displaystyle d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+\frac{|Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}-Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}|}{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P}(d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}},Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}})=

\displaystyle\left|d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})-\left|\frac{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}-Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}{2Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}}\right|\right|,

so that
\mathcal{U}_{P}\neq\mathcal{L}_{P}
in this case.

The second motivation for considering lower bounds is to provide additional evidence for the increasing sensitivity of posteriors to perturbations in misfits or priors as the posteriors become more concentrated.
A posterior
(\mu_{1})_{\Phi_{1}}
is said to be ‘more concentrated’ than another posterior
(\mu_{2})_{\Phi_{2}}
if the corresponding evidence terms
Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}
and
Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}
satisfy
0<Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}<Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}
.
A posterior
\mu_{\Phi_{1}}
is said to be ‘more sensitive’ with respect to a perturbation
\Delta\Phi
in the misfit than another posterior
\mu_{\Phi_{2}}
, if
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{1}+\Delta\Phi})
is larger than
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{\Phi_{2}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}+\Delta\Phi})
. Increased sensitivity with respect to perturbations of the prior is defined analogously.
The upper bounds in
[
15
]
on the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the Hellinger, total variation, and 1-Wasserstein metrics show that increasing sensitivity
can
occur. This is because the upper bounds involve division by the evidence, and thus increase as the values of the evidence become smaller; see
[
15
, Remark 9, Remark 18]
.
In contrast, lower bounds that involve division by the evidence will show that increasing sensitivity
must
occur.

Below, we discuss conditions under which equality holds in the upper and lower bounds.
However, we do not expect our bounds to be tight in general, due to the inequalities that we apply in our proofs.
Instead, we aim for bounds that highlight the contributions from two sources: differences in evidences or functions thereof, and differences due to
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{\mathcal{P}(\Theta)}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
for misfit and prior perturbations respectively.
In doing so, we obtain sufficient conditions for which the misfit-to-posterior map and the prior-to-posterior map are locally bi-Lipschitz continuous.

We believe it is natural to investigate sufficient conditions for local bi-Lipschitz continuity of the misfit-to-posterior and prior-to-posterior maps, given that sufficient conditions for the local Lipschitz continuity of these maps were presented in
[
15
]
.
The lower bounds that we investigate below relate to the local Lipschitz continuity of the inverses of these maps, when the inverses exist.
Our results suggest that one in general cannot expect these maps to be locally bi-Lipschitz on their natural domains of definition.
This is because the evidence term can lead to noninjectivity.
On the other hand, our results show that one can partition the natural domains of each map according to level sets with respect to the evidence, such that the restriction of each map to each level set is bi-Lipschitz continuous.

Inverting the misfit-to-posterior map (resp., the prior-to-posterior map) may be relevant when one is interested in determining what misfit (resp. prior) leads to a posterior distribution for a given fixed prior (resp. misfit).
This idea is similar to the idea of finding a reverse or backward diffusion process to pair with a forward diffusion process, in the context of diffusion probabilistic models or score-based generative models. We defer the investigation of this idea to future work.

1.2
Related literature

This work focuses on local Lipschitz stability of the posterior with respect to perturbations in the misfits and perturbations in the priors.
As was shown in
[
15
]
, well-posedness of Bayesian inverse problems with respect to the data is a consequence of the local Lipschitz stability of the posterior with respect to perturbations in the misfits.
While well-posedness with respect to the data is important in Bayesian inverse problems, it is not the main subject of investigation in this work, and we do not consider it further. Readers interested in well-posedness can see
[
16
, Section 4]
for prototypical well-posedness results, as well as
[
10
]
and the references therein for more recent results in this context.

The local Lipschitz stability bounds in
[
15
]
of posteriors with respect to misfit perturbations have been applied in different contexts.
The result
[
15
, Corollary 19]
, which gives conditions for well-posedness in 1-Wasserstein metric of the posterior with respect to perturbations in the data, was used to study the robustness of conditional generative models with respect to perturbations in the data; see e.g.
[
1
, Lemma 3]
.
The result
[
15
, Theorem 15]
, which concerns local Lipschitz stability in the 1-Wasserstein metric of posteriors with respect to prior perturbations, was used to prove error bounds for approximate posteriors that were obtained using a generative adversarial neural network
[
13
, Theorem 2]
.
The results
[
15
, Theorem 5, Theorem 8]
give sufficient conditions for local Lipschitz stability in the total variation and Hellinger metrics with respect to perturbations in the misfit.
These results were used to carry out a quantitative error analysis of an exploratory policy improvement algorithm in the context of
q
-learning; see the proof of
[
18
, Lemma 3.5]
.
The result
[
15
, Theorem 11]
, which gives conditions for local Lipschitz stability in the Kullback–Leibler divergence with respect to misfit perturbations, was applied to study the experimental design problem of choosing observation operators to mitigate errors in the forward model
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
.

The literature on Bayesian inverse problems contains local Lipschitz stability results that are distinct from those in
[
15
]
.
For example, in
[
17
, Section 4]
, stability results are given for the approximate posterior measures in the Hellinger metric, where the approximations result from Gaussian process approximations of the forward model or the misfit.
The expected mean squared Hellinger metric of random approximate posterior measures resulting from random approximations of the misfit and forward model was investigated in
[
11
]
, without the assumption that the approximations are given by Gaussian processes.
In
[
9
]
, local Lipschitz stability in the total variation metric and 1-Wasserstein metric of a deterministic, doubly intractable distribution with respect to a normalising constant was investigated; random distributions were also considered.
In the context of Bayesian optimal experimental design, the stability of the design with respect to the likelihood was shown in
[
5
, Proposition 3.3]
, and convergence analysis was performed for the design under convergence hypotheses on the misfits and forward models in Theorem 3.5 and Theorem 4.4 of
[
5
]
respectively.
Local Lipschitz stability of posteriors with respect to perturbations in misfits and priors was considered for a class of integral probability metrics associated to distance-like costs in
[
6
, Section 3]
.

Lower bounds of the type shown in (
1.4
) do not appear in any of the above-cited work.

2
Noninjectivity

In this section, we show that the misfit-to-evidence map
\Phi\mapsto Z_{\Phi,\mu}
and the prior-to-evidence map
\mu\mapsto Z_{\Phi,\mu}
are in general not injective.
The proofs of the results in this section are given in
Appendix

B
.

We first consider the noninjectivity of functions of misfits.

Lemma 2.1
.

Fix an arbitrary
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
.

(i)

If
\supp{\mu}
is uncountable or if
\textup{card}({\supp{\mu}})\geq 2
, then the map
\Phi\mapsto Z_{\Phi,\mu}
is not injective on
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

(ii)

If
\Phi
is such that
Z_{\Phi,\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, then for every
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
Z_{\Phi+c,\mu}=\exp(-c)Z_{\Phi,\mu}
.

(iii)

If
\Phi_{i}\in L^{0}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
is such that
Z_{\Phi_{i},\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
i=1,2
, then
\log\ell_{\Phi_{1},\mu}=\log\ell_{\Phi_{2},\mu}

\mu
-a.s. if and only if
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
is
\mu
-a.s. constant. In turn,
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
is
\mu
-a.s. constant if and only if it is equal to
\log Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}-\log Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}
.

The significance of
Lemma

2.1
(iii)
is that the map
\Phi\mapsto\log\ell_{\Phi}
is not invertible on
L^{p}_{\mu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.
In particular, it is not in general true that for some
M>0
, there exists
C(M)>0
such that for every
\Phi_{i}
with
\|\Phi_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}}\leq M<\infty
,

\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}}\leq C(M)\|\log\ell_{\Phi_{1},\mu}-\log\ell_{\Phi_{2},\mu}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}}.

(2.1)

For example, fix
\Phi_{1}
and let
\Phi_{2}=\Phi_{1}+c
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the left-hand side in (
2.1
) will be strictly positive, while the right-hand side will be equal to zero.

Example

\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
is not

Example 2.2

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)

\Theta
, it follows that

\displaystyle Z_{\Phi_{n},\mu}=\exp(-M)\int_{S_{n}}\exp(-\Phi_{1})\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{S_{n}^{\complement}}\exp(-\Phi_{1})\mathrm{d}\mu\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\exp(-M)Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu},

which is equivalent to
\lim_{n\to\infty}(-\log Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}+\log Z_{\Phi_{n},\mu})=-M
.
Thus, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
,

\displaystyle\|\log\ell_{\Phi_{1},\mu}-\log\ell_{\Phi_{n},\mu}\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\left\|-\Phi_{1}-\log Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}+\Phi_{n}+\log Z_{\Phi_{n},\mu}\right\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|M\mathbb{I}_{S_{n}}-\log Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}+\log Z_{\Phi_{n},\mu}\right\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

where the first equation follows from the definition (
1.6b
) of
\ell_{\Phi_{i},\mu}
, the second equation follows from (
2.2
), and the convergence statement follows from the hypothesis that
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
increases to
\Theta
.
Thus, the right-hand side of (
2.1
) converges to zero for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and there does not exist some
0<C(M)<\infty
such that (
2.1
) holds.

For an arbitrary
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Theta)
, define

D(\nu)\coloneqq\{\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\ :\ \mu^{\prime}\ll\nu\}.

(2.3)

We now describe the noninjectivity of the prior-to-evidence map on
D(\nu)
and a sufficient condition on the misfit
\Phi
for the prior-to-posterior map on
D(\nu)
to be injective.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\Theta)
,
D(\nu)
be as in (
2.3
), and
\Phi\in L^{0}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
be such that
Z_{\Phi,\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for every
\mu\in D(\nu)
.

(i)

If
\supp{\nu}
is uncountable or
\textup{card}({\supp{\nu}})\geq 3
, then the map
D(\nu)\ni\mu\mapsto Z_{\Phi,\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is not injective.

(ii)

Suppose
\mu_{i}\in D(\nu)
,
i=1,2
and
\exp(-\Phi)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\nu
-a.s.
Then
(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}=(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}
if and only if
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
.

The restriction to the set of priors that admit a common dominating measure is not new, and has an important advantage of ensuring that misspecification of the prior does not occur
[
7
, pp.6-7]
.
By
Lemma

A.1
(i)
, if
\Phi\in L^{\infty}_{\nu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
, then for every
\mu\in D(\nu)
,
\Phi\in L^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
, which in turn implies that
Z_{\Phi,\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Thus, the set of misfits
\Phi
such that
Z_{\Phi,\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for every
\mu\in D(\nu)
contains
L^{\infty}_{\nu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
, and the hypothesis on
\Phi
of
Lemma

2.3
is not vacuous.
We comment on the assumption of bounded misfits in
Remark

3.4
below.

We provide an example where the prior-to-evidence map
D(\nu)\ni\mu\mapsto Z_{\Phi,\mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is noninjective.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\Theta=\mathbb{R}
,
\nu
be Lebesgue measure,
\mu_{1}
be the uniform measure on
[-2,1]
,
\mu_{2}
be the uniform measure on
[-1,2]
, and

\Phi\coloneqq\mathbb{I}_{[-2,-1]\cup[1,2]}
. Then
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}=\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{I}_{[-2,1]}
,
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}=\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,2]}
,
\Phi\in L^{\infty}_{\mu_{i}}(\Theta,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
for
i=1,2
, and

\exp(-\Phi)(x)=\begin{cases}\exp(-1),&x\in[-2,-1]\cup[1,2]\\
1,&x\notin[-2,-1]\cup[1,2],\end{cases}

which imply

\displaystyle Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}=

\displaystyle\int\exp(-\Phi)\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}=\frac{1}{3}\int_{-2}^{-1}\exp(-1)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{3}\int_{-1}^{1}1\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\exp(-1)+2}{3}

\displaystyle Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}=

\displaystyle\int\exp(-\Phi)\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}=\frac{1}{3}\int_{-1}^{-1}1\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{3}\int_{-1}^{2}\exp(-1)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{2+\exp(-1)}{3}.

The following modification of
Example

2.4
shows that without the hypothesis that
\exp(-\Phi)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

\nu
-a.s., the conclusion of
Lemma

2.3
(ii)
need not hold, i.e. the prior-to-posterior map for a fixed misfit
\Phi
need not be injective.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\Theta=\mathbb{R}
,
\nu
be Lebesgue measure,
\mu_{1}
be the uniform measure on
[-2,1]
,
\mu_{2}
be the uniform measure on
[-1,2]
, and

\exp(-\Phi)(x)=\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}(x).

Then

Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}=\frac{1}{3}\int_{-2}^{1}\exp(-\Phi)(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{3}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{3}\int_{-1}^{2}\exp(-\Phi)(x)\mathrm{d}x=Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}.

The above and (
1.6b
) together imply that

\ell_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}=\frac{\exp(-\Phi)}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}=\frac{\exp(-\Phi)}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}}=\ell_{\Phi,\mu_{2}},\quad\text{$\nu$-a.s.}

(2.4)

By the choices of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}=\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{I}_{[-2,1]}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}=\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,2]}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}.

(2.5)

Now

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}=\ell_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}=

\displaystyle\ell_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}

\displaystyle\exp(-\Phi)=\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}

by (
2.4
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}

by (
2.5
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}

\displaystyle\exp(-\Phi)=\mathbb{I}_{[-1,1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}}{\mathrm{d}\nu},

which implies that
(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}=(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}
. Since
\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}
, it follows that
D(\nu)\ni\mu\mapsto\mu_{\Phi}\in D(\nu)
is not injective for this choice of
\Phi
.

3
Bounds in the total variation metric

Let
\nu,\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
be such that
\mu_{i}\ll\nu
for
i=1,2
.
The total variation metric is defined by

d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\coloneqq\sup_{A\in\Sigma}|\mu_{1}(A)-\mu_{2}(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\right|\mathrm{d}\nu.

(3.1)

The value of
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
does not depend on the choice of
\nu
, and
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
takes values in
[0,1]
.
The maximal value of 1 is attained if and only if the densities
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
and
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
have disjoint regions of positivity, i.e.
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are singular; see e.g.
[
7
, Section B.1]
.
If
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are not singular, this does not imply that either
\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{2}
or that
\mu_{2}\ll\mu_{1}
, as the example below shows.

Example 3.1
.

Let
\nu
be the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
,
\mu
be the uniform measure on
[0,1]
, and for
\tau\in(-1,1)
, let
\mu_{\tau}
be the uniform measure on
[\tau,1+\tau]
.
If
\tau\neq 0
, then
[0,1]\setminus[\tau,1+\tau]
and
[\tau,1+\tau]\setminus[0,1]
are both nonempty, so neither
\mu\ll\mu_{\tau}
nor
\mu_{\tau}\ll\mu
hold.
On the other hand, by (
3.1
),

d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu,\mu_{\tau})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{[0,1]\setminus[\tau,1+\tau]}\mathrm{d}\nu+\int_{[\tau,1+\tau]\setminus[0,1]}\mathrm{d}\nu\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(|\tau|+|\tau|\right)=|\tau|.

Thus as
\tau\to 0
,
\mu_{\tau}
converges to
\mu
in the total variation metric.

.

The upper and lower bounds on the total variation metric in the next result follow from the local Lipschitz continuity (

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and
\Phi_{i}\in L^{0}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
be such that
\mu_{\Phi_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)

(i)

If in addition

(ii)

If in addition

,

, then

d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\exp(-\left\|\Phi_{1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}})}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}}\wedge\frac{\exp(-\left\|\Phi_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}})}{Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}}\right)\left\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}+\log\frac{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}}{Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}}\right\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}.

If
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
is constant

-a.s., then equality holds in both the upper and lower bounds.

Remark 3.3

(Nonnegativity hypothesis for misfits bounded from below)
.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and
\Phi\in L^{0}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
satisfies
\essinf_{\mu}\Phi>-\infty
, then we may assume that
\essinf_{\mu}\Phi=0
.
There is no loss of generality, since if
-\infty<\essinf_{\mu}\Phi<0
, then by defining
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi-\essinf_{\mu}\Phi
, we have
\essinf_{\mu}\Psi=0
.
By
Lemma

2.1
(iii)
it follows that
\log\ell_{\Psi,\mu}=\log\ell_{\Phi,\mu}
, and by (
1.6c
) it follows that
\mu_{\Psi}=\mu_{\Phi}
.

Remark 3.4

(Bounded misfits)
.

The assumption that
\Phi_{i}\in L^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
for
i=1,2
that we made in
item

(ii)
appears to be restrictive.
It is not satisfied in the case where
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for

and
\Phi_{i}
is generated by a linear forward map and Gaussian observation noise, for example.
However, similar boundedness assumptions have appeared in the theory of nonlinear Bayesian statistical inverse problems; see e.g.
[
14
, equation (1.17)]
.
The boundedness assumption can hold if the space
\Theta
of admissible parameters is itself bounded; such boundedness conditions were shown to be important in the Gaussian regression with random design setting in
[
3
]
, for example.
Another sufficient condition for the boundedness assumption to hold is that the prior
\mu
has bounded support.
We discuss this condition in
Remark

6.2
below.

The next result obtains upper and lower bounds for the total variation and misfit perturbations by using the triangle inequality.
The upper bound was stated in
[

The lower bounds are new, to the best of our knowledge.
However, for the lower bound to be nontrivial, one requires both

The lower bound in

(ii)

Proposition 3.5
.

Let

If

\displaystyle|Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}-Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}|\leq

\displaystyle d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}\vee Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}}\left(\|e^{-\Phi_{1}}-e^{-\Phi_{2}}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}+|Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}-Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}|\right),

(3.3)

If
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}

\mu
-a.s., then equality holds in the upper bound in (

Remark 3.6
.

Item

(i)
states that
|Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}-Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}|\leq\left\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\right\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}
.
In general, one cannot bound
|Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}-Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}|
from below by
\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}
.
If such a bound were possible, then it would imply that
\Phi\mapsto Z_{\Phi,\mu}
is injective, thus contradicting
Lemma

2.1
(i)
.
One situation in which such a lower bound is possible is if
\Phi_{i}\in L^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
for
i=1,2
and either
\mu(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\geq 0)=1
or
\mu(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\leq 0)=1
.
Under these hypotheses,

|Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}-Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}|=\|e^{-\Phi_{1}}-e^{-\Phi_{2}}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}\geq\left[\exp(-\|\Phi_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}})\wedge\exp(-\|\Phi_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}})\right]\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}},

where the equation and inequality follow by
Lemma

A.3
(ii)
and (
1.5b
) respectively.

Comparison of approaches

The upper bounds on
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}})
obtained by Lipschitz continuity and by the triangle inequality are

\|-\Phi_{1}-\log Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}+\Phi_{2}+\log Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}
and
\|e^{-\Phi_{1}}-e^{-\Phi_{2}}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}+|Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}-Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}|
respectively; see
Proposition

3.2
(i)
and
Proposition

3.5
(i)
.
Both upper bounds hold if
\essinf_{\mu}\Phi_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,2
.
Equality can be attained in the upper bound obtained by Lipschitz continuity if
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
is
\mu
-a.s. constant.
For the upper bound obtained by the triangle inequality, equality is attained in the more restrictive case where
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}

\mu
-a.s.

Similarly, for the lower bounds on
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{\Phi_{1}},\mu_{\Phi_{2}})
obtained by Lipschitz continuity and the triangle inequality in
Proposition

3.2
and
Proposition

3.5
respectively, equality can be attained in the lower bound in
Proposition

3.2
, but not in the lower bound in
Proposition

3.5
.
Indeed, the lower bound in

3.2
is zero if and only if
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
is
\mu
-a.s. constant, which is consistent with
Lemma

2.1
.
On the other hand, the lower bounds in
Proposition

3.5
can be zero even if
\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}
is not
\mu
-a.s. constant: for example, if
\Phi_{1}>\Phi_{2}

-a.s., then

(i)

(iii)
.

The preceding observations indicate that for bounds on the total variation metric of posteriors resulting from perturbed misfits, the triangle inequality-based approach yields bounds that are not as sharp as the bounds obtained by Lipschitz continuity.

.
Since the lower bound in

For both the upper bound and lower bound, equality holds if and only if

.

Remark 3.8
.

Item

(i)
states that
|Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}-Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}|\leq 2d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
. In general, one cannot bound
|Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}-Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}|
from below by
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
. If such a bound were possible, then it would imply that
\mu\mapsto Z_{\Phi,\mu}
is injective, which contradicts
Lemma

2.3
(i)
in the case where the domain of this map is taken to be
D(\nu)
for some common dominating measure
\nu
.

Item

(ii)
implies that if
\Phi\in L^{\infty}_{\mu_{i}}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
, then
(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}=(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}
implies
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\left|\tfrac{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}-Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}{2Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}}\right|
. The statement
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\left|\tfrac{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}-Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}{2Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}}\right|
is weaker than the statement
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
, which was shown in
Lemma

2.3
(ii)
to be equivalent to
(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}=(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}
.

It is possible to obtain bounds on
d_{\textup{TV}}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi},(\mu_{2})_{\Phi})
using the local Lipschitz continuity of the exponential function.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\Phi\in L^{0}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
and
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
,

, be such that
(\mu_{i})_{\Phi}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and

\mu_{1}

(i)

If in addition

Comparison of approaches

The bounds obtained by the Lipschitz continuity approach in

hold under the hypotheses that the Radon–Nikodym derivative

is
\mu_{1}

.
For the bounds in

Furthermore, the bounds in

We conclude this section on upper and lower bounds in the total variation metric for prior perturbations by recalling the motivations for the lower bounds given in

.
Since the lower bound in

(ii)
involves division by the evidence terms

or

approach zero from above.
This provides additional evidence for the increasing sensitivity that was deduced using upper bounds; see

Proposition

4
Bounds in the Hellinger metric

Let
\nu,\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
be such that
\mu_{i}\ll\nu
for
i=1,2
.
The Hellinger metric is defined by

d_{\textup{H}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\coloneqq\left(\int\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}}-\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{1/2}.

(4.1)

The value of
d_{\textup{H}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
does not depend on the choice of
\nu
, and
d_{\textup{H}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
as defined above takes values in
[0,\sqrt{2}]
.
The maximal value of
\sqrt{2}
is attained if and only if the densities
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
and
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
have disjoint regions of positivity, i.e.
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are singular; see e.g.
[
7
, Section B.1]
.
By the inequalities

d_{\textup{H}}^{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq 2d_{\textup{H}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),

see e.g.
[
7
, Lemma B.1]
, it follows that values of
d_{\textup{H}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
between 0 and
\sqrt{2}
do not in general imply that either
\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{2}
or
\mu_{2}\ll\mu_{1}
.
For example, if
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})<1
, then by the inequality
d_{\textup{H}}^{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
above,
d_{\textup{H}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})<1
as well.
However, by
Example

3.1
,
d_{\textup{TV}}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})<1
does not imply either that
\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{2}
or
\mu_{2}\ll\mu_{1}
.

5
Bounds in the Kullback–Leibler divergence

Let
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
,
i=1,2
.
The Kullback–Leibler divergence is defined by

d_{\textup{KL}}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{2})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\int\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1},&\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{2}\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5.1)

The Kullback–Leibler divergence does not satisfy the triangle inequality.
For this reason, we cannot combine bounds for misfit perturbations with bounds for prior perturbations to address the case where both the misfit and prior are jointly perturbed.
Instead, we directly prove bounds for the case where both the misfit and prior are jointly perturbed.
By setting
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
(respectively,
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
) in the bounds below, one obtains the bounds in the cases where only the misfit is perturbed and the prior is held fixed (resp. the prior is perturbed and the misfit is held fixed).

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and
\Phi_{i}\in L^{0}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
be such that
(\mu_{i})_{\Phi_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
for
i=1,2
.

(i)

If
\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{2}
,
\Phi_{i}\in L^{1}_{\mu_{1}}
for
i=1,2
, and
\essinf_{\mu_{1}}\Phi_{1}=0
, then the following upper bounds hold:

\displaystyle d_{\textup{KL}}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi_{1}}\|(\mu_{2})_{\Phi_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}\left\|\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{1}\right\|_{L^{1}_{\mu_{1}}}+\left|\log\frac{Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}\right|+\frac{1}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}\left(d_{\textup{KL}}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{2})+\sqrt{2d_{\textup{KL}}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{2})}\right).

If in addition
\essinf_{\mu_{2}}\Phi_{i}=0
for
i=1,2
, then

\displaystyle\left|\log\frac{Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}\right|\leq\frac{\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu_{2}}}}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{2}}\wedge Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}}+\frac{\sqrt{2d_{\textup{KL}}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{2})}}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{2}}\wedge Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}}.

(ii)

If
(\mu_{1})_{\Phi_{1}}\ll(\mu_{2})_{\Phi_{2}}
and
\Phi_{1}\in L^{\infty}_{\mu_{1}}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-\|\Phi_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu_{1}}})}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}\left|\int\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{1}~\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}+\log\frac{Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}+d_{\textup{KL}}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-\|\Phi_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu_{1}}})}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}\left(\int\left|\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{1}\right|\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}+\left|\log\frac{Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu_{2}}}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu_{1}}}\right|+\int\left|\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}}\right|\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}\right)

\displaystyle d_{\textup{KL}}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi_{1}}\|(\mu_{2})_{\Phi_{2}})+\sqrt{2d_{\textup{KL}}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi_{1}}\|(\mu_{2})_{\Phi_{2}})}.

See
Appendix

E
for the proof of
Proposition

5.1

6
Bounds in the 1-Wasserstein metric

Let
d
be a metric on
\Theta
, such that
(\Theta,d)
is a Polish space.
Given

the corresponding
p
-Wasserstein metric on
\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and Wasserstein space of order

are defined by

\displaystyle\textup{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int_{\Theta\times\Theta}d(x,y)^{p}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)\right)^{1/p},

\displaystyle|\mu|_{\mathcal{P}_{p}}^{p}\coloneqq

In inference problems, the measures used for inference may be mutually singular, for example if the measures are empirical distributions supported on finite, disjoint subsets of the parameter space.
In this case the total variation metric, Hellinger metric, or Kullback–Leibler divergence of any two such measures will assume maximal values and thus be uninformative.
The
p
-Wasserstein metrics do not exhibit this property, and are thus advantageous in such settings.
The first instances of 1-Wasserstein bounds on posteriors appear in
[
15
, Section 5]
.

Given a metric space
(X,\mathbf{d})
and a function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, define the Lipschitz constant of
f
and its supremum norm by

\|f\|_{\textup{Lip}(X,\mathbf{d})}\coloneqq\sup_{x\neq y}\tfrac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\mathbf{d}(x,y)},\quad\|f\|_{\infty,X}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|.

(6.2)

Recall that
\supp{\mu}
denotes the support of a measure
\mu
.
For an arbitrary
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\Theta)
, define the radius of the support of
\mu
,

R(\mu)\coloneqq\textup{diam}({\supp{\mu}})=\sup_{x,y\in\supp{\mu}}d(x,y),

(6.3)

so that
R(\mu)
is finite if and only if
\supp{\mu}
is bounded.

In this section, we focus on the 1-Wasserstein metric, and we shall use the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality formulas applied to
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Theta)
:

\displaystyle\textup{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu+\nu},d)}\leq 1}\left\{\int f\mathrm{d}\mu-\int f\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}

(6.4a)

\displaystyle\textup{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu+\nu},d)}\leq 1,f(x_{0})=0}\left\{\int f\mathrm{d}\mu-\int f\mathrm{d}\nu\right\},

(6.4b)

where (
6.4b
) is valid for any fixed
x_{0}
, and follows from (
6.4a
): if
f(\cdot)
satisfies
\|f\|_{\textup{Lip}}\leq 1
then
g(\cdot)\coloneqq f(\cdot)-f(x_{0})
satisfies
\|g\|_{\textup{Lip}}\leq 1
and
g(x_{0})=0
.
Some auxiliary results about Wasserstein metrics are collected in

Remark 6.2

(Priors with bounded support)
.

In the context of nonparametric Bayesian inverse problems, priors with bounded supports have been used to prove Bernstein–von Mises theorems, via a so-called ‘localisation approach’.
This approach involves restricting the prior to a bounded subset and then renormalising; see e.g.
[
12

[

The hypothesis that a prior on a possibly unbounded metric space has bounded support is not stronger than the hypothesis that a prior is supported on a bounded metric space.
The latter hypothesis was used in

Proposition 6.5
.

\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Theta)

\textup{W}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leq Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}\|g_{\textup{opt}}e^{\Phi}\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}},d)}\textup{W}_{1}((\mu_{1})_{\Phi},(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}),

where equality holds if
\|g_{\textup{opt}}e^{\Phi}\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}},d)}=0

\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and

be finite. If
\Phi_{i}\in L^{\infty}_{\mu}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu}}\left|\int f\left(e^{-\Phi_{1}}-e^{-\Phi_{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu+\int f\mathrm{d}\mu_{\Phi_{2}}(Z_{\Phi_{2},\mu}-Z_{\Phi_{1},\mu})\right|

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we have revisited the bounds and ideas relating to local Lipschitz stability results for the misfit-to-posterior and prior-to-posterior maps that were presented in
[
15
]
.
In addition, we investigated lower bounds on the metrics or divergences between the priors. The motivation for doing so is to better understand the extent to which one can consider the upper bounds to be sharp with respect to the magnitude of the perturbations, in the sense that up to prefactors, the same function of the perturbations appears in both the upper and lower bounds.
In particular, for the misfit-to-posterior map, we seek one function
\mathcal{F}_{M}
such that we may set
\mathcal{U}_{M}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{M}
and
\mathcal{L}_{M}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{M}
in (
1.3a
) and (
1.4a
).
Similarly, for the prior-to-posterior map, we seek one function
\mathcal{F}_{P}
such that we may set
\mathcal{U}_{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{P}
and
\mathcal{L}_{P}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{P}
in (
1.3b
) and (
1.4b
).

The bounds that we have obtained above show that for the misfit-to-posterior map, the goal of finding sharp upper bounds is possible when using the total variation metric and the Hellinger metric; in these cases
\mathcal{F}_{M}
is an
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
norm of the difference of the log-likelihoods respectively. We leave the problem of determining sharp upper bounds for the Kullback–Leibler divergence and 1-Wasserstein metric for future work.

For the prior-to-posterior map, we found that if the evidence is conserved, i.e. if
Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}=Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}
, then
\mathcal{F}_{P}
is given by the same metric that is used to measure the difference in the posterior, for the case of the total variation metric, the Hellinger metric, and the 1-Wasserstein metric.
We leave the problem of determining an analogous result for the Kullback–Leibler divergence for future work.

Our results complement those of
[
15
]
concerning the increasing sensitivity of posteriors to misfit or prior perturbations as posteriors become more concentrated.
In particular, while the latter show that posteriors can become more sensitive with respect to misfit or prior perturbations as posteriors become more concentrated, our results—specifically, our lower bounds—prove that posteriors in fact must become more sensitive to misfit or prior perturbations as they become more concentrated.

Lemma A.5

(Local Lipschitz continuity of unnormalised likelihoods)
.

Let

be a metric space and

and

and

, and by the definition (

) of the Lipschitz constant, we have for

(

\displaystyle 1+\|e^{-\Phi}\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}},d)}R(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}),

By combining Step 2 and 3, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\int f_{\textup{opt}}e^{-\Phi}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int f_{\textup{opt}}e^{-\Phi}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\right|\leq\left(1+\|e^{-\Phi}\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}},d)}R(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})\right)\textup{W}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}).

\displaystyle\frac{1+\|e^{-\Phi}\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}},d)}R(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}\textup{W}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{|Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}-Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}|}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}R(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}).

Switching
\mu_{1}
and

(iii)

The second inequality follows from the bound on

(i)

See

Proof of
Proposition

6.5
.

By the hypothesis that
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
for

, it follows from

that there exists a 1-Lipschitz function

Now

\displaystyle\left|\int g_{\textup{opt}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\right|=\frac{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}\left|\int g_{\textup{opt}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}\left|\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\frac{e^{-\Phi}}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\frac{e^{-\Phi}}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}\left|\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\frac{e^{-\Phi}}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\frac{e^{-\Phi}}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}+\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\frac{e^{-\Phi}}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\frac{e^{-\Phi}}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}\left|\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}+\int g_{\textup{opt}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}\left(\frac{1}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}}-\frac{1}{Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}}\right)\right|.

This proves the first statement of the proposition.

For the second statement of the proposition, suppose that
Z_{\Phi,\mu_{2}}=Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}
and that

is constant, so that its Lipschitz constant vanishes. Then

\displaystyle\textup{W}_{1}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=

\displaystyle Z_{\Phi,\mu_{1}}\left|\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}\right|

\displaystyle\|g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}},d)}\frac{\left|\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1})_{\Phi}-\int g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\mathrm{d}(\mu_{2})_{\Phi}\right|}{\|g_{\textup{opt}}~e^{\Phi}\|_{\textup{Lip}(\supp{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}},d)}}

where the last equation follows from the duality formula (

Appendix G

We recall the following definition.

Definition G.1

(

)
.

Let
(X,\mathbf{d})
be a Polish space and
p\in[1,\infty)

\int d(x_{0},\cdot)^{p}~\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}\to\int d(x_{0},\cdot)^{p}~\mathrm{d}\nu

\varphi
with

,

By

\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
