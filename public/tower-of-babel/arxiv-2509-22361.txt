Quantum sensing of a quantum field
Ricard Ravell Rodríguez, Martí Perarnau-Llobet, Pavel Sekatski

Abstract
Estimating a classical parameter encoded in the Hamiltonian of a quantum probe is a fundamental and well-understood task in quantum metrology. A textbook example is the estimation of a classical field’s amplitude using a two-level probe, as described by the semi-classical Rabi model. In this work, we explore the fully quantum analogue, where the amplitude of a coherent quantized field is estimated by letting it interact with a two-level atom. For both metrological scenarios, we focus on the quantum Fisher information (QFI) of the reduced state of the atomic probe. In the semi-classical Rabi model, the QFI is independent of the field amplitude and grows quadratically with the interaction time τ \tau . In contrast, when the atom interacts with a single coherent mode of the field, the QFI is bounded by 4, a constant dictated by the non-orthogonality of coherent states. We find that this bound can only be approached in the vacuum limit. In the limit of large amplitude α \alpha , the QFI is found to attain its maximal value 1.47 1.47 at τ = O ⁡ ( 1 ) \tau=O(1) and τ = O ⁡ ( α 2 ) \tau=O(\alpha^{2}) , and also shows periodic revivals at much later times. When the atom interacts with a sequence of coherent states, the QFI can increase with time but is bounded to scale linearly due to the production of entanglement between the atom and the radiation (back-action), except in the limit where the number of modes and their total energy diverge. Finally, in the continuous limit, where the atom interacts with many weak coherent states, this back-action can be simply interpreted as spontaneous emission, giving rise to the optimal interaction time and QFI rate.



I
introduction

Quantum metrology is a field of research where the theoretically promised quantum advantage
Caves 1981
;
Wineland et al. 1992
;
Holland and Burnett 1993
;
Giovannetti et al. 2004
;
Paris 2009
is practically explored on a wide range of platforms: gravitational wave detection
Aasi et al. 2013
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2013
, magnetometry
Wolfgramm et al. 2010
;
Li et al. 2018
, quantum plasmonic sensing
Dowran et al. 2018
, or lossy phase shift estimation
Kacprowicz et al. 2010
, to name a few. The textbook case study in quantum metrology is a two-level quantum probe interacting with a
classical
field of unknown amplitude – a scalar parameter in the Hamiltonian.

We will specifically focus on the resonant (semi-classical) Rabi model
Rabi 1937
, which describes the interaction of a two-level atom with a resonant harmonic electric field. In the interaction picture, the Hamiltonian of the atom reads

H=Eg\,\mathrm{i}\big(\outerproduct{e}{g}-\outerproduct{g}{e}\big)

(1)

where
E
is the field amplitude,
g
the coupling constant, and we chose the field quadrature such that the propagator is real. In this model, the evolution of the atom is simple – its state undergoes the Rabi flops governed by the unitary propagator

V_{\tau}=e^{-\mathrm{i}tH}=\exp( E \tau\big(\ketbra{e}{g}-\ketbra{g}{e}\big))

(2)

where
\tau=tg
is the interaction parameter that we simply refer to as the interaction time (here and below, we assume that the interaction time is perfectly known). After a certain time
\tau
, one can measure the probe in order to estimate the field amplitude
E
. The precision of the estimation is often quantified by the quantum Fisher information (QFI)
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Paris 2009
of the evolved state
\rho_{\tau|E}=\outerproduct{\Psi_{\tau|E}}{\Psi_{\tau|E}}
with
\ket{\Psi_{\tau|E}}=V_{\tau}\ket{\Psi_0}
, see next section. In the case of a unitary evolution, the QFI takes a very simple form, and for
\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{g}
or
\ket{e}
, one finds

{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\tau|E})=4\tau^{2}=4g^{2}t^{2}.

(3)

Importantly, we see that
(i)
it scales quadratically with time and
(ii)
it is independent of the field amplitude
E
.

The goal of this paper is to compare this simple analysis and its conclusions with a model where the atom interacts with a coherent quantized field of unknown amplitude. To do so, we first focus on the resonant Jaynes-Cummings model
Jaynes and Cummings 1963
;
Greentree et al. 2013
where the atom interacts with a single mode of the electromagnetic field prepared in a coherent state. In a second step, we consider a collision model
Ciccarello et al. 2022
built upon the Jaynes-Cummings interaction with a sequence of coherent modes, and its continuous limit. In both cases, we derive upper bounds on the QFI of the probe and processes approaching them. The article is structured as follows: in Section
II
we introduce the QFI as a benchmark for quantum sensing. Next, in Section
III
, we study the interaction of the atom with a single mode of the field. Then in Section
IV
, we discuss the interactions with a sequence of field modes and the continuous limit. Finally, we present our conclusions.

II
The quantum Fisher information, a benchmark for sensing

Consider the task of estimating a parameter
\theta
by sampling a discrete random variable
X_{\theta}
, whose distribution
\bm{p}_{\theta}
depends on the parameter. It can be analyzed by introducing an estimator
\hat{\theta}
– another random variable that is a function of
X_{\theta}
. It is natural to only consider estimators that are locally unbiased
Berger and Casella 2001
(around
\theta_{0}
), i.e., for which the expected value equals the true value

\mathds{E}\left[\hat{\theta}(X_{\theta})\right]=\theta

for a small interval of parameter values around
\theta_{0}
. Furthermore, for an unbiased estimator, a natural quantifier of its precision is the associated mean-squared error

{\rm MSE}(\hat{\theta})=\mathds{E}\left[\left(\hat{\theta}(X_{\theta})-\theta\right)^{2}\right].

The renowned Cramér-Rao bound
Rao 1992
;
Cramér 1999
relates the MSE of an unbounded estimator

{\rm MSE}(\hat{\theta})\geq\frac{1}{{\rm FI}(X_{\theta})}

(4)

to the Fisher Information (FI) of the random variable
X_{\theta}
. The latter is best understood as the susceptibility of
X_{\theta}
with respect to several statistical distances, e.g. Kullback–Leibler divergence or Hellinger distance. In particular, the Fisher information is induced by the Euclidean norm on the normalized vector of probability “amplitudes”
\sqrt{\bm{p}_{\theta}}
via

\displaystyle{\rm FI}(X_{\theta})

\displaystyle=4\left(\frac{\|\sqrt{\bm{p}_{\theta+\differential\theta}}-\sqrt{\bm{p}_{\theta}}\|}{\differential\theta}\right)^{2}

(5)

\displaystyle=8\,\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{\bm{p}_{\theta+\differential\theta}}\sqrt{\bm{p}_{\theta}}}{\differential\theta^{2}}\right).

(6)

In the quantum estimation task we consider, the parameter is encoded into a state
\rho_{\theta}
of the probe system. Thus, to define an estimator, one must first choose the measurement (POVM
\{E_{x}\}
) to obtain the random variable
X_{\theta}
distributed accordingly to the Born rule
p_{x|\theta}=\tr E_{x}\rho_{\theta}
. Nevertheless, one can show that optimizing over this measurement gives rise to the so-called Quantum Fisher Information (QFI)
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Paris 2009

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\max_{\{E_{x}\}}\,{\rm FI}(X_{\theta})

(7)

\displaystyle=8\left(\frac{1-\|\sqrt{\rho_{\theta+\differential\theta}}\sqrt{\rho_{\theta}}\|_{1}}{\differential\theta^{2}}\right)

(8)

with trace norm
\|A\|_{1}=\tr\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}
. Here, one recognizes the Uhlmann’s fidelity
F(\rho,\sigma)=||\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}||_{1}
and the Bures distance
d_{B}(\rho,\sigma)=\sqrt{2(1-||\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}||_{1})}
, giving a geometric interpretation for the QFI. Furthermore, the QFI of a state can be computed
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Paris 2009
via

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\Tr(L^2 \rho_\theta)\quad\text{for}

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{L\rho_{\theta}+\rho_{\theta}L}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\differential\rho_{\theta}}{\differential\theta},

(10)

where
L
is the so-called symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD) defined by Eq. (
10
). Note that some care has to be taken with this expression at parameter values where
\rho_{\theta}
changes its rank and
L
can become unbounded
Ye and Lu 2022
.

By definition, the QFI bounds the mean-squared errors of all unbiased estimators obtained by measuring
\rho_{\theta}
, which is known as the quantum Crámer-Rao bound
{\rm MSE}(\hat{\theta})\geq\frac{1}{{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\theta})}
. For this reason, the QFI of the probe’s state
\rho_{\theta}
is commonly used as a figure of merit in quantum sensing, which is also the approach we take.

II.1
Quantum Fisher Information of a two-level probe

In this article, we focus on two-level probes, whose state can be expressed as
\rho_{\theta}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}+\bm{r}_{\theta}\cdot\tilde{\bm{\sigma}})
, where
\tilde{\bm{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
is the vector of the three Pauli matrices. The QFI can easily be obtained (see Appendix (
A
) or
Zhong et al. 2013
for an equivalent formula) as

{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\theta})=\frac{\|\dot{\bm{r}}_{\theta}\|^{2}-\|\dot{\bm{r}}_{\theta}\crossproduct\bm{r}_{\theta}\|^{2}}{1-\|\bm{r}_{\theta}\|^{2}}=\|\dot{\bm{r}}^{\perp}_{\theta}\|^{2}+\frac{\|\dot{\bm{r}}^{=}_{\theta}\|^{2}}{1-\|\bm{r}_{\theta}\|^{2}}

(11)

where
\|\bullet\|
is the Euclidean norm,
\dot{\bm{r}}_{\theta}=\frac{\differential}{\differential\theta}\bm{r}_{\theta}
, and
\perp(=)
denotes its orthogonal (parallel) component to
\bm{r}_{\theta}
. Note that the expressions on the right-hand side appear to diverge in the limit where
\rho_{\theta}
is pure. We distinguish two cases.

First, in the limit where
\|\bm{r}_{\theta}\|\to 1
while
\|\dot{\bm{r}}^{=}_{\theta}\|
does not vanish, the QFI is indeed unbounded. In fact, this is also the case for the FI of a random variable with distribution
\bm{p}_{\theta}=\binom{1-\theta}{\theta}
at
\theta\to 0
. This case can be safely ignored for our study as nonphysical.

In contrast, when both
\|\bm{r}_{\theta}\|\to 1
and
\|\dot{\bm{r}}^{=}_{\theta}\|\to 0
, the QFI remains finite. It can be computed by either taking the limit
Šafránek 2017
of the expression in Eq. (
11
), or by directly applying Eq. (
8
) to obtain

{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\theta})=\|\ddot{\bm{r}}^{=}_{\theta}\|

(12)

with
\ddot{\bm{r}}_{\theta}=\frac{\differential^{2}}{\differential\theta^{2}}{\bm{r}}_{\theta}
.

III
Interaction of an atom with a single mode

In this section, we study the problem of estimating the amplitude
\alpha
of a single mode of the electromagnetic field, prepared in some coherent state
\ket{\alpha}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}e^{-\nicefrac{{\alpha^{2}}}{{2}}}\ket{n}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, via a two-level atom probe coupled to the field with the resonant Jaynes-Cummings interaction. Contrary to the resonant Rabi model with a classical field (Eq.
1
), where the QFI is independent of the field amplitude and grows quadratically with the interaction time (Eq.
3
), we will see in this section that in the Jaynes-Cummings model the QFI has a complicated dependence on time and
\alpha
and is always bounded by a constant.

III.1
The QFI of coherent states themselves

The first radical contrast with the estimation of a classical field in the Rabi model comes from the uncertainty principle. Indeed, the task of estimating
\alpha
is intrinsically limited by the fact that coherent states are not orthogonal
|\innerproduct{\alpha}{\beta}|=\exp(-\frac{|\alpha-\beta|^2}{2})
. In particular, by Eq. (
8
) the QFI of the coherent states (with respect to the amplitude parameter
\alpha
) is given by

{\rm QFI}\Big(\outerproduct{\alpha}{\alpha}\Big)=8\frac{1-|\innerproduct{\alpha}{\alpha+\dd\alpha}|}{\differential\alpha^{2}}=4.

(13)

Now, let
\rho_{\tau|\alpha}
be the state of the two-level atom after an interaction with the coherent field mode
\ket{\alpha}
. Since QFI is monotonic (can not increase by post-processing), Eq. (
13
) immediately implies

{\rm QFI}\Big(\rho_{\tau|\alpha}\Big)\leq 4,

(14)

showing that the QFI of the probe is bounded for all amplitudes
\alpha
of the field, and all interaction times
\tau
.

The interaction between the field and the atom is mapping the information encoded in the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space of the field mode into the qubit carried by the atom. In particular, the bound (
14
) can always be saturated by swapping the two-dimensional subspace spanned by
\ket{\alpha}
and
\ket{\dot\alpha}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}(n-\alpha^{2})}{\alpha\sqrt{n!}}e^{-\nicefrac{{\alpha^{2}}}{{2}}}\ket{n}
, with
\innerproduct{\dot\alpha}{\dot\alpha}=1
and
\innerproduct{\alpha}{\dot\alpha}=0
, into the probe; e.g. with the SWAP unitary

V=\mathds{1}+\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{e,\alpha}+\outerproduct{e, \alpha}{g,\dot\alpha}-\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{g,\dot\alpha}-\outerproduct{e, \alpha}{e, \alpha}.

(15)

As we show below, in the vacuum limit
\alpha\ll 1
the Jaynes-Cummings interaction attains the theoretical upper-bound by realizing
V
in Eq. (
15
). Nevertheless, this interaction is fine-tuned to work around a specific value of
\alpha
. In general, there exists no
fixed
atom-field interaction saturating
{\rm QFI(\rho_{\tau|\alpha})}=4

for all

\alpha

Rodríguez et al. 2025
. Quite remarkably, as we also show, for
\tau=1
the Jaynes-Cummings interaction gives a descent
{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\tau|\alpha})=\nicefrac{{4}}{{e}}\approx 1.47
for all
\alpha\gg 1
(see Fig.
3
). It is an interesting open question to identify the interaction
V
that maximizes the worst-case value
\min_{\alpha}{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\tau|\alpha})
. We conjecture that the optimal value is
\nicefrac{{4}}{{e}}
, or very close to it, as suggested by another natural interaction model discussed in Sec.
III.6
.

III.2
The resonant Jaynes-Cummings model

Next, we consider the resonant Jaynes-Cummings model where the two-level atom interacts with a single mode of the electromagnetic field, with associated creation and annihilation operators
a^{\dagger},a

Jaynes and Cummings 1963
;
Gerry and Knight 2023
. In the interaction picture, the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian is given by

H=\mathrm{i}g\left(\sigma_{+}a-\sigma_{-}a^{\dagger}\right).

(16)

Assuming that the field and the atom are initially pure states
\ket{\mathfrak{F}}
and
\ket{\Psi_0}
, their joint state after interaction time
\tau=tg
becomes

U_{\tau}\ket{\Psi_0}\ket{\mathfrak{F}}\quad\text{for }\quad U_{\tau}=\exp(\tau\left( \sigma_+ a - \sigma_- a^\dag\right)).

(17)

It is a standard exercise in quantum optics to see that in the computational basis of the atom
\{\ket{g},\ket{e}\}
the propagator in Eq. (
17
) can be expressed as (see e.g.
Sekatski et al. 2010
for an explicit calculation)

\displaystyle U_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n})&-\frac{\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n })}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}a^{\dagger}\\
\frac{\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1})}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}a&\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1})\end{array}\right),

where
\hat{n}=a^{\dagger}a
is the photon number operator. The reduced dynamics of the atom is then given by the completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) map
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\mathfrak{F}}[\bullet]=\tr_{\rm field}U_{\tau}\left(\bullet\otimes\outerproduct{\mathfrak{F}}{\mathfrak{F}}\right)U_{\tau}^{\dagger}
. Straightforward algebra allows one to write

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\mathfrak{F}}[\bullet]=\sum_{i,j=0}^{3}G^{\mathfrak{F}}_{ij}\,L_{i}\bullet L_{j}^{\dagger},

(20)

where
\{L_{0},L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\}=\{\outerproduct{g}{g},\outerproduct{e}{e},\sigma_{-},\sigma_{+}\}
and
G^{\mathfrak{F}}_{ij}=\innerproduct{\mathfrak{F}_j}{\mathfrak{F}_i}
, termed the channel-matrix, is the Gram matrix of the unnormalized states

\begin{split}\ket{\mathfrak{F}_0}&=\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n })\ket{\mathfrak{F}},\quad\qquad\ket{\mathfrak{F}_2}=-\frac{\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n })}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}a^{\dagger}\ket{\mathfrak{F}},\\
\ket{\mathfrak{F}_1}&=\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1 })\ket{\mathfrak{F}},\quad\ket{\mathfrak{F}_3}=\frac{\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1})}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}a\ket{\mathfrak{F}}.\end{split}

By construction the Gram matrix is Hermitian
G^{\mathfrak{F}}_{ji}=G^{\mathfrak{F}*}_{ij}
. In addition, the fact that the map is trace preserving implies the equality constraints
G_{00}^{\mathfrak{F}}+G_{33}^{\mathfrak{F}}=G_{11}^{\mathfrak{F}}+G_{22}^{\mathfrak{F}}=1
and
G_{02}^{\mathfrak{F}}+G_{31}^{\mathfrak{F}}=0
. The Kraus operators for the channel
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\mathfrak{F}}
can be obtained by diagonalizing the matrix
G^{\mathfrak{F}}
, however a more intuitive representation of the dynamics of the two-level atom is given by the induced affine transformation of the Bloch vector
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\mathfrak{F}}[\bm{\sigma}]
with
\bm{\sigma}=(\mathds{1},\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
.

We now let the field be in a coherent state
\ket{\mathfrak{F}}=\ket{\alpha}
, and investigate how well its amplitude
\alpha
can be estimated from the atomic probe. To do so, we focus on the QFI of the reduced state of the atom

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau|\alpha}=\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}\left[\rho_{0}\right].

(21)

In this case, using the form of the coherent state, the states
\ket{\mathfrak{F}_i}=\ket{\alpha_i}
in Eq. (
III.2
) can be expressed as
\ket{\alpha_i}=f_{i}(\hat{n})\ket{\alpha}
(see App.
C
), with

\begin{split}f_{0}(\hat{n})&=\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n }),\quad\qquad f_{2}(\hat{n})=-\frac{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}{\alpha}\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n }),\\
f_{1}(\hat{n})&=\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1 }),\quad f_{3}(\hat{n})=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1}).\end{split}

(22)

This allows us to compute the elements of the Gram matrix

G^{\alpha}_{ij}=\bra{\alpha}f_{j}(\hat{n})f_{i}(\hat{n})\ket{\alpha}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\rm P}(n|\alpha)f_{i}(n)f_{j}(n)

as expected values of simple functions of a Poisson random variable

{\rm P}(n|\alpha)=|\innerproduct{n}{\alpha}|^{2}=\frac{\alpha^{2n}}{n!}e^{-\alpha^{2}}
. In turn, using
\dot{\rm P}(n|\alpha)=2\frac{n-\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}{\rm P}(n|\alpha)
, we can also express the derivatives
\dot{G}_{ij}^{\alpha}=\frac{\differential}{\differential\alpha}G_{ij}^{\alpha}
in the same form.

For concreteness, let us now focus on the case where the atomic probe is initialized in the ground or excited state. After time
\tau
its state remains real

\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathds{1}+x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}\sigma_{x}+z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}\sigma_{z}\right).

(23)

The QFI of the final state can be computed with the help of the Eq. (
11
), which simplifies to

{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}+\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}-(\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}z_{\tau|\alpha}-\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}x_{\tau|\alpha})^{2}}{1-x_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}-z_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}}.

(24)

The quantities
x_{\alpha}
with
z_{\alpha}
and their derivatives
\dot{x}_{\alpha}
with
\dot{z}_{\alpha}
are linear combinations of
G_{ij}^{\alpha}
and
\dot{G}_{ij}^{\alpha}
and can thus be expressed as expected values of certain functions
\mathds{E}[g(\hat{n})]
of the photon number
\hat{n}
– a Poisson random variable. In particular, for the ground state, we find

\displaystyle x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\,\mathds{E}\left[{\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n})\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1})\,}\right],

(25)

\displaystyle z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\,\mathds{E}\left[2\cos^{2}(\tau\sqrt{\hat{n}})\right]-1,

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\,\,\mathds{E}\left[{\frac{4\hat{n}-4\alpha^{2}+2}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}}\,\cos(\tau\sqrt{\hat n})\sin(\tau\sqrt{\hat n+1})\right],

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\,\mathds{E}\left[{\frac{4\hat{n}-4\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}\cos^{2}(\tau\sqrt{\hat{n}})}\right],

which are derived in App.
C
together with similar expressions for the excited initial state.

Although in general, we cannot compute these expected values analytically (the limits of small and big
\alpha
are discussed in the following sections), these are well-behaved sums that can be well approximated numerically, e.g., using a cutoff on the photon number. The same holds for the QFI, provided that the state is not pure, so that the denominator in
Eq. (
24
) remains bounded from zero. This can be verified by computing the purity of the state

\mathcal{P}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}]:=2\tr\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}-1=x_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}+z_{\tau|\alpha}^{2},

(26)

where the last equality assumes a real qubit state. Since the global dynamics is unitary, and
1-\mathcal{P}
is a Schur-convex function of the eigenvalues of
\rho_{\tau|\alpha}
, it is also a measure of atom-field entanglement.

III.4
The vacuum limit
\alpha\ll 1

We start by examining the regime of a very weak field
\alpha\ll 1
. Recall that at
\alpha=0
one recovers the famous vacuum Rabi oscillations, with the reduced dynamics of the atom given by the spontaneous-decay channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\tau|0}[\bullet]

\displaystyle=K_{0}\bullet K_{0}^{\dagger}+K_{1}\bullet K_{1}^{\dagger}\quad\text{with}

(27)

\displaystyle K_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\sin(\tau)\\
0&0\end{array}\right),\quad K_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&\cos(\tau)\end{array}\right).

To compute the QFI of the atomic state, we need the expression of the channel
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}
and its derivative up to the second order in
\alpha
. It can be computed using
\ket{\alpha}\propto\ket{0}+\alpha\ket{1}+\nicefrac{{\alpha^{2}}}{{2}}\ket{2}
(see Appendix
B
). In particular, for the atom initially prepared in the ground or excited state, we find

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1-{\rm s}^{2}_{\tau}\alpha^{2}&\alpha\,{\rm s}_{\tau}\\
\alpha\,{\rm s}_{\tau}&\alpha^{2}{\rm s}^{2}_{\tau}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(e)}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm s}^{2}_{\tau}+\alpha^{2}\left({\rm s}_{\sqrt{2}\tau}^{2}-{\rm s}^{2}_{\tau}\right)&-\alpha\,{\rm s}_{\tau}{\rm c}_{\sqrt{2}\tau}\\
-\alpha\,{\rm s}_{\tau}{\rm c}_{\sqrt{2}\tau}&{\rm c}_{\tau}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\left({\rm c}_{\sqrt{2}\tau}^{2}-\rm{c}_{\tau}^{2}\right)\\
\end{array}\right),

where
{\rm s}_{\gamma}=\sin(\gamma)
,
{\rm c}_{\gamma}=\cos(\gamma)
. Using Eqs. (
25
) and (
24
), it is also direct to compute the QFI

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}]

\displaystyle=4\sin^{2}(\tau)+O(\alpha^{2}),

(36)

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(e)}]

\displaystyle=4\sin^{2}(\tau)\cos^{2}\left(\sqrt{2}\tau\right)+O(\alpha^{2}).

(37)

Remarkably, in both cases we see that the QFI periodically approaches the maximum value of
4
at some well-chosen times
\tau
, reminiscent of Rabi oscillations. In fact, this is the case for all real initial states
\cos(\theta)\ket{g}+\sin(\theta)\ket{e}
of the atom.

III.5
The large
\alpha\gg 1
limit

A very crude “classical field approximation” that one can sometimes encounter consists of saying that for large enough
\alpha
one has
a^{\dagger}\ket{\alpha}\approx\alpha\ket{\alpha}
in addition to
a\ket{\alpha}=\alpha\ket{\alpha}
. Replacing the creation
a^{\dagger}
and annihilation
a
operators with the scalar
\alpha
in the Hamiltonian gives rise to the approximation
H\approx g\,\alpha\,\mathrm{i}\big(\outerproduct{e}{g}-\outerproduct{g}{e}\big)
reproducing the semi-classical Rabi model in Eq. (
1
). As we will see, this approximation is only accurate in the limit
\tau\ll 1
. Instead, we now derive the exact reduced evolution of the atom.

The Jaynes-Cummings interaction with a coherent state in the large
\alpha
limit has been extensively studied in the literature, see e.g. Refs.
Cummings 1965
;
Eberly et al. 1980
;
Rempe et al. 1987
;
Phoenix and Knight 1988
;
Gea-Banacloche 1990
;
Gea-Banacloche 1991
;
Bužek et al. 1992
;
Shore and Knight 1993
;
Fleischhauer and Schleich 1993
;
Azuma 2010
;
Berman and Ooi 2014
;
Zhang et al. 2015
;
Pavlik 2023
. Our goal here is modest, as we are only interested in the reduced dynamics of the atom. Despite the large body of literature, we could not find a reference describing the full reduced dynamics of the atom at all times. Hence, we begin this section by deriving such an expression. We will then interpret the obtained result and discuss the atomic QFI in different time regimes.

Result
.

In the large
\alpha\gg 1
limit, the atomic dynamics induced by the resonant Jaynes-Cummings interaction with a coherent field is at all times given by

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}=\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}}+\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\nu},\Phi_{\nu}}^{\nu}+\mathcal{D}_{P_{\nu},\Omega_{\nu}}^{\nu}\right),

(38)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}}[\bm{\sigma}]=(\mathds{1},0,0,0)
is the totally depolarizing channel, the linear maps are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{r,\varphi}^{\nu}[\bm{\sigma}]

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
q\big((-1)^{\nu}\cos(\varphi)\sigma_{x}-\sin(\varphi)\sigma_{z}\big)\\
0\\
q\big((-1)^{\nu}\sin(\varphi)\sigma_{x}+\cos(\varphi)\sigma_{z}\big)\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\nu}_{p,\omega}[\bm{\sigma}]

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}p(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\omega)\sigma_{x}\\
0\\
p(-1)^{\nu}\cos(\omega)\sigma_{y}\\
0\end{array}\right),

and the functions
Q_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha),\Phi_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha),P_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)
and
\Omega_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)
are
given in Eqs. (
52
-
55
) for
\nu=0
and Eqs. (
57
-
60
) for
\nu\geq 1
.

Proof.

To understand the atomic dynamics in the large amplitude limit, it is insightful to observe that the entries of the channel-matrix

G^{\alpha}_{ij}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\rm P}(n|\alpha)f_{i}(n)f_{j}(n),

(47)

with
f_{i}(n)
in Eq. (
22
), result from the interference of terms oscillating on two separate time scales – with the rapid
\widetilde{S}(n)\in\{2\tau\sqrt{n},\tau(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{n+1}),2\tau\sqrt{n+1}\}
and the slow
\bar{S}(n)=\tau(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n+1})
phases. Using
\frac{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}{\alpha},\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}=1+O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})
we can thus decompose the channel-matrix as

G=\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}+\bar{\mathcal{G}}+O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})

(48)

where each term
\mathcal{G}
is a linear combination of terms
\mathds{E}[e^{\mathrm{i}S(n)}]:=\sum_{n}P(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S(n)}
(and their Hermitian conjugates) for the rapid and slow phases, respectively. By linearity, the same holds for the channel, which in the large
\alpha
limit reads

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}=\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}+\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}.

(49)

Here,
\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}}[\bm{\sigma}]=(\mathds{1},0,0,0)
is the totally depolarizing channel coming from
G=\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}
, it maps any input state to the maximally mixed one
\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}}[\rho_{0}]=\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}
. The linear maps
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}
and
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}
, coming from rapid and slow phase terms, describe very different dynamics and manifest at different time scales, as we now show. All the technical details underlying the following discussion can be found in Appendix
D
.

For relatively short times, the phases can be linearized. Indeed in the large
\alpha
limit the Poisson distribution concentrates in a width of
O(\alpha)
, so the rescaled random variable satisfies
\hat{\delta}=\frac{\hat{n}-\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}=O(1)
, implying

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}(n)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\tau+\tau\,\delta+O(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha}}),

(50)

\displaystyle\bar{S}(n)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}+\frac{\tau}{4\alpha^{2}}\delta+O(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha^{3}}}).

(51)

Using the moment generating function of the Poissonian
\mathds{E}[e^{\mathrm{i}\mu n}]=\exp(-\alpha^2+\alpha^2 e^{i \mu})
we directly obtain the expressions of the maps

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{Q_{0},\Phi_{0}}^{0},

\displaystyle\quad Q_{0}(\tau|\alpha)

\displaystyle:=e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}

(52)

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(\tau|\alpha)

\displaystyle:=2\alpha\,\tau

(53)

valid in the limit
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha}}\ll 1
, and

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{0}_{P_{0},\Omega_{0}}

\displaystyle\quad P_{0}(\tau|\alpha)

\displaystyle:=e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}

(54)

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}(\tau|\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{2\alpha}}

(55)

valid for
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha^{3}}}\ll 1
, respectively.

In contrast, for longer times
\tau\geq O(\alpha)
, resp.
\tau\geq O(\alpha^{3})
, when these approximations break down, the phases
\widetilde{S}(n)
, resp.
\bar{S}(n)
, oscillate rapidly as compared to the envelope
P(n|\alpha)
. Hence, following
Fleischhauer and Schleich 1993
, we can compute
\mathds{E}[e^{\mathrm{i}S(n)}]
in two steps. First, using the Poisson summation formula, we replace the discrete sum with a series of integrals

\mathds{E}[e^{\mathrm{i}S(n)}]=\sum_{\nu=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\differential n\,{\rm P}(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S(n)}e^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi\nu n},

(56)

where we neglected the boundary term
\frac{1}{2}{\rm P}(0|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S(0)}
.
Second, we use the stationary phase approximation to evaluate the integrals. As a result, we find that the phases only interfere constructively around well-separated times: respectively
\tau\approx 2\pi\alpha\nu
and
\tau\approx 8\pi\nu\alpha^{3}
with positive integer
\nu\geq 1
. This leads to sharp revivals of the short-time dynamics, with

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}=\mathcal{R}_{Q_{\nu},\Phi_{\nu}}^{\nu},

\displaystyle\quad Q_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\frac{e^{-\left(\frac{\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu}\right)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}

(57)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\nu}(\tau)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2\pi\nu}-\frac{\pi}{4}

(58)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}=\mathcal{D}^{\nu}_{P_{\nu},\Omega_{\nu}},

\displaystyle\quad P_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\pi\nu}}e^{-\frac{\left(\tau^{1/3}-2(\pi\nu)^{1/3}\alpha\right)^{2}}{2(\pi\nu)^{2/3}}}

(59)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\nu}(\tau)

\displaystyle:=\frac{3(\pi\nu)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\tau^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}{2}+\frac{\pi}{4}.

(60)

As in the large
\alpha
limit the linear phase regime and the subsequent revivals are well separated in time, we can at all times write
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{R}_{R_{\nu},\Phi_{\nu}}^{\nu}
and
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}_{P_{\nu},\Omega_{\nu}}^{\nu}
which completes the derivation of the result.
∎

Let us now interpret the result in different time regimes and investigate the atomic QFI.

III.5.1
Short times
\tau\ll\alpha

In the short time limit
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha}}\to 0
, which was also analyzed in
Gea-Banacloche 1991
, we find the non vanishing contributions to be
Q_{0}=\exp(-\nicefrac{\tau^2}{2})
and
\Phi_{0}=2\alpha\tau
, implying

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}\\
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\big(\cos(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{x}-\sin(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{z}\big)\\
\sigma_{y}\\
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\big(\sin(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{x}+\cos(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{z}\big)\end{array}\right).

(61)

This channel describes a rotation around the
y
-axis by the angle
2\alpha\tau
(like in the semi-classical Rabi model) combined with the dephasing in the
y
-basis with parameter
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}
(due to the atom-field entanglement). Notice that the channel is not fully entanglement-breaking as long as the dephasing is not complete,
r>0
.

For the ground or excite initial state, one finds
x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=\pm e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\sin(2\alpha\tau)
and
z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=\pm e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\cos(2\alpha\tau)
, which makes it easy
to compute the QFI via Eq. (
24
)

{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}]=4\,\tau^{2}e^{-\tau^{2}}\leq 1.47.

(62)

The upper-bound is saturated at time
\tau=1
independent of
\alpha
, and yields a QFI which is about three times lower than the theoretical upper bound
{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau=1|\alpha}]\leq 4
. This shows that for a relatively short interaction time
\tau\approx 1
, a constant nonzero QFI is encoded in the atomic state for
all
values of
\alpha
. This is consistent with the numerical result in Figs.
1
and
2
, showing damped oscillation of the atomic population and coherence and a finite QFI which quickly decays. In Fig.
3
, we plot the QFI for
\tau=1
, ground and excited initial states, and different values of
0\leq\alpha\leq 40
.

Figure 3:
The
{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau=1|\alpha}^{(g/e)}]
of the ground (full line) and excited (dashed line) initial state after an interaction with the coherent mode of duration

To conclude the discussion of the fixed time limit, it is worth mentioning the “textbook case”, where the effective interaction time
\tau=\frac{\theta}{2\alpha}
decreases with
\alpha\to\infty
. The reduced dynamics of the atom becomes
\mathcal{E}_{\tau=\frac{\theta}{2\alpha}|\alpha}[\bullet]=e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_{y}}\bullet e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_{y}}

and describes a unitary Rabi-rotation by the desired angle. However, this regime is very suboptimal for the purpose of field amplitude estimation, with the QFI vanishing together with the interaction time.

III.5.2
Long times
1\ll\tau\ll\alpha^{2}

When time increases
\tau\gg 1
but remains relatively short
\tau\ll\alpha^{2}
, we find that the non-vanishing contributions are

Q_{\nu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-\left(\frac{\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu}\right)^{2}},\quad\Phi_{\nu}=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2\pi\nu}-\frac{\pi}{4},

(63)

with
\nu\geq 1
, and

P_{0}=1
with
\Omega_{0}=\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}
. As
Q_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)
decays exponentially with
(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\pi\nu}}-\alpha)^{2}
, the revivals are well separated in time, and the atomic evolution is given by

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}+\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\sigma_{x}\\
Q_{\nu}\big((-1)^{\nu}\cos(\Phi_{\nu})\,\sigma_{x}-\sin(\Phi_{\nu})\,\sigma_{z}\big)\\
\cos(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\sigma_{y}\\
Q_{\nu}\big((-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Phi_{\nu})\sigma_{x}+\cos(\Phi_{\nu})\sigma_{z}\big)\end{array}\right)

(64)

where
\nu
is to be chosen as the closest revival to
\tau
, i.e., minimizing
(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\pi\nu}}-\alpha)^{2}
.
For the ground or excited initial condition, this gives

\displaystyle x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\pm\,Q_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,(-1)^{\nu}\sin( \frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4}),

(65)

\displaystyle z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\pm\,Q_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,\cos( \frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4} ).

(66)

This time regime can be further divided into two cases.

At revivals
|\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu|=O(1)
.
— We find
\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})=0
,
\cos(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})=(-1)^{\nu}
in Eq. (
64
), hence the short-time dynamics is recovered up to an eventual phase.
Such periodic revivals of rapidly changing atomic population
z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}
have been recognized in the literature

;
Phoenix and Knight 1988
;
Gea-Banacloche 1990
;
Bužek et al. 1992
;
Shore and Knight 1993

Fleischhauer and Schleich 1993

Rempe et al. 1987

and
\tau=4\pi\alpha

\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\nu}}}
, at longer times revivals disappear as
(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha}})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
. This is also the timescale at which the atomic population is scrambled
z_{\tau|\alpha}\to 0
and the atomic dynamics becomes entanglement breaking, albeit for generic times (outside of revivals) this happens exponentially fast with
\tau^{2}
, as given by Eq. (
61
). Using Eq. (

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{8R_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)}{\pi\nu\,e^{2R_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)}-1}\approx\frac{0.47}{\nu}

(67)

for
\tau\approx 2\pi\alpha\nu\pm\pi\nu
(around the first revival

, see Appendix

). This conclusion is in good agreement with the numerical results presented in Fig.

\tau=4\pi\alpha\pm 2\pi

Outside of revivals
|\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha|\gg 1
.
— We find
Q_{\nu}\approx 0
and the atomic dynamics is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}+\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\sigma_{x}\\
0\\
\cos(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\sigma_{y}\\
0\end{array}\right),

describing complete dephasing in the
\sigma_{y}
basis, followed by a generalized amplitude-damping channel. This channel is entanglement-breaking, since it involves a measurement in the
y
-basis. For the atom prepared in the ground or excited states, the slow phase only contributes to the atomic coherence
x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha}),

z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=0
.
These slow oscillations of the atomic coherence are well visible in the numerical simulation of Fig.
1
. Particularly interesting are times
\tau\approx 2\pi\alpha(\nu+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
with integer
\nu

independent of the initial condition. This can be seen in Fig.

\tau=\pi\alpha
and

, respectively. Here, the Jaynes-Cumming interaction acts as a SWAP gate — the atomic state is prepared in a fixed pure state uncorrelated with the field, while, by unitarity, the latter encodes the initial state of the atom. This state-transfer effect has been discussed in Refs.
Gea-Banacloche 1990
;
Zhang et al. 2015
. Since the dynamics is governed by slow variations of the coherence with
\left|\frac{\differential}{\differential\alpha}\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}\right|=\frac{\tau}{2\alpha^{2}}=0

{\rm QFI}(\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)})=0

III.5.3
Longer times
\alpha\ll\tau\ll\alpha^{3}

For even longer times
\tau=O(\alpha^{2})
, the revivals are no longer well separated; nevertheless, their contribution to the atomic dynamics is suppressed with
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\alpha}}}
. The atomic evolution is then found to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}+e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\sigma_{x}\\

O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\alpha}}})\end{array}\right).

(76)

III.5.4
Very long times
\tau\gg\alpha^{2}

At even longer times
\tau=O(\alpha^{3})
, we find that due to the constructive interference of the slow phase, revivals of the atomic coherence also occur at much later times
\tau\approx 8\pi\nu\alpha^{3}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}+(-1)^{\nu}P_{\nu}\sin(\Omega_\nu)\sigma_{x}\\

(-1)^{\nu}P_{\nu}\,\cos(\Omega_\nu)\sigma_{y}\\

with

P_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\pi\nu}}\exp(-\frac{\left(\tau^{1/3}-2 (\pi\nu)^{1/3} \alpha\right)^2}{2 (\pi\nu)^{2/3}})

and
\Omega_{\nu}(\tau)=\frac{3(\pi\nu)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}}{2}\tau^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}+\frac{\pi}{4}
.

This shows remarkable resilience of the atomic dynamics to chaos. While for generic times
\tau\gg\alpha
, the convergence of the atomic state to the maximally mixed one
\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}\to\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}
is governed by
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}
in Eq. (
III.5.3

P_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)>0
and
\cos\big(\Omega_\nu(\tau)\big)\approx 1

-basis is partially recovered

. The QFI follows the same trend: it is rapidly oscillating close to revivals and reaches values up to

III.5.5
Fully mixing times
\tau\gg\alpha^{3}

In the limit
\tau\gg\alpha^{3}
the atomic evolution is fully mixing

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\rho_{0}]=\mathcal{T}_{\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}}[\rho_{0}]=\nicefrac{{\mathds{1}}}{{2}}.

(82)

We have also seen that it becomes entanglement-breaking at a shorter time-scale
\tau\gg\alpha
.

III.5.6
Fisher information of the population measurement

Saturating the QFI of an atomic state requires measuring the atom in a specific direction in the Bloch sphere. When this direction does not coincide with the
z
-axis, such a measurement needs a local oscillator in phase with the coherent mode of the field. It is therefore natural to also look at the Fisher information obtained from a passive measurement of the atomic population. This is equivalent to computing the QFI of a fully dephased state (setting the
x
and
y
components of the Bloch vector to zero), i.e.

{\rm FI}_{z}(\rho_{\tau|\alpha})=\frac{\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}}{1-z_{\tau|\alpha}^{2}}.

(83)

Revisiting the general expression of the QFI, we find that the Fisher information of the population measurement is rapidly oscillating together with the population. However, for well-selected times, it is equal to the QFI in the short times regime
\tau\ll\alpha
(Eq.
62
) and at early revivals
\tau\approx 2\pi\alpha\nu
(Eq.
67
). It vanishes at later times
\tau\gg\alpha
, where the atomic population is scrambled,
z\approx 0
. For
\alpha=100
, the Fisher information of the population measurement is plotted in the inset of Fig.
4
.

III.6
Beyond the Jaynes-Cummings encoding

To conclude this section, let us come back to the general question — what is the interaction encoding the real coherent states
\{\ket{\alpha}\}
into a qubit probe with the globally optimal QFI (high for all
\alpha
) — and briefly discuss another candidate.

As the real coherent states are aligned with the
\hat{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a+a^{\dagger})
quadrature of the field, a very natural interaction is given by
\widetilde{H}=\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{y}(a+a^{\dagger})
and

\widetilde{U}_{\tau}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\tau\tilde{H}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\tau x}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{y}}\otimes\outerproduct{x}{x}

(84)

where
\ket{x}
are the eigenstate of
\hat{x}
. It describes a controlled rotation

\widetilde{U}:\ket{g}\ket{x}\mapsto\left(\cos(\frac{\tau x}{\sqrt 2})\ket{g}+\sin(\frac{\tau x}{\sqrt 2})\ket{e}\right)\ket{x}

(85)

of the probe by an angle proportional to the field quadrature
\hat{x}
. In other words,
\widetilde{U}
realizes a von Neumann measurement of the field quadrature with the qubit pointer. For the real displacement operators
D(\alpha)=e^{\alpha(a-a^{\dagger})}
, it has the natural covariance property
\widetilde{U}_{\tau}\,\mathcal{D}(\alpha)=\left(e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\tau\alpha}{2}\sigma_{y}}\otimes\mathcal{D}(\alpha)\right)\,\widetilde{U}_{\tau}
, ensuring that
\widetilde{U}
is equally suitable for all field amplitudes
\alpha
.

In this case, the reduced dynamics of the atom is immediately obtained, using
|\innerproduct{x}{\alpha}|^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp(-(x -\sqrt{2} \alpha)^2)
, one finds

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bullet]

\displaystyle=\tr_{\rm field}\widetilde{U}_{\tau}(\bullet\otimes\outerproduct{\alpha}{\alpha})\widetilde{U}_{\tau}^{\dagger}

(86)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int\differential x\,e^{-(x-\sqrt{2}\alpha)^{2}}\,\mathcal{Y}_{\frac{\tau x}{\sqrt{2}}}[\bullet]

(87)

where
\mathcal{Y}_{\varphi}[\bullet]=e^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi\sigma_{y}}\bullet e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi\sigma_{y}}
is the unitary rotation around
\sigma_{y}
. Performing the Gaussian integral gives

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}\\
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\big(\cos(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{x}-\sin(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{z}\big)\\
\sigma_{y}\\
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\big(\sin(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{x}+\cos(2\alpha\tau)\sigma_{z}\big)\end{array}\right).

(88)

Remarkably, we recognize here the dynamics induced by the Jaynes-Cummings interaction (Eq.
61
) in the limit of short time
\tau\ll\alpha
and large amplitude
\alpha\gg 1
. Hence, the atomic
{\rm QFI}[\widetilde{\rho}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}]\leq 1.47
is given by the same expression maximized at
\tau=1
.

IV
Interactions of an atom with a source of coherent radiation

As we have seen, regardless of the amplitude of the coherent field, the model where a single mode interacts with the atom is very different from an atom interacting with a classical field of unknown amplitude. A natural generalization that we now consider is the interaction of the atom with a
source
of coherent radiation. We discuss this situation in the context of a simple collision model, where the atom sequentially interacts with a series of coherent modes
\ket{\alpha}
with unknown amplitude, and the interaction is again described by the resonant Jaynes-Cummings model.

IV.1
A sequence of
N
interactions

First, consider the situation where the atom interacts with a sequence of a fixed number
N
of coherent modes. Each interaction has a duration
\tau=gt
resulting in the channel
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}
in Eq. (
20
). After the
N
steps, the reduced evolution of the atom is thus governed by

\mathcal{E}^{\circ N}_{\tau|\alpha}=\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}}_{N}.

(89)

We are interested in the final state of the atom
\rho_{\tau,N|\alpha}:=\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}^{\circ N}[\Psi_{0}]
and the maximal
{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau,N|\alpha}]

that can be obtained after
N
interactions.

In the limit
\alpha\gg 1
, we can use the general expression of the atomic evolution, computed in Sec.
III.5.3
, to derive the optimal quantum Fisher information.

First, consider the regime where the time
\tau
is short (Eq.
61
) or close to revivals (Eq.
64
with
\cos(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})=1
). In both cases, the atomic evolution is given by a rotation around
y
by the angle
\Phi_{\nu}
composed with a dephasing in the
y
basis of strength
Q_{\nu}
, plus a potential phase flip
\sigma_{x(y)}\to(-1)^{\nu}\sigma_{x(y)}
. Composing such channels
N
times, we find

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}^{\circ N}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}\\
Q_{\nu}^{N}\big(\cos(N \Phi_\nu)\sigma_{x}-\sin(N \Phi_\nu)\sigma_{z}\big)\\
\sigma_{y}\\
Q_{\nu}^{N}\big(\sin(N \Phi_\nu)\sigma_{x}+\cos(N \Phi_\nu)\sigma_{z}\big)\end{array}\right)

(90)

for even
\nu
. While for odd
\nu
, subsequent rotations cancel each other because of the phase flip, so the rotation angle is
\Phi_{\nu}
or
0
depending on the parity of
N
.

For
\nu=0
, it is only the phase
\Phi_{\nu}=2\alpha\tau
that depends on
\alpha
, while
Q_{0}=e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}
. For the QFI of any real initial state (which is optimal), we find

{\rm QFI}=4N^{2}\tau^{2}\,e^{-N\tau^{2}}.

(91)

By comparing the expressions for
N=1
and
2
, we conclude that it is only beneficial to increase the number of interactions beyond one if
\tau\leq\sqrt{\log 4}\approx 1.18
.

At revivals
\nu\geq 1
, it is only the dephasing rate
Q_{\nu}=e^{-\left(\frac{\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu}\right)^{2}}/\sqrt{\pi\nu}
which depends on
\alpha
, and the QFI for any real initial state is given by

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}=\frac{8N^{2}R_{\nu}^{2}}{(\pi\nu)^{N}e^{2NR_{\nu}^{2}}-1}

\displaystyle\leq-4N\,W_{0}\left(-\frac{1}{e(\pi\nu)^{N}}\right)

(92)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4N}{e(\pi\nu)^{N}},

(93)

where we optimized over
R_{\nu}=\frac{\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu}
to get the inequality. Here, we find that the optimal integer value of
N
is, in fact, always
N=1
, so applying the channel more than once never increases the QFI.

In turn, for times
1\ll\tau\ll\alpha^{2}
and outside of the revivals, the atomic evolution composed
N
times reads (see Eq. (
III.5.2
))

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}^{\circ N}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}+\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\sigma_{x}\\
0\\
\cos^{N}(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{2\alpha}})\sigma_{y}\\
0\end{array}\right).

(94)

Here again, more than one channel application can never improve the QFI. In fact, one can verify that this conclusion also remains true for longer interaction times
\tau
.

We conclude that it is only in the short time limit
\tau\leq 1.18
(or
t\leq\nicefrac{{1.18}}{{g}}
), where the subsequent interactions are beneficial for field estimation. We can then optimize the expression of the QFI in Eq. (
91
) with respect to the individual interaction time
\tau
to obtain

\max_{\tau}{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau,N|\alpha}]=\frac{4}{e}N\approx 1.47N

(95)

for
\tau=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}}
(or
t=\nicefrac{{1}}{{g\sqrt{N}}}
) independent of
\alpha
.
This can be compared to the theoretical upper-bound implied by monotonicity. The QFI of the final atomic state can not exceed that of the initial states
\ket{\alpha}^{\otimes N}
of the radiation modes, which is additive for a product state and equals
4N
, i.e.

{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau,N|\alpha}]\leq 4N.

(96)

Again, we find that, also in the
N
interactions scenario, the Jaynes-Cummings model is only a factor
e
lower than the theoretical maximum. It is natural to ask whether the upper-bound
4N
is saturable in the sequential scenario with a two-level probe. We show in the App.
E
that this is indeed the case; however, similarly to
V
in Eq. (
15
), the derived interactions are fine-tuned to the value of
\alpha
and moreover change between successive interaction steps. It is an interesting open question whether the QFI in Eq. (
95
) can be exceeded with an interaction suitable for a large range of
\alpha
and/or time-independent.

Next, we discuss the scaling of the QFI with respect to the total interaction time
T=N\tau
. Eq. (
96
)
{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau,N|\alpha}]\leq 4\frac{T}{\tau}
implies that for any
fixed

\tau
, QFI is bound to a linear scaling with total time. With this in mind we examine the limit
\tau\to 0
, where the QFI could a priori scale super-linearly with
T
. We consider two different ways to take the limit, depending on whether the total energy of the coherent modes is allowed to diverge or must be kept bounded.

IV.2
The infinite modes limit

First, we explore the limit where the single interaction time
\tau=g\,\differential t\to 0
is small while
N=\frac{1}{\differential t}\to\infty
and the amplitude of the coherent states
\alpha
remains constant.
To recover the dynamics of the atom in the limit, we expand the states in Eq. (
III.2
) in small
\tau

\begin{split}\ket{\alpha_0}&=\left(1-\frac{\tau^{2}\hat{n}}{2}\right)\ket{\alpha},\quad\qquad\,\,\,\ket{\alpha_2}=-\tau a^{\dagger}\ket{\alpha},\\
\ket{\alpha_1}&=\left(1-\frac{\tau^{2}(\hat{n}+1)}{2}\right)\ket{\alpha},\quad\ket{\alpha_3}=\tau a\ket{\alpha}.\end{split}

(97)

Tracing out the field mode, we find (see App.
F
for details) that the channel is unitary
\mathcal{E}_{\differential t|\alpha}[\bullet]=\bullet-\mathrm{i}\,\alpha\,g\,\differential t[\sigma_{y},\bullet]+O(\differential t^{2})
, and the atomic evolution is dictated by the master equation

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\rho_{t|\alpha}=-\mathrm{i}g\,\alpha\,[\sigma_{y},\rho_{t|\alpha}],

(98)

exactly reproducing the semi-classical Rabi model of Eq. (
1
) for
\alpha=E
with
{\rm QFI}=4\,g^{2}t^{2}
. Furthermore, for large
\alpha\gg 1
this can be seen to be the maximal atomic QFI for the Jaynes-Cummings interaction with an unlimited number of modes and total time
t
. Indeed, plugging
\tau=g\,\differential t
and
N=\frac{t}{\differential t}
in Eq. (
91
) we find

{\rm QFI}=4\,g^{2}t^{2}e^{-\differential t}\leq 4\,g^{2}t^{2}

(99)

saturated in the limit
\differential t\to 0
. The infinite modes limit is an example of the ideal Lamb-shift metrology theorized in
Sekatski and Perarnau-Llobet 2022
: the coupling of the atomic probe to the field modes (the sample) is fully captured by a parametric Hamiltonian term (Lamb-shift) in Eq. (
98
) and does not introduce any dissipation.

However, this limit is arguably nonphysical, as it corresponds to a diverging energy of the field modes
N\alpha^{2}\to\infty
, and diverging photon flux
\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\differential t}\to\infty
(also diverging total QFI of the coherent modes
4N\to\infty
). For this reason, we now consider an alternative limit, where these quantities remain bounded.

IV.3
The continuous field limit

To obtain a physical limit with a bounded energy, we set the interaction parameter
\tau=\sqrt{\kappa\,\differential t}
and take
\alpha^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}\differential t
, such that
\varepsilon^{2}
is the photon flux and our parameter of interest is the amplitude
\varepsilon
. In the Appendix
G
, we show that in this limit, one recovers the model with the atom coupled to a one-dimensional bosonic field with the time-dependent Hamiltonian

H(t)=\mathrm{i}\,\sqrt{\kappa}\Big(\sigma_{-}(\varepsilon+a^{\dagger}(t))-\sigma_{+}(\varepsilon+a(t))\Big),

(100)

where the bosonic field operators obey the usual commutation relations
[a(t),a^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})]=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and have vacuum statistics. See e.g. Refs.
Fischer et al. 2018
;
Dąbrowska et al. 2021
where this model is discussed.

To obtain the reduced dynamics of the atom in the continuous field limit, we plug
\tau=\sqrt{\kappa\differential t}
and
\alpha=\varepsilon\sqrt{\differential t}
in Eq. (
97
), giving the infinitesimal channel

\mathcal{E}_{\differential t|\varepsilon}[\bullet]=\bullet+\differential t\left(-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\kappa}\,\varepsilon[\sigma_{y},\bullet]+\kappa\,\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{-}}[\bullet]\right)+O(\differential t^{2}),

(101)

where
\mathcal{L}_{A}[\bullet]=A\bullet A^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}A,\bullet\}
(see Appendix
G
for details). Rearranging the terms, one concludes that the atomic dynamics is governed by the master equation

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\rho_{t|\varepsilon}=-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\kappa}\,\varepsilon\,[\sigma_{y},\rho_{t|\varepsilon}]+\kappa\,\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{-}}[\rho_{t|\varepsilon}],

(102)

where one recognizes the semi-classical Rabi model with the additional spontaneous emission term given by the dissipator
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{-}}
.
The calculation we just did is not new, and the conclusion is even older
Einstein 1969
– coupling an atom to quantized radiation enables spontaneous emission. For our task of sensing
\varepsilon
with the atomic probe, this implies that its QFI can not increase indefinitely.

To solve the master equation (
102
), it is convenient to work in time units
s=\kappa t
dictated by the coupling constant and introduce the rescaled field amplitude
\bar{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
. With this variable change the master equation simply reads

\frac{\differential}{\differential s}\rho_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}}=-\mathrm{i}\,\bar{\varepsilon}\,[\sigma_{y},\rho_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}}]+\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{-}}[\rho_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}}].

(103)

(Left)
The optimal QFI as a function of
\bar{\varepsilon}
for the initial ground state (blue) and for the initial excited state (green). The dashed line corresponds to the steady QFI computed in Eq. (
108
).
(Right)
The optimal QFI rate as a function of
\bar{\varepsilon}
for the initial ground state (blue) and for the initial excited state (green). The theoretical upper bound of 4 (also valid for adaptive processes with unlimited auxiliary entanglement) follows from Refs.
Sekatski et al. 2017
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2018
.

This equation (
103
) is straightforward to solve analytically; however, the expressions one obtains are not very insightful. Instead, we now focus on its consequences for the field estimation task.

IV.3.1
The optimal QFI

As the first quantity of interest, we discuss the maximal QFI reached by the atomic state (at some optimal time
s
)

{\rm QFI}^{\max}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}=\sup_{s}{\rm QFI}[\rho_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}}].

(104)

The QFI of the state
\rho_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}}
can also be computed analytically. It admits a particularly simple expression at
\bar{\varepsilon}=0
, where for the ground and excited initial states one finds (see App.
H
for an arbitrary real initial state)

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g)}_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}=0}]

\displaystyle=16\,e^{-s/2}\left(e^{s/4}-1\right)^{2},

(105)

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(e)}_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}=0}]

\displaystyle=16\,e^{-s}\left(e^{s/2}-3\,e^{s/4}+2\right)^{2}.

(106)

The dependence of the maximal QFI on
\bar{\varepsilon}
is plotted in Fig.
5
(left panel) for the ground (blue) and excited (green) initial states. We see that it is maximal at zero field
\bar{\varepsilon}=0
, where
\lim_{s\to\infty}{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}=0}]=16
follows from Eqs. (
105
,
106
). While for
\varepsilon\gg 1
it saturates the value
{\rm QFI}^{\rm max}_{\infty}\approx 0.97
, for which the optimal interaction time
s\approx 2.52
is again independent of
\bar{\varepsilon}
.

IV.3.2
The steady-state QFI

Next, let us consider the steady state
\rho^{*}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}
of the dynamics. Solving
0=-\mathrm{i}\,\bar{\varepsilon}\,[\sigma_{y},\rho^{*}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}]+\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{-}}[\rho^{*}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}]
we find the atomic steady-state read

\rho^{*}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathds{1}+\frac{1}{1+8\bar{\varepsilon}^{2}}\,\sigma_{z}+\frac{4\bar{\varepsilon}}{1+8\bar{\varepsilon}^{2}}\,\sigma_{x}\right),

(107)

and gives

{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\bar{\varepsilon}}^{*}]=\left(\frac{4}{(1+8\,\bar{\varepsilon}^{2})}\right)^{2}.

(108)

The QFI of the steady state is equal to
16
at
\bar{\varepsilon}=0
and vanishes in the limit of large
\bar{\varepsilon}
, where the steady state is maximally mixed. This is in sharp contrast with the optimal transient
{\rm QFI}^{\max}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}
, which is finite for all
\bar{\varepsilon}
.

IV.3.3
The optimal QFI rate

In the limit where the total interaction time is very long, the best possible strategy (from the Fisher information perspective) is to repeat the measurement procedure for which the QFI rate is maximized

{\rm QFIRate}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}:=\sup_{s}\frac{{\rm QFI}[\rho_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}}]}{s},

(109)

see e.g. Refs.
Correa et al. 2015
;
Mehboudi et al. 2019
;
Sekatski et al. 2017
.

In Fig.
5
(right panel), the maximal QFI rate as a function of
\bar{\varepsilon}
for the initial ground and excited states. For the initial ground state, the behavior is simple since the
{\rm QFIRate}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}
is monotonically decreasing w.r.t.
\bar{\varepsilon}
. For
\bar{\varepsilon}=0
we can compute the optimal rate using Eq. (
105
), we find that

{\rm QFIRate}_{\bar{\varepsilon}=0}^{(g)}\approx 1.63

(110)

is attained at
s\approx 5.03
.

Recall that the atomic
{\rm QFI}[\rho_{s|\bar{\varepsilon}}]\leq N\,{\rm QFI}\big(\outerproduct{\alpha_{\bar\varepsilon}}{\alpha_{\bar\varepsilon}}\big)
is bounded by that of the field itself. Plugging in
N=\frac{t}{\differential t}=\frac{s}{\kappa\differential t}
and
\alpha_{\bar{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{\differential t}}{\sqrt{\kappa}}
we find the theoretical upper-bound

{\rm QFIRate}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}\leq 4,

(111)

which can also be derived by applying the general techniques of
Sekatski et al. 2017
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2018
to the master equation (
103
). Note that approaching this bound in general requires entangling the atom with an auxiliary system and using fast adaptive control. Furthermore, this entanglement and control are known to be helpful in the case of spontaneous emission noise, as they enable error-detection techniques. It is thus natural that our simple sequential probing of the field does not saturate the theoretical maximum.

IV.3.4
The impact of
\kappa

Finally, let us interpret these results in terms of the physical time
t=\frac{s}{\kappa}
and the physical amplitude
\varepsilon=\sqrt{\kappa}\bar{\varepsilon}
. For a state
\rho_{\varepsilon}
we denote the QFI with respect to the
\varepsilon
as
\widetilde{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\varepsilon}]
. To relate it to the quantities computed above, note that the parameter rescaling gives
\widetilde{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\varepsilon}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\bar{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon/\sqrt{\kappa}}]
.

For a single interaction, the maximal reachable QFI, expressed in the physical amplitude
\varepsilon
, can thus be increased when the interaction parameter
\kappa
is lowered. This can be understood from the fact that lowering
\kappa
delays the onset of decay in Eq. (
102
), relatively to the coherent term, effectively prolonging the coherent sensing time.
For
\varepsilon\neq 0
, in the limit of small
\kappa\ll\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
the maximal QFI becomes

\widetilde{\rm QFI}^{\max}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}{\rm QFI}^{\max}_{\varepsilon/\sqrt{\kappa}}\approx\frac{{\rm QFI}^{\max}_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\approx\frac{0.97}{\sqrt{\kappa}}.

(112)

The optimal transient QFI is thus diverging for all
\varepsilon
in the limit of vanishing
\kappa
. However, the price to pay is the increase of the interaction time
t\sim\nicefrac{{1}}{{\kappa}}
required to reach the optimal atomic state.

In fact, looking at the optimal QFI rate expressed in terms of the physical quantities, we find the opposite conclusion

\widetilde{\rm QFIRate}_{\varepsilon}=\sup_{t}\frac{\widetilde{\rm QFI}[\rho_{t|\varepsilon}]}{t}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,{\rm QFIRate}_{\varepsilon/\sqrt{\kappa}}.

(113)

It logically goes to zero when the interaction parameter
\kappa
is vanishing. Conversely, in the limit of large
\kappa\gg 1/\varepsilon^{2}
the rate is given by

\widetilde{\rm QFIRate}_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,{\rm QFIRate}_{0}\approx 1.63\sqrt{\kappa}

(114)

for the atom prepared in the ground state, and can thus become arbitrarily large. However, attaining this limit requires measuring and re-initializing the atom with a frequency increasing with
\kappa
.

The optimal choice of
\kappa
thus depends on the considered scenario, for instance, whether the total interaction time or the total number of shots (e.g., limited by long re-initialization time) is the limiting resource. Moreover, in any realistic scenario, where both the interaction time and the measurement frequency can not be arbitrarily high, the total QFI and QFI rate will remain bounded for all
\kappa
and
\varepsilon
. Interestingly, for the steady state, we find the bound

\widetilde{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\varepsilon}^{*}]=\frac{16\,\kappa^{3/2}}{\left(8\,\varepsilon^{2}+\kappa\right)^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

(115)

reached for
\kappa=24\,\varepsilon^{2}
and remaining finite for
\varepsilon>0
.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the task of estimating the amplitude of a coherent quantum field with a two-level atomic probe, as modeled by the resonant Jaynes-Cummings interaction. We have considered two scenarios – where the atom interacts with a single coherent mode of the electromagnetic field, or with a sequence of such modes (modeling a source of coherent radiation). Focusing on the probe’s QFI to quantify the estimation precision, we have seen that in both scenarios its dependence on the field amplitude and the interaction time
\tau
is very different from the semi-classical Rabi model, where the field amplitude enters as a scalar parameter of the Hamiltonian and
{\rm QFI}=4\tau^{2}
for all field amplitudes.

In the single-mode scenario, the QFI is bounded by 4, a constant dictated by the non-orthogonality of the coherent states
\ket{\alpha}
themselves, and can not increase indefinitely with the interaction time. In addition, approaching this bound with the atomic probe requires an interaction that faithfully encodes the subspace
\{\ket{\alpha},\ket{\dot\alpha}\}
into the two-level atom. For the Jaynes-Cummings interaction, we found that this is only realized in the vacuum limit
\alpha\ll 1
, where the QFI periodically approaches the maximal value of 4 for both ground and excited initial states of the atom. In the opposite limit
\alpha\gg 1
, the reduced evolution of the atom can also be solved analytically, see Eq. (
38
) of Sec.
III.5.3
, a result that might be of independent interest in quantum optics. Here, we found that the maximal
{\rm QFI}\approx 1.47
is attained at
\tau=1
and
\tau=4\alpha^{2}
. The first maximum is attained at a fixed time (independent of
\alpha
) and is conjectured to realize the optimal encoding of the real coherent states into a qubit probe. The second maximum requires measuring the atomic coherence and is absent for the population measurement.

In the sequential scenario, the atom interacts with a sequence of
N
coherent modes
\ket{\alpha}
for a total time
T=N\tau
. In the large
\alpha\gg 1
limit, using the analytic expression of the reduced dynamics, we found
that the optimal
{\rm QFI}\approx 1.47\,N
is achieved for the interaction times
\tau=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}}
independent of
\alpha
, and is close to the theoretical limit
{\rm QFI}\leq 4N
. Motivated by this bound, we then studied the continuous limit
N\to\infty
and
\tau\to 0
, which can be taken in two ways. First, for
\tau=g\,\differential t
,
N=\frac{t}{\differential t}
and constant
\alpha
one recovers the semi-classical Rabi model with
E=\alpha
. However, this limit corresponds to a diverging photon-flux
\nicefrac{{\alpha^{2}}}{{\differential t}}\to\infty
, and is ultimately nonphysical. Second, for

\tau=\sqrt{\kappa\,\differential t},\alpha=\varepsilon\sqrt{\differential t}
and
N=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\differential t}}
, one recovers the model where the atom interacts with a continuous one-dimensional field and
\varepsilon
becomes the parameter of interest. Here, the atom follows a simple Lindbladian evolution that resembles the semiclassical Rabi model with an important caveat: it is driven by the Rabi frequency
\sqrt{\kappa}\,\varepsilon
, but it is also subject to spontaneous emission noise with rate
\kappa
, which forbids the QFI from increasing indefinitely. We then explored two figures of merit: the maximal QFI (achieved by a single interaction of arbitrary duration) and the maximal QFI rate. The two quantities are found to be inversely proportional and directly proportional to the coupling constant
\kappa
, respectively.

Our results show that the semi-classical Rabi model is only a good approximation for the quantum-field estimation task in a specific limit, which is ultimately nonphysical. Outside this limit, we found that the QFI of the atomic probe behaves very differently from the semi-classical prediction: it is limited by the non-orthogonality of coherent states in a single-mode scenario, and by the spontaneous emission noise in the continuous field scenario. Throughout the paper, we have identified several open questions regarding the optimal encoding of the QFI carried by large quantum systems (the field modes) into a small quantum probe (the two-level atom). We believe that many more such questions remain to be uncovered in the topic of quantum estimation of “quantum parameters”.

Noting that

Appendix D
The atomic dynamics in the large
\alpha
limit

Our goal is to compute the expressions of the form (Eq.
149
)

\displaystyle G_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\rm P}(n|\alpha)f_{i}(n)f_{j}(n)=\mathds{E}[f_{i}(\hat{n})f_{j}(\hat{n})]

(158)

for the seven values
G_{00},G_{11},G_{03},G_{12},G_{01},G_{02},G_{23}
determining the reduced dynamics of the atom, where the functions
f_{i}
are given in Eq. (
148
) and
\hat{n}
is a Poisson random variable with mean
\alpha^{2}
. Using the complex exponential representation of sine and cosine, all the products

f_{i}(n)f_{j}(n)=\Gamma_{ij}(n)\sum_{k,\pm}c_{i,j}(k,\pm)\,e^{\pm\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)}

(159)

can be expressed as linear combinations of some phases term
e^{\pm\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)}
with

\big(S_{0}(n),S_{1}(n),S_{2}(n),S_{3}(n)\big)=\big(2\tau\sqrt{n},2\tau\sqrt{n+1},\tau(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{n+1}),\tau(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n+1})\big)

(160)

times a factor
\Gamma_{ij}(n)\in\{1,\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\alpha},\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n+1}},\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n+1}}\}
, and suitable weights
c_{i,j}(k,\pm)
. Note already, that by expanding in
\hat{\delta}=\frac{\hat{n}-\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}=O(1)
we obtain
\Gamma_{ij}(n)=O(\alpha^{-1})
in all the four cases. We are thus interested in computing the expressions of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)\Gamma_{ij}(n)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)}+O(\alpha^{-1}).

(161)

We will now separately discuss the limits where the phases
S_{0}(n),S_{1}(n),S_{2}(n)
and
S_{3}(n)
are approximately linear or not.

D.1
The linear phase regime

When the interaction time
\tau
is relatively slow, the phases are well approximated by linear functions on the support of the Poissonian distributions. More precises expanding in
\hat{\delta}=\frac{\hat{n}-\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}=O(1)
one finds

\displaystyle S_{0}(n),S_{1}(n),S_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\tau+\tau\frac{n-\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}+\tau O(\alpha^{-1})

(162)

\displaystyle S_{3}(n)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}+\frac{\tau}{4\alpha^{2}}\frac{n-\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}+\tau O(\alpha^{-3}).

(163)

Using the moment generating function of the Poissonian, it is then immediate to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,0}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)}=\exp(-\frac{\tau^2}{2}+\ii\, 2 \alpha\, \tau\, )\qquad\text{for }\,k=1,2,3.

(164)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3,0}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{3}(n)}=\exp(-\frac{\tau^2}{32 \alpha^4}-\ii\, \frac{\tau}{2\alpha}).

(165)

up to the order
O(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha}})
and
O(\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha^{3}}})
respectively.

D.2
The rapid phases regime

In the limit where the linear phase approximation fails, to compute such sums in the right-hand side of Eq. (
161
), we follow the approach presented in
Fleischhauer and Schleich 1993
. For simplicity we now focus on the phases for
k=0,1,2
and come back to the case
k=3
at the end of the section. First, we use the Poissonian summation
Courant and Hilbert 1989
to express the discrete sum over
n
with an integral by introducing the summation over an additional index

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)}=\frac{1}{2}P(0|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{k}(0)}+\sum_{\nu=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)-\mathrm{i}2\pi n\nu}\differential n,

(166)

where the first term
P(0|\alpha)\approx 0
can be already neglected in the limit of interest. Note that in the limit of interest the envelope
P(n|\alpha)
is a function varying slowly, on the scale
n-n^{\prime}=O(\alpha)
. In comparison, the phases
\varphi_{k,\nu}(n):=S_{k}(n)-2\pi n\nu\approx 2\tau\sqrt{n}-2\pi n\nu
are varying much faster. This is immediate to see for
\nu\neq 0
. For the case
\nu=0
, expanding in
\hat{\delta}=\frac{\hat{n}-\alpha^{2}}{\alpha}=O(1)
, one notices that

\tau\sqrt{n}=\alpha\tau+\frac{\tau(n-\alpha^{2})}{\alpha}+\tau O(\alpha^{-1})

(167)

also varies fast compared to the envelope in the limit of interest
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha}}\geq O(1)
. Therefore the integrals in Eq. (
166

can be approximated with help of the stationary phase approximation,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)e^{\mathrm{i}\,\varphi_{k,\nu}(n)}\differential n\approx\mathcal{I}_{k,\nu}:=P(n_{k,\nu}|\alpha)\exp(\ii\varphi_{k,\nu}(n_{k,\nu}) +\ii\frac{\pi}{4}{\rm sign}(\varphi''_{k,\nu}(n_{k,\nu})))\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{k,\nu}(n_{k,\nu})|}},

(168)

where
n_{k,\nu}\in[0,\infty]
is the unique solution of

\varphi^{\prime}_{k,\nu}(n_{k,\nu})=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S^{\prime}_{k}(n_{k,\nu})=2\pi\nu,

(169)

and is only a function of
\tau
and
\nu
. In all of the cases, we find that
S^{\prime}_{k}(n)>0
(on the support of the Poissonian), hence only the cases
\nu\geq 1
give rise to a stationary phase
n_{k,\nu}
inside the integration domain, and we only consider these values. Furthermore, we find that
\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{k,\nu}(n)=S^{\prime\prime}_{k}(n)\leq 0
, giving

\mathcal{I}_{k,\nu}:=P(n_{k,\nu}|\alpha)e^{-\mathrm{i}\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{4}}}\exp\big(\ii\, S_{k}(n_{k,\nu})- \ii 2\pi\nu\, n_{k,\nu} \big)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|S^{\prime\prime}_{k}(n_{k,\nu})|}}.

(170)

For
k=0,1

n_{1,\nu}=\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}-1

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{2,\nu}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{2,\nu}+1}}=\frac{4\pi\nu}{\tau}

\frac{2}{\sqrt{n+1}}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}<\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}

n_{1,\nu}=n_{0,\nu}-1<n_{2,\nu}<n_{0,\nu}

n_{2,\nu}=n_{0,\nu}-\Delta=\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}-\Delta

0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{2,\nu}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{2,\nu}+1}}-\frac{4\pi\nu}{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}(2\Delta-1)\left(\frac{1}{n_{0,\nu}}\right)^{3/2}+O(n_{0,\nu}^{-\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}}),

showing that
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}
and
n_{2,\nu}=\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

Plugging these values in Eq. (
170

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,\nu}

\displaystyle=P\left(n_{k,\nu}\middle|\alpha\right)e^{-\mathrm{i}\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{4}}}\exp(\ii\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu}+\ii\pi\nu c_k )\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}},

(171)

c_{0}=0,c_{1}=2

Johnson et al. 2005

Fleischhauer and Schleich 1993

(172)

we see that our expressions are only non-vanishing when
\frac{\tau}{2\pi\nu}-\alpha=O(1)

\frac{\nu}{\tau}\to 0

(173)

This leads to simple asymptotic expressions

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}=\mathcal{I}_{1,\nu}=(-1)^{\nu}\mathcal{I}_{2,\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}\exp(-\frac{(\tau- 2\pi\nu\alpha)^2}{2\pi^2\nu^2})\exp(\ii(\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4}))

(174)

where we used
\exp(\ii 2\pi\nu)=1

\exp(\ii\pi\nu)=(-1)^{\nu}

\displaystyle S_{3}^{\prime\prime}(n_{3,\nu})

D.3
The full reduced dynamics of the atom

Summarizing the above calculations, we find that in the limit of large
\alpha
with
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha^{3}}}\to 0
and up to
O(\alpha^{-1})
the expected values in Eq. (

) are given by

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)\Gamma_{ij}(n)e^{\mathrm{i}S_{k}(n)}\approx\begin{cases}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}&k=0,1\\
\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}(-1)^{\nu}&k=2\\
\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}&k=3\end{cases},

(181)

with all the terms given in Eqs. (
174
,
165
,
164
). This allows us to express the matrix
G
, representing the channels, as

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}+\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{(0)}+\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{(3)})\qquad\text{with}

(182)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}&(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}&-\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}&(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}\\
(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}&\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}&-(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}&\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}\\
-\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}&-(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}&-\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}&(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}\\
(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}&\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}&(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}&-\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}\end{array}\right)\qquad\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{(3)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Re}&&-\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Im}\\
\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Re}&&-\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Im}&\\
&-\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Im}&&-\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Re}\\
-\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Im}&&-\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Re}&\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\exp(-\frac{\tau^2}{2}+\ii\, 2 \alpha\, \tau\, )&\nu=0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}\exp(-\frac{(\tau- 2\pi\nu\alpha)^2}{2\pi^2\nu^2})\exp(\ii(\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4}))&\nu\geq 1\end{cases}

(191)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\exp(-\frac{\tau^2}{32 \alpha^4}-\ii\, \frac{\tau}{2\alpha})&\nu=0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\pi\nu}}\exp(-\frac{\left(\tau^{1/3}-2 (\pi\nu)^{1/3} \alpha\right)^2}{2 (\pi\nu)^{2/3}})(-1)^{\nu}\exp( -\ii\frac{3 (\pi\nu)^{\nicefrac{1}{3}}\tau^{\nicefrac{2}{3}}}{2} - \ii\frac{\pi}{4})&\nu\geq 1\end{cases}.

(192)

All matrices
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{(k)}
correspond to the linear maps
\mathcal{E}_{\nu}^{(k)}
, which are neither positive nor trace preserving. In contrast, the first matrix
\bar{\mathcal{G}}:=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}
corresponds to the fully depolarizing channel

\bar{\mathcal{E}}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{array}\right)\quad\implies\quad\bar{\mathcal{E}}[\rho]=\frac{\mathds{1}}{2},

(193)

which is the long time limit
\tau\gg\alpha^{3}
of the atomic dynamics, where all the other terms vanish. For the other maps, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\nu}^{(0)}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
(-1)^{\nu}\,\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}\,\sigma_{x}-\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}\,\sigma_{z}\\
0\\
(-1)^{\nu}\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Im}\,\sigma_{x}+\mathcal{I}_{0,\nu}^{\rm Re}\,\sigma_{z}\end{array}\right),\qquad\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\nu}^{(3)}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Im}\,\sigma_{x}\\
0\\
\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu}^{\rm Re}\,\sigma_{y}\\
0\end{array}\right),

where
\bm{\sigma}=(\mathds{1},\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
.
With the help of these expressions, we now discuss the exact form of the channel
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}
in different time limits.

Short times
\tau<O(\alpha)
.
First, consider the short time limit
\tau<O(\alpha)
. Here, the only non-vanishing terms are
\mathcal{I}_{3,0}=1
and
\mathcal{I}_{0,0}=\exp(-\frac{\tau^2}{2}+\ii\, 2 \alpha\, \tau\, )
, implying

\tau\leq O(\alpha):\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}\\
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\Big(\cos(2 \alpha\,\tau)\,\sigma_{x}-\sin(2 \alpha\,\tau)\,\sigma_{z}\Big)\\
\sigma_{y}\\
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\Big(\sin(2 \alpha\,\tau)\,\sigma_{x}+\cos(2 \alpha\,\tau)\,\sigma_{z}\Big)\end{array}\right).

(202)

Longer times
1\ll\tau\ll\alpha^{3}
.
In this regime the term
\mathcal{I}_{0,0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{3,\nu\geq 1}
are negligible. Hence the atomic evolution is given by
G=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}+\mathcal{G}_{0}^{(3)}+\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{(0)}

with

1\ll\tau\ll\alpha^{3}:\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}+e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\,\sigma_{x}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}\exp(-\frac{(\tau- 2\pi\nu\alpha)^2}{2\pi^2\nu^2})\Big((-1)^{\nu}\cos(\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4})\,\sigma_{x}-\sin(\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4})\,\sigma_{z}\Big)\\
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\cos(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\,\sigma_{y}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}\exp(-\frac{(\tau- 2\pi\nu\alpha)^2}{2\pi^2\nu^2})\Big((-1)^{\nu}\sin(\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4})\,\sigma_{x}+\cos(\frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu} -\frac{\pi}{4})\,\sigma_{z}\Big)\end{array}\right)

(203)

and
\nu
can be taken to minimize
|\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu|
. Remarkably,
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\sigma_{x}]
and
\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\sigma_{z}]
are only nonzero for
|\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha|=O(1)
, where
\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\approx 0
and
\cos(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\approx(-1)^{\nu}
. Hence, this time regime exhibits two very different behaviors: close and outside of revivals:

\displaystyle|\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha|=O(1)\qquad

\displaystyle\implies\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\mathds{1}]=\mathds{1},\,\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\sigma_{y}]=(-1)^{\nu}e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\sigma_{y},

(204)

\displaystyle|\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha|\gg 1\quad

\displaystyle\implies\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\sigma_{x}]=\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\sigma_{z}]=0.

(205)

Very long times
\tau\gg\alpha^{2}
:
Finally at even longer times
\tau\gg\alpha^{2}
, the only non-vanishing contribution is
\mathcal{G}_{\nu}^{(3)}
with
\nu\geq 1
, showing revivals around specific times with

|\tau^{1/3}-2\alpha(\pi\nu)^{1/3}|=O(1):\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\alpha}[\bm{\sigma}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathds{1}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\pi\nu}}e^{-F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau,\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\frac{3}{2} (\pi\nu\tau^2)^{1/3} + \frac{\pi}{4})\,\sigma_{x}\\
0\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\pi\nu}}e^{-F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau,\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\cos(\frac{3}{2} (\pi\nu\tau^2)^{1/3} + \frac{\pi}{4})\,\sigma_{y}\\
0\end{array}\right)

(206)

and
F_{\nu}(\tau,\alpha)=\frac{\tau^{1/3}-2\alpha(\pi\nu)^{1/3}}{\sqrt{2}(\pi\nu)^{1/3}}
.

D.4
The atomic state and its QFI

To discuss the final state for an atom prepared in the ground or excited state and its QFI, we again separately consider the three different time regimes

D.4.1
Quantum Fisher information at short times
\tau=O(1)

For
\tau\ll\alpha
using Eq. (
202
) one immediate obtains
x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=\pm e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\sin(2\alpha\tau)
,
z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=\pm e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{2}}}\cos(2\alpha\tau)
(where the sign
\pm
is determined by the initial condition), and

{\rm QFI}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}]=4\tau^{2}e^{-\tau^{2}}.

(207)

The QFI reaches its maximum at
\tau=1
and then quickly decays to zero, together with the coherence and the population.

Let us also compute the Fisher information
{\rm FI}_{z}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}]
for the population measurement of the atom. This is equivalent to computing the QFI of the atomic state, dephased in the
z
-basis, i.e., setting
x=\dot{x}=0
.

{\rm FI}_{z}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}]=4\tau^{2}\frac{\sin^{2}(2\alpha\tau)}{e^{\tau^{2}}-\cos^{2}(2\alpha\tau)}\leq 4\tau^{2}e^{-\tau^{2}},

(208)

saturated for
\sin^{2}(2\alpha\tau)
. It is equal to the QFI of the state, for well-chosen times.

D.4.2
Quantum Fisher information at long times
\tau=O(\alpha)

At longer times
1\ll\tau\ll\alpha^{2}
by Eq. (
203
) the atomic state is found to be

\displaystyle z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\pm\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}\cos(\Phi_{\nu})

(209)

\displaystyle x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\pm\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Phi_{\nu}),

(210)

where we introduced
R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha):=\frac{\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu}
,
\Phi_{\nu}=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2\pi\nu}-\frac{\pi}{4}
to shorten the equations. Deriving with respect to the parameter
\alpha
, one finds

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\pm\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}\cos(\Phi_{\nu})

(211)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau}{2\alpha^{2}}e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\left(\cos(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha})-\frac{\tau}{4\alpha^{3}}\sin(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha})\right)\pm\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Phi_{\nu}),

(212)

where the term
\frac{\tau}{4\alpha^{3}}\sin(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha})
can be neglected. To compute the QFI, we discuss the times at revivals and outsider separately.

At revivals
|\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha|=O(1)
First, let us consider the expressions of QFI close to the revival times
\tau\approx 2\pi\alpha\nu
, i.e., with
\delta\tau=\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha=O(1)
. For
\tau\ll\alpha^{2}
, the terms coming with the prefactor
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}
can be neglected. The atomic dynamics is given by

\displaystyle z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}\cos(\Phi_{\nu})

\displaystyle\quad\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}\cos(\Phi_{\nu})

\displaystyle x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Phi_{\nu})

\displaystyle\quad\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Phi_{\nu})

leading to

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{8R_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)}{\pi\nu\,e^{2R_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)}-1}.

(213)

Figure 6:
The function
-4W_{0}\left(-\frac{1}{e\pi\nu}\right)
(full line) and the linear lower bound
\frac{0.468}{\nu}
(dashed line).

By fine-tuning the time
\tau
this expression can be maximized with respect to
R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)
. Doing so, we find the following optimal value

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=-4W_{0}\left(-\frac{1}{e\pi\nu}\right)\approx\frac{0.468}{\nu},

(214)

where
W_{0}
is the principal branch of the Lambert
W
function. This linear approximation works well for large
\nu
, as illustrated in Fig.
6
. The bound is saturated at

\tau=2\pi\alpha\nu\pm\pi\nu\sqrt{1+W_{0}\left(-\frac{1}{e\pi\nu}\right)}\approx 2\pi\alpha\nu\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{0.12}{\nu}}\pi\nu\approx 2\pi\alpha\nu\pm\pi\nu

(215)

For
\nu=1
or 2 the QFI can be as high as
{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]\approx 0.54
and 0.25.

For the Fisher information of the population measurement, we find

{\rm FI}_{z}[\rho_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}]=\frac{8R_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)\cos^{2}(\Phi_{\nu})}{\pi\nu\,e^{2R_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)}-\cos^{2}(\Phi_{\nu})},

(216)

which is equal to the full QFI in Eq. (
213
) at times with
\cos^{2}(\Phi_{\nu})=1
. Note that
\Phi_{\nu}
changes much faster than
R_{\nu}
, and can thus be optimized independently.

Outside of revivals
|\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha|\gg 1
.
For generic times
\tau-2\pi\nu\alpha>O(1)
the contributions of the
\nu
terms are vanishing, and the atomic state is given by
z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=0
and

\displaystyle x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\qquad\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}=-\frac{\tau}{2\alpha^{2}}e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\cos(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha})

(217)

in the leading order of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
. For
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha^{3}}}\to 0
, the second term in
\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}
can be neglected and the quantum Fisher information is given by

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\,\alpha^{4}}\frac{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}\right)}{\,e^{\frac{\tau^{2}}{16\alpha^{4}}}-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}\right)}.

(218)

For relatively short times
\tau\ll\alpha^{2}
this expression is generically suppressed QFI
\approx 0
by the prefactor.

Special care must be taken for times where the state is approximately pure, which occurs at
\tau\approx 2\pi\alpha(\nu-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
where
x^{(e/g)}_{\alpha|\tau}=\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})\approx 1
. Specifically, let us consider
\tau=\pi\alpha+\delta\tau
with
\delta\tau=O(1)
, and focus on the atomic coherence for the ground initial state. With the help of Eqs. (
154
), we obtain

\displaystyle x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}\sin(\tau(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{\hat n}))

(219)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P(n|\alpha)\frac{(2\hat{n}-2\alpha^{2}+1)}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}\sin(\tau(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{\hat n}))

(220)

by neglecting the fast phases. In this case, by taking the expansions to the next order, we have

\displaystyle\sin(\tau(\sqrt{\hat n+1}-\sqrt{\hat n}))

\displaystyle=1-\frac{(\pi\delta-2\delta\tau)^{2}}{32\alpha^{2}}+O(\alpha^{-3})

(221)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\delta}{2\alpha}+\frac{3\delta^{2}-4}{8\alpha^{2}}+O(\alpha^{-3})

(222)

\displaystyle\frac{(2\hat{n}-2\alpha^{2}+1)}{\sqrt{\hat{n}+1}}

\displaystyle=2\delta+\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{3\left(\delta^{3}-2\delta\right)}{4\alpha^{2}}+O(\alpha^{-3})

(223)

leading to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}=1-\frac{4\delta\tau^{2}+\pi^{2}+4}{32\alpha^{2}}+O(\alpha^{-3})\\
\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)}=\frac{\pi\delta\tau}{4\alpha^{2}}+O(\alpha^{-3})\end{cases}\implies\,{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=O(\alpha^{-2}).

(224)

The same conclusion
{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=O(\alpha^{-2})
can be repeated for the initially excited atom, showing that the QFI is indeed vanishing for
\alpha=O(\tau)
outside of the revival times.

Contrasting this asymptotic conclusion, we see that the numerical results of Fig.
2
show a nonzero
{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]
around
\tau\approx\pi\alpha
for the excited initial condition. However, this effect disappears if we numerically compute the contribution to the QFI solely coming from the atomic coherence
\frac{(\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(e)})^{2}}{1-(x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g)})^{2}}
. This suggests that the nonzero quantum Fisher information it is due to a residual nonzero
\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(e)}
contribution to the numerator.

D.4.3
Quantum Fisher information at longer times
\tau=O(\alpha^{2})

Here the expressions in Eqs. (
209
-
212
) are still valid, but for longer times
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha^{2}}}=O(1)
, the terms with
e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}
can not be ignored. In this regime, only the “late” revivals are relevant, as
\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu=0
implies
\nu=\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}=O(\alpha)
. Moreover, the subsequent revivals stop being well separated in time

R_{\nu}(\tau=2\pi\alpha(\nu+1)|\alpha)=\frac{\tau-2\pi\alpha\nu}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu}=\frac{2\pi\alpha(\nu+1)-2\pi\alpha\nu}{\sqrt{2}\pi\nu}=\sqrt{2}\,\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{\nu}}=O(1).

(225)

In fact, there is a
O(1)
number of integer values
\nu
giving non-vanishing contributions at a fixed time
\tau
, we will denote this set with
{\rm N}_{\tau}
. Nevertheless, because of the
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\nu}}}
prefactor in the expressions of the atomic state, the contributions of the revivals can be treated as a small perturbation

\displaystyle z_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=0+\delta z,

\displaystyle\quad\delta z

\displaystyle:=\pm\sum_{\nu\in{\rm N}_{\tau}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}\cos(\Phi_{\nu}),

(226)

\displaystyle x_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\sin(\nicefrac{\tau}{2\alpha})+\delta x,

\displaystyle\quad\delta x

\displaystyle:=\pm\sum_{\nu\in{\rm N}_{\tau}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Phi_{\nu}),

(227)

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=0+\delta\dot{z},

\displaystyle\quad\delta\dot{z}

\displaystyle:=\pm\sum_{\nu\in{\rm N}_{\tau}}\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}\cos(\Phi_{\nu}),

(228)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau}{2\alpha^{2}}e^{-\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{32\alpha^{4}}}}\cos(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha})+\delta\dot{x},

\displaystyle\quad\delta\dot{x}

\displaystyle:=\pm\sum_{\nu\in{\rm N}_{\tau}}\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,R_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,}{\sqrt{\pi\nu}}e^{-R^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Phi_{\nu}),

(229)

where we neglected the second terms in
\dot{x}_{\tau|\alpha}^{(g/e)}
using
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\alpha^{3}}}\to 0
.
In the leading order, we recover

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\,\alpha^{4}}\frac{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}\right)}{\,e^{\frac{\tau^{2}}{16\alpha^{4}}}-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}\right)}+O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\nu}}}).

(230)

The correction term is complicated, and we come to it later.
For the leading term, we see that the argument
\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{2\alpha}}
of sine and cosine varies on a much faster time-scale than the exponential
e^{\frac{\tau^{2}}{16\alpha^{4}}}
, and can be optimized independently. It is easy to see that the QFI is always maximized at
\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}\right)=1
. Tuning the time to ensure this, we obtain a very simple expression

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\,\alpha^{4}}e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{16\alpha^{4}}}\leq e^{-1}\approx 1.47,

(231)

saturated around
\tau\approx 4\,\alpha^{2}
, where the QFI oscillates fast between 0 and the maximal value.

Setting
\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{2\alpha}\right)=1
, the first order correction also simplifies

\displaystyle{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau^{2}}{4\,\alpha^{4}}e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{16\alpha^{4}}}-\frac{\tau}{\alpha^{2}}e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{32\alpha^{4}}}\,\delta\dot{x}.

(232)

The expression of
\delta\dot{x}
involves the oscillating terms
\sin( \frac{\tau^2}{2\pi\nu}-\frac{\pi}{4})
that vary quickly on a timescale of
\frac{1}{\alpha}
and are different for all involved
\nu
. To conclude this discussion, we simply note that at finite
\alpha
the expression of QFI in Eq. (
231
) is modified by an oscillating contribution that only vanishes with
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\alpha}}}
. These oscillations are well visible in the numerical simulation for
\alpha=100
of Fig.
4
.

D.4.4
Quantum Fisher information at very long times
\tau=O(\alpha^{3})

For very long times
\tau\gg\alpha^{2}
using Eq. (
206
) we find (for any real intial state)

\displaystyle x^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\pi\nu}}e^{-F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Omega_\nu(\tau)),\qquad z^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}=0,

(233)

with
F_{\nu}(\tau,\alpha)=\frac{\tau^{1/3}-2\alpha(\pi\nu)^{1/3}}{\sqrt{2}(\pi\nu)^{1/3}}
and
\Omega_{\nu}(\tau)=\frac{3}{2}(\pi\nu\tau^{2})^{1/3}+\frac{\pi}{4}
, and its derivative

\dot{x}^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,F_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}{\sqrt{3\pi\nu}}e^{-F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}(-1)^{\nu}\sin(\Omega_\nu(\tau))=2\sqrt{2}F_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)\,x^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}.

(234)

The QFI is then found to be

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{(\dot{x}^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha})^{2}}{1-(x^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha})^{2}}=\frac{8F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}{\nicefrac{{1}}{{(x^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha})^{2}}}-1}=\frac{8F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}{\frac{3\pi\nu\,e^{2F_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)}}{\sin^{2}\Omega_{\nu}}-1}.

(235)

Again, the phase
\Omega_{\nu}
changes on a faster scale than
F_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)
and can be optimized independently. Varying the phase, we see that the QFI oscillates between zero and the maximal value of

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=\frac{8F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)}{3\pi\nu\,e^{2F_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)}-1},

(236)

very similar to Eq. (
213
). Close to a fixed revival, we can choose
F_{\nu}^{2}(\tau|\alpha)
by varying the time. Choosing the optimal value, we find that the maximal value

{\rm QFI}[\rho^{(g/e)}_{\tau|\alpha}]=-8\,W_{0}\left(-\frac{1}{3e\pi\nu}\right)\approx\frac{0.31}{\nu}

(237)

is attained for
F^{2}_{\nu}(\tau|\alpha)=1+W_{0}\left(-\frac{1}{3e\pi\nu}\right)\approx 1-\nicefrac{{0.04}}{{\nu}}
, that is for
\tau\approx\pi\nu\left(2\alpha\pm\left(2-\frac{0.039}{\nu}\right)\right)^{3}

\alpha=100

Appendix E
Optimal successive interaction

In this section, we derive a successive interaction model, where the two-level probe saturates the
{\rm QFI}=4N
of the coherent modes
\ket{\alpha}^{\otimes N}
.

Before, it will be insightful to relax the requirement that the interaction is sequential. Consider the two-dimensional space spanned by the two orthonormal states

\displaystyle\ket{\alpha_N}:=\ket{\alpha}^{\otimes N}\qquad\ket{\dot\alpha_N}:=\frac{(\mathds{1}-\ket{\alpha_N})\frac{\differential}{\differential\alpha}\ket{\alpha_N}}{\left\|(\mathds{1}-\ket{\alpha_N})\frac{\differential}{\differential\alpha}\ket{\alpha_N}\right\|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\big(\ket{\dot\alpha}\ket{\alpha}^{\otimes(N-1)}+\dots+\ket{\alpha}^{\otimes(N-1)}\ket{\dot\alpha}\Big)

(238)

The QFI of the coherent modes is encoded in the two-dimensional subspace spanned by these states, with
\ket{\alpha+ \dd\alpha}^{\otimes N}=\ket{\alpha_N}+\differential\alpha\sqrt{N}\ket{\dot\alpha_N}+O(\differential\alpha^{2})
. It can be mapped onto the two-level probe with the help of the SWAP interaction (as in Eq.
15
)

V^{(N)}=\outerproduct{g,\alpha_N}{g,\alpha_N}+\outerproduct{e,\alpha_N}{g,\dot\alpha_N}+\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha_N}{e,\alpha_N}+\outerproduct{e,\dot\alpha_N}{e,\dot\alpha_N}.

(239)

For
\ket{\alpha+\dd\alpha}^{\otimes N}
, this unitary maps any initial state of the atom into
\ket{g}+\differential\alpha\sqrt{N}\ket{e}
, whose QFI is
4N
. However, the mapping only works well around a fixed
\alpha
,
as the coherent modes in a different state
\ket{\beta}^{\otimes N}
quickly become orthogonal to the two-dimensional subspace described above. Moreover, this effect is amplified with
N
as
\innerproduct{\alpha_N}{\beta_N}=\innerproduct{\alpha}{\beta}^{N}=e^{-N(\alpha-\beta)^{2}/2}
.

Based on this discussion, one can also construct a sequential interaction model achieving the same QFI. To do for the first interaction step, we set

V_{1}=V=\mathds{1}-\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{g,\dot\alpha}-\outerproduct{e, \alpha}{e, \alpha}+\outerproduct{e,\alpha}{g,\dot\alpha}+\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{e,\alpha}

(240)

This maps the state
\ket{\alpha+ \dd\alpha}=\ket{\alpha}+\differential\alpha\ket{\dot\alpha}
of the field mode into
\ket{g}+\differential\alpha\ket{e}
. Right before the second interaction, the state of the atom and the second mode is thus

\Big(\ket{g}+\differential\alpha\ket{e}\Big)\otimes\Big(\ket{\alpha}+\differential\alpha\ket{\dot\alpha}\Big)=\ket{g,\alpha}+\differential\alpha(\ket{e, \alpha}+\ket{g,\dot\alpha})+O(\differential\alpha^{2}).

(241)

It is now the two-dimensional subspace
\left\{\ket{g,\alpha},\ket{\psi_2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{e, \alpha}+\ket{g,\dot\alpha})\right\}
that carries the QFI of the global state. This subspace can again be swapped into the probe by means of the unitary interaction

V_{2}=\mathds{1}-\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{g,\dot\alpha}-\outerproduct{e,\alpha}{e,\alpha}+\outerproduct{e,\alpha}{ \psi_2}+\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{ \psi_2^\perp}

(242)

with
\ket{\psi_2^\perp}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{g,\dot\alpha}-\ket{e, \alpha})
, preparing the atomic state in
\ket{g}+\differential\alpha\sqrt{2}\ket{e}
.

This construction can be generalized to all steps. Using the appropriate interaction

V_{k}=\mathds{1}-\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{g,\dot\alpha}-\outerproduct{e,\alpha}{e,\alpha}+\outerproduct{e,\alpha}{ \psi_k}+\outerproduct{g,\dot\alpha}{ \psi_k^\perp}

(243)

with
\ket{\psi_k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}+1}(\sqrt{k}\ket{e,\alpha}+\ket{g,\dot\alpha})
and
\ket{\psi_k^\perp}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}+1}(\ket{e,\alpha}-\sqrt{k}\ket{g,\dot\alpha})
, one can always swap the appropriate global qubit subspace into the probe

V_{k}:\ket{g,\alpha}+\differential\alpha(\sqrt{k}\ket{e,\alpha}+\ket{g,\dot\alpha})\quad\mapsto\quad\ket{g,\alpha}+\differential\alpha\sqrt{k+1}\ket{e,\alpha},

(244)

ensuring that the probe’s QFI is given by
4k
at all steps.
Hence, applying the successive interactions
V_{1},\dots,V_{N}
with a series of coherent modes
\ket{\alpha}
, leads to the marginal state of the probe with
{\rm QFI}=4N
saturating the upper bound. Importantly, the interactions
V_{k}
are step-dependent and fine-tuned to work around a specific value of
\alpha
.

Appendix G
The continuous quantum field limit

In this section, we compute the limit where the interaction parameter for a single discrete mode is
\tau=\sqrt{\kappa\differential t}
and the number of photons per mode is
\bar{n}=\varepsilon^{2}\differential t
, where
\varepsilon^{2}
is the photon flux and the dimensionless field amplitude
\varepsilon
is our estimated parameter.

First, let us show that in this limit one recovers the atom coupled to a bosonic field models, studied in e.g.
Dąbrowska et al. 2021
;
Fischer et al. 2018
;
Schäfer et al. 2021
. For a single mode, the propagator reads

U_{\tau}^{(t)}=\exp(\tau(a_t \sigma_+ -a_t^\dag\sigma_-))=\exp(\sqrt{\kappa} (\sqrt{\dd t} a_t \sigma_+ -\sqrt{\dd t} a_t^\dag\sigma_-))

(254)

Taking the limit
\differential t\to 0
, one notices that the rescaled operators
a(t)=\frac{a_{t}}{\sqrt{\differential t}}
satisfy the commutation relations of a bosonic field
[a(t),a^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})]=\frac{\delta_{t,t^{\prime}}}{\differential t}\to\delta(t-t^{\prime}).
Hence, we have

U_{\tau}^{(t)}=\exp(\sqrt{\kappa} ( a(t) \sigma_+ - a^\dag(t) \sigma_-)\dd t)=1-\mathrm{i}\,\differential t\,H(t)+O(\differential t^{2})

(255)

with the time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\kappa}(a^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{-}-a(t)\sigma_{+})
coupling the atom to the the quantum field. Next, consider the initial state of the field modes. By assumption, the discrete time modes
a_{t}
are initially prepared in the coherent states
\ket{\alpha}=\mathcal{D}(\alpha)\ket{0}
with
\alpha^{2}=\bar{n}=\varepsilon^{2}\differential t
. The unitary disparagement operators
\mathcal{D}(\alpha)
transform the creation annihilation operators as

\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)a_{t}\mathcal{D}(\alpha)=a_{t}+\alpha\implies\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)a(t)\mathcal{D}(\alpha)=a(t)+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\differential t}}=a(t)+\varepsilon.

(256)

We can rewrite the interaction Hamiltonian with the expression given in the main text as

H(t)=\mathrm{i}\,\sqrt{\kappa}\Big(\sigma_{-}(\varepsilon+a^{\dagger}(t))-\sigma_{+}(\varepsilon+a(t))\Big),

(257)

with
a(t),a^{\dagger}(t)
now describing a vacuum quantum field.

This expression is, however, not needed to derive the reduced dynamics of the atom. Rather, we take the expression (
20
) of the CPTP map

\mathcal{E}_{\tau|\mathfrak{F}}[\bullet]
and expand it for a short times
\tau=\sqrt{\kappa\differential t}\to 0
. Assuming that the state has bounded energy
\tau\sqrt{\hat{n}}\ll 1
, we expand the trigonometric function in the leading and obtain for the Gram matrix

G\approx\tilde{G}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1-\tau^{2}\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle&1-\tau^{2}(\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2})&-\tau\left\langle a^{\dagger}\right\rangle&\tau\left\langle a\right\rangle\\
1-\tau^{2}(\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2})&1-\tau^{2}(\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle+1)&-\tau\left\langle a^{\dagger}\right\rangle&\tau\left\langle a\right\rangle\\
-\tau\left\langle a\right\rangle&-\tau\left\langle a\right\rangle&\tau^{2}(\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle+1)&-\tau^{2}\left\langle a^{{\dagger}2}\right\rangle\\
\tau\left\langle a^{\dagger}\right\rangle&\tau\left\langle a^{\dagger}\right\rangle&-\tau^{2}\left\langle a^{2}\right\rangle&\tau^{2}\left\langle\hat{n}\right\rangle\end{array}\right),

(258)

where the expected values
\left\langle A\right\rangle=\tr A\outerproduct{\mathfrak{F}}{\mathfrak{F}}
are taken on the initial state of the incident radiation mode. Taking the field mode in the coherent state
\ket{\alpha}=\ket{\varepsilon\sqrt{\dd t}}
, we further obtain
G=\tilde{G}+\mathcal{O}((\tau\alpha)^{3})
with

\displaystyle\tilde{G}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1-\tau^{2}\alpha^{2}&1-\tau^{2}(\alpha^{2}+\frac{1}{2})&-\tau\alpha&\tau\alpha\\
1-\tau^{2}(\alpha^{2}+\frac{1}{2})&1-\tau^{2}(\alpha^{2}+1)&-\tau\alpha&\tau\alpha\\
-\tau\alpha&-\tau\alpha&\tau^{2}(\alpha^{2}+1)&-\tau^{2}\alpha^{2}\\
\tau\alpha&\tau\alpha&-\tau^{2}\alpha^{2}&\tau^{2}\alpha^{2}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&1-\frac{\kappa\differential t}{2}&-\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t&\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t\\
1-\frac{\kappa\differential t}{2}&1-\kappa\differential t&-\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t&\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t\\
-\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t&-\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t&\kappa\differential t&0\\
\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t&\sqrt{\kappa}\varepsilon\differential t&0&0\end{array}\right),

where we only kept the leading orders in
\differential t
in the last expression. It is then straightforward to verify that the resulting channel is of the form

\mathcal{E}_{\differential t|\varepsilon}[\bullet]=\sum_{i,j=0}^{3}G_{ji}\,L_{i}\bullet L_{j}^{\dagger},=\bullet+\differential t\left(-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\kappa}\,\varepsilon[\sigma_{y},\bullet]+\kappa\,\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{-}}[\bullet]\right)+O(\differential t^{2}),

(267)

with the dissipator
\mathcal{L}_{A}[\bullet]=A\bullet A^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}A,\bullet\}
(and
\{L_{0},L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\}=\{\outerproduct{g}{g},\outerproduct{e}{e},\sigma_{-},\sigma_{+}\}
). The reduced dynamics of the atom are thus governed by the master equation

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\rho_{t|\varepsilon}=-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\kappa}\,\varepsilon\,[\sigma_{y},\rho_{t|\varepsilon}]+\kappa\,\mathcal{L}_{\sigma_{-}}[\rho_{t|\varepsilon}],

(268)

featuring coherent driving and spontaneous emission.
