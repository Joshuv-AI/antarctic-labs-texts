Estimation of Optimal Causal Bounds via Covariate-Assisted Optimal Transport
Sirui Lin, Zijun Gao, José Blanchet, Peter Glynn

Abstract
We study the estimation of causal estimand involving the joint distribution of treatment and control outcomes for a single unit. In typical causal inference settings, it is impossible to observe both outcomes simultaneously, which places our estimation within the domain of partial identification (PI). Pre-treatment covariates can substantially reduce estimation uncertainty by shrinking the partially identified set. Recently, it was shown that covariate-assisted PI sets can be characterized through conditional optimal transport (COT) problems. However, finite-sample estimation of COT poses significant challenges, primarily because, as we explain, the COT functional is discontinuous under the weak topology, rendering the direct plug-in estimator inconsistent. To address this issue, existing literature relies on relaxations or indirect methods involving the estimation of non-parametric nuisance statistics. In this work, we demonstrate the continuity of the COT functional under a stronger topology induced by the adapted Wasserstein distance. Leveraging this result, we propose a direct, consistent, non-parametric estimator for COT value that avoids nuisance parameter estimation. We derive the convergence rate for our estimator and validate its effectiveness through comprehensive simulations, demonstrating its improved performance compared to existing approaches. Keywords: Conditional optimal transport, adapted Wasserstein distance, partial identification, causal inference.

Introduction

The potential outcome model
[
35
,
22
]
is prevalent in causal inference, where each unit is associated with two potential outcomes: one under treatment and one under control.
Since the two potential outcomes are never observed simultaneously, their joint distribution is not identified, making the causal estimands that depend on the joint distribution only partially identifiable.
Optimal transport
[
39
]
, which minimizes an expected cost over joint distributions respecting the marginals, has been used to recover partial identification (PI) sets of causal estimands
[
19
]
.
When pre-treatment covariates are available, they can often be used to reduce uncertainty about the joint distribution of potential outcomes, and produce smaller, more informative PI sets.
Conditional optimal transport (COT) has been used to characterize covariate-assisted PI sets
[
28
,
14
]
by finding the optimal joint distribution preserving the outcome-covariate distributions.

Despite the potential to yield more meaningful covariate-assisted PI sets, COT poses more substantial statistical challenges compared to OT, especially with continuous covariates.
First, it is rare to observe any continuous covariate value in both treatment and control groups, leaving the conditional marginal distribution ill-defined at that value for at least one group.
Second, even if each covariate value is associated with both a treated and a control unit (such as in twin studies), there is typically only one observation per covariate value in each group.
The plug-in estimator of COT value, which uses point masses to estimate the conditional marginal distributions, may not converge to the true COT value as the sample size goes to infinity (
[
11
]
and Example
3.1
).
This issue suggests that the COT functional may not be continuous under the weak topology, rendering the COT value estimation more challenging than standard OT problems.

In this paper, we demonstrate the continuity of the COT functional under a stronger topology induced by the adapted Wasserstein distance, which requires uniform convergence of the outcome’s conditional distribution at each covariate value.
Leveraging the continuity result, we propose a direct, consistent, non-parametric estimator for the COT value.
Our estimator is constructed using an adapted empirical distribution, which discretizes the covariate space by assigning observed covariates to a finite number of cells.
The convergence of the adapted empirical distribution under the adapted Wasserstein distance ensures the consistency of our estimator, and we further establish its finite-sample error bounds.
Compared to existing estimators for COT value that rely on relaxations of the original COT formulation
[
31
,
28
]
, or utilize indirect approaches that require estimating nuisance functions
[
23
]
, or resort to heuristic procedures without statistical guarantees
[
37
,
9
,
21
]
, our proposed COT value estimator is both direct and statistically consistent.

2.2
PI Sets of Causal Estimands

In this paper, we focus on causal estimands of the form

\displaystyle V^{*}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[h(Y(0),Y(1))].

Here
h:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

A fundamental challenge in causal inference is that
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
cannot be observed simultaneously for the same unit, making the joint distribution
\mu_{Y(0),Y(1)}
inherently unidentifiable. As a result, for causal estimands depending on the joint distribution, their values are not identifiable from observed data
2
2

2

Non-identifiability refers to the fact that two different joint distributions can yield different values of the causal estimand while producing the same distribution over observable quantities.

.
This issue is commonly known as

; see Examples
2.1
,
2.2
for illustration.

Example 2.1

(Variance of treatment effects)
.

For
h(y_{0},y_{1})=(y_{0}-y_{1})^{2}
, the corresponding estimand represents the variance of the treatment effect
3
3

3

This estimand can be used to construct a confidence interval for the average treatment effect
\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)]
in the classic Neymanian framework
[
36
]
.
and is only partially identifiable
[
28
]
.

Example 2.2

(Distributional treatment effects)
.

For
h(y_{0},y_{1})=\mathbb{I}(y_{1}-y_{0}\leq t)
,

(3)

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h(Y(0),Y(1))]:\pi\in\Pi_{\textup{c}}\right\}=[V_{\textup{c}},\tilde{V}_{\textup{c}}].

Here we use the fact that the coupling set

To construct this PI interval, it suffices to compute its lower and upper bounds, starting with

We conclude this section with a discussion of related work on PI sets.
Partial identification has been extensively studied in causal inference and econometrics (see
[
27
]
for a review), and we review three particularly relevant threads. First, copula models
[
32
]
, and particularly the Fréchet–Hoeffding bounds
[
20
,
30
,
13
,
25
]
, have been used to construct PI sets. However, these bounds are limited to univariate responses, only pointwise sharp in settings with more than two margins, and do not necessarily translate into bounds on causal estimands based on an objective function

The second thread of related works explicitly employs the optimal transport

[
18
]

Third,
[

]

In this paper, we estimate the covariate-assisted PI set by directly investigating the primal formulation of a COT problem.

2.3
Conditional Optimal Transport

We begin by reviewing optimal transport (OT), and then extend the discussion to conditional optimal transport (COT).

OT has a rich literature
[
39
]
with broad applications in machine learning and statistics. In particular, OT has recently emerged as a powerful tool in causal inference
[
7
,
10
,
12
,
38
]
.
In an OT problem, the goal is to couple two distributions in a way that minimizes a specified cost function (e.g.,
h
) while preserving their marginal distributions. Formally, the OT distance is defined as

\mathds{W}_{h}(\mu,\nu)\triangleq\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h(X,X^{\prime})],

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is defined in Eq. (
1
). When
h(x,x^{\prime})=\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}
, the OT distance is called Wasserstein
1
-distance and denoted by
\mathds{W}_{1}
. As discussed in Section
2.2
, OT naturally connects to the PI set problem, where
\mathds{W}_{h}(\mu_{Y(0)},\mu_{Y(1)})
can be interpreted as a lower bound of
V^{*}
.

Compared to OT, COT aligns conditional measures across values of the covariates, which
has seen various applications, such as conditional sampling
[
40
,
21
,
4
]
, domain adaptation
[
34
]
, and Bayesian flow matching
[
26
,
11
]
.
In this paper, we focus on the natural connection between COT and the PI problem when covariates are available.
Particularly, recall that in the PI set problem with
Z_{i}
, it becomes essential to preserve the outcome-covariate distributions of
(Y_{i}(0),Z_{i})
and
(Y_{i}(1),Z_{i})
, where
Z_{i}
is shared across both marginals.
The corresponding covariate-assisted lower bound is defined in Eq. (
5
),
which, as shown below, is the optimal objective value of a COT problem.

Proposition 2.2

(Recursive formulation)
.

For a measurable function
h:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
we have

V_{\textup{c}}=\int\mathds{W}_{h}\left(\mu_{Y(0)}^{z},\mu_{Y(1)}^{z}\right){\rm d}\mu_{Z}(z).

We conclude this section with the topology for the COT functional, which motivates our finite-sample estimator.
As we will demonstrate in Section
3
, a key distinction between OT and COT as functionals lies in their topological properties: while the OT functional is continuous with respect to the outcome marginals under the weak topology (metrized by
\mathds{W}_{1}
), the COT functional
V_{\textup{c}}
is discontinuous with respect to the outcome-covariate marginals under the weak topology. However, as shown in Theorem
3.2
,
V_{\textup{c}}
becomes continuous on the set of
\mu
with Lipschitz kernel (see Assp.
3.4
below) under a stronger topology induced by the so-called adapted Wasserstein distance.

Definition 2.1

(Adapted Wasserstein distance)
.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z})
, the adapted Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

\mathds{W}_{\textup{bc}}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{Z},\nu_{Z})}\int\left\|{z-z^{\prime}}\right\|_{{2}}+\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu_{Y}^{z},\nu_{Y}^{z^{\prime}})~{\rm d}\pi(z,z^{\prime}).

3
Basic Properties of COT Value

We first state a set of technical assumptions to enable the analysis below.

Assumption 3.1

(Compact covariate domain)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq[0,1]^{d_{Z}}
.

Assumption 3.2

(Moments of outcome)
.

Assume that, there is a constant
M\in[0,\infty)
, such that

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\left\|{Y(0)}\right\|_{{2}}^{3}+\left\|{Y(1)}\right\|_{{2}}^{3}|Z=z\right]\leq M.

Assumption 3.3

(Smoothness and bound of objective)
.

Assume that
h
is
L_{h}
-Lipschitz continuous:

\left|h(y_{0},y_{1})-h(y_{0}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime})\right|\leq L_{h}\left(\left\|{y_{0}-y_{0}^{\prime}}\right\|_{{2}}+\left\|{y_{1}-y_{1}^{\prime}}\right\|_{{2}}\right)\quad\forall(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime},y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}^{4}.

Assumption 3.4

(Lipschitz kernel)
.

Assume that there is a constant
L_{Z}>0
such that for
k=0,1
,

\displaystyle\mathds{W}_{1}\left(\mu_{Y(k)}^{z_{0}},\mu_{Y(k)}^{z_{1}}\right)\leq L_{Z}\left\|{z_{0}-z_{1}}\right\|_{{2}}\quad\textup{for}~\textup{$\mu_{Z}$-a.e.}~z_{0},z_{1}\in\mathcal{Z}.

Assumption
3.1
and
3.2
impose technical tail conditions on the distribution
\mu_{Z}
and
\mu_{Y(0)}^{z},\mu_{Y(1)}^{z}
. Specifically, we assume that the covariate
Z
is supported on a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
, which is often satisfied in practice—for example, when
Z
includes bounded physical measurements (e.g., age, height, or weight), socioeconomic indicators (e.g., income capped by a known threshold), or categorical variables embedded in Euclidean space. Assumption
3.3
and
3.4
are introduced to establish finite-sample convergence rates, and are aligned with standard regularity conditions in nonparametric estimation theory (e.g.,
[
41
, Theorem 5.65]
).

3.1
Optimal Covariate-assisted PI Sets

In this section, we show that if we only have access to i.i.d. samples drawn from

Theorem 3.1

(Optimal PI bounds)

Suppose that

3.2
Stronger Topology for COT Functional

As indicated by Proposition
2.2
, the estimation of
V_{\textup{c}}
requires estimating the conditional distributions
\mu_{Y(0)}^{z},\mu_{Y(1)}^{z}
. Thus, a direct plug-in estimator does not yield consistent estimates of

, a fact also observed in
[
11
]
. To further illustrate this issue, we present an example adapted from
[
11
, Example 9]
.

Example 3.1

(Inconsistent estimator)
.

Let
m=n
and
h=(y_{0}-y_{1})^{2}
. Suppose that under
\mu
,
Y(0)
,
Y(1)
,
Z
are independent, and
\mu_{Y(0),Z}=\mu_{Y(1),Z}
, thus
V_{\textup{c}}=0
. Let
\hat{\mu}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}(k),Z_{i}(k)},k=0,1
. Further, assume that (i) the
i
-th covariate in the treated group is identical to that in the control group, i.e.,
Z_{i}(1)=Z_{i}(0)~\forall i
, and (ii) the distribution
\mu_{Z}
has a density. Then,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\min_{\pi\in\hat{\Pi}_{\textup{c},n}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h(Y(0),Y(1))]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(Y(0)-Y(1))^{2}]>0=V_{\textup{c}}\qquad\text{a.s.},

where
\hat{\Pi}_{\textup{c},n}=\left\{\pi:\pi_{Y(0),Z}=\hat{\mu}_{0},\pi_{Y(1),Z}=\hat{\mu}_{1}\right\}
. This is because we aim for a perfect match in
Z
while almost surely the values of covariates in the treated group are distinct, i.e.,
Z_{i}(1)\neq Z_{j}(1),i\neq j
. Therefore,
\hat{\Pi}_{\textup{c},n}
only contains a single coupling:
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1),Z_{i}(0)}

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}(Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0))^{2}
, which converges to the population quantity
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[(Y(1)-Y(0))^{2}\right]
.

From a topological perspective, if
V_{\textup{c}}
is viewed as a functional of
\mu_{Y(0),Z}
and
\mu_{Y(1),Z}
, Example

implies that

is discontinuous under the weak topology. However, under mild assumptions,

becomes continuous at

(Continuity under
\mathds{W}_{\textup{bc}}
)
.

Let

(Continuous kernel)
.

Assp.
3.4
can be relaxed to:
g_{k}(z)=\mu_{Y(k)}^{z}\ k=0,1
are continuous under the weak topology. Since

is compact, we get

4.1

4.1.1
Adapted COT value estimator

In this section, we introduce our adapted COT value estimator in the setting where the treatment is independent of both the observed outcome and covariates. This setting is related to a scenario referred to as the Bernoulli design in causal inference, where

\mu_{Y(0),Z}
, with an analogous interpretation for the treated group.

Assumption 4.1

(Bernoulli design)
.

Assume that
((Y_{i}(0),Z_{i}(0)),i\in[n])

\mu_{Y(0),Z}
, and
((Y_{j}(1),Z_{j}(1)),j\in[m])
are drawn i.i.d. from
\mu_{Y(1),Z}
.

Inspired by

3
]

Definition 4.1

(Cell-center projection)

Let

into a disjoint union of

\varphi_{r}^{n}:[0,1]^{d_{Z}}\to[0,1]^{d_{Z}}

Definition 4.2

(Adapted empirical distribution)
.

The adapted empirical distributions are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(0),Z(0),n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}(0),\varphi_{r}^{n}(Z_{i}(0))},\quad\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(1),Z(1),m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{Y_{j}(1),\varphi_{r}^{m}(Z_{j}(1))},

(6)

where
\varphi_{r}^{n},\varphi_{r}^{m}
are defined in Definition
4.1
.

Compared to
[
3
]
, our construction of
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
has a key distinction: we discretize only the covariate
Z
, not the outcome
Y
, since discretization is required only for the variables being conditioned on.

If
z\mapsto\mu_{Y(i)}^{z}
is continuous under the weak topology, the adapted empirical distributions (
6
) provide consistent approximations to the conditional distributions
\mu_{Y(k)}^{z},k=0,1
, thereby mitigating the issue that each sample of
Z(0)
or
Z(1)
is associated with only a single observed outcome
Y
when
\mu_{Z}
has a density. Further,
[
3
]
shows that
(\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(k),Z(k),n},k=0,1)
converges to their weak limits under
\mathds{W}_{\textup{bc}}
, which is aligned with the conditions introduced in Theorem
3.2
.

Note that larger discretization cells contain more observations, and the resulting local distribution estimator is associated with smaller variance but larger bias. We can choose the cell size to balance the bias-variance trade-off, where the optimal cell size depends on factors including the sample size. As the sample size may differ between treatment and control groups, for example, when units are assigned to treatment with relatively low probability, we discretize each group separately to allow for distinct optimal cell sizes.
In addition, in typical causal inference datasets, the covariate data for the treatment and control groups are not perfectly matched, also leading to a potential mismatch in the supports of the adapted empirical distributions in (
6
). To address the mismatch issue, we match discretized covariates
Z(0)
from the control group with discretized covariates
Z(1)
from the treated group that may differ slightly in value. This matching is formalized through a coupling with marginals
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0),n}
and
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(1),m}
.
Based on this coupling, we define our adapted COT value estimator as follows.

Definition 4.3

(Adapted COT value estimator)
.

Given a coupling
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}\in\Pi(\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0),n},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(1),m})
, the COT value estimator is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{n,m}\triangleq\int\mathds{W}_{h}(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{z_{0}}_{Y(0),n},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{z_{1}}_{Y(1),m}){\rm d}\hat{\pi}_{n,m}(z_{0},z_{1}).

(7)

The COT value estimator achieves consistency as the sample size grows, provided that the coupling
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}
converges to a joint distribution that exactly matches the limiting marginals.

Theorem 4.1

(Consistency)
.

Under Assp.
3.1
-
3.4
, and assume that as
n,m\rightarrow\infty
,
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}
weakly converges to
(\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id})_{\#}\mu_{Z}
almost surely, then
\hat{V}_{n,m}
converges to
V_{\textup{c}}
almost surely.

4.1.2
Convergence Rate Analysis

Next, we provide the finite-sample convergence rate for the adapted COT value estimator. We first introduce an assumption to quantify the discrepancy between the estimated coupling
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}
and the identity coupling
(\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id})_{\#}\mu_{Z}
, and to ensure that this error aligns with the granularity of the discretization, as controlled by the parameter
r
in the map
\varphi_{r}^{n}
.

Assumption 4.2

(Coupling gap)
.

Assume that
\mathbb{E}[\int\left\|{z_{0}-z_{1}}\right\|_{{2}}{\rm d}\hat{\pi}_{n,m}(z_{0},z_{1})]\leq C_{\hat{\pi}}(n\wedge m)^{-r}
for
n,m\geq 1
, where
C_{\hat{\pi}}
is a constant independent of
n,m
.

Theorem 4.2

(Finite-sample complexity)
.

Under Assp.
3.1
-
4.1
, and Assp.
4.2
with
r=\frac{1}{d_{Z}+2\vee d_{Y}}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{V}_{n,m}-V_{\textup{c}}|\right]\leq\bar{C}\gamma_{d_{Y},d_{Z}}(m\wedge n),

where

\gamma_{d_{Y},d_{Z}}(N)\triangleq\begin{cases}N^{-\frac{1}{d_{Z}+2\vee d_{Y}}}\log(N)&\textup{if}\ d_{Y}\neq 2,\\
N^{-\frac{1}{d_{Z}+2\vee d_{Y}}}&\textup{if}\ d_{Y}=2.\end{cases}

Here,
\bar{C}
is a constant that depends on
d_{Z},d_{Y}
,
M
(Assp.
3.2
),
L_{h}
(Assp.
3.3
), and
L_{Z}
(Assp.
3.4
).

One natural choice of
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}
that satisfies Assp.
4.2
is an optimal coupling of
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0),n}
and
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(1),m}
:

\hat{\pi}_{n,m}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\pi\in\Pi(\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0),n},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(1),m})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|Z(0)-Z(1)\|_{2}].

(8)

Remark 4.1

(Robustness)
.

An additional advantage of using the coupling
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}
defined above is its robustness to potential data perturbations that cause a mismatch between the covariate distributions of the treatment and control groups at the population level. Specifically, consider a perturbation such that
\mu_{Z}=\mu_{Z(0)}
, while the distribution of covariates in the treatment group satisfies
\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu_{Z},\mu_{Z(1)})\leq\epsilon
. Then, the adapted COT value estimator based on
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}
enjoys a robustness guarantee in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{V}_{n,m}-V_{\textup{c}}|\right]\leq L_{h}L_{Z}\epsilon+\bar{C}\gamma_{d_{Y},d_{Z}}(m\wedge n).

This advantage is further demonstrated empirically in Section
5
.

Remark 4.2
.

In the literature of COT, a variety of algorithmic strategies have been explored to deal with the inherent computational complexity of COT, including adversarial optimization via GANs
[
4
]
, entropic regularization
[
5
]
, relaxations based on KL divergence
[
37
,
31
]
, and optimal transport approximations
[
11
]
. While computational methods for COT have advanced significantly, rigorous statistical guarantees for value estimation have only begun to emerge in recent work
[
23
,
31
,
28
]
.
Our work contributes to the statistical aspect of the COT literature by proposing a finite-sample estimator of the COT value with a provable convergence rate.

4.2
Covariate-Dependent Treatment Assignment

Unlike Bernoulli design where treatment assignment is independent of covariates by design, observational studies typically exhibit covariate-dependent treatment assignment. This results in non-constant propensity scores

e(z)=\mathbb{P}(W_{i}=1\mid Z_{i}=z),

reflecting heterogeneity in treatment likelihood across covariate profiles. In this section, we discuss how to modify the adapted COT value estimator to fit this situation and characterize the associated finite-sample convergence rate.

Under the unconfoundedness assumption (Assp.
2.2
), the conditional distributions of potential outcomes given covariates remain identifiable, i.e., consistent with
\mu_{Y(k)}^{z}
for
k=0,1
. However, since the treatment assignment probability depends on covariates, the marginal distribution of covariates may differ between the treatment group and the control group, inducing a covariate shift.
To formalize this setting with covariate shift, we consider the following super-population model of
Y(0),Y(1),Z,W
following the joint distribution
\mu=\mu_{Y(0),Y(1),Z,W}
.

Assumption 4.3

(Covariate shift)
.

Assume that
((Z_{i}(0),Y_{i}(0)),i\in[n])
are drawn i.i.d. from
\mu_{Z|W=0}\otimes\mu_{Y(0)}^{Z}
, and
((Z_{j}(1),Y_{j}(1)),j\in[m])
are drawn i.i.d. from
\mu_{Z|W=1}\otimes\mu_{Y(1)}^{Z}
.

Since our estimand of interest
V^{*}
relies on the marginal covariate distribution, irrespective of treatment assignment, it is essential to correct for the distributional shift induced by non-constant propensity scores. Specifically, we reweight the samples to align with the marginal covariate distribution.
By Proposition
2.1
, when the propensity score
e(z)
is known, the appropriate population-level weighting is given by

\displaystyle w_{i}=\frac{\mu_{Z}(z)}{\mu_{Z\mid W=0}(z)}\propto(1-e(z))^{-1},\ W_{i}=1,\quad\xi_{j}=\frac{\mu_{Z}(z)}{\mu_{Z\mid W=1}(z)}\propto e^{-1}(z),\ W_{j}=0.

On finite samples, when the propensity score
e(z)
is unknown but estimated by
\hat{e}(z)
, we adopt the self-normalized weight,

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{i}=\frac{(1-\hat{e}(Z_{i}(0)))^{-1}}{\sum_{l=1}^{n}(1-\hat{e}(Z_{l}(0)))^{-1}},\ i\in[n],\quad\hat{\xi}_{j}=\frac{\hat{e}^{-1}(Z_{j}(1))}{\sum_{l=1}^{m}\hat{e}^{-1}(Z_{l}(1))},\ j\in[m].

(9)

to avoid extreme values and ensure the weights sum to one.
To adapt the construction of the adapted COT value estimator with discretization to the setting in Assp.
4.3
, we modify the weights in Eq. (
9
) by evaluating the estimated propensity score at the projected covariate values rather than the original values.
Finally, denoting the cells corresponding to projection map
\varphi_{r}^{n}
by
\Phi_{r}^{n}\triangleq\left\{(\varphi_{r}^{n})^{-1}(\{x\}):x\in\varphi_{r}^{n}([0,1]^{d_{Z}})\right\}
,
our reweighted adapted empirical distributions are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(0),Z(0),n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{G\in\Phi_{r}^{n}}\sum_{i:Z_{i}(0)\in G}\frac{(1-\hat{e}(\varphi_{r}^{n}(G)))^{-1}}{\sum_{G}(1-\hat{e}(\varphi_{r}^{n}(G)))^{-1}|\{i:Z_{i}(0)\in G\}|}\delta_{Y_{i}(0),\varphi_{r}^{n}(Z_{i}(0))},

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(1),Z(1),m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{G\in\Phi_{r}^{m}}\sum_{j:Z_{j}(1)\in G}\frac{\hat{e}(\varphi_{r}^{m}(G))^{-1}}{\sum_{G}\hat{e}(\varphi_{r}^{m}(G))^{-1}|\{j:Z_{j}(1)\in G\}|}\delta_{Y_{j}(1),\varphi_{r}^{m}(Z_{j}(0))},

where
\varphi_{r}^{n}(G)
is the cell center of
G\in\Phi_{r}^{n}
.
We further employ
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(0),Z(0),n}
and
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(1),Z(1),m}
, along with
\hat{\pi}_{n,m}
defined in Eq. (
8
),
to construct the adapted COT value estimator introduced in Definition
4.3
.

We implement a two-fold cross-fitting procedure to ensure the independence between the estimated propensity score
\hat{e}
and the adapted empirical distributions
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
. Specifically, we randomly split the data into two folds of approximately equal size. For each fold, we construct
\hat{e}
using data from the other fold, and then construct
\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(0),Z(0),n},\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(1),Z(1),m}
on the current fold with the estimated propensity score plugged in. This yields two estimates of the lower bound, and we further average them to form our final estimator
\hat{V}_{n,m}
.

We introduce the assumptions on the estimation error of
\hat{e}(\cdot)
, which are used by the convergence analysis of
\hat{V}_{n,m}
.

Assumption 4.4

(Lipschitz
\hat{e}
)
.

Assume that there is a constant
L_{e}
, such that
|\hat{e}(z)-\hat{e}(z^{\prime})|\leq L_{e}\left\|{z-z^{\prime}}\right\|_{{2}}
for any
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{2}
.

Assumption 4.5

(Bounds of
\hat{e}
)
.

Assume that there is a constant
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, such that
\eta\leq\hat{e}(z)\leq 1-\eta
for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Assumption 4.6

(Average error of propensity score)
.

Assume that for
m,n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|n\hat{w}_{i}-\frac{{\rm d}\mu}{{\rm d}\mu_{|W=0}}(Z_{i})\right|\right]\leq C_{w}n^{-r},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|m\hat{\xi}_{i}-\frac{{\rm d}\mu}{{\rm d}\mu_{|W=1}}(Z_{i})\right|\right]\leq C_{w}m^{-r},

where
C_{w}
is a constant independent of
n,m
.

Assp.
4.6
characterizes the average estimation error of the estimated propensity score function
\hat{e}
. Notably, we impose a mild convergence rate requirement of order
O(n^{-r})
, where
r
is chosen to be less than
1/d_{Z}
. This rate aligns with typical nonparametric convergence rates for nuisance parameter estimation. Assp.
4.4
and
4.5
impose boundedness and smoothness conditions on
\hat{e}(\cdot)
. Particularly, the smoothness condition is necessary because we evaluate
\hat{e}
at the centers of discretized cells, as discussed in the following.

Theorem 4.3

(Finite-sample complexity with covariate shift)
.

Under Assp.
2.3
,
3.1
-
3.4
,

4.3
-
4.6
with
r=\frac{1}{d_{Z}+2\vee d_{Y}}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{V}_{n,m}-V_{\textup{c}}|\right]\leq C^{\dagger}\gamma_{d_{Y},d_{Z}}(m\wedge n),

where
C^{\dagger}
is a constant that depends on
d_{Z},d_{Y}
,

\delta
(Assp.
2.3
),
M
(Assp.
3.2
),
L_{h}
(Assp.
3.3
),
L_{Z}
(Assp.
3.4
),

L_{e}
(Assp.
4.4
),
\eta
(Assp.
4.5
) and
C_{w}
(Assp.
4.6
).

The error rate of
\hat{V}_{n,m}
under non-constant propensity scores (Theorem
4.3
) matches that in the case of homogeneous propensity scores (Theorem
4.2
).

Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we obtain the optimal covariate-assisted partial identification sets for a class of causal estimands by solving COT problems.
Our finite-sample estimator of the COT value avoids nuisance function estimation, does not require well-specified models, and enjoys a provable statistical convergence rate.
Furthermore, our adapted COT value estimator answers the question proposed in the discussion of
[
28
]
on how to combine the adapted Wasserstein distance into the COT value estimation. Specifically, our adapted estimator is their
V_{\text{causal}}(\eta)
when
\eta
approached infinity.

A direction for future research is to replace the current equal-sized cells with data-driven counterparts.
Specifically, we can apply tree-based methods to the control and treatment group to identify regions where the distribution of
Y_{i}(0)
is approximately homogeneous, and then project the covariates of control units onto the centers of these regions. Such data-driven partitions avoid splitting on covariates that are not influential to the outcome distribution and thus mitigate the curse of dimensionality. Another future direction is to move beyond estimation and conduct inference for the PI interval.
One possible approach is deriving the asymptotic distribution of our estimator, potentially leveraging recent central limit theorem results for optimal transport mappings
[
29
]
, at least for moderately large
d_{Y}
,
d_{Z}
.

A.1
Regular Kernel

In this paper, we always assume that joint distribution like
\mu_{Y,Z}
that can be written as
\mu_{Y,Z}=\mu_{Z}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{Z}
, i.e. for any measurable function
g
,
\int g(y,z){\rm d}\mu=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}g(y,z){\rm d}\mu_{Y}^{z}(y){\rm d}\mu_{Z}(z)
, with a regular kernel
\mu_{Y}^{z}
. The definition of a regular kernel is stated as follows.

Definition A.1

(Regular kernel)
.

A kernel
\mu_{Y}^{z}
is regular if (i) for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,
\int\mathbf{1}(y\in\cdot){\rm d}\mu_{Y}^{z}(y)
is a probability measure, and (ii) for any measurable set
B\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,
z\mapsto\int\mathbf{1}(y\in B){\rm d}\mu_{Y}^{z}(y)
is a measurable function.

We can always assume that
\mu
has a regular kernel due to the following result.

Proposition A.1

(Disintegration
[
24
, Theorem 5.3, 5.4]
)
.

If
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}
and is equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, then for a measure
\mu_{Y,Z}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z})
, there is a regular kernel
\mu_{Y}^{z}
such that
\int g(y,z){\rm d}\mu=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}g(y,z){\rm d}\mu_{Y}^{z}(y){\rm d}\mu_{Z}(z)
for any measurable function
g
. Further,
\mu_{Y}^{z}
is unique
\mu_{Z}
-a.e.

A.2
Wasserstein vs. Adapted Wasserstein: A Distance Metric Comparison

This section presents the two metrics employed to study the continuity of COT.

Definition A.2

(Wasserstein distance)
.

The
1
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)\triangleq\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\left\|{X(0)-X(1)}\right\|_{{2}}].

The
1
-Wasserstein distances possess key properties that will be instrumental in our proof.

Lemma A.1

(Triangle inequality)
.

\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)+\mathds{W}_{1}(\nu,\mu^{\prime})
.

Lemma A.2

(Convexity)
.

The map
\mu\mapsto\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
is convex.

For
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Y}
, the so-called adapted Wasserstein distance induces a topology that is typically stronger than the weak topology, which can be metrized by the Wasserstein distances.

Definition A.3

(Adapted Wasserstein distance)
.

The adapted Wasserstein distances between
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathds{W}_{\textup{bc}}(\mu,\nu)\triangleq\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\textup{bc}}(\mu,\nu)}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\left\|{X(0)-X(1)}\right\|_{{2}}],

where
X(0)=(Z(0),Y(0)),X(1)=(Z(1),Y(1))
, and

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textup{bc}}(\mu,\nu)\triangleq

\displaystyle\left\{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu):\text{under}~\pi,~Z(1)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y(0)\text{ conditioned on }Z(0),\right.

\displaystyle\ \left.Z(0)\perp\!\!\!\perp Y(1)\text{ conditioned on }Z(1)\right\}.

The following proposition is stated to maintain consistency with Definition
2.1
introduced earlier.

Proposition A.2

(Reformulation of adapted Wasserstein distance; see, e.g.
[
2
, Proposition 5.2]
)
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{Z}\otimes\mu_{Y}^{Z}
and
\nu=\nu_{Z}\otimes\nu_{Y}^{Z}
, we have

\mathds{W}_{\textup{bc}}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{Z},\nu_{Z})}\int\left\|{z-z^{\prime}}\right\|_{{2}}+\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu_{Y}^{z},\nu_{Y}^{z^{\prime}})~{\rm d}\pi(z,z^{\prime}).

Since
\Pi_{\textup{bc}}(\mu,\nu)\subseteq\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, clearly
\mathds{W}_{1}\leq\mathds{W}_{\textup{bc}}
, but the reverse may not hold. Consider an i.i.d. sample
(X_{i}=(Z_{i},Y_{i}),i\in[n])
drawn from a distribution
\mu
, and let
\hat{\mu}_{n}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
. It is well known that
\mathds{W}_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)
converges to zero almost surely as
n
goes to infinity. However, as shown in
[
33
, Proposition 1]
,
\mathds{W}_{\textup{bc}}(\hat{\mu}_{n},\mu)
may fail to converge to zero, and a remedy to ensure convergence is to replace
\hat{\mu}_{n}
with its convolution with a suitable kernel function. The intuition behind this approach is to ensure that the kernel of the convoluted empirical distribution becomes smooth in the variable it is conditioned on.
This idea aligns with the findings of
[
8
]
, which show that for distributions with Lipschitz kernels, the weak topology and the topology induced by the adapted Wasserstein distance are equivalent.

However, since the convolved empirical distribution is not discrete, its practical implementation requires an additional sampling step for computation. To address this,
[
3
]
propose a discrete alternative—the adapted empirical distribution—which mitigates the convergence issue by assigning the sample
(X_{i})_{i\in[n]}
to a finite number of cells, which is the approach we adopt in this paper.

\displaystyle\mathds{W}_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{Y(0)}^{G},\mu_{Y(0)}^{G})+\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu_{Y(0)}^{G},\mu_{Y(0)}^{\zeta}).

\displaystyle(\textup{by Lemma~\ref{lemm:C7}})

By the convexity of

(Lemma
A.2
) and Assp.
3.4
, for

\displaystyle\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu^{G}_{Y(0)},\mu^{\zeta}_{Y(0)})=

\displaystyle\mathds{W}_{1}\left(\int_{G}\mu^{\zeta^{\prime}}_{Y(0)}{\rm d}\mu_{Z}(\zeta^{\prime}),\mu^{\zeta}_{Y(0)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{G}\mathds{W}_{1}(\mu^{\zeta^{\prime}}_{Y(0)},\mu^{\zeta}_{Y(0)}){\rm d}\mu_{Z}(\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{G}L_{Z}\left\|{\zeta^{\prime}-\zeta}\right\|_{{2}}{\rm d}\mu_{Z}(\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle(\textup{due to Assp.~\ref{a:Lip_kernel}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{Z}\sup_{\zeta^{\prime}\in G}\left\|{\zeta-\zeta^{\prime}}\right\|_{{2}}.

\displaystyle(\textup{due to Assp.~\ref{a:compact_domain}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{Z}\sqrt{d_{Z}}n^{-r}

Back to (

), by Lemma
E.1
that
\hat{\mu}_{Z(0)}(G)=\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0)}(G)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\zeta\in\varphi_{r}^{n}([0,1]^{d_{Z}})}\mathds{W}_{1}(\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Y(0)}^{\zeta},\mu_{Y(0)}^{\zeta})\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0)}(\zeta)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{G\in\Phi_{r}^{n}}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0)}(G)\left(\mathds{W}_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{Y(0)}^{G},\mu_{Y(0)}^{G})+L_{Z}\sqrt{d_{Z}}n^{-r}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{G\in\Phi_{r}^{n}}\hat{\bm{\mu}}_{Z(0)}(G)\mathds{W}_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{Y(0)}^{G},\mu_{Y(0)}^{G})+L_{Z}\sqrt{d_{Z}}n^{-r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{G\in\Phi_{r}^{n}}\hat{\mu}_{Z(0)}(G)\mathds{W}_{1}(\hat{\mu}_{Y(0)}^{G},\mu_{Y(0)}^{G})+L_{Z}\sqrt{d_{Z}}n^{-r}.

\displaystyle(\textup{by Lemma~\ref{lemm:C7}})

Plugging back to (
17
) proves the desired result.
∎

Proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
