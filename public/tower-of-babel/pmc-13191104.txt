Topical framework nucleic acid transdermal delivery system for reprogramming cutaneous dendritic cells to maintain graft immune homeostasis
Authors: Xin Qin, Tong Wei, Zhaoying Zhang, Han Qin, Yunfeng Lin, Jinlin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune rejection is a major challenge for the long-term survival of allogeneic skin grafts. Current immunosuppressive therapies are usually administered systemically to mitigate rejection; however, they have substantial side effects. Therefore, here, a localized immunomodulatory strategy based on tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) is proposed to improve the survival of allogeneic skin grafts. A mircoRNA (miR-23b) and a dendritic cell-targeting aptamer is conjugated to tFNAs to create a complex (termed TR), which is embedded in a four-armed PEG hydrogel (MixPEG) matrix to form MixPEG-TR. TR targets dendritic cells (DCs) in the skin to induce tolerogenic DCs by leveraging the transdermal delivery capability of tFNAs and the sustained release provided by the hydrogel, thereby establishing persistent local immune tolerance. The local application of this formulation reduces inflammatory cell infiltration, promotes uniform dermal collagen deposition, and markedly prolongs graft survival in a mouse allogeneic skin graft model. These findings highlight a transdermal, DC-targeted nucleic acid delivery strategy capable of enhancing local immune tolerance and alleviating inflammatory responses, thereby potentially maintaining graft-associated immune homeostasis and providing a safer, site-specific platform for immunomodulation in skin transplantation and other cutaneous immune-mediated conditions.Keywords: Allogenic graft, Graft rejection, Immunosuppressive therapy, Dendritic cells, Tetrahedral Framework Nucleic Acids (tFNAs), Nanodrug delivery systems

Introduction

Allogeneic skin grafts are commonly used as temporary biological dressings for severe burns and extensive trauma, aiding in rapid wound closure when autologous skin is insufficient, thereby reducing fluid loss and infection risk [1].However, skin tissue is highly immunogenic; grafts are rich in dendritic cells (DCs) and express high levels of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, readily eliciting acute rejection [2,3].While systemic immunosuppressants (e.g., cyclosporine A and tacrolimus) can delay rejection, their long-term use often leads to infection, malignancies, and hepatorenal toxicity [4].Consequently, most allogeneic skin grafts undergo necrosis and sloughing within a few weeks, limiting their clinical utility [5].Therefore, inducing immune tolerance in skin grafts through localized, noninvasive delivery could potentially prolong graft survival while reducing systemic toxicity, thereby improving treatment safety and efficacy, highlighting the clinical and translational importance of establishing stable local immune tolerance in allogeneic skin transplantation.

DCs are key antigen-presenting cells that initiate immune responses during allogeneic skin graft rejection, and their activation state determines whether naïve T cells differentiate into effector T cells (Teffs) or regulatory T cells (Tregs) [6,7]. After capturing donor antigens at the graft site, DCs become activated, migrate to draining lymph nodes and the spleen, and present MHC-bound peptides to initiate primary T cell responses [6,7]Mature DCs upregulate MHC II along with co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, providing strong co-stimulation that drives Teff expansion and amplifies inflammation [8,9].In contrast, tolerogenic DCs (tolDCs) downregulate co-stimulatory molecules and secrete IL-10, thereby suppressing Teffs, promoting Tregs, and maintaining local immune homeostasis through negative feedback [10].

Unlike approaches targeting downstream elements, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), macrophage polarization, or immune checkpoints (PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4) [11,12], directly intervening at the level of DCs – the “source” of the immune response – can more effectively redirect T cell differentiation and fundamentally reduce the risk of rejection. Consequently, various strategies have been explored to induce tolDCs, including immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β) [13], pharmacological agents such as glucocorticoids combined with vitamin D3 [14], IL-10-engineered DC therapies [15],and nano-delivery systems [16]. However, most rely on systemic administration or complex ex vivo manipulation, limiting local efficacy and increasing systemic side effects.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play pivotal regulatory roles in innate and adaptive immunity [17].Among them, miR-23b is particularly important for DC differentiation and autoimmune inflammation. Zheng et al. demonstrated that upregulating miR-23b in murine bone marrow–derived and human monocyte–derived DCs drives a tolerogenic phenotype (elevated IL-10, reduced IL-12) and enhances the induction of Foxp3+ Tregs by inhibiting the Notch1/NF-κB pathway [18]. Similarly, Stumpfová et al. reported that tolDCs induced by IL-10/TGF-β exhibit a characteristic miRNA expression profile that includes miR-23b, consistent with their tolerogenic phenotype. miR-23b is downregulated in rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus models; therefore, restoring miR-23b expression suppresses NF-κB activation by targeting TAB2/TAB3/IKK-α, thereby inhibiting IL-17–mediated autoimmune inflammation [19]. Additionally, in an experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis model, miR-23b overexpression reduced Th1/Th17 responses and ameliorated disease by targeting CCL7 to inhibit leukocyte migration [20]. Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-23b has the capacity to modulate the NF-κB–IL-17 axis and antigen presentation through multiple targets, making it a promising nucleic acid agent for inducing immune tolerance.

Aptamers are single-stranded nucleic acid ligandsgenerated by Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential Enrichment (SELEX) that bind target proteins with high affinity and specificity [21,22]. Using SELEX, a DNA aptamer against the DC surface receptor, CD209a, was identified as a feasible tool for DC-targeted delivery [23]. Compared to antibodies, aptamers have a smaller molecular size, low immunogenicity, are easy to synthesize and chemically modify, readily conjugate with nanocarriers, and have shown great diagnostic and therapeutic potential in various infectious and immune-related diseases [24].However, both miRNAs and aptamers are susceptible to nuclease degradation in vivo, resulting in short half-lives, while transdermal delivery must additionally overcome the stratum corneum and dense dermal barriers that severely restrict penetration [25,26]. Therefore, an efficient carrier capable of protecting nucleic acids, enabling transdermal transport, and achieving targeted delivery of miR-23b to skin-resident DCs is urgently needed to induce local immune tolerance at graft sites [27]. Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) are well-defined three-dimensional DNA nanostructures that self-assemble from oligonucleotides and possess programmable architecture, high stability, and low immunogenicity, making them promising platforms for nucleic acid delivery [[28], [29], [30], [31]].

Notably, tFNAs resist nuclease degradation under physiological conditions, and their nanoscale size and specific spatial configuration facilitate crossing biological barriers (such as the skin) and ensure stable distribution within tissues [32]. Recent studies have demonstrated their effectiveness as programmable carriers for transdermal and systemic delivery, including microRNA transport, wound repair, blood–brain barrier penetration, and immunomodulation across inflammatory and autoimmune disease models [33].For example, a pH-responsive DNA tetrahedron/methotrexate complex targets the inflammatory synovial tissue, modulates macrophage polarization, and alleviates rheumatoid arthritis [34].Additionally, a tFNAs-based nanovaccine integrating an antigenic peptide, RNA adjuvant modifications induced tolDCs in a systemic lupus erythematosus model in situ, re-establishing antigen-specific immune tolerance [35]. [36] Together, these studies demonstrate that tFNAs possess excellent barrier-penetration capability and the capacity to modulate immune cells, rendering them suitable carriers for immunoregulatory nucleic acids.

However, free tFNAs applied to the skin exhibit limited residence time, are readily diluted by body fluids, and require time to penetrate the dermis, making it difficult to maintain effective local concentrations during the critical early post-transplant period [37]. Therefore, we introduced a four-arm polyethylene glycol hydrogel (MixPEG) as an adhesive sustained-release matrix to prolong local retention. MixPEG forms rapidly in situ through NHS-ester/amine crosslinking of 4-arm PEG derivatives, generating a stable three-dimensional network capable of encapsulating diverse therapeutics.

Previous studies have demonstrated that MixPEG hydrogels gel rapidly, adhere tightly to tissue surfaces, and enable sustained release while providing protective sealing and microenvironment modulation, thereby facilitating wound repair and inflammation control [38]. [39] These findings indicate that MixPEG hydrogels have excellent tissue adhesiveness, sealing capacity, and tunable release characteristics, making them well-suited as transdermal delivery platforms at skin barrier sites.

Accordingly, to address the limitations of systemic immunosuppression and the lack of precise local nucleic acid delivery in allogeneic skin transplantation, we developed a multifunctional tFNAs-based system for targeted immune modulation. A DC-targeting aptamer and miR-23b were integrated into tFNAs to construct tFNAs@Apt@miR-23b (TR), enabling selective delivery to skin-resident DCs and induction of immune tolerance. TR was further embedded within the adhesive MixPEG hydrogel to provide sustained local retention and barrier protection at the graft surface. We hypothesized that this combined “MixPEG-TR” platform would achieve transdermal, targeted, and durable reprogramming of DC function, thereby promoting local immune tolerance and prolonging graft survival, offering a new strategy for localized treatment of immune-mediated disorders.

Results and discussion

Characterization of tFNAs and TR

The synthesis and assembly of TR and MixPEG-TR are outlined in Fig. 1a. In the 8% PAGE analysis (Fig. 1b), the bands corresponding to tFNAs, an intermediate aptamer-functionalized tetrahedron (TDC), and TR migrated more slowly (i.e., appeared at a higher molecular weight) than the single-stranded components (S1-DC, S2, S3-end, and S4). Moreover, the band mobility decreased stepwise from tFNAs to TDC to TR, indicating the successful sequential assembly of the aptamer and miR-23b onto the tFNAs framework. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements (Fig. 1c) showed that the average hydrodynamic diameter of tFNAs was 12.44 ± 1.93 nm, which increased to 16.78 ± 2.47 nm upon loading with miR-23b (forming TR). This increase in size confirms that the miRNA was successfully loaded onto the tetrahedral framework. Additionally, the particle size distribution curves for the tFNAs and TR were monomodal with narrow peak widths, indicating a uniform particle size distribution and good dispersion of the complexes. Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 1d) revealed that the ζ-potential of tFNAs was −5.60 ± 3.25 mV, which shifted to −6.30 ± 3.36 mV for TR, reflecting the successful complexation of negatively charged miR-23b with negatively charged tFNAs) via complementary base pairing. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Fig. 1e) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, Fig. 1f) revealed the characteristic triangular geometries of tFNAs and TR, further verifying the formation of the desired tetrahedral nanostructures.

Intracellular distribution of TR

Confocal fluorescence microscopy was used to examine the intracellular distribution of TR proteins in DCs. One of the single strands (S2) was labeled with a red fluorescent dye (Cy5) to assemble the Cy5-labeled TR (Cy5-TR) for tracking purposes. DCs were treated with Cy5-TR or Cy5-labeled S2 (as a control), following which, the cells were stained for F-actin (with phalloidin, green) and nuclei (DAPI, blue) and imaged by confocal microscopy (Fig. 1g). Cy5 fluorescence (red) was broadly distributed in the cytoplasm of DCs treated with Cy5-TR, whereas the red signal remained mainly around the cell periphery in cells treated with S2-Cy5 (lacking the tetrahedral structure). This indicates that the tetrahedral nanostructure significantly enhanced the intracellular delivery efficiency of the nucleic acid payload. Flow cytometry was then performed to quantitatively assess the cellular uptake (Fig. 1h). After 24 h of incubation, the percentage of Cy5-positive cells in the Cy5-TR group (approximately 86.2%) was markedly higher than that in the S2-Cy5 group (approximately 34.6%), demonstrating that TR was efficiently internalized by DCs and broadly distributed in the cytoplasm.

Construction and characterization of MixPEG-TR

We encapsulated TR in a four-armed PEG-crosslinked hydrogel (MixPEG) to construct a composite MixPEG-TR system to improve its local retention and transdermal delivery efficiency. MixPEG hydrogels are biocompatible, have tunable porous structures, and exhibit inherent tissue adhesion, enabling stable drug loading and sustained release. PEG-based hydrogels have been extensively studied and used in wound healing, drug delivery, hemostasis, and anti-infection applications, supporting their use in our system. The MixPEG-TR precursor solution was fluid and transparent before curing but rapidly converted into a semi-solid gel upon mixing the two 4-arm PEG components, indicating the rapid formation of a stable crosslinked network (Fig. 1i). SEM imaging (Fig. 1j) revealed that MixPEG, MixPEG-tFNAs, and MixPEG-TR hydrogels all exhibited a continuous, uniform porous microstructures, with pore sizes predominantly in the 120–200 μm range. This homogeneous porous network provides ample structural space for loading TR and modulating its release. Subsequent rheological measurements (Fig. 1k) showed that the storage modulus G′ exceeded the loss modulus G″ for all hydrogel samples, consistent with characteristic gel behavior. Among them, MixPEG-TR exhibited a higher G′ than the other groups, indicating that incorporation of TR enhanced the elasticity and structural integrity of the hydrogel. Attenuated total reflectance FTIR (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was used to analyze the chemical compositions of the hydrogels (Fig. 1l). MixPEG, MixPEG-tFNAs, and MixPEG-TR all showed O–H and C–H stretching vibration bands near 3353 cm−1 and 2879 cm−1, corresponding to functional groups common to the PEG network. Compared to plain MixPEG, no new peaks were evident in the MixPEG-tFNAs spectrum; however, the amide I band around 1648 cm−1 (C=O stretch) was noticeably intensified in MixPEG-TR, suggesting that the introduction of the nucleic-acid-bearing TR altered local hydrogen bonding and carbonyl environments in the network. Notably, MixPEG-TR showed markedly enhanced absorbance at ∼1097, 960, and 843 cm−1—peaks corresponding to phosphate diester bonds and the sugar–phosphate backbone vibrations of nucleic acids—which were not prominent in MixPEG alone. These results collectively demonstrate that nucleic acid-based TR was successfully embedded into the MixPEG hydrogel network, effectively modifying the chemical composition of the hydrogel. Finally, we evaluated the transdermal penetration and DC-targeting ability of TR delivered via MixPEG (TR@MixPEG) by confocal microscopy (Fig. 1m). Cy5-labeled TR applied to MixPEG-TR penetrated the epidermal surface and produced a distinct red fluorescent signal in the dermis. Notably, the red fluorescence of TR overlapped extensively with regions stained green for CD11c+ cells (skin dendritic cells), indicating that TR was effectively delivered into the dermis and bound specifically to DCs. To distinguish the intrinsic transcutaneous penetration of TR from the delivery enhancement conferred by MixPEG loading, we included a TR-only group as a control. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, TR alone displayed a measurable capacity for transcutaneous delivery and was taken up by a small proportion of CD11c+ cells. In contrast, after incorporation into MixPEG, the percentage of TR-positive cells within the CD11c+ population increased significantly, indicating that MixPEG enhances the transdermal delivery of TR by prolonging its residence time on the floor of the mouth. This demonstrated that the MixPEG hydrogel could ferry TR cells across the skin barrier to reach cutaneous DCs, providing a delivery basis for subsequent local immunomodulation. The CCK-8 assay (Supplementary Fig. S1) showed that TR and the treatments had no toxic effect on cell viability.

TR induces a tolerogenic phenotype in dendritic cells in vitro

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a classic stimulus for inducing DC maturation, characterized by the upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80 and CD86) and enhanced release of inflammatory cytokines, thereby triggering robust immune activation. Therefore, we evaluated the effects of TR on the induction of a tolerogenic state in DCs following LPS stimulation (Fig. 2a). Scanning electron microscopy revealed that DCs displayed a markedly increased number of surface protrusions after LPS treatment, indicating a typical mature activated morphology. In contrast, DCs treated with TR showed fewer protrusions and a more rounded shape, closely resembling those of unstimulated control cells (Fig. 2b). This morphological change suggested that TR attenuated the activating effects of LPS on DCs. As CD80 and CD86 are co-stimulatory molecules on DCs, elevated levels of these markers usually signify a transition from an immature to a mature/activated phenotype (enhancing the ability of DCs to activate naïve T cells), whereas reduced levels are often associated with a tolerogenic orientation. Therefore, we examined the effects of the TR treatment on the expression of CD80 and CD86. The flow cytometry gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Figure S3. Flow cytometry results (Fig. 2c–f) showed that LPS stimulation significantly increased the percentage of CD80+ and CD86+ DCs compared to those in the control. While treatment with tFNAs alone had a modest suppressive effect, TR treatment led to a greater reduction in the proportion of CD80+ and CD86+ cells, approaching comparable to those in controls. This trend was consistent with the morphological observations; the maturation phenotype induced by LPS was substantially dampened in the presence of TR, indicating that TR maintains DCs in a less activated state.

Next, we examined the expression of key inflammatory and anti-inflammatory markers using immunofluorescence to further assess whether these phenotypic changes were accompanied by altered inflammatory signaling (Fig. 2g–k). Specifically, we probed for the transcription factor NF-κB and the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α, as well as the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, in the different treatment groups. LPS stimulation increased the nuclear localization of NF-κB and elevated levels of IL-1β and TNF-α, coupled with a marked reduction in IL-10 expression, reflecting a strongly inflammatory profile. In contrast, tFNAs and TR treatments mitigated this LPS-induced inflammatory response, with the TR group showing the weakest NF-κB, IL-1β, and TNF-α signals and the highest IL-10 levels. This indicates that TR effectively blunted LPS-driven inflammatory signaling in DCs, promoting a tolerogenic profile characterized by diminished pro-inflammatory mediator expression and enhanced IL-10 production. Furthermore, we quantitatively measured the secretion levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in the culture supernatants of each treatment group by ELISA, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. S4). The results showed that LPS stimulation markedly induced robust secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β while suppressing IL-10 production, consistent with a typical inflammatory activation state. In contrast, both tFNAs and TR alleviated the LPS-induced pro-inflammatory response to varying extents. Notably, in the TR-treated group, TNF-α and IL-1β levels were significantly reduced, whereas IL-10 secretion was significantly increased, indicating the most pronounced anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. These ELISA findings closely agreed with the immunofluorescence observations and further validated, at the secretion level, the advantageous role of TR in suppressing inflammatory DC activation and promoting an immunoregulatory cytokine profile.

We then performed integrated transcriptomic and protein expression analyses to gain a deeper insight into the molecular mechanisms by which TR induces a tolerogenic state in DCs. First, we compared the gene expression profiles of LPS + TR-treated and control DCs at the transcriptome level. The volcano plot (Fig. 3a) shows that 413 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the LPS + TR and Control groups, with 249 up-regulated and 164 down-regulated genes (|log2FC| > 1, Padjust < 0.05). Representative upregulated genes included Csf3, Irf7, Oas1a, Isg15, Rsad2, and Ccl5, many of which are associated with innate immune responses and inflammation. Subsequently, we performed a Venn analysis of DEGs across the LPS, LPS + tFNAs, and LPS + TR groups relative to the control (Fig. 3b). The three treatments shared 19 core DEGs; LPS + TR and LPS + tFNAs, LPS + TR, and LPS alone groups shared 137, 204 and 39 DEGs, respectively. This suggests that, compared to tFNAs alone, TR induced more extensive and specific transcriptional reprogramming. A clustering heatmap of the DEGs (Supplementary Fig. S5) further showed that samples were clustered tightly within each treatment group and that the LPS + TR group was clearly separated from the control group in expression patterns, indicating the robust and reproducible effects of TR on the transcriptome. Overall, in the context of LPS stimulation, TR preserved a subset of the core response observed with tFNAs treatment and recruited a large number of unique genes related to immune regulation and stress defense, establishing a distinct and stable expression program. This suggests that TR has a stronger capacity than tFNAs alone to reshape the immune response of DCs.

Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs (Fig. 3c) revealed that were mainly enriched in biological process terms related to “response to stimulus/stress/biotic stimulus” and “defense response”; at the molecular function level, protein binding was a major enriched category. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 3d) showed that the DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways involved in immune signaling and inflammation, such as cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and the NOD-like receptor and IL-17 signaling pathways, which converge downstream on NF-κB. These results suggest that TR primarily impacts processes related to stress responses and host defense. In particular, TR appears to reshape the upstream immune signal inputs in a way that reduces the activity of NF-κB–related pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs.

This transcriptome-based inference was further corroborated at the protein level. Western blotting analysis (Fig. 3e) showed that TLR4, the receptor that recognizes LPS and initiates downstream inflammatory signaling, was markedly upregulated in DCs upon LPS stimulation; however, its expression was reduced after TR treatment. This suggests that the upstream inflammatory signaling input was dampened by TR. Meanwhile, IκBα levels, which were decreased due to LPS stimulation (due to activation of NF-κB), were partially restored in TR-treated cells, implying that the inhibitory constraint on NF-κB was re-established. Consistently, phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 (a marker of NF-κB activation and nuclear translocation) was significantly reduced by TR treatment, indicating suppression of NF-κB activation. Additionally, at the effector cytokine level, LPS stimulation led to strong upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α, whereas TR treatment lowered these levels; conversely, IL-10, which plays an anti-inflammatory and tolerogenic role, was markedly increased under TR treatment. Collectively, these changes indicate an overall suppression of the TLR4–NF-κB inflammatory pathway by TR. In summary, TR significantly weakened pro-inflammatory maturation-associated signaling in DCs, while promoting a shift toward a more tolerogenic state. Densitometric quantification of the blots (Fig. 3f) was consistent with these trends; the LPS group showed elevated levels of pro-inflammatory markers, whereas TR-treated DCs showed a reduction in these markers and an increase in the anti-inflammatory marker IL-10.

Local immune tolerance effects of MixPEG-TR in an allogeneic skin graft model

We established a full-thickness allogeneic skin transplantation mouse model to evaluate the ability of MixPEG-TR to induce local immune tolerance in vivo and promote graft healing and monitored graft appearance over time on postoperative days 0, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 (Fig. 4a). The wound healing process and graft contraction differed significantly between the groups during the observation period (Fig. 4d). In the Control group (allogeneic skin transplantation), the grafts became noticeably darker in color, with some areas appearing dusky. Additionally, the graft tissue became stiff, and at later time points, some grafts showed signs of necrosis and began to detach. The MixPEG alone and MixPEG - tFNAs groups had gross appearances similar to those of the control group, characterized by substantial graft contraction and clearly demarcated edges that did not integrate well with the surrounding host skin. In contrast, grafts in the MixPEG-TR group retained a coloration similar to that of the adjacent normal skin; the tissue remained soft and pliable, and adhered firmly with the graft edges gradually blending into the host skin without any necrotic regions. These observations indicated that MixPEG-TR treatment markedly alleviated the rejection response. We outlined the graft boundaries at each time point and overlaid them to further compare graft contraction across groups. Changes in the graft area mirrored the gross observations. Specifically, the control grafts underwent pronounced contractions with irregular receding edges, and the MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs groups showed a similar pattern, with notable border contraction. In contrast, grafts in the MixPEG-TR group exhibited the smallest reduction in area over time, indicating preservation of graft size.

We also semiquantitatively scored the gross pathology of the grafts based on surface integrity and contraction (scale 0–5), with higher scores indicating more severe rejection-related damage [40].The scoring criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The MixPEG-TR group received consistently lower scores (mostly 1–2) than the control group (which had higher scores clustered around 3–4) (Fig. 4b). This suggests that the grafts treated with MixPEG-TR experienced significantly milder rejection. Additionally, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining on day 14 (Fig. 4e) showed that the control grafts had severe disruption of normal skin architecture in the epidermis and dermis, with widespread tissue necrosis, dense inflammatory cell infiltration, and marked loss of skin appendages (hair follicles and glands). Grafts in the MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs groups had relatively more intact epidermal and dermal layers than the controls, but still exhibited inflammatory infiltrates, disorganized dermal cellularity, and reduced appendage numbers. In contrast, grafts from the MixPEG-TR group maintained a continuous epidermal layer and a well-organized dermis with only minimal inflammatory cells, and largely preserved skin appendages, indicating significantly less tissue damage. Subsequently, the degree of graft rejection in each group was graded using the 2007 Banff criteria for transplantation pathology (Fig. 4c) [41]. Detailed scoring criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 2. The Control group showed severe rejection, predominantly in the grade 3R–4R range. The MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs groups exhibited less severe rejection, with some samples in the 2R–3R range. Meanwhile, the MixPEG-TR group showed the least evidence of rejection, mainly within the 1R–2R range.

Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 5a) results showed that the control grafts had weak and discontinuous collagen staining with disordered fiber orientation and uneven staining intensity around the necrotic areas. The MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs groups showed stronger collagen staining than the control group, with expanded areas of collagen deposition, although collagen fibers still appeared in broken bundles with inconsistent orientation. In contrast, the MixPEG-TR group showed the most uniform and continuous collagen staining, with fibers exhibiting a more consistently aligned arrangement. Similarly, quantitative analysis of collagen content (Fig. 5d) showed that collagen deposition was the lowest in the control grafts and highest in the MixPEG-TR grafts, with intermediate levels in the MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs groups. Collectively, these findings indicated that MixPEG-TR treatment promoted more complete and organized collagen remodeling in the grafts, consistent with improved healing.

Next, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining to examine T-cell infiltration and local cytokine expression on day 14 (Fig. 5b). CD3, CD4, and CD8 were used as T cell markers (CD3 for total T cells, CD4 for helper T cells, and CD8 for cytotoxic T cells). The Control group had the highest density of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ cells, which were concentrated in the superficial dermis and around the blood vessels, indicating a robust ongoing rejection response. The MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs groups had fewer T cells than the control group, corresponding to a reduced inflammatory response. Notably, the MixPEG-TR group showed sparse T-cell staining, reflecting minimal T-cell infiltration, suggesting that immune attack on the graft was greatly reduced. Additionally, we stained for TNF-α and IL-10 to evaluate pro-vs anti-inflammatory cytokine presence in the graft. TNF-α, was strongly positive in Control grafts, had a lower but intensity in the MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs groups, and was the weakest in the MixPEG-TR group, indicating a much lower level of local inflammation. Conversely, IL-10 was most strongly expressed in the MixPEG-TR group (widespread and intense staining), intermediately expressed in the MixPEG and MixPEG - tFNAs group, and was minimally expressed in the control group. These patterns suggest that the MixPEG-TR treatment shifted the local immune environment toward an anti-inflammatory, tolerogenic profile (high IL-10, low TNF-α), in line with the improved graft outcomes. Quantification of IHC staining (percentage of area positive, Fig. 5e–i) confirmed that the MixPEG-TR group had the lowest CD3/CD4/CD8/TNF-α positive areas and the highest IL-10 positive area among the groups.

We then co-stained for CD11c (a pan-DC marker) and the co-stimulatory molecules CD86 and CD80, which together indicate the abundance of DCs and their maturation/activation state within the graft (Fig. 5c). Fluorescent signals were mainly observed in the superficial dermis and perivascular regions, consistent with the typical locations of immune cell infiltration. Clear differences were observed between the groups. Control grafts had densely distributed CD11c+ cells with strong CD86 and CD80 signals, indicating a high number of activated/mature DCs present. The MixPEG and MixPEG-tFNAs grafts showed reduced fluorescence intensity and fewer CD11c, CD86 and CD80 positive cells than the control. The MixPEG-TR group showed the weakest CD11c, CD86, and CD80 signals, and the smallest positive areas, suggesting that the number of DCs and their activation status were significantly reduced. This implies that MixPEG-TR treatment effectively suppressed the local DC-mediated immune response that drives rejection. Quantitative analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity of CD11c, CD80, and CD86 (Fig. 5j–k) further supported this conclusion, with the MixPEG-TR group showing significantly lower intensity levels than the other groups.

To further evaluate whether immunoregulatory signals elicited by local administration extend to secondary lymphoid organs, we first performed flow-cytometric analysis of the graft-draining lymph nodes (dLNs) on day 14 post-transplantation to assess changes in Treg frequency. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S7, compared with the control group, all treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of increase in the proportion of Tregs (CD3+CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) in the dLNs, with the MixPEG-TR group showing the most pronounced elevation. These results suggest that local immunomodulatory signals may initially promote the establishment of a regulatory T-cell–associated phenotype at the level of the draining lymph nodes. Subsequently, flow cytometric analysis of splenocytes on day 14 post-transplantation (Fig. 6a–h) was performed to assess the systemic immune effects of the treatment. Details of the gating strategy are provided in Supplementary Fig. S6. In the MixPEG-TR group, splenic DCs showed reduced expression of CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 6a, b, 6e, 6f), suggesting that antigen-presenting cell activation was inhibited and that DCs in secondary lymphoid organs adopted a more tolerogenic phenotype. We also examined the proportions of Tregs and effector T cells in the spleen (Fig. 6c, d, 6g, 6h). The MixPEG-TR group exhibited a higher percentage of Tregs (CD3+ CD4+ CD25+ Foxp3+ cells) and a lower percentage of CD8+ effector T cells (CD3+ CD8+ CD62LlowCD44high) compared to the control group. The complete blood count of mouse retro-orbital venous plexus blood is shown in Supplementary Fig. S8. No obvious abnormalities in routine hematological parameters were observed in the MixPEG-TR group, indicating that this treatment did not induce significant systemic toxicity.

We performed untargeted metabolomic profiling of graft tissues to assess the overall impact of TR on the local metabolic environment of the graft. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) of the metabolomic data (Fig. 6i) revealed complete separation between the metabolite profiles of MixPEG-TR and control groups in the model space. In the score scatter plot, the horizontal axis (Predictive Component 1, PC1) represents the primary component distinguishing the two groups and the vertical axis (Orthogonal Component 2, OC2) represents the variation unrelated to group discrimination. The MixPEG-TR and Control samples were clearly separated along the PC1 axis, with samples in each group clustered tightly together, indicating that the TR treatment induced a stable and consistent shift in the graft metabolome. We then performed 200 permutation tests to verify that the OPLS-DA model did not overfit (Supplementary Figure S9). All permuted Q2 values were significantly lower than those of the original model, and the regression line of the permutation results had a negative slope, confirming that the model's ability to discriminate between groups was based on real metabolic differences rather than random chance.

Subsequently, univariate analyses were used to identify individual metabolites that were differentially regulated by the TR treatment and the results were visualized using a volcano plot (Fig. 6j). In this plot, the x-axis is log2(fold change), indicating the magnitude of change in metabolite abundance in the MixPEG-TR group relative to Control, and the y-axis is –log10(P-value), indicating the statistical significance of the change. Metabolites significantly upregulated in the TR group are shown as red dots and those significantly downregulated are shown as blue dots (with gray dots representing non-significant changes), based on the chosen thresholds for fold change and P-value. The volcano plot revealed a broad distribution of numerous red and blue dots, suggesting that TR treatment caused widespread metabolic alterations; multiple metabolites were significantly increased or decreased, rather than only minor shifts in a few metabolites. We further visualized the pattern of metabolic changes via hierarchical clustering of differential metabolites, which is displayed as a heat map (Fig. 6k). In the heatmap, each column corresponds to a graft sample, and each row corresponds to a differential metabolite; the color intensity from blue to red indicates the relative abundance from low to high. The samples were clearly segregated into two clusters corresponding to the MixPEG-TR and Control groups, with the samples within each group clustered closely together. This indicates that TR-induced metabolic reprogramming was consistent across individuals, reinforcing the robustness of this effect.

Based on the list of differential metabolites, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify significantly affected metabolic pathways. As shown in Fig. 6l, under the positive ion mode, the top enriched pathways of differential metabolites between the MixPEG-TR group and the control grafts were ranked by statistical significance. After excluding pathways with limited immunological relevance to graft biology or those containing only a single differential metabolite, 14 pathways were ultimately retained. The results demonstrated that TR-associated metabolic alterations were primarily clustered into three functional modules: (1) lipid-related pathways, including linoleic acid metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and glycerophospholipid metabolism, suggesting remodeling of membrane lipid composition and reorganization of fatty acid pools; (2) amino acid–related pathways, including arginine biosynthesis and arginine/proline metabolism, indicating reprogramming of local nitrogen metabolism and amino acid utilization; and (3) the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter pathway, reflecting altered transmembrane transport capacity. Upregulated and downregulated metabolites within each pathway are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S10 and Table S3.

Within the lipid module, the upregulation of linoleic acid metabolism and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, accompanied by alterations in glycerophospholipid metabolism, indicated membrane lipid remodeling and fatty acid pool rebalancing (Table S3) [42]. Lipid metabolic processes are known to influence dendritic cell (DC) membrane organization and signal transduction [43], enhancing membrane fluidity and reducing Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) clustering within lipid rafts, thereby attenuating sensitivity to pro-inflammatory stimulation [44]. Unsaturated fatty acids and their derivatives exhibit immunosuppressive and pro-resolution properties [45]. Certain arachidonic acid metabolites, including prostaglandins and lipoxins, can induce anti-inflammatory mediators and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production through activation of PPARγ/LXR or inhibition of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways [45], ultimately promoting a tolerogenic phenotype characterized by increased IL-10 and decreased TNF-α/IL-1β expression [46].

In the amino acid module, enrichment of arginine biosynthesis and arginine/proline metabolism suggested a shift in arginine utilization [47]. Arginine can be converted by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) to produce nitric oxide (NO), which mediates inflammatory responses. During graft rejection, the TLR4–NF-κB axis frequently drives elevated iNOS expression in DCs, thereby exacerbating inflammation [46]. TR treatment may suppress the iNOS pathway by downregulating TLR4–NF-κB signaling and pro-inflammatory mediators, consequently redirecting arginine metabolism toward the arginase-1 (Arg1) pathway [46]. Enhanced Arg1 activity depletes local L-arginine and inhibits effector T-cell proliferation and function, representing a well-recognized mechanism by which tolerogenic DCs suppress Teff responses [46].

Regarding the transport module, enrichment of the ABC transporter pathway indicated that TR may enhance the transmembrane efflux of lipids and immunologically active metabolites in DCs [48]. ABCA1 and ABCG1 mediate cholesterol efflux and prevent excessive lipid accumulation [49]. Their deficiency leads to intracellular cholesterol accumulation, hyperactivation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, increased IL-1β secretion, and Th1/Th17 polarization, thereby disrupting immune tolerance [48]. Conversely, TR may enhance ABC transporter activity to facilitate efflux, suppress aberrant inflammasome activation, and reduce excessive IL-1β production [50], thereby maintaining DCs in a low maturation/activation state and limiting their stimulatory capacity toward effector T cells [50].

Based on KEGG enrichment results, we further selected several core regulatory genes involved in lipid metabolism pathways—Fads2, Scd1, Lpcat3, and Ptgs2—for validation analysis. These genes occupy key regulatory positions within the enriched pathways and are functionally linked to the TLR4–NF-κB inflammatory signaling axis. Lpcat3 mediates membrane phospholipid remodeling and influences lipid raft stability, and alterations in membrane lipid composition regulate TLR4 clustering and downstream NF-κB activation intensity. Fads2 and Scd1 determine the biosynthetic capacity of unsaturated fatty acids; polyunsaturated fatty acids and their metabolites negatively regulate NF-κB activity by inhibiting IKK activation and reducing p65 nuclear translocation, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory transcription and favoring an immunoregulatory phenotype.

Accordingly, we assessed the mRNA expression levels of these genes in DCs under LPS stimulation in the LPS + TR and control groups. qPCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of Fads2, Scd1, Lpcat3, and Ptgs2 (Supplementary Fig. S11), suggesting that TR transcriptionally activates key nodes involved in unsaturated fatty acid synthesis, membrane lipid remodeling, and eicosanoid metabolism. These findings provide direct functional support for the significant enrichment of lipid metabolic pathways observed in metabolomic profiling. Combined with immunophenotypic data, the upregulation of these metabolism-related genes was consistent with suppression of NF-κB signaling and an anti-inflammatory cytokine profile (IL-10↑, IL-1β/TNF-α↓) in TR-treated DCs, indicating that lipid metabolic reprogramming may contribute to and support the establishment of a TR-induced tolerogenic DC phenotype.

In summary, these results suggest that TR promotes the transition of DCs from an immunogenic, stimulatory state to a tolerogenic regulatory phenotype through coordinated modulation of lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and metabolite transport [54], [51] ultimately generating a tolerance-associated phenotype characterized by high IL-10 expression and NF-κB suppression. This study demonstrates that TR facilitates tolerogenic reprogramming of DCs and promotes graft tolerance by reshaping the local metabolic microenvironment, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for localized immunomodulatory therapies targeting transplant rejection and other immune-mediated diseases.

Although this study achieved encouraging therapeutic outcomes in a murine allogeneic skin transplantation model, several key translational barriers still need to be systematically evaluated before clinical application. Beyond experimental limitations such as the relatively small sample size and limited follow-up duration, it is more critical to comprehensively validate the suitability of this system in the human setting across multiple dimensions, including delivery efficiency, targeting specificity, biosafety, and feasibility for scalable use.

First, interspecies differences in skin barrier properties may substantially affect transdermal delivery efficiency. Previous studies have shown pronounced differences between mouse and human skin in stratum corneum thickness, lipid organization, and hair follicle density. Because human skin generally exhibits a stronger barrier function, the penetration depth of nanocarriers and the required dosing often change accordingly. Therefore, the relatively high transdermal efficiency observed in rodents may not be directly extrapolated to the clinic [52]. Nevertheless, multiple studies indicate that small-sized tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) still possess measurable permeability in human skin and can elicit biological effects in vitro or in clinically relevant models [52,53].In the future, combining physical enhancement approaches such as microneedles or electroporation may further improve delivery depth and stability, thereby strengthening applicability in human tissues [54].

On the basis of achieving effective penetration, precise cellular targeting is likewise essential for successful translation. In this study, we used an aptamer targeting mouse DC-surface CD209a to enhance delivery specificity. Although the human genome lacks a molecule fully homologous to CD209a, the functionally related C-type lectin receptor DC-SIGN is highly expressed on human dendritic cells, and high-affinity DC-SIGN aptamers have been successfully identified via SELEX in prior work [23,55].Owing to the highly programmable nature of tFNAs, replacing the aptamer sequence is not expected to compromise assembly or transdermal performance. Accordingly, this strategy is, in principle, cross-species adaptable and may even achieve higher targeting precision in human systems.

Furthermore, the long-term safety of localized miRNA delivery is an important consideration. Free miRNAs may activate TLR7/8 or the STING pathway and trigger non-specific inflammatory responses [56],while conventional liposomal or viral vectors may carry potential long-term toxicity risks [57,58].By contrast, tFNAs exhibit favorable biocompatibility and controllability, with features such as sequence conservatism, a low propensity to provoke innate immune recognition, and rapid degradation within lysosomes. In addition, aptamer-mediated targeting can further reduce miRNA off-target effects and systemic exposure, providing an added safeguard for long-term application.

After addressing delivery efficiency, targeting, and safety, clinical accessibility also depends on the feasibility of large-area application and scalable manufacturing. In this regard, the MixPEG hydrogel platform developed in this study provides a more engineerable carrier format for tFNAs [59]. Built on PEG materials that have already been clinically approved, this system offers good biocompatibility and scalable manufacturability. It can be applied by injection or spraying to cover irregular or large-area wounds, and its effective coverage of full-thickness skin defects and promotion of wound healing have been demonstrated in animal models [59,60].This “DNA–polymer” hybrid hydrogel concept thus provides a practical path toward large-scale deployment of nucleic acid therapeutics in real clinical scenarios [61].

Notably, this strategy also aligns closely with recent trends in tolerogenic therapy. Emerging studies emphasize that spatiotemporally coordinated delivery of antigens and immunomodulatory cues using smart biomaterials is pivotal for inducing antigen-specific immune tolerance; for example, programmable tolerogenic nanoparticles have been shown to mimic apoptotic cell clearance and establish durable immune tolerance. Within this context, the TR system proposed here, together with its defined tolerance-inducing mechanism, not only supports the feasibility of reprogramming dendritic-cell function via local nucleic acid delivery, but also provides a new bioactive module for developing next-generation programmable, precise, and scalable immunomodulatory platforms [62].

Overall, this study systematically supports the clinical feasibility of a DC-targeted nucleic acid delivery strategy across several key dimensions—transdermal delivery, cell-specific targeting, long-term biosafety, and engineered scalability—thereby laying an important foundation for building a programmable, localized immune-tolerance therapeutic platform and outlining a clear path toward translational application in transplantation and tissue-repair settings.

Conclusion

In summary, the MixPEG-TR system constructed in this study, based on tetrahedral framework nucleic acids, exhibits excellent structural stability and local transdermal delivery capacity, markedly enhancing the enrichment and retention of TR in the skin graft area. In vitro, TR inhibits the inflammatory maturation of dendritic cells and induces their transition toward a tolerogenic phenotype; in a murine allogeneic skin graft model, local application of MixPEG-TR significantly alleviates rejection and tissue damage, promotes organized collagen remodeling, and prolongs graft survival.

Experimental section

Materials

According to the design scheme, the single-stranded DNA sequences (S1, S1-DC, S2, S2-Cy5, S3, S3-end, and S4) were synthesized and purified by the phosphoramidite triester method by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Among them, S1-DC is an oligonucleotide strand bearing a dendritic cell (DC)-targeting aptamer sequence at the 5′ end; the S2 strand was modified with a Cy5 fluorescent label at the 5′ or 3′ end (denoted S2-Cy5), the S3-end strand contains a specifically designed single-stranded sticky-end sequence at the 3′ end that is fully complementary to a partial sequence of the target miR-23b, enabling subsequent sequence-specific binding; and the miR-23b sequence was also provided by Sangon Biotech (Supplementary Table 4). All other chemical reagents were of analytical grade (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., China) and were used as received unless otherwise specified.

Synthesis of tFNAs and TR

Four DNA single strands (S1, S2, S3, and S4) were mixed at an equimolar ratio in TM buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl and 50 mM MgCl2; pH 8.0). The mixture was heated to 95 °C for 10 min and then slowly cooled to 4 °C in a thermal cycler, assembling the tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) in a one-pot process. Using a similar one-pot method, a functionalized (tFNAs) for targeted delivery was assembled from strands S1-DC, S2, S3-end, and S4: in this construct, the S1 strand was modified at the 5′ end with a dendritic cell (DC)-targeting aptamer, and the S3 strand was modified at the 3′ end with a sticky end. Subsequently, miR-23b was added to the functionalized tFNAs solution at a ratio of 100:1 (v/v)(1:1,mol/mol) and incubated at 4 °C overnight, yielding the TR complex (tFNAs-aptamer@miR-23b).

Preparation of MixPEG-TR hydrogel

MixPEG is a two-component, ready-to-use PEG hydrogel system (EFL-MixPEG-001), composed of 4ArmPEG-NHS (10 kDa) and 4ArmPEG-NH2 (10 kDa). Both components are supplied as lyophilized powders and were purchased from EFL (Suzhou, China). The chemical preparation of the two powder precursors is described as follows. 4-arm PEG-NH2 is synthesized from 4-arm PEG-OH by low-temperature activation and reaction with TsCl/TEA in anhydrous dichloromethane, followed by reaction with 25% aqueous ammonia in DMSO to yield the amine-terminated product. The product is then purified by precipitation, ethanol/water washing, and lyophilization to obtain the powder. 4-arm PEG-NHS is prepared from 4-arm PEG-COOH via EDC/NHS activation in anhydrous DMF under nitrogen protection, and subsequently obtained as a powder through filtration, ether precipitation, dialysis, and vacuum drying. We prepared the solutions and formed the hydrogel according to the manufacturer's instructions. After warming the two powders from −20 °C to room temperature for ∼30 min, each component was dissolved in TR solution at 4% (w/v) (with gentle shaking at room temperature for ∼1–2 min). The solutions were prepared fresh and recommended for use within 30 min. The two solutions were then mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio to allow in situ crosslinking at room temperature, yielding a TR-loaded MixPEG-TR hydrogel (stored at 4 °C in the dark until use) for local adhesion and sustained release.

DC2.4 cell culture and treatment

The mouse bone marrow–derived dendritic cell line DC2.4 was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in a specialized medium (CM-0545), comprising RPMI-1640 (PM150110) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 164210-50) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S, PB180120), in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under 5% CO2 atmosphere. When the cells reached the logarithmic growth phase, they were assigned to the Control, LPS, LPS + tFNAs, or LPS + TR group and treated accordingly.

Establishment of mouse allogeneic skin transplantation model and treatment

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the School of Stomatology, Chongqing Medical University (ethics approval no. 2024 Lunshen [154]). Male C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing approximately 20 g, were acclimatized for 7 days under a 12-h light/dark cycle and then randomly assigned as donors and recipients. All animals were purchased from Ensville Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The transplantation model was established as follows:

Anesthesia: Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 4% chloral hydrate (10 μl/g). During surgery, the mice were kept on a warming surgical pad to maintain body temperature.

Donor skin harvesting: The dorsal fur of donor mice was shaved and residual hair removed with a depilatory cream. The skin was disinfected with povidone-iodine followed by 75% ethanol. A full-thickness skin graft, approximately 1 cm in diameter, was excised from the donor's back using a circular scalpel or scissors.

Recipient wound preparation: Recipient mice underwent similar hair removal and disinfection on the dorsum. An equally sized (∼1 cm diameter) full-thickness skin defect was created on the recipient's back.

Graft transplantation: The donor skin graft was immediately placed onto the recipient's wound bed. The graft was secured in place by interrupted sutures using 4-0 surgical thread at the edges. The graft area was then covered with a sterile dressing, and an elastic bandage was wrapped around the mouse to protect the graft and maintain its position.

Post-operative care: Postoperative analgesia was provided using meloxicam (2 mg/kg, subcutaneously, once daily) for 3 days following surgery, and an antibiotic ointment was applied to the graft area to prevent infection. Mice were allowed to recover in a warmed chamber and were closely monitored for vital signs and general condition.

Grouping and treatment: Mice were randomly divided into five groups (n ≥ 3 per group): Control (allogeneic skin transplantation),MixPEG (hydrogel only), MixPEG - tFNAs), MixPEG - TR, and Blank (no treatment). Starting from the day of surgery (Day 0), 50 μL of the assigned hydrogel formulation was evenly applied topically onto the surface of the skin graft in each mouse. Treatments were administered every 2 days for a total of 14 days. The Blank group received no hydrogel or treatment on the graft.

Monitoring: The grafted skin was observed and photographed every other day to monitor color, signs of rejection, and graft viability.

Tissue Harvesting: On day 14, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The grafts and adjacent tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological and immunological analyses, and the spleens were harvested and placed in pre-cooled complete medium for subsequent cellular assays.

Flow cytometric analysis of immune cells

The spleens harvested from each group were used to evaluate systemic immune modulation by the treatments. Single-cell suspensions were prepared by mechanically dissociating the spleen tissue and filtering it through a 70-μm cell strainer. After red blood cell lysis (if applicable) and washing with PBS, cells were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (Fc receptor blocker) and then stained with fluorescently conjugated antibodies for surface markers, including CD11c, CD80, CD86, MHC-II (for dendritic cells), and CD3, CD4, CD8, Foxp3 (for T cell subsets). After staining and washing, the cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer. FlowJo v10 software was used to gate cell populations and calculate the proportions of various cell subsets, such as the percentage of CD11c+ DCs expressing high or low levels of CD80/CD86/MHC-II, the ratio of CD4+Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) to effector T cells, and other relevant phenotypic distributions. These data were used to assess how MixPEG + TR treatment influenced dendritic cell status and T cell responses in the spleen.

Transcriptomics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cell samples in each group using TRIzol reagent. RNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer and agarose gel electrophoresis, and only samples with purity, integrity and yield meeting conventional quality control criteria for RNA-seq library preparation were included in subsequent analyses. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. RNA-seq data were processed using a standard bioinformatics pipeline, including quality control, read alignment and transcript assembly, followed by expression quantification, differential expression analysis and GO/KEGG-based functional enrichment analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed to evaluate the metabolic effects of MixPEG-TR treatment on skin grafts. Skin tissues were homogenized under pre-cooled conditions and metabolites were extracted with methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v); after ultrasonication, low-temperature precipitation and centrifugation, the supernatants were concentrated, reconstituted in 50% methanol containing internal standards, filtered, and the resulting extracts were analyzed on a UPLC–Orbitrap MS platform (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column; gradient elution with water and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid; Orbitrap Exploris 120 operated in polarity-switching mode with a resolving power of 60,000 at MS1). Raw data were processed in Compound Discoverer™ 3.3 for peak detection, alignment and normalization, metabolites were annotated against relevant databases, and differential metabolites were defined as those with VIP >1.0 and P < 0.05 and subsequently subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), with n ≥ 3 for each group. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Comparisons among multiple groups were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001 denote statistically significant differences, while “ns” indicates no significant difference.
