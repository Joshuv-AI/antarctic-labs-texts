A Cross-Sectional Analytical Study: Prevalence and Clinical Profile of Metabolic Syndrome Among Middle Eastern, Mainly Jordanians, Cohort Presented with Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Jafar Alsheyyab, Nuseibah Al Qtaish, Hamza AbuZamzam, Wesam Saleh Al-Saud, Afnan Saleh Al-Soud, Khaled Abdul-Aziz Ahmed, Naser Albekaen, Mohammad Bani Baker, Razan Musa, Bothainh Yahya Al Tawarh, Ahmad Mustafa Abualhayja’a, Bashar Kiwan, Mohammad S Albakri, Omar Omari, Ahmed Shukairi, Siwar Albeka’en, Hala Mousa, Abdel-Ellah Al-Shudifat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains the leading cause of mortality for people living in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Accumulation of modifiable ASCVD risk factors such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), dyslipidemia, smoking, obesity, and physical inactivity leads to a higher magnitude of ASCVD burden. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a fundamental clinical factor associated with increased incidence and mortality of ASCVD. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and clinical profiles of MetS among Middle Eastern patients with ASCVD using a newly proposed definition incorporating six modifiable risk factors.MethodsWe used data from the Jordan absence of standard modifiable risk factors (SMuRF-Less) study, to evaluate demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics along with the presence and co-existence of six modifiable risk factors between ASCVD patients with and without MetS.ResultsA total of 5540 patients with ASCVD (mean age 57.13 ± 12.31 years) were included. MetS status was available for 1,016 patients, of whom 434 (42.7%) met the criteria for MetS. Patients with MetS were more likely to be aged 46–65 years and had a higher prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia (notably hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL levels), T2DM, obesity, and physical inactivity. Additionally, MetS patients showed higher rates of heart failure, chronic kidney disease, obstructive sleep apnea, lower educational levels, higher smoking prevalence, and lack of health insurance.ConclusionNearly half of ASCVD patients in this Middle Eastern cohort had MetS. In regions with a high burden of cardiovascular risk factors, effective ASCVD prevention strategies should prioritize early detection and comprehensive management of MetS through control of modifiable risk factors and the establishment of dedicated MetS clinics.Trial Registration Number: NCT06199869.Keywords: atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, obesity, physical inactivity, metabolic syndrome, standard modifiable risk factors, Middle Eastern cohorts, Jordan

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for a substantial proportion of global deaths, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, including those in the Middle East.1–3 Consequently, ASCVD represents a major public health priority at both regional and global levels. Many well-recognized modifiable risk factors are involved in the etiology of ASCVD, such as hypertension, T2DM, physical inactivity, dyslipidemia, obesity, and smoking.

MetS is defined as a cluster of interrelated metabolic abnormalities that add up to compound cardiovascular risk at higher than individual risk factors of the disease alone.4,5 Epidemiological studies have showed high prevalence of MetS in the MENA populations, ranging from 25% to 40% among adults.6 This widespread prevalence has been attributed to various factors such as rapid urbanization, social changes, changes in diet patterns to a Westernized lifestyle, and decreased levels of physical activity. Further, the MENA region carries one of the highest global prevalence rates of T2DM, a critical component and consequence of MetS.7

Aside from the central metabolic elements, lifestyle has also a substantial effect on MetS development, including physical inactivity and unhealthy dietary patterns, that are pivotal for formation and advancement of the disease.8 The large burden of MetS is significantly associated with the rising prevalence of ASCVD in the MENA region.9 These results highlight the need for targeted screening programs, to improve the effectiveness of the management of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors at an early stage in patients across all age ranges and sexes for an aggressive prevention of ASCVD.10

The concept of “SMuRF-less” was introduced by Figtree et al11 in the SWEDEHEART study to define patients presenting with no traditional modifiable vascular risk factors: hypertension, T2DM, dyslipidemia, and smoking.11 Studies have followed to investigate the frequency, signs, and results of SMuRF-less patients in the study setting.11–15 Evidence supports that additional modifiable risk factors such as obesity and physical inactivity, are important contributors to the pathogenesis of ASCVD. The more recent study, for example, suggested that up to 60% of incident cases of ASCVD have five or more modifiable risk factors in the presence of obesity and other common SMuRFs.15

Prof. Hammoudeh and co-investigators have presented a prevalence of 3.9% SMuRF-less patients among 5,540 Middle Eastern individuals with ASCVD in 2025.16 It is significant to mention that controversy on whether a limitation of obesity and physical inactivity should be excluded from the standard SMuRF definition since they have significantly added to the risk of ASCVD globally.17 Focusing on these risk factors, lifestyle and clinical effects could decrease ASCVD morbidity, mortality and improve quality of life.17–19 Although the shared pathophysiology between MetS and cardiovascular disease is well known, data fully characterizing the prevalence and clinical profile of MetS within an expanded six-factor SMuRF framework are remarkably lacking in the Middle East. Most regional registries focus extensively on isolated traditional risk factors, leaving a crucial knowledge gap on how metabolic syndrome clusters with socioeconomic markers and non-traditional risk profiles in established Middle Eastern ASCVD cohorts.

Due to the significant occurrence of MetS in the MENA regions, mainly Jordan, and its crucial role in increasing the risk of ASCVD, this study objective is to utilize data from the Jordan SMuRF-less study to analyze the prevalence and coexistence of six modifiable cardiovascular risk factors among ASCVD patients with and without MetS. The findings could serve as a foundation for focused preventive initiatives, enhance clinical management, and guide regional health policies designed to mitigate the impact of ASCVD.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This was a cross-sectional analytical study to identify MetS prevalence and clinical features among ASCVD patients. Data from the Jordan SMuRF-less Study, a large multicenter cardiovascular population cohort study with standardized demographic, clinical and laboratory data from diverse institutions across Jordan, were collected.16

Study Population

The research had adult patients diagnosed with ASCVD with the presence of coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebrovascular disease such as ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, carotid artery disease, or peripheral arterial disease. Patients diagnosed with CAD were classified based on the presence of acute coronary syndrome, which includes either ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), as well as chronic coronary syndrome. The latter encompasses cases with angiographically verified conditions or those validated through coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA).

Study Groups

After participants reported MetS status, they were divided into two groups: (1) ASCVD patients with MetS and (2) ASCVD patients without MetS. These groups were compared in terms of demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, comorbid conditions, and lifestyle-related variables.

Exclusion criteria for the final analysis were: (1) age younger than 18 years, (2) pregnant women, (3) patients with missing key clinical or laboratory data used for diagnosis of metabolic syndrome (eg, lipid profiles, fasting blood glucose), and (4) patients with active malignancies or end-stage renal disease to avoid confounding effects on metabolic and inflammatory markers.

Criteria for Diagnosis of Metabolic Syndrome

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is defined according to the guidelines set forth by the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III).20 A diagnosis of MetS is made when an individual meets three or more of the following criteria, or receiving treatment for any related conditions:

Central obesity: a waist circumference greater than 102 cm in men or 88 cm in women.

Central obesity: a waist circumference greater than 102 cm in men or 88 cm in women.

Elevated triglycerides: levels at or exceeding 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or currently undergoing lipid-lowering therapy.

Reduced HDL cholesterol: less than 40 mg/dL in men or below 50 mg/dL in women.

Elevated blood pressure: a systolic blood pressure of 130 mmHg or above and/or a diastolic pressure of 85 mmHg or higher, or being treated with antihypertensive medications.

Clinical and Lifestyle Variables

In the present study, patients were evaluated based on an expanded framework of six modifiable cardiovascular risk factors including the four conventional standard modifiable risk factors (SMuRFs) of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia and smoking as well as two lifestyle-related factors, obesity and physical inactivity.

Hypertension was defined as a prior physician diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive medications, or documented elevated blood pressure at study entry. Those with T2DM were defined by previous diagnosis, use of blood glucose lowering drugs, or HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) ≥6.5%. Dyslipidemia was previously diagnosed, administered lipid-lowering therapy, or abnormal level of lipids according to threshold of the guideline. Obesity was defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2. Physical inactivity was defined as the total absence of getting at least 30 minutes of exercise over three or more days per week. The other variables, such as heart failure, chronic kidney disease, obstructive sleep apnea, smoking status, educational level, and health insurance coverage, were also documented.

Ethical Considerations

This research was carried out hand by hand with the Declaration of Helsinki and got approval from the Institutional Review Boards related to the participating clinical care units, data in Jordan SMuRF-less Study (ClinicalTrials.gov, identifier number NCT06199869) between January 10, 2024, and August 20, 2024, across three community hospitals and six tertiary care centers, which included three Ministry of Health hospitals, two university hospitals, and one private teaching hospital in Jordan. The second data source was a post hoc analysis involving patients with ASCVD who were part of six registries in MENA. These registries include the First Jordan Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Registry (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01841346), the ASCVD Novel and Classical Risk Factors in Young Middle Eastern Women Study (NCT04975503), the Surviving a Decade or More after Coronary Revascularization in a Middle Eastern Population Study (NCT03491722), the Jordan Atrial Fibrillation Study (NCT03917992), the Statin Eligibility Among MENA Patients Presenting with Acute Myocardial Infarction study (NCT03485742), and the Jordan Covid-19 Pandemic Acute Cardiovascular Events Study (NCT04368637).A written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians before enrollment. The study obtained prince hamza hospital review board approval at 9th July 2024, document number 7277. Data were gathered via trained healthcare professionals using standardized case report forms. Demographic and anthropometric features, medical history, standard modifiable and non-modifiable and novel, nontraditional risk factors, comorbidities, utilization of pharmacotherapy for secondary cardiovascular prevention and one-year survival after the first cardiovascular event were documented.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As continuous variables were not normally distributed, comparisons between patients with and without metabolic syndrome were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Missing data were handled using available-case analysis for descriptive and univariate analyses, whereby participants with available data for each variable were included in the corresponding analysis. No imputation of missing data was performed. To identify factors independently associated with metabolic syndrome, a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. Variables included in the multivariable model were selected based on their clinical relevance as potential confounders. Complete-case analysis was applied for the regression model, and participants with missing values in any variable included in the model were excluded from the analysis. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A two-sided p-value.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with metabolic syndrome among patients with ASCVD. A total of 778 participants with complete data were included in the analysis. After adjustment for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, heart failure, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), higher BMI (adjusted OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.09–1.17; p<0.001), current smoking (adjusted OR 1.59, 95% CI 1.13–2.24; p=0.009), heart failure (adjusted OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.45–0.94; p=0.018), CKD (adjusted OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.23–0.64; p<0.001), and OSA (adjusted OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.32–0.76; p=0.002) remained independently associated with metabolic syndrome. Age and sex were not independently associated with metabolic syndrome after multivariable adjustment. The final model demonstrated acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, p=0.145) and explained 21.2% of the variance in metabolic syndrome (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.212).

Discussion

This study is the first to challenge, and propose expanding, the role of MetS in ASCVD by examining the impact of six standard modifiable risk factors (SMuRFs), rather than the traditional four, on MetS prevalence. In this study, we evaluated the prevalence of SMuRFs among metabolic and non-metabolic patients within a Middle Eastern cohort with established ASCVD. The major findings of this study can be summarized as follows: (A) a high prevalence of MetS among MENA patients with ASCVD, with nearly half of the cohort affected; (B) patients with MetS were significantly more likely to have hypertension, T2DM, and dyslipidemia compared with those without MetS. Physical inactivity was also more common among MetS patients, along with higher rates of heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and obstructive sleep apnea; and (C) lower educational attainment was significantly associated with MetS, reflecting the clustering of metabolic and socioeconomic risk factors that amplify cardiovascular risk.

Obesity and sedentary behavior were highly prevalent among patients with MetS, consistent with global evidence linking visceral adiposity and physical inactivity to increased ASCVD risk. Excess visceral fat promotes chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby accelerating the atherosclerotic process.21

The increased risk of all-cause mortality observed in individuals with obesity has been shown to be largely dependent on the coexistence of other cardiometabolic risk factors rather than obesity alone,19,22 and adiposity itself may not represent the primary driver of advanced atherosclerosis.23 Furthermore, emerging treatment strategies for obesity, including bariatric surgery, have demonstrated favorable cardiovascular outcomes, highlighting the importance of targeted obesity management in high-risk populations.24–27 Patients with MetS in our cohort also exhibited a clustering of hypertension, dyslipidemia, elevated fasting glucose, and obesity, which synergistically increases ASCVD risk compared with isolated risk factors.28,29 Conversely, regular physical activity has been shown to improve vascular function, insulin sensitivity, and lipid profiles, resulting in a reduced incidence of ASCVD across populations.30 Globally, approximately one in four adults exhibits sedentary behavior,31 and both sedentary behavior and physical inactivity are well-established modifiable risk factors for ASCVD, including subclinical atherosclerosis, acute coronary syndromes, stroke, and heart failure.32–34 Regular exercise exerts direct beneficial structural and functional effects on the endothelium and myocardium, improves coagulation and hemostatic balance, and reduces abdominal adiposity. MetS provides an important explanatory framework for these observations, as its prevalence increases alongside most major cardiometabolic risk factors, with the notable exception of smoking and low socioeconomic status.35 In the present study, the prevalence of low HDL cholesterol (<60 mg/dL) and elevated triglyceride levels (>150 mg/dL) was significantly higher among patients with MetS, whereas total cholesterol levels did not differ significantly between groups, emphasizing the importance of assessing individual lipid components rather than total cholesterol alone. Smokers often exhibit lower body weight due to nicotine-induced appetite suppression and increased resting metabolic rate, which may explain why smoking does not follow the typical obesity–MetS association related to insulin sensitivity, glycemic status, lipoprotein levels, and blood pressure regulation.36 Individuals with lower income or without health insurance are also less likely to consume energy-dense fast foods and may experience food insecurity, which can result in lower obesity prevalence compared with higher-income groups who have greater access to calorie-dense diets.27 These behavioral and socioeconomic factors help explain why MetS tends to cluster with hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and physical inactivity, but not consistently with smoking or lack of insurance, underscoring the need to reassess cardiometabolic risk profiles in patients with MetS.27

Among younger females aged 18–50 years, none of the assessed reproductive or hormonal risk factors, including gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, or polycystic ovary syndrome, were significantly associated with MetS. This finding suggests that classical metabolic and lifestyle-related factors may play a more prominent role than female-specific reproductive factors in MetS-related ASCVD risk among Middle Eastern women.37 Such results go hand in hand with totally different study guided by prof. Al-Shudifat “Coexistence of Standard Modifiable, Other Classical, and Novel and Classical Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk Factors in Middle Eastern Young Women” that revealed young women with ASCVD, had a high rate of coexistence of classical risk factors (SMuRFs) and socioeconomic RFs. In addition, clustering patterns of risk facts are important for both the physician and the patient to direct the management of ASCVD primary and secondary prevention strategies.38

Our study has some limitations. Its observational, cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and selection bias is possible given the inclusion of patients from tertiary care centers. In addition, physical activity data were incomplete for a subset of participants. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other Middle Eastern populations and even MENA population or to global settings. Nevertheless, this study provides robust evidence regarding the prevalence of MetS and its significant impact on ASCVD in a high-risk cohort.

Strengths and Weaknesses of This Cross-Sectional Clinical Trial

There are several important strengths of this study. It is among the earliest clinical studies in the Middle East that explored the prevalence and clinical profile of metabolic syndrome (MetS) based on an expanded set of six modifiable risk factors (SMuRFs) including lifestyle determinants along with the traditional SMuRFs. Second, the findings provide generalizable clinical insights across different healthcare settings in the region using data from a robust multicenter registry.

There are some limitations to be noted nevertheless. This study was cross-sectional, and we cannot directly infer the causality between the specific clustering of metabolic components and long-term progression of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).

Additionally, because the clinical registry was primarily based on patients evaluated within specialized and tertiary care centers, there may be an inherent selection bias, potentially reflecting a cohort with a higher severity of cardiovascular risk factors than the general population. Future prospective and longitudinal studies are warranted to trace the temporal impact of expanded risk factor profiles on clinical outcomes in this demographic.

Conclusion

There are a high prevalence and serious metabolic and clinical burden of MetS in Middle Eastern ASCVD patients mainly Jordanians. Early detection and targeted management of MetS components —specifically obesity, physical inactivity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and T2DM— is crucial for reducing cardiovascular risk and slowing disease progression. Lifestyle interventions, pharmacologic treatment, and public health strategies may slow metabolic syndrome progression and improve outcomes. These findings further highlight the importance of a comprehensive regional strategy, specifically for early detection, lifestyle modification, and comprehensive management of MetS with the aim to reduce the burden of ASCVD in the Middle East generally and in Jordan mainly.
