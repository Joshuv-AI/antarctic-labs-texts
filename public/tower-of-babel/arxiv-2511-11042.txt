Autonomous motion in changing environment, fibrations and reaction mechanisms
Michael Farber, Stefan Kurz, Mathias Pillin

Abstract
. In this paper we develop further the formalism [ 1 ] of fibrations of configuration spaces as a tool for modelling motion of autonomous systems in variable environments. We analyse the situations when the external conditions may change during the motion of the system and analyse two possibilities: (a) when the behaviour of the external conditions is known in advance; and (b) when the future changes of the external conditions are unknown but we can measure the current state and the current velocity of the external conditions, at every moment of time. We prove that in the case (a) the complexity of the motion algorithm is the same as in the case of constant external conditions; this generalises the result of [ 4 ] . In case (b) we introduce a new concept of a reaction mechanism which allows to take into account unexpected and unpredictable changes in the environment. A reaction mechanism is mathematically an infinitesimal lifting function on a fibre bundle, a nonlinear generalisation of the classical concept of an Ehresmann connection. We illustrate these notions by examples which show that nonlinear infinitesimal lifting function (reaction mechanisms) appear naturally, are inevitable and ubiquitous.



1.
Modeling autonomous systems moving in varying environments

1.1.

Consider an autonomous system
M
,
“the machine”
, moving in variable environment.
As an example one may think of a robot or an autonomous vehicle
[
10
]
operating in an area populated with some other moving objects, robots and humans.
As another example we may mention a flock of automated drones
cruising in space with the enemy drones and airplanes forming the moving environment.
Yet another example is an autonomous submarine
navigating in waters populated with moving mines.
In this paper we analyse the question of how to build a motion algorithm for our autonomous system
M
so that it achieves the goal regardless of the behaviour and unpredictable changes of the environment?
We shall answer this question by employing the language of differential and algebraic topology and developing the mathematical concepts
of
infinitesimal motion algorithm
or
infinitesimal reaction mechanisms
.

The notion of infinitesimal reaction mechanism is a nonlinear generalisation of the classical notion of an Ehresmann connection, it allows selection of velocity of the autonomous system
M
as function of the velocity of changes in the environment.

1.2.

The notion of a configuration space is well-known in modern robotics
[
9
]
. This notion describes the space of all admissible states of the system moving in stationary environment. The configuration space can be viewed as is a topological space equipped with some additional structures (smooth manifold structure, a Riemannian metric etc).

1.3.

In the recent papers
[
1
,
2
]
the technique of configuration spaces was extended to the case of varying external conditions, and a new
parametrised approach
was developed, see also
[
5
]
. In this theory, instead of a single configuration space, one studies the whole family of configuration spaces parametrised by the configuration space of external conditions. In this approach we have a fibration

(1)

\displaystyle p:E\to B,

where the base
B
is the configuration space of the environment (the external conditions) and the total space
E
is the configuration space of the compound system consisting of the machine
M
and the environment. More specifically, the points
b\in B
represent various states of the environment and for each

b\in B
the fibre
E_{b}=p^{-1}(b)\subset E
is the configuration space of the machine
M
constrained by the external conditions
b
. Thus, the total space
E

is the disjoint union
\bigsqcup_{b\in B}E_{b}
of the configuration spaces of our system
M
which are
“related”
by means of the topology of the space
E
. We shall refer to (
1
) as to
fibration of configuration spaces
.

In many practically interesting situations the projection (
1
) is
a locally trivial bundle
. This means that the
space of external conditions
B
admits an open cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in J}
with the property that each
preimage
p^{-1}(U_{i})
is homeomorphic to the product
X\times U_{i}
(where
X
is the fibre). More precisely, this means that there is a continuous map
F_{i}:p^{-1}(U_{i})\to X
such that the map

p^{-1}(U_{i})\to X\times U_{i},\quad e\mapsto(F_{i}(e),p(e)),\quad\mbox{where}\quad e\in p^{-1}(U_{i}),\quad i\in J,

is a homeomorphism. We see that the spaces of configurations
E_{b_{1}}=p^{-1}(b_{1})
and
E_{b_{2}}=p^{-1}(b_{2})
living under close enough external conditions
b_{1}\sim b_{2}
can be naturally identified.

As a specific important example the authors studied in
[
5
]

the algorithms of motion of multiple objects in space avoiding collisions with multiple obstacles.

1.4.

Next we recall the notion of
a parametrised motion planning algorithm
, see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
.
Such an algorithm takes as input pairs of configurations
(e,e^{\prime})
living under the same external conditions (i.e. such that

) and produces as output a continuous motion of the system
\gamma:[0,1]\to E

with the properties
\gamma(0)=e
,
\gamma(1)=e^{\prime}
and, moreover,
p(\gamma(t))=p(e)=p(e^{\prime})\in B
for any

; the latter property means that the motion of the system is performed under the
constant
external conditions.

Hence, we see, that while parametrised motion planning algorithms work for varieties of external conditions, they are based on the assumption that
the external conditions do not change during the implementation of the motion
. This is the main distinction with the novel approach which we develop in this paper.

1.5.

Next we recall the notion of parametrised topological complexity

]
,
[

Given a locally trivial bundle

A parametrised motion planning algorithm

\displaystyle s:E^{2}_{B}\to E^{I}_{B}

of the fibration

In some situations the parametrised topological complexity

This happens in the case of the trivial bundle and also for the principal bundles

]
,
[

,
[

]
.
An example where
{\mathsf{TC}}[p:E\to B]>{\mathsf{TC}}(X)
is the Fadell - Neuwirth bundle

\displaystyle p:F(\mathbb{R}^{d},n+m)\to F(\mathbb{R}^{d},m)

studied in
[

]
and
[

]
. Here the symbol

is the fibration of configuration spaces (

The parametrised topological complexity of the bundle

We refer to the original articles for details.

2.
Lifting functions and reaction mechanisms

In this section we introduce terminology and give literature references.

2.1.

Consider an autonomous system
M
moving in variable environment. We assume that the environment is changing and this forces our system
M
to move such that (a)
M
remains consistent with the motion of the environment and (b)
M
implements its ultimate goal.
As an example one can think of a robot
M
moving in an environment occupied by some other moving objects (robots, machines, humans); the task (a) in this case means that
M
avoids collisions with the others.

A reaction mechanism
is a device which solves the tasks (a) and (b) mentioned above. The problem can be stated
using the language of fibrations
p:E\to B
of configuration spaces (
1
) as follows. The present state of the system
M
is described by the point
e\in E
and the present state of the external conditions is described by the point
b=p(e)\in B
. The external conditions move, their motion is represented by a continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to B
satisfying
\gamma(0)=b
. A reaction mechanism
L
associates with
the curve
\gamma
and the point
e\in E

a lifted curve

\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\to E
satisfying
\tilde{\gamma}(0)=e
and
p(\tilde{\gamma}(t))=\gamma(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
. In other words,
L
is the map
\gamma\mapsto\tilde{\gamma}
.

We shall denote by
I=[0,1]
the unit interval and by
B^{I}
and
E^{I}
the spaces of continuous maps
I\to B
and
I\to E
correspondingly. We equip the path-spaces
B^{I}
and
E^{I}
with the compact-open topology,
[
11
]
. We shall consider also the space
E\times_{B}B^{I}\subset E\times B^{I}
, which is defined by

(7)

\displaystyle E\times_{B}B^{I}\,=\,\{(e,\gamma)\in E\times B^{I};p(e)=\gamma(0)\}.

The projection

(8)

\displaystyle\pi:E^{I}\to E\times_{B}B^{I}

associates with any path
\tilde{\gamma}\in E^{I}
the pair
(e,\gamma)\in E\times_{B}B^{I}
where
e=\tilde{\gamma}(0)
and
\gamma=p\circ\tilde{\gamma}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A lifting function for the map
p:E\to B
is a continuous map

(9)

\displaystyle L:E\times_{B}B^{I}\to E^{I},\quad L(e,\gamma)=\tilde{\gamma},

satisfying
\pi\circ L={\rm id}
, i.e.
e=\tilde{\gamma}(0)
and
\gamma=p\circ\tilde{\gamma}
.

This definition appears in
[
11
]
, chapter 2.

Corollary 2.2
.

A lifting function
L
for the fibration of configuration spaces
p:E\to B
provides a Reaction Mechanism on changing external conditions for the system
M
.
The motion of the compound system produced by the reaction mechanism

\tilde{\gamma}(t)=L(e,\gamma)(t),\quad t\in[0,1],

is consistent with the variable external conditions changing as described by the path
\gamma(t)
.

2.2.

At an intermediate moment of time
t\in(0,1]
the automated system
“knows”
only a part of the trajectory
\gamma
of the external conditions,
namely only
\gamma|_{[0,t]}
can be measured by the sensing and observation block of the autonomous system by time
t
. We see that the reaction mechanism
L
must possess
some additional properties to be able to process the information about the external conditions and at the same time continuously produce the motion of the controlled system. The motion mechanism of this type are
“incremental”
, they will be considered and formalised in §
4
.

2.3.

The existence of a Reaction Mechanism (i.e. a lifting function) imposes certain restrictions on the map
p:E\to B
.

Theorem 2.3

(See
[
11
]
, Chapter 2, §7, Theorem 8)
.

A map
p:E\to B
admits a lifting function if and only if is a fibration in the sense of Hurewicz.

We also note the following result:

Theorem 2.4

(See
[
11
]
, chapter 2, §7, Theorem 13)
.

If
B
is Hausdorff and paracompact then
any locally trivial map
p:E\to B
is a fibration in the sense of Hurewicz and hence it admits a lifting function.

3.
The case when variation of external conditions is known in advance

3.1.

In this section we shall analyse the situation when an autonomous system
M
moves in variable external conditions and the external conditions change during the motion of our system.
We shall assume that

the variation of the external conditions is known in advance.
We show that in this case the complexity of the motion algorithms equals the complexity of the motion algorithms under
constant
external condition, i.e. it equals the parametrised topological complexity
{\mathsf{TC}}[p:E\to B]
.

3.2.

In practical situations the assumption about the variation of external conditions being known in advance can be justified if the system employs a closed loop algorithm involving continuous observations under the changes of the external conditions as well as
“the block of predictions”

suggesting the most likely trajectory of the motion of external conditions. The error of the obtained prediction will be small if the time interval is short; in that case the predicted motion of the external conditions will have small deviation from their real motion.

3.3.

We shall use terminology introduced earlier in §
1.4
, §
1.5
, §
2
.
Consider a parametrised motion planning algorithm

(10)

\displaystyle s:E^{2}_{B}\to E^{I}_{B}

and a lifting function

\displaystyle L:E\times_{B}B^{I}\to E^{I}.

We want to show how to construct a modified motion algorithm which will work under
variable external conditions
assuming that their variation
is known in advance
.
The extended algorithm
\tilde{s}
will take as input triples

(\gamma,e,e^{\prime}),

where
\gamma\in B^{I}
,
e,e^{\prime}\in E
are such that
p(e)=p(e^{\prime})=\gamma(0)
. Here the path
\gamma\in B^{I}
describes the motion of the external conditions,
e\in E
is the initial state of the system and
e^{\prime}\in E
is the target state of the system.
The output of the algorithm
\tilde{s}(\gamma,e,e^{\prime})(t)=\tilde{\gamma}(t)
is a path
\tilde{\gamma}\in E^{I}
such that:

(11)

\displaystyle p\circ\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma,\quad\tilde{\gamma}(0)=e,\quad\tilde{\gamma}(1)=L(e^{\prime},\gamma)(1).

In other words, the output
\tilde{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{s}(\gamma,e,e^{\prime})(t)

is the motion of the compound system consisting of our controlled system and the external conditions
such that (a) it is consistent
with the given motion of the external conditions, (b) it starts with the current state
e
of the system and (c) it ends in the ”new” location
L(e^{\prime},\gamma)(1)

of the system which represents (by means of the given lifting function) the target state
e^{\prime}
.
We may write

(12)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}:B^{I}\times_{B}E^{2}_{B}\to E^{I},

and the extended algorithm
\tilde{s}
is a section of the bundle

(13)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}:E^{I}\to B^{I}\times_{B}E^{2}_{B},

where for
\tilde{\gamma}\in E^{I}
one has

\tilde{\Pi}(\tilde{\gamma})=(\gamma,e,e^{\prime})\quad\mbox{where}\quad\gamma=p\circ\tilde{\gamma}\in B^{I},\quad e=\tilde{\gamma}(0),

and the point
e^{\prime}\in E
satisfying
p(e)=p(e^{\prime})
is uniquely determined by the equation

L(e^{\prime},\gamma)(1)=\tilde{\gamma}(1),
see Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
The dotted line represents the path
L(e^{\prime},\gamma)
and
\tilde{\gamma}=\tilde{s}(\gamma,e,e^{\prime})
.

The extended algorithm
\tilde{s}
can be defined (using
s
and
L
) as follows:

(14)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}(\gamma,e,e^{\prime})(t)=L(s(e,e^{\prime})(t),\gamma)(t).

If we denote

\tilde{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{s}(\gamma,e,e^{\prime})(t)=L(s(e,e^{\prime})(t),\gamma)(t)

then

p(\tilde{\gamma}(t))=\gamma(t),\quad\tilde{\gamma}(0)=s(e,e^{\prime})(0)=e\quad\mbox{ and}\quad\tilde{\gamma}(1)=L(e^{\prime},\gamma)(1).

This shows that the map
\tilde{s}
defined by formula (

) is a section of
\tilde{\Pi}
as required.

More generally, one may consider the 2-dimensional surface

(t,\tau)\mapsto L(s(e,e^{\prime})(\tau),\gamma)(t),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\tau,t\in[0,1]

and define the output of the extended algorithms
\tilde{s}
by

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}(\gamma,e,e^{\prime})(t)=L(s(e,e^{\prime})(\phi(t)),\gamma)(t),

where
\tau=\phi(t)
is an arbitrary smooth function with
\phi(0)=0
and
\phi(1)=1
.

3.4.
Complexity of the extended motion algorithm

Theorem 3.1
.

The extended algorithm
\tilde{s}
, see (
12
), has complexity equal to the complexity of the initial algorithm
s
, see (
10
).
Therefore, under the assumption that the motion of external conditions is known in advance, the motion planning problem in the situation with changing external conditions has complexity equal the complexity of the initial problem with stationary external conditions.

Proof.

To find the topological complexity of the extended algorithm
\tilde{s}
,
suppose that the initial parametrised algorithm
A
was continuous on an open subset

U\subset E^{2}_{B}
. Practically this means that the output of the parametrised algorithm
A
varies continuously with variations of the input (i.e. the initial and final configurations and the state of the external conditions) while the input remains in
U
.
From formula (
14
) we see that the extended algorithm
\tilde{s}
will be continuous on the subset

\tilde{U}=Q^{-1}(U)\subset B^{I}\times_{B}E^{2}_{B},

where
Q:B^{I}\times_{B}E^{2}_{B}\to E^{2}_{B}
is the projection.

Let
k={\mathsf{TC}}[p:E\to B]
denote the complexity of the initial problem. Then there exists an open cover

E^{2}_{B}=U_{0}\cup U_{1}\cup\dots\cup U_{k}
with a continuous section
s_{i}:U_{i}\to E^{I}_{B}
of fibration (
2
).
Then the sets
Q^{-1}(U_{i})
form an open cover of the set

B^{I}\times_{B}E^{2}_{B}
with sections of the bundle (
13
). This means that the sectional category of the bundle

\tilde{\Pi}
is smaller than or equal to
k={\mathsf{TC}}[p:E\to B]
. On the other hand, the bundle
\tilde{\Pi}
contains the bundle
\Pi
as a restriction of a subset of the constant paths

which implies the opposite inequality. We see that the sectional category of the bundle (

) equals
the parametrised topological complexity
{\mathsf{TC}}[p:E\to B]
.

3.5.
Navigation to a moving target

In this subsection we shall generalise the discussion of §
3.3
. We shall consider our controlled system
M
moving in variable environment as described by fibration
p:E\to B
. As in §
3.3
we shall assume that the motion of the external conditions is known in advance. However, unlike §
3.3
, we shall assume that the target state employs its own reaction mechanism which might be different from the reaction mechanism of our system
M
. We shall also assume that
the motion of the target is known in advance
. Our goal in this subsection is
to explain how a parametrised motion algorithm (as defined in §
1.4
) can be adapted to serve this more general purpose.

Let
s:E^{2}_{B}\to E^{I}_{B}
be a parametrised motion algorithm. Let
L:E\times_{B}B^{I}\to E^{I}
be a lifting function (the reaction mechanism of our system).
Denote by
\gamma:I\to B
the motion of the external conditions.
Consider
e\in E
(the initial state) and
e^{\prime}\in E
(the target state) satisfying
p(e)=p(e^{\prime})=\gamma(0)
, i.e. the initial state and the target live under the same external conditions. We denote by
\nu:I\to E
the motion of the target state
e^{\prime}
. Here
\nu(0)=e^{\prime}
and
p(\nu(t))=\gamma(t)
for any
t\in I
, i.e. the motion of the target is consistent with the changes of the external conditions.
We see that formally the path
\gamma
is fully determined by
\nu
(since
\gamma=p\circ\nu
).
The extended algorithm takes as input the current state of the system
e\in E
and the motion of the target
\nu\in E^{I}
and produces the motion of the system from the current state to the final state of the target
\nu(1)
. We shall define the extended algorithm

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}:E\times_{B}E^{I}\to E^{I}

as follows:

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}(e,\nu)(t)=s(L(e,p\circ\nu)(t),\nu(t))(t),\quad t\in I=[0,1].

Here the symbol
E\times_{B}E^{I}
denotes the space of all pairs
(e,\nu)\in E\times E^{I}
such that
p(e)=p(\nu(0))\in B
.
Note that the pair
(L(e,p\circ\nu)(t),\nu(t))
belongs to the space
E^{2}_{B}
and hence the value of the algorithm
s
on this pair is well-defined.
The resulting motion

\tilde{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{s}(e,\nu)(t)=s(L(e,p\circ\nu)(t),\nu(t))(t)\,\in\,E

satisfies

p\circ\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma=p\circ\nu,

i.e. it is consistent with the variation of the external conditions
\gamma
.
Besides,
\tilde{\gamma}(0)=e
and
\tilde{\gamma}(1)=\nu(1)
, i.e. it starts with the initial state of the system and ends at the predicted final state of the target.

We see that formulae (
14
) and (
17
)
”blend“
the parametrised motion algorithm
s
and the lifting function
L
.

4.
Infinitesimal lifting functions and infinitesimal reaction mechanisms

4.1.

In the previous sections we considered motion algorithms for autonomous systems which work under the assumptions that either the external conditions are constant during the implementation of the algorithm, or the external conditions change but their variation is known in advance.
In this section we shall discuss a new class of motion algorithms which are suitable for situations when neither of the above assumptions is not valid. These algorithms are applicable when the system has comprehensive sensing capabilities, and performs continuous observations under the changes of the external conditions.

The main feature of these new motion algorithms is that they are
incremental
in nature.
The resulting motion of an autonomous system can be viewed as the outcome of a continuous sequence of decisions consisting of a choice of the velocity at each moment of time.
Since the future changes in the external conditions are not known,
the motion algorithm for the autonomous system can use only information available at the time (which is the current state of the external conditions, the current velocity of the external conditions, and, maybe, the higher derivatives of the motion of the external conditions as well).
The algorithm produces the instantaneous velocity of the automated system and the automated system moves with this velocity for a very short period of time
t_{0}
(
the time scale
). Then the new measurements of the external conditions are made and the new instantaneous velocity vector is produced, and the system moves again during
t_{0}
seconds, and the process repeats. We formalise this approach below in terms of a differential equation.

We make an assumption that our autonomous system is equipped with sensing devices and is able to measure the state, the velocity and the acceleration of the external conditions without error and without time delay.

The infinitesimal motion algorithm, which we define below,
is
“Markov type”
, i.e. the outcome of the algorithm at time
t

depends only
on the current state, the velocity and the acceleration of the system and external conditions at time
t
, and not on their past behaviour.
Clearly, there could be situations when information about the past behaviour of the external conditions can also be useful, however we do not consider this point further in this work.

4.2.
Infinitesimal lifting functions and infinitesimal reaction mechanisms

Consider the fibration of configuration spaces
p:E\to B
involving the configuration spaces of the automated system and of the external conditions, see §
1.3
. In this section we shall assume that
E
and
B
are smooth manifolds and that
p
is a smooth map. By

\pi_{E}:TE\to E\quad\mbox{ and}\quad\pi_{B}:TB\to B

we denote the tangent bundles
E
and
B
correspondingly. The differential
dp:TE\to TB
is a smooth map satisfying

(18)

\displaystyle\pi_{B}\circ dp=p\circ\pi_{E}.

We denote by
E\times_{B}TB
the space of all pairs
(e,X)\subset E\times TB
such that

p(e)=\pi_{B}(X)
. One has the map

(19)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}:TE\to E\times_{B}TB,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathfrak{P}(Y)=(\pi_{E}(Y),dp(Y)),\quad\mbox{for}\quad Y\in TE.

Definition 4.1
.

An infinitesimal lifting function is a continuous map

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE,

satisfying
\mathfrak{P}\circ\mathcal{L}={\rm id}
.

In other words, given a point
e\in E
and a tangent vector
X\in T_{p(e)}B
, the function
\mathcal{L}
produces a tangent vector
Y=\mathcal{L}(e,X)
in the tangent space
T_{e}E
satisfying
dp_{e}(Y)=X
.

4.3.

We shall now restate the purpose of
\mathcal{L}
using the language of robotics:
given a state
e\in E
of the system and of the external conditions which are consistent with each other, and the velocity of the motion of the external conditions
X
, the function
\mathcal{L}
produces the velocity
Y=\mathcal{L}(e,X)
of the system consistent with
X
.

This explains why an infinitesimal lifting function serves as
an infinitesimal reaction mechanism
.

4.4.

An infinitesimal lifting function
\mathcal{L}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE
defines a lifting function

L:E\times_{B}B^{I}\to E^{I},

in the sense of Definition
2.1
as follows. For a regular curve
\gamma:I\to B
and for a point
e\in E
satisfying
p(e)=\gamma(0)
the lift
\tilde{\gamma}:I\to E
, where
\tilde{\gamma}=L(e,\gamma)
, is the solution of the initial value problem

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\tilde{\gamma}(t)\right]=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\gamma}(t),\frac{d}{dt}\left[\gamma(t)\right]),\quad\tilde{\gamma}(0)=e.

The solution exists locally and is unique.
We shall say that the infinitesimal lifting function
\mathcal{L}

is complete
if the solution exists globally for any pair
(e,\gamma)\in E\times_{B}B^{I}
. Note that any infinitesimal lifting function
\mathcal{L}
is complete if the fibre of the bundle
p:E\to B
is compact.

4.5.
Ehresmann connections.

The classical construction of Ehresmann connections
[
3
]
,
[
12
]
leads to infinitesimal lifting functions which have additional property of
linearity
. We recall this notion below.

For a smooth bundle
p:E\to B

the family of the subspaces
\mathcal{V}=\{V_{e}\}_{e\in E}
, where

V_{e}=\ker[dp_{e}:T_{e}E\to T_{b}B],

form
the vertical distribution
. This is the space of tangent vectors which are tangent to the fibres. The family of vertical tangent spaces
V_{e}

form a sub-bundle

(22)

\displaystyle T^{V}E\to E,\quad T^{V}E\subset TE

of the tangent bundle
TE
.

An Ehresmann connection
for the bundle
p:E\to B
is a differentiable distribution

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\{H_{e}\}_{e\in E}

which is complementary to the vertical distribution
\mathcal{V}
.
Given an Ehresmann connection (
23
), each tangent space
T_{e}E
, where
e\in E
, is the direct sum

T_{e}E=V_{e}\oplus H_{e}

and the differential

dp_{e}
maps the horizontal subspace
H_{e}

isomorphically
onto
T_{b}B
, where
b=p(e)\in B
. Thus, for every tangent vector
X\in T_{b}B
and for every
e\in E
with
p(e)=b
there exists
a unique lift

Y\,\in H_{e}\subset T_{e}E\quad\mbox{with}\quad dp_{e}(Y)=X.

We shall denote

Y=\mathcal{L}(e,X).

This defines
an infinitesimal lifting function
associated with the Ehresmann connection
\mathcal{H}
,

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE.

Definition 4.2
.

An Ehresmann connection is said to be complete if every smooth regular curve
\gamma:[a,b)\to B
in the base
B
admits a lift

\tilde{\gamma}
with an arbitrary initial condition
\tilde{\gamma}(a)=e
, where
p(e)=\gamma(a)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

[
7
]

Every smooth locally trivial bundle
p:E\to B
admits a complete Ehresmann connection.

We state the following obvious lemma:

Lemma 4.4
.

The infinitesimal lifting function (
24
) arising from an Ehresmann connection has the following linearity properties:

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(e,X_{1}+X_{2})=\mathcal{L}(e,X_{1})+\mathcal{L}(e,X_{2})\quad\mbox{for}\quad X_{1},X_{2}\in TB,

and

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(e,\lambda X)=\lambda\mathcal{L}(e,X)\quad\mbox{for every}\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

And wise versa, any infinitesimal lifting function (as defined in Definition
4.1
) possessing the linearity properties (
25
) and (
26
) originates from an Ehresmann connection.

We refer to the book
[
8
]
for additional information and some general results.

4.6.
Affine structure on the space of infinitesimal lifting functions and reaction forms

Note that the space of all infinitesimal lifting functions (infinitesimal reaction mechanisms) has a natural affine structure.
Let

\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE

be two infinitesimal lifting functions, and
let

\theta:E\to\mathbb{R}

be a smooth function. We can now define
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE
as an affine combination

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(e,X)=\theta(e)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{1}(e,X)+(1-\theta(e))\cdot\mathcal{L}_{2}(e,X).

If
Y_{i}=\mathcal{L}_{i}(e,X)
for
i=1,2
, then
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(e,X)=\theta(e)\cdot Y_{1}+(1-\theta(e))\cdot Y_{2}
and

dp_{e}(\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(e,X))=\theta(e)\cdot dp_{e}(Y_{1})+(1-\theta(e))\cdot dp_{e}(Y_{2})=\theta(e)\cdot X+(1-\theta(e))\cdot X=X.

We see that
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
is an infinitesimal lifting function.

One may be interested in the linear space associated with the space of infinitesimal lifting functions. This is the space of
reaction forms
defined as maps

(28)

\displaystyle\ell:E\times_{B}TB\to T^{V}E

such that the composition

(29)

\displaystyle E\times_{B}TB\,\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ell}}{{\to}}T^{V}E\,\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{E}}}{{\to}}\,E

coincides with the projection
E\times_{B}TB\to E
on the first factor. The latter condition simply means that
\ell(e,X)
lies in the space
T^{V}_{e}E
, i.e.
\ell(e,X)
is a vector tangent to the fibre at the point
e
.
The reaction form
\ell
produces a vertical tangent vector representing the velocity of the autonomous object

\ell(e,X)\in T^{V}E,\quad\mbox{where}\quad e\in E,\quad X\in T_{p(e)}B,

which depends on the velocity of the external conditions
X\in T_{p(e)}B
; the vector
\ell(e,X)
can be viewed as
“a reaction”
to the change represented by
X
.

Note that the reaction form
\ell(e,X)
does not need to be linear with respect to
X
, as illustrated by Example
4.6
and some other examples below.

4.7.
Combining reaction forms

The set of all reaction forms is a linear space:
if
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are two reaction forms on the bundle
p:E\to B
then their sum
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
is a well defined reaction form. Moreover, if

\mathcal{L}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE
is an infinitesimal reaction mechanism
and
\ell:E\times_{B}TB\to T^{V}E
is a reaction form
then the sum

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}+\ell:E\times_{B}TB\to TE

is another infinitesimal reaction mechanism.

The above discussion leads to the following:

Corollary 4.5
.

A general infinitesimal reaction mechanism
\mathcal{L}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE
can be constructed as the sum

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\dots+\ell_{N},

where
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is a fixed infinitesimal reaction mechanism (say, an Ehresmann connection) and
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N}
are reaction forms.

Typically, the support of the reaction forms
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N}
is concentrated near the boundary components of the compound configuration space
E
.

Example 4.6

(The “Moving away manoeuvre”)
.

Here we give an example of a nonlinear reaction form. Suppose that the fibre
X
of the bundle of configuration spaces
p:E\to B
is a manifold with boundary,
\partial X\not=\emptyset
. Then
\partial E\not=\emptyset
.
Let
N
be a smooth vector field on
E
which is tangent to the fibres of the fibration
p:E\to B
, i.e. such that

dp_{\ast}(N)=0.

We shall assume that the vector field
N
has support in a neighbourhood of the boundary
\partial E

and that
N
points inside of
E
at the boundary
\partial E
.

Figure 3.
Vector field
N
near
\partial X
.

We may define a reaction form
\ell
by

(31)

\displaystyle\ell(e,X)=\psi(e)\cdot|X|\cdot N(e),

where
|X|
denotes the length of the tangent vector
X\in T_{p(e)}B
with respect to a Riemannian metric and

\psi:E\to[0,1]

is a smooth function
which vanishes outside a neighbourhood of the boundary
\partial E
and equals
1
near
\partial E
.
We see that (
31
) is a positive homogeneous (but not linear) reaction form. The effect of
\ell
can be described as
“pushing away from the boundary”
.

We say that
\psi
is
an actuation function
, it switches on the action of the reaction form only when the system approached the boundary
\partial E
.

Note that the boundary
\partial E
limits the space of admissible configurations and the system should
start “reacting”
when it is near the boundary.
The configurations lying on the boundary
\partial E
represent tangencies with the obstacles and the velocity of our system at such configurations is limited.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE
be a linear infinitesimal reaction mechanism, i.e. an Ehresmann connection. If
e\in\partial E
then for any
tangent vector

X\in T_{p(e)}B
the tangent vector
Y=\mathcal{L}(e,X)
belongs to the codimension one subspace
T_{e}(\partial E)\subset T_{e}E
of vectors tangent to the boundary.

Proof.

The tangent space
T_{e}E
is divided by the subspace
T_{e}(\partial E)
into two half-spaces

T_{e}E=T_{e}^{+}\cup T_{e}^{-},\quad\mbox{where}\quad T_{e}^{+}\cap T_{e}^{-}=T_{e}(\partial E).

Here
T_{e}^{+}
are admissible velocity vectors, i.e. the vectors which point either inside
E
or are tangent to the boundary
\partial E
.
An infinitesimal reaction mechanism should satisfy

\mathcal{L}(e,X)\in T_{e}^{+}\quad\mbox{ for any}\quad X\in T_{p(e)}B.

If
Y=\mathcal{L}(e,X)\in T_{e}^{+}
then, due to linearity, one has

\mathcal{L}(e,-X)=-Y\in T_{e}^{-}\cap T_{e}^{+}=T_{e}(\partial E).

Thus, we see that
\mathcal{L}(e,X)\in T_{e}(\partial E)
for all
X\in T_{p(e)}B
.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

No linear reaction mechanism (an Ehresmann connection) can implement the task of pushing away from the boundary and hence, nonlinearity which appears in
(
31
), is unavoidable.

4.8.
Navigation target

Typically, our ultimate goal is to create an infinitesimal reaction mechanism for our autonomous system which
eventually takes our system to a prescribed target state, regardless of the behaviour of the external conditions. In the following section we shall analyse a specific situation of this kind and will see limitations of this approach. In this subsection we formalise the notion of a target in our setting involving fibrations of configuration spaces
p:E\to B
, see (
1
).

The target is a specific state of our system which we wish it to achieve. The target state can be consistent with various external conditions. If a point

b\in B
represents a state of the external conditions then the target consistent with
b
is a specific point
s(b)\in p^{-1}(b)
. We see that the target can be viewed as being a section
of the fibration
p:E\to B
, i.e. it is a continuous map

s:B\to E\quad\mbox{satisfying}\quad p\circ s=1_{B},

where
1_{B}:B\to B
is the identity map. In fact, as we shall see in the following §
5
,
in general
the target
should be viewed as a section
s:A\to E
defined on a subset
A\subset B
of admissible states of external conditions. For external conditions
b\in B-A
the target is not available.

5.
Example: two autonomous robots (vehicles) on the plane

The purpose of this section is to illustrate the material of the previous §
4
by a specific toy example which resembles the situation in automated driving.
We shall examine several infinitesimal reaction mechanisms and their effect on the motion of the system.

5.1.

We consider in this section two robots (vehicles),
M
and
N
, moving along the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The vehicle
M
represents an automated system (ego) and our task is to design its motion algorithm.
N
is another vehicle which moves according to its own goals and
M
“does not know” the future behaviour of
N
. However, we assume that
M
is able to measure the positions of both systems
M
and
N
and their velocities.

We shall represent
M
and
N
by Euclidean disks of radius
1
, and the symbols
c_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
c_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
will denote their centres.
For us the disks are not exactly the robots themselves; they rather represent certain
safety zones
around each of the vehicles.

Figure 4.
A configuration of two robots
M
and
N
.

The safety requirements impose the rule that
the interiors of the disks must be disjoint
.

We think of the robots as of rigid bodies, their states are determined by the positions of their centres
c_{M},c_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and by the angles
\phi_{M},\phi_{N}\in S^{1}
, indicating their rotational positions, see Figure
4
. However, because of angular symmetry, we shall ignore the angles
\phi_{M},\phi_{N}
and consider the positions of the centres
c_{M},c_{N}
as their states.
The admissible configurations of
M
and
N
satisfy

(32)

\displaystyle|c_{M}-c_{N}|\geq 2.

In other words, we allow the states when the safety disks are disjoint or they
touch each other along their boundaries and we require that the interiors of the disks are disjoint at all times.

As we focus on a motion algorithm for
M
, we shall view the robot
N
as an obstacle (the external conditions). The motion algorithm for
M
must (a) ensure that no collision with
N
occurs and (b)
M
arrives at a prescribed target state at certain moment of time. With this purpose in mind we shall analyse infinitesimal reaction mechanisms for
M
as defined in §
4.2
.

5.2.

We denote by
E
the configuration space of the pair
M,N
. Clearly,
E
can be identified with

(33)

\displaystyle E=\{(c_{M},c_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\,;\,|c_{M}-c_{N}|\geq 2\}.

We note that
E
is a manifold with boundary. The configuration space of
N
(the external conditions) is

B=\{c_{N}\}=\,\mathbb{R}^{2}

and the fibration of configuration spaces

p:E\to B

(see §
1.3
) is the map

(c_{M},c_{N})\mapsto c_{N}.

The fibre
p^{-1}(c_{N})
can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{2}-D
, where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the open ball of radius
2
with centre

c_{N}
. It is easy to see that the fibration
p:E\to B
is trivial in this case, i.e. it admits a global trivialisation.

Consider the differential
dp:TE\to TB
. A tangent vector at a configuration
e=(c_{M},c_{N})\in E
can be identified with the tuple

(v_{M},v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
v_{M},v_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the velocities of the centres
c_{M},c_{M}
.
If
|c_{M}-c_{N}|>2
then any such tangent vector can be realised by a smooth motion in the configuration space
E
. However if
|c_{M}-c_{N}|=2
then we need to require that

(34)

\displaystyle\langle v_{M}-v_{N},c_{M}-c_{N}\rangle\,\geq 0.

This condition guarantees that the distance between the centres of the disks does not decrease infinitesimally and hence the system remains in the space of admissible configurations, as given by (
32
).

An infinitesimal reaction mechanism
\mathcal{L}:E\times_{B}TB\to TE
(as defined in Definition
4.1
) in this specific case acts as follows.
If
X=v_{N}
and
e=(c_{M},c_{N})
then

(35)

\displaystyle Y=\mathcal{L}(e,X)=(v_{M},v_{N}),

i.e.
\mathcal{L}
“supplies” the velocity
v_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the tangent vector (
35
) is admissible.
If
|c_{M}-c_{N}|>2
then
v_{M}
can be arbitrary.
However, if
|c_{M}-c_{N}|=2
the velocity vector
v_{M}
must satisfy inequality
(
34
).
We mention below a few specific infinitesimal reaction mechanisms available for the system
M
and their effects on the mutual position of
M
and
N
.

5.3.
Infinitesimal reaction mechanism 1

Setting

(36)

\displaystyle v_{M}=v_{N}

defines an infinitesimal reaction mechanism.
In this case the system
M
simply “copies” the velocity of
N
. The rule
(
36
) defines an Ehresmann connection on the bundle
p:E\to B
. If the autonomous system
M
follows the reaction mechanism (
36
) then

(c_{M}-c_{N})^{\prime}=v_{M}-v_{N}=0,

i.e. the vector
c_{M}-c_{N}
remains constant, and the system
M
essentially repeats the movements of
N
shifted by a fixed nonzero vector, see Figure

5
.

Figure 5.
Motion of
c_{M}
and
c_{N}
under the infinitesimal reaction mechanism 1.

Note that if initially the balls
M
and
N
were touching, i.e. if
|c_{M}(0)-c_{N}(0)|=2
, then
|c_{M}(t)-c_{N}(t)|=2
for any
t\geq 0
, i.e. the balls will remain touching all the time. This is consistent with Lemma
4.7
.

5.4.
Infinitesimal reaction mechanism 2

“No reaction”
corresponds to setting

v_{M}=0
. This will define an infinitesimal reaction mechanism in areas where
|c_{M}-c_{N}|>2
, i.e. when the balls are not touching.
In the case when
|c_{M}-c_{N}|=2
this reaction rule may not satisfy (
34
). To rectify this problem we set

(37)

\displaystyle v_{M}=\psi(|c_{M}-c_{N}|)\cdot v_{N},

where
\psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
is an actuation function, i.e. a smooth function which is identically 1 on
[0,2]
and has support in
[0,3]
, see Figure
6
.

Figure 6.
The graph of the actuation function
\psi
.

Formula (
37
) defines an Ehresmann connection.
In the area
|c_{M}-c_{N}|\geq 3
the infinitesimal reaction mechanism (
37
) acts as “no reaction” and if
|c_{M}-c_{N}|=2
(the balls are touching)
we shall have
v_{M}=v_{N}
, i.e. the response will be identical to the previous example.

5.5.
Infinitesimal reaction mechanism 3

As another example of a reaction mechanism consider the following rule:

(38)

\displaystyle v_{M}=v_{N}+\lambda\cdot(c_{M}-c_{N}),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\quad\mbox{is a constant}.

The case
\lambda=0
reduces to the example of §
5.3
. We shall assume below that
\lambda\not=0
.
The infinitesimal reaction mechanism (
38
) is nonlinear and hence is not an Ehresmann connection.

Lemma 5.1
.

If the autonomous system
M
employs the infinitesimal reaction mechanism (
38
) then, regardless of the behaviour of
N
,
the vector
c_{M}-c_{N}
has constant direction and
the distance
d(t)=|c_{M}(t)-c_{N}(t)|
between the centres of the disks
M
and
N
satisfies

(39)

\displaystyle d(t)=e^{\lambda t}\cdot d(0),\quad t\geq 0.

Proof.

Differentiating the function
d^{2}(t)=\langle c_{M}-c_{N},c_{M}-c_{N}\rangle
with respect to the time
t
and using (
38
) one obtains

\frac{d}{dt}(d^{2})=2\lambda d^{2},

which implies (
39
) by integration. On the other hand, for any fixed vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
the scalar product
\langle v,c_{M}-c_{N}\rangle
satisfies

\langle v,c_{M}-c_{N}\rangle^{\prime}=\lambda\langle v,c_{M}-c_{N}\rangle
, i.e.
\langle v,c_{M}-c_{N}\rangle=Ce^{\lambda t}
, where
C
is a constant. Hence, if
v
is perpendicular to the vector

c_{M}-c_{N}
at time
t=0
then
C=0
and therefore
v\perp(c_{M}-c_{N})
for all
t
, i.e.
c_{M}
lies on the line passing through
c_{N}
in the direction perpendicular to the vector
v
.
∎

In the case
\lambda>0
, the automated vehicle
M
moves away from
N
exponentially far. In the case
\lambda<0
the distance between
M
and
N
exponentially decreases and at certain moment of time it will become
d=2
, i.e. we shall arrive at the boundary of the configuration space
E
; at this moment the vehicles
M
and
N
will touch each other.

We see that the infinitesimal motion algorithms (
38
)
with
\lambda\geq 1

“guarantee safety,”
i.e. under the assumption
\lambda\geq 1

the vehicle
M
will achieve safety regardless of the behaviour of
N
.

5.6.
Infinitesimal reaction mechanism 4

As another example, consider the infinitesimal reaction mechanism of the form

(40)

\displaystyle v_{M}=v_{N}+\mu\cdot(c_{M}-c_{N})^{\perp},

where
(c_{M}-c_{N})^{\perp}
is the vector obtained from
c_{M}-c_{N}
by a 90 degrees rotation in the positive direction, and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
c
denotes the vector
c_{M}-c_{N}
then (
40
) gives
\dot{c}=\mu c^{\perp}
and we have

\langle c,c\rangle^{\prime}=2\cdot\langle\dot{c},c\rangle=0,

i.e. the norm
|c|=r
is constant. We may write
c=re^{i\phi}
and then
\dot{c}=i\phi^{\prime}\cdot re^{i\phi}
and since
c^{\perp}=ic=ire^{i\phi}
we obtain

\phi^{\prime}=\mu.

We see that in this case the distance between the centres
c_{M}
and
c_{N}
remains constant and
M
rotates around
N
with angular velocity
equal the value of
the parameter
\mu
.

6.
Linear Infinitesimal Reaction Mechanisms with constant coefficients

6.1.

In this section we continue to consider the model with two balls on the plane as described in §
5
and analyse several linear infinitesimal reaction mechanisms for this model.
Recall that in this model we have two round balls of radius 1 on the plane, where
M
is our ego vehicle and
N
is “the other vehicle” whose motion is not known to
M
in advance.

The differential equations (
21
) can easily be integrated in the case of linear infinitesimal reaction mechanism
with constant coefficients
which we consider in this section.
The general infinitesimal reaction mechanism of this kind has the form

(41)

\displaystyle v_{M}=\alpha\cdot v_{N}+\beta\cdot v_{N}^{\perp},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R},

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are constants. Here
v_{N}
is the velocity of the ball
N
, i.e.
v_{N}=\dot{c}_{N}
, and
v_{N}^{\perp}
stands for the result of 90 degrees rotation of the vector
v_{N}
in the positive direction. We shall assume that
(\alpha,\beta)\not=(0,0)
, i.e., we exclude the possibility that both parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
simultaneously vanish, compare §
5.4
.

If
c_{N}(t)
and
c_{M}(t)
denote the motions of the centres of the balls
N
and
M
then equation (
41
) means

(42)

\dot{c}_{M}=\alpha\cdot\dot{c}_{N}+\beta\cdot J\dot{c}_{N}=(\alpha I+\beta J)\dot{c}_{N}=A\dot{c}_{N},

where
I=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]
is the identity map
I:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
J:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the 90 degrees rotation, i.e.
J=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]
, and
A=\alpha I+\beta J
, i.e.
A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&-\beta\\
\beta&\alpha\end{array}\right]
.
From (
42
), integrating, we obtain

(43)

c_{M}(t)=Ac_{N}(t)-c_{0},

where
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a fixed vector.

6.2.

There are two essentially distinct cases.
If
A=I
, i.e.,
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
, then

c_{M}(t)=c_{N}(t)-c_{0},

where
c_{0}
is a constant vector. This is the infinitesimal reaction mechanism of
§
5.3
. In this case the system
M

“repeats”
the motion of
N
with a shift by vector
c_{0}
. The distance between the systems
M
and
N
remains constant and is equal
|c_{0}|
, i.e.

|c_{M}(t)-c_{N}(t)|=|c_{0}|\quad\mbox{for all}\quad t\geq 0.

6.3.

Consider now the remaining general case
(\alpha,\beta)\not=(1,0)
. We have

(44)

\displaystyle c_{M}(t)-c_{N}(t)=Bc_{N}(t)-c_{0},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where

B=A-I=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha-1&-\beta\\
\beta&\alpha-1\end{array}\right].

Since
\det B=(\alpha-1)^{2}+\beta^{2}\not=0
, we see that the matrix
B
is invertible. We may write
c_{0}=B\tilde{c}_{0}
for a unique vector
\tilde{c}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and then
equation (
44
) can be presented as

(45)

c_{M}(t)-c_{N}(t)=B(c_{N}(t)-\tilde{c}_{0}).

We see from (
45
) that the distance between the balls
M
and
N
tends to 0 when
c_{N}(t)
tends to
\tilde{c}_{0}
.
The vector
\tilde{c}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be expressed through the positions of the vehicles
M
and
N
at time
t=0
, i.e. via
c_{M}(0)
and
c_{N}(0)
, as follows:

(46)

\tilde{c}_{0}=(I+B^{-1})\cdot c_{N}(0)-B^{-1}\cdot c_{M}(0).

Below the notation
|B|
stands for the norm of the operator
B:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Assume that the autonomous (ego) vehicle
M
employs the linear infinitesimal reaction mechanism (
41
) with constant coefficients, and
(\alpha,\beta)\not=(1,0)
.
Consider the ball
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with centre
\tilde{c}_{0}
and radius
2\cdot|B|^{-1}
, as well as the ball
D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with centre
\tilde{c}_{0}
and radius

2\cdot|B^{-1}|,
where
\tilde{c}_{0}
is given by (
46
) and depends only on the initial positions
c_{M}(0)
and
c_{N}(0)
. Since
|B|^{-1}\leq|B^{-1}|,
we have

D\subset D^{\prime}.
We claim:

(a)

If for some
t>0
, the position of the centre
c_{N}(t)
of the vehicle
N
arrives to the ball
D
, i.e. if
c_{N}(t)\in D
, then at the moment
t
the vehicles
M
and
N
are in collision;

(b)

If at time
t
the position of the centre
c_{N}(t)
of the vehicle
N
is situated outside of the disk
D^{\prime}
, i.e.
c_{N}(t)\notin D^{\prime}
,
then the configuration of the vehicles
M
and
N
is admissible at time
t
.

Figure 7.
The balls
D\subset D^{\prime}
with the centre
c_{0}
.

Proof.

From equation (
45
) we see that if

(47)

|c_{N}(t)-\tilde{c}_{0}|<2\cdot|B|^{-1},

then

|c_{M}(t)-c_{N}(t)|\leq|B|\cdot|c_{N}(t)-\tilde{c}_{0}|<2,

i.e. the balls
M
and
N
are in collision; this proves (a).
On the other hand, if

(48)

|c_{N}(t)-\tilde{c}_{0}|\geq 2\cdot|B^{-1}|,

then

|c_{M}(t)-c_{N}(t)|\geq|B^{-1}|^{-1}\cdot|c_{N}(t)-\tilde{c}_{0}|\geq 2,

i.e. the configuration of
M
and
N
is admissible; this proves (b).
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Lemma
6.1
does not give an answer to the question what happens when the centre
c_{N}(t)
lies in the complement
D^{\prime}-D
.
From (
45
) we see that a non-admissible configuration
(c_{M}(t),c_{N}(t))
happens iff the vector
c_{N}(t)-\tilde{c}_{0}
belongs to the image of the ball of radius
2
under the operator
B^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The domain

\mathcal{D}=\{B^{-1}(x)+\tilde{c}_{0};|x|\leq 2\}

satisfies
D\subset\mathcal{D}\subset D^{\prime}
and, complementing the statement of Lemma
6.1
, one may state that
the vehicles
M
and
N
are in collision iff
c_{N}(t)\in\mathcal{D}
. The statement of Lemma
6.1
gives simple and easily verifiable criteria for absence or presence of collisions.

Corollary 6.3
.

If the autonomous vehicle
M
employs a linear infinitesimal reaction mechanism with constant coefficients (
41
) with
(\alpha,\beta)\not=(1,0)

then “the other vehicle”
N
is always capable of “forcing” a collision.

Proof.

Indeed, the vehicle
N
can simply move into the ball
D
.
∎

6.4.

To illustrate the above discussion we consider now a specific example of a linear infinitesimal reaction mechanism (
41
) with
\beta=0
and
\alpha\not=1
,
\alpha>0
. Thus we have
v_{M}=\alpha\cdot v_{N}
. Then the matrix
B
has the form

B=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha-1&0\\
0&\alpha-1\end{array}\right],\quad B^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(\alpha-1)^{-1}&0\\
0&(\alpha-1)^{-1}\end{array}\right]

and, according to (
46
),

(49)

\tilde{c}_{0}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\cdot c_{N}(0)-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\cdot c_{M}(0).

We see that
\tilde{c}_{0}
lies on the straight line connecting
c_{M}(0)
and
c_{N}(0)
. The matrix
B
is diagonal and hence

|B|^{-1}=|B^{-1}|=(\alpha-1)^{-1},

i.e. the discs
D
and
D^{\prime}
coincide in this special case.

If
\alpha>1
then
c_{N}(0)
lies between
c_{M}(0)
and
\tilde{c}_{0}
, see Figure
8
(left).

\alpha>1

N
moves towards
\tilde{c}_{0}
with velocity

then, according to the motion algorithm (
41
),
M
moves towards
\tilde{c}_{0}

\displaystyle|\tilde{c}_{0}-c_{M}(0)|=\alpha\cdot|\tilde{c}_{0}-c_{N}(0)|,

and hence, if

c_{M}(t)
and

arrive at the point

Thus, for

\alpha>1

the distance between

and

becomes smaller than
2

If

Conclusions:

(2) This discussion illustrates the necessity to use
