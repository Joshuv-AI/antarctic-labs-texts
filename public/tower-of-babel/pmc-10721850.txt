Effects of insulin-like growth factor-1 on the mRNA expression of estradiol receptors, steroidogenic enzymes, and steroid production in bovine follicles
Authors: Ahmad Farid RAWAN, Hikmatullah LANGAR, Maho MUNETOMO, Yuki YAMAMOTO, Kohei KAWANO, Koji KIMURA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) plays a crucial role in follicular growth and stimulates steroid hormone production in bovine follicles. Steroid hormones are synthesized through the actions of steroidogenic enzymes, specifically STAR, CYP11A1, HSD3B, and CYP19A1 in both theca cells (TCs) and granulosa cells (GCs), under the influence of gonadotropins. Particularly, estradiol 17β (E2) assumes a central role in follicular development and selection by activating estrogen receptors β (ESR2) in GCs. We assessed ESR2 mRNA expression in GCs of developing follicles and investigated the impact of IGF-1 on the mRNA expression of ESR2, CYP19A1, FSHR, and LHCGR, STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD17B in cultured GCs and TCs, respectively. Additionally, we assessed the influence of IGF-1 on androstenedione (A4), progesterone (P4), and testosterone (T) production in TCs. Small-sized follicles (< 6 mm) exhibited the highest levels of ESR2 mRNA expression, whereas medium-sized follicles (7–8 mm) displayed higher levels than large-sized follicles (≥ 9 mm) (P < 0.05). IGF-1 increased the mRNA expression of ESR2, CYP19A1, and FSHR in GCs of follicles of both sizes, except for FSHR mRNA in medium-sized follicles (P < 0.05). IGF-1 significantly elevated mRNA expression of LHCGR, STAR, CYP11A1, and CYP17B in TCs of small- and medium-sized follicles (P < 0.05). Moreover, IGF-1 augmented the production of A4 and P4 but had no impact on T production in TCs of small- and medium-sized follicles. Taken together, our findings indicate that IGF-1 upregulates steroidogenic enzymes and steroid hormone production, underscoring the crucial role of IGF-1 in follicle development and selection.Keywords: Estradiol receptor, Follicle, Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), Steroidogenic enzymes

The selection of a single dominant follicle is associated with differential growth rates in a cohort of developing follicles, maintaining the ovulatory capabilities of cattle. The growth rate change between the largest and second-largest follicles is defined as follicular deviation [1]. During the common growth phase that precedes deviation, the concentrations of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) decrease [2], and the concentrations of estradiol 17β (E2) in follicular fluid begin to increase in the future dominant follicle [3]. E2 promotes follicular growth and aromatase activity in granulosa cells (GCs) [4, 5]. The action of E2 is mediated by estradiol receptors (ER) [6]. One of these ERs, ESR2, is primarily detected in GCs, whereas ERα is found in theca cells (TCs) [7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ESR2 knockout mice exhibited delayed follicular growth [8] and failed to demonstrate efficient ovulation even when treated with exogenous gonadotropins [9]. These findings suggest that ESR2 plays a crucial role in follicular development and ovulation.

Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) assumes a central role in bovine follicular growth. It has been posited that IGF-1 is requisite for follicular dominance in cattle [1] and may participate in the deviation mechanism by elevating E2 concentration in bovine follicles [3] or augmenting the responsiveness of GCs to their specific gonadotropin receptors [10]. IGF-1 augments the FSH responsiveness of GCs by increasing the expression of the FSH receptor (FSHR) in mice [11]. In an in vitro investigation, IGF-1 upregulated the expression of luteinizing hormone receptors (LHCGR) in GCs from small-sized follicles in cattle [12]. However, no study has thus far investigated the effects of IGF-1 on the mRNA expression of ESR2 in bovine GCs, either in vitro or in vivo.

Androgens serve as substrates for E2 synthesis, a process crucial for follicular growth and selection. In the context of steroidogenesis, androstenedione (A4) is synthesized through the concerted actions of steroidogenic acute regulatory (STAR) [13], cytochrome P450 side chain cleavage (CYP11A1), cytochrome P450 17α-hydroxylase (CYP17A1) [14], 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (HSD17B), and 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (HSD3B) in TCs under LH stimulation. A4 then serves as the substrate for E2 synthesis by cytochrome P450 aromatase (CYP19A1) under FSH stimulus, interacting with specific receptors in GCs [14, 15]. The biosynthesis of P4 is under the regulation of HSD3B [16], and P4 is subsequently converted to testosterone (T) through the action of HSD17B in TCs [17]. Previous studies have revealed that IGF-1 enhances the mRNA expression of CYP11A1, HSD3B, and CYP19A1 in bovine GCs in vitro [18, 19]. Additionally, IGF-1 significantly promotes the mRNA expression of STAR, CYP19A1, and HSD3B in cultured mouse TCs [20]. However, the effects of IGF-1 on steroidogenic enzymes in bovine TCs remain less understood. Several studies have demonstrated that A4 and T stimulate follicular growth and development in cows [21]. In vitro culture of mouse preantral follicles with T and A4 has been shown to enhance follicular growth and development [22, 23]. In cattle, IGF-1 has been found to enhance androgen synthesis from TCs [24]. Furthermore, in an in vivo study conducted on heifers, the effects of IGF-1 on follicular fluid factors were investigated. In this study, IGF-1 was injected into the second-largest follicle at the expected onset of deviation, resulting in increased concentrations of A4 in the follicular fluid after 3 h [25]. Nevertheless, our current understanding of the effects of IGF-1 on steroid hormone production in bovine TCs remains limited.

Considering the aforementioned findings, we hypothesized that IGF-1 increases the expression of ESR2 mRNA in GCs and A4 production in the theca cells of small-sized follicles, which are the predominant factors for the development and selection of follicles. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to determine the effects of IGF-1 on ESR2 expression in bovine GCs of small- and mid-sized follicles. In addition, the effects of IGF-1 on mRNA expression of steroidogenic proteins and production of steroid hormones (A4, P4, and T) were examined in bovine GCs and TCs derived from small- and medium-sized follicles.

Materials and Methods

Collection of bovine ovaries

The ovaries of healthy cows were collected from a local abattoir following exsanguination and transported to the laboratory on ice to obtain TCs and GCs from small- (< 6 mm) and medium-diameter (7–8 mm) follicles, as previously described [26]. The ovaries were rinsed with sterile saline and 70% ethanol and stored in physiological saline at 4°C until the aspiration of GCs and dissection of follicles for TC collection. For uncultured GCs, samples were collected from each pair of ovaries between days 1–4 post-ovulation, during which the first follicular wave typically initiates [27]; therefore, it is easy to identify the healthy, growing follicles in the ovaries.

Isolation and culture of theca and granulosa cells

Bovine TCs were enzymatically harvested from dissected small- and medium-sized follicles in accordance with previously established methods [28, 29]. Briefly, the follicles were dissected from the ovaries, with subsequent removal of the surrounding tissue using forceps. Subsequently, the dissected follicles were cut into hemispheres, and gentle scraping with forceps was employed to remove GCs and a portion of the theca cell layer. The resulting thin theca layer was then rinsed with DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 100 IU/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, and 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). Following this, the theca layer was minced with DMEM and treated with 6600 units/ml collagenase (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan), 8000 units/ml DNase (DNP2, BBI Solutions, Newport, UK), 1 mg/ml dispase (ood Shusei Co., Ltd., Chiba, Japan), and 1 mg/ml hyaluronidase (Nacalai Tesque). The cell dissociation process continued for 60 min at 37°C under continuous stirring at 800 rpm. Subsequent to incubation, the cell suspension underwent separation from undigested tissue via filtration through metal meshes (100 µm × 2, 80 µm × 2), and the dispersed theca cells were centrifuged (800 × g, 5 min at 4°C) and subsequently resuspended in Tris-NH4CL for hemolysis after the supernatant was discarded. The dispersed cells were subjected to two successive washes with DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 100 IU/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, and 0.1% BSA. Cell viability was assessed, yielding results in the range of 90–95%, employing the trypan blue exclusion test.

The GCs and follicular fluid were aseptically collected from small- and medium-sized follicles employing a 2.5-ml disposable syringe equipped with a 24-gauge needle. These specimens were then transferred to a plastic Petri dish containing phenol red-free DMEM/Ham’s F-12 in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The culture medium consisted of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 20 µg/ml amphotericin B (Sigma Aldrich), and 50 IU heparin sodium salt (Nacalai Tesque). Next, the GCs, along with the associated follicular fluid, were centrifuged (800 × g, 5 min at 4°C) and subsequently resuspended in Tris-NH4CL for hemolysis, with the supernatant being discarded. The cell suspensions underwent an additional centrifugation step and were resuspended in DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 100 IU/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, and 0.1% BSA. Subsequently, the cell suspensions were filtered through metal meshes (100 µm × 2, 80 µm × 2) to remove cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs). After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the washing process was repeated twice. The cell suspensions were then resuspended in an appropriate volume of phenol red-free DMEM/F-12 containing 10% FBS and 20 µg/ml gentamicin (Sigma-Aldrich). The viability of GCs was assessed, with a viability range of 75–80%, as determined by the exclusion of trypan blue. The dispersed GCs and TCs were separately cultured at a concentration of 1.0 × 105 viable cells per 1 ml in phenol red-free DMEM/F-12 culture medium containing 10% FBS. The culture was conducted in 75-cm2 culture flasks (20 ml/flask; Greiner Bio-One, Frickenhausen, Germany) under controlled conditions of 95% air and 5% CO2 at 38.5°C for 2–3 days. The culture medium was replaced every 24 h Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, the cultured cells were passaged using 0.1% bovine trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich) and sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The dispersed cells in four replicate wells were preincubated with and without IGF-1 by culturing for 24 h in a serum-free medium, both in the absence (control) and in the presence of varying concentrations of IGF-1.

Total RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured and non-cultured GCs and TCs using RNAiso Plus (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA extracted from each sample was quantified, and 2 µg of total RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA remover (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan).

The mRNA expressions of bovine ESR2, FSHR, CYP19A1, LHCGR, STAR, CYP11A1,HSD17B, β-actin (ACTB), and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) were determined according to a previously described method [30]. The expression of each mRNA was quantified using Brilliant III Ultra-Fast SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix with Low ROX (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), starting with 2 ng of reverse-transcribed total RNA. To standardize the relative levels of ESR2, FSHR, and CYP19A1 mRNA expression in GCs, ACTB was employed as an internal control, and to standardize the relative levels of LHCGR, STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD17B in TCs, GAPDH served as an internal control. Five housekeeping genes, MRPL4, H2A, GAPDH, ACTB, and 18S, were assessed to determine the optimal reference gene for qRT-PCR analysis of isolated and cultured bovine GCs and TCs. ACTB for GCs and GAPDH for TCs were selected as reference genes using NormFinder [31], as the expression of ACTB and GAPDH displayed the highest stability among the samples when compared to other genes. To quantify the mRNA expression levels, PCR was performed using the AriaMx Real-Time PCR System (Agilent Technologies). The amplification began with an initial hot start at 95ºC for 30 sec, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95ºC for 10 sec, annealing at the temperature indicated in Table 1 for 10 sec, and extension at 72ºC for 15 sec, followed by a dissociation program (95°C for 1 min, with temperature intervals as indicated in Table 1, increasing by 0.5°C, and 5 sec per interval). Serial dilutions (20–20,000,000 copies) of each PCR product extracted from the agarose gel were employed as standards for the analysis of the respective mRNA expression levels.

Enzyme immunoassay (EIA)

The concentrations of A4, P4, and T in the culture medium were determined using enzyme immunoassays, as previously described [32]. The A4 standard curve ranged from 3.9062 to 4000 ng/ml. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation of the TCs of small- and medium-sized follicles were, on average, 5.81% and 11.52%, and 5.14% and 15.01%, respectively. The P4 standard curve ranged from 0.0078125 to 2 ng/ml. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation of the TCs of the small- and medium-sized follicles averaged 4.22% and 12.57%, and 3.53% and 10.64%, respectively. The T standard curve ranged from 0.625 to 12.8 ng/ml. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation of the TCs of the small- and medium-sized follicles averaged 4.16% and 16.34%, and 7.8% and 14.32%, respectively. To accommodate the range of standard concentrations, the culture media were diluted 10–40 times in assay buffer.

DNA assay

The DNA content of the cultured TCs was measured as previously described [33]. Briefly, following cell disruption with an ultrasonic homogenizer, cell lysates were incubated with Hoechst 33258 (Sigma-Aldrich). Following a 10-min incubation period, the fluorescence of each sample and standard was determined employing a microplate fluorometer (Fluoroskan Ascent, Labsystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The standard curve spanned a concentration range from 0.625 to 30 µg/ml.

Experiment 1: ESR2 mRNA expression in uncultured granulosa cells

The three largest healthy follicles (i.e., those exhibiting robust vascularization and a transparent follicular wall with fluid) were excised from paired ovaries. They were placed in a dish on ice and subsequently categorized into three distinct size groups based on their respective diameters: small (< 6 mm), medium (7–8 mm), and large (≥ 9 mm) on days 1–4 post-ovulation. GCs were acquired through the hemisection of these follicles at room temperature, and the process was performed in a dish also placed on ice. Then, GCs were isolated from each follicle after centrifugation, immediately combined with 400 µl of RNAiso Plus (Takara Bio Inc.), and stored at –80°C until the total RNA extraction process for subsequent quantitative RT-PCR analysis.

Experiment 2: Effects of IGF-1 on the mRNA expression of ESR2, FSHR, and CYP19A1 in cultured granulosa cells

Cultured GCs from small- and medium-sized follicles were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 105 viable cells in 1 ml of phenol red-free DMEM/F-12 in each well of 24-well plates (Greiner Bio-One). The cells were incubated at 38.5°C, and 2 days after seeding, they were incubated with a medium (v/v) containing 0.1% BSA, 5 µg/ml holotransferrin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 2 µg/ml insulin (Sigma-Aldrich), and treated with various concentrations (10, 50, and 100 ng/ml) of IGF-1 (Bio Vision, Waltham, MA, USA) for 24 h. The selection of IGF-1 concentrations in our study was based on previous studies [12, 18]. In each group, the cells were mixed with 400 µl of RNAiso Plus in a 1.5-ml tube and stored at –80°C for subsequent RNA extraction.

Experiment 3: Effects of IGF-1 on the mRNA expression of LHCGR, STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD17B in cultured theca cells

The culture condition for Experiment 3 was identical to that of Experiment 2. However, in this experiment, the cultured TCs were exposed to varying concentrations (10, 50, and 100 ng/ml) of IGF-1 for 24 h. In each experimental group, the cells were combined with 400 µl of RNAiso Plus in a 1.5 ml tube and subsequently stored at –80°C for subsequent RNA extraction.

Experiment 4: Effects of IGF-1 on steroid hormone (A4, P4, and T) production by cultured bovine theca cells

To investigate the impact of IGF-1 on the production of A4, P4, and T, cultured TCs derived from small- and medium-sized follicles were initially seeded at a density of 0.5 × 105 viable cells in 250 µl of phenol red-free DMEM/F-12 within 48-well plates (250 µl per well; Greiner Bio-One). The cells were incubated at 38.5°C, and 2 days following the seeding process, they were exposed to a medium (v/v) comprising 0.1% BSA, 5 µg/ml holotransferrin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 2 µg/ml insulin (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, the cells were treated with varying concentrations (10, 50, and 100 ng/ml) of IGF-1 for 24 h. Upon completion of the incubation period, the medium was collected in a 1.5-ml tube, and the concentrations of A4, P4, and T were assessed using enzyme immunoassay. Furthermore, the DNA content was measured to standardize the concentrations of androgen and P4.

Purity of granulosa and theca cells

Isolated GCs and TCs were individually cultured at a concentration of 1.0 × 105 viable cells per 1 ml in phenol red-free DMEM/F-12 culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS. These cultures were maintained in 6-well plates and incubated at 38.5°C in an atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 for 2 days. The purity of the isolated GCs and TCs was assessed using a microscope (FSX100, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Morphologically, the TCs appeared thin, elongated, and spindle-shaped, while the GCs exhibited a thicker, flat-shaped morphology and were smaller compared to the TCs. The purity of the isolated GCs and TCs was found to be 95% and 90%, respectively (Fig. 1).

Isolated bovine granulosa cells (GCs, A) and theca cells (TCs, B) were cultured separately at a density of 1.0 × 105 viable cells per 1 ml in phenol red-free DMEM/F-12 culture medium containing 10% FBS for 2 days. The cultured cells were subsequently examined under a microscope. TCs exhibited a slender, elongated, and spindle-shaped morphology, while GCs displayed a thicker, flat shape and were smaller in size compared to TCs.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Normality and homoscedasticity were verified utilizing the Shapiro-Wilk test and the Brown-Forsythe test in R [34], respectively. The data were analyzed through ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison tests in R. The statistical significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Experiment 1. ESR2 mRNA expression in uncultured granulosa cells

The three largest healthy follicles were extracted from each pair of ovaries (Days 1–4 post-ovulation). The expression of ESR2 mRNA in GCs from small- (< 6 mm), medium- (7–8 mm), and large-sized (≥ 9 mm) follicles is presented in Fig. 2. ESR2 mRNA expression in GCs was observed to be low in large-sized follicles (≥ 9 mm), moderately higher in medium-sized follicles (7–8 mm), and highest in small-sized follicles (< 6 mm) (P < 0.05). ESR2 mRNA expression was higher in small-sized follicles compared to both medium- and large-sized follicles.

Gene expression of E2 receptor β (ESR2) mRNA relative to the amount of β-actin (ACTB) was assessed in isolated GCs obtained from the three largest follicles in each paired ovary between days 1 and 4 post-ovulation (follicles per group). The expression values are presented as the means ± SEM. The follicles were categorized into size groups as follows: small-sized (< 6 mm), medium-sized (7–8 mm), and large-sized (≥ 9 mm) in diameter. Different superscript letters (a–c) indicate significant differences (P < 0.05; ANOVA) among the groups, as determined by Tukey’s multiple comparison test.

Experiment 2. Effects of IGF-1 on the expression of mRNA of ESR2, FSHR, and CYP19A1 in cultured granulosa cells

GCs were treated with varying doses of IGF-1 for 24 h. The results demonstrated a significant increase (P < 0.05) in the mRNA expression of ESR2,FSHR, and CYP19A1 when subjected to a dose of 100 ng/ml of IGF-1 in cultured GCs derived from small-sized follicles. However, the other doses exhibited no discernible effect on the mRNA expression of ESR2,FSHR, and CYP19A1 in GCs from small-sized follicles. In the case of medium-sized follicles, IGF-1 (10–100 ng/ml) elicited an increase in ESR2 mRNA expression, with a statistically significant (P < 0.05) elevation observed only at the 100 ng/ml concentration for CYP19A1 mRNA expression. FSHR mRNA expression in cultured GCs from medium-sized follicles remained unaffected following a 24-h treatment with IGF-1 (Fig. 3).

Effects of IGF-1 on mRNA expression of ESR2 (A), FSHR (B), and CYP19A1 (C) in GCs. The genes were quantified relative to β-actin (ACTB) in cultured GCs from small- and medium-sized follicles. The expression values are presented as means ± SEM of five replicates. Different superscript letters indicate statistically significant differences (P < 0.05; ANOVA) as determined by Tukey’s multiple comparison test.

Experiment 3: Effects of IGF-1 on the mRNA expression of LHCGR, STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD17B in cultured theca cells

The cultured TCs were exposed to specified doses of IGF-1 for 24 h. IGF-1 (100 and 50–100 ng/ml) produced a statistically significant (P < 0.05) increase in the mRNA expression of LHCGR and HSD17B in small- and medium-sized follicles, respectively. IGF-1 (100 ng/ml) elevated the expression of STAR mRNA in TCs derived from small-sized follicles, while IGF-1 (10–100 ng/ml) augmented STAR mRNA expression in TCs from medium-sized follicles. IGF-1 (50 and 100 ng/ml) significantly elevated (P < 0.05) the CYP11A1 mRNA expression in TCs from small- and medium-sized follicles, respectively (Fig. 4).

The genes were quantified relative to GAPDH in cultured TCs from small- and medium-sized follicles. The expression values are presented as the means ± SEM of five replicates. Different superscript letters indicate statistically significant differences (P < 0.05; ANOVA) as determined by Tukey’s multiple comparison test.

Experiment 4. Effects of IGF-1 on androstenedione, progesterone, and testosterone production in cultured bovine theca cells.

The cultured TCs were exposed to varying doses of IGF-1 (10, 50, and 100 ng/ml) for 24 h. IGF-1 (50 ng/ml) elicited an increase in A4 production within TCs derived from small-sized follicles, whereas IGF-1 (10–50 ng/ml) significantly enhanced (P < 0.05) A4 production within TCs from medium-sized follicles (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, IGF-1 significantly enhanced P4 production within TCs (50–100 ng/ml in small-sized follicles, 100 ng/ml in medium-sized follicles, respectively) (see Fig. 5B). However, IGF-1 exhibited no discernible impact on T production in TCs derived from either small- or medium-sized follicles (Fig. 5C).

Different superscript letters indicate significant differences (P < 0.05; ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test.

Discussion

The intraovarian actions of ESR2 are essential for fully differentiated follicles and are necessary for the enzymatic activity required to convert androgen precursors to E2 [35], a critical process for follicular growth and deviation. The ESR2 mRNA receptor mediates E2 actions in GCs [8, 15, 16]. In the present study, the expression of ESR2 mRNA in GCs exhibited a decrease with increasing follicular size. This finding is consistent with prior research indicating a decrease in mRNA ESR2 levels in bovine GCs of large-sized follicles [36,37,38]. These results suggest that elevated ESR2 mRNA expression in GCs of small- and medium-sized follicles is crucial for their growth and deviation. In a previous study, IGF-1 was shown to increase E2 production in cultured GCs of small-sized follicles [12], a process that might necessitate a higher level of ESR2 mRNA expression to maintain its bioactivity. The impact of IGF-1 on ESR2 mRNA expression in GCs has not been comprehensively investigated in cattle previously. Therefore, we investigated the effect of IGF-1 on ESR2 mRNA expression in bovine GCs. Our results demonstrate that IGF-1 (100 ng/ml) increased ESR2 mRNA expression in the GCs of small-sized follicles, whereas lower concentrations of IGF-1 (10–100 ng/ml) increased ESR2 mRNA expression in the GCs of medium-sized follicles. These findings indicate that (i) the effect of IGF-1 on ESR2 mRNA in GCs is dose-dependent; (ii) the stimulatory effect of IGF-1 on ESR2 mRNA supports our hypothesis that IGF-1 enhances ESR2 mRNA expression in GCs of small-sized follicles to maintain E2 bioactivity for follicular growth and deviation; and (iii) the effects of IGF-1 may depend on the presence of IGF1R in GCs, warranting further investigation into the effects of IGF-1 on mRNA expression of IGF1R in GCs at different stages of follicular development.

The synthesis of steroid hormones in bovine GCs is regulated by ESR2 [35], FSHR [39], and CYP19A1 [18]. IGF-1 assumes a central role in these interactions concerning steroidogenesis [40, 41]. In this study, we investigated the impact of IGF-1 on gene expression that governs E2 synthesis in GCs. Our previous research demonstrated a significant increase in E2 production in vitro in response to IGF-1 (1–100 ng/ml) in GCs from small-sized follicles [12]. In this study, IGF-1 (100 ng/ml) elevated the mRNA expressions of CYP19A1 and FSHR, and even a low dose of IGF-1 (10 ng/ml) stimulated CYP19A1 mRNA expression in GCs from small-sized follicles, responsible for the conversion of A4 to E2 in the GCs. While IGF-1 (100 ng/ml) increased CYP19A1 mRNA expression, it had no impact on FSHR mRNA expression in GCs from medium-sized follicles. Prior studies have reported that IGF-1 (10 ng/ml) elevated FSHR mRNA levels in mice [20], and IGF-1 (50 ng/ml) enhanced the mRNA expressions of both FSHR and CYP19A1 in bovine GCs (2–8 mm) treated for 48 h [18, 19]. The species and culture protocol employed can influence the mRNA expression of these genes. These results unequivocally indicate that IGF-1 significantly augments CYP19A1 and FSHR mRNA levels in GCs from small-sized follicles, which in turn regulate E2 synthesis and play a pivotal role in follicular development.

Androgen is synthesized through steroidogenesis-related enzymes in TCs, which serve as precursors for E2 [42, 43]. The binding of LH to its receptor on TCs stimulates the activities of steroidogenic enzymes necessary for A4 synthesis [44]. In our study, IGF-1 significantly increased the expression of LHCGR mRNA in TCs in both sized follicles, consistent with previous reports in cultured follicles of mice [20] and in bovine GCs [12]. Our study also demonstrated the effects of IGF-1 on three genes regulating A4 synthesis in TCs, namely STAR,CYP11A1, and HSD17B [13, 17, 45,46,47]. IGF-1 (100 ng/ml) increased the mRNA of STAR, CYP11A1, and HSD17B in TCs of small-sized follicles, whereas lower doses of IGF-1 enhanced these enzymes in TCs of medium-sized follicles, similar to previous studies where IGF-1 increased STAR mRNA levels in cultured follicles of mice [20] and CYP11A1 mRNA levels in porcine GCs [48]. IGF-1 had little effect on CYP11A1 mRNA in bovine-cultured TCs in the presence of LH [18]. Interestingly, in the present study, IGF-1 alone increased the expression of CYP11A1 mRNA in TCs. These results indicate that IGF-1 enhances gene expression involved in A4 biosynthesis.

In fact, IGF-1 (50 ng/ml) and IGF-1 (10–100 ng/ml) increased A4 production in the TCs of small- and medium-sized follicles, respectively. This study provides further evidence that IGF-1 enhances A4 production in TCs of small-sized follicles, which play a pivotal role in follicular growth. Moreover, it indicates that enhancing A4 production and the associated gene expression in TCs of small- and medium-sized follicles is dependent on the dosage of IGF-1. Pregnenolone is converted to P4 via HSD3B in TCs [14]. In vivo, no discernible variations in P4 concentration were observed in the two largest follicles prior to the onset of deviation in heifers [3, 49]. Conversely, IGF-1 increased P4 production in bovine TCs derived from large-sized follicles [50, 51]. In our study, IGF-1 augmented the P4 production in TCs of small- and medium-sized follicles. However, P4 does not play a critical role in follicular growth; rather, it may serve as a notable precursor for A4 synthesis in TCs. IGF-1, conversely, did not induce an increase in T production in cultured TCs obtained from either small- or medium-sized follicles. These results demonstrate that IGF-1 has the capacity to enhance the expression of steroidogenic enzymes and A4 production in TCs of small-sized follicles, which serve as a significant source for E2 synthesis in GCs (Fig. 6). This E2 synthesis is known to play a crucial role in follicular growth and deviation.

Illustration depicting our conclusions. IGF-1 increased A4 production by enhancing the gene expression of steroidogenic enzymes and LHCGR in TCs and promoting mRNA expression of ESR2 in GCs of small-sized follicles. These findings suggest that IGF-1 is the optimal choice for enhancing factors involved in bovine follicular deviation and selection.

In summary, IGF-1 primarily impacts pre-antral or early antral follicles. This study demonstrates the involvement of IGF-1 in certain gene expressions and hormone production processes that play crucial roles in various aspects of follicular growth in small- and medium-sized antral follicles. The findings of this study reveal that the effects of IGF-1 on small- and medium-sized follicles follow a dose-dependent pattern. Specifically, the higher dose of IGF-1 (100 ng/ml) proves more effective in enhancing the targeted variables in small-sized follicles, whereas the lower doses exhibit their effects on medium-sized follicles. These results indeed support our hypothesis that IGF-1 increases the expression of ESR2 mRNA in GCs and A4 production in TCs of small-sized follicles. These molecular changes enhance follicular growth and are imperative for follicular deviation.
