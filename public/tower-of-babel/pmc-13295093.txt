IMFrag: A Tool to Recognize In-Source Fragmentation in Ion Mobility-Enabled Data-Independent Acquisition Workflows
Authors: Ryan Nguyen, Anvitaa Anandkumar, Griffin Rangel, Jennifer Liem, Yvonne S. Lin, Libin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-resolution mass spectrometry is a powerful tool for untargeted analysis. However, in-source fragmentation (ISF) could lead to the misidentification of compounds in untargeted metabolomics or exposomics studies. To prevent misidentification and to strengthen compound identification through MS/MS spectral library matching, we developed IMFrag, a Jupyter notebook-based tool that utilizes structural information gained from ion mobility-mass spectrometry (IM-MS) as an orthogonal technique to differentiate independent precursor ions from fragments formed via ISF. We first examined l-tryptophan, which is an essential amino acid that undergoes extensive fragmentation during electrospray ionization (ESI). IM-enabled data-independent acquisition (IM-DIA) analysis revealed distinct mobility signatures for identical fragment ions formed at different instrument sites, enabling discrimination between ISFs generated prior to the IM drift tube and fragments produced via postmobility collision-induced dissociation. Similar patterns were observed for a structurally diverse collection of small molecules. Additional structural information could also be inferred from the IM-DIA workflow, such as unique dimers, protonation sites, and distinct ion types that were not apparent from LC–MS alone. These insights were shown to be useful when applied to healthy human plasma samples, which served as a more complex and biologically relevant matrix that contained ambiguities, such as coeluting, isobaric candidate structures. Thus, IMFrag was developed as an accessible framework for interrogating MS1 and post-IM MS2 chemical features in untargeted data sets and can be integrated into untargeted analysis pipelines or used to support the development of ISF-derived MS/MS spectral libraries.

Introduction

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) is a powerful tool for untargeted analysis, which aims to globally profile small molecules (<1500 Da) in a sample without prior knowledge of its contents. HRMS coupled to liquid chromatography (LC) enables researchers to survey thousands of features within a single experiment. Achieving unbiased detection across vast chemical spaces, however, remains a significant challenge for both endogenous , and exogenous , small molecules. For example, the human metabolome exhibits extreme physicochemical diversity , and comprises >200,000 compounds, including amino acids, nucleosides, lipids, small peptides, carbohydrates, hormones, and vitamins. Xenobiotic chemical exposures and their associated biological responses (i.e., the exposome), although not necessarily prioritized in metabolomics, also contribute to human health and disease outcomes. − The exposome is remarkably complex; small molecules and metals derived from food sources, drugs, and pollutants span hundreds of chemical classes and 11 orders of magnitude in human blood concentrations (picomolar to micromolar).

To maximize chemical coverage and sensitivity, MS source parameters such as capillary voltage, source temperature, and gas flowswhich collectively govern ion formation, transmission, and droplet desolvationare typically optimized prior to analysis. This process, which involves trade-offs between analyte signals and background interferences, becomes challenging in untargeted workflows that simultaneously profile thousands of diverse unknown compounds. , Conditions improving ionization efficiency, for example, can also impart excess internal energy to gas-phase ions during their transfer to the MS vacuum chamber, even with the use of soft ionization techniques, such as electrospray ionization (ESI). , One important consequence of these effects is the occurrence of in-source fragmentation (ISF). Because ISF unintentionally generates product ions that appear as independent precursor ions, these chemical features are often difficult to recognize and may lead to false positives during compound annotation. ISF is known to be a highly frequent phenomenon for several important chemical classes. In their analysis of 214 natural products derived from plants, Shi et al. reported that 82% of the tested compounds underwent ISF. The occurrence of unintentional ISF in lipidomics, which often generates ions with identical masses to those of other lipids, can lead to misannotations that are especially challenging to identify without adequate chromatographic separation. Through mining the METLIN MS/MS database, Giera et al. estimated that ISF could account for over 70% of observed peaks in typical LC–MS/MS metabolomic data sets. Although the prevalence of ISF features in LC-HRMS-based metabolomics remains under debate, with recent studies highlighting that approximately 10% of MS1 features are attributable to ISF in complex biological samples, there is broad agreement that the accurate recognition of ISFs is critical for reducing misannotation and improving confidence in compound identification.

Recent efforts to address ISF in untargeted analysis have involved developing postacquisition data-cleaning strategies. ,,− While these tools rely on different techniques (e.g., common neutral losses, coelution profiles, MS1, and MS2 patterns) to identify ISFs, they are designed exclusively for LC–MS and LC–MS/MS workflows. ISFrag, for example, is an R package that utilizes a retention time and spectral similarity approach to group chemical features arising from the same precursor ion, identifying those most likely to be ISFs. Ion mobility–mass spectrometry (IM–MS) offers an orthogonal dimension of separation that can further distinguish ISFs from independent precursor ions. In IM–MS, gas-phase analyte ions are rapidly separated on the basis of their size, shape, and charge as they traverse a neutral buffer gas (e.g., N2) under the influence of a dynamic (traveling wave IM, TWIM) or static (drift tube IM, DTIM) electric field, or as the ions are trapped by a forward gas flow and reverse electric field, followed by slow release by decreasing the electric field [trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS)]. − Millisecond-scale IM separations typically occur between LC and mass analysis (e.g., time-of-flight, TOF). Taking advantage of the IM separation, post-IM fragmentation (i.e., high-definition MSE or HDMSE) allows the alignment of all fragments with their respective precursors based on drift time. Because ISFs form prior to entering the IM drift tube, they often exhibit drift times that are smaller than their originating ions since the latter are generally larger; these ISFs can therefore be distinguished from the same fragments that are formed from post-IM fragmentation in complex data sets, even without baseline LC separation. , Therefore, in IM-enabled data-independent workflows (e.g., HDMSE) where the MS continuously alternates between low- and high-energy scanning acquisitions, m/z values observed in ISFs will likely display multiple peaks in themobilograms at high-energy collision, which would provide information regarding their origins and site(s) of formation within the instrument (Figure ).

The ability to distinguish ISFs from other ion types in untargeted data sets is critical for reducing misannotations. Furthermore, recent work has highlighted that ISF can be leveraged to enhance compound identification, as reproducible ISFs may serve as additional diagnostic ions for structural elucidation and library matching. , Indeed, Xue et al. generated ESI–ISF fragmentation patterns comparable to those of METLIN MS/MS spectra acquired at higher collision energies, enabling confident molecular identification via MS1 spectral profiling for a broad range of metabolites. Existing approaches for ISF recognition and utilization, however, have been largely limited to LC–MS/MS workflows and do not take advantage of the additional structural information provided by IM–MS. Here, we demonstrate that the IM-DIA workflow can reveal characteristic mobility signatures associated with ISFs and introduce IMFrag, a Jupyter notebook-based tool that leverages these patterns to identify ISFs and reconstruct fragment–precursor ion relationships. Using a structurally diverse set of small molecules and human plasma samples, we show how IM-guided ISF recognition enables confident interpretation of ambiguous features, particularly in scenarios that challenge conventional LC–MS/MS-based annotation.

Experimental Section

Materials

Optima (LC–MS grade) methanol, acetonitrile, water, and formic acid were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA). Methylquinoline isomers (2-, 3-, 4-, 5-, 6-, 8-) and l-tryptophan were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). 7-Methylquinoline was purchased from Fisher Scientific. ToxCast chemical standards used to develop and assess IMFrag were generously provided by Dr. Thomas Metz (Pacific Northwest National Laboratory) and diluted in acetonitrile (10 μM) using automated liquid handling platforms at the Quellos High-Throughput Screening Core (University of Washington).

Direct Infusion ESI-MS and ESI-MS/MS Analysis

A stock solution of l-tryptophan (10 μg/mL) prepared in water and acetonitrile (4:1 v/v) was directly infused into the electrospray source of a Waters Synapt XS Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Waters Corp., Milford, MA) using a 0.5 mL syringe (Hamilton Company, Reno, NV) and a syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA). A flow rate of 10 μL/mL was delivered throughout each acquisition (0.25 s, 1 s per scan in the positive ion “Resolution” mode). As described in Figure S1, collision energies in the trap and the transfer traveling wave ion guide (TWIG) regions of the instrument were maintained at 0 V. Additional source parameters and experimental details are listed in Table S1.

LC–IM–MS/MS Analysis

Chemical standards used to develop and assess IMFrag were prepared at 10 μM in acetonitrile and individually injected with a Waters Acquity ultraperformance LC (UPLC) system, which was equipped with a quaternary solvent pump and refrigerated autosampler (10 °C) coupled to a reversed-phase analytical column (Phenomenex Kinetex, 2.6 μm, polar C18, 100 Å, 30 × 2.1 mm). Samples (5 μL) were eluted with the chromatographic gradient described in Table S2 and detected on a Waters Synapt XS Q-TOF mass spectrometer, operating in the positive ion (ESI+) HDMSE (DIA) mode. Source parameters are listed in Table S3. Prior to analysis, the mass analyzer was calibrated over the range of 50–1200 m/z in “Resolution” mode via IntelliStart.

Extended LC–MS Method for Human Plasma Analysis

An extended chromatographic gradient was developed for the separation of 7 methylquinoline isomers and l-tryptophan. Stock solutions (1 μg/mL) of each compound prepared in water and acetonitrile (4:1 v/v) were individually injected into a reversed-phase analytical column (Thermo, Hypersil GOLD, 100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm particle size). The optimized chromatographic gradient is described in Table S4.

Human Plasma Collection

Blood samples from healthy volunteers (18–49 years old) with a BMI of 18–27 kg/m2 were collected under the approval of the University of Washington Institutional Review Board. All participants completed an online screening questionnaire with the following exclusion criteria: diagnosis of any gastrointestinal, cardiac, or renal medical conditions; autoimmune disease, diabetes, or other metabolic diseases; or currently pregnant or breast-feeding. Consented participants were asked to fast overnight and arrive at the collection site between 8:30 to 10:30 AM the following morning. Approximately 14 mL of blood was drawn into an EDTA tube (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ), centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the plasma layer from white and red blood cell fractions, and immediately stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Preparation and Analysis of Human Plasma Samples

Plasma samples were completely thawed at room temperature and vortexed. For extraction, 30 μL of ice-cold acetonitrile was added, followed by 10 μL of plasma. The resulting mixtures were rested on ice for 15 min to facilitate protein precipitation. Following centrifugation (16,000g for 15 min at 4 °C), the supernatant (20 μL) was transferred to glass autosampler vials containing 40 μL of 2:1 (v/v) water and methanol. Plasma extracts (5 μL) were subsequently analyzed via LC–IM–MS/MS using the abbreviated LC gradient described in Table S2, followed by the extended LC gradient described in Table S4 to achieve separation of the methylquinoline isomers and l-tryptophan. The source parameters listed in Table S5 were used for plasma analysis. Isotopic distribution patterns and chemical formula were investigated with the Waters MassLynx Elemental Composition tool (version 4.2).

Code and Data Availability

Experimental raw data files are available on MassIVE (MSV000100609). The IMFrag Jupyter notebook and example IM-DIA data sets are freely available on GitHub (https://github.com/libinxulab/xulab_software.git).

Results and Discussion

In-Source Fragments Exhibit Distinct Gas-Phase Mobility Behavior

To understand the gas-phase mobility behavior of ISFs, we began our investigations with l-tryptophan (Trp), an essential amino acid that extensively generates fragments during the ESI process. As expected from previous studies on protonated indolamines, − the ESI–MS analysis of Trp (m/z 205) produced an intense spectrum of fragment ions corresponding to the loss of NH3 (m/z 188) and CO2 (m/z 144) in the absence of collisional activation (Figure A). Several ESI–MS/MS experiments were conducted to confirm that the formation of these Trp fragments occurred prior to the quadrupole. As shown in Figure S1A, the isolation of protonated Trp and the subsequent addition of collision energy (20 V) in the trap TWIG region (Figure B) generated fragments at m/z 188 and m/z 144, establishing these ions as products of Trp. To further probe the site of formation of these fragments, ESI-MS/MS spectra targeting m/z 188 and m/z 144 were acquired under conditions minimizing postquadrupole collisional activation (i.e., no collision energy was added to the trap or transfer regions). Robust signals corresponding to the selected ions were observed (Figure S1B,C), supporting their assignment as ISFs generated during the ESI process.

(B) Simplified scheme of the quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) mass spectrometer used for analysis, indicating the location of each TWIG region (not to scale). Note that this layout is specific to the Waters Synapt XS Q-TOF. (C) Extracted ion mobilograms (±0.025 Da from theoretical m/z) of the [M + H–NH3–CO2]+ in-source fragment of l-tryptophan (m/z 144, solid black trace) acquired without (bottom panel) and with (middle panel) the addition of collision energy in the transfer region. The extracted ion mobilogram (±0.025 Da from theoretical m/z) of the [M + H–NH3]+ in-source fragment of l-tryptophan (m/z 188, dashed red trace) acquired with the addition of collision energy (20 V) in the transfer region is displayed in the top panel.

We next examined the gas-phase mobility behavior of Trp-derived ISFs using ion mobility-enabled data-independent acquisition (IM-DIA). Fragmentation in this acquisition mode occurs simultaneously for all coeluting analytes within a wide m/z transmission window, enabling the concurrent observation of ions formed at different sites within the instrument. As shown in Figure C, the extracted mobilogram of [M + H–NH3–CO2]+ (m/z 144) acquired without collisional activation exhibited a single major peak, consistent with the formation of this ion prior to the IM drift tube and during the ESI process. A second, later-arriving population of ions with m/z 144 began to emerge with increasing intensity upon the addition of collision energy (15 V) in the postmobility transfer region. Notably, this later-arriving population of m/z 144 ionswhich increased in relative intensity with additional transfer collision energy (25 V)displayed a drift time identical to that of m/z 188, suggesting the postmobility formation of [M + H–NH3–CO2]+ via collision-induced fragmentation of [M + H–NH3]+. This observation is consistent with previous ESI–MS/MS investigations of protonated Trp by Zhang et al., who reported a sequential fragmentation pathway involving the initial loss of NH3, followed by CO2 elimination.

These results demonstrate that identical fragment ions originating from distinct instrument sites can be resolved by IM using simple pre- and postmobility drift–time alignment principles. Importantly, this behavior reconciles earlier ESI–MS/MS observations in which m/z 144 was detected upon quadrupole isolation of m/z 188 (Figure S1B), reflecting contributions from both prequadrupole ISF and postquadrupole (i.e., transfer region collisional activation) pathways. Our findings also suggest that ISFs display characteristic patterns in IM-DIA workflows. First, ISFs formed prior to the drift tube are transmitted to the IM cell at the same time as their precursors, resulting in mobility-resolved features within low-energy (MS1) scans. In contrast, the secondary generation of these fragments via intentional postmobility collisional activation, as demonstrated in Figure C, produces high-energy (MS2) features that are drift time-aligned with their precursors. Because IM-DIA methods routinely employ alternating low- and high-energy acquisition scans within a single analysis, these complementary mobility signatures provide a powerful framework for distinguishing fragment ions that form prior to the IM drift tube and elucidating their origins within complex data sets.

Development of IMFrag

On the basis of our investigations of Trp, we developed IMFrag, a Jupyter notebook-based workflow designed to facilitate the identification of ISFs in IM-DIA data sets. IMFrag integrates data conversion, processing, and visualization tools that enable researchers to closely evaluate the gas-phase mobility behavior of suspected ISFs and other ions. An overview of the IMFrag workflow is listed in Figure A.

(A) Overview of the IMFrag workflow. Following conversion of native instrument files (.raw) to the Hierarchical Data Format (.h5), a user-defined list of MS1 mass peak is used to extract LC ion chromatograms. Within each LC peak, mobilograms are constructed from both the low-energy (MS1) and high-energy (MS2) acquisition scans. Mobilogram peak picking is then performed to identify mobility-resolved ion populations. In cases where fragments are formed prior to IM separation (e.g., via ISF or other premobility processes), these ions are transmitted to the drift tube independently of their precursor(s) and therefore exhibit distinct drift times within MS1 acquisition scans. The intentional postmobility generation of these fragments via collision-induced activation (CID) generates MS2 features that are drift time-aligned with their precursor ion(s), and, when ISF is present, a complex mobilogram containing distinct ion populations arising from both pre- and postmobility formation is observed. Drift time bounds corresponding to unique MS2 ion populations are used to generate filtered MS1 mass spectra, enabling the identification of precursor ion(s) that are aligned in both the LC and IM dimensions with the postmobility fragment ion population, thereby supporting precursor–fragment association. Representative examples for (B) atorvastatin (m/z 559) and (C) penconazole (m/z 284), illustrating ions (extracted ±0.025 Da from theoretical m/z) that are observed in MS1 scans but can be readily distinguished as fragments formed prior to IM separationand dissociated from higher m/z precursor ionsbased on their characteristic mobility signatures. P → F refers to post-IM fragmentation of the precursor.

To assess the broader applicability of our initial gas-phase observations, 30 structurally diverse chemical standardsincluding pesticides, fungicides, pharmaceutical agents, cosmetic ingredients, and endogenous metabolites (Table S6)were individually analyzed via LC–IM–MS/MS with HDMSE acquisition. Data were collected using alternating low-energy (MS1) and high-energy (MS2) scans, with collision energy intentionally added to the postmobility transfer region of the instrument (see Tables S2 and S3 for experimental details). For each compound, candidate ISFs were defined as MS1 features consistent with the known fragmentation pathways of the precursor, as supported by MS/MS fragment ions observed at elevated collision energies in public spectral libraries. Importantly, the MS/MS information was used solely to establish chemical plausibility rather than infer the instrument site(s) of formation, since HDMSE experiments alone cannot distinguish between fragments formed at the source versus those formed between the source and the IM drift tube. The gas-phase mobility behavior of each candidate ISF was then examined in both the MS1 and MS2 acquisition scans to determine whether the observed mobilogram profiles were informative enough to support pre- and/or postmobility formation. IMFrag automated this analysis by enabling LC peak selection, construction of MS1 and MS2 mobilograms, and putative assignment of associated precursor ion(s) based on mobility, LC, and MS2 alignment. Drift-time resolution (R S), defined as the MS1–MS2 peak separation normalized to the average fitted full width at half-maximum (fwhm), was used to identify clearly resolved (R s ≥ 1), high-confidence ISFs. Similarity between the corresponding MS1 and MS2 mobilograms was also evaluated, with low Pearson correlation (r ≤ 0.6) supporting the presence of distinct mobility profiles (see Figure S2 and https://github.com/libinxulab/xulab_software.git for additional details). These metrics were used to determine whether candidate ISFs exhibited mobility-resolved behavior consistent with formation prior to IM separation, with LC coelution and spectral similarity criteria applied as complementary evidence of ISF.

Among the 30 chemical standards investigated, the vast majority (87%) displayed evidence of fragmentation prior to IM separation, producing complex MS2 mobilograms composed of both a mobility-resolved fragment ion population and a later-arriving, precursor-aligned ion population consistent with postmobility collision-induced dissociation (CID) (Figure S3). Compounds that did not form intense ISFs included quaternary ammonium compounds (e.g., didecyldimethylammonium and cetylpyridinium), which belong to a large class of permanently charged chemicals commonly found in disinfecting and personal care products. The lack of a labile proton on these compounds may contribute to their reduced susceptibility to ISF. Unlike [M + H]+ analytes that form during the ESI process, quaternary ammonium species and other permanently charged molecules do not possess a mobile proton to form labile protomers, thereby suppressing these fragmentation pathways under typical MS1 source conditions. The extent of ISF for small molecules is strongly dependent on molecular structure and instrument parameters, , however, and should be carefully evaluated in alternative experimental settings.

Example data for atorvastatin, a widely prescribed statin used to manage dyslipidemias, and the triazole fungicide penconazole are shown in Figure B,C, respectively. The mobilograms for these compounds illustrate the utility of IM for distinguishing ion types that are not readily apparent from LC–MS/MS alone. For both atorvastatin and penconazole, IM successfully resolved the candidate ISF from the corresponding precursor within the MS1 dimension, consistent with its formation prior to IM separation. An additional peak in the mobilogram at the same m/z was observed upon the intentional addition of postmobility collision energy, providing a clear link between these fragment ions and their precursors. Similar mobility signatures were observed for a majority of the candidate ISFs investigated in Table S6, even for fragment ions arising from neutral losses with relatively small masses (<40 Da). IM-DIA analysis of the broad-spectrum fungicide fluoxastrobin (m/z 459), for example, revealed the formation and partial gas-phase separation of a distinct MS1 fragment ion at m/z 427 (–CH4O), highlighting the sensitivity of IM to subtle structural changes arising from the elimination of common neutral losses (Figure S3E). The steroids fluocinolone acetonide (Figure S3M,N) and testosterone propionate (Figure S3O) also displayed strong evidence of premobility dissociation. In the case of fluocinolone acetonide, the shared appearance of a later-arriving ion population (∼7.6 ms) in both the MS2 mobilograms of the candidate ISFs (m/z 413 and 433) and the [M + H]+ precursor (m/z 453) immediately led to the identification of a unique [2M + H]+ dimer (m/z 905) in the underlying mass spectra (Figure S4), revealing the contribution of oligomeric precursor populations that could be more difficult to infer solely from LC–MS/MS data.

Important structural information could also be inferred from our IM-DIA investigations of the fluoroquinolone antibiotics ciprofloxacin and sarafloxacin. The fragmentation pathways of these compounds, which are known to form distinct gas-phase protomers − due to the presence of multiple protonation sites, have been extensively studied. − Indeed, the MS1 mobilogram of both ciprofloxacin and sarafloxacin displayed two major peaks, consistent with the formation of the corresponding piperazine N-protomers and the keto-O-protomers (Figure ). ,

(C) Extracted ion mobilograms of the candidate ISF (m/z 342) and the precursor ion of sarafloxacin. Peak assignment of each individual protomer was possible utilizing the mobility-resolved MS2 spectra. The unique fragmentation pathways observed for the corresponding keto O-protomers and later-arriving N-protomers, as previously described by Hines et al., are displayed in Figure A,D. For both sarafloxacin (Figure C) and ciprofloxacin (Figure F), the dominant ISF resulting from the premobility loss of CO2 (44 Da) exhibited a single MS1 mobilogram peak, consistent with the elimination of the secondary protonation site required to form the conformationally distinct keto-O-protomers. These observations are consistent with the MS2 drift-time alignment of these ISFs with the later-arriving mobilogram peaks of sarafloxacin and ciprofloxacin, supporting their exclusive postmobility formation from the N-protomers of these precursor ions. It should be noted that small, mass-dependent offsets between MS1 and MS2 drift times are expected in IM-DIA experiments, owing to ion velocity differences under higher voltages.

In cases involving small neutral losses, the IM resolving power was not always sufficient to separate ISF and precursor ion populations. The herbicide quinclorac (m/z 241), for example, generated an intense premobility fragment (m/z 223) corresponding to the loss of H2O that was not fully resolved from the precursor in IM space (Figure S3C). Under similar circumstances where the neutral loss results in only modest changes in gas-phase size and shape, such as fragmentation pathways involving small structural rearrangements, IM alone may be insufficient to unambiguously distinguish premobility fragments from those formed intentionally via postmobility dissociation. The ability to resolve such species is governed by the relationship between collision cross-section (CCS) differences and IM resolving power. Our TWIMS Q-IM-TOF platform, for example, typically requires CCS differences on the order of 2–3% for clear separation between two ion populations, and in cases where fragmentation resulted in smaller CCS differences, precursor and fragment ions remained partially or fully unresolved in IM space. To account for these potential cases, IMFrag incorporates complementary criteria based on LC coelution and MS/MS spectral similarity for accurate ISF recognition. Furthermore, the complex MS2 mobilogram profiles described above are dependent on both pre- and postmobility fragmentation pathways; as such, unstable ISFs that are observable in low-energy MS1 acquisition scans may decompose prior to detection in subsequent MS2 experiments, leading to mobility-resolved MS1 features but MS2 mobilograms dominated by the postmobility ion population (see Figure S3F for example). Conversely, candidate ISFs that form exclusively prior to the IM drift tube would not be easily recognized using the principles of pre- and postmobility alignment described here; for example, when the precursor ions of the ISF are completely fragmented before IM. However, as supported by our results and previous studies on ISF patterns, the ions generated during ISF are typically consistent with higher-energy fragments formed in the collision cell. ,

Evaluation of IMFrag

To quantitatively evaluate the performance of IMFrag, we tested both a true negative (e.g., the intact precursor ion) and one or more known ISFs for each analytical standard listed in Table S7. IMFrag classifications were then assessed based on whether each feature was correctly identified as an ISF and, when applicable, whether the expected precursor ion was retrieved from the DIA data. IMFrag demonstrated high specificity across this structurally diverse data set, with all intact precursor ions correctly labeled as non-ISF features (Table S7). Known ISFs were also successfully identified in 100% of the analytical standards that were investigated. Unlike existing approaches for ISF recognition, such as ISFrag and AntDAS, , which rely primarily on LC coelution and MS/MS spectral similarity patterns from precursor-selected fragmentation data (e.g., data-dependent acquisition), IMFrag leverages IM as an orthogonal dimension to directly evaluate fragment–precursor relationships in complex DIA data. Because IMFrag integrates these IM patterns with LC coelution and MS/MS spectral similarity scoring, ISFs lacking resolvable mobility populations could still be correctly labeled in the evaluated data set. For labeled ISFs, IMFrag retrieved the expected precursor ion in all cases except for the herbicide quinclorac due to both poor mobility separation and low precursor intensity in the underlying MS1 data. These results demonstrate that IMFrag provides a highly specific framework for the ISF identification in complex DIA workflows.

Recognizing In-Source Fragments in Complex IM-DIA Workflows

A common challenge in untargeted analysis occurs when ISFs overlap in mass with known metabolites or exposome molecules. In these scenarios, fragments appearing in the MS1 dimension can easily be mistaken for true molecular ions, potentially leading to compound misidentification and overestimation of sample complexity. This challenge is especially pronounced when ISFs coelute with intact analytes in traditional LC–MS/MS workflows, further obscuring the true origin(s) of observed signals.

To investigate how IM can be leveraged to resolve these ambiguities in complex biological samples, we analyzed healthy human plasma samples (n = 5) via LC–IM–MS/MS. This approach enabled the untargeted collection of MS1 and MS2 data via postmobility CID, resulting in a comprehensive data set containing both intact analytes and their fragment ions (Table S5). Initial analyses were conducted using abbreviated LC conditions to deliberately promote coelution and challenge conventional LC–MS/MS-based annotation (Table S2). As a representative case study for recognizing ISFs in complex IM–DIA workflows, we focused on a relatively intense MS1 signal at m/z 144 observed across the plasma samples (Figure A, bottom panel).

First, the drift time window of the postmobility ion population is determined, removing all misaligned features that were sufficiently resolved from the ISF’s true precursor(s) in the IM drift tube. A small drift time offset is then applied to accurately project the drift time window into MS1 space, generating a mobility-filtered MS1 mass spectrum that ideally contains only ions from which the candidate ISF may have dissociated. Isotopic pattern analysis of this plasma feature was found to be consistent with the chemical formula C10H9N. Searching this formula against PubChemLite , a subset of approximately 567,000 PubChem compounds compiled for metabolomic and exposomic applicationsreturned 33 unique candidate structures, including those consistent with the [M + H–NH3–CO2]+ ISF of l-Trp (discussed above in Figure ). Multiple aromatic amine (AA) compounds were also returned as candidate structures, including the potent bladder carcinogen , 2-naphthylamine (2-NA) and the methylquinoline (MQ) isomers 2-MQ, 4-MQ, and 6-MQ, which have been identified as common environmental pollutants due to their use in the production of dyes, food coloring agents, and petroleum products.

To further illustrate the limitations of LC–MS/MS in this scenario, we acquired reference standards for several AA candidates to compare their retention times and fragmentation spectra to those of the plasma feature of interest. As expected, the abbreviated LC gradient did not sufficiently resolve the methylquinoline isomers, 2-NA, and l-Trp, precluding unambiguous annotation of the plasma m/z 144 ion based on retention time alone (Figure S5). Inspection of the underlying MS1 spectra did not reveal a detectable signal corresponding to protonated l-Trp (m/z 205; Figure A, top panel), highlighting the difficulty in correctly identifying this feature as an ISF without a comprehensive neutral-loss database or prior knowledge of the same components. Furthermore, quadrupole-selected MS/MS spectra of the plasma-derived m/z 144 feature and the AA standards were highly similar (Figure B), indicating that conventional LC–MS/MS was insufficient to distinguish true methylquinoline exposures from an ISF with endogenous origins.

Guided by our previous observations for l-Trp and common exposome molecules, we next investigated the gas-phase mobility behavior of the observed m/z 144 plasma feature. As shown in Figure C, the extracted MS1 and MS2 mobilograms of this ion provided strong evidence of mobility-resolved populations, consistent with a mixture of m/z 144 ions formed both prior to and following the IM separation. Recognizing that fragment ions produced intentionally via postmobility CID are drift time-aligned with their precursors, we leveraged the later-arriving MS2 mobilogram as a guide to infer the potential origin(s) of the m/z 144 plasma feature. Using IMFrag, the drift time window corresponding to the later-arriving MS2 IM population was projected into MS1 space to extract a corresponding mobility-filtered MS1 spectrum, containing only ions with drift times and retention times matching those from which m/z 144 may have originated (Figure D). This targeted extraction yielded an MS1 spectrum with a dominant signal at m/z 188 (Figure E), consistent with the l-Trp-derived [M–NH3] ion observed in our targeted ESI-MS and ESI-MS/MS studies (see Figure ). These results were subsequently confirmed by extended LC–MS analysis with a longer LC gradient, wherein the isobaric candidate structures were chromatographically resolved in order to confidently annotate the m/z 144 plasma feature as an ISF of l-Trp in all plasma samples (Figure S6).

These results demonstrate the value of IM-guided ISF recognition in untargeted analysis, particularly under conditions where chromatographic separation is limited, and MS/MS spectra are highly similar across isobaric candidate structures. Notably, in this example, intact l-Trp (m/z 205) fell below the limits of detection in both MS1 and MS2 acquisitions scans, a scenario that could easily result in false negatives (i.e., missed l-Trp) or false positives (i.e., misannotation) without the use of comprehensive neutral loss databases or chemical standards. In contrast, the presence of mobility-resolved ion populations in IM space rapidly enabled IMFrag to associate the m/z 144 signal with a higher-mass precursor through the principles of drift-time alignment, ultimately revealing its derivation from the stable [M + H–NH3]+ ISF of l-Trp. These findings highlight the importance of developing MS/MS libraries containing both precursor and ISF fragmentation patterns, a strategy that IM-enabled workflows are uniquely positioned to support. In this context, the capability to infer precursor–fragment relationships directly from IM-DIA datawithout requiring prior knowledge or observation of the intact molecular ionprovides a powerful mechanism for distinguishing ISFs from independent analytes.

To further evaluate the specificity of IMFrag in complex biological matrices, a structurally diverse mixture of small-molecule drugs was spiked into extracted human plasma and analyzed via LC–IM–MS/MS with HDMSE acquisition (see Section Preparation and Analysis of Human Plasma Samples and Table S8). Across this data set, intact precursor ions spanning a wide mass range were correctly identified as non-ISF features, even in the presence of complex endogenous background signals. Notably, several coeluting compounds (e.g., alprenolol, ondansetron, and acetaminophen) were incorrectly classified as ISFs when evaluated using only the LC–MS/MS data. Consideration of the underlying IM data was necessary to correctly resolve these features as non-ISF intact analytes (Figure S7).

Conclusion

We demonstrate that ISFs exhibit characteristic mobility signatures in IM-DIA workflows that enable their systematic recognition and association with higher-mass precursors. Building on these observations, we developed IMFrag as an accessible framework for interrogating MS1 chemical features in untargeted data sets. IMFrag may be applied to individual features of interest, integrated into untargeted analysis pipelines, or used to support the development of ISF-derived MS/MS spectral libraries that can facilitate metabolite identification and discovery in complex samples. Although this study was performed using a Q-IM-TOF (Waters) platform, the underlying principles described here are vendor-agnostic. While commercial IM-QTOF instruments from other vendors (e.g., Agilent and Bruker) differ in hardware layout with respect to the position of the quadrupole and IM operating principle, the IMFrag strategy is expected to be broadly applicable to IM-enabled DIA workflows employing postmobility CID. Importantly, the preservation of precursor–fragment relationships through postmobility fragmentation is supported across multiple commercially available platforms, including TWIM, DTIM, and TIMS. While IM provides an orthogonal dimension of separation, we note that analogous strategies for ISF recognition have been developed and applied to conventional LC-HRMS data sets lacking IM separation. ,− The R package ISFrag, for example, leverages retention time coelution and MS/MS spectral similarity patterns to perform de novo recognition of ISF features in untargeted workflows. In this context, IMFrag extends these principles into the IM dimension, where drift-time (or inverse reduced mobility in the case of TIMS) alignment provides an orthogonal and highly specific criterion for resolving precursor–fragment relationships. As IM-enabled platforms continue to expand across metabolomics and exposomics, incorporating ISF recognition strategies can be critical for improving annotation confidence and fully leveraging the power of multidimensional mass spectrometry.
