Liposomal Salvianolic Acid B Enhances ALA-PDT in Oral Leucoplakia via ROS and AKT/mTOR Signalling
Authors: Ying Zhang, Xiaoxian Zhao, Xinyi Han, Xuemin Shen, Runyu Huang, Wei Liu, Lan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salvianolic acid B (SalB), a major component of Danshen, the ancient Chinese herb Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge, has demonstrated inhibitory effects against various solid tumours. Our previous study explored a SalB-loaded nanocarrier as a photosensitizing formulation for photodynamic therapy (PDT) in vitro in oral leucoplakia (OLK) and oral cancer cells. This study aims to explore the underlying mechanisms and in vivo effects. In this study, different concentrations of SalB and 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) were incorporated into liposomes to synthesize the nano-photosensitizer (nano-SalB-ALA). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in Cal27 and Leuk1 cells were evaluated by DCFH-DA staining, and the intracellular distribution of fluorescently labelled liposomes was visualized by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Western blot was used to analyse the expression of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling proteins. In addition, a 4NQO-induced OLK mouse model was used to evaluate in vivo effects. Based on the most obvious intracellular ROS fluorescence signals and the greatest reduction in PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway expression observed in the nano-SalB10-ALA250 (10 mg SalB@250 µM ALA) group, we selected it for subsequent in vitro analyses. Both in vitro and in vivo, nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT inhibited the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway. These results indicate that SalB, in a nano-photosensitizer formulation, modulates ROS levels and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, which may contribute to the enhanced efficacy of PDT for OLK. This study may provide new strategies for improving the efficacy of PDT for OLK in clinical practice.

Introduction

Oral leucoplakia (OLK) is a white, irreversible, nonscrapable lesion of the oral mucosa with the potential for malignant transformation.1,2 The global prevalence of OLK is 4.11%, with the highest incidence in Asian populations (7.77%).3 OLK is one of the most common oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for over 90% of oral malignancies,4 most of which develop from OPMDs. Photodynamic therapy (PDT), as a minimally invasive treatment, plays an indispensable role in the management of OLK. Unlike surgical excision, PDT offers high selectivity, repeatability, and minimal scarring, making it particularly valuable in areas with functional structures.5,6

5-Aminolevulinic acid (ALA) is a water-soluble prodrug metabolized intracellularly via the heme pathway to protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), the active photosensitizer. Upon light irradiation (400-410 nm or 635 ± 5 nm), PpIX generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), inducing mitochondrial damage, cytochrome c release, and apoptosis.7,8 While PpIX preferentially accumulates in dysplastic or malignant cells, the efficacy of ALA-PDT can be limited by insufficient local drug delivery and incomplete light exposure, which contributes to clinical recurrence.9 To address these limitations, we developed a liposomal nanoparticle system coloading SalB with ALA. This design aims to enhance local delivery and cellular accumulation of ALA, thereby increasing PpIX-mediated ROS generation in lesion cells. In addition, SalB provides complementary inhibition of oncogenic PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, potentially enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic exposure. Thus, this approach integrates optimized photodynamic cytotoxicity with targeted molecular modulation, improving PDT outcomes for oral leucoplakia.

The most common risk factors for OLK include smoking (primarily) or smokeless tobacco, as well as chronic alcohol consumption, but the exact aetiology remains unclear.4,10 The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway plays a crucial role in the carcinogenesis of OLK. Compared to OLK, pAkt and pmTOR are significantly enhanced in OSCC.11,12 Previous studies have shown that drugs inducing apoptosis in OLK cells via this pathway can inhibit its malignant transformation.13 Salvianolic acid B (SalB) exerts potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, suppressing NF-κB signalling, and reducing ROS accumulation in various cell and animal models, making it a promising candidate for preventing inflammation-driven disease progression.14, 15, 16, 17 SalB is the most abundant water-soluble component in Danshen, which is widely used to treat conditions such as heartache, stomachache, joint pain, and irregular menstruation.18 The molecular formula of salvianolic acid B is C36H30O16, with a relative molecular weight of 718.62. It is formed through the condensation of three molecules of Danshen and one molecule of caffeic acid, and its stability in water is poor. SalB is a natural compound known for its safety, nontoxicity, and effectiveness. However, its poor stability limits its broader application. Enhancing its stability and delivery is essential to maximize its therapeutic effects.19 Therefore, developing new formulations and administration routes, especially those that enable targeted delivery and sustained release, can improve its clinical efficacy.19,20

In this study, we synthesized a novel nano-photosensitizer by loading SalB onto a liposomal carrier, coloading the traditional photosensitizer ALA (nano-SalB-ALA). After observing the distribution of fluorescently labelled nano-SalB-ALA in Cal27 and Leuk1 cells, we examined the effects of nano-SalB-ALA on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway and ROS production. The aim was to elucidate mechanisms by which SalB enhances PDT for OLK, promising to provide a theoretical basis for improving photosensitizer applications in the clinic.

Materials and methods

Preparation of liposome nanoparticles loaded with SalB and ALA

Soybean lecithin, cholesterol, and DSPE-MPEG 2000 were dissolved in ethanol at defined ratios and subjected to rotary evaporation overnight to form a thin lipid film. After ethanol removal, the film was hydrated with a prewarmed (37 °C) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution containing fully dissolved SalB and ALA (group 1= 0 mg SalB@125 µM ALA, group 2 = 10 mg SalB@125 µM ALA, group 3 = 0 mg SalB@250 µM ALA, group 4 = 10 mg SalB@250 µM ALA) using an ultrasonic bath. The resulting mixture was further sonicated with a metal probe (10 s on/15 s off, six cycles) and stirred at 800 rpm for 30 minutes with a magnetic stirrer. The liposomal suspension was then extruded through a 100 nm polycarbonate membrane, stored overnight at 4 °C in the dark, and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 minutes at room temperature. This process yielded SalB and ALA coloaded liposomes (nano-SalB-ALA), while ALA-loaded liposomes (nano-ALA) served as a control.

Characterization of liposome nanoparticles loaded with SalB and ALA

Nano-SalB-ALA and nano-ALA were diluted 1:10 in PBS, and their average particle size and zeta potential were measured using a Nano Z3000 Zetasizer (Nicomp). The UV–visible absorption spectra of SalB, ALA, and nano-SalB-ALA were recorded using a multifunctional microplate reader (TECAN Infinite F200, Tecan Austria GmbH, Austria). To establish standard curves, absorbance values of ALA and SalB at various concentrations were measured, with concentration plotted on the x-axis and absorbance on the y-axis. Samples were negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate and imaged using a FEI Tecnai F20 transmission electron microscope.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human oral leucoplakia cell line, Leuk1, was obtained from the University of Maryland School of Dentistry (Baltimore, MD, USA), and the human OSCC cell lines Cal27, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Virginia, USA). Human OSCC Cal27 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (GIBCO, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (GIBCO) and 100 units/mL each of penicillin and streptomycin. Human OLK Leuk1 cells were cultured in K-SFM medium (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA) in a humidified incubator. Both cell lines were incubated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Acute therapeutic ROS detection

Leuk1 and Cal27 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight at 37 °C with bovine serum albumin. The medium was replaced with serum-free solution, and the cells were incubated with a DCFH-DA probe in the dark for 20 minutes. Excess probe was washed away three times for 10 minutes each. Then, liposomal nanoparticles with varying concentrations of SalB and ALA were added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Intracellular fluorescence was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (TCS SP8, Leica).

Cellular uptake assay under CLSM

Liposome nanoparticles were stained with 0.5 mg/mL Nile red and cocultured with Cal27 and Leuk1 cells in the dark. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and the cell cytoskeleton was stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate for 1 hour, while cell nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 10 minutes. Cellular uptake of the liposome nanoparticles was then observed using CLSM. All fluorescent signals observed in the images correspond to these three dyes (Nile red, FITC, and DAPI).

Western blot

Cal27 and Leuk1 cells were cultured under hypoxic conditions, and total protein was extracted using a lysis buffer with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured via the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% skim milk. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, mTOR, p-mTOR, and Hif-1α (Abcam, UK). After five washes with TBST, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) under an Epson Perfection V600 Photo (EPSON), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, with β-actin as the loading control.

Establishment of 4NQO-induced OLK mouse model

Female C57BL/6 mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, were purchased from Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital (SH9H-2023-A89-1). After a 1-week acclimation period, the mice were randomly assigned to two groups: a 4-nitroquinoline-1-oxide (4NQO)-induced group (n = 24), which received drinking water containing 100 µg/mL of 4NQO (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and a healthy control group (n = 8), which was given distilled water. At the end of the 8th, 12th, and 16th week, one mouse in the healthy control group and two mice in the 4NQO group were sacrificed respectively. The tongue dorsal leucoplakia was observed and the leucoplakia tissues were collected for H&E Staining. During the rearing process, two mice died naturally, and another one was sacrificed because the lesion was located in the tongue abdomen. At the end of the experiment, all mice were euthanized, and their tongues were excised and fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 24 hours. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

In vivo efficacy and safety evaluation

Following successful establishment of the model, all mice were provided with distilled water, and two experienced oral mucosal clinicians assessed the tongue leucoplakia using scoring criteria based on area, type, colour, and texture.21,22 According to the scores, the mice in the 4NQO-induced group were nearly equally divided into two treatment groups: one group received nano-SalB10-ALA250 (liposome nanoparticles loaded with 10 mg SalB@250 µM ALA), and the other received nano-ALA250 (liposome nanoparticles loaded with 250 µM ALA). The nanoparticles were applied directly to the surface of the leucoplakia lesions, and the affected area was isolated from saliva for 2 hours. The lesions were then irradiated with a 635 nm laser at 100 mW/cm2 (irradiation for 3 minutes, followed by a 2-minute rest, repeated for three cycles, and then irradiated again for 3 minutes). The lesions were irradiated with a 635 nm laser at a power density of 100 mW/cm². The irradiation was delivered in repeated fractions of 3 min each with 2-min dark intervals between successive irradiations, resulting in a total effective irradiation time of 900 s and a total light dose of 90 J/cm². The tongues were photographed on the 3rd, 7th, and 14th days following PDT. The same scoring criteria were used to evaluate the results. All mice were then sacrificed, and their tongues were excised for H&E staining. The body weight of mice in different groups was recorded from day 0 to 14. On the 14th day after PDT, the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney of all mice were collected for H&E staining.

Chronic tissue oxidative status in leucoplakia lesions

Fourteen days after PDT, mice were euthanized and tongue tissues were collected. Tissue sections were incubated with dihydroethidium (DHE) staining solution (Thermo Fisher, USA) at 37 °C for 30 minutes in the dark. DHE reacts with superoxide anion (O2•-) to form ethidium, which intercalates into DNA and emits red fluorescence. At this time point, DHE staining was used to assess ROS content in the leucoplakia tissue, reflecting chronic lesion-associated oxidative stress rather than the direct, short-lived ROS generated during PDT. After staining, slides were washed three times with PBS, mounted with antifade medium, and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ci-L, Nikon). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and compared between the nano-SalB10-ALA250 and nano-ALA250 groups.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded sections of leucoplakia tissues were processed for immunohistochemistry (IHC). Tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide (Abcam, UK) to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0, Abcam, UK) for 7 min in a microwave. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PI3K (dilution 1:400), AKT (1:200), p-AKT (1:100), mTOR (1:800), p-mTOR (1:200), and Hif-1α (1:400) (all from Abcam, UK), diluted in PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin. Immunodetection was performed using the Dako EnVision Detection System (K5007, DAKO, Denmark), a ready-to-use polymer-based system. Slides were incubated with the HRP-conjugated polymer reagent (applied without dilution) for 60 min at room temperature. Staining was visualized using the 3,3’-diaminobenzidine solution provided in the kit. The stained slides were scanned using the CaseViewer application (3DHISTECH Ltd.) and analysed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All results were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using a two-tailed t-test. For comparisons among more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Nano-SalB10-ALA250 uptake by Leuk1 and Cal27 cells

Fluorescence microscopy indicated that nano-SalB10-ALA250 liposome nanoparticles accumulated around the cell membrane after 0.5 hours, with some merging into the membrane. After 1 hour, the nanoparticles were visible within the cytoplasm, and by 2 hours, they were concentrated near the nucleus, confirming the successful uptake of nano-SalB10-ALA250 by both Leuk1 and Cal27 cells (Figure 1E). The difference in nuclear colour appearance between the two cell types is attributable to variations in DAPI staining intensity.

Inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway in vitro

Overall, following PDT with nano-SalB10-ALA250, the expression levels of PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, mTOR, p-mTOR, and their downstream effector Hif-1α were all reduced in Leuk1 cells, indicating that nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT can inhibit the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway. Moreover, compared with nano-ALA250, nano-SalB10-ALA250 exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect (Figure 2A, B). Notably, when the phosphorylation ratios were calculated, both p-AKT/AKT and p-mTOR/mTOR were also decreased in the nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT group relative to controls (Figure 2C). Acute therapeutic ROS generation by nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT

The DCFH-DA probe indicated that the intracellular ROS fluorescence signal in the nano-SalB-ALA group was stronger than that of the nano-ALA group, despite equivalent ALA content. Additionally, the ROS fluorescence signals of nano-SalB10-ALA125 and nano-SalB10-ALA250 were more intense than those of nano-SalB5-ALA125 and nano-SalB5-ALA250, respectively. These results demonstrated that nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT significantly increased early intracellular ROS generation immediately following light irradiation in Cal27 and Leuk1 cells (Figure 2D).

Inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway in vivo

In the 4NQO-induced mouse model, IHC staining revealed elevated expression of PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, mTOR, p-mTOR, and Hif-1α in leucoplakia compared to healthy tongue tissue, indicating pathway activation in leucoplakia. Following PDT, expression levels of these factors decreased significantly, with nano-SalB10-ALA250 exhibiting a more pronounced effect than nano-ALA250 (Figure 4C, D). However, analysis of phosphorylation ratios revealed a more complex pattern: compared with healthy controls, leucoplakia tissues showed increased p-AKT/AKT but decreased p-mTOR/mTOR. Following nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT, both p-AKT/AKT and p-mTOR/mTOR were increased relative to untreated leucoplakia. Despite these divergent changes in phosphorylation ratios, the consistent downregulation of total PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and the downstream effector Hif-1α supports the conclusion that nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT effectively inhibits PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling in vivo. These findings suggest that SalB contributes to enhanced PDT efficacy for OLK, although the regulation of phosphorylation dynamics in the tumour microenvironment warrants further investigation. On the 14th day after treatment, the HE staining of the mouse viscera exhibited no histopathological changes (Figure 4E). Compared with the healthy control and leucoplakia control, the weight of the mice after nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT remained rather stable (Figure 4F).

Discussion

PDT relies on three core components: oxygen, light, and a photosensitizer (PS). Upon activation by a specific light source, the PS generates ROS, leading to targeted cell death.21,22 In this study, we developed a liposomal nanoparticle formulation coloading the conventional photosensitizer ALA with salvianolic acid B (SalB), a water-soluble component of Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge with known anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and tumour-modulating properties.23, 24, 25 However, the poor lipophilicity of SalB limits its therapeutic application. To address this, we incorporated SalB into a liposomal carrier to enhance local delivery, stability, and selective accumulation in oral lesion tissues, aiming to improve the efficacy of ALA-PDT for oral leucoplakia (OLK).

Our preliminary studies suggested that SalB acts as an effective adjuvant in ALA-PDT for OLK26; however, the underlying mechanism remained unclear. In the present study, we first optimized the formulation and identified that a combination of 250 μM ALA with 10 mg SalB (nano-SalB10-ALA250) maximized intracellular ROS production, as determined by DCFH-DA staining, and potently inhibited the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway, as confirmed by Western blotting. This finding aligns with a study by Jie Wei et al, showing that SalB can inhibit oral carcinogenesis through suppressing PI3K/AKT signalling.27

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) confirmed efficient cellular uptake of nano-SalB10-ALA250 by Leuk1 and Cal27 cells. Using a 4NQO-induced mouse model of tongue leucoplakia, we found that nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT significantly alleviated leucoplakia lesions. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed elevated expression of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway components and downstream factors in leucoplakia tissues compared to healthy controls, consistent with the established role of this pathway in OLK carcinogenesis.28, 29, 30 Notably, 14 days after PDT, IHC scores for these proteins were markedly reduced. Moreover, downregulation of Hif-1α was observed in treated mice, suggesting that nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT suppresses both the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and its downstream signalling (Figure 4G).

We further examined the phosphorylation status of AKT and mTOR to gain deeper insight into pathway regulation. In vitro, nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT reduced both total protein levels and the phosphorylation ratios of AKT and mTOR in Leuk1 cells, indicating consistent inhibition of pathway activation. In vivo, however, a more complex pattern emerged: although total protein expression of key pathway components was downregulated following treatment, the p-mTOR/mTOR ratio was increased in treated tissues compared to untreated leucoplakia. This discrepancy may reflect the inherent complexity of the in vivo tumour microenvironment, where dynamic interactions among stromal cells, inflammatory infiltrates, and tumour cells influence post-translational modifications—a phenomenon well documented in oral and head and neck cancers.31 Additionally, basal phosphorylation levels in dysplastic tissues may differ substantially from those in cultured cell lines, and feedback activation of compensatory survival pathways cannot be excluded. Indeed, feedback activation of AKT following mTOR inhibition has been observed in multiple tumour types, representing a common adaptive resistance mechanism.32 Notably, despite these differences in phosphorylation ratios, the consistent downregulation of total PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and Hif-1α both in vitro and in vivo supports the conclusion that nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT effectively suppresses PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling.

Beyond pathway inhibition, the role of ROS in cancer biology and therapy is context-dependent. In pathological conditions such as 4NQO-induced leucoplakia, chronically elevated ROS can promote tumorigenesis, evidenced by increased Hif-1α expression and activation of oncogenic pathways including PI3K/AKT/mTOR.33 In contrast, PDT harnesses a transient burst of ROS for therapeutic purposes. Our findings illustrate this distinction: in leucoplakia tissues, nano-SalB10-ALA250 PDT reduced DHE fluorescence 14 days post-treatment, indicating mitigation of the chronic oxidative environment. Conversely, in Leuk1 and Cal27 cells, intracellular ROS increased immediately after laser irradiation, consistent with the established photochemical mechanism of PDT, in which light-activated photosensitizers generate ROS that primarily induce mitochondrial damage and apoptosis.34 Notably, PDT-induced ROS effects are confined to photosensitizer-accumulating cells within the irradiated area, sparing normal tissues where basal ROS balance is crucial for homeostasis.35 Thus, the strategy of lowering chronic pathological ROS to impede cancer progression versus leveraging acute ROS bursts to trigger selective cell death represents two distinct and context-dependent therapeutic dimensions of ROS modulation.36

Limitations of this study should be noted. First, RT-qPCR analysis was not performed due to limited tissue availability and insufficient RNA integrity, precluding transcriptional validation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR regulation. Second, the clear causal relationship between ROS elevation and pathway inhibition has not been experimentally substantiated. In the future, we will conduct more in-depth researches to explore.

Conclusions

In conclusion, incorporating SalB into a liposome nanoparticle system coloaded with the traditional photosensitizer ALA significantly improved the therapeutic efficacy of PDT against OLK. This improvement was demonstrated by greater reductions in leucoplakia gross appearance and scoring, and more substantial inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway compared with ALA liposome nanoparticle monotherapy. The observed enhancement is likely due to ROS-mediated inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, which suppresses carcinogenesis in OLK. These results indicate that SalB, when coloaded in a liposome nanoparticle delivery system, has the potential to augment the efficacy of traditional PDT for OLK.
