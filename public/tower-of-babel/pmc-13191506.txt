Synthesis, Crystal Growth and Characterization of Iodo- and Bromobismuthates of Sarcosine with Sarcosinium···Sarcosinium Dimeric Cation
Authors: Aram M. Petrosyan, Gerald Giester, Vahram V. Ghazaryan, Gayane S. Tonoyan, Gohar A. Tonoyan, Marek Szafrański
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the present paper, we report on the synthesis and determination of the crystal structures of two halogenobismuthate salts of sarcosine: (SarH)3(Bi2I9) (I) and (SarH)2(BiBr5) (II). The salt (I) crystallizes in the centrosymmetric space group P21/c and the salt (II) in the polar space group Pna21. Sarcosinium···sarcosinium dimeric cations, present in both compounds, were observed for the first time. IR and Raman spectra were registered and discussed on the basis of their structures. Bandgaps were determined by powder diffuse reflectance measurements. The polar symmetry of (II) has been confirmed by the pyroelectric studies.

Introduction

Environmentally friendly energy production is one of humanity’s greatest challenges. The use of solar energy via photovoltaics is a promising approach for sustainable energy production. Currently, solar cells based on single-crystal silicon are manufactured on an industrial scale, however, their relatively high costs motivate the search for cheaper substitutes. In recent years, researchers have focused on organic–inorganic hybrid materials. Among them, methylammonium lead iodide, (CH3NH3)­PbI3, has been studied in most detail. Despite good optoelectronic properties, the presence of toxic lead and low stability limit wide application. Currently, intensive research is underway for new materials free of these disadvantages.

Materials based on nontoxic metals (tin, bismuth and some other metals) as well as other cations are of obvious interest. Park et al. proposed (CH3NH3)3(Bi2I9) and Cs3Bi2I9 as potential photovoltaic materials with Bi2I9 3– anions. The authors of − also investigated (CH3NH3)3(Bi2I9) as a lead-free and stable material. Fabian and Ardo proposed another bismuth based material (1,6-hexanediammonium)­(BiI5) for solar cells. Amino acids form different types of salts and could be of interest as a cation for hybrid materials. Salts of amino acids with suitable anions may be of interest as promising “solar” materials. We started a systematic search for such salts. − In particular, the crystals (GlyH)3(BiCl6)·2H2O, (GlyH)3(BiBr6)·2H2O, (GlyH)3(BiBr6)·3H2O, (GlyH)4(BiBr6)­(Br)·H2O and (GlyH)3(BiI6)·2H2O were obtained. The centrosymmetric dimeric cation of the type (Gly+···Gly+) was observed for the first time in the structures of (GlyH)4(BiBr6)­(Br)·H2O and (GlyH)3(BiI6)·2H2O. Sarcosine is the N-monomethyl-derivative of glycine and the salts it can form are relatively poorly studied.

In the present paper, we report our results on the synthesis, structure determination and characterization of sarcosine-based halogenobismuthates (SarH)3(Bi2I9) (I) and (SarH)2(BiBr5) (II).

Experimental Section

Synthesis

As initial reagents, we used sarcosine (≥98%), bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (≥98%), bismuth bromide (97%) (Thermo Scientific), hydrobromic acid (48%) and hydriodic acid (57% w/w, distilled, stabilized with <1.5% hypophosphorous acid, 99.95%) from Sigma-Aldrich, nonstabilized hydriodic acid (54.6% w/w) from Vekton, and acetonitrile (≥99.9%) from PanReac AppliChem.

The title compounds were crystallized from solutions by spontaneous evaporation of the solvent under ambient conditions (Figure ). The compound (SarH)3(Bi2I9) (I) was obtained from an aqueous solution with the following component ratio: 3Sar + Bi­(NO3)3·5H2O + 13HI. Using the same proportion for hydrobromic acid, namely 3Sar + Bi­(NO3)3·5H2O + 13HBr, leads to the formation of the nonanalogous salt (SarH)2(BiBr5) (II). The salts were also synthesized from stoichiometric compositions using a different bismuth precursor: 2Sar + BiBr3 + 2HBr, and 3Sar+Bi2O3+12HI with an excess of hydriodic acid (instead of 9 mol eq) and a few acetonitrile to facilitate dissolution.

As grown crystals of (SarH)3(Bi2I9) (I) and (SarH)2(BiBr5) (II).

To prepare the solutions, typically 1 g sarcosine and the respective amount of bismuth compounds were placed in a 25 mL polypropylene beaker and moistened with distilled water. The corresponding acids (and in one case a few acetonitrile) were then added and mixed with a magnet stirrer until a homogeneous solution was obtained.

For pyroelectric measurements, single crystals of (SarH)2(BiBr5) (II) were grown. The solution with the 2Sar +1/2 Bi2O3 + 5HBr (6.0000 g, 7.8454 g, and 19.04 mL, respectively) stoichiometric composition was prepared using about 80 mL of acetonitrile–water solvent (90:10 v/v) as optimized solvent for improved crystallization. The solution was saturated at 24 °C due to solvent evaporation and crystals were grown over 1 week on high quality seeds introduced into solution.

Infrared and Raman Spectroscopy

Attenuated total reflection Fourier-transform infrared spectra (ATR-FTIR) were recorded on an Agilent Cary 630 spectrometer using a germanium (Ge) ATR sampling module (Ge crystal, Happ-Genzel apodization, ATR distortion corrected, 64 scans, 4 cm–1 resolution). The Raman spectra were acquired using a Thermo Scientific DXR3 SmartRaman spectrometer with a laser wavelength of 785 nm (2 cm–1 resolution). The laser power was set to 1 mW, aperture slit width 25 μm.

Structure Determination

Optically preselected crystals were mounted on MiTeGen loops with silicone grease and used for single crystal X-ray experiments. Data collections were performed on a Bruker APEX II diffractometer equipped with a CCD area detector, an Incoatec Microfocus Source IμS (30 W, multilayer mirror, Mo-Kα) and an Oxford Cryosystems Cryostream 800 Plus LT device. Data were registered at 200 K up to 60° 2θ full sphere by combining several runs of scans with frame widths of 1° for (I) and 2° for (II). Further data processing (integration and scaling) was done with the Bruker APEX4 software suite, the absorption was corrected by the evaluation of multiscans. The structures were solved (SHELXT) and refined using the SHELXL software implemented in the ShelXle GUI tool. Non-hydrogen atoms were refined with independent anisotropic displacement parameters. Positions of hydrogen atoms, approximately located by difference electron density maps, were finally optimized by applying a riding model. In compound (I), the angle beta of 90.027° suggests the possibility of a higher symmetry. However, a corresponding analysis using the Platon software package does not indicate an orthorhombic space group, but a twin (1 0 0/0 −1 0/0 0 −1) is present with approximately equal volume fractions. Structural data are available as Supporting Information and have been deposited as CIF to the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Base (CCDC No. 2519057 and 2519058 for (I) and (II), respectively). Optical Measurements

UV–vis diffuse reflectance data were collected using an Agilent Cary 60 UV–Vis spectrophotometer equipped with a Remote Diffuse Reflectance Accessory (DRA). The 100% reflectance baseline was determined using a white PTFE reference plate. Data were registered at room temperature (spectral range 200–1000 nm, scanning rate 10 nm/s, data interval 1.00 nm) on finely powdered crystalline samples.

Dielectric Measurements

The (SarH)2BiBr5 crystals were oriented by X-ray diffraction to cut plates perpendicular to the [c] direction, i.e., perpendicular to the polar axis of the crystal. The parallel faces of the samples were coated with silver paste. Dielectric measurements were performed as a function of temperature in the frequency range of the electric field from 10 kHz–3 MHz with a Hewlett-Packard 4192A impedance analyzer. The amplitude of the ac measuring electric field was of about 3.5 V/cm. The temperature of the samples was varied at a rate of 0.5 K/min. The switching of spontaneous polarization in an external electric field was tested at varied temperatures using a low-frequency method with a Keithley 6517B electrometer. The pyroelectric charge was measured as a function of temperature with a Keithley 6514 electrometer at a cooling/heating rate of 2 K/min. Changes in polarization were determined relative to the starting point of the temperature cycle, at which the sample electrodes were short-circuited for 15 min at the stabilized temperature to eliminate surface charges.

Thermal Analysis

The thermal stability of the crystals was determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The polycrystalline sample was loaded into a TGA Q50 analyzer (TA Instruments) and heated under a nitrogen atmosphere at a rate of 10 K/min. Differential scanning calorimetric (DSC) measurements were performed with a Q2000 calorimeter (TA Instruments). The sample prepared from a single ground crystal was enclosed in a hermetically sealed aluminum vessel and cooled/heated at a temperature change rate of 10 K/min.

Results and Discussion

The Structure and Infrared and Raman Spectra of (SarH)3(Bi2I9) (I)

The salt (SarH)3(Bi2I9) (I) crystallizes in monoclinic space group P21/c, with two formula units in the asymmetric unit (Table ). These consist of six A-, B-, C-, D-, E-, F-sarcosinium cations and two discrete Bi2I9 3– anions. The molecular structure of (I) is shown in Figure .

Selected bond lengths and angles are listed in Table . Sarcosinium cations exhibit expected geometrical parameters. In the first anion Bi(1)2I9 3–, the terminal Bi–I bonds range from 2.8865 to 3.0451 Å, with a mean of 2.9565 Å. The bridging bonds are longer with an average value of 3.2609 Å, and vary between 3.1244 and 3.4287 Å. The mean I–Bi–I trans-angle is 171.98°, values ranging from 165.98 to 174.90°. The mean of the other angles is 89.87° and ranges from 76.98 to 96.73°. The Bi2–I–Bi1 bridging angles vary from 78.617 to 81.701°, with a mean of 79.65°. The second anion, Bi(2)2I9 3–, exhibits very similar geometrical features. The terminal Bi–I distances range from 2.8823 to 3.0513 Å (mean: 2.9523 Å), and the bridging bonds between 2.9945 and 3.4359 Å (mean: 3.2631 Å). The mean bridging angle is 171.76° (range 165.09 to 177.76°), while the mean of the other angles is 89.86° (range 77.55 to 98.21°). The mean Bi4–I–Bi3 angle is 80.88° (range 80.26 to 81.22°).

The packing diagram in the structure of (I) is shown in Figure S1 and hydrogen bond parameters are listed in Table .

To further validate the structural parameters obtained from single-crystal X-ray diffraction, bond valence sum (BVS) calculations were performed for the Bi atoms using the bond-valence model: s=exp(R0−RB) where R is the observed bond distance and R0 and B are empirical parameters. The parameters for Bi–I and Bi–Br interactions were taken from the bond-valence parameter database reported by Brown and Altermatt and further summarized in subsequent works on the bond-valence method. − Using the Bi–I bond lengths listed in Table (2.8823–3.0513 Å for terminal bonds and 3.1230–3.4361 Å for bridging bonds) and the Bi–Br bond lengths listed in Table (2.7505–3.0350 Å), bond valence sum (BVS) calculations were performed for the Bi atoms. The calculated BVS values are 3.00, 2.97, 3.07, and 2.95 for Bi1, Bi2, Bi3, and Bi4 in compound (I), and 3.01 for Bi1 in compound (II). These values are very close to the expected oxidation state Bi3+, corresponding to the (Bi2I9)3– and (BiBr5)2– anions, respectively. The obtained BVS results confirm that the coordination environments determined from the crystallographic refinement are chemically reasonable and consistent with the expected oxidation states of the bismuth atoms. Therefore, the bond-valence analysis provides additional validation of the structural model obtained from the X-ray diffraction data.

As can be seen in Figure and Table , A–B, C–D and E–F couples of sarcosinium cations form noncentrosymmetric A+···A+ type dimeric cations, (SarH···SarH)3(Bi2I9)2. The O···O distances within these dimers range from 2.651 to 2.737 Å, with a mean value of 2.696 Å (Table ). This A+···A+ type dimeric cation was discovered in 1979 by Kirkwood and Willett in the structure of β-alaninium tetrachloroferrate­(III), (β-AlaH)­(FeCl4). Later, it was observed in several salts of β-alanine, as well as in two salts of other amino acids, dl-aspartic acid nitrate monohydrate and dl-valinium perchlorate. As mentioned above, we succeeded to obtain glycine salts with similar dimeric cations. ,

In addition to O–H···O type hydrogen bonds there are also N–H···I and C–H···I hydrogen bonds and an intramolecular contact N1F–H12F···O2F in the structure of (I) (Table ). Furthermore, there is a supramolecular halogen bond I7···I18 between Bi2I9 3– anions with an I···I distance of 3.8509(7) Å (Figure S2).

The IR and Raman spectra of compound (I) are shown in Figure . The observed vibrational bands are consistent with the presence of protonated sarcosine cations and the Bi2I9 3– anion and agree well with the structural features determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. IR spectra of numerous compounds containing the sarcosine cation have been previously studied in the literature. − In the high-frequency region, one can expect the appearance of manifestations of the valence vibrations ν­(NH), ν­(OH) and ν­(CH) of NH2, OH, CH2 and CH3 groups. In the region around 3000 cm–1, a characteristic broad absorption band is observed in the IR spectrum, caused by the superposition of the vibrational modes mentioned above. In the Raman spectrum, the most intense lines in this region are the vibrations of the CH2 and CH3 groups, observed as a peak at 2955 cm–1. In the IR spectrum, these correspond to weak peaks at 2971 and 2991 cm–1. The presence of carboxyl groups manifests as a strong absorption band at 1718 cm–1. The Raman spectrum in this region shows a weak peak at 1734 cm–1, as well as one at 1684 cm–1. We assume that in the presence of quasi-symmetric dimeric cations of the A+···A+ type, the peaks at 1718 cm–1 and 1734 cm–1 relate to asymmetric, and the peak at 1684 cm–1 to symmetric stretching vibrations of the carboxyl groups. The Structure and Infrared and Raman Spectra of (SarH)2(BiBr5) (II)

The salt (II) crystallizes in the orthorhombic polar space group Pna21, with one formula unit in the asymmetric unit consisting of two sarcosinium cations and one BiBr5 2– anion (Figure a).

Selected bond lengths and angles are listed in Table . The geometric parameters of sarcosinium cations are close together and have expected values. The BiBr5 2– anion forms a zigzag chain of corner-sharing BiBr6 octahedra (Figure b) with the Br5 bridging atom exhibiting very similar Bi–Br lengths of 3.0206(10) Å and 3.0353(9) Å. The mean Bi–Br bond length in the slightly distorted BiBr6 octahedron is 2.8734 Å and ranges from 2.7502 to 3.0353 Å. The trans-angles are 176.26, 178.60 and 171.03° (mean: 175.3°). The mean of other angles is 90.0° and ranges from 84.78 to 96.48°. The angle Bi1–Br5–Bi1 is 152.17°.

Hydrogen bond parameters are listed in Table . As can be seen from Table and Figure , the sarcosinium cations A- and B- form an A+···A+ type dimeric cation, (SarH···SarH)­(BiBr5). The O···O distances within the dimer are 2.630 and 2.639 Å, and are thus notably shorter than those observed in (I). Therefore, in the present work, we report for the first time the preparation of two sarcosine salts with a dimeric cation of the A+···A+ type. In the structure of (II) hydrogen bonds of type N–H···Br and C–H···Br also exist. Each bromine atom of the BiBr5 2– anion forms one hydrogen bond of the both types, N–H···Br and C–H···Br (Table ).

The diffuse reflectance values were transformed using the Kubelka–Munk function and expressed as Tauc plots to estimate the energy gap. − We assumed that the low-energy peaks/shoulders are of excitonic nature and therefore do not correspond to the fundamental absorption of the crystals. This estimation indicates that the iodide compound has a narrower energy gap than the bromide salt.

For halogenobismuthate compounds, the valence band is mainly composed by iodine/bromine s- and p-orbitals, while the conduction band is primarily composed of Bi 6s and 6p orbitals with some halide contribution.

Thermal Characteristic of (SarH)2BiBr5

The results of the TGA measurements shown in Figure a indicate that (SarH)2BiBr5 is thermally stable to about 450 K. At higher temperatures, a weight loss is observed, indicating decomposition of the substance. The stability of the room-temperature orthorhombic phase of the crystal was studied by the DSC method. As seen in Figure b no phase transitions occur between 95 K and the melting point at 447 K, which was defined as the onset of the thermal anomaly revealed during the heating run. The thermal anomaly associated with crystallization exhibited an irregular shape and large temperature hysteresis that varied between cooling runs (Figure b). However, in the subsequent runs melting was observed at the same well-defined temperature. This indicates that the substance most probably crystallizes in the same initial orthorhombic phase after melting.

Dielectric and Pyroelectric Properties of (SarH)2BiBr5

High-quality large single crystals of (SarH)2BiBr5 (Figure a), prepared by solution growth, enabled dielectric studies. The space group symmetry Pna21 of the crystals implies that spontaneous polarization can only occur along the [001] direction. Therefore, we performed a series of tests of the polarization switching for two samples cut perpendicular to this direction. We could not detect any sign of polarization reversal in an external electric field up to 15 kV/cm and at varied temperatures between 250 and 360 K.

Large single crystals of (SarH)2BiBr5 grown from solution (a), electric permittivity (b), and temperature-dependent changes in spontaneous crystal polarization (c).

The temperature dependence of the electric permittivity, measured along the polar direction of the crystal for selected electric field frequencies is shown in Figure b. It should be noted that the values of electric permittivity are relatively high for a material that exhibits neither dielectric relaxation nor ferroelectric properties. The frequency dispersion is small and widens only at elevated temperatures, which is typical for organic–inorganic hybrid materials, whose dielectric losses increase with rising temperature. The high value of electric permittivity may indicate spontaneous polarization in the [001] direction of the crystal. Similar dielectric response was observed along the polar direction in the pyroelectric crystal (DMG···Sar)­ClO4.

The presence of spontaneous polarization was confirmed by pyroelectric studies. The temperature-induced changes in polarization, determined from the pyroelectric charge measurements during heating and cooling cycles, are shown in Figure c. The absolute values of the pyroelectric coefficient, determined from the slopes of the nearly linear dependencies between 250 and 330 K, are 6.8 and 6.4 μC m–2 K–1, for heating and cooling runs, respectively. These values exceed that of tourmaline (4 μC m–2 K–1 at 298 K).

Conclusions

Two new sarcosine halogenobismuthate salts, (SarH)3(Bi2I9) (I) and (SarH)2(BiBr5) (II), were synthesized and structurally characterized by vibrational and powder diffuse reflectance spectroscopy. Compound (I) crystallizes in a centrosymmetric structure, whereas compound (II) exhibits a polar structure. For the first time in sarcosine salts, a dimeric cation of the type (A+···A+) was observed in the synthesized compounds: (SarH···SarH)3(Bi2I9)2 (I) and (SarH···SarH)­(BiBr5) (II). The iodide-based compound exhibits a narrower bandgap than the bromide analogue, highlighting the influence of halide substitution on the optical properties of these hybrid materials.

The polar symmetry of (SarH)2(BiBr5) was confirmed by pyroelectric studies. However, the spontaneous polarization cannot be switched by an external electric field, which means that the crystal does not exhibit ferroelectric properties. It remains pyroelectric and retains the orthorhombic polar symmetry across the entire temperature range up to the melting point.
