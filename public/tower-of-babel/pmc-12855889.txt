Exploring the association between pulse wave velocity and CKD progression: a longitudinal study in Mashhad, Iran
Authors: Susan Darroudi, Naiemeh Varasteh, Hayder Taha Yaseen, Reza Salehi Manzari, Vahid Mahdavizade, Abbas Mohtashamian, Faezeh Nematolahi, Amir Mohammad Kamali, Amirhosein Zardast, Majid Ghayour-Mobarhan, Habibollah Esmaily, Eisa Nazar, Narges Fereydouni, Gordon A. Ferns, Mohsen Moohebati, Maryam Saberi-Karimian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research is distinctive in its emphasis on non-invasive arterial stiffness as a critical vascular risk factor for renal failure. Our objective was to evaluate this correlation through both invasive and non-invasive techniques, representing an innovative strategy in the discipline. The study took place in Mashhad, Iran, and was specifically designed to investigate the association between diminished glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and arterial stiffness. We performed an extensive analysis involving 787 participants aged between 35 and 65, who were randomly selected from the Mashhad Stroke and Heart Atherosclerotic Disorder (MASHAD) cohort study. We carefully documented the baseline characteristics of these participants, which encompassed demographic information, biochemical and hematological markers, as well as measurements of systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and indicators of arterial stiffness, including pulse wave velocity (PWV), central augmentation index (CAI), and central aortic pressure (CAP). The participants were categorized into four groups according to their serum creatinine and estimated GFR-MDRD levels: Group 1 (GFR: 60–89), Group 2 (GFR: 45–59), Group 3 (GFR: 30–44), and Group 4 (GFR: 15–29) based on clinical practice guidelines for chronic kidney disease. The velocity of pulse waves exhibited a notable increase as the estimated GFR decreased, reaching its peak in Group 4 (9.92 ± 4.29 m/s) and its lowest point in Group 2 (6.9 ± 2.35 m/s) (P < 0.05). No significant variations were detected in CAP, CAI, and GFR across the study groups. Two analytical models were employed to evaluate the relationship between arterial stiffness markers and different stages of chronic kidney disease (CKD). The first model (unadjusted data) revealed a significant correlation between Group 3b and CAP (P = 0.034), as well as between Group 4 and PWV (P = 0.021). In the second model (adjusted for sex, age, hypertension, and diabetes), a significant correlation persisted between Group 4 and PWV (P = 0.049). The results of this research highlight the importance of arterial stiffness as a marker of deteriorating kidney function. The study concluded that PWV is associated with a reduction in eGFR, indicating that each unit rise in PWV corresponds to a 41.8% decline in eGFR. It is advisable for subsequent research to investigate the mechanisms that link arterial stiffness to impaired kidney function.

Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is an urgent global health issue, ranking among the primary causes of death and illness. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that in 2012, CKD was responsible for 1.5% of worldwide deaths and linked to 1.3% of years lost due to death1. Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the most common causes of fatality in patients with reduced kidney function and advanced CKD (stages 3 and 4), as well as cancer and infection2.

Considering the importance of both traditional and non-traditional risk factors for vascular diseases in patients with CKD, researchers anticipated a high prevalence of CVD in this population3. Arterial stiffness, a significant vascular risk factor, arises from the decreased distensibility of large arteries. Both invasive and non-invasive methods are employed to assess arterial stiffness, with the pulse wave velocity (PWV) being recognized as the gold standard among non-invasive techniques4. Arterial stiffness is linked to diabetes, atherosclerosis, and renal insufficiency, with diminished kidney function contributing to vascular calcification through hormonal changes and inflammatory processes.

Conversely, arterial stiffness leads to microvascular injury and pulse wave nephropathy5. Much research has examined the correlation between arterial stiffness and kidney failure4,6–8. Nevertheless, the majority of these studies have been carried out within a limited and particular group of patients with CKD6. Furthermore, limited research has focused on the parameters of arterial stiffness across various stages of renal failure.

Our main goal was to examine the correlation between glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and arterial stiffness metrics, such as central aortic pressure (CAP), central augmentation index (CAI), and PWV across four stages of CKD. This investigation seeks to enhance the understanding of CKD progression and the possible influence of arterial stiffness in this context.

We hypothesize that increased arterial stiffness, as reflected by higher PWV and related indices, is independently associated with faster CKD progression over time.

Methods and materials

Population

The data for this research were derived from the Mashhad Stroke and Heart Atherosclerotic Disorder (MASHAD) cohort study, which commenced in 2010. Participants were selected from three areas within Mashhad, the second most populous city in Iran (according to the 2016 Census), situated in the north-eastern region of the country, utilizing a stratified cluster random sampling technique. The criteria for inclusion were as follows: (I) individuals aged between 35 and 65 years, (II) possessing the physical and mental ability to engage in the clinical examination, and (III) having no definitive plans to relocate from the area after the initial and subsequent phases of the study. Exclusion criteria in this study included individuals with incomplete clinical or laboratory data, diagnosed acute kidney injury at baseline, or a history of major cardiovascular events (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke) within the six months before enrollment. All participants were thoroughly informed, and their written consent was obtained.

Over the course of the 10-year follow-up, out of the original 9,704 participants, 1,715 individuals were lost to follow-up due to non-responsiveness, reluctance, lack of access, and migration. In total, 432 participants passed away, resulting in 7,560 individuals remaining for data analysis. For this study, we randomly selected 1,000 subjects due to budget limitations, from which 787 eligible subjects were included (those excluded had consumed coffee, did not fast, engaged in vigorous physical activity or sexual activity in the last three days, or were on dialysis). These subjects were classified into four groups according to the MDRD criteria (Fig. 1).

Study population flowchart.

Estimated GFR (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) equation:

eGFR = 186 × (Serum creatinine) ^−1.154 × (Age)^−0.203 × (0.742 if female) × (1.212 if Black), as described by Levey et al.9.

The baseline characteristics of the participants encompassed demographic information, biochemical and hematological markers, as well as systolic and diastolic blood pressure measurements. The study protocol, informed consent form, and all related documents/methods were reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, with the ethical approval code R.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1398.228. All research stages were conducted in compliance with applicable guidelines, regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measurement of arterial stiffness

We conducted the PWV, CAI, and CAP assessments utilizing a SphygmoCor XCEL System from AtCor Medical Incorporated. All SphygmoCor measurements were conducted by a single trained operator to minimize inter-observer variability. The operator underwent formal training and performed a calibration phase before data collection to ensure consistency in measurement procedures. Participants were instructed to fast for a minimum of 6 h and to refrain from smoking, consuming alcohol, or drinking caffeine for at least 12 h before the assessments10. Following a period of 10 to 15 min of rest in a supine position, the assessment was carried out non-invasively in two phases.

Study variables and definitions of terms

Serum creatinine levels were assessed through an enzymatic assay. We standardized the measurements by correlating the average creatinine values with those of participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey III, classified by sex and age groups11. The estimation of GFR was conducted utilizing serum creatinine levels in conjunction with MDRD study equation. In accordance with the guidelines established by the National Kidney Foundation in 2002, participants were categorized into four distinct groups: Group 1 (GFR: 60–89), Group 2 (GFR: 45–59), Group 3 (GFR: 30–44), and Group 4 (GFR: 15–29) based on clinical practice guidelines for chronic kidney disease12.

Statistical analysis

We assessed the relationship between qualitative variables utilizing Fisher’s exact test and Chi-Square tests, while the association between two quantitative variables was examined through Pearson’s correlation coefficient. One-way ANOVA was employed to compare the means of quantitative variables across independent groups. Both qualitative and quantitative variables, along with descriptive statistics, were presented as the mean (%) ± SD. A multinomial logistic regression model was utilized to analyze the impact of arterial stiffness, adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, and diabetes, as dependent variables. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 21 (IBM SPSS, Inc., Armonk, NY, USA) with a significance level set at 0.05.

Results

This study included a total of 787 participants, with a mean age that varied significantly across the study groups, the highest being 67.12 ± 5.62 years for group G4. Additionally, there were notable differences in the frequency of the groups. However, the study population exhibited homogeneity regarding body mass index. In contrast, significant differences were observed in biochemical and hematological markers among the participants. Comprehensive data on the baseline characteristics can be found in Table 1. The evaluation of arterial stiffness markers indicated that PWV differed significantly between the study groups, with the maximum PWV recorded in group G4 at 9.92 ± 4.29 m/s, and the minimum in group G2 at 6.9 ± 2.35 m/s. Nonetheless, no significant differences were noted in CAP and CAI across the study groups (Fig. 2).

The relationship between arterial stiffness parameters and various stages of CKD was evaluated through two methodologies. The initial model utilized unadjusted data, while the subsequent model incorporated adjustments for sex, age, hypertension, and diabetes. The initial model revealed a significant correlation between stage G3 and CAP (P = 0.034), as well as between stage G4 and PWV (P = 0.021). Discussion

We have evaluated an evaluation of the relationship between indicators of arterial stiffness and the stages of CKD. In addition, we examined how markers of arterial stiffness correlate with the demographic and laboratory data of patients.

In the current study, the evaluation of arterial stiffness markers showed that PWV significantly differed between the study groups, with the highest PWV recorded in group G4, similar to some comparable investigations13. However, multiple previous studies have identified a distinct relationship between arterial stiffness and CKD. Nevertheless, certain studies have produced conflicting outcomes14,15. Various methodologies and study populations have been utilized in these investigations. Shinohara et al. identified a notable correlation between dialysis patients and individuals with end-stage CKD16. In a separate study involving primarily participants at the initial stages of CKD (stages 1 and 2), researchers demonstrated a notable relationship between GFR and PWV17. A noteworthy study involving 767 patients also revealed that arterial stiffness worsened as the glomerular filtration rate decreased18. Additionally, highlighting the inconsistency in the literature, findings from the Framingham Offspring Cohort revealed that arterial stiffness is linked to the onset of albuminuria, yet not to mild to moderate CKD14.

Arterial stiffness serves as a risk factor for the advancement of CVD by inducing end-organ damage through increased pressure on vascular beds10. Various additional explanations have been offered regarding the pathophysiology; it is believed that arterial stiffness contributes to elevated central pulse pressure and blood pressure due to the early return of waves during late systole. Furthermore, the rise in afterload results in an increased demand for myocardial oxygen19,20.

In total, multiple mechanisms have elucidated the relationship between arterial stiffness and CKD. Initially, research indicates that a decline in GFR results in endothelial dysfunction, subsequently contributing to the onset of atherosclerosis21. Secondly, the disruption of mineral metabolism in these patients exacerbates arterial calcification, promoting the conversion of smooth muscle cells in the arterial wall into arterial stenosis22. Conversely, we can analyze this relationship from the opposite perspective. The occurrence of atherosclerosis results in an increase in shear stress on the arterial lumen, which subsequently leads to ischemia and renal damage23.

In alignment with earlier research, we recognized diabetes and hypertension as cardiovascular risk factors linked to an increased PWV24,25. Earlier research has linked the relationship between hypertension and arterial stiffness to medial hypertrophy26. Moreover, the excessive production of advanced glycation end products (AGE) has been identified as a contributing factor to the structural alterations in the arterial wall associated with diabetes27,28. An elevated CAP was linked to central obesity, diabetes, and hypertension, with CAI measurements showing significant differences when categorizing patients based on these conditions. Conversely, the study’s results indicated that the relationship between CKD markers and arterial stiffness was only significant for PWV values in stage 4 of CKD. Consequently, the findings suggest that an alternative mechanism may contribute to the heightened risk of cardiovascular diseases in patients with CKD.

There were some strong points in our study. The present investigation examined the association between GFR and arterial stiffness across four stages of CKD based on clinical practice guidelines for chronic kidney disease12. This study considers four arterial stiffness metrics, such as CAP, CAI, and PWV, across four stages of CKD in a large sample size, including 787 participants. Standard methodologies were utilized to assess PWV, CAI, CAP, and GFR. The investigation population exhibited homogeneity regarding body mass index. However, the current study faced multiple limitations and challenges that need to be considered. Firstly, it utilized a cross-sectional design. Secondly, the markers were evaluated only once; however, we have the capability to conduct follow-up measurements over a longitudinal timeline. Although the CKD-EPI equation has been shown to improve eGFR estimation accuracy, we used the MDRD formula to maintain consistency with previous phases of this cohort study. This may represent a minor limitation of our analysis. Furthermore, significant differences were detected in biochemical and hematological markers among the participants. Moreover, there were remarkable differences in the frequency of the study groups. The participants of the current investigation were categorized into four distinct groups based on clinical practice guidelines for chronic kidney disease12. The number of patients with a GFR of 15–29 mL/min was small, as in most of the same studies. Patients with a GFR of 15–29 mL/min, classified as G4, are usually the oldest group among the four GFR categories, as demonstrated in our study and similar investigations. They are less likely to participate in research.

Conclusion

The results of this research emphasize the significance of arterial stiffness as a marker for deteriorating kidney function. The study concluded that PWV is associated with a reduction in eGFR, indicating that each unit rise in PWV corresponds to a 41.8% decline in eGFR. It is advisable for subsequent research to investigate the mechanisms that link arterial stiffness to impaired kidney function.
