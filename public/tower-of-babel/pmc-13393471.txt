Pathogenic characterization and genome-wide evolutionary analysis of Bacillus anthracis from a cutaneous anthrax case in Inner Mongolia, China
Authors: Jinrui Hu, Lixin Zhang, Changshan Guo, Guizhen Wang, Hua Cai, Yi Yang, Haitao Yuan, Hongyu Ji, Haijian Zhou, Liping Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnthrax remains an important zoonosis in pastoral areas of China, yet genomic evidence directly linking human cases, contaminated animal products, and environmental sources is still limited for Hinggan League in Inner Mongolia. We investigated a cutaneous anthrax event reported in 2023 and characterized the associated Bacillus anthracis isolates using epidemiological and genome-based approaches.MethodsA field investigation was initiated after a suspected case was reported on 2 November 2023. Eighteen samples were collected from the patient, residual products from the slaughtered sheep, and the local environment. Bacillus anthracis was detected by real-time PCR and isolated by culture with phenotypic confirmation. Four isolates underwent canSNP typing, short-read whole-genome sequencing, average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis, and core-genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) against a contextual public genome dataset.ResultsOne confirmed case of cutaneous anthrax was identified after the patient slaughtered and skinned a moribund sheep. Four of 18 samples were PCR-positive, and four Bacillus anthracis isolates were recovered from the patient, mutton, sheepskin, and a transport vehicle. All four isolates belonged to the A.Br.001/002 lineage and showed very high genome-wide similarity. Combined epidemiological and cgMLST evidence supported a single-source event linking the infected sheep, contaminated animal products, the transport environment, and the human case.ConclusionsThis study documents a localized zoonotic anthrax outbreak associated with the slaughter of an infected sheep and demonstrates the value of integrating routine microbiological investigation with genome-based typing for source tracing in pastoral settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05156-8.

Introduction

Anthrax is an acute zoonotic infection caused by Bacillus anthracis (B. anthracis). Human disease is usually acquired through direct contact with infected animals or contaminated animal products, although gastrointestinal and inhalational anthrax also occur [1]. In China, anthrax remains a persistent public health concern in several pastoral and semi-pastoral provinces, including Inner Mongolia [2–6].

The pathogenicity of B. anthracis depends largely on two major virulence plasmids. The pXO1 plasmid encodes the toxin components protective antigen (pagA), lethal factor (lef), and edema factor (cya), whereas pXO2 carries capsule-associated genes that contribute to immune evasion [7, 8]. Because the species is highly monomorphic, low-resolution lineage markers such as canSNP are useful for broad phylogenetic placement but have limited power for outbreak-scale discrimination; higher-resolution whole-genome approaches are therefore needed for source tracing [9, 10].

Recent lineage-focused work has improved understanding of the A.Br.001/002 population circulating in China [10, 11]. However, publicly available genomes from Hinggan League remain scarce, and direct molecular evidence linking patient, animal-product, and environmental isolates from the same local event is still limited. This gap reduces the precision of outbreak reconstruction in pastoral settings where animal movement and informal slaughter may facilitate spillover.

In this study, we combined field epidemiology, conventional microbiological testing, canSNP typing, whole-genome sequencing, and cgMLST analysis to investigate a 2023 cutaneous anthrax event in Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia. Our aims were to reconstruct the local transmission chain, define the genetic relatedness of the outbreak isolates, and place the event within the broader Chinese B. anthracis population while avoiding over-interpretation of regional or international spread.

Methods

Case investigation and epidemiological timeline

The index patient was identified in Horqin Right Wing Front Banner, Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia. According to field investigation, the patient slaughtered and skinned a moribund sheep on 26 October 2023. The patient noticed that the sheep blood did not clot during slaughter. Some internal organs were cooked and fed to domestic dogs, and part of the meat was consumed by the patient and several other individuals.

An erythematous lesion developed on the patient’s left forearm on 31 October 2023 and progressed within 48 h to a black eschar with surrounding edema and vesicles. The patient also developed fever, chills, and limb pain. On 2 November 2023, the case was reported to the local Center for Disease Control and Prevention, diagnostic testing was initiated, and field sampling was conducted. Follow-up investigation identified one close household contact and three additional individuals who had consumed mutton from the same sheep; none developed compatible disease during the observation period.

Sample collection and routine laboratory testing

A total of 18 samples were collected during field investigation: one lesion swab and one blood sample from the patient; four mutton samples, two sheepskin samples, and four hoof samples from the slaughtered sheep; and six environmental samples, including soil from the slaughter site, soil from non-slaughter areas, and swabs from the transport vehicle.

Samples were transported at 2–8℃ and processed immediately according to routine anthrax diagnostic procedures. Real-time PCR for B. anthracis was performed using a commercial fluorescence assay (Yizhi Technology, YZB1402-50) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and WS 283–2020. The RT-PCR system was 20 μl, TaqDNA polymerase 10 μl, upstream and downstream primers and probes 0.4 μl each, and the DNA template 1 μl. The amplification conditions were as follows 95 °C for 30 s, 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 20 s, 45 cycles, and 40 °C for 30 s. Amplification was performed using a QuantStudi005 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument.

Culture was performed on nutrient agar and blood agar, and suspected colonies were confirmed by Gram staining, phage lysis testing, and penicillin inhibition testing according to the national diagnostic standard.

canSNP typing

The strain DNA was amplified with TaqMan probe based on the 13 SNP loci of B. anthracis reported in the literature [12]. The PCR system was 20 μl, TaqDNA polymerase 10 μl, upstream and downstream primers and probes 0.4 μl each, and the DNA template 1 μl. The amplification conditions were as follows 95 °C for 30 s, 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 20 s, 45 cycles, and 40 °C for 30 s. Amplification was performed using a QuantStudi005 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. The nucleotides of the detected SNP sites were determined based on the detected fluorescence signal against the probe sequence.

Whole-genome sequencing, FastANI, cgMLST and phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from pure cultures using the QIAamp DNA extraction kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Following nucleic acid extraction, validation experiments were performed to confirm the absence of viable pathogens before further downstream processing. DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and qualified DNA samples were used for library preparation with the Illumina MFEasy Rapid Library Preparation Kit. Whole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform using paired-end sequencing, with a target sequencing depth of approximately 100× and at least 1 Gb of raw data generated for each isolate.

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC and then de novo assembled. Assembly quality, completeness, and contamination were evaluated using CheckM [13]. Virulence-associated genes and antimicrobial resistance genes were screened using ABRicate against the VFDB and CARD databases, respectively.

A total of 73 Chinese Bacillus anthracis genomes and 109 international genomes were retrieved from the NCBI database. Genome-wide genetic similarity among strains was evaluated by pairwise comparison using FastANI v1.33 [14]. cgMLST analysis was performed using chewBBACA v2.8.5 [15] based on a scheme comprising 3,803 loci derived from the B. anthracis Ames reference genome (NC_007530.2). The isolates generated in the present study were analyzed together with 182 publicly available B. anthracis genomes downloaded from NCBI. During pairwise allele distance calculation, loci missing because of sequencing gaps or incomplete assemblies were treated as missing data and excluded from the comparison. The resulting pairwise cgMLST allelic distances were used to construct a minimum spanning tree.

Phylogenetic relationships were further inferred using IQ-TREE [16] based on the cgMLST allelic profiles, and the minimum spanning tree was visualized using GrapeTree [17].

Results

Epidemiological findings

The epidemiological investigation supported a single exposure event associated with the slaughter and skinning of a moribund sheep on 26 October 2023. Five days later, the patient developed a typical cutaneous anthrax lesion on the left forearm, followed by systemic symptoms. After receiving clinical treatment, including intravenous clindamycin, the patient recovered and was discharged from hospital. No secondary human cases were identified during follow-up. Notably, the domestic dogs that consumed the cooked viscera of the infected sheep did not develop any apparent illness during the observation period. The sheep had reportedly circulated as breeding stock among neighboring villages before slaughter, providing a plausible route for introduction of the infection into this area.

Public health response

Upon laboratory confirmation of the anthrax case, an immediate public health response was initiated. All identified close contacts were placed under medical observation through local health centers and village clinics, with daily symptom monitoring conducted by trained healthcare personnel. Health education was provided to the local community, with emphasis on the risks associated with handling sick or dead livestock and the importance of seeking medical attention promptly after suspected exposure. The patient’s residence and the slaughter site were disinfected using chlorine-containing disinfectants, and potentially contaminated materials, including sheep products and slaughter tools, were collected and disposed of by incineration or deep burial with lime. In parallel, the livestock authorities were notified to strengthen surveillance of diseased and dead animals, ensure safe carcass disposal, and manage potentially exposed herds. Active case finding was conducted in surrounding village clinics and township health centers, and no additional suspected or confirmed anthrax cases were identified during follow-up.

Virulence- and resistance-associated genomic features

Screening against the VFDB database showed that all four outbreak-related Bacillus anthracis isolates carried the major anthrax-associated virulence genes, including capA, capB, capC, dep/capD, capE, inhA, cya, lef, and pagA (Table 2). Resistance gene prediction using NCBI AMRFinderPlus identified genes associated with β-lactam, carbapenem, fosfomycin, and streptothricin resistance, whereas no additional acquired resistance determinants were detected. Because these findings were based on in silico prediction only, they should be interpreted cautiously in the absence of phenotypic validation.

cgMLST-based phylogenetic analysis

To place the outbreak strains in a broader genomic context, cgMLST analysis was performed using a scheme comprising 3,794 core loci across 186 B. anthracis genomes, including the four isolates recovered in this study. In the broader comparative dataset, most Chinese isolates grouped within several closely related clusters, whereas isolates from other countries were distributed across more divergent branches. Some recent Chinese isolates, including strains from Ningxia, appeared in neighboring branches, indicating genetic relatedness within the A.Br.001/002 lineage. However, given the strong clonality and slow evolutionary rate of B. anthracis, these relationships should not be interpreted as direct evidence of recent interregional transmission without additional epidemiological support.

The minimum spanning tree further showed that the outbreak isolates formed a compact cluster with limited allelic variation, supporting a common-source outbreak. Most genomes included in the analysis showed high core-locus detection rates, indicating that the dataset was suitable for cgMLST-based comparison. Overall, these results support a close genetic relationship among the isolates recovered from the patient, mutton, sheepskin, and transport vehicle, consistent with a single outbreak-associated transmission chain.

Discussion

This study combined epidemiological investigation, laboratory diagnosis, and genome-based analysis to characterize a cutaneous anthrax outbreak in Hinggan League, Inner Mongolia, China, in 2023. Four Bacillus anthracis isolates were recovered from the patient, mutton, sheepskin, and a transport vehicle, and all were assigned to the A.Br.001/002 lineage [18]. The close genetic relatedness of these isolates, together with the epidemiological evidence, supports their involvement in a single outbreak event associated with the slaughter and handling of an infected sheep [19]. These findings provide genome-based evidence linking the human case with contaminated animal products and the surrounding environment within the same outbreak context.

Anthrax remains a major zoonotic disease in pastoral and livestock-producing regions, where human infection is often associated with the slaughter, processing, or handling of diseased animals [20–22]. In China, most reported human anthrax cases are cutaneous and are linked to occupational or household exposure to infected livestock or contaminated animal products. In this study, the patient had direct contact with a moribund sheep during slaughtering and skinning, which represents the most plausible route of infection. At the same time, B. anthracis was detected and isolated from mutton, sheepskin, and the transport vehicle, indicating that contamination extended beyond the animal itself to livestock products and environmental surfaces. This pattern is consistent with the known ecology of anthrax and illustrates how a single infected animal may generate multiple exposure routes during informal slaughtering and subsequent handling.

The genomic analyses provided useful support for outbreak investigation, but their interpretation requires caution. All four isolates showed very high genomic similarity and clustered closely in the cgMLST analysis, which was consistent with a common-source outbreak. However, B. anthracis is a highly clonal pathogen with limited short-term sequence variation [19], and therefore even whole-genome approaches may have restricted resolution for distinguishing very closely related strains. For this reason, the genomic results in the present study should be interpreted primarily as supporting evidence for epidemiological linkage rather than as stand-alone proof of the precise direction or timing of transmission [23]. In particular, although the outbreak isolates were genetically related to some previously reported Chinese strains within the same lineage, these relationships alone do not constitute direct evidence of recent interregional transmission.

The assignment of all four isolates to the A.Br.001/002 lineage is in agreement with previous reports showing that this lineage is widely distributed in China [2, 21, 24]. This finding indicates that the present outbreak strain belongs to a recognized domestic lineage rather than representing a distinctly unusual genotype. The value of the present study therefore lies less in identifying a novel lineage than in documenting a well-supported, multi-source outbreak event from a region where genome-based anthrax data remain limited. By integrating isolates from the patient, animal-derived materials, and an environmental source, this study provides a more complete picture of outbreak-associated contamination and reinforces the utility of combining field epidemiology with genomic typing in anthrax investigations.

The detection of major anthrax-associated virulence genes in all four isolates is consistent with their pathogenic potential. However, the virulence- and resistance-associated findings in this study were based on in silico prediction only. No phenotypic assays were performed to confirm toxin expression, capsule production, or antimicrobial susceptibility. Therefore, these genomic findings should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence of phenotypic virulence or clinical drug resistance. Instead, they should be viewed as supportive genomic characteristics that require further experimental validation in future work.

From a public health perspective, the outbreak highlights several practical risks that remain relevant in pastoral settings. First, the slaughter of a moribund animal outside formal veterinary supervision created an opportunity for direct human exposure and environmental contamination. Second, the detection of B. anthracis in livestock products and on the transport vehicle suggests that secondary contamination may occur rapidly during slaughtering, processing, and transport. Third, the history that the sheep had circulated among neighboring villages as breeding stock suggests a plausible mechanism for local spread of infection risk, even though the exact origin of the animal could not be definitively established. Together, these observations emphasize the importance of timely recognition of abnormal livestock deaths, avoidance of private slaughtering of sick animals, and prompt coordination between public health and veterinary authorities.

This study has several limitations. First, it describes a single outbreak involving one confirmed human case, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the exact origin and movement history of the infected sheep could not be fully reconstructed, preventing definitive source tracing. Third, only limited environmental sampling was performed during the outbreak investigation, and no longitudinal follow-up sampling was available to evaluate the persistence or extent of environmental contamination. Fourth, the comparative genomic framework was constrained by the availability and quality of public B. anthracis genomes. Finally, no phenotypic validation was performed for the virulence- or resistance-associated genomic findings. These limitations should be considered when interpreting both the epidemiological and genomic results.

In conclusion, this study documents a cutaneous anthrax outbreak linked to the slaughter of an infected sheep and provides integrated epidemiological and genomic evidence connecting the human case, contaminated livestock products, and the local environment. The findings support the value of combining field investigation with WGS-based typing for anthrax outbreak analysis, while also underscoring the need for cautious interpretation of genomic relatedness in a highly clonal pathogen. Strengthening livestock surveillance, discouraging unsupervised slaughter of sick animals, and improving coordination between human and animal health sectors remain essential measures for reducing anthrax risk in pastoral regions.
