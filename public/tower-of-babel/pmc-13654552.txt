Elucidating nutrient composition and biosynthesis in Macadamia integrifolia through multi-omics profiling across developmental stages
Authors: Guangzheng Guo, Zhuanmiao Kang, Qin Zhang, Qing Shang, Fengping He, Hu Cai, JianJian Geng, Ximei Song, Hui Zeng, Wenlin Wang, Yuhui Niu, Liang Tao, Xinghao Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Macadamia (Macadamia integrifolia), a high-value cash crop, has garnered significant attention due to its rich nutritional profile and unique health benefits. Previous studies have primarily focused on analyzing nutritional components, without systematically elucidating the mechanisms underlying their synthesis during kernel development. To elucidate the molecular and metabolic basis of kernel quality formation, we integrated morphological, nutritional, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses across five developmental stages (T1–T5, 60–190 days after fruit setting). Rapid kernel expansion occurred during T1–T2, and T5 was designated as the mature stage. Compared with T1, fruit diameter and weight at T5 increased by 30% and 82%, respectively, the contents of crude fat, protein, and total amino acids increased by 10%, 30%, and 127%, respectively, whereas soluble sugar content declined by 38%. Transcription factors such as bHLH, NAC, and WRKY, together with auxin- and cytokinin-related genes, were highly expressed during T1–T2, coordinately regulating early cell expansion. Jasmonic acid accumulated markedly during both the T1–T2 period and the T5 stage, coinciding with active oil synthesis. During the T1 stage, serine and glutamate metabolism predominated, laying the foundation for protein and fatty acid biosynthesis. This metabolic program transitioned to a stress-responsive state at the T5 stage, characterized by the accumulation of proline and arginine and increased levels of ERF-family transcription factors, thereby enhancing cellular resilience to late-stage stress. Additionally, citrate and isocitrate levels peaked during oil synthesis, potentially supplying energy and acetyl-CoA for lipid biosynthesis. This study systematically elucidates the dynamic network underlying macadamia kernel development and offers actionable targets for optimizing harvest timing and breeding high-quality cultivars.

Introduction

Macadamia integrifolia, often called the Hawaiian nut, is indigenous to Queensland, Australia, and is a member of the Proteaceae family (Nock et al., 2019). Its distinctive taste and abundant nutritional content make it highly popular with consumers. China has the world's largest cultivation area for this crop, accounting for approximately 60% of the global total (Yang et al., 2025), mainly distributed in Yunnan, Guangxi, Guangdong, and Guizhou (Aquino-Bolaños et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2020). Macadamia kernels are rich in high-quality oils, protein, and vitamins, containing approximately 9% protein, 14% carbohydrates, and minerals including manganese, iron, and magnesium (Du et al., 2010; Wei et al., 2019). High-quality kernels have an oil content of 72% to 78%, with unsaturated fatty acids constituting about 84% of the total oil. Notably, the levels of oleic (60%), arachidonic (16%), and palmitoleic (10%) acid are significantly higher than those in common nuts like walnuts and cashews (Hare et al., 2019; Shuai et al., 2022). Studies indicate that these unsaturated fatty acids help lower serum cholesterol and reduce cardiovascular disease risk (Hare et al., 2019). Additionally, macadamia nuts are rich in amino acids and minerals with anti-inflammatory and blood pressure-regulating effects (Bitok et al., 2018; Hosseini-Bai et al., 2019).

Studies on macadamia up to now have largely centered on tolerance to stress and the breeding of new varieties (Kang et al., 2023; Poudel et al., 2024). Within the nutritional domain, particularly regarding lipids and amino acids, studies have largely emphasized fatty acid composition (Hu et al., 2021) together with the determinants shaping their biosynthesis. As for amino acids, earlier investigations have mainly characterized their composition and levels (Nkosi et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022). Yet the molecular processes that orchestrate the synthesis and accumulation of lipids, as well as amino acid formation, throughout macadamia growth and development are still poorly understood and call for deeper investigation.

High-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) serves as a powerful approach to dissect these mechanisms, including studies on soybean (Lakhssassi et al., 2021), Korean pine seeds (Hao et al., 2025), yaks (Ran et al., 2022), and virus-altered lipid metabolism in hepatocytes (Shiota et al., 2023). In macadamia, a comprehensive germplasm and genome database has been developed (Wang et al., 2022), and integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics have been applied to analyze ascorbic acid biosynthesis (Yang et al., 2024). Niu et al. (2022) identified 187 genes encoding oil biosynthesis enzymes, though regulatory pathways were not addressed. Overall, the precise composition of nutritional substances in macadamia and their corresponding biosynthetic pathways are still not clearly defined.

To address this gap, we conducted integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of macadamia kernels collected at five sequential developmental stages (T1–T5, covering 60 to 190 days after fruit setting), spanning rapid enlargement, lipid deposition, and ripening. This study aimed to characterize the dynamics of key nutritional components, elucidate their biosynthetic pathways, and explore the underlying molecular regulatory networks. The findings provide a theoretical foundation for developing functional foods and breeding high-oil varieties, while also offering novel insights into lipid and amino acid biosynthesis in macadamia.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Macadamia kernels of the ‘Nanya 116’ variety were used in this study. Sampling was conducted in an orchard located in Xingyi, Guizhou Province, China (104°59′36″ E, 24°52′46″ N), at an elevation of approximately 780 m. Kernels were harvested at five developmental stages—60, 95, 125, 155, and 190 days after fruit setting. The period from 0 to 60 days after fruit setting was excluded because kernel tissue was insufficient for multi-omics analyses and storage accumulation was negligible during these early phases. In 2024, three nine-year-old macadamia trees under standard orchard management were selected for sample collection. From the upper-middle, outer part of the canopy, five fruits with intact stems were randomly picked from each of the four cardinal directions (east, west, south, and north), giving a total of 20 fresh fruits per tree. The fruits collected from the three trees were pooled to form one biological replicate consisting of 60 fruits, and three such replicates were collected per sampling event. After being transported to the laboratory, all fruits were shelled, the kernels were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C. All subsequent physiological measurements, transcriptomic profiling, and metabolomic assays were performed using three independent biological replicates, and each measurement was conducted with three technical replicates. For each analysis, the kernel sample was split into two portions: one subset underwent nutritional component determination, while the other was used for transcriptome sequencing and non-targeted metabolomics, with RNA and metabolite extractions carried out separately for each replicate. For ease of reference, the 60, 95, 125, 155, and 190-day stages are designated as T1, T2, T3, T4, and T5, respectively.

Measurement of fruit phenotypic parameters

Fruits at different developmental stages were photographed using a digital camera (Canon EOS 90D, Japan). After the samples were transported to the laboratory, 10 fruits were randomly selected from each sample, and their fresh weight and diameter were measured using an electronic balance and a vernier caliper, respectively.

Determination of major nutritional components

Soluble sugars and total amino acids were assayed with kits purchased from Shang Tongrui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The specific procedures are as follows:

Soluble sugar content was determined using the anthrone method. Briefly, kernel tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in 1 mL distilled water. This homogenate was placed in a centrifuge tube, heated for 10 min in boiling water, cooled, and then spun at 4000 r/min for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was diluted with distilled water to a final volume of 10 mL for subsequent testing. A 1-mL aliquot of the diluted extract was combined with 5 mL anthrone reagent and vortexed thoroughly. The mixture was heated again in boiling water for 10 min, allowed to cool, and its absorbance read at 620 nm. A calibration curve was generated using glucose standards prepared at 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 100 mg/mL. Absorbance for each standard was recorded at 620 nm, employing the blank as reference. The Soluble sugar content (mg/g) was computed via the following formula:

Soluble sugar content (mg/g) = 10 × C × V/W. Here, C represents the concentration derived from the standard curve, V the volume assayed, and W the fresh weight of the sample.

For the quantification of total amino acids, kernel tissue (0.5 g) was weighed and then homogenized in a mortar with 5 mL of 10% acetic acid. The slurry was then diluted to 25 mL with distilled water, mixed thoroughly, and filtered into a conical flask using dry filter paper for subsequent use. A 1 mL aliquot of this filtrate was transferred to a clean 10 mL tube, and 1.0 mL of ninhydrin color reagent was added; the mixture was homogenized. The tube was capped, placed in a boiling water bath for 15–20 min to achieve full color development, and afterwards rapidly chilled to room temperature with cold water. Subsequently, 5.0 mL of 50% ethanol solution was added to each tube to dilute and stabilize the color, followed by thorough mixing. Using a spectrophotometer, the absorbance of the standard solutions was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm, with the blank tube serving as the zero reference. A standard curve was constructed by preparing amino acid standard solutions at 0, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.10 mg/mL; their absorbances at 570 nm were measured against the blank. Total amino acid content (mg/g) was calculated using the formula:

Total amino acid content(mg/g) = 258C × V/W, where C denotes the concentration calculated from the calibration curve, V the measured volume, and W the fresh weight of the sample.

Crude protein and crude fat contents were determined using the Kjeldahl and Soxhlet methods, respectively, in compliance with GB 5009.5–2016 (the national food safety standard for protein) and GB 5009.6–2016 (the corresponding standard for fat).

Metabolite-related analysis

For metabolite extraction, 100 mg of tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen, then suspended in pre-cooled 80% methanol and incubation on ice. Following a 20 min spin at 15,000 ×g and 4 °C, the supernatant was brought to a final methanol concentration of 53% using LC-grade water, then re-centrifuged under identical parameters. The resulting supernatant was retained for analysis by LC-MS/MS (Want et al., 2013). Global metabolic profiling was conducted using UPLC-MS/MS by Wiecom Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). The acquired data were processed via total peak area normalization, and metabolites were identified by matching against a proprietary database. It should be noted that metabolite identification in this study was based on a proprietary database without validation using authentic standards, which represents a limitation of the current metabolomic analysis. Each metabolite's relative abundance was determined from the integrated peak areas of its product ions. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to evaluate overall differences in metabolic profiles among the samples. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified using cutoffs of p < 0.05 and |fold change| > 2 (Huang et al., 2022). Functional annotation of these metabolites was performed with the KEGG, HMDB, and LIPIDMaps databases (all accessed on 28 April 2025).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis process

Total RNA from macadamia kernels was isolated with a kit supplied by Tiangen (Beijing, China). Purity of the RNA was determined on a NanoPhotometer® (IMPLEN, USA), and its integrity was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer together with the Agilent RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit. After quality checks, libraries for sequencing were prepared with the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA), indexed to distinguish individual samples, and then subjected to 150-bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina instrument (e.g., Novaseq or HiSeq). Raw sequencing data were cleaned using fastp software to obtain clean reads for downstream analyses (Chen et al., 2018). We quantified gene expression levels as FPKM values. and an FPKM of 10 or above was taken as the cutoff for high expression. Differential expression analysis was conducted with the DESeq2 R package (version 1.16.1), genes meeting the criteria of adjusted p-value <0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 2 were designated differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Enrichment analysis of GO terms and KEGG pathways for these DEGs was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 3.4.4).

Functional annotation and enrichment of differentially expressed genes

A systematic bioinformatic analysis was conducted to characterize the functions of the DEGs. GO enrichment analysis was extracted using the clusterProfiler R package, with an adjusted p-value <0.05 treated as significant. Simultaneously, we examined KEGG pathway associations for these DEGs through a combined strategy that utilized both the KOBAS repository and the clusterProfiler tool. A corrected p-value <0.05 was uniformly employed as the statistical threshold for all these evaluations.

Gene expression analysis by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

To corroborate the RNA-seq data, we selected six DEGs with elevated transcript levels in the significantly enriched KEGG pathways. The same RNA preparations were applied to both the sequencing and the RT-qPCR verification. For cDNA synthesis, we followed the recommendations of the reverse transcription kit (R223, Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing) to convert the RNA templates into first-strand cDNA. The qPCR mixtures contained β-actin as the endogenous control, and the cycling conditions followed those described by Kan et al. (2021). Relative gene expression was quantified via the 2 − ΔΔCt approach, as detailed by Livak et al. (2001). To ensure experimental robustness, all reactions were run in triplicate biological replicates, and the corresponding primer sequences are given in Table S1 of the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical and data analysis

Microsoft Excel was utilized for initial data handling, specifically to compute averages and standard errors from the replicate measurements. Group differences were tested using one-way ANOVA and Duncan's test (p < 0.05) in SPSS 22.0. All figures and charts were prepared using GraphPad Prism (8.0.2 or 9.4.1). Metabolomic profiles underwent PCA via the web-based platform at https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/. In addition, PCA was carried out on both transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets to inspect overall variance and the clustering of replicates. Heatmaps and integrated multi-omics analyses were visualized with TBtools 1.068.

Results

Analysis of macadamia kernels morphological characteristics

Observations across stages T1 to T5 captured the complete developmental progression of the kernel. At T1 (60 days after fruit set), the kernel was in its early developmental phase, characterized by small size, preliminary morphology, and a light green endocarp (Fig. 1a). By T2 (95 days after fruit set), kernel volume had increased significantly, indicating a period of rapid expansion, internal structures continued to mature, and endocarp lignification had begun (Fig. 1b). At T3 (125 days after fruit set), the kernel approached its mature size, with a largely established morphology and evident endocarp lignification (Fig. 1c). During T4–T5 (155–190 days after fruit set), the kernel reached or approached its final size. At T5, the kernel became plump and attained full maturity, and the endocarp was fully lignified, forming a hard shell (Fig. 1d-e). As maturity advanced, nut weight increased progressively, by T5, it had increased by 82% compared with T1. Transcriptome analysis of macadamia kernels at different developmental stages

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on macadamia kernel samples across developmental stages T1 to T5. After quality control, clean reads ranged from 42.57 to 72.88 million per stage, while the Q20 and Q30 rates surpassed 99.52% and 97.44%, respectively (Table S2). The mapping rates to the macadamia reference genome ranged from 84.10% to 89.80%. Through this alignment, 30,676 known genes were identified (80.13% of the total, i.e., 30,676/38,284), along with 1483 novel genes not previously included in the reference genome. Among the 1483 novel genes, a small number were identified as differentially expressed across developmental stages. PCA demonstrated that the two primary components, PC1 and PC2, accounted for 28.60% and 16.49% of the variance, respectively. The five sample groups (T1 to T5) displayed distinct clustering patterns with clear separation between groups, while samples within each group were relatively homogeneous, indicating good reproducibility (Fig. 3a). Correlation heatmap analysis further revealed high consistency among biological replicates at each stage (R2 > 0.76), confirming the stability and reliability of the transcriptomic data (Fig. 3b). To verify the transcriptome data and gene expression profiles, six genes whose expression changed substantially were subjected to RT-qPCR. The resulting qPCR values matched closely with those from the transcriptomic analysis (Fig. S1), thereby confirming data reliability and providing robust evidence for the earlier analyses.Fig. 3Transcriptome sequencing results. Metabolomic analysis of macadamia kernels at different developmental stages

Metabolomic analysis was conducted on 15 macadamia kernel samples across different developmental stages (Fig. 6). PCA revealed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 28.1% and 17.2% of the total variation, with distinct clustering among the different stages, thereby supporting the reliability of our metabolomic data (Fig. 6a). Across the entire sample set, we detected 2330 distinct metabolites in total (Fig. 6b). Among these, 75 metabolites possess well-defined biological roles. The biologically active metabolites are primarily distributed across seven major classes: peptides (19 species), lipids (17 species), carbohydrates (10 species), steroids (9 species), organic acids (8 species), nucleic acids (6 species), and vitamins and cofactors (6 species) (Fig. 6c). Further analysis of amino acids, peptides, and analogues identified a total of 304 metabolites, among which amino acids and their derivatives accounted for 165 types, covering all essential amino acids required by the human body. Integrated analysis of differentially expressed genes and differential metabolites

Following pathway enrichment analysis of the transcriptomic and metabolomic data, we identified a total of 13 pathways that were significantly enriched across both omics datasets (Table S5). Several of these pathways are closely associated with amino acid metabolism, underscoring the central role of amino acid metabolism during fruit development. In addition to amino acid-related pathways, carbon metabolism and α-linolenic acid metabolism, both of which are essential for controlling unsaturated fatty acid conversion and breakdown, also showed significant enrichment across the two omics datasets.

Plant hormone pathway co-analysis

In the transcriptomic analysis, hormone signaling pathways were significantly enriched across the T1–T5 developmental stages, revealing a synergistic regulatory network of plant hormones during macadamia kernel development. Notably, different hormones precisely regulated key biological processes of kernel development through specific spatiotemporal expression patterns: auxin and cytokinin acted primarily synergistically during early fruit development, jointly promoting cell division and tissue expansion. In the middle and late phases of kernel development, abscisic acid (ABA) is a dominant regulator of lipid storage and the triggering of maturation, while jasmonic acid (JA) functions throughout the entire developmental process, particularly in flavor compound formation and stress responses. Based on these findings, this study conducted a systematic analysis of key hormone pathways and their core gene families. The results (Fig.8) showed that during the early rapid growth stage (T1), genes closely associated with auxin signal transduction-including the auxin influx carrier AUX1, the AUX/IAA repressor family, and SAUR growth-responsive genes-exhibited high expression levels. During the stages of material accumulation and maturation, the ABA content increased markedly, coinciding with rapid lipid synthesis (T2) and the onset of maturation (T3 and T4). Key genes in the ABA signaling pathway, including the ABA receptors PYR/PYL, the negative regulator PP2C, and ABF transcription factors, were significantly up-regulated during these corresponding stages. This expression pattern aligned closely with the physiological functions of ABA in promoting oil synthesis, regulating dehydration and maturation, and enhancing storage tolerance. Additionally, genes associated with the JA pathway, such as JAR1, the receptor COI1, the repressor JAZ, and the transcription factor MYC2, exhibited expression peaks during both the active lipid synthesis stages (T1 and T2) and the late maturation stage (T5). This expression profile suggested that JA not only participated in the regulation of lipid metabolism but might also, by influencing secondary metabolic pathways, coordinately regulate the formation of nut-specific flavor compounds and contribute to stress resistance and storage stability during the later maturation phase (Fig. 8).

Amino acid metabolic co-analysis

This study revealed that the rapid expansion phase of the kernel (T1 stage) was accompanied by a marked increase in amino acids involved in protein synthesis and lipid metabolism, most notably serine, glutamate, methionine, threonine, and isoleucine. These amino acids served dual roles: providing raw materials for rapid protein synthesis and supporting vigorous metabolic activity by acting as lipid precursors or signaling molecules. In close agreement with this metabolic profile, transcript levels of essential genes responsible for synthesizing and converting these amino acids reached their maximum values at the same time. For instance, serA and serC are genes that encode principal enzymes within the serine synthesis route, GOT1 associated with amino acid transamination, glyA involved in glycine synthesis, as well as ItaE and metE responsible for isoleucine and methionine synthesis, all exhibited the highest expression levels during the T1 stage. This molecular evidence confirms the active nitrogen metabolism and robust biosynthetic demand characteristic of this phase.

With the substantial completion of reserve accumulation at the T5 stage, the kernel's primary physiological objective shifted from rapid growth to maintaining homeostasis and coping with environmental stress. Correspondingly, the amino acid metabolic profile underwent a marked shift, characterized by the pronounced activation of stress-responsive pathways. In particular, proline and arginine levels were substantially elevated at T5. Proline, which acts as both an osmolyte and a quencher of reactive oxygen species, helps maintain cellular homeostasis and mitigate environmental challenges such as drought and chilling. Integrated analysis of carbon metabolism

In this study, integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed a significant up-regulation of carbon metabolism pathways during macadamia kernel development. Notably, at the T5 stage, the key TCA cycle intermediates citrate and isocitrate accumulated markedly, reaching levels 5.03- and 1.35-fold higher, respectively, than those at the initial stage. Concurrently, the expression of their rate-limiting enzyme genes, aceB (encoding malate synthase) and ICL (isocitrate lyase), increased by approximately 54,308-fold and 44,467.64-fold, respectively, indicating a strong activation of the glyoxylate cycle. Fatty acid metabolism co-analysis

Our findings demonstrate that fatty acid metabolism in macadamia kernels follows the classical de novo biosynthesis pathway: initiated from acetyl-CoA, the carbon chain is progressively extended through iterative cycles of condensation, reduction, dehydration, and additional reduction, all catalyzed by distinct enzyme systems. As macadamia nuts progressed from T1 to T5, the relative levels of key fatty acids rose substantially, as revealed by metabolite profiling across these stages. This pattern directly confirms the continuous synthesis and accumulation of oils during the kernel's later development stages. At the molecular level, expression levels of key fatty acid biosynthesis genes were concurrently up-regulated from T1 to T5, aligning closely with the observed metabolite accumulation. These genes included acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA), the Fab gene family responsible for carbon chain elongation, and the acyl-ACP thioesterase genes (FATA and FATB), which terminate synthesis and release fatty acids. Discussion

Effects of plant hormones on the development and quality of macadamia kernels

Macadamia is an important nut crop increasingly valued worldwide for its kernel flavor and nutritional value (Rogiers et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2022). Nut weight and diameter serve as key external indicators of maturity (Vinson et al., 2010). In this study, both parameters increased progressively with advancing maturity, consistent with the rapid expansion around 6 weeks post-anthesis previously reported in macadamia (De et al., 2022). At the molecular level, auxin-related genes (AUX1, AUX, SAUR) were highly expressed during T1, while cytokinin-related genes (AHP, A-ARR, B-ARR) were highly expressed at T1 and T2 (Fig. 8). This pattern supports the crucial roles of auxin and cytokinin in early fruit development, cell division, and expansion (Liu et al., 2018; Sutton et al., 2023). Furthermore, ABA content increased during the oil accumulation (T2) and maturity initiation (T3 and T4) stages. Consistent with this, ABA-related genes (PYR/PYL, PP2C, ABF) were highly expressed from T2 to T4. The importance of these regulatory pathways is underscored by studies showing that induced β-oxidation in mutants such as hisnaa can break down storage oils and reduce oil content.

ABA mediates the regulation of His in β-oxidation, counteracting its effect and thereby promoting oil accumulation (Ma et al., 2016). Furthermore, ABA accelerates ethylene synthesis, which participates in fruit ripening (Gan et al., 2020; Kou et al., 2021). Beyond ABA and ethylene, jasmonic acid-related genes (JAR1, CoI1, JAZ, MYC2) also showed high expression levels during oil synthesis (T1, T2) and nut maturation (T5) stages. Earlier investigations have suggested that jasmonic acid enhances oil accumulation by up-regulating the activities of key enzymes involved in fatty acid metabolism. Jasmonates also regulate multiple hormonal activities during fruit development, activate ripening-related genes, and influence the entire growth process from fruit set to maturity (Chen et al., 2022; Shil et al., 2025).

Earlier investigations have suggested that jasmonic acid enhances oil accumulation by up-regulating the activities of key enzymes involved in fatty acid metabolism. Jasmonates also modulate multiple hormonal pathways during fruit development, activate ripening-related genes, and influence the entire growth process from fruit set to maturity (Chen et al., 2022; Shil et al., 2025). Thus, the elevated expression of jasmonic acid-related genes during T1, T2, and T5 likely supports both oil synthesis and nut maturation. At the transcriptional level, key transcription factor families such as bHLH, MYB, NAC, and WRKY are widely recognized as important regulators of growth and development.

This study found that transcription factors belonging to the bHLH, NAC, and WRKY families were particularly highly expressed during the T1 and T2 stages, suggesting their potential involvement in early morphogenesis (Xing et al., 2025). In contrast, ERF and MYB family genes showed higher expression during the T4–T5 stages. Supporting this, representative genes from the bHLH, ERF, and MYB families showed distinct stage-specific expression patterns. Three bHLH genes (LOC122062105 and LOC122077212) were up-regulated at T1 and then continuously down-regulated, consistent with a role in early developmental processes. Two ERF genes (LOC122087137 and LOC122087560) were down-regulated during T1–T2 but gradually up-regulated from T3 to T5, coinciding with the transition from active growth to maturation. Two MYB genes (LOC122058827 and LOC122075723) were down-regulated at T1–T3 and up-regulated at T4–T5, suggesting their involvement in later developmental events. These stage-specific expression patterns provide gene-level evidence supporting the potential regulatory roles of these transcription factor families during kernel development. Notably, the elevated expression of ERF transcription factors during later developmental stages parallels the greater physiological vulnerability of maturing fruits, given that these factors are established to augment abiotic stress tolerance through the regulation of downstream stress-responsive genes, which aligns with the physiological vulnerability of fruits to environmental stress during maturation and represents a critical adaptive mechanism enabling the maturing nut to withstand environmental fluctuations (Su et al., 2025). However, that these inferences are primarily based on temporal expression correlations, and their regulatory targets and pathways remain to be experimentally validated in future studies.

The impact of amino acid metabolic pathways on the development and quality of macadamia kernels

Amino acids and proteins are crucial for the development of macadamia kernels (Tiwari et al., 2017). Consistent with this, we observed a continuous increase in both crude protein and total amino acid contents from T1 to T5, suggesting a sustained investment in nutrient reserves throughout development. During the rapid expansion phase (T1 stage), amino acids closely related to protein synthesis and lipid metabolism, such as serine, glutamate, methionine, threonine, and isoleucine, were notably abundant, and their corresponding biosynthetic genes (e.g., serA, serC, GOT1) were also significantly up-regulated. These amino acids served dual roles: as direct substrates for protein synthesis, underpinning cell division and tissue construction (Shu et al., 2025; Stolarz et al., 2025), and as vital sources of carbon skeletons and reducing power for fatty acid metabolism. For instance, serine provided methyl donors for lipid synthesis via one‑carbon metabolism (Newman et al., 2017), while glutamate was converted to α-ketoglutarate, feeding the TCA cycle to supply carbon and energy for de novo fatty acid synthesis (Zhang et al., 2024). Thus, active amino acid metabolism acted as a key engine, driving protein and lipid synthesis to promote kernel morphogenesis and dry matter accumulation at T1. In contrast, a distinct metabolic shift occurred at maturation (T5), characterized by increased levels of proline and arginine, elevated ornithine (a precursor), and up-regulation of the key biosynthetic gene argE. Proline acted as a compatible solute and reactive oxygen species scavenger, effectively maintaining cellular osmotic balance and protecting macromolecular structures (Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), while arginine served as a polyamine precursor, delaying senescence and enhancing stress resistance (Lou et al., 2020; Wojtyla et al., 2024). The concerted accumulation of both amino acids during the T5 stage thus represented an adaptive strategy to mitigate oxidative stress and pathogen threats late in development. In summary, the amino acid metabolic network played a dynamic regulatory role in macadamia kernel development: initially focused on supporting biosynthesis and energy reserve accumulation, it later shifted toward promoting maturation stability and stress resistance. These findings deepen our understanding of nut quality formation and provide a theoretical basis for optimizing nutritional composition or postharvest tolerance through metabolic regulation.

The impact of fatty acid metabolism on the development and quality of macadamia kernels

Fatty acids, key nutritional components in nuts, are broadly categorized as saturated or unsaturated (Lim et al., 2024; Vecka et al., 2019). In this study, the crude fat content in macadamia kernels increased progressively with maturation, rising by approximately 10% from T1 to T5, indicating that lipid accumulation is a central metabolic process during later kernel development. Soluble sugars serve as crucial carbon sources and energy precursors for fatty acid synthesis. Consistent with this role, soluble sugars were present at higher levels during the T1–T3 stages, when lipid synthesis was active, and declined in later stages. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that all significantly enriched pathways were related to carbon metabolism. Specifically, key TCA cycle intermediates, citrate and isocitrate, accumulated markedly, while their synthesis-related genes, aceB and ICL, were upregulated. These metabolites could be cleaved by ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) to generate acetyl-CoA, thereby linking glycolysis to lipid biosynthesis and releasing energy (Bader et al., 2025). Within the fatty acid metabolism pathway, multiple steps downstream of acetyl-CoA were upregulated. The high expression of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA), the elongase Fab gene family, and the chain-terminating thioesterase genes (FATA and FATB) collectively drove the synthesis and and buildup of saturated fatty acids with medium and short chains (decanoic acid, lauric acid, and palmitic acid) as well as the monounsaturated palmitoleic acid. These results reveal a coordinated upregulation of ACACA, Fab genes, and FATA/FATB, constituting the transcriptional basis for palmitoleic acid biosynthesis during macadamia kernel development. Notably, the accumulated palmitic acid and lauric acid are major components of palm oil (Kim et al., 2022; Mancini et al., 2015). Additionally, oleic acid content peaked at T5, providing a precursor pool for the synthesis of downstream unsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic acid (Tang et al., 2024).In summary, lipid synthesis is tightly coupled with carbon metabolism, which supplies carbon and energy and ultimately generates acetyl-CoA via TCA cycle intermediates. Acetyl-CoA then serves as the substrate for a series of enzymatic reactions that produce various fatty acids, thereby driving efficient oil accumulation in macadamia kernels. It should be noted that the proposed “TCA cycle–glyoxylate cycle coupling” model is primarily inferred from transcriptomic and metabolomic correlations rather than from direct measurements of enzyme activities or metabolic fluxes. Although the coordinated upregulation of aceB and ICL, together with the accumulation of citrate and isocitrate, provides strong correlative evidence for glyoxylate cycle activation, direct validation through enzymatic assays or isotope-labeled metabolic flux analysis will be required to confirm this model. Future studies incorporating these approaches will be essential to establish the causal role of this metabolic remodeling in macadamia kernel development.

The findings of this study also have practical implications for improving macadamia kernel quality. The identification of T2–T4 as the critical window for lipid synthesis suggests that optimized nutrient management during this period could enhance oil accumulation. Huang et al. (2026) analyzed 429 macadamia kernel samples and found that phosphorus content had a highly significant positive effect on oil content, while nitrogen and potassium were positively correlated with protein content. Harvest timing could also be adjusted based on these metabolic milestones, as kernel maturity directly affects oil content and quality (Tsvakai et al., 2020). For postharvest management, the elucidation of key metabolic nodes involved in fatty acid accumulation provides a basis for refining storage conditions. Low-temperature storage (4 °C) has been shown to effectively maintain kernel quality by preserving lipid stability and delaying oxidative rancidity (Guo et al., 2021). For molecular breeding, candidate genes identified in this study, including ACACA, the Fab gene family, and FATA/FATB, represent promising targets for marker-assisted selection or genetic improvement. Niu et al. (2022) cloned the MiKASII gene from macadamia kernels, demonstrating the feasibility of gene-based approaches to study fatty acid biosynthesis.

In addition to these core pathways, our transcriptome analysis also identified ` novel genes that were not previously annotated in the macadamia reference genome. Among them, a small subset was differentially expressed during kernel development. However, these novel DEGs were not enriched in the pathways examined in this study, which may be attributed to their limited functional annotation, involvement in other biological processes not investigated here, or potential regulatory roles not readily captured by conventional pathway enrichment analysis. Future functional characterization of these novel genes may provide further insights into macadamia kernel development and quality formation.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidated the morphological, nutritional, and metabolic dynamics underlying macadamia kernel development. Three core findings emerged from our integrated multi-omics analysis. First, kernel development progressed through three functional stages: the early expansion stage (T1–T2) was characterized by rapid increases in fruit diameter and weight, coordinated by auxin and cytokinin signaling and the high expression of bHLH and WRKY transcription factors; the oil synthesis stage (T2–T4) was marked by active lipid accumulation, driven by abscisic acid signaling and the coordinated up-regulation of ACACA, the Fab gene family, and FATA/FATB; and the maturation stage (T5) was defined by stress-responsive adaptation, with up-regulated ERF and MYB family genes and the accumulation of proline and arginine for osmotic regulation and stress resistance. Second, amino acid metabolism underwent a functional shift during kernel development. At early stages (T1–T3), serine and glutamate metabolism actively supported protein and fatty acid biosynthesis, whereas at the late maturation stage (T5), metabolic flux shifted toward the accumulation of proline and arginine, which enhanced osmotic regulation and stress resistance. Third, lipid biosynthesis was tightly coupled with carbon metabolism. During the oil synthesis stage, the accumulation of citrate and isocitrate provided acetyl-CoA and energy for fatty acid metabolism. We propose a “TCA cycle–glyoxylate cycle coupling” model, in which the glyoxylate cycle may enhance the directional conversion of carbon sources into storage lipids while maintaining energy homeostasis during high-energy-demand developmental stages. These findings identified key metabolic nodes from cell expansion to maturation and provided a theoretical foundation for improving nut nutritional quality, fatty acid composition, and postharvest characteristics through cultivation practices or molecular breeding. Future research should focus on quantifying carbon and nitrogen fluxes in key metabolic pathways and elucidating their upstream transcriptional regulatory networks.
