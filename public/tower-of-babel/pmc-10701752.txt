Radiofrequency thermocoagulation for the treatment of trigeminal neuralgia associated with a focal pontine lesion: illustrative case
Authors: Vadym Biloshytsky, Anna Skorokhoda, Inna Buvailo, Maryna Biloshytska
Published: 2023

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is a debilitating neurological condition with brief attacks of facial pain restricted to the trigeminal distribution and with an electric shock-like shooting, stabbing, or sharp quality. TN-associated pain is one of the most severe pains known, often referred to as “suicidal,” and is triggered by innocuous stimulation of the face and intraoral mucosa such as touching the face, talking, chewing, drinking, washing the face, shaving, etc.1,2

TN etiology can be classified as classic, secondary, or idiopathic. The classic type accounts for 75% of cases and is diagnosed when there is nerve root atrophy and/or displacement due to trigeminal neurovascular compression (NVC) identified by imaging or intraoperatively. Secondary TN is most closely associated with multiple sclerosis (MS), mass lesions, or arteriovenous malformations. The presence of a demyelinating lesion at the trigeminal root entry zone (REZ) or in the pons affecting the intrapontine primary afferents is a diagnostic criterion of TN attributed to MS (MS-TN). Idiopathic TN is diagnosed when no underlying disease can be found.3

The first-line treatment for TN is pharmacological, with carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, gabapentin, pregabalin, botulinum toxin type A, and/or baclofen used to prevent TN attacks. Surgery should be considered if the pain is poorly controlled, or the medical treatments are poorly tolerated. Trigeminal microvascular decompression (MVD) removes the NVC and is the surgery of first choice in classic TN.4 Other surgical treatment options include percutaneous neuroablative techniques and Gamma Knife stereotactic surgery. The aforementioned techniques are performed by passing a cannula through the foramen ovale, followed by lesioning of the trigeminal ganglion or root by the application of heat (radiofrequency thermocoagulation or RFTC), mechanical ablation (balloon compression), or chemical (glycerol) rhizolysis. Ablative techniques can be effective in various forms of TN, for example, secondary TN, including those cases associated with pontine MS lesions.1,4,5

TN associated with a focal pontine lesion that does not meet the diagnostic criteria for MS is a rare condition. A 2020 study6 reported on a rare sample of 24 patients with TN presenting with a single pontine non-MS lesion, noting that only a total of 13 such cases had been previously reported. The authors stressed that those patients’ conditions were refractory to conservative treatment and MVD. Currently, no recommendations exist for managing such patients or selecting the best surgical option for their condition.

We present a case of successful surgical treatment of TN associated with a focal pontine lesion by RFTC.

Illustrative Case

A 36-year-old female patient presented, complaining of 10- to 20-second attacks of electric shock-like shooting pain in the right cheek and cheekbone area, above the upper lip. The attacks could occur frequently throughout a 1- to 2-hour period and were triggered by talking, chewing, and brushing the teeth, and their frequency and severity were minimally improved with high-dose carbamazepine. The pain had started 3.5 years earlier with no inciting incident.

Two weeks after disease onset, the patient visited a local neurologist. Neurological examination was unremarkable (normal sensation and no pain on percussion of trigeminal nerve exit points), except some pain on bilateral temporomandibular joint (TMJ) palpation. The patient was diagnosed with TN. Throughout the disease course, her carbamazepine dose gradually increased from 200 to 1,600 mg/d, but she became less responsive to high-dose carbamazepine treatment over time.

Two months after disease onset, the patient was referred to an orthodontist and diagnosed with TMJ dysfunction. She received facial muscle kinesiotherapy, which resulted in symptom regression over months, but thereafter the pain returned to pretreatment levels. She continued visiting orthodontists, and they tried treating her by having her wear gumshields and bracket systems. This did not improve the condition but could be a trigger for attacks.

Brain 1.5-T MRI was done. Sequences included axial two-dimensional T2-weighted turbo-spin echo (TSE), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), sagittal three-dimensional T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) with a 1-mm isotropic resolution, and precontrast and postcontrast axial three-dimensional T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) with a 1-mm isotropic resolution. At the junction of the right pons and the right middle cerebellar peduncle, in the area of the right pontine trigeminal pathway (PTP), MRI revealed an elongated lesion hyperintense on T2-weighted TSE and T2-FLAIR imaging and hypointense and nonenhancing on T1-MPRAGE (Fig. 1). The lesion showed no signs of restricted diffusion on the DWI sequence (no MRI signs of acute stroke) and no hemorrhage or supposed malformation on the SWI sequence. In addition, the MRI showed focal T2-FLAIR–hyperintense and T1-MPRAGE–hypointense lesions in the corpus callosum and left thalamus, with no restricted diffusion or contrast enhancement (Fig. 2). Comparison of the patient’s brain MRI with that performed 2 years earlier showed that all lesions were stable, and no new lesions were seen.

Axial MRI demonstrating a lesion (white arrows) at the junction of the pons and the right middle cerebellar peduncle: T2-FLAIR (A), T2-weighted TSE (B), precontrast T1-MPRAGE (C), and postcontrast T1-MPRAGE (D). DWI (E) shows no restricted diffusion. SWI (F) shows neither hemorrhagic inclusion nor supposed malformation.

MRI demonstrating a lesion in the corpus callosum (white arrows) and another lesion in the left thalamus (black arrows): T2-FLAIR (A–C) and postcontrast T1-MPRAGE (D–F).

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination was unremarkable, with no oligoclonal bands.

The patient underwent RFTC of the right trigeminal ganglion. The procedure was performed with the patient supine and under intravenous propofol sedation. Under aseptic conditions, the skin was infiltrated with 1% lidocaine. A 20-gauge radiofrequency cannula was placed into the foramen ovale under fluoroscopic guidance, and lateral fluoroscopy showed the needle tip advancing to the clivus. After the stylet was removed, CSF leakage was noted, and the stylet was replaced by a radiofrequency electrode. Sensory stimulation at 50 Hz (1-msec pulse width, 0- to 0.2-V amplitude) was performed to generate paresthesia in a particular dermatome and identify the second trigeminal division. The lesion was made at 80°C for 90 seconds.

Pain attacks ceased, and hypoesthesia developed in the maxillary trigeminal distribution after the procedure. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient reported no recurrence.

Discussion

Observations

TN Secondary to a Focal Pontine Lesion: A Special Clinical Entity

TN associated with a focal pontine non-MS lesion is a rare condition. Opinions vary regarding the origin of this type of lesion, which does not exclude variation in its etiology. Kim and Yu7 described a case of dorsolateral pontine ischemic infarction involving the cisternal segment of the trigeminal nerve adjacent to the REZ. The clinical picture included decreased facial sensation in the V2 and V3 distributions and brief facial electric shock-like pain triggered by light stimuli. Thin-slice 3-T MRI performed 10 days after disease onset demonstrated a subacute ischemic lesion in the aforementioned location with contrast enhancement in the REZ. The symptoms resolved over 3 months with pharmacological treatment, and follow-up MRI after 13 months showed a trigeminal pathway lesion described as chronic ischemic. The authors concluded that pontine infarction should be considered a rare cause of TN. They noted that trigeminal nerve demyelination with ephaptic transmission and a reduced inhibitory function of the trigeminal nucleus were presumed to be involved in the development of TN.

Other authors8 reported on a male who, shortly after a myocardial infarction, awoke with hemifacial numbness, which resolved over 9 months. Brain imaging was not performed at that time. Approximately a year after disease onset, he began to experience lancinating facial pain in the left VI and V2 distributions. Symptoms could be triggered by chewing or tactile stimuli and were poorly responsive to carbamazepine. MRI demonstrated an 8 × 4 mm wedge-shaped lesion adjacent to the trigeminal REZ. The lesion was nonenhancing and hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, consistent with encephalomalacia from an old infarction. In the authors’ opinion, this patient’s pontine lesion most likely represented a small embolic stroke of a perforating pontine vessel.8 There are some more isolated case reports of TN developing after ischemic pontine lesions.9–14

Others have pointed to an association of pontine lesions with TN but have not associated them with brain infarction. Arrese et al.15 reviewed 68 cases of non-MS-TN and found that four of them showed hyperintense lesions in the dorsolateral pons on T2-weighted MRI sequences. They noted that although the nature of these lesions remains unknown, demyelination or ischemia is the most likely cause. The efficacy of the medications recommended for TN was limited in the 4 cases. Chang et al.16 described two cases of typical TN in the presence of linear or wedge-shaped lesions along the PTP and postulated that the lesions resulted from an old viral neuritis. Neetu et al.17 described four patients with TN without NVC who showed focal nonenhancing T2-hyperintense lesions in the PTP. They also used diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to evaluate these lesions. No other lesions were evident on MRI to suggest a definitive diagnosis of MS. The mean fractional anisotropy value on the affected side was significantly lower, and the mean apparent diffusion coefficient value, significantly higher on the affected side, indicated focal demyelination of the trigeminal pathway.

A group of Toronto researchers coined the term “trigeminal neuralgia associated with a solitary pontine lesion (SPL),” or “SPL-TN,” in 2020.6 They described a separate sample of 24 patients who had clinical characteristics of TN and received medications and neurosurgical treatment as per guidelines. The patients exhibited the following common features: an SPL and no other brain lesions, no response to trigeminal MVD, lesions distributed along the affected PTP, and microstructural properties of the lesions that were significantly different from MS plaques based on DTI metrics and tractography. The lesions showed radiological evidence of abnormal white-matter microstructure, with lower fractional anisotropy and higher mean radial and axial diffusivities compared with the unaffected side and characteristics of MS plaques. The authors6 underlined the appropriateness of considering SPL-TN as a new clinical entity based on the following criteria: 1) diagnosis of idiopathic TN according to the third edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders and 2) the presence of a single brainstem lesion along the trigeminal nerve pathway and no other supratentorial or infratentorial lesions.

A more recent report18 on a case of SPL-TN highlights the rarity of the condition. The MRI characteristics of the lesion, along with the presence of oligoclonal bands in the CSF, suggested a demyelinating etiology, allowing the authors18 to describe an SPL as a possible manifestation of solitary sclerosis (SS), a rare condition that has been described by others.19,20

In our patient’s case, the supratentorial and infratentorial brain lesions detected required clarification and meticulous clinical, MRI, and laboratory investigation to exclude or confirm a demyelinating disease (MS). Periventricular and subcortical lesions typical for MS were not seen. Furthermore, the patient had stable MRI findings with no new lesions (no dissemination in time) over 2 years of follow-up, which reduced the possibility of MS. The lesion at the junction of the right pons and the right middle cerebellar peduncle could be considered separately as a manifestation of SS, and its vascular origin (due to a possible previous history of ischemic stroke) or inflammatory origin could not be excluded. Moreover, the lesions in the corpus callosum and left thalamus could be nonspecific for MS and possibly attributed to gliosis, a manifestation of cerebral angiopathy, or an incidental finding. The above MRI characteristics along with CSF examination findings provided no definitive evidence of MS or SS defined by the 2017 McDonald diagnostic criteria.21 Follow-up visits of the patient will include assessment for the evidence of MS or SS. In addition, our patient’s case may not be categorized as SPL-TN based on the criteria by Tohyama et al.6 given the presence of the above supratentorial lesions. Therefore, the origin of the three aforementioned lesions in this case is still to be determined.

Controversies Regarding the Selection of a Surgical Approach to TN

The selection of a surgical option for TN needs to be clarified. Currently, there are no comprehensive guidelines for the management of TN. At least in Europe, patients with TN are managed by many different specialties including neurology, neurosurgery, anesthesiology, dentistry, and family medicine.22 Neurosurgeons often prefer MVD for TN because the technique has become a routine part of the neurosurgical armamentarium.6 The management of this condition requires a patient-tailored approach, although the surgeon’s experience or the patient’s preference are often still decisive in the selection of a surgical option.23 A telling example in this regard is the surgical treatment of MS-TN. Montano et al.22 reported that MVD often remains an option for MS-TN despite the absence of clear indications for this procedure in such patients. This is caused by the lack of a full understanding of the complex pathogenesis of TN in MS and results in improper selection of patients for different surgical procedures and, therefore, a worse prognosis after surgery.22 A recent treatment comparison study24 concluded that percutaneous procedures (glycerol rhizolysis or balloon compression), but not MVD, provided acceptable outcome and complication rates and should be offered to the majority of patients with MS-TN who need surgery. MVD should only be considered in patients with pontine MS plaques when they have an NVC.4,24

The neuroimaging indices to be considered while selecting the surgical option most appropriate for a patient with TN also need to be clarified. Wang et al.25 indicated that NVC is not a pathognomonic feature of TN and can be found in 25% to 49% of healthy individuals and 14% to 39% of cadavers with no history of TN. They believed that neuroimaging assessment in TN should not be restricted to verifying NVC and its severity. They also used quantitative imaging techniques to evaluate trigeminal nerve volume and whole-brain gray matter volume and found that a decreased volume of the ipsilateral trigeminal nerve drove gray matter volume abnormality in the left insula and further led to increased pain ratings in patients with primary TN.

In patients with MS-TN, pontine demyelinating lesions along the PTP are a negative prognostic factor for pain relief after MVD.22 Given the role of pontine MS and non-MS lesions in the genesis of TN, we believe that preoperative neuroimaging should also assess for these lesions.

Surgery for TN Associated With a Focal Pontine Lesion: Advantages of Percutaneous Ablative Techniques

There are also scant reports on the surgical treatment of TN associated with a pontine non-MS lesion. Glycerol rhizotomy provided relief of pain in a case of poststroke TN refractory to medical treatment.8 Of the four cases of TN associated with a pontine lesion, the two that underwent an ablative procedure (percutaneous microcompression of the gasserian ganglion or partial trigeminal rhizotomy), but not two others that underwent MVD, experienced pain relief after surgery.15

Foroohar et al.26 reported on two successful cases of RFTC for TN associated with a pontine lesion. Both patients had a clinical picture typical of TN with T2-weighted MRI findings of hyperintense pontine lesions, which the authors believed to be consistent with infarction. These patients, however, had neither a history of stroke nor neurological findings of the loss of trigeminal nerve function (normal sensation to pinprick and light touch, corneal reflexes, jaw jerk reflex, and no masseter muscle weakness). In both cases aggressive pharmacological management failed, but after RFTC, symptoms did not recur.

Chang et al.16 reported on two cases of TN associated with a focal pontine lesion successfully treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery. Shanker et al.14 presented a case of medically refractory TN treated with neuronavigated pontine descending tractotomy. Although the patient did not have radiographic evidence of NVC in his most recent studies, retrospective review of imaging revealed a T2 hyperintensity located at the dorsolateral pontomedullary junction, likely secondary to chronic cerebral small vessel disease.

Although the reports are scant on different surgical techniques for TN associated with a pontine lesion, one can see that ablative (particularly, percutaneous) procedures tend to have greater efficacy than a traditional neurosurgical procedure of trigeminal MVD.

Mechanism of Action and Possible Associated Risks of RFTC

The mechanism of action of percutaneous ablative techniques (e.g., RFTC) is briefly discussed below. Both peripheral and central pathophysiological theories have been proposed to explain the development of pain in TN.15 According to the latter theory, it is the nucleus that suffers secondary changes due to injury to the nerve, generating impulses able to induce pain. The peripheral theory posits that pain results from lesions located before the entry of the sensory fibers into the trigeminal nucleus. After entering the brainstem, trigeminal sensory fibers run down in parallel to the trigeminospinal nucleus, forming the PTP. Ephaptic transmission or generation of abnormal impulses in areas of damaged myelin may cause innocuous stimuli to abnormally activate neighboring pain fibers.8 Foroohar et al.21 suggest that RFTC may be effective by decreasing the peripheral afferent input into the spinal trigeminal nucleus. We believe that because RFTC is performed peripherally to a lesion presumed to cause the symptoms and TN pain is triggered by external stimuli, it is by a decrease in the peripheral sensory input that the effect of percutaneous techniques may be explained within the framework of the peripheral theory.

The most common adverse effects and complications of RFTC for TN are facial numbness and masticatory muscle weakness,27 whereas ptosis, keratitis, corneal ulcers, diplopia, abducens nerve damage, transient vision loss and blindness, hearing loss, CSF leakage, and death are rarely seen.28 RFTC is, however, advantageous to MVD in terms of the risk of serious postprocedural complications and presents an effective, safe, and more cost-effective alternative to other surgical treatment options for patients with TN, especially those over 70 and with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification system class II or higher.27

Study Limitations

The major limitation of this report is that it is based on a single case. To date, there are no randomized controlled trials comparing the effectiveness of MVD with percutaneous ablative techniques for different forms of TN.5

Lessons

TN associated with a focal pontine lesion is a rare but challenging condition. The origin of the lesions, which do not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for MS, remains disputable. Pain in such a condition is often refractory to conventional treatment including MVD. However, as in other forms of nonclassic TN (e.g., MS-TN), percutaneous ablative techniques can be successful in this condition. Preoperative MRI should assess for brainstem lesions along the PTP. An ablative procedure may be considered in the presence of such a lesion. Percutaneous RFTC localizes the appropriate trigeminal division by intraprocedural electric stimulation and can be considered in medication-refractory TN associated with a focal pontine lesion.
