Gender-affirming psychiatry: a minority stress and phenomenological framework for child and adolescent mental health
Authors: Jamie D. Agapoff, E. Diane Stapleton, Gerrit van Schalkwyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This perspective article introduces Gender Affirming Psychiatry (GAP), a conceptual framework that integrates queer phenomenology, the gender minority stress model, and gender-affirming care to guide psychiatric assessment, formulation, and treatment of transgender and gender diverse (TGD) youth. This approach acknowledges the cultural and institutional structures that negatively impact the mental health of gender diverse youth, while leveraging resilience, community connection, and support structures that are known to improve mental health outcomes. In this time of greater awareness and increased scrutiny, GAP is a practical, person-centered approach that is clinically sound and ethically grounded.

Background

The concept of minority stress was introduced Dr. Winn Kelly Brooks (formerly Virginia Rae Brooks) in her 1981 work Minority Stress and Lesbian Women. Instead of seeing the struggles of queer women as signs of mental illness, she believed these problems arose out of social inequities [1]. Dr. Ilan H. Meyer would apply this idea to gay men, developing it into a model linking internal and external stressors to negative mental health outcomes [2]. Researchers Hendricks and Testa would adapt the model to the trans and gender-diverse community. They identified gender-specific stressors such as rejection, non-affirmation, and internalized transphobia linked to poorer mental health, as well as buffering factors like gender identity pride and family support [3].

Based in phenomenological philosophy, queer phenomenology is a way to understand how lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ+) individuals experience sex and gender differently as they move through the world [4]. It draws from the work of Edmund Husserl, who believed individual meaning and value arise from personal experience [5]. Psychiatrist Karl Jaspers applied this approach to psychiatry, using it to explore inner worlds shaped by psychological distress. Instead of just focusing on symptoms or brain chemistry, Jaspers turned his attention instead to the lived experiences of his patients [5].

The thread connecting these phenomenological approaches is a commitment to witnessing rather than judging. Sara Ahmed suggests social spaces are organized around cisheteronormative assumptions. They expect individuals to follow specific paths based on those assumptions [4]. Examples of this might include adopting pronouns and/or gendered behaviors based on one’s assigned sex. But, when someone moves in a direction different, their existence reveals how rigid those paths really are. Identities and expressions that don’t align with normative expectations clash with systems and infrastructures that are restrictive at best, exclusive at worst. Not fitting can make a person “feel odd, even disturbed” (pp. 170) and cause a sense of disruption to their life-world. Within the queer phenomenological framework, not fitting becomes evidence of the confines of cisnormativity, not personal failure.

Cisheteronormative bias has largely contributed to the pathologizing approaches toward gender-diverse youth throughout the 20th century. This is reflected in diagnostic systems that framed gender diversity as a mental illness [6]. As a result, many clinicians promoted interventions aimed at discouraging or extinguishing gender-diverse expression in youth, prioritizing alignment with assigned sex over psychological well-being [7]. Retrospective and contemporary research has since demonstrated that such gender-change efforts were not only ineffective but caused significant harm, including suicidality [8].

Modern standards of care have shifted away from pathologization toward gender-affirmation, recognizing that adverse mental health outcomes in this population are largely driven by minority stress [3]. The new diagnostic nomenclature (e.g. Gender Dysphoria, Gender Incongruence) normalizes gender variance, reduces stigma, and ensures access to medically necessary healthcare [9, 10].

Developed through decades of research, advocacy, and clinical insights, gender-affirming care (GAC) reflects medicine’s larger shift toward patient-centered approaches that honor autonomy, well-being, and lived experience. GAC includes a range of medical, social, and psychological services aimed at reducing dysphoria and aligning personal embodiment goals [11, 12]. Instead of one path for everyone, care unfolds through conversations grounded in informed consent and shared decision-making [11] At its core, this approach is about helping trans and gender-diverse youth live their most authentic selves [11].

What is gender affirming psychiatry?

Starting with how people experience gender, gender affirming psychiatry (GAP) blends clinical insight with the lived experiences of transgender and gender diverse (TGD) youth. Because stigma and bias show up in interpersonal encounters and broader systems, care begins by explicitly labeling harms from social pressures and minority stress [2, 3]. This orientation draws on a phenomenological approach that prioritizes lived experience, moving attention away from distress as primarily internal or biological and toward the environments and experiences that shape emotional well-being [4, 5, 13].

The minority stress model contextualizes this experience by identifying the distal and proximal stressors that may contribute to psychological symptoms [2, 3]. Gender-affirming care then guides the clinical response, ensuring that assessment and treatment respect and support the youth’s gender identity while addressing both dysphoria and broader mental health needs [11]. Together, these approaches allow clinicians to formulate distress not merely as individual pathology, but as the interaction between experience and social context [13]. This allows treatment plans to be developed that are diagnostically rigorous, affirming, and responsive to social and environmental factors contributing to the youth’s distress (see Table 1).

What makes this work is the clinical relationship. Instead of rushing to assign labels, the clinician listens and engages with the patient’s experiences of dysphoria and alienation within one’s body and environment, shaped by ongoing pressures, stigma, and fears of rejection [4, 13]. Diagnosis becomes less about fitting individuals into categories and more about facilitating access to appropriate supports. Healing involves naming distress without reducing it to checklists and aligning care with practical, affirming interventions. This includes psychological and medical interventions (e.g. puberty blockers, hormones) and family involvement when possible. In this way, GAP supports care that is individualized and grounded in the realities of each youth’s experience.

Clinical tools for targeting minority stress

• Assertively label true stigma as unfair and wrong

• Offer school and community education through committees, organizations, and institutions

• Work with policymakers to promote evidence-based policies to reduce discrimination and increase acceptance

• Provide expert testimony, editorials, and opinion pieces

• Connect with legal resources as necessary

• Share educational and resource materials (e.g. Family Acceptance Project [14]) to help families understand the impacts of various acceptance and rejection behaviors

• Facilitate family therapy to help families understand gender diversity

• Design clinical spaces to breakdown heteronormative assumptions, ensuring that bathroom facilities are centered around privacy and sanitation rather than gendered norms

• Advocate for inpatient environments that are affirming in design and through deliberate signaling

• Ensure that medical records systems offer resistance free ways of reinforcing the patient’s gender identity

• Work with institutions to ensure culturally competent practices and policies that affirm identity and ensuring access to gender-congruent spaces

• Work with families to educate about gender diversity and the impact of using affirmed names and pronouns

• Working to prevent the proximal stressors that lead to this

• Gradual exposure to expressing authentic self, while being mindful of safety (i.e., ensuring this is done in spaces where patient will not experience harassment, victimization, or rejection)

• Challenging the cognitive distortions that lead to shame related to gender diversity

• Gradual exposure to expressing authentic self, while being mindful of safety (as above)

• Connecting to local youth support groups or therapy groups

• Connecting to community centers and organizations that support sexual and gender diverse persons

• If in-person spaces are not available, consider safe online spaces

• Educate families about gender diversity and the importance of family acceptance, including principles from the Family Acceptance Project [14, 15]

• Facilitate family therapy to help families openly communicate about gender in a safe supportive environment

• Provide parents with a therapeutic space in which they can talk through their concerns related to gender identity, to avoid voicing rejecting messages that are not their fully formed thoughts

• Teach communication and validation skills around gender

• Identify gender diverse role models

• Discuss the contributions transgender people have made to local, national, and international communities

• Recommend age-appropriate reading materials about transgender people

Gender affirming psychiatry as an emerging evidence-based practice

Transgender visibility has brought greater social recognition and access to care, along with greater sociopolitical efforts to restrict that care. Although important questions remain regarding optimal assessment, timing of interventions, and long-term outcomes, these uncertainties continue to be actively investigated. Nevertheless, current recommendations from major professional organizations consistently support individualized, developmentally appropriate gender-affirming care delivered within comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment [11, 12, 17]. Contemporary restrictions on gender-affirming care often diverge from these recommendations. Accordingly, clinical approaches that reject a young person’s expressed gender identity as inherently invalid are inconsistent with contemporary diagnostic frameworks and current standards of care. Likewise, delaying access to indicated gender-affirming interventions is itself an active clinical decision with potential psychological consequences and should not be regarded as a neutral approach. From a phenomenological perspective, psychiatric care begins by taking patients’ lived experiences seriously, regardless of age.

What we have come to know as “desistance” is best understood as the impact of social pressure rather than a true loss of gender identity. Methodological reviews have shown many desistance studies overestimate rates by including gender-nonconforming children who did not actually have gender dysphoria or were lost to follow-up [17]. More recent longitudinal studies show that when gender-diverse youth are supported, rates of persistence are high, suggesting that stability of gender identity is the norm in affirming environments [18]. The most common reasons for desistance are external such as family rejection, stigma, and discrimination (i.e. minority stress) rather than intrinsic changes in gender identity [19]. Desistance, like concealment, may be better understood as an adaptive response in some to adverse social conditions, rather than evidence against the stability of transgender identities.

For child and adolescent psychiatrists, GAP provides a practical framework for integrating contemporary standards of gender-affirming care with minority stress theory and phenomenological psychiatry. Whereas existing models of gender-affirming care mostly focus on medical interventions and social transition, GAP described a broader, clinical approach to psychiatric formulation and longitudinal mental health care. By centering patients’ lived experiences while recognizing the influence of social and institutional factors on mental health, GAP supports individualized psychiatric care that is evidence-informed, developmentally appropriate, and responsive to each young person’s needs. And, while the evidence base continues to evolve, current research and clinical recommendations consistently support individualized gender-affirming care informed by comprehensive assessment and shared decision-making. GAP provides clinicians with a practical model for applying these principles within everyday psychiatric practice, reflects the established findings of existing science, and creates room for incorporating new findings into an evolving practice.
