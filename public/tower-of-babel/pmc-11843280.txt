Sonopharmacology: Regulation of drug activity through ultrasound
Authors: Wenjing Zhang, Fangyin Song, Zhiyuan Shi, Xin Li, Kuailu Lin
Published: 2025

Introduction

The introduction of photoswitches into the bioactive substances is the fundamental idea behind photopharmacology. Upon light irradiation, the drug activity can be spatiotemporally controlled by the photon-induced variety of photoswitch's structures or characteristics [1]. However, some challenges are still faced in photopharmacology including phototoxicity and limited tissue penetration depth (about a few millimeters). Compared to light, ultrasound (US) offers some irreplaceable advantages, such as extensive clinical applications, high biosafety, and deep tissue penetration ability (up to about 10 cm). Low-frequency US (20 kHz) having strong cavitation effect is typically utilized to regulate drug activation. Nevertheless, the low-frequency US is not biosafe because of its damage to normal tissues and cells. In contrast, US with higher frequency (>1 MHz) is widely used in clinical theranostics towing to its biosafety, with applications including US imaging and high-intensity focused US (HIFU). In recent years, the mechanism of mechanochemistry has been used to regulate drug activity through molecule structural changes mediated by mechanical forces (e.g., US, extrusion, and ball milling). This emerging field is called sonopharmacology, which aims to achieve spatiotemporally controlled regulation of drug activity by mechanochemical reactions. Among them, US is the only tool that can transmit mechanical force to the solution. Therefore, it is crucial to develop US-activated prodrug systems, which not only have accurate and spatiotemporal control, but also address the bottlenecks of conventional US-responsive drug delivery systems, such as the inevitable drug leakage in the blood circulation and the sudden drug release due to the instantaneous rupture of systems. Here, we comprehensively introduce recent advances in sonopharmacology, which are categorized into three types according to the interaction mechanisms between US effects and drug activation: (1) Sonothermal effect: US-mediated temperature increase cleaves thermosensitive groups for reactive oxygen species (ROS) release. (2) Sonochemical effect: US-mediated generation of chemicals (e.g., free radicals or electrons) in the presence of sonosensitizers or reducing agents can further cleave the corresponding sensitive groups to trigger drug activation. (3) Sonomechanical effect: Mechanical forces of US directly cleave the embedded mechanophores in polymer systems for controlled drug activation. Finally, we discuss the opportunities and challenges of sonopharmacology in clinical translation.

Sonothermal effect

During US propagation, part of its energy is absorbed by the medium and converted into heat. US-mediated temperature increase can cleave thermosensitive bonds and then trigger drug activation. In general, low-frequency US exhibits strong cavitation effects, while high-frequency US has excellent thermal effects. Some thermosensitive bonds, such as Diels-Alder (DA) and azo bonds, are adopted to design the sonothermal effect-activated systems. In recent work, Lin et al. [2] developed a 2,2′-azobis[2-(2-imidazolin-2-yl)propane]dihydrochloride (AIPH)-loaded liposomes for US-induced cancer therapy (Fig. 1A). AIPH, a water-soluble azo compound, can rapidly produce alkyl radicals upon sonothermal stimulation, where the radicals will further induce ROS generation. Meanwhile, the nitrogen generated upon US enables the enhanced US imaging. In the free radical capture experiment, alkyl radicals are successfully captured by radical capture agents, indicating that azo groups exhibit good sonothermal responsiveness. Moreover, after intravenous injection of AIPH-loaded liposomes, the tumor growth in mice is significantly inhibited with the treatment of US (1.0 MHz, 2.5 W/cm2). The system allows US imaging-guided therapy of hypoxic tumors with negligible toxic side effects. The ROS generated by this system is oxygen-independent, thus avoiding the treatment issue of tumor hypoxic microenvironment tolerance. However, the controllability and accuracy of the US-activated system by sonothermal effect are relatively insufficient. Even if clinical HIFU devices can focus US on a minimum point of about 1 cm, the sonothermal effect-mediated temperature increase is still uncontrollable. Moreover, excessive temperature will lead to irreversible damage to the normal tissues surrounding the disease area.

Sonochemical effect

To address the insufficiency of sonothermal effect-activated systems, the sonochemical effect has been developed to utilize US energy to decompose water into ROS, and further regulate drug activity through ROS-mediated chemical reactions. Besides, with the assistance of sonosensitizers, US energy can also convert molecular oxygen into ROS or improve the efficiency of ROS generation by water decomposition. Compared to thermosensitive bonds, the types of ROS-responsive bonds are more diverse, including diselenide, phenylboronic ester, thioketal and ferrocene bonds, etc., which makes it possible to match a wide range of drugs. Notably, this strategy is limited by the reducing tumor microenvironment-mediated ROS quenching. In 2022, Tong et al. [3] developed a US-activated system using urea bonds as ROS-responsive groups for controlled drug activation. In the reaction, the production of ROS and subsequent bond cleavage occur at the gas-liquid interface of US-mediated cavitation bubbles. Due to the space confined property, US-triggered drug activation exhibits excellent selectivity and efficiency even if at high glutathione concentrations of tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, they synthesized the prodrugs compound (MBU-Mel) of methylene blue urea derivatives (MBU) and antitumor drugs (Mel), which displays an effective activation of Mel with 50 % efficiency upon US irradiation (1 MHz, 1.5 W/cm2). To facilitate drug activation, sonosensitizers are used in the US-activated systems to generate more ROS. Compared to US alone, sonosensitizers can not only improve the efficiency of US-mediated ROS production and subsequent drug activation, but also achieve the combined sonodynamic therapy and chemotherapy.

The above strategies improve the accuracy of drug activation and release in US-activated systems by avoiding ROS quenching or enhancing ROS production. However, ROS is naturally present in some normal tissues and cells, which will result in erroneous activation of drugs in non-lesion regions. Therefore, the development of electron-activated prodrug systems is critical to improve the accuracy of sonopharmacology. Recently, Liu et al. [4] synthesized the US-activated prodrug systems of cyaninplatin with Pt(IV) scaffold and heptamethine cyanine sonosensitizer (Fig. 1B). In the presence of sodium ascorbate as an electron donor, the cyaninplatin is reduced to carboplatin by a sono-sensitized electron transfer process. During US-activated reduction process, the consumption of reductants in the intracellular microenvironment of cancers can further increase ROS-induced therapeutic efficacy. According to experimental results, 95 % of the cyaninplatin is reduced upon US (1.75 MHz, 4 W). In the absence of sodium ascorbate, cyaninplatin is hardly reduced, suggesting that US-triggered reduction process requires an external electron donor and proceeds via intermolecular electron transfer. Some strategies have been developed to reduce the influence of the in vivo microenvironments on sonochemical effect-mediated drug activation. However, the physiological microenvironment is too complex for these strategies to achieve precise sonochemical reactions.

Sonomechanical effect

To solve the dilemma faced by the sonochemical effect-activated systems, the development of US-activated systems that can directly respond to the sonomechanical effect is expected to achieve higher controllability and accuracy of drug activation in vivo. Polymer mechanochemistry, which involves chemical reactions induced by mechanical forces, is central to this approach. Mechanophores are force-sensitive functional groups embedded in polymer chains that undergo bond cleavage or conformational changes upon US. In polymer mechanochemistry, US is a particularly effective tool for triggering mechanophore decomposition to activate drugs due to its precision and controllability [5]. Most reported mechanophores feature mechanically weak bonds, strained rings, or isomerizable bonds, making them ideal candidates for US-triggered activation and release. Compared to sonothermal effect- and sonochemical effect-mediated drug activation, the emerging polymer mechanochemistry provides a promising path for controllable and precise US-mediated drug activation and release.

In covalent systems, US-triggered bond scission facilitates drug activation. In 2020, Shi et al. [6] introduced two methods for US-mediated disulfide bond cleavage: (1) an intermolecular system where thiyl radicals trigger a retro-DA reaction to release various drugs, and (2) an intramolecular system utilizing 5-exo-trig cyclization for precise prodrug activation. Despite these advances, current mechanophores are often limited to releasing specific drugs. To overcome this limitation, Hu et al. [7] investigated mechanical triggered cascade reactions for small molecule activation, focusing on substituted 2-furanylmethanol derivatives. They identified favorable substitutions, such as a phenoxy group at the 3-position of the furan ring combined with an α-methyl substituent, to enhance mechanochemical reactivity. By conjugating molecules with various functional groups (e.g., alcohol, alkylamine, carboxylic acid, and sulfonic acid functional groups) to these mechanophores, they achieved controlled activation upon US (20 kHz, 8.2 W/cm2), expanding the applicability of mechanophores.

However, polymer chains typically break after a single US activation, limiting drug release to one or two molecules per chain. To address this limitation, Chen et al. [8] designed the rotaxane-based mechanophores (Fig. 1C). When activated by US (20 kHz, 13 W/cm2), the pillar [5]arene macrocycle slid along the axis, triggering a retro-DA reaction to release up to five cargo molecules.

Traditional US treatment for drug release relies on the strong cavitation effect, which poses biosafety risks from tissue and cell damage. Yao et al. [9] introduced gas vesicles (GVs) as acoustic-mechanical transducers to lower cavitation threshold. Under focused US (330 kHz, 3.6 W/cm2), the GVs expanded and burst, generating mechanical effects that activated mechanophores to release aminocoumarin and camptothecin. Future studies may explore the co-injection of GVs and polymers or their confinement in semipermeable implants to enable in vivo applications.

Covalent systems often require prolonged US irradiation, posing challenges for clinical translation. Non-covalent systems, which rely on weaker interaction, offer a more easily dissociable alternative. Huo et al. [10] proposed two methods: (1) RNA aptamers bound to aminoglycoside antibiotics, where US disrupts the host-guest interaction and restores antibiotic activity, and (2) supramolecular assemblies of gold nanoparticles, where US dissociates the assemblies to recover antimicrobial properties. These systems offer faster response times and broader clinical applicability, addressing limitations of prolonged US exposure in covalent systems (Fig. 1D).

Conclusion

In future studies, there are several challenges and opportunities for the development of sonopharmacology. For sonothermal effect-activated systems, they are highly tolerant to complex microenvironments in vivo. However, US-mediated heat generation is not directional, resulting in poor controllability and accuracy. Meanwhile, excessively high temperature can also damage normal tissues surrounding the disease. In addition, the existing types of thermosensitive bonds are limited, especially those that can bind to releasable drug groups. In contrast, sonomechemical effect-activated systems offer diverse designs due to the abundance of sensitive bonds. However, their clinical translation is hindered by uncontrollable in vivo reactions. Some strategies have been proposed to improve the selectivity and accuracy of sonochemical effect-activated systems in vivo, such as reducing ROS quenching, enhancing ROS generation, or even utilizing electron-mediated reactions, but these systems still need to be evaluated for their biosafety caused by the uncontrollable drug activation. It is worth mentioning that the emerging sonomechanical effect is being developed by responding to US forces to cleave mechanophores in polymer systems, thereby achieving drug activation and release. As a comparison, these systems exhibit high controllability and accuracy upon US. However, this field is still in the early stages, and many fundamental studies should be carried out. For example, various mechanophores should be developed to effectively match the drugs with different functional groups to satisfy the requirements of the treatment of different diseases. The interactions between mechanophores activity and US parameters should be explored to improve the controllability and accuracy of drug activation. Besides, due to the limitation of frequency, power, and action time of US, these systems have not been integrated with clinical US equipment and have not even completed animal experiments, which urgently needs to be addressed. In recent reports, the ingenious design (e.g., the knotted polymer systems, the crosslinked polymer networks and the interlocked rotaxane architectures) enable iterative activation of mechanophores and improved drug activation, though they only respond to low-frequency US (20 kHz) with dozens of minutes. On the other hand, the crosslinked polymer networks and US-mediated bubble rupture have been developed to achieve the responsiveness of mechanophores to high-frequency US (hundreds of kHz). However, these systems still do not meet the requirements of clinical US equipment and biosafe therapy. In addition, several smart nanocarriers, especially clinically approved nanocarriers, should be employed to acquire optimized pharmacokinetics and tumor accumulation. The combination of sonopharmacology with other therapeutic methods should be developed to further improve therapeutic efficacy. Overall, sonopharmacology is an emerging field with an important research prospect. It is highly valuable in biomedical applications due to its exceptional characteristics and clinical translational potential. With the continuous updating of technology, sonopharmacology will develop into a powerful medical tool for precise disease treatment with low toxic side effects.
