Integrated analysis of transcriptome, metabolome, and lipidome revealed key genes associated with meat quality traits in pigs
Authors: Keyan Huo, Mei Qiao, Shouyuan Wang, Junjie Liu, Jialiang Yu, Di Sun, Mengjie Huang, Lin Liang, Bugao Li, Chang Lu, Guoqing Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the improvement of people’s living standards, public attention to pork quality has grown increasingly. This study aimed to identify the key genes affecting meat quality in pigs. Castrated Mashen (MS) pigs (n = 6) with an average body weight of 112.5 kg (300 days of age) and Duroc × (Landrace × Yorkshire) (DLY) pigs (n = 6) with an average body weight of 114.5 kg (165 days of age) were selected for slaughter. The longissimus dorsi muscle (LDM) was collected for meat quality traits determination and omics analyses. The results showed that MS pigs exhibited significantly superior meat color, intramuscular fat (IMF) content, marbling, shear force, and pH compared with DLY pigs (p < 0.05). A total of 1330 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 94 differentially expressed lipids (DELs), and 185 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened between MS and DLY pigs (p < 0.05). Further weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed that glycerophospholipids (GP) and glycerolipids (GL) were key lipids affecting pork quality. Additionally, integration of lipidomic and metabolomic data identified “lipids-metabolites strong correlation pairs” influencing meat quality, such as the significant positive correlation between carnitine derivatives and various triglycerides (TG) (|r|> 0.8, p < 0.05). Furthermore, we conducted transcriptome-metabolome and transcriptome-lipidome correlation analyses based on expression abundance and functional annotations, and screened four key gene sets. Finally, after the intersection of these key genes, 19 core genes such as ALDOB, ASS1, CYP2A13, CYP2E1, DMGDH, PLCB1, and MAT1A may be related to pork quality. This study provides potential targets for further exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying pork quality by identifying several key genes linked to pork quality traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12887-5.Keywords: Pigs, Meat quality, Transcriptome, Metabolome, Lipidome, Candidate genes

Background

In recent years, consumers’ focus of pork has shifted from quantity to quality, so improving meat quality traits has accordingly emerged as critical research priorities in food science and animal husbandry. Meat quality encompasses core traits such as meat color, pH, water-holding capacity (WHC), intramuscular fat (IMF) content and tenderness [1]. Meat color is the main visual factor affecting consumer preference, which is determined by the content and state of myoglobin [2]. pH reflects the glycolysis rate of muscle and serves as a key indicator of meat freshness and stability [3]. WHC, typically assessed via drip loss and cooking loss, is governed by the structure and composition of muscle [4]. High levels of IMF are typically associated with better taste and flavor [5], while marbling refers to the distribution of IMF within muscle tissue [6]. Tenderness, commonly evaluated by shear force value, is primarily influenced by muscle fiber type, thickness and spacing [7]. Pork quality is affected by genetic, nutritional, environmental and other factors to varying degrees, of which, the genetic factors are the fundamental determinants [8].

Mashen (MS) pigs, a valuable Chinese indigenous breed, are distinguished by superior meat quality, characterized by higher IMF content, optimized fatty acid profiles, and finer muscle fiber characteristics compared to commercial breeds such as Duroc × (Landrace × Yorkshire) (DLY) pigs [9]. Prior omics investigations have identified differential genes, lipids, and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) implicated in muscle development and lipid metabolism in MS pigs [10, 11]. However, systematic investigations revealing the molecular characteristics and regulatory mechanisms underlying the superior meat quality of MS pigs using integrated multi-omics approaches remain limited.

Single-omics analysis cannot systematically unravel the regulatory mechanisms of the complex physiological processes involved in meat quality formation. Thus, with the advancement of high-throughput sequencing technology, multi-omics association analysis has become a powerful tool for investigating complex biological processes. For example, a study combined metabolomic with lipidomic data of skeletal muscle from Laiwu and Yorkshire pigs. The high triglyceride of Laiwu pig makes the IMF content higher. Ketoglutaric acid, fumaric acid and L-aspartic acid are important flavor precursors for improving pork flavor in Laiwu pigs. This is the reason why Laiwu pork quality is better [12]. For instance, One previous study investigated the lipidomic and transcriptomic profiles of the longissimus dorsi muscle in Luchuan and Duroc pigs. Luchuan pigs exhibited significantly higher IMF content, with upregulated triglycerides (TG) and diglycerides (DG). This phenotypic difference was attributed to the elevated expression of lipid synthesis-related genes (e.g., FASN and SREBP1) in Luchuan pigs, which activate the glycerolipid metabolism pathway [13]. Another study combined transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data of longissimus dorsi muscle from Qinling Black (QLB) and Large White (LW) pigs. QLB pigs had higher IMF content, lower shear force, and higher water-holding capacity due to increased levels of beneficial metabolites (creatine, alanine, and n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids). The Rap1 signaling pathway and key gene RapGEF1 were identified as core regulators, with positive correlations to lipid metabolism-related metabolites [14].

MS pig is a typical indigenous pig breed in northern China with excellent meat quality characteristics, including high intramuscular fat content, tender muscle texture, and good meat color stability. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying its superior meat quality remain largely unclear. This study identified several key genes that may affect pork quality through integrated analysis of transcriptomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic data from the LDM of MS pigs and DLY pigs. Therefore, systematic exploration of meat quality traits and key regulatory molecules in MS pigs is critical for the conservation and utilization of Chinese indigenous pig germplasm resources, and the present findings provide a theoretical basis for revealing the molecular mechanisms of pork quality and optimizing breeding strategies to improve meat quality.

Materials and methods

Animals and samples preparation

Pigs were raised in Datong Agricultural Germplasm Resources Conservation and Research Center (Datong, China). All pigs were surgically castrated males, reared in the same environment with ad libitum access to feed and water. DLY pigs and MS pigs (n = 6) were randomly selected for the experiment. The individual pre-slaughter body weight of all experimental animals is provided in Table S1. The average weight of MS pigs was 112.5 kg at 300 days of age, while the average weight of DLY pigs was 114.5 kg at 165 days of age. Weight matching is preferred because the growth rates of the two breeds are different, and the same weight cannot be achieved at the same age, and slaughter weight is a key factor affecting pork quality traits. This design avoids the confounding effects of body weight on meat quality and ensures that differences are due to genetic background.

Pigs were slaughtered by electric shock, exsanguination, depilation, peeling, evisceration and segmentation. LDM tissue samples were collected immediately, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80℃ for subsequent analysis. The LDM is the standard muscle in the international standardized meat evaluation procedure. Its tenderness, IMF, meat color, pH value and WHC are highly correlated with pig carcass quality. In order to ensure the consistency of the sampling, all LDM were taken from the fixed depth and position between the last rib of the left half carcass and the first lumbar vertebra according to the standardized process. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Shanxi Agricultural University (Shanxi, China, approval no. SXAU-EAW-2021P.XM.007005184, approved on 4 July 2021).

Measurement of meat quality traits

Meat quality was assessed in accordance with the technical specifications for pork quality determination (NY/T 821–2019). The time point of 1 h after slaughter reflects the early glycolysis process and the initial formation of meat quality, while the time point of 24 h represents the completion of the rigor mortis process and the stable final meat quality. These two time points are widely used in standardized pork quality assessment. The meat color was measured by colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) at 1 h and 24 h after slaughter. The pH was measured by a hand-held pH meter (pH-STAR, SFK-Technology, Denmark) at 1 h and 24 h after slaughter. Electrical conductivity was measured using a conductivity meter (LF-STAR, MATTHAUS, Germany). Drip loss was determined by suspending meat samples collected immediately post-slaughter in sealed bags at 4℃ for 24 h. Cooking loss was measured by placing meat samples in heat-resistant containers in a 75℃ water bath. The shear force was measured by shear force tester (MultiTest 2.5-I, Mecmesin, UK) after 72 h of acid removal and ripening at 4℃. Marbling was scored by comparing with standard charts immediately after slaughter. IMF content was determined via the Soxhlet ether extraction method.

Lipidome profiling

Lipidome services were performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC, Agilent, US) combined with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Q-TOF MS, AB Sciex, US) was used for the separation and identification of samples. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, Ireland) using the hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) mode. The mobile phase consisted of (A) an aqueous solution containing 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide, and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was set as follows: initial 95% B (maintained for 0.5 min), linearly reduced to 65% B (6.5 min), then adjusted to 40% B (maintained for 1 min) within 1 min, and finally re-equilibrated to 95% B for 3 min. Mass spectrometry detection was performed using an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, with data collected in both positive and negative ion modes.

Raw data were converted to MzXML format using ProteoWizard (v3.0.6428). Peak alignment, retention time correction and peak area extraction were conducted with the following parameters: centWave m/z = 10 ppm, peakwidth = c (10, 60), prefilter = c (10, 100). For peak grouping, bw = 5, mzwid = 0.025, minfrac = 0.5 were used. During data preprocessing, ion peaks with > 50% missing values were removed, remaining missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbor (KNN) method, and characteristic peaks with relative standard deviation (RSD) > 50% were filtered out.

Differentially expressed lipids (DELs) were screened via univariate T-test. Lipids with p-value < 0.05, Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) ≥ 1, and |log2(FC)|≥ 1 were considered DELs between two breeds.

Non-targeted metabolome profiling

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis was also performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). UHPLC-MS analysis was performed using a UHPLC (Agilent, US) combined with Q-TOF MS (AB Sciex, US). Samples were injected onto ACQUITY UPLC BEH column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, Ireland). In both ESI positive and negative modes, the mobile phase comprised (A) an aqueous solution of 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide, and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was: 85% B (maintained for 1 min), linear decrease to 65% B (at 11 min), further decrease to 40% (held for 4 min) within 0.1 min, increase back to 85% within 0.1 min, and a 5-min re-equilibration period.

Raw data were converted to MzXML files using ProteoWizard MSConvert before being imported into XCMS (v3.8.2). Peak picking was conducted with parameters: centWave m/z = 10 ppm, peakwidth = c (10, 60), prefilter = c (10, 100). For peak grouping, bw = 5, mzwid = 0.025, minfrac = 0.5 were used. CAMERA was applied for isotope and adduct annotation. Ion features with > 50% nonzero values in at least one group were retained. Metabolite identification relied on matching accurate m/z (< 10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra to an in-house authentic standard database. Missing data were filled via KNN, extreme values deleted, and data normalized by total peak area for sample-metabolite parallelism.

Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened using a univariate T-test. Metabolites with p-value < 0.05, VIP ≥ 1, and |log2(FC)|≥ 1 were considered as DAMs between the two breeds. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

RNA-seq profiling

Total RNA extraction and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis were commissioned by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Total RNA was extracted from LDM samples using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Japan), and RNA quality was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent, US). After fragmentation, the enriched mRNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Illumina NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, US). The cDNA library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequences were filtered using fastp (v0.18.0) to obtain high-quality reads, which were then aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). RSEM (v1.3.4) was used to quantify the transcript expression as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). The sequencing data have been deposited in the CNCB Genome Sequence Archive database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/, passed on October 27, 2025), and the accession number was CRA032148.

DEGs were screened using DESeq2 (v1.40.0) (FDR < 0.05, |log2(FC)|≥ 2). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the GO database, and pathway enrichment analysis was conducted based on the KEGG database.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was used to identify lipid molecules contributing to significant differences in meat quality between the two pig breeds. A soft threshold power of β = 8 was selected to construct a scale-free network with a scale-free topology fit index of R2 ≥ 0.8. Modules were identified based on the clustering of lipid abundance profiles, and lipids with a similarity of > 0.75 were assigned to the same module by cutting the clustering tree. Each module was represented by a unique color.

Association analysis

To explore potential upstream–downstream metabolic links or synergistic regulatory patterns between lipids and metabolites, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to screen for “strong lipids-metabolites correlation pairs” with the thresholds set as |r|> 0.8 and p-value < 0.05 for the top 20 VIP-ranked DELs and DAMs in MS-LDM and DLY-LDM.

Metabolome-transcriptome association analysis was based on the integration of expression abundance and functional annotations. Prior to correlation analysis, we first screened for metabolites and genes that exhibited consistent differential expression in both omics datasets, with the threshold set as |log2(FC)|≥ 1. Subsequently, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the pre-screened metabolites and genes, and strong correlation pairs were identified using the dual criteria of absolute correlation coefficient |r|> 0.99 and p-value < 0.05. Concurrently, functional-based correlation analysis of metabolites and genes was performed. KEGG co-enrichment analysis was conducted on the top 20 VIP-ranked DAMs and DEGs (FDR ≤ 0.05) to identify co-enriched genes in meat quality-related pathways.

Lipids-genes association analysis followed identical procedures, including pre-screening of molecules with consistent differential expression, Pearson correlation analysis for strong pairs, and KEGG co-enrichment analysis of key DELs with DEGs to identify meat quality-related co-enriched genes.

qRT-PCR analysis

The LDM of MS pigs and DLY pigs were selected for qRT-PCR verification of RNA-Seq results. In order to verify the reliability of RNA-seq data, there are clear functional associations and detectable expression levels based on significant differential expression patterns of genes, biological processes related to pork quality. Five genes (CYP2E1, DGKE, PEMT, UGT8, CLDN3) were selected for qRT-PCR verification. Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using TransScript TM RT Reagent Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The qRT-PCR primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST (Table S2). TransStart TM Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and Bio-Rad CFX Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) were used to detect the expression of the differential genes. Amplification system (10 μL): template cDNA (2 μL), SYBR qPCR Master Mix (5 μL), forward and reverse primers (0.2 μL each), and RNase-free ddH2O (2.6 μL) to make up the total volume. Amplification program: 94℃ 30 s for activation; 94℃ 5 s, 60 ℃ annealing-extension 30 s, a total of 40 cycles of amplification. The melting curves were collected at 94℃ 30 s, 60℃ 30 s, and 94℃ 15 s. Three biological replicates were analyzed per sample, and the relative expression levels of genes were calculated using the 2−△△Ct method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v27.0) and GraphPad Prism (v10.0). Independent samples T-test was used to assess significance, with p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Meat quality traits

As shown in Table 1, MS pigs exhibited superior meat quality compared with DLY pigs. Specifically, MS pigs had significantly higher a* and b* values at 24 h, higher IMF content and marbling score, and significantly lower L* value at 24 h, pH1 value, and shear force (p < 0.05). In summary, according to China’s agricultural industry standard NY/T 821–2019, MS pigs exhibit significant advantages in key meat quality attributes, including a redder meat color, moderate IMF deposition, an optimal early post-mortem pH, and premium tenderness.

Lipidomic, metabolomic and transcriptomic data analysis

Comparison of lipid molecules between MS pigs and DLY pigs

In this study, a total of 2402 lipid molecules were identified in the LDM of MS and DLY pigs by non-targeted lipidomics technology. These lipids were classified as glycerophospholipids (GP), glycerolipids (GL), fatty acyls (FA), sphingolipids (SP), sterol lipids (ST), saccharolipids (SL) and prenol lipids (PR). Among them, GL were the most abundant, accounting for 48.38% of the total lipid molecules, followed by GP (32.29%) and SP (14.36%), while FA, ST, SL and PR had fewer species. Among the 41 lipid subclasses, triglycerides (TG) (32.26%), diglycerides (DG) (15.82%), phosphatidylcholine (PC) (9.83%) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (8.78%) accounted for a relatively high proportion (Fig. 1A).

Comparison of lipid molecules between Mashen (MS) pigs and Duroc × (Landrace × Yorkshire) (DLY) pigs. A total lipid primary classification and secondary classification. B principal component analysis (PCA). C MS vs DLY volcano plot. D cluster heat map of differentially expressed lipids (DELs). E variable importance in projection (VIP) map of differentially expressed lipids (DELs)

Significant differences in lipid profiles between the two breeds were observed via principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1B). After difference analysis, we identified a total of 94 DELs between MS pigs and DLY pigs (Table S3), of which 81 DELs were upregulated and 13 DELs were downregulated (Fig. 1C). Cluster analysis of all the DELs molecules of MS pigs and DLY pigs showed that the DELs were mainly concentrated in GL, GP, SP and ST (Fig. 1D). The top 20 DELs with VIP values were displayed (Fig. 1E), 18 of which were significantly upregulated in MS pigs. However, only PC (34:2) and PC (23:0_11:2) were significantly downregulated in MS pigs. This indicates that GL and GP may be the important lipid molecules that cause the difference in meat quality between MS pigs and DLY pigs.

Comparison of metabolites between MS pigs and DLY pigs

A total of 10,921 metabolites were identified by non-targeted metabolomic analysis. The metabolites in the positive and negative ion modes were combined, and PCA of the metabolomics data showed that there was a significant separation between the two breeds (Fig. 2A).

Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) between MS pigs and DLY pigs. A principal component analysis (PCA). B MS vs DLY volcano plot. C cluster heat map of differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs). D kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of DAMs

A total of 185 DAMs were found between MS and DLY pigs (Table S4), of which 73 were upregulated and 112 were downregulated (Fig. 2B). The DAMs were mainly classified into lipids, amino acids, and organic acids (Fig. 2C). The top 20 DAMs were carnitine, nucleotide, glycerophospholipid, and choline.

Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis of DAMs revealed 156 enriched metabolic pathways. The following shows the top 20 pathways of FDR values that may be associated with meat quality (Fig. 2D). Key pathways include bile secretion, thyroid hormone synthesis, sulfur metabolism, C5-Branched dibasic acid metabolism, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, and so on.

Comparison of genes between MS pigs and DLY pigs

After bioinformatic analysis of RNA-Seq data, we obtained 21,333 genes that were expressed in the LDM of MS and DLY pigs. PCA revealed that the triplicate biological samples from MS and DLY pigs clustered tightly within breed and were distinctly separated between the two breeds (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, 1330 DEGs were identified between MS and DLY pigs (Table S5), of which 545 genes were upregulated and 785 genes were downregulated (Fig. 3B).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between MS pigs and DLY pigs. A principal component analysis (PCA). B MS vs DLY volcano plot. C gene Ontology (GO) function enrichment analysis. D kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis

Finally, GO enrichment analysis revealed 58 enriched biological processes, with the top 30 including lipid metabolic process and steroid metabolic process—two biological processes potentially linked to meat quality (Fig. 3C). Also, KEGG pathway analysis showed that 525 DEGs were enriched in 298 signaling pathways. The top 30 pathways included several associated with meat quality, such as the PPAR signaling pathway, fat digestion and absorption, cholesterol metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism (Fig. 3D).

WGCNA analysis of lipids

WGCNA was applied to 2402 lipid molecules from MS and DLY pigs to identify key lipid molecules associated with the four significantly different meat quality phenotypes (meat color, pH, shear force, and IMF content) between the two breeds, resulting in the identification of 13 lipid modules. Among them, the turquoise module contained the most lipid molecules (512) related to the differential phenotype (Fig. 4A, B). The turquoise and green modules showed a significant correlation with each phenotype (Fig. 4C). Then, the turquoise module and the green module were analyzed respectively, and the first 100 connection relationships were used to generate a visual lipid interaction network diagram using the Cytoscape (v3.10.3). The main lipid molecules in the green module are GP, including PC, PE, PS, LPC, LPE and CL, especially PE (11:0_10:2)-H and LPE (20:4e) (Fig. 4D). The main lipid molecules in the turquoise module are GL, including TG and DG, especially TG (18:1_12:0_18:2) and TG (19:1_18:1_18:2) (Fig. 4E).

Correlation analysis

Association analysis between lipidome and metabolome

Correlation analysis between 94 DELs and 185 DAMs showed a strong correlation between the two datasets, as illustrated by a cluster heat map (Fig. 5A). Correlation analysis of the top 20 VIP-ranked DELs and DAMs also revealed strong associations (Fig. 5B). Next, the threshold |r|> 0.8, p-value < 0.05 was used to screen the strong correlation between the lipidome and the metabolome (Fig. 5C). Then we visualized these “lipids-metabolites strong correlation pairs” with chord diagrams. The results showed that 5-aminovaleric acid betaine and 2-methylbutyryl-L-carnitine were negatively correlated with most TG and PC species. Carnitine derivatives (L-carnitine, acetylcarnitine), stachydrine, and a variety of TG were positively correlated; betaine was negatively correlated with both DGs (Fig. 5D).

Association analysis between transcriptome and metabolome

We performed a correlation analysis of 1330 DEGs and 185 DAMs. This nine-quadrant plot illustrates the association patterns between the differential changes of genes and metabolites (Fig. 6A). Among all the association pairs, we used |r|> 0.99 and p-value < 0.05 as the standard to screen out strong association pairs. DEGs with strong association with at least one DAM were defined as hub genes (821) (Table S6).

Association analysis between transcriptome and metabolome. A correlation quadrant diagram of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs). B kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) co-enriched pathways of DEGs and DAMs

KEGG co-enrichment analysis of the top 20 VIP-ranked DAMs and DEGs identified 13 enriched pathways (Table S7), of which 9 may be related to meat quality, including metabolic pathways, purine metabolism, ABC transporters, histidine metabolism, β-Alanine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, ether lipid metabolism, lysine degradation and glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (Fig. 6B). The genes enriched in these pathways are regarded as the hub genes (140) (Table S8).

Association analysis between transcriptome and lipidome

Moreover, we performed a correlation analysis of 1330 DEGs in the transcriptome and 94 DELs in the lipidome. Consequently, we screened out the “gene-lipid pairs” that meet the criteria, and drew a nine-quadrant diagram of “gene-lipid relationship pairs” (Fig. 7A). Similarly, we selected the 172 hub genes (Table S9).

Association analysis between transcriptome and lipidome. A correlation quadrant diagram of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed lipids (DELs). B kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) co-enriched pathways of DEGs and DELs

Notably, four key lipid molecules identified by WGCNA and 7 lipid molecules in “lipids-metabolites strong correlation pairs” were used as core lipid molecules. These 11 core lipid molecules and DEGs were subjected to KEGG co-enrichment analysis. The results showed that a total of 7 pathways were enriched, of which 5 may be related to meat quality, including metabolic pathways, arachidonic acid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism and α-Linolenic acid metabolism (Fig. 7B). A total of 133 hub genes were enriched in these pathways (Table S10).

Screening of core genes

In order to identify the key genes related to pork quality, we intersected the above four groups of core genes (they were derived from the correlation analysis of transcriptome-metabolome based on expression abundance, the KEGG co-enrichment analysis of transcriptome- metabolome, the correlation analysis of transcriptome-lipidome based on expression abundance, and the KEGG co-enrichment analysis of transcriptome-lipidome), and visualized them through Venn diagram. Results showed that 19 core genes were screened (Fig. 8A). GeneCards was used to query the function of these core genes and display it in the Table 2. The expression levels of the seven genes ALDOB, CYP2E1, CYP2A13, PLCB1, ASS1, DMGDH, and MAT1A showed a consistent correlation with the phenotype, and their annotation functions were closely related to the core pathways regulating pork quality.

We selected five genes and analyzed their expression levels by qRT-PCR. We found that DGKE and PEMT were significantly increased in MS pigs, and CYP2E1, UGT8 and CLDN3 were significantly decreased in MS pigs, which was consistent with the RNA-Seq results (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

The quality of meat includes meat color, pH, water-holding capacity, IMF content, tenderness, fatty acid composition, and so on [15]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that indigenous pig breeds exhibit superior meat quality compared to commercial pig breeds [12, 16, 17]. In this study, we found that compared with DLY pigs, MS pigs had better meat color, more moderate IMF content (marbling), superior muscle fiber quality, and pH values that were more within the standard range. Therefore, we integrated the transcriptome, metabolome, and lipidome data of the LDM of the two pig breeds to explore the key genes affecting pork quality.

The change of pH is mainly regulated by the glycolysis pathway [18]. After slaughter, aerobic respiration of muscle was stopped, glycolysis became the main way of energy metabolism, and lactic acid accumulation led to a decrease in pH [19]. If the pH drops too fast or too low, it will cause myoglobin denaturation, resulting in pale flesh, muscle protein coagulation, thereby increasing the shear force. In this study, the content of D-lactic acid of DLY pigs was significantly higher than that of MS pigs. Accordingly, compared with MS pigs, DLY pigs exhibited a more rapid pH decline and lower pH24, which was associated with paler meat color (higher L*) and higher shear force, whereas MS pigs maintained a more stable pH, contributing to redder meat (lower L*) and better tenderness. These phenotypic differences may be mediated by ALDOB, which is a key enzyme in the downstream of fructose metabolism and glycolysis pathway, and can catalyze the decomposition of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (a key glycolysis intermediate) [20, 21]. The high expression of ALDOB in DLY pigs was positively correlated with glycolytic flux, lactic acid and D-lactic acid production, and may be associated with the accelerated post-mortem pH decline.

The oxidation state of myoglobin directly determines the change in meat color, and oxidative stress is the main reason for myoglobin oxidation [22]. The meat color of MS pigs is redder than that of DLY pigs, which may be related to the low expression of CYP2E1 in the muscle of MS pigs. CYP2E1 catalyzes lipid peroxidation under stress conditions, producing oxidative products such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and accelerating myoglobin oxidation [23]. The low expression of CYP2E1 in MS pigs was positively correlated with reduced lipid peroxidation, which may be linked to the increased stability of meat color.

Shear force reflects muscle tenderness, which is mainly related to muscle fiber type, collagen content and crosslinking degree [24]. High proportion of slow muscle fibers, less collagen cross-linking, low shear force [25]. CYP2A13 mediates the lipid peroxidation pathway by catalyzing the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acid chains in TG, thereby generating lipid peroxides [26]. In the present study, lower CYP2A13 expression in MS pigs was associated with weakened lipid peroxidation and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) content. These changes were correlated with maintained muscle membrane integrity, decreased sarcoplasmic protein loss, and reduced collagen cross-linking, which may contribute to the lower shear force. Meanwhile, reduced oxidative damage to myoglobin was associated with the redder and brighter meat color observed in MS pigs [27].

The essence of marbling is the content and distribution of IMF, which is mainly composed of TG and DG [28], such as TG (18:0_16:0_18:1), TG (16:0_18:1_18:2) and DG (36:2e). The filling of IMF can destroy the structure of muscle fiber bundles, reduce the cross-linking of collagen and muscle fibers, and directly reduce the shear force [29]. PLCB1 is the core enzyme of the phospholipid signaling pathway, which can specifically hydrolyze PC in lipids to generate DG [30]. DG binds with fatty acids under the action of triglyceride synthase, generates TG, and stores it in intermuscular adipocytes [31]. The high expression of PLCB1 in MS pigs was positively correlated with TG enrichment and may contribute to the more obvious marbling. Fatty acid β-oxidation pathway modulates IMF homeostasis through carnitine metabolism. L-carnitine and acetylcarnitine function to transport long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation [32], and their synthesis relies on the synergistic action of three core genes (ASS1, DMGDH, MAT1A) in the carnitine biosynthesis pathway. Specifically, ASS1 catalyzes the synthesis of arginine, which is the precursor of carnitine synthesis [33]. DMGDH is involved in glycine metabolism, glycine provides amino groups for carnitine synthesis [34]. MAT1A converts methionine into S-adenosylmethionine to provide methyl for carnitine methylation [35]. Coordinated expression of these three genes enhances carnitine synthesis and accelerates fatty acid β-oxidation, which prevents excessive TG accumulation and makes the marbling moderate.

In this study, the strategy of “WGCNA module screening, multi-omics association analysis and core gene intersection” was adopted, which effectively avoided the limitations of single omics analysis. WGCNA clustered 2402 lipid molecules into 13 modules, and accurately mapped the turquoise and green modules strongly related to meat quality traits, which narrowed the scope for subsequent lipid-gene-metabolite association analysis. In the correlation analysis, we combined the expression abundance and function for screening. At the same time, we set strict thresholds such as |r|> 0.99 and p-value < 0.05 were set to ensure the reliability of the core gene. This idea of “focusing on key modules first and then conducting multi-dimensional association” can provide a methodological reference for multi-omics research of other complex traits. Among the 19 genes finally screened, the expression levels of seven genes: ALDOB, CYP2E1, CYP2A13, PLCB1, ASS1, DMGDH, MAT1A were consistent with the phenotype, and their annotation functions were closely related to the core pathways regulating pork quality (such as glycolysis, lipid metabolism, resistance to oxidative stress), so they were identified as key candidate regulatory genes for pork quality. The remaining 12 genes were speculated to be also involved in the regulation of pork quality, but the potential regulatory mechanisms based on the currently known pathways are not fully elucidated and need further study.

This study provides a clear target for the mining of excellent genes in local pig breeds and the improvement of meat quality in commercial pig breeds. In the future, through molecular marker-assisted selection (MAS), the excellent alleles of CYP2E1, CYP2A13, and other genes in MS pigs can be introduced into other pig breeds to improve meat quality, or select individuals with high abundances of key molecules (e.g., TG (18:0_16:0_18:1), L-carnitine, L-propionylcarnitine, acetylcarnitine) for selective breeding.

As a local pig breed in China, MS pigs focus more on meat flavor and adaptability, while DLY pigs focus on growth rate and feed conversion rate. The difference in breeding direction leads to the “selective retention” of meat quality regulatory genes. In contrast, in order to pursue rapid growth, DLY pigs may sacrifice gene loci related to oxidative stress resistance in breeding, resulting in their meat quality being vulnerable to oxidative damage. This phenomenon poses a key problem in animal breeding: When animal husbandry production focuses on high-efficiency traits such as high growth and high feed conversion, how to achieve a balance between production efficiency and meat quality genetic resources? The genetic difference between MS and DLY pigs highlights that the local breeds may be “genetic reservoirs” of meat quality-related gene loci. Therefore, it is of great significance to continue to explore genes related to meat quality.

Of course, there are still some limitations in this study. Firstly, the sample size is small, with only 6 samples in each group, which may lead to deviations in the detection of some low-abundance lipids or metabolites. Secondly, the functional verification of core genes still remains at the level of association analysis. Subsequent in vitro cell experiments need to be supplemented for verification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, MS pigs have better meat quality. Through the association analysis of transcriptome, metabolome, and lipidome, we found some key genes (ALDOB, CYP2E1, CYP2A13, PLCB1, ASS1, DMGDH, MAT1A, etc.) that may affect pork color, pH, marbling and tenderness. Our study provides a molecular basis for pig genetic breeding. However, the mechanisms of action and specific functions of these genes remain unclear and require further investigation.
