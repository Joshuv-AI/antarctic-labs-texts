Optimized technique for experimental multivisceral transplantation
Authors: J.C. Abate, P. Stringa, C. Largo, L. Vecchio, N. Lausada, M. Machuca, O. Camps, J. Sanchez-Verde Bilbao, F. Hernández Oliveros, A.M. Andrés Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere has been a shift toward combined liver-intestine and multivisceral transplantation (MVT), whereas isolated intestinal transplantation has taken a backseat, mainly because of its higher rejection rates. Despite this, experimental models tend to focus on intestinal transplantation or isolated liver transplantation, likely because of the technical complexity of MVT. This study aimed to outline a refined surgical protocol for MVT in rats.Materials and MethodsSyngeneic heterotopic MVT was performed in five Sprague–Dawley rat under sevoflurane anesthesia. The grafts included liver, stomach, pancreas, and small bowel tissues. These were anastomosed en bloc, end-to-end, to the infrarenal vena cava and aorta.ResultsA 100% survival rate was achieved. Initially, the clinical discomfort scores increased during the first 48 h but gradually improved by days 6–7, coinciding with weight recovery and normalization of behavior. Histological analysis revealed preserved architecture of the stomach, pancreas, and native organs. The intestinal tissue remained largely unchanged, with mild villous tip edema noted in one case. One grafted liver showed necrosis and hemorrhage, whereas the remaining grafts were viable, with mild congestion in two cases. Biochemical analyses revealed normal levels of total bilirubin, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, lipase, urea, and creatinine. Aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, alkaline phosphatase, and lactate dehydrogenase levels were elevated, but none reached statistical significance when compared with the pre-transplant values.ConclusionsThis model offers a dependable and reproducible platform for experimental MVT, providing a well-documented and accessible tool to encourage the incorporation of MVT into current translational and immunological research.

Introduction

In recent years, translational research has become increasingly important in organ transplantation. By connecting laboratory discoveries to clinical applications, research has provided valuable insights into improving patient care. It helps shape new treatment strategies and enhance transplant outcomes and innovation. The impact of translational research is evident in the development of advanced technologies and personalized approaches that are transforming how organ transplants are carried out and managed, offering new hope to patients worldwide.

In pediatric visceral transplantation, grafts that include both the liver and intestine are being used with increasing frequency, particularly compared with visceral transplants in adults. However, this clinical practice is not well represented in experimental research, and most studies have focused on isolated intestinal [1], [2] or hepatic transplantation models [3], [4], [5], [6]. This research gap underscores the need for experimental models that more closely mirror the complexities of multivisceral grafts [7], particularly those involving both the liver and small bowel, to better reflect real-world transplant scenarios [8].

Although procedures for multivisceral and combined transplantation in rodents have been described previously, scientific evidence using these models has been scarce in recent decades [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. Our aim is to add to the existing literature a detailed description of a heterotopic multivisceral transplantation (MVT) procedure in rats, in order to provide a useful tool for its implementation.

Materials and Methods

Animal´s Use and Care

Male and female Sprague–Dawley rat were selected, with an average weight of 268.8 ± 12 g for the donor and 435.8 ± 63 g for the recipient [17]. No immunosuppressive therapy was administered. The protocol was meticulously adjusted to align with the prevailing regulations in the European Union (EC86/L609) and provisions stipulated in RD 1201/2005. Animals were housed individually in a climate-controlled room (21 ± 2°C and relative humidity of 45 ± 15%) on a 12-h light–dark cycle at our institution’s animal facilities. Rat chow and water were provided ad libitum for 1 week of adaptation. Before the experiments, the donors were fasted for 12 h, with water provided ad libitum. After transplantation, recipients had unrestricted access to water and food. Surgical procedures were performed under inhalation anesthesia with 8% sevoflurane for induction and 2% for maintenance, with oxygen at 0.5–1 L/min. Postsurgical antibiotic therapy consisted of ceftriaxone (65 mg/kg/24 h). Buprenorphine (0.04 mg/kg/12 h) and meloxicam (2 mg/kg/24 h) were administered as analgesics. Local bupivacaine (5 mg/mL) was administered after laparotomy.

All procedures were performed by a single surgeon with experience in microsurgery and solid-organ transplantation in rats.

A video recording of the surgical technique was produced to provide a detailed visual explanation of the key steps (Video 1). The procedure was recorded with two cameras: a HY−2307 digital color microscope camera with a 48 MP, 1/2.33-inch CMOS image sensor with a multimedia interface, and a 3-megapixel Full HD 1080p webcam. A considerable portion of the video was played at three times its normal speed to ensure a suitable duration.

Presurgical Preparation and Common Procedures

The animal was placed in the supine position, and the abdomen was shaved and antiseptically prepared with 70% alcohol. A xiphopubic incision was made through the skin and aponeurosis, and abdominal retractors were placed bilaterally. The small intestinal loops were exteriorized to the left and covered with warm isotonic sodium chloride solution-moistened gauze.

Donor Procedure

The retroperitoneum supporting the small intestine was incised distal to the superior mesenteric artery, without dissecting the artery. The aorta (AO) and inferior vena cava (IVC) were dissected proximally from their bifurcation, and adhesions to the lumbar vessels were coagulated. The right renal artery, vein, and suprarenal pedicle were individually coagulated and divided. The left renal vein and proximal AO were dissected from their attachments and lumbar branches, extending beyond the celiac trunk (CT) while preserving the space between the superior mesenteric artery and CT anteriorly.

A 5 mL syringe was placed posteriorly beneath the upper abdomen, and two lateral retractors were secured to a Lone Star retractor fixed to the table in the cephalad region. The suspensory and right triangular ligaments of the liver, avascular adhesions, and the lesser omentum were divided. The spleen was then removed.

Prior to gastric transection, the gastric antrum was coagulated and ligated using 6–0 nylon sutures to ensure preservation of the gastric artery. The loops of the small intestine were displaced to the right, and the vasculature supplying the distal two-thirds of the small intestine was ligated and divided, retaining the proximal third as part of the graft. The remaining colon and small intestine were exteriorized to clear the operative field.

The hepatic artery and portal vein of the left lateral lobe and lateral middle lobe were dissected, ligated, and divided before removal of the liver parenchyma. The vasculature of the right medial lobe (RML) was identified and ligated before graft flushing to complete 70% hepatectomy [18], [19] (Fig. 1). The left renal vessels were coagulated and divided. Graft Extraction

The proximal AO was ligated above the CT with 6–0 nylon, clamped, and divided between the two points. The distal AO and IVC were clamped proximal to the bifurcation. The RML vessels were ligated using 6–0 nylon, and the IVC was divided midway between the left renal vein and its bifurcation.

The graft was flushed through the distal AO with 20 mL of cold heparinized lactated Ringer's solution (50 IU heparin) using a 10 mL syringe and 21 G needle at slow, constant pressure. An immediate color change from pink to a homogeneous pale tone was confirmed [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. The graft was then divided above the RML hepatic ligation and at the AO at a level corresponding to the IVC, retrieved, and immersed in lactated Ringer's solution at 4 °C (Fig. 2).

Back Table

With the graft immersed in lactated Ringer's solution at 4 °C, the vascular pedicles were inspected and trimmed as needed to ensure adequate surfaces for anastomosis. The AO was flushed with 10 mL of cold lactated Ringer's solution using a 10 mL syringe and 20 G catheter, while the IVC was simultaneously irrigated to ensure complete removal of residual clots. The absence of leaks and correct ligation of the superior vena cava were confirmed. The graft was wrapped in gauze and immersed in a cold solution until implantation (Fig. 3A).Fig. 3(A) Macroscopic appearance of the multivisceral graft during the back-table phase (cold ischemia). Recipient

Five milliliters of warm physiological solution were administered subcutaneously.

The infrarenal AO and IVC were meticulously dissected from the surrounding tissues, ensuring coagulation of the lumbar branches and sufficient space to facilitate anastomosis.

Vascular Anastomosis

Both anastomoses were performed end-to-side, beginning with the IVC-IVC followed by the AO-AO. The IVC and AO were clamped together sequentially, first proximally and then distally, and the sevoflurane concentration was reduced by 1%–1.5% during this period. For video-recording purposes, clamping and anastomosis were performed separately, first on the vein and then on the artery, to allow better visualization.

Both anastomoses were completed using a 9–0 nylon suture with a round-tip needle. Venotomy and arteriotomy were initiated with a 25 G needle puncture, extending the buttonhole cephalad and flushing with heparinized saline. Each anastomosis was constructed using a continuous suture along the posterior wall, followed by the anterior wall, with a final flush with heparinized saline prior to definitive closure.

Declamping was performed in reverse order, releasing the distal clamp first, followed by the proximal clamp. Adequate graft perfusion and strict hemostasis at both anastomotic sites were confirmed, and the graft was moistened with warm isotonic sodium chloride solution (Fig. 3B).

The transplanted organ block was subsequently positioned on the right side of the abdominal cavity to avoid compromising vascular supply. The previously exteriorized intestinal loops were reintroduced into the abdominal cavity on the left side.

A representative surgical scheme for the experimental MVT procedure, including the donor, graft composition, and recipient, is shown in Fig. 2.

Ostomy

The distal end of the small intestine was exteriorized through an ostomy created in the medial third of the right abdominal wall, approximately 2 cm from the midline. A buttonhole was fashioned in the muscular wall using blunt scissor dissection, and a small skin incision was made at the corresponding position using a clamp for elevation to match the intestinal diameter. The distal intestinal end was then brought through the ostomy and secured with approximately 6–8 interrupted 7–0 Prolene sutures, each incorporating the skin, aponeurosis, and full thickness of the intestinal wall.

Closure

If minor bleeding was observed during reperfusion, 5 mL of warm isotonic sodium chloride solution was administered subcutaneously. The laparotomy was then closed in two layers, aponeurosis and skin, using a continuous 3–0 nylon suture.

Postoperative Care

The animals were checked every 6–12 h for signs of pain and/or discomfort. As previously reported, a standardized quantitative score was used for clinical monitoring of the recipients. Briefly, animal weight, ocular secretions, hair appearance, and posture, among other parameters, were considered. Each recipient received a general score resulting from the addition of each evaluated parameter [25], [26]. Endpoint criteria were applied to animals with persistent signs of pain and/or discomfort.

Experimental Design

Immediate and long-term survival rates were assessed. In addition, the macroscopic and microscopic appearances of the graft were studied at different post-reperfusion times to validate the surgical technique.

Tissue samples were obtained from each transplanted organ (liver, stomach, pancreas, and small intestine) at the time of euthanasia. The corresponding native organs were biopsied to facilitate comparative analysis. All samples were fixed in 10% formalin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Blood samples were collected for subsequent analysis of various enzymes and chemicals, including aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase, lipase, urea, and creatinine.

Results

Surgical Times and Monitoring of the Recipients

The duration of donor surgery was 102 ± 2.7 min, whereas bench surgery lasted 12 ± 2.7 min and recipient surgery lasted 83 ± 4 min. The venous anastomosis time was 14 ± 0.7 min, and the arterial anastomosis time was 9.6 ± 0.5 min. The total cold ischemia time was 69.2 ± 7.6 min. Five transplantations were performed, resulting in a 100% survival rate. The tenth day was designated for euthanasia and sample collection. However, in one case, an endpoint was reached on the seventh day owing to a 20% loss of baseline body weight (Fig. 4A). The clinical discomfort score increased during the first 48 h, accompanied by piloerection, instances of nasal pigmentation, and general weight loss until the sixth or seventh day, when the participants began to regain weight. Post-Transplant Graft Histopathological Study

Histopathological analyses were performed on the transplanted organs. No abnormalities were observed in the pancreas or stomach, both of which maintained normal architecture and histological features throughout the post-transplantation period (Fig. 5A and 5B). Similarly, intestinal analysis revealed no significant changes, with enterocytes and intestinal villi remaining unaffected. However, in one of five cases, mild edema was observed at the tip of the villi (Fig. 5C). In addition, transplanted livers were analyzed. Only one case exhibited major lesions characterized by necrosis and hemorrhage. The remaining grafts appeared viable with preserved architecture, and mild congestion was observed in only two cases (Fig. 5D). Post-Transplant Serum Measurements

Elevated levels of aspartate aminotransferase (175.25 ± 19.5 IU/L), alanine aminotransferase (55.75 ± 36.1 IU/L), alkaline phosphatase (235.6 ± 148.2 IU/L), and lactate dehydrogenase (292 ± 66.3 IU/L) were detected. Conclusions

The heterotopic MVT model described in this study proved to be feasible and reproducible, yielding good long-term survival rates consistent with those previously reported for other experimental intestinal transplantation models. Histological evaluation confirmed normal or minimally altered architecture of the transplanted organs beyond 1 week post-transplant, supporting the validity and reliability of this technique. Heterotopic models offer several practical advantages over orthotopic transplants, including improved recipient survival, a less complex postoperative course with less clinical compromise for the recipient, and easier access to the graft through ostomies, facilitating serial sampling and rigorous immunobiological monitoring. Therefore, this model is a valuable experimental tool for investigating key aspects of organ transplantation, including ischemia-reperfusion injury, acute and chronic rejection, graft-versus-host disease, and immunological tolerance. By providing a standardized and reproducible platform, this approach contributes to the advancement of translational research and may ultimately support the development of improved therapeutic strategies for transplant-related complications.

Undoubtedly, the novel contributions made by Murase and Zhong in the 1990s remain seminal references for all teams performing microvascular microsurgical models of intestinal transplantation and MVT [10], [11], [12]. Nevertheless, follow-up studies addressing complex techniques, such as transplantation, in experimental animals are warranted. One of the main contributions of this article is the detailed description of each step of the surgical procedure, including the preparation and development of the donor, the back table surgery, and the recipient, as well as postoperative monitoring and care. This article serves as a step-by-step guide for learning and reproducing the heterotopic MVT technique in rats to achieve acceptable survival rates. To further this objective, we also provide a complete video of the MVT, which is a valuable tool for surgeons wishing to reproduce the model and which clarifies the technique in a simple and conclusive manner.

A distinctive feature of the present study is its emphasis on the meticulous description of each surgical step supported by video documentation, which ensures that the procedure can be reliably reproduced by any investigator with sufficient technical expertise. We believe that the technical complexity inherent in MVT, together with the specialized requirements of experimental animal handling, has historically limited the widespread adoption of this model. By providing a comprehensive and transparent methodological account, this study aims to lower the barriers to entry for other research groups and encourage the incorporation of experimental heterotopic MVT into current immunological and translational research.
