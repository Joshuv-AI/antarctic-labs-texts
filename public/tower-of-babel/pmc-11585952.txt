Genome assembly of an endemic butterfly (Minois aurata) shed light on the genetic mechanisms underlying ecological adaptation to arid valley habitat
Authors: Wenqian Hu, Yi Wang, Xiaoxiao Chen, Jialong Huang, Jingge Kuang, Lei Wang, Kangshan Mao, Liang Dou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Hengduan Mountains, one of the global biodiversity hotspots with exceptional species richness and high endemism, contains numerous arid valleys that create a distinctive geographical and ecological landscape. However, the adaptive evolutionary mechanisms of organism in the arid valley remain poorly understood. Minois aurata, an endemic butterfly species found exclusively in the arid valley of the upper Minjiang River, represents an attractive model system for studying adaptive evolutionary mechanisms to arid valley environments.ResultsHere, we present the first chromosome-level genome assembly for Minois aurata, with a total size of approximately 609.17 Mb, and a scaffold N50 size of 23.88 Mb. These scaffolds were further clustered and anchored onto 29 chromosomes based on Hi-C data. A total of 16,163 protein-coding genes were predicted, of which 91.83% were functionally annotated. The expansion of transposable elements (TEs) accounts for the relatively large genome size of M. aurata, potentially aiding its adaptation to environmental conditions. Phylogenomic analyses based on 3,785 single-copy genes revealed that M. aurata is most closely related to Hipparchia semele. Further mitochondrial genome analysis of four Minois species placed M. aurata in a basal position within the genus, supporting it as an independent species. A total of 185 rapidly evolving and 232 specific gene families were identified in M. aurata. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these gene families were mainly associated with ultraviolet radiation, heat and hypoxia responses. We also identified 234 positive selected genes in M. aurata, some of which are related to compound eye photoreceptor development, osmotic stress, and light stimulus response. Demographic analysis indicated that the effective population size of M. aurata decreased around 0.4 and 0.04 million years ago, respectively, coinciding with the localized sub-glaciation.ConclusionThe chromosome-level genome offers a comprehensive genomic basis for understanding the evolutionary and adaptive strategies of Minois aurata in the unique arid valley environment of the Hengduan Mountains, while also providing valuable insights into the broader mechanisms of organism adaptation to such habitats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11058-8.

Introduction

The Hengduan Mountains (HDMs), located at the eastern margin of the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau sensu lato (QTPsl), stand as a biodiversity hotspot and a significant center of endemism [1, 2]. Since the late Miocene, the uplifts of the HDM and the descent of the river valley contributed to highly rugged terrain and environmental heterogeneity with climate fluctuation in this region [3–5]. Deep valleys carved through the mountains have given rise to distinct arid valley ecosystems. The lush, moist windward side of the range, rich in rainfall, is a stark contrast to the leeward side, where the water vapor condenses in the deep valley, causing heat release, reducing the water availability, and increasing the air temperature, resulting in a climate defined by warm, high evaporation and scant rainfall [6, 7]. Over millions of years, the HDMs have nurtured many highly adaptable animals and plants, which have evolved a series of specialized adaptation mechanisms to thrive in a variety of challenging environments. However, past studies on adaptability to the HDMs environment have primarily focused on plants, mammals, and other vertebrates [8–10]. There is still a lack of understanding regarding the adaptive evolution mechanisms of insects in arid valley environments.

Mountain butterflies, an attractive and iconic insect group, exhibit heightened sensitivity to climate change due to their narrow climatic niche [11–13], making them excellent biological indicators for studying adaptability in arid valley environments.

The QTPsl, especially Hengduan Mountains, harbors a vast array of butterfly species, serving as a central hub for butterfly diversity, origin, and dispersal [14, 15]. However, genomic studies on these butterflies are still limited. The genus Minois, a group within the Satyrinae subfamily of the Nymphalidae family, features distinctively distributed species including widespread species in the Palearctic zone and endemic species in mountains of southwestern China (Fig. 1). This group typically feeds on grasses (Poaceae), has one generation per year, exhibits female reproductive diapause, overwinters as larvae, and shows sexual dimorphism. Minois aurata is a medium-sized xerothermophilous butterfly that inhabits arid or rapidly draining soils. It is endemic to the arid valley regions of the upper Minjiang River, the Hengduan Mountains (Figs. 2 and 1) [16]. It is a species that has received very little attention and is hardly found in biological catalogs. However, to the best of our knowledge, compared to many endangered species, such as Bhutanitis thaidina and Troides aeacus, it has a much narrower distribution range and more extreme habitat conditions. It deserves more focused attention and conservation efforts. In the context of global warming, the harsh conditions of arid valleys are intensifying. Simultaneously, the extinction of insects due to climate change has been widely reported over recent decades. Thus, understanding how species adapt to the conditions in arid valley environments is critical for global biodiversity protection in a warming future. Habitats of M. aurata are characterized by intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation, higher temperature, and less precipitation [6], which together provide a unique environment to study adaptive evolution. Previous studies on M. aurata have been scarce and primarily focused on morphological classification. High-quality genomes are required for studies that span the biological sciences, from gene family research to macroevolutionary phylogenetics and demographic history. However, available genomes vary widely in quality and taxonomic coverage [19, 20]. Here, we assembled the first chromosome-level genome of the butterfly genus Minois using PacBio Sequel Revio long-read sequencing and Hi-C technology. We aimed to explore the genomic mechanisms underlying the adaptative evolution of M. aurata to the arid valley habitat by conducting a series of bioinformatics analyses. This high-quality reference genome will provide essential information for further molecular genetic studies and will be invaluable for evolutionary research and conservation efforts concerning butterfly species.

Results

Genomic sequencing and assembly

Based on K-mer frequency analysis with 50.62 Gb Illumina short reads (~ 83 ×), the genome size of M. aurata was estimated to be 611.52 Mb (K = 17, Fig. S1, Table S1). To obtain a high-quality of genome, we first generated a total of 31.3 Gb (~ 51 ×) PacBio long-reads (Table 1), with an average length of 15,947 bp and an N50 length of 15,767 bp. The assembled genome size was ~ 614.37 Mb with an N50 contig length of ~ 23.85 Mb (Table 1). We then used 113 Gb (~ 185 ×) Hi-C reads to assist the assembly correction. Consequently, nearly 99.15% (609.17 Mb) of the total sequence length were anchored to the 29 chromosomes, with scaffold N50 length of 23.88 Mb (Fig. 3a and b, Table S2). The BUSCO analysis revealed that 98% (including 97% single-copy and 1% duplicated BUSCOs) of complete BUSCO genes were covered in M. aurata genome assemblies. In addition, about 99.84% of PacBio long reads could be properly mapped onto the genome assembly. Together these results indicate the relatively high completeness and continuity of our newly generated M. aurata genome.

Gene annotation

The M. aurata genome contains massive repeat sequences, comprising approximately 62.82% of the whole genome. Transposable elements (TEs) account for around 55.42% (337.6 Mb) of the M. aurata genome, with DNA transposons being the most abundant group (29.01%; 176.97 Mb) (Fig. 3b). Followed by long terminal repeat-retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) at 22.79% and Non-LTR represent a smaller fraction, comprising only 3.58% of the genome (Table S4). For function annotation, in total, 16,163 protein-coding genes were predicted in the M. aurata genome, of which 91.83% had blast hits in the eggNOG, NR, SwissProt, GO and KEGG databases (Table S5, Fig. S2). Furthermore, 8,072 genes were assigned to GO terms and 7,515 genes were mapped to at least one KEGG pathway (Table S5). The mean lengths of exons, introns, and coding sequence (CDS) were 339, 2739 and 1655, respectively, while the average number of exons and introns per gene was 7.7 and 6.6, respectively (Table S6). Variations in gene density were observed across different chromosomes, and the highest gene density was detected on chromosome 26 (Fig. 3a). BUSCO was employed to predict protein sequence for genomes with integrity of 96.7% in protein model, attesting to the high-quality annotation of the genome. Finally, we totally identified 704 ncRNAs in the genome of M. aurata, including 106 ribosomal RNAs, 66 microRNAs, 15 small nucleolar RNAs, 83 small nuclear RNAs, and 434 transfer RNAs (Table S7).

Chromosome synteny

A total 18,741 collinear genes pairs between Minois aurata and Maniola hyperantus were identified, which accounting for 64.79% of all annotated genes, indicating a high level of collinearity (Fig. 3c). Chr21 may underwent chromosomal breakage and recombination events. The fusion events on Chr02 and Chr05 exhibit collinearity with the Mh2 of M. hyperantus genome. Additionally, through the analysis of chromosomal collinearity and the identification of conserved protein markers, we have successfully identified the Z chromosome among the 29 chromosomes. Our results indicated that chromosome 17 of M. aurata is syntenic to the Z chromosome of M. hyperantus. By matching the protein sequences of the M. aurata genome to eight conserved Z-linked genes (ldh, ftz-f1, ap, cat, ybp, tpi, kettin and titin2) of Bombyx mori [21], we further confirm that chromosome 17 of M. aurata represents the Z chromosome (Fig. 3c).

Phylogenetic analysis at the mitochondrial and nuclear genome level

The genus Minois consists of four primary species (Fig. 1). In our study, we assembled the mitochondrial genomes of Minois aurata and Minois nagasawae, with lengths of approximately 15,195 bp and 15,207 bp, respectively (Fig. S3). These genomes exhibited sequence similarities of 96.98% and 97.43% with that of Minois dryas (NCBI accession No. NC046591, Fig. S4), respectively. Phylogenetic analysis based on mitochondrial genome revealed that all four species of Minois clustered into a monophyletic group, and M. aurata was on the basal position of the genus The divergence time between M. aurata and the common ancestor of the other three Minois species was approximately 6.96 million years ago (Mya; 95% HPD = 5.17–8.89 Mya). Subsequently, M. nagasawea, which is endemic to the high mountains of Taiwan, diverged with its sister clade around 4.86 Mya (95% HPD = 3.5–6.29 Mya). The widespread Palearctic species M. dryas and the Tibetan endemic species M. paupera diverged approximately 3.23 Mya, (95% HPD = 2.2–4.33 Mya). Subsequently, we identified 3785 one-to-one orthologous genes from the gene family analysis to infer deeper phylogenetic relationships. The results showed that M. aurata and Hipparchia semele are closely related, both belonging to the tribe Satyrina. Molecular dating suggests that subfamily Satyrinae diverged from its sister clade (in the Nymphalidae family) around 59.3 Mya (95% HPD = 50.89–67.55 Mya). Furthermore, the ancestor of tribe Satyrina, including M. aurata and H. semele, separated from its sister clade (of subfamily Satyrinae) at about 23.53 Ma (95% HPD = 19.83–27.65 Mya) (Fig. S5).

Expansion and contraction of gene families

Compared the gene families with another 12 Lepidoptera species, we identified 571 expanded, 1995 contracted and 185 rapidly evolving gene families, respectively (Fig. 3a and b, Table. S8). A total of 232 species-specific genes families of M. aurata were detected (Fig. 3c, Table S9), and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for these gene families showed that they are predominantly enriched in pathways involved in the cellular response to UV (GO:0034644), UV-damage excision repair (GO:0070914), heat response (GO:0009408), retinal metabolic process (GO:0042574) and response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979) (p < 0.05, Fig. 4d, Table S10). In addition, the rapidly evolving gene families are significantly associated with negative regulation of photoreceptor cells (GO:0046533), regulation of the force of heart contraction (GO:0002026), kidney development (GO:0001822), temperature homeostasis (GO:0001659), response to DDT (GO:0046680) and regulation of renal sodium excretion (GO:0035813) (p < 0.05, Fig. 4e, Table S11). It should be noticed that the expanded, rapidly evolving and species-specific genes with significant KEGG pathways are related to the organismal systems, environmental Information processing and metabolism, similarly to the significantly enriched GO terms (p < 0.05, Table. S14-S16).

Positively selected genes in xerothermophilous butterflies

We used single-copy genes to detect the signatures of positively selected genes (PSGs) for each of the thirteen Lepidoptera species (branch-site likelihood ratio test with a p-value < 0.05). A total of 234 PSGs in the genomes of M. aurata and H. semele were identified through our strict screening criteria (Table S11). We further investigated the identified PSGs in M. aurata and H. semele including oc, mus201, ninaC, Ric, upf1, Pmm2 (Table S12). These genes primarily involved in compound eye photoreceptor development [22], UV-damage repair [23], response to light stimulus [24], response to heat, osmotic stress and oxidative stress [25], and involved in cellular response to hypoxia [26], which were consistent with the functions of the unique and rapidly evolving gene families.

Demographic history analyses

We employed PSMC method to infer changes in population size (Ne) over time for 4 individuals of M. aurata. The ancestral Ne of M. aurata shows a peak at ~ 0.5 Mya followed by two moderate declines, coinciding with two localized sub-glaciation during the Pleistocene (Fig. 5). The first decline occurred ~ 0.4 Mya, coinciding with the Erdaoping sub-glaciation [27, 28], then reaching its lowest Ne between 0.1 and 0.2 Mya. The second decline occurred ~ 0.04 Mya, coinciding with the Zagunao sub-glaciation.

Discussion

Organisms that live in the arid valley face a variety of abiotic stresses and presumably have been subjected to a series of adaptive evolutionary changes, yet few is known about genomic mechanisms during these processes. Here, we assembled a high-quality genome of M. aurata, representing the first chromosome-level of assembly in the genus Minois, aimed to investigate its adaptive evolution at genomic level by comparing with published genomes of 12 Lepidoptera species. Compared to previously reported genomes of the Satyrinae subfamily, our new generated M. aurata genome exhibits the highest N50 value and the lowest number of contigs, demonstrating clear improvements in contiguity and accuracy (Table S2).

First, comparison among genome size and composition of the Satyrinae subfamily suggest that the expansion of transposable elements explains the relatively large genome size of M. aurata, and this may have acted as one of the factors that facilitate its environmental adaptation. The genome sizes of eight species the Satyrinae subfamily vary significantly, ranging from 334 Mb to 618 Mb [29]. The genome size of M. aurata is 609.17 Mb, representing a relatively large genome in Satyrinae. The expansion of repetitive sequences, particularly transposable elements (TEs), is one of the most critical factors contributing to the enlargement of genome size, a phenomenon well-documented in many insects [17]. In the case of M. aurata, we identified approximately 62.82% (382.68 Mb) of its assembled genome as consisting of repetitive sequences, which is notably higher than that of most currently sequenced genomes within the Satyrinae subfamily (Table S3). The high percentage of repetitive sequences, particularly TEs (55.42% of genomes), is likely a major contributor to its genome size enlargement in M. aurata. We compared the transposable element content across 16 different butterfly species and found that M. aurata exhibits significantly higher levels of DNA and LTR elements. In contrast to the predominance of LINE elements in Parnassius species, the dominance of DNA elements (28.5%) in M. aurata likely contributes to genome size variation (Table S17, Fig S6), a pattern similarly observed in Coleopteran families [30]. In animals, TE insertions can lead to the changes of gene expression, chromatin accessibility or cellular signaling pathways, influencing the evolution and adaptation of species [31]. Abundant reported cases suggested that TE insertions hold great potential to improve adaptability of insects, for example, the acquisition of insecticide resistance [32–34], gene expression regulations [35], or the evolution of climate adaptation [14, 36–38]. We therefore hypothesize that the expansion of TEs and the accompanied TE insertions might contribute to the environment adaptation of M. aurata.

Second, we have demonstrated that M. aurata is an independent species that diverged from the other three samples congeners during the late Miocene. The taxonomic status of M. aurata has been subject to differing interpretations. While some researchers have classified M. aurata Oberthür as a variety or subspecies of M. paupera Alphéraky, recent morphological studies support its recognition as a distinct species. However, the lack of available mtDNA data for M. aurata limits a clearer understanding of its taxonomic position [18, 39]. In this study, we have assembled the mitochondrial genomes of M. aurata (15,195 bp) and M. nagasawae (15,207 bp) and complied mitochondrial genomes of two species from previously publications to reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships of four species within the genus Minois based on mitochondrial genomes, found that M. aurata occupies a basal position within Minois, supported the taxonomic treatment that M. aurata is an independent species. In addition, the cladogenetic event between M. aurata and the other three Minois species occurred during the late Miocene (~ 5.17–8.89 Mya), which is coincides with the rise of the HDMs region [5], with consequent profound changes in environment and diversification of organisms through the influence of topography, temperatures and precipitation [40]. The geographical isolation caused by mountain uplift and the local warm and dry climate of the Minjiang river valley may have led to the differentiation of the endemic species in the arid valley.

Third, M. aurata, known in Chinese as the “High Mountain Snake-eyed Butterfly”, residing in the arid valleys along the Minjiang River, exhibits genomic adaptations potentially linked to its unique environmental conditions. The genome-wide phylogenetic tree shows that M. aurata is most closely related to H. semele, both of which are xerothermophilous butterflies. Gene family analysis found that M. aurata has undergone a relatively high number of rapid gene family changes, possibly indicating its ability to rapidly adapt to specific functions or environmental (Fig. 2c, Table S8). Further detection of positively selected genes in xerothermophilous butterflies (234), along with GO and KEGG enrichment of expanded (571), rapidly evolving (185) gene families, and species-specific genes (232), has revealed genetic adaptations in M. aurata associated with heat response, radiation response, phototransduction in compound eyes, hypoxic adaptation, and osmoregulation (Fig. 4, Table S12, S14 to S16), which were consistent with previous studies of high-altitude insects [41, 42]. These genetic features likely correlate with the region’s abundant solar and thermal resources, intense ultraviolet radiation (9 ± 2% per 1000 m) [43], rapid soil drainage, and generally arid and hot climate conditions [44]. Additionally, the vertical migration capabilities of M. aurata are crucial for adapting to the wide altitudinal range (1,326 to 5,922 m) of this area, which may have led them to evolve genes related to the hypoxic conditions at higher elevations. Overall, M. aurata has evolved a series of genes through long-term natural selection that promote environmental adaptation, enabling it to better survive and reproduce in the arid valley habitats.

At last, although M. aurata has experienced two population contraction in its demographic history, it is high likely that its current narrow distribution is mainly caused by human activities, and conservation efforts are needed to reduce its extinction risk. The distribution of the M. aurata is now limited to the Zagunao River Valley of the Minjiang River in the Longmen Mountain Range, which is located on the northeastern edge of the HDMs. The highly rugged mountain ranges and deep gorges of the HDMs, in contrast to the QTPsl plateau platform and the Himalayas, were less affected by the Quaternary glaciations. These valleys likely provided multiple refuges for populations of various organisms during the Quaternary climatic oscillations [45]. For M. aurata, we identified two instances of moderate declines in effective population size (Ne). These population contraction may be associated with sub-glacial periods unique to this region, such as the Erdaoping and Zagunao sub-glacial periods [27]. Following the glacial periods, relatively warmer climates facilitated the recovery of the butterfly’s populations. However, currently the species have a relatively narrow distribution, which is in sharp contrast with its large effective population size. It is high likely that human activities such as land use changes, herding, farming, have caused habitat lost, fragmentation and degradation of this butterfly, and hence become endemic and threatened. We advocate that thorough study of population structure and genetic diversity, combining with thorough field survey of geographic distribution, population size and extinction risk assessment following the IUCN Red List criteria, should be conducted to better understand this species to provide a better conservation and management of its populations.

Methods

Insect samples

A total of twelve adult M. aurata individuals were collected from Zagunao Valley, a tributary of the Minjiang River in Sichuan Province, China, between July and August 2023. After in vivo starvation treatment, all samples immediately transferred into liquid nitrogen and stored in an ultra-cold storage freezer at −80℃ in the College of Life Sciences, Sichuan University.

Genomic sequencing and assembly

We selected one adult individual for PacBio HiFi sequencing to generate the initial genome assembly, 1 for Illumina genome survey, 7 for Hi-C sequencing to map chromosome structure, and 3 for DNBseq to estimate demographic history. One individual underwent Illumina RNA-seq for transcriptome analysis to assist with genome annotation by capturing gene expression and alternative splicing.

Genomic DNA was extracted from tissue samples using the CTAB method, and transcriptome RNA using TRIzol™ Reagent. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), gel electrophoresis, and a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen). RNA integrity, quality, and concentration were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies).

For PacBio sequencing, a 15 kb library was constructed using the SMRTbell® Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, PN 101–853-100) and sequenced on the PacBio Revio platform. For Illumina genome survey, a 150 bp paired-end (PE150) library with an insert size of 350 bp was constructed using standard Illumina protocols and sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. The Hi-C library was prepared following previously described methods [46, 47] and sequenced in PE150 mode on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. For resequencing of three individuals, the MGIEasy FS DNA Prep Kit (BGI, China) was used for library construction, and sequencing was performed in PE150 mode on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform at Smartgenomics Technology Institute (Tianjin, China). Additionally, the RNA-seq library was prepared using the TruSeq Illumina protocol and sequenced in PE150 mode on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

To minimize artificial bias, we filtered adapter and low-quality sequences in the raw PacBio polymerase reads by removing: (i) reads shorter than 50 bp; (ii) reads with a quality value below 0.8; and (iii) reads containing self-ligated adaptors. Adapter sequences within polymerase reads were also removed. CCS reads were generated using SMRTLink 9.0 software with the parameters --min-passes = 3 and --min-rq = 0.99. A k-mer analysis (K = 17) was performed on M. aurata Illumina paired-end libraries to estimate genome size, repeat sequences, and heterozygosity rate, with k-mer depth calculated using jellyfish v1.1.11 [48]. The draft genome assembly was generated using Hifiasm v0.15.2 [49] with CCS (Circular Consensus Sequencing) reads. Minimap2 v2.26 [50] was used for read mapping, and Purge_Dups v1.2.5 [51] was employed to remove redundant heterozygous regions. Contaminant sequences were excluded by alignment with the NT (nucleotide) library (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/db/FASTA/).

To generate linear chromosome-scale genome assemblies of M. aurata, we first filtered the Hi-C data by following criteria: (i) reads with 3nt unidentified nucleotides (N); (ii) reads aligned to the adaptor; (iii) reads with ≥ 20% bases having phred quality ≤ 5). After passing all quality control assessments, the filtered Hi-C reads were aligned to the initial draft genome using HICPRO v2.11.4 [52]. Only uniquely mapped and valid paired-end reads were used for assembly with 3D-DNA v180922 [53]. Scaffolds were manually ordered to finalize the chromosome assembly using Juicebox v1.11.08 [54]. Contact maps were plotted with HiCExplorer v3.7.2 [55].

Post-assembly quality assessment was performed. For DNA sequencing data, minimap2 software v2.13 was used to align HiFi Reads to evaluate the completeness and accuracy. The contamination evaluation was evaluated by the read depth and GC content with 10Kb windows. We used the lepidoptera_odb10 database (n = 5,286) in Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) [56] to evaluate genome assembly completeness.

Gene annotation

The combination of de novo and homology-based method was used to repeat elements prediction. For de novo annotation, MITE-Hunter v1.0 (-n 5 -c20) [57] was used to identify the mini-inverted repeat transposable elements (MITEs) which were widely present in the genome and belonging to type II transposable elements. LTRharvest v1.6.2 [58] and LTR Finder v1.07 (-D 15000 -d 1000 -L 7000 -l 100 -p 20 -C -M0.9) [59] were used to detected the long terminal repeated sequences (LTRs) in the genome and LTR retriever v2.9.0 [60] was used to integrate the prediction results of those two software. For homolog evidence, RepeatMasker v4.1.1 [61] was used to search the genome sequence for the sequence similar to the known repetitive sequence in the repetitive sequence database RepBase v20170127 (http://www.girinst.org/server/RepBase/index.php) to obtain the known repetitive sequence in the target genome. Combination the de novo and known repetitive sequences in the target genome, RepeatMasker v4.1.1 was used to mask the repetitive sequences of the target genome. RepeatModeler v2.0.2a [62] was used to de novo identify other repetitive sequences with repeat-masked genome. Finally repetitive sequences were obtained by the above multiple methods.

We used a comprehensive strategy combing homology-based, de novo predictions and transcriptome-based approaches to predict the high-quality protein coding genes. For homology-based prediction, GeMoMa v1.9 [63] was used to perform homology prediction and then obtain exon and intron boundary information based on the comparison between the transcript and the genome. The protein sequences of Kallima inachus, Papilio bianor, Bicyclus anynana, Maniola jurtina, Maniola hyperantus, Junonia coenia were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and InsectBase (http://v2.insect-genome.com/). For de novo prediction, AUGUSTUS v3.5.0 [64], SNAP v6.0 [65], GlimmerHMM v3.0.4 [66] and GeneMark-ET v4.32 [67] were used to predict gene structure in the repeat-masked genome. For transcriptome-based prediction, all RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) reads were initially aligned against to the reference genome by hisat2 v2.2.1 [68], and Cufflinks v2.2.1 [69] was used to assemble transcripts for obtaining the full-length transcript sequences. PASA v2.5.2 [70] was used to predict the open reading frame (ORF) based on the obtained full-length transcript sequence. Results from all three approaches were integrated with EVidenceModeler (EVM) [71], and UTR and other variable cut annotation was predicted by PASA v2.5.2.

All protein-coding genes were aligned to three integrated protein sequence databases: NR, SwissProt, eggnog-mapper v2.1.12 (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/) [72]. Protein domains were annotated by InterProscan v5.59 (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/download.html) and the Gene Ontology (GO) terms for each gene were obtained from the corresponding InterPro entry. The pathways in which the genes might be involved were assigned by Blastall v2.2.25 [73] against the KEGG databases (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/brite.html). The protein-coding gene functional annotation results were merged from the above methods.

Chromosome synteny

The collinearity relationship between M. aurata and M. hyperantus was established with a One − Step MCScanx of Tbtools v2.119 [74]: the CPU for BlastP was set to 2, the E − value was set to 1 × 10−10, and the number of blast hits was set to 0.5. We then put the links, gff, and multiple synteny files for visualization in Multiple Synteny Plot of TBtools with default parameters.

Identification of orthology

To investigate the evolutionary history of the M. aurata genome, the protein sequences of 13 Lepidoptera species were downloaded from NCBI or InsectBase (http://v2.insect-genome.com/), including Bombyx mori, Pararae aegeria, Erebia ligea, Maniola hyperantus, Hipparchia semele. Bicyclus anynana, Melitaea cinxia, Danaus plexippus, Lycaena phlaeas, Pieris rapae, Hesperia comma and Papilio bianor. Orthofinder v2.5.1 [75] was used to identify the orthologous and paralogous genes of the 13 Lepidoptera genomes.

Phylogenetic analysis at the mitochondrial and nuclear genome level

The mitochondrial genome of M. aurata was assembled through Hifiasm v0.18.5 [49]. Next, we aligned the mitochondrial genome, assembled using first-generation sequencing sequences and third-generation data, with MUMmer v4.0.0beta2 [76] to validate the circular features. The repetitive boundary structure (91–105 bp and 15,282 − 15,296) indicated the complete mitochondrial sequence (106 ~ 15,296 bp segment of Contig) was finally extracted. Finally, after adjustment with second-generation sequencing, we obtained a complete mitochondrial genome of 15,195 bp and annotated it using Geseq (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) [36] and MITOS2 Web Server [77]. For M. nagasawae, the mitochondrial genome was assembled from Illumina sequencing data using SPAdes v3.15 [78]. Gaps in the contigs were filled with GapFiller v1.11 [79], and sequences were corrected with PrInSeS-G v1.0.0 [80].

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using PhyloSuite v1.2.3 [81, 82] based on concatenated nucleotide sequences of 13 protein coding gene (PCGs) and two rRNAs. To construct phylogenetic tree, 12 mitochondrial genomes of Satyrinae species as ingroups, 11 from Minois, Oeneis, Davidina, Hipparchia, Maniola, Melanargia, Triphysa, Melanitis, Elymnias and 1 from Kallima as outgroups were downloaded (Table S13), and initially aligned in batches with mafft v7.429 [83]. Ambiguously aligned fragments within the alignments of the 13 PCGs were removed by Gblocks v0.91b [84], and the gap sites within the rRNA by trimAl v1.2rev57 [85]. Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenies were inferred using IQ-TREE v2.2.0 [86, 87] for 1000 bootstraps.

A total of 3785 single-copy conserved orthologues, identified by orthofinder v2.5.1 [75] and shared among 13 Lepidoptera species, were extracted for phylogenetic analysis. The protein sequences in each orthogroup were independently aligned by mafft v7.429 [83] and filtered by trimAL v1.4 [85] with default parameters. These sequences were concatenated to generate a supergene sequence, which was used for tree construction. The maximum likelihood tree was constructed using the RAxML v.8.2.12 [88], with a bootstrap value of 1000.

The MCMCtree program in PAML v4.9 [89] was used to estimate divergence time based on nucleotide/protein sequences. We then selected three fossil calibration points to apply in molecular dating at genome level based on previous reports: the crown of Papilionoidea (110.3–86.9 Ma) [90], the crown of Satyrinae (28.3 ~ 65 Ma) [91] and the root set to < 201 Ma, which was from the oldest known lepidopteran fossil [92] applied at the genome level. Two fossil: the crown of Satyrinae (28.3 ~ 65 Ma) [91] and Minois dryas with Oeneis urda (13.6 ~ 18.6 Ma) (http://www.timetree.org) were applied at the mitochondrial level. The trees were visualized using figtree v1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree).

Gene families analysis

Utilizing the gene families derived from OrthFinder v2.5.2, the CAFÉ v.4.2.1 (-p 0.01 -t 16) [93] was employed to discern gene family expansions, contractions, significant expansions, as well as species-specific gene families. The gene families were subjected to functional enrichment analysis for Gene Ontology (GO) with GO.database, which including muti-species databases by the topGO package (ontology = “Biological Process”, algorithm="weight01”, statistic = “fisher”) [94], and for KEGG enrichment conducted by tbtools v2.119 (with Animals.20211128.TBtoolsKeggBackEnd as backend file).

Identification of positively selected genes

We used PAML v4.9 [89] to detect positively selected genes (PSGs) on the xerothermophilous butterfly in the subfamily Satyrinae (M. aurata and H. semele) [95] branch using a branch-site model based on single-copy conserved orthologues of 13 Lepidoptera species. The two xerothermophilous species were set as the foreground branches, and the generalist species were set as the background branches. Then, we compare the zero model with the substitution model to find positive selection sites. Finally, candidate PSGs were annotated by EGGNOG as described above.

Demographic history analyses

To infer the population dynamics history more accurately, we additionally collected 3 individuals. Combined with the individual from the survey, a total of 4 individuals were analyzed. Changes in effective population size (Ne) for M. aurata was conducted using the Pairwise Sequentially Markovian Coalescent (PSMC) method [96]. Illumina paired-end reads to the genome assembly was performed using the bwa v0.7.17 mem algorithm [97]. Then the genotypes, vcf files and diploid sequence were produced by samtools v1.9 [98], bcftools v1.9 and vcfutils.pl respectively. We set the minimum depth of coverage is 10 and the maximum is 160. Finally, we estimated the atomic time interval was “28 × 2 + 3 + 5” and nucleotide substitution rate was 3.59e-09 per site per generation which based on previous butterfly genome studies [99, 100]. The generation time scaled assuming 1 year.
