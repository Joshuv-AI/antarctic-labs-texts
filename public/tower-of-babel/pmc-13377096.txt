Clinician engagement shapes the impact of AI-based ECG screening for chronic liver disease in primary care
Authors: Alberto Calleri, Yigit Yazarkan, Kan Liu, Blake A Kassmeyer, Ryan J Lennon, Puru Rattan, Amir Seid, Matthew E Bernard, Gagandeep Singh, Mark E Deyo-Svendsen, Graham King, Stephen K Stacey, Amy Olofson, Alina Allen, Joseph C Ahn, Paul A Friedman, Patrick S Kamath, Zachi I Attia, Peter A Noseworthy, Vijay H Shah, David Rushlow, Douglas A Simonetto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI)-based screening tools show promise for early identification of chronic liver disease (CLD), yet their effectiveness in real-world settings may depend on clinician response to AI-generated recommendations. We performed a post hoc analysis of the intervention arm of the pragmatic, cluster-randomized DULCE trial, in which primary care clinicians received electrocardiogram-based machine learning (ECG-ML) alerts indicating elevated risk for CLD. Clinicians were categorized as high engagement (HE; top quartile) or low engagement (LE), and diagnostic yield was defined as the proportion of ECG-ML-positive cases with confirmed CLD. Among 110 clinicians receiving ≥1 alert (1385 ECG-ML-positive patients), overall engagement was 29.8%. HE was associated with higher detection of advanced CLD (OR 2.12, 95% CI 1.36–3.30; p = 0.001) and any CLD (OR 2.59, 95% CI 1.83–3.68; p < 0.001) compared with LE. Diagnostic yield was 10.6% versus 2.9% for advanced CLD and 22.3% versus 5.0% for any CLD in HE versus LE (OR 2.99, 95% CI 1.73–5.16; p < 0.001 and OR 3.74, 95% CI 2.44–5.75; p < 0.001, respectively). These findings suggest that the effectiveness of AI-based screening may depend not only on algorithm performance but also on clinician engagement with AI recommendations and highlight the importance of accounting for engagement when designing and interpreting AI-enabled clinical trials. Introduction

Although the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) in gastroenterology is accelerating, its translation into routine clinical practice with meaningful patient benefit has remained limited1–3. There is growing interest in implementing AI to address the rising global burden of chronic liver disease (CLD), which is driven largely by metabolic dysfunction- and alcohol-associated liver disease4,5. Thus, an urgent need has been recognized for population-based screening approaches to enable earlier detection of fibrosis and cirrhosis in at-risk individuals, prompting calls for large-scale randomized trials to assess feasibility and effectiveness6. Screening for CLD in primary care is supported by guidelines and population-based studies, as earlier detection allows timely intervention and improves outcomes relative to later-stage diagnosis7. Given the high prevalence of liver disease in the general population, primary care clinicians play a central role in case-finding, especially where access to specialty care is limited8–10. Indeed, patients from rural settings may face worse health outcomes due to geographic isolation, limited medical infrastructure, provider shortage, and barriers to preventive care11. In these contexts, AI-assisted approaches could provide valuable support to enhance clinical decision-making and expand access to timely evaluation12. AI has the potential to facilitate earlier diagnosis and reduce health disparities, but its success requires careful real-world implementation3. Clinician confidence, often described as trust, is central to the adoption and appropriate use of AI systems and depends on transparency, performance, and clinical validation13.

The recently completed DULCE (Detection of Undiagnosed Liver Cirrhosis via AI-enabled Electrocardiogram) pragmatic cluster-randomized trial evaluated the effectiveness of an electrocardiogram-based machine learning (ECG-ML) algorithm for identifying patients with advanced CLD in routine primary care14. In this trial, 248 clinicians were randomized to access ECG-ML results, with outcomes evaluated over 6 months. Among 15,596 adults undergoing routine ECGs, detection of advanced CLD increased from 0.5% in the control group to 1.0% in the intervention group (OR 2.09; 95% CI 1.22–3.55; p = 0.007), and diagnoses of any stage of liver fibrosis increased from 0.5% to 1.7% (OR 3.17; 95% CI 1.86–5.40; p < 0.001).

However, the rate of follow-up testing after a positive ECG-ML alert was moderate, suggesting that real-world effectiveness may depend not only on model performance but also on clinician action. Therefore, this post hoc analysis evaluated whether higher engagement to recommended follow-up testing was associated with increased detection of CLD and examined factors associated with responses to ECG-ML alerts. This analysis seeks to clarify a key determinant of real-world AI effectiveness and to inform the design and implementation of future screening strategies in primary care.

Results

Baseline demographics and characteristics by engagement status

Of the 126 clinicians randomized to the intervention arm, 13 had no patients with ECG-ML-positive results during the trial and were therefore excluded from the current analysis. Three clinicians withdrew from the study at weeks 3, 8, and 20, citing unintentional enrollment, limited capacity to engage with the program and a high administrative burden, respectively. The remaining 110 clinicians received at least one AI alert and constituted the study cohort for engagement and outcome analyses (Fig. 1). Clinicians in the top quartile of engagement were classified as high engagement (HE; n = 27), and those in the lower three quartiles as low engagement (LE; n = 83). Among the 93 clinicians who completed the baseline survey, 62 (66.7%) were physicians, with a mean age of 45.4 years (SD 10.0) and 57% identifying as female. The majority (n = 85, 91.4%) practiced in family medicine, with an average of 13.7 years (SD 10.1) in clinical practice (Supplementary Table 1).

Study flowchart of the intervention arm of the DULCE (Detection of Undiagnosed Liver Cirrhosis via AI-enabled Electrocardiogram) pragmatic randomized trial.

A total of 8034 patients in the intervention arm underwent ECG testing, of whom 1385 had a positive ECG-ML result. The mean age was 69.8 years (SD 16.2), and 52.6% were women. Most participants were White (98.1%), non-Hispanic (97.7%), married (67.8%), and insured through Medicare or Medicaid (71.3%). Participants were recruited across urban and rural sites in Minnesota and Wisconsin, with 40.6% residing in rural areas. The cohort had a high prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, including hypertension (78.8%), diabetes (51.3%), and chronic kidney disease (40.5%), with a mean body mass index of 37.6 kg/m². Alcohol use was reported by 42.6%, and 52.1% had a current or former smoking history (Table 1).

The mean clinician engagement rate was 29.8%. Engagement in the HE group was 89.1% (244 actions out of 274 ECG-ML-positive alerts) versus 15.2% in the LE group (169 actions out of 1111 alerts). Engagement varied by site, with higher rates by clinicians practicing in urban Rochester sites (49%) and lower rates across rural sites (19.5–24.8%). HE and LE clinicians were similar in age, gender, race/ethnicity. No differences were found between internal medicine and family medicine as well as years of experience. Confidence in managing advanced liver disease did not differ between groups (20% vs 14.9% reporting “comfortable” or “completely comfortable,” p = 0.79). Frequent hepatology consultation (“often” or “always”) was more common among HE clinicians than LE clinicians (92% vs 72%, p = 0.047) (Table 2). Clinicians in the HE group managed fewer ECG-ML-positive patients than those in the LE group, with a median of 5.0 (IQR 4.0–10.5) compared with 14.0 (IQR, 6.0–17.5) in the LE group (p = 0.11). A trend toward an inverse correlation was observed between clinician engagement and the number of AI alerts per clinician (Pearson r = −0.17; 95% CI −0.35 to 0.02; p = 0.077) (Supplementary Fig. 1). In additional analyses adjusting for the number of ECG-ML-positive alerts per clinician, the association between engagement and outcomes remained consistent, suggesting that the observed findings were not solely explained by differences in workload.

Effectiveness of detecting chronic liver disease by engagement status

Follow-up liver assessment tests were ordered for 151 patients out of 274 ECG-ML+ (55.1%) in the HE group, compared with 101 patients out of 1111 ECG-ML+ (9.1%) in the LE group. In the HE group, clinicians provided a clinical rationale for not ordering follow-up tests in 69 patients, compared with 40 in the LE group (25.2% vs. 3.6%). The main reasons for not following AI-prompted recommendations included low clinical concern for cirrhosis, previously normal laboratory results or liver imaging, and other clinician judgment factors, such as advanced age, active malignancy, or time constraints (Supplementary Table 2). Additionally, patient preference influenced the decision not to order follow-up tests for 24 patients in the HE group and 28 in the LE group.

Among 1622 patients managed by HE clinicians, in the 6-month follow-up period post-ECG, 31 (1.9%) received a new diagnosis of advanced CLD, and 65 (4.0%) received a diagnosis of CLD with any stage of liver fibrosis. Among 6363 patients managed by LE clinicians, 45 (0.7%) were diagnosed with advanced CLD, and 73 (1.1%) were diagnosed with CLD with any fibrosis. Thus, high engagement was associated with significantly greater odds of detecting advanced CLD compared with low engagement (OR, 2.12; 95% CI, 1.36–3.30; p = 0.001). Similarly, the odds of detecting any stage of liver fibrosis were higher in the HE group than in the LE group (OR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.83–3.68; p < 0.001). When compared with the control group, the diagnosis rate of advanced CLD was significantly higher in the HE group (OR 3.88; 95% CI, 2.04–7.39; p < 0.001), whereas it did not differ significantly in the LE group (OR 1.42; 95% CI, 0.82–2.46; p = 0.21). However, both engagement groups diagnosed significantly more cases of any fibrosis compared with the control group (HE: OR 7.02; 95% CI, 3.90–12.64; p < 0.001; LE: OR 1.90; 95% CI, 1.12–3.23; p = 0.017) (Fig. 2). Diagnostic yield was 10.6% for advanced CLD and 22.3% for any CLD in the HE group, compared with 2.9% and 5.0% in the LE group. Accordingly, high engagement was associated with a significantly higher yield for both advanced (OR 2.99, 95% CI 1.73–5.16; p < 0.001) and any CLD (OR 3.74, 95% CI 2.44–5.75; p < 0.001). In patients with negative ECG-ML results, rates of follow-up test were low and did not differ between clinician engagement groups, occurring in 18 of 1348 patients (1.3%) managed by HE clinicians and 71 of 5252 (1.4%) managed by LE clinicians (p = 0.96). Diagnoses of advanced CLD were rare, identified in 2 patients (0.12%) in the HE group and 13 (0.20%) in the LE group (p = 0.50). Similarly, CLD with any fibrosis was infrequently diagnosed and occurred at low rates in both groups (0.25% in HE and 0.27% in LE; p = 0.88) (Table 3). In sensitivity analyses treating engagement as a continuous variable, higher engagement remained significantly associated with increased detection of CLD outcomes. For each 10% increase in engagement, the odds of detecting any CLD increased (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.19–1.34; p < 0.001), as did advanced CLD (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.12–1.30; p < 0.001). No association was observed among ECG-ML-negative patients.

Discussion

This post-hoc analysis suggests that clinician engagement with AI-driven recommendations may influence the effectiveness of ECG-based screening strategy for liver disease in primary care. In the DULCE trial, although the ECG-ML algorithm identified a substantial proportion of patients at elevated risk for advanced CLD, clinician engagement with recommended follow-up remained moderate and highly heterogeneous. In turn, higher engagement was significantly associated with increased utilization of liver disease assessments and a two-fold increase in the detection of advanced fibrosis relative to lower engagement. Compared with the control group lacking access to ECG-ML outputs, a four-fold increase in advanced CLD detection (the primary outcome of the trial) was observed among patients managed by HE clinicians, whereas no significant difference was evident in the LE group. By contrast, the diagnosis of any CLD was seven-fold higher in the high-engagement group and nearly doubled in the low-engagement group compared with controls. Importantly, even with lower AI engagement, the LE group still demonstrated higher odds of CLD diagnosis compared with the control group, underscoring the clinical relevance of identifying earlier stages of liver fibrosis as emerging antifibrotic therapies hold promise for halting disease progression15,16.

Despite longstanding consensus recommendations for liver fibrosis stratification in at-risk populations, implementation in primary care remains highly variable17. Although several blood-based screening algorithms exist, we previously reported that among patients who tested ECG-ML positive, only one-third had the basic laboratory values required to calculate commonly used noninvasive fibrosis scores, such as FIB-4. This underscores persistent gaps in routine risk assessment and highlights the limitations of guideline-driven screening strategies in real-world practice18. Consequently, a considerable proportion of patients with chronic liver disease continue to be diagnosed at advanced stages, often after the onset of hepatic decompensation19. These challenges support the potential role of AI-integrated screening modalities as complementary approaches to improve early detection4,20.

Several AI-assisted imaging tools have been reported in the literature; however, their clinical utility is often constrained by the high cost and limited accessibility of imaging modalities21. The DULCE trial introduced an ECG-based AI tool as a potential strategy to mitigate these limitations, thereby promoting the identification of previously unrecognized liver disease through opportunistic screening. However, the real-world effectiveness of this approach depends on clinician engagement, which in our study was lower than rates reported for other AI models in the literature22–24. This may reflect the counterintuitive use of ECGs for detecting liver cirrhosis, as clinicians are trained to interpret ECG findings within a cardiac framework rather than as signals of hepatic disease25–27.

Importantly, clinician engagement as defined in this study represents a composite construct reflecting not only clinician behavior but also patient preferences and clinical context. As such, engagement should not be interpreted as a direct measure of clinician performance, but rather as a reflection of real-world implementation of an AI-enabled screening strategy. This perspective acknowledges the inherent complexity of care delivery and emphasizes that the effectiveness of AI-based screening is strongly influenced by clinical context and shared decision-making28. Accordingly, AI-based risk scores warrant evaluation beyond internal and external validation, given the frequent attenuation of performance observed in real-world clinical settings3,29. Notably, clinicians in the HE group managed fewer ECG-ML-positive patients than those in the LE group, and a trend toward an inverse correlation was observed between clinician engagement and the number of AI alerts per clinician. This pattern suggests that alert volume and associated workflow burden may influence clinician responsiveness to AI-driven recommendations, although adjusted analyses suggest that this does not fully explain the observed associations. Indeed, evidence from a recent pragmatic trial conducted in the UK and published in The Lancet confirmed that AI effectiveness is diminished when integration into routine clinical workflows is suboptimal30,31. Consistent with our findings, this study demonstrated substantial variability in adoption across practices, with only 21% classified as medium or high users over 12 months, underscoring the gap between AI deployment and real-world clinical uptake.

The similar rates of liver disease assessment and diagnosis observed among patients with negative ECG-ML results indicate comparable baseline evaluation practices between groups. However, clinicians in the high-engagement group (who demonstrated a greater frequency of hepatology consultation) were more receptive to ECG-ML-based screening recommendations, which in turn led to increased follow‑up diagnostic testing. These findings suggest that responsiveness to ECG-ML screening may be influenced by clinical practice patterns and the availability of specialist referral pathways. In this regard, liver disease assessments were more frequently pursued following ECG-ML alerts in Rochester, an urban academic setting, likely due to greater familiarity with AI-enabled tools and enhanced access to hepatology clinics. Nevertheless, while the low cost and widespread availability of ECG-based AI may reduce certain financial and logistical barriers, inequities related to downstream evaluation and access to specialty care may persist and warrant targeted attention in future implementation studies. Finally, Rushlow and colleagues found that advanced practice providers, family medicine clinicians, and those caring for less clinically complex patients were more likely to be high adopters of AI for heart failure screening23,32. In contrast, we did not identify significant differences in clinician characteristics associated with engagement, which may reflect increasing AI awareness and broader adoption among clinical providers over time.

This study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, this analysis was post hoc, and comparisons across clinician engagement groups were observational. As such, residual confounding from unmeasured clinician-, or site-level factors cannot be excluded, despite accounting for clustering. Second, participating clinicians were enrolled from a health care system in which AI utilization is relatively widespread, which may limit generalizability to other practice settings. Indeed, successful AI implementation depends heavily on human and organizational factors, including clinician trust in the technology, supportive social and governance structures, and regulatory frameworks that enable integration into routine workflow5,33. Third, clinician survey data were incomplete, which limited the power to detect associations between provider characteristics and engagement. Fourth, follow-up was limited to six months, precluding assessment of longer-term clinical outcomes, health care utilization, and the additional clinical workload associated with delayed diagnosis. Moreover, clinician survey data were incomplete (e.g., confidence in using AI, workload), with a higher proportion of missing responses among LE clinicians, potentially introducing response bias in analyses of clinician characteristics. Finally, although patient preference was documented as a reason for non-engagement, the relative contributions of patient and clinician factors to incomplete follow-up testing could not be fully differentiated, thereby limiting causal interpretation.

In conclusion, this analysis demonstrates that clinician engagement appears to play an important role in the real-world impact of AI-driven screening in primary care. High engagement with AI recommendations was associated with substantially greater detection of liver disease, whereas lower engagement attenuated benefit, particularly for advanced cases. These findings underscore that successful integration of AI into hepatology and primary care depends not only on accurate and well-validated models, but also on the thoughtful implementation of workflows that support clinicians in acting on AI recommendations across diverse practice settings. Importantly, these observations highlight that clinician engagement should be considered as a core design parameter in AI-enabled trials when defining analytic strategies, estimating statistical power, and planning implementation at scale. Future studies should therefore prioritize focused implementation interventions to ensure that AI-enabled screening translates into clinical benefits for patients and manageable workload implications for clinicians. Addressing known social and contextual barriers, such as cost and accessibility, as well as incorporating co-design with both clinicians and patients to promote usability and trust, will be critical for successful and scalable implementation.

Methods

Participants

The trial involved 248 clinicians from 98 care teams: 122 were randomized to the control group (no access to ECG results) while 126 to the intervention group (receiving alerts from the ML model applied to routine ECGs when suspicious for advanced CLD).Primary care clinicians included physicians, nurse practitioners, and physician assistants, organized in teams providing care for adult patients (≥18 years of age). The practices included both academic and community clinics across urban and rural communities in Southern Minnesota and Wisconsin. The DULCE trial was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (n. 22-009726) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05782283; registered March 23, 2023), and the detailed protocol has been previously published34. The trial was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Mayo Clinic (IRB no. 22-009726). Primary care clinicians signed the consent for participating in the study, while patient consent was waived as per pragmatic design.

Clinician and patient characteristics

Clinician information, including age, gender, specialty (i.e., internal medicine or family medicine), position (i.e., physician or advanced practice provider), and years of practice were captured at the time of informed consent via a link to a REDCap survey. Patient clinical data were obtained from the electronic health records. Adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent a standard 12-lead ECG for any indication during the trial period (4/18/2023–10/16/2023) were eligible for inclusion. Only the first ECG per unique patient was considered for analysis. Patients were excluded if they had a prior diagnosis of cirrhosis based on International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision (ICD-9/10) codes, or radiologic evidence of advanced CLD. Patients who did not provide authorization for the use of their medical records for research purposes were also excluded. Educational materials, including presentations and FAQs, were shared with intervention clinicians. Clinicians were asked to complete a questionnaire reporting the reasons for declining recommended follow-up testing.

ECG-ML results and recommendations

The ECG-ML model for detecting patients at high risk for cirrhosis was applied weekly to digital ECG files stored in the institutional cloud. Clinicians in the intervention arm were notified of patients with a positive ML-ECG result, while those in the control arm did not receive notifications or have access to the results. Positive results prompted a recommendation for sequential noninvasive liver disease assessment (NILDA) in appropriate clinical context, including age >40 years, type 2 diabetes, obesity, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, or alcohol misuse. Decision regarding whether to proceed with NILDA were made by the treating provider, reflecting individual clinical judgment and available patient information. FibroTest was recommended as a blood-based NILDA, whereas vibration-controlled transient elastography or magnetic resonance elastography were recommended as imaging-based NILDA. Outcomes from liver biopsy and other imaging-based investigations were also considered for the detection of CLD.

Outcomes

The evaluation of clinician response patterns was pre-specified in the study protocol. Engagement to AI-driven recommendations was assessed at the clinician level and included only clinicians managing at least one patient with an ECG-ML-positive (ECG-ML+) result. Engagement was defined as either ordering the recommended follow-up test or documenting a rationale for not ordering it. Non-engagement was defined as a complete lack of response to the AI recommendation, with neither a follow-up test ordered nor a documented justification. The engagement rate was calculated as the number of actions taken divided by the total number of ECG-ML-positive results. Clinicians were subsequently categorized into high- engagement (HE) and low- engagement (LE) groups, with HE defined as those in the top engagement quartile and LE defined as those in the lower three quartiles. Engagement was categorized into quartiles to facilitate interpretability and maintain adequate statistical power, consistent with prior implementation studies. As a sensitivity analysis, engagement was also modeled as a continuous variable (per 10% increase). The primary outcome was the association between engagement and the detection of new CLD diagnoses (advanced CLD and any CLD). Diagnostic yield was defined as the proportion of patients diagnosed with CLD following an ECG-ML-positive result. The secondary outcome was the relationship between engagement and clinician characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between the HE and LE groups using unadjusted Student’s t tests for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical variables. At the patient level, the incidence of newly diagnosed CLD was compared between engagement groups using a mixed-effects logistic regression model adjusting for randomized care teams, with effect estimates reported as odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted using ECG-ML results. Linear correlation between variables was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. All tests were two-sided with a Type I error rate of 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to the inclusion of electronic health records consented for research use exclusively by Mayo Clinic investigators, but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

The ECG-AI algorithm has been previously published but cannot be made publicly available due to proprietary intellectual property protections (patent US20230218238A1). The algorithm can be made available upon reasonable request for use in research studies in collaboration with Mayo Clinic.
