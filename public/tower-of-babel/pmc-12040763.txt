Mechanism of RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death in HT22 cells: crucial role of protein disulfide isomerase
Authors: Ming-Jie Hou, Xuanqi Huang, Bao Ting Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) was recently shown to be an upstream mediator of erastin-induced, glutathione depletion-associated ferroptosis through its catalysis of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) dimerization and nitric oxide (NO) accumulation. A recent study reported that RSL3, a known ferroptosis inducer and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inhibitor, can inhibit thioredoxin reductase 1 (TrxR1). The present study seeks to test the hypothesis that RSL3 may, through its inhibition of TrxR1, facilitate PDI activation ( i. e., in a catalytically active, oxidized state), thereby enhancing RSL3-induced ferroptosis through NOS dimerization and NO accumulation. Using HT22 mouse neuronal cells as an in vitro model, we show that treatment of these cells with RSL3 strongly increases NOS protein levels and that PDI-mediated NOS dimerization is activated by RSL3, resulting in NO accumulation. Mechanistically, we find that PDI is activated in cells treated with RSL3 because of its inhibition of TrxR1, and the activated PDI then catalyzes NOS dimerization, which is followed by the accumulation of cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS and ultimately ferroptotic cell death. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of PDI or TrxR1 partially abrogates RSL3-induced NOS activation and the subsequent accumulation of cellular NO, ROS/lipid-ROS, and ultimately ferroptosis in HT22 cells. The results of this study clearly show that PDI activation resulted from RSL3 inhibition of TrxR1 activity contributes crucially to RSL3-induced ferroptosis in a cell culture model through the PDI→NOS→NO→ROS/lipid-ROS pathway, in addition to its known inhibition of GPX4 activity.

Introduction

Ferroptosis is a form of oxidative cell death [1] and is morphologically distinct from apoptosis-associated characteristics [ 1, 2]. Mechanistically, ferroptosis can be selectively induced through depletion of cellular glutathione (GSH) and/or through suppression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), an enzyme involved in the reduction of lipid peroxides. RSL3 is a prototypical inducer of ferroptosis [3], and over the past few years, it has been widely thought that RSL3 induces ferroptosis primarily through its suppression of GPX4 function, which then leads to the accumulation of cellular lipid reactive oxygen species (lipid-ROS) [4]. However, a recent study reported that RSL3 can strongly inhibit the catalytic activity of thioredoxin reductase 1 (TrxR1) [5]. Because TrxR1 uses NADPH as a cofactor to reduce the active-site disulfide bonds in thioredoxins, when TrxR1 is inhibited by an inhibitor (such as RSL3), the active site of the thioredoxins would remain in the oxidized state ( i. e., containing a disulfide bond in their active sites) [6].

Protein disulfide isomerase (PDI or PDIA1) is the prototype of the PDI family of proteins, which are ubiquitous dithiol/disulfide oxidoreductases of the thioredoxin superfamily [ 7 – 9]. PDI is primarily localized in the endoplasmic reticulum of mammalian cells, although a small fraction of this protein is also found in the nucleus, cytosol, mitochondria, plasma membrane and extracellular space [10]. PDI is involved in protein processing by catalyzing the formation of intra- and intermolecular disulfide bridges in proteins [11]. Our recent studies showed that PDI plays an important role in mediating glutamate- and erastin-induced, GSH depletion-associated oxidative cytotoxicity through PDI-mediated NOS activation ( i. e., homodimer formation through disulfide bond linkages), which is followed by the accumulation of cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS and ultimately the induction of ferroptotic cell death [12].

In the present study, we sought to examine the role of PDI in mediating RSL3-induced ferroptosis in HT22 neuronal cells and the mechanism of PDI activation. We found that PDI plays a crucial role in mediating RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death in these cells through its ability to catalyze NOS dimerization, which is followed by the accumulation of cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS, and ultimately ferroptotic cell death. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of PDI function could abrogate RSL3-induced ferroptosis. The mechanism by which PDI is activated in RSL3-treated cells is caused by TrxR1 inhibition by RSL3, and this inhibition leaves more PDI proteins in the oxidized state, which is the active form that catalyzes the dimerization of NOS (including both nNOS and iNOS) in HT22 cells.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

RSL3 (#SML2234) and S-nitroso- N-acetylpenicillamine (SNAP, #N3398) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, USA). CPZ (chlorpromazine, #S5749), ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, #S7243), cystamine (#S3695) and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, #S6821) were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, USA). Diaminofluorescein-FM diacetate (DAF-FM-DA, #S0019) and diaminofluorescein-2 diacetate (DCF-DA, #S0033) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), and C11-BODIPY-581/591 (#D3861) was obtained from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, USA). Anti-PDI (#3501), anti-TrxR1 (#6925) and anti-β-actin (#4970) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, USA), and anti-GPX4 (#ab125066), anti-nNOS (#ab1376) and anti-iNOS (#ab3523) antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Waltham, USA). TrxR1-siRNAs (#sc-36751) and iNOS-siRNAs (#sc-36092) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, USA); PDI-siRNAs and nNOS-siRNAs were obtained from Ruibo Biotechnology (Guangdong, China). The siRNA sequences used to target mouse TrxR1 (siTrxR1) were 5′-UUCAAGUAGAUUAGCCAAGtt-3′ (#1), 5′-UACGUUAUGAACAGCUUCCtt (#2), and 5′-UUACGUUAUGAACAGCUUCtt-3′ (#3). The siRNA sequences used to target mouse iNOS (si-iNOS) were 5′-UUUCCUUUGUUACAGCUUCtt-3′ (#1), 5′-UUCAUGAUAACGUUUCUGGtt (#2), and UAGUAGUCCACAAUAGUACtt-3′ (#3). The siRNA sequences used to target mouse PDI (siPDI) were 5′-CCAAGTACCAGCTGGACAA-3′ (#1), 5′-GAACGGTCATTGATTACAA-3′ (#2), and 5′-TGCTAAGATGGACTCAACA-3′ (#3). The siRNA sequences used to target mouse nNOS (sinNOS) were 5′-GCTGCCATCCCATCACATA-3′ (#1), 5′-CCTCGTGAATGCACTCATT-3′ (#2), and 5′-GCGACAATTTGACATCCAA-3′ (#3). The siRNA sequences for the negative control (siCon) were 5′-AUCCGCGCGAUAGUACGUAtt-3′ (#1), 5′-UUACGCGUAGCGUAAUACGtt (#2), and 5′-UAUUCGCGCGUAUAGCGGUtt-3′ (#3). The MDA (malondialdehyde) assay kit (#M496) was purchased from Dojindo Molecular Technologies (Kumamoto, Japan), and the TrxR1 activity assay kit (#BC1155) was purchased from SolarBio (Beijing, China).

Cell culture and cell viability assay

HT22 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HT22 murine hippocampal neuronal cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% ( v/ v) fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin and incubated at 37°C under 5% CO 2. The cells were subcultured and used for subsequent experiments when they reached approximately 80% confluence.

The MTT reduction assay was used to determine the change in gross cell viability. HT22 cells were usually seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells/well 24 h prior to receiving different experimental treatments. To test the effect of a modulating compound, the cells were jointly treated with RSL3 and the compound for 24 h. Afterwards, MTT at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated for 2.5 h at 37°C under 5% CO 2. After incubation, the MTT-containing medium was removed, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the MTT formazan. The absorbance of the MTT formazan was measured with a UVmax microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Palo Alto, USA) at 560 nm.

siRNA transfection

For siRNA transfection, the procedures were described in our earlier study [12]. Briefly, siRNAs (at a final concentration of 60 nM) for targeted genes (PDI, iNOS, nNOS, and TrxR) were transfected into HT22 cells 24 h after seeding with Lipofectamine RNA iMAX (Invitrogen). Twenty-four hours after siRNA transfection, the cells were treated with selected drugs and subsequently processed for cell viability measurement, fluorescence staining and measurement, and western blot analysis.

Measurement of cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS levels

The cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS levels were jointly determined via analytical flow cytometry and fluorescence/confocal microscopy. HT22 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 ×10 4 cells/well 24 h before treatments. Afterwards, the cells were treated with RSL3 ± different modulating compounds for 6 h or as indicated. Notably, the 6-h RSL3 treatment time was selected on the basis of our initial analysis of the time course of NO, ROS and lipid-ROS accumulation. Prior to being stained with fluorescent probes, the cells were washed with HBSS twice. The cellular levels of NO, ROS and lipid-ROS were detected after the addition of respective cell-permeant fluorescent probes (10 μM DAF-FM-DA for NO detection, 10 μM DCFH-DA for ROS detection, and 10 μM BODIPY TM-581/591-C11 for lipid-ROS detection), which were dissolved in 200 μL of phenol red-free and serum-free DMEM. The cells were incubated at 37°C for an additional 30 min and then washed twice with HBSS. The fluorescence images of cellular NO and ROS were taken via a fluorescence microscope (AXIO; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The fluorescence images of the cellular lipid-ROS were captured via a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 900; Carl Zeiss) and analyzed with Zen software (Carl Zeiss). The quantitative fluorescence intensity reflecting the accumulation of cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS was detected via a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA). Three or more experiments were usually conducted to confirm the observations, and the average fluorescence intensity (mean ± SD) based on the replicate measurements in one representative experiment was calculated with FlowJo software (FlowJo; LLC, Ashland, USA) and selected for presentation. To measure intracellular MDA levels, an MDA assay kit was used, and all procedures followed the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of TrxR1 enzyme activity

TrxR1 enzyme activity was determined via a commercial kit, which is based on the measurement of NADPH-dependent reduction of the disulfide bond in the substrate 5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB). To determine the effects of RSL3 or auranofin on TrxR1 enzyme activity in cultured HT22 cells, the cells were treated with RSL3 (100 nM) or auranofin (1 μM) for 6 h, washed with PBS twice, lysed and centrifuged at 9500 g for 10 min (4°C) to collect the cellular supernatants. Aliquots of the supernatants were properly diluted and then added to a 96-well plate for determination of TrxR1 enzyme activity according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The change in absorbance at 412 nm was recorded via a UVmax microplate reader (Molecular Devices). The relative TrxR1 enzyme activity was calculated as a percentage of that of the vehicle-treated control cells. To determine the direct inhibition of TrxR1 catalytic activity by RSL3 and auranofin in the in vitro enzymatic assay, untreated HT22 cells were washed with PBS twice, lysed and then centrifuged at 9500 g for 10 min (4°C) to prepare supernatants containing the TrxR1 enzyme. Different concentrations of RSL3 and auranofin were included in the incubation, and the enzyme activity in each treatment group was determined by monitoring the change in absorbance at 412 nm once every 2 min for up to 150 min. In this enzyme activity assay, it is assumed that the NADPH-dependent specific catalytic activity of TrxR1 is inhibited by high concentrations of auranofin (its IC 50 for TrxR1 is approximately 1.1 nM, as determined in this study), and the baseline reading at an absorbance of 412 nm, which cannot be inhibited by 1000 nM auranofin, is subtracted.

Western blot analysis

After treatment with RSL3 for different durations as indicated, the HT22 cells were collected via trypsinization and centrifugation and then lysed in RIPA buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail on ice for 30 min. Protein concentrations were determined via a BCA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For total nNOS and iNOS analysis (including both monomeric and dimeric forms of nNOS and iNOS), samples were heated at 95°C for 5 min with reducing buffer before being loaded onto the electrophoresis gel. To analyze the monomeric and dimeric forms of nNOS and iNOS, the samples were prepared with nonreducing buffer and were not heated, and the temperature of the gel was maintained below 15°C during electrophoresis. The proteins were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE (for total nNOS and iNOS analysis) or 6% SDS-PAGE (for monomeric and dimeric nNOS and iNOS analysis) and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated for 1 h in 5% skim milk at room temperature and then incubated with the primary antibody overnight. Afterwards, the membranes were washed three times with TBST at room temperature (10 min each time). The membranes were then incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature and washed with TBST three times before visualization. The desired protein bands were visualized via a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200; Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

All the quantitative experiments and data described in this study were repeated at least three times. The data are usually presented as the mean ± SD of multiple replicate measurements from one selected representative experiment. Statistical analysis was conducted via one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons as needed (GraphPad Prism 7; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, USA). Statistical significance was denoted by P < 0.05 for significant difference. In most cases, * denotes the comparisons for statistical significance between the control group (cells treated with vehicle only) and the cells treated with a chemical (such as RSL3), whereas # denotes the comparisons between the cells treated with RSL3 alone and the cells treated with both RSL3 and another compound. For western blot quantification, one representative dataset is shown.

Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a commonly used lipid-ROS scavenger [ 13, 14], strongly protected against RSL3-induced cytotoxicity in these cells ( Figure 1D), which confirms earlier observations [ 15, 16] reporting that RSL3 induces ferroptotic cell death in cultured HT22 cells. Our recent studies revealed that, during erastin-induced, GSH depletion-associated ferroptotic cell death in HT22 cells, there was a time-dependent sequential induction of NO and ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation [12]. To determine whether RSL3-induced ferroptosis in HT22 cells is also related to cellular NO and ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation, we determined the time-dependent changes in cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS levels following RSL3 treatment ( Figure 1E–J). Based on measurements via fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry, we found that treatment of HT22 cells with RSL3 elicited a time-dependent increase in cellular NO levels ( Figure 1E,G and Supplementary Figure S1A). Similarly, the cellular levels of ROS and lipid-ROS also increased in a time- and dose-dependent manner in RSL3-treated cells ( Figure 1E,H and Supplementary Figure S1B for ROS; Figure 1F,I for lipid-ROS). Malondialdehyde (MDA), one of the end products of lipid peroxidation and often used as an indicator of cellular lipid peroxidation [17], was increased by RSL3 treatment in a time-dependent manner ( Figure 1J). NO accumulation occurred at a very similar time as does ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation.

To determine whether NO accumulation subsequently leads to ROS and lipid-ROS accumulation and ultimately ferroptotic cell death, we studied the effects of 2-(4-carboxyphenyl)-4,4,5,5-tetramethylimidazoline-1-oxyl-3-oxide (cPTIO), an NO scavenger, in RSL3-treated cells [18]. cPTIO effectively suppressed the RSL3-induced accumulation of NO ( Figure 2A), ROS ( Figure 2B) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 2C) in HT22 cells. In addition, cPTIO attenuated RSL3-induced cell death ( Figure 2 D). 6-Hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox), which has strong ROS-scavenging activity plus weaker NO-scavenging activity [19], partially abrogated the RSL3-induced accumulation of NO ( Figure 2E) but strongly abrogated the accumulation of ROS ( Figure 2F) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 2G), and these effects were associated with very strong protection against RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death ( Figure 2H).

N-acetyl- L-cysteine (NAC), another commonly used antioxidant [20], strongly abrogated the RSL3-induced accumulation of cellular NO ( Figure 2I,J), ROS ( Figure 2K) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 2L), and these effects were also associated with strong protection against RSL3-induced cell death ( Figure 2M).

Enhancement of RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death by sodium nitroprusside

Sodium nitroprusside (SNP) is a NO donor that can rapidly release NO inside cells [21]. To provide further support for an upstream role of NO in RSL3-induced ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation and ferroptosis, we sought to determine the effects of SNP on RSL3-induced ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation and ferroptosis in HT22 cells. First, treatment of HT22 cells with SNP alone caused a rapid increase in cellular NO levels ( Figure 3A–C), which was accompanied by increases in cellular ROS levels ( Figure 3A,B,D) and lipid-ROS levels ( Figure 3E). Notably, while NO accumulation peaked within 0.5 h after SNP exposure, the accumulation of ROS and lipid-ROS lagged slightly behind the increase in NO, reaching a peak at approximately 1 h after SNP exposure. In addition, treatment of HT22 cells with SNP alone also elicited dose-dependent cytotoxicity ( Figure 3F). Notably, combined treatment of HT22 cells with RSL3+SNP (200 μM) drastically sensitized these cells to RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death ( Figure 3G). Role of NOS induction and activation in RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death

The generation of cellular NO is catalyzed mostly by nitric oxide synthase (NOS), which uses L-arginine as its primary substrate [ 22– 24]. There are three known subtypes of NOS, namely, nNOS, iNOS and eNOS [ 22– 24]. Our earlier study revealed that while nNOS was readily detected in untreated HT22 cells, iNOS was mostly undetectable [25]. In this study, we found that treatment of HT22 cells with increasing concentrations of RSL3 for different durations did not significantly affect total cellular nNOS protein levels (including both monomeric and dimeric forms) ( Figure 4A). In contrast, while few or no iNOS bands were detected in vehicle-treated control HT22 cells as reported previously [25], treatment of these cells with RSL3 significantly increased the total cellular iNOS level in a time- and dose-dependent manner ( Figure 4A). The catalytic activity of NOS is activated through the formation of NOS dimers [ 22– 24], which are stabilized by the formation of an intermolecular disulfide bond [26]. Our earlier studies revealed that NOS proteins are activated to form dimers in erastin-treated cells [ 12, 27]. Next, we sought to determine whether nNOS and iNOS were activated ( i. e., dimerized) in RSL3-treated HT22 cells. For nNOS, treatment of HT22 cells with RSL3 significantly increased the formation of nNOS dimers ( Figure 4B), but the total nNOS protein level did not significantly change ( Figure 4A). RSL3 not only increased iNOS activation (dimerization) in a time- and dose-dependent manner ( Figure 4B) but also markedly increased the total iNOS protein level ( Figure 4 A).

Next, we determined the modulatory effects of S-methylisothiourea (SMT), a preferential inhibitor of iNOS [ 28, 29], and chlorpromazine (CPZ), a preferential inhibitor of nNOS [30], on RSL3-induced cytotoxicity in HT22 cells. We found that joint treatment of HT22 cells with SMT abrogated the RSL3-induced accumulation of cellular NO ( Figure 4C,E), ROS ( Figure 4D,F) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 4G), and these effects were associated with strong protection (~100% restoration of cell viability) against RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death ( Figure 4H). In comparison, the effects of CPZ on RSL3-induced accumulation of NO ( Figure 4C,E), ROS ( Figure 4D,F) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 4G) were markedly weaker, which was also associated with much weaker protection against RSL3-induced cell death ( Figure 4I). The combined use of CPZ and SMT at lower concentrations which only produced a partial cytoprotection when each chemical was used alone elicited a stronger protective effect ( Figure 4J).

Finally, we also evaluated the contributing role of nNOS and iNOS in mediating RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death by using a siRNA approach to selectively downregulate their expression levels in HT22 cells. The efficiency of iNOS and nNOS knockdown was confirmed by measuring the changes in the cellular nNOS and iNOS protein levels ( Figure 5A). Knockdown of iNOS abrogated the RSL3-induced increase in iNOS protein levels ( Figure 5A) and significantly protected against RSL3-induced cytotoxicity ( Figure 5B). In contrast, selective knockdown of nNOS in HT22 cells did not have a significant protective effect ( Figure 5C). Notably, the nNOS knockdown experiment was repeated multiple times, and similar results were obtained, which revealed a lack of significant protective effects against RSL3-induced cytotoxicity. Together, these results indicate that NO accumulation resulted from the induction and activation (dimerization) of iNOS and nNOS in RSL3-treated cells is involved in mediating RSL3-induced ferroptosis in HT22 cells. It is apparent that iNOS plays a more dominant role than nNOS in this process.

PDI mediates NOS dimerization in RSL3-treated cells

In this study, we also sought to determine whether PDI can catalyze nNOS and iNOS dimerization in RSL3-treated HT22 cells. First, we found that the total PDI protein level was not significantly affected by RSL3 treatment ( Figure 4A). To support the hypothesis that PDI is responsible for catalyzing nNOS and iNOS dimerization, we determined the effect of cystamine on RSL3-induced ferroptosis. Cystamine is an organic disulfide molecule capable of inhibiting PDI enzymatic activity through covalent modification of the cysteine residue(s) in its catalytic site [ 31, 32]. For nNOS, treatment of HT22 cells with RSL3 stimulated its activation (dimerization), and combined treatment with cystamine abrogated RSL3-induced nNOS dimerization ( Figure 5D). RSL3 markedly increased total iNOS protein levels and dimer formation in HT22 cells ( Figure 5D), and combined treatment with cystamine abolished the RSL3-induced increase in total iNOS protein levels and dimerization ( Figure 5D). These effects of cystamine on nNOS and iNOS dimerization and total iNOS protein levels in RSL3-treated cells were associated with strong abrogation of NO ( Figure 5E), ROS ( Figure 5F) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 5G) accumulation, as well as strong protection against RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death ( Figure 5H).

We recently showed that 4-hydroxyestrone (4-OH-E 1) is an endogenous estrogen metabolite that can effectively inhibit the enzymatic activity of PDI [33]. We found that joint treatment of HT22 cells with 4-OH-E 1 abrogated RSL3-induced increase in total iNOS protein levels ( Figure 6A). Because nNOS dimers are relatively easier to detect than iNOS dimers are, we used nNOS dimers as a representative parameter to reflect PDI-mediated NOS dimerization (activation) in RSL3-treated HT22 cells. We found that joint treatment of HT22 cells with 4-OH-E 1 abrogated RSL3-induced nNOS dimerization ( Figure 6A). This effect was associated with decreased accumulation of cellular NO ( Figure 6B), ROS ( Figure 6C) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 6D), together with increased cell survival ( Figure 6E). Our earlier study revealed that PDI in untreated HT22 cells is mostly present in the catalytically inactive S-nitrosylated form and that it undergoes S-denitrosylation following GSH depletion [25]. To probe the involvement of PDI S-denitrosylation in mediating RSL3-induced cytotoxicity in HT22 cells, we studied the effect of S-nitroso- N-acetylpenicillamine (SNAP), a protein S-nitrosylating agent [34]. We found that joint treatment of HT22 cells with SNAP abrogated RSL3-induced NOS activation (based on detection of the nNOS dimer; Figure 6F), and this effect was associated with reduced accumulation of NO ( Figure 6G), ROS ( Figure 6H), and lipid-ROS ( Figure 6I) and protection against ferroptotic cell death ( Figure 6J). These results indicate that S-nitrosylation of PDI is indeed associated with the suppression of PDI activity; a reduction in NOS dimerization; a reduction in cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS levels; and ultimately, protection from ferroptosis.

Based on the observations with different PDI inhibitors, we next investigated the effect of PDI by using specific siRNAs to selectively downregulate its expression. The effectiveness of PDI knockdown was assessed by western blot analysis of the protein level of PDI ( Figure 6K). As expected, PDI knockdown elicited partial protection against RSL3-induced cytotoxicity ( Figure 6L), but the degree of protection in PDI-knockdown cells was lower than expected, likely because transient PDI knockdown may not last long enough for cell viability experiments.

Collectively, these results clearly show that PDI is a key upstream mediator of RSL3-induced ferroptosis by catalyzing iNOS dimerization in RSL3-treated HT22 cells, which then leads to the accumulation of cellular NO and ROS/lipid-ROS and, ultimately, oxidative cell death.

Role of TrxR1 in RSL3-induced ferroptosis

A recent study reported that RSL3 can inhibit the catalytic activity of TrxR1 [5]. Since PDI belongs to the thioredoxin superfamily and is a substrate of TrxR1 [ 35, 36], we next sought to determine the potential role of TrxR1 in RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death. First, we used TrxR1-siRNAs to transiently downregulate TrxR1 expression in HT22 cells. The effectiveness of TrxR1 knockdown was confirmed by western blot analysis of TrxR1 protein level ( Figure 7A). While TrxR1 knockdown alone (in the absence of RSL3) reduced the baseline cell viability compared with that of cells transfected with the control siRNAs alone ( Supplementary Figure S2A), TrxR1 knockdown markedly reduced the sensitivity of cells to the cytotoxicity induced by RSL3 and auranofin (a prototypical inhibitor of TrxR1 [ 5, 37, 38 ]) ( Figure 7B). We also determined whether TrxR1 activity in HT22 cells was inhibited by treating the cells with RSL3 or auranofin. HT22 cells were first treated with 0.1 μM RSL3 or 1 μM auranofin for 5 h, after which the cells were washed and harvested for preparation of cell lysates. Even after all these preparative steps, the TrxR1 activity in the cell lysates was still markedly reduced in the RSL3- or auranofin-treated cells ( Figure 7C); in comparison, the TrxR1 protein levels remained largely unchanged or slightly increased in these cells ( Figure 7D,E,G,H). Taken together, these observations suggest that RSL3 and auranofin can bind to TrxR1 very tightly (most likely covalently) to exert strong and sustained inhibition of enzyme activity.

Next, we determined the iNOS dimer level in the RSL3-treated HT22 cells prior to TrxR1 knockdown. As shown in Figure 7 F, TrxR1 knockdown alone (without RSL3 treatment) caused a significant increase in the iNOS dimer level, but it significantly abrogated the RSL3-induced increase in iNOS dimer formation. Notably, the cellular PDI level also appeared to be reduced in TrxR1-knockdown cells treated with RSL3 ( Figure 7F). These results suggest that TrxR1 plays an important role in meditating PDI-catalyzed iNOS dimerization and subsequent ferroptotic cell death in HT22 cells.

It is of note that in this study, we also investigated the effect of the combined knockdown of PDI and TrxR1 in HT22 cells and then determined its effect on RSL3-induced cell death. We found that joint knockdown of PDI and TrxR1 reduced the sensitivity to RSL3 cytotoxicity in a similar manner as that observed with PDI knockdown alone ( Supplementary Figure S2B). This result is expected because PDI is a substrate of TrxR1, and when PDI is mostly absent (resulted from its knockdown), little effect remains, even when TrxR1 is further knocked down.

Next, we determined the ability of RSL3 and auranofin to directly inhibit the enzymatic activity of TrxR1 via biochemical assays. Whole-cell lysates were prepared from untreated HT22 cells, and the resulting lysates (containing the TrxR1 enzyme) were subsequently used to assay TrxR1 enzyme activity in the presence or absence of RSL3 or auranofin. We found that TrxR1 enzyme activity could be very potently inhibited in a concentration-dependent manner in the presence of auranofin, with an apparent IC 50 of approximately 1.1 nM ( Figure 7I). Notably, TrxR1 enzyme inhibition by auranofin became stronger but with prolonged incubation until it reached a plateau ( Figure 7J). This pattern of inhibition is consistent with the earlier observation that auranofin can covalently inhibit TrxR1 [39]. Similarly, TrxR1 enzyme activity was also inhibited by RSL3 in a dose-dependent manner, with an apparent IC 50 of approximately 4 μM ( Figure 7K), which is in agreement with the results of a recent study in which the purified TrxR1 enzyme was used in an in vitro assay [5]. Notably, the overall pattern of TrxR1 inhibition by RSL3 was similar to that of inhibition by auranofin, i. e., stronger inhibition was observed with prolonged reaction time until it reached a plateau ( Figure 7L).

Next, we determined whether auranofin can induce cytotoxicity in HT22 cells. Treatment of HT22 cells with auranofin alone elicited concentration-dependent cytotoxicity ( Figure 8A). The IC 50 of auranofin’s cytotoxicity is 0.8 μM, which is much greater than its IC 50 for TrxR1 inhibition. In addition, auranofin-induced cell death was not protected by Fer-1 ( Figure 8B) or the iron chelator DFO ( Figure 8C), two commonly used protective agents for chemically induced ferroptosis. Similarly, while auranofin (at a concentration of 1 μM) elicited the accumulation of cellular NO ( Figure 8D) and ROS ( Figure 8E), it did not elicit a significant accumulation of lipid-ROS ( Figure 8F). Together, these observations suggest that the cell death induced by auranofin at high concentrations (much higher than its IC 50 for TrxR1 inhibition) is not ferroptosis. In addition, the apoptosis inhibitor z-VAD-FMK and necrosis inhibitor Nec-1 also failed to prevent auranofin-induced cell death ( Figure 8G,H), indicating that auranofin-induced cytotoxicity in HT22 cells is not typical apoptosis or necrosis. Based on the above observations, we also investigated the effect of jointly treating HT22 cells with auranofin + BSO, which is a GSH synthase inhibitor [ 40, 41]. BSO is expected to facilitate the induction of GSH depletion-associated ferroptosis when these cells are jointly treated with auranofin. In this experimental design, BSO was used at a concentration (such as 6 μM) that did not cause significant cytotoxicity when present alone ( Figure 9A). We found that joint treatment of HT22 cells with very low concentrations of auranofin + 6 μM BSO caused a concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability ( Figure 9B). Interestingly, the GPX4 protein level was also decreased in the cells treated with auranofin + BSO ( Figure 9C). Notably, a similar decrease in GPX4 protein level was observed in cells treated with RSL3 alone ( Figure 7D). The cytotoxicity of auranofin+BSO was partially rescued by Fer-1 ( Figure 9D). Cystamine, an inhibitor of PDI that can effectively prevent RSL3-induced ferroptosis ( Figure 5H), also partially protected against the cell death jointly induced by auranofin and BSO ( Figure 9E). Similarly, 4-OH-E 1, which can effectively rescue RSL3-induced cell death ( Figure 6E), also partially protected against the cell death jointly induced by auranofin and BSO ( Figure 9E). These observations suggest that when very low concentrations of auranofin (which are close to its IC 50 for TrxR1 inhibition) are used under conditions in which cellular GSH is depleted, the cell death induced by auranofin is largely ferroptosis, and as expected, cell death can be partially prevented by both ferroptosis inhibitors and PDI inhibitors. To provide further support for the above observations, we also analyzed changes in cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS accumulation and NOS dimerization following the treatment of cells with very low concentrations of auranofin + BSO. We found that under these joint treatment conditions, there was a time-dependent accumulation of cellular NO ( Figure 9F), ROS ( Figure 9G) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 9H). In addition, dimeric nNOS (a marker of NOS dimerization) was also significantly increased in a time-dependent manner ( Figure 9C). Combined treatment of these cells with cystamine partially abrogated the accumulation of NO ( Figure 9I), ROS ( Figure 9J) and lipid-ROS ( Figure 9K) induced by treatment with auranofin + BSO. Similar observations of the abrogation of NO, ROS and lipid-ROS accumulation were also made when the HT22 cells were treated with both 4-OH-E 1±auranofin and BSO ( Figure 9I–K). Together, these data indicate that PDI is activated in cells treated with RSL3 through its inhibition of TrxR1, and the activated PDI then catalyzes NOS dimerization, which is followed by the accumulation of cellular NO, ROS and lipid-ROS and ultimately ferroptotic cell death.

Discussion

RSL3 is a prototypical ferroptosis inducer, and its ferroptosis-inducing ability is thought to be associated with the inhibition of GPX4 [ 4, 42, 43]. However, a recent study showed that in addition to GPX4 inhibition, RSL3 can also inhibit the enzymatic activity of TrxR1 [5], which would shift the pool of thioredoxins to their oxidized forms. PDI is a member of the thioredoxin superfamily, and we hypothesize that RSL3 might, through its inhibition of TrxR1 enzymatic activity [5], keep more cellular PDI proteins in the oxidized form, thereby facilitating RSL3-induced ferroptosis through catalyzing NOS dimerization, followed by the accumulation of cellular ROS and lipid-ROS, and ultimately ferroptotic cell death. As discussed below, a large amount of experimental data is provided in this study, which jointly offers strong support for this hypothesis.

We found that in the development of RSL3-induced ferroptosis in HT22 cells, NO accumulation is an early event that subsequently leads to the formation and accumulation of ROS and lipid-derived ROS, contributing to the development of ferroptotic cell death. The supporting evidence includes the following: First, we find that RSL3 can induce the accumulation of both NO and ROS/lipid-ROS in a time- and dose-dependent manner, although there appears to be no clear time differential in their buildup (the reason for this is provided later). The observations made with SNP, which can release NO [21], support this suggestion. We found that SNP can quickly lead to the accumulation of cellular ROS and lipid-ROS when present alone in HT22 cells and that it also dramatically sensitizes the cells to RSL3-induced ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation and ferroptosis. Notably, our earlier study revealed that during erastin-induced ferroptosis, NO accumulation occurred earlier than did ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation in HT22 cells [12]. In addition, when HT22 cells were jointly treated with erastin+SNP, NO accumulation also occurred much earlier (due to the rapid release of NO from SNP), and in parallel, ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation also occurred much earlier, lagging shortly behind NO accumulation [12]. These experimental observations suggest that during chemically induced ferroptosis, NO accumulation occurs first, which then leads to ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation. The second line of evidence comes from the study of NO scavengers and NOS inhibitors. We found that cPTIO, an NO scavenger, can abrogate RSL3-induced NO accumulation, and this effect is accompanied by abrogation of ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation and ferroptosis. In addition, SMT, an iNOS inhibitor, strongly protects against RSL3-induced NO accumulation, which is accompanied by abrogation of ROS/lipid-ROS accumulation and ferroptotic cell death. Together, these observations suggest that RSL3-induced NO accumulation contributes to the subsequent accumulation of ROS and lipid-ROS, and ultimately the induction of ferroptotic cell death.

Although RSL3-induced NO accumulation leads to the accumulation of cellular ROS/lipid-ROS in HT22 cells, it is interesting that the RSL3-induced buildup of cellular NO and cellular ROS (which include lipid-ROS) does not display a distinguishable time difference; in fact, they occur nearly at the same time. This unexpected experimental observation is likely attributable to the fact that RSL3 can increase both NO and lipid-ROS accumulation at the same time. RSL3 increases NO biosynthesis through its direct inhibition of TrxR1, which then leads to PDI activation and NOS dimerization; on the other hand, RSL3 also directly increases cellular lipid-ROS accumulation (independent of cellular NO accumulation) through its inhibition of GPX4. Therefore, the buildup of cellular lipid-ROS (which is part of the cellular ROS) in RSL3-treated cells is the result of both GPX4 inhibition (which likely occurs first) and NO accumulation (which likely occurs at a slightly later time). For these reasons, the accumulation of cellular NO and ROS/lipid-ROS occurs rapidly and almost simultaneously after exposure to RSL3.

Dimeric NOS is catalytically active and catalyzes NO formation [ 12– 16]. In this study, we show that RSL3 exposure increases the formation of nNOS and iNOS dimers in a time- and dose-dependent manner in HT22 cells. The results of the iNOS and nNOS inhibitor experiments support the idea that both iNOS and nNOS are involved in RSL3-induced ferroptosis, but the results of the iNOS and nNOS knockdown experiments are inconclusive. iNOS knockdown is associated with partial abrogation of RSL3 cytotoxicity, but nNOS knockdown fails to exert significant protection ( Figure 5). Two potential reasons may explain the inability to observe a significant protective effect with nNOS knockdown. First, the relative contribution of cellular nNOS (which is not induced following RSL3 exposure) to the cellular NO pool is likely markedly smaller than the contribution of cellular iNOS (which is strongly induced by RSL3). The other reason might be the low effectiveness of sustained nNOS knockdown in live HT22 cells. Overall, iNOS clearly plays a more important role than nNOS in mediating RSL3-induced NO accumulation and ferroptosis in this neuronal cell culture model.

By jointly using chemical inhibitors or PDI knockdown, strong evidence is provided in this study to show that PDI is involved in catalyzing NOS dimer formation in RSL3-treated HT22 cells. For example, joint treatment of HT22 cells with cystamine (a PDI inhibitor) strongly reduces the RSL3-induced dimerization of both nNOS and iNOS, the accumulation of NO and ROS/lipid-ROS, and oxidative cytotoxicity. Another example involves the S-nitrosylation of the cysteine residues in PDI’s active site, which inactivates PDI’s enzyme activity [ 44 , 45]. Our earlier study revealed that PDI in untreated HT22 cells was mostly present in the S-nitrosylated form, but following glutamate treatment (which induces cellular GSH depletion), PDI underwent S-denitrosylation [25]. In the present study, RSL3-induced NO, ROS and lipid-ROS accumulation was abrogated by SNAP (which causes PDI S-nitrosylation), and this effect was associated with protection against cell death. Together, these observations support the notion that PDI plays an important role in catalyzing NOS dimerization in RSL3-treated HT22 cells, which then leads to the accumulation of NO, ROS and lipid-ROS and ultimately ferroptotic cell death.

ERO1 is an enzyme that oxidizes PDI by forming disulfide bonds in its active site [46]. Our recent work revealed that EN460 (an inhibitor of ERO1) has a strong protective effect against erastin-induced ferroptosis in HT22 cells [12]. However, EN460 only had a very weak protective effect against RSL3-induced ferroptosis, as observed in this study ( Supplementary Figure S3A–D). This difference occurred because RSL3 is an inhibitor of TrxR1, which catalyzes the reduction of disulfide bonds in PDI’s active site. When cellular TrxR1 activity is inhibited by RSL3, the majority of PDI will be in the oxidized form; as such, the presence of ERO1 will not add significantly more oxidized PDI (because not much reduced PDI is left as a substrate for ERO1). This is why it is difficult for EN460 to display a strong protective effect when TrxR1 is already inhibited by RSL3. This lack of a significant protective effect of EN460 also supports the important role of PDI in driving RSL3-mediated ferroptosis.

On the basis of the findings of this study, it is evident that RSL3-induced ferroptosis involves two important mechanistic components: one is mediated by the activation of PDI (resulted from TrxR1 inhibition by RSL3), and the other component is the inhibition of GPX4-mediated protection against lipid-ROS. Because of the combination of these two effects, a PDI inhibitor that does not have direct antioxidant activity is expected to be less efficacious in protecting against RSL3-induced ferroptosis than a PDI inhibitor that also has strong antioxidant activity. For example, 4-OH-E 1, which was recently identified as an effective PDI inhibitor [33], was found to have a strong protective effect against RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death. The protective effect of 4-OH-E 1 against RSL3-induced cell death is more potent than its protection against erastin-induced cell death in some cell lines (unpublished data). This observation suggests that the direct antioxidant activity of 4-OH-E 1 against lipid-ROS also contributes, to some degree, to its cytoprotective effect. This explanation is also in line with observations with cystamine, which can inhibit PDI function but does not have any antioxidant properties. Compared with 4-OH-E 1, cystamine exerts less efficacious protection against RSL3-induced ferroptosis in HT22 cells; however, both cystamine and 4-OH-E 1 exert similarly strong protection (90%–100% protection) against erastin-induced ferroptosis in the same cells (data not shown).

In this study, we found that TrxR1 enzyme activity can be directly and potently inhibited by auranofin (with an apparent IC 50 of approximately 1.1 nM), which is slightly lower than the reported IC 50 (~20 nM) for human TrxR1 [47]. Notably, when the in vitro reaction time was prolonged, the reduction in product formation became progressively greater, and there was a sustained reduction in product formation (reflecting sustained TrxR1 inhibition). This observation suggests that auranofin inhibits TrxR1’s catalytic activity via covalent modification of the enzyme. Indeed, earlier studies reported that auranofin can inactivate TrxR1 by forming covalent diselenide bridges with its Sec498 residue [ 39, 48].

We found that the ability of RSL3 to inhibit TrxR1 in vitro was markedly lower than that of auranofin. The apparent IC 50 (approximately 4 μM) of RSL3 detected in this study is in line with the results of an earlier study [5]. On the basis of the similar patterns of TrxR1 inhibition by RSL3 and auranofin observed in this study, RSL3 may also inhibit TrxR1 in a covalent manner. This suggestion is partially supported by observations in HT22 cells, which revealed high-potency inhibition of TrxR1 activity by RSL3. Notably, the apparent discrepancy between high-potency RSL3 inhibition in intact cells and low-potency inhibition of TrxR1 catalytic activity in an in vitro enzymatic assay may suggest that RSL3 cannot readily access the catalytic site of TrxR1 in an aqueous reaction system in vitro. A similar phenomenon was also observed in an earlier study with flavonoid-type compounds: while these compounds can strongly activate the catalytic activity of cyclooxygenase in live cells in culture when present at low nM concentrations, they needed to be present at 100–200 μM concentrations to exert similar activation of the pure enzymes in the in vitro enzyme assays [49].

In conclusion, the results of our present study demonstrate that PDI plays a critical role in mediating RSL3-induced ferroptotic cell death in HT22 cells by catalyzing the dimerization of both nNOS and iNOS, followed by the accumulation of NO, ROS and lipid-ROS and ultimately ferroptotic cell death (summarized in Supplementary Figure S4). The discovery of an important role of PDI in mediating chemically induced ferroptosis suggests that PDI might serve as a potential drug target for the protection against ferroptosis-related neuronal degeneration.
