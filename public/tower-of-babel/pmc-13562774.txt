Diffuse MRI Edema Predicts Relapse in Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy–Related Inflammation
Authors: G. Abbas Kharal, Gowtham Yerneni, Kim J. Griffin, Nicolas Thompson, Ken Uchino, Irene Katzan, Dolora Wisco, Abeer Farrag, Jayashree Sundarajan, Andrew Russman, M. Shazam Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo identify MRI predictors of relapse and quantify relapse and mortality risk in cerebral amyloid angiopathy–related inflammation (CAA‐RI). A secondary objective was to assess the association between mycophenolate mofetil use and relapse risk.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of 36 patients with CAA‐RI treated at the Cleveland Clinic from January 2020 to December 2024. Relapse was defined as recurrent vasogenic edema on MRI after corticosteroid treatment. Edema was classified as focal (1 lobar region), multifocal (2–3 regions), or diffuse (≥ 4 regions). Cause‐specific Cox models and Gray's test evaluated predictors of relapse and mortality, and the effect of mycophenolate on relapse.ResultsAmong 36 patients (mean age 71.6 ± 8.2 years; 66.7% female), 11 (30.6%) relapsed, 9 (25.0%) died without having relapse, and 16 (44.4%) had neither event after a median prednisone taper of 4.5 months. 31 patients had follow‐up data for time‐to‐event analyses, with a median follow‐up time of 18.7 months. Cumulative relapse rates were 22.4%, 26.3%, and 44.0% at 6, 12, and 24 months, respectively. Diffuse edema strongly predicted relapse (hazard ratio [HR] = 12.26; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.95–76.94; p = 0.007). Mycophenolate was used in 11 patients; relapse occurred in 18.2% versus 30.0% without mycophenolate treatment. Initiation of mycophenolate at steroid taper in high‐relapse‐risk patients was associated with a statistically nonsignificant reduction in relapse risk (HR = 0.36; 95% CI = 0.07–1.09, p = 0.231).InterpretationRelapse in CAA‐RI is common and extends beyond the early post‐treatment phase. Diffuse edema on baseline MRI strongly predicts recurrence, enabling effective risk stratification. Mycophenolate shows promise as a steroid‐sparing therapy for preventing relapse in high‐risk patients and merits prospective evaluation.

Introduction

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy–related inflammation (CAA‐RI) is an immune‐mediated syndrome characterized by T‐cell–mediated inflammatory responses to beta‐amyloid deposits in the cerebral vasculature, leading to cerebral edema in patients with cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) [1, 2, 3]. Although T‐cell–mediated mechanisms are supported by histopathologic demonstration of perivascular CD3+ T‐cell infiltrates and CSF lymphocytic inflammation in patients with CAA‐RI, evidence of anti‐amyloid antibodies suggests a more complex immunopathogenesis [4, 5]. It represents a serious but potentially reversible manifestation of CAA, which occurs in millions of patients worldwide above the age of 50, particularly those with Alzheimer's pathology, typically presenting with subacute cognitive decline, seizures, encephalopathy, and, in some cases, coma [6, 7, 8]. Prompt recognition and immunosuppressive therapy, most commonly high‐dose corticosteroids, often lead to dramatic clinical and radiographic improvement [9]. However, the disease course can be unpredictable as the response to steroids is often short‐lived, and thus relapse following steroid taper or cessation is reported in many patients [9, 10, 11, 12]. Little is known about the incidence and predictors of recurrent inflammation in CAA and longer‐term treatment‐related outcomes [12].

Recent neuropathological and imaging studies have improved recognition of the prevalence and spectrum of CAA‐RI [6, 13, 14]. A pivotal study by Amin et al. found that radiographic evidence of inflammation was present in up to 10.8% of patients with sporadic CAA, and up to 28.5% in patients with CAA and moderate to severe white matter disease, highlighting that CAA‐RI may be underdiagnosed and more prevalent than previously appreciated [15]. Despite growing awareness, the risk factors that predispose patients to relapse after corticosteroid therapy remain poorly defined.

Moreover, while corticosteroids are the mainstay of initial treatment, the role of additional immunosuppressive therapies is increasingly recognized [9, 16, 17]. Regenhardt et al. reported that immunosuppressive therapy was associated with better functional outcomes in patients with CAA‐RI; however, data on which specific immunosuppression agent to use and how this influences relapse rates are unknown [16]. Similarly, a systematic review of 90 published CAA‐RI cases reported generally favorable outcomes with immunosuppression but did not delineate which patients are most susceptible to inflammatory relapse or which adjunct immunosuppressive strategies are most effective [18].

As histopathologic evidence supports the involvement of T‐cell‐mediated inflammation in the pathogenesis of CAA‐RI, prior studies have largely relied on cyclophosphamide as a steroid‐sparing agent in severe or relapsing cases. Cyclophosphamide is an alkylating chemotherapeutic agent, administered as monthly intravenous infusions or daily oral therapy, that suppresses immune activity by interfering with DNA replication and transcription, thereby inhibiting lymphocyte proliferation and reducing regulatory T‐cell populations [19]. Although potentially effective, cyclophosphamide is associated with clinically significant toxicities, including myelosuppression, infection risk, hemorrhagic cystitis, and secondary malignancy—complications that are particularly consequential in older patients with CAA‐RI, who often have reduced physiologic reserve, higher baseline comorbidity burden, and increased vulnerability to treatment‐related infectious and hematologic complications [3, 19, 20].

In contrast, mycophenolate mofetil is an oral inhibitor of inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, a key enzyme in de novo purine synthesis, that exerts a cytostatic effect on both T‐ and B‐lymphocytes by selectively suppressing their proliferation and antibody production and may represent a mechanistically relevant alternative in the context of the complex immunopathogenesis of CAA‐RI [21]. It is generally better tolerated than alkylating agents, with a lower risk of cumulative toxicity, cytopenias, and treatment‐limiting adverse effects. Its oral administration and favorable safety profile have supported its widespread use as a steroid‐sparing agent in other immune‐mediated disorders, yet it remains underexplored in CAA‐RI [20, 22].

Another major knowledge gap involves imaging biomarkers that may predict relapse risk in CAA‐RI [9, 23]. The radiographic presentation of CAA‐RI includes patchy or confluent hyperintensities on T2 fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain, that are frequently asymmetrical, often reaching the immediately adjacent subcortical white matter and accompanied by evidence of CAA such as cerebral microbleeds (CMB), macrohemorrhages (MH), and cortical superficial siderosis (cSS) [6, 8]. Moreover, recent data suggest that incorporating leptomeningeal enhancement (LE) and/or sulcal non‐nulling, that is, sulcal hyperintensity on T2 FLAIR imaging, enhances the sensitivity for diagnosing CAA‐RI [12]. However, the prognostic significance of specific edema patterns or associated imaging features in predicting recurrence remains largely unexplored [12].

The objective of this study was to characterize the clinical and imaging features of patients with CAA‐RI treated at a large tertiary center, evaluate rates of inflammatory relapse and mortality, and identify predictors of relapse after corticosteroid discontinuation. In particular, we sought to determine whether MRI features such as edema distribution or burden of hemorrhagic markers were associated with relapse. Additionally, we assessed the effect of mycophenolate mofetil as a steroid‐sparing immunosuppressive agent on relapse and survival outcomes.

Methods

This was a retrospective observational cohort study conducted at the Cleveland Clinic Cerebrovascular Center, adhering to the STROBE reporting guidelines for observational studies. The study included patients diagnosed with CAA‐RI between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. Diagnosis was based on established clinicoradiologic criteria consistent with validated diagnostic frameworks for probable CAA‐RI, or definite CAA‐RI in case of a biopsy‐proven diagnosis [2]. All patients were enrolled from the Cerebrovascular Inflammatory Disorders Clinic and were longitudinally followed throughout the study period. Due to the observational and retrospective nature of the study, patients were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to treatment assignments during data extraction and analysis. The study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB# 20‐1234) with a waiver of informed consent due to its retrospective nature. No pre‐defined data exclusions were applied; all patients meeting inclusion criteria for CAA‐RI were analyzed. De‐identified data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Clinical data were abstracted from the electronic medical record and included demographics, vascular risk factors, clinical features at presentation, treatment details, laboratory values, MRI characteristics, and longitudinal outcomes. Imaging features were reviewed by a board‐certified neurologist and vascular neurologist using an a priori classification framework developed for edema pattern assessment in this study. Edema patterns were categorized as focal (T2 FLAIR hyperintensity in a single lobar region), multifocal (2–3 lobar regions), or diffuse (≥ 4 lobar regions). Additional MRI features included the presence of edema with temporal lobe involvement (TLI) and validated markers of CAA‐RI including CMB burden (categorized as 5–10, 11–20, 21–50, 51–99, or ≥ 100), presence of asymmetric clustering of CMBs adjacent to regions of vasogenic edema, MH (> 5 mm), presence of cSS, and LE [2, 14, 24].

The primary outcome was inflammatory relapse, defined as the radiographic recurrence of CAA‐related inflammation or vasogenic edema on follow‐up MRI, with or without accompanying clinical symptoms, occurring after completion of an institutionally standardized prednisone taper (1 mg/kg up to a maximum dose of 60 mg/day, tapered by 5 mg every other week). Patients underwent systematic follow‐up MRI scans every 3 months during the first year and every 3–6 months thereafter for up to 4 years, allowing for rigorous longitudinal monitoring of inflammation and relapse. The secondary outcome was all‐cause mortality. Based on outcome status during follow‐up, patients were stratified into three groups: (1) experienced a relapse after completing corticosteroid taper, (2) died without relapse, or (3) neither relapsed nor died by the last recorded follow‐up.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics for the entire cohort and stratified by outcome group. Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons were performed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal‐Wallis tests for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Given the advanced age and comorbidities of the cohort, and the substantial proportion of deaths during follow‐up, we treated death as a competing risk in all time‐to‐relapse analyses. Time to event was calculated from the date of corticosteroid discontinuation to the earliest of relapse, death, or last follow‐up. Patients who neither relapsed nor died were censored at their most recent follow‐up.

We examined predictors of relapse using both cumulative incidence functions and single‐predictor cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards models. Variables included age, sex, race (White vs. non‐White), edema type (focal, multifocal, diffuse), presence of TLI, CMB count, CMB asymmetry, presence of cSS, MH, LE, initial clinical features (headache, cognitive impairment, subacute encephalopathy or altered mental status, seizure, focal neurological deficits), and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking). Treatment‐related variables assessed included steroid use, steroid‐sparing therapy (SST) at diagnosis, and documented noncompliance with immunosuppressive therapy. In instances where a categorical variable included no relapse or death events in a given subgroup, Cox models could not be fit, and Gray's test was used to evaluate differences in cumulative incidence curves.

Building on our previously published analysis of 22 patients, which identified diffuse edema as a strong predictor of relapse and suggested a potential benefit of mycophenolate in reducing relapse risk, institutional practice was updated to offer mycophenolate at initial presentation to high‐risk patients with diffuse edema [25]. The expanded cohort of 36 patients thus included individuals treated with mycophenolate either at diagnosis or after a first relapse. To assess its effect on relapse risk, we conducted three analyses. First, we evaluated whether initiating mycophenolate at steroid taper reduced first relapse using cause‐specific Cox models with steroid discontinuation as time zero. Second, we assessed whether any use of mycophenolate, at steroid taper or after a first relapse, was associated with delayed time to relapse; for patients treated after relapse, time zero was defined as edema resolution following the first relapse. Third, among patients who relapsed, we explored whether mycophenolate initiation after relapse prolonged relapse‐free survival. Due to sample size constraints, Cox modeling was not feasible for the latter analysis; Gray's test was therefore applied to compare cumulative incidence of second relapse, using edema resolution after the initial relapse as time zero.

We conducted exploratory analyses to assess the relationship between imaging biomarkers and clinical symptoms. Specifically, we evaluated the association of edema subtype (focal, multifocal, or diffuse) with seizure occurrence and cognitive impairment using Fisher's exact test. Additionally, we explored associations between apolipoprotein E (ApoE) genotype and imaging features, including edema distribution and burden.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 36 patients with CAA‐RI (35 probable, 1 definite) met the inclusion criteria. The mean age at diagnosis was 71.6 years (SD = 8.2), and 24 patients (66.7%) were female (Table 1). The majority were White (75.0%), with 19.4% Black and 2.8% identifying as “other” race. Edema patterns on initial MRI included diffuse pattern in 41.7%, focal in 30.6%, and multifocal in 27.8% of patients (Figure 1). Interrater reliability for edema pattern classification demonstrated near‐perfect agreement (Cohen κ = 0.96). CMBs were present in nearly all patients (94.4%), with 41.7% having > 100 CMBs, and asymmetric clustering in regions of vasogenic edema was noted in all patients with CMBs. MH, cSS, and edema with TLI were present in 41.7%, 50%, and 63.9% of patients, respectively. MRI brain scans with gadolinium contrast were available for review in 34 patients, of whom 16 (47.1%) had abnormal LE. Individual longitudinal MRI follow‐up patterns across patients are shown in Figure S1.

Headache (50.0%), subacute encephalopathy (47.2%), and cognitive impairment (38.9%) were the most common presenting symptoms. Two patients developed atrial fibrillation during the study period, both of whom underwent left atrial appendage closure with subsequent short‐term anticoagulation per standard clinical indications. One patient had asymmetric sulcal non‐nulling on T2 FLAIR and underwent a brain biopsy, which showed focal perivascular inflammation around amyloid‐laden cortical and leptomeningeal blood vessels, consistent with definite CAA‐RI. All patients received corticosteroid therapy with pulse‐dose intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 to 5 days, followed by an oral prednisone taper at 1 mg/kg for a median prednisone taper duration of 4.5 months (IQR, 3.5–6.4).

Of the 36 patients, 11 (30.6%) experienced a relapse, 9 (25.0%) died without experiencing relapse, and 16 (44.4%) remained alive and relapse‐free at a median follow‐up of 18.7 months. All relapse events reflected the predefined composite endpoint of radiographic recurrence of vasogenic edema with or without clinical symptoms after corticosteroid taper. Among the 11 patients who experienced relapse, 2 later died. Patients who relapsed or died were more likely to have hypertension (81.8% in the relapse group, 100% in the death group vs. 56.2% in the event‐free group; p = 0.049). Patients who died were older (mean age 77.9 vs. 69.3 years in relapsing patients and 69.6 years in the event‐free group; p = 0.024) and had lower baseline and follow‐up MoCA scores (baseline median, 9 vs. 24.5 overall; follow‐up median, 12 vs. 22 overall; p = 0.023). To our knowledge, autopsies were not performed on those who died. LE was most common in the event‐free group (68.8% vs. 18.2% in the relapse group and 42.9% in the death group, p = 0.029) (Table 1).

Five patients were excluded from time‐to‐event analyses: two died before completing corticosteroid therapy, and three had no follow‐up after treatment completion. Among the remaining 31 evaluable patients for time‐to‐event analyses, 11 experienced a relapse, 7 (63.6%) of whom had accompanying clinical symptoms, with a median time to relapse of 5.7 months (interquartile range [IQR] = 3.6–19.4; range = 3.0–41.9 months). The cumulative incidence of relapse rose over time, reaching 22.4% at 6 months, 26.3% at 12 months, and 44.0% at 24 months (Figure 2). Death incidence was 3.7% at 6 months, 20.7% at 12 months, and 26.6% at 24 months (Figure 2).

In univariable cause‐specific Cox regression models (Table 2), diffuse edema was the most potent imaging predictor of relapse (hazard ratio [HR] = 12.26; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.95–76.94; p = 0.007) compared to focal edema, and also remained a significant predictor of symptomatic CAA‐RI relapse (hazard ratio [HR] = 8.58; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.23–60.01; p = 0.030; Table S1). Multifocal edema was associated with an increased but nonsignificant relapse risk (HR = 2.69; 95% CI = 0.32–22.56; p = 0.361). Figure 3 shows the cumulative incidence of relapses by edema type. Moreover, having more than a hundred CMBs was associated with a significantly higher risk of symptomatic relapse (hazard ratio [HR] = 6.74; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.49–30.57; p = 0.013, Table S1). Older age, cognitive impairment at presentation, and history of smoking were each associated with increased hazard of death, consistent with their known adverse prognostic implications (Table 2).

Mycophenolate mofetil was used in 11 patients overall. Seven received it at the time of steroid taper, and four were started on it after an initial relapse. Of the 11 treated with mycophenolate, 2 (18.2%) had relapse, 1 (9.1%) died, and 8 (72.7%) neither died nor had relapse. Of the 20 not treated with mycophenolate, 6 (30.0%) had relapse, 6 (30.0%) died, and 8 (40.0%) neither had relapse nor died. Patients initiated on mycophenolate at the time of steroid taper had a lower relapse rate (18%) compared with those not receiving mycophenolate (30%), suggesting a potential steroid‐sparing benefit, although this did not reach statistical significance (HR = 0.36; 95% CI = 0.07–1.09; p = 0.231, Figure S2). There was no association between mycophenolate use at the time of steroid taper and time to death (HR = 1.39, 95% CI = 0.29–6.73, p = 0.680). The second analysis revealed that mycophenolate use at steroid taper or after initial relapse was not significantly associated with time to initial or second relapse (HR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.16–3.02, p = 0.619, Figure S3). Among the four patients initiated on mycophenolate following relapse, only one experienced a second relapse, and none died during follow‐up. Gray's test revealed no significant association between mycophenolate treatment after 1st relapse and time to second relapse (p = 0.855) for the third analysis of mycophenolate (Figure S4).

Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) genotyping was available in a limited subset of patients (n = 13) and was not significantly associated with relapse risk in our cohort, although those with diffuse edema were more likely to be homozygous carriers of the ApoE4 allele whereas none of the patients with focal edema were homozygous ApoE4 carriers (Table S2). Similarly, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) parameters, including white cell count, total protein, and IgG index, did not differ significantly by clinical outcome, although patients who died had a higher median CSF IgG index (0.86 vs. 0.47 in relapsing patients; p = 0.044), suggesting a higher burden of intrathecal protein synthesis.

Discussion

Our study provides novel insights into the longitudinal risk of relapse in CAA‐RI by leveraging competing risk models and detailed imaging phenotyping to identify patients at high risk of relapse and to inform treatment strategies for relapse prevention. Our cumulative incidence analysis adds to existing data and demonstrates that the risk of relapse of CAA‐RI increases steadily over time, with rates of 22.4% at 6 months, 26.3% at 12 months, and 44.0% by 24 months post‐steroid taper [12]. These findings highlight that relapse is not confined to the early post‐treatment period but remains a significant clinical challenge well into the second year of follow‐up.

Diffuse vasogenic edema pattern at presentation emerged as a significantly potent imaging predictor of relapse, conferring a twelvefold increased hazard compared with focal edema. Diffuse edema and a higher CMB burden at baseline were significant predictors of symptomatic relapse also. This perhaps suggests that a more widespread blood–brain barrier disruption, inflammatory burden, and microvascular fragility at baseline predispose to recurrent disease activity. In contrast, focal and multifocal edema patterns exhibited lower and non‐significant associations with relapse, underscoring the importance of quantifying edema distribution when risk‐stratifying patients for recurrence of inflammation and consideration of second‐line immunosuppressive treatment. Furthermore, the variability in edema patterns may add to the recently proposed notion that there exists a spectrum of CAA‐RI, with varying risks of relapse and underlying risk factors, and thus, treatment may need to be individualized in patients presenting with varying presentations along the CAA‐RI spectrum [6].

While corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of acute management, prolonged exposure is linked to substantial adverse effects, especially in older adults [26]. Our analysis of mycophenolate mofetil, a better‐tolerated steroid‐sparing agent compared to cyclophosphamide, revealed a consistent direction of effect toward reduced relapse risk across multiple analytic frameworks, although these associations did not reach statistical significance. Notably, despite limited statistical power related to the sample size, patients who received mycophenolate at the time of steroid discontinuation had a 64% lower hazard of relapse compared with those not treated with mycophenolate, and none of the patients initiated on mycophenolate post‐relapse died during follow‐up. These observations are consistent with our clinical experience and institutional approach to risk‐adapted management of CAA‐RI and support prospective evaluation of early introduction of mycophenolate in patients with high‐risk imaging features to mitigate relapse risk and reduce corticosteroid‐related toxicity.

Previous studies have underscored the prevalence and general benefit of immunosuppression in CAA‐RI but have fallen short of identifying specific agents or imaging predictors of relapse [9, 12, 15, 16]. Our work contributes to addressing these critical gaps by providing granular data on edema patterns, relapse timing, and the role of targeted immunotherapy. However, the impact of chronic immunosuppression on the underlying hemorrhagic risk associated with CAA remains unknown. Given the intrinsic vascular fragility in CAA, it is essential to determine whether immunosuppressive therapy modifies the risk of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage over time. This remains an important area for future investigation. Additionally, building upon prior data suggesting that advanced age at presentation may be associated with higher all‐cause mortality in CAA‐RI, our study highlights that not only advanced age but baseline cognitive impairment and smoking history are also associated with increased mortality, underscoring the need for comprehensive risk assessment beyond imaging markers alone [12].

In clinical practice, these findings advocate for a more personalized approach: patients presenting with diffuse edema on baseline MRI and other high‐risk features should be counseled about their elevated risk of relapse and considered for early initiation of steroid‐sparing immunosuppression such as mycophenolate. While our data support the use of mycophenolate as a potentially safer and effective steroid‐sparing option in high‐risk patients, these findings are observational and should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating. Randomized controlled trials are critically needed to compare immunosuppressive strategies, including mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, and emerging agents, to determine the optimal treatment approach for CAA‐RI.

Furthermore, given the shared amyloid‐driven inflammatory mechanisms between CAA‐RI and amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities with edema (ARIA‐E), both characterized by vasogenic edema as an immune‐mediated response to vascular amyloid, distinct from ARIA‐H (hemorrhage subtype) which reflects a hemorrhagic manifestation due to microvascular fragility, our findings provide mechanistic insights that may inform risk stratification, prevention, and management of ARIA‐E in Alzheimer's patients receiving anti‐amyloid therapies [27].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential bias in patient selection and outcome ascertainment, and reliance on medical record documentation may have limited the completeness of some variables. Although patients were managed within a specialized cerebrovascular inflammatory disorders clinic with standardized follow‐up, residual confounding by unmeasured variables cannot be excluded in this retrospective study. In addition, MRI acquisition was not uniformly timed across all patients, which may have introduced variability in the detection of radiographic inflammation and limited precise temporal alignment of imaging findings across the cohort. Second, despite being relatively large for a rare condition, the sample size remains modest, limiting statistical power for multivariable modeling and subgroup analyses.

Third, we relied on univariable Cox regression due to the number of outcome events, which may not fully account for confounding between predictors. Fourth, the use of mycophenolate reflected evolving institutional practice rather than randomized treatment assignment, introducing potential treatment‐selection bias and precluding direct comparison with other immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide. Furthermore, treatment with mycophenolate was not randomized and reflected clinical decision‐making, with patients exhibiting more severe disease, particularly those with diffuse edema, more likely to receive mycophenolate after an interim analysis. This confounding by indication in the latter half of the study period likely biases toward the null or underestimates the treatment benefit, as higher‐risk patients were preferentially treated. Finally, although the observed associations with mycophenolate are clinically compelling, they should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in larger, prospective, multicenter studies designed to define optimal immunosuppressive strategies for CAA‐RI.

Conclusion

CAA‐RI is a serious, immune‐mediated manifestation of CAA that can cause rapid neurologic deterioration. In our study, nearly one‐third of patients experienced a relapse after steroid therapy, with diffuse edema on baseline MRI emerging as a significant predictor of recurrence. While long‐term management strategies have remained uncertain, our findings suggest that mycophenolate may be an effective and safer steroid‐sparing option for older and medically fragile patients, who CAA‐RI often affects, offering a mechanistic alternative to more toxic agents such as cyclophosphamide.

Our results support integrating MRI biomarkers into relapse risk stratification and considering early initiation of steroid‐sparing therapy in patients at high risk for recurrence. Future prospective studies are needed to identify additional predictors of inflammation and to define the optimal duration, timing, and selection of immunosuppressive agents. Comparative studies of mycophenolate and other therapies will be essential to establish evidence‐based, long‐term treatment strategies for CAA‐RI.
