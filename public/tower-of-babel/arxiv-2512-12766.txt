Eigen, singular, cosine-sine, and Autonne–Takagi vectors distributions of random matrix ensembles
Yihan Guo, Lek-Heng Lim

Abstract
. We show that some of the best-known matrix decompositions of some of the best-known random matrix ensembles give us the unique G G -invariant uniform distributions on some of the best-known manifolds. The eigenvectors distributions of the Gaussian, Laguerre, and Jacobi ensembles are all given by the uniform distribution on the complete flag manifold. The singular vectors distributions of Ginibre ensembles are given by the uniform distribution on a product of the complete flag manifold with a Stiefel manifold. Circular ensembles split into two types: The cosine-sine vectors distributions of circular real, unitary, and quaternionic ensembles are given by the uniform distributions on products of a (partial) flag manifold with copies of the orthogonal, unitary, or compact symplectic groups. The Autonne–Takagi vectors distributions of circular orthogonal, Lagrangian, and symplectic ensembles are given by the uniform distributions on Lagrangian Grassmannians.



1.
Introduction

A flag manifold
\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the set of all
(k_{1},\dots,k_{p})
-flags, i.e., nested sequences of subspaces of dimensions
k_{1}<\dots<k_{p}
, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It has two noteworthy boundary cases: The
p=1
case is the Grassmannian or Grassmann manifold, the set of all
k
-planes, i.e.,
k
-dimensional subspaces, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
p=n-1
case is the
complete flag manifold
where
k_{1}=1
,
k_{2}=2,\dots,k_{n-1}=n-1
. These are given special notations:

\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\Flag(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad\Flag(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\Flag(1,2,\dots,n-1,\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Closely related is the Stiefel manifold
\V(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
k
-frames, i.e., ordered orthonormal basis of
k
-planes, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which will also play a role in this article.

The set-theoretic description above defines the flag manifold as an abstract manifold. However, one may also characterize it as a homogeneous space:

\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\O(n)/\bigl(\O(k_{1})\times\O(k_{2}-k_{1})\times\dots\times\O(k_{p}-k_{p-1})\times\O(n-k_{p})\bigr),

or as an embedded submanifold:

(1)

\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\{Q\Delta_{a}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in\mathsf{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):Q\in\O(n)\}.

Here
\Delta_{a}\coloneqq\diag(a_{1}I_{k_{1}-k_{0}},a_{2}I_{k_{2}-k_{1}},\dots,a_{p+1}I_{k_{p+1}-k_{p}})
is a diagonal matrix with arbitrary, fixed, and distinct
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{p+1}\in\mathbb{R}
appearing with multiplicities
k_{1}-k_{0},k_{2}-k_{1},\dots,k_{p+1}-k_{p}
where we set
k_{0}\coloneqq 0
and
k_{p+1}\coloneqq n
. We denote the space of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices by
\mathsf{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
The homogeneous space structure endows
\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{R}^{n})
with a unique
\O(n)
-invariant probability measure (see Section
2
). The submanifold structure allows points on
\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be uniquely represented as actual matrices (as opposed to equivalence classes of matrices).

A world apart from the flag manifold is the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE), a well-known probability distribution on
\mathsf{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with density

f_{\goe(n)}(X)=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}\pi^{-\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tr(X^{2})}.

Write
X=Q\Lambda Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
with
Q\in\O(n)
and
\Lambda\coloneqq\diag(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
for an eigenvalue decomposition of
X\in\mathsf{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with eigenvalues ordered
\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{n}
(or any other fixed prescribed order). For
X\sim\goe(n)
, both
\Lambda
and
Q
are random matrices. The distribution of
\Lambda
has been thoroughly studied. The distribution of
Q
has however received much less attention. A common belief is that there is not much to study — the latter is simply the uniform distribution on
\O(n)
, its normalized Haar measure, see
[
9
, p. 1623]
and
[
13
, p. 289]
for example. This is not quite right. An indication that it is inaccurate is that
Q
is evidently not unique, even when the eigenvalues are distinct and ordered, and all eigenvectors normalized to have unit norm. One may enforce uniqueness on the matrix of eigenvectors by further requiring that the first row of
Q
have all entries positive, as was done in
[
24
, p. 367]
; but this is a coordinate-dependent remedy, and describes the GOE eigenvectors distribution on a strictly smaller subset
\{Q\in\O(n):q_{1j}>0,\;j=1,\dots,n\}
that is not even a subgroup.
1
1

1

This issue was also raised in
https://mathoverflow.net/a/413187
but with no resolution. Our work here provides an answer: The eigenvectors should be regarded as points on a flag manifold, not the unitary group.

We will show in this article that the space underlying this distribution is in fact the complete flag manifold
\Flag(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and the GOE eigenvectors distribution its unique
\O(n)
-invariant uniform probability distribution. The treatment in
[
26
, p. 4]
gets close to this picture but stops short of mentioning the complete flag manifold.
We highlight two departures from existing works:

(i)

The issue above does not happen to the widely known QR decomposition of a random matrix from the Ginibre Ensemble
[
25
]
, i.e., an
n\times n
matrix of i.i.d. standard normal variates. In this case the distribution of the
Q
-factor is indeed the uniform distribution on
\O(n)
. The reason is that the
Q
-factor in a QR decomposition
A=QR
of a generic matrix
A
is unique as long as we restrict
R
to have all diagonal entries positive (or negative). On the other hand, the
Q
-factor in an eigenvalue decomposition
X=Q\Lambda Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
of a generic symmetric matrix
X
cannot be unique no matter what restriction we impose on
\Lambda
. The typical way to ensure uniqueness is to impose restrictions on
Q
itself; but once we do this, as we saw, we will no longer obtain a distribution defined on all of
\O(n)
.

(ii)

Our plural nomenclature, i.e.,
eigenvectors
distribution, is deliberate. Existing studies about the
eigenvector
distribution of GOE, or, more generally, of Wigner matrices, are limited to one eigenvector, i.e., a column of
Q

[
19
,
26
]
, or even one entry of
Q

[
26
]
. Here we study the distribution of the eigenbasis, i.e., the complete set of eigenvectors assembled in a matrix
Q
. It turns out that it is easier to study the whole
Q
than it is to study its column vectors or its entries in isolation. By using the characterization of flag manifold as a set of matrices in (
1
), the matrix of eigenvectors gives a well-defined map
\mathsf{S}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\Flag(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that takes
\goe(n)
on the former to the uniform distribution on the latter. Indeed, we will go further to show that one may obtain the unique
\O(n)
-invariant uniform probability distribution on
any
flag manifold
\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{R}^{n})
in much the same way.

Unsurprisingly, we will see that
(ii)
applies nearly verbatim when
\mathbb{R}
is replaced by
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{H}
: The eigenvectors distribution of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) yields the
\U(n)
-invariant uniform probability distribution on any complex flag manifold
\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Likewise the eigenvectors distribution of the Gaussian Symplectic Ensemble (GSE) yields the
\Sp(n)
-invariant uniform probability distribution on any quaternionic flag manifold
\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{H}^{n})
. These will be discussed alongside
(ii)
in Section
3
and then extended to the Laguerre and Jacobi ensembles.

In Section
4
, we will similarly study the singular vectors distributions of Ginibre ensembles over
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, and
\mathbb{H}
, known in the literature by the names Ginibre Orthogonal Ensemble (
GinOE
), Ginibre Unitary Ensemble (
GinUE
), and Ginibre Symplectic Ensemble (
GinSE
) respectively. These would also yield the uniform distributions on the real, complex, and quaternionic Stiefel manifolds as a side product.

We conclude with two sections looking into two kinds of ensembles of quite distinct nature but both falling under the heading of “circular ensembles.” In Section
5
, we discuss the cosine-sine vectors distributions of the Circular Real Ensemble (CRE), Circular Unitary Ensemble (CUE), and Circular Quaternion Ensemble (CQE). In Section
6
, we discuss the Autonne–Takagi vectors distributions of the Circular Orthogonal Ensemble (COE), Circular Lagrangian Ensemble (CLE), and Circular Symplectic Ensemble (CSE). What we call “Circular Lagrangian Ensemble” does not currently have a name in the existing literature; we will see in Section
6
why this is a fitting name.

2.
Matrices, manifolds, and ensembles

For easy reference, and to set notations and nomenclature, we will go over some essential background materials. While most of these are not new, they are not a mere replica of existing literature and cannot be found in any single source. There is perhaps some value in tabulating them here.

2.1.
Quaternions

We treat quaternions in the standard way as
x=a+b\mathrm{i}+c\mathrm{j}+d\mathrm{k}
with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}
denoting the imaginary units. As a reminder, multiplication is given by
(a_{1}+b_{1}\mathrm{i}+c_{1}\mathrm{j}+d_{1}\mathrm{k})(a_{2}+b_{2}\mathrm{i}+c_{2}\mathrm{j}+d_{2}\mathrm{k})=(a_{1}a_{2}-b_{1}b_{2}-c_{1}c_{2}-d_{1}d_{2})+(a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}a_{2}+c_{1}d_{2}-d_{1}c_{2})\mathrm{i}+(a_{1}c_{2}-b_{1}d_{2}+c_{1}a_{2}+d_{1}b_{2})\mathrm{j}+(a_{1}d_{2}+b_{1}c_{2}-c_{1}b_{2}+d_{1}a_{2})\mathrm{k}
; the conjugation of
x=a+b\mathrm{i}+c\mathrm{j}+d\mathrm{k}
is defined as
\bar{x}=a-b\mathrm{i}-c\mathrm{j}-d\mathrm{k}
; and modulus as
\lvert x\rvert\coloneqq\sqrt{x\bar{x}}
. We also regard
\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{H}
in the usual way, i.e., as an inclusion of real
*
-subalgebras.

2.2.
Matrices

We write

Proposition 3.1

(Quaternionic Stiefel, Grassmann, flag manifolds)
.

We have
\Sp(n)
-equivariant diffeomorphisms:

\displaystyle\V(k,\mathbb{H}^{n})

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times k}:X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}X=I\}\cong\Sp(n)/\Sp(n-k),

\displaystyle\Gr(k,\mathbb{H}^{n})

\displaystyle\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{H}^{n})

\displaystyle=\{Q\Delta_{a}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}\in\mathsf{Q}^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n}):Q\in\Sp(n)\}\cong\Sp(n)/\bigl(\Sp(k_{1})\times\dots\times\Sp(n-k_{p})\bigr).

Note that we identify these manifold with their matrix representations.

Proof.

The unitary symplectic group
\Sp(n)
acts transitively on
\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{H}^{n})
by conjugation. The stabilizer of
\Delta_{a}
is its centralizer

Z_{\Sp(n)}(\Delta_{a})=\Sp(k_{1})\times\dots\times\Sp(n-k_{p}).

-invariant; we must have
\varphi_{k_{1},\dots,k_{p}}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{H}^{n})\bigr)
. The other two cases follow by restriction as discussed previously.
∎

A word about the interpretation of Theorem
3.3
is in order. It says that any increasing sequence of eigenspaces of
X\sim\goe(n)
,

\spn\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k_{1}}\}\subseteq\spn\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k_{2}}\}\subseteq\dots\subseteq\spn\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k_{p}}\}

is uniformly distributed in the space of all
(k_{1},\dots,k_{p})
-flags. Here
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
are the column vectors of
Q
, i.e., the eigenvectors of
X
.

The most informative special case is when
p=n-1
, which yields the distribution of a complete set of eigenvectors of these random matrices. Note that in this case
\Delta_{a}=\diag(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is a diagonal matrix with
n
distinct diagonal entries
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. While the following corollary follows from Theorem
3.3
, we include a proof to explain why eigenvalue-ordered eigenbases are naturally parameterized by the complete flag manifold.

Corollary 3.4

(Eigenvectors distributions of Gaussian ensembles)
.

(i)

If
X\sim\goe(n)
, then
\varphi_{1,2,\dots,n-1}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
X\sim\gue(n)
, then
\varphi_{1,2,\dots,n-1}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(\mathbb{C}^{n})\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
X\sim\gse(n)
, then
\varphi_{1,2,\dots,n-1}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(\mathbb{H}^{n})\bigr)
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, any two eigenvectors of
X\sim\gse(n)
for the same (simple) eigenvalue differ by an element of
\Sp(1)
. It follows that the space of eigenbases is
\Sp(n)/\Sp(1)^{n}
. By Proposition
3.1
,
\Sp(n)/\Sp(1)^{n}\cong\Flag(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with the isomorphism given precisely by
\llbracket Q\rrbracket\mapsto Q\Delta_{a}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}
, where
\llbracket Q\rrbracket
denote equivalence class.
∎

The
p=1
special case of Theorem
3.3
deserves special highlight too. In this case, we view the result as a method to generate uniformly distributed points on the Grassmannian, arguably the most important flag manifold, using Gaussian ensembles.

Corollary 3.5

(Uniform sampling on Grassmannian)
.

Let
0<k<n
be integers.

(i)

If
X\sim\goe(n)
, then
\varphi_{k}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Gr(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
X\sim\gue(n)
, then
\varphi_{k}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Gr(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
X\sim\gse(n)
, then
\varphi_{k}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Gr(k,\mathbb{H}^{n})\bigr)
.

Observant readers might also have noticed that our proof of Theorem
3.3
(iii)
primarily relies only on the
\Sp(n)
-invariance of the probability density function for
\gse(n)
. Clearly, we could also have used the
\O(n)
-invariance of
\goe(n)
and the
\U(n)
-invariance of
\gue(n)
to deduce Theorem
3.3
(i)
and
(ii)
. This observation shows that the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
would in fact apply to any random matrix ensemble with
G
-invariant densities. From Table
5
, the probability densities of the Laguerre ensembles are

f_{\beta,m,n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}(X)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{mn/2}\Gamma_{n}^{1}(m/2)}(\det X)^{(m-n+1)/2-1}e^{-\tr(X)/2}&\text{if }\beta=1,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{1}{\Gamma_{n}^{2}(m)}(\det X)^{m-n}e^{-\tr(X)}&\text{if }\beta=2,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{1}{(1/2)^{2mn}\Gamma_{n}^{4}(2m)}(\det X)^{2m-2n+1}e^{-2\tr(X)}&\text{if }\beta=4,\end{cases}

and those of the Jacobi ensembles are

f_{\beta,l,m,n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{J}}(X)=\begin{cases}\frac{\Gamma_{n}^{1}((l+m)/2)}{\Gamma_{n}^{1}(l/2)\Gamma_{n}^{1}(m/2)}(\det X)^{(l-n+1)/2-1}\det(I_{n}-X)^{(m-n+1)/2-1}&\text{if }\beta=1,\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\Gamma_{n}^{2}(l+m)}{\Gamma_{n}^{2}(l)\Gamma_{n}^{2}(m)}(\det X)^{l-n}\det(I_{n}-X)^{m-n}&\text{if }\beta=2,\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{\Gamma_{n}^{4}(2l+2m)}{\Gamma_{n}^{4}(2l)\Gamma_{n}^{4}(2m)}(\det X)^{2l-2n+1}\det(I_{n}-X)^{2m-2n+1}&\text{if }\beta=4.\end{cases}

Clearly the
\beta=1,2,4
cases are each invariant under conjugation by
\O(n)
,
\U(n)
,
\Sp(n)
respectively. Hence we deduce the following:

Corollary 3.6

(Eigenvectors distributions of Laguerre and Jacobi ensembles)
.

Let
l\geq n
and
m\geq n
be positive integers. Let
\varphi_{k_{1},\dots,k_{p}}
be as in Definition
3.2
. Then we have the following:

(i)

If
X\sim\loe(m,n)
or
\joe(l,m,n)
, then
\varphi_{k_{1},\dots,k_{p}}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
X\sim\lue(m,n)
or
\jue(l,m,n)
, then
\varphi_{k_{1},\dots,k_{p}}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{C}^{n})\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
X\sim\lse(m,n)
or
\jse(l,m,n)
, then
\varphi_{k_{1},\dots,k_{p}}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(k_{1},\dots,k_{p},\mathbb{H}^{n})\bigr)
.

This shows that the Gaussian, Laguerre, and Jacobi ensembles differ only in their eigenvalues distributions, but have identical eigenvectors distributions.

4.
Singular vectors distributions of Ginibre ensembles

As we saw in the last section, the key to the results therein is to guess the “correct” manifold that parameterizes the eigenvectors. Once we have this, an appropriately chosen map would take the Gaussian ensemble to the uniform distribution on this manifold. For eigenvectors, the complete flag manifold is by and large the obvious candidate; furthermore, as we saw in (
1
) and the last column of Table
3
, it can be readily realized as a matrix submanifold. In this section, we will see that the “correct” matrix submanifold that parameterizes left and right singular vector pairs would not be that obvious.

From Table
5
, the densities of Ginibre ensembles are given by

f_{\beta,m,n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{G}}(Y)=\begin{cases}(1/2\pi)^{mn/2}e^{-\tr(Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Y)/2}&\text{if }\beta=1,\\[4.30554pt]
(1/\pi)^{mn}e^{-\tr(Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}Y)}&\text{if }\beta=2,\\[4.30554pt]
(2/\pi)^{2mn}e^{-2\tr(Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}Y)}&\text{if }\beta=4.\end{cases}

For our purpose, the only point to note is that
\gio(m,n)
is
\O(m)\times\O(n)
-invariant,
\giu(m,n)
is
\U(m)\times\U(n)
-invariant, and
\gis(m,n)
is
\Sp(m)\times\Sp(n)
-invariant.

Without loss of generality, we will assume that
m\geq n
throughout this section; the
m<n
case just follows from applying the results to
Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
or
Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}
. So any
Y\sim\gis(m,n)
has
n
district singular values probability one and we arrange them in descending order as in Table
2
.

Proposition 4.1

(Singular vectors distributions of Ginibre ensembles I)
.

Let
0<n\leq m
be integers and

\displaystyle\psi:\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\to[\V(n,\mathbb{R}^{m})\times\O(n)]/\O(1)^{n},\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\O},

\displaystyle\psi:\mathbb{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\to[\V(n,\mathbb{C}^{m})\times\U(n)]/\U(1)^{n},\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\U},

\displaystyle\psi:\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\to[\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Sp(n)]/\Sp(1)^{n},\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\Sp},

where the equivalence classes of left and right singular vectors are given by

\displaystyle\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\O}

\displaystyle=\llbracket(UD,VD)\rrbracket_{\O}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for any }D

\displaystyle\in\O(1)^{n},

\displaystyle\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\U}

\displaystyle=\llbracket(UD,VD)\rrbracket_{\U}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for any }D

\displaystyle\in\U(1)^{n},

\displaystyle\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\Sp}

\displaystyle=\llbracket(UD,VD)\rrbracket_{\Sp}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for any }D

\displaystyle\in\Sp(1)^{n}.

Then we have the following:

(i)

If
Y\sim\gio(m,n)
, then
\psi(Y)\sim\unif\bigl([\V(n,\mathbb{R}^{m})\times\O(n)]/\O(1)^{n}\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
Y\sim\giu(m,n)
, then
\psi(Y)\sim\unif\bigl([\V(n,\mathbb{C}^{m})\times\U(n)]/\U(1)^{n}\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
Y\sim\gis(m,n)
, then
\psi(Y)\sim\unif\bigl([\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Sp(n)]/\Sp(1)^{n}\bigr)
.

Proof.

For any
(A,B)\in\Sp(m)\times\Sp(n)
,
Y
and
AYB^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}
are identically distributed. So the equivalence classes
\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\Sp}
and
\llbracket(AU,BV)\rrbracket_{\Sp}
are also identically distributed. Since

[\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Sp(n)]/\Sp(1)^{n}\cong[\Sp(m)\times\Sp(n)]/[\Sp(m-n)\times\Sp(1)^{n}],

it follows from Proposition
2.1
that
\psi(Y)\sim\unif\bigl([\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Sp(n)]/\Sp(1)^{n}\bigr)
. The
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
cases are specializations of the result for
\mathbb{H}
.
∎

There is in fact another isomorphism,

(9)

[\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Sp(n)]/\Sp(1)^{n}\cong\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

embedding the space of (equivalence classes of) left and right singular vectors as a matrix submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{n\times(k+n)}
, and similarly over
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
. This characterization allows us to avoid quotient spaces and equivalence classes altogether. We regard the following as our main result of this section.

Theorem 4.2

(Singular vectors distributions of Ginibre ensembles II)
.

Let
0<n\leq m
be integers and

\displaystyle\xi:\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\to\V(n,\mathbb{R}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto(UV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},V\Delta_{a}V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}),

\displaystyle\xi:\mathbb{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\to\V(n,\mathbb{C}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto(UV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},V\Delta_{a}V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}),

\displaystyle\xi:\mathbb{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\to\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{H}^{n}),\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto(UV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},V\Delta_{a}V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}).

Then we have the following:

(i)

If
Y\sim\gio(m,n)
, then
\xi(Y)\sim\unif\bigl(\V(n,\mathbb{R}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
Y\sim\giu(m,n)
, then
\xi(Y)\sim\unif\bigl(\V(n,\mathbb{C}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{C}^{n})\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
Y\sim\gis(m,n)
, then
\xi(Y)\sim\unif\bigl(\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{H}^{n})\bigr)
.

Proof.

With probability one, the singular value decomposition is unique modulo the equivalence relation in Proposition
4.1
, i.e.,
\llbracket(U,V)\rrbracket_{\Sp}=\llbracket(UD,VD)\rrbracket_{\Sp}
for any
D\in\Sp(1)^{n}
. Since
UDD^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}=UV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}
and
V\Delta_{a}V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}=VD\Delta_{a}D^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}V^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}
, we always have a well-defined
\xi(Y)
. As in all earlier proofs, it remains to observe that the
\Sp(m)\times\Sp(n)
-invariance of
Y\sim\gis(m,n)
passes on to
\xi(Y)
, forcing the distribution of
\xi(Y)
to be the uniform distribution on
\V(n,\mathbb{H}^{m})\times\Flag(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, again a consequence of the uniqueness in Proposition
2.1
. The
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
cases are specializations of the result for
\mathbb{H}
.
∎

It follows immediately from Theorem
4.2
that we may obtain the uniform distribution on a Stiefel manifold by projecting the image of
\xi
.

Corollary 4.3

(Uniform sampling on Stiefel manifold)
.

Let
0<k<n
be integers and

\displaystyle\rho:\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\times}}

\displaystyle\to\V(k,\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto UV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},

\displaystyle\rho:\mathbb{C}^{n\times k}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\times}}

\displaystyle\to\V(k,\mathbb{C}^{n}),\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto UV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},

\displaystyle\rho:\mathbb{H}^{n\times k}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\times}}

\displaystyle\to\V(k,\mathbb{H}^{n}),\quad

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\mapsto UV^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}.

Then we have the following:

(i)

If
Y\sim\gio(n,k)
, then
\rho(Y)\sim\unif\bigl(\V(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
Y\sim\giu(n,k)
, then
\rho(Y)\sim\unif\bigl(\V(k,\mathbb{C}^{n})\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
Y\sim\gis(n,k)
, then
\rho(Y)\sim\unif\bigl(\V(k,\mathbb{H}^{n})\bigr)
.

5.
Cosine-sine vectors distributions of circular ensembles

Evidently, the uniform distributions in (
8
) fall into two types most clearly seen from the two hierarchies

(10)

\O(n)\subseteq\U(n)\subseteq\Sp(n)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\LGr(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\subseteq\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})\subseteq\LGr(\mathbb{H}^{2n}).

Here we will discuss the first trio of circular ensembles, with the second deferred to Section
6
.
Our commentary in the beginning of Section
4
is particularly apt for these circular ensembles — figuring out the “correct” matrix submanifold that parameterizes the left and right cosine-sine vectors pairs is the trickiest part of this section.

Circular ensembles are uniform distributions and thus their probability densities are constant functions entirely determined by their respective volume in Table
4
. Explicitly, the densities for
\cre(n)
,
\cue(n)
,
\cqe(n)
are

(11)

f_{\beta,n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{C}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\frac{\gamma(1,n,1)}{\theta(2n,n(n+1))}&\text{if }\beta=1,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\gamma(1,n,2)}{\sqrt{n}\theta(n+1,2n(n+1))}&\text{if }\beta=2,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\gamma(1,n,4)}{\theta(2n,4n(n+1))}&\text{if }\beta=4,\end{cases}

with respect to the Riemannian volume form of
\O(n)
,
\U(n)
, and
\Sp(n)
respectively.

Proposition 5.1

(Cosine-sine vectors distributions of circular ensembles I)
.

Let
0<k<n
be integers and

\displaystyle\eta:\O_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}(n)

\displaystyle\to\bigl(\O(k)^{2}\times\O(n-k)^{2}\bigr)/\bigl(\O(1)^{k}\times\O(n-2k)\bigr),\quad

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\O},

\displaystyle\eta:\U_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}(n)

\displaystyle\to\bigl(\U(k)^{2}\times\U(n-k)^{2}\bigr)/\bigl(\U(1)^{k}\times\U(n-2k)\bigr),\quad

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\U},

\displaystyle\eta:\Sp_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}(n)

\displaystyle\to\bigl(\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)^{2}\bigr)/\bigl(\Sp(1)^{k}\times\Sp(n-2k)\bigr),\quad

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\Sp},

where the equivalence classes of left and right cosine-sine vectors are defined by

\displaystyle\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\O}

\displaystyle=\llbracket(U_{1}D,V_{1}D,U_{2}\diag(D,W),V_{2}\diag(D,W))\rrbracket_{\O},\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\in\O(1)^{k},\;W\in\O(n-2k),

\displaystyle\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\U}

\displaystyle=\llbracket(U_{1}D,V_{1}D,U_{2}\diag(D,W),V_{2}\diag(D,W))\rrbracket_{\U},\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\in\U(1)^{k},\;W\in\U(n-2k),

\displaystyle\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\Sp}

\displaystyle=\llbracket(U_{1}D,V_{1}D,U_{2}\diag(D,W),V_{2}\diag(D,W))\rrbracket_{\Sp},\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\in\Sp(1)^{k},\;W\in\Sp(n-2k).

Then we have the following:

(i)

If
Q\sim\cre(n)
, then
\eta(Q)\sim\unif\bigl([\O(k)^{2}\times\O(n-k)^{2}]/[\O(1)^{k}\times\O(n-2k)]\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
Q\sim\cue(n)
, then
\eta(Q)\sim\unif\bigl([\U(k)^{2}\times\U(n-k)^{2}]/[\U(1)^{k}\times\U(n-2k)]\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
Q\sim\cqe(n)
, then
\eta(Q)\sim\unif\bigl([\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)^{2}]/[\Sp(1)^{k}\times\Sp(n-2k)]\bigr)
.

Proof.

We remind the reader of the cosine-sine decomposition in Table
2
: For any
k\leq n/2
, a matrix
Q\in\Sp(n)
may be decomposed as

Q=\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&0\\
0&U_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}C&S&0\\
-S&C&0\\
0&0&I_{n-2k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}&0\\
0&V_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}\end{bmatrix},

where
U_{1},V_{1}\in\Sp(k)
,
U_{2},V_{2}\in\Sp(n-k)
, and
C=\diag(c_{1},\dots,c_{k}),S=\diag(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
with
c_{i},s_{i}\in[0,1]
,
c_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2}=1
for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

For a random matrix
Q\sim\cqe(n)
,
U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2}
are all random matrices and, with probability one, we may assume that
c_{1}<\dots<c_{k}
and
s_{1}>\dots>s_{k}
. For any
A_{1},B_{1}\in\Sp(k)
,
A_{2},B_{2}\in\Sp(n-k)
, the random matrix
\diag(A_{1},A_{2})Q\diag(B_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},B_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}})
is identically distributed as
Q
. It follows that cosine-sine vectors
\llbracket(A_{1}U_{1},B_{1}V_{1},A_{2}U_{2},B_{2}V_{2})\rrbracket_{\Sp}
are identically distributed as
\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\Sp}
. Since
(A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2})\in\Sp(k)\times\Sp(k)\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Sp(n-k)
, this shows that the cosine-sine vectors distribution on the space

[\Sp(k)\times\Sp(k)\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Sp(n-k)]/[\Sp(1)^{k}\times\Sp(n-2k)],

is invariant under the action of
\Sp(k)\times\Sp(k)\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Sp(n-k)
. By Proposition
2.1
, there is only one such distribution, implying that

\eta(Q)\sim\unif\bigl([\Sp(k)\times\Sp(k)\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Sp(n-k)]/[\Sp(1)^{k}\times\Sp(n-2k)]\bigr).

The
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
cases are specializations of the result for
\mathbb{H}
.
∎

As we did with (
9
) in the last section, here we would also like our parameter space for left and right cosine-sine vector pairs to be a matrix submanifold, with points that are actual matrices instead of equivalence classes. We accomplish this with the isomorphism

[\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)^{2}]/[\Sp(1)^{k}\times\Sp(n-2k)]\cong\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{H}^{n-k}),

and with it we arrive at our main result of this section.

Theorem 5.2

(Cosine-sine vectors distributions of circular ensembles II)
.

Let
0<k<n
be integers and

\displaystyle\zeta:\O_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}(n)

\displaystyle\to\O(k)^{2}\times\O(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{R}^{n-k}),\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\mapsto(U_{1}(V_{2})_{kk}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},V_{1}(V_{2})_{kk}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},U_{2}V_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},V_{2}\Delta_{a}V_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}),

\displaystyle\zeta:\U_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}(n)

\displaystyle\to\U(k)^{2}\times\U(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{C}^{n-k}),\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\mapsto(U_{1}(V_{2})_{kk}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},V_{1}(V_{2})_{kk}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},U_{2}V_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},V_{2}\Delta_{a}V_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}),

\displaystyle\zeta:\Sp_{\scriptscriptstyle\times}(n)

\displaystyle\to\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{H}^{n-k}),\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\mapsto(U_{1}(V_{2})_{kk}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},V_{1}(V_{2})_{kk}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},U_{2}V_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}},V_{2}\Delta_{a}V_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}),

where
(V_{2})_{kk}
denotes the
k\times k
leading principal submatrix of
V_{2}
. Then we have the following:

(i)

If
Q\sim\cre(n)
, then
\zeta(Q)\sim\unif\bigl(\O(k)^{2}\times\O(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{R}^{n-k})\bigr)
.

(ii)

If
Q\sim\cue(n)
, then
\zeta(Q)\sim\unif\bigl(\U(k)^{2}\times\U(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{C}^{n-k})\bigr)
.

(iii)

If
Q\sim\cqe(n)
, then
\zeta(Q)\sim\unif\bigl(\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{H}^{n-k})\bigr)
.

Proof.

Let
Q\sim\cqe(n)
. With probability one,
(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})
is uniquely defined up to the equivalence relation

\llbracket(U_{1},V_{1},U_{2},V_{2})\rrbracket_{\Sp}=\llbracket(U_{1}D,V_{1}D,U_{2}\diag(D,A),V_{2}\diag(D,A))\rrbracket_{\Sp}

for any
D\in\Sp(1)^{k}
and
A\in\Sp(n-2k)
. So
\zeta(Q)
is well-defined as a random matrix. Similar to the proof of Proposition
5.1
, the
\Sp(n)\times\Sp(n)
-invariance of
Q
leads us to the
\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)^{2}
-invariance of
\zeta(Q)
, forcing the distribution of the latter to be the uniform distribution on
\Sp(k)^{2}\times\Sp(n-k)\times\Flag(1,\dots,k,\mathbb{H}^{n-k})
. The
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
cases are specializations of the result for
\mathbb{H}
.
∎

When
n
is even and
k
is chosen to be
n/2
, the results above take a particularly neat form:

Let

be a positive integer. Let
\eta
and
\zeta
be as in Proposition

and Theorem

respectively.

(i)

If

, then

and

.

6.
Autonne–Takagi vectors distributions of circular ensembles

The Autonne–Takagi decompositions and Lagrangian Grassmannians will be important in this section. So before getting to the circular ensembles, we will need to fill in some gaps in the literature: (i) The standard Autonne–Takagi decomposition
[
16
, Corollaries 2.2.6(a) and 4.4.4(c)]
applies to complex symmetric matrix but we will need a version for Lagrangian and symplectically symmetric matrices. (ii) While the matrix submanifold representation for real Lagrangian Grassmannian in the fourth column of Table
3
is known
[
1
, pp. 929–930]
, i.e.,

\LGr(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\cong\U(n)/\O(n)\cong\{QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}:Q\in\U(n)\},

we will need to establish the complex analogue and (part of) the quaternionic one.

In the case of the complex Lagrangian Grassmannian, there are definitions that deviate from the one we used
[
18
, pp. 79–80]
. A notable example is Arnold’s definition
[
2
]
, where
\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
is taken to be the set of Lagrangian subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
with respect to the
real
symplectic form in
\mathbb{R}^{4n}
, as opposed to the complex symplectic form in
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
. The result is that Arnold’s complex Lagrangian Grassmannian is isomorphic to
\U(n)
instead of
\Sp(n)/\U(n)
.

6.1.
Complex Lagrangian Grassmannian

We begin with an Autonne–Takagi decomposition decomposition for
X\in\mathsf{V}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
. Recall from (
3
) that
I_{n,n}=\diag(I_{n},-I_{n})
.

Proposition 6.1

(Lagrangian Autonne–Takagi decomposition)
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
with
X=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}
. Then there exist
Q\in\U(2n)
and
\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
such that

X=Q\Sigma Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}.

Proof.

Let
Z=I_{n,n}X
. Then
Z^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}I_{n,n}
. Since
X=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}
, we have
I_{n,n}X=I_{n,n}X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}I_{n,n}
, and thus
Z=Z^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}
. So
Z
has an eigenvalue decomposition
Z=U\Lambda U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}
with
U\in\U(2n)
and diagonal
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
. Therefore,

X=I_{n,n}Z=I_{n,n}U\Lambda U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}=(I_{n,n}U)(\Lambda I_{n,n})(I_{n,n}U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}).

Now let
Q=I_{n,n}U
and
\Sigma=\Lambda I_{n,n}
. Clearly,
Q\in\U(2n)
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
is diagonal. Furthermore,
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}=I_{n,n}U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}I_{n,n}I_{n,n}=I_{n,n}U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}=Q
, as required.
∎

The next result will also be useful for Theorem
6.8
.

Lemma 6.2

(
\U(n)
as a subgroup of
\Sp(n)
)
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Then

(12)

\displaystyle\U(n)

\displaystyle\cong\{Q\in\Sp(n):Q=I_{n,n}QI_{n,n}\}

(13)

\displaystyle=\{Q\in\Sp(n):Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}Q=I\}.

Proof.

For convenience, we define the involution

(14)

\tau(Q)\coloneqq I_{n,n}QI_{n,n}

for any
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
. So
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}=\tau(Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}})
and the condition in (
12
) is
\tau(Q)=Q
. Recall that
\Sp(n)=\U(2n)\cap\Sp(2n,\mathbb{C})
. Write
Q=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}\in\Sp(n)
. Then
\tau(Q)=Q
implies that
Q=\diag(A,D)
. As
Q\in\Sp(2n,\mathbb{C})
, we have
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}Q=J_{2n}
and thus

Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}Q=\begin{bmatrix}0&A^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}D\\
-D^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}A&0\end{bmatrix}.

Hence
A^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}D=I
. As
Q\in\U(2n)
, we have
A^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}A=I
and thus
D=\bar{A}
. Hence

\{Q\in\Sp(n):Q=\tau(Q)\}\subseteq\{\diag(A,\bar{A}):A\in\U(n)\}\cong\U(n).

We verify that
\tau\diag\bigl((A,\bar{A})\bigr)=\diag(A,\bar{A})
and so “
\subseteq
” may be replaced by “
=
” to give (
12
). It remains to show that for
Q\in\Sp(n)
, the conditions
Q=\tau(Q)
and
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}Q=I
are equivalent. If
Q=\tau(Q)
, then

Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}Q=I_{n,n}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}I_{n,n}Q=I_{n,n}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}I_{n,n}(I_{n,n}QI_{n,n})=I_{n,n}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}QI_{n,n}=I.

If
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}Q=I
, then

Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}\tau(Q)=Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}I_{n,n}QI_{n,n}=I_{n,n}(I_{n,n}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}I_{n,n})QI_{n,n}=I_{n,n}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}QI_{n,n}=I_{n,n}^{2}=I.

Hence we have (
13
).
∎

Theorem 6.3

(Complex Lagrangian Grassmannian as matrix submanifold)
.

For any positive integer
n
, we have

(15)

\displaystyle\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})

\displaystyle\cong\Sp(n)/\U(n)\cong\{QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}:Q\in\Sp(n)\}

\displaystyle=\Sp(n)\cap\mathsf{V}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2n})=\{X\in\Sp(n):X=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}\}.

Proof.

As an abstract manifold,
\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
is the set of Lagrangian subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
with respect to its standard complex symplectic form. The first diffeomorphism may be found in
[
5
, Proposition 2.11]
. We will establish the remaining two characterizations. Note that
\tau
in (
14
) restricts to an involutive automorphism on
\Sp(n)
. By (
12
),
\{Q\in\Sp(n):Q=\tau(Q)\}\cong\U(n)
. Since
\Sp(n)/\U(n)
and
\{Q\in\Sp(n):\tau(Q)=Q^{-1}\}
are connected, by
[
21
, Proposition 12]
we have

\Sp(n)/\U(n)\cong\underset{\displaystyle\overset{\mkern 4.0mu\rotatebox{90.0}{$\,=$}}{\{X\in\Sp(n):X=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}\}}}{\{Q\in\Sp(n):\tau(Q)=Q^{-1}\}}=\underset{\displaystyle\overset{\mkern 4.0mu\rotatebox{90.0}{$\,=$}}{\{QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}:Q\in\Sp(n)\}}}{\{Q\tau(Q)^{-1}:Q\in\Sp(n)\}}

as required.
∎

6.2.
Quaternionic Lagrangian Grassmannian

To get an Autonne–Takagi decomposition for
X\in\mathsf{Y}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
, we will need the following decomposition of complex skew-symmetric matrices, regarded variously as a skew-symmetric analog of Autonne–Takagi decomposition
[
16
, Corollary 2.2.6(b)]
or a special case of Youla decomposition
[
28
, p. 694]
. Recall from (
3
) that
J_{2n}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&-I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
with
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}=-X
. Then there exists
U\in\U(n)
such that

X=U\diag(\sigma_{1}J_{2},\dots,\sigma_{k}J_{2},0,\dots,0)U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},

where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
are the nonzero singular values of
X
and
J_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
.

We emphasize that the following symplectic Autonne–Takagi decomposition differs from both the quaternionic Autonne–Takagi decomposition in
[
17
]
and the QDQ decomposition in
[
7
, p. 16]
.

Proposition 6.5

(Symplectic Autonne–Takagi decomposition)
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
with
X=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
. Then there exist
Q\in\U(2n)
and
\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
such that

X=Q\Sigma Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}.

Proof.

Let
Z=J_{2n}X
. Then

Z^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}=(-J_{2n}J_{2n})X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}(-J_{2n})=-J_{2n}(-J_{2n}X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n})=-J_{2n}X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}=-J_{2n}X=-Z.

By Lemma
6.4
, there exist
U\in\U(2n)
and
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}>0
so that

Z=U\diag(\sigma_{1}J_{2},\dots,\sigma_{k}J_{2},0\dots,0)U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}.

Observe that

\diag(\sigma_{1}J_{2},\dots,\sigma_{k}J_{2},0\dots,0)=\diag(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k},0,\dots,0)\diag(J_{2},\dots,J_{2}),

and that there exists a permutation matrix
P
with
\diag(J_{2},\dots,J_{2})=PJ_{2n}P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
. Hence,

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=J_{2n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}Z=J_{2n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}U\diag(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k},0,\dots,0)PJ_{2n}P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}

(16)

\displaystyle=(J_{2n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}UP)\bigl(P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\diag(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k},0,\dots,0)P\bigr)(J_{2n}P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}).

Let
Q\coloneqq J_{2n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}UP
. Then

Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}=-J_{2n}P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}J_{2n}=J_{2n}P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}U^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},

and clearly
Q\in\U(2n)
. Let
\Sigma\coloneqq P^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\diag(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k},\sigma_{k},0,\dots,0)P
. Noting that conjugation by a permutation matrix simply permutes the diagonal entries, we see that
\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
after relabeling of the diagonal entries. Hence the last term in (
16
) takes the form
Q\Sigma Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
.
∎

Using the symplectic Autonne–Takagi decomposition, we deduce the description of a quaternionic Lagrangian Grassmannian in the last column of Table
3
.

Corollary 6.6

(Quaternionic Lagrangian Grassmannian as matrix submanifold)
.

For any positive integer
n
, we have

\displaystyle\LGr(\mathbb{H}^{2n})

\displaystyle\cong\U(2n)/\Sp(n)\cong\{QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}:Q\in\U(2n)\}

(17)

\displaystyle=\U(2n)\cap\mathsf{Y}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2n})=\{X\in\U(2n):X=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}\}.

Proof.

As an abstract manifold,
\LGr(\mathbb{H}^{2n})
is the set of Lagrangian subspaces in
\mathbb{H}^{2n}
with respect to its standard quaternionic skew-Hermitian form. The first and second isomorphisms may be found in
[
5
, Proposition 2.32]
and
[
23
, Section 1]
. It remains to show the alternate characterization in (
17
). Let
X\in\U(2n)
with
X=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
. By Proposition
6.5
, we must have
X=Q\Sigma Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
for some
Q\in\U(2n)
and
\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{2n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}^{2n\times 2n}
. But since
X\in\U(2n)
, we must also have that
\Sigma\in\U(2n)
, which is only possible if
\Sigma=I
. Hence
X=QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
. Conversely, for any
Q\in\U(2n)
, we clearly have that
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}=(QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
, and thus
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}\in\U(2n)\cap\mathsf{Y}^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
.
∎

The next result will be useful for Theorem
6.8
.

Lemma 6.7

(
\Sp(n)
as a subgroup of
\U(2n)
)
.

For any positive integer
n
, we have

\Sp(n)=\{Q\in\U(2n):Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}Q=I\}.

Proof.

As
\Sp(n)=\U(2n)\cap\Sp(2n,\mathbb{C})
, we need to show that the conditions
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}Q=J_{2n}
and
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}Q=I
are equivalent. Suppose
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}Q=J_{2n}
, then

Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}Q=-J_{2n}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}Q=-J_{2n}^{2}=I.

Conversely, suppose
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}Q=I
, then
-J_{2n}Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}Q=I
. So
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}J_{2n}Q=-J_{2n}^{-1}=J_{2n}
.
∎

6.3.
Distributions on Lagrangian Grassmannians

As in Section
5
, the densities of
\coe(n)
,
\cle(n)
,
\cse(n)
are constant functions determined by their respective volume in Table
4
. Explicitly,

g_{\beta,n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{C}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\frac{\gamma(1,n,2)}{\sqrt{n}\theta(1-n,n(n+1))\gamma(1,n,1)}&\text{if }\beta=1,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\sqrt{n}\gamma(1,n,4)}{\theta(n-1,2n(n+1))\gamma(1,n,2)}&\text{if }\beta=2,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\gamma(1,2n,2)}{\sqrt{2n}\theta(1,4n^{2})\gamma(1,n,4)}&\text{if }\beta=4,\end{cases}

with respect to the Riemannian volume form of
\U(n)/\O(n)
,
\Sp(n)/\U(n)
, and
\U(2n)/\Sp(n)
respectively. To the best of our knowledge, the space
\Sp(n)/\U(n)
made a passing appearance in
[
7
, p. 19]
but is otherwise not studied in the random matrix literature. For easy reference, we call it the circular Lagrangian ensemble in this article.

Theorem 6.8

(Autonne–Takagi vectors distributions of circular ensembles)
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and

\displaystyle\omega:\LGr(\mathbb{R}^{2n})

\displaystyle\to\U(n)/\O(n),\quad

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket Q\rrbracket_{\O},

\displaystyle\omega:\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})

\displaystyle\to\Sp(n)/\U(n),\quad

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket Q\rrbracket_{\U},

\displaystyle\omega:\LGr(\mathbb{H}^{2n})

\displaystyle\to\U(2n)/\Sp(n),\quad

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto\llbracket Q\rrbracket_{\Sp},

where the equivalence classes of Autonne–Takagi vectors are defined by

\displaystyle\llbracket Q\rrbracket_{\O}

\displaystyle=\llbracket QW\rrbracket_{\O},\quad

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\in\O(n),

\displaystyle\llbracket Q\rrbracket_{\U}

\displaystyle=\llbracket QW\rrbracket_{\U},\quad

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\in\U(n),

\displaystyle\llbracket Q\rrbracket_{\Sp}

\displaystyle=\llbracket QW\rrbracket_{\Sp},\quad

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\in\Sp(n).

Then we have the following:

(i)

If
X\sim\coe(n)
, then
\omega(X)\sim\unif(\U(n)/\O(n))
.

(ii)

If
X\sim\cle(n)
, then
\omega(X)\sim\unif(\Sp(n)/\U(n))
.

(iii)

If
X\sim\cse(n)
, then
\omega(X)\sim\unif(\U(2n)/\Sp(n))
.

Proof.

We begin with the
\cse(n)
case. Using the characterization in Corollary
6.6
, we let
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}=ZZ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
be two Autonne–Takagi decompositions of
X\in\LGr(\mathbb{H}^{2n})
. Then as
(Z^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}Q)(Z^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}Q)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}=I
, we have that
Z^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{H}}Q\in\Sp(n)
by Lemma
6.7
. Hence
\omega(X)
is well-defined. Now let
X\sim\cse(n)
. The density of
\cse(n)
, being constant, is trivially invariant under any group action. So
X
and
AXA^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
are identically distributed for any
A\in\U(2n)
. Hence so are
\omega(X)
and
\omega(AXA^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}})
. Let
X=QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
be an Autonne–Takagi decomposition, noting that
Q
is now a random matrix. Then

\omega(AXA^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}})=\omega\bigl(AQ(AQ)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}\bigr)=\llbracket AQ\rrbracket_{\Sp}=A\llbracket Q\rrbracket_{\Sp}=A\omega(X).

So
\omega(X)
and
A\omega(X)
are identically distributed. Since this holds for all
A\in\U(2n)
, it follows from Proposition
2.1
that
\omega(X)\sim\unif(\U(2n)/\Sp(n))
.

The
\cle(n)
case is similar, with Corollary
6.3
taking the place of Corollary
6.6
and (
13
) taking the place of Lemma
6.7
, together with corresponding replacement of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}
by
X^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}
and
\U(2n)/\Sp(n)
by
\Sp(n)/\U(n)
. The
\coe(n)
case is also similar.
∎

We conclude with a brief mention of the relations between the circular ensembles in this section with those in Section
5
.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Then we have the following:

(i)

If
Q\sim\cue(n)
, then
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\sim\coe(n)
.

(ii)

If
Q\sim\cqe(n)
, then
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}\sim\cle(n)
.

(iii)

If
Q\sim\cue(2n)
, then
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{S}}\sim\cse(n)
.

Proof.

We will just prove
(ii)
as the other two cases are well-known
[
23
, Section 1]
. The group
\Sp(n)
acts on the submanifold characterization of
\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
in (
15
) via
(A,QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}})\mapsto AQQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}A^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}
for any
A\in\Sp(n)
and
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}\in\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
. Let
Q\sim\cqe(n)
. Then
AQ
and
Q
are identically distributed for any
A\in\Sp(n)
. Thus
AQQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}A^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}
and
QQ^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{L}}
are also identically distributed on
\LGr(\mathbb{C}^{2n})
. Since this holds for all

, we have

by Proposition
2.1
.
∎

7.
Conclusion

The map that takes a matrix to its matrix of eigen, singular, cosine-sine, or Autonne–Takagi vectors is often a
G
-equivariant map between well-known spaces. By representing all spaces as matrix submanifolds, we may view such a map as taking random matrices to random matrices, mapping well-known random matrix ensembles to the
G
-invariant distributions on manifolds of geometric importance.

Another observation is that the distributions in the last column of Table
1
are all uniform distributions. This leads us to the interpretation that the matrix decompositions in Table
2
resolve the randomness in these random matrix ensembles into two parts: a uniform part captured by the eigen, singular, cosine-sine, or Autonne–Takagi
vectors
; and a non-uniform part captured by the eigen, singular, cosine-sine, or Autonne–Takagi
values
.

The maps in this article are all univariate maps. For example,
\varphi_{1,\dots,n-1}
takes one random matrix
X\sim\goe(n)
to another random matrix
\varphi_{1,2,\dots,n-1}(X)\sim\unif\bigl(\Flag(\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)
. We might ask about the multivariate analogue, in the spirit of free probability. We leave this to future work for interested readers.

Appendix A
Alternative definitions of the ensembles

Let
\nor_{\mathbb{R}}(0,\sigma^{2})
,
\nor_{\mathbb{C}}(0,\sigma^{2})
, and
\nor_{\mathbb{H}}(0,\sigma^{2})
denote the real, complex, and quaternionic normal distributions respectively, i.e., with densities

f_{\beta,\sigma^{2}}(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-y^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}&\text{if }\beta=1,\;y\in\mathbb{R},\\[6.45831pt]
\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2}}e^{-|y|^{2}/\sigma^{2}}&\text{if }\beta=2,\;y\in\mathbb{C},\\[6.45831pt]
\frac{4}{\pi^{2}\sigma^{4}}e^{-2|y|^{2}/\sigma^{2}}&\text{if }\beta=4,\;y\in\mathbb{H},\end{cases}

respectively. One way to view Gaussian, Jacobi, Laguerre, and Gini ensembles is that they are random matrices that can be generated from the univariate normal distribution:

(i)

X\sim\goe(n)
iff
X
is a real symmetric
n\times n
random matrix with independent entries

x_{ii}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1),\quad 1\leq i\leq n;\qquad x_{ij}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(0,\frac{1}{2}\Bigr),\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n.

(ii)

X\sim\gue(n)
iff
X
is a complex Hermitian
n\times n
random matrix with independent entries

x_{ii}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1),\quad 1\leq i\leq n;\qquad x_{ij}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1),\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n.

(iii)

X\sim\gse(n)
iff
X
is a quaternionic self-dual
n\times n
random matrix with independent entries

x_{ii}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1),\quad 1\leq i\leq n;\qquad x_{ij}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{H}}(0,2),\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n.

(iv)

Y\sim\gio(m,n)
iff
Y
is a real
m\times n
random matrix with independent entries

y_{ij}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1),\quad 1\leq i\leq m,\;1\leq j\leq n.

(v)

Y\sim\giu(m,n)
iff
Y
is a complex
m\times n
random matrix with independent entries

y_{ij}\sim\nor_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1),\quad 1\leq i\leq m,\;1\leq j\leq n.

(vi)

Y\sim\gis(m,n)
iff
