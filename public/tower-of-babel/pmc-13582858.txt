Structural insights into ubiquitin recognition by USP15 revealed through a covalent activity-based probe
Authors: Bing Liu, Ting Wang, Hui Luo, Lipeng Lang, Xinyi Liu, Zijun Gong, Tao Wang, Wei Cheng, Luocheng Xie, Xingwei Zhang, Xianjie Fang, Xiaoling Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUbiquitin-specific protease 15 (USP15) catalyzes deubiquitination of multiple substrates involved in immune responses and cancer through diverse biological processes. However, its ubiquitin recognition and conformational transition mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we report the 2.3 Å crystal structure of the USP15 catalytic domain covalently bound to ubiquitin-propargylamine. This structure reveals that ubiquitin binding induces a conformational switch from an inactive-closed to an active-open state, driven by realignment of the catalytic triad and a hinge-like outward swing of the fingers subdomain. Importantly, we characterize two conserved structural features—an α8 helix and an α3-α4 loop—that are essential for maintaining the functional architecture of USP15 for ubiquitin engagement. Deletion of α8 destabilizes USP15 and abrogates its catalytic activity, while cancer-associated mutation P331S destabilizes the α3-α4 loop, weakens ubiquitin binding, and reduces catalytic efficiency. These previously less-characterized structural elements are critical for the enzymatic activity and structural integrity of USP15 and its paralogues, providing potential targets for selective inhibition of deubiquitinases.Subject terms: X-ray crystallography, Proteases, Proteases

Introduction

The dynamic equilibrium between ubiquitination and deubiquitination, governed by ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs), is fundamental to cellular homeostasis. USP15, a member of the USPs, has emerged as a central regulator of immune responses and cancer through diverse biological processes1, including TGF-β2 and NF-κB3 signaling, mRNA splicing4, and mitophagy5. USP15 suppresses T cell activation by stabilizing MDM2-mediated degradation of NFATc2, whereas its inhibition enhances antitumor T cell responses and promotes apoptosis6. It also amplifies innate immune responses by deubiquitinating cGAS, promoting dimerization and phase separation to enhance DNA sensing7. During viral infection, USP15 stabilizes HPV16 E68 and cooperates with TRIM25 to attenuate RIG-I signaling, thereby suppressing antiviral cytokine production9.

In cancer, USP15 contributes to cell proliferation, migration, and invasion1. It has been reported to exert complex context-dependent roles across different cancer types. USP15 overexpression promotes tumor progression in glioblastoma10, multiple myeloma11, chronic myeloid leukemia12, and cancers of the breast13, stomach14, and ovary15,16. On the other hand, it can stabilize tumor-suppressive E3 ligases17 or inhibit NF-κB signaling by deubiquitinating IκBα18. Multiple somatic variants of USP15 are cataloged in the COSMIC database19, but their effects on USP15 structure and catalytic activity remain to be investigated.

Structurally, USPs share a conserved catalytic domain comprising three subdomains—Palm, Thumb, and Fingers—that form a thiol protease active site. This core contains a catalytic triad of cysteine, histidine, and either aspartate or asparagine. During catalysis, the Asp/Asn residue polarizes histidine, enabling the cysteine to attack the isopeptide bond or resolve the resulting tetrahedral intermediate20. USP15 is evolutionarily conserved from Xenopus to humans and shares high sequence identity with close paralogues USP4 (56.9%) and USP11 (41.4%)21,22. All three proteins feature an N-terminal DUSP (domain in USP) followed by a ubiquitin-like (Ubl1) domain and a C-terminal catalytic domain, which consists of D1 and D2 subdomains separated by an insertion21–23. USP15 exists in two predominant isoforms: isoform 1 (981 amino acids; UniProt Q9Y4E8-1) and isoform 2 (952 amino acids; UniProt Q9Y4E8-2), differing by a 29-residue serine-rich insertion in the linker region exclusive to isoform 1.

Although the catalytic cores of USPs are structurally conserved, their enzymatic specificity and regulation are shaped by auxiliary motifs, regulatory domains, and conformational plasticity. For instance, the N-terminal DUSP-Ubl module of USP4 enhances its deubiquitinating activity, whereas the same domain exerts minimal effects in USP11 and USP1523,24. The USP4 catalytic domain has been structurally characterized in its apo form, with the catalytic triad in a catalytically competent configuration (despite the catalytic cysteine binds to β-mercaptoethanol)23, and USP11 has recently been solved in complex with a substrate mimetic22. In contrast, structural data on USP15 remain limited to the apo form and its complex with the inhibitors ubiquitin-variant and mitoxantrone21,25,26. However, the mechanisms by which USP15 recognizes ubiquitin and undergoes conformational transitions remain unclear, limiting our understanding of their essential roles in orchestrating deubiquitination events in immune responses and cancer.

To fill these gaps, we solved the 2.3 Å crystal structure of the USP15 catalytic domain covalently linked to ubiquitin-propargylamine (Ub-PA), an activity-based ubiquitin probe that captures a thioester-mimetic intermediate. Extensive structural and enzymatic analyses define the structural basis for ubiquitin-induced conformational transitions in USP15 and underscore noncanonical structural features (the α8 helix and a conserved α3-α4 loop) for functional integrity, which could be targeted for rational design of the selective inhibitors for USP15 and its paralogues.

Results

Overall structure of the USP15-Ub complex

To investigate the structural basis of ubiquitin recognition by USP15, we engineered the catalytic domain of human USP15 isoform 1 by replacing its catalytic core (D1D2, Glu284-Phe941) insertion with a short ASTSK linker (Supplementary Fig. 1), following the design of the previously reported USP15 core structure (PDB: 6GHA)21 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Hydrolytic activity against a panel of eight diubiquitin (diUb) chains showed robust activity toward M1-, K6-, K11-, K29-, K33-, K48-, and K63-linked diUb, with relatively slower cleavage of K27-linked diUb (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These results indicate that USP15D1D2 functions as a broadly active deubiquitinase with moderate substrate preference.

The USP15D1D2 was then incubated with Ub-PA, an activity-based probe that covalently binds active-site cysteines27,28, to form a stable USP15D1D2–Ub-PA complex (hereafter termed “USP15–Ub” for simplicity) (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Crystals were obtained in the orthorhombic space group C222₁ and diffracted to 2.3 Å resolution. An asymmetric unit contained two independent USP15–Ub complexes (copies A/C and B/D), both of which exhibited well-resolved electron densities across the entire structure, enabling unambiguous model building (Supplementary Figs. 3a and 4a). The two USP15–Ub copies in the asymmetric unit aligned closely with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) value of 0.305 Å over 408 Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The blocking loop 1 (BL1) in the two copies adopted distinct conformations (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c), which are likely attributed to different crystal packing constraints (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Its intrinsic conformational flexibility was further supported by the B-factor analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6b). For simplicity, all subsequent analyses focus on the A/C pair.

The resolved USP15 catalytic domain contains a canonical USP fold, comprising the Palm, Thumb, and Fingers subdomains (Fig. 1b). The fingers subdomain features a central three-stranded β-sheet (β1, β2, and β7) and two short β-strands (β5 and β6) at the tip, which coordinate a Zn²⁺ ion via C448, C451, H809, and C812 (Fig. 1b, d). A distal ubiquitin is bound at the S1 site in a cleavage-competent pose (Fig. 1b). It is engaged by the fingers subdomain, the BL1 and BL2, the switching loop (SL) and the catalytic cleft loop (CCL) from the palm and thumb subdomains. The USP15–Ub contacting interfaces, analyzed using PISA29, revealed a buried surface area of 1878.1 Å² involving 43 ubiquitin residues (Fig. 1b). These direct interactions are further stabilized by numerous water-mediated polar contacts (Supplementary Fig. 6c).

Ubiquitin binding induces inactive-closed to active-open conformational transitions

USP15 in its apo state adopts an inactive conformation with the misaligned catalytic triad and the disordered BL1, BL2, and SL loops21. We aligned the USP15–Ub complex with the apo structure (PDB: 6GHA), resulting in an RMSD of 0.616 Å over 326 Cα atoms. The most notable structural shifts occur in the fingers subdomain, BL1, BL2, SL, and CCL regions (Fig. 2a). The fingers subdomain adopts an “open” conformation to accommodate ubiquitin (Fig. 2a). The zinc ion of the zinc finger swings away by ~8.8 Å relative to the apo state (Fig. 2a, b) and shows reduced B-factors (Supplementary Fig. 6b). The C809A mutation in the zinc finger abolishes catalytic activity (Figs. 1d and 2e), confirming that this region is essential for structural integrity and function.

The BL1, BL2, and SL are highly flexible and unresolved in the apo structure but become well-ordered upon ubiquitin binding (Fig. 2c). Specifically, the BL1 stretches along and clamps the ubiquitin C-terminal peptide (-V70LRLRG-PA76), which is tightly coordinated in a cleft formed by the BL2, SL and CCL (Fig. 2c). Hydrogen bonds are formed between the BL2 (-M887GGG890-) and the ubiquitin R74-PA76 mainchain nitrogen and oxygen atoms. Additionally, salt bridges are established between E383 and R72, as well as between Q379 from the SL and R74. Hydrophobic interactions between the BL1 (Y840 and F838) and L71 further stabilize binding (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Mutation of Y840 to alanine drastically reduces the catalytic activity of USP15, confirming its critical role in ubiquitin engagement (Fig. 2e). Sequence analysis of USP15 from 150 species shows strong conservation in the BL2, CCL, and SL regions contacting the ubiquitin C-terminus (Supplementary Fig. 6e), suggesting that this mode of ubiquitin recognition is evolutionarily conserved among USP15 orthologs.

Although the CCL region is well conserved and structurally stable even in the apo state, it undergoes coordinated rearrangements that stabilize the binding interface and facilitate assembly of the catalytic triad. The catalytic triad consists of C298 from the CCL, H891 at the beginning of β14, and two adjacent aspartates, D908 and D909 at the end of β15 (Fig. 2d). Ub-PA forms a covalent bond with the catalytic cysteine of thiol DUBs, mimicking an activated thioester intermediate formed during catalysis27. Upon Ub-PA binding, a covalent bond is formed between the quaternary carbon of PA76 and the Sγ atom of C298 (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Fig. 3b), which relocates by ~5.5 Å to engage H891 and D908/D909 (Fig. 2c and d). The C298 Sγ atom lies ~3.7 Å from the imidazole ring of H891, which is positioned ~3.0 Å from D908 and ~3.6 Å from D909 (Fig. 1d). The relatively shorter distance between D908 and H891 could contribute to the initial polarization of H891 for C298 activation. By contrast, D909 lies much closer to C298 (~3.5 Å vs. ~5.9 Å for D908), suggesting it has a role in resolving the tetrahedral intermediate. This model supports previous studies showing that D908 is dispensable for catalysis, whereas mutation of D909 abolishes activity after the first catalytic turnover30.

Superposition of the Ub-bound state with mitoxantrone-bound USP15 structures (PDB: 7R2G and 6GH9) shows pronounced differences in the fingers subdomain (the zinc ion position shifts ~6.9 Å and ~8.0 Å, respectively) and the regions that accommodate the ubiquitin C-terminal tail (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c and 10a–c). Relative to the apo state, the zinc ion position shifts by ~1.8 Å in 7R2G and ~1.2 Å in 6GH9 (Supplementary Figs. 8d and 10d). However, as mitoxantrone binds to a site distal to the fingers subdomain, these conformational variations in the fingers are likely attributed to crystal packing constraints rather than the inhibitor-induced effects (Supplementary Figs. 7a–d and 9a–d). These observations indicate that the fingers subdomain adopts a fully open conformation only upon ubiquitin binding (Fig. 2b). Notably, while mitoxantrone occupies the same binding site in both structures, subtle differences in its binding mode correlate with localized rearrangements in the BL2 loop and the catalytic cysteine (C298) (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). Specifically, while C298 in the 7R2G structure maintains a position similar to that in the Ub-bound state (Supplementary Fig. 8c), it exhibits a substantial ~7.3 Å shift in 6GH9, resulting in a more inactive-like triad configuration (Supplementary Fig. 10c).

Overall, the ubiquitin binding induces a transition from an inactive-closed conformation to an active-open conformation of the fingers subdomain (Fig. 2f). This structural transition not only repositions the catalytic triad but also stabilizes the otherwise disordered loops, enabling precise ubiquitin recognition and catalysis.

Comparison of USP15 with paralogues USP4 and USP11

USP15 shares high sequence and structural similarity with its paralogues, USP11 and USP4. While a ubiquitin-bound structure22 is available for USP11 (PDB: 8OYP), only the apo structure has been solved for USP4 (PDB: 2Y6E)23. Superposition of USP15–Ub with apo USP4 reveals marked differences in BL1, BL2, SL, and the zinc finger, whereas the CCL remains virtually unchanged (Fig. 3a). In contrast, alignment with USP11–Ub represents local conformational variations at the BL1, BL2, and SL (Fig. 3b), which recognize ubiquitin through distinct sets of amino acid residues (Fig. 3c–e), while the CCL remains nearly identical (Supplementary Fig. 11a).

In BL1, USP11 directly engages ubiquitin through Y833 and R838, whereas USP15 adopts a distinct conformation and its R845 side chain does not contact ubiquitin (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). The BL2 folds similarly in both paralogues; however, USP11 R885 forms a salt bridge with ubiquitin Q40—a contact that is not preserved in USP15 due to a glycine replacement at position 888 (Fig. 3d). And the ubiquitin C-terminus is coordinated by Q378 and G889 in USP15, while it is stabilized by Q398 and D886 in USP11 (Fig. 3d). In the SL, USP11’s H399 makes a closer contact with ubiquitin R74 than USP15’s Q379 (Fig. 3e). Mutating G888 and Q379 in USP15 to their USP11 counterparts (G888R, Q379H) had no significant effect on catalytic efficiency in Ub-AMC assays (Supplementary Fig. 11b), suggesting these residues modulate substrate interaction without being essential for catalysis.

Comparison with USP7 reveals the essential role of α8 helix in maintaining stability

To highlight the unique structural features of USP15, we superimposed the USP15–Ub structure with USP7 (PDB: 1NBF)31, which serves as a representative of the classic USPs that lack several auxiliary elements. Notably, USP15 features two noncanonical structural elements that are absent in USP7: an α8 helix and an α3-α4 loop, both of which are essential for the functional integrity of USP15 (Fig. 4a). In the USP15–Ub structure, R429 within the α8 forms a 3.1 Å hydrogen bonding interaction with ubiquitin N60. While N430 forms a hydrogen bond with H393 (2.9 Å), helping the α8 helix orients into a catalytically competent conformation for engaging the ubiquitin K48 in a ~4.2 Å distance (Fig. 4b). Upon ubiquitin binding, R429 undergoes a notable conformational shift, whereas N430 and H393 remain the same conformations (Fig. 4c). Consistent with these structural insights, R429A and N430A mutants exhibit a marked loss of catalytic activity (Fig. 4d).

To further explore the function of α8 helix, we introduced an E421P point mutation to disrupt its stability. The E421P mutant retained similar catalytic activity to the wild-type (WT) (Fig. 4d), indicating a single proline substitution is insufficient to destabilize the α8 helix. Deletion of the entire α8 helix (Δ413–430) completely abolished the catalytic activity in both the Ub-AMC hydrolysis and Ub-PA activity probe labeling assays (Fig. 4d and e), demonstrating its essential role for the functional integrity. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy confirmed a global alteration of the secondary structures in the Δ413–430 mutant (Fig. 4f). Urea-induced denaturation assays revealed that the Δ413–430 mutant exhibited a significantly reduced half-transition concentration (C1/2 = 3.71 ± 0.08 M) compared to that of the WT USP15 (C1/2 = 5.11 ± 0.06 M) (mean ± SD, n = 3, p < 0.0001 by unpaired two-tailed t-test; Fig. 4g). These results indicate that the α8 helix is indispensable for the structural integrity and stable folding of the USP15 catalytic domain. Therefore, the observed loss of enzymatic activity is likely a consequence of protein destabilization, highlighting the α8 helix as a critical structural scaffold required for maintaining a functional enzyme.

We identified nine USP family members that contain structurally analogous α8 helices (Fig. 4h), all phylogenetically related to USP1520. Structural analysis suggests that the α8 helix functions as a critical structural scaffold element. It is anchored to the fingers subdomain through a network of conserved internal interactions across USP15, USP4, and USP8, where D415/428/903 engage with K443/456/931 and F458/471/946, and W423/436/911 interacts with L441/454/Q929 (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Additionally, N430 on the α8 helix is positioned to interact with H393, likely contributing to the stability of the overall fold, while R429 provides auxiliary contacts with ubiquitin (Fig. 4b, c). These key residues are strongly conserved across the α8-containing USPs (Fig. 4h). Deletion of α8 likely disrupts these contacts, leading to significantly reduced protein stability (Fig. 4g) and impairing the catalytic triad’s ability to perform nucleophilic attack (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 13). Structural comparison further revealed remarkably consistent α8 conformations in these USPs (USP15, USP4, USP11, USP8, USP21, and USP2) in available apo and/or ubiquitin-bound states (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). These analyses suggest that the α8 helix plays an equally important role in these USPs.

The α3-α4 loop is essential for USP15 activity in vitro and in cells

Between the α3 and α4 helices of USP15 thumb subdomain, an insertion (refer to the α3-α4 loop) contains a P331 residue that is mutated in various cancers, including malignant melanoma and basal cell carcinoma (COSMIC database)19 (Supplementary Table 1). Sequence alignment shows that the α3-α4 loop is present in USP family members that are closely related to USP1520 (USP4, USP11, USP32, USP6, USP19, and USP8) (Fig. 5b). Structural overlays confirm that USP15, USP11, USP4, and USP8 share the α3-α4 loop architecture (Fig. 5c). In contrast, distantly related USPs, such as USP21 and USP2, confer truncated or disordered conformations at the α3-α4 loop position (Fig. 5d).

In the structure, the highly conserved P331 residue is located between the N330 and L332 in the α3-α4 loop (Fig. 5a, b). The N330 forms a hydrogen bond with E394, which mediates the ubiquitin K48 engagement, while the sidechain of L332 is positioned in a ~3.6 Å distance from ubiquitin R54. The USP11-Ub adopts similar α3-α4 loop and ubiquitin interactions to those in the USP15-Ub (Fig. 5c), supporting the role of the α3-α4 loops in ubiquitin recognition. While in USP7, the E394 counterpart N309 has no direct contact with the ubiquitin K48, due to the lack of the α3-α4 loop (Fig. 5a). To test the functional relevance of the α3-α4 loop, we generated USP15 mutants P331S and L332A. The L332A mutant exhibited a sharp loss of catalytic activity. Despite lacking direct contacts with ubiquitin, the cancer-associated P331S mutant showed significantly reduced catalytic activity (Fig. 5e), indicating its important role in maintaining USP15 enzymatic activity.

To investigate the structural basis of the reduced activity of the loop mutants, we performed CD spectroscopy and urea-induced denaturation assays. The CD spectra of P331S and L332A were nearly identical to that of WT, indicating the secondary structures of these mutants are not affected (Fig. 5f). Urea-denaturation assays revealed comparable half-transition concentrations (C1/2) for the L332A mutant (5.09 ± 0.04 M) and WT USP15 (5.11 ± 0.06 M) (mean ± SD, n = 3, p = 0.63). In contrast, the P331S mutant showed a significantly lower C1/2 of 4.64 ± 0.08 M (p = 0.00118 vs. WT; Fig. 5g), indicating it is less stable than the WT. Notably, the P331S mutant confers a significantly higher stability than the α8 helix deletion mutant (Δ413–430), which showed a C1/2 of 3.71 ± 0.08 M (p < 0.0001 vs. WT). Given the role of the α8 helix in maintaining the structural stability or integrity of the USP15 catalytic domain, these analyses suggest that the activity loss of the loop mutants is not merely attributed to structural destabilization (Fig. 5g).

We next examined the effects of P331S mutation in a cellular context. Consistent with previous reports of USP15 as a deubiquitinase for cGAS7, co-expression of full-length USP15 with cGAS in HEK293T cells markedly reduced cGAS ubiquitination. And the catalytic-dead mutant C298S failed to reduce cGAS ubiquitination. Importantly, the P331S mutant was similarly defective in deubiquitination, resulting in elevated cGAS ubiquitination levels comparable to the C298S mutant (Fig. 5h). These results demonstrate that the α3-α4 loop, and specifically the P331 residue, is essential for USP15 enzymatic function both in vitro and in cells.

Cancer-related P331S mutation destabilizes the α3-α4 loop and impairs ubiquitin binding

To uncover the mechanistic basis by which the P331S mutation impairs USP15 activity, we performed two molecular dynamics (MD) simulations in triplicate using the monomeric USP15–Ub complex: one for the WT and one for the P331S mutant (Fig. 6a). These simulations allowed us to analyze mutation-induced conformational changes and altered residue interactions over time. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analysis indicated that both systems reached a stable equilibrium during 100 ns simulations (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b).

In the P331S simulation, the serine substitution introduces a new hydrogen bond—direct or water-mediated—between S331 and R335 in approximately 52.6% of frames (Fig. 6b). This interaction does not occur in the WT complex, where the α3-α4 loop remains flexible and facilitates ubiquitin engagement through an interaction network involving L332–R54 and N330–E394–K48 (Fig. 5a). The aberrant S331–R335 hydrogen bond destabilizes the α3-α4 loop, thereby disrupting both L332–R54 and E394–K48 interactions necessary for ubiquitin binding. To validate this mechanism, we introduced an R335A substitution into the P331S background. Strikingly, the double mutant (P331S/R335A) restored the enzymatic activity to WT levels (Fig. 6c). These analyses directly implicate the S331–R335 interaction as the cause of the functional defects in the P331S mutant.

We further quantified the binding affinity of WT and P331S using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 14). WT USP15 binds ubiquitin with an average dissociation constant (KD) of 1.32 ± 0.28 µM (mean ± SD, n = 3) and a stoichiometry (N) of 0.90 ± 0.17, while the P331S mutant exhibits an average KD of 2.49 ± 0.64 µM (mean ± SD, n = 3, p = 0.044 by two-tailed unpaired t-test) and an N of 0.77 ± 0.06. These analyses indicate that P331S mutant exhibits weaker binding affinity compared to the WT. To assess the functional consequences of the impaired ubiquitin binding, we performed Michaelis–Menten kinetic analysis using Ub-AMC as a substrate (Fig. 6e and Table 2). Consistent with the ITC analyses, the P331S mutant exhibited both a reduced Vmax and increased Km, reflecting slower turnover rates and weaker substrate affinity relative to the WT. Compared to WT, the P331S kcat decreased ~1.94-fold, and the catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) dropped ~3.78-fold. These results confirm that the P331S mutation compromises both substrate engagement and catalytic turnover.

Discussion

In this study, we report the first structure of USP15’s catalytic domain in complex with an activity-based ubiquitin probe. Unlike previously solved apo or inhibitor-bound USP15 structures21,25,26, this model captures the enzyme in its active, ubiquitin-bound conformation, in which the catalytic triad realigns and the fingers subdomain swings outward upon substrate engagement. Critically, we elucidate the functional importance of two structural features—the α8 helix and the α3-α4 loop—that are essential for maintaining USP15’s functional integrity. The cancer-associated P331S mutation induces an S331–R335 interaction that destabilizes the α3-α4 loop and disrupts the ubiquitin-binding network, weakening the ubiquitin affinity and reducing catalytic activity.

Among the 56 USPs encoded in the human genome32, structural data are only available for about one-third of their catalytic domains. While the ubiquitin-bound conformations of USPs are generally conserved, their apo states display marked structural diversity. These differences are especially pronounced in the fingers subdomain, which undergoes distinct conformational transitions upon ubiquitin binding. Based on the magnitude and the direction of these rearrangements, USPs can be grouped into three mechanistic classes. In Type I (e.g., USP7 and USP14), the fingers subdomain exhibits minimal movement upon ubiquitin binding31,33. Type II USPs, such as USP34 and USP47, adopt an open apo conformation, which closes around upon ubiquitin binding34,35. In Type III USPs, including USP15, the fingers subdomain adopts a closed conformation in the apo state that sterically clashes with ubiquitin and must swing outward for substrate accommodation (Fig. 2b, e; Supplementary Figs. 8b and 10b). Other USPs with inward-facing fingers in the apo state, such as USP4 and USP823,36, may also fall into this third category.

This pronounced conformational shift in Type III USPs likely operates via a hinge-like mechanism, wherein the fingers subdomain moves as a rigid body pivoting at its base36. In USP15, USP4, and USP8, the α8 helix appears to interfere with this hinge region through conserved residues D415/428/903 and W423/436/911 (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 12b). First described in the apo USP8 structure36, the α8 helix has not been functionally characterized until now. Our data demonstrate that the α8 helix serves as a critical scaffold essential for the structural stability of USP15. Notably, although nine human USPs contain a defined α8 helix, USP15 is the only one for which both the apo and ubiquitin-bound structures are available. Given the structural conservation, it is likely that USP4 and USP8 undergo α8-associated transitions in their fingers subdomains similar to those observed in USP15 upon ubiquitin binding (Supplementary Fig. 12b). For USP237, USP1122, and USP2138,39, only the ubiquitin-bound structures have been determined, leaving it unclear whether their fingers subdomains undergo similar α8-associated transitions.

While USP mutations have been frequently identified in cancer, the functional consequences of most remain poorly understood. Some, however, have been shown to directly impact enzymatic activity: for example, P535L in USP25 (associated with astrocytoma and adenocarcinoma in the COSMIC database) enhances activity by disrupting tetramer formation40, D199A in USP1 abolishes activity in endometrial cancer41, and USP7 mutations linked to Hao-Fountain syndrome reduce catalytic output42. Our work demonstrates that the cancer-associated P331S mutation in USP15 destabilizes the α3-α4 loop via the S331–R335 hydrogen bond, which disrupts key contacts with ubiquitin and significantly impairs the catalytic activity (Fig. 7). Interestingly, in USP15-related paralogues—including USP4, USP11, USP32, USP6, and USP8—the residue equivalent to R335 is a lysine, which could similarly form hydrogen bonds with a serine substituted at the P331-equivalent position. This suggests that analogous P-to-S mutations could broadly impair the catalytic function across this subgroup. The USP15 P331S variant appears in four independent cancer genome studies in the COSMIC database, and equivalent mutations have been identified in USP11 (once) and USP32 (six times)19 (Supplementary Table 1). These findings raise the possibility that α3-α4 loop destabilization may be a recurrent mechanism of loss-of-function in this USP subfamily, although further experimental validation is required.

Structural impact of the P331S mutation on the α3-α4 loop and USP15 activity.

A schematic comparison between USP15 WT (left) and the P331S mutant (right), showing that the native α3-α4 loop (blue) maintains a stable conformation through balanced residue interactions. In the P331S mutant, aberrant hydrogen bonding and accumulation of water molecules (red dots) lead to structural destabilization, weakening ubiquitin binding and reducing catalytic activity.

Taken together, our findings reveal that USP15 relies on previously uncharacterized structural features outside the canonical palm–thumb–fingers core for its function. Like the β-box in USP3434 or the IL-loop in USP2540,43,44, these auxiliary motifs confer regulatory control and open new avenues for therapeutic intervention. The cancer-associated P331S mutation underscores functional importance of the α3-α4 loop, where conformational destabilization compromises catalytic performance and may contribute to oncogenesis. Targeting these regulatory motifs—such as by stabilizing an inactive α3-α4 loop conformation or disrupting α8–fingers interactions—could yield selective inhibitors for USP15 and its paralogues. Since these motifs are absent in most USPs, such strategies may provide specificity for a subset of related enzymes. Future studies should explore the roles of the α8 helix and the α3-α4 loop in the context of full-length USP15 in cells, and high-throughput screening efforts will be necessary to evaluate their potential as druggable regulatory sites.

Methods

Plasmids and mutagenesis

The USP15 catalytic domain (USP15D1D2, residues 284–941, with residues 468–786 replaced by an ASTSK linker) was PCR-amplified from the pFastBacHT_USP15FL vector and subcloned into the pET28-Smt3 vector (kindly provided by Prof. David Reverter) to generate an N-terminal His₆-Smt3-tagged construct, which contains a SENP-cleavable site after Smt3. The pTXB1-Ub-intein-CBD expression vector, encoding ubiquitin (residues 1–75) fused to a C-terminal intein and a chitin-binding domain (CBD), was kindly provided by Prof. Sonja Lorenz. For mammalian expression, full-length USP15, cGAS, and ubiquitin were cloned into pcDNA3.1 to produce pcDNA3.1-USP15-Myc-N, pcDNA3.1-cGAS-3×Flag-C, and pcDNA3.1-Ub-HA, respectively. Site-directed point mutations were introduced by PCR-based mutagenesis. Except for the pET28-Smt3 and pTXB1-Ub-intein-CBD vectors, all other gene fragments and vectors were provided by Abiotech Co., Ltd (Jinan, China). All oligonucleotide primers were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) and their sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Protein expression and purification

The USP15D1D2 construct in pET28-Smt3 was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (Novagen, USA). Cultures were grown at 37 °C to an OD600 of 0.6, induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and incubated overnight at 28 °C. Cells were harvested and lysed by sonication in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 10 mM imidazole). The clarified lysates were loaded onto a Ni²⁺-NTA column (Cytiva, USA) and were washed with the same buffer. The USP15D1D2 samples were eluted with 250 mM imidazole. The His₆-Smt3 tag was cleaved using SENP2 protease, and the USP15D1D2 was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 16/600 GL column (Cytiva, USA), which was pre-equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol. The eluted USP15D1D2 fractions were concentrated using Amicon Ultra-10K centrifugal filters (Millipore, USA).

USP15–Ub complex preparation

The Ub(1–75)-PA probe was prepared using an intein-mediated chemical ligation strategy45. Briefly, the pTXB1-Ub-intein-CBD construct was overexpressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM IPTG and maintained at 20 °C for 16 h. Following cell harvest and lysis, the clarified supernatant was incubated with chitin resin (New England Biolabs, USA) in Buffer A (50 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) at 4 °C for 5 h. After thorough washing with Buffer A, the ubiquitin-MESNA thioester was generated by incubating the resin with 150 mM sodium 2-mercaptoethanesulfonate (MESNA; Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C to induce intein cleavage. The released protein was dialyzed overnight against Buffer A at 4 °C to remove excess MESNA. To generate the final functionalized probe, the ubiquitin-MESNA thioester was reacted with 150 mM propargylamine (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 5 h. The resulting Ub-PA probe was further purified by dialysis against Buffer A followed by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 75 16/600 GL column. Then the probe was incubated with USP15 at a 1:3 molar ratio at 30 °C for 1 h, ensuring formation of the covalent linkage via the catalytic cysteine. The reaction mixture was then purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 16/600 GL column, which was pre-equilibrated in 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol.

Crystallization, data collection, and structure determination

The USP15–Ub complex was concentrated to 15 g L⁻¹ in buffer containing 75 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol. Crystals were grown at 18 °C using hanging-drop vapor diffusion method by mixing 0.5 µL protein with 0.5 µL reservoir solution containing 50% 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol (MPD) and 0.1 M imidazole. Crystals suitable for diffraction were obtained directly from screening and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen without cryoprotection. Diffraction data were collected at beamline BL10U2 of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF)46 and processed using the Aquarium pipeline47. The structure was solved by molecular replacement with PHENIX48, using USP15D1D2 (PDB: 6GHA21) and ubiquitin (PDB: 1NBF31) as search models. Model building and refinement were performed using Coot49 and PHENIX48. Structural figures were generated using PyMOL50. The data collection and refinement statistics are presented in Table 1.

Ubiquitin-AMC hydrolysis and kinetic assays

Ub-AMC hydrolysis was performed in black 96-well plates using a 200 µL reaction volume in buffer containing 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.05% Tween-20. Reactions were carried out at 25 °C using 25 nM USP15D1D2 and 100 nM Ub-AMC, and fluorescence was measured at 355 nm excitation and 455 nm emission using a Synergy Neo2 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). For kinetic analysis, 2 nM USP15D1D2 was incubated with serial dilutions of Ub-AMC. Initial reaction rates were calculated by linear regression and fitted to the Michaelis–Menten equation using GraphPad Prism 9.0. The kinetic analyses were conducted in three biologically independent experiments. The purity and concentration of all mutant proteins were verified by SDS-PAGE (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Diubiquitin hydrolysis assays

Reactions were conducted in buffer containing 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 1 mM DTT, and 0.05% Tween-20 at 30 °C. Reactions were quenched at 0, 15, 30, and 60 min by adding SDS loading buffer, followed by SDS-PAGE, and staining with SYPRO Ruby (Bio-Rad). Diubiquitin substrates were purchased from Hefei KS-V Peptide Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Hefei, China).

Active site probe assays

USP15D1D2 wild-type (WT) and mutant proteins (2 µM) were incubated with Ub-PA (20 µM) in buffer containing 250 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, and 1 mM DTT at 30 °C for 0, 1, and 15 min. Reactions were terminated with 2× SDS loading buffer, resolved by SDS-PAGE, and visualized by Coomassie staining.

Cell culture analysis and transfection

HEK293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were transfected with pcDNA3.1-USP15-Myc-N (WT or mutant), pcDNA3.1-cGAS-3×Flag-C, and pcDNA3.1-Ub-HA using Polyethylenimine (Cat. 23966, Polysciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 h, cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) with protease inhibitors.

Immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting

HEK293T cell lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. A 60 µL aliquot was reserved as the whole-cell lysate; the remainder was incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Flag M2 magnetic beads (Beyotime, P2115). Beads were washed three times with RIPA buffer, the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer. Samples were then resolved on 8% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in TBST with 5% (w/v) BSA, probed with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at room temperature. The following primary antibodies were used for immunoblotting (IB) at the indicated dilutions: anti-MYC (Cell Signaling Technology, #2276, 1:1000), anti-HA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-7392, 1:1000), and anti-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, F3165, 1:1000). HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Beyotime, A0216, 1:5000) was employed as the secondary antibody for IB. Chemiluminescence signals were detected using an iBright 1500 imager (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

ITC experiments were performed on a PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern, Panalytical, UK) at 25 °C. Ubiquitin (400 µM) was titrated into 40 µM USP15 in 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl. Injections were performed at 180-s intervals with stirring at 750 rpm. Data were analyzed using PEAQ-ITC analysis software and fitted to a single-site binding model.

Urea-induced unfolding assays

Protein unfolding was assessed as previously described51. USP15 samples (0.15 g L⁻¹) were incubated overnight at 25 °C with urea (0–9 M) in PBS in 96-well plates. Tryptophan fluorescence was measured with excitation at 280 nm and emission from 300–400 nm. The spectral center of mass (ν) was calculated according to Eq. (1):

where unfolding curves were plotted as normalized ν values versus urea concentration. Data were acquired using a Synergy Neo2 reader (BioTek Instruments, USA) and analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

CD spectra were recorded using a JASCO J-810 spectropolarimeter (JASCO Corporation, Japan) at 20 °C. Protein samples were prepared at 0.4 g L⁻¹ in a 50 mM phosphate buffer (1:1 potassium to sodium, pH 7.5) and were measured in a 0.01 cm pathlength quartz cuvette. Spectral scans were collected from 190–280 nm with a step size of 1 nm, scanning speed of 20 nm min⁻¹, and a bandwidth of 1 nm. Fifteen scans were acquired and averaged. Buffer spectra were subtracted, and the results are expressed as molar ellipticity (Θ).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the pmemd.CUDA module of AMBER53 under periodic boundary conditions. Atomic charges were assigned at pH 7.0 using the AMBER force field via PDB2PQR software52. Simulations employed the Amber19SB force field for proteins54. Parameters for the zinc-coordinating cysteines at the catalytic site were generated with the MCPB.py module55 using the B3LYP-D3 level of theory, with the SDD basis set for Zn and 6–31 G* for all other atoms56–58.

The protein–ligand complex was solvated in an explicit OPC water box (16 Å buffer)59 and subjected to a multistep protocol consisting of energy minimization, heating, equilibration, and production. Initially, solvent molecules were energy-minimized while all non-water atoms were restrained with a harmonic force constant of 10 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻². This was followed by a 100 ps MD relaxation stage allowing water molecules to equilibrate while maintaining positional restraints on the solute. The complex was then minimized with harmonic restraints applied to the protein backbone atoms (C, CA, N, and O), followed by whole-system minimization with a reduced restraint force constant. Single-residue mutations were introduced using the Rotamers tool in UCSF Chimera with side-chain rotamers selected from the Dunbrack library based on minimal steric clashes and highest probability.

The system was gradually heated from 0 to 300 K over 400 ps under constant-volume (NVT) conditions using a Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 2 ps⁻¹, while maintaining backbone restraints of 5 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻². Subsequently, 400 ps of density equilibration was carried out under constant-pressure (NPT) conditions at 300 K and 1 atm. An extended equilibration stage of 5 ns was then performed under NPT conditions with all positional restraints removed.

Production MD simulations were carried out under NPT conditions at 300 K using a 2 fs time step. Bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, and a non-bonded cutoff of 8.0 Å was applied. Each system was simulated using three independent production trajectories, each with a duration of 100 ns. RMSD and RMSF analyses were performed using the entire 100 ns trajectories with CPPTRAJ. Hydrogen-bond analyses were conducted using the last 50 ns of each trajectory, from which 100 evenly spaced frames were extracted for analysis. Visualizations were generated using PyMOL50. Topologies and coordinate files used for the MD simulations are provided in Supplementary Data1.

Statistics and reproducibility

All biochemical and biophysical measurements, including urea-induced unfolding assays, enzyme kinetics, and ITC, were performed as n = 3 biologically independent experiments. MD simulations were carried out in triplicate as three independent runs. Statistical significance for the urea denaturation and ITC data was determined using a two-tailed unpaired t-test. Unless otherwise stated, data are presented as mean ± SD. All statistical analyses and curve fitting were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.
