Occupational trauma and probable PTSD among sworn and civilian police employees
Authors: Bjørn Lau, Brita Bjørkelo, Knut Inge Fostervold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Police personnel are frequently exposed to potentially traumatic events (PTEs), increasing their risk for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). While much research has focused on sworn police officers (SPOs), less is known about civilian employees (Civilians) who work alongside them. This study examined trauma exposure and probable PTSD in both groups within the Norwegian Police Service (NPS).Methods: A random sample of 4,000 employees received an invitation to a web-based survey in 2021, of which 560 responded (61.8% SPOs, 38.2% Civilians). Trauma exposure was assessed with the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5), and probable PTSD was measured with the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5). Logistic regression analyses examined group differences in trauma exposure and PTSD prevalence, adjusting for sociodemographic covariates.Results: SPOs reported significantly greater overall lifetime trauma exposure than Civilians (87.3% vs. 69.2%) and substantially higher occupational trauma exposure (72.0% vs. 21.5%). Probable PTSD was 5.6% among civilians and 2.6% among sworn officers; the crude group difference was not statistically significant (OR = 0.45, 95% CI [0.19, 1.09]). However, after adjusting for different trauma exposure indicators, the association reversed and became statistically significant (adjusted ORs = 0.12–0.16), consistent with a suppressor effect whereby the higher exposure among sworn officers masked their relatively lower risk of PTSD.Discussion: This is the first study to directly compare trauma exposure and probable PTSD among SPOs and Civilians in the NPS. While SPOs reported markedly higher trauma exposure, Civilians also faced substantial risks despite lower levels of direct occupational trauma. PTSD prevalence in both groups was lower than commonly reported in international police samples and broadly comparable to estimates from the Norwegian general population. Nevertheless, the high levels of trauma exposure underline that posttraumatic symptoms remain an important occupational health concern.

Introduction

Law enforcement personnel are regularly exposed to potentially traumatic events, including violence, death, and profound human suffering, placing them at elevated risk for developing posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Among police officers, PTSD has been associated with cognitive impairments in executive functioning and processing speed (Bisson Desrochers et al., 2021), as well as poorer health outcomes, increased sick leave, and impaired occupational functioning (Violanti et al., 2017). A recent global meta-analysis estimated the pooled point prevalence of PTSD among police officers at approximately 14.2% (Syed et al., 2020), nearly four times higher than the roughly 3.6% twelve-month PTSD prevalence observed in the general population of high-income countries (Koenen et al., 2017). At the same time, reported PTSD rates among police range widely across studies, from below 5% to over 40% in different samples (Wagner et al., 2020). This wide variation largely reflects differences in methodology, assessment tools, and study contexts. In Western countries, most point-prevalence estimates for police fall between approximately 7.8% and 16.5% (Wagner et al., 2020), underscoring that while PTSD represents a significant concern in policing, its documented prevalence is far from uniform.

A substantial proportion of police personnel are exposed to events that meet the DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria for traumatic exposure; that is, events involving actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence, experienced directly, witnessed, learned about in relation to a close other, or encountered repeatedly through occupational duties (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; World Health Organization, 2018). Events of this nature, particularly those involving repeated exposure to violence, death, and severe injury, have been associated with higher levels of PTSD symptoms among police officers (Hansen et al., 2025; Hartley et al., 2013; Wagner et al., 2020). However, only a subset of those exposed to such events develop PTSD. PTSD is diagnosed when persistent symptoms of intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in mood and cognition, and hyperarousal arise following a traumatic event. These reactions reflect a prolonged and disruptive stress response that exceeds the expected trajectory of post-trauma adjustment.

Research suggests that the nature and perceived severity of a critical incident, together with the officer’s subjective appraisal of the event, may be more predictive of PTSD risk than exposure alone (Wagner et al., 2020). Prospective police studies further indicate that early acute stress reactions and peritraumatic responses, including tonic immobility, are associated with later PTSD symptoms (Inslicht et al., 2011; Maia et al., 2015; Marchand et al., 2015). At the same time, outcomes vary even among officers exposed to similar incident types, and recent evidence suggests that work-based social support may function as a protective resource in relation to PTSD symptoms (Campos et al., 2023).

In addition to incident severity, the cumulative burden of trauma exposure in police work has been examined, with evidence generally pointing to a dose–response pattern. Both cross-sectional and prospective studies suggest that greater frequency and variety of critical-incident exposure are associated with higher PTSD symptom levels and increased risk of later posttraumatic stress, although the strength of these associations varies across contexts and study designs (Hansen et al., 2025; Hartley et al., 2013; Wagner et al., 2020). By contrast, in a South African sample, exposure frequency did not predict PTSD, whereas the experience of personal threat during the incident did (Peltzer, 2001). Notably, in a longitudinal cohort of junior police officers, greater early-career exposure to ‘sad’ incidents (e.g. discovering a corpse or severely mutilated victims) was associated with lower subsequent posttraumatic stress symptoms, interpreted as a possible inoculation effect (Hodgins et al., 2001).

In parallel with the individual-level risk factors outlined above, structural and organizational changes within police services have introduced new dimensions to the understanding of occupational trauma. Since the late twentieth century, many agencies have undergone substantial civilianization, reallocating a variety of tasks traditionally held by sworn officers to civilian staff (Forst, 2000; Mousseau et al., 2025). Civilians now routinely occupy roles in forensic analysis, communications and dispatch, intelligence, crime-scene processing, records, and victim services, where work can entail direct or vicarious exposure to potentially traumatic material (Huey et al., 2023; Lentz et al., 2020). In Norway, recent workforce figures underscore this shift: at the end of 2024, 35% of positions were civilian (Politidirektoratet, 2025). Despite their growing presence in these high-demand environments, the mental health of civilian police employees has historically received less empirical attention than that of sworn officers (Lentz et al., 2020).

Emerging evidence suggests that civilian personnel in police organizations may experience mental health burdens equal to or even greater than those of sworn officers. In a large Canadian survey, Lentz et al. (2020) found that 32.8% of civilian employees screened positive for at least one mental disorder versus 22.7% of sworn officers; civilians also showed higher mean PTSD, anxiety, and depression scores, with significantly greater odds of screening positive for major depression. Similarly, following a large-scale critical incident, civilian support staff demonstrated higher PTSD symptom severity and general distress than police officers (Kerswell et al., 2020). Other recent work reports broadly comparable levels of work-related stress and PTSD symptoms across sworn and non-sworn personnel (Nam et al., 2025), and a recent scoping review likewise concluded that civilian staff often experience comparable or higher stress levels than officers, while remaining insufficiently integrated and supported within police organizations (Mousseau et al., 2025). At the same time, civilians may be at greater risk of longer-term difficulties due to lower self-reported resilience and a lower willingness to access organization-based supports, despite reporting broadly similar barriers to care as sworn officers (Martin et al., 2021).

Qualitative findings by Huey et al. (2023) further illuminate the often-overlooked psychological toll experienced by civilian staff in police organizations. Civilian employees frequently describe exposure to distressing material, such as transcribing statements, reviewing graphic images, or reading detailed accounts of violence, constituting vicarious trauma. They also report substantial emotional labour in interactions with victims and members of the public, alongside a perceived lower status and under-recognition within police organizational culture. Participants often noted limited training for handling potentially psychologically traumatic event-related tasks and instances of being excluded from critical-incident debriefings, emphasizing a need for access to supports beyond standard employee assistance programmes. Collectively, these insights underscore that civilian personnel constitute a vital yet understudied group in occupational trauma research, meriting greater empirical attention and targeted interventions (Huey et al., 2023; Mousseau et al., 2025).

Despite increasing recognition of trauma-related risks in policing, research directly comparing trauma exposure and PTSD outcomes between sworn and civilian personnel remains limited, particularly in the Nordic context, where such comparisons have rarely been examined empirically. In Norway, sworn police officers hold full law-enforcement authority, whereas civilian personnel provide specialist and support functions, including investigative or operational tasks alongside sworn staff. While both groups may be exposed to critical incidents, they differ in role expectations and organizational integration, factors that may plausibly shape vulnerability to probable PTSD. This reasoning aligns with research suggesting that the impact of traumatic exposure depends not only on exposure frequency, but also on individual and contextual vulnerability factors, including broader occupational and organizational conditions (e.g. Hansen et al., 2025; Violanti et al., 2017).

Building on this rationale, the present study aimed to: (1) quantify total lifetime and work-related (occupational) trauma exposure in each group; (2) estimate the prevalence of probable PTSD; and (3) examine whether any between-group differences are accounted for by trauma exposure and sociodemographic covariates.

Based on prior literature, we hypothesized that: H1: Sworn police officers (SPOs) would report greater overall exposure to critical incidents than civilian employees. H2: SPOs would report greater occupational (work-related) exposure than civilian employees.

H1: Sworn police officers (SPOs) would report greater overall exposure to critical incidents than civilian employees.

H2: SPOs would report greater occupational (work-related) exposure than civilian employees.

We further explored whether the prevalence of probable PTSD differed between these groups after accounting for trauma exposure and covariates.

Methods

Study design and procedures

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from May to September 2021 among employees of the Norwegian Police Service (NPS). A simple random sample of 4,000 NPS employees (representing a total workforce of 17,109 employees at the time) was invited via email to participate in an online questionnaire. Each invitation included a link to the study website with detailed information and an electronic consent form, and only those who consented could proceed to the survey. Up to three reminder emails were sent to non-responders during the data collection period.

A total of 560 employees completed the survey, corresponding to a response rate of 14%.

No formal a priori power calculation was conducted, as the study was based on a random sample drawn from the Norwegian Police Service workforce. Thus, the final study size was determined by the number invited (n = 4000) and the achieved response during the data collection period.

The study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REC South-East; ref. 2020/140335) and by the Norwegian Police Directorate (ref. 201900307).

The present study builds on the same national dataset previously used to examine organizational and operational stress in the Norwegian Police Service (Rabbing et al., 2025), extending the focus to trauma exposure and posttraumatic outcomes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were employees of the Norwegian Police Service who were included in the random sample and consented to participate in the survey. Of the 4,000 invited employees, 560 completed the questionnaire and constituted the main analytic sample. No additional exclusion criteria were applied to the analyses of demographic characteristics and trauma exposure.

For the PTSD analyses, participants were required to report at least one potentially traumatic event on the LEC-5 and to identify an index event meeting DSM-5 Criterion A. Of the 560 respondents, 450 reported at least one potentially traumatic event, and 243 identified an index event meeting Criterion A and were therefore included in the PTSD analyses.

Measures

All instruments were administered in Norwegian. The LEC-5 and PCL-5 were used in officially translated Norwegian versions, previously applied in population-based studies (e.g. Heir et al., 2019). Minor wording adjustments were made to ensure clarity in a police context.

Demographic and background variables

Participants provided a range of demographic and occupational information, including: gender, age, marital status, and current job role. We categorized each participant’s role as either a police officer or a civilian, based on their self-reported position, and verified that this classification aligned with their reported education (police training vs. other). Those serving in operational law enforcement roles (e.g. patrol, investigations, emergency response) were classified as SPOs, while those in civilian or specialist support roles were classified as civilians. In addition, a chi-square analysis of self-reported primary work tasks (results not shown) confirmed a clear division of labour: police officers were primarily engaged in frontline and operational duties such as patrol, emergency response, investigations, and crime prevention, whereas civilians most often worked in administrative support, forensic services, civil enforcement, and governance-related functions. This occupational role classification was used as the primary grouping variable in analyses.

Trauma exposure (LEC-5)

Lifetime exposure to potentially traumatic events was assessed with the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5; Weathers et al., 2013), a 17-item self-report inventory covering various trauma types (e.g. natural disaster, serious accident, physical assault, sudden violent death). The LEC-5 is widely used and has demonstrated good test–retest reliability and construct validity (Gray et al., 2004; Weathers et al., 2013). As a formative index assessing exposure to diverse trauma categories rather than a single latent construct, internal consistency is not a relevant metric.

For each event category, participants indicated whether the event had happened to them directly, whether they had witnessed it happening to others, whether they had learned it occurred to someone close, or that it did not apply. Any endorsement of direct, witnessed, or learned exposure was counted as experiencing that type of trauma. We then calculated the total number of different trauma categories each participant had experienced (range = 0–17) as an index of cumulative trauma exposure. We also recorded whether each participant had experienced at least one qualifying traumatic event (any exposure vs. none) and tallied the number of categories the participant experienced directly (direct exposure count). Participants additionally reported, for each endorsed LEC-5 event, whether it had occurred in the context of their police work. Based on these responses, we created two variables: (1) the total number of work-related trauma categories experienced and (2) a dichotomous indicator showing whether the participant had experienced any work-related trauma exposure or not.

If participants endorsed more than one trauma on the LEC-5, they were asked to identify the single most distressing or impactful event as their index event for the PTSD assessment (Silverstein et al., 2017). This index event was then used to anchor the subsequent PTSD symptom assessment. The selected index event was required to meet DSM-5 Criterion A for traumatic exposure, defined as direct or threatened exposure to death, serious injury, or sexual violence (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Participants who selected an index event that did not meet Criterion A (for example, the anticipated natural death of a loved one, which is not considered a Criterion A trauma), or who did not complete the relevant LEC-5 items needed to determine Criterion A status, were excluded from the PTSD symptom analyses.

PTSD symptoms (PCL-5)

PTSD symptomatology was measured with the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015), a 20-item self-report questionnaire corresponding to the DSM-5 symptom criteria for PTSD. The PCL-5 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency and convergent validity, in prior validation studies (Ashbaugh et al., 2016; Blevins et al., 2015). In the present Criterion A subsample (n = 243), internal consistency for the total 20-item PCL-5 was excellent (Cronbach’s α = .95). The PCL-5 assesses symptoms across the four PTSD clusters: intrusion (Criterion B), avoidance (Criterion C), negative alterations in cognition and mood (Criterion D), and hyperarousal (Criterion E). Participants rated how much they were bothered by each symptom in the past month, with instructions to anchor all responses to their identified index event.

We scored the PCL-5 by applying the DSM-5 diagnostic algorithm for probable PTSD, following established scoring guidelines (Ashbaugh et al., 2016; Blevins et al., 2015). In this approach, each symptom item was considered ‘present’ if it was rated 2 (‘Moderately’) or higher. A respondent was classified as meeting criteria for probable PTSD if they endorsed at least one intrusion symptom, one avoidance symptom, two negative mood/cognition symptoms, and two hyperarousal symptoms (thus satisfying DSM-5 Criteria B through E for PTSD). Individuals who met all symptom cluster criteria in reference to their index trauma were coded as probable PTSD cases.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were based on complete data, as the online questionnaire required responses to proceed through the survey. We first performed descriptive analyses to assess the sample’s representativeness and to compare basic characteristics between groups. The demographic profile of respondents (N = 560) was compared to that of the full invited sample (N = 4000) and the overall NPS workforce (N = 17,109) on available variables (gender, age, geographic region, organizational affiliation, and occupational role) using aggregated data from the National Police Directorate. Because individual-level data for non-respondents were not available, no statistical tests were conducted for these comparisons. Next, we examined differences between SPOs and civilians on key background variables. Independent-samples t-tests were used for continuous variables (e.g. age, seniority), and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables (e.g. gender, marital status), accompanied by effect size estimates (Cohen’s d for t-tests and φ or Cramér’s V for chi-squares). These bivariate analyses informed the selection of covariates for subsequent regression models.

To test Hypothesis 1 (that SPOs have greater overall trauma exposure than civilians), we compared the two groups on the prevalence of any lifetime trauma exposure and on the mean number of trauma categories reported. We also conducted separate logistic regression analyses for each of the 17 trauma categories in the LEC-5, with occupational role as the predictor and exposure to that specific event type (no/yes) as the outcome.

To test Hypothesis 2 (that SPOs have greater work-related trauma exposure than civilians), we used a similar approach focusing on traumatic events encountered in the line of duty. We compared the groups on the total number of work-related trauma categories experienced, and ran logistic regression analyses for each of the 17 work-related trauma exposure variables (each indicating whether a given trauma type was experienced in a work context), using occupational role as the predictor.

Finally, to examine whether any difference in probable PTSD between sworn police officers and civilians could be explained by variation in trauma exposure or other background characteristics, we conducted a series of logistic regression analyses.

In the first step, we estimated an unadjusted model with occupational role as the sole predictor of probable PTSD status to assess the crude association between role and PTSD.

Next, we ran bivariate (unadjusted) logistic regression models for each potential covariate – gender, age, marital status, seniority (defined as the total number of years of service in the police force rather than tenure in the current position), total number of trauma event types, number of direct personal traumatic events, and number of work-related traumatic events – to examine their individual associations with probable PTSD.

In the final step, we constructed separate adjusted models in which occupational role was entered alongside each covariate individually. This approach allowed us to assess the extent to which the association between occupational role and PTSD was influenced by each covariate in isolation, while avoiding multicollinearity and identifying potential confounding or suppression effects. All results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals and corresponding p-values. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographic and occupational characteristics of the study respondents (N = 560), alongside data from the invited sample (N = 4000) and the total workforce of the NPS (N = 17,109). Women comprised 43.2% of the respondent sample, closely aligning with the proportions observed in the invited sample (45.4%) and the total police workforce (46.2%). The mean age of respondents was 44.9 years, with a slight underrepresentation of individuals under 30 years of age compared to both the invited sample and the total population. Regarding job role, 61.8% of respondents were sworn police officers (SPOs), and 38.2% held civilian positions. This distribution closely mirrors that of both the invited sample and the broader police service, suggesting that the respondent sample was generally representative of the workforce in terms of occupational role. SPOs were also several times more likely to report exposure to toxic substances, fires or explosions, and severe human suffering.

Hypothesis 2: group differences in occupationally related trauma exposure

PTSD prevalence and bivariate predictors

Among the 450 participants who reported at least one potentially traumatic event on the LEC-5, 243 identified an index event that met DSM-5 Criterion A. Of these, 21 respondents met the PCL-5 algorithmic threshold for probable PTSD, corresponding to 8.6% of the Criterion A subsample and 3.8% of the full sample. Prevalence was higher among civilian employees (Civilians; 5.6%) than among sworn police officers (SPOs; 2.6%).

Results of the bivariate logistic regressions are summarized in Table 5. In the unadjusted logistic model, SPOs had lower – but not statistically significant – odds of probable PTSD relative to Civilians (OR = 0.45, 95% CI [0.19, 1.09], p = .076). Regarding trauma-related predictors, both cumulative lifetime exposure burden and the number of personally experienced exposure categories were positively associated with probable PTSD in bivariate models. Each additional lifetime exposure category was associated with 16% higher odds (OR = 1.16, 95% CI [1.05, 1.27], p = .003), and each additional category of personally experienced exposure with 29% higher odds (OR = 1.29, 95% CI [1.13, 1.46], p < .001). By contrast, the number of job-related exposure categories was not significantly associated with probable PTSD (OR = 1.08, 95% CI [0.99, 1.19], p = .099; Table 5). Together with the bivariate results (Table 5), this pattern reflects a classic suppressor effect (Tu et al., 2008). Sworn officers report much higher exposure to traumatic events, and this exposure increases the likelihood of PTSD. At the same time, sworn officers are less likely than civilians to meet criteria for probable PTSD once exposure is considered. Because exposure is positively associated with both being a sworn officer and with PTSD, it attenuates the crude SPO–PTSD association (OR = 0.45), obscuring the true relationship. Once exposure is controlled, the underlying inverse association becomes evident (ORs = 0.12–0.16), corresponding to 84–88% lower odds of probable PTSD among sworn officers compared with civilian employees at equivalent levels of exposure.

Discussion

Main findings

This study is the first to compare trauma exposure and probable PTSD between sworn officers and civilian employees in NPS. Both groups reported extensive exposure to potentially traumatic events. Consistent with the detailed exposure analyses, sworn officers reported higher exposure than civilian employees across nearly all examined categories of occupationally related traumatic events, particularly those involving violence, serious injury, and sudden death. Overall prevalence of probable PTSD was low compared to international estimates, yet analyses revealed a suppressor effect: SPOs showed lower odds of PTSD than civilians once exposure burden was taken into account.

Prevalence of probable PTSD

The prevalence observed in this study (3.8% overall; 8.6% among Criterion A cases) is lower than the global pooled prevalence of 14.2% in police samples (Syed et al., 2020) and the 7.8–16.5% range reported in systematic reviews (Wagner et al., 2020). International estimates vary widely due to differences in diagnostic instruments, scoring rules, and sampling designs, with prevalence often reported to be several times higher when symptom cut-off scores are used instead of diagnostic algorithms (Syed et al., 2020; Wagner et al., 2020). Instead, the estimate is closer to the Norwegian general population (Heir et al., 2019). Methodological aspects may account for this discrepancy. We applied the DSM-5 algorithm for the PCL-5, which requires endorsement of symptoms across all four clusters and is more conservative than using symptom cut-off scores (Ashbaugh et al., 2016).

The low prevalence estimate should nevertheless be interpreted with caution. In addition to the conservative DSM-5 algorithm, the reliance on self-report may have contributed to underestimation of probable PTSD. In a policing context, disclosure concerns may have influenced symptom reporting. Thus, the relatively low prevalence observed in the present study may reflect not only methodological features and contextual factors, but also some degree of underreporting. This issue is considered further below in relation to the suppressor effect and the interpretation of group differences. Nonetheless, the presence of clinically significant symptoms in a subset of employees highlights the need for continued monitoring. Further insight into these differences can be gained by examining the contrasting patterns of trauma exposure and PTSD between sworn and civilian employees.

Occupational role differences and suppressor effect

SPOs reported markedly higher lifetime and occupational trauma exposure compared with civilians, but did not show higher crude PTSD prevalence. Adjusted analyses revealed a suppressor effect whereby SPOs had lower odds of PTSD once trauma exposure was considered. The identified group differences suggest that the crude association between occupational role and probable PTSD was shaped primarily by differences in exposure profile rather than by sociodemographic composition. Compared with civilian employees, sworn officers were younger, more often male, had longer seniority, and were somewhat more likely to be married or cohabiting. However, adjustment for these variables changed the odds ratio for occupational role only modestly and did not alter the overall pattern of findings. By contrast, adjustment for trauma exposure substantially strengthened the inverse association between sworn status and probable PTSD. This indicates that the most influential group differences were related less to demographic composition per se than to the markedly different exposure profiles of the two groups.

A further consideration is that the two groups may have differed not only in level of trauma exposure, but also in the predominant type and mechanism of exposure. For SPOs, elevated exposure was concentrated in operational, duty-related incidents such as assault, accidents, and sudden death. By contrast, civilian employees reported far lower levels of operational exposure, suggesting that the PTSD cases observed in this group may partly reflect different forms of burden, including vicarious exposure to distressing material, repeated contact with graphic case information, and organizational or role-related stressors. Qualitative research on civilian police support personnel indicates that such employees may be exposed to traumatic material through reviewing photographs, statements, and case files, while also experiencing lower role recognition, less tailored training, and exclusion from support structures following critical incidents (Huey et al., 2023).

This interpretation is also consistent with findings that civilian police personnel involved in large-scale events may show elevated distress despite lower direct operational exposure (Kerswell et al., 2020). Moreover, recent work on police-specific trauma measurement suggests that general trauma checklists may not fully capture the forms of occupational exposure that are most salient in policing, and that more police-specific measures may be needed to reflect indirect, cumulative, or situationally compounded trauma exposure (Miller et al., 2022). Accordingly, one limitation of the present study is that although the LEC-5 captures DSM-5-consistent traumatic exposure, it may not fully capture work-related burdens that are more indirect, cumulative, or organizational in nature, and this may be particularly relevant for understanding PTSD risk among civilian staff.

Several factors may explain this pattern, but the findings do not permit a single interpretation. One possibility is that SPOs benefit from training, peer support, and stronger occupational identification, which may reduce vulnerability following repeated exposure. However, repeated exposure under professional conditions should not be understood as a straightforward protective factor. Longitudinal research among police responders indicates that chronic and delayed PTSD trajectories can occur even after prolonged occupational exposure, and treatment-seeking first responders may present with dysphoric symptom profiles characterized by emotional numbing and social detachment rather than simple recovery (Bryant, 2022; Cone et al., 2015). Accordingly, repeated exposure in SPOs may reflect adaptation in some individuals, but in others may coexist with suppressed, dysphoric, or delayed symptom expression rather than simple protection.

Previous studies document elevated vulnerability among civilians in policing. Canadian research shows higher prevalence of depression and greater overall mental health burden among civilians compared with sworn staff (Lentz et al., 2020; Martin et al., 2021). Qualitative interviews further highlight how vicarious trauma and feelings of marginalization may amplify distress (Huey et al., 2023). A recent U.S. study similarly found comparable levels of PTSD symptoms across sworn and non-sworn personnel (Nam et al., 2025). These findings underscore that civilian personnel cannot be assumed to be at lower risk and should be explicitly included in occupational health models.

At the same time, stigma and career concerns may lead SPOs to underreport symptoms (Berg et al., 2006). Because the study relied entirely on self-report, differential symptom reporting remains a plausible alternative explanation for part of the suppressor effect and the low crude prevalence among SPOs. The inverse association observed after adjustment for trauma exposure should therefore not be interpreted uncritically as evidence of greater resilience or habituation among sworn officers. Prior research indicates that stigma surrounding mental health problems remains a major barrier to help-seeking in policing and may contribute to underreporting of distress and limited use of support services (Heffren & Hausdorf, 2016; Soomro & Yanos, 2019; Velazquez & Hernandez, 2019). Within police culture, emotional control and resilience are often highly valued, and officers may fear being perceived as weak or unfit for duty if they disclose psychological difficulties. Organizational norms and leadership practices may therefore shape willingness both to report symptoms and to seek care (White et al., 2016). Such dynamics may partly explain the relatively low prevalence of probable PTSD observed among SPOs in this study despite their higher exposure burden.

Methodological considerations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the response rate was modest (14%), although comparable to other mental-health surveys in policing (e.g. Lentz et al., 2020). Selection bias cannot be ruled out: employees experiencing greater distress may have been more motivated to participate, whereas stigma or concerns about disclosure may have reduced participation among those most affected. Nevertheless, the sampling frame was drawn from official NPD registers, ensuring that the initial sample reflected the composition of the police workforce, and the demographic characteristics of respondents closely matched those of the national police population. Low response rates do not necessarily compromise validity when internal data quality is high and demographic comparability is maintained. According to the concept of a ‘functional response rate’ (Holtom et al., 2022), the achieved sample can still provide reasonable population inferences under these conditions. Practical issues – such as the digital login solution and survey length – may also have contributed to lower participation and should be considered in future police workforce studies.

A further limitation is the substantial reduction in the sample available for PTSD modelling. Although 560 employees completed the survey, only 450 reported at least one potentially traumatic event, 243 identified an index event meeting Criterion A, and only 21 met criteria for probable PTSD. Accordingly, the adjusted models in Table 6 are based on small numbers, and the resulting odds ratios should be interpreted with caution. The low number of cases resulted in wide confidence intervals and increases the risk of type II errors. To reduce model instability and avoid overfitting, the adjusted PTSD analyses were therefore kept parsimonious, with occupational role entered together with one covariate at a time. Future studies should seek to replicate these analyses in larger samples with a greater number of PTSD cases.

The study relied on self-report instruments, which are susceptible to underreporting and recall bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Tourangeau et al., 2000). In addition, collecting data using a single method at one time point may raise concerns about common method variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003). However, the magnitude of such bias has been questioned, and its effects may be overstated in many survey-based studies (Spector, 2006, 2019).

Several potentially relevant variables, including coping strategies, prior mental health problems, and perceived organizational support, were not assessed and may have influenced the observed associations (Shadish et al., 2002).

Finally, although trauma exposure was assessed across the lifetime, PTSD symptoms were measured with reference to the past month only. This temporal mismatch complicates the establishment of temporal ordering between exposure and symptoms and limits causal inference. Consequently, longitudinal designs that assess both trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms over time are needed to clarify causal pathways (Maxwell & Cole, 2007; Shadish et al., 2002).

Implications and future research

The results indicate that PTSD prevalence among police personnel in the NPS is lower than often reported in international police samples, and the top three forms of occupationally related traumas with significant group differences were physical, sexual and weapon assault respectively. Yet civilians may be relatively more vulnerable when differences in trauma exposure are accounted for. Occupational health initiatives should therefore extend beyond sworn staff to include civilian employees.

Interestingly, trauma exposure acted as a statistical suppressor in this study, revealing a reversed association between occupational role and PTSD once exposure was controlled for. This unexpected pattern underscores the complexity of how exposure, role, and vulnerability interact, and future research should examine whether similar effects appear in other contexts or when additional covariates are included. Longitudinal studies are particularly needed to clarify trajectories of symptoms and resilience over time.

Conclusion

Norwegian police employees reported high exposure to traumatic events but relatively low PTSD prevalence compared with international estimates. A suppressor effect suggested that civilians may be more vulnerable to PTSD than sworn officers once exposure is taken into account. These findings underline the importance of including civilians in research, monitoring, and intervention strategies and of developing role-sensitive approaches to occupational trauma in police organizations.
