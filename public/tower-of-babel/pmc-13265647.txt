Hospital-Wide Safety Performance Affects Complication Rates After Primary Total Hip and Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Audrey R Byrne, Wyatt H Buchalter, Alexander S Dash, Lauren H Redler, Nattaly E Greene, Nana O Sarpong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are among the most commonly performed inpatient surgical procedures in the United States, with nearly 600,000 THAs and 1,000,000 TKAs performed among Medicare beneficiaries in 2022 [1]. Among Medicare beneficiaries, hospital payments for the index hospitalization for primary THA and TKA have been reported to range from approximately $11,772 to $22,784 per procedure depending on the presence of major complications, highlighting the substantial contribution of these procedures to overall Medicare inpatient expenditures [2,3]. These figures reflect payments for the initial surgical admission and do not include downstream costs related to 90-day readmissions or bundled episode reconciliation. As procedure volume continues to rise, attention has increasingly shifted from technical success alone to value-based care and measurable quality outcomes [4]. Complication rates within 90 days of surgery are central to contemporary quality initiatives and bundled payment models, including the Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement program, which held hospitals accountable for episode-of-care performance [5].

Outcomes following THA and TKA are influenced by patient comorbidity burden, surgical technique, implant selection, and procedural volume [6,7]. Institutional factors, including hospital case volume and perioperative care pathways, have also been associated with variation in arthroplasty outcomes [7,8]. Less is understood about whether broader hospital-wide safety performance translates to procedure-specific arthroplasty results. Total joint arthroplasty is often conceptualized as a discrete surgical event. Yet, many of the complications captured within 90 days—including pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, wound complications, mechanical failure, and death—reflect systemic issues in perioperative care that extend beyond the operating room [9,10].

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) publicly reports hospital-level, risk-adjusted outcome measures derived from Medicare fee-for-service claims [11]. The CMS Patient Safety Indicator composite (PSI-90) is a validated measure summarizing potentially preventable adverse events across inpatient populations and is intended to capture overall institutional safety performance [12]. The effect of hospital-wide safety performance, as measured by PSI-90, on arthroplasty-specific complication rates has not been directly evaluated.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the association between hospital-wide safety performance, as measured by PSI-90, and risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rates using nationally reported CMS data spanning April 1, 2021, through June 30, 2024. We hypothesized that hospitals with worse overall safety performance during this reporting period would demonstrate higher arthroplasty complication rates.

Material and methods

Data source

We performed a retrospective hospital-level analysis of publicly reported CMS Complications and Deaths–Hospital data spanning April 1, 2021, through June 30, 2024 [5]. This dataset contains provider-level, risk-adjusted outcome measures reported by CMS for U.S. hospitals participating in the Medicare program. Measures are calculated using standardized CMS methodologies and are publicly reported for quality benchmarking purposes. The dataset is publicly available and contains deidentified hospital-level data without patient identifiers; therefore, this study did not constitute human subjects research and was exempt from institutional review board approval in accordance with federal regulations (45 CFR 46).

The dataset includes hospital-level performance metrics across multiple domains, including procedure-specific complication rates, hospital-wide patient safety indicators, and 30-day mortality measures. All analyses were conducted at the hospital level; no patient-level data were available. The measures analyzed in this study reflected CMS reporting periods spanning April 1, 2021, through June 30, 2024.

Study measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the CMS risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rate (COMP_HIP_KNEE), an episode-based measure capturing the occurrence of ≥1 specified complication within 90 days among Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries undergoing elective primary THA and/or TKA. Complications included acute myocardial infarction, pneumonia, sepsis/septic shock, surgical site bleeding, pulmonary embolism, periprosthetic joint infection, mechanical complications (including periprosthetic fracture or device failure), wound infection, and death. Because CMS reports these procedures as a combined measure rather than as separate procedure-specific metrics, analyses were conducted using the composite arthroplasty complication rate.

Primary exposure

The primary exposure was hospital-wide patient safety performance, measured using the CMS PSI-90. PSI-90 is a validated composite measure summarizing multiple postoperative adverse event indicators and is reported as a continuous score, with higher values indicating worse hospital-wide safety performance [13].

Study cohort

Hospitals were eligible for inclusion if they reported both COMP_HIP_KNEE and PSI-90 measures during the same CMS reporting period. Hospitals with fewer than 25 reported hip or knee arthroplasty cases were excluded to reduce estimate instability and imprecision associated with low procedural volume and to align with CMS public reporting standards. Hospitals with missing or non-reportable values for either measure were excluded.

Data processing

CMS-reported measure values, denominators, and confidence interval bounds were extracted and converted to numeric formats. Entries labeled as “notAvailable” or “notApplicable” by CMS were treated as missing and excluded from analyses as appropriate. PSI-90 scores were analyzed as a continuous variable and additionally categorized into quartiles, with quartile 1 representing hospitals with the best safety performance and quartile 4 representing hospitals with the worst performance.

State-level aggregation and visualization

To explore geographic variation in hospital-level arthroplasty outcomes, the CMS risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rate (COMP_HIP_KNEE) was aggregated at the state level. For each state, the mean hospital-reported arthroplasty complication rate was calculated using all hospitals with available COMP_HIP_KNEE data during the reporting period. Hospitals with missing or non-reportable complication values were excluded from state-level averaging.

State-level averages of the CMS risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rate (COMP_HIP_KNEE) were visualized using a choropleth map of the United States. For each state, the mean hospital-reported COMP_HIP_KNEE complication rate was calculated using all hospitals with available data during the reporting period (April 1, 2021-June 30, 2024). Color intensity corresponded to the magnitude of the state-level mean complication rate, with lighter colors indicating lower complication rates (better performance) and darker colors indicating higher complication rates (worse performance). This visualization was intended to provide a descriptive overview of geographic variation rather than to infer causal or policy-level conclusions.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize hospital-level arthroplasty complication rates overall and across PSI-90 quartiles. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with ranges, as appropriate.

The association between hospital-wide safety performance and arthroplasty complication rates was evaluated using linear regression. The primary analysis used weighted linear regression, with weighting by the COMP_HIP_KNEE procedural denominator to account for differences in estimate precision related to hospital arthroplasty volume. PSI-90 score was modeled as a continuous predictor. An unweighted linear regression model was performed as a sensitivity analysis to assess robustness.

In a secondary analysis, hospitals were additionally classified as urban or rural using ZIP code–level Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes. Primary RUCA codes 1–3 were categorized as urban and codes ≥4 as rural. A weighted linear regression model incorporating geographic classification was performed to evaluate whether the association between PSI-90 and arthroplasty complication rates persisted after accounting for hospital location.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was defined as a P value <.05. Analyses were performed using R statistical software (R version 2026.01.0 + 392; Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA)

Results

Study cohort

A total of 1551 hospitals met inclusion criteria, reporting both the CMS hip and knee arthroplasty complication rate (COMP_HIP_KNEE) and the hospital-wide PSI-90 during the study period spanning April 1, 2021, through June 30, 2024. All hospitals included reported at least 25 hip or knee arthroplasty cases, consistent with CMS public reporting standards.

Hospital-wide safety performance and arthroplasty complication rates

Across the study cohort, hospitals demonstrated substantial variation in both overall safety performance and arthroplasty complication rates. When hospitals were divided into quartiles based on PSI-90 performance, mean risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rates increased stepwise as hospital-wide safety performance worsened (Table 1). Hospitals in the best PSI-90 quartile had a mean complication rate of 3.5%, compared with 3.7% among hospitals in the worst safety quartile. After accounting for differences in arthroplasty case volume, this steady increase in complication rates across PSI-90 quartiles remained statistically significant (P < .001). Median complication rates showed a similar pattern, with modest but consistent increases across worsening safety categories.

Association between hospital-wide safety and arthroplasty outcomes

In weighted linear regression analyses accounting for arthroplasty procedural volume, worse hospital-wide safety performance was significantly associated with higher arthroplasty complication rates. Specifically, each 1-unit increase in PSI-90 score was associated with a 0.47–percentage-point increase in the risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rate (β = 0.47; P < .001).

In unweighted sensitivity analyses, this association remained statistically significant, though with a smaller effect size (β = 0.26; P = .002), confirming the robustness of the observed relationship. Scatterplot analysis demonstrated a positive association between PSI-90 scores and arthroplasty complication rates, with greater variability observed among lower-volume hospitals (Fig. 1). Quartile-based comparisons similarly demonstrated a monotonic increase in complication rates across worsening PSI-90 categories (Fig. 2).

Geographic distribution of hospital-level outcomes

State-level aggregation of hospital-reported arthroplasty complication rates (COMP_HIP_KNEE) demonstrated notable geographic variation across the United States (Fig. 3). While substantial heterogeneity existed within individual states, regional patterns were observed, with clusters of higher and lower average values across different geographic areas. This descriptive analysis reflects regional variation but does not account for hospital-level characteristics or adjust for potential confounders. These findings provide contextual support for the observed interhospital differences identified in the primary analyses.

Discussion

In this national hospital-level analysis of CMS-reported data spanning April 1, 2021, through June 30, 2024, worse hospital-wide safety performance was significantly associated with higher risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rates. Mean complication rates increased progressively across PSI-90 quartiles, with a significant weighted trend observed across safety strata. In weighted linear regression analyses accounting for procedural volume, each 1-unit increase in PSI-90 score corresponded to a 0.47 percentage-point increase in arthroplasty complication rate. This association remained statistically significant in unweighted sensitivity analyses. These findings, derived from contemporary national data, suggest that arthroplasty outcomes are not solely reflective of procedure-specific technical factors but may also be influenced by broader institutional safety infrastructure.

The CMS hip/knee replacement complication rate captures clinically meaningful adverse events occurring within 90 days of elective primary arthroplasty, including cardiopulmonary events, thromboembolic complications, surgical site bleeding, mechanical complications, periprosthetic joint infection, and death [5]. Many of these events overlap conceptually with domains reflected in PSI-90, such as postoperative respiratory failure, venous thromboembolism, sepsis, and wound complications [14]. Hospitals with more robust surveillance systems, rapid response protocols, and standardized perioperative pathways may be better positioned to prevent, detect, and manage complications across service lines [15,16]. The observed association during the 2021–2024 reporting period supports the hypothesis that institutional safety culture and systems-level organization contribute to measurable variation in arthroplasty outcomes.

The observed association between hospital-wide safety performance and arthroplasty complications may be further understood by considering the types of complications captured within the CMS measure. Many complications—including pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, and mortality—are highly dependent on perioperative medical management, early mobilization, nursing care, and rapid response systems, and therefore reflect broader hospital-level processes rather than purely surgical technique [17]. In contrast, mechanical complications such as periprosthetic fracture or implant failure may be more closely related to procedural factors, though still influenced by postoperative care and rehabilitation [18]. Other complications, including periprosthetic joint infection and wound-related issues, likely represent a combination of surgical and system-level influences, including sterile technique, antibiotic protocols, and postoperative monitoring [19]. These distinctions suggest that hospitals with poorer overall safety performance may experience higher rates of complications that are sensitive to system-level coordination, surveillance, and response, providing a conceptual framework linking PSI-90 domains to arthroplasty outcomes.

These findings have implications within the evolving landscape of value-based reimbursement. Under bundled payment programs such as Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement, hospitals are evaluated on 90-day episode-of-care performance [20]. While efforts to improve arthroplasty outcomes frequently focus on surgical technique and enhanced recovery pathways, our results suggest that broader hospital-wide safety initiatives may also meaningfully influence complication rates [21,22]. Investments in institutional quality infrastructure during the contemporary post-pandemic era may therefore have downstream benefits for elective arthroplasty care [23,24].

Geographic factors may also contribute to variation in hospital performance. State-level aggregation demonstrated geographic heterogeneity in hospital-reported outcomes across the same reporting period, providing descriptive context for regional differences. Although this analysis was not designed to evaluate policy or socioeconomic determinants, it highlights meaningful variation across healthcare environments [25]. Building on this, our hospital-level analysis using RUCA classification allowed for direct evaluation of geographic effects on complication rates. In the present study, rural hospitals demonstrated modestly higher arthroplasty complication rates compared to urban hospitals, which may reflect differences in resource availability, perioperative infrastructure, or patient populations [26,27]. Importantly, the association between worse hospital-wide safety performance and increased complication rates remained significant after accounting for geographic classification, suggesting that institutional safety systems influence arthroplasty outcomes independent of hospital location.

In addition to geographic variation, the temporal context of the study period may also influence the interpretation of these findings. The study period overlaps with the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in 2021, during which U.S. hospitals experienced increased mortality, patient acuity, and system-level strain [28]. This timeframe was selected because it represents the most recent period for which both PSI-90 and COMP_HIP_KNEE measures were concurrently available through CMS, ensuring temporal alignment between exposure and outcome measures. Because both metrics are derived from the same reporting period, the analysis reflects hospital-wide safety performance and arthroplasty outcomes under shared healthcare system conditions. However, pandemic-related factors—including increased baseline patient risk, staffing shortages, resource constraints, and disruptions in elective surgical care—may have influenced complication rates, particularly cardiopulmonary events and mortality [29,30]. Importantly, because these factors affected hospitals broadly, the observed association between PSI-90 and arthroplasty complication rates likely reflects relative differences in institutional performance under similar system stress rather than isolated pandemic effects.

These considerations highlight important contextual factors and lead into several additional limitations that merit consideration in the present study. First, this was a hospital-level analysis of publicly reported CMS data covering reporting periods between April 1, 2021, and June 30, 2024; patient-level and surgeon-level variables were not available. Although geographic classification was incorporated using ZIP code–level RUCA codes, other hospital structural characteristics such as teaching status, ownership, and safety-net designation were not available within the CMS dataset. Second, CMS measures are derived from Medicare fee-for-service claims and do not include beneficiaries enrolled in Medicare Advantage or younger, commercially insured populations. Third, although CMS applies risk adjustment and hierarchical modeling, residual confounding may persist. Fourth, the magnitude of association, while statistically significant in a large national sample, was modest, and causality cannot be inferred. Finally, PSI-90 is a composite metric summarizing multiple adverse event domains and does not permit granular identification of which specific safety components most strongly drive the observed association. As such, while this analysis demonstrates a relationship between overall institutional safety performance and arthroplasty outcomes, it cannot identify discrete process targets for intervention.

However, the composite nature of PSI-90 may also represent a strength. Arthroplasty outcomes likely reflect the cumulative performance of multiple institutional systems—including rapid response infrastructure, perioperative medical optimization pathways, infection prevention protocols, and postoperative surveillance mechanisms—rather than a single isolated process failure. The present findings therefore suggest that broad institutional safety culture and systems-level organization may meaningfully influence arthroplasty performance, even if individual component contributions cannot be disentangled within this dataset.

Despite these limitations, this study leverages contemporary national data and standardized, risk-adjusted quality metrics to evaluate the relationship between institutional safety performance and arthroplasty outcomes across U.S. hospitals. Future research incorporating patient-level data or disaggregated PSI component measures may help identify specific institutional targets most strongly associated with arthroplasty outcomes.

Conclusions

Using contemporary nationally reported CMS data, worse hospital-wide safety performance was independently associated with higher risk-adjusted hip and knee arthroplasty complication rates. These findings suggest that optimization of institutional safety systems and hospital-wide quality infrastructure may represent an important strategy for improving outcomes following total hip and knee arthroplasty in the contemporary value-based care environment.
