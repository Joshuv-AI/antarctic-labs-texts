What is the true target population for biologics in real-life COPD or asthma-COPD overlap patients?
Authors: Maéva Zysman, Fanchon Herman, Léo Grassion, Camille Taillé, Jesus Gonzalez-Bermejo, Marina Guecamburu, Nicolas Roche, Arthur Pavot, Pierre-Olivier Girodet, Arnaud Bourdin, Nicolas Molinari, Patrick Berger, on behalf of the COBRA Study Group
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBiologics provide significant benefits in asthma, reducing exacerbations and symptoms. Some biologics have shown promising results in small subgroups of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and frequent exacerbations. Nevertheless, real-life data on the size of the COPD target population remain scarce.MethodsWe analysed the characteristics of COPD and coexisting asthma and COPD patients included in the prospective multicentre, French COhort of BRonchial obstruction and Asthma, between 2008 and 2023 and evaluated the number of patients who could correspond to the inclusion criteria of randomised controlled trials evaluating various biologics targeting interleukin 33 (IL-33) (-receptor), IL-5 (-receptor), IL-4Rα or TSLP, in routine clinical practice.ResultsAmong 434 COPD patients, only 21.7% met inclusion criteria for at least one biologic. Among patients with asthma, 54 (3.5%) had coexisting features of COPD in terms of age, smoking status and airflow obstruction and met inclusion criteria for at least one biologic. Notably, these patients were predominantly female, with worse lung function. Globally, the target chronic airway diseases population of the eagerly awaited biologics remains limited to a small part (ie, 1.3%–8%, depending on the biologic).ConclusionIn a real-life COPD and asthma population (including asthmatic patients with features of COPD), the proportion of patients satisfying selection criteria applied in randomised controlled trials assessing the efficacy of biologics remains limited to less than 10% of the whole population.Keywords: COPD Pharmacology, Asthma Pharmacology, Eosinophil Biology, Asthma in primary care

Introduction

Biomarker-guided use of biologics targeting type 2 inflammation has been shown to provide significant benefits in asthma,1 reducing exacerbations and symptoms.2 In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), however, blocking interleukin 5 (IL-5) or its receptor, using either mepolizumab or benralizumab, has failed to provide any significant reduction in acute exacerbations although some efficacy might be present in subgroups of patients with exacerbations and higher blood eosinophil counts.3 4 Besides, patients with coexisting asthma and COPD who have clinical features of both diseases may represent around 25% of COPD patients2 hence could become an important target for biologics. More recently, the BOREAS and the NOTUS studies evaluated the efficacy and safety of dupilumab, which blocks the IL-4 receptor alpha and thus the effects of both IL-4 and IL-13.5 6 939 COPD patients with a blood eosinophil count of at least 0.3×109/L, symptoms of chronic bronchitis and frequent exacerbations despite optimised inhaled therapy were included. A 30% reduction in the annual rate of moderate or severe exacerbations was observed in the dupilumab group (0.78 vs 1.10 exacerbation/year).5 Nevertheless, type 2 inflammation only affects 10%–40% of COPD patients7 and may vary over time,8 underlining the need for other biologics targeting other inflammatory pathways.

New biologics targeting alarmins (ie, IL-33, TSLP) may fill this gap and promising results have been reported among COPD patients (especially ex- rather than current smokers) with frequent exacerbations (at least two moderate exacerbations or one severe exacerbation in the previous year), respiratory symptoms (CAT score ≥10) or chronic bronchitis despite dual or triple inhaled therapy.9 10 Altogether, several studies are currently evaluating the efficacy and safety of these various new agents. Awaiting their results, it appears important to delineate the corresponding potential target populations in a real-life chronic airway diseases population. Along this line, our aim was to assess the proportion of real-life COPD patients who would be eligible for the currently studied biologics according to inclusion criteria from randomised controlled trials.

Methods

For this purpose, we analysed the characteristics of the whole COPD and asthmatic patients included in the prospective multicentre, observational French COhort of BRonchial obstruction and Asthma (COBRA) sponsored by INSERM, between 2008 and 2023.11 COPD and asthma were physician diagnosed disease according to GOLD and GINA criteria, respectively.

Patient and public involvement

No patient involved.

We evaluated the number of patients who could correspond to the inclusion criteria of studies evaluating the following biologics in COPD: astegolimab (anti-IL-33 receptor ST2, NCT05037929), benralizumab (anti IL-5-receptor, NCT04053634), dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα, NCT03930732 and NCT04456673), itepekimab (anti-IL-33, NCT05326412) mepolizumab (anti-IL-5, NCT04133909), tezepelumab (anti-TSLP, NCT04039113), tozorakimab (anti IL-33, NCT05166889, NCT05158387 and NCT06040086), summarised in online supplemental table 1. Briefly, all patients should be at least 40 years old, with an airflow obstruction defined by forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.7 and have experienced at least two moderate exacerbations or one severe exacerbation in the past year despite maximal inhaled therapy (dual or triple therapy). All patients were former or current smokers with a tobacco history of at least 10 pack-years at the inclusion. In addition to COPD patients, we considered that patients satisfying these criteria in the asthma population could be considered as asthma-COPD overlap, sharing with COPD the ‘fixed airflow obstruction treatable traits’. Of note, blood eosinophil counts higher than 0.3×109/L at least once during follow-up was sufficient to consider that a patient would meet this criterion when required.

Results

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to evaluate the proportion of COPD and asthma-COPD overlap patients that may be eligible to biologics, in clinical practice. COBRA is a national cohort with centres from tertiary care only. The relatively small number of patients and more specifically of subjects with blood eosinophil count ≥0.3×109/L may limit the generalisability of our results. However, the proportion of ‘eosinophilic’ patients with COPD has been reported to range from 5% to 40%.12 Besides, included patients correspond to a broad range of lung function and clinical severity, with relatively low proportions of patients with FEV1 below 30%pred (76 (20%) from COPD and 14 (0.9%) from asthmatic patients), one severe or two moderate exacerbations in the previous year (91 (18.1%) and 61 (14.7%) patients, respectively). Indeed, even if the number of patients is low, the proportion of patients with frequent exacerbations were similar to those previously described in other cohorts.13 So, we do believe that the patients from COBRA represent a snapshot of real-life clinical practice in many industrial countries. The relatively low percentage of COPD patients eligible for new biologics deserves some comments. First, the situation is roughly comparable in asthma, where the percentage of patients eligible to biologics is also very low,14 since these agents are restricted to severe asthma which represents less than 5% of the whole asthmatic population.2 Similarly, in COPD, frequent exacerbations despite optimised inhaled therapy affect only a minority of patients.15 16 Second, while one would welcome new disruptive ‘universal’ treatments capable of dramatically improving the status of all patients with COPD or asthma-COPD overlap, for now the new agents to come target very specific populations. The main challenge is not to miss this specific population, which underlines the need to regularly assess patients and, when the situation is ‘uncontrolled’, to confront their needs and characteristics to the indications of new targeted therapies. It seems then crucial to periodically assess the likelihood and the representativeness of patients included in randomised controlled trials compared with daily practice patients. Last but not least, efficacy data are not yet available for all drugs discussed in this study, and the upcoming results of phase 3 studies will address these points. Besides, a negative clinical trial could be either related to the lack of efficacy of the drug or to the study design, notably the choice of inclusion criteria and primary outcomes. For example, first results of mepolizumab in asthma have been disappointing as participants did not have severe disease or eosinophilic airway inflammation.17 However, data from one first phase 3 trial has revealed encouraging results regarding dupilumab,5 and although preliminary, some data from phase 2 studies seem promising.9 10

Conclusion

The target chronic airway diseases population of the eagerly awaited biologics remains limited to a small part (ie, 1.3%–8%). Despite the high prevalence of COPD and asthma, its significant morbidity and mortality, therapeutic resources remain very limited. Recent results may pave the way to biologics in the management of a subtype of exacerbating chronic airway diseases patients. However, we must keep in mind that the small number of eligible patients at this time required extensive research in chronic airway diseases to identify new disruptive and effective targets.
