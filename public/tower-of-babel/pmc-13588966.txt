Strategies for High-Quality Spine Surgery in Resource-Limited Settings: The Five-P Framework for Equitable Surgical Delivery
Authors: Andrei Fernandes Joaquim, Shanmuganathan Rajasekaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignNarrative review with a Conceptual Framework.ObjectivesLow- and middle-income countries (LMICs), home to approximately 80–85% of the global population, face a disproportionate burden of spinal disorders and limited access to timely, safe, and affordable surgical care. This review proposes a practical framework for delivering high-value spine care in LMICs by integrating evidence on health systems, value-based healthcare, and spine surgery into five strategic domains: Policy & Governance, Patient Selection, Perioperative Efficiency, Products & Technology, and Protection (the Five-P Framework).MethodsA narrative review with a structured literature search was conducted using PubMed and gray literature. Studies on spinal care delivery, health systems, cost-effectiveness, and surgical strategies in LMICs or comparable settings were qualitatively synthesized into thematic domains.ResultsFive domains were identified: (1) Policy & Governance, including centralization of care, regulation of financial incentives, anti-corruption measures, and complication reduction; (2) Patient Selection, emphasizing evidence-based indications; (3) Perioperative Efficiency, including enhanced recovery pathways, ambulatory surgery, and standardized protocols; (4) Products & Technology, focusing on resource optimization and context-appropriate technologies, including locally manufactured implants; and (5) Protection, promoting balanced medicolegal frameworks. Together, these domains aim to maximize value, improve access, and reduce unnecessary procedures and inefficient resource utilization.ConclusionThe Five-P Framework is a theoretical model that provides a system-level strategy to improve access to high-value spine care in LMICs through appropriate patient selection, efficient perioperative care, and optimal use of limited resources. Future studies to validate this proposal are necessary.Keywords: spine surgery, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), cost-effectiveness, health systems strengthening, global equity

Introduction

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are defined by the World Bank according to gross national income (GNI) per capita, and currently include economies with a GNI per capita below established high-income thresholds, encompassing low-, lower-middle-, and upper-middle-income categories.1,2 These countries account for the majority of the global population and continue to experience the fastest rates of population growth, particularly in regions such as Latin and Central America, Sub-Saharan Africa, and South Asia.1,2 Despite their demographic predominance, LMICs bear a disproportionate burden of disease, including an increasing prevalence of non-communicable conditions such as degenerative spinal disorders, while facing significant limitations in access to surgical care. It is estimated that nearly 5 billion people lack access to safe, timely, and affordable surgical services, underscoring a major global health inequity. Addressing these disparities is central to global health agendas advanced by organizations such as the World Health Organization and initiatives like the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery.1-3

The global volume of spine surgery has increased substantially in recent decades, making it one of the fastest-growing areas in modern surgical practice. Estimates suggest that millions of spinal procedures are performed annually, with continued expansion driven by demographic and technological factors. Recent market and epidemiological analyses estimate that global spine procedure volumes exceed 5 million cases per year, with projections approaching 8–9 million annually in the current decade.4,5

In the United States, spine surgery volume increased significantly over recent years, surpassing 1 million procedures annually. 6 This growth has been particularly pronounced in spinal fusion procedures.7,8 This global expansion is multifactorial, largely driven by aging populations, increasing prevalence of degenerative spinal conditions, advances in surgical techniques (including minimally invasive approaches), and broader access to healthcare services. 9 In this context, more than 80% of the world population lives in LMICs, and spinal surgery volume is increasing. Considering this fact, the main question is how LMICs can do better to decrease the untreated burden of spinal surgery in their population.

Accordingly, this narrative review synthesizes the current evidence and proposes the Five-P Framework, a conceptual model designed to guide the delivery of equitable, high-value spine care in resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Study Design

This study was conducted as a narrative review with a structured literature search to identify, synthesize, and organize evidence on strategies for improving the delivery of spine care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Rather than quantitatively summarizing outcomes, the objective was to integrate evidence from the literature into a practical conceptual framework applicable to resource-constrained health systems.

Literature Search

A structured literature search was performed in PubMed using combinations of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms including spine surgery, global surgery, low- and middle-income countries, health systems, value-based healthcare, cost-effectiveness, resource-limited settings, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS), quality improvement, and health policy from database inception through June 2026. To capture additional evidence relevant to global health and health system organization, gray literature was also reviewed, including publications from the World Health Organization (WHO), the World Bank, the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery, the Disease Control Priorities Project, and other international organizations. Only English-language publications were included.

Study Selection

Eligible publications included original studies, systematic reviews, narrative reviews, consensus statements, clinical guidelines, and health policy reports addressing the organization, delivery, financing, efficiency, or quality of spine surgery or surgical care in LMICs or comparable resource-constrained settings. Articles focusing exclusively on technical surgical outcomes without implications for health system organization were excluded.

Development of the Five-P Framework

Evidence identified through the literature review was qualitatively synthesized using an iterative thematic analysis. Strategies with similar objectives were grouped into broader conceptual domains according to their primary role in improving the access, efficiency, quality, and sustainability of spine care. Through repeated discussion among the authors, five major strategic domains emerged: Policy & Governance, Patient Selection, Perioperative Efficiency, Products & Technology, and Protection. These domains collectively constitute the proposed Five-P Framework, which serves as a conceptual model for organizing high-value spine care strategies in resource-constrained settings.

Expert Integration

Because many aspects of healthcare organization in LMICs are insufficiently addressed in the published literature, the synthesized evidence was interpreted in the context of the authors’ clinical and academic experience in spine surgery, health system organization, and surgical care delivery in economically constrained environments. This expert perspective was used to contextualize the literature, identify implementation challenges, and improve the practical applicability of the proposed framework. Both authors are senior spinal surgeons in referral centers of LMICs. The resulting framework should therefore be considered a conceptual policy model intended to stimulate future validation rather than a formal clinical guideline, developed from iterative thematic synthesis combined with expert interpretation by two senior spine surgeons from LMIC referral centers.

Policy and Governance

Centralization of care

Centralizing spine care in high-volume referral centers has been consistently associated with improved clinical outcomes, lower complication rates, and greater cost-efficiency due to specialization and resource concentration. 10 High-volume centers have also shortened hospital stays and optimized resource utilization – as demonstrated across several surgical specialties – concentrating human expertise and maximizing the value of limited healthcare investments.10-12 Finally, referral centers play a crucial role in managing complex cases, as smaller hospitals may be unable to treat them due to higher costs, increased risk of complications, and limited surgical expertise. Important to say that centralization of spine care depends on adequate workforce capacity, perioperative resources, and coordinated referral and transportation networks to ensure equitable access across diverse LMIC settings.

Financial incentives and spinal surgery

Unnecessary spinal surgery occurs when the intervention is either not needed or not in the patient´s best interest when weighed against other available options. 13 Potential reasons for unnecessary spinal surgery are wrong diagnosis, lack of conservative management options, and financial gain. Potential measures to decrease these problems are improved spine referral systems (patients who need surgery may have a reduction in their waiting time, and those who do not need spinal surgery may be treated in rehab clinics). In spinal surgery, most of the cases of unnecessary surgery are related to low back pain or degenerative lumbar conditions. Although precise estimates remain uncertain, particularly in LMICs, studies from high-income healthcare systems suggest that a substantial proportion of spine procedures for degenerative conditions may represent low-value or potentially inappropriate care, particularly in patients with non-specific low back pain.7,14 To mitigate this, establishing governmental centers of excellence linked to medical schools, implementing medical ethics education and workshops, appointing a non-biased second-opinion surgeon for these conditions, and standard institutional protocols are necessary. 15 Additionally, misaligned financial incentives may contribute to unnecessary procedures, particularly the overuse of complex spinal instrumentation. Aligning reimbursement models with evidence-based practices is essential to mitigate supplier-induced demand. 16 High-performing professionals who achieve favorable outcomes, as measured by standardized assessment tools, should be appropriately and transparently reimbursed by payers—both public and private—without conflicts of interest. A high-performing professional in an ideal system should combine a stable base salary with performance-based incentives aligned with value rather than volume. Compensation should reward adherence to evidence-based guidelines, appropriate surgical indications, and patient-centered outcomes, while mechanisms such as bundled payments and audit systems help discourage unnecessary procedures and promote cost-effective care, particularly in resource-limited settings.17,18 Finally, especially in LMICs, corruption and lack of transparency further compromise healthcare delivery, leading to inequitable access and inefficient allocation of limited resources. Strengthening governance frameworks, auditing systems, and accountability mechanisms is therefore fundamental to improving the delivery of surgical care, reducing corruption, and focusing on the patient. 3

Complication reduction

Aggressive complication reduction strategies are among the most effective mechanisms for controlling costs and improving quality in spine surgery. Surgical complications generate disproportionately high expenditures through prolonged hospitalization, readmissions, revision surgeries, intensive care utilization, rehabilitation demands, and medicolegal consequences.19-21 In addition to financial burden, complications negatively affect team morale, reduce institutional efficiency, and compromise patient confidence in healthcare systems. Consequently, minimizing preventable adverse events should be considered not only a clinical priority but also a central economic strategy. Continuous audit systems, including morbidity and mortality conferences, prospective outcome registries, infection surveillance, and protocol-based quality assessment, are essential components of high-performing surgical units. Such systems enable early identification of systemic weaknesses and promote accountability, standardization, and process optimization. Evidence from both high-income countries and LMICs suggests that structured quality-improvement initiatives can significantly reduce complication rates while simultaneously lowering overall healthcare expenditures.22,23

Patient Selection

Optimization of surgical indications

Appropriate patient selection remains one of the most critical determinants of cost-effectiveness in spine surgery. Inappropriate surgical indications are likely a major driver of wasteful healthcare spending, particularly through the overuse of complex and high-cost procedures without clear clinical benefit.16,18 Beyond financial incentives, inappropriate surgical indications may also result from deficiencies in evidence-based practice, variability in clinical decision-making, and inadequate clinical and surgical training. 24 Improving education, adherence to clinical guidelines, standardized decision-making protocols, multidisciplinary case discussions, and shared decision-making can reduce unwarranted practice variation while optimizing patient outcomes and resource utilization, particularly in resource-constrained settings.25,26 When clinically appropriate, less invasive procedures should be preferred over more complex interventions, as randomized trials have demonstrated that decompression alone for selected patients with lumbar spinal stenosis without instability provides outcomes comparable to fusion while reducing complications and healthcare costs.27,28

Conversely, inappropriate patient selection also includes withholding surgery when operative treatment is clearly indicated. In patients with traumatic spinal injury (TSI), prolonged non-operative management may result in avoidable disability despite lower initial costs. Lessing et al demonstrated, in a cost-effectiveness analysis from Tanzania, that surgical treatment of TSI produced substantial reductions in disability with highly favorable cost-effectiveness ratios, ranging from US$41–58 per disability-adjusted life year (DALY) averted in neurologically intact patients to US$112–158 per DALY averted in patients with quadriplegia. These values compare favorably with several widely accepted public health interventions, supporting the inclusion of surgical management for appropriately selected TSI patients within essential surgical care packages. 29

Minimalism and value-based spine care in LMICs

A fundamental principle for improving the sustainability of spine care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is the adoption of a value-based minimalist approach throughout the entire continuum of patient management. Minimalism in this context does not imply under-treatment, but rather the avoidance of unnecessary interventions that increase costs without proportional improvement in outcomes. Excessive imaging, redundant investigations, premature surgical indications without adequate conservative treatment, and overly aggressive surgical strategies all contribute substantially to escalating healthcare expenditures. 30

Overdiagnosis and overtreatment have become increasingly recognized as major drivers of inefficiency in modern healthcare systems, particularly in high-income countries where technological availability may unintentionally promote intervention-based medicine. 31 In spine care, several studies have demonstrated substantial geographic variability in imaging utilization and surgical rates without corresponding differences in patient outcomes, suggesting that more intensive care does not necessarily translate into better results.7,31,32

Perioperative Efficiency

Improving perioperative efficiency is a key strategy for reducing healthcare costs without compromising outcomes. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols have demonstrated reductions in length of stay, complications, and overall costs across multiple surgical specialties, including spine surgery. 33 In the context of spinal surgery, ERAS protocols encompass preoperative patient education, optimization of comorbidities, multimodal analgesia with opioid-sparing strategies, minimizing operative blood loss, and early mobilization with hospital discharge. These protocols can significantly decrease costs, increasing surgical capacity.

Similarly, the expansion of outpatient spine surgery for selected cases has shown promising results in terms of safety, patient satisfaction, and cost reduction. 34 For patients who require less complex procedures, such as lumbar decompression and single-level fusion, this model is associated with reduced length of stay, lower risk of hospital complications, faster mobilization, and improved patient satisfaction. Besides, it can free hospital resources for more complex cases.

High-output surgical performance and system efficiency

Economic sustainability in spine surgery depends not only on cost reduction but also on operational efficiency and high-output performance. In many healthcare systems, operating room underutilization, prolonged turnover times, excessive administrative barriers, and restrictive workflow regulations significantly reduce surgical productivity. 35 Low operating room throughput increases fixed costs per procedure, prolongs waiting lists, and limits access to care. In contrast, several high-volume centers in Asia have demonstrated that streamlined perioperative workflows and optimized surgical scheduling can substantially increase the number of procedures performed per operating room each day while maintaining acceptable safety standards. A practical illustrative example of these principles is the Ganga Hospital model in India, which has shown that high-quality spine surgery can be delivered at relatively low cost through standardized clinical pathways, high surgical volumes, efficient operating room utilization, multidisciplinary teamwork, and locally adapted technologies. Although the Ganga Hospital represents a single institutional experience, similar value-based organizational principles have been reported across other high-volume LMICs, suggesting that the concepts underlying perioperative efficiency are broadly applicable across diverse resource-constrained settings. 36

Differences in healthcare organization between regions may have major economic implications. In some centers, extensive administrative protocols, staffing limitations, and rigid work-hour regulations may restrict operating room productivity, sometimes limiting complex operating theaters to a single major case per day. Conversely, many institutions in China and South Asia routinely perform multiple major spinal procedures daily within the same infrastructure. 10 Although workforce protection and patient safety remain essential, excessive bureaucratic inefficiency may inadvertently reduce system-wide access and increase healthcare costs. A balanced approach that combines safety protocols with efficient workflow optimization is therefore critical for sustainable spine care delivery, particularly in LMICs where unmet surgical demand remains substantial.

Products & Technology

Use of locally manufactured implants

Adapting surgical techniques to local realities is a cornerstone of delivering sustainable spine care in LMICs. In spinal surgery, many common diseases can be treated successfully using different surgical approaches. The concept of “frugal innovation” emphasizes simplifying procedures and leveraging available resources while maintaining acceptable clinical outcomes and maintaining its core functionality. 37 For example, rationalizing implant density in spinal deformity surgery does not necessarily affect the outcomes and can reduce costs, operative time, and blood loss. 38 This is such an example of technical adaptation in a low-resource environment. Another example is to perform a short fusion versus a long fusion or combined (anterior-posterior) for some fractures, such as thoracolumbar burst fractures, with similar clinical outcomes. 39 Similar to this, in spinal surgery, there are a multitude of surgical approaches that are less morbid and less expensive and should be strongly considered in low-income countries to increase spinal surgery access. The use of locally manufactured implants represents another important strategy to reduce costs. It is important to emphasize that local manufacturing should always be accompanied by appropriate quality assurance. The goal is not simply to produce cheaper implants, but to make safe spinal implants more accessible. Hospitals should prioritize implants with documented compliance with recognized quality-management and mechanical testing standards, together with material certification and product traceability. Post-market surveillance should also be an integral part of this process. In this way, local manufacturing can improve affordability without compromising the safety and durability required for spine surgery, particularly in complex procedures. 40

Selective use of high-technology in complex cases

Image-guided navigation, despite improving the accuracy of spinal instrumentation and the potential to reduce intraoperative radiation exposure, has substantial upfront costs and also maintenance costs. From an economic and health systems perspective, the value of navigation is highly context-dependent. In high-resource settings with high surgical volumes, improved accuracy and reduced revision rates may offset initial costs over time.41-43 Conversely, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where financial constraints are more pronounced, widespread adoption may not be feasible or cost-effective for routine cases.44,45 In such settings, optimized use of conventional fluoroscopy—combined with surgical experience, standardized techniques, and selective use of navigation for complex cases—may represent a more balanced and scalable strategy.43,46 Ultimately, the goal is not technological maximalism but value-based implementation, where advanced tools are deployed selectively to maximize patient benefit while preserving system sustainability. 10

Selective technological investment and physician-led administration

Technological innovation has transformed spine surgery, but indiscriminate adoption of expensive technologies without clear outcome benefit may threaten the sustainability of healthcare systems. In value-based spine care, investment decisions should prioritize technologies that demonstrably improve patient safety, surgical accuracy, complication reduction, or long-term outcomes.17,42 Technologies introduced primarily for convenience, prestige, or market competitiveness may increase system-wide expenditures without proportional clinical benefit.

Effective allocation of technological resources often requires strong physician participation in administrative decision-making. Clinicians directly involved in patient care are uniquely positioned to evaluate whether specific technologies meaningfully improve outcomes or simply add cost and complexity.47,48 In spine care, selective investment strategies—such as reserving advanced navigation systems for deformity or revision surgery rather than routine cases—may represent a more rational and sustainable model of innovation adoption.

Telemedicine

Telemedicine may represent complementary strategies to expand access and strengthen local capacity in resource-limited settings.49,50 Remote consultation and telemedical support can facilitate preoperative assessment, postoperative follow-up, and provider-to-provider consultation, potentially reducing the need for long-distance travel and extending specialist expertise to underserved regions, which is important to decrease costs and healthcare system overload.

Protection

The rapid expansion of spinal surgery worldwide has been paralleled by a growing burden of medicolegal claims, particularly in environments where health systems are fragmented and regulatory frameworks are inconsistent. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), litigation is not only underdeveloped in terms of structure but also unevenly applied, creating a paradoxical scenario in which both patients and providers lack adequate protection.10,51,52

Unregulated or poorly structured medicolegal environments may lead to two equally harmful consequences: under-accountability, which permits unsafe or low-quality care, and over-defensive medical practice, which increases costs and restricts access to necessary surgical interventions. In spine surgery, where procedures are complex, outcomes are variable, and complications may occur despite appropriate care, this imbalance can significantly impact clinical decision-making and system efficiency.53-55

A rational medicolegal framework should aim to balance patient protection with the sustainability of surgical services. Standardization of informed consent processes, development of nationally endorsed clinical guidelines, and the implementation of transparent outcome registries are critical components of this approach. These measures not only reduce variability in care but also provide objective benchmarks of adverse events.26,55,56

Importantly, litigation should not be the primary mechanism for quality control. Instead, structured peer-review systems, institutional morbidity and mortality conferences, and non-punitive reporting mechanisms can foster a culture of safety while minimizing adversarial legal processes. In LMIC settings, where resources are constrained, such approaches are often more cost-effective and scalable than reliance on judicial systems alone.26,56-58

From a policy perspective, governments and professional societies should collaborate to establish clear legal pathways that distinguish between negligence and recognized complications. The absence of such differentiation contributes to defensive medicine, delays in surgical indication, and, ultimately, worse patient outcomes. In contrast, predictable and transparent medicolegal environments can enhance provider confidence, improve access to timely surgical care, and reduce unnecessary healthcare expenditures. 58

Ultimately, strengthening medicolegal systems should be viewed not as a legal necessity alone, but as a strategic component of health system design. In the context of global spine care, particularly in LMICs, balanced litigation frameworks can play a pivotal role in improving quality, equity, and efficiency.

Discussion

The present review proposes the Five-P Framework as a conceptual model to guide the delivery of high-value spine care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Although previous global surgery initiatives, including the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery and the Global Spine Care Initiative, have highlighted the urgent need to improve access to safe and affordable surgical care, relatively little attention has been devoted to spine-specific health system organization in resource-constrained settings.1-3 Our framework integrates current evidence on health system governance, value-based healthcare, perioperative optimization, and resource stewardship into five complementary domains that can be adapted to different healthcare environments. Rather than representing isolated recommendations, these domains should be viewed as interdependent components of a comprehensive strategy to improve access, quality, and sustainability of spine care.2,10,17,45,47 Differently from previous publications, this current proposal focuses specifically on spine surgery delivery and integrates governance, patient selection, perioperative efficiency, technology adoption, and medicolegal protection into a single conceptual model.

An important strength of the Five-P Framework is its emphasis on value maximization rather than cost reduction alone. In resource-constrained environments, expanding access to surgery depends not only on increasing financial investment but also on improving the efficiency of existing resource use. Appropriate patient selection, reduction of preventable complications, implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathways, selective adoption of technology, and transparent governance all contribute to maximizing health outcomes while preserving healthcare sustainability. These principles closely align with the modern concept of value-based healthcare proposed by Porter and colleagues and with current recommendations emphasizing quality improvement and rational resource allocation in surgery.17,18,28,33

Some degree of overlap between domains is inherent to complex health system frameworks and does not diminish the conceptual integrity of the Five-P model. Each domain was defined by its primary operational focus, although certain strategies contributed to more than one domain. Future validation and implementation studies may further refine the boundaries between domains and improve the framework’s operational clarity. Finally, the Five-P Framework should be regarded as a conceptual policy model that requires prospective validation rather than as a prescriptive clinical guideline. Although each pillar is supported by published evidence, the integrated framework itself has not yet been formally evaluated. Future research should focus on implementation studies across different LMIC settings, cost-effectiveness analyses, and the development of measurable performance indicators for each domain. Such studies could determine whether adoption of this framework translates into improved access to spine surgery, better patient outcomes, and more efficient resource utilization. If validated, the Five-P Framework may provide policymakers, professional societies, and healthcare systems with a practical roadmap for implementing equitable, high-value spine care worldwide.10,29,40,57 It is important to say that medicolegal reforms must be adapted to each country´s legal, political, and regulatory context, within a tailored implementation to optimize systems instead of standardized measures across different LMICs.

Unlike previous global surgery initiatives, which primarily focus on expanding surgical capacity and workforce development, the Five-P Framework specifically addresses the organization and delivery of spine care by integrating governance, patient selection, perioperative optimization, technology adoption, and medicolegal protection into a unified policy model.

Policy Implications - Limitations

The Five-P Framework should not be interpreted as a strategy for indiscriminate cost containment. Rather, it promotes high-value care, in which resource stewardship is balanced with patient outcomes, safety, and equitable access to surgery. Frugal innovation and efficient resource utilization are intended to support evidence-based care—not to justify under-treatment, delayed intervention, or the adoption of lower-quality surgical practices. Accordingly, reimbursement policies and health system reforms should align financial incentives with quality metrics, functional outcomes, and patient safety to ensure that economic sustainability complements, rather than compromises, high-quality spine care. Importantly, the Five-P Framework is not intended as a one-size-fits-all model. Given the marked heterogeneity among LMICs, the relative priority and sequence of its domains should be adapted according to local health system maturity, workforce availability, governance capacity, financing structures, and existing healthcare infrastructure.

Due to its theoretical nature, future validation using structured Delphi consensus involving spine surgeons, policymakers, economists, anesthesiologists, rehabilitation team, and patient representatives from LMICs is necessary.

Conclusion

The Five-P Framework provides a practical conceptual model to guide policymakers, professional societies, and spine surgeons toward equitable, high-value spine care in resource-constrained settings. Future implementation studies are needed to validate its impact on access, quality, and sustainability.
