Outcomes of lynch syndrome endometrial surveillance in the Netherlands
Authors: Malcolm Scott, Adam N. Rosenthal
Published: 2026

We commend Elkenboon et al. for their study,1 highlighting that endometrial cancer surveillance in Lynch syndrome may enable earlier detection and improved prognosis. However, due to its non-randomized design, conclusions about survival are uncertain. Surveillance did however identify some endometrial hyperplasias, potentially justifying hysterectomy, and thus preventing cancer development.

The study lacks data on atypical versus non-atypical hyperplasia, and since a third of general population atypical hyperplasia can coexist with endometrial cancer,2 hysterectomy is usually advised. While non-atypical hyperplasia in Lynch syndrome is poorly understood, only around 5% of non-atypical hyperplasia in the general population progresses to endometrial cancer over 20 years.3 We would suggest that non-atypical hyperplasia in Lynch syndrome is a cause for concern and warrants serious consideration of hysterectomy. Furthermore, attributing high number of hyperplasias to over-diagnosis in a non-randomized study could be misleading.

The study does not address surveillance intervals, nor whether endometrial cancer was diagnosed between or during surveillance visits, precluding surveillance performance calculations. Crucially, detailed information about adjuvant therapy in ≥ stage 2 cases is omitted. 13.5% of endometrial cancer on surveillance vs. 21% not on surveillance were ≥ stage 3 and would be expected to require more morbid external beam radiotherapy.4

Given the (non-significant) stage-shift (Fig. 1), it would appear that surveillance may have substantially reduced morbidity in the patients diagnosed with endometrial cancer. Based on our experience, offering hysteroscopy and endometrial biopsy under sedation or general anaesthesia may improve uptake and adherence.
