Dynamical Similarity in Multisymplectic Field Theory
Callum Bell, David Sloan

Abstract
Symmetry under a particular class of non-strictly canonical transformations may be used to identify, and subsequently excise degrees of freedom which do not contribute to the closure of the algebra of dynamical observables. In this article, we present a mathematical framework which extends this symmetry reduction procedure to classical field theories described within the covariant De-Donder Weyl formalism. Both the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms are discussed; in the former case, the Lagrangian is introduced as a bundle morphism ℒ : J 1 E → ∧ m M \mathcal{L}:J^{1}E\rightarrow\wedge^{m}M , whilst the Hamiltonian description takes as its multiphase space the restricted multimomentum bundle J 1 ​ E ∗ J^{1}E^{*} . The contact reduction process is implemented in a number of particular cases, and it is found that the geometry of the reduced space admits an elegant physical interpretation.



I
Introduction

When attempting to formulate a symplectic description of classical field theories, there exist two distinct approaches, both of which have proved highly fruitful in their respective areas of application. On the one hand, the spacetime-covariant formalism, described within the framework of multisymplectic geometry, treats events at a single spacetime point, considering all coordinates (including time) equally
aldaya1980geometric
;
crnkovic1987covariant
; the canonical (or
3+1
) construction, by contrast, aims to study the evolution of a dynamical system from the perspective of the initial-value problem, defined on some suitably-chosen Cauchy surface
arnowitt1962dynamics
;
fischer1979initial
. In this article, we shall almost exclusively be concerned with the covariant formalism, which in the context of the Hamiltonian description, we refer to as the
Hamilton De-Donder Weyl
(HDW) formulation
de1930theorie
;
weyl1935geodesic
.

The use of multisymplectic geometry to describe classical field theories presents a number of advantages over its canonical counterpart; in particular, the instantaneous Hamiltonian formalism, almost ubiquitously employed as a precursor to quantum field theory, takes as its canonical variables field functions, evaluated at some fixed instant of time
sardanashvily1995generalized
. Consequently, the resulting phase space, upon which we are obliged to work, is infinite-dimensional
kanatchikov1994basic
. Additionally, since the HDW formalism makes no distinction amongst spacetime coordinates when constructing conjugate momenta
klusovn2023weyl
, it is, by its very nature, manifestly covariant
vasak2024ccgg
. These two features (finite-dimensionality and manifest covariance) have led to significantly hightened research interest within the field of multisymplectic geometry in recent years.

In spite of this, the geometrical formulation is certainly not without its challenges; it is still not fully understood, for example, how the Poisson bracket should be constructed, which, naturally, is an essential prerequisite for most potential geometric quantisation scheme
woodhouse1992geometric
;
carosso2018geometricquantization
;
nair2016elements
. Some progress has been made in this field
kanatchikov1997field
;
forger2005covariant
, and a multisymplectic bracket is well-defined between semi-basic forms on the covariant phase space
kanatchikov2013donder
; however, a fully rigorous construction, applicable to arbitrary forms remains, for the most part, an open question. Additionally, the equal treatment of all spacetime coordinates - which confers the attractive quality of manifest covariance - leads to a discrepancy in the number of field and momentum degrees of freedom: to each field variable, there correspond as many conjugate momenta as dimensions of the underlying spacetime manifold.

Our most precise and accurate description of nature is currently formulated in the language of field theory, both at a classical and quantum level
nair2006quantum
. Indeed, the advent of a field-theoretic viewpoint has arguably been one of the most significant shifts in perspective of the last few centuries. It has been argued by philosophers such as Ismael and van Fraassen that, when seeking a theoretical description of the natural world, one should first construct a framework which amply describes the phenomena of interest, before progressively reducing its superfluous structure, until a minimally-sufficient yet consistent theory remains
ismael2021symmetry
;
ismael2023rethinking
;
vanFraassen_2012
. It is therefore of interest to examine the structural elements of our field-theoretic models which may be deemed dispensable, and further, to explore any novel insights which are made manifest, as a result of adopting this minimalistic viewpoint. To this end, it is necessary to formulate a precise and rigorous prescription for the identification and subsequent elimination of any structure which is not truly fundamental to our theory.

Our characterisation of mathematical structure as ‘redundant’ shall follow closely the Principle of Essential and Sufficient Autonomy (PESA)
gryb2021scale
, which posits that we should seek the minimal ontology that closes the algebra of dynamical observables. This viewpoint is in-line with the ideas of Leibniz, whose Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) motivates a theoretical framework, in which only those elements which affect the dynamics of physically-observable quantities are considered
rodriguez2014leibniz
;
frankel1981leibniz
.

The second facet of our minimalist approach - the elimination of the structure we have labeled as redundant - will rely upon the identification of ‘dynamical similarities’
sloan2018dynamical
; these are a class of non-strictly canonical transformations, which leave the dynamics of the observable degrees of freedom invariant. Theories which possess a dynamical similarity may be subjected to a symmetry reduction procedure, in which a single redundant degree of freedom is excised from the ontology. The main objective of the present work is to generalise this process, which is well-established for classical theories of particles, to a field-theoretic context.

This article is structured in the following way: in section (
II
), we begin by introducing the necessary mathematical framework of multisymplectic geometry, which we apply to the construction of both the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations of classical field theory. We then present a similar treatment of multicontact geometry; together, these constitute the preliminary geometrical ideas required to precisely formulate both conservative and dissipative field theories.

Following this mathematical introduction, we present the concept of dynamical similarity, as defined for theories of particles, constructed on the (co)tangent bundle of some smooth configuration space manifold. As mentioned above, the identification of a dynamical similarity allows a symmetry reduction procedure to be carried out, the details of which are described at the beginning of section (
IV
). The remainder of this section is then dedicated to presenting a novel generalisation of the reduction procedure for classical field theories, described within the multisymplectic framework.

Dynamical similarities have been studied in a field-theoretic context in
sloan2025dynamical
; however, this analysis was conducted within the framework of
k
-symplectic geometry
de2015methods
, which has a number of disadvantages, as compared to the multisymplectic formalism presented here. Of particular note is that, whilst the vast majority of our results focus on regular systems, the framework we construct is a precursor to a full treatment of contact-reduced gauge theories. The fibred manifolds of multisymplectic geometry are the most natural arena in which to develop such a scheme, and thus justifies our choice of formalism. Furthermore, our principal motivation for developing a rigorous method for treating singular theories lies in the study of General Relativity, where it is known that the Einstein-Hilbert action admits a scaling symmetry; we provide further details of this in section (
IV.1
). Finally, we close with a number of examples, discussing the relevance and practical implications of our results, before providing several open questions and lines of further investigation.

II.3
Hamiltonian Field Theory

\begin{split}\Theta_{h}&=p^{\mu}_{a}\,dy^{a}\wedge d^{m-1}x_{\mu}-H\,d^{m}x\\
\vskip 0.56905pt\Omega_{h}=-\,&dp^{\mu}_{a}\wedge dy^{a}\wedge d^{m-1}x_{\mu}+dH\wedge d^{m}x\end{split}

(14)

From
\Omega_{h}
, we shall also define the following 2-forms

\omega_{h}^{\mu}:=-\,\iota_{\partial_{m}}\cdots\,\iota_{\partial_{1}}(\Omega_{h}\wedge dx^{\mu})=dy^{a}\wedge\,dp^{\mu}_{a}

(15)

which will be used extensively in section (
IV.2
), when constructing vector fields corresponding to dynamical similarities.

In a similar spirit to the results of section (
II.2
), the Hamiltonian field equations, like their Lagrangian counterpart, may also be deduced from a variational principle. This so-called
Hamilton-Jacobi principle
seeks a critical section
\psi\in\Gamma(M,J^{1}E^{*})
, which, in this context is defined as

\psi\;\,\textrm{critical}\quad\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad\frac{d}{dt}\biggr|_{t=0}\,\int_{M}\psi_{t}^{*}\Theta_{h}=0

In a similar manner to the corresponding Lagrangian principle, the variations
\psi_{t}=f_{t}\circ\psi
are constructed using a one-parameter family of transformations
\{f_{t}\}
which generate any compactly-supported
\bar{\sigma}
-vertical vector field on
J^{1}E^{*}
.

The equivalent geometrical formulation of the Hamilton-Jacobi principle seeks an
m
-dimensional,
\bar{\sigma}
-transverse, integrable distribution
\mathcal{D}
, whose integral manifolds are critical sections of this variational problem. As in the Lagrangian case, such distributions are associated with classes of locally decomposable,
\bar{\sigma}
-transverse multivector fields
\{\boldsymbol{X}\}
, which may be expressed locally as

\boldsymbol{X}=\bigwedge\limits_{\mu=1}^{m}\,f\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}+F^{a}_{\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{a}}+G^{\nu}_{a\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\nu}_{a}}\right)

(16)

for some non-vanishing function
f\in C^{\infty}(J^{1}E^{*})
. Continuing to draw parallels between the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian cases, if
\psi\in\Gamma(M,J^{1}E^{*})
is a critical section of
\bar{\sigma}
, then

\psi^{*}\,\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle X}\Omega_{h}=0\quad\quad\quad\quad\textrm{for all}\;X\in\mathfrak{X}^{\infty}(J^{1}E^{*})

(17)

Additionally, if we express
\psi
locally as
\psi(x)=(x^{\mu},\psi^{a}(x),\psi^{\mu}_{a}(x))
, since (
17
) holds for all vector fields

, using the local expression (
14
) for the Hamilton-Cartan form
\Omega_{h}
, we obtain the following Hamilton De-Donder Weyl equations

\frac{\partial\psi^{a}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\biggr|_{x}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{\mu}_{a}}\biggr|_{\psi(x)}\quad\quad\quad\frac{\partial\psi^{\mu}_{a}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\biggr|_{x}=-\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial y^{a}}\biggr|_{\psi(x)}

(18)

Finally,
\psi
is an integral section of a class of
\bar{\sigma}
-transverse integrable multivector fields
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{h}\}
, each of which satisfies

\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{X}_{h}}\Omega_{h}=0

(19)

We conclude this discussion describing the relationship between the critical sections of each of the two variational principles introduced thus far. Suppose that

II.4
An Example: The Free Scalar Field

The tone we have adopted thus far has been highly formal; in order to illustrate the utility of the multisymplectic formalism, and to provide a less abstract presentation of the ideas we have developed, we shall consider a simple, albeit relevant example: that of a free scalar field in a Minkowski background
ryder1996quantum
.

Spacetime coordinates on
M=\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
are denoted
x^{\mu}
, with
\mu=0,1,2,3
; since we have a single real scalar field, the covariant configuration space
E
is simply
E=M\times\mathbb{R}
, and we shall take local coordinates on
J^{1}E
to be
(x^{\mu},y,y_{\mu})
. In this way, the Lagrangian function
L:J^{1}E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by

L(x^{\mu},y,y_{\mu})=\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}y_{\mu}y_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}y^{2}

(20)

in which we have adopted the mostly minus convention
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\textrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)
, more commonly employed throughout the quantum field theory literature
peskin2018introduction
;
itzykson2006quantum
. The scalar energy
E_{L}\in C^{\infty}(J^{1}E)
for this system is simply

E_{L}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{\mu}}\,y_{\mu}-L=\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}y_{\mu}y_{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}y^{2}

(21)

The Poincaré-Cartan form is of degree 4, and has the following local coordinate expression

\Theta_{L}=\eta^{\mu\nu}y_{\nu}\,dy\wedge d^{3}x_{\mu}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}y_{\mu}y_{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}y^{2}\right)d^{4}x

(22)

The equations of motion for a holonomic section
\phi(x)=(x^{\mu},y(x))
, calculated according to (
8
), read

\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}y-m^{2}y=0

which, as anticipated, is simply the Klein-Gordon equation for a single scalar field in flat spacetime. We also highlight that the derivatives in (
8
) are to be evaluated along the canonical lifting of
\phi
, which implies that
y_{\mu}\rightarrow\partial_{\mu}y
.

The momenta
p^{\mu}
canonically conjugate to the field variable
y
are given by

p^{\mu}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{\mu}}=\eta^{\mu\nu}y_{\nu}

Consequently, the Hamiltonian function reads

H=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}y^{2}

Consider a locally decomposable, integrable multivector field
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathfrak{X}^{4}(J^{1}E^{*})
, expressed in local coordinates as

\boldsymbol{X}=\bigwedge\limits_{\mu=0}^{3}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}+F_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+G^{\nu}_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\nu}}\right)

in which, referring to (
16
),
\bar{\sigma}
-transversality has been enforced setting the multiplicative function
f
to unity. Using the following expression for the Hamilton-Cartan 5-form

\Omega_{h}=-\,dp^{\mu}\wedge dy\wedge d^{3}x_{\mu}+(\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}\,dp^{\nu}+m^{2}y\,dy)\,\wedge\,d^{4}x

it follows that the coordinate-free equations of motion (
19
) become

F_{\mu}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{\mu}}=\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}\quad\quad\quad\quad G^{\mu}_{\mu}=-\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}=-\,m^{2}y

Supposing that this holonomic multivector field
\boldsymbol{X}
has integral sections of the form
\psi(x)=\left(x^{\mu},y(x),p^{\mu}(x)\right)
, we may write

\frac{\partial y}{\partial x^{\mu}}=F_{\mu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}\quad\quad\quad\quad\frac{\partial p^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\mu}}=G^{\mu}_{\mu}=-\,m^{2}y

and these equations may easily be shown to be equivalent to the Klein-Gordon equation, as deduced from the Lagrangian formalism.

II.5
Singular Lagrangians

The free scalar field theory of the previous example was a particularly simple case of a hyperregular Lagrangian; such functions admit globally-defined multivector field solutions which are locally decomposable,
\widehat{\pi}
-transverse and have holonomic integral sections. Many of the Lagrangian functions encountered in more physically interesting contexts are not of this nature, and require the implementation of a geometric constraint algorithm, as developed in
de2005pre
;
de1996geometrical
, for example.

As we have seen, the differential form
\Theta_{L}
associated with a singular Lagrangian is, in general, pre-multisymplectic; as such, it is not guaranteed that
\widehat{\pi}
-transverse, integrable multivector field solutions to the equation
\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{X}_{L}}\Omega_{L}=0
exist. Moreover, if such objects do exist, they are, with almost complete certainty, not defined on the entirety of
J^{1}E
. In the most favourable of cases, solutions exist on some submanifold
S_{f}\subset J^{1}E
; this is the
final constraint submanifold
, obtained through successive iteration of the geometric constraint algorithm.

In practice, this algorithm is implemented locally, following a number of steps, in which we define spaces of successively more restricted multivector fields. In particular, we begin by introducing the space

\textrm{ker}^{m}\,\Omega_{L}:=\{\boldsymbol{X}_{L}\in\mathfrak{X}^{m}(J^{1}E)\;|\;\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{X}_{L}}\Omega_{L}=0\,\}

consisting of all
m
-multivector fields which are solutions to the equations of motion, but are not necessarily
\widehat{\pi}
-transverse or integrable. Within this space, we then seek the subset of those multivector fields which are locally decomposable and
\widehat{\pi}
-transverse, denoting the resulting subspace
\textrm{ker}^{m}_{\widehat{\pi}}\,\Omega_{L}\subset\textrm{ker}^{m}\,\Omega_{L}
.

Following this, we further restrict
\textrm{ker}^{m}_{\widehat{\pi}}\,\Omega_{L}
to the subset of semi-holonomic solutions, which amounts to setting
F_{\mu}^{a}=y^{a}_{\mu}
in the decomposition (
6
). In general, this step gives rise to further consistency conditions, and possibly to new constraints; in order to ensure that the dynamics of the system remain confined to the constraint submanifold, it is necessary to impose tangency conditions on each of the constraints. This is achieved by demanding that the Lie derivative of any constraint along each of the vector fields which constitute
\boldsymbol{X}_{L}
vanish.

Finally, we perform an analysis of the integrability of our semi-holonomic solutions, in order to reach the final subspace
\textrm{ker}^{m}_{\scriptscriptstyle H}\,\Omega_{L}
, consisting of locally decomposable,
\widehat{\pi}
-transverse, holonomic multivector fields. Examples of the local implementation of this geometric algorithm may be found in
gaset2018new
and
gomis2023multisymplectic
, for example.

Throughout this analysis, we have assumed the singular Lagrangian to be almost-regular; consequently, we know that
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{FL}(J^{1}E)
is a closed submanifold of
J^{1}E^{*}
. In such cases, it is possible to define the Hamilton-Cartan forms
\Theta_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{I}}
and
\Omega_{h}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{I}}
restricted to
\mathcal{I}
, and formulate the Hamilton-Jacobi principle, as above, on the (generally) pre-multisymplectic manifold
(\mathcal{I},\Omega^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{I}}_{h})
. As in the Lagrangian case, the multivector field solutions to the equations of motion are not guaranteed to exist, and in the case that they do, we must apply the above algorithm in order to deduce the appropriate final constraint submanifold of
\mathcal{I}
.

III
Multicontact Formulation of Classical Field Theories

Over the course of the preceding sections, we have dedicated significant effort to understanding how multisymplectic geometry may be used to formulate a rigorous description of classical field theories. Since the formalism we have developed generalises the concepts encountered in standard symplectic geometry, it should be relatively clear that all field theories treated within this framework are conservative in nature.

As we shall see, the identification of a dynamical similarity, and subsequent implementation of a symmetry reduction procedure, will inevitably necessitate the introduction of a framework which accommodates systems of a frictional (or dissipative) nature
sloan2018dynamical
;
sloan2021scale
;
bell2025dynamical
;
gryb2021scale
. The most appropriate arena in which to study such a class of field theories is the multicontact formalism
de2023multicontact
;
de2025practical
;
rivas2025symmetries
, whose construction we shall summarise somewhat more succinctly than our treatment of multisymplectic geometry.

III.1
Lagrangian Formalism

Throughout this section, we shall continue to work with the fibred manifolds
\pi:E\rightarrow M
, and
\kappa:J^{1}E\rightarrow E
, with
\textrm{dim}\,M=m
and
\textrm{dim}\,E=n+m
. The novelty of the multicontact formalism is that the Lagrangian density is now an
m
-form on the manifold

\mathcal{S}:=J^{1}E\times_{M}\wedge^{m-1}T^{*}M\cong J^{1}E\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

(23)

The space
\mathcal{S}
is of dimension
2m+n+nm
, and is a bundle over both
E
, with projection
\tau:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow E
, and
M
, with
\beta=\pi\circ\tau:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow M
. We take local coordinates on
\mathcal{S}
to be
(x^{\mu},y^{a},y^{a}_{\mu},s^{\mu})
, in which the
s^{\mu}
span the factor of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in (
23
), and have the field-theoretic interpretation of an action density. Pulling back the volume form
\omega\in\Omega^{m}(M)
to
\mathcal{S}
, the Lagrangian density is written locally as

\mathcal{L}(x^{\mu},y^{a},y^{a}_{\mu},s^{\mu})=L(x^{\mu},y^{a},y^{a}_{\mu},s^{\mu})\,\beta^{*}\omega

(24)

The Lagrangian function
L
is referred to as
regular
if the Hessian matrix

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial y^{a}_{\mu}\partial y^{b}_{\nu}}\right)

is regular at all points of
\mathcal{S}
. In close analogy to the multisymplectic case, we have the Lagrangian
m
-form
\Theta_{L}
, which, in local coordinates, is given by

\Theta_{L}=\left(ds^{\mu}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{a}_{\mu}}\right)\,dy^{a}\wedge d^{m-1}x_{\mu}+\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{a}_{\mu}}\,y^{a}_{\mu}-L\right)\,d^{m}x

(25)

Note that, in contrast to the case of multisymplectic Lagrangian functions, if
L
is singular, then the associated
m
-form
\Theta_{L}
is not strictly required to be pre-multicontact, since the corresponding Reeb distribution is not guaranteed to exist. Such pathological examples will not be of interest to us, and so we shall consider any singular Lagrangian to define a pre-multicontact system.

Given an
r
-form
\xi\in\Omega^{r}(\mathcal{S})
, we introduce the following operator

\begin{split}\bar{d}\;:\;\Omega^{r}(\mathcal{S})\;&\longrightarrow\;\Omega^{r+1}(\mathcal{S})\\
\xi\;&\longmapsto\;\bar{d}\xi:=d\xi+\sigma_{\Theta_{L}}\wedge\,\xi\end{split}

where
\sigma_{\Theta_{L}}\in\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{S})
is known as the
dissipation form
, and is expressed in local coordinates as

\sigma_{\Theta_{L}}=-\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\mu}}\,dx^{\mu}

(26)

Note that, in general, the operator
\bar{d}
is
not
nilpotent, so that there exists no sensible way to formulate cohomology groups, unless
d\sigma_{\Theta_{L}}=0
, in which case, we obtain the so-called Lichnerowicz-Jacobi cohomology
deleon1999lichnerowiczjacobicohomologyhomologyjacobi
. Our motivation for introducing this ‘twisted‘ differential operator is that the Lagrangian
(m+1)
-form associated with
\Theta_{L}
is
not
(as in the multisymplectic case) defined simply as
\Omega_{L}=-\,d\Theta_{L}
; instead, we have

\Omega_{L}=\bar{d}\Theta_{L}=d\Theta_{L}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\mu}}\,dx^{\mu}\wedge\,\Theta_{L}

(27)

Note that this
(m+1)
-form is defined
without
the minus sign used in the multisymplectic case.

Within the context of multisymplectic geometry, we define a conserved quantity to be an
(m-1)
-form
\zeta^{\mu}\,d^{m-1}x_{\mu}
, such that, on-shell, we have
\partial_{\mu}\zeta^{\mu}=0

gaset2021symmetries
;
echeverria1999multivector
. Action-dependent field theories, by contrast, contain dissipated quantities; in particular, when the equations of motion are satisfied, instead of a conservation law of the form
\partial_{\mu}\zeta^{\mu}=0
, we find that

\partial_{\mu}\zeta^{\mu}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\mu}}\zeta^{\mu}

which is captured precisely by the dissipation form (
26
). It is interesting to note that all quantities within an action-dependent field theory follow the same dissipation law, and that this is determined by the Lagrangian (or Hamiltonian) function. Additionally, not all dissipation laws correspond to empirically measurable losses: this fact will be of particular relevance when discussing the physical interpretation of the symmetry-reduced models presented in later sections.

Given a regular multicontact Lagrangian system
(\mathcal{S},\Theta_{L})
, the equations of motion for a holonomic section
\Psi\in\Gamma(M,\mathcal{S})
are given by

\Psi^{*}\Theta_{L}=0\quad\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad\quad\quad\Psi^{*}\iota_{Z}\Omega_{L}=0\quad\textrm{for all}\;Z\in\mathfrak{X}^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})

(28)

Since
\Psi
is holonomic, it may be expressed locally as

\Psi(x)=\left(x^{\mu},y^{a}(x),\frac{\partial y^{a}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\biggr|_{x},s^{\mu}(x)\right)

with which the coordinate-free equations (
28
) become

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{a}_{\mu}}\circ\Psi\right)=\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{a}}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{a}_{\mu}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial s^{\mu}}\right)\circ\Psi\\
\frac{\partial s^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\mu}}=L\circ\Psi\end{split}

(29)

We shall refer to these expressions as the
Herglotz-Lagrange field equations

Gaset_2024
. As in the multisymplectic case, the dynamical evolution of our system may also be expressed in terms of multivector fields; in the current context, the equations of motion for locally decomposable
\beta
-transverse multivector fields
\boldsymbol{X}_{L}\in\mathfrak{X}^{m}(\mathcal{S})
are

\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{X}_{L}}\Theta_{L}=0\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{X}_{L}}\Omega_{L}=0

(30)

III.2
Hamiltonian Formalism

Recall that the Hamiltonian formalism of multisymplectic dynamics was developed on the restricted multimomentum bundle
J^{1}E^{*}
; in order to provide an analogous description for dissipative field theories, we shall work on the space

\mathcal{S}^{*}:=J^{1}E^{*}\times_{M}\wedge^{m-1}T^{*}M\cong J^{1}E^{*}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

Introducing the projection
\bar{\tau}:\mathcal{S}^{*}\rightarrow M
, we obtain a bundle over
M
, of dimension
2m+n+nm
, upon which local coordinates are taken to be
(x^{\mu},y^{a},p^{\mu}_{a},s^{\mu})
.

Given a (hyper)regular Lagrangian system
(\mathcal{S},\Theta_{L})
, there exists a map
\mathcal{FL}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{*}
, which acts on local coordinates according to

\mathcal{FL}^{*}\,x^{\mu}=\;x^{\mu}\quad\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{FL}^{*}\,y^{a}=y^{a}\quad\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{FL}^{*}\,p^{\mu}_{a}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial y^{\mu}_{a}}\quad\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{FL}^{*}\,s^{\mu}=s^{\mu}

(31)

This Legendre map then allows us to define the Hamiltonian
m
-form on
\mathcal{S}^{*}
as
\Theta_{L}=\mathcal{FL}^{*}\Theta_{H}
; in local coordinates,
\Theta_{H}
is given by

\Theta_{H}=\left(ds^{\mu}-p^{\mu}_{a}\,dy^{a}\right)\,\wedge\,d^{m-1}x_{\mu}+H\,d^{m}x

(32)

in which we have introduced the Hamiltonian function
H:\mathcal{S}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, related to the Lagrangian energy function according to
E_{L}=\mathcal{FL}^{*}H
. When studying the reduction of multisymplectic field theories to their frictional multicontact counterparts, it will be of benefit to work with the following 1-forms

\eta^{\mu}:=-\,\iota_{\partial_{m}}\cdots\;\iota_{\partial_{1}}\,(\Theta_{H}\wedge dx^{\mu})=ds^{\mu}-p^{\mu}_{a}\,dy^{a}

(33)

The dissipation form for action-dependent field theories within the Hamiltonian formalism is given by

\sigma_{\Theta_{H}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial s^{\mu}}\,dx^{\mu}

(34)

which is then used to construct the
(m+1)
-form
\Omega_{H}=\bar{d}\Theta_{H}
, as in (
27
).

A geometric formulation of the field equations may be given in terms of holonomic sections
\psi:M\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{*}
, or locally decomposable,
\bar{\tau}
-transverse multivector fields
\boldsymbol{X}_{H}\in\mathfrak{X}^{m}(\mathcal{S}^{*})
. In the former case, we have

\psi^{*}\Theta_{H}=0\quad\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad\quad\quad\psi^{*}\iota_{Z}\,\Omega_{H}=0\quad\textrm{for all}\;Z\in\mathfrak{X}^{\infty}(\mathcal{S}^{*})

(35)

whilst the corresponding equations for multivector fields are

\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{X}_{H}}\Theta_{H}=0\quad\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad\quad\quad\iota_{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{X}_{H}}\Omega_{H}=0

(36)

If we decompose the multivector field
\boldsymbol{X}_{H}
locally as

\boldsymbol{X}_{H}=\bigwedge\limits_{\mu=1}^{m}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}+A^{a}_{\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{a}}+B^{\nu}_{a\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\nu}_{a}}+C^{\nu}_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial s^{\nu}}\right)

(37)

then the coordinate-free expressions (
36
) become

A_{\mu}^{a}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{\mu}_{a}}\quad\quad\quad B^{\mu}_{a\mu}=-\,\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial y^{a}}+p^{\mu}_{a}\frac{\partial H}{\partial s^{\mu}}\right)\quad\quad\quad C^{\mu}_{\mu}=p^{\mu}_{a}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{\mu}_{a}}-H

(38)

Let us suppose that
\psi:M\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{*}
is an integral section of
\boldsymbol{X}_{H}
; writing
\psi(x)=\left(x^{\mu},y^{a}(x),p^{\mu}_{a}(x),s^{\mu}(x)\right)
, the equations (
35
) are given locally by

\frac{\partial y^{a}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\biggr|_{x}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{\mu}_{a}}\biggr|_{\psi(x)}\quad\quad\quad\frac{\partial p^{\mu}_{a}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\biggr|_{x}=-\,\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial y^{a}}+p^{\mu}_{a}\frac{\partial H}{\partial s^{\mu}}\right)\biggr|_{\psi(x)}\quad\quad\quad\frac{\partial s^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\biggr|_{x}=\left(p^{\mu}_{a}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{\mu}_{a}}-H\right)\biggr|_{\psi(x)}

(39)

We shall refer to (
38
) and (
39
) as the
Herglotz De-Donder Weyl equations
for multivector fields and holonomic sections respectively; they are the appropriate modification of the standard Hamilton De-Donder Weyl equations, adapted to describe field theories which are frictional in nature

. Note that the last of the expressions in (
39
) is simply the condition
\partial_{\mu}s^{\mu}=L^{H}
, expressed in canonically conjugate variables.

III.3
The Scalar Field Revisited

In order to conclude our study of multisymplectic and multicontact geometry, we shall revisit the example of section (

), in which we considered a single free scalar field in a flat background spacetime. In an attempt to present an example which highlights the features of the preceding sections, but does not introduce further unnecessary complications, we shall content ourselves with a Lagrangian function which differs from that of (

L(x^{\mu},y,y_{\mu},s^{\mu})=\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}y_{\mu}y_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}y^{2}+\lambda_{\mu}s^{\mu}

The Lagrangian 4-form

\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}y-m^{2}y=\eta^{\mu\nu}(\partial_{\mu}y)\lambda_{\nu}

It is a simple exercise to show that the Hamiltonian function

\Omega_{H}\in\Omega^{5}(\mathcal{S}^{*})

\sigma_{\Theta_{H}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial s^{\mu}}\,dx^{\mu}=-\lambda_{\mu}\,dx^{\mu}

It thus follows that

\Omega_{H}=d\Theta_{H}+\sigma_{\Theta_{H}}\wedge\,\Theta_{H}=dH\wedge d^{4}x-dp^{\mu}\wedge dy\wedge d^{3}x_{\mu}-\lambda_{\nu}dx^{\nu}\wedge(ds^{\mu}-p^{\mu}dy)\wedge d^{3}x_{\mu}

Finally, suppose that

\boldsymbol{X}_{H}=\bigwedge\limits_{\mu=0}^{3}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}+A_{\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+B^{\nu}_{\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\nu}}+C^{\nu}_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial s^{\nu}}\right)

is a locally decomposable
\bar{\tau}
-transverse multivector field satisfying

V
Field Theory Classification

In order to better understand the geometric insight offered by our symmetry reduction procedure, it is instructive to consider the different multisymplectic bundle structures that arise within a variety of physically interesting field theories. Since the vast majority of such models are gauge theories
taylor2001gauge
;
o2000gauge
, we shall attempt to construct a broad system of classification, based upon the gauge group, and thence deduce important features common to each type. Our discussion will closely follow that presented in
gotay2004momentum
and

II.2

In general, we shall consider two principal classes of theory, whose distinction is determined by the relationship between the gauge group
G\subset\textrm{Aut}(E)
, and the spacetime diffeomorphism group
\textrm{Diff}(M)
. Given a diffeomorphism
f:M\rightarrow M
, we say that an automorphism
\Phi:E\rightarrow E

covers

if
\pi\circ\Phi=f\circ\pi
. Intuitively, the automorphism can be considered to ‘lift’ the diffeomorphism from the spacetime manifold to the configuration space, in a manner which is consistent with the action of
f
on
M

munoz2025lifting
.

A theory is described as being
parameterised
if all (or a suitably large subset of) spacetime diffeomorphisms are covered by the transformations in
G
. Physically, this means that the theory is invariant under arbitrary coordinate reparameterisations; this is the case of General Relativity, for example, in which the entirety of
\textrm{Diff}(M)
is covered by
G
.

A
background theory
, by contrast, refers to any system in which the gauge group is a subset of those automorphisms which cover only the identity on
M
. A Yang-Mills theory defined on a flat background spacetime, for instance, has as its gauge group a set of transformations which are completely internal, and thus unrelated to spacetime diffeomorphisms.

All theories we shall consider throughout will possess a metric; however, the precise status of this metric within the particular field theory of interest will affect the underlying multisymplectic bundle structure. In the simplest of cases, the metric is a fixed object, so that it neither evolves dynamically, nor may it assume different values. Whenever we consider theories defined on a flat static background, the metric is fixed, and will most often be the Minkowski metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}
.

Another possibility is that the metric enters the theory parametrically; in this case, it is not constrained to be fixed, and we may choose to assign different values to the metric, as the context befits. Whilst this kind of theory does allow the metric to be changed, the equations of motion are derived with the metric held fixed. For this reason, we must include the components of the metric as additional configuration space variables; however, since these are not variational in nature, the Lagrangian should not contain any metric velocity variables. Consequently, when treating theories of this type, we modify the configuration space
E
and the corresponding jet bundle
J^{1}E
in the following way

E\;\longrightarrow\;E\times_{M}\textrm{Sym}_{2}^{d-1,1}(M)\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad J^{1}E\;\longrightarrow\;J^{1}E\times_{M}\textrm{Sym}_{2}^{d-1,1}(M)

in which
\textrm{Sym}_{2}^{d-1,1}(M)
denotes the space of symmetric covariant tensors of rank two and Lorentzian signature
(d-1,1)
on
M
; sections of
\textrm{Sym}_{2}^{d-1,1}(M)
then correspond to metrics on
M
. Note that we do not consider the jet bundle over the whole space
E\times_{M}\textrm{Sym}_{2}^{d-1,1}(M)
, but rather append the factor of
\textrm{Sym}_{2}^{d-1,1}(M)
to the original jet bundle; this is precisely so that the components
g_{\mu\nu}
of the metric are amongst the field variables, but the derivatives
g_{\mu\nu,\rho}
are not.

The final possibility we shall consider is that of a variational metric; typically, theories which possess variational metrics are coupled to gravity, and are thus described by Lagrangians which
do
incorporate derivatives of the metric. As an example, we consider a principal
G
bundle
\pi:P\rightarrow M
, with gauge group

; denote by

the bundle of connections of

upon which

provide local coordinates. For concreteness, we have here restricted ourselves to a

gauge theory in
d=3+1
spacetime dimensions. The gauge-invariant Lagrangian is now taken to be a function on

\mathcal{P}:=J^{1}C(P)\times_{M}J^{1}(\textrm{Sym}_{2}^{3,1}(M))

Note that this space differs from that which we use when describing theories whose metrics are parametric; the use of the jet bundle

introduces velocity variables for the components

\partial_{t}(\partial_{t}\chi+2(s^{t}+s^{x}))-\partial_{x}(\partial_{x}\chi+2(s^{t}+s^{x}))+1=(\partial_{t}\chi-\partial_{x}\chi)^{2}-4(s^{t}\partial_{t}\chi+s^{x}\partial_{x}\chi)-8(s^{t}+s^{x})^{2}

(82)

\chi

The elimination of the scaling coordinate from this theory has an interesting physical interpretation. In the previous example, we considered the case of non-interacting scalar fields, where it was observed that the Herglotz Lagrangian assumed the form of a sum of a piece describing the reduced space scalar field dynamics, together with an
independent
component containing the action density variables. In this case, by contrast, when the original scalar fields exhibit some kind of interaction (which here, is mediated by the mixed derivative term
(\rho_{t}-\rho_{x})(\chi_{t}-\chi_{x})
) we observe that the corresponding Herglotz Lagrangian contains terms which couple
\chi
to the action density. In this way, upon eliminating the scaling variable
\rho
, the coupling between the two original scalar fields manifests as an interaction between the remaining field and the frictional degrees of freedom.

Let us now consider the Hamiltonian formalism for this dynamical system; since our Lagrangian is regular, we know that the Legendre map is a diffeomorphism, and thus expect that the momenta be invertible, allowing us to unambiguously solve for the velocities
\rho_{\mu}

\chi_{\mu}

VI
Conclusions and Outlook

The use of multisymplectic geometry to provide a manifestly covariant description of classical field theory has recently assumed a more prominent role within the literature, due in part to the success of standard symplectic geometry, which continues to provide insightful connections between classical mechanics and differential geometry
de2006symplectic
. The finite dimensionality of the multisymplectic phase space is highly attractive, and constitutes a notable advantage over the canonical field-theoretic framework. The formalism of multicontact geometry, by contrast, is far less developed; action-dependent field theories provide a way to describe systems which exhibit non-conservative effects, and are thus encountered somewhat less frequently.

Following a brief review of the mathematical preliminaries required to treat classical field theories in a manifestly covariant manner, we introduced the notion of dynamical similarity, and demonstrated that the process by which a redundant ‘scaling’ degree of freedom may be eliminated has a well-defined generalisation to a field-theoretic context. In particular, we showed that a multisymplectic Lagrangian (or Hamiltonian) system admitting a dynamical similarity could be reduced to an action-dependent theory, defined on a lower-dimensional space. From a geometrical perspective, this symmetry reduction process had a simple yet elegant interpretation: the removal of a single degree of freedom
\rho
leaves each of the velocities
\rho_{\mu}
with no corresponding field variable; in the reduced theory, each of these unpaired velocities assumes the role of a component of the action density
s^{\mu}
, which, together, span a Reeb distribution, making the resulting theory multicontact.

Through use of a pair of simple examples, we found that, in general, a theory of
N
non-interacting massive scalar fields, which possesses a scaling symmetry, is dynamically-equivalent to a configuration of
(N-1)
massless fields moving in a non-zero potential, set by the value of the original field mass, together with a completely independent frictional component, which we interpret as arising due to the empirical inaccessibility of the scaling variable. Furthermore, it was observed that any coupling between the scalar fields of the original multisymplectic theory destroys this separation into independent pieces, and introduces terms which mix the frictional degrees of freedom with those of the remaining scalar fields.

The results presented throughout constitute a foundational framework, within which a number of interesting areas of future work may be explored. For example, we have focused exclusively on regular Lagrangian systems; the regularity of our field theory guarantees that symmetry reduced space admits a well-defined multicontact structure. The vast majority of theories of physical interest possess gauge symmetries, which give rise to singular Lagrangian systems. In this case, we must develop a formalism adapted to the treatment of such theories, and, in particular, it will be of great interest to study the interaction between gauge fixing and contact reduction. At present, it remains unclear as to whether the process of pre-symplectic reduction, used to define the physical phase space, commutes with the elimination of a scaling degree of freedom
echeverria1999reduction
. Such a framework will also provide an arena within which to study General Relativity; it has been mentioned several times that, upon making a conformal decomposition, the Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian possesses a dynamical similarity. Having developed a sound understanding of the interaction between gauge and scaling symmetries, it will be of great interest to examine the solution space of contact-reduced General Relativity, particularly at points where our conventional description becomes singular and thus ill-defined. It is known, for instance, that within the cosmological sector, both FLRW and certain classes of Bianchi models may be extended beyond the initial singularity
sloan2019scalar
;
hoffmann2024continuation
.

In addition to the treatment of singular field theories, it will also be of interest to consider the behaviour of systems possessing fermionic degrees of freedom. Some progress has been made in this area; for example a De-Donder Weyl Hamiltonian description of a simple two-dimensional supersymmetric theory has been developed in
lindstrom2020covariant
. Moreover, generalised Kähler geometries have been used to construct covariant Hamiltonian formulations of non-linear sigma models
lindstrom2006brief
, and so an extension of our formalism to encompass anticommuting variables is highly desirable.

Finally, we have mentioned several times throughout the advantages presented by the covariant De-Donder Weyl formulation of classical field theories. Whilst manifest covariance and finite-dimensional phase spaces are indeed attractive features of this approach, when contrasted with the canonical formalism, it cannot be overlooked that the latter provides a clear and rigorous path to quantisation, whereas the former does not. In light of this, of particular importance is the development of a standardised framework within which the restricted multimomentum bundle
J^{1}E^{*}
may be quantised, in much the same way that geometric quantisation offers a scheme for performing such calculations on the tangent bundle.
