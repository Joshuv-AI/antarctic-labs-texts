Many-body post-processing of density functional calculations using the variational quantum eigensolver for Bader charge analysis
Erik Schultheis , Alexander Rehn , Gabriel Breuil

Abstract
Quantum chemistry and condensed matter physics are among the most promising applications of quantum computers. Further, estimating properties of a material is crucial to evaluate its industrial applications. To investigate charge distributions of weakly and strongly correlated systems we calculate Bader charges for various periodic systems by solving many-body Hamiltonians using the variational quantum eigensolver. The Hamiltonians are computed from Kohn-Sham orbitals obtained from a prior DFT calculation. We first demonstrate the accuracy of our method on various doped MgH 2 supercells. Further, we show that our approach, compared to standard DFT, significantly improves the Bader charge values for strongly correlated transition metal oxides, where we take DFT+U results as a reference. The computational framework behind our many-body calculations, called Dopyqo, is made openly available as a software package.

Introduction

Solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation for molecules and materials gives access to their electronic structure. From this solution, one can calculate relevant electronic properties to characterize quantum systems and to describe their real-world applications. For example, excitation energies, band structures, and optical spectra show their usefulness in many fields such as opto-electronic materials
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, while the Fermi level, work function and also the ionization energies are of relevance in the characterization of materials to control the transportation of charge carriers

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. Another relevant electronic property is the charge distribution in a quantum system. It is a valuable property for various technologies and materials, such as electrospinning
[
8
]
, conductive polymers
[
9
]
, 2D materials such as transition metal dichalogenides
[
10
]
, and doping effects in a material
[
11

. A feasible way to evaluate the charge distribution in a material is to compute partial charges such as Löwdin
[

]
or Bader
[
13
]
charges. They can give insight on the charge locality or the nature of the bonding between atoms
[
14
]
. Löwdin charges are defined by atomic orbitals making them dependant on the chosen basis set
[
15
]
. Bader charges require only the real-space electronic density and, therefore, depend on the accuracy of that density
[
16
]
. Löwdin charge are used for molecules while Bader charges are often used in combination with plane-wave DFT codes to describe charge distributions in periodic systems
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20

. Bader charge analysis has shown its efficiency in the description of the activation of CO
2
by a Ti- and Fe- doped graphene
[

2

[
22

[

showed that the Bader charge is ”a good parameter for predicting oxidation state in most of binary metal oxides”. Moreover, Bader charges are also used as charge locality descriptors to evaluate charge transfer properties during electrochemical processes
[
24
]

The partial charges are dependant on the computational method and to fully use their potential in the description of material properties, they should be used with other electronic properties such as oscillator strength or dipole moment in order to provide relevant characterization

,
26
,
27
]
.

As mentioned above, the accuracy of the Bader charges depends strongly on the of the real-space electronic density which is directly linked to the accuracy of the wave-function solving the Schrödinger equation. However, challenges remain in solving the Schrödinger equation for materials. Indeed, to obtain the electronic wavefunction or electronic density by solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation, one has to usually approximate the Coulomb interaction between the electrons, since it is very computationally demanding. This approximation makes it difficult to correctly describe materials showing strong electronic correlation. It is expected that such materials deliver relevant industrial value
[
28
]
but methods like Kohn-Sham density functional theory (DFT)
[
29
,
30
]
are unable to correctly replicate their properties
[
31
]
since the exchange-correlation (XC) functional, describing the electron-electron interaction, is approximating the electronic interaction.
To increase the precision of electronic structure calculations based on DFT, one can extend the XC-functional by a Hubbard-term, i.e., DFT+U
[
32
,
33
]
or use many-body approaches
[
34
,
35
]
. Especially many-body calculations are computationally demanding when using methods like coupled-cluster
[
36
,
37
]
, configuration-interaction
[
38

[
39
,
40
]
to solve the many-body Hamiltonian. In order to reduce the computational cost one can solve the Schrödinger equation within a selected active space built with the orbitals that encapsulate the strong correlations.

Besides algorithms that purely utilize classical computers, there exist various algorithms that either fully run on a quantum computer or are hybrid algorithms that use both classical and quantum resources. Pure quantum algorithms like the quantum phase estimation
[
41
]
use quantum circuits with large gate counts even for small systems, reducing its usefulness for currently available quantum hardware. Variational quantum algorithms
[
42
]
like the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)
[
43

are hybrid algorithms that currently are commonly used quantum algorithms for solving electronic structure problems. The VQE algorithm was already successfully used to determine the ground state of molecules
[
44
]
, periodic systems
[
45
]
and materials
[
34
]
.

In this work, we compute Bader charges for various material systems by performing quantum many-body simulations with the VQE algorithm within an active space built from Kohn-Sham orbitals computed with Quantum ESPRESSO
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
. Moreover, a method is presented to compute Bader charges from electronic densities obtained from VQE optimized wavefunctions.
In the theory section we recall how the matrix elements of the many-body Hamiltonian are computed from the plane-waves based Kohn-Sham orbitals. From the VQE optimized many-body ground state we compute the charge density and from this the Bader charges on each atom. The results section presents the Bader charges obtained for MgH
2
supercells with different elements for the central atom, and various transition metal oxides where DFT calculations typically struggle due to strong correlations. We compare our results to Bader charges calculated with DFT and DFT+U to evaluated how the strong correlations described by our many-body method have an impact on the Bader charges. All many-body calculations are performed with our, openly available, software package Dopyqo
[
49
]
.

2.1
Density functional theory

DFT
[
29
]
is a reformulation of the many-body time-independent Schrödinger equation in terms of the electronic density. When performing DFT calculations one typically uses the Kohn-Sham (KS) implementation of DFT
[
30
]
where the, formally exact but unknown, exchange-correlation functional is approximated.
During a KS-DFT calculation in a plane-wave basis set representation, each KS orbital is expressed as

\braket{\bm{\mathrm{r}}|\psi^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{t}}=\psi_{t}^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},\,G\leq G_{\mathrm{cut}}}c^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}\,e^{i(\bm{\mathrm{k}}+\bm{\mathrm{G}})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\,,

(1)

where
\psi_{t}^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
is the
t
-th KS orbital at the
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point in real space. The sum runs over reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
with their length
G
being smaller than a cutoff
G_{\mathrm{cut}}
defined by an energy threshold
E_{\mathrm{cut}}=G^{2}_{\mathrm{cut}}/2
. In the following, every sum over reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
implicitly has the condition that
G\leq G_{\mathrm{cut}}
. The complex plane-wave coefficient
c^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}
is associated with the
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point on which the
t
-th KS orbital is defined. The
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point is a point in the Brillouin zone and, therefore, a vector in reciprocal space. Quantities like the electronic density are defined by an integration over the Brillouin zone. In practice, this integration is approximated by a sum over multiple
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-points, defined on a k-mesh, where a DFT calculation is performed at every
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point. In a self-consistent DFT calculation, the
c^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}
coefficients are optimized to reach the lowest total energy.
It is worth noting here that at the
\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}
-point, i.e.
\bm{\mathrm{k}}=\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
, the coefficients
c^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}
can be chosen to be real.

2.2
Many-body Hamiltonian and matrix elements

In the following, we present the Hamiltonian and its matrix elements as they can be found in the literature
[
52
,
50
,
51
,
53
,
54
]
. We calculate single-electron and two-electron matrix elements to construct a many-body Hamiltonian in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation using the KS orbitals computed from a prior DFT calculation. The single-electron matrix elements
h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tu}
comprise the kinetic energy of the electrons and the pseudopotential while the two-electron matrix elements
h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tuvw}
describe the exact coulomb interaction. Additionally, the nuclear interaction between all (infinitely many) nuclei and the interaction between each electron and its periodic images contribute constant energies, the nuclear repulsion energy
E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}
and the electron self-energy
E_{\mathrm{e\text{-}self}}
, respectively.
We consider the following electronic structure Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{elec}}
in second quantization:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\mathrm{elec}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{tu}h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tu}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{t}\hat{a}_{u}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tuvw}h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tuvw}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{t}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{u}\hat{a}_{v}\hat{a}_{w}

(2)

\displaystyle+E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}+E_{\mathrm{e\text{-}self}}\,

where
h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tu}
and
h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tuvw}
are the single- and two-electron matrix elements at the
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point, respectively. The indices
t
,
u
,
v
, and
w
denote the indices of the KS band at the
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point. We define the KS state corresponding to the
t
-th KS band at the
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point as

\ket{\psi^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{t}}=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}}c^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{k}}+\bm{\mathrm{G}}}\,,

(3)

which is expanded in a plane-wave basis set using complex coefficients
c^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}
and states
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{k}}+\bm{\mathrm{G}}}
where
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
is a reciprocal lattice vector. Since all quantities can easily defined for arbitrary
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-points, we will omit the
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
-point in the following for the sake of clarity, i.e.,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{t}}

\displaystyle\to\ket{\psi_{t}}\,,

(4)

\displaystyle c^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}

\displaystyle\to c_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}\,,

\displaystyle\ket{\bm{\mathrm{k}}+\bm{\mathrm{G}}}

\displaystyle\to\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}}\,,

\displaystyle h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tu}

\displaystyle\to h_{tu}\,,

\displaystyle h^{(\bm{\mathrm{k}})}_{tuvw}

\displaystyle\to h_{tuvw}\,.

Every matrix element in the KS basis can be written as a weighted sum of matrix elements in the plane-wave basis
[
52
]
. For single-electron matrix elements this means

A_{tu}=\bra{\psi_{t}}\hat{A}\ket{\psi_{u}}=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}c^{\ast}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1},t}c_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2},u}\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{A}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}\,.

(5)

We note that
h_{tu}
and
h_{tuvw}
have several symmetries in general and
h_{tuvw}
has additional symmetries if the wavefunctions
\psi_{t}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
are real
[
52
]
.
The symmetries reduce the number of matrix elements that have to be explicitly calculated. We list all relevant symmetries in Appendix
A
.

Periodicity of the matrix elements

Let

be a cell-periodic function with

is a lattice translation vector. Then its Fourier transform

55
,
52
]

\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\frac{1}{V}\int_{C}f(\bm{\mathrm{r}})e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}\,,

(6)

where
V
is the volume of the computational cell
C
and
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
is a reciprocal lattice vector. So,
\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathrm{p}})=0
if
\bm{\mathrm{p}}
is not a reciprocal lattice vector. We note that for calculating Eq. (
6

inside the computational cell. All potentials used in Sections

,
2.2.3
, and
2.3
are cell-periodic potentials. There, we build a cell-periodic potential

from a non-periodic potential

. The Fourier transform of this cell-periodic potential is

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{(\bm{\mathrm{T}})}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\int_{C}V^{(\bm{\mathrm{T}})}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\,e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\,e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}\,.

For clarity we will only define the non-periodic potential
V(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
, although the actual potential the electrons are experiencing is
V^{(\bm{\mathrm{T}})}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
. We then use Eq. (
7
) to reflect the cell-periodicity in reciprocal space. The periodic potential
V^{(\bm{\mathrm{T}})}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
can be obtained from
\tilde{V}^{(\bm{\mathrm{T}})}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
with
[

52

V^{(\bm{\mathrm{T}})}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}}\tilde{V}^{(\bm{\mathrm{T}})}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})e^{i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\,.

(8)

2.2.2
Single-electron matrix elements

We compute matrix elements
h_{tu}
in the KS basis, i.e.,

h_{tu}=\bra{\psi_{t}}\hat{T}+\hat{V}_{\mathrm{pp}}\ket{\psi_{u}}\,,

(9)

where
\hat{T}
and
\hat{V}_{\mathrm{pp}}
are the kinetic energy and pseudopotential operators, respectively. The kinetic energy operator in the KS basis reads

\bra{\psi_{t}}\hat{T}\ket{\psi_{u}}=\frac{1}{2}\bra{\psi_{t}}\hat{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}^{2}\ket{\psi_{u}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}}c^{\ast}_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},t}c_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},u}\,\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{2}\,,

(10)

where we used that the momentum operator
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}
is diagonal in the plane-wave basis, i.e.,
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{\bm{\mathrm{p}}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}=\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}\,\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}
where
\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}}
is the Kronecker-delta.

To calculate the matrix elements
\bra{\psi_{t}}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{pp}}\ket{\psi_{u}}
we focus on norm-conserving pseudopotentials in a non-local Kleinman-Bylander form
[
53
]
.
Then

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\mathrm{pp}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\left[\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{nl}}\right]

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\left[\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\sum_{ijlm}D^{(I)}_{ij}\ket{\beta^{(I)}_{ilm}}\bra{\beta^{(I)}_{jlm}}\right]\,,

consists of a local (
\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}
) and a non-local (
\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{nl}}
) part for each nuclei
I
. The states
\ket{\beta^{(I)}_{ilm}}
are eigenstates of the angular momentum operator.

The local part
\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is given as a radial-dependent real-space function
V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)=\bra{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}
, with its Fourier-transform being

\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}
. Note that
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}}=V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)\,\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})
, since
\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is a local potential.
Introducing an electronic background potential to treat the singularity at
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}
and subtracting
-Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}(r)/r
in real-space and adding it again in reciprocal space, yields

\displaystyle\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{V}e^{-i(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}}

(12)

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[-Z_{I}\frac{e^{-\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right)^{2}/4}}{\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\left(V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)+\frac{Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}(r)}{r}\right)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\sin\left(\left|\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right|r\right)}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right|}\mathrm{d}r\Bigg]\,,

where
V
is the volume of the computational cell in real space.
Z_{I}
and
\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}
are the charge and the position of the
I
-th nuclei, respectively. The charges
Z_{I}
are most often effective core charges since the pseudopotential potentially absorbs some core electrons.
The zero-momentum part of
V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)
equals the zero-momentum part of its non-Coulombic part which is

\left.\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}\right|_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}=\frac{4\pi}{V}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!r^{2}\left(V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)+\frac{Z_{I}}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}r\,,

(13)

since the Coulombic part cancels with the electronic background. See Appendix
B
for derivations of Equations (
12
) and (
13
).

In the non-local part
\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{nl}}
of the pseudopotential, the states
\ket{\beta^{(I)}_{ilm}}
are short-ranged in real space:

\braket{\bm{\mathrm{r}}|\beta^{(I)}_{ilm}}=\beta^{(I)}_{ilm}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\beta^{(I)}_{il}(r)\,Y_{lm}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\,,

(14)

with the spherical harmonics
Y_{lm}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
. Using the Rayleigh expansion of plane-waves, the orthonormality of the spherical harmonics, and defining

F^{(I)}_{il}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\beta^{(I)}_{il}(r)j_{l}(xr)\mathrm{d}r\,,

(15)

where
j_{l}(x)
are the spherical Bessel functions, yields

\displaystyle\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{nl}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(4\pi\right)^{2}}{V}e^{-i(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}}

(16)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{ijlm}D^{(I)}_{ij}Y_{lm}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1})Y^{\ast}_{lm}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2})

\displaystyle\times F^{(I)}_{il}(G_{1})F^{(I)}_{jl}(G_{2})\,.

Wo apply Eq. (
5
) to Eq. (
12
), Eq. (
13
), and Eq. (
16
) to obtain
\bra{\psi_{t}}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{pp}}\ket{\psi_{u}}

2.2.3
Nuclear interaction

The interaction between the nuclei constitutes a constant nuclear repulsion energy
E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}
in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation and can be written as

E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I,J}^{N}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\frac{Z_{I}Z_{J}}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{R_{I}}}-\bm{\mathrm{R_{J}}}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|}\,,

(17)

where we sum over all lattice translation vectors
\bm{\mathrm{T}}
and the prime in the summation indicates that we omit self-interaction terms with
\bm{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{0}
and
I=J

[
56
]
.
E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}
converges slowly when calculated with Equation (
17
) but converges rapidly when using the Ewald summation technique
[
57
]
. Ewald summation splits Eq. (
17
) into a short- and a long-range term which are calculated in real- and reciprocal space, respectively. Then, a self-energy and a charged-system term have to be added to respect the primed summation in Eq. (
17
) and the correct zero-momentum term, respectively. Then

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I,J,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}Z_{I}Z_{J}\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(\left|\bm{\mathrm{R_{I}}}-\bm{\mathrm{R_{J}}}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|\sqrt{\sigma})}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{R_{I}}}-\bm{\mathrm{R_{J}}}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|}

(18)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\pi}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{0}}}\sum_{I,J}\frac{Z_{I}Z_{J}}{G^{2}}e^{i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\bm{\mathrm{R_{I}}}-\bm{\mathrm{R_{J}}})-\frac{1}{4\sigma}G^{2}}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\pi}}\sum_{I}Z_{I}^{2}-\frac{\pi}{2V\sigma}\left(\sum_{I}Z_{I}\right)^{2}\,,

where
\sigma>0
partitions Eq. (
17
) into real and reciprocal space sums, and can be chosen such that the sums converge rapidly in terms of lattice translation vectors
\bm{\mathrm{T}}
and reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
. In our calculations we choose
\sigma
such that
[
46
,
47
,
48
]

\sum_{I}2Z_{I}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\pi}}\,\mathrm{erfc}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\left(4G_{\mathrm{cut}}\right)^{2}}{4\sigma}}\right)\leq 10^{-7}\,,

(19)

where
G_{\mathrm{cut}}
is the cutoff energy from Eq. (
1
). A derivation of Eq. (
18
) can be found in Appendix
C
.

2.3
Two-electron matrix elements and electron self-energy

The potential
V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})

\bm{\mathrm{r}}
generated by an electronic point charge at
\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}

V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\frac{1}{|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}|}-\Phi_{b}(r)\,,

(20)

where
\bm{\mathrm{T}}
is a lattice translation vector,
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}
lie within the computational cell,
and
\Phi_{b}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
is the potential modelling the uniform cancelling background charge of the nuclei. We define
V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})
only where
\bm{\mathrm{r}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}
. Equation (
20
) can be written as an Ewald sum and its Fourier transform
\tilde{V}_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
is
[
54
]

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})e^{i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}\right)}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}

(21)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}4\pi/\left(VG^{2}\right),&\text{if }G\neq 0\\
0,&\text{if }G=0\end{cases}\,,

where
V
is the volume of the computational cell,
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
is a reciprocal lattice vector, and the potential
\Phi_{b}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
sets
V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{0}})
to zero
[
54
]
. Note that in the definition of
V_{\text{e-e}}
we removed the electron self-interaction
E_{\mathrm{e\text{-}self}}
, describing the interaction between each electron and its periodic images, which contributes a constant energy and is added back to the Hamiltonian
[
54
]

E_{\mathrm{e\text{-}self}}=N_{e}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}\neq\bm{0}}\frac{1}{|\bm{\mathrm{T}}|}\,,

(22)

where the term with
\bm{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{0}
is unphysical and excluded from the summation.
E_{\mathrm{e\text{-}self}}
can be calculated using an Ewald summation (cf. Eq. (
18
)). We can now define the two-electron matrix elements as

\displaystyle h_{tuvw}=

\displaystyle\int\!\!\!\int\psi^{\ast}_{t}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1})\,\psi^{\ast}_{u}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2})\,V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2})

(23)

\displaystyle\times\psi_{v}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2})\,\psi_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2}\,.

Now we define
\rho_{uv}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
as the Fourier transform of pair-densities
\rho_{uv}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\psi^{\ast}_{u}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\psi_{v}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
which is equivalent to a convolution in reciprocal space, i.e.,
\tilde{\rho}_{uv}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\tilde{\psi}^{\ast}_{u}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})*\tilde{\psi}_{v}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
, where
*
is the convolution operation.
We note that the Fourier transform of the pair density satisfies
\tilde{\rho}_{il}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\tilde{\rho}^{\ast}_{li}(-\bm{\mathrm{G}})
.
Using Eq. (
21
) we find

h_{tuvw}=\frac{4\pi}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}},\bm{\mathrm{G}}\neq\bm{0}}\frac{\tilde{\rho}^{\ast}_{tw}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})\tilde{\rho}_{uv}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}{G^{2}}\,.

(24)

2.4
Frozen core approximation

We use the frozen core approximation
[
50
,
51
]
to perform active space calculations involving only some of the electrons in a limited number of orbitals. The complete system involving all electrons is split into two parts, an active space and an environment. Only electrons in the active space are simulated explicitly while all other electrons in the environment are modelled by modifying the matrix elements of the Hamiltonian. This reduces the computational effort since the number of electronic configurations now depends only on the size of the active space.

In the frozen core approximation the orbitals in the environment are assumed to be fully-occupied and electrons cannot be transferred between the environment and the active space. This results in an energy offset, the frozen core energy
E_{\mathrm{frozen}}
, and an effective single-electron potential with matrix elements
g_{tu}
. In the following we use the indices
\{a,b\}
for orbitals in the environment and
\{t,u,v,w\}
for orbitals in the active space. The frozen core energy equals

E_{\mathrm{frozen}}=2\sum_{a}h_{aa}+\sum_{ab}\left(2h_{abba}-h_{abab}\right)\,,

(25)

where we
assume that the spin-orbitals are identical for both spins, and
a
and
b
run over all fully-occupied spatial orbitals in the environment. The matrix elements of the effective single-electron potential equal

g_{tu}=\sum_{a}\left(2h_{taau}-h_{taua}\right)\,,

(26)

where the
t
and
u
index spatial orbitals in the active space. See Appendix
D
for a derivation and a spin-resolved definition of the frozen core approximation.

With this the active space Hamiltonian within the frozen core approximation in second quantization reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{elec}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{tu}\left(h_{tu}+g_{tu}\right)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{t}\hat{a}_{u}

(27)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tuvw}h_{tuvw}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{t}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{u}\hat{a}_{v}\hat{a}_{w}

\displaystyle+E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}+E_{\mathrm{e\text{-}self}}+E_{\mathrm{frozen}}\,.

3
Variational quantum eigensolver

The VQE
[
43
]
is a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm that utilizes a quantum computer to prepare a parameterized quantum state which is classically optimized with regard to its energy w.r.t to a Hamiltonian. The state preparation is implemented as a unitary operation, often called ansatz, acting on qubits of the quantum computer.
We use a VQE to find the ground state of the Hamiltonian defined in Eq. (
27
).

We use the VQE in combination with a particle-number preserving UCCSD ansatz
[
58
,
59
]
to prepare a parameterized quantum state. The UCCSD ansatz
\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})
is composed of fermionic single excitations

\hat{G}^{p}_{i}=i\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p}\hat{a}_{i}-\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\,,

(28)

and fermionic double excitations

\hat{G}^{pq}_{ij}=i\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{q}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{a}_{i}-\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\,.

(29)

A first-order Trotter-Suzuki
[
60
,
61
]
decomposition yields

\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})=\prod_{p,i}\exp\left(-i\theta^{p}_{i}\hat{G}^{p}_{i}\right)\prod_{p,q,i,j}\exp\left(-i\theta^{pq}_{ij}\hat{G}^{pq}_{ij}\right)\,,

(30)

where the indices
i
,
j
and
p
,
q
go over all occupied and virtual orbitals, respectively. We note that
\hat{U}(\bm{\theta})
is applied in the quantum circuit after a reference determinant is prepared. In this work, we use as reference determinant the Slater determinant where the first
N
active orbitals are occupied and the rest are unoccupied, where
N
is the number of active electrons. We note that this is not a Hartree-Fock state since no Hartree-Fock calculation was performed.

4
Bader charges

Bader charges are commonly used in theoretical and experimental chemistry to obtain chemical insight on particular physical behavior of materials. They have been introduced first by Richard Bader
[
13
]
to quantify charges in molecules. The method is based on the discretization of the electronic charge density, making it independent of the chosen basis set. Thus, Bader charge analysis is equally defined for molecules described with atomic orbitals as for periodic cells described with plane-waves. The accuracy of the Bader charges depends only on the precision of the electronic structure calculation which in turn relies on the accuracy of the basis set.

The Bader method is solely based on the charge density
\rho(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
which is a central quantity in DFT and can be calculated from a many-body wavefunction. To compute Bader charges, first, the real-space is divided into Bader regions bound by zero-flux surfaces running through minima of the charge density. Integrating the charge density within one Bader region yields the corresponding Bader charge of that region. If a Bader region encapsules one nucleus the Bader charge of that region is assigned to the atom of that nucleus.
The charge densities in our calculations are defined on a grid. To obtain Bader charges from such a charge density, specialized algorithms exist that carefully determine and assign each grid point to a Bader region
[
62
,
16
,
63
,
64
]
.
The Bader charge
Q_{I}
associated with the
I
-th atom reads

Q_{I}=\int_{B_{I}}\rho(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\bm{\mathrm{r}}\,,

(31)

where we integrate the real-space charge density
\rho(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
over the Bader region
B_{I}
.
In DFT, the charge density is expressed as

\rho(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\sum_{t}f_{t}\left|\psi_{t}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\right|^{2}\,,

(32)

where
f_{t}
is the occupancy of the
t
-th KS orbital
\psi_{t}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
. When performing a many-body calculation, we define the occupancy
f_{t}^{\mathrm{MB}}
of the
t
-th KS orbital as

f_{t}^{\mathrm{MB}}=\bra{\Psi_{\mathrm{MB}}}\hat{n}_{t}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{MB}}}\,,

(33)

where
\hat{n}_{t}=a^{\dagger}_{t}a_{t}
is the occupation number operator acting on the
t
-th orbital. When running a quantum simulation using VQE and using the Jordan-Wigner fermion-to-qubit mapping
[
65
]
we can obtain the occupancy
f_{t}^{\mathrm{VQE}}
with

f_{t}^{\mathrm{VQE}}=\frac{1}{2}\bra{\Psi_{\mathrm{VQE}}}\hat{Z}_{t}-\hat{I}_{t}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{VQE}}}\,,

(34)

where
\hat{I}_{t}
and
\hat{Z}_{t}
are the identity and Pauli-Z operators acting on the qubit representing the
t
-th orbital, respectively.

6
Discussion

We present the computation of Bader charges using our many-body post-processing tool Dopyqo that builds and solves a many-body Hamiltonian from the results of a DFT calculation. We calculate BECs for various systems with both DFT and Dopyqo and compare the results to literature values
[
66
,
67
,
68
]
. The DFT calculations we compare to are done using DFT
[
66
]
or DFT+U
[
67
,
68
]
using PAW pseudopotentials and k-meshes while all our results are based on norm-conversing pseudopotentials and
\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}
-point calculations.

For the
2\times 2\times 2
MgH
2
supercells, the post-processing with Dopyqo has no significant impact on the BEC of the central atom. These systems have been extensively studied with standard DFT in the literature and we considered them as being weakly correlated
[
66
,
81
,
82
,
83
]
. Thus, no improvement on the BECs is expected which fits our results. We can therefore assess our software as being accurate in the calculation of BECs.

Our results on the BECs of
\mathrm{TMO}_{2}
show significant differences between the compared methods. For the two systems TiO
2
and MoO
2
, we already obtain DFT-NC BECs within the range of the reference values obtained with DFT+U method. In this case, using Dopyqo does only yield slightly different BECs. However, in all remaining tested
\mathrm{TMO}_{2}
and
\mathrm{TMS}_{2}
(TiS
2
, RuO
2
, RhO
2
, CrO
2
), Dopyqo is able to significantly increase the BECs from their DFT-NC values towards the DFT+U reference values. From this we see that our Dopyqo calculations are able to capture the strong correlations in the tested systems while we attribute the remaining differences to the references to the lack of using k-meshes and the usage of norm-conserving pseudopotentials.

By comparing the BECs from Dopyqo to reference DFT+U data we evaluate how much our many-body approach in combination with VQE can improve the BECs from DFT for strongly correlated systems where DFT+U is used to obtain a more accurate description. As a general conclusion, the spin-unpolarized electronic structure calculations at the
\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}
-point with Dopyqo compete with the results of the spin-polarized DFT+U calculations at a k-mesh level by improving the BECs at the DFT-NC level toward the BECs from the references. Although we perform a generally computationally expensive many-body calculation on top of DFT, our method shows noteworthy advantages compared to DFT+U. First of all, DFT+U calculations are generally time consuming and cumbersome because the Hubbard U parameter has to be tuned carefully to either fit experimental data or by using computationally costly methods based on e.g. linear-response
[
84
]
. Further, the U value is not easily transferable between systems since it depends heavily on local stoichiometry and structure
[
85
]
. Also, one has to carefully investigate on which orbitals the Hubbard correction has to be applied, since sometimes also
s
and
p
orbitals contribute to the strongly correlated nature of a system
[
86
]
.
In contrast, our workflow is very straightforward: We perform a spin-unpolarized DFT calculation at the
\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}
-point and apply our post-processing code within a selected active space to solve a many-body problem. We do not have to tune any parameters in the Hamiltonian and obtain BECs that come close to the DFT+U references. Additionally, our method can easily be used in combination with quantum resources while DFT is optimized for classical computers although some works propose the use of quantum computers for DFT calculations
[
87
,
88
]
. Further, our workflow can readily extended to take a spin-polarized k-mesh calculation as input. Indeed, since we only use the KS orbitals and information about the crystal structure to build our many-body Hamiltonian, we could as well use the KS orbitals of any DFT calculation as input for our code. We leave the investigations, to use DFT calculations based on different pseudopotentials and spin-polarized k-meshes as input to our code, as future work.

V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)=\bra{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}
, with its Fourier-transform being
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}
. We now derive Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
). To handle the singularity at
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}
, we use

V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)\to V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)+\frac{Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}(r)}{r}-\frac{Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}(r)}{r}-\Phi^{(I)}_{b}(r)\,,

(37)

where, on the right hand side, the first two terms together are short-ranged and, therefore, non-singular for
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}
. The electronic charges in the background, which are keeping the computational cell charge neutral, make the remaining two terms also non-singular. The potential
\Phi^{(I)}_{b}(r)
is modelling this electronic background for the
I
-th nucleus and solves the Poisson equation for a constant charge density
\rho^{(I)}_{b}
:

\nabla^{2}\Phi^{(I)}_{b}(r)=-\rho^{(I)}_{b}=+\frac{Z_{I}}{V}\,,

(38)

and the Fourier transform of
\Phi^{(I)}_{b}(r)
is

\tilde{\Phi}^{(I)}_{b}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=-\frac{4\pi Z_{I}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}{VG^{2}}\,,

(39)

where
\delta(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
is the Dirac delta distribution.
Alternatively, in Eq. (
37
) one could also add and subtract
\frac{Z_{I}}{r}
, or any function that behaves like
\frac{Z_{I}}{r}
for large
r
. We use the error function since
\mathrm{erf}(r)/r
is non-singular for
r\to 0
. The error function
\mathrm{erf}(r)
is defined as

\mathrm{erf}(r)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{r}e^{-x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\,.

(40)

The Fourier transform of the error function is found to be

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{erf}(r)\,e^{-iGr}\,\mathrm{d}r=-2i\,\frac{e^{-G^{2}/4}}{G}\,.

(41)

From this, using Eq. (
7
) and recognizing that
\mathrm{erf}(r)\sin(Gr)
is an even function, follows

\frac{1}{V}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mathrm{erf}(r)}{r}\,e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}=\frac{4\pi}{V}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{erf}(r)\,\frac{\sin(Gr)}{G}\,\mathrm{d}r=-\frac{4\pi}{V}\frac{1}{2G}\Im\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{erf}(r)\,e^{-iGr}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)=\frac{4\pi}{V}\frac{e^{-G^{2}/4}}{G^{2}}\,,

(42)

where
\Im(x)
is the imaginary part of
x
. With this and using the spherical symmetry of
V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)
we find

\displaystyle\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)+\frac{Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}(r)}{r}\right)\,e^{-i\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right)\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\frac{4\pi Z_{I}}{V}\frac{e^{-\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right)^{2}/4}}{\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right)^{2}}-\tilde{\Phi}^{(I)}_{b}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2})

(43)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{V}\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\left(V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)+\frac{Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}(r)}{r}\right)\,\frac{\sin\left(\left|\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right|r\right)}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right|}\mathrm{d}r-\frac{4\pi Z_{I}}{V}\frac{e^{-\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right)^{2}/4}}{\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}\right)^{2}}-\tilde{\Phi}^{(I)}_{b}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2})\,,

where we used
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}}=V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)\,\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime})
and
x=|\bm{\mathrm{x}}|
. Equation (
43
) is non-singular for
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}
and can be used to calculate all matrix elements
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}
. The expression for
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}
of Eq. (
43
) is

\left.\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}\right|_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}=\frac{4\pi}{V}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\,\left(V^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}(r)+\frac{Z_{I}}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}r\,,

(44)

where we used Eq. (
42
) and
\mathrm{erf}(r)=1-\mathrm{erfc}(r)
with the complementary error function
\mathrm{erfc}(r)
. This result can also be obtained when using
\frac{Z_{I}}{r}
instead of
\frac{Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}(r)}{r}
in Eq. (
37
).
In practice we use Eq. (
44
) to compute all matrix elements
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}
where
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}
and use Eq. (
43
) to compute the remaining matrix elements. We note that Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
) are only valid for
\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
, for
\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}\neq\bm{\mathrm{0}}
we need to apply the Fourier shift theorem such that we perform

\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}\to e^{-i(\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}}\bra{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1}}\hat{V}^{(I)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2}}\,.

(45)

Appendix C
Nuclear interaction derivation

We use the Ewald method
[
57
]
to derive Eq. (
18
). For this we first define a charge distribution
\rho_{[I]}(r)
for all
N
nuclei in the computational cell except the
I
-th one,

\rho_{[I]}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\sum_{J=1}^{N}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}Z_{J}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{J}-\bm{\mathrm{T}})-\rho_{b}=\sum_{J=1}^{N}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\left[Z_{J}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{J}-\bm{\mathrm{T}})-\rho^{G}_{J,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\right]-\rho^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{0}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})+\sum_{J=1}^{N}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\rho^{G}_{J,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})+\rho_{b}\,,

(46)

where we sum over all lattice translation vectors
\bm{\mathrm{T}}
and the prime in the summation indicates that we omit self-interaction terms with
\bm{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{0}
and
I=J
.
\rho_{b}
is a constant density modelling the electronic background with

\rho_{b}=-\sum_{I}\frac{Z_{I}}{V}\,.

(47)

We added and subtracted Gaussian charge distributions with

\rho^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\left(\frac{\sigma}{\pi}\right)^{3/2}Z_{I}\,e^{\sigma\left(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}}\,,

(48)

where
\sigma>0
. Now we employ the Ewald method to calculate the electrostatic potential generated by the first two terms in Eq. (
46
) in real space and evaluate the remaining two terms in reciprocal space. The result will be independent of the parameter
\sigma
, but in practice
\sigma
can be chosen such that the real and reciprocal parts converge fastest.

Let’s begin with the real space part. To obtain the electrostatic potential
\phi^{R}_{[I]}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
we solve the Poisson equation

\nabla^{2}\phi^{R}_{[I]}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=-\left\{\sum_{J=1}^{N}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\left[Z_{J}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{J}-\bm{\mathrm{T}})-\rho^{G}_{J,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\right]-\rho^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{0}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\right\}\,.

(49)

The electrostatic potential
\phi^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
for one Gaussian charge distribution
\rho^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
is

\phi^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=Z_{I}\,\frac{\mathrm{erf}\left(\sqrt{\sigma}\left|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|\right)}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|}\,,

(50)

with the error function
\mathrm{erf}(x)
, while the electrostatic potential
\phi^{\delta}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
of a point charge
Z_{I}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}-\bm{\mathrm{T}})
is given by

\phi^{\delta}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\frac{Z_{I}}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|}\,.

(51)

With this we find

\phi^{R}_{[I]}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\sum_{J=1}^{N}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\frac{Z_{J}\,\mathrm{erfc}\left(\sqrt{\sigma}\left|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{J}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|\right)}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{J}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|}-\frac{Z_{I}\,\mathrm{erf}\left(\sqrt{\sigma}\left|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}\right|\right)}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}\right|}\,,

(52)

where we used the complementary error function
\mathrm{erfc}(x)=1-\mathrm{erf}(x)
.

To compute the reciprocal space part we solve the Poisson equation in reciprocal space:

\tilde{\phi}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\frac{4\pi\tilde{\rho}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})}{G^{2}}\,

(53)

where
\tilde{\phi}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
and
\tilde{\rho}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
are the electrostatic potential and charge density in reciprocal space, respectively. We first Fourier transform
\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\rho^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
using Eq. (
7
) by integrating over the computational cell
C
:

\tilde{\rho}^{G}_{I}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\frac{1}{V}\int_{C}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{T}}}\rho^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\,e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}=\frac{Z_{I}}{V}\,e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}-G^{2}/(4\sigma)}\,.

(54)

With the Fourier transform of the electronic background
\rho_{b}
being
\rho_{b}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{G}})/V
where
\delta(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
is the Dirac delta distribution, we obtain the electrostatic potential

\tilde{\phi}^{G}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\frac{4\pi}{VG^{2}}\left(\sum_{I}\tilde{\rho}^{G}_{I,\bm{\mathrm{T}}}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})+\rho_{b}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{G}})\right)\,.

(55)

Fourier transforming
\tilde{\phi}^{G}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})
back into real space and carefully handling the
\bm{\mathrm{G}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
term, using the Taylor series of
e^{-G^{2}/(4\sigma)}
, yields
\phi^{G}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
with

\phi^{G}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\sum_{I}\frac{4\pi Z_{I}}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}\neq 0}\frac{1}{G^{2}}\,e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I}-G^{2}/(4\sigma)}e^{i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}}}-\frac{\pi}{\sigma V}\sum_{I}Z_{I}\,.

(56)

To now obtain the result from Eq. (
18
), we calculate the electrostatic energy
E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}
with

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{n\text{-}n}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}Z_{I}\left(\phi^{R}_{[I]}(\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I})+\phi^{G}(\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{I})\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I,J,\bm{T}}Z_{I}Z_{J}\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{\sigma}\left|\bm{\mathrm{R_{I}}}-\bm{\mathrm{R_{J}}}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|)}{\left|\bm{\mathrm{R_{I}}}-\bm{\mathrm{R_{J}}}-\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right|}-\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\pi}}\sum_{I}Z_{I}^{2}

(57)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\pi}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}\neq\bm{\mathrm{0}}}\sum_{I,J}\frac{Z_{I}Z_{J}}{G^{2}}e^{i\bm{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\bm{\mathrm{R_{I}}}-\bm{\mathrm{R_{J}}})-G^{2}/(4\sigma)}-\frac{\pi}{2\sigma V}\left(\sum_{I}Z_{I}\right)^{2}\,,

where we used that
\lim_{x\to 0}\,\mathrm{erf}(ax)/x=2a/\sqrt{\pi}
.

Appendix D
Spin-resolved frozen core approximation

In this section we derive the spin-polarized version the frozen core energy and potential defined which can be used to obtain Eq. (
25
) and Eq. (
26
).
In the following we use the indices
\{a,b,c,d\}
for orbitals in the environment,
\{t,u,v,w\}
for orbitals in the active space and
\{i,j,k,l\}
for general orbitals. These indices runs over spatial orbitals. We use
\sigma
and
\tau
as indices for the two different spin channels, i.e.,
\sigma,\tau\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
. We divide a state
\ket{\psi}
into a environment (
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{env}}}
) and active space (
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{active}}}
) part where the environment is assumed to be fully occupied:

\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{env}}}\otimes\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{active}}}\,.

(58)

We explicitly consider spin in the operators and the matrix elements, i.e.,

\begin{gathered}a^{(\dagger)}_{i}\to a^{(\dagger)}_{i,\sigma}\,,\\
h_{ij}\to h_{ij,\sigma}\,,\\
h_{ijkl}\to h_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}=\int\int\psi^{\ast}_{t}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1},\sigma)\,\psi^{\ast}_{u}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2},\tau)\,V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2})\,\psi_{v}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2},\tau)\,\psi_{w}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1},\sigma)\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2}\,,\end{gathered}

(59)

with
V_{\text{e-e}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{2})
defined in Eq. (
20
). We use that the eigenvalues of
\hat{n}_{i,\sigma}=a^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}a_{i,\sigma}
w.r.t. the state
\ket{\psi}
are
0
and
1
, where
\hat{n}_{i,\sigma}
is the occupation number operator acting on spin orbitals. With this we note that
a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a_{a,\sigma}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{env}}}=1\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{env}}}
since all environment spin orbitals are fully occupied with occupation
1
.
Further, we will use the following commutation and anticommutation relations:

\begin{gathered}\{a_{i,\sigma},a^{\dagger}_{j,\tau}\}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{\sigma,\tau}\,,\\
\left[a^{(\dagger)}_{i,\sigma},a^{\dagger}_{j,\tau}a_{j,\tau}\right]=0\quad\mathrm{if}\quad i\neq j\quad\mathrm{or}\quad\tau\neq\sigma\,.\end{gathered}

(60)

We proceed as follows. We will first split the single- and two-electron sums into multiple sums containing all possible combinations of environment and active orbitals. There we will omit the sums involving an odd number of environment indices since this would change the number of environment electrons which we want to keep constant. Further, we omit the terms where any creation operator acting in the environment space has no annihilation counterpart since this would also change the number of environment electrons.
At this point the Hamiltonian is decomposed such that we obtain terms that only act on
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{env}}}
and terms that only act on
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{active}}}
. The two-electron matrix elements
h_{tuvw}
will, additionally, yield mixing terms acting on both
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{env}}}
and
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{active}}}
.

Let’s start with the single-electron sum.

\displaystyle\sum_{ij,\sigma}h_{ij,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}a_{j,\sigma}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{tu,\sigma}h_{tu,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{u,\sigma}+\sum_{ab,\sigma}h_{ab,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a_{b,\sigma}\right)\ket{\psi}

(61)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{tu,\sigma}h_{tu,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{u,\sigma}+\sum_{ab,\sigma}h_{ab,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a_{b,\sigma}\,\delta_{ab}\right)\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{tu,\sigma}h_{tu,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{u,\sigma}+\sum_{a,\sigma}h_{aa,\sigma}\hat{n}_{a,\sigma}\right)\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{tu,\sigma}h_{tu,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{u,\sigma}\ket{\psi}+\sum_{a,\sigma}h_{aa,\sigma}\,,

where the Kronecker-delta
\delta_{ab}
was introduced to explicitly show that if
a\neq b
the operator
a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a_{b,\sigma}
acts as a zero operator since the environment orbitals are fully occupied. When the matrix elements are independent of the spin we obtain the result used in Equation (
27
).

Let’s move on to the two-electron matrix elements. We omit the factor
\frac{1}{2}
for brevity in the following and add it back at the end of the derivation.
We start with

\displaystyle\sum_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}h_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{j,\tau}a_{k,\tau}a_{l,\sigma}\ket{\psi}=\bigg[\sum_{\sigma\tau}\bigg(

\displaystyle\sum_{abcd}h_{abcd,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{b,\tau}a_{c,\tau}a_{d,\sigma}

(62)

\displaystyle+\sum_{tuvw}h_{tuvw,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{u,\tau}a_{v,\tau}a_{w,\sigma}

\displaystyle+\sum_{aucw}h_{aucw,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{u,\tau}a_{c,\tau}a_{w,\sigma}\,\delta_{ac}\delta_{\sigma\tau}

\displaystyle+\sum_{auvd}h_{auvd,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{u,\tau}a_{v,\tau}a_{d,\sigma}\,\delta_{ad}

\displaystyle+\sum_{tbcw}h_{tbcw,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{b,\tau}a_{c,\tau}a_{w,\sigma}\,\delta_{bc}

\displaystyle+\sum_{tbvd}h_{tbvd,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{b,\tau}a_{v,\tau}a_{d,\sigma}\,\delta_{bd}\delta_{\sigma\tau}\bigg)\bigg]\ket{\psi}\,,

where we introduced the Kronecker-deltas to keep the number of environment electrons constant. Now we apply the Kronecker-deltas for all terms and use the relations from Eq. (
60
) on the third and sixth terms. Further we use that
\hat{n}_{a,\sigma}
acts like a scalar
1
on our state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{env}}\psi_{\mathrm{active}}}
. We find

\displaystyle\sum_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}h_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{j\tau}a_{k,\tau}a_{l,\sigma}\ket{\psi}=\bigg(

\displaystyle\sum_{abcd,\sigma\tau}h_{abcd,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{b,\tau}a_{c,\tau}a_{d,\sigma}

(63)

\displaystyle+\sum_{tuvw,\sigma\tau}h_{tuvw,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{u,\tau}a_{v,\tau}a_{w,\sigma}

\displaystyle-\sum_{auw,\sigma}h_{auaw,\sigma\sigma}\,a^{\dagger}_{u,\sigma}a_{w,\sigma}

\displaystyle+\sum_{auv,\sigma\tau}h_{auva,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{u,\tau}a_{v,\tau}

\displaystyle+\sum_{tbw,\sigma\tau}h_{tbbw,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{w,\sigma}

\displaystyle-\sum_{tbv,\sigma}h_{tbvb,\sigma\sigma}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{v,\sigma}\bigg)\ket{\psi}\,.

Applying the swap symmetry (see Eq. (
35
)) to the third and fifth term, and combining same terms yields

\displaystyle\sum_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}h_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{j\tau}a_{k,\tau}a_{l,\sigma}\ket{\psi}=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\sum_{abcd,\sigma\tau}h_{abcd,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{b,\tau}a_{c,\tau}a_{d,\sigma}

(64)

\displaystyle+\sum_{tuvw,\sigma\tau}h_{tuvw,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{u,\tau}a_{v,\tau}a_{w,\sigma}

\displaystyle+2\sum_{btu,\sigma}\left[\left(\sum_{\tau}h_{tbbu,\sigma\tau}\right)-h_{tbub,\sigma\sigma}\right]\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{u,\sigma}\Bigg\}\ket{\psi}\,.

Now we need to further investigate the first term. The indices
a,b,c,d
cannot be all different since we want to keep the number of environment electrons constant. This means that the creation operators need to create the electrons that have been annihilated by the two annihilation operators. This means that

a^{\dagger}_{a}a^{\dagger}_{b}a_{c}a_{d}\ket{\psi}=\left(a^{\dagger}_{d}a^{\dagger}_{c}a_{c}a_{d}\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}+a^{\dagger}_{c}a^{\dagger}_{d}a_{c}a_{d}\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}\right)\ket{\psi}\,,

(65)

and

\sum_{abcd,\sigma\tau}h_{abcd,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{a,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{b,\tau}a_{c,\tau}a_{d,\sigma}\ket{\psi}=\left(\sum_{ab,\sigma\tau}h_{abba,\sigma\tau}-\sum_{ab,\sigma}h_{abab,\sigma\sigma}\right)\ket{\psi}\,,

(66)

where we used the relations from Eq. (
60
) and that
\hat{n}_{a,\sigma}
acts like a scalar
1
on our state
\ket{\psi}
.
Combining all terms and adding back the prefactor of
\frac{1}{2}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl,\sigma\tau}h_{ijkl}\,a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{k}a_{l}\ket{\psi}=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{tuvw,\sigma\tau}h_{tuvw,\sigma\tau}\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{u,\tau}a_{v,\tau}a_{w,\sigma}

(67)

\displaystyle+\sum_{btu,\sigma}\left[\left(\sum_{\tau}h_{tbbu,\sigma\tau}\right)-h_{tbub,\sigma\sigma}\right]\,a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{u,\sigma}\Bigg\}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab,\sigma}\left[\left(\sum_{\tau}h_{abba,\sigma\tau}\right)-h_{abab,\sigma\sigma}\right]\,.

When not considering spin
a^{\dagger}_{t}a_{u}=\sum_{\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{t,\sigma}a_{u,\sigma}
and we obtain the result used in Eq. (
27
).
