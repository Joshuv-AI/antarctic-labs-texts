Inverse association between serum sLRP-1 levels and obstructive sleep apnea in snoring individuals: a case-control study based on polysomnography and machine learning models
Authors: Zeli Sun, Zhifang Lv, Qiong Huang, Haizhen Wu, Ling Wang, Guozheng Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common but underdiagnosed sleep disorder characterized by intermittent hypoxia and systemic inflammation. Soluble low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (sLRP-1) is a multifunctional receptor involved in lipid metabolism, inflammation, and vascular regulation, potentially linking metabolic and vascular pathways to OSA pathophysiology.Methods This case-control study included 180 adults presenting with snoring symptoms. Participants underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG) and were categorized into OSA (AHI ≥ 5/h, n = 100) and non-OSA (AHI < 5/h, n = 80) groups. Serum soluble low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (sLRP-1) levels were measured using ELISA. Multivariable logistic regression and subgroup analyses were conducted to assess associations. Machine learning models, including XGBoost, were developed to evaluate classification performance and identify important features associated with OSA.Results Serum sLRP-1 levels were significantly lower in the OSA group compared to the non-OSA group (P < 0.001). Logistic regression revealed that sLRP-1 was independently and inversely associated with OSA risk (fully adjusted OR = 0.35; 95% CI, 0.22–0.56). Subgroup analyses confirmed consistency across age and C-reactive protein (CRP) strata. Naive Bayes achieved an AUC of 0.94 in distinguishing OSA from non-OSA cases, with sLRP-1 ranked among the most influential features based on SHAP analysis.Conclusion Lower serum sLRP-1 levels were associated with OSA status in adults with snoring symptoms referred for polysomnography. Its contribution to internally validated machine-learning models suggests potential exploratory value, but external validation is required before clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11325-026-03761-5.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common yet frequently underrecognized sleep disorder characterized by repeated upper airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation [1]. These disruptions trigger a cascade of physiological responses—ranging from sympathetic nervous system activation to systemic inflammation and oxidative stress—that contribute to a broad spectrum of adverse health outcomes [2]. Over the past two decades, OSA has emerged not only as a sleep-related breathing disorder but also as an important contributor to cardiometabolic diseases, neurocognitive dysfunction, and even increased mortality risk [3, 4].

Despite the growing burden of OSA worldwide, the majority of individuals remain undiagnosed. The gold standard diagnostic tool—overnight polysomnography (PSG)—while accurate, is resource-intensive, time-consuming, and often impractical for widespread screening in general or high-risk populations [5, 6]. Alternative screening tools, including clinical questionnaires and portable monitoring devices, lack consistency in performance across different populations and settings [7, 8]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to identify simple, objective, and accessible biomarkers that can assist in early detection and risk stratification of OSA.

In recent years, there has been increasing interest in the systemic effects of OSA, particularly the inflammatory and metabolic alterations it induces [9, 10]. These systemic disturbances suggest that OSA is not merely a localized upper airway issue but a condition with far-reaching physiological consequences. Identifying circulating molecules that reflect these biological changes has become a focal point of biomarker research in sleep medicine.

Among the candidates attracting attention is soluble low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (sLRP-1), a multifunctional protein involved in lipid metabolism, inflammation modulation, and cellular signaling [11]. sLRP-1 has been implicated in a range of diseases that share common pathophysiological features with OSA, including atherosclerosis, insulin resistance, and neurodegeneration. Its role as a systemic modulator places it in a potentially strategic position in the cascade linking sleep-disordered breathing with chronic disease development [12–15]. However, little is known about how sLRP-1 behaves in the context of OSA, or whether it could serve as a meaningful biomarker for this condition.

Advances in computational methods, particularly machine learning, have opened new avenues for identifying complex, non-linear relationships among clinical and biochemical variables [16]. These tools have shown promise in enhancing the classification accuracy of models aimed at distinguishing individuals with and without OSA, especially when traditional linear methods are insufficient. Incorporating promising molecular biomarkers like sLRP-1 into such models may offer deeper insights into disease-related patterns and support the development of exploratory tools for risk stratification.

Thus, there is a growing rationale to explore the potential relationship between circulating sLRP-1 and OSA, particularly within frameworks that leverage both statistical and machine learning methodologies. Doing so may help bridge current diagnostic gaps and enrich our understanding of how systemic biomarkers can reflect—and possibly predict—the presence and severity of sleep-disordered breathing.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center, case-control study conducted in the Department of Respiratory Medicine, Anqing Municipal Hospital, between June 2022 and October 2023.

A total of 180 consecutive adult outpatients presenting with snoring symptoms were enrolled and underwent overnight polysomnography (PSG). According to the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), subjects were classified into the OSA group (AHI ≥ 5 events/h, n = 100) and the non-OSA control group (AHI < 5 events/h, n = 80). The control group therefore represented individuals with snoring complaints but normal PSG findings, reflecting a symptomatic referral population rather than an asymptomatic general screening population.

All participants were evaluated by the same investigator prior to PSG for demographic, anthropometric, and clinical data, including age, sex, BMI, smoking and alcohol history and comorbidities (hypertension, heart failure). Between-group comparability was assessed statistically, and multivariable models were adjusted for these potential confounders.

Inclusion criteria were:Age ≥ 18 years;Underwent overnight PSG and fasting blood sampling;For the OSA group, diagnosis met the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Obstructive Sleep Apnea–Hypopnea Syndrome (OSAHS) [17];No prior OSA treatment (continuous positive airway pressure, mandibular advancement devices, or upper airway surgery).Exclusion criteria were:History of malignancy within the past 10 years;Acute infection within 2 months;Autoimmune disorders;Acute exacerbation of chronic diseases (e.g., respiratory failure, or uncontrolled diabetes);Current statin use (atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin, pravastatin, pitavastatin, or fluvastatin);Other sleep disorders (central sleep apnea, insomnia, narcolepsy, or restless legs syndrome).All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anqing Municipal Hospital (Approval No. Medical Lun Audit [2022] No. 80).

Data collection

General clinical data were collected from all the included subjects in this study, and they underwent polysomnography (PSG) and laboratory related tests. General data including gender, age, height, weight, smoking and alcohol history, and hypertension comorbidity were collected by uniform criteria. All subjects received all-night PSG monitoring (effective sleep time > 7 h) operated by the same sleep monitoring technician, which included electroencephalogram, oculomotorgram, chin electromyogram, snoring, electrocardiogram, chest and abdominal respiratory movements, body position, and oxygen saturation, and also recorded pre-sleep and post-wake blood pressures. For laboratory tests, peripheral blood was drawn from all subjects in the early morning of the following day on an empty stomach, and routine hematologic parameters such as blood counts, lipid levels, fasting blood glucose, and C-reactive protein (CRP) were tested. Serum sLRP-1 concentrations were measured using a Human sLRP-1 ELISA kit (Shanghai Yansheng Industrial Co., Ltd., China; catalog no. YS-S8162; lot no. 202212 A). The assay dynamic range was 2–85 pg/mL. Serum samples were assayed with a 5-fold dilution according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and concentrations were calculated from the standard curve and reported in pg/mL, and all the operations were carried out in strict accordance with the instructions of the kits, and were completed by specialized laboratory technicians.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess the association between sLRP-1 and OSA. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, BMI), and Model 3 (adjusted for all covariates). Subgroup analyses were conducted by age (< 45 vs. ≥45) and CRP levels (< 3 vs. ≥3). Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was initially considered but was excluded from subgroup analysis due to small and uneven sample sizes across categories. To account for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and adjusted P-values are presented in Supplementary Materials.

For machine learning analyses, five supervised algorithms—logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), and naive Bayes—were implemented to develop diagnostic models for OSA. Each model was trained using fivefold cross-validation with balanced stratification. Grid search with minor manual tuning was used to optimize hyperparameters. To avoid data leakage, preprocessing steps dependent on data distribution (e.g., normalization) were fitted exclusively on the training folds and then applied to the test folds.

Model performance was evaluated by AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Calibration curves were plotted to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Feature interpretability was examined using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to identify predictors contributing most to OSA classification, with feature stability across folds assessed by coefficient of variation (CV) and Spearman rank correlation. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and analysed by Python (version 3.9) .

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Baseline characteristics of the 180 participants (OSA group: n = 100, non-OSA group: n = 80) are presented in Table 1 and Table S1. Patients in the OSA group had significantly higher BMI [27.7 (24.8–30.1) vs. 24.4 (22.8–26.0) kg/m², P < 0.001], higher NLR [2.6 (1.8–3.1) vs. 1.7 (1.4–2.0), P < 0.001], and lower HDL cholesterol levels [1.2 (0.9–1.4) vs. 1.4 (1.1–1.6) mmol/L, P < 0.001] compared to the non-OSA group. No significant differences were found between groups regarding age, sex, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, or uric acid levels. Furthermore, OSA patients demonstrated significantly lower serum sLRP-1 levels compared to controls [6.5 (5.0–7.5) vs. 11.0 (9.0–12.4) pg/ml, P < 0.001].

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression was conducted to further evaluate the independent association between sLRP-1 levels and OSA (Table 2). Three regression models were progressively established: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, BMI); Model 3 fully adjusted for all relevant covariates. Across all models, sLRP-1 remained consistently and significantly inversely associated with OSA risk. Specifically, in the fully adjusted Model 3, each unit increase in serum sLRP-1 was associated with a 66% reduction in OSA risk (OR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.30–0.55; P < 0.001). These robust findings emphasize that sLRP-1 is an independent protective factor against OSA. And serum sLRP-1 levels showed a stepwise decrease with increasing OSA severity, being highest in the normal group and lowest in patients with severe OSA (Table S2).

Subgroup analyses based on age and CRP levels

We used multivariable logistic regression to analyze all the parameters and found that Age, NLR(Neutrophil to Lymphocyte Ratio) and CRP had a significant effect on OSA, so subsequent subgroup analyses were conducted based on these three parameters (Fig. 1). Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying participants based on age (< 45 years and ≥ 45 years) and CRP levels (< 3 mg/L and ≥ 3 mg/L) (Fig. 2). The association between sLRP-1 and reduced OSA risk was consistently significant across all subgroups. Specifically, in the younger age subgroup (< 45 years), each unit increase of sLRP-1 corresponded to a 70% lower risk of OSA (OR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.17–0.52, P < 0.001), and similarly, a 65% lower risk in the older subgroup (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.23–0.55, P < 0.001). Comparable results were observed when stratified by CRP, confirming the stability and consistency of sLRP-1’s protective effects against OSA. The inverse association between serum sLRP-1 and OSA was consistent across age and CRP strata. After FDR correction, all associations remained statistically significant (Supplementary Table S3). These results suggest that the relationship between reduced sLRP-1 and OSA risk was robust across different inflammatory and metabolic conditions.

Among these, the Naive Bayes model demonstrated the highest discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.94 in the test set) and balanced overall performance (precision = 0.84, recall = 0.96, F1-score = 0.90), with good calibration between predicted and observed probabilities (Supplementary Fig. S2).

These findings indicate that sLRP-1 was the most influential and robust predictor among all evaluated variables.

Discussion

In this case-control study, we identified a statistically significant inverse relationship between serum sLRP-1 levels and the presence of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Participants with OSA exhibited consistently lower concentrations of sLRP-1 compared to those without OSA. This association persisted after adjusting for major demographic and clinical confounders, including age, sex, and BMI. The finding was further supported by subgroup analyses stratified by age and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, indicating a robust and consistent pattern across different patient profiles.

Although limited research has directly examined the role of sLRP-1 in sleep-related disorders, our findings provide novel evidence that decreased circulating levels of this protein are associated with OSA [18]. The reproducibility of this association in multivariable models further supports its robustness. This phenomenon may be linked to systemic inflammation. Previous studies have shown that LRP-1 shedding is enhanced in inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and various malignancies, although the exact pathological implications of this process remain unclear [19]. Given that LRP-1 exerts key anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, endothelial-protective, and metabolic regulatory effects, a reduction in its soluble form may reflect diminished protective capacity across these domains.We therefore hypothesize that lower sLRP-1 levels may contribute to OSA pathophysiology by promoting chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances.

Among the various models constructed, the Naive Bayes achieved the best discriminative performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.94 in the test dataset [20]. This strong classification ability highlights the potential of combining biochemical and clinical variables for distinguishing individuals with and without OSA. Importantly, SHAP value [21] analysis identified sLRP-1 as one of the most influential features contributing to the model’s output, reinforcing its relevance from a data-driven perspective. While machine learning cannot replace clinical judgment, it may serve as a supportive tool to improve classification accuracy and assist in risk stratification, particularly when based on objectively measurable serum markers.

The clinical implications of our findings are worth considering. At present, the diagnosis of OSA relies heavily on overnight polysomnography (PSG), a costly and resource-intensive procedure [22]. PSG and other sleep monitoring tools require specialized equipment and trained personnel, limiting their accessibility, especially in primary care or population-based settings. Meanwhile, screening questionnaires, though convenient, often suffer from low specificity and limited predictive value in identifying OSA severity [23]. The identification of a peripheral blood biomarker that correlates with OSA status may help bridge this gap. sLRP-1 may be explored as an adjunct marker for classifying OSA status among symptomatic snoring outpatients referred for PSG; however, the present study does not establish its utility for primary-care, community-based, or asymptomatic population screening [24].

This study also adds value by exploring the utility of machine learning in identifying and interpreting features associated with OSA. Traditional regression models are limited in capturing non-linear relationships or assessing the relative contribution of high-dimensional variables. By incorporating tree-based ensemble learning, we were able to generate more nuanced insights and detect hidden patterns not easily identified through conventional methods. Integrating sLRP-1 into this framework enhanced the model’s ability to distinguish between groups, demonstrating the added value of combining clinical phenotypes with serum biomarkers in exploratory classification analysis.

Nonetheless, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First, the observational case-control design prevents the establishment of temporal or causal relationships between sLRP-1 levels and OSA. It remains unclear whether reduced sLRP-1 is a predisposing factor for OSA or a consequence of disease processes. Longitudinal studies will be necessary to explore directionality and potential utility in disease monitoring. Second, this study was performed in a single-center Chinese cohort, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Because no independent external validation cohort was available, all reported machine-learning performance metrics should be regarded as internally validated and potentially optimistic. Larger multicenter and ethnically diverse cohorts are needed to confirm the reproducibility and generalizability of these findings. Although the sample size was sufficient for statistical analyses, replication in broader populations would further enhance the reliability and applicability of these results.

Third, while we adjusted for several major confounders, the influence of unmeasured variables—such as diet, physical activity, genetic polymorphisms, and other comorbidities—cannot be fully excluded. Data on smoking and alcohol consumption were collected and analyzed, while NYHA classification was assessed for all participants, who were uniformly class I, indicating preserved functional capacity. Medication use (e.g., amlodipine, nimodipine, valsartan) was recorded but not included in regression models due to small sample size and uneven distribution, to avoid model overfitting. Information on menopausal status was unavailable; however, all female participants were under 60 years, and age distribution was comparable between groups. These factors may have introduced residual confounding, which we acknowledge as a limitation.

While polysomnography (PSG) remains the diagnostic gold standard, serum sLRP-1 cannot differentiate OSA from other sleep disorders such as central sleep apnea. Finally, all participants were recruited from a snoring outpatient population referred for PSG rather than from the general community. Therefore, the findings support only an association between sLRP-1 and OSA status in symptomatic individuals, and cannot be extrapolated to asymptomatic individuals, primary-care populations, or population-level screening settings.

Despite these limitations, the strengths of the study include the rigorous phenotyping using PSG, the measurement of sLRP-1 in a standardized laboratory setting, and the comprehensive statistical approach combining classical regression and modern machine learning. Because substantial inter-assay differences may exist for sLRP-1 measurement, the absolute sLRP-1 concentrations reported here should be interpreted in the context of the specific ELISA kit and calibration system used in this study and should not be directly compared with values obtained using different assay platforms. The consistency of the inverse association across multiple models and patient subgroups strengthens the robustness of the results.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that lower serum sLRP-1 levels are associated with the presence of OSA in adult patients. Although mechanistic explanations remain speculative given the limited literature on sLRP-1 in this context, our findings indicate that sLRP-1 may represent a novel and objectively measurable marker associated with OSA status. Future studies should replicate these findings in larger external cohorts, including community-based and asymptomatic populations, before sLRP-1 can be considered for screening or risk-assessment applications.
