Avian GCGR-mediated continuous fat utilization offers perspectives for obesity treatment
Authors: Chang Zhang, Xue Wang, Chenxi Feng, Huaping Zheng, Song Leng, Yiheng Jiang, Qian Sun, Xiaoou Li, Ting Zhang, Jiacheng Zhang, Haidi Chen, Shanshan Lai, Wojciech Makalowski, Jürgen Brosius, Yujun Shi, Yang He, Peng Hu, Qiu Sun, Cheng Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human obesity is the fifth leading cause of global mortality. Fat mass loss and maintenance are central challenges for obesity management. In contrast, birds exhibit modest weight gain and limited fat accumulation due to rapid fat utilization as flight fuel. The physiology of this adaptation remained a mystery for a century. Here we show that cross-species single-nucleus RNA-sequencing analysis revealed high GCGR expression levels in avian adipocytes, while minimal in the adipose tissue of other vertebrates. Avian GCGR employs a unique rapid fat utilization mechanism for retaining a light body. Avian GCGR or other constitutively active GCGR variants (human GCGRH339R) expressed in white adipose tissue of male mice with obesity effectively promoted fat mobilization and sustained body weight loss, with decreased food intake partially contributing to the observed weight reduction. We suggest that avian GCGR plays this crucial role for fat utilization and may offer therapeutic potential for human obesity.

Introduction

Obesity, the excessive accumulation of white adipose (fat) tissue (WAT), was declared a global epidemic by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 19971–3. Currently, approximately one-third of the world’s population has overweight (BMI > 25 kg/m2) or obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2) (http://www.who.int/). It has become a significant concern for global public health and the economy and is identified as the fifth leading cause of global mortality4. As the prevalence of obesity and related diseases are continuing to rise, there is a growing concern about the escalating economic and social costs4–6. There is an urgent need for innovative management strategies beyond primary prevention and traditional lifestyle interventions7–11, especially sustaining weight loss and maintenance1,12,13.

Birds are among the most diverse vertebrates, possessing distinctive physiological adaptations for flight; for example, they exhibit rapid fat utilization for flight fuel, robust musculature, and high metabolic rate14–16. Outside of the preparation for the migratory state, birds usually exhibit remarkably low body weight in relation to size, even when enough food is available17. This phenomenon has attracted significant interest, yet it has remained a mystery for nearly a century17–20. In contrast to running mammals, flying birds use fat more frequently for flight16,21. This is because the energy density of fat (39.6 kJ/g) is far higher than that of carbohydrates (17.5 kJ/g) and proteins (17.8 kJ/g), providing a more significant advantage as a fuel source during flight16,21. As flight capability declines, such as in chickens, their lipid metabolic pathway tends to favor fat storage over fat degradation compared to flying birds22. Birds efficiently utilize fat to ensure the sustenance of high aerobic demands during continuous flapping in flight or long-distance flight, and transport of its constituent fatty acids to muscles is crucial for the flight of birds23–26. Moreover, this adaptation resulted in limited fat accumulation and low body weight in relation to body size, prerequisites for migrating birds during long-distance flights27.

Fat mass loss and maintenance play a vital role in human obesity management. All vertebrates have similar fat (lipid) metabolism, especially lipid storage28. Obesity in mammals arises from an imbalance between fat synthesis and breakdown rates in white adipose tissue (WAT)29. The increased fat mass in people with obesity is primarily due to increased triglyceride (TG) synthesis, and decreased TG breakdown in WAT was indicated30. As neutral lipids, TG and total cholesterol (CHO) are safely stored within specialized organelles known as lipid droplets (LDs)31. For intracellular lipolysis, TG breakdown involves several hydrolytic enzymes, including adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), and monoacylglycerol lipase32,33, which can hydrolyze adipocyte TGs into fatty acids (FAs) and monoacylglycerol (MGs)27,34,35. Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) remains functionally important in lipid metabolism and fat storage within adipose tissue36, where it hydrolyzes triglycerides (TGs) into fatty acids (FAs) and monoacylglycerol (MGs)37. Non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs), the more stable formation of FAs, can be transferred to muscles by binding to plasma albumin32.

Triglycerides (TGs) in the liver and adipose tissue enter circulation through the action of soluble protein carriers16,21. The liver can synthesize fatty acids de novo or absorb NEFAs from the blood and generate glycerol through esterification reactions, producing very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) released into circulation29. Using plasma VLDL as a source of fatty acids for working muscles requires the action of lipoprotein lipase (LPL) in the capillary endothelium, which hydrolyzes VLDL into NEFAs27,38. NEFAs bind to membrane fatty-acid-binding protein (FABPpm) and are transferred to muscle tissue27,38. The NEFAs breakdown process, called β-oxidation, occurs in muscles, liver, and adipose tissue39,40. Birds usually have lower body weight in relation to body size, even compared to small mammals, where certain bats have a lower body weight in relation to body size in comparison to birds41,42. Like most flying birds with high basal metabolic rate (BMR), small mammals with high BMR also tend to have low body weights43,44. Although lipid metabolism is highly conserved across birds, mammals, and other vertebrates18, the molecular mechanisms leading to low body weight have not been fully elucidated.

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) comprise the most prominent membrane protein family, with more than 800 members in vertebrate genomes. They are essential in physiological processes such as neuronal activities, metabolism, and reproduction45,46. In all vertebrates, lipids and their role in obesity are mainly regulated by a subfamily of type B GPCR glucagon receptors, including glucagon receptor (GCGR), glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor (GLP-1R), glucagon-like peptide 2 receptor (GLP-2R), and gastric inhibitory polypeptide receptor (GIPR), with high sequence similarity and functional conservation among vertebrates47,48. Glucagon (GCG), a hyperglycemic factor produced in the pancreas during animal fasting and hypoglycemia, binds to its functional cognate receptor, GCGR, located on liver plasma membranes. It is considered an effective target in managing and preventing obesity and diabetes49–51. Research has shown that the co-activation of GCGR with GLP1 receptors can enhance weight loss while mitigating the hyperglycemic effects of GCGR agonism52. Clinical studies using dual or triple agonists that target GCGR, GLP-1R, and GIP receptors have demonstrated significant weight reduction, improved glucose control, and enhanced insulin sensitivity compared to single hormone therapies53. When the gene products involved in lipid metabolism downstream of the GCG-Gs-cAMP axis are upregulated, they concurrently activate the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), and other enzymes associated with lipid degradation, thereby promoting lipid metabolism32,54–56.

Glucagon is a key hormone in glucose and lipid metabolism and is also linked to weight reduction. This weight loss effect is partly mediated by its ability to enhance fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) secretion and stimulate bile acids (BA)57,58. FGF21 plays a significant role in regulating energy expenditure and improving metabolic homeostasis, while bile acids can influence lipid metabolism and energy balance57,59,60. However, research on non-mammalian models, such as birds, remains limited. Further studies are needed to deepen our understanding of the evolutionary role of GCGR in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism and energy balance.

Constitutive activity has been documented in many GPCRs and other receptors, producing a second messenger without a ligand. This form of GPCR activity is associated with various potential physiological functions, such as basal neuronal activity, positive inotropic effects, and hyperfunctioning thyroid adenomas61,62. The downstream signaling of constitutively active GPCRs can be further enhanced by ligand binding. Some constitutively active GPCRs, like the human adenosine A2B receptor (A2BR), human histamine H4 receptor (hH4R), and human histamine H3 receptor (hH3R), have been demonstrated that their cognate ligands can further activate the corresponding receptor63–65. Moreover, many GPCR mutants with constitutive activity induce spontaneous stimulation, leading to severe disease61. In this study, we have discovered that the avian but not mammalian GCGR exhibits high constitutive activity and maintains high expression levels in WAT. We confirmed that highly expressed and constitutively active GCGR has the potential to treat obesity effectively.

Results

sNuc-RNA-seq demonstrated that avian GCGR regulates fat utilization by maintaining high expression levels in WAT

To investigate GCGR, a regulatory gene in avian fat metabolism, we examined the GCGR mRNA and/or protein levels in the WAT of mammals (Rattus norvegicus, Mus musculus, Canis lupus familiaris, Petaurus breviceps, Rhinolophus ferrumequinum), birds (Gallus gallus, Melopsittacus undulatus, Lonchura striata, Serinus canaria, Columba livia), and other vertebrates (Eublepharis macularius, Elaphe carinata, Xenopus laevis, Oreochromis mossambicus). The endogenous expression levels of GCGR protein were consistently high only in avian WAT; GCGR expression was barely detectable in mammalian adipose tissue (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). In all birds assayed, GCGR expression was highest in budgerigars (Fig. 1a, b). Thus, we selected adult budgerigars (Melopsittacus undulatus, mu) as our animal model (Fig. 1c–n). In male budgerigars, a significant correlation was observed between lower fat mass and body weight and the higher expression of GCGR in white adipose tissue (WAT) (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). To control for body size consistency, we selected a group with similar tarsus length and body weight for the experiment (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To identify cell distinct clusters expressing different genes in the adipose tissue of budgerigar, we conducted single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (sNuc-RNA-seq) for WAT. We annotated many cell clusters in budgerigar adipocytes, including mature adipocytes, preadipocytes, endothelial cells, stem cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages (Fig. 1c, d). Through cross-species sNuc-RNA-seq analysis, we observed that the respective endogenous GCGR genes were highly expressed in mature avian adipocytes, but not in human (Homo sapiens) or mouse (Mus musculus) adipocytes (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1f). To interfere with endogenous GCGR levels in WAT, we conducted subcutaneous fat in situ injection to deliver a shRNA-producing adeno-associated virus vector directed against the budgerigar (mu)GCGR, AAV-sh (muGCGR) and as a control the empty viral vector, AAV-sh (CTL) (Fig. 1f). Compared to the non-injected and AAV-null injected groups, budgerigars in the AAV-shGCGR group showed increased fat mass and body weight (Fig. 1g, h and Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Subcutaneous fat in situ injection did not impact levels of blood glucose and GCG (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h) for 3 days post-injection. However, the duration (3 days) of GCGR reduction was not long enough owing to the short expression period of shRNA delivered by AAV (Fig. 1i). Among budgerigars of similar age and tarsus length, the AAV-shGCGR group exhibited higher body weight compared to other groups, with a greater body fat percentage (fat mass) than the AAV-null and wild-type groups on day three post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). However, no differences in body temperature were detected (Supplementary Fig. 2d). The overall basal metabolic rate was reduced (Supplementary Fig. 2g–l) in the AAV-shGCGR group. Similarly, sNuc-RNA-seq was performed on the WAT from both the AAV-null and AAV-shGCGR budgerigar groups at day 3 after injection (Fig. 1f). Between the AAV-null and the AAV-shGCGR group, there was no difference on cell ratio of preadipocytes and mature adipocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1l–n). By downstream gene enrichment analysis, sNuc-RNA-seq results indicated that the downregulation of GCGR expression in WAT led to significantly decreased expression of browning-related genes (Fig. 1m), such as the decreased mRNA level of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (Pparγ), PPARγ coactivator 1-alpha (Pgc1α) (Fig. 1j) and beige adipocyte marker genes TNF receptor superfamily member 9 (Cd137, also known as Tnfrsf9) and T-box transcription factor 1 (Tbx1) (Supplementary Fig. 1j). Furthermore, a significant decrease in the lipolysis-related gene (Lpl) and the fatty-acid-β-oxidation-related gene (carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1A - Cpt1a) might be indicative of a downregulation of lipid metabolism (Fig. 1k, l). The mRNA levels of lipogenesis-related genes (acyl-CoA thioesterase 7-Acot7 and fatty acid synthase-Fasn) decreased as well (Supplementary Fig. 1i). We also observed consistent changes concerning these genes in sNuc-RNA-seq data (Fig. 1n and Source Tables 1–3). In female budgerigars, the phenotypes following GCGR knockdown were consistent with those observed in males (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, the effects on regulation of downstream signaling pathways were less pronounced in females, associated with higher variability within the group (Supplementary Fig. 3i–m). This may suggest that males exhibit better consistency in metabolic levels, resulting in more stable experimental outcomes. Additionally, in male chickens (Supplementary Fig. 4a), the lipid metabolism levels decreased in parallel with the reduction in GCGR expression following subcutaneous fat in situ injection of AAV-shGCGR (Supplementary Fig. 4b–h), consistent with the results observed in budgerigars. These findings reaffirmed the potent and coordinated regulatory role of the GCGR gene in browning and lipid metabolism in avian WAT.

Avian GCGR exhibits vigorous constitutive activity

To explore differences between avian and mammalian GCGRs concerning constitutive activity, we cloned DNA synthesized 23 GCGRs from various vertebrate species (eleven placental mammals, four non-placental mammals, eight birds) and subcloned them into the expression vector pcDNA3.1 (Source Table 4). As shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5a–c, with elevated expression levels, all aves (orange), encompassing non-placental mammalian GCGR (purple), exhibit a significant increase in constitutive activity of Gs signaling in the absence of ligands. However, no rise in Gs signal constitutive activity was observed in placental mammals (green) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5c). Within the avian family, the GCGR of Serinus canaria demonstrated markedly higher constitutive activity than other vertebrates (Fig. 2a). In the absence of ligands, high levels of intracellular cAMP accumulation were detected after avian GCGR expression in the chicken hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial cell line (LMH). In contrast, no such accumulation was observed following mammalian GCGR overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 5d). This is consistent with the functional behavior of avian GCGR observed in HEK293 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5d). A more comprehensive validation with expanding the number of vertebrate species, demonstrated that GCGRs from all non-placental vertebrates exhibit constitutive activity66. Regardless of the presence of GCG, cross-stimulus in vitro experiments can explicitly show that human, mouse, chicken, canary, and budgerigar GCGR do not respond to stimulation with gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) or glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP1) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Avian GCGR, expressed in mouse iWAT, continuously facilitates fat utilization for weight loss and maintenance

To ascertain the functionality of avian GCGR in adipose tissue, we synthesized Serinus canaria (sc) GCGR, Homo sapiens (hs) GCGR, and empty vector (null) for AAV delivery. We employed diet-induced obese (DIO) mice (fed with a 60% high-fat diet, HFD) (Fig. 2b). After HFD for 23 weeks, which corresponds to the final stage of diet-induced obesity, blood glucose levels rose to ~10–11 mmol/L from ~7–8 mmol/L at the onset of HFD, a value normal for 5-week-old mice68. We did not notice much fluctuation, with levels remaining ~10 mmol/L. Following the subcutaneous fat in situ injection in mice, which previously were already subjected to 23 weeks of HFD feeding, scGCGR was highly expressed in iWAT without elevating blood glucose, and significantly mitigated weight gain, about 18% compared to control groups (Fig. 2c, e, g). The body weight in mice from the scGCGR group was effectively reduced (Fig. 2c). Through X-ray imaging, the body fat of the scGCGR group was significantly lower than that of the other control groups, within populations exhibiting similar body size (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 8a, c). However, approximately 18 days after AAV injection, the weight loss effects in mice showed some reversal (Fig. 2c) associated with the gradually reduced expression of scGCGR in iWAT (Supplementary Fig. 7b). In contrast, the expression of hsGCGR in mouse iWAT was maintained for at least 30 days (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Due to the high constitutive activity of scGCGR, even expressed at lower iWAT levels, body weight was effectively reduced by about 10% (Fig. 2c, g). In contrast, Petaurus breviceps (pb) GCGR, with weak constitutive activity, although expressed at very high levels in iWAT, only yielded a temporary individual weight reduction of ~3% (Supplementary Fig. 11), because pbGCGR was expressed for only about 3–5 days in mice (Supplementary Fig. 11f, g). The introduction of murine (mm) GCGR, which is not constitutively active into adipose tissue had no significant effect on mouse body weight, showing results similar to those observed in the human GCGR (hsGCGR) group (Supplementary Fig. 9). No exogenous GCGR (scGCGR or hsGCGR) expression was detected in the livers of mice (Supplementary Figs. 8i and 9t). Food and water intake did not change significantly in the scGCGR group (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). As shown in Fig. 2h, i, in the scGCGR group, there was an improvement in the glucose tolerance test and also an increase in insulin sensitivity.

RNA-seq data of iWAT also indicated that the differentially expressed genes in the scGCGR group mainly involved lipid metabolism, browning, PPAR, and insulin resistance pathways (Fig. 2j, k, Supplementary Fig. 7n–p and Source Table 5). In the scGCGR group, the levels of lipolysis-related genes (Atgl and Lpl) and the fatty-acid-β-oxidation-related gene (carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1B, Cpt1b, carnitine palmitoyltransferase-2, Cpt2) increased (Fig. 2l and Supplementary Fig. 7g), while the lipogenesis-related genes (Acetyl-CoA carboxylase-Acc, Fasn, Acot7) showed no significant differences (Supplementary Fig. 7h). Notably, the browning-related genes of iWAT (uncoupling protein 1-Ucp1, Pgc1α, Prdm16, Cd137) were upregulated (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 7i). Notably, UCP1 was markedly upregulated in the iWAT of the scGCGR group, indicating the beiging or browning of WAT (Supplementary Fig. 7j). In the serum, we observed elevated lipid metabolism byproducts such as NEFAs and total bile acids (TBAs) (Fig. 2n). Concurrently, there was a decrease in TGs and total cholesterol (TCHO) (Fig. 2n). Multiphoton microscopy (MPM) imaging by femtosecond label-free imaging, double-labeling fluorescence and Oil Red staining revealed that scGCGR overexpression in iWAT resulted in significantly smaller lipid droplets in adipocytes compared to the hsGCGR and empty vector (null) injected group (Fig. 2o, p and Supplementary Fig. 7k, l). The H&E staining of iWAT demonstrated a noticeable decrease in adipocyte size (Fig. 2q, r). Whole body temperature did not change (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Complete blood count analysis showed no significant differences in the absolute values of red blood cells, white blood cells, monocytes, neutrophils, or lymphocytes, indicating that the mice did not exhibit any other immune or inflammatory responses (Supplementary Fig. 8d–h). Mice that were 8 weeks old were fed with an HFD for four, 13, or 23 weeks, representing the early, intermediate, and late stages of obesity, respectively. After injection of scGCGR into iWAT, we observed about 18% weight loss (Supplementary Fig. 12b, h) with upregulated serum TBA (Supplementary Fig. 12f, l) at all three stages. The GCGR downstream genes for lipid metabolism and browning in 3T3-L1 and primary white adipocytes expressing scGCGR were consistent with in vivo data (Supplementary Fig. 13a–n). After induction of 3T3-L1 and primary white adipocytes differentiation, double-labeling fluorescence (GCGR and lipids) indicated that scGCGR overexpression (green) resulted in less and smaller lipid droplets (red) compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 13f, m). The Oil Red O staining revealed that lipid droplets were smaller and fewer in primary adipocytes overexpressing scGCGR (Supplementary Fig. 13n).

Moreover, we constructed scGCGR conditional knock-in mice crossed with Fabp4-iCre mice (Supplementary Fig. 14a). After HFD 4 weeks, the specific expression of scGCGR in adipose tissue was induced by doxycycline (Dox) (Fig. 3a). Despite its relatively low expression levels (Fig. 3d), the individuals from the CKI-scGCGR-Fabp4-iCre group exhibited strong anti-obesity effects under HFD-induced conditions (Fig. 3b). Both food and water intake were not significantly altered in the scGCGR group (Supplementary Fig. 14c, d). Also, blood glucose of CKI-scGCGR-Fabp4-iCre mice was not elevated (Fig. 3c). H&E staining of iWAT showed a decreased size of adipocytes (Fig. 3e, f). The mRNA levels of lipolysis-related genes (Atgl, Hsl and Lpl) increased (Fig. 3g). Notably, browning of iWAT was increased as indicated by upregulated Pgc1α, Ucp1 and Cd137 (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 14f). In comparison, the group induced with Dox showed no significant changes in lipogenesis-related gene expression (Acc, Fasn, Acot7) (Supplementary Fig. 14e), while serum NEFAs and TBA increased and TCHO decreased (Fig. 3i). Regulation of lipid metabolism by avian GCGR is ligand-independent

To exclude the impact of endogenous GCG, we generated GCG-knockout mice (Fig. 3j). These mice were fed with HFD for 4 weeks, followed by subcutaneous fat injection of AAV-scGCGR in situ, resulting in a phenotype consistent with that of the Dio mice individuals mentioned above (Fig. 2b–f), exhibiting normal blood glucose levels accompanied by reduced body weight as low as about 15% with highly exogenous scGCGR expression (Fig. 3k–m). Twenty days post-injection, the weight rose again (Fig. 2c), associated with decreasing GCGR expression (Supplementary Fig. 7b). However, in the scGCGR group that was treated with agonist IUB28858 beginning at day 6, the weight continued to decline for 20 days (Fig. 3k). Administration of IUB288 did not cause an increase in blood glucose levels or body temperature (Supplementary Fig. 15c, d). There was no difference in food intake (Supplementary Fig. 15e, f). Injection of IUB288 significantly upregulated lipid metabolism and browning of iWAT of mice and increased the mRNA levels of Fgf21 gene in the liver (Supplementary Fig. 15g–l). There were no significant differences in food intake and water intake between the scGCGR group and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 14h–i). We observed an upregulation in lipolysis-related genes, including Atgl, Hsl, and Lpl, as well as iWAT browning-related genes such as Ucp1, Pgc1α, and Pparγ in the scGCGR group (Fig. 3p, q). However, the lipogenesis-related genes, Acc and Fasn, were upregulated in GCG-knockout mice (Supplementary Fig. 14j), which underscores the more crucial role of the GCG-GCGR axis in lipid genesis. The scGCGR group had a smaller adipocyte size as detected by H&E staining (Fig. 3n, o). Overall, regulating lipid metabolism mediated by avian GCGR is ligand-independent but could also be augmented by the GCGR agonist.

Constitutively active GCGR leads to low RQ

The GCG-GCGR axis directly increases the metabolic rate and energy expenditure by inducing expression of the energy metabolism-related fibroblast growth factor 21 (Fgf21) in rodents58,69. We further investigated the relationship between constitutively active GCGR in adipose tissue and the basal metabolic rate for budgerigars (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3) and DIO mice (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. 10 and 16). Indirect calorimetry analysis revealed that the total volumes of oxygen consumption (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2), and energy expenditure were significantly lower in the AAV-shGCGR-injected budgerigars, especially for male budgerigars (Supplementary Figs. 2g–j and 3o–r). The respiratory quotient (RQ) was much higher compared to the control group (Supplementary Figs. 2j and 3r). However, the total volumes of oxygen consumption (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2), and the energy expenditure rate were not significantly different in the scGCGR, hsGCGR and null injected mice during both light and dark phases (Fig. 3s, t and Supplementary Figs. 10a–d and 16a–e). Notably, the RQ of the scGCGR-injected mice was much lower compared to the hsGCGR group, indicating that scGCGR-overexpressing mice tended to chiefly utilize lipids as a metabolic substrate for energy supply (Fig. 3v and Supplementary Fig. 10d).

Humans also express high levels of GCGR in the liver, and the human GCGRH339R is a natural genome mutation. Because hsGCGRH339R has gained constitutive activity, we synthesized an AAV vector for delivery (high and low titer) into the liver and adipose tissue of DIO mice to investigate the regulatory mechanism (Fig. 4d, k). Discussion

Birds have many distinctive physiological adaptations for flight, such as rapid fat utilization. Through cross-species single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis of mammalian and Melopsittacus undulatus adipose tissues, we have identified avian GCGR to be highly expressed in adipose tissue, especially in flying birds, such as Serinus canaries and Lonchura striata, and to exhibit high constitutive activity (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, birds exhibiting such elevated GCGR expression have lower fat content and body weight (Fig. 1g–i and Supplementary Fig. 1d).

Obesity is a widespread epidemic affecting over one-third of the global population, increasing the risk of conditions such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. There is an urgent need for safer and more effective treatments to address this growing health crisis. To stem the tide of this epidemic, there is an urgent need for safer and more effective treatment methods. Previous studies for weight loss mostly focused on the hepatic function of GCGR and proposed a combination with GLP1 and/or GIP to counteract the side effects of GCGR activation on glucose metabolism52,53,70–73. Current obesity treatments based on glucagon receptor family are mostly by ligand application, such as liraglutide or semaglutide74,75. The application of the GCGR signal pathway in treating obesity and diabetes is based on extensive research in mammals. Compared to mammals, many avian species usually maintain a low body weight in relation to body size throughout their lifetimes17. Integrating insights from extensive physiological studies in avian species reveals significant differences between birds and mammals, particularly rodents, regarding preferred energy sources and overall energy metabolism, despite glucose and lipid metabolism being relatively conserved76–81. Further investigations into the downstream molecular mechanisms of constitutively active GCGR in bird energy metabolism could potentially contribute to strategies for reducing mammalian obesity rates.

Migrant birds only accumulate fat before long-distance migration27, and unlike mammals, avian GCGR is highly expressed in their adipose tissue (Fig. 1a, b, e and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Chicken, which tends to accumulate fat have highly constitutively active GCGR but lower expression levels in adipose tissue (Fig.1 and Supplementary Fig. 4). The ancestor of the domestic chicken, the Gallus gallus bankiva or red jungle fowl, had to be lean in order to roost on trees and thus be better protected from predators82–84. The Gcgr gene has no amino acid primary structure differences between the red jungle fowl and the broiler breed. In chickens and other domesticated birds, selective breeding for fattening might have led to other mechanisms countering the effects of GCGR. Since birds lack brown adipose tissue, the mechanism of non-shivering thermogenesis has been unclear85,86. As shown in Fig. 1i, j, m, decreased expression of GCGR in adipose tissue of budgerigars can significantly down-regulate browning-related genes. In DIO mice, the expression of avian GCGR in adipose tissue facilitated highly effective fat utilization, leading to weight loss and maintenance without increased blood glucose (Figs. 2 and 3 and Supplementary Figs. 7, 9, 12 and 15). Although birds do not have brown adipose tissue, the high expression of avian GCGR still participates in the regulation of browning-related gene expression in WAT of mice and birds (Figs. 1j, 2m, 3h, q and 5i), indicating that avian GCGR plays a crucial role in the browning of WAT instead of BAT. In mouse iWAT, the GCGR (scGCGR) with high constitutive activity could mediate significant weight loss in mice with a lower expression level (Fig. 2g). Additionally, avian GCGR strongly promoted lipolysis in adipose tissue, demonstrating a high rate of fat utilization (Figs. 2l, 3g, p and 5h,k). There were no statistically significant differences in food and water intake; however, food intake was moderately reduced in mice with constitutively active GCGR (Supplementary Figs. 7, 9, 11, 14, 15, 18 and 19). Calculations of energy intake and expenditure revealed that this reduction in food intake partially contributed to the decrease in body weight observed in mice with obesity (Source Table 9). Although overt behaviors suggesting severe nausea were not observed under the present experimental conditions, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that mild discomfort may have contributed to the reduced food intake. Nonetheless, it was not the sole factor driving the overall weight loss. Rather, the upregulation of lipid metabolism and browning of iWAT induced by constitutively active GCGR represents a major contributor to the weight loss phenotype. Screening human SNP data from NCBI, we uncovered naturally occurring point mutations (H339R) in human GCGR with a weak constitutive activity (Fig. 4a–c and Supplementary Fig. 17b). Mice, when injected with weaker constitutive active GCGR (hsGCGRH339R or pbGCGR) but expressed at a high level in iWAT, also showed weight reduction (Fig. 4e, l). Hence, hsGCGRH339R exhibited an avian-like phenotype regulating lipid metabolism (Fig. 4k–v), which could be exploited as a tool to manage obesity.

The lipid metabolism of birds and mammals may have evolved differently. Bird GCGR is constitutively active and ligand-independent, whereas this activity has been lost in mammals66. In birds, the highly expressed constitutively active GCGR in adipose tissue mediates both lipolysis and lipogenesis. When GCGR expression is reduced, genes related to lipid breakdown and synthesis are down-regulated (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Fig. 1i). In contrast, mouse GCGR lacks constitutive activity and is not expressed in adipose tissue. Overexpression of avian GCGR in mouse adipose tissue promotes lipid and energy metabolism (Figs. 2 and 3). Furthermore, in GCG-knockout mice overexpressing bird GCGR, a second injection of IUB288 may suppress lipid synthesis genes compared to those without IUB288 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 15), suggesting potential differences between ligand-independent constitutive activity and ligand-dependent activation, particularly in regulating lipogenesis. Interestingly, co-overexpression of avian GCGR in mouse adipose and liver tissues increases lipid synthesis gene expression in adipose tissue (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 18). This may be due to enhanced glucose metabolism in the liver, leading to elevated blood glucose levels, which, upon binding to GLUT2 on adipocytes, stimulates de novo lipogenesis1,87. These evolutionary differences are intriguing, and the mechanisms underlying avian lipid metabolism signaling warrant further investigation.

In adipocytes, cAMP is a key regulator of lipolysis, glucose uptake, and thermogenesis88,89. Activation of Gs-coupled receptors elevates intracellular cAMP, which in turn activates protein kinase A (Pka) to stimulate WAT lipolysis, primarily mediated by hormone-sensitive lipase (Hsl), adipose triglyceride lipase (Atgl), and monoacylglycerol lipase (Magl)90–92, while cAMP–CREB signaling upregulates C/EBPβ, facilitating healthy adipocyte differentiation during browning93–95. The cAMP/PKA/p38 pathway further induces thermogenic markers such as Ucp1 and Pgc1a, enhancing thermogenic capacity and energy metabolism96–100. Our findings indicate that constitutively active GCGR markedly elevates intracellular cAMP levels. In vivo, this was associated with improved metabolic status and resistance to diet-induced obesity, likely via enhanced energy expenditure and WAT browning. Consistent with this, we observed robust upregulation of Atgl, Hsl, Lpl, Ucp1, and Pgc1α (Figs. 2–5), suggesting that GCGR/cAMP/PKA signaling promotes both lipolysis and the browning of white adipose tissue. Notably, high-level overexpression of avian GCGR in mouse adipocytes failed to increase circulating NEFAs (Fig.2n). Based on our experimental results, we hypothesize that the high-level expression of constitutively active GCGR in adipose tissue leads to substantial intracellular accumulation of cAMP, which in turn activates the cAMP/CREB signaling pathway. This activation promotes the transcription of Cpt1 and Cpt2, ultimately enhancing fatty acid β-oxidation and thereby consuming NEFAs generated from lipolysis1. In mice with lower levels of constitutively active GCGR expression, elevated serum NEFAs levels were observed (Fig. 3i), indicating that in these cases, NEFAs release from lipolysis in adipose tissue exceeds the consumption of NEFAs through β-oxidation. When constitutively active GCGR is overexpressed in the liver, serum NEFAs levels increase significantly, indicating that lipolysis in the liver play a dominant role (Fig. 5l). Additionally, hepatic GCGR/PKA signaling also promotes de novo fatty acid synthesis, resulting in TGs storage. Moreover, GCGR/cAMP/PKA signaling facilitated the browning of white adipose tissue, and during this process, the abundant mitochondria require NEFAs to fuel the tricarboxylic acid cycle101. As an allosteric activator of UCP1 function, NEFAs are necessary to support adipose-tissue browning101–103.

Respiratory quotient (RQ) is commonly used to infer which substrates are oxidized, with glucose yielding RQ = 1 and fat commonly thought to yield an average of RQ = 0.71104. In contrast to running mammals, flying birds use lipids, providing a more significant advantage as a fuel source during flight16,21. As shown in Fig. 3v, the scGCGR-injected group exhibited a much lower RQ than the hsGCGR group, suggesting that highly expressed avian GCGR in adipose tissue would lead to increased fat utilization, reaffirming the notion that birds primarily rely on fat as their primary energy source for flight (Fig. 6). Our hypothesis is that when WAT absorbs nutrition from peripheral blood, it tends to undergo more lipid synthesis (manifested as increased TG). The high expression of constitutively active GCGR in adipocytes continuously activated the downstream lipolysis-related genes, resulting in acceleration of TG breakdown and promotion of WAT browning (Fig. 6). As flight capability declines, such as in chickens (with low expression of GCGR in their fat), the lipid metabolism favors lipid synthesis over lipid degradation. It tends to put on more fat mass compared to flying birds22. The highly expressed constitutively active GCGR in WAT promotes lipid metabolism and browning, sustaining the remarkably high energy metabolism required for avian flight. Mechanistic model of constitutively active GCGR in regulating lipid metabolism, β-oxidation, and browning of white adipose tissue.

Among all vertebrates, only avian adipose tissues exhibit high expression of constitutively active GCGR. When mammalian adipose tissues are engineered to express constitutively active GCGR at high levels, they acquire a metabolic regulatory profile resembling that of birds. Independent of agonists or ligands, elevated GCGR expression enhances lipid metabolism, β-oxidation, and browning of white adipose tissue, thereby promoting triglyceride (TG) hydrolysis and the release of non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) from adipocytes, while simultaneously facilitating fatty acid β-oxidation and boosting overall energy metabolism. Upon stimulation by agonists or ligands, constitutively active GCGR further amplifies downstream signaling.

Our study faces several limitations, including providing substantial animal experimental data to demonstrate that fat accumulation in wild, free-living mammals results from losing adipose tissue-specific expression of a single gene, GCGR. And, it was not possible to collect subcutaneous fat from the same anatomical location across species. Moreover, GCGR knockdown in budgerigars via AAV injection showed limited efficacy and a short duration of action. Due to ethical and sourcing constraints, we selected budgerigars with high GCGR expression for our experiments, as they were among the few avian species available in sufficient quantities. However, the absence of data across diverse bird species limits our understanding of the potential impact on flight capability, which we intend to investigate further in future studies. We focused on the effects of avian GCGR expression in mouse white adipose tissue, which markedly induced a browning process characterized by the emergence of multilocular lipid droplets and upregulation of browning-related genes such as Pgc1α and Ucp1, etc. However, the work remains limited in scope, as we did not investigate mitochondrial abundance and function, nor did we explore classical thermogenic pathways (β-adrenergic signaling, AMPK, or p38) in detail.

Further research is needed to identify human GCGR-based variants with an enhanced constitutive activity that could maintain prolonged expression for optimal obesity treatment in humans. Current constitutively active GCGR alleles, expressed in liver and adipose tissue, mimic avian-like metabolism, leading to elevated blood glucose levels in high-fat diet (HFD) mice while promoting fat utilization and reducing body weight in relation to body size. To minimize hyperglycemia, the human GCGRH339R variant may serve as a better basis for developing further optimized variants with higher constitutive activity as a potential tool for AAV-based gene therapy that can serve as a long-term treatment of human obesity.

Methods

Plasmids and transfection

GCGR genes were synthesized by General Biology System (Anhui) Co., Ltd., China, and inserted into pcDNA3.1-FLAG-His vector. The GCGR sequences of different species are organized in Source Table 4. The GCGR mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using the QuikChange mutagenesis protocol (Agilent Technologies, USA). Plasmids were transfected with HEK293 using Lipofectamine 2000/3000 (Invitrogen-Life Technologies, USA). Ten thousand cells were transfected with 0.2 μg of each plasmid of interest.

Cell lines

HEK293 (human [Homo sapiens] fetal kidney) and 3T3-L1 (mouse pre-adipocyte) cell lines were all obtained from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China). HEK293 cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 I.U./mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. 3T3-L1 was maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 10% heat-inactivated newborn bovine calf serum (NCS) and 100 μg/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Two cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. LMH cells (chicken hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC CRL2117), cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM/F12, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 I.U./mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. LMH was incubated at 39 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

Peptides

IUB288, GCG, GLP1, and GIP were synthesized from GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd., China. All peptides were > 95% pure. Peptide sequences are organized in Source Table 10.

AAV production

Recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) serotype 2/8 vectors that overexpress GCGR from species of interest with a V5 tag (pAAV-CAG-GCGR-V5 tag-tWPA) were constructed by OBiO Technology (Shanghai) Corp., Ltd., China.

For GCGR knockdown in budgerigar, AAV serotype 2/9 vectors carrying shRNA (pAAV-U6-shRNA (GCGR)-CMV-EGFP-WPRE) were used. AAV-scramble shRNA-GFP was used as control (OBIO, China). The shGCGR sequences are provided in Source Table 10.

Animals

C57BL/6J (wild type and DIO) mice (5-week-old) and rats (wild type, 5-week-old) were purchased from the GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., China. Mice and rats were housed under conventional conditions in filter-top protected cages and cared for in accordance with the Experimental Animal Center of Sichuan University, and provided food and water ad libitum. Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chickens (SEOUTO25768) were purchased by Guangdong Wens Nanfang Poultry Breeding Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China. Canis lupus familiaris (TLO4G05), Petaurus breviceps (TLO6MDWO2), Lonchura striata (TLO9BYWNO1), Serinus canaria (TL10JSO03), Melopsittacus undulates (TL11HPYWO2), Columba livia (TL12GZ04), Eublepharis macularius (TL21BWSG02), Elaphe carinata (TL24WJS04), Oreochromis mossambicus (TL25LFY01) were purchased from Taole Animal Breeding Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China. Xenopus tropicalis (XB-STRAIN-161) was purchased from Xenopus Resource Center, Hangzhou, China. Rhinolophus ferrumequinum was captured from the Shennongjia area in China. Animals information is organized in Source Table 14.

For the other species—Canis lupus familiaris, Petaurus breviceps, Eublepharis macularius, Elaphe carinata, Oreochromis mossambicus, Xenopus tropicalis, and Rhinolophus ferrumequinum—we directly collected adipose tissue samples without maintaining them in captivity. The coop has a 16/8 h light/dark cycle, with an exhaust fan installed to maintain proper air circulation inside the coop. The water dispenser provides water continuously, and the feed trays are refilled with food twice a day at scheduled times. The floor is cleaned and disinfected regularly every day.

For mouse feeding, two diets were used: a standard maintenance diet (chow diet, FFR13051001) purchased from KEAO XIELI FEED CO., LTD, Tianjin, China, and a 60% high-fat diet (XTHF60) purchased from Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd, China. Nuclei preparation

The nucleus was isolated using a Nucleus Isolation Kit (SHBIO 52009-10) as instructions. RNase inhibitors (Sigma 3335399001) were added to the reagents before use. The samples were cut and transferred to a 5 mL tube containing lysate, mixed and lysed for 3 min on ice, then filtered through a 40 µm cell filter (Sigma BAH136800040). Nucleus count and viability was estimated using fluorescence Cell Analyzer (Countstar® Rigel S2) with AO/PI reagent. Furthermore, the nuclei were stained with 0.4% trypan blue and placed under a microscope (40×). Subsequent experiments were performed if the nuclear envelope was intact and there were fewer impurities.

Single nuclei RNA-sequencing library construction and sequencing

Single nuclei RNA (sNuc-RNA-seq) libraries were prepared using SeekOne® Digital Droplet Single Cell 3′ library preparation kit (SeekGene Catalog No. K00202). Briefly, appropriate number of cell nucleus were mixed with reverse transcription reagent and then added to the sample well in SeekOne® DD Chip S3. Subsequently, Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil were dispensed into corresponding wells separately in chip S3. After emulsion droplet generation, reverse transcription was performed at 42 °C for 90 min and inactivated at 80 °C for 15 min. Next, cDNA was purified from broken droplets and amplified in PCR reaction. The amplified cDNA product was then cleaned, fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to sequencing adapter. Finally, the indexed PCR was performed to amplify the DNA representing 3′ polyA part of expressing genes, which also contained Cell Barcode and Unique Molecular Index. The indexed sequencing libraries were cleanup with SPRI beads, quantified by quantitative PCR (KAPA Biosystems KK4824), and then sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with PE150 read length.

Processing the single-cell RNA-sequencing data

Cleaned reads were processed using the Seek Soul Tools pipeline to generate a transcript-level expression matrix. First, cell barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were extracted according to predefined patterns specifying the positions of barcodes, linkers, and UMIs within each read. Barcodes were then corrected against a whitelist, and the corrected barcode–UMI pairs were recorded in the corresponding read headers. Next, reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR (v2.5.1b). Aligned reads containing both barcode and UMI information were assigned to the transcriptome using featureCounts from the Subread package (v1.6.4). Subsequently, a raw UMI count matrix—analogous to the raw_feature_bc_matrix output from Cell Ranger—was generated based on barcodes and transcript identities. A cell-calling algorithm, conceptually similar to those used in Cell Ranger and EmptyDrops, was then applied to filter the raw matrix and produce a filtered_feature_bc_matrix. This algorithm involved two key steps:Identification of cells with high RNA content based on a total UMI count threshold for each barcode.Classification of the remaining barcodes as “empty” or “cell-containing” partitions based on their RNA expression profiles, thereby retaining low-RNA-content cells whose UMI counts might otherwise overlap with background noise.

Cross-species integration, clustering, visualization, and differential expression

Integration, clustering, visualization, and differential expression analyses were conducted using Seurat. To compare cellular profiles across species, WAT expression matrices from Melopsittacus undulatus, Mus musculus, and Homo sapiens were compiled and reanalyzed using consistent criteria. We first generated homologous genes of Melopsittacus undulatus, Mus musculus, and Homo sapiens through Orthofinder (Version 2.5.4). We considered one-to-one orthology from all species. In order to compare and analyze WAT cell profiles from all species mentioned, we used canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to integrate their data as anchor datasets with dims = 1:40 to batch correction. Based on the shared structure, all batches of data were finally pooled into a single object for downstream analysis.

For clustering, 2000 variable genes were used, and mitochondrial genes were removed from the variable gene set before running PCA and calculating clusters using 20 principal components, and a resolution of 0.6. The non-linear dimensional reduction technique was used to visualize and explore these datasets. We used the Wilcoxon rank sum test to find differential expression. We identified differentially expressed genes by the following criteria: (1) P-value ≤ 0.05; (2) log2(fold change [FC]) ≥ 0.2; and (3) the percentage of cells where the gene is detected in a specific cluster >10%.

Differentially expressed genes: KEGG enrichment analysis

Genes usually interact with each other to play roles in certain biological functions. Pathway-based analysis helps to further understand biological gene functions. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (https://www.kegg.jp/) pathway enrichment analysis identified significantly enriched metabolic pathways or signal transduction pathways in differentially expressed genes comparing to the whole genome background. Taking P-value ≤ 0.05 as a threshold, pathways meeting this criterion were defined as significantly enriched pathways in differentially expressed genes.

Primary preadipocytes culture and differentiation

Primary preadipocytes were isolated from male mouse inguinal white adipose tissue. The adipose tissue was minced into a paste-like consistency using surgical scissors and then resuspended in Type I collagenase solution (3 mL/g adipose tissue). The centrifuge tube was tightly sealed and incubated in a horizontal water bath at 37 °C with shaking (60–70 bpm) for 90 min. After incubation, the suspension was centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant containing damaged cells and mature adipocytes was discarded, while the pellet containing primary preadipocytes was retained. The pellet was resuspended, treated with red blood cell lysis buffer, and filtered through a 150-µm mesh. The filtrate was centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min at room temperature, resuspended, and filtered three times, with the final filtration using a 70-µm mesh. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of maintenance medium (DMEM/F12 basal medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% PS, 5 µg/mL insulin,1 µM rosiglitazone, and 50 nM T3). The cells were counted, and an appropriate volume of seeding medium was used for resuspension (e.g., 150,000 cells/mL for subsequent plating at 1 mL per 4.5 cm2 culture dish). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Culture medium was labeled as maintenance medium A. Differentiation medium B: DMEM/F12 basal medium supplemented with 5 µg/mL insulin, 1 µM rosiglitazone, 50 nM T3, 1 µM dexamethasone, and 0.5 mM isobutyl methylxanthine (IBMX). Cells were sequentially cultured in medium A (48 h), B (48 h), A (48 h), and B (48 h), followed by a final 24-h incubation in medium A. Samples were collected on day 9 for analysis.

Indirect calorimetry

Air samples were analyzed by an indirect open circuit calorimeter (TSE Systems, Bad Homburg, Germany) with a high-speed Siemens Ultramat/Oxymat 6 O2/CO2 analyzer (TSE)113–115. Mice (one per cage) were acclimated to the metabolic chambers for 24 h, and data were collected in each cage every 27 min, during a 48-h period. Rates of oxygen consumption (VO2, mL/min per kilogram lean body weight) and carbon dioxide production (VCO2, mL/min per kilogram lean body weight) were calculated by TSE software, using the reference cage as measurement of (adjusted) oxygen and carbon dioxide inflow, and used to calculate the RER (RER = VCO2/VO2) and EE (EE = [3.941*VO2 + 1.106*VCO2]/1000 in kcal/min per kilogram lean body weight). Samples were taken during light and dark cycles and were adjusted by whole body weight and lean weight, respectively.
