PRSS55 regulates BCAA metabolism and interacts with BCKDK and BCKDHA in mouse testes and sperm
Authors: Haoyang Ge, Chunling Shen, Wenfeng Xiong, Shunyuan Lu, Chaojie Li, Lingyun Tang, Yan Shen, Hongxin Zhang, Jian Fei, Zhugang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsRecent research has emphasized the significance of testis-specific serine proteases in regulating various aspects of sperm maturation and function. Among them, serine protease 55 (PRS55) plays an important role in the energy metabolism of sperm and is essential for male fertility in mice. A recent case study further suggests its potential importance to human fertility. However, the underlying molecular mechanism by which PRS55 influences sperm function are still not well understood. The present study aims to investigate these mechanisms further.ResultsIn this study, we found impaired mitochondrial function in Prss55−/− testes and sperm with low ATP production, and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential in sperm. We then validated that PRSS55 is mainly localized in mitochondria by immunofluorescence staining of sperm and transfected NIH-3T3 cells and immunoblotting of testis and transfected HEK293 cells. In HEK293 cells, overexpression of PRSS55 improved mitochondrial function in vitro, as increased ATP production and NAD+ /NADH ratio. By proteomics, we identified 1593 and 711 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs, fold change > 1.5 and corrected P ≤ 0.05) in PRSS55-enriched testicular cells and sperm between wt and Prss55−/− mice, respectively. Functional annotation revealed that these DEPs were mainly associated with energy metabolic pathways, especially branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation process. In subsequent metabolomic analysis, we observed significant accumulations of BCAAs (valine, leucine and isoleucine) in Prss55−/− testes. Using LC-MS/MS and Co-IP assays, we discovered and validated that PRSS55 was interacting with two key molecules in BCAA metabolism as branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase kinase (BCKDK) and its substrate mitochondrial branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase E1α (BCKDHA).ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that PRSS55 interacts with BCKDK and BCKDHA, and regulates BCAA metabolism and energy homeostasis, thereby facilitating sperm migration. Our study provides a biological rationale for PRSS55 as a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of male infertility in clinical.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01511-w.Keywords: Male infertility, Sperm migration, Energy metabolism, Proteomics

Background

An estimated 16% of reproductive-age couples suffer from infertility [1], and male factor infertility contributes to almost half of these cases [2]. The normal reproductive function requires spermatogenesis in testes, maturation through the epididymis, sperm migration across the female reproductive tract, biochemical and morphological modifications to sperm (capacitation), and sperm-oocyte interaction to work in concert [3, 4]. A sufficient supply of energy is required for all these processes. ATP generation via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation that taken place in mitochondria constitutes the core of cellular energy metabolism [5]. In this process, acetyl-CoA is oxidized to CO₂ through eight enzymatic steps of the TCA cycle, producing NADH and FADH₂, which subsequently fuel ATP synthesis during oxidative phosphorylation [6]. Metabolites of the TCA cycle including acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA and citrate have been documented to significantly impact cellular energetics and intermediary metabolism [7, 8]. Efficient oxidative phosphorylation critically depends on an intact mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm, MMP), the primary electrical component of the chemiosmotic proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane [9]. In mammals, each mature sperm contains 50-70 mitochondria with a condensed morphology and compacted matrix, which guarantees sufficient energy production to power sperm motility [10]. Fully functional mitochondria with intact morphology are prerequisites for sperm acrosin activity and acrosome reaction capability as well [11]. Thus, healthy mitochondria are indispensable for ATP generation, which in turn supports sperm motility and fertilization capacity.

Serine proteases comprise about one-third of known proteases, featuring their uniquely activated serine residue to catalyze peptide bond hydrolysis [12]. Members of this family are highly diversified in structure and function, playing essential physiological roles in mammalian growth and development [13]. In recent years, an increasing number of trypsin-like serine peptidases (clan PA family S1 subfamily A) and their inhibitors have been identified in the reproductive system. [14, 15]. The trypsin-like protease activity of Acrosin (located in the acrosome) is critical for sperm to penetrate the cumulus matrix and zona pellucida (ZP) in vitro [16]. Sperm-specific serine protease TMPRSS12 regulates sperm-uterotubal junction (UTJ) migration and ZP binding [17]. It has been reported that the knockout of Tmprss12 leads to a decrease in cellular MMP and ATP levels, which impacts sperm motility [18]. Our previous research demonstrated that another two putative serine proteases, PRSS37 and PRSS55, are both indispensable for male fertility. Sperms from Prss37−/− and Prss55−/− mice both exhibited significant defects in in vivo sperm migration across the UTJ and sperm binding to the ZP [19, 20]. We further demonstrated that PRSS37 was engaged in energy metabolism, as disruption of PRSS37 led to a marked reduction in intermediate metabolites of the TCA cycle (citrate and fumarate), as well as a decrease in the terminal metabolite ATP [21]. Given that mitochondria are the main source of ATP, their proper function is essential for sperm motility, we hypothesized that PRSS55 may similarly play key role in regulating energy metabolism and contribute to male fertility. A recent study by Zhu et al. [22], also supports the notion that PRSS55 may be a potential causative gene for human male infertility [23]. They found sperm from Prss55−/− mice showed decreased expression of electron transfer chain molecules and lower ATP contents. They speculated that there is a link between PRSS55 and type II muscle myosin in the testis, which is involved in many processes requiring cell migration [22]. However, the molecular mechanism of PRSS55 remains elusive.

To clarify the underlying mechanism of PRSS55 and its biological roles in male fertility, in this study, we employed a multi-omics analysis based on proteomics, metabolomics, and interactomics to systematically explore the mode of action of PRSS55 at the molecular level. Metabolomic analysis confirmed significant accumulation of BCAAs in Prss55−/− deletion mice. Subsequent IP-MS and Co-IP analyses demonstrated that PRSS55 regulates BCAA metabolism and directly interacts with BCKDK and BCKDHA in mouse testes and sperm. By uncovering the molecular mechanisms underlying PRSS55-mediated regulation of mitochondrial function, our findings may provide novel insights into sperm motility and the broader role of PRSS55 in male infertility.

Materials and methods

Mice

The mouse strain used in this study was maintained on a mixed 129 Sv/C57BL/6 background. The generation of Prss55−/− and V5-PRSS55-Flag knock-in mice (Prss55KI/KI) have been previously described [20, 24]. Mice of all genotypes were housed in specific pathogen-free and controlled conditions (22-24 °C, 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to water and standard rodent chow diet. All the protocols of animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Rui-Jin Hospital Affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of mature spermatozoa

The mature sperm collection from the caudal epididymis was adapted from our previously published study [20]. In brief, wild-type (wt) and Prss55−/− male mice at 8 weeks old were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Sperm cells were acquired from dissected caudal epididymis and incubated in Toyoda, Yokoyama, Hoshi (TYH) medium for 30 min at 37 °C in the air with 5% CO2, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min at RT. The supernatant was carefully aspirated and discarded and samples were rinsed in 1 × PBS three times. The sperm samples were then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at - 80 °C until subsequent analyses.

In vitro sperm migration assay

In vitro sperm migration assay was conducted by a modification of established methods [25]. In both genotypes, the mature sperm from four mice were collected from the caudal epididymis as previously described. Sperm sample was counted using Neubauer hemocytometer and adjusted into 1.6 × 107 per mL. After incubation in TYH medium for 1 h for capacitation at 37 °C, 100 μL motile sperm were collected from the top and counted again. Borosilicate capillary micro glass slides (0.20 × 4.0 mm, 50 mm, #63,842-05, Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) were perfused with 10 mg/mL methyl cellulose viscosity: 4000 cP (MC4000, M0512, Sigma-Aldrich) in TYH to stimulate cervical mucus viscosity. The capillary slides were then placed vertically in the tubes containing motile sperm samples. Sperm were incubated 37 °C for 30 min for migration. The sperm counts that migrate over 1 cm from the base of the capillary slide were determined by counting under a microscope (Eclipse Ti-S, Nikon). The assessment was performed independently by two blinded authors. Final count was normalized to the starting capacitated sperm count.

In vitro adenosine triphosphate (ATP) measurement

ATP levels in testes and spermatozoa of wt and Prss55−/− mice, and HEK293T cells were detected using Enhanced ATP Detection Kit (S0027, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the instructions. Briefly, samples were lysed using ATP lysis buffer, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then incubated with the ATP detection working solution. ATP levels were quantified on a BioTek Synergy H1 hybrid reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA), calculated based on a calibration curve, and normalized to total protein concentration. Testes and sperm from at least three mice from wt and Prss55−/− mice, respectively, were used for each measurement. Liver tissues of both genotypes served as negative controls.

In Vitro NAD+ /NADH Measurement

NAD+ /NADH levels in testes of wt and Prss55−/− mice, and HEK293T cells were detected using NAD+ /NADH Assay Kit with WST-8 (S0175, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the instructions. Briefly, samples were lysed using NAD+ /NADH extraction buffer, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Extracted samples were separated into two aliquots. One was used to measure total levels of NAD+ and NADH (as NADtotal). For the measurement of NADH, samples were heated at 60 °C for 30 min to decompose NAD + . Samples were then incubated with NADH developing solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek hybrid reader and NAD+ and NADH contents were calculated using a standard curve of purified NADH, and normalized to total protein concentration. The levels of NAD+ was calculated with the equation NAD+ = NADtotal-NADH. Testes from at least three mice from wt and Prss55−/− mice, respectively, were used for each measurement.

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

Mitochondrial membrane potential of sperm from wt and Prss55−/− mice was detected using mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (C2003S, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the instructions. Briefly, sperm were incubated with JC-1 solution for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by two rinses with JC-1 buffer. The JC-1 signal was analyzed by flow cytometry, and images were acquired using a BioTek Cytation 5 cell imaging reader. The mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated as the ratio of red (JC-1 polymer) to green (JC-1 monomer) fluorescence. Sperm from at least three mice from wt and Prss55−/− mice, respectively, were used for each measurement.

Plasmid construction, cell culture and transfection

The construction of plasmid PRSS55-EGFP has been previously described [20]. The full-length cDNAs of PRSS55, BCKDK and DBT were cloned into pcDNA3.1(-) b and pCAG vectors, respectivly. BCKDK and DBT were fused with HA-tag at the C-terminus. Plasmid pCMV-Bckdha(mouse)-FLAG was obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid, China. All plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing. Primers for plasmid construction are listed in Table S2. HEK293T and NIH-3T3 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, SH30243.01, HyClone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, FBSV500, Dongling Biotech, Suzhou, China) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Plasmid transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (L3000, Invitrogen) at a density of 60-70% confluence.

Mitochondria isolation

Mitochondrial fraction was isolated by using Mitochondria Isolation Kit for Cultured Cells (ab110171, Abcam) according to the manufactures’ instruction. Briefly, cells were collected and rinsed in NKM buffer provided for three times. Then cells were resuspended in 6 packed cell volumes of homogenization buffer and lysed in a pre-cooled Dounce Homogenizer. After mixed with 1 packed cell volume of 2 M sucrose solution, cell lysate was centrifuged at 1200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was washed three times with mitochondrial suspension buffer at 9000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C for 3 times. The mitochondria fraction was finally collected at 7000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Plasmid pcDNA3.1(-) b-PRSS55 and empty plasmid (pcDNA3.1(-) b) transfected HEK293T cells and single-cell suspension from whole testis were used. Mitochondrial pellets and total cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer. The purity of the fractions was determined by immunoblotting using COXIV (1:1000, ab14744, Abcam) as a mitochondrial marker, Tubulin (1:1000, 11224-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) as a cytosolic marker, and Histone H2A (1:1000, #12349, Cell Signaling Technology) as a nuclear marker.

Preparation of testicular single-cell suspension

Testicular single-cell suspensions were prepared from bilateral testes of adult age-matched wt and Prss55−/− mice by grinding through a 70 μm cell strainer. Then samples were further separated by Percoll density gradient centrifugation as described previously [21]. Isolated layer 1 (L1), layer 2 (L2), and layer 3 (L3) cells were subsequently employed for flow cytometry, propidium iodide staining, immunoblotting, and mass spectrometry analyses.

Flow cytometry analysis and propidium iodide (PI) nuclear staining

Three layers of the testicular cells were fixed in 75% alcohol at 4℃ overnight. The next day, samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. Cells were rinsed three times with 1 × PBS, and then treated with PI (P4170, Sigma-Aldrich, 50 μg/μL) and RNase A (B600476, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, 100 μg/μL) for 30 min in an ice bath. The DNA types of stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACS, Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

Immunofluorescence

NIH-3T3 cells were collected and transfected to create cell-climbing pieces. Mitochondria in live cells were treated with MitoTraker Red (M7512, Invitrogen, 100 nM) at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, slices, sperm, and testicular cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min. Samples were rinsed with 1 × PBS and blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Sigma-Aldrich). Then, samples were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S1) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Table S1) for 1 h at room temperature (RT), then co-labeled with DAPI (D9542, Sigma-Aldrich, 1 μg/mL) and/or fluorescein Peanut Agglutinin (PNA) (FL-1071Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA, 5 μg/mL) for 1 h at RT. Cells were finally mounted with Dako fluorescence mounting medium (S3023, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark), covered with a coverslip, and observed under a confocal microscope (LSM880, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Colocalization image analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institute of Mental Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

Immunoblotting

The total proteins from each testicular fraction and sperm of both genotypes and were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (4,693,159,001 and 4,693,159,001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in an ice bath for 30 min. Transfected HEK293T cells were collected and extracted with NP-40 lysis buffer (0.5% NP-40, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl) with protease inhibitor cocktails in an ice bath for 30 min. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The total protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (23,225, Pierce BCA Protein assay kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Samples were then electrophoresed under reducing conditions in SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (10,600,001, Amersham, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Blots were blocked using 5% non-fat milk (LP0031, Oxoid Ltd, UK) in PBS for 1 h at RT, and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 1 × PBST and incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. β-Actin (1:1000, sc-1615, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and GAPDH (1:1000, D110016, Sangon Biotech) were used as protein loading controls. PRSS55 antibody (1:500, MA43587) was from Aviva Systems Biology; BCKDK (1:1000, ab151297) antibody and BCKDHA (1:1000, ab126173) antibody were from Abcam. Full list of antibodies used were supplemented in Table S1. Specific protein bands were visualized with the Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). The experiment was repeated at least three times and representative results are shown. Testes and sperm from at least three mice from wt and Prss55−/− mice, respectively, were used for each measurement.

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) analysis

Testicular layer 3 cells and mature sperm samples from wt and Prss55−/− mice were treated with the same protocol. Each set of experiments was replicated in triplicate. Briefly, samples were denatured by incubation in 2% SDS buffer with 50 mM dithiothreitol at RT for 20 min followed by heating at 100℃ for 5 min. Proteins were alkylated with 200 mM iodoacetamide in the dark at RT for 1 h. Then ice-cold acetone was added to precipitate proteins at − 20 °C overnight. Protein digestion and preparation were performed as described in [21]. To generate the spectral library, data-dependent acquisition (DDA) was performed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer connected to an Easy-nLC 1200 liquid chromatography system through an Easy Spray (all Thermo Fisher Scientific). The sample was separated using a linear gradient of 95% solvent A (0.1% formic acid/2% acetonitrile) to 28% solvent B (0.1% formic acid/80% acetonitrile) for 2 h at a flow rate of 250 nL/min at 50 °C. Raw DIA-MS data were analyzed using the Pulsar search engine (Spectronaut Pulsar, Biognosys, Schlieren, Switzerland) by searching against the mouse Uniprot FASTA database with default parameters. Then, the entire DIA-MS data acquisition was performed by compiling the procedure of DDA runs including LC linear gradient using the same parameters as in [21].

DDA spectra were analyzed using Spectronaut Pulsar X software (Biognosys) with default settings for targeted DIA analysis. Briefly, retention time prediction type was set to dynamic iRT, with correction factors applied to window 1. MS2-level interference correction was enabled, while systematic variance underwent local normalization strategy. We implemented the mProphet approach to control false discovery rates (FDR) at both peptide precursor (1%) and protein levels (1%). The minimum number of unique peptides per protein was ≥ 2; the sequence coverage was 0.5–96.6% and average level is 34.8%. Protein intensities were derived from summed peptide intensities, calculated via MS2 fragment ion peak areas. A differentially expressed protein (DEP) was defined as a fold change > 1.5 and a corrected P ≤ 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was used to explore cellular components, molecular function, and biological process terms of the DEPs (http://www.geneontology.org). A significantly enriched GO term in DEPs was selected as the corrected P ≤ 0.05 after Bonferroni correction. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database was performed to investigate the pathway enrichment of DEPs (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). A significant KEGG enriched pathway was determined with a P ≤ 0.05. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA, Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands) was used to identify significant canonical pathways. The overall activation/inhibition states of canonical pathways are predicted based on a z-score algorithm that compared our dataset with the QIAGEN Knowledge Base.

Metabolomics analysis by UPLC-Q-TOF/MS

The untargeted and targeted metabolomics analysis of testes and sperm from wt and Prss55−/− mice were performed by the mass spectrometry platform of the national facility for translational medicine (Shanghai, China). For the untargeted metabolomics, the testes form 6 wt mice and 8 Prss55−/− mice were used. For the targeted metabolomics, testes were from 4 pairs of wt and Prss55−/− mice and sperm were from 3 pairs of wt and Prss55−/− mice were used. Briefly, metabolic profiling employed an LC-30A HPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) coupled to a Triple Q-TOF 6600 + mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA). Chromatographic separation utilized an Acquity HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters). Lyophilized specimens underwent reconstitution in 100 μL acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v), with 2 μL injected for analysis. Gradient elution was carried out using mobile phases A (water containing 0.1% formic acid, v/v) and B (acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid). The gradient program initiated at 1% B (0–1.5 min), ramped linearly to 99% B (1.5-13 min), maintained for 3.5 min (13–16.5 min), returned to initial conditions in 0.1 min (16.5-16.6 min), and re-equilibrated for 3.4 min (16.6-20 min). Operating parameters included: Flow rate: 0.3 mL/min; column temperature: 40 °C; ESI voltages: + 5.5 kV (positive), - 4.5 kV (negative); Source temperature: 550 °C; Gas pressures (psi): curtain (40), GS1 (55), GS2 (55). Data acquisition implemented information-dependent acquisition (IDA) triggering MS/MS scans for the ten most intense precursors (60-1000 m/z) per cycle. Collision energies were applied at ± 35 V with with a collision energy spread of 15 V for comprehensive metabolite identification. Raw mass spectrometry data were analyzed using SCIEX OS software. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching against an in-house spectral library based on accurate mass, retention time, and MS/MS spectral similarity. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching against an in-house spectral library based on accurate mass, retention time, and MS/MS spectral similarity. The KEGG pathway analysis of differential metabolites (fold change > 1.2 and FDR ≤ 0.1) was performed with MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

Levels of L-valine, L-leucine and L-isoleucine from Sigma Supelco and LGC standards (Sesto San Giovanni, Milan, Italy) and testes and sperm samples were quantified by targeted metabolomics. Standard curves were established for targeted metabolite quantification. Linearity assessment employed concentration gradients as the horizontal axis and analyte-to-internal standard peak area ratios as the vertical axis. All calibration curves exhibited correlation coefficients exceeding 0.99, confirming optimal linearity.

Quantification of valine, leucine and isoleucine by ELISA

The concentration of valine, leucine, and isoleucine in testes and mature sperm from wt and Prss55−/− mice was detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (ZLm00135, ZLm00246, and ZL0089, respectively, Shanghai Zhili Biological Technology Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China) according to the instructions. Briefly, testes and sperm were collected and lysed in a pre-cooled Dounce Homogenizer. Cell lysate was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was subjected into ELISA microplates and incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugates for 1 h at 37 °C. Then the solution was discarded and the plates were washed for 5 times with washing buffer. Substrate solution was added and incubated at dark for 15 min at 37 °C. After mixed with the stop solution, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek hybrid reader. The content of valine, leucine and isoleucine was calculated using their respective calibration curves, and normalized to total protein concentration.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

IP and Co-IP were performed using Magnetic Bead separation according to the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, the mouse testicular cells and HEK293T cells were lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer with protease inhibitor cocktails in an ice bath for 30 min. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. For lysates from wild-type and Prss55 knock-in mice, 1 mg of testicular protein was incubated with 50 μl anti-V5 mAb magnetic beads (M167-11, MBL, Nagoya, Japan) overnight at 4℃. For lysates from HEK293T cells, 1 mg of protein was incubated with 50 μl anti-GFP and anti-HA mAb magnetic beads (D153-11, MBL and 88,837, Thermo Fisher Scientific, respectively) overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed five times with NP-40 lysis buffer with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) before elution by boiling and immediate analysis by western blotting. Antibodies used were listed in Table S1.

Co-IP coupled DIA-based quantitative proteomic analysis

V5-IP precipitates (beads) from wild-type and knock-in mice were prepared before MS identification. The preparation of the precipitates and subsequent identification by MS were performed as previously reported [24]. The MS data generated by Triple TOF 5600 (SCIEX, USA) were retrieved by ProteinPilot. Paragon was used as the algorithm of retrieval. The database used for retrieval was the mouse proteome reference database in Uniprot. Reverse library entries, proteins identified solely as modified forms, and common contaminating proteins were excluded from the search results. The remaining identified proteins and their quantitative information were employed for subsequent analysis. The ratio of protein spectral number (ratio) and the double sample equal variance t-test significance (P) were calculated.

Statistical analysis

All experiments in vitro and animal tests were performed and assessed in a blinded-fashion. At least three biological replicates were performed. All the animal experiments and cell assay data were analyzed for normality by Shapiro-Wilk test through the GraphPad Prism Version 9.0. All data are presented as mean ± standard error (SEM) (n ≥ 3), and statistical differences between the two groups were determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η2) by the GraphPad Prism Version 9.0., defined as trivial (≤ 0.04); minimum (0.04-0.25); moderate (0.25-0.64); and strong (> 0.64).

Results

PRSS55 deletion leads to impaired mitochondrial function in mouse testes and sperm

Our previous research demonstrated that Prss55−/− sperm failed to traverse the UTJ in vivo [20]. To investigate whether this phenotype is due to reduced motility caused by insufficient energy supply, we utilized methyl cellulose (MC4000) solution instead of regular TYH buffer in CASA [25], thereby simulating the physical properties of cervical mucus for in vitro analysis of sperm performance. In the viscous MC4000 solution, capacitated Prss55−/− sperm exhibited a significant motility deficit in penetrating as far as wt sperm (η2 = 0.7893; P = 0.0032, Fig. 1A and S1A). These findings may reflect their impaired phenotype in vivo and prompt further inspection into the correlation between PRSS55 and energy supply.

Capacitated sperm that migrate over 1 cm from the bottom of the capillary slide were counted under a microscope, 200 × magnification. Sperm were highlighted in white circle. B ATP levels in Prss55−/− testes and sperm is shown as mean ± SE (n = 3). The liver in which PRSS55 is not expressed was used as unrelated control. C NAD+ , NADH levels and NAD+ /NADH ratio in wt and Prss55−/− testicular cells were determined and presented as mean ± SE (n = 3). D The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of spermatozoa from wt and Prss55−/− mice was determined using JC-1 probes (n = 4). JC-1 polymer/JC-1 monomer fluorescence ratios were calculated and shown as mean ± SE (n = 4). (*, P ≤ 0.05, **, P ≤ 0.01, ***, P ≤ 0.001, n.s., no significant difference.)

Mitochondria are the center for cellular energy metabolism. Therefore, to further assess changes in energy metabolism between wt and Prss55−/− mice, we first measured the intermediate metabolites NAD + /NADH and the end product ATP form testes. We also detected the ATP levels and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of sperm from wt and Prss55−/− mice. We demonstrated a substantial reduction in ATP levels in both Prss55−/− testes (η2 = 0.6804; P = 0.0433) and sperm samples (η2 = 0.7893; P = 0.0032) compared to wt counterparts (Fig. 1B). As an unrelated control, no difference in liver ATP content (η2 = 0.0024; P = 0.9267) was found between the two genotypes. In Prss55−/− testes, the drop of NAD+ level (η2 = 0.8166; P = 0.0135) was accompanied by a rise of NADH level (η2 = 0.9170; P = 0.0027), resulting in a low NAD+ to NADH ratio (Fig. 1C). The abnormal NAD + /NADH ratio (η2 = 0.7676; P = 0.0221) implied that the physiological functions of the glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation were dysregulated and their homeostatic state was disrupted. Subsequently, we noticed a reduction in MMP of Prss55−/− sperm (η2 = 0.3952; P = 0.0316) was detected by JC-1 staining (Fig. 1D and S1B), which is essential to maintain the physiological functions of mitochondria. These findings demonstrated an energy deficit and metabolic imbalance occurred in testes and sperm after Prss55 depletion.

PRSS55 is localized in mitochondria and required for energy metabolism in transfected cells

As previously reported by other researchers [22], PRSS55 appears to play a role in regulating mitochondrial function and energy homeostasis in mouse testicular cells and sperm. Given its potential role in mitochondrial regulation, we next investigated whether PRSS55 localizes to mitochondria. Therefore, NIH-3T3 cells were transfected with a PRSS55 expression vector and subsequently subjected to dual labeling with anti-PRSS55 and MitoTracker, a fluorescent dye that selectively accumulates in mitochondria. The results demonstrated that PRSS55 is evidently co-localized with mitochondria in the transfected cells (Fig. 2A). Then we conducted immunofluorescence assay to determine the location of PRSS55 in matured sperm. As shown in Fig. 2B, PRSS55 protein shows an extensive colocalization with the mitochondrial marker protein COXIV in the midpiece of the sperm tail. In accordance with this observation, PRSS55 was identified and found to be particularly abundant in the lysate of the mitochondrial fraction isolated from HEK293T cells transfected with a PRSS55 expression vector (Fig. 2C). The results suggested that PRSS55 may function as a mitochondrial protein, potentially in testicular cells and sperm as well, thereby facilitating energy supply. As anticipated, ATP production (η2 = 0.8484; P = 0.0012) were significantly increased in PRSS55-overexpressing HEK293T cells compared to the empty control (Fig. 2D). The observed increase in NAD + levels (η2 = 0.2585; P = 0.2438) and the NAD + /NADH ratio (η2 = 0.5165; P = 0.0688), together with the decrease in NADH levels (η2 = 0.7521; P = 0.0115) (Fig. 2E) and enhanced ATP production, point to improved mitochondrial function in the overexpressing cells, supporting the involvement of PRSS55 in mitochondrial energy metabolism and cellular energy regulation. Therefore, we proceeded to investigate in detail the potential role of PRSS55 in energy metabolism pathways.

Differential protein expression profiles between wt and prss55−/− testicular layer 3 cells (TL3 cells) and spermatozoa

PRSS55 initiated its expression in the middle stages of spermatogenesis (stage V), while it was absent at stages I-IV and supporting cells such as Leydig and Sertoli cells [20]. To further explore differential protein expression between wt and Prss55−/− mice, we first examined the distribution pattern of PRSS55 during spermatogenesis. Immunofluorescence staining of PRSS55 and PNA indicated its localization at the luminal side of the seminiferous tubules (Fig. 3A and Fig. S2A), corresponding to the elongated spermatids. We isolated the PRSS55 enriched testicular cells using Percoll gradient centrifugation (Fig. 3B). There was no significant variation in cell composition of each layer between wt and Prss55−/− mice, according to flow cytometry DNA ploidy analysis and nuclear propidium iodide (PI) staining (Fig. 3C). We discovered that layer 1 (L1) was mainly cell debris, layer 2 (L2) was almost round spermatozoa, and layer 3 (L3) was chiefly elongating spermatid cells based on morphological analysis (Fig. S2B, C) [26, 27]. Likewise, the immunoblotting study revealed that PRSS55 expression was predominantly concentrated in the layer 3 cells compared to the other two (Fig. 3D). This phenomenon was consistent with our previous finding that PRSS55 was mainly exhibited in the round and elongating spermatids. Therefore, testicular Layer 3 cells (TL3 cells) samples were more suitable for subsequent proteomics analysis.

We employed a DIA-based quantitative method to screen the global proteomic profiles of wt and Prss55−/− TL3 cells as well as mature sperm samples (Fig. 3). For TL3 cells, we identified a total of 6869 proteins, and to determine which of these proteins were expressed significantly different between the two groups, we required a fold-change (FC > 1.5) and a corrected P (P ≤ 0.05), yielding a total of 1593 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) (Table S3). In Prss55−/− TL3 cells samples, 865 DEPs were up-regulated compared to wt controls, while 728 DEPs were down-regulated (Fig. 3E). DEPs were also visualized with a heatmap after hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 3G). For sperm, we quantified 711 DEPs out of 3095 proteins (Table S3), including 79 up-regulated DEPs and 632 down-regulated DEPs, vis-à-vis the wt controls (Fig. 3F). The heatmap represented the hierarchical clustering of DEPs (Fig. 3H). The fluctuation in protein abundance hinted a potential regulatory role of PRSS55 during spermatogenesis.

DEPs in Prss55−/− TL3 cells and sperm are enriched in metabolic pathways

We conducted Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) canonical pathway enrichment to analyze the molecular functions of DEPs in TL3 cells and sperm separately (Fig. 4A, B). Remarkably, the vast majority of DEPs annotated in the BP category are involved in energy metabolism pathways, representing metabolic process (GO:0008152), single-organism metabolic process (GO:0044710), small molecule metabolic process (GO:0044281). During the late stages of sperm development, many proteins are no longer expressed and discarded during morphological remodeling, while the retained DEPs may contribute to PRSS55-related regulation, supporting the continuous and dynamic process of spermatogenesis. Accordingly, as the Venn plot shown, we found 153 common DEPs synchronously regulated in both TL3 cells and sperm, including 14 up-regulated DEPs and 139 down-regulated DEPs (Fig. 4C, Table S3). Then, we proceeded with Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) to investigate the status of canonical metabolism pathways affected by common DEPs. IPA z-score infers onto activation (positive z-score) or inhibition (negative z-score) of the significant enriched pathways. Based on IPA algorithm (Fig. 4D), severe dysfunction of mitochondria in Prss55−/− mice was indicated. Other prominent predicted canonical metabolic pathways disrupted in both TL3 cells and sperm include oxidative phosphorylation, as well as isoleucine and valine degradation. Collectively, as anticipated, bioinformatic analyses indicate that PRSS55 plays a role in the energy metabolism of spermatogenesis and mature sperm, particularly in oxidative phosphorylation and branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism.

Branched chain amino acids were accumulated in Prss55−/− testes and sperm

Our proteomics analysis has revealed that DEPs in TL3 cells from Prss55−/− mice were mainly enriched in the BCAA degradation pathway, while those in sperm were associated with oxidative phosphorylation. Based on these findings, we further performed metabolomic analyses. In total, we detected 173 metabolites from untargeted metabolomics (Fig. 5A). Orthogonal partial least squares discrimination analysis (OPLS-DA) was carried out as an exploratory analysis of this metabolomics data set, and the resulting OPLS-DA model was proved valid (Fig. 5B). The metabolites with fold change ≥ 1.2 and FDR ≤ 0.1 were selected as differential metabolites. Here, we obtained 42 up-regulated metabolites and 2 down-regulated metabolites from Prss55−/− testes (Fig. 5C).

Among them, leucine and its metabolite leucinic acid [28] were found to have significantly accumulated (Table 1). We also detected another BCAA, isoleucine, which increased considerably in abundance (fold-change = 1.8958) with an FDR slightly above the threshold (FDR = 0.1082). Furthermore, it was observed that the level of NAD + was markedly diminished in comparison to the wild-type, which was consistent with the in vitro NAD + determination (Fig. 1C), suggesting a deficiency in energy intermediates in the knock-outs. KEGG analysis of the differential metabolites between wt and Prss55−/− testes revealed the significantly enriched metabolism pathways of riboflavin, carbohydrates, amino acids, glycerophospholipid, etc. Of note, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis and degradation pathways were annotated as well (Fig. 5D). Therefore, in subsequent targeted metabolomics analysis, we quantified the level of valine, leucine, and isoleucine using their respective standards. As expected, we detected a significant accumulation of valine (η2 = 0.8599; P = 0.0009), leucine (η2 = 0.7998; P = 0.0027) and isoleucine (η2 = 0.7869; P = 0.0033) in Prss55−/− testes (Fig. 5E) and the same trend in the sperm (valine, η2 = 0.3587; P = 0.2078; leucine, η2 = 0.4132; P = 0.3779; isoleucine, η2 = 0.4039; P = 0.1446; Fig. 5F). Next, ELISA was employed to validate the mass spectrometry results (Fig. 5G, H). In Prss55−/− testes, valine (η2 = 0.5851; P = 0.0451) and isoleucine (η2 = 0.8383; P = 0.0038) are remarkably accumulated. In Prss55−/− sperm, valine (η2 = 0.5093; P = 0.0468) and isoleucine (η2 = 0.5314; P = 0.0402) are also statistically increased. This same tendency was also observed in leucine from Prss55−/− testes (η2 = 0.2707, P = 0. 2312) and sperm samples (η2 = 0.3922, P = 0. 0967). Combined with proteomics analysis, these data hinted that PRSS55 may maintain energy homeostasis in the mouse testes through its regulatory effects on multiple metabolic pathways, in particularly BCAA metabolism.

PRSS55 interacts with BCKDK and BCKDHA

To explore the molecular mechanisms by which PRSS55 regulates mitochondrial energy metabolism, we carried out anti-V5 immunoprecipitation using protein extracts from Prss55KI/KI (KI) mouse testes tissues, with wild-type samples serving as negative controls. As shown in Fig. 6A, V5-tagged PRSS55 was detected in V5 precipitates by immunoblotting using anti-V5 and anti-PRSS55 antibodies, respectively. To identify the protein co-immunoprecipitated with V5-PRSS55, the precipitates were subjected to quantitative mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis. The results showed that a total of 56 proteins were identified in the V5-IP precipitates. A volcano plot was plotted using the log2 ratio (FC) and the negative logarithmic P (- log10 (P)) (Fig. 6B). Compared to wt controls, 8 proteins were significantly enriched in V5-PRSS55 precipitates (FC > 1 and P ≤ 0.05) (Table 2). Of the eight enriched proteins, DBT (lipoamide acyltransferase component of the branched-chain alpha-keto acid dehydrogenase complex, mitochondrial) and BCKDK (branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase kinase) stand out as mitochondrial proteins involved in the BCAA metabolism pathway, attracting special attention due to the aforementioned proteomics and metabolomics findings (Fig. 6B).

To confirm the interaction capacity between PRSS55 and the candidate interacting proteins, we conducted immunoprecipitation with anti-GFP from the cell lysates from PRSS55-EGFP transfected HEK293T cells and control (empty vector). As expected, the immunoblotting results demonstrated that both endogenous DBT and BCKDK were pulled down by PRSS55-EGFP (Fig. 6C). Co-IP from HEK293T cell lysates co-expressing PRSS55-EGFP and DBT-HA or BCKDK-HA was performed using two different tag antibodies, respectively. Subsequent immunoblotting showed that only the tagged proteins were able to be precipitated by PRSS55 and BCKDK (Fig. 6D, E), but not by DBT (Fig. S3A, B). Immunofluorescence staining shows co-localization of PRSS55 and BCKDK in wild-type mouse testicular cells (Fig. S3C). These results suggested an interaction between PRSS55 and BCKDK.

BCKDK is responsible for the post-translational control of the E1α subunit of mitochondrial branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKDHA), which is the rate-limiting enzyme in the BCAA catabolism pathway. To investigate the potential regulatory effect of PRSS55 and BCKDK on BCKDHA function, we measured the endogenous protein levels of BCKDHA following the overexpression of PRSS55 and/or BCKDK. As shown in Fig. 6F, the dual overexpression of PRSS55 and BCKDK led to a significant increase in endogenous BCKDHA levels (FC = 149%; η2 = 0.9020; P = 0.0037). When compared to the individual overexpression of PRSS55 or BCKDK, BCKDHA accumulation was also significantly higher, with an FC of 115% (η2 = 0.9165; P = 0.0027) for PRSS55 overexpression and an FC of 120% (η2 = 0.7541; P = 0.0249) for BCKDK overexpression, respectively. Next, Immunoprecipitation with anti-GFP antibodies indicated that endogenous BCKDHA were interacted with PRSS55-EGFP fusion protein in HEK293T cells (Fig. 6G). Subsequent reciprocal IP from HEK293T cell lysates co-expressing PRSS55-Myc and BCKDHA-Flag or BCKDHA-Flag and PRSS55-EGFP were performed using two different tag antibodies, respectively. Immunoblotting showed that only the tagged proteins were able to be precipitated by PRSS55 and BCKDHA (Fig. 6H, I), indicating a protein-protein interaction between PRSS55 and BCKDHA. To further examine how PRSS55 may regulate and BCKDK and BCKDHA function, we assessed the abundance and activity of BCKDK and BCKDHA in isolated mitochondria between wt and Prss55−/− testes. As shown in Fig. 6J, PRSS55 is enriched in the mitochondrial fraction. Notably, in the absence of PRSS55, we observed a significant increase in BCKDK (FC = 168%; η2 = 0.7806; P = 0.0196) and p-BCKDHA levels (FC = 163%; η2 = 0.8657; P = 0.0071), suggesting a potential inhibition of BCKDHA activity. Taken together, our results implied that PRSS55 might be involved in BCKDK-BCKDHA mediated regulation of BCAA metabolism by interacting with them.

Discussion

Our previous study found that the testis-specific protein PRSS55 is indispensable for male fertility by contributing to sperm-UTJ migration and sperm-oocyte binding [20]. PRSS55 is highly conserved across mammalian evolution [20], further suggesting that it possesses critical biological functions. Coincidentally, a homozygous mutation in PRSS55 was recently found to be a potential cause of infertility in male patients [23]. In our exploration, capacitated Prss55−/− sperm exhibited a significant motility deficit in a stimulated viscous solution (Fig. 1A). Sperm capacitation is the process by which sperm undergo physiological changes to acquire the ability to initiate the acrosome reaction and penetrate the ZP for fertilization. It takes place in the female reproductive tract, specifically in the uterus and the fallopian tubes [29]. Capacitation-associated processes necessitate additional energy to support enhanced mobility [30]. This phenomenon was consistent with our previously reported phenotype, in which sperm from Prss55−/− mice failed to penetrate the UTJ, prompting us to investigate the underlying mechanisms.

Sufficient energy supply is a prerequisite for successful fertilization [4, 5, 31]. The physiological mechanisms that govern energy balance are intertwined with those that govern reproduction, and together these mechanisms optimally manage reproductive success under fluctuating metabolic conditions [32]. PRSS55 expression begins in elongated spermatids and persists in mature sperm [20]. Since sperm formation and motility relies on mitochondrial energy production, the timely expression of PRSS55 appears to play a crucial role in regulating mitochondrial energy metabolism. Therefore, the low ATP content of Prss55−/− testes and sperm (Fig. 1B) might be a causative factor for the deficiency of sperm-UTJ migration [20]. Sperm utilize both glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation for the production of ATP [33]. NAD+ , a common intermediate of mitochondrial pathways including glycolysis, TCA cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation, was at a lower level than its wild-type testes (Fig. 1C). The NAD(H) steady state is necessary for maintaining cellular redox balance, neutralizing Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) and generating energy [34]. The accumulation of NADH and low NAD+ /NADH ratio (Fig. 1C) were characteristic features of reductive stress and were associated with mitochondrial disorders [35]. It is noteworthy that spermatozoa from Prss55−/− mice exhibited lower MMP compared to wt mice (Fig. 1D), indicating a loss of the mitochondrial electron transport chain and low energy metabolism, thereby compromised sperm motility [36]. The strong connection between PRSS55 and energy metabolism led us to hypothesize that PRSS55 is involved in regulating processes within the mitochondria. The subcellular localization indicates functional specialization. In sperm and transfected NIH3T3 and HEK293 cells, we demonstrated that overexpressed PRSS55 is located in mitochondria by immunofluorescence staining and immunoblotting assays (Fig. 2A-C). We also detected significantly enriched PRSS55 band in isolated mitochondria fraction from testicular cells than total cell lysate (Fig. 6J). These results suggest that PRSS55 is predominantly localized in mitochondria. Moreover, overexpression of PRSS55 effectively rescued mitochondrial dysfunction as evidenced by restored ATP levels and improve NAD + /NADH redox status (Fig. 2D, E). The in vitro results collectively support the notion that that PRSS55 enhances energy production. However, the specific metabolic pathway through which this regulation occurs remains unknown. Therefore, we conducted a series of differential proteomics and metabolomics analyses to explore the regulatory pathways that PRSS55 might be involved in.

Based on DIA data, we discovered the deterioration of metabolism pathways (particularly BCAA degradation) and their downstream TCA cycles and oxidative phosphorylation in Prss55−/− TL3 cells and sperm (Figs. 3, 4). In targeted metabolomics and subsequent ELISA analyses, we quantified that valine, leucine and isoleucine were accumulated in both Prss55−/− testes and sperm samples. Valine, leucine and isoleucine belong to the class of branched chain amino acids classified as essential amino acids, which are widely distributed throughout the body. In recent years, many literatures have reported that cellular energy supply is closely related to BCAA metabolism [37–40]. In the reproductive system, it has been reported that a combined dietary supplement of leucine, isoleucine, and valine can positively enhance sperm motility and testosterone production in mice [41]. Among the BCAAs, leucine has been found to promote sperm migration capacity through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in zebrafish [42]. On the other hand, the accumulation of BCAAs has been linked to various metabolic disorders [43]. Elevated levels of BCAAs are often attributed to reduced expression of enzymes involved in BCAA degradation pathway [44], or through the abolished function of the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex (BCKDH) [38, 45]. These findings demonstrated the importance of BCAA homeostasis in energy metabolism and reproductive performance. Conversely, the accumulation of BCAAs in Prss55−/− testes and sperm suggests a disruption of BCAA homeostasis, indicating that BCAAs may not have been utilized as efficiently as under normal conditions. These metabolomics results aligned with proteomics analyses suggest that Prss55 knockout might have a negative impact on BCAA metabolism in mouse testes.

To further explore the role of PRSS55 in regulating energy metabolism, especially BCAA catabolism, we employed Co-IP/MS to identify proteins that interact with PRSS55. Among eight hits, we picked DBT and BCKDK, as mitochondrial proteins that involved in BCAA degradation, for further validation (Fig. 6A, B). Through in vitro Co-IP and immunoblotting analyses, we identified BCKDK as potential components of the PRSS55 interactome (Fig. 6C-E). BCKDK is the key regulatory protein of BCAA metabolism in vivo [46]. BCKDK phosphorylates and inactivates the E1α subunit of BCKDH (also known as BCKDHA) to suppress the catabolism of BCKAs [47]. The BCKDH reaction is the rate-limiting step of BCAA catabolism, irreversibly converting BCKAs into acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, which feed into the TCA cycle [47], and ultimately serving as cellular fuel for ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation [48]. As reported previously, the lack of control over BCKDH activity in Bckdk-knockout mice led to significantly growth abnormalities, accompanied by a decrease in fertility [49]. Based on the tight regulatory relationships between BCKDK and BCKDHA, we were curious to see if PRSS55 also binds to BCKDHA. As expected, we validated the protein interaction between PRSS55 and BCKDHA by reciprocal IP assays (Fig. 6F-I). We also observed that the activity of BCKHA was inhibited in the mitochondria of Prss55−/− testes as the protein level of BCKDK and phosphorylation level of BCKDHA were raised (Fig. 6J). According to literatures, the phosphorylation activity of BCKDK can be suppressed by binding with it, which induces conformational movements within the N-terminal domain helix and leads to the dissociation of BCKDK from BCKDHA [50]. Correspondingly, our co-IP results suggested that PRSS55 may also be involved in modulating BCKDK and BCKDHA by interacting with these proteins to help maintain their active conformation, as supported by increased phosphorylation of BCKDHA observed in Prss55−/− testicular mitochondria.

Collectively, our findings reveal that in Prss55−/− models, uncontrolled BCKDK excessively inhibits the activity of BCKDH, leading to a metabolic block in BCAAs catabolism. Consequently, the conversion of BCKAs into acyl-CoA derivatives was limited, and the excess BCKAs were reversibly converted back to BCAAs via branched-chain aminotransferase-mediated reactions. The elevated BCAA levels may induce oxidative stress [51], as indicated by an imbalanced NAD+ /NADH ratio, which in turn compromises mitochondrial membrane potential and promotes depolarization of the mitochondria [52]. Additionally, the reduction of downstream acyl-CoA derivatives, including acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, could impair the TCA cycle and reduce ATP production through oxidative phosphorylation [6]. These metabolic disturbances ultimately reduced mitochondrial energy supply and impair sperm migration (Fig. 7). Consistently, our metabolomics data demonstrated significant accumulation of valine, leucine, and isoleucine in Prss55−/− testes and sperm.

Schematic illustration of PRSS55 participating in BCAA metabolism and energy homeostasis

As a partial explanation for PRSS55’s function in maintaining reproductive ability, we demonstrated that it may enhance energy metabolism and ATP production and interact with BCKDK and BCKDHA in testes and sperm. However, our study has certain limitations. Rescue experiments aimed at restoring BCAA metabolism in Prss55−/− mice and evaluating the consequent effects on sperm motility are still needed to fully validate these findings in future studies. Further studies are also required to elucidate how PRSS55, as a putative serine protease, contributes to sperm-egg recognition and the acrosome reaction, as well as whether its function is mediated through enzymatic or non-enzymatic mechanisms.

In conclusion, our study reveals that PRSS55 deficiency leads to sperm-UJT migration defects through impaired mitochondrial function and disrupted energy homeostasis, suggesting a potential role of PRSS55 in BCAA metabolic regulation and interaction with BCKDK and BCKDHA. These findings provide new insights into the molecular basis of male infertility, offering directions for future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
