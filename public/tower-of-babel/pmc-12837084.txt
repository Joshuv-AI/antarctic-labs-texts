Conductive hydrogel combined with electrical stimulation remodels the microenvironment for nerve regeneration to promote spinal cord injury repair
Authors: Haorui Du, Xinting Yang, Zhiping Qi, Jie Zhao, Renfeng Zhang, Jintao Wang, Andrew K Whittaker, Donghong Yu, Xiaoyu Yang, Quan Lin, Su Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe secondary inflammatory response and disruption of electrical signaling following spinal cord injury (SCI) present significant challenges to neurological recovery. Modulating the inflammatory microenvironment and reconstructing the spinal cord’s electrophysiological network are essential for effective SCI repair. To address these challenges, we designed a biomimetic 3D soft scaffold composed of phenylboronic acid-modified sodium alginate(Alg-PBA), dopamine-modified methacrylated gelatin(GelMA-DA), and Zn@EGCG modified MXene. This scaffold demonstrated excellent injectability, with an elastic modulus and electrical conductivity that closely matched those of native spinal cord tissue. The release of Zn@EGCG from the scaffold effectively suppressed inflammatory factors, promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, supported tissue regeneration, and reduced neuronal apoptosis. Simultaneously, under electrical stimulation (ES), the 3D soft scaffold generated stable electrical signals, which enhanced the differentiation of endogenous neural stem cells into neurons, thereby facilitating neural circuit reconstruction and functional motor recovery in rats with complete spinal cord transection. RNA sequencing analysis revealed that this therapeutic effect was linked to the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Overall, this study presents a multifunctional biomimetic 3D soft scaffold that modulates immune responses and promotes neuronal differentiation, offering a promising strategy for SCI repair.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03969-9.Keywords: Injectable conductive hydrogels, Biomimicking, Tissue repair, Electrical stimulation, Spinal cord injury

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) inflicts severe damage to the central nervous system, resulting in permanent loss of motor and sensory functions below the injury site. This leads to profound physical and psychological consequences for affected individuals [1, 2]. Following SCI, activation of both central and peripheral immune cells triggers the excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These factors collectively generate a hostile microenvironment that hinders axonal regeneration, resulting in failed tissue repair and subsequent cystic cavity formation [3–5]. Compounding these challenges, the inherently limited regenerative capacity of spinal neurons and disruption of electrophysiological signaling pathways critically impede functional recovery post-SCI [6, 7]. Consequently, the development of integrated strategies to reconstruct the spinal cord’s electrochemical niche, modulate neuroinflammation, prevent cystic cavitation, and promote the survival and differentiation of endogenous neural stem cells (eNSCs) becomes a critical therapeutic objective.

Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a polyphenolic compound derived from green tea, exhibits potent antioxidant properties by scavenging excess free radicals and inhibiting lipid peroxidation, thereby reducing oxidative stress-induced cellular damage and preserving membrane integrity. Simultaneously, EGCG suppresses the activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and downregulates cytokine secretion, creating an immunomodulatory microenvironment that promotes spinal cord repair and regeneration [8, 9]. Zinc, an essential trace element in human metabolism, plays a crucial role in maintaining physiological homeostasis. Emerging evidence indicates that low-dose zinc ions polarize microglia/macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory, pro-repair phenotype (M2), thereby enhancing neuronal survival, key mechanisms for neural regeneration [10–12]. The Zn@EGCG complex, formed through coordination interactions, not only enhances the stability of EGCG, prolonging its bioavailability in physiological environments but also synergistically mitigates post-SCI inflammatory cascades and fosters a pro-regenerative niche for neuronal survival and differentiation.

Beyond the acute inflammatory cascade, disruption of bioelectrical signaling represents another fundamental barrier to spinal cord repair [13]. Bioelectricity, an endogenous electrophysiological property of neural tissue, manifests as dynamically regulated transmembrane potentials that drive physiological information processing. This electromechanical coupling regulates critical processes, including action potential propagation, synaptic transmission, and phenotype modulation, all of which are essential for functional recovery [14, 15]. Conventional hydrogels have emerged as promising treatments for spinal cord injuries due to their ability to deliver therapeutic agents such as drugs and growth factors in a controlled manner [16, 17]. However, their inherent lack of conductivity prevents the recapitulation of the spinal cord’s electrophysiological microenvironment, ultimately limiting functional recovery. In contrast, conductive hydrogels can mimic the physical and bioelectrical properties of tissues, facilitating bioelectrical transmission between damaged tissues. These materials have been widely demonstrated in the repair of electrically active tissues such as those in trauma, myocardium, and neural tissues [18, 19].

MXene nanosheets represent an emerging class of two-dimensional transition metal carbides/nitrides that combine exceptional electrical conductivity, biocompatibility, and controlled degradability [20]. As a novel 2D material, MXene nanosheets have versatile applications across biomedical engineering, catalytic systems, and photovoltaics [21–23]. With extremely high metal conductivity and outstanding electrochemical stability, MXene nanosheets can form an efficient permeation network even when the loading ratio in the hydrogel matrix is relatively low. This significantly enhances the overall conductivity of the hydrogel, ensuring that the scaffold maintains excellent electrical performance in biological environments [24–26]. Furthermore, the surface of MXene nanosheets is end-capped with numerous hydrophilic functional groups (such as -OH, -O, and -F), which makes them highly dispersible in water and easy to modify [27]. These properties make MXenes an extremely attractive option for conductive hydrogels.

This study engineered a biomimetic 3D soft scaffold composed of Zn@EGCG-modified MXene nanosheets, phenylboronic acid-functionalized sodium alginate, and dopamine-grafted gelatin methacryloyl. The scaffold exhibits exceptional injectability, conformally adapting to irregular spinal cord defects. Its elastic modulus and electrical conductivity closely align with those of native spinal cord tissue, which is crucial for neural circuit reconstruction [28]. Boron ester bonds in conductive hydrogels facilitate the controlled release of Zn@EGCG in a high-reactive oxygen species (ROS) environment following spinal cord injury, thereby enhancing the inflammatory microenvironment to promote cell survival and tissue repair by scavenging excess ROS and promoting macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype.Additionally, exogenous electrical stimulation(ES), in combination with the conductive hydrogel, promoted cell migration, differentiation, and the restoration of motor function [29, 30]. The synergistic integration of conductive hydrogel and electrical stimulation generates stable endogenous electric fields, which serve as bioelectrical cues to guide and activate neural repair processes. This dual intervention significantly improved locomotor function in SCI rats, with RNA sequencing confirming the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. In this study, we developed a multifunctional conductive hydrogel for spinal cord injury repair through various mechanisms, including immunomodulation and promotion of neuronal differentiation. This innovative approach offers a promising new strategy for SCI repair.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of the GME hydrogel

Preparation of the GME hydrogel

As shown in Fig. 1, Inspired by the compositional, structural, and functional properties of the native extracellular matrix (ECM), we developed an ECM-mimicking hydrogel (GME) through dynamic boronate ester bonding and covalent crosslinking of Zn@EGCG-MXene nanosheets, phenylboronic acid-modified sodium alginate (Alg-PBA), and dopamine-functionalized gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA-DA) [31, 32].

Scheme illustration showing the GME + ES for the regeneration of spinal cord

The Zn@EGCG-MXene nanosheets, assembled through supramolecular assembly in the hydrogel, play a pivotal role in the therapeutic process by imparting high electrical conductivity, excellent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and promoting nerve repair. Consequently, the preparation process and formation mechanism of Zn@EGCG-MXene nanosheets were investigated. Thin sheet-like MXene nanosheets were successfully prepared by etching and ultrasonically layering the Ti3AlC2 precursor. The polyphenolic structure of EGCG was then employed to encapsulate EGCG in the outer layer of MXene nanosheets via hydrogen bonding interactions with the hydroxyl groups on the MXene surface, forming the MXene-EGCG nanosheets. Zn²⁺ was immobilized on the surface polyhydroxyl groups of the nanosheets through chelation with metal ligands, resulting in the formation of Zn@EGCG-MXene nanosheets.

The interactions involved in the preparation of Zn@EGCG-MXene nanosheets were confirmed by measuring the zeta potential of different materials. After coating the MXene nanosheets with EGCG, the zeta potential decreased from the initial − 48.5 mV to −51.1 mV, indicating the presence of additional negatively charged groups on the surface. When the positively charged Zn²⁺ chelated with the hydroxyl groups on the surface of the MXene-EGCG nanosheets, the potential increased to −22.9 mV. These results confirm that EGCG and Zn²⁺ successfully modified the MXene nanosheets (Figure S1A). Ultraviolet-visible (UV) spectroscopy analysis of the nanosheets revealed a distinct absorption peak near 276 nm, characteristic of catechol, further supporting the successful encapsulation of EGCG on the surface of the MXene nanosheets (Figure S1B). As shown in Figure S1C, the crystal peaks of MXene modified with Zn@EGCG were reduced, indicating that EGCG modification obscured the crystal peaks of MXene, thus confirming successful EGCG modification. Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were employed to examine the morphology and microstructure of Zn@EGCG-MXene nanosheets. The results demonstrated that the Zn@EGCG-MXene nanosheets maintained their lamellar structure, with Ti, O, and Zn elements uniformly distributed across the nanosheets, confirming the successful modification of Zn²⁺. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) also verified the presence of Zn²⁺, further confirming that Zn²⁺ was successfully coated onto the surface of MXene (Figure S1D-E).

The overall gelation process of GME hydrogels is illustrated in Fig. 2A, and the gelation was evaluated using the vial inversion method. The gelation mechanism of GME hydrogels is attributed to the formation of a dynamic borate bond between the -OH group on GelMA-DA and the -B group on Alg-PBA. Following this, UV curing was applied to covalently bond the GelMA-DA, forming an interpenetrating network hydrogel that provides excellent mechanical support. The microstructure of GME was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), revealing a porous network structure with pore sizes ranging from 100 to 300 μm (Fig. 2B). This structural feature makes the hydrogel an ideal scaffold for spinal cord repair, promoting neural stem cell growth and differentiation under physiological conditions. The hydrogels were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD), and the absence of distinct characteristic peaks in the XRD spectra indicated uniform distribution and homogeneity of the component materials in the hydrogels (Figure S2). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 2C) revealed absorption peaks at 3340 cm–1 and 1550 cm–1, indicating the presence of catechol from GelMA-DA and Zn@EGCG-MXene. Additionally, absorption peaks at 870 cm–1 and 1330 cm–1 confirmed the presence of B-O bonds from Alg-PBA. The peak at 1150 cm–1 confirmed the formation of a borate bond between Alg-PBA and GelMA-DA. In Fig. 2D, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed the presence of Zn and Ti, verifying the uniform dispersion of Zn@EGCG-MXene in the hydrogel.

Mechanical properties of the GME hydrogel

Previous studies have demonstrated that hydrogels with a modulus ranging from 300 to 1000 Pa are most conducive to the differentiation of neural stem cells [33]. Therefore, we adjusted the ratios of Alg-PBA and GelMA-DA to match the modulus of the hydrogel with that of spinal cord tissue. As shown in Fig. 2E, when the ratio of GelMA-DA to Alg-PBA is 1:1, the storage modulus of the hydrogel is 850.5 Pa, which aligns with the modulus of spinal cord tissue. Furthermore, we examined the impact of Zn@EGCG-MXene content on the hydrogel’s modulus. As shown in Fig. 2F, the results indicate that as the concentration of Zn@EGCG-MXene increases, the modulus of the hydrogel first increases and then decreases. This behavior is primarily attributed to the formation of hydrogen bonds between Zn@EGCG-MXene and the hydrogel network, which enhances the hydrogel’s modulus. However, excessive Zn@EGCG-MXene impedes the formation of borate ester bonds between GelMA-DA and Alg-PBA, resulting in a reduction of the hydrogel’s modulus. The rheological behavior of GME was further analyzed through strain amplitude scanning over a strain range of 0.01% to 1000%. As shown in Fig. 2G, the curves of G’ and G’’ intersect at a strain of 750%, which marks the critical strain point of the hydrogel network. Below this critical point, the energy storage modulus (G’) of the hydrogel is greater than G’’, indicating that the hydrogel predominantly remains in the gel state. At this stage, the hydrogel structure remains stable, exhibiting strong solid-state behavior that effectively resists external strain or stress. When the strain or stress exceeds the critical point, G’’ surpasses G’, and both curves display a decreasing trend. This indicates that the hydrogel structure is compromised, leading to the collapse of the gel network and the transition from the initial gel state to a sol-gel state. This process reflects the change in the mechanical behavior of GME hydrogels when subjected to strain or stress.

Hydrogel soft scaffolds possess injectable and self-repairing properties, which are beneficial for tissue repair treatments. These properties help minimize secondary damage caused by traditional implantation procedures and allow the hydrogel to rapidly fill irregular gaps in the injured area after injection into the target site [34, 35]. As shown in Fig. 2H, in the macroscopic self-repair experiment, the GME hydrogel was cut into two parts with a razor blade. After 3 minutes of contact, the two parts were successfully rehealed into a whole. The self-repairing property of GME was quantitatively analyzed using rheological leap-order strain scans, based on the results of strain amplitude scans. Successive strains of 1000% and 1% were applied to the GME, each lasting for 120 s. When a 1000% strain was applied, G’’ remained greater than G’, indicating that the three-dimensional network of the GME had collapsed. After equilibrium, when a 1% strain was applied, G’ exceeded G’’, demonstrating that the GME network was rebuilt and restored to the initial gel state (Fig. 2I). The excellent self-repairing property of GME can be attributed to the presence of dynamic boron ester bonds in the hydrogel network, which confer excellent dynamic reversibility. Flow scanning rheology of GME revealed a gradual decrease in viscosity as the shear rate increased from 0.1 to 100, indicating shear-thinning behavior. This property enhances the fluidity of the hydrogel under shear stress. Consequently, GME can be effectively injected into the target site after being subjected to shear, allowing it to be extruded through the needle of a two-component syringe (Fig. 2J).

Electrical properties of the GME hydrogels

The spinal cord tissue contains abundant neuroelectrical signals, and the repair of spinal cord injury is influenced not only by the structural and mechanical properties of the microenvironment but also by its electrical characteristics [36]. As shown in Fig. 3A, connecting the GME hydrogel into the circuit causes the LED light to illuminate, indicating that GME is electrically conductive. Furthermore, the LED light turned off when the GME was cut into two parts, and it illuminated again when the two parts were reconnected and healed, demonstrating that GME possesses not only self-repairing mechanical properties but also self-repairing electrical properties.

Electrical properties of GME hydrogel. (A) Circuit comprising the GME hydrogel with a green LED indicator. (B) EIS curves. (C) Conductivity. (D) Bode plots. (E) CV curves of GME hydrogels with different Zn@EGCG-MXene concentrations

The impedance of hydrogels with varying concentrations of Zn@EGCG-MXene was measured using the AC impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of an electrochemical workstation. As shown in Fig. 3B, the impedance of GME decreases monotonically with increasing Zn@EGCG-MXene concentration. The EIS images of the four hydrogel groups generally display a linear trend, indicating that the conductive process of the hydrogel does not involve redox reactions, and there is no charge transfer at the solution interface, signifying a non-Faradaic process. In Fig. 3C, the conductivity of GME increases with higher MXene concentrations. Notably, the conductivities of the 0.6 and 0.9 mg/mL concentrations were 0.93 S/m and 1.25 S/m, respectively, both within the conductivity range of native spinal cord tissue [37] This suggests that MXene, enriched with metallic elements, exhibits excellent electrical properties, endowing the GME hydrogel with superior conductivity. The communication frequency of neural stem cells ranges between 300 Hz and 1 kHz. Comparing the impedance of the four hydrogel groups in this frequency range (Figs. 3D-E), it was found that the 0.6 mg/mL and 0.9 mg/mL concentrations of GME exhibited lower impedance. This indicates that these concentrations of GME facilitate efficient communication and fast electron transfer kinetics within the neuronal cell communication frequency range, thereby promoting the rapid propagation of bioelectrical signals and enhancing communication between cells and soft scaffolds.

Biocompatibility of the GME hydrogel

Biocompatibility is a crucial prerequisite, particularly for bridging and repairing delicate tissues such as the spinal cord [38, 39]. As shown in Fig. 4A, the survival rate of PC12 cells in hydrogels with varying Zn@EGCG-MXene concentrations was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. When the Zn@EGCG-MXene concentration was below 0.6 mg/mL, the cell survival rate remained above 87%. However, it decreased to 63% at a concentration of 0.9 mg/mL. Considering the elastic modulus, electrical conductivity, and biocompatibility, the 0.6 mg/mL Zn@EGCG-MXene was selected as the biomimetic soft scaffold material for subsequent experiments. PC12 cells and RAW cells were then co-cultured with each group of hydrogels, and the biocompatibility of the hydrogels was evaluated using CCK-8 (Fig. 4B), Live/Dead (Fig. 4C, S4), and EdU assays (Figure S5). The results indicated that the cell viability across all hydrogel groups remained high, above 90%. Notably, the cell survival rate was significantly higher in the GME + ES group, suggesting that electrical stimulation promoted cell proliferation. Subsequently, PC12 cells were cultured in 3D hydrogel for 5 days, and Live staining revealed that the cells were able to grow into the hydrogel, supporting neural network formation and laying the foundation for subsequent animal studies(Fig. 4D). As a biomaterial that gradually degrades in vivo, the hydrogel must ensure that its degradation products do not produce toxic effects on biological organs and tissues after implantation [40]. We conducted in vivo degradation experiments on the hydrogel (Figure S6-7), performing H&E staining and Masson staining analyses on local tissues (skin and muscle) at the implantation site and major metabolic organs (liver, kidney) at 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks post-implantation. Histological findings revealed no significant inflammatory cell infiltration, fibrous encapsulation, or pathological structural alterations in either local tissues or major organs throughout the degradation cycle (Figure S8-9). To further investigate whether the degradation products of the various hydrogel fractions have toxic effects on rat organs, SCI rats were treated with each hydrogel group. After 8 weeks, HE staining revealed no significant pathological changes in the major organs of the rat (Figure S10). These results suggest that GM hydrogels not only exhibit good biological safety but also significantly promote cell proliferation when combined with Zn@EGCG and ES.

ROS scavenging and Anti-Inflammatory properties of the GME hydrogel

After spinal cord injury (SCI), the release of large amounts of inflammatory factors and the excessive production of ROS lead to oxidative stress injury, activating both blood-borne macrophages and microglia, which differentiate into M1 or M2 macrophages [41, 42]. M1-type macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory factors that inhibit cell survival and further damage surrounding tissues, while M2 macrophages promote tissue repair [43, 44]. A favorable microenvironment is essential for the regeneration of damaged neural tissues. To evaluate whether the hydrogel regulates the microenvironment after injury, antioxidant and macrophage polarization experiments were conducted using PC12 cells and RAW 264.7 cells. First, H2O2 was used to construct a cell oxidative stress injury model, and DCFA and DHE immunofluorescence staining were performed to observe intracellular ROS levels after co-culture with hydrogels (Fig. 5A-B). DCFH-DA (2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate) is a probe that is non-fluorescent in its parent form. It freely crosses the cell membrane to enter the cell, where it is hydrolyzed by intracellular esterases to form DCFH. DCFH cannot permeate the cell membrane and is thus loaded into the cell. In the presence of ROS, DCFH is oxidized to DCF, which emits strong green fluorescence. Consequently, the detected green fluorescence intensity is proportional to the overall intracellular ROS level. Concurrently, we employed the Dihydroethidium fluorescent probe, which is oxidized by ROS such as superoxide anions. Upon incorporation into nuclear DNA, it generates red fluorescence and is commonly used to detect superoxide anion levels. The free radical scavenging ability of the hydrogel was determined by using the DPPH and ABTS methods (Fig. 5C-F). DPPH is a stable radical with maximum absorption at 517 nm. Upon reaction with antioxidants, its absorption weakens and its color changes from deep purple to light yellow. The change in absorbance can be used to evaluate a material’s radical scavenging capacity. The ABTS assay, on the other hand, involves the generation of the blue-green ABTS⁺· radical cation by oxidants, which exhibits maximum absorption at 734 nm. Antioxidants cause its decolorization, enabling quantitative measurement of scavenging capacity. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity in the GME group was lower than that in the control and GM groups, while the DPPH and ABTS scavenging rates in the GME group were higher than those in the control and GM groups, with statistically significant differences. These findings demonstrated that the hydrogel effectively scavenged ROS after the addition of Zn@EGCG. Subsequently, an inflammation model was established using lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and immunofluorescence staining was performed after 2 days of co-culture with hydrogels. The M1 phenotype is marked by iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase), which catalyzes the production of large amounts of nitric oxide, contributing to inflammatory amplification and tissue damage. The M2 phenotype is marked by Arg-1 (arginase-1), which inhibits iNOS activity by competing for the substrate L-arginine and promotes the synthesis of polyamines and collagen, thereby facilitating tissue repair and regeneration. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that when Zn@EGCG was introduced into the hydrogel, M1 macrophage expression decreased, while M2 macrophage expression increased, indicating that the hydrogel exerted a sustained effect in suppressing inflammation (Fig. 5G-H). Fluorescence intensity analysis showed statistically significant differences in the GME and GME + ES groups compared to the previous groups (Fig. 5I, J,M, N). Additionally, qPCR(Quantitative Real-time polymerase chain reaction) was employed to analyze the gene expression levels of iNOS, Arg-1, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). IL-6 and IL-1β are major pro-inflammatory cytokines that drive neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis. qPCR analysis revealed a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines and an increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines in the GME hydrogel group compared to the LPS-stimulated group, suggesting that the hydrogel inhibited pro-inflammatory factor production and promoted anti-inflammatory factor secretion (Fig. 5K, L,O, P). These findings indicate that the addition of Zn@EGCG hydrogel effectively removes excessive ROS and induces macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, creating a favorable anti-inflammatory microenvironment for spinal cord repair.

Neural differentiation of NSCs on the GME hydrogel

Endogenous Neural Stem Cells (NSCs) are a population of cells capable of self-renewal and differentiation into neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes, which are present in the adult mammalian central nervous system [45, 46]. Migration and differentiation of NSCs to the injured site following spinal cord injury are critical for neural network remodeling [47]. NSCs were first isolated from fetal mice, and their purity was characterized by Nestin expression (Fig. 6A, Figure S11) [48]. Subsequently, NSCs were co-cultured with hydrogels for 7 days, with 30-minute electrical stimulation administered every two days at parameters of 100 mV and 100 Hz. Immunofluorescence staining was performed after 7 days (Fig. 6B-C). The effects of conductive hydrogels on NSC differentiation were evaluated using Tuj-1, a marker for neuronal differentiation, and GFAP, a marker for astrocyte differentiation. With the addition of conductive hydrogel and electrical stimulation, Tuj-1 expression increased, while GFAP expression decreased, facilitating neural network formation (Fig. 6D-E). The axon lengths of each group were then measured, with the GME + ES group showing the longest axons, averaging 147 μm (Figure S12). Additionally, qPCR analysis demonstrated that NSCs grown in conductive hydrogels exhibited higher Tuj-1 expression and lower GFAP expression compared to the blank control group. Notably, this trend was further enhanced with the application of electrical stimulation (Fig. 6F-G). These findings suggest that the combination of hydrogel and electrical stimulation not only promotes NSC differentiation and neural synapse growth but also enhances the formation of neural synaptic networks, offering a more effective strategy for repairing spinal cord injury and restoring spinal cord function.

GME + ES promotes functional recovery in SCI rats

The therapeutic effect of the conductive hydrogels combined with electrical stimulation was further explored in rats with SCI, with the experimental timeline presented in Fig. 7A. Functional recovery following SCI was assessed using footprint analysis and the BBB scale. Initially, all rats exhibited normal locomotion, with a BBB score of 21. Following total spinal cord resection, the animals experienced immediate paralysis of the lower limbs, with scores dropping significantly to 0. Over time, the conductive hydrogel group demonstrated a significantly faster functional recovery compared to the SCI group, with scores reaching 10 by week 8 post-injury, surpassing all other groups (Fig. 7G). Footprint analysis and motor imaging revealed that rats in both the GME and GME + ES groups showed some degree of plantar placement and support. Notably, rats in the GME + ES group exhibited significantly better stepping ability compared to the other three groups (Fig. 7C, S13, 7K). This evidence strongly suggests that enhancing the post-injury microenvironment through electrical stimulation and hydrogel treatment is effective in improving motor function after SCI in rats.

The function of the spinal cord is closely linked to its highly differentiated organization and structure, and disruption of this structure results in severe deficits in neurological function. As observed macroscopically in Figs. 7D-F and 8 weeks post-implantation, the GME + ES group exhibited improved connectivity and integration between the damaged spinal cord region and the surrounding healthy tissue. In contrast, tissue defects and cystic cavity formation remained present in the other three groups. Bladder and gastrocnemius muscles from each group were also observed and subjected to HE staining. The bladder and gastrocnemius muscles in the GME and GME + ES groups were well-aligned, with well-developed muscle fibers (Figure S14-S15). Additionally, body weight recovery in the GME and GME + ES groups was significantly better compared to the SCI and GM groups (Fig. 7H). These in vivo results further confirm that the conductive hydrogel facilitated the growth of the SCI defect and accelerated tissue regeneration and motor function recovery. The effect of the hydrogel on the early inflammatory microenvironment following spinal cord injury was also assessed through immunofluorescence staining. Cardiac perfusion sampling was performed one week post-injury, followed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7B). The SCI group showed a higher density of the macrophage M1 polarization marker, iNOS, and a lower density of the macrophage M2 polarization marker, Arg-1. Notably, the introduction of Zn@EGCG resulted in an increase in M2 macrophages in the conductive hydrogel group, indicating that Zn@EGCG promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype (Fig. 7I-J). We observed the antioxidant capacity of the conductive hydrogel in vivo through immunogold staining of Nrf2 and Caspase-3.Nrf2 (Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) is a master regulator of the cellular antioxidant response, orchestrating the expression of cytoprotective genes against oxidative stress. Caspase-3 (Cysteinyl aspartate specific proteinase-3) is the key executioner protease in apoptosis, responsible for the cleavage of numerous cellular substrates to orchestrate programmed cell death. Immunofluorescence staining for Nrf2 and Caspase-3 revealed that following spinal cord injury, the SCI group exhibited a large number of Caspase-3-positive apoptotic cells in the tissue, while Nrf2 nuclear translocation levels remained low. Following Zn@EGCGadministration, nuclear localization of Nrf2 was significantly enhanced, boosting overall cellular antioxidant capacity while effectively suppressing Caspase-3 activation, indicating markedly reduced apoptosis levels. These findings suggest that EGCG-Zn may mitigate oxidative stress-induced cellular damage by activating Nrf2-mediated cellular protective pathways, thereby inhibiting the Caspase-3-dependent apoptotic pathway.(Figure S16)Meanwhile, the GME hydrogels can minimize the deleterious effects of inflammation by releasing Zn@EGCG, thus providing a more favorable microenvironment that supports the survival of damaged neural tissue and long-term neuronal regeneration.

Neural regeneration following SCI treated with GME + ES

To evaluate the progression of nerve repair, cardiac perfusion sampling was conducted after 8 weeks of spinal cord injury (SCI) treatment, followed by immunofluorescence staining of the spinal cord tissue. (Fig. 8A) [49]. A small number of Tuj1-stained neurons and a large number of GFAP-stained astrocytes were observed around the injury site in the SCI group. Following the introduction of Zn@EGCG and electrical stimulation, there was an increase in Tuj1-stained neurons and a decrease in astrocytes in the hydrogel group, with the most significant findings in the GME + ES hydrogel group. Tuj1-positive staining was widely distributed in the regenerated area of the spinal cord (Fig. 8B-C). Additionally, a large number of neural stem cells and axons were observed in the spinal cord of the GME + ES hydrogel group (Fig. 8D-E). These results suggest that the conductive hydrogel, combined with electrical stimulation, promotes the migration of NSCs, enhances their differentiation toward neurons, and stimulates axon growth.

To elucidate the mechanism by which GME + ES promotes motor function recovery in rats with SCI, high-throughput transcriptome sequencing was performed to analyze the differential expression of mRNAs between the GME + ES and GME groups. After electrical stimulation, 455 mRNAs were differentially expressed, of which 224 were up-regulated and 231 were down-regulated (Fig. 9A). Gene ontology (GO) analysis was subsequently conducted to investigate the potential biological functions of the differentially expressed mRNAs. Electrical stimulation was found to be involved in functions related to the regulation of the immune system, cell growth, and metabolic processes, which are closely associated with cytoskeletal motility and skeletal muscle movement (Fig. 9B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis further identified 30 related pathways, among which the PI3K-AKT signaling pathways contained a large number of differential genes (Fig. 9C). Western blot and statistical analysis revealed that the GME + ES hydrogel group exhibited higher protein expression levels of both P-PI3K/PI3K and P-Akt/Akt compared to the GME group. (Fig. 9D-F). The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway contributes to cell proliferation, migration, and induces neural stem cells to differentiate into neurons [50, 51]. These findings indicate that GME + ES may regulate the recovery of motor function in spinal cord-injured rats primarily through PI3K-AKT signaling pathway.

Disccusion

A long-standing critical challenge in spinal cord injury repair lies in constructing multifunctional repair platforms capable of simultaneously regulating complex pathological microenvironments and restoring neural function. Through rational molecular design, this study developed a multifunctional hydrogel (GME) integrating injectability, self-healing capability, electrical conductivity, and immune regulation. Combined with exogenous electrical stimulation (ES), it offers an innovative solution for spinal cord repair.

At the material design level, we employed dopamine-modified gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA-DA) and phenylboronic acid-modified sodium alginate (Alg-PBA) based on dynamic covalent chemistry principles. The formation of dynamic borate bonds between these components enables autonomous cross-linking of the hydrogel. Rheological characterization confirms that this dynamic bond not only endows the hydrogel with exceptional self-healing properties, enabling it to maintain structural integrity under in vivo mechanical stress, but also confers significant shear-thinning characteristics. This ensures the material can perfectly conform to irregular injury sites via minimally invasive injection.

At the functional mechanism level, GME hydrogels demonstrate multi-modal synergistic therapeutic effects. In terms of microenvironment regulation, the material employs a dual-track strategy of “reactive oxygen species scavenging and immune signaling reprogramming” to synergistically reverse oxidative stress and inflammatory states in damaged areas. This efficacy stems from the synergistic interaction between its core components, EGCG and Zn²⁺. On one hand, the loaded EGCG-Zn complex achieves active remodeling of the damaged microenvironment through ROS-responsive release. As a natural polyphenolic antioxidant, EGCG directly neutralizes reactive oxygen species (e.g., ·OH, ONOO⁻) and free radicals via its phenolic hydroxyl group, interrupting radical chain reactions. More critically, it activates the Nrf2 signaling pathway—the central hub of cellular antioxidant defense—promoting its nuclear translocation and enhancing intrinsic antioxidant capacity [52, 53]. Regarding anti-inflammatory effects, EGCG has been extensively reported to exert its action by inhibiting the activation of the NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway. This occurs by preventing the degradation of IκBα, thereby suppressing the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and reducing the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors [54]. Simultaneously, the introduction of Zn²⁺ not only enhances its stability through coordination with EGCG, but also jointly drives the polarization of macrophages/microglia from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the reparative M2 phenotype, thereby creating a favorable immune microenvironment for nerve regeneration [55–57].

In promoting neural regeneration, the GME hydrogel establishes a virtuous cycle of “electrical conductivity-electrical stimulation” synergy. The MXene-based conductive network mimics the physiological electrical microenvironment of the spinal cord and functions as an “electronic highway” during applied electrical stimulation, significantly enhancing electrical signal transmission efficiency. Our prior systematic parameter optimization study identified 100 mV and 100 Hz as optimal electrical stimulation conditions, which effectively promote neural stem cell differentiation into neurons (Figure S17) [37, 58]. Through neural stem cell differentiation results, we observed a significant increase in neuronal marker expression following the introduction of Zn@EGCG and ES. This effect stems from two mechanisms: firstly, the directed induction by electrical stimulation; secondly, the sustained release of Zn²⁺ supports mitochondrial enzyme function, ensuring ATP production and providing the energy foundation for highly energy-demanding neuronal differentiation and axonal growth. Concurrently, Zn@EGCG inhibits apoptosis-related proteins such as Caspase-3, thereby enhancing the survival rate of newly generated neurons [12, 59–61].

In vivo experiments confirmed that GME + ES significantly promotes motor function recovery in rats with spinal cord injury. We further explored how electrical stimulation facilitates motor recovery through transcriptomic sequencing. RNA sequencing and Western blot results collectively demonstrated that GME + ES treatment effectively activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, specifically manifested by the upregulation of key protein p-AKT expression levels. Furthermore, electrical stimulation promotes cytoskeletal reorganization, providing a structural foundation for axonal extension and regeneration. Numerous studies have reported that activation of this pathway is one of the core mechanisms promoting neural stem cell differentiation into neurons and motor function recovery [36, 62–64].

In recent years, extensive research has focused on the application of hydrogels in central nervous system repair. Hydrogels can bridge injured areas, mimic extracellular matrix structures, and guide axonal ingrowth. Simultaneously, hydrogels enable sustained drug release, promote nerve regeneration, and consequently improve motor and urinary functions in SCI rats [65–67]. However, the importance of electrical signal network reconstruction in spinal cord injury is often overlooked. Against this backdrop, this study aligns with existing trends while achieving key innovations [68, 69]. First, the elastic modulus and electrical conductivity of GME hydrogels closely match those of spinal cord tissue, effectively preventing secondary injury while providing a biomimetic microenvironment; Second, its injectability and self-healing properties enable perfect adaptation to irregular injury sites. More importantly, through ingenious chemical design and physical composites, we have organically integrated a dynamic key-controlled release system (Zn@EGCG) with a stable conductive network (MXene) onto a single platform. This achieves “spatiotemporal control” and “functional synergy”—precisely releasing active components during critical pathological stages while simultaneously enabling the synergistic effects of immune modulation and electrical signal guidance.

This study has several limitations. Although we confirmed activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, its necessity for functional recovery and cell-specific effects require further validation through inhibitor intervention or cell-specific gene knockout techniques. Additionally, long-term safety assessment only extended to 8 weeks post-implantation; tissue reactions following complete material degradation necessitate more prolonged observation. All experiments were conducted in rodent models, and their clinical translational value requires further validation in large animal models.

Based on these findings and limitations, future research will focus on: conducting systematic long-term biosafety evaluations; elucidating regenerative mechanisms in large animal models using advanced lineage tracing techniques; and advancing this multifunctional synergistic strategy toward clinical translation by optimizing material parameters and stimulation protocols, thereby offering new therapeutic hope for patients with spinal cord injury.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the challenge of treating SCI lies in the complex pathological processes, including the inflammatory response and the limited intrinsic regenerative capacity of the spinal cord. Restoring an appropriate electrochemical microenvironment has proven to be increasingly crucial in SCI treatment. Therefore, biomaterials with high electrical conductivity are promising candidates for neurological repair. We developed an electrically conductive hydrogel to improve the spinal cord microenvironment after injury, with tunable mechanical and electrical properties that mimic the mechanical strength and electrochemical environment of rat spinal cord tissues. Both in vivo and in vitro studies, we demonstrated that our material effectively targets two of the most important pathological processes. The design incorporates reversible covalent bonding, enabling controlled release of Zn@EGCG at the site of injury, which counteracts the acute inflammatory response, reduces cystic cavity formation, and promotes tissue healing. Upon administration of electrical stimulation, the hydrogel promoted the differentiation of neural stem cells, axonal growth, reduction of glial scar formation, and the establishment of neural networks. As confirmed by RNA sequencing, the possible mechanism by which electrical stimulation promotes functional recovery is the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This study demonstrates that the use of a microenvironment-improving conductive hydrogel combined with electrical stimulation to repair severe spinal cord lesions provides a multifaceted approach to SCI treatment.
