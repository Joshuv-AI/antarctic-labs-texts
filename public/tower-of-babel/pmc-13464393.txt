Non-operative versus operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis: a randomized controlled study in Sierra Leone
Authors: Constance Cummings-John, Abdulai Jawo Bah, Aiah Lebbie, Temidayo Ogundiran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute appendicitis is the leading cause of acute abdomen in young adults, typically managed by appendectomy. A growing body of evidence supports non-operative management as an alternative for uncomplicated cases. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of non-operative management (NOM) versus operative management (OM) for uncomplicated acute appendicitis.MethodsA prospective, longitudinal, randomized controlled trial was conducted at Connaught Hospital, Sierra Leone. Sixty patients were randomized: 30 to NOM and 30 to OM. NOM patients received intravenous metronidazole and ceftriaxone for 48 h, followed by oral metronidazole and ciprofloxacin for 5 days. OM patients underwent open appendectomy. NOM patients were monitored for symptom resolution or progression; failure required appendectomy. Both groups were followed up on days 7, 10, 30, 60, and 90 post-admissions to assess recurrence, cost of care, and quality of life (QoL).ResultsThe median age was 26 years (IQR 14–42) for NOM and 27 years (IQR 18–54) for OM. Most patients were males (NOM: 66.7%, OM: 63.3%). Antibiotic treatment was successful in 18 (60%) NOM patients. Failure occurred in 12 (40%) NOM patients, 11 during the index admission, with 1 (3.3%) experiencing recurrence within a week of discharge. Successful NOM resulted in a significantly improved QoL score (5.98), a shorter mean hospital stays (4 days), a lower mean cost of care (NLe 482.29; $49.08), and no catastrophic health expenditure, compared to both OM and failed NOM. Statistically significant differences were observed in favor of successful NOM over surgery (including OM and failed NOM requiring surgery) for the cost of care and time to return to normal activities.ConclusionNOM for uncomplicated acute appendicitis achieved a 60% success rate with a low recurrence rate (3.3%). It is associated with lower costs, reduced risk of catastrophic health expenditure, shorter hospital stays, faster return to normal activities, and improved QoL compared to operative management.Trial registrationThe Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR).Clinical trial numberPACTR202605655002550. Introduction

Acute appendicitis remains the leading cause of surgical acute abdomen worldwide [1, 2] affecting people of different socio-demographic characteristics [3] with peak incidence between 15and 19 years of age [4, 5]. It is the leading cause of abdominal surgical emergencies in sub-Saharan Africa [6, 7]. An increase in the incidence of acute appendicitis has been reported [5, 8]. This rising incidence is due to the increased adoption of a westernized diet and a shift away from a high-fibre diet, both of which increases the formation of appendicoliths [5, 9]. A systematic analysis of the burden of acute appendicitis in 204 countries showed an increase in the burden of the disease, especially in Western sub-Saharan countries [4]. Several factors, such as health-seeking behavior [10, 11], high cost of care [10], inadequate hospital care [1, 8] and increased morbidity [6, 12], are known to affect the care of patients with acute appendicitis in health facilities in sub-Saharan Africa. In the African sub-region, patients with appendicitis usually present late to healthcare facilities, which is associated with increased morbidity [13].

Though the incidence of acute appendicitis is not known in Sierra Leone, surgical management of acute appendicitis is the most common surgical procedure performed in both public and private health facilities in the country [14, 15]. It is also the leading cause of infection requiring surgical procedures in the paediatric population [16], which highlights increased burden in the country. Open appendectomy is the primary treatment for acute appendicitis in sub-Saharan Africa [8, 9]. Complications such as wound infection, intrabdominal abscesses, and wound dehiscence are reported amongst patients who have had open appendectomy in sub-Saharan Africa, and it is usually associated with a more extended hospital stay and an increase in cost of care [17, 18]. The provision of safe surgical services in Sierra Leone is hindered by several factors, such as a low number of skilled health personnel, shortages in medical supplies, poorly equipped health facilities, and financial barriers [15, 19, 20].

In recent years, studies have investigated non-operative management (NOM) approaches, particularly the use of antibiotics, as a viable alternative in the management of acute uncomplicated appendicitis. NOM is cost-effective, associated with shorter hospital stays and lower morbidity and mortality rates than appendectomy [21–24]. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials involving 900 patients with uncomplicated appendicitis, managed with antibiotics or surgery, showed that antibiotics are effective and safe [22]. Furthermore, systematic reviews and meta-analyses in children found NOM to be safe and feasible, especially in those without an appendicolith [25]. Other benefits of NOM include faster recovery and return to work, reduced rates of negative appendectomies, and a suitable alternative for adults with high surgical risk [21, 26, 27].

Though studies have been done globally comparing non operative and operative management of acute appendicitis, few have been done in sub-Saharan countries, with limited resources in providing safe surgical care. Currently, there is no study on the non-operative management of acute appendicitis in Sierra Leone. In resource-constrained settings such as Sierra Leone, there is a need for safe, alternative, evidence-based, informed methods of treatment for surgical care. Appendectomy is an economic burden on both patients and the health system, especially in low-income countries like Sierra Leone, where the majority of the cost of health care is usually paid for out of pocket [8, 28]. A study on an alternative method of treatment that is evidence-based is therefore imperative in informing policy and practice on the management of acute appendicitis.

This study aims to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of operative versus non-operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis in patients presenting at a tertiary health facility in Sierra Leone during the pandemic, a time when patients were hesitant to seek surgical care. By comparing clinical outcomes, time to recovery, and healthcare costs associated with operative and non-operative management, this research sought to provide empirical evidence to inform local healthcare policies and treatment guidelines for the management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis.

Methods

Study site

The study was conducted at the Connaught Hospital, a tertiary health facility in Freetown. It is the central facility among the University of Sierra Leone Teaching Hospitals Complex and the public referral health centre for medicine and surgery. It is a 300-bed facility with departments including pathology, radiology, dentistry, medicine, and surgery. It was founded in 1912 and became a teaching hospital by an act of parliament in 2016. The surgical department provides specialty services, including plastic surgery, general surgery, orthopaedics, neurotrauma and paediatric surgery. It has four wards for admission of adult patients, a paediatric ward, four operating theatres for adults and a paediatric theatre.

Study design and population

This was a hospital-based, prospective, longitudinal, randomized, controlled study conducted among patients diagnosed with uncomplicated acute appendicitis at the Connaught Hospital from July 2020 to September 2022. Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio using concealed colored envelopes with fixed block sizes of 30.

Inclusion criteria

The study participants were patients who were diagnosed with uncomplicated acute appendicitis at the emergency department, outpatient clinic, and surgical outpatients’ clinic of the Connaught Hospital. These included patients aged 18 years and above, who presented within 48 h with clinical features of uncomplicated acute appendicitis. It also included those with an abdominal ultrasound scan confirming uncomplicated acute appendicitis and those with an Alvarado Score of 6 and above.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded non-consenting patients, those who were immunocompromised, and those with clinical or radiological features of complicated appendicitis, e.g., generalized peritonitis, appendiceal abscess, or perforated appendicitis. Moreover, patients managed non-operatively with antibiotics at an outside facility, pregnant women and patients allergic to medications in the study protocol were also excluded.

Patient recruitment

Patients visiting the Connaught Hospital who were diagnosed with uncomplicated acute appendicitis confirmed by ultrasound, had an Alvarado score of at least 6, and were willing to participate were recruited into the study. They were taken through the participants’ information sheet, and informed consent was obtained. Using sealed envelopes, they were randomly assigned to either the operative or non-operative group.

Data collection

The principal investigator or study lead (CCJ) primarily collected data from participants; the house officers on the PI’s team helped with clinical data collection. We used a specially designed proforma to collect patients’ data. Data collection commenced at first contact between the study lead (CCJ) and patients at the hospital. The research team designed two proformas for the two patient groups. The team created Proforma A and Proforma B for patients managed through the non-operative and operative arms of the study, respectively.

Proforma A (NOM)

The socio-demographic information of the patients, Alvarado score and pain score on admission, clinical findings, treatment given and outcome on days one and two of admission were collected. Information on the length of stay and quality of life on the second and fourth days was documented. Patients were followed up on the 7th,10th, 30th, 60th, and 90th postoperative days for recurrence of symptoms through hospital review and follow-up calls.

Proforma B (OM)

We documented the socio-demographic information of patients, Alvarado’s score and pain score on admission, and intraoperative findings (position and length of the appendix, gross features of the appendix). We also documented quality of life on the second and fourth postoperative days, as well as the length of hospital stay. Patients were followed up on the 7th, 10th, 30th, 60th, and 90th postoperative days through hospital review and follow up calls.

Protocol for patients in non-operative arm

Patients recruited into this arm of the study were admitted to the surgical ward for 48 h. They were given intravenous (IV) antibiotics (Metronidazole 500 mg 8 hourly and Ceftriaxone 1 g b/d) and analgesics (Paracetamol (IV) 1 g 6 hourly and Diclofenac (IM) 75 mg 12 hourly) for 48 h. We placed them on a light diet, had their vital signs monitored, and assessed their pain score. We reassessed them daily to monitor their clinical condition. Complete blood count analysis was performed on admission and on day 2 post-non-operative care. Failure of non-operative management was defined as lack of clinical improvement within 48 h of antibiotic initiation, evidenced by persistent or worsening abdominal pain, fever (≥ 38.0 °C), and tachycardia, requiring escalation to surgical intervention. The 48-hour threshold reflects standard surgical practice, as clinical deterioration or failure to improve within this period suggests complicated disease or treatment failure and warrants operative management.

Protocol for patients in the operative arm

We performed open appendectomies via a Lanz incision on patients who were in the operative arm of the study, after obtaining informed consent, with no oral intake for at least 4 h before the surgical procedure. An appendectomy was done either by a senior or junior resident. Patients were admitted to the ward post-surgery and placed on IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone and metronidazole) and analgesics paracetamol (IV) and diclofenac (IM) for 48 h. Patients commenced a light diet on the first postoperative day and were discharged on the third day and placed on oral paracetamol and diclofenac and oral antibiotics metronidazole and ciprofloxacin for five days. The operative site was reviewed on the 3rd post operative day and stitches were removed on the 10th post operative day.

Outcome measures

Successful non-operative management

These were patients with improvement or resolution of signs and symptoms, without elevation of white blood cell (WBC) count, and no evidence of complications, e.g., an appendiceal mass, following 48 h of IV antibiotics and analgesics. They were discharged home after 48 h of non-operative management and placed on oral paracetamol and diclofenac and oral antibiotics metronidazole and ciprofloxacin for five days and followed up for 3 months for recurrence of symptoms or signs of acute appendicitis.

Treatment failure

These patients had no clinical improvement or worsening of their symptoms whilst on admission, following non-operative management with antibiotics and analgesics for at least 48 h. The non-operative management for these patients was abandoned, and they were counselled and worked up for an appendectomy.

Recurrence of uncomplicated acute appendicitis

These included patients managed non-operatively for uncomplicated acute appendicitis who had been successfully treated with antibiotics, discharged, and later presented with clinical features of acute appendicitis during the 3-month follow-up period.

Assessment of quality of life

The European Quality of Life 5-dimension tool and the visual analogue scale were used to assess patients’ quality of life. The European Quality of Life Five Dimension Assessment tool (Eq. 5-D5L) measures quality of life across five components: mobility, self-care, performing usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression. The tool was used on the second and fourth days of operative or non-operative management in both patient groups. The quality-of-life questionnaires were administered on Days 2 and 4 to capture the early recovery period, when differences between surgical and antibiotic management are expected to be most pronounced. These time points correspond to clinically relevant milestones in postoperative and post-intervention recovery, including pain resolution, mobilization, and readiness for discharge. In Sierra Leone, most patients with uncomplicated appendicitis are discharged within 2–4 days, and longer-term follow-up is often challenging due to health-system and socioeconomic constraints. Early in-hospital assessments therefore ensured high data completeness and minimized attrition bias, while providing patient-centered outcomes relevant to the pragmatic non-inferiority design.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into an Excel sheet by a research assistant and checked for accuracy and completeness by the study lead (CCJ). The data was then exported to and analyzed using the IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 25 (SPSS version 25). The results are presented in tables and figures, and the summarised categorical data is presented using frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, we report the mean and standard deviation, and the median and interquartile range for variables that were not normally distributed. The association between categorical variables was tested using the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact test, and the Mann-Whitney U test for variables that were not normally distributed. ANOVA and post hoc analysis were used to assess within- and between-group differences. The level of significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval.

This randomised controlled trial is reported in accordance with CONSORT 2010 statement. The CONSORT checklist and flow diagram are included as supplementary material.

Discussions

In this study, the overall success rate of non-operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis over the 3 months was 60%. Similar results were obtained in other studies comparing antibiotics and appendectomy in the management of acute uncomplicated appendicitis, with success rates of 63% and 67.9% [22, 31]. The variations in success rate observed in this study compared with other studies are due to differences in selection criteria, antibiotic choice, and follow-up period [23, 24, 27, 32]. The most important consideration in the selection criteria of most studies is whether patients with appendicolith should be recruited [33, 34]. The presence of a fecalith is considered complicated appendicitis, and patients with a fecalith were not recruited for NOM of uncomplicated acute appendicitis in some studies [23, 32, 33]. A fecalith is associated with luminal obstruction, a high risk of perforation, and treatment failure with NOM [35]. In this study, fecalith was seen on ultrasound in 6 patients in the NOM group and in 2 patients in the OM group, and on histology in 4 patients who failed conservative treatment with antibiotics and underwent appendectomy.

A large randomized trial in adults comparing antibiotics with appendectomy for acute appendicitis showed a high failure rate of 41% in patients with fecalith managed with antibiotics, compared to 25% in those without a fecalith. The complication rate was also higher in the antibiotic group, which was attributed to the presence of fecalith [36].

In this study, the negative appendectomy rate was 9.5%, compared with previous studies reporting rates of 0.6–2%, 5.9%, 6.5%, and 12.3% [31, 32, 37, 38]. Non-operative management reduces the number of negative appendectomies and reduces unwarranted surgery on patients [39]. Surgical site infection, abscess, enterocutaneous fistula, and adhesive bowel obstruction are complications reported amongst patients who have undergone an appendectomy [27]. NOM patients are usually free from these complications [40].

In this study, surgical site infection was the only post-operative complication seen during the 3-month follow-up period. Two patients in the operative group and two patients who failed non-operative management had this complication. None of the patients in this study reported any side effects from the antibiotics administered, and no deaths were recorded.

Only one patient presented with a recurrence of symptoms within the 3-month follow-up. The short follow-up period of 3 months could account for the low recurrence rate of 3.3% in this study. The accepted risk of recurrence after non-operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis is 38% [39]. More extended follow-up periods are associated with a higher rate of recurrence [35], hence, further research with a more extended follow-up period is needed to determine the recurrence rate in our setting.

In our study protocol, patients on NOM were given intravenous medications and admitted. The minimum length of stay for NOM was 48 h. In this study, patients who had successful NOM had a shorter hospital stay, as reported in other studies [41, 42]. A significant finding in our study was that patients who failed NOM stayed longer in the hospital, a finding that was statistically significant [43]. An explanation for this is that patients who failed NOM and had surgery spent a few more days in admission to recuperate from the surgery.

The findings of this study showed that 60% of the patients earned less than NLe490 ($50) per month. Findings were similar to those of a previous study by Phull et al. [44] on the financial burdens patients face in accessing surgical care at the Connaught Hospital [44]. In this study, only one patient had private health insurance that was used to settle the hospital bill. Most patients settled their hospital bills out of pocket, with the support of relatives and friends; some took short-term loans, and others sold their assets to pay [44]. This is very common in a collectivist society like Sierra Leone, where individuals see themselves as part of a social web and come together to support one another in times of need. This is similar to the results obtained in Uganda and Malawi, where patients undergoing surgical care took loans and had to liquidate their assets to settle hospital bills [18, 45]. In Ghana, despite having a national health insurance scheme (NHIS), both insured and uninsured patients undergoing surgical treatment at a tertiary health facility faced financial challenges [46].

This study shows that patients managed successfully non-operatively are less likely to incur catastrophic costs than those managed surgically. In a qualitative study on coping strategies and payment for emergency surgical services amongst slum and non-slum dwellers in southern Nigeria, over 65% of respondents incurred catastrophic health expenditure despite some having access to the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) [47]. In Ghana, the availability of a health insurance scheme did not guarantee protection against catastrophic health expenditure; 53% and 87% of insured and uninsured patients, respectively, were at increased risk of catastrophic expenditure while seeking surgical services at the country’s central tertiary health facility [42].

A cost-benefit of over NLe 500 ($65) was associated with non-operative management. Similar results were also obtained in other studies [23, 48, 49]. A study by Khanal et al. on non-operative management of uncomplicated appendicitis during the COVID pandemic showed that non-operative management of acute, uncomplicated appendicitis was associated with a cost benefit of $100 [23]. Turhan et al. comparing non-operative and operative management of acute appendicitis in patients presenting at an emergency department, showed that non-operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis was associated with a cost savings of $126 compared to the appendectomy group [48]. Sippola et al. (2019) reviewed the economic benefit of non-operative vs. operative management in the Appendicitis Acuta (APPAC) trial of 530 patients managed for uncomplicated acute appendicitis in six Finnish hospitals, and found a higher total cost of care in patients who were managed operatively, which was associated with higher societal costs and productivity loss [49].

Patients in the surgery arm and those with successful non-operative management reported better quality of life than those who failed non-operative management. Mixed reports are found in other studies assessing the quality of life of patients after non-operative management. In the Comparison of Outcomes of Antibiotic Drugs and Appendectomy (CODA) trial, patients in the non-operative group were non-inferior in quality of life compared with those in the appendectomy group [50]. In the Conservative versus Open Management of Acute Uncomplicated Appendicitis (COMMA) trial, a randomized clinical trial evaluating the efficacy and quality of life of antibiotic treatment of uncomplicated appendicitis, patients in the surgery group reported a better quality of life score at 3- and 6-months post-surgery than those managed with antibiotics [42]. In the Appendectomy and Conservative Treatment for Patients with Uncomplicated Acute Appendicitis (ACTUAA) prospective multicentre trial, patients in the antibiotics group had lower VAS scores at discharge and at 30 days follow-up compared to the appendectomy group [51].

In recent years, there has been an increase in the acceptance of non-operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis. Several considerations, such as avoiding anaesthetic and surgical complications, reducing the cost of care, and enabling an early return to work, are among its associated benefits [27, 31]. In Sierra Leone and other countries, surgical cases were scaled down, and non-emergent or elective cases were reduced to conserve resources and decrease the risk of patients contracting COVID-19 in hospitals [23, 52]. This study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, and non-operative management was an appropriate treatment modality, given the prevailing circumstances.

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study has several strengths. First, its randomized controlled design strengthens the validity of the findings by reducing selection bias and enabling a reliable comparison between non-operative and operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis. Second, by being conducted in a tertiary hospital in Sierra Leone, the study generates contextually relevant evidence for low-resource settings with limited surgical infrastructure and significant financial barriers to care. Third, the comprehensive follow-up schedule—on days 7, 10, 30, 60, and 90—allowed for a detailed assessment of patient outcomes, including symptom resolution, recurrence, and quality of life. Additionally, the inclusion of an economic evaluation that examines both direct costs and catastrophic health expenditures provides valuable insights for health policymakers and system planners.

However, the study also has notable limitations. The relatively small sample size of 60 patients may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations or different health system contexts. Moreover, the 90-day follow-up period may be insufficient to fully capture long-term outcomes, such as delayed recurrence or complications, particularly among those initially managed non-operatively. As a single-centre study, there is also a risk of site-specific bias, which may affect the external validity of the results. Due to the nature of the interventions, double blinding was not feasible, introducing potential performance and detection biases, especially in the assessment of subjective measures like quality of life. In addition, while antibiotic-based treatment showed promising short-term results, the study did not assess antibiotic resistance patterns, an important consideration for antibiotic stewardship and public health in low-income settings.

Another limitation of this study is the reliance on abdominal ultrasound to exclude complicated appendicitis. Ultrasound is operator-dependent and less accurate than computed tomography scan in distinguishing uncomplicated from complicated disease [9]. However, ultrasound reflects routine diagnostic practice in this low-resource setting, enhancing the pragmatic relevance of our findings.

Implications for policy and practice

This study provides important evidence to inform surgical and health systems policy, particularly in low-resource settings. The finding that non-operative management of uncomplicated acute appendicitis is successful in 60% of cases, with minimal recurrence and significant cost savings, supports its integration into national clinical guidelines as a viable first-line treatment option.

From a policy perspective, adopting a non-operative pathway can alleviate pressure on surgical theatres, reduce hospital bed occupancy, and optimize healthcare resource allocation—critical considerations in countries with limited surgical capacity and workforce constraints. Cost analyses indicate that non-operative care significantly reduces direct medical expenses and avoids catastrophic health expenditure for patients, reinforcing its potential to promote financial risk protection and universal health coverage.

For clinical practice, these findings call for a shift toward shared decision-making, where clinicians present non-operative treatment as a legitimate option alongside surgery, especially for stable patients with confirmed uncomplicated appendicitis. Clear clinical protocols and training for antibiotic-first approaches should be developed and standardized to ensure safety and consistency in their implementation.

Additionally, the study highlights the need for follow-up systems to monitor outcomes and recognize treatment failures early. National and institutional protocols should be adapted to include structured follow-up, particularly during the critical 90-day post-treatment window.

Future policies should support implementation research to evaluate scalability and long-term outcomes of non-operative management at primary and secondary care levels. As antimicrobial stewardship remains a global health priority, treatment algorithms must balance antibiotic use with local resistance patterns and supply chains.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that non-operative management using antibiotics is a safe, effective, and cost-efficient alternative to surgery for selected patients with uncomplicated acute appendicitis. With a 60% success rate, low recurrence, shorter hospital stay, and reduced financial burden, antibiotic therapy offers significant clinical and economic benefits—particularly in low-resource settings like Sierra Leone, where surgical capacity is limited. While appendectomy remains the definitive treatment, our findings support a shift toward patient-centred, context-sensitive approaches that include non-operative management as a viable option. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are recommended to validate these results and guide national clinical guidelines and health policy.
