Psychological experiences of patients with Stanford type B aortic dissection during the preoperative waiting period: a qualitative study
Authors: Chun Liu, Xiaoli Xie, Anni Hu, Wen Qi, Yunyan Su, Ping Zhang
Published: 2026

Background

Stanford type B aortic dissection (TBAD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency characterized by rapid progression and substantial mortality. Pathologically, an intimal tear allows blood to enter the medial layer of the aortic wall, resulting in the formation of a false lumen and compromised aortic integrity [1, 2]. TBAD accounts for approximately 25%–40% of all aortic dissection cases, and its incidence continues to increase with population aging and the growing prevalence of hypertension [3, 4]. For patients with acute uncomplicated TBAD, thoracic endovascular aortic repair is currently regarded as the preferred treatment strategy and is often preceded by a period of medical stabilization. As a result, many patients experience a preoperative waiting phase ranging from 24 h to 90 days, during which strict blood pressure control and close monitoring are required despite the ongoing risk of disease progression [5–7].

The preoperative waiting period for TBAD constitutes a clinically important context of illness uncertainty. According to Mishel’s Uncertainty in Illness Theory (UIT), uncertainty arises when individuals are unable to assign meaning to illness-related events because of ambiguity, complexity, insufficient information, and unpredictability [8]. Within the UIT framework, patients’ appraisal of uncertainty plays a central role in shaping coping behaviors and psychological adaptation. Although UIT has been widely applied in chronic illnesses, its relevance in acute conditions involving delayed intervention remains insufficiently explored [9–11].

For patients with TBAD, preoperative waiting represents a distinctive clinical experience in which a confirmed life-threatening diagnosis coexists with a necessary delay in definitive treatment. Throughout surgical delay, patients are required to tolerate persistent risks in the absence of immediate intervention, often accompanied by anxiety, fear of deterioration, and uncertainty regarding prognosis and treatment outcomes [7]. These psychological responses may extend beyond emotional distress and contribute to physiological and behavioral consequences, including heightened sympathetic activation, reduced treatment adherence, and impaired surgical preparedness, thereby potentially influencing clinical recovery and perioperative outcomes [12, 13]. Despite these concerns, current clinical management primarily focuses on physiological stabilization, while psychological experiences throughout preoperative waiting remain insufficiently addressed.

Previous studies have identified substantial perioperative psychological challenges among patients with aortic dissection; however, a theory-informed understanding of psychological adaptation across the waiting phase remains limited [14]. Given the strong conceptual alignment between this clinical context and the core constructs of UIT, applying this framework may provide deeper insight into how patients perceive, appraise, and manage uncertainty before surgery. Therefore, this study adopted a qualitative phenomenological design guided by Uncertainty in Illness Theory to explore the psychological experiences of patients with TBAD during the preoperative waiting period, with the aim of informing the development of targeted psychosocial support strategies.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a descriptive phenomenological qualitative design conceptually informed by Mishel’s Uncertainty in Illness Theory (UIT). Phenomenology aims to explore individuals’ lived experiences and the meanings they attribute to those experiences [15, 16]. In this study, UIT served as a conceptual reference to support the exploration of illness uncertainty within the clinical context of TBAD, rather than as a predetermined analytical structure imposed on the data. Reflexive bracketing was undertaken throughout the study to minimise the influence of researchers’ prior assumptions and clinical experiences. Researchers maintained reflexive notes to document preconceptions, interpretive decisions, and analytic reflections during data collection and analysis. This process supported an open and inductive exploration of participants’ experiences while preserving conceptual coherence. Given the complex and subjective nature of preoperative waiting in TBAD, a phenomenological approach was considered appropriate to capture how patients perceived and responded to illness-related uncertainty. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, and reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [17].

Sampling and recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants from the Cardiac and Vascular Surgery Center of a tertiary hospital in Eastern China between July and December 2025. To ensure variation in experiences, sampling considered demographic and clinical characteristics, including gender, age, education level, marital status, health insurance type, and duration of preoperative waiting. The preoperative waiting period was defined as the interval between the clinical decision to undertake elective surgical treatment and the scheduled operation.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) TBAD confirmed by thoracoabdominal computed tomography angiography according to the 2014 European Society of Cardiology guidelines [1]; (2) assessed as requiring elective surgical treatment; (3) aged ≥ 18 years, conscious, and able to communicate effectively; (4) able to provide written informed consent; and (5) absence of severe mental illness, cognitive dysfunction, major unstable chronic disease, or communication barriers.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) requirement for emergency surgery; (2) severe mental illness or cognitive dysfunction affecting communication; (3) severe unstable comorbid organic disease; and (4) refusal to participate or inability to complete the interview process.

Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights emerged from the interviews [18]. Saturation was reached after 15 interviews, and two additional interviews were conducted to confirm thematic redundancy. A total of 17 participants were ultimately included. Research team

The research team comprised both clinical and academic researchers. Clinical researchers (CL, XX, WQ), experienced in cardiac surgery, were responsible for participant recruitment and data collection, while academic researchers (PZ, CL, AH), with expertise in qualitative methodology, oversaw data analysis and interpretation. Coding and theme development were conducted collaboratively to integrate clinical insight with methodological rigor. Interviewers were not directly involved in participants’ clinical decision-making. Participants were informed that their responses would not affect their treatment to minimise potential power imbalances between researchers and participants.

Data collection

Data were collected through individual, face-to-face semi-structured interviews conducted in a private consultation room within the Cardiac Surgery Center. Eligible participants were identified in collaboration with charge nurses. Prior to each interview, participants were informed of the study purpose, procedures, confidentiality, and audio recording, and written informed consent was obtained.

A brief questionnaire was used to collect demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, education level, marital status, occupation, and duration of the preoperative waiting period. The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on Uncertainty in Illness Theory, informed by a literature review and expert consultation. It included open-ended questions exploring patients’ experiences of uncertainty during the preoperative waiting period.

The interview guide was pilot-tested with three patients to assess clarity and relevance; these data were not included in the final analysis. Minor revisions were made to improve question wording and flow. The final interview guide is provided in the Supplementary Materials, with key questions summarised in Table 2.

Semi-structured interview guide

All interviews were audio-recorded in full. Non-verbal cues, including facial expressions, body language, and vocal tone, were documented through field notes. Interview questions were flexibly adapted to participants’ responses to encourage in-depth exploration while avoiding leading prompts. Each interview lasted approximately 20–30 min and was conducted once per participant. Interviews were paused if participants experienced fatigue or emotional distress and resumed as appropriate. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 h of each interview and supplemented with detailed field notes capturing contextual information and researchers’ reflections. Clinical records were reviewed only to verify eligibility and provide contextual background and were not treated as primary qualitative data.

Data analysis

All interview data were managed using NVivo 12. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Preliminary analysis began immediately after each interview, allowing emerging insights to inform subsequent data collection.

Data were analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method [19]. Analysis was primarily inductive, with UIT used in later stages as a sensitising framework to support interpretation. Researchers first immersed themselves in the data through repeated reading of transcripts and review of audio recordings and field notes. Significant statements related to participants’ experiences during the preoperative waiting period were identified and extracted. These statements were coded and condensed into meaning units, which were compared and grouped into themes based on conceptual similarity. Themes were continuously checked against the original data to ensure fidelity to participants’ accounts. Exhaustive descriptions of each theme were developed and supported by representative quotations. Themes were subsequently synthesised to capture the essential structure of patients’ experiences. Preliminary findings were returned to a subset of participants who agreed to follow-up contact for feedback, and this input was considered in refining the analysis. Disagreements during coding and theme development were resolved through team discussion. Peer debriefing was conducted to enhance analytical rigor and minimise potential bias.

Quality control

Trustworthiness was ensured in accordance with Guba and Lincoln’s framework [20]. Credibility was established through in-depth interviews, iterative data immersion, and member checking. Dependability and confirmability were enhanced by maintaining an audit trail, alongside dual independent coding and peer debriefing. Transferability was enhanced through rich contextual descriptions and the inclusion of illustrative quotations. Additional strategies included pilot testing of the interview guide, verification of transcripts against audio recordings, and the maintenance of a reflexive and non-directive research stance. Interviews were conducted in a quiet and private setting to facilitate open and authentic participant responses.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (Approval No. 2024-089-03), and was conducted in accordance with established ethical principles.

All participants were provided with clear information regarding the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to decline to answer any question or withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their clinical care. Given the clinical condition of patients with TBAD, particular attention was paid to participants’ physical and emotional well-being during interviews. Interviews were paused or terminated if participants experienced physical or emotional distress, and appropriate medical support was sought when necessary. All data were used solely for research purposes. Audio recordings were anonymised using alphanumeric codes (H1–H17) and stored on password-protected devices. Identifiable information was removed from all transcripts.

Results

Based on interviews with 17 patients awaiting elective surgery for TBAD, four interrelated themes were identified: (1) constructing illness uncertainty, (2) appraisal of the waiting experience, (3) coping strategies, and (4) the dynamics of trust. Together, these themes depict a dynamic process of psychological adaptation during the preoperative waiting period.

Although the waiting period varied considerably among participants (4–63 days), the themes did not follow a strictly sequential temporal pattern. Instead, different appraisal and coping patterns often coexisted, although acceptance-oriented perspectives were more commonly observed among patients with longer waiting durations.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the preoperative waiting period for patients with TBAD is not merely a passive interval before surgery, but a dynamic process of psychological adaptation. During this period, patients continuously interpreted illness-related uncertainty, assigned meaning to waiting, adjusted coping strategies, and negotiated trust within interactions with the healthcare system. These findings suggest that preoperative waiting should be understood not only as a temporal issue, but also as a multidimensional psychosocial experience.

A key finding of this study was that illness uncertainty was shaped not only by insufficient or inconsistent information, but also by patients’ active interpretation of the disease. Participants frequently described uncertainty surrounding surgical timing, inconsistent communication from healthcare professionals, and unpredictable prognosis, which closely aligns with the dimensions of ambiguity and inconsistency proposed in Mishel’s Uncertainty in Illness Theory [21]. Similar findings have been reported among kidney transplant candidates, who often experience persistent health-related uncertainty and elevated informational needs during prolonged waiting periods [22]. In the absence of adequate professional explanations, patients in the present study commonly relied on previous experiences and culturally grounded lay beliefs to interpret their illness. This phenomenon can be understood through Leventhal’s Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation, which proposes that individuals construct illness representations based on prior experiences and personal beliefs [23]. Comparable patterns have also been observed in patients with heart failure, whose symptom interpretation often depends on experiential knowledge [24]. Although such interpretations may help patients establish an initial understanding of their condition, they may also contribute to misconceptions when professional guidance is lacking. These findings suggest that clinical communication should extend beyond simple information delivery and address how patients interpret and integrate information. Providing clear and consistent explanations while actively exploring patients’ existing beliefs may help establish a coherent cognitive framework and reduce uncertainty-related distress.

This study further found that patients’ emotional responses were influenced more by their appraisal of the waiting experience than by the objective duration of waiting itself. Different meanings assigned to waiting shaped how uncertainty was perceived and managed. Some participants viewed waiting as a period of threat and potential deterioration, whereas others regarded it as a necessary preparation phase for safer surgery. In contrast, patients who perceived waiting as meaningless or stagnant reported greater anxiety, irritability, and emotional distress. These findings are consistent with Lazarus and Folkman’s Stress and Coping Theory, which emphasizes that emotional responses are primarily determined by individuals’ subjective appraisal of a situation rather than by the event itself [25]. Similar coexistence of hope and anxiety has been widely reported among patients awaiting organ transplantation, who often struggle between uncertainty, fear, and hope during prolonged waiting periods [26]. Importantly, this study suggests that when waiting was framed as medically meaningful—such as allowing time for stabilization and safer surgical conditions—patients appeared more psychologically accepting of the delay. This finding highlights the importance of meaning-focused interventions in clinical practice. By clearly explaining the medical rationale for waiting, clarifying treatment goals, and emphasizing the benefits of preoperative preparation, healthcare professionals may help patients reinterpret waiting as an active and purposeful process rather than a passive delay, thereby alleviating uncertainty-related distress.

At the coping level, this study identified a continuum ranging from active information seeking to trust-based delegation, reflecting different pathways through which patients attempted to regain psychological control under uncertainty. Some patients actively sought information and compared treatment options in an effort to restore predictability and control, which is consistent with the uncertainty-reduction pathway described in UIT [21]. In contrast, other patients coped through trust-based delegation and emotional disengagement, transferring decision-making responsibilities to healthcare professionals or family members to reduce cognitive and emotional burden. Similar dependence-oriented coping patterns have been observed among patients with systemic lupus erythematosus when perceived personal control is limited [27]. Emotional distancing and cognitive reframing were also commonly observed in this study and are consistent with findings from chronic illness research showing that cognitive reappraisal may buffer anxiety and emotional distress [28]. However, when coping resources or support were insufficient, some patients shifted toward passive endurance and catastrophic thinking, characterized by helplessness and persistent fears of deterioration or death. These findings suggest that coping strategies are not stable individual traits, but dynamic responses shaped by available resources, informational environments, and situational stressors. Therefore, clinical interventions should not focus solely on providing information, but should also assess patients’ coping patterns and offer individualized psychological support.

Another important finding was the central role of trust in shaping patients’ experiences of uncertainty during the waiting period. Trust in institutional reputation, prior treatment experiences, and professional competence provided patients with an important sense of safety and reassurance. Previous studies have similarly shown that higher levels of trust in healthcare providers are associated with lower preoperative anxiety among patients undergoing lung cancer surgery [29]. However, this study further demonstrated that trust was not static; rather, it was continuously strengthened or weakened through ongoing interactions with healthcare professionals. When patients perceived sustained attention, responsiveness, and clear communication, their trust and acceptance of treatment were reinforced. Conversely, inconsistent information and insufficient communication often undermined trust and intensified uncertainty, which is consistent with previous literature on physician–patient communication and medical trust [30–31]. In addition, family-mediated trust emerged as an important pattern in this study. When direct communication with healthcare professionals was limited, some patients relied heavily on family members to obtain information and participate in decision-making. Although this approach may alleviate emotional burden, it may also reduce patients’ direct involvement in care. Collectively, these findings suggest that trust is a dynamic and relational process influenced by communication quality, informational consistency, and social support. The findings further suggest that trust may function as a relational mechanism shaping how uncertainty is appraised and managed during preoperative waiting, thereby extending the interpersonal dimension of uncertainty adaptation within acute cardiovascular contexts. Clinically, maintaining trust requires transparent communication, coordinated information delivery, and appropriate integration of family support to enhance patients’ psychological security during the waiting period.

Overall, psychological adaptation during the preoperative waiting period among patients with TBAD was shaped by the interaction among illness uncertainty, meaning appraisal, coping strategies, and trust, rather than by any single factor alone. From a clinical perspective, improving patient experiences should involve not only reducing waiting time, but also optimizing how patients understand and cope with the waiting process. Providing clear and consistent information, facilitating adaptive meaning appraisal, and supporting individualized coping may help improve adjustment during this vulnerable period. Future studies may further explore how these mechanisms evolve across different cultural and clinical contexts, as well as their long-term influence on treatment adherence and postoperative outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample was predominantly male, with only one female participant included. Although TBAD is more common among men, the limited representation of women may have restricted the exploration of gender-specific psychological experiences during the preoperative waiting period. Gender differences in emotional expression, coping styles, family roles, and healthcare interactions may influence how patients perceive uncertainty, construct meaning, and develop trust. Therefore, the findings may not fully capture the experiences of female patients. Future studies should include more female participants to further examine potential gender-related differences in psychological adaptation during the waiting period.

Second, the waiting duration varied substantially among participants, ranging from 4 to 63 days. Patients at different stages of waiting may experience distinct psychological concerns and adaptation patterns. Although no strictly sequential progression of themes was identified, patients with longer waiting durations appeared more likely to demonstrate acceptance-oriented perspectives and adaptive meaning reconstruction. Due to the cross-sectional design, this study could not capture how psychological experiences evolved over time within individuals. Future longitudinal studies are needed to further explore the dynamic trajectories of uncertainty, appraisal, coping, and trust throughout the preoperative waiting process.

Conclusion

The preoperative waiting period for patients with TBAD represents a dynamic process of psychological adaptation characterized by illness uncertainty, meaning appraisal, coping adjustment, and evolving trust in healthcare providers. Patients’ psychological experiences were shaped not only by the objective uncertainty of the disease, but also by how they interpreted waiting, managed emotional distress, and interacted with the healthcare system. Clear and consistent communication, adaptive meaning reconstruction, individualized coping support, and sustained trust-building may help alleviate uncertainty-related distress during this vulnerable period. These findings extend the application of Uncertainty in Illness Theory to the preoperative context of TBAD and provide clinically relevant insights for developing patient-centered psychological support strategies during surgical waiting periods.
