Abstract
This paper develops a novel method to simulate financial market reactions to European Central Bank (ECB) press conferences using a Large Language Model (LLM). We create a behavioral, agent-based simulation of 30 synthetic traders, each with distinct risk preferences, cognitive biases, and interpretive styles. These agents forecast Euro interest rate swap levels at 3-month, 2-year, and 10-year maturities, with the variation across forecasts serving as a measure of market uncertainty or disagreement. We evaluate three prompting strategies, naive, few-shot (enriched with historical data), and an advanced iterative “LLM-as-a-Judge” framework, to assess the effect of prompt design on predictive performance. Even the naive approach generates a strong correlation (~0.5) between synthetic disagreement and actual market outcomes, particularly for longer-term maturities. The LLM-as-a-Judge framework further improves accuracy at the first iteration. These results demonstrate that LLM-driven simulations can capture interpretive uncertainty beyond traditional measures, providing central banks with a practical tool to anticipate market reactions, refine communication strategies, and enhance financial stability.

Introduction

“It is hard to imagine that we could train a computer to read Federal Reserve transcripts
the way we do. […] We thoroughly expect to be made largely redundant by computers
eventually, but perhaps not for a few years to come.”
Romer and Romer, 2023

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have opened up a plethora of possibilities for economic and financial research. Due to the way these models are trained, on vast and diverse textual data, and their ability to generate contextually rich and realistic responses, LLMs can now be viewed as implicit computational representations of human behavior. Much like the economic construct of homo economicus, these models can be endowed with information, placed in hypothetical scenarios, and observed for their responses
(
Horton, 2023
)
. Notwithstanding their growing capabilities, the application of LLMs in finance has so far been relatively limited.

1

A notable exception is, for example,
Lopez-Lira and Tang, 2023
.
Yet, the financial domain may be one of the most promising areas for their use. Unlike traditional economic models that assume rational agents and efficient markets, real-world financial markets are often driven by sentiment, narrative, and behavioral biases, factors that are difficult to capture with purely mathematical models, especially in high-frequency settings.
2
2

2

Agent-based models (ABMs) have emerged as a flexible framework to incorporate such heterogeneity
(
Farmer and Foley, 2009
)
. In the context of monetary policy, ABMs have been used to simulate expectation formation, market volatility, and policy transmission
(
Delli Gatti et al., 2011
)
. However, most ABMs rely on stylized behavioral rules imposed by the researcher
(
Horton, 2023
)
.

A key area where LLMs can offer significant value is in modeling reactions to central bank communication, a cornerstone of modern monetary policy. It is well-established that the effectiveness of monetary policy hinges not just on the decisions themselves, but on the language used and its interpretation by economic agents. Press conferences, speeches, and official statements play a pivotal role in shaping market expectations and, consequently, asset prices
(
Gurkaynak et al., 2005
;
Altavilla et al., 2019
)
. In previous research, we moved beyond the general direction of the market response to highlight the crucial role of response heterogeneity; in the Euro area, high disagreement following a statement transmits to financial markets through a separate channel compared to the standard monetary policy surprise channel, and can dampen the intended impact of policy tightening by lowering the term premium of the yield curve
(
Collodel and Kunzmann, 2025
)
.
3
3

3

(
Bauer et al., 2021
)
find similar results for the US.
Capturing this nuanced, belief-driven heterogeneity is a challenge for traditional models but is a natural fit for the capabilities of LLMs.

This paper introduces a novel approach to modeling market reactions to ECB communication by leveraging the capabilities of LLMs. We develop a behavioral, agent-based simulation of 30 synthetic traders through Gemini LLM, one of Google’s leading AI products. These agents respond to the texts of ECB press conferences by forecasting Euro interest rate swap levels across three key maturities, short (3m), medium (2y), and long-term (10y). The main idea is that the model can capture a range of possible interpretations of these conferences. Thus, the variation in the resulting forecasts, measured by their standard deviation, serves as a proxy for market uncertainty or disagreement. By correlating this synthetic measure with actual market outcomes, we study the ability of LLMs to replicate the heterogeneity of investors’ beliefs.

To further explore how the design of prompting influences this correlation, we implement three distinct prompting strategies. The first is a naive or ”zero-shot” prompt
(
Radford et al., 2019
)
, which serves as the baseline: each agent is assigned a simple, pre-defined level of risk aversion, a set of cognitive biases, and a unique interpretive style. The second is a ”few-shot” prompt
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
, which enriches the agent’s context by including the past three ECB press conferences along with the corresponding changes in market-based disagreement resulting from them. Finally, we introduce an LLM-as-a-Judge prompt, our most advanced strategy
(
Zheng et al., 2023
)
. In this iterative setup, a separate LLM, the Judge, acts as a critical evaluator, systematically reviewing the analyst prompt and its resulting in-sample correlation score. The Judge then rewrites the prompt to improve alignment with market reactions. This revised prompt is fed back into the Analyst LLM, forming a feedback loop that continues over different iterations. The different prompt versions are subsequently evaluated out-of-sample to assess their robustness and decrease concerns relative to model over-fitting.

We find that LLM-based agents can effectively replicate the complex and varied reactions of investors to ECB press conferences, achieving a remarkable correlation of around 0.5 between the synthetic disagreement measure and actual market outcomes for medium- and long-term maturities, a substantial result given the inherent complexity and noise of financial markets. Contrary to expectations, a more detailed few-shot prompt does not improve this correlation; however, the LLM-as-a-Judge framework, significantly enhances predictive accuracy upon its initial application, with performance degrading thereafter. This breakthrough offers policymakers a powerful new tool to preemptively evaluate and refine central bank communications, identifying language that may trigger market disagreement and volatility well before public release. By moving beyond traditional sentiment analysis, our approach provides a nuanced understanding of how monetary policy signals are perceived and transmitted through financial markets. Integrating insights from macroeconomic communication, behavioral finance, and artificial intelligence, this framework presents a transformative lens for both scholars and decision-makers to better anticipate and manage the financial market impact of monetary policy announcements.

Literature Review

The paper contributes to three intersecting literatures: monetary policy communication, behavioral expectations, and the emerging use of large language models (LLMs) in economic analysis.

Our first contribution is to the literature on monetary policy communication, particularly the strand that identifies policy shocks using high-frequency movements in asset prices within narrow windows around policy announcements. Seminal studies for the United States show that Federal Reserve statements influence asset prices not only through policy rate changes but also through the qualitative content of the accompanying communication
(
Gurkaynak et al., 2005
;
Gertler and Karadi, 2015
;
Hansen and McMahon, 2016
;
Bauer and Swanson, 2022
)
. In these studies, changes in risk-free market rates measured before and after announcements serve as proxies for monetary policy shocks. In the euro area, similar evidence shows that ECB press conferences contain significant informational content beyond the policy decision itself
(
Altavilla et al., 2019
)
. Recent work by
Bauer et al., 2021
further distinguishes between the direction of policy surprises and the uncertainty surrounding the policy stance, showing that elevated uncertainty depresses equity prices, raises sovereign spreads, and increases exchange rate volatility, a pattern we also find for the Euro area
(
Collodel and Kunzmann, 2025
)
. Furthermore,
Tillmann, 2020
finds that hawkish surprises are less effective in periods of heightened short- and medium-term uncertainty, as investors shift toward longer maturities in search of safety, thereby flattening the yield curve. While this literature has convincingly demonstrated that communication shocks move markets, it remains silent on how they do so. By relying on market-price changes as statistical proxies, the existing approach leaves the interpretive process of agents as a “black box.” Our work opens that box: we construct an agent-based model in which heterogeneous participants directly process the natural language of policy announcements, allowing us to trace how communication shapes expectations, generates uncertainty, and propagates into financial market dynamics.

The second body of work we engage with concerns behavioral expectations. The rational expectations hypothesis, foundational to much of modern macro-finance, has been increasingly challenged by empirical evidence documenting systematic biases and bounded rationality among investors
(
Barberis and Thaler, 2003
;
Bordalo et al., 2018
)
. Standard representative-agent models struggle to reconcile such behavioral deviations with observed phenomena like excess volatility and momentum. Agent-based models (ABMs) have emerged as a flexible alternative
(
Farmer and Foley, 2009
)
, allowing for heterogeneous, adaptive agents whose interactions generate emergent macroeconomic patterns. Within monetary policy research, ABMs have been used to model expectation formation and policy transmission in bottom-up settings
(
Delli Gatti et al., 2011
)
. Yet two key limitations remain. First, behavioral rules are typically imposed ex ante as stylized heuristics
(
Horton, 2023
)
, rather than learned or emergent. Second, conventional ABMs cannot interpret qualitative information directly, despite the fact that monetary policy signals are largely communicated in natural language. This omission bypasses an essential channel of transmission. Our framework addresses both limitations by enabling agents to process and act upon policy-relevant textual information.

Finally, our study connects to a rapidly developing literature applying LLMs to economic analysis. By processing vast amounts of unstructured text, ranging from macroeconomic reports and corporate disclosures to central bank statements, LLMs extend the reach of empirical economics beyond numerical datasets. In forecasting applications, LLMs can be used directly
(
Castro and Leibovici, 2023
;
Hansen et al., 2024
;
Carriero et al., 2025
)
or indirectly via sentiment extraction, where the derived sentiment serves then as an input to other models.
4
4

4

For example, recent work at the ECB demonstrates that ChatGPT-generated sentiment from PMI commentary improves GDP nowcasts relative to traditional benchmarks
(
de Bondt and Sun, 2025
)
.
In financial markets, fine-tuned models such as FinBERT or OPT have achieved substantially higher predictive accuracy and trading performance than dictionary-based approaches
(
BIS, 2024
;
Kirtac and Germano, 2024
)
.

A second application is the construction of LLM-powered simulated economic agents, “homo silicus”
(
Horton, 2023
)
—who are endowed with preferences, information, and decision rules, and can interact in artificial market environments. Frameworks like EconAgent
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
and ASFM
(
Li et al., 2024
)
produce heterogeneous, adaptive behavior that is richer and more realistic than the stylized rules of conventional ABMs, while also capturing the interpretation of complex qualitative signals.

A third and especially relevant strand applies LLMs to the analysis of central bank communication. This includes large-scale classification of monetary policy documents by topic, stance, and sentiment, as in the IMF’s recent work on over 75,000 documents from 169 central banks worldwide
(
Gelos et al., 2025
)
, as well as the development of domain-specific models such as CB-LMs, which improve detection of nuanced policy stances
(
BIS, 2025
)
, and CentralBankRoBERTa, which classifies macroeconomic agents and sentiment toward them
(
Gabriel et al., 2023
)
. Other advances, like the Bundesbank’s MILA system, employ role-based prompt engineering to analyze ECB statements sentence-by-sentence, providing interpretable assessments of tone
(
Bundesbank, 2024
)
. These innovations have revealed systematic shifts in policy stances and measurably improved the prediction of policy decisions
(
DIW, 2025
)
.

Our approach delivers a methodological advance with clear policy relevance. We provide the first micro-founded framework in which heterogeneous, boundedly rational agents interpret central bank communication in real time and translate qualitative policy signals into market-relevant actions. This design closes a long-standing gap between the empirical identification of communication shocks and the theoretical modeling of expectation formation. By combining sentiment extraction, agent-based modeling, and LLM-driven language interpretation in a unified environment, we move beyond reduced-form event studies and open the “black box” of how policy language is processed by market participants. The framework is adaptable across institutional settings, enabling comparative analyses of communication strategies by central banks worldwide. This makes it directly relevant to current debates on transparency, forward guidance, and the role of central bank credibility in shaping financial conditions in both advanced and emerging market economies.

3
Methodology

Our methodology proceeds in three main steps: (i) construction of a structured database of ECB communication events and corresponding market reactions; (ii) development of LLM-based synthetic agents embedded in an agent-based simulation framework; and (iii) evaluation of the correspondence between synthetic measures of belief heterogeneity and actual market outcomes. We focus on European Central Bank (ECB) press conferences, which constitute the primary vehicle for conveying monetary policy decisions in the Euro area. Following the literature on high-frequency identification of monetary policy shocks
(
Gurkaynak et al., 2005
;
Altavilla et al., 2019
)
, we define event dates as the days of regularly scheduled ECB Governing Council monetary policy meetings, limiting the sample to those that include both a written policy statement and a subsequent press conference by the ECB President. For each event, we collect the full transcript of the press conference as published on the ECB website as well as the associated OIS post-conference volatility for the short (3-months), medium (2-years), and long (10-years) term.
5
5

5

Following the monetary policy communication literature, we use OIS rates as they reflect pure interest rate expectations without the credit risk component present in government bond yields
(
Gurkaynak et al., 2005
)
.
Our final sample comprises 283 ECB conferences between 1998-06-09 and 2025-04-17.

The data reveal pronounced spikes during periods of heightened policy uncertainty, notably the 2008-2009 financial crisis, the European sovereign debt crisis, and the recent tightening cycle initiated in 2022. Volatility exhibits a clear maturity structure, with consistently higher levels at longer tenors reflecting greater uncertainty about the medium- and long-term policy trajectory compared to near-term rate expectations.

3.1
Agents

We construct a population of 30 synthetic agents, each instantiated through Gemini 2.5-Flash model, one of Google’s most recent large language model.
6
6

6

We use 30 agents to balance computational and cost efficiency with sufficient heterogeneity to capture cross-sectional variation.
The agents are designed to interpret the natural language of ECB press conferences and produce quantitative forecasts of Euro IRS rates at the three maturities. The simulation proceeds as follows. Each agent is endowed with:

1.

Risk aversion parameter
– fixed at the start of the simulation, drawn from a pre-defined distribution to induce heterogeneity in risk preferences.

2.

Cognitive bias profile
– encompassing tendencies such as optimism, pessimism, anchoring, or overreaction, assigned to influence interpretation.

3.

Interpretive style
– a qualitative descriptor shaping the reading of ECB communication (e.g., focusing on inflation outlook, growth projections, or geopolitical risks).

These attributes are embedded in the prompt given to the LLM for each agent, ensuring that the same press conference produces different interpretations across agents.
7
7

7

We set the temperature parameter equal to 1. Temperature is a parameter that controls the randomness of a language model’s output. Lower values (e.g., 0.2) make the model more deterministic and focused, while higher values (e.g., 1.0 or above) increase creativity and variability by making the model more likely to sample less probable words.

3.2
Prompting Strategies

We test three prompting strategies of increasing complexity:

Zero-Shot Prompt:

The baseline approach, where the agent receives only the ECB press conference transcript along with its fixed attributes.

Few-Shot Prompt:

Augments the baseline by including the transcripts of the three most recent ECB press conferences and the associated historical changes in market-based disagreement, providing a short memory of past communication–market interactions.
8
8

8

Big LLMs are notoriously difficult to fine-tune because of computational and cost-related issues. Moreover, we do not have enough observations for this use case. In these instances, a ”few-shot” technique has been shown to improve over a ”zero-shot” benchmark
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
.

LLM-as-a-Judge Prompt:

Introduces a second LLM, the ‘‘Judge,’’ which reviews each agent’s initial prompt and in-sample performance (correlation with market disagreement) and iteratively rewrites the prompt to improve alignment. The improved prompt is fed back into the ‘‘Analyst’’ LLM, forming an adaptive feedback loop. The process continues over multiple iterations before all the prompts provided by the analyst are tested out-of-sample.

9

The split train-test is 75/25 in chronological order.

Following the literature, we use for the ”Judge” a more potent model compared to the ”Analyst”, namely Gemini 2.5-Pro

3.3
Evaluation

We evaluate the ability of the LLM to reproduce markets’ heterogeneity by correlating the synthetic disagreement measures with the empirical disagreement observed in market data. Our main metric is the Spearman correlation coefficient, computed separately for short, medium, and long maturities. We also compare in-sample and out-of-sample performance for each prompting strategy in the LLM-as-Judge approach to gauge the extent of overfitting.
15
15

15

The LLM-as-a-Judge approach is expected to produce higher out-of-sample correlations if the adaptive prompt optimization captures persistent features of agent interpretation rather than idiosyncratic event noise.
Finally, we analyze whether certain maturities are more sensitive to synthetic disagreement, providing insights into which parts of the yield curve are most affected by interpretive heterogeneity in response to central bank communication. Figure

Results

4.1
Zero-Shot Prompt

The zero-shot specification provides the most direct test of whether LLM agents endowed only with fixed behavioral traits and no historical training context can reproduce the ebb and flow of market sentiment. Before turning to formal correlation measures, Figures
4(a)
and
4(b)
offer a visual narrative of the simulated output.

Panel
4(a)
plots the share of agents predicting an increase, decrease, or no change in rates after each ECB press conference. Even in this naive setup, the agents’ directional calls broadly mirror well-known phases of ECB policy: a tightening bias before the Great Recession, a pronounced easing wave thereafter, a prolonged “on hold” stance at the short end during the zero lower bound (ZLB) years (contrasted with a slow grind lower for long maturities), followed by the sharp pandemic loosening and rapid tightening-partial reversal of the recent cycle. This ability to trace policy phases without explicit historical anchoring suggests that the agents’ built-in interpretive rules are sufficient to generate economically coherent directional patterns.

Panel
4(b)
shows the cross-sectional standard deviation of forecasts. Dispersion contracts during periods of unambiguous policy stance, such as the post-crisis easing and much of the ZLB era, when consensus is high. It spikes at turning points—the onset of the Great Recession, the sovereign debt crisis, and recent tightening cycles—where disagreement arises less from direction and more from uncertainty over speed, magnitude, and endpoint.
17
17

17

This is consistent with the ECB 2025 Monetary Policy Strategy Review
(
ECB, 2025
)
findings. The report shows that, once forward guidance was abandoned, the sensitivity of OIS rates to macroeconomic news increased significantly, hence amplifying excess volatility. Relatedly, the early phase of the 2022 hiking cycle was marked by historically large monetary policy shocks, including the unexpected 50 basis point hike in July 2022 and the largest hawkish surprise since the global financial crisis in December 2022, reflecting the challenge of aligning communication with policy moves.
These patterns mirror survey-based and market-implied measures of disagreement, where even well-telegraphed policy paths can generate substantial heterogeneity in rate forecasts if the terminal rate remains uncertain.

4.2
Few-Shot Prompt Results

The few-shot specification augments the prompt with the previous three ECB press conferences alongside their subsequent market-based measures of disagreement. This additional context is designed to help the LLM agents anchor their interpretations in patterns historically observed between ECB communication and subsequent market reactions, potentially enabling more informed directional and dispersion forecasts.

The absence of improvement in the few-shot setting is consistent with two well-documented mechanisms in expectations formation. First, supplying a small and potentially unrepresentative set of recent examples can induce imitation and anchoring, compressing cross-sectional variance (e.g.
Banerjee, 1992
;
Morris and Shin, 2002
). Second, when those examples originate from a different policy or macroeconomic regime, anchoring can miscalibrate forecasts, especially at turning points
(
Orphanides and Williams, 2005
)
. In our setup, a static few-shot context shared across agents, these forces plausibly reduce heterogeneity without yielding stronger market correlations.

Thus, that LLM agents exhibit stronger anchoring than market-based measures likely reflects the simulation design. The few-shot exemplars provide a fixed, shared reference point for all agents, with no access to broader or regime-specific information. LLMs in this setup cannot down-weight unrepresentative precedents, detect regime shifts, or benefit from incentives to differentiate. By contrast, human forecasters draw on heterogeneous information, adjust for changing regimes, and face strategic incentives that sustain disagreement.
23
23

23

For example, price discovery actively amplifies minority views.
For applications where distributional realism and market-like disagreement are more important than point accuracy, supplying a few recent examples can unintentionally induce consensus. Prompt design should therefore be tailored to each unique use case.

4.3
LLM-as-a-Judge

The
Judge
framework introduces an explicit feedback loop into the simulation. In this setting, a separate LLM evaluates each analyst’s past forecasting performance and rewrites the prompt to improve alignment with observed market reactions. This design allows the forecasting agents to adapt iteratively. We continue this back-and-forth for a total of 5 iterations, testing the new prompts on a training set, and then use every prompt produced for the test set.
24
24

24

As for the other models, each prompt is run 5 times to get a robust standard deviation measure. This means we are running a total of 50 simulations: 5 simulations per iteration, 5 iterations, and 2 different datasets, train and test. This needs, however, also to be multiplied by the total number of ECB press conferences in our sample.

An analysis of the model’s performance reveals a high degree of robustness and effective generalization from the training to the test dataset. Simple Benchmark Comparison

Our LLM-based framework captures heterogeneous market reactions to ECB press conferences with substantial accuracy. Correlations between the simulated disagreement and actual post-announcement OIS volatility reach roughly 0.5 for medium- and long-term tenors, with performance further enhanced under the LLM-as-a-Judge framework at early iterations. These results remain stable across training and test datasets, indicating that the model captures fundamental communication-market relationships rather than memorizing patterns.

5
Conclusions

This paper introduces a novel methodology using Large Language Models to simulate heterogeneous market reactions to ECB press conferences. Through an agent-based framework with 30 synthetic traders, each endowed with distinct behavioral characteristics, we demonstrate that LLMs can effectively capture market disagreement patterns in response to monetary policy communication.

Our key findings advance both theoretical understanding and practical application in monetary economics. First, even a naive zero-shot approach achieves substantial correlations (~0.5) with actual market disagreement, particularly for medium and long-term maturities. This suggests LLMs possess intrinsic capabilities to decode central bank communication in economically meaningful ways. Second, contrary to other studies employing LLMs for macroeconomic forecasting (i.e.
Hansen et al., 2024
), few-shot prompting with historical context does not improve performance. This highlights that shared examples can induce excessive anchoring when employed in volatily simulation rather than pure point estimates. Third, the LLM-as-a-Judge framework substantially enhances performance at early iterations (average correlation across tenors reaching 0.57), though subsequent iterations show degradation, emphasizing the need for human oversight in automated prompt optimization. The stability of results across training and test datasets demonstrates that our approach captures fundamental communication-market relationships rather than memorizing patterns.

These results have significant practical implications. For central banks, our framework offers a powerful
ex-ante
tool to evaluate communication strategies and identify potentially market-disrupting language before release. For market participants, it provides insights into the drivers of post-announcement volatility. For researchers, it opens the ”black box” of expectation formation by providing a micro-founded framework that explicitly models interpretive processes, moving beyond traditional reduced-form event studies. The methodology is readily adaptable to other central banks and policy contexts.

Despite these contributions, a few limitations remain. The replicability of our results is partly constrained by the versioning and closed-source nature of contemporary LLMs, meaning that future iterations of the same model may yield slightly different outputs. A natural way forward would be to employ open-source models, which would allow for greater control, transparency, and long-term reproducibility.
30
30

30

See
Rogers and Luccioni, 2023
for a thorough discussion.
Regarding potential contamination, while ECB press conferences are relatively niche compared to mainstream internet content, the possibility that fragments of such communication—or analyses thereof—were included in the model’s pretraining data cannot be completely dismissed. In our setup, we explicitly instruct the model to use only information available up to the date of each press conference; however, this constraint affects its responses, not its underlying parameters. Nevertheless, we consider the risk of substantial contamination to be limited, as discussions of post-ECB press conference volatility are relatively rare in the public domain, with only a small number of academic papers addressing the topic. Future research should focus on tackling these issues. Moreover, we consider these results a lower bound: testing with domain-specific LLMs or fine-tuned models could shed light on the trade-off between interpretive diversity and specialized expertise, potentially improving both the accuracy and the behavioral realism of simulated market reactions.

In conclusion, this paper establishes a new paradigm for analyzing monetary policy communication at the intersection of artificial intelligence, behavioral finance, and central banking practice. By showing that LLMs can simulate heterogeneous trader interpretations and capture market disagreement patterns, we provide policymakers with a powerful tool to anticipate market reactions and refine communication strategies, while offering researchers a framework to better understand the micro-foundations of expectation formation.
