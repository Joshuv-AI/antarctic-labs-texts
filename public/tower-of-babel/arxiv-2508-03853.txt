Norms with this property

As such, unitarily invariant norms are of great importance and utility to a wide range of disciplines, ranging from multiple areas of pure mathematics and theoretical physics, to algorithm design, signal processing, and data analysis.

Examples of unitarily invariant norms include the Schatten von-Neumann trace class
\mathsf{S}_{p}^{n}

Let

Remark 1

Formally, when

Problem 4

It remains an interesting open question to determine the growth rate of
\mathsf{e}(\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n})

does not provide an upper bound on

\mathsf{e}(\mathbf{X})

and an

\mathbf{X}=\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}

\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}
is replaced by any unitarily invariant norm on

As another application of Theorem
2
, if we are given oracle access to (approximate) norm computations in a unitarily invariant normed space
\mathbf{X}=(\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{X}})

\mathsf{SEP}(\mathbf{X})
up to universal constant factors in oracle polynomial time.

3

A weak membership oracle suffices here (one can obtain from such a membership oracle a norm evaluation oracle by binary search of possible upper bounds on the norm of the queried vector). See
[
27
]
for the relevant (standard) background in algorithmic convex geometry. In particular, “oracle polynomial time” means that the algorithm consists of a binary Turing machine that is augmented by the given oracle, and its running time is polynomial in the usual sense, with the understanding that the time required by each call to the oracle is only what is needed to write the question onto, and read the answer from, a tape of the Turing machine. Thus, here we demand that the algorithm outputs a number that is guaranteed to be bounded from above and from below by positive universal constant multiples of
\mathsf{SEP}(\mathbf{X})
, yet it performs only
n^{O(1)}
norm evaluations.

1.1.
Reverse Faber–Krahn

The new ingredient of our proof of Theorem
7
is an asymptotic evaluation of the spectral gap of the Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions on the unit ball
B_{\mathbf{X}}\subseteq\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
of any unitarily invariant norm
\mathbf{X}=(\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{X}})
. We will next describe this; all of the relevant (classical and rudimentary) background on the spectral properties of the Dirichlet Laplacian can be found in
[
52
,
20
]
.

Fix
n\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
. For a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\lambda(\Omega)
be the smallest
\lambda>0
such that there exists a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
that is smooth on the interior of
\Omega
, vanishes on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
, and satisfies
\Delta f=-\lambda f
on the interior of
\Omega
, where
\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{2}/(\partial x_{i}^{2})
is the standard Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Set
\lambda(\mathbf{Y})=\lambda(B_{\mathbf{Y}})
for each normed space
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{Y}})
. Here, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 6
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, every unitarily invariant normed space
\mathbf{X}=(\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{X}})
satisfies:

\lambda(\mathbf{X})\asymp n^{3}\|\mathsf{I}_{n}\|_{\mathbf{X}}^{2}.

(12)

In the setting of Theorem
6
, the following asymptotic equivalence is a different way to express (
12
):

\lambda(B_{\mathbf{X}})\mathrm{vol}_{\dim(\mathbf{X})}^{\frac{2}{\dim(\mathbf{X})}}(B_{\mathbf{X}})\asymp\dim(\mathbf{X}),

(13)

where for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant m
the
\alpha
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is denoted
\mathrm{vol}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
; the only values of
\alpha
that occur herein are
\alpha\in\{m,m-1\}
(Lebesgue measure and surface area measure, respectively). To see that (
13
) and (
12
) coincide, note that
\dim(\mathbf{X})=n^{2}
and by
[
55
, equation (2.2)]
we have:

\mathrm{vol}_{n^{2}}(B_{\mathbf{X}})^{\frac{1}{n^{2}}}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\|\mathsf{I}_{n}\|_{\mathbf{X}}}.

(14)

The Faber–Krahn inequality
[
21
,
37
]
(originally conjectured in
[
53
]
) states that the left hand side of (
13
) is minimized when
B_{\mathbf{X}}
is replaced by the unit ball of the standard Euclidean metric (in our case
\mathsf{S}_{2}^{n}
, though the analogous statement holds if
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is replaced by
\mathbb{R}^{m}
of any dimension). By a direct computation (see e.g.
[
48
, page 32]
), this classical extremal statement implies that the left hand side of (

) is bounded from above and from below by positive universal constant multiples of
\dim(\mathbf{X})
.

The new content of (
13
) is therefore the reverse inequality, i.e., the estimate
\lambda(\mathbf{X})\lesssim n^{3}\|\mathsf{I}_{n}\|_{\mathbf{X}}^{2}
in Theorem
6
. This confirms for (unit balls of) unitarily invariant matrix norms the following much more general reverse Faber–Krahn phenomenon that was conjectured in
[
48
]
:

Conjecture 7

(reverse Faber–Krahn)
.

For every origin-symmertric convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
(thus,
x\in K
if and only if
-x\in K
) there is a volume-preserving linear transformation
S\in\mathsf{SL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
with
\lambda(SK)\mathrm{vol}_{m}(K)^{2/m}\asymp m

Theorem
6
demonstrates that Conjecture

holds for any unitarily invariant norm on
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
with the matrix
S
being the (
n^{2}
-by-
n^{2}
) identity on
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
; this too was conjectured in
[
48
]
(see specifically Conjecture 50 there, as well as
[
48
, Problem 44]
and
[
48
, Remark 172]
).

Proof of Theorem

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. It was proved in
[
48
, Remark 172]
that if Theorem (

) holds for
\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}
, then Theorem (
9
) holds for every unitarily invariant normed space
\mathbf{X}=(\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{X}})
. By the equivalence of Theorem
6
and Theorem
8
that was proved in
[
48
]
and we discussed in Section
1.2
, it therefore suffices to prove Theorem
6
when
\mathbf{X}=\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}
, where we recall that
\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}
is
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
equipped with the
\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}\to\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}
operator norm. So, our goal in the rest of this section will be to demonstrate that
\lambda(\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n})\asymp n^{3}
. Only the upper bound

\lambda(\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n})\lesssim n^{3}

(16)

remains to be proved, thanks to the Faber–Krahn inequality, as we explained in Section
1.1
.

For every
p,n\in\mathbb{N}
define a function
f_{n}^{p}:B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}\to[0,\infty)
by setting:

\forall\mathsf{A}\in B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}},\qquad f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathrm{det}\big(\mathsf{I}_{n}-\mathsf{A}\mathsf{A}^{\!\!*}\big)^{p}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\big(1-s_{k}(\mathsf{A})^{2}\big)^{p}.

(17)

Then,
f_{n}^{p}
is smooth (in fact, it is a polynomial in the entries of
\mathsf{A}
), and the second equality in (
17
) makes it evident that
f_{n}^{p}
vanishes on
\partial B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}=\{\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}):\ s_{1}(\mathsf{A})=1\}
. The standard (see e.g.
[
20
]
) Rayleigh quotient characterization of the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)
therefore gives:

\lambda(\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n})\leqslant\frac{\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}\|\nabla f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{2}^{n}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}}{\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}|f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}}.

(18)

Theorem
6
will thus be proven if we will evaluate exactly the right hand side of (
18
) as follows:

\frac{\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}\|\nabla f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{2}^{n}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}}{\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}}=\frac{pn^{2}(4p+n)^{2}}{2(2p-1)(4p+1)}.

(19)

Indeed, the right hand side of (
19
) is bounded from above and from below by positive universal constant multiples of
n^{2}\max\{n^{2}/p,p\}
. Therefore, up to universal constant factors setting
p=n
minimizes the right hand side of (
19
). For this choice of
p
, the Rayleigh quotient estimate (
18
) becomes
\lambda(\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n})\lesssim n^{3}
, which is our current goal (
16
). Observe that the above reasoning shows that any choice of

other than

does not yield the desired estimate (
16
) . The rest of this section will be devoted to establishing (
19
).

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we will denote by
V_{\!n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
the following (Vendermunde determinant) function:

\forall x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad V_{\!n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}|x_{i}-x_{j}|.

(20)

Define a probability density
\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}:[0,1]^{n}\to[0,\infty)
by setting:

\forall x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n},\qquad\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{V_{\!n}(x)}{Z_{n}(p)}\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{x_{r}^{2p}}{\sqrt{1-x_{r}}},

(21)

where
Z_{n}(p)>0
denotes the normalization factor such that
\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
, i.e.,

Z_{n}(p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}V_{\!n}(x)\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{x_{r}^{2p}}{\sqrt{1-x_{r}}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{n!}{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}\prod_{s=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma\big(2m+\frac{1}{2}(s+1)\big)\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}s\big)^{2}}{\Gamma\big(2m+\frac{1}{2}(n+s+1)\big)}.

(22)

The second equality in (
22
) is a special case of the classical Selberg integral formula
[
56
]
. The parameter
Z_{n}(p)
is commonly denoted in the literature (see e.g.
[
24
]
) as
S_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\upgamma)
for the setting of parameters
(\alpha,\beta,\upgamma)=(2p+1,1/2,1/2)
; using the ad hoc shorter notation
Z_{n}(p)
is beneficial here since we will not need to consider probability densities other than
\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}
. Also, we chose to use the letter
\mathfrak{J}
for the probability density on
[0,1]^{n}
in (
21
) to indicate that it belongs to the
3
-parameter family of densities of Jacobi random
n
-by-
n
matrix ensembles; specifically, using the parametrization of
[
3
, equation (4.1.5)]
, the relevant setting of parameters in the present case are
(\beta,r,s)=(1,4p+1,0)
.

There exists
c_{n}>0
such that for every Borel-measurable function
f:[0,\infty)^{n}\to[0,\infty)
that is invariant under permutations of the coordinates, i.e.,
f(s_{\pi(1)},\ldots,s_{\pi(n)})=f(s)
for every permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and every point

, the following identity holds:

\int_{\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})}f\big(s_{1}(\mathsf{A}),\ldots,s_{n}(\mathsf{A})\big)\,\mathrm{d}A=c_{n}\int_{[0,\infty)^{n}}f(s)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\prod_{j=s+1}^{n}\big|s_{i}^{2}-s_{j}^{2}\big|\,\mathrm{d}s.

(23)

(
23
) is a special case of the Weyl integration formula
[
58
]
; see e.g.
[
3
, Proposition 4.1.3]
. The exact value of the parameter
c_{n}
will not have any role in the ensuing reasoning (it will cancel out in our computations), but it is worthwhile to note in passing that
c_{n}^{1/n^{2}}\asymp 1/\sqrt{n}
; see e.g.
[
25
]
for much more on such matters.

Thanks to (
23
), the denominator in the left hand side of (
19
) can be evaluated as follows:

\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{testfxndef}\wedge\eqref{eq:weyl}}}{{=}}c_{n}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\prod_{r=1}^{n}\big(1-s_{r}^{2}\big)^{2p}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\prod_{j=s+1}^{n}\big|s_{i}^{2}-s_{j}^{2}\big|\,\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{normalization formula}}}{{=}}\frac{c_{n}}{2^{n}}Z_{n}(p),

(24)

where for the last step of (

, whose Jacobian is
(-1)^{n}/(2^{n}\prod_{r=1}^{n}\sqrt{1-x_{r}})
.

Next, we need to understand the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the gradient of

. For this, as a special case of

(25)

then for every matrix
\mathsf{A}
in the interior of

such that

\nabla f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})=\mathsf{U}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial g_{n}^{p}}{\partial x_{1}}\big(s_{1}(\mathsf{A}),\ldots,s_{n}(\mathsf{A})\big)&0&\dots&0\\
0&\frac{\partial g_{n}^{p}}{\partial x_{2}}\big(s_{1}(\mathsf{A}),\ldots,s_{n}(\mathsf{A})\big)&\ddots&\vdots\\

By substituting the definition (

, we thus have:

\nabla f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})=2p\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big(1-s_{i}(\mathsf{A})^{2}\big)^{p}\mathsf{U}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{s_{1}(\mathsf{A})}{1-s_{1}(\mathsf{A})^{2}}&0&\dots&0\\

\|\nabla f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{2}^{n}}^{2}=4p^{2}\prod_{r=1}^{n}\big(1-s_{r}(\mathsf{A})^{2}\big)^{2p}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{s_{k}(\mathsf{A})^{2}}{\left(1-s_{k}(\mathsf{A})^{2}\right)^{2}}.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}\|\nabla f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{2}^{n}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:weyl}\wedge\eqref{eq:norm of gradient}}}{{=}}4p^{2}c_{n}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{s_{k}^{2}}{\left(1-s_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)\prod_{r=1}^{n}\big(1-s_{r}^{2}\big)^{2p}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\prod_{j=s+1}^{n}\big|s_{i}^{2}-s_{j}^{2}\big|\,\mathrm{d}x\\

(27)

\frac{\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}\|\nabla f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{2}^{n}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}}{\int_{B_{\mathsf{S}_{\infty}^{n}}}|f_{n}^{p}(\mathsf{A})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{A}}=4p^{2}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\Big(\sum_{s=1}^{n}\frac{1-x_{s}}{x_{s}^{2}}\Big)\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=4p^{2}n\bigg(\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)}{x_{n}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)}{x_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg).

(28)

To evaluate the right hand side of (
28
), consider the following symmetric polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots x_{n}]
:

P(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\prod_{t\in[n]\smallsetminus\{s\}}x_{t}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}\sum_{s=1}^{n-1}\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}x_{s}x_{t}\prod_{k\in[n]\smallsetminus\{s,t\}}x_{k}^{2},

(29)

where in (

), and throughout, we use the common notation
[n]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Thus,

\frac{1}{Z_{n}(p)}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}P(x)V_{\!n}(x)\prod_{s=1}^{n}\frac{x_{s}^{2p-2}}{\sqrt{1-x_{s}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:J density def}}}{{=}}n\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)}{x_{n}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{n(n-1)}{3}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)}{x_{n-1}x_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(30)

The final integral that appears in (
30
) can be evaluated as follows:

\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)}{x_{n-1}x_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:J density def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{Z_{n}(p)}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}V_{\!n}(x)\bigg(\prod_{t=1}^{n-2}x_{t}\bigg)\prod_{s=1}^{n}\frac{x_{s}^{2p-1}}{\sqrt{1-x_{s}}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(4p+n)(4p+n+1)}{4p(4p+1)},

(31)

where the second equality that appears in (
31
) is an instantiation of the Aomoto integral formula
[
6
]
(see also e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 8.1.2]
). To evaluate the integral that appears in the left hand side of (
30
), observe that the polynomial
P
that is defined in (
29
) coincides with the Jack polynomial that is denoted in
[
24
]
by
P^{(1/\upgamma)}_{\lambda}
, where
\upgamma=1/2
and
\lambda
is the vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
whose first
n-1
coordinates equal
2
and the whose last coordinate equals
0
; this is seen by substituting the aforementioned parameters
\upgamma,\lambda
into equations (3.2) and (3.10) of
[
24
]
. The Kadell integral formula
[
30
]
, which appears in
[
24
]
as equation (3.13) thus gives:

\frac{1}{Z_{n}(p)}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}P(x)V_{\!n}(x)\prod_{s=1}^{n}\frac{x_{s}^{2p-2}}{\sqrt{1-x_{s}}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{n(n+2)(4p+n)(4p+n-2)}{24p(2p-1)}.

(32)

The first integral that appears in the right hand side of (
28
) is therefore evaluated as follows:

\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)}{x_{n}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:integrate P}\wedge\eqref{eq:n-2}\wedge\eqref{eq:use kadell}}}{{=}}\frac{(n+2)(4p+n)(4p+n-2)}{24p(2p-1)}-\frac{(n-1)(4p+n)(4p+n+1)}{12p(4p+1)}.

(33)

Now, the second integral that appears in the right hand side of (

\int_{[0,1]^{n}}\frac{\mathfrak{J}_{n}^{p}(x)}{x_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:J density def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{Z_{n}(p)}\int_{[0,1]^{n}}V_{\!n}(x)\bigg(\prod_{t=1}^{n-1}x_{t}\bigg)\prod_{s=1}^{n}\frac{x_{s}^{2p-1}}{\sqrt{1-x_{s}}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{4p+n}{4p},

(34)

where the second step of (

3.
Proof of Theorem
5

Observe that every
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies the following estimates:

\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}}\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{n}|x_{s}|\cdot\|e_{s}\|_{\mathbf{E}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:norms are reasonable}}}{{\leqslant}}e^{n^{D}}\sum_{s=1}^{n}|x_{s}|\leqslant\sqrt{n}e^{n^{D}}\|x\|_{\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}},

(35)

where the last step of (
35
) is an application of Cauchy–Schwarz, and also:

\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:1 symmetric}}}{{=}}\max_{s\in[n]}\frac{\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}}+\|(-x_{1},\ldots,-x_{s-1},x_{s},-x_{s+1},\ldots,-x_{n})\|_{\mathbf{E}}}{2}\geqslant\max_{s\in[n]}|x_{s}|\cdot\|e_{s}\|_{\mathbf{E}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:norms are reasonable}}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{\|x\|_{\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}}{\sqrt{n}e^{n^{D}}},

(36)

where the penultimate step of (
36
) is an application of the triangle inequality for
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{E}}
. Therefore,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}e^{n^{D}}}B_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:use Cauchy schwarz}}}{{\subseteq}}B_{\mathbf{E}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:use convexity}}}{{\subseteq}}\sqrt{n}e^{n^{D}}\!\!B_{\!\ell_{\!\!2}^{n}}.

(37)

Using the terminology of
[
27
]
, the inclusions (
37
) mean that
B_{\mathbf{E}}
is well–bounded and centered. Since in Theorem
5
we are assuming that
B_{\mathbf{E}}
is given by a weak membership oracle, the Judin–Nemirovskiĭ theorem
[
29
]
(see
[
27
, Section 4.3]
), which is a striking application of the Shallow-Cut Ellipsoid Method
[
29
]
(see
[
27
, Section 3.3]
), implies it is possible to optimize linear functionals efficiently on
B_{\mathbf{E}}
. In particular, since the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}
is given by
\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}=\max_{y\in B_{\mathbf{E}}}\langle x,y\rangle
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the standard scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it follows that there is an algorithm
\mathrm{Eval}_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}
which takes as input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and outputs in oracle time that is polynomial in both
\log(1/\delta)
and
n^{D}
a number
\mathrm{Eval}_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}\!(x)\in[0,\infty)
that is guaranteed to satisfy the following estimates:

\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}\leqslant\mathrm{Eval}_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}\!(x)\leqslant\Big(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}.

(38)

Define
q=p/(p-1)
if
p>1
, and
q=\infty
if
p=1
. By
[
26
, Lemma 2]
, if we denote

K_{q}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big\{a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \|a\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}\leqslant 1\ \mathrm{and}\ a_{1}\geqslant a_{2}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant a_{n}\geqslant 0\big\},

(39)

then for every
0<\delta<1
there exists a subset
\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)
of
K_{q}^{n}
such that:

\forall x\in K_{q}^{n},\quad d_{\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}\big(x,\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)\big)=\min_{a\in\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)}\|x-a\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2},

(40)

i.e.,
\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)
is
(\delta/2)
-dense in
K_{q}^{n}
with respect to the
\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}
norm, yet its size satisfies:

|\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)|\leqslant n^{\frac{2\log\left(\frac{30}{\delta}\right)}{\log\left(1+\frac{\delta}{6}\right)}}.

(41)

Therefore, by (
41
) for fixed
\delta
the size of
\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)
grows like a fixed power of
n
, in marked contrast to the fact (which is a straightforward consequence of volume comparison) that every
(\delta/2)
-dense subset of a unit ball of an
n
-dimensional normed space must have size at least
(2/\delta)^{n}
.

The algorithm
\mathrm{Alg}_{p}
of Theorem
5
evaluates
\mathrm{Eval}_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}\!(a)
for each
a\in\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)
and outputs the number:

\mathrm{Alg}_{p}(n,D,\delta,\mathbf{E})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}2\max_{a\in\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)}\mathrm{Eval}_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}\!(a).

(42)

The oracle running time of this simple procedure is a polynomial in both
\log(1/\delta)
and
n^{D}
times the size of
\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)
, so by (
41
) it is at most
n^{d}
where the degree
d
satisfies the desired bound (
9
). Furthermore,

\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(B_{\mathbf{E}})=2\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbf{E}}}\|x\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}=2\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbf{E}}}\sup_{y\in B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}}\langle x,y\rangle=2\sup_{y\in B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}}\sup_{x\in B_{\mathbf{E}}}\langle x,y\rangle=2\sup_{y\in B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}}\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}.

(43)

Since
\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)
is a subset of
B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}
, this implies in particular that the following a priori upper bound on the output of
\mathrm{Alg}_{p}
holds, which gives the second inequality in the desired conclusion (
11
) of Theorem
5
:

\mathrm{Alg}_{p}(n,D,\delta,\mathbf{E})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:alg for E star}\wedge\eqref{eq:def our alg}}}{{\leqslant}}2\max_{a\in\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)}\Big(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\|a\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}\leqslant 2\Big(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\sup_{y\in B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}}\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:duality}}}{{\leqslant}}\Big(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(B_{\mathbf{E}}).

Define
\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)\subseteq B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}
by:

\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\Big\{\big(\varepsilon_{1}a_{\pi(1)},\varepsilon_{2}a_{\pi(2)},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}a_{\pi(n)}\big):\ (\pi,(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}),a)\in S_{n}\times\{-1,1\}^{n}\times\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)\Big\}.

(44)

Then,
\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)
is
(\delta/2)
-dense in
B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}
. Indeed, for each
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}
fix a permutation
\pi=\pi^{x}\in S_{n}
such that
|x_{\pi(1)}|\geqslant|x_{\pi(2)}|\geqslant\ldots\geqslant|x_{\pi(n)}|
. Denoting
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{x}=\mathrm{sign}(x_{i})\in\{-1,1\}
for each
i\in[n]
, the vector
y=(\varepsilon_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,\varepsilon_{\pi(n)}x_{\pi(n)})=(|x_{\pi(1)}|,\ldots,|x_{\pi(n)}|)
belongs
K_{q}^{n}
, by (
40
). Now, use (
40
) to get
a\in\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)
with
\|y-a\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!q^{n}}}\leqslant\delta/2
, so that the point
b=(\varepsilon_{1}a_{\pi^{-1}(1)},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}a_{\pi^{-1}(n)})
belongs to
\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)
by (
44
), and satisfies
\|x-b\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}=\|y-a\|_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}\leqslant\delta/2
.

Observe that the convex hull
\mathrm{conv}(\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta))
of
\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)
satisfies the following inclusion:

\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)\right)\supseteq\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}.

(45)

In fact, more generally, for any normed space
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{X}})
, if
\mathscr{C}\subseteq B_{\mathbf{X}}
is
(\delta/2)
-dense in
B_{\mathbf{X}}
with respect to the metric induced by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{X}}
, then
\mathrm{conv}(\mathscr{C})\supseteq(1-\delta/2)B_{\mathbf{X}}
. Indeed, suppose contrapositively that there exists
x\in(1-\delta/2)B_{\mathbf{X}}\smallsetminus\mathrm{conv}(\mathscr{C})
. Then, by the separation theorem we can fix
y^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|y^{*}\|_{\mathbf{X}^{*}}=1
such that
\langle y^{*},x\rangle>\langle y^{*},c\rangle
for every
c\in\mathscr{C}
. By the Hahn–Banach theorem, we can fix
y\in B_{\mathbf{X}}
such that
\langle y^{*},y\rangle=1
. Finally, because
\mathscr{C}
is
(\delta/2)
-dense in
B_{\mathbf{X}}
, we can fix
c_{y}\in\mathscr{C}
such that
\|y-c_{y}\|_{\mathbf{X}}\leqslant\delta/2
. We now arrive at the desired contradiction as follows:

1-\frac{\delta}{2}\geqslant\|x\|_{\mathbf{X}}\geqslant\langle y^{*},x\rangle>\langle y^{*},c_{y}\rangle=\langle y^{*}y\rangle-\langle y^{*},c_{y}-y\rangle\geqslant 1-\|c_{y}-y\|_{\mathbf{X}}\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{2},

(46)

where the second and the penultimate steps of (
46
) use the fact that
y^{*}
has norm
1
as a linear functional in
\mathbf{X}^{*}
, the first step of (
46
) uses the assumption
x\in(1-\delta/2)B_{\mathbf{X}}
, the third step of (
46
) uses the assumed property of the separating functional
y^{*}
for
c=c_{y}\in\mathscr{C}
, and the final step of (
46
) uses the choice of
c_{y}
.

We can now justify the first inequality of the desired conclusion (
11
) of Theorem
5
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Alg}_{p}(n,D,\delta,\mathbf{E})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:alg for E star}\wedge\eqref{eq:def our alg}}}{{\geqslant}}2\max_{a\in\mathcal{G}(n,q,\delta)}\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\|a\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def DD}}}{{=}}2\max_{a\in\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)}\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\|a\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}

(47)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ =2\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\max_{x\in\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathscr{D}(n,q,\delta)\right)}\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}

(48)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:conve inclusion}}}{{\geqslant}}2\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\max_{x\in\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}}\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ =2\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)^{2}\max_{x\in B_{\!\ell_{\!\!q}^{n}}}\|x\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:duality}}}{{=}}\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)^{2}\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(B_{\mathbf{E}})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ >(1-\delta)\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(B_{\mathbf{E}}),

where (
47
) is the step in which the assumption that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{E}}
is a symmetric norm is used, as this implies (by definition) that its dual
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}
is also a symmetric norm, and (
48
) holds since
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{E}^{*}}
is a convex function. ∎

Remark 11
.

In the Introduction we stated the contrast between Theorem
5
and the known nonexistence of any oracle polynomial time algorithm that computes a
O(1)
-factor approximation to the
\ell_{p}^{n}
-diameter of an arbitrary (so, not necessarily symmetric, as in Theorem
5
) convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is given by a weak membership oracle, where
1\leqslant p<\infty
is fixed. This contrast is even more severe because the known impossibility results rule out approximation factors that tend to
\infty
with
n
, as we will next recall.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\geqslant 3
and
1\leqslant p<\infty
, let
D_{p}^{h}(n)
be the smallest
\alpha\geqslant 1
for which there is a deterministic algorithm that takes as input a convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by a weak membership oracle, and outputs in oracle time at most
n^{h}
a number
\Delta_{p}(K)
satisfying
\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(K)\leqslant\Delta_{p}(K)\leqslant\alpha\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(K)
. Similarly, let
R_{p}^{h}(n)
denote the smallest
\alpha\geqslant 1
for which there is a randomised algorithm that outputs in oracle time at most
n^{h}
a number
\Delta_{p}(K)
satisfying
\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(K)\leqslant\Delta_{p}(K)\leqslant\alpha\diam_{\ell_{\!\!p}^{n}}(K)
with probability at least, say,
1/3
.

Using the above notations, for
2\leqslant p<\infty
we have
D_{p}^{h}(n)\asymp_{h}R_{p}^{h}(n)\asymp_{h}(n/\log n)^{1/p}
; when
p=2
these bounds on
D_{2}^{h}(n)
are due to
[
9
]
, and these bounds for both
D_{p}^{h}(n)
and
R_{p}^{h}(n)
, and arbitrary
2<p<\infty
, are due to
[
16
,
17
]
. If
1\leqslant p<2
, then
R_{p}^{h}(n)\asymp_{h}\sqrt{n/\log n}
by
[
33
,
34
]
, improving by unbounded polylog factor over the corresponding bounds of
[
16
,
17
]
. For deterministic algorithms, when
1\leqslant p<2
we have
D_{p}^{h}(n)\lesssim_{h}\sqrt{n}/(\log n)^{1-1/p}
by
[
16
,
17
]
, and the best-known lower bound is
D_{p}^{h}(n)\gtrsim_{h}\sqrt{n/\log n}
, which follows from the trivial lower bound
D_{p}^{h}(n)\geqslant R_{p}^{h}(n)
and the aforementioned asymptotic evaluation of
R_{p}^{h}(n)
. Thus, for fixed
1\leqslant p<2
there remains a gap of
(\log n)^{1/p-1/2}
, which tends to
\infty
with
n
, between the best-known upper and lower bounds on the deterministic approximation ratio
D_{p}^{h}(n)
. While it seems reasonable to expect that this gap (noted in
[
16
,
17
,
15
]
) could be closed using available methods, to the best of our knowledge this has not yet been carried out, even though it would be worthwhile to do so.

4.
Entires in the complex numbers and the quaternions

Here we will show how to deduce Theorem
8
for unitarily invariant norms on
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{H})
from its statement about such norms on
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
that we already proved. This will imply Theorem
2
and Theorem
6
for complex and quaternionic entries by the reductions from
[
48
]
that were recalled in the Introduction. Suppose that
\mathbf{E}=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{E}})
is a symmetric norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To not supress the underlying entries, we will henceforth denote by
\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R}),\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C}),\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{H})
the corresponding unitary ideals on
\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{H})
, respectively. Thus,
\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
coincides with the space that we previously denoted
\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}
, and the dimensions over
\mathbb{R}
of
\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R}),\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C}),\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{H})
satisfy
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R}))=n^{2},\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))=2n^{2},\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{H}))=4n^{2}
.

For complex entries, our goal is to deduce from Theorem
8
that there exists an origin-symmetric convex body
L_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
that satisfies the following three properties:

L_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}}(L_{\mathbb{C}})^{\frac{1}{2n^{2}}}\gtrsim\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}}(B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})})^{\frac{1}{2n^{2}}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathrm{iq}(L_{\mathbb{C}})\lesssim n.

(49)

We will henceforth use the natural identification

\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),

(50)

which is obtained by assigning to each complex matric
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
the pair
(\Re(\mathsf{A}),\Im(\mathsf{A}))\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
of real matrices, where
\Re(\mathsf{A}),\Im(\mathsf{A})
denote the entry-wise real part and imaginary part of
\mathsf{A}
, respectively.

Every
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
satisfies:

\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}=\|\Re(\mathsf{A})+i\Im(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}\leqslant\|\Re(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}+\|\Im(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}\\
=\|\Re(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\Im(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 2\max\big\{\|\Re(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\Im(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\big\}.

Equivalently, under the identification (
50
) the following inclusion holds:

B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\times B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\subseteq 2B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}.

(51)

Conversely, letting
\overline{\mathsf{A}}
denote the entry-wise complex conjugate of
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

\forall\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad\|\Re(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathsf{A}+\overline{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathsf{A}+\overline{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}\leqslant\frac{\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}+\|\overline{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}}{2}=\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})},

where the last step uses the fact that
\mathsf{A}
and
\overline{\mathsf{A}}
have the same singular values, whence

\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}=\|\overline{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}
. For the same reason, we also have the following estimate:

\forall\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad\|\Im(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2}\|{-i\mathsf{A}+i\overline{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2}\|{-i\mathsf{A}+i\overline{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}\leqslant\frac{\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}+\|\overline{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}}{2}=\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}.

We have thus checked that:

\forall\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad\max\big\{\|\Re(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\Im(\mathsf{A})\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\big\}\leqslant\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}.

Equivalently, under the identification (
50
) the following inclusion holds:

B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}\subseteq B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\times B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}.

(52)

Now, an application of Theorem
8
yields an origin-symmetric convex body
L_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
that satisfies:

L_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathrm{vol}_{n^{2}}(L_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{n^{2}}}\gtrsim\mathrm{vol}_{n^{2}}(B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})})^{\frac{1}{n^{2}}}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\mathrm{iq}(L_{\mathbb{R}})\lesssim n.

(53)

Under the identification (
50
), define
L_{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
by:

L_{\mathbb{C}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\big(L_{\mathbb{R}}\times L_{\mathbb{R}}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:weak revese iso for unitarily real}}}{{\subseteq}}\frac{1}{2}\big(B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\times B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:first inclusion}}}{{\subseteq}}B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})}.

(54)

Thus, the first part of (
49
) holds. The second part of (
49
) also holds because:

\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}}(L_{\mathbb{C}})^{\frac{1}{2n^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def LC}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{vol}_{n^{2}}(L_{\mathbb{R}})^{\frac{1}{n^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:weak revese iso for unitarily real}}}{{\gtrsim}}\mathrm{vol}_{n^{2}}(B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})})^{\frac{1}{n^{2}}}=\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}}(B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\times B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})})^{\frac{1}{2n^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:second inclusion}}}{{\geqslant}}\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}}(B_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{C})})^{\frac{1}{2n^{2}}}.

Finally, the third part of (
49
) is justified as follows, thus completing the treatment of complex entries:

\mathrm{iq}(L_{\mathbb{C}})=\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:def LC}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}-1}\big(\partial(L_{\mathbb{R}}\times L_{\mathbb{R}})\big)}{\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}}(L_{\mathbb{R}}\times L_{\mathbb{R}})^{1-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}}}=\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}-1}\big((\partial L_{\mathbb{R}})\times L_{\mathbb{R}}\big)+\mathrm{vol}_{2n^{2}-1}\big(L_{\mathbb{R}}\times(\partial L_{\mathbb{R}})\big)}{\mathrm{vol}_{n^{2}}(L_{\mathbb{R}})^{2-\frac{1}{n^{2}}}}=2\mathrm{iq}(L_{\mathbb{R}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:weak revese iso for unitarily real}}}{{\lesssim}}n.

In the case of quaternionic entries one applies mutatis mutandis the analogous reasoning by writing uniquely each
\mathsf{A}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{H})
as
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{B}+i\mathsf{C}+j\mathsf{D}+k\mathsf{E}
for
\mathsf{B},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D},\mathsf{E}\in\mathsf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
that satisfy the following bounds:

\max\left\{\|\mathsf{B}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\mathsf{C}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\mathsf{D}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\mathsf{E}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\right\}\leqslant\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{H})}\leqslant 4\max\left\{\|\mathsf{B}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\mathsf{C}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\mathsf{D}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})},\|\mathsf{E}\|_{\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{E}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}\right\}.
