Intracoronary Electrocardiography During Dobutamine Stress for Functional Assessment of Myocardial Bridging
Authors: Yoshichika Miyazaki, Ami Kimpara, Mitsuhito Soh, Otoya Sekine, Masayuki Aoyama, Nayuka Sumida, Takafumi Nishimura, Kohei Inagawa, Munehisa Sakamoto, Takumi Toya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial bridging (MB) can cause exertional ischemia, but functional assessment is challenging when ischemia is localized and undetected by surface electrocardiography (ECG).Case SummaryAn 83-year-old man with vasospastic angina presented with recurrent exertional chest discomfort. Coronary angiography revealed MB in the mid–left anterior descending artery without significant epicardial stenosis. Intracoronary acetylcholine induced complete occlusion of a diagonal branch with symptom reproduction. Dobutamine stress testing using a pressure-sensor guidewire showed a resting full-cycle ratio ≤0.76 at 20 μg/kg/min, indicating functionally significant ischemia. Surface ECG remained unchanged; however, intracoronary ECG demonstrated marked ST-segment depression distal to the bridged segment, confirming localized ischemia. Switching medical therapy from nifedipine to diltiazem led to symptomatic improvement.Take-Home MessageIntracoronary ECG performed during dobutamine stress testing may enable direct visualization of localized myocardial ischemia associated with MB, even in the absence of ischemic changes on surface ECG.

History of Presentation

The patient was an 83-year-old man who had been transported to our hospital 15 years earlier with chest pain occurring at rest. At that time, 12-lead electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF (Figure 1A). Sublingual nitroglycerin administered in the emergency department promptly alleviated his symptoms and resulted in complete resolution of the ST-segment elevation (Figure 1B). Emergent coronary angiography revealed no significant coronary artery stenosis, leading to a diagnosis of vasospastic angina (VSA). More recently, he was referred to our department because of recurrent exertional chest pain occurring predominantly in the morning. Coronary computed tomography angiography suggested possible progression of coronary artery disease in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) and identified myocardial bridging (MB) in the mid-LAD, prompting hospital admission for further diagnostic evaluation.

Past Medical History

The patient’s medical history was notable for hypertension, hyperuricemia, and an inguinal hernia. He had never smoked and reported habitual alcohol consumption of approximately 50 g of ethanol per day. At baseline, his medications included aspirin 100 mg once daily, sustained-release nifedipine 20 mg once daily, and benzbromarone 50 mg once daily.

Differential Diagnosis

Based on the findings of coronary computed tomography angiography, the differential diagnoses included obstructive coronary artery disease and functional myocardial ischemia caused by dynamic systolic compression from MB, in the context of known VSA.

Investigations and management

Coronary angiography demonstrated no significant epicardial coronary artery stenosis; MB was identified in the mid-LAD (Video 1). Discussion

MB, characterized by an intramyocardial course of an epicardial coronary artery, has traditionally been regarded as a benign and asymptomatic anatomical variant. However, accumulating evidence indicates that MB is frequently associated with coronary vasomotor dysfunction and is now recognized as a potential mechanism of ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries.1,2 This association appears to be particularly relevant under conditions of increased heart rate and myocardial contractility, which may exacerbate systolic compression of the bridged segment and impair diastolic coronary perfusion. Accordingly, physiological assessment using dobutamine stress has been widely adopted to unmask the functional significance of MB. Importantly, systolic compression of the bridged segment can result in overshooting of intracoronary systolic pressure, potentially leading to overestimation of conventional fractional flow reserve, which reflects the mean pressure ratio over the entire cardiac cycle. In this context, diastolic fractional flow reserve (dFFR) has been proposed as a more appropriate index for evaluating flow limitation caused by MB, given its focus on diastolic perfusion.3 A dFFR cutoff value of ≤0.76 has therefore been widely adopted to define functionally significant MB. More recently, dobutamine stress–derived nonhyperemic pressure indices, including instantaneous wave-free ratio and RFR, have been proposed as practical alternatives to dFFR. An RFR cutoff value of ≤0.76 has been reported to reliably identify functionally significant ischemia associated with MB.4 However, it should be noted that this cutoff value originates from a small Japanese study involving 46 patients with intermediate coronary artery stenosis, in which thresholds for fractional flow reserve, dFFR, and coronary flow reserve were correlated with noninvasive stress testing modalities.5 As such, the diagnostic validity of a uniform cutoff value of 0.76 for dobutamine stress–derived dFFR, instantaneous wave-free ratio, or RFR in the specific context of MB-related ischemia has not been fully established.

Exercise stress ECG remains a widely used diagnostic modality for the detection of obstructive coronary artery disease; however, its modest sensitivity and specificity have resulted in a lower class of recommendation in contemporary guidelines for the evaluation of chronic coronary syndromes. In recent years, growing evidence has demonstrated that positive exercise stress ECG findings in patients without obstructive coronary artery disease—previously regarded as false-positive results—are frequently associated with coronary vasomotor dysfunction.6,7 Furthermore, we recently reported that MB is independently associated with positive exercise stress ECG findings, particularly in patients without obstructive coronary artery disease, suggesting a potential pathophysiological role of MB in exercise-induced ischemia.8 Nevertheless, the extent to which ischemic changes on surface 12-lead ECG can be directly attributed to MB remains uncertain.

Given these limitations, we sought to directly detect localized ischemic ECG changes distal to the bridged segment using intracoronary ECG recorded from a pressure-sensor–tipped guidewire during dobutamine stress RFR assessment. To date, no reports have demonstrated direct visualization of localized ischemic ECG changes attributable to MB. Although the utility of intracoronary ECG has previously been reported for identifying culprit vessels in acute coronary syndromes and for detecting localized ischemia not apparent on surface ECG, its application in the assessment of MB-related ischemia has not been described.9,10 In the present case, no intracoronary ECG abnormalities were observed at baseline; however, ST-segment depression became evident during dobutamine stress when the RFR decreased to ≤0.76. Importantly, no corresponding ischemic changes were detected on surface 12-lead ECG at the same time point, suggesting that intracoronary ECG enabled the detection of localized myocardial ischemia confined to the myocardial territory distal to the bridged segment.

On the basis of these findings, the patient was diagnosed with angina caused by MB complicated by VSA. In patients with symptomatic MB, beta blockers are generally considered first-line therapy to reduce heart rate and attenuate systolic compression of the bridged segment. However, in the presence of concomitant coronary vasospasm, calcium channel blockers are preferred. In the present case, the patient was already receiving nifedipine; therefore, medical therapy was switched to diltiazem to achieve additional heart rate reduction while maintaining antispasmodic effects, resulting in marked symptomatic improvement.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After switching medical therapy from nifedipine for diltiazem, the patient reported no further episodes of chest pain and remained stable on follow-up.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that intracoronary ECG obtained from a pressure-sensor–tipped guidewire during dobutamine stress testing can directly visualize localized myocardial ischemia associated with MB, even in the absence of ischemic changes on surface ECG. The combined use of invasive physiological assessment and intracoronary ECG may therefore provide incremental diagnostic value in selected patients with MB complicated by ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries.
