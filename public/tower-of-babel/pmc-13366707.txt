Determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage among women who gave birth in Wachemo University Nigist Eleni Mohammed Memorial Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Central Ethiopia: a case-control study
Authors: Efrem Kidane, Tekle Ejajo, Mesfin Menza Jaldo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary postpartum hemorrhage is a leading cause of maternal death worldwide, particularly in developing countries such as Ethiopia. A significant proportion of maternal deaths are attributed to postpartum hemorrhage. Despite its public health importance, there is a paucity of evidence regarding the determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage in our country.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify the determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage among mothers who gave birth at Wachemo University Nigist Eleni Mohammed Memorial Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Central Ethiopia.MethodsA hospital-based unmatched case-control study was conducted from May to July 2023 by reviewing records from the previous two consecutive years (January 2021 to December 2022). A total of 348 maternal charts (87 cases and 261 controls) were selected using a simple random sampling technique, with the obstetric and gynecologic registration book serving as the sampling frame. Data were collected from medical records using a data abstraction form. Data entry was performed using EpiData version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Binary and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and a p-value ≤ 0.05 were used to determine statistical significance.ResultThe findings of this study showed that obstructed labor (AOR = 5.2; 95% CI: 3.14–10.55), antenatal care follow-up (AOR = 1.42; 95% CI: 1.02–4.05), a history of antepartum hemorrhage (AOR = 3.45; 95% CI: 1.95–8.26), and maternal age > 35 (AOR = 3.51; 95% CI: 1.24–6.42) were significant determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage.ConclusionMaternal age, antenatal care follow-up during pregnancy, antepartum hemorrhage, and obstructed labor were identified as determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage. Therefore, healthcare providers should closely monitor labor progression and take timely and appropriate interventions when necessary.Keywords: Determinants, Primary postpartum hemorrhage, Central Ethiopia

Background

Primary postpartum hemorrhage (PPPH) is defined as blood loss exceeding 500 ml following spontaneous vaginal delivery or 1000 ml after cesarean section within the first 24 h after birth [1]. Although some bleeding after delivery is physiological, excessive or uncontrolled bleeding can rapidly become life-threatening and lead to maternal death [2].

Globally, postpartum hemorrhage is the leading preventable cause of maternal mortality, accounting for approximately 25% of maternal deaths each year [1–4]. It contributes to about 8% of maternal deaths in developed countries and up to 20% in developing countries [5], with an estimated 18% of all deliveries affected [6]. Primary PPH alone accounts for a substantial proportion of pregnancy-related deaths worldwide [7]. The burden is particularly high in Africa and Asia, where nearly one-third of maternal deaths are attributed to PPH [3, 4]. The maternal mortality rate in Ethiopia is among the highest in the world of which primary PPH remains to be the leading cause [5]. The magnitude ranges from 3.06% [6] to 32.8% [7].

Uterine atony is the most common cause, followed by trauma, retained tissue, and coagulation disorders [5]. The risk of death depends on the volume and rate of blood loss as well as the woman’s overall health status. Most deaths occur within the first 24 h, often within a few hours if timely intervention is not provided [1]. Early diagnosis relies on clinical signs, including changes in vital signs and the shock index. Primary PPH can lead to severe complications such as organ failure, chronic illness, and increased risk of mortality for both the mother and her child [8].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends active management of the third stage of labor, including uterotonic administration and close monitoring of uterine tone, to prevent PPH [7]. When it occurs, management depends on the underlying cause and includes fluid resuscitation, removal of retained tissue, surgical repair, and blood transfusion if necessary [9]. Primary PPH is the leading cause of maternal death which is defined as the death of a woman termination of pregnancy with in the first 24 h, from pregnancy aggravated causes or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes [10]. A woman could die within a few hours due to the absence of timely and proper intervention for it [9].

Several risk factors have been identified, including prolonged labor, cesarean delivery, advanced maternal age, anemia, hypertensive disorders, abnormal placentation, and previous history of PPH. However, most studies in Ethiopia are cross-sectional and do not adequately establish causal relationships [11, 12]. Hence there is limited evidence of contributing factors which influence the occurrence of primary PPH. Besides the occurrence of the problem is rare, but its public health importance regarding with morbidity and mortality of the mother is remained as a leading cause in developing countries, including Ethiopia [13]. Therefore, further investigation is needed to better understand the determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage. This study aims to identify the determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage among mothers who gave birth at Wachemo University Nigist Elleni Mohammed Memorial Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (WUNEMMCSH), Central Ethiopia.

Method and Materials

Study area and period

The study was conducted at WUNEMMCSH, located in Hossana town, Hadiya Zone, Central Ethiopia. Hossana is situated 232 km South of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, and serves as the administrative center of Central Ethiopia. According to the 2012 population projection by the Central Statistical Agency, Hadiya Zone has a total population of 1,231,196, of whom 619,170 were women. The Zone covers an area of 3,593.31 square kilometers. Geographically, it lies between 7°22′ to 7°45′ North latitude and 37°40′ to 38°00′ East longitude, with an altitude range of 1,501 to 2,500 m above sea level. The mean annual rainfall ranges from 1,001 mm to 1,200 mm.

The hospital has seven departments, among which the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology is one of the major units. The department is staffed by gynecologists, general practitioners, midwives, and clinical nurses. On average, it serves about 800 mothers per month for delivery services. The study was conducted from May to July 2023 by reviewing records from the previous two consecutive years (January 2021 to December 2022).

Study design

Hospital based unmatched case control study design was employed.

Population

The source populations were all mothers who gave birth in Wachemo University Nigist Elleni Mohammed Memorial comprehensive specialized hospital. All mothers who gave birth during study period in Wachemo University Nigist Elleni Mohammed Memorial comprehensive specialized hospital are the study population.

Study unit

Selected maternal cards from the registration book.

Eligibility criteria

A woman of any age or parity who gave birth and developed primary postpartum hemorrhage was included in the study. Maternal charts that were missing or lost, women who gave birth outside the hospital without a referral letter, and those who developed postpartum hemorrhage after 24 h were excluded from the study.

Accordingly, the largest sample size was taken to reduce random error and to increase the quality of data three controls for each case was used. The total sample size was 348 (87 case and 261 controls).

Sampling procedure

A simple random sampling technique was used. The sampling frame was developed from the compiled registration book of the gynecology and obstetrics ward. Medical record numbers were listed based on the date and month of admission.

First, all primary PPH cases were identified using recorded diagnoses from registration books of all possible sources, including the labor and delivery suite, operating room, and obstetrics and gynecology ward logbooks. For every one case, three controls were included in the study. Controls were selected from the remaining maternal records that were not diagnosed with primary PPH.

Charts were accessed using medical record numbers corresponding to serial numbers assigned in the sampling frame. From January 2021 to December 2022, a total of 17,192 mothers gave birth in the hospital, of whom 98 were diagnosed with primary PPH. Finally, a computer-based lottery method was used to select participants from each group.

Study variables

Primary postpartum hemorrhage was the outcome variable.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic characteristics

Age and residence.

Obstetric related factors

Gravidity, parity, Antenatal care (ANC) visit, status of labor, uterine atony, retained placenta, laceration, mode of delivery, previous history of PPH, fetal weight, multiple pregnancy and antepartum hemorrhage.

Maternal health related factors

Hypertension, anemia, past chronic illness, Polyhydraminos and infection.

Operational definition and term definition

PPH

Is a delivery-related blood loss that is excessive in nature and results in symptoms such as pallor, light-headedness, weakness, palpitations, diaphoresis, restlessness, confusion, or syncope, and/or leads to signs of hypovolemia such as hypotension and tachycardia [14].

Primary PPH

By reviewing patient charts, primary postpartum hemorrhage was defined as bleeding from the genital tract that meets at least one of the above-mentioned criteria and occurs within 24 h of fetal delivery. To ensure accuracy, the time interval between fetal delivery and the onset of bleeding was obtained from maternal charts by reviewing physicians’ notes in each patient’s medical record.

Cases

Mothers who had blood loss of > 500 ml within 24 h following vaginal delivery or > 1000 ml following cesarean delivery were considered as having postpartum hemorrhage. In this study, mothers who developed primary PPH were identified from their medical charts and included as cases.

Controls

Mothers who gave birth at Wachemo University Nigist Eleni Mohammed Memorial Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and were not diagnosed with primary PPH were identified from medical charts and included as controls.

Uterine atony

Is defined as a soft and weak uterus after delivery and it happens when the uterine muscles don’t contract enough to clamp the placental vessels shut after child birth.

Parity

Is the number of times a woman gives birth, irrespective of the birth outcome.

APH

Is vaginal bleeding after 28 weeks of gestation.

Pre-eclampsia

Is pregnancy-related high blood pressure (140/90 mmHg) and protein in the Urine.

Anemia

Defined as the hemoglobin (Hb) level of less than 11 g/dL according to the World Health Organization (WHO).

Mode of delivery

Is whether a woman gives birth spontaneous vaginally, assisted vaginally with instruments (forceps and vacuum) or via cesarean section.

Hypertension

High blood pressure is when the pressure in the blood vessels is to high (140/90mmHg or higher).

Data collection procedure and measurements

Four BSc midwife professionals and one Masters of Public Health holder supervisor were recruited. Structured data review checklist was developed by reviewing different literatures. The data was collected from maternal charts/records through intense review. The data review checklist has three parts which includes maternal socio-demographic characteristics, obstetric related factors and maternal health related factors. All the necessary data was reviewed from the chart.

Data quality management

A one-day orientation was provided to data collectors and supervisors on the data collection procedure, the data extraction tool, and the purpose of the study prior to the actual data collection. The completeness and consistency of the data extraction checklist were checked daily by supervisors, and timely feedback was provided to data collectors to ensure data quality.

A pretest was conducted on 5% of the total sample size at Worabe Comprehensive Specialized Hospital to assess the clarity, consistency, and appropriateness of the data collection tool. Based on the findings of the pretest, necessary modifications and corrections were made to improve the clarity and coherence of variables before the actual data collection began.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered into EpiData version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 26.0 for further statistical analysis. Logistic regression analysis was used to examine the association between independent variables and the outcome variable. First, binary logistic regression was performed for all variables, and those with a p-value < 0.25 were selected as candidates for multivariable logistic regression to control for potential confounders. In the final model, variables with a p-value ≤ 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval (CI) were considered statistically significant determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% CI were used to interpret the findings. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was conducted, and a p-value > 0.05 was considered indicative of a good model fit.

Result

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 348 maternal charts (87 cases and 261 controls) were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 100%. The mean ages of cases and controls were 30.13 years (SD ± 8.63) and 29.64 years (SD ± 5.61), respectively. About 46% of cases and 21.8% of controls were aged > 35 years. Additionally, 20 (23.0%) of cases and 183 (70.1%) of controls were in the age group of 21–34 years. The majority of participants 44 (50.6%) of cases and 126 (48.3%) of controls were from rural areas (Table 2).

Obstetric related factors

Seventy-six (87.4%) cases and 250 (95.6%) controls had a history of antenatal care (ANC) follow-up. A history of previous antepartum hemorrhage (APH) was documented in 11 (5.2%) cases and 9 (2.1%) controls. Fifty-six (26.4%) cases and 5 (1.2%) controls had a previous history of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), whereas only 3 (1.4%) cases and 9 (2.1%) controls had a history of previous cesarean section. Twelve (13.2%) cases and 3 (1.1%) controls had a history of placental abruption during the index pregnancy. Six (6.9%) cases developed placenta previa, while none of the controls had this condition. Polyhydramnios was also reported among 25 (28.7%) cases and 18 (6.9%) controls. Fifty-eight (66.7%) cases developed primary PPH following spontaneous vaginal delivery, while 29 (33.3%) occurred after cesarean section. All cases and 98.5% of controls received active management of the third stage of labor. However, 49 (56.3%) cases and 1 control (0.4%) experienced prolonged third stage of labor (Table 3).

Factors associated with primary PPH of the study participants

In the bivariate analysis, maternal age, obstructed labor, antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, history of cesarean section, prolonged labor, preeclampsia/eclampsia, previous cesarean delivery, history of antepartum hemorrhage (APH), polyhydramnios, and hematocrit level were significantly associated with primary postpartum hemorrhage (PPH).

However, in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, prolonged labor, history of antepartum hemorrhage, antenatal care follow-up, and maternal age were found to be significantly associated with primary PPH.

The likelihood of primary postpartum hemorrhage was approximately five times higher among mothers with obstructed labor compared to those without obstructed labor (AOR = 5.2; 95% CI: 3.14–10.55). Mothers who did not attend antenatal care (ANC) follow-up were about 1.4 times more likely to develop primary PPH compared to their counterparts (AOR = 1.42; 95% CI: 1.02–4.05). Participants with a previous history of antepartum hemorrhage were about three and a half times more likely to develop primary PPH than those without such a history (AOR = 3.45; 95% CI: 1.95–8.26).

Maternal age was also significantly associated with primary PPH. Mothers aged ≥ 35 years were nearly three times more likely to develop primary PPH compared to those aged 21–34 years and < 20 years (AOR = 3.51; 95% CI: 1.24–6.42) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage. Accordingly, maternal age, antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, prolonged labor, and a history of antepartum hemorrhage were identified as significant determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage.

In this study, maternal age ≥ 35 years was found to be the significant determinants of primary PPH. This finding was found to be a universal fact; the mother’s age increases the risk of obstetric complications including PPH [17]. Similar findings were also reported from the studies done in France [18], Uganda [19] and Pakistan [20] which revealed that mothers aged 35 or above were more likely to experience a PPH. However, another study showed age of participant was not significantly associated with primary PPH [12]. This inconsistency could be due to socio economic and study design difference among study areas or difference among study setting.

It was found that not using the ANC was a significant determinant of primary PPH, with cases having a greater likelihood of not using the ANC than controls. This finding supported by prior study conducted at Nigeria [21], Dessie referral hospital [22], public hospital of Southern Tigray [13]. This could be because mothers who missing ANC Visits are more likely to give birth without being hospitalized and to have undetected problems, both of which raise the possibility of developing primary PPH. By offering early diagnosis and treatment of preexisting conditions and their complications, as well as health promotion and disease prevention. All needed to lower pregnancy related complications like PPH [13]. Antenatal care (ANC) is a significant area of maternal health intervention that lowers the incidence of PPH [8]. On the other hand, a Sidama region found no association between primary postpartum hemorrhage and antennal care follow-up [11]. This inconsistency could be due to variation of accessibility and availability of the health care service delivery. Furthermore the variation of habits of health seeking behavior could be possible explanation of this difference.

In this study, prolonged labor was one of the major factors for primary PPH Similarly, studies conducted at Bonassama Hospital in Afghanistan [23] and Cameroon [24] corroborate this conclusion. This result was consistent with earlier findings from western Ethiopia (47) and Yirgalem hospital, southern Ethiopia [11]. This could be explained by the idea that pronged labor increases the risk of pelvic blood vessel and soft tissue laceration. This considerably reduces the contraction of the uterus. Because of this, the chance of blood loss after delivery could considerably rise. On the other hand, another study from Southern Tigray showed no significant association between prolonged labor and primary PPH (46). The difference could be due to difference in study setting healthcare infrastructure availability and quality of the service delivery.

Furthermore, this study found that one of the main causes of primary postpartum hemorrhage was antepartum hemorrhage during the present pregnancy. In a similar vein, it agreed with research from Ethiopia (47) and Afghanistan [23]. These studies’ conclusions demonstrated that antepartum hemorrhage in a recent pregnancy and cervical or vaginal laceration were risk factors for primary PPH [25].

In summary, this study showed an association between primary postpartum hemorrhage and the underutilization of essential maternal health services. Strengthening these services is critical to significantly reducing maternal mortality in Ethiopia. Therefore, policymakers and stakeholders should give special attention to implementing life-saving interventions. The findings of this study also have important implications for the prevention and control of primary PPH. They provide useful insights for policymakers and program implementers regarding key risk factors, supporting the prioritization of interventions. In addition, the results may assist healthcare providers in identifying common determinants of primary PPH and serve as a useful basis for developing preventive strategies.

Strength and limitation

One of the strengths of this study is the use of data from multiple sources, including registration books and patient chart reviews, which enhances data quality and provides more accurate information. In addition, case-control study designs are more appropriate than cross-sectional studies for identifying multiple risk factors associated with a single outcome.

However, the study has several limitations. Being an institution-based study, its findings may have limited generalizability to the wider population. Furthermore, socioeconomic factors and other variables that require direct interviews were not included due to the use of secondary data.

The study may also be subject to selection bias, information bias, and confounding. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

In this study, maternal age, antenatal care follow-up during pregnancy, antepartum hemorrhage, and prolonged labor were identified as determinants of primary postpartum hemorrhage.

Based on these findings, the following recommendations are made:

Healthcare providers should closely monitor labor progress and take appropriate and timely interventions when necessary. Early identification of risk factors during antenatal care and adherence to recommended clinical guidelines are essential to reduce the risk of primary PPH. Particular attention should be given to mothers with a history of antepartum hemorrhage, and labor should be managed carefully and appropriately.

Hospital administrators and relevant stakeholders should strengthen health education and promotion activities to increase awareness among mothers about the importance of early antenatal care follow-up and facility-based delivery.
