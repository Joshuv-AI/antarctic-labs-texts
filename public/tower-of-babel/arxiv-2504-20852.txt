Machine Learning (ML)–Physics Fusion Model Outperforms Both Physics-Only and ML-Only Models in Typhoon Predictions


Abstract
Data-driven machine learning (ML) models, such as FuXi, exhibit notable limitations in forecasting typhoon intensity and structure. This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of FuXi–SHTM, a hybrid ML–physics model, using all 2024 western North Pacific typhoon cases. The FuXi–SHTM hybrid demonstrates clear improvements in both track and intensity forecasts compared to the standalone SHTM, FuXi, and ECMWF HRES models. Compared to FuXi alone, FuXi–SHTM reduces typhoon track forecast errors by 16.5% and 5.2% at lead times of 72 h and 120 h, respectively, and reduces intensity forecast errors by 59.7% and 47.6%. Furthermore, FuXi–SHTM simulates cloud structures more realistically compared to SHTM, and achieves superior representation of the 10-m wind fields in both intensity and spatial structure compared to FuXi and SHTM. Increasing the resolution of FuXi-SHTM from 9 km to 3 km further enhances intensity forecasts, highlighting the critical role of the resolution of the physical model in advancing hybrid forecasting capabilities.

Introduction

Recent data-driven machine learning (ML) models, including PanGu, GraphCast, FuXi, and AIFS (Bi et al., 2023; Lam et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Lang et al., 2023), demonstrate substantial improvements in typhoon track forecasts compared to global and regional numerical weather prediction (NWP) models. However, they notably underestimate the intensity of the typhoon. Two primary factors contribute to this underestimation: (1) these ML models are typically trained using ERA5 reanalysis data at a coarse resolution (around 28 km), which fails to resolve critical mesoscale structures. Sun et al. (2024) showed that removing cyclone cases from the FourCastNet training data set led to significant intensity underestimation for Hurricane Lee (2023), highlighting the critical role of extreme cyclone samples in ML training. (2) Most ML models utilize the mean squared error (MSE) loss function, causing a "double penalty" effect that overly smooths fine-scale features and thereby underestimates the intensity of the cyclone (Subich et al., 2025).

Improving ML model resolution and enhancing typhoon intensity forecast skill are thus critical research priorities. Recent studies have begun to develop high-resolution regional ML models, enabling kilometre-scale weather prediction (Nipen et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Adamov et al., 2025). Nevertheless, substantial challenges remain, particularly in handling lateral boundary conditions and implementing local-scale data assimilation. Furthermore, the absence of high-resolution reanalysis datasets that adequately capture typhoon structures significantly limits the advancement of regional ML models tailored specifically for typhoon forecasting. Encouragingly, recently proposed hybrid ML–physics frameworks offer a promising alternative, integrating large-scale ML predictions into physics-based models through spectral nudging (Xu et al., 2024, 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Husain et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2025). Such hybrid approaches maintain accurate large-scale forecasts, enhance mesoscale realism, and deliver comprehensive physical-field outputs (Olivetti and Messori, 2024), thus constituting an optimal operational paradigm for contemporary typhoon prediction.

Currently, three regional typhoon operational models are used in China: the China Meteorological Administration Typhoon Model (CMA
-
TYM; Zhang et al., 2023), the Tropical Regional Atmospherical Model System of South China of the China Meteorological Administration (CMA
-
TRAMS; Huang et al., 2025), and the Shanghai Typhoon Model (SHTM; Niu et al., 2024). In 2024, SHTM upgraded its initial conditions from the NCEP Global Forecast System (GFS) to the ECMWF High-Resolution System (HRES), substantially enhancing track forecasts. Compared to CMA-TYM and CMA-TRAMS, SHTM generally produced smaller mean track errors except at 12- and 24-hour lead times (Table 1). However, at longer lead times, especially at 120 hours, SHTM’s track errors (501.4 km) remain much larger than those of FuXi (296.6 km) and AIFS (239.2 km). Clearly, ML models have elevated operational track forecasting to a higher standard (Rasp et al., 2024). Thus, operational forecasting agencies are actively exploring ML–physics hybrid frameworks. While current hybrid model studies have focused mainly on individual typhoon cases, systematic evaluations across numerous cases remain limited. To address this gap, this study constructs a hybrid model by integrating FuXi and SHTM, systematically evaluating its track and intensity forecasts throughout the entire 2024 typhoon season, demonstrating that hybrid models represent a leading paradigm for future operational forecasts.

2
Method and Data

In 2024, 26 tropical cyclones formed over the western North Pacific, of which eight reached Super Typhoon status and ten made landfall along China’s coastline (Fig. 1a). Among these, Super Typhoon Yagi (2024) set several historical records, causing economic losses of approximately 72.028 billion CNY. However, traditional NWP models such as SHTM significantly underperformed ML models in predicting the tracks of extreme cyclones like typhoon Yagi (2024) and typhoon Krathon (2024).

The SHTM is based on the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model version 4.3 coupled with the Gridpoint Statistical Interpolation (GSI, version 3.7) data assimilation system. SHTM operates on a 953 × 701 grid at a horizontal resolution of 9 km, with 56 vertical eta-levels extending up to 50 hPa. Its domain covers 0.5°S–61.5°N and 58.3°E–172.7°E (cyan boundary in Fig. 1). Initial and boundary conditions for SHTM are derived from ECMWF HRES, with forecasts initialized twice daily (0000 UTC and 1200 UTC) and extending to 120 hours. Key physics schemes employed include the Thompson microphysics scheme, multi-scale Kain–Fritsch cumulus scheme, and Yonsei University planetary boundary layer scheme. In 2024, SHTM produced smaller mean track errors than the other two regional models, but at forecast lead times beyond 72 hours, its errors were significantly larger compared to ML models such as FuXi and AIFS (Table 1).

Similar to other ML weather models, FuXi is a purely data-driven transformer-based cascade forecasting system, providing global weather forecasts up to 15 days ahead. Trained on 39 years of ERA5 reanalysis data, FuXi demonstrates forecast performance comparable to ECMWF ensemble means. FuXi outputs global forecasts at 0.25°×0.25° resolution across 13 pressure levels, providing geopotential height, temperature, relative humidity, and u- and v-components of wind at upper levels, along with surface variables including surface temperature, mean sea level pressure, 2-m temperature, 10-m wind, and precipitation. Over the past two years, FuXi has been widely adopted operationally, significantly improving typhoon track forecasts.

Therefore, this study leverages spectral nudging (SN) to integrate FuXi’s large-scale forecasts into SHTM’s mesoscale predictions, creating a hybrid ML–physics forecasting system. As shown in Fig. 1b, spectral nudging blends ML large-scale forecasts into the physical model during model integration, progressively aligning SHTM’s large-scale features with FuXi. Only atmospheric variables U, V, and T are nudged; relative humidity is excluded as previous studies indicate this weakens typhoon intensity (Husain et al., 2024). Planetary-scale features (wavelength >1000 km, corresponding to Rossby radius) are targeted, with spectral truncation numbers of 8 (zonal) and 7 (meridional). The nudging relaxation time (tau), equivalent to an e-folding time scale, is set to six hours to match FuXi’s output intervals, preventing interpolation artifacts. As FuXi lacks sea surface temperature (SST) forecasts, SST is prescribed from ECMWF HRES analysis fields to avoid systematic underestimation of typhoon intensity.

3
Results

3.1
Typhoon Track and Intensity Errors

To demonstrate the operational potential of the hybrid model, this study systematically evaluated the SHTM, FuXi, FuXi-SHTM and ECMWF-IFS forecasts (specifically referring to ECMWF HRES) using CMA’s best-track data set for the entire 2024 cyclone season. Figure 2 presents mean track and intensity forecast errors at lead times of 0–120 hours, evaluated using consistent samples (171, 145, 131, 115, 101, 89, 78, 67, 59, 44, and 35 samples respectively). Results show FuXi–SHTM significantly outperforms SHTM, FuXi, and ECMWF-IFS in track forecasts, with average errors within 200 km up to 108 hours. While SHTM and FuXi, both driven by ECMWF HRES analysis, show similar track errors for forecasts up to 48 hours, FuXi clearly outperforms at longer lead times, emphasizing ML’s strength in predicting large-scale dynamics. Regarding intensity forecasts, FuXi–SHTM achieves errors (around 7.5 m/s) comparable to SHTM and substantially lower than ECMWF-IFS (around 10 m/s), which may reflect differences in physical parameterizations. FuXi alone exhibits large intensity errors (around 15 m/s), lacking useful predictive skill. Notably, FuXi, FuXi–SHTM, and SHTM share identical initial positions and intensities at the initial forecast time (0 h), as all use real-time BABJ advisories operationally.

To further assess the scenario-dependent performance of typhoon forecasts, the 2024 dataset is subdivided into nine landfalling and seventeen non-landfalling typhoons. Figure 3 presents scatterplots comparing mean track errors among SHTM, FuXi, and FuXi–SHTM for each subset. For landfalling typhoons, FuXi–SHTM and FuXi show comparable performance, both clearly outperforming SHTM (Fig. 3a). In the case of non-landfalling typhoons, FuXi–SHTM consistently achieves the lowest track errors, markedly reducing extreme forecast deviations observed with standalone FuXi (Fig. 3b). This improved performance highlights the stability and robustness of the hybrid approach, underscoring the synergistic benefits derived from integrating physical and machine learning methodologies.

3.2
Typhoon Cloud and Wind Structures

To further assess the performance of the hybrid FuXi–SHTM model in forecasting typhoon structures, brightness temperature (TB) simulations from channel 13 (aorund 10.7 um) of the Fengyun (FY)-4B Advanced Geosynchronous Radiation Imager (AGRI) are compared between FuXi–SHTM and SHTM. The all-sky TB simulations are computed using the Community Radiative Transfer Model (CRTM, version 2.3.0), with model forecast fields interpolated spatially and temporally to match the satellite observation points. Inputs to the CRTM include vertical profiles of atmospheric temperature and water vapor mixing ratio, hydrometeor cloud water contents and associated effective radii, surface variables (surface temperature, 2-m temperature, and 10-m wind), and satellite geometry parameters. The effective radii for liquid water, ice, rain, snow, and graupel are specified as 20, 40, 400, 600, and 800 um, respectively. Figure 4 shows the observed FY-4B AGRI channel-13 TBs (left panels, unit: K) along with simulated TBs from FuXi–SHTM and SHTM at forecast lead times of 12 h, 36 h, 48 h, and 96 h for Typhoon Yagi (2024), initialized at 0000 UTC on 3 September 2024. Since the FuXi model lacks cloud predictions, only the FuXi–SHTM and SHTM simulations are compared. Overall, FuXi–SHTM produces cloud structures more consistent with satellite observations than SHTM, particularly evident at 36 h (Figs. 4b, f, l) and 48 h (Figs. 4c, g, m) forecast lead times. FuXi–SHTM captures the peripheral cloud bands more realistically and simulates the typhoon eye with a size closely matching observations, whereas SHTM consistently produces an oversized typhoon eye. Furthermore, FuXi–SHTM also shows superior performance in reproducing broader environmental cloud structures, as exemplified by the eastern rainband at the 96-h forecast (Figs. 4d, h, n), closely matching the observed distribution.

In addition, Figure 5 presents the spatial distributions of 10-m wind speeds (unit: m/s) for Typhoon Yagi (2024), derived from RCM-2 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) observations (Fig. 5a) and forecasts from FuXi at 60 h, FuXi–SHTM at 58 h, and SHTM at 58 h, all initialized at 0000 UTC on 3 September 2024. FuXi significantly underestimates the wind intensity (Fig. 5b), while SHTM forecasts exhibit notable positional errors (Fig. 5d). In contrast, FuXi–SHTM (Fig. 5c) forecasts a typhoon wind structure closely matching SAR observations in terms of eye size and spatial distribution, though its intensities are slightly weaker. Therefore, from upper-level cloud-top brightness temperatures down to lower-level surface wind structures, FuXi–SHTM forecasts exhibit improved consistency with observational data, clearly demonstrating the unique advantages of the hybrid model framework in forecasting typhoon track, intensity, and structural features.

3.3
Impacts of Model Resolution

Improvements in the hybrid ML–physics model generally originate from iterative updates in the ML model, advancements in physical parameterizations, and enhancements in data assimilation methods. This section specifically investigates how increasing the model resolution affects the performance of the hybrid system. The horizontal resolution of SHTM is increased from 9 km to 3 km, expanding the horizontal grid to 2400×2000, slightly reducing the domain coverage compared to the original 9-km SHTM, and deactivating the cumulus parameterization scheme. Figure 6 shows the track errors and maximum wind speed forecasts for Typhoon Yagi (2024) from 6 to 120 hours initialized at 0000 UTC on 3 September 2024, comparing SHTM, 9-km FuXi–SHTM, and 3-km FuXi–SHTM. The track forecast from the 3-km FuXi–SHTM closely resembles that from the 9-km FuXi–SHTM, largely due to the constraining influence of large-scale fields provided by the FuXi model, thus limiting substantial changes in typhoon tracks. However, the intensity forecasts from the 3-km FuXi–SHTM are significantly improved, showing better agreement with observations than the 9-km FuXi–SHTM. This result highlights that improvements in physical modeling substantially enhance the performance of hybrid ML–physics forecasting systems.

4
Discussion and Conclusions

This study constructs a hybrid typhoon forecasting model, FuXi–SHTM, by integrating the Shanghai Typhoon Model with the FuXi machine learning model via spectral nudging. The model is systematically evaluated using forecasts from the entire 2024 typhoon season. Results demonstrate that FuXi–SHTM significantly outperforms both the original SHTM and FuXi models in mean track errors at all forecast lead times, achieving notably high predictive accuracy. Intensity forecasts from FuXi–SHTM are comparable to those of the original SHTM and considerably superior to the standalone FuXi model and ECMWF HRES. These findings indicate that the hybrid approach effectively combines the advantages of both FuXi and SHTM, resulting in forecast improvements greater than the sum of their parts, highlighting the potential of ML–physics hybrid approaches in operational typhoon forecasting. Additionally, FuXi–SHTM produces more accurate forecasts of typhoon cloud and surface wind structures than both FuXi and SHTM. Increasing the model resolution from 9 km to 3 km does not significantly alter track forecasts, yet notably reduces intensity errors, underscoring the importance of model resolution for typhoon intensity prediction.

Furthermore, the study finds that the FuXi model occasionally produces excessively large track errors for non-landfalling typhoons, markedly degrading its overall performance metrics. These results indicate that ML forecasts should be treated cautiously; notably, previous research (Xu et al., 2025) has found that ML models still lag behind traditional numerical models in predicting sudden-turning typhoons. By contrast, the hybrid FuXi–SHTM significantly reduces occurrences of such extreme errors, demonstrating enhanced stability and robustness, and illustrating clear synergistic benefits from combining physical modeling and ML models.

At present, ML models have evolved into Machine Learning Numerical Weather Prediction (MLNWP) systems that substantially outperform traditional numerical weather prediction models, such as ECMWF HRES, particularly in forecasting large-scale patterns indicated by higher Anomaly Correlation Coefficients (ACCs) for 500-hPa geopotential height fields (Z500). MLNWP systems have made rapid progress in recent years. Newer systems, such as the ECMWF AIFS, have incorporated additional forecast variables including solar radiation, cloud cover, and snowfall. A fully end-to-end data-driven weather prediction framework has also been developed (Allen et al., 2025). Despite these advances, MLNWP models still suffer from fundamental limitations, particularly in achieving sufficient spatial resolution and accurately resolving extreme events such as typhoon intensity. Consequently, this study advocates for operational typhoon forecasting agencies to adopt ML–physics hybrid modeling frameworks, providing a robust interim solution until the next transformative breakthrough in Artificial Intelligence (AI) weather forecasting emerges.
