On k ℝ k_{\mathbb{R}} -spaces and s ℝ s_{\mathbb{R}} -spaces
Saak Gabriyelyan, Evgenii Reznichenko

Abstract
We give new characterizations of spaces X X which are k ℝ k_{\mathbb{R}} -spaces or s ℝ s_{\mathbb{R}} -spaces. Applying the obtained results we provide some sufficient and necessary conditions on X X for which C p ​ ( X ) C_{p}(X) is a k ℝ k_{\mathbb{R}} -space or an s ℝ s_{\mathbb{R}} -space. It is proved that C p ​ ( X ) C_{p}(X) is a k ℝ k_{\mathbb{R}} -space for any space X X with one non-isolated point; if, in addition, | X | |X| is not sequential, then C p ​ ( X ) C_{p}(X) is even an s ℝ s_{\mathbb{R}} -space. Under ( C ​ H ) (CH) , it is shown that there exists a separable metrizable space X X such that C p ​ ( X ) C_{p}(X) is an Ascoli space but not a k ℝ k_{\mathbb{R}} -space.

All spaces are assumed to be Tychonoff. We denote by

-continuous function

-continuous

A space

All spaces are assumed to be Tychonoff. Let

A family

Universally
\sigma
-measurable and universal
\sigma
-measure zero spaces

For a compact space
K
, we denote by
C(K)
the Banach lattice of all continuous real-valued functions on
K
endowed with the
\sup
-norm, and let

P(K)=\{\mu\in C(K)^{\prime}:\|\mu\|=1,\mu\geq 0\}

be the positive unit sphere of the dual Banach space
C(K)^{\prime}
, endowed with the weak
∗
topology. By the fundamental Riesz Theorem, the functionals
\mu\in P(K)
can be identified with Borel regular probability measures on
K
, see
[
12
, 1.2]
. Every continuous function
f:X\to Y
between compact spaces induces a map
P(f):P(X)\to P(Y)
defined by

P(f)(\mu)(g):=\mu(g\circ f)\;\mbox{ for }\;g\in C(Y).

Let
X
be a space. A countably additive measure
\mu
on the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)
of Borel subsets of
X
is called a
Borel
measure.
A measure
\mu
is called
continuous
if
\mu(\{x\})=0
for every
x\in X
.
A measure
\mu
is called
discrete
if
|\mu|(C)=1
for some countable
C\subseteq X
. For
x\in X
, we denote by
\delta_{x}
the Dirac measure at
x
.
The set of all probability discrete measures on
X
is denoted by
P_{d}(X)
. Each
\mu\in P_{d}(X)
can be uniquely represented in the form
\mu=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\delta_{x_{n}}
, where
x_{n}\in X
are distinct,
\lambda_{n}>0
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}=1
.

We denote by
P_{b}(X)
the family of all probability Borel
\sigma
-additive measures on a space
X
. A measure
\mu\in P_{b}(X)
is called
Radon
if for every Borel set
B\subset X
and for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact
K\subseteq B
such that
\mu(B\setminus K)<\varepsilon
. The set of all probability Radon measures on
X
is denoted by
P(X)
. A measure
\mu
is called
\tau
-additive
if

\mu\big(\bigcup\mathcal{U}\big)=\sup\big\{\mu\big(\bigcup\mathcal{V}\big):\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{U},\ |\mathcal{V}|<\omega\big\}

for any family
\mathcal{U}
of open subsets of
X
. The set of all probability
\tau
-additive measures on
X
is denoted by
P_{\tau}(X)
.

Following
[
5
]
, we consider the aforementioned sets of probability measures as subspaces of
P(\beta X)
:

\bullet

P_{d}(X)=\{\mu\in P(\beta X):\mu(A)=1
for some
A\subseteq X,|A|\leq\omega\}
;

\bullet

P(X)=\{\mu\in P(\beta X):\mu(A)=1
for some
\sigma
-compact
A\subseteq X\}
;

\bullet

P_{\tau}(X)=\{\mu\in P(\beta X):\mu(K)=0
for every compact
K\subseteq\beta X\setminus X\}
;

\bullet

P_{\sigma}(X)=\{\mu\in P(\beta X):\mu(K)=0
for every compact
K\subseteq\beta X\setminus X
of
G_{\delta}
type
\}
.

Then

P_{d}(X)\subseteq P(X)\subseteq P_{\tau}(X)\subseteq P_{\sigma}(X)\subseteq P(\beta X).

By the general Riesz theorem, the elements of
P(\beta X)
can be identified with finitely additive regular probability measures defined on the algebra of Baire subsets of
X
(i.e., the smallest algebra generated by all functionally closed subsets of
X
). Under this identification, the elements of
P_{\sigma}(X)
correspond to
\sigma
-additive probability measures defined
on Baire subsets of
X
(see
[
12
, Section 1]
).

Proposition 3.1
.

If
X
is a perfectly normal space, then
P_{b}(X)=P_{\sigma}(X)
.

Proof.

Since in a perfectly normal space every closed set is a zero-set, the algebra of Baire subsets of
X
coincides with the algebra of Borel subsets of
X
.
Thus,
P_{b}(X)=P_{\sigma}(X)
.
∎

Let
X
be a subspace of a space
Y
. The family

can be naturally considered as a subspace of

: a measure
\mu\in P_{b}(X)

such that
\tilde{\mu}(B)=\mu(B\cap Y)

. We shall always identify

with its image in

and write

.

Let
A
be a subset of a space
X
, and let

. The

\displaystyle\mu^{\ast}(A)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mu(B):A\subseteq B\in\mathcal{B}(X)\},

\displaystyle\mu_{\ast}(A)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mu(B):A\supseteq B\in\mathcal{B}(X)\}.

A subset

\mu
-measurable

\mu_{\ast}(M)=\mu^{\ast}(M)
. A subset

is called
universally measurable
if

\mu
-measurable for any
\mu\in P_{\sigma}(X)
. A subset

is called

\mu^{\ast}(A)=0

.

X

be spaces, and let

\displaystyle P(\beta f)\big(P_{d}(X)\big)\subseteq P_{d}(Y),

\displaystyle P(\beta f)\big(P(X)\big)\subseteq P(Y),

\displaystyle P(\beta f)\big(P_{\tau}(X)\big)\subseteq P_{\tau}(Y),

\displaystyle P(\beta f)\big(P_{\sigma}(X)\big)\subseteq P_{\sigma}(Y).

Proposition 3.2

Let

Let us call the space

\sigma

. Clearly, any universal
\sigma
-measure zero space is universally
\sigma
-measurable. By Theorem 1 of

Now we characterize universal

Theorem 3.4
.

A space

is universal
\sigma
-measure zero if, and only if,

is universally
\sigma
-measurable and any compact subspace of

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Since

(\Leftarrow)
Let

. Since

is universally
\sigma
-measurable, we have

. Then
\mu(S)=1

\sigma

N. Noble’s results on infinite products of spaces

In this section we formulate some results and their consequences from Noble’s article
[

]
that are necessary in what follows.
In
[

he considered
\Sigma^{0}
-products, which are now called
\sigma
-products. Accordingly,
\Sigma^{0}
-continuous mappings will be called

Let

be a product of spaces. For

\displaystyle\delta(x,y)

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}:x_{\alpha}\neq y_{\alpha}\},

\displaystyle\sigma(X,x)

\displaystyle=\{y\in X:|\delta(x,y)|<\omega\}.

Sets of the form
\sigma(X,x)
are called
\sigma
-products
. A mapping
f
from

to a space

\sigma
-continuous
if
f
is continuous on every
\sigma
-product in

is called
2
-continuous
if

is continuous on every subset of

of the form

|Y_{\alpha}|\leq 2
. The following result was proved in Theorem 1.1 of Noble
[

]

Theorem 4.1

(
[
38
]
)
.

Let

be a product of spaces, and let

be a space. If the mapping

is
\sigma
-continuous and

-continuous, then

is continuous.

An ultrafilter on a set
A
is
\tau
-complete if it is closed under intersections of families of cardinality
<\tau
. Let us say that a cardinal

is

if there exists an
\omega_{1}
-complete free ultrafilter on

As usual

an uncountable cardinal

\tau

In
[
38

Denote by

On the other hand, Mazur showed in

In

If the continuum

5
Spaces which are (strongly) functionally generated by a cover

The importance of covers which (strongly) functionally generate a space
X
is explained by numerous important result obtained in Section III, § 4 of
[
2
]
. Such covers are important also to obtain new characterizations of
k_{\mathbb{R}}
-spaces and
s_{\mathbb{R}}
-spaces.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a cover of a space
X
. Recall that
X
is called

\bullet

generated
by the family
\mathcal{M}
if for each non-closed set
A\subseteq X
, there is an
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that
M\cap A
is not closed in
M
;

\bullet

strongly functionally generated
by the family
\mathcal{M}
if for each real-valued discontinuous function
f
on
X
, there is an
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that the function
f{\restriction}_{M}:M\to\mathbb{R}
is discontinuous;

\bullet

functionally generated
by the family
\mathcal{M}
if for every discontinuous function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, there is an
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that the function
f{\restriction}_{M}
cannot be extended to a real-valued continuous function on the whole space
X
.

It is clear that
X
is a
k
-space (resp., a sequential space or
X
has countable tightness) if it is generated by the family
\mathcal{K}(X)
(resp., by
\mathcal{S}(X)
or by
\mathcal{C}(X)
). The space
X
has countable
\mathbb{R}
-tightness if, and only if, it is functionally generated by
\mathcal{C}(X)
.

Below we characterize spaces generated by a cover, from which we deduce in Proposition
5.3
that any such space is strongly functionally generated by that cover.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a cover of a space
X
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

X
is generated by the family
\mathcal{M}
;

(ii)

the natural mapping
I:\bigoplus\mathcal{M}\to X
, defined by
I(x)=x
for
x\in M\in\mathcal{M}
, is quotient;

(iii)

for each non-closed
Q\subseteq X
, there are
x\in\overline{Q}{\setminus}Q
and
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that
x\in M\cap\overline{M\cap Q}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii) Let
Q\subseteq X
be non-closed. Then, by (i), there is
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that
M\cap Q
is not closed in
M
. So, there is a point
x\in M
such that
x\in\overline{M\cap Q}{\setminus}(M\cap Q)
; in particular,
x\in\overline{Q}
. It remains to note that
x\not\in Q
(otherwise, we would have
x\in M\cap Q
that is impossible).

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) Let
Q
be a non-closed subset of
X
. By (iii), choose
x\in\overline{Q}{\setminus}Q
and
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that
x\in M\cap\overline{M\cap Q}
. Since
x\not\in Q
and
x\in M
it follows that
M\cap Q
is not closed in
M
, as desired.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) A space
X
is generated by the family
\mathcal{M}
if, and only if, the following condition is satisfied: a set
F\subseteq X
is closed if and only if
F\cap M
is closed in
M
for each
M\in\mathcal{M}
. It remains to apply the definitions of the mapping
I
and the definition of a quotient mapping.
∎

The implication (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) of the following statement is Theorem 2(1) of
[
10
]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a cover of a space
X
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

X
is strongly functionally generated by the family
\mathcal{M}
;

(ii)

the natural mapping
I:\bigoplus\mathcal{M}\to X
is
R
-quotient;

(iii)

the adjoint mapping

I^{\#}\colon C_{p}(X)\to C_{p}\left(\bigoplus\mathcal{M}\right)=\prod_{M\in\mathcal{M}}C_{p}(M)

is an embedding with closed image.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) It is clear that
I
is continuous and surjective. Now, let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
f\circ I
is continuous. Assuming that
f
is discontinuous we could find
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that
f{\restriction}_{M}
is discontinuous, too. Since
f{\restriction}_{M}=f\circ I{\restriction}_{M}
we obtain a contradiction. Thus
f
is continuous, and hence

is
R
-quotient.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a discontinuous function. Since

is
R
-quotient, we obtain that

is also discontinuous. Therefore there is

such that

is discontinuous. Thus

is strongly functionally generated by the family
\mathcal{M}
.

The implication (ii)

2.1

∎

Let
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and

In this section we give two types of characterizations of

-quotient mappings and (strongly) functionally generated families (which are closely related by Propositions

Theorem 6.1
.

For a Tychonoff space
X
, the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

X
is a
k_{\mathbb{R}}
-space;

(ii)

X
is strongly functionally generated by the family
\mathcal{K}(X)
;

(iii)

X
is functionally generated by the family
\mathcal{K}(X)
;

(iv)

is strongly functionally generated by a family of

-subspaces of

(v)

is an

-quotient image of some topological sum of compact spaces;

(vi)

X
is an

-quotient image of a locally compact space;

(vii)

X
is an
R
-quotient image of a
k
-space;

(viii)

X
is an
R
-quotient image of a
k_{\mathbb{R}}
-space.

Proof.

The equivalence (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) is essentially the definition of
k_{\mathbb{R}}
-spaces.

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) follows from Proposition

because each compact subset of

is

-embedded in

.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iv) is trivial, and (ii)
\Rightarrow
(v) follows from Proposition

.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(viii) Let
\mathcal{M}
be a family of

-subspaces of

such that

is strongly functionally generated by
\mathcal{M}
. It follows from Proposition

that

is an

-quotient image of the discrete sum of

-spaces

. It remains to note that

-space.

The implications (v)
\Rightarrow

(viii)

Proposition 8.1

(vi) implies that

Proof.

Let
I=[-1,1]
and
Y=C_{p}(X,[-1,1])
.

(\Leftarrow)

Since
\mu\in P_{d}(X)
then
\mu=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\delta_{x_{n}}
, where
x_{n}\in X
,
\delta_{x_{n}}
is the Dirac measure at
x_{n}
,
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}=1
. Each measure
\delta_{x}
is continuous and bounded by unity on

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\delta_{x_{n}}
converges uniformly to

. Therefore,

is continuous.

(\Rightarrow)
Since
\mu
is continuous on
Y
and

is dense in

, it follows from Factorization Lemma 0.2.3 of
[
2
]
that there exists a countable

, a continuous function

\mu{\restriction}_{Y}=\psi\circ\pi_{A}
, where

\pi_{A}\colon Y\to C_{p}(X|Y,I),\ f\mapsto f{\restriction}_{A}

is the projection and

. If

.

We prove that
\mu(A)=1
. Assume the contrary, that is,
\mu(A)<1
. Then there exist

such that

\tilde{\mu}(A)=1
and
\nu(A)=0
, where

. Since

is countable, we have
\tilde{\mu}\in P_{d}(X)
. It follows from the proved implication
(\Leftarrow)
that

is continuous. Consequently, the functions
\nu{\restriction}_{Y}
and

\varphi\colon C_{p}(X|A,I)\to\mathbb{R},\ f\mapsto\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\big(\psi(f)-(1-\lambda)\tilde{\mu}(f)\big)

are continuous. Then
\nu{\restriction}_{Y}=\varphi\circ\pi_{A}
. Let
\mathbf{0}_{A}
be the zero function on
A
. Since
\mathbf{0}_{A}\in C_{p}(X|A,I)
and
\varphi(\mathbf{0}_{A})=0
, there exists a finite

and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varphi(W)\subseteq(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
for the neighborhood

W=\big\{f\in C_{p}(X|A,I):f(B)\subseteq(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\big\}

of
\mathbf{0}_{A}
. Since
\nu(B)=0
, there exists a neighborhood

of

in
\beta X
such that

. Let

Proposition 10.2
.

Let

. Then

is
k
-continuous on

.

Proof.

be a compact subspace. Since

, there is a
\sigma
-compact subset

of

for each

. Set

, and for every

\pi_{n}:C_{p}(X)\to C_{p}(Q_{n}),\quad\pi_{n}(f):=f{\restriction}_{Q_{n}}.

For every

such that

. Recall that if

as a continuous functional of the Banach space

Proposition 10.3
.

Let

be a space, and let

is

(\Rightarrow)

. Then

(\Leftarrow)

A
Sierpiński set
is an uncountable subset of
\mathbb{R}
whose intersection with every measure-zero (= Lebesgue zero) set is countable. The existence of Sierpiński sets is independent of the axioms of ZFC. In
[
48
]
, Sierpiński showed that they exist in
\mathbb{R}
if the continuum hypothesis is true. On the other hand, they do not exist if Martin’s axiom for
\aleph_{1}
is true.

Proposition 11.19
.

If
X
is a Sierpiński set, then
C_{p}(X)
is an Ascoli space which is not an
s_{\mathbb{R}}
-space. Consequently, under
(CH)
, there exists a separable metrizable space
X
such that
C_{p}(X)
is an Ascoli space but not a
k_{\mathbb{R}}
-space.

Proof.

Every Sierpiński set is a
\lambda
-space
[
34
, 10.1]
. Hence, by Theorem 3.2 of
[
46
]
,
X
has the property
(\kappa)
. Therefore, by Theorem
1.2
,
C_{p}(X)
is an Ascoli space.
Since a Sierpiński set is an uncountable subset of
\mathbb{R}
whose intersection with every Lebesgue zero set is countable, it follows that the Lebesgue measure of
X
is not equal to zero. Since the Lebesgue measure is continuous, the space
X
is not a universal measure zero set. It follows from Theorem
3.7
that
X
is not a universal
\sigma
-measure zero space. Therefore, by Theorem
11.18
,
C_{p}(X)
is not an
s_{\mathbb{R}}
-space.

It follows from Proposition
11.12
that
C_{p}(X)
is not a
k_{\mathbb{R}}
-space.
∎
