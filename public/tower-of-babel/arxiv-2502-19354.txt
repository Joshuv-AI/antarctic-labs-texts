Two-Stage Weighted Projection for Reliable Low-Complexity Cooperative and Non-Cooperative Localization
Harish K. Dureppagari, R. Michael Buehrer, Harpreet S. Dhillon

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a two-stage weighted projection method (TS-WPM) for time-difference-of-arrival (TDOA)-based localization, providing provable improvements in positioning accuracy, particularly under high geometric dilution of precision (GDOP) and low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions. TS-WPM employs a two-stage iterative refinement approach that dynamically updates both range and position estimates, effectively mitigating residual errors while maintaining computational efficiency. Additionally, we extend TS-WPM to support cooperative localization by leveraging two-way time-of-arrival (TW-TOA) measurements, which enhances positioning accuracy in scenarios with limited anchor availability. To analyze TS-WPM, we derive its error covariance matrix and mean squared error (MSE), establishing conditions for its optimality and robustness. To facilitate rigorous evaluation, we develop a 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)-compliant analytical framework, incorporating 5G New Radio (NR) physical layer aspects as well as large-scale and small-scale fading. As part of this, we derive a generalized Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB) for multipath propagation and introduce a novel non-line-of-sight (NLOS) bias model that accounts for propagation conditions and SNR variations. Our evaluations demonstrate that TS-WPM achieves near-CRLB performance and consistently outperforms state-of-the-art weighted nonlinear least squares (WNLS) in high GDOP and low SNR scenarios. Moreover, cooperative localization with TS-WPM significantly enhances accuracy, especially when an insufficient number of anchors (such as 2) are visible. Finally, we analyze the computational complexity of TS-WPM, showing its balanced trade-off between accuracy and efficiency, making it a scalable solution for real-time localization in next-generation networks.

Localization is an essential component for numerous applications spanning navigation, autonomous systems, the Internet of Things (IoT), and next-generation communication networks like 5G Advanced and 6G. Over the years, significant advancements have been made in positioning methodologies, leveraging techniques such as TOA and TDOA-based localization. II
Novel Two-Stage Weighted Projection Method

In this section, we introduce 1) the novel low-complexity TS-WPM, designed for improved accuracy, especially in challenging conditions such as low SNR or high GDOP, and 2) an extension of its framework to support cooperative localization, leveraging collaborative measurements to enhance accuracy in scenarios with limited visible anchors.

II-A

Novel TS-WPM: TDOA Localization

To achieve a balance between improved accuracy and maintaining computational efficiency, we introduce a novel TS-WPM designed for TDOA-based localization. This new approach is particularly crucial for addressing the challenges associated with conditions such as high GDOP or low SNR regimes. In this approach, we introduce additional variables to the estimation along with the target UE location. Specifically, we estimate the range or TOA between the reference anchor and the target UE, iteratively updating this estimate using the latest UE location. This updated range estimate is then utilized to refine the UE location estimate further.

Consider a network of
\small B
anchors assisting a target UE to obtain pseudorange or TOA measurements. The pseudorange measurements between an anchor
\small b
and a target UE, denoted by
\small\hat{r}_{b}
, are generally defined as

\small\displaystyle\hat{r}_{b}=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\mathbf{a}_{b}\|+\Delta_{b}+v_{b},\hskip 9.24994ptb\in\{1,\cdots,B\}.

(1)

where
\small\mathbf{a}_{b}
denotes known location coordinates of the anchor
\small b
,
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
is the true UE location,
\small\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\mathbf{a}_{b}\|
is the range between the anchor
\small b
and the UE,
\small\Delta_{b}
is the bias induced by multipath, and
\small n_{b}
is additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN). Assuming the pseudorange measurements
\small\hat{r}_{b}
are statistically independent across anchors, the covariance matrix of the TOA measurement vector
\small\hat{\mathbf{r}}=[\hat{r}_{1},\cdots,\hat{r}_{B}]^{T}
is diagonal and takes the form
\small\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\cdots,\sigma_{B}^{2})
, where
\small\sigma_{b}^{2}
is the variance of
\small v_{b}
.

Now, without loss of generality, we assume anchor
\small 1
as the reference anchor. The TDOA measurements are then defined as

\small\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{b}=\hat{r}_{b}-\hat{r}_{1},\hskip 9.24994ptb\in\{2,\dots,B\},

(2)

Typically, the anchor with the strongest SNR is selected as the reference anchor
[
3
]
. This choice minimizes the variance of the common reference term
\small\hat{r}_{1}
, thereby reducing the overall correlation among TDOA measurements and improving localization robustness. Unlike pseudorange measurements, TDOA measurements share the common reference term
\small\hat{r}_{1}
, making them mutually dependent. As a result, the covariance matrix of the TDOA measurement vector
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=[\tilde{r}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{r}_{B}]^{T}
becomes non-diagonal. It takes the form
\small\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\mathrm{diag}([\sigma_{2}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{B}^{2}])+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{T}
, where
\small\mathbf{u}
is a
\small(B-1)\times 1
vector of ones, capturing the correlation induced by the common reference measurement
\small\hat{r}_{1}
.
Denoting an estimate of the UE location by
\small\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, as part of TDOA-localization, we aim to minimize the TDOA residual error calculated as

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\leftarrow\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=2}^{B}\left(\tilde{r}_{b}-\tilde{r}_{b,e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)^{2},

(3)

where
\small\tilde{r}_{b,e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
represents an estimate of TDOA associated with the anchor
\small b
calculated as

\small\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{b,e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{a}_{b}\|-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{a}_{1}\|.

(4)

In our proposed approach, we introduce an additional unknown parameter
\small r_{1}
in the estimation process alongside
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where
\small r_{1}
enables an accurate estimation of the range between the reference anchor and the UE, thereby reducing TDOA residual errors and improving the localization accuracy. To facilitate this, we define a new variable

\small\displaystyle\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{b}=\hat{r}_{b}-\hat{r}_{1}^{e},\hskip 9.24994ptb\in\{1,2,\cdots,B\},

(5)

where
\small\hat{r}_{1}^{\,e}
denotes an estimate of true range from the reference anchor to the UE. Unlike the pseudorange
\small\hat{r}_{1}
, which includes unknown clock biases and is inherently noisy,
\small\hat{r}_{1}^{\,e}
is iteratively refined to approach the true (bias-free) range.
Importantly, we are not modifying the actual TDOA measurements but rather introducing a new variable that serves as the basis for the proposed approach and the corresponding analytical study. The proposed method still uses conventional TDOA measurements to update UE location, residual error, and range estimates, but the analyses and refinement process are guided by
\small\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{b}
. Given
\small\hat{r}_{1}^{e}
,
\small\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{b}
become independent across anchors resembling TOA-like measurements. This ensures that, given
\small\hat{r}_{1}^{e}
,
\small{\rm var}(\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{b})
is essentially equal to
\small{\rm var}(\hat{r}_{b})
. To incorporate this refinement into the estimation process, we redefine the residual error to be minimized as

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\leftarrow\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\tilde{r}_{b}-\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{b,e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hat{r}_{1}^{e})\right)^{2},

(6)

where
\small\tilde{r}_{1}=0
for the reference anchor (
\small b=1
), and
\small\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{b,e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hat{r}_{1}^{e})
represents the refined TDOA estimate, computed as

\small\displaystyle\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{b,e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hat{r}_{1}^{e})=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{a}_{b}\|-\hat{r}_{1}^{e}.

(7)

We now discuss the significance of this new formulation and its advantages in simplifying the process of generating weights by avoiding the estimation of the full covariance matrix, as required in methods like WNLS
[
3
]
. As mentioned earlier, with
\small\hat{r}_{1}^{e}
available, the measurements described in (
5
) become independent of each other. As a result, the covariance matrix becomes a diagonal matrix, where the diagonal elements represent the variances of the different TOA equivalent measurements, as opposed to the non-diagonal covariance matrix we observe for the conventional TDOA measurements. Therefore, the new covariance matrix, denoted by
\small\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
, takes the form

\small\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}={\rm diag}({\rm var}(\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{1}),\cdots,{\rm var}(\tilde{r}^{\prime}_{B}))={\rm diag}({\rm var}(\hat{r}_{1}),\cdots,{\rm var}(\hat{r}_{B})).

(8)

This newly defined covariance matrix
\small\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is then utilized to generate weights for the proposed TS-WPM. A key advantage of this approach is the significant reduction in computational complexity, as it eliminates the need to estimate and invert the full covariance matrix. Since
\small\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is a diagonal matrix, inverting it simply involves inverting the individual diagonal elements, which is computationally efficient. The weights are generated as

\small\displaystyle W_{b}=\frac{\frac{1}{{\rm var}(\hat{r}_{b})}}{\sum_{i=1}^{B}\frac{1}{{\rm var}(\hat{r}_{i})}},\hskip 9.24994ptb={1,2,\cdots,B}.

(9)

The two-stage iterative refinement in TS-WPM has a conceptual resemblance to Turbo decoding principles
[
26
]
, where extrinsic information is exchanged between decoders to improve error correction. Similarly, in TS-WPM, refining range estimates and UE location updates iteratively improves positioning accuracy. However, unlike Turbo decoding, where exchanged information is independent in terms of redundant parity, the refined information in TS-WPM is correlated, as both updates are derived from the same set of TDOA measurements.

II-B

Novel TS-WPM: Cooperative Localization

In this section, we extend the novel TS-WPM approach described in Algorithm
1
to enable cooperative localization, where multiple target UEs leverage internode measurements to enhance positioning accuracy, especially when only a limited number of anchors (such as 2) are visible. In our cooperative localization framework, we assume that TDOA measurements are available between visible anchors and UEs, while TW-TOA measurements are utilized for inter-target UE measurements. In particular, modern positioning technologies such as sidelink positioning (SLP)
[
27
]
enable a wide range of measurement techniques, including TOA, angle of arrival (AOA), round–trip time (RTT), and TDOA, between target UEs. However, we specifically adopt TW-TOA for internode measurements for the following key reasons: 1) TW-TOA eliminates the need for precise synchronization among collaborating nodes
[
3
]
. 2) Many existing positioning technologies, such as ultra-wideband (UWB)
[
28
]
and WiFi-based localization
[
29
]
, predominantly rely on TW-TOA measurements for ranging. By utilizing TW-TOA for cooperative localization, our approach remains flexible and interoperable with different positioning technologies, facilitating seamless integration across various deployment scenarios. 3) The geometric configuration of collaborating target UEs is often suboptimal, particularly in indoor environments or clustered deployments. Relying on relative measurements such as TDOA in these cases can lead to significant localization errors due to high GDOP. By leveraging TW-TOA measurements instead, we improve localization robustness, especially in non-ideal geometries.

III
Optimality and Robustness of TS-WPM: Analytical Study

In this section, we present a comprehensive analytical proof demonstrating the conditions under which the proposed two-stage approach is optimal and when it outperforms WNLS. To achieve this, we first derive the error covariance matrix for the novel TS-WPM by using the alternative formulation outlined in (
5
) as basis. We then compare it with the error covariance for the two-stage estimation employing the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) as described in
[
30
]

Key Preliminaries

Method 1: Conventional TDOA Formulation.
The conventional TDOA measurements are as given in (
2
). Defining the TDOA measurement vector as
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=[\tilde{r}_{2},\dots,\tilde{r}_{B}]^{T}
and the variance of
\small\hat{r}_{b}
as
\small\sigma_{b}^{2}
, the covariance matrix of
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
is given by

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{1}=\mathrm{diag}([\sigma_{2}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{B}^{2}])+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{T},

(10)

where
\small\mathbf{u}
is a
\small(B-1)\times 1
vector of ones, capturing the correlation induced by the common reference measurement
\small\hat{r}_{1}
. Assuming 2D estimation, we define the parameter vector with UE coordinates as
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}=[x,y]
. The Jacobian matrix of
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
with respect to
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
is expressed as

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{x-x_{2}}{d_{2}}-\frac{x-x_{1}}{d_{1}}&\frac{y-y_{2}}{d_{2}}-\frac{y-y_{1}}{d_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{x-x_{B}}{d_{B}}-\frac{x-x_{1}}{d_{1}}&\frac{y-y_{B}}{d_{B}}-\frac{y-y_{1}}{d_{1}}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

With this formulation, the FIM for
\small\hat{\mathbf{r}}

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{1}=\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}.

(12)

Method 2: Alternative TDOA Formulation.
As outlined in Section
II-A
, we introduce an additional parameter
\small r_{1}
to the parameter vector. Considering 2D estimation, the parameter vector is defined as
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}=[\boldsymbol{\theta},r_{1}]=[x,y,r_{1}]
, where
\small r_{1}
facilities an accurate range estimation associated with the reference anchor. To incorporate the estimate of
\small r_{1}

5

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{2}=\mathrm{diag}([\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{B}^{2}]).

(13)

Denoting a vector of alternative TDOA measurements by
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
, the Jacobian matrix of
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
with respect to
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
is then defined as

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{2}=[\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\mathbf{v}],

(14)

where
\small\mathbf{H}_{t}
is given by

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{x-x_{1}}{d_{1}}\cdots\frac{x-x_{B}}{d_{B}}\\
\frac{y-y_{1}}{d_{1}}\cdots\frac{y-y_{B}}{d_{B}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(15)

\small\mathbf{v}
is a
\small B\times 1

The FIM for
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
is given by

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{2}=\mathbf{H}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{2}.

(16)

Expanding
\small\mathbf{F}_{2}

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}&-\mathbf{H}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\\
-\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}&\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\end{bmatrix}.

(17)

Applying Schur complement

\small\boldsymbol{\theta}=[x,y]

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{2,[x,y]}=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\left(\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}.

The second term in (

\small r_{1}

\small r_{1}

\small r_{1}

Not surprisingly, both formulations in (

\small\mathbf{F}_{1}=\mathbf{F}_{2,[x,y]}

III-C

Alternative Intuition: Robustness of TS-WPM

In WNLS, we have the normalization factor taking the form
\small\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)^{-1}
, where
\small\mathbf{W}_{1}
is an inverse of the covariance matrix. If
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}
is ill-conditioned (often observed in high GDOP scenarios), direct inversion can lead to numerical instability and excessively large step sizes for certain TDOA measurements, resulting in overshooting. To understand this, consider the eigendecomposition. Since
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}
is a symmetric matrix and
\small\mathbf{W}_{1}
is symmetric and positive semi-definite, it can be diagonalized as

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{T},

(27)

where
\small\mathbf{U}
is an unitary matrix and
\small\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix containing eigenvalues
\small\lambda_{i}
of
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}
. Then, the inverse is given by

\small\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)^{-1}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{T}.

(28)

For an ill-conditioned matrix,
\small\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}
amplifies directions with small
\small\lambda_{i}
, leading to large updates and overshooting. A computationally stable alternative is to normalize the location update using

\small\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{1}{\text{trace}\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)},

(29)

which scales the TDOA measurements based on the sum of eigenvalues, reducing sensitivity to small
\small\lambda_{i}
. This approach mitigates numerical instability while maintaining effective convergence. Note that, in the proposed approach outlined in Algorithm
1
and (
19
), we use a variant of (
29
) by using
\small\frac{1}{{\rm trace}(\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1})}
as a normalization factor. This, in addition to the two-stage refinement process, results in the proposed approach providing superior performance to WNLS, particularly in challenging conditions such as high GDOP. Our evaluations also show that, in low SNR conditions under multipath propagation, the proposed TS-WPM surpasses WNLS, which we discuss in detail in Section
V
.

IV
Analytical Evaluation Framework

In this section, we develop a comprehensive analytical framework to evaluate the proposed TS-WPM, accounting for the effects of multipath propagation and AWGN. The CRLB serves as the principal tool for this framework. For line-of-sight (LOS) channels, the CRLB for TOA and position error has been well-documented, with accurate closed-form expressions readily available
[
31
,
32
]
. However, analysis for NLOS channels presents substantial challenges. A common approach is to incorporate a Gamma-distributed NLOS bias into the analysis
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
, which lacks a functional relationship with the underlying radio propagation conditions and the physical layer aspects. This limitation makes this approach impractical when we consider a system model that accounts for large-scale fading, small-scale fading, and physical layer aspects, e.g., positioning reference signal (PRS) configurations. To address these shortcomings, we propose an analytical framework capable of determining the best achievable TOA error and positioning accuracy in the presence of multipath propagation. It is important to note that this framework does not aim to mitigate the effects of NLOS bias or multipath. Instead, it aims to characterize the best achievable performance under these conditions. To this end, we first derive the CRLB for TOA estimation between an anchor
\small b
and a target UE without loss of generality. We then extend this analysis to derive the CRLB for position estimates of the target UE based on TOA and TDOA measurements. Additionally, we derive the CRLB of TOA under AWGN as a special case of the multipath scenario. Furthermore, using the developed analytical framework, we propose a novel approach to model NLOS bias. This approach leverages detailed link characteristics, including physical layer parameters, large-scale fading, and small-scale fading, to accurately capture the bias induced by multipath propagation. By incorporating these elements, our approach ensures a more realistic and practical representation of NLOS effects, effectively bridging the gap between theoretical models and real-world scenarios.

IV-A

CRLB Analysis: CIR and AWGN

In this section, we derive the CRLB for TOA error in the presence of multipath propagation. As a special case of this, we also derive the CRLB for TOA error under AWGN. Let
\small\mathcal{N}_{b}=\{1,2,\ldots,B\}
represent the set of anchors assigned to a target UE for PRS transmission.
We adopt an orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM)-based received signal model, as 5G NR employs OFDM as its primary waveform
[
23
]
. However, OFDM-specific impairments such as timing and frequency synchronization errors, phase noise, and high peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR) are not explicitly modeled, as they fall outside the scope of this work. This is because we assume that TOA measurements are already available, and the received signal model is used solely for deriving CRLB expressions, which in turn are used to formulate TOA error models for Monte Carlo simulations in evaluating the performance of the proposed TS-WPM algorithm.
The time domain OFDM received signal between the anchor
\small b
and the target UE can be represented in matrix form as

\small\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{b}=\sqrt{P_{b}}\boldsymbol{F}^{H}\boldsymbol{X}_{b}\boldsymbol{\Gamma_{b}}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}\boldsymbol{h}_{b}+\boldsymbol{v},\hskip 9.24994ptb\in\mathcal{N}_{b},

(30)

where
\small\boldsymbol{r}_{b}
denotes the time-domain received PRS signal of length
\small N
,
\small P_{b}
is the received power,
\small\boldsymbol{v}\sim{\cal CN}({\bf 0},\sigma^{2}{\bf I})
represents AWGN,
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{b}
represents transmitted PRS symbols,
\small\boldsymbol{h}_{b}
is the observed channel impulse response (CIR) between anchor
\small b
and the target UE of length
\small L
,
\small\boldsymbol{F}_{L}
is a Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) matrix of size
\small N\times L
, while
\small\boldsymbol{F}^{H}
is an inverse DFT (IDFT) matrix of size
\small N\times N
. Note that each delay tap of
\small\boldsymbol{h}_{b}
translates to a phase ramp in the frequency domain, which is captured in

\small\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}={\rm diag}\left(e^{-j2\pi\nabla f\tau_{d,b}(-\frac{N}{2})}\ldots e^{-j2\pi\nabla f\tau_{d,b}(\frac{N}{2}-1)}\right),

where
\small\nabla f
denotes the sub-carrier spacing (SCS) and
\small\tau_{d,b}
is the propagation delay, determining TOA between anchor
\small b
and the target UE.

We now present the CRLB of TOA estimation between the target UE and anchor
\small b
as follows.

Theorem 2

(CRLB of TOA for Multipath)
.

Denoting the TOA estimate as
\small\hat{\tau}_{d,b}
, the variance of the TOA estimate between the target UE and anchor
\small b
can be lower-bounded as:

\small\displaystyle{\rm var}(\hat{\tau}_{d,b})\geq\mathbb{I}_{e,\theta_{\hat{\tau}}^{b}}^{-1},

(31)

where vector parameter
\small\theta_{\hat{\tau}}^{b}
comprises
\small\tau_{d,b}
and
\small\boldsymbol{h}_{b}
to facilitate the joint estimation of TOA and channel state information (CSI) and
\small\mathbb{I}_{e,\theta_{\hat{\tau}}^{b}}
is the effective FIM (EFIM) representing TOA information. EFIM
\small\mathbb{I}_{e,\theta_{\hat{\tau}}^{b}}
can be derived as

\small\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{e,\theta_{\hat{\tau}}^{b}}

\small\displaystyle=2\gamma_{b}{\boldsymbol{h}_{b}}^{H}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}^{H}{\boldsymbol{X}_{b}}^{H}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{b}\boldsymbol{D}{\boldsymbol{X}_{b}}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}\boldsymbol{h}_{b},

(32)

\small\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{b}

\small\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{I}_{N}-\boldsymbol{X}_{b}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}\big(\boldsymbol{F}_{L}^{H}{\boldsymbol{X}_{b}}^{H}\boldsymbol{X}_{b}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}^{H}{\boldsymbol{X}_{b}}^{H},

(33)

where
\small\gamma_{b}=\frac{P_{b}}{\sigma^{2}}
is the SNR of the received signal,
\small\boldsymbol{D}={\rm diag}\big(2\pi\nabla f(-\frac{N}{2}),\dots,2\pi\nabla f(\frac{N}{2}-1)\big)
, and
\small\boldsymbol{I}_{N}
is the identity matrix of size
\small N\times N
.

Due to space constraints, we cannot provide detailed proof in this paper. A proof sketch can be found in one of our previous conference papers
[
13
]
.

We now derive the variance of the TOA estimate for an AWGN channel, i.e., the LOS scenario, as a special case of Theorem
2
. For the AWGN channel, the CIR
\small\boldsymbol{h}_{b}
in (
32
) reduces to a single path. Assuming Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK) symbols for PRS, which aligns with 5G NR
[
23
]
, we have
\small\boldsymbol{X}_{b}\boldsymbol{X}_{b}^{H}=\boldsymbol{X}_{b}^{H}\boldsymbol{X}_{b}=\boldsymbol{I}_{N}
. Similarly, the DFT matrix
\small\boldsymbol{F}_{L}
satisfies
\small\boldsymbol{F}_{L}^{H}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}=\boldsymbol{I}_{L}
. Moreover, for large
\small N
, we can approximate
\small\boldsymbol{F}_{L}\boldsymbol{F}_{L}^{H}
as
\small\mathbf{0}_{N}
, a zero matrix of size
\small N\times N
. Based on these results, we observe that
\small\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{b}
in (
33
) approaches
\small\boldsymbol{I}_{N}
. Using these simplifications, we present below the CRLB for TOA under AWGN as a corollary.

Corollary 2.1

(CRLB of TOA for AWGN)
.

The variance of the TOA estimate between the target UE and anchor
\small b
under the AWGN channel is lower-bounded as:

\small\displaystyle{\rm var}(\hat{\tau}_{d,b})

\small\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{I}_{e,\theta_{\hat{\tau}}^{b}}^{-1},

(34)

\small\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{e,\theta_{\hat{\tau}}^{b}}

\small\displaystyle=2\gamma_{b}\boldsymbol{D}^{2}=8\pi\gamma_{b}\nabla f^{2}\sum_{n=-\frac{N}{2}}^{\frac{N}{2}-1}n^{2}.

(35)

Having obtained the CRLB for the variance of the TOA error and denoting the vector parameter for TDOA estimation as
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\tilde{\tau}}
, the FIM for TDOA is expressed as

\small\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{\tilde{\tau}}}=\big(\mathbf{H}_{\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\tilde{\tau}}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{\hat{\tau}}}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\tilde{\tau}}\big)^{-1},

(36)

where
\small\mathbf{H}_{\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\tilde{\tau}}
is a transformation matrix from TOA to TDOA.

Then, the FIM for position parameter
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}
using TOA-based localization (denoted by
\small\mathbb{I}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}}
) and TDOA-based localization (denoted by
\small\mathbb{I}_{\tilde{\theta}_{p}}
) can be obtained as follows

\small\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}}

\small\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{\hat{\tau}}}\mathbf{H}_{\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}},\,\mathbb{I}_{\tilde{\theta}_{p}}=\mathbf{H}_{\tilde{\tau}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}^{T}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{\tilde{\tau}}}\mathbf{H}_{\tilde{\tau}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}},

(37)

where
\small\mathbf{H}_{\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}
and
\small\mathbf{H}_{\tilde{\tau}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{p}}
are the transformation matrices from TOA and TDOA measurements to the position parameter, respectively. Denoting position estimate by
\small\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
for TOA-based localization and
\small\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}
for TDOA, the variance of the position estimate, also known as position error bound (PEB), is then given by

\small\displaystyle{\rm var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p})

\small\displaystyle\geq\text{trace}(\mathbb{I}^{-1}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}}),\,{\rm var}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p})\geq\text{trace}(\mathbb{I}^{-1}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{p}}).

(38)

IV-B

NLOS Bias Modeling

To address the limitations of traditional NLOS bias modeling, which relies on Gamma-distribution-based methods, we propose a novel approach for modeling NLOS bias. As part of this, we first define TOA measurements under AWGN and multipath propagation scenarios; using these, we will provide a more realistic representation of NLOS bias.

Without loss of generality, the TOA measurement between an anchor and a target UE under the AWGN channel is expressed as

\small\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{d}=\tau_{d}+v,

(39)

where
\small\tau_{d}
represents the true range between the anchor and the target UE,
\small v
is AWGN with zero mean and variance specified in (
34
), and
\small\hat{\tau}_{d}
is the measured range. The variance of
\small\hat{\tau}_{d}
, denoted as
\small\sigma_{n}^{2}
, is equivalent to the variance of
\small v
. Thus,

\small\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}={\rm var}(v)\geq\frac{1}{8\pi\gamma\nabla f^{2}\sum_{n=-\frac{N}{2}}^{\frac{N}{2}-1}n^{2}},

(40)

where
\small\gamma
denotes the SNR of the considered link. In Monte Carlo simulations, TOA errors for AWGN channels can be modeled by adding AWGN noise with variance
\small\sigma_{n}^{2}
to the true range as follows

\small\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{d}=\tau_{d}+u,\hskip 9.24994ptu\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2}).

(41)

Similarly, TOA measurements between an anchor and a target UE under multipath propagation are defined as

\small\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{d}=\tau_{d}+b+v,

(42)

where
\small b
is the NLOS bias induced by multipath propagation with non-zero mean and finite variance. The measured range,
\small\tilde{\tau}_{d}
, incorporates the NLOS bias and AWGN, with variance specified in (
31
). The variance of
\small\tilde{\tau}_{d}
, denoted as
\small\sigma_{c}^{2}
, can be expressed as

\small\displaystyle\sigma_{c}^{2}={\rm var}(b+v)={\rm var}(b)+{\rm var}(v)=\sigma_{b}^{2}+{\rm var}(\hat{\tau}_{d}),

(43)

where
\small\sigma_{b}^{2}
is the variance of
\small b
. Using this, we model NLOS bias offset
\small\sigma_{b}
as

\small\displaystyle\sigma_{b}=\sqrt{{\rm var}(\tilde{\tau}_{d})-{\rm var}(\hat{\tau}_{d})}.

(44)

Note that
\small\sigma_{b}
differs for each anchor-UE link. To model TOA errors for Monte Carlo simulations in the presence of multipath, NLOS bias offset
\small\sigma_{b}
and AWGN are added to the true range with the variance
\small\sigma_{c}^{2}
as follows

\small\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}_{d}=\tau_{d}+\sigma_{b}+u,\hskip 9.24994ptu\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{c}^{2}).

(45)

By explicitly linking the NLOS bias offset to the SNR, multipath effects, and physical layer characteristics, our proposed approach provides a more realistic and comprehensive representation of the NLOS bias. The proposed approach bridges the gap between theoretical analyses and practical channel conditions, ensuring improved accuracy in modeling and simulation. To further illustrate NLOS bias modeling, we assess the TOA error performance with an anchor-UE drop presented in Fig.
2
. Fig.
1

Fig.

This superior performance is attributed to the amplified errors in TOA measurements at low SNRs, which are further exacerbated due to multipath propagation, where the NLOS bias offset depends on SNR and the underlying propagation conditions. To better understand this phenomenon, consider TOA estimation under multipath propagation for both high and low SNR scenarios. In high SNR conditions, the TOA estimation process is generally able to lock onto the first path or a path close to the first one, as these paths are distinct and detectable.
Conversely, under low SNR conditions, the first-arriving path may become insignificant or undetectable–not because only the first path is degraded, but because in multipath-rich environments (e.g., indoor settings), the first path is often weaker than subsequent reflected paths. Due to rich scattering, it may carry less energy and is more likely to fall below the detection threshold used in practical TOA estimation algorithms, such as correlation-based methods. In contrast, later-arriving stronger multipath components may still exceed the threshold and be detected, thereby biasing the TOA estimate toward delayed paths.

Although our evaluation framework does not explicitly estimate TOA, the TOA error modeling in (
41
) and (
45
) captures these effects by accounting for the prevailing SNR and propagation characteristics. Consequently, when TOA errors are significant–primarily due to a pronounced NLOS bias offset–the performance of WNLS deteriorates. In contrast, as described in Section
II-A
, the proposed approach incorporates a two-stage refinement process, which mitigates the impact of the NLOS bias offset by accurately estimating the range measurements associated with the reference anchor range and iteratively updating the UE position estimate and vice versa. As a result, the proposed approach ensures enhanced positioning even under low SNR conditions. Remarkably, even in high GDOP or low SNR scenarios, the proposed method achieves performance levels very close to the CRLB.
Notably, the absolute magnitude of positioning errors is influenced by the system parameters used for evaluation, such as carrier frequency and bandwidth. While our evaluation adopts a representative 5G NR-compliant configuration (3.5 GHz carrier frequency with 5 MHz bandwidth) for demonstration purposes, the proposed TS-WPM is agnostic to these parameters and would yield significantly improved accuracy under wider bandwidths (e.g., 20-100 MHz) and lower carrier frequencies, as supported in 5G NR and beyond.

Computational Complexity

Having established the superior positioning performance of TS-WPM, we now analyze the computational complexity of the proposed TS-WPM and compare it with state-of-the-art algorithms, including NLS, WNLS, and IPPM. As outlined in Algorithm
1
, each iteration of TS-WPM involves updating the UE location estimate, computing residuals, and refining the reference anchor range estimate across all
\small B
anchors (see steps
1
,
1
, and
1
). Each update step comprises basic vector norms and arithmetic operations, resulting in a per-iteration complexity of
\small O(B\cdot D)
. Consequently, the overall complexity of TS-WPM is
\small O(N\cdot B\cdot D)
, where
\small N
represents the number of iterations required for convergence and
\small D
is the dimension of UE location coordinates (2 in our case). Similarly, the computational complexity of IPPM is also
\small O(N\cdot B\cdot D)

[
12
,
11
]
, which is identical to that of TS-WPM. In contrast, the computational complexity of NLS and WNLS is significantly higher due to additional matrix inversion and multiplication operations. Specifically, both methods exhibit a complexity of
\small O(N\cdot B\cdot D^{4})

[
3
]
, where
\small D
denotes the number of spatial dimensions of the UE coordinates. NLS and WNLS typically require fewer iterations than IPPM and TS-WPM, with TS-WPM necessitating additional iterations due to its two-stage refinement process. Our evaluations indicate that, on average, NLS and WNLS converge in 16-17 iterations, while IPPM and TS-WPM require more than 20 iterations. Despite its higher iteration count, TS-WPM remains computationally efficient compared to NLS and WNLS, as it avoids costly matrix inversions and minimizes the number of matrix multiplications.

Practical Considerations and Generalizability.
While our evaluation framework assumes the availability of TOA/TDOA measurements, we acknowledge that in real-world deployments, TOA measurements are often subject to imperfections such as multipath-induced bias (NLOS bias), thermal noise, anchor synchronization errors, and residual UE clock offsets. To address these, our framework explicitly incorporates the effects of thermal noise and multipath through 3GPP-compliant CRLB formulations and the associated TOA error models, including a novel NLOS bias model that accounts for radio propagation characteristics and SNR, as described in Section
IV
. Moreover, the TDOA formulation inherently eliminates UE clock offset errors, assuming that the TOA measurements are acquired within a narrow measurement window (e.g., a few ms), during which the UE clock remains stable. While the current model assumes perfect synchronization among anchors, this is a valid assumption in many practical scenarios, such as 5G gNBs, Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)-synchronized UAV-mounted gNBs, or Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites equipped with GNSS receivers. Importantly, our framework is flexible enough to allow seamless integration of practical TOA estimation methods (e.g., correlation-based, Multiple Signal Classification (MUSIC), and Estimation of Signal Parameters via Rotational Invariant Techniques (ESPRIT))–as well as clock offset modeling and compensation mechanisms.

VI
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we proposed a novel low-complexity TS-WPM for TDOA-based localization and extended it to support cooperative localization. The proposed method employs a two-stage refinement process that iteratively updates both the range estimate associated with the reference anchor and the UE location estimate, thereby significantly enhancing positioning accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. This refinement process proves particularly effective in challenging scenarios such as high GDOP and low SNR. Furthermore, we provided an analytical derivation establishing the conditions under which TS-WPM is asymptotically optimal and demonstrating its performance advantages over WNLS. To rigorously evaluate the proposed method, we developed a comprehensive 3GPP-compliant analytical framework that integrates physical layer and fading characteristics, using the CRLB as a principal tool. Additionally, we proposed a novel NLOS bias modeling approach that explicitly accounts for underlying propagation conditions and SNR, offering a more accurate representation of the bias induced by multipath. Through extensive Monte Carlo simulations, we demonstrated that the proposed TS-WPM consistently outperforms state-of-the-art techniques such as IPPM and NLS. Our results illustrated that, under non-ideal conditions, TS-WPM surpasses WNLS, achieving performance close to the CRLB even in scenarios with high GDOP, low SNR, and multipath propagation. Furthermore, we highlighted the effectiveness of the proposed approach when incorporating cooperative localization, enabling accurate positioning even when an insufficient number of anchors (such as 2) are visible. Finally, we performed a computational complexity analysis comparing TS-WPM with IPPM, NLS, and WNLS, demonstrating that the proposed approach exhibits complexity identical to IPPM and lower complexity than NLS and WNLS, as it avoids complex operations like matrix inversion and minimizes matrix multiplications. Overall, the proposed TS-WPM presents a balanced trade-off between positioning accuracy and computational complexity, making it a promising candidate for real-time localization applications across diverse environments.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR).
Based on the received signal model in (29), the SNR between anchor
\small b
and the target UE, denoted by
\small\gamma_{b}
, is defined as

\small\displaystyle\gamma_{b}=\frac{P_{b}}{\sigma^{2}+NF_{l}},

(46)

where
\small P_{b}
represents the received signal power,
\small\sigma^{2}
is variance of AWGN, and
\small NF_{l}
denotes the noise figure in linear scale. To accurately compute
\small P_{b}
and, consequently,
\small\gamma_{b}
, we adopt the dual-stripe channel model recommended in
[
24
]
. This model partitions the environment into line-of-sight (LOS) and NLOS regions, referred to as “stripes”. The stripe selection depends on the scenario–LOS stripes are used for outdoor UEs, while NLOS stripes are used for indoor UEs. Since our study assumes all UEs are located indoors, the NLOS stripe is employed to model large-scale fading.

The large-scale fading model accounts for distance-dependent path loss, shadowing, and penetration losses. The total path loss (in dB) is calculated as

\small\displaystyle PL

\small\displaystyle=\max(15.3+37.6\log_{10}R,\,38.46+20\log_{10}R)

\small\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt0.7d_{2D,\text{indoor}}+18.3\,n^{\left(\frac{n+2}{n+1}-0.46\right)}+L_{iw}+L_{ow},

(47)

where
\small R
is the 3D distance between the anchor and the UE,
\small d_{2D,\text{indoor}}
is the horizontal indoor distance in meters, and
\small n
is the number of floors penetrated, capturing the impact of buildings with multiple floors.
\small L_{iw}
denotes the loss from internal walls (typically 5 dB per wall), and
\small L_{ow}
accounts for penetration through external walls (typically 20 dB per wall).

Given the path loss, the SNR in dB is expressed as

\small\displaystyle\gamma=P_{t}-PL-SF-N_{0}-NF,

(48)

where
\small P_{t}
is the transmit power in dBm (assumed to be 23 dBm, except for the low SNR case where it is reduced to 13 dBm),
\small PL
is the path loss in dB as defined above,
\small SF
is the shadow fading loss in dB,
\small NF
is the noise figure in dB, and
\small N_{0}
is the thermal noise power in dBm computed as

\small\displaystyle N_{0}=-174+10\log_{10}(B),

(49)

assuming a room temperature of
\small T=290
K and a system bandwidth
\small B
in Hz. The SNR computed using (
48
) is used throughout our evaluation to quantify signal quality for each anchor-UE link.

Geometric Dilution of Precision (GDOP).
GDOP is a fundamental metric that quantifies the impact of anchor geometry on the accuracy of location estimation. Even when range measurements are accurate, unfavorable anchor distribution can amplify positioning errors due to high GDOP. Conversely, well-distributed anchors result in low GDOP and improved localization performance. To formalize this, consider a network of anchors where
\small\mathbf{r}
denotes the true range measurements,
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
is the position parameter comprising target UE coordinates, and
\small\mathbf{H}
is the corresponding Jacobian matrix relating the measurements to the UE location. The measurement model can be expressed as

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\theta}.

(50)

The GDOP is then defined by

\small\displaystyle{\rm GDOP}=\sqrt{{\rm trace}\left(\left(\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{H}\right)^{-1}\right)},

(51)

where
\small(\cdot)^{T}
denotes matrix transpose. In GPS settings
[
38
]
, the matrix
\small(\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{H})^{-1}
takes the form

\small\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{H}\right)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}D_{11}&D_{12}&D_{13}&D_{14}\\
D_{21}&D_{22}&D_{23}&D_{24}\\
D_{31}&D_{32}&D_{33}&D_{34}\\
D_{41}&D_{42}&D_{43}&D_{44}\end{bmatrix}.

(52)

from which various DOP metrics, including position DOP (PDOP), horizontal DOP (HDOP), vertical DOP (VDOP), and time DOP (TDOP) are derived.

\small\displaystyle{\rm GDOP}

\small\displaystyle=\sqrt{D_{11}+D_{22}+D_{33}+D_{44}},

\small\displaystyle{\rm PDOP}

\small\displaystyle=\sqrt{D_{11}+D_{22}+D_{33}},

\small\displaystyle{\rm HDOP}

\small\displaystyle=\sqrt{D_{11}+D_{22}},{\rm VDOP}=\sqrt{D_{33}},\,{\rm TDOP}=\sqrt{D_{44}}.

Here, PDOP quantifies the impact of anchor geometry on 3D positioning accuracy, HDOP on horizontal positioning accuracy, VDOP on vertical accuracy, and TDOP on the estimation of UE clock offset. GDOP reflects the overall geometric impact on all these components combined. For simplified scenarios with equal SNRs across anchors, the positioning error
\small P_{\text{err}}
can be approximated as a function of PDOP and range error
\small r_{\text{err}}
as

\small\displaystyle P_{\text{err}}={\rm PDOP}\times r_{\text{err}}.

(53)

In this article, we focus on 2D localization assuming no UE clock offset errors (or assuming TDOA inherently cancels out UE clock offset errors); hence, HDOP is of primary interest and is treated equivalently to GDOP and PDOP in our evaluation. \small r_{1}
as the only unknown, the log-likelihood function for the first stage is formulated as

\small\displaystyle L_{1}(r_{1})=-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{m}(r_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\Bigr)^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\Bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{m}(r_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\Bigr).

(63)

Alternatively, treating both
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\small r_{1}
as unknowns, we formulate the log-likelihood function for the second stage estimation as

\small\displaystyle L_{2}(r_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{m}(r_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta})\Bigr)^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\Bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{m}(r_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta})\Bigr).

(64)

Then, we obtain the following matrices

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{1}

\small\displaystyle=-E\Biggl\{\frac{\partial^{2}L_{1}}{\partial r_{1}^{2}}\Biggr\},\,\mathbf{R}_{2}=-E\Biggl\{\frac{\partial^{2}L_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}\,\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}}\Biggr\}\,,

(65)

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{3}

\small\displaystyle=-E\Biggl\{\frac{\partial^{2}L_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}\,\partial r_{1}}\Biggr\},\,\mathbf{R}_{4}=E\Biggl\{\frac{\partial L_{1}}{\partial r_{1}}\frac{\partial L_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}}\Biggr\}.

(66)

A standard derivation yields

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{1}

\small\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{R}_{2}=\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\,,

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{3}

\small\displaystyle=-\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{R}_{4}=-\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{J}.

where
\small\mathbf{u}
is a vector of
\small B-1
ones and
\small\mathbf{J}
is the Jacobian transformation of
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
with respect to
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Note that
\small\mathbf{R}_{3}=\mathbf{R}_{4}
. Then, according to
[
30
]
, it can be shown that the error covariance of the second stage estimate, denoted by
\small\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\rm MLE}
, is given by

\small\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\rm MLE}=\mathbf{R}_{2}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}.

(67)

where
\small\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}
is a correction term introduced due to the first-stage estimate and is given by

\small\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{C}=\mathbf{R}_{2}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{R}_{3}^{T}\mathbf{R}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{3}-\mathbf{R}_{4}^{T}\mathbf{R}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{3}-\mathbf{R}_{3}^{T}\mathbf{R}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{4}\right)\mathbf{R}_{2}^{-1}.

(68)

Using
\small\mathbf{R}_{3}=\mathbf{R}_{4}=-\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{u}
, one can show that the expression in (
67
) simplifies to

\small\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\rm MLE}=\left(\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{J}-\frac{\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{J}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{u}}\right)^{-1}.

(69)

As derived earlier, the TS-WPM estimator yields an error covariance (as specified in (
56
))

\small\displaystyle{\rm cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{tswpm}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{\left({\rm trace}(\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1})\right)^{2}}\left(\mathbf{H}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{2}\right).

(70)

TS-WPM approaches MLE, i.e.,
\small{\rm cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{tswpm}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})
becomes equal to
\small\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\rm MLE}
when the cross-information between
\small r_{1}
and
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
vanishes, i.e.,
\small\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{0}
. This condition is met when the measurement noises are uncorrelated. This situation occurs when the covariance matrix
\small\mathbf{C}_{1}
reduces to
\small\mathbf{C}_{2}
, which occurs when the reference anchor does not introduce additional error dependencies–in practice when its SNR approaches infinity (i.e.,
\small\sigma_{1}^{2}\rightarrow 0
). In other words, in a high SNR regime, the proposed novel TS-WPM is
asymptotically optimal
.

-D

Proof of Lemma
2

Let
\small\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k}
represent the iterative update of
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
at iteration
\small k
. As described in
[
3
,
2
]
, the WNLS update is expressed as

\small\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k-1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k-1}\big(\mathbf{H}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k-1})\big)^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k-1})\big),

(71)

where
\small\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k-1}=\big((\mathbf{H}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k-1}))^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k-1})\big)^{-1}
, and
\small\mathbf{W}_{1}
is defined as
\small\mathbf{W}_{1}=\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}
, with
\small\mathbf{C}_{1}
given in (
10
). The term
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k-1})
represents a vector of TDOA estimates evaluated at
\small\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{k-1}
as given in (
4
).

Assuming convergence, the final WNLS estimate can be approximated as

\small\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{wnls}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{H}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big),

(72)

where
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
represents the true position parameter,
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
\small\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
with respect to
\small\boldsymbol{\theta}
, equal to
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}
as specified in (
11
), and
\small\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left((\mathbf{H}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{-1}
. In the following derivation, we will use
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}
instead of
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

The MSE associated with the estimate
\small\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{wnls}}
quantifies the positioning error of WNLS. To derive the MSE, we first determine the covariance of
\small(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{wnls}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})
as follows

\small\displaystyle{\rm cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{wnls}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}{\rm cov}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T},

(73)

where
\small{\rm cov}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
is essentially
\small\mathbf{C}_{1}
as defined in (
10
). Substituting
\small\mathbf{C}_{1}
into (
73
) yields

\small\displaystyle{\rm cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{wnls}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{C}_{1}\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}.

(74)

Further, substituting
\small\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\small\mathbf{W}_{1}
into (
74
) and simplifying leads to

\small\displaystyle{\rm cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{wnls}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)^{-1}.

(75)

Consequently, the MSE associated with the WNLS estimate is

\small\displaystyle{\rm MSE}_{\mathrm{wnls}}={\rm trace}\left(\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)^{-1}\right).

(76)

The expression in (
76
) can be expressed in terms of eigenvalues as

\small\displaystyle{\rm MSE}_{\mathrm{wnls}}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)},

(77)

where
\small\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}
, and
\small K
indicates the number of non-zero eigenvalues. For 2D estimation, the maximum value of
\small K
is 2.

-E

Proof of Proposition
1

Considering the MSE of WNLS given in (
77
), in high GDOP scenarios, for 2D estimation, one eigenvalue of
\small\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}
is quite significant, while the other remains relatively insignificant. Consequently, the MSE in (
77
) can be approximated as

\small\displaystyle{\rm MSE}_{\mathrm{wnls}}\approx\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)}.

(78)

Similarly, we approximate the MSE of TS-WPM given in (
59
) in high GDOP scenarios. To facilitate this, we first simplify the second term in (
18
). We denote the expression
\small\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\left(\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}
in (
18
) as
\small\mathbf{C_{v}}
and evaluate it to obtain

\small\displaystyle\mathbf{C_{v}}=\left(\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{\sigma_{b}^{2}}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)^{2}&\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{B}^{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}}&\frac{1}{\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}}&\cdots&\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{B}^{2}}\right)^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(79)

In high GDOP scenarios, when the anchors are colocated, we observe similar SNR across all the anchors and the target UE links, resulting in identical
\small\sigma_{b}^{2}
values for all the anchors. This behavior causes
\small\mathbf{C_{v}}
to have identical entries. Consequently, we can verify that
\small{\rm trace}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{C_{v}}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)
approaches 0. Using this result, we can show that in high GDOP scenarios,

\small\displaystyle{\rm trace}\left(\mathbf{F}_{2,[x,y]}\right)\approx{\rm trace}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right).

(80)

By using this result along with
\small{\rm trace}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)={\rm trace}\left(\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\right)
, the MSE in (
58
) can be approximated for high GDOP scenarios as

\small\displaystyle{\rm MSE}_{\mathrm{tswpm}}\approx\frac{1}{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{\sigma_{b}^{2}}}.

(81)

This result is significant, as it shows that even under high GDOP conditions, the MSE of the proposed two-stage WPM remains directly proportional to the variance of pseudorange measurements, which is not commonly observed in non-ideal settings
[
3
]
. By comparing (
78
) and (
81
), we observe that under high GDOP,
\small\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbf{H}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)
becomes insignificant, resulting in high MSE for the WNLS case. In contrast, the MSE with the novel TS-WPM in (
81
) trends similarly to low GDOP or favorable conditions, maintaining robust performance and reinforcing its effectiveness in non-ideal situations.
