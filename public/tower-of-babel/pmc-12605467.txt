Diversity of Yeast and Drosophila Species Associated with Grape Sour Rot in China
Authors: Jie Han, Qiandong Fang, Yifan Hao, Xiaoqing Huang, Fanfang Kong, Haoyu Chen, Yongqiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sour rot is a complex disease of grapes, primarily caused by the synergistic effects of yeast and Drosophila species, leading to fruit decay, poor quality, and significant economic losses. The lack of information on species distribution and diversity of these organisms in China limits the efficacy of control strategies. In this study, we sequenced the 26S rRNA D1/D2 region in yeasts and the COI region in Drosophila. Nine yeast genera were identified. Hanseniaspora spp. (45.76% of total) were the most abundant, followed by Starmerella spp. (23.62%) and Saccharomyces spp. (17.34%). At the species level, 14 yeast species were identified. Starmerella bacillaris (23.62%), Saccharomyces cerevisiae (17.34%), H. opuntiae (17.34%), and H. uvarum (15.13%) predominated. Of the five species of Drosophila present, Drosophila melanogaster (63.16%) was the dominant species. This study is the first to characterize the diversity of yeasts and Drosophila across major grape-producing regions in China. The findings provide a scientific foundation for elucidating the key drivers of sour rot and designing targeted control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02616-y.

Introduction

Grape sour rot is a complex, multifactorial disease [1–3] that is prevalent in the major grape-producing regions of China. It primarily emerges when the grapes begin to change color, causing fruit decay, juice leakage [4], and a reduction in the sugar-to-acid ratio and overall flavor quality of the fruit [5, 6]. This disease is mainly driven by a synergistic interaction between yeasts and Drosophila species, which form a unique ecological relationship. The yeasts commonly associated with grape sour rot include Hanseniaspora spp., Saccharomyces spp., Pichia spp., Issatchenkia spp., and Candida spp. [7–9].

Yeast species differ significantly in their pathogenicity and virulence on grape. H. uvarum and H. vineae, the dominant yeasts in numerous grape-producing regions [10], have strong acid-producing capabilities and pectinolytic activity, enabling them to degrade sugars in grape juice and produce metabolites such as ethanol and acetic acid [5], which turns the fruit rancid. Meanwhile, S. cerevisiae, Pichia kudriavzevii, and others also participate in enzymatic hydrolysis and alcoholic fermentation [11, 12]. However, interactions between microorganisms are complex, and the invasion of one microbial species can disrupt the original ecological balance, potentially facilitating invasion by others, including pathogenic bacteria [13]. The presence of yeast may also create favorable conditions for invasion by other pathogens, thereby exacerbating disease development [13, 14].

Drosophila species (fruit flies) are a key disseminator in the ecological chain. Among Drosophila species, D. melanogaster and D. suzukii are the most widely distributed and harmful species [14–16]. Other common species include D. immigrans, D. simulans, D. mojavensis, D. arizonensis, D. ritae, and D. starmeri [17–19]. Fruit flies damage grapes primarily by disseminating yeasts carried on their body and in their digestive tract through contact and feeding [20, 21] or through the enzymatic activity of larvae, promoting pulp degradation [22]. Based on the above contents, the spread of Drosophila and the pathogenicity of yeast form a close synergistic cycle. This cycle is the key driving factor for the large-scale outbreak of grape sour rot.

The grape-producing regions in China span a vast area with diverse climates and cultivation practices that influence dynamic variations in grape-associated microbial communities [10, 23]. In addition, differences in the physicochemical properties of various grape varieties also impact the composition of the microbial communities [24]. Meanwhile, in current research, investigations into the species, geographic distribution, and Drosophila vectors of pathogenic yeasts remain relatively limited. This gap has significantly hindered the development and implementation of effective prevention and control strategies. To address these challenges, we utilized DNA barcode technology, sequenced the 26S rRNA D1/D2 region for yeasts and the COI gene for Drosophila, to identify yeast species associated with sour rot in grape samples and to determine Drosophila species present in major grape-producing regions of China. This approach enabled a comprehensive analysis of their biodiversity. Our findings provide insights that should facilitate more effective measures against grape sour rot and sustainable development of China’s grape industry.

Materials and Methods

Sampling

Yeasts and Drosophila were isolated from grape fruit samples from 10 grape varieties across four major grape-producing regions in China, covering 12 provinces in Central China and East China (CCEC), North China and Bohai Bay area (NCBB), Southwest China (SC), and Northwest China and Loess Plateau (NCLP). Detailed collection information is provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

The yeasts and Drosophila were collected from the same sample bunch of grapes with sour rot. When we found a grape bunch with symptoms of sour rot, we first removed Drosophila larvae and adults in and around the fruit. Then the remaining grape samples were used to isolate yeasts.

Isolation and Purification of Yeasts

The 5–6 grapes with sour rot were put into the YEPD broth (peptone 20 g/L, yeast extract 10 g/L, glucose 20 g/L, water to 1 L) (Beijing Kulaibo Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and shaken on a rotary shaker at 150 rpm for 0.5 h. The culture was then serially diluted with 0.90% sterile NaCl solution to 10⁻1, 10⁻2, 10⁻3 concentrations, and 20 µL was spread onto YEPD agar (20 g/L agar) supplemented with 0.1 g/L penicillin. Three replicate plates were prepared for each dilution. Plates were incubated at 28 ℃ for 3–5 days. Fresh YEPD agar plates were then streaked with morphologically distinct yeast colonies to obtain pure isolates. The purified yeast colonies were mixed in separate solutions of 30% sterilized glycerol at a 1:1 volume ratio and stored at –70 °C.

Molecular Identification of Yeast Strains

Yeast cells were collected from log-phase cultures in YEPD broth, washed with sterile water, and centrifuged to obtain a purified cell pellet. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the rapid extraction kit of fungal genomic DNA (Omega Bio-Tek, New Orleans, LA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The internal transcribed spacer region (D1/D2) of the 26S rRNA was amplified with NL1/NL4 primers (NL1: 5′–GCATATCAATAAGCGGAGGAAAAG–3′. NL4: 5′–GGTCCGTGTTTCAAGACGG–3′) (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) [25].

A 50.0-µL reaction mixture containing 25.0 µL 1.1 × T3 Super PCR Mix (Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), 1.0 µL genomic DNA, 1.5 µL of each primer, and 21.0 µL of ddH2O was used for the PCR described by Morgan et al. [26] using a SimpliAmp thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The cycling conditions were initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 ℃ for 20 s, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 1 min; final extension at 72 ℃ for 7 min. The PCR products were separated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and sequenced (Sangon Biotech).

Identification of Drosophila Species

Collected Drosophila larvae, pupae and adults were immersed completely in 75% v/v ethanol for 30 s, then in 1% v/v NaClO for 1 min, rinsed with sterile water for three times, then air-dried on sterile filter paper. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijng, China) and the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoPhotometer N50 spectrophotometer (Implen, Munich, Germany). DNA samples with A260/A280 ratios of 1.8–2.0 and A260/A230 > 1.8 were used for PCR at 20–50 ng/μL final concentration. Primers were ZBJ–ArtF1c (5′–AGATATTGGAACWTTATATTT TATTTTTGG–3′)/ZBJ–ArtR2c (5′–TACTAATCAATTTCCAAATCCTCC–3′) (Sangon Biotech). The PCR mixture and thermal cycling parameters for Drosophila samples were the same as those used for yeasts.

Statistical Analyses

The sequencing data of yeasts and Drosophila were first collated and organized using Excel. Based on the collection regions and grape varieties, the proportions of different yeast samples and Drosophila individuals in their respective total sample populations were calculated separately to facilitate subsequent analyses. Visual analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Normality of data distribution was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and non-normal data were log-transformed before analysis to meet parametric test assumptions. Yeast and Drosophila species in their respective sample populations were tested for a correlation with Pearson’s correlation analysis using Origin 2021 (Origin 2021, Northampton, Massachusetts, USA).

Phylogenetic Analysis

All sequences were initially aligned using NCBI BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and sequences with similarity less than 98% were filtered out. The remaining sequences were aligned and trimmed using BioEdit software (Ibis Biosciences, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Haplotypes were extracted with DnaSP v5 software (Universitat de Barcelona, Barcelona, Spain). The obtained haplotype sequences and reference sequences were used to construct a phylogenetic tree in MEGA software 7.0 (University Park, PA, USA) using the neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Penicillium oxalicum was used as the outgroup.

Results

Analysis of Genetic Diversity of Yeast Isolates

The 26S rRNA D1/D2 sequences of 271 yeast isolates from the four grape regions in China were aligned by BioEdit, and the haplotypes of all yeast isolates were extracted and analyzed by DnaSP v5 after filtering and alignment. The results showed that 271 samples were distributed across 47 haplotypes. The nucleotide diversity (π) was 0.17003, the average nucleotide difference (k) was 73.45270, and the haplotype diversity (Hd) was 0.8804. Among the identified haplotypes, Hap35 was the dominant type, represented by 59 yeast DNA sequences. Hap1, Hap5 and Hap13 were next most represented with 44, 40, and 40 sequences, respectively. In contrast, 29 haplotypes were classified as singletons, each being represented by only one yeast DNA sequence.

The phylogenetic tree based on the 26S rRNA D1/D2 gene and constructed with the neighbor-joining method is presented in Fig. 1. Regional and Host-associated Diversity of Isolated Yeasts

At the genus level, nine yeast genera were identified from grape samples with sour rot across China's four major grape-producing regions (Fig. 2A). Among them, Hanseniaspora (45.76% of total) had the highest relative abundance, followed by Starmerella (23.62%) and Saccharomyces (17.34%). The remaining six genera, including Pichia spp., each accounted for less than 10% of the total relative abundance. At the species level, 14 yeast species were identified (Fig. 2B). S. bacillaris (23.62%), S. cerevisiae (17.34%), H. opuntiae (17.34%) and H. uvarum (15.13%) were the dominant yeast species. In the test for a correlation between grape varieties and associated yeast communities, yeast species from 10 different grape varieties were analyzed (Fig. 3B). Species Composition and Regional Distrubution of Drosophila

Sequencing of the mitochondrial COI region from 95 Drosophila individuals collected in the four grape-producing regions revealed five distinct species (Fig. 4A). D. melanogaster was the most dominant species (63.16% of total samples), followed by D. simulans (28.42%). Each of the other three species represented less than 5% of the total Drosophila population. Only D. melanogaster was common to all four regions (Fig. 4B), and it was the dominant species in each region, with relative abundance of 65.00% in CCEC, 61.29% in NCBB, 66.67% in SC and 61.11% in NCLP (Fig. 4C–F). The abundance of yeast and Drosophila species were tested for a correlation with the different grape-producing regions and grape varieties using Pearson’s correlation analysis in Origin software, and a heatmap was generated (Fig. 5A–B) to reveal any co-occurrence patterns of each group in the grape regions. Among the yeast species, dark red blocks dominated, indicating widespread significant positive correlations among members of the same group, and a synergism among Hanseniaspora spp. was the most prominent. In contrast, compared with other yeasts, P. kudriavzevii, P. manshurica, and R. mucilaginosa had mostly negative correlations. Among the Drosophila species, dark red blocks also prevailed. Discussion

Hanseniaspora was identified as the dominant yeast genus among 271 grape samples collected from the four grape-producing regions in China (Fig. 2A), consistent with previous studies [27, 28]. Hanseniaspora species are frequently reported as the predominant yeast on mature and intact grape berries. Species such as H. opuntiae and H. uvarum play a crucial role in the early stages of grape fermentation, contributing enzymes and aromatic compounds that enhance wine flavor [29–31]. Starmerella spp. and Saccharomyces spp., which followed Hanseniaspora spp. in abundance, also participate in fermentation. Notably, S. bacillaris is fructophilic, and S. cerevisiae is glucophilic, allowing them to coexist and complement each other during fermentation [32]. In addition, Rossouw et al. [33] reported that P. kudriavzevii is capable of fermentation, although its growth and persistence are lower than those of H. opuntiae and H. uvarum.

Grape-associated microorganisms are shaped by interactions among vineyard geography, climate, soil characteristics, and cultivation practices within the vineyard ecosystem [34–36]. When yeasts have adapted to the local environment over a long period, region-specific dominant communities become established [37]. Drumonde-Neves et al. [35] reported significant differences in yeast community composition among vineyards on five islands in the Azores. Barata et al. [9] showed that in warm and humid grape-producing regions, H. uvarum and S. cerevisiae tend to dominate, whereas in hot and dry regions, yeasts such as Metschnikowia pulcherrima are more prevalent [38]. Here, we identified 11 yeast species in CCEC and 10 in NCBB, but only two in SC and three in NCLP (Fig. 3A). The dominant yeast species were similar to those in previous studies.

Yeast biodiversity has been shown to be strongly influenced by grapevine cultivar [24], with peel thickness and sugar content acting as key determinants. In the Rhine region of Germany, ‘Riesling’ grapes, characterized by high sugar content and low acidity, were predominantly associated with M. pulcherrima and S. bacilliaris, contributing to a high incidence of sour rot [39]. In contrast, ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ grapes have thick skins, which hinder yeast proliferation and reduce sour rot incidence. In this cultivar, S. cerevisiae was identified as the dominant yeast. For ‘Pinot Noir’ with a thin peel, H. uvarum and Candida krusei were the dominant species, and a higher incidence of sour rot was observed [40]. These results indicate that there is a degree of correlation between grape varieties and the colonizing yeast species. Environmental factors (e.g., humidity, temperature) and grape variety characteristics can jointly affect the type of yeast and the occurrence of acid rot. In Australia, ‘Shiraz’ grapes are particularly prone to cracking at high temperatures, which promotes rapid proliferation of dominant yeasts such as H. uvarum and S. cerevisiae [41]. In this study, different grape varieties (that differed in characteristics such as skin thickness, sugar content) and production areas (that differed in climate) had significant effects on the co-occurrences of different yeast and Drosophila species. For example, compared with characteristics of ‘Ruby Seedless’, the skin of ‘Summer Black’ fruit is thin, the sugar content is high, and it is more susceptible to yeast infection, which enables H. opuntiae, S. cerevisiae and S. bacilliaris to multiply prolifically, resulting in sour rot and hence the positive correlation between ‘Summer Black’ and these three species (Fig. 3B, 5A–B). Although the skin of ‘Kyoho’ is thick, its sugar content is high, and sour rot is more likely in the CCEC and NCBB areas where humidity is higher than in NCLP; thus, it also has a positive correlation (Fig. 3B, 5A–B) This indicates that the biological characteristics of grape varieties (such as skin thickness and sugar content) affect the degree of their association with specific yeast species.

D. melanogaster is a key inducing factor and vector of yeasts that contribute to sour rot [42]. In South Korea, D. suzukii is the primary Drosophila pest on various berry crops [43]. In a study of the effects of cherry varieties on the population dynamics of Drosophila in northern China, Man et al. [44] reported that D. melanogaster, D. suzukii, Drosophila hydei, and D. immigrans, were present, with D. melanogaster being the dominant species. They also revealed that the composition and abundance of Drosophila species varied significantly among cherry cultivars. Similarly, D. suzukii had distinct preferences for five grape varieties that differed in surface physicochemical properties [45]. These findings are largely consistent with ours. The observed regional differences in Drosophila species composition and dominance are likely influenced by variations in climate, local environmental factors, and grapevine cultivar characteristics.

Drosophila primarily mediate disease occurrence through oviposition in grape berries and larval feeding [42]. Ultimately, they facilitate yeast conversion of sugars in grapes into ethanol and bacteria oxidize ethanol into acetic acid [5]. Meanwhile, specific volatile compounds such as ethanol, ethyl acetate, and 2-phenylethanol are released, which are known to attract D. melanogaster [46, 47]. Through contact, oviposition and feeding on grapes, Drosophila species spread yeasts to more grape berries [21, 48], forming a synergistic interaction cycle. In this study, H. uvarum and D. melanogaster fit this scenario and the presence of the two was positively correlated (Fig. 5A–B). Our results provide further evidence that the yeast–Drosophila interaction is a major factor causing grape sour rot, though the underlying interaction mechanism(s) requires further investigation.

Negative correlations between a Drosophila and yeast species, such as between D. takahashii and P. kudriavzevii, indicate an ecological antagonism, such as competition for resources or inhibition of Drosophila growth by yeast metabolites [49, 50]. However, the low proportion of blue in the heatmaps (Fig. 5) suggests that the competitive relationship between yeasts and fruit flies in the grape ecosystem is relatively weak. Kleman et al. [51] found that H. uvarum exhibited the strongest attraction to D. melanogaster and D. suzukii, suggesting that its volatiles may contribute significantly to the ecological dominance of Hanseniaspora spp. Similarly, Becher et al. [49] demonstrated that D. melanogaster is strongly attracted to S. cerevisiae. Attraction and oviposition rates were significantly reduced when non-fermented grape juice was used, and yeast-free grapes did not support larval development. They also identified a synthetic blend of ethanol, acetic acid, acetoin, 2-phenylethanol, and 3-methyl-1-butanol that mimics the yeast odor and was as attractive as fermenting grape juice to Drosophila.

Therefore, considering the interactions between the dominant yeast and Drosophila species, subsequent work should explore the underlying mechanisms from ethological and metabolomic perspectives and, specifically, assess the impact of volatile compounds released by yeast-infected, damaged grape berries on host selection by fruit flies. This research will provide an important basis for in-depth understanding of the mechanism(s) and interactions that lead to sour rot, which help in the design of prevention and control strategies.

Conclusion

The yeast and Drosophila species detected in the four major grape-producing regions of China were comparable to those reported in vineyards worldwide. Hanseniaspora spp. and Starmerella spp. were the dominant yeast genera, and S. bacillaris, S. cerevisiae, H. opuntiae, and H. uvarum were the most prevalent species. D. melanogaster was identified as the predominant Drosophila species. Geographic region and grape cultivar influenced the composition of the yeast and Drosophila communities, offering a scientific foundation for developing targeted strategies to prevent and control grape sour rot in the different grape-producing regions in China.
