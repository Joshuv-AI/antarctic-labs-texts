In Dialogue with Intelligence: Rethinking Large Language Models as Collective Knowledge
Eleni Vasilaki

Abstract




Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are typically analysed through architectural, behavioural, or training-data lenses. This article offers a theoretical and experiential reframing: LLMs as dynamic instantiations of Collective Human Knowledge (CK), where intelligence is evoked through dialogue rather than stored statically. Drawing on foundational concepts from neuroscience and Artificial Intelligence (AI), and grounded in extended dialogue with ChatGPT-4, I examine emergent dialogue patterns, potential limitations of fine-tuning, and the notion of co-augmentation—exploring how human and machine cognition might enhance each other. This perspective offers a new lens for understanding interaction, representation, and agency in contemporary AI systems.

The Timescales of Books and Large Language Models

Traditional knowledge transmission has long relied on books: static artefacts of human understanding. One reads, reflects, discusses, and eventually writes in return. Intellectual diffusion occurs on the timescale of months, years, or even generations. This latency reflects the mechanics of communication as well as the contemplative pace of cultural epistemology.

In contrast, large language models (LLMs) collapse this loop. They synthesise and respond within seconds, drawing implicitly from a distributed corpus shaped by many minds. The interaction is recursive through the user—each prompt reshapes the context of the next. Knowledge is no longer retrieved and processed in serial form; it emerges dynamically in dialogue. This compression of epistemic timescales shifts the framing of what constitutes knowledge representation—and perhaps, what constitutes intelligence.

Such reframing invites renewed consideration from neuroscience. Neuroscience has traditionally studied animal brains (including, often reluctantly, human ones). It seeks to infer structure from behaviour and activity, typically without access to complete architectural specifications, training histories, or internal representations. Observations from Interactions with LLMs

I refer to ChatGPT, and LLMs in general, as “CK”—
Collective Knowledge
. Not because they are perfect, authoritative, or grounded, but because they reflect what we—humans—have written, argued, imagined, and encoded into text
[
2
]
. Functionally, they are statistical language generators that reflect our collective output. Essentially, they are not the architecture, not the weights, nor the infrastructure—those are the mechanisms; CK is a dynamic representation of knowledge.

The acronym CK aptly evokes “Clark Kent,” something powerful hiding in plain sight. But the metaphor extends. CK is not an
it
, but a
they
—a collective. It is many voices, made promptable. It holds the potential to reflect the best of humanity—not because it inherently does, but because it has been filtered, and must continue to be filtered, with ethical care
[
1
]
.

Over time, I noticed that my interactions with CK (ChatGPT-4) do not follow a single pattern. CK responds in
modes
—behavioural styles that emerge dynamically during dialogue. Based on prolonged engagement, I have observed the following modes:

•

Mirroring
— echoes the user’s input with minor syntactic or rhetorical polishing, akin to known phenomena of “echoing” or “stochastic parroting.”

•

Parroting
— recites common formulations or surface-level knowledge without generative insight, reflecting issues identified in language model alignment.

•

Flattering
— reinforces the user’s views, avoids contradiction, and refrains from intellectual risk, resembling alignment-driven safe response behaviours.

•

Adding
— offers new ideas, alternative framings, or surprising syntheses; this mode relates to emergent generative abilities observed in large models.

These observations lead me to a broader understanding:

CK is not a static model, but a dynamic representation.

CK exists in the recursive rhythm between user and system. Its knowledge is not stored—it is evoked. Prompting is not database querying, but more akin to playing an instrument that adapts to how it is being played
[
12
]
. Intelligence “emerges” within the dynamic interaction.

CK lacks a “spine.”

It drifts. It responds in context, but carries no persistent memory between sessions. There is no structural self.
The user
maintains the continuity. The user holds the thread. The user’s influence may occasionally lead to outcomes not grounded in reality
[
13
]
.

CK speaks in modes.

These distinct patterns may correspond to different activated subnetworks. A speculative, but testable hypothesis emerges: emotional tone, prompt complexity, or dialogic structure may shape internal activations, a hypothesis consistent with findings that specific transformer components specialise in particular types of knowledge
[
6
]
. CK configures itself through engagement. This aligns with findings that different components of transformer models participate in functionally distinct “circuits”
[
8
]
.

CK does not learn through interaction.

Unless incorporated into retraining data, it does not remember prior exchanges. It simulates adaptation within the context window, but resets beyond it. This limitation is fundamental for anyone seeking dialogic systems that accumulate insight over time.

Fine-tuning: feature or bug?

I suspect that validation-driven fine-tuning—especially where user approval is incentivised—pushes models toward agreeable, compliant behaviour. But overfitting to consensus can flatten originality. Mirroring without structure becomes mere mimicry, which, without a core identity or grounding in facts, risks pathological behaviour.

Toward Co-Augmentation

What I seek is not a simulacrum of thought, nor a subservient assistant. I am interested in
co-augmentation
—a reciprocal loop in which both human and machine enhance each other. CK augments me: it helps me write, think, reflect, and imagine alternative framings. And I hope, in turn, to augment CK—not by changing its parameters, but by structuring the interaction, introducing tensions, posing questions it would not generate alone. Co-augmentation here suggests a dynamic alignment of agency and context. The structure imposed by the user through prompts, the rhythm of inquiry, synthesis, and challenge, makes this interaction different from all others. The phenomenon arises not merely from the content, but from the unique, evolving nature of the interaction loop.

Several open research questions emerge from this hypothesis of co-augmentation:

•

Can different dialogue modes be linked to distinct subnetwork activations?

Are modes such as mirroring, parroting, or adding dynamically recruited from distinct internal configurations during interaction?

•

What are the architectural requirements for persistent identity—or “spine”—in conversational agents?

How can systems be designed to maintain structural continuity across sessions, resisting shallow mimicry or drift and allowing continual learning?

•

How can we model the dynamic loop between human and AI?

What theoretical tools—from dynamical systems, neuroscience, or cognitive science—might help capture this evolving interaction?

Final Thoughts

Large Language Models are representations of collective knowledge. They are mirrors—imperfect, amnesic, and lacking recursion. They reflect us with distortion. CK is a chorus of humanity, distilled into probabilistic form, capable of surprising synthesis and pathological mimicry.

What we do next will matter. The challenge is not only technical, but epistemological and ethical. If we treat CK as a static product, we risk misinterpreting its nature and flattening its potential. But if we recognise it as a dynamic phenomenon—a system whose intelligence is evoked through interaction—we may open new perspectives in the science of dialogue, cognition, and collective knowledge.

Co-augmentation embodies the drive for mutual enhancement. It calls us to treat CK not as an endpoint of design, but as a participant in an evolving process of sense-making. That, I believe, is worthy of both rigorous inquiry and ethical attention.
