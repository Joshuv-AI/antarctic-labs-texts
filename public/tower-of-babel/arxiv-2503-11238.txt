Computational Complexity of Finding Subgroups of a Given Order
K. Lakshmanan

Abstract
We study the problem of finding a subgroup of a given order in a finite group, where the group is represented by its Cayley table. We establish that this problem is NP-hard in the general case by providing a reduction from the Hamiltonian Cycle problem. Additionally, we analyze the complexity of the problem in the special case of abelian groups and present a linear-time algorithm for finding a subgroup of a given order when the input is given in the form of a Cayley table. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has addressed the complexity of this problem under the Cayley table representation. Our results also provide insight into the computational difficulty of finding subgroup across different ways of groups representations. Keywords: Computational group theory, Cayley table representation, NP-hardness, subgroup enumeration, abelian groups, algorithmic group theory Mathematics Subject Classification 2020: 68Q17, 20-08

Introduction

In this paper, we study the problem of computing a subgroup of a given order in a finite group. We establish that this problem is NP-hard in the general case and present a linear-time algorithm for abelian groups when given in the Cayley table representation. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work explicitly addresses these results in the literature.

We begin by discussing the Cayley table representation, which provides a fundamental but computationally expensive way to describe a group. While representations such as generators and relations or permutation representations often allow structural insights for efficient algorithms, the Cayley table offers a general, uniform description applicable to any finite group. Hardness results in this representation often indicate worst-case intractability unless alternative representations permit structural exploitation.

Despite its high space complexity, the Cayley table representation appears in several computational settings. It is used in group isomorphism testing
[
13
]
, where small groups are explicitly stored as lookup tables. In computational algebra systems, finite groups of moderate size are often manipulated via Cayley tables for efficient lookup operations. Additionally, in automata theory and symbolic computation, transition functions of finite-state systems can sometimes be encoded as group operations, making the Cayley table a natural representation in certain algorithmic contexts.

Next, we analyze conditions under which hardness results transfer to other representations, highlighting implications for different group structures.

1.1
Cayley Table Representation

The Cayley table representation provides a brute-force encoding of a group, explicitly listing the results of all group operations. While this representation is often used to establish worst-case complexity results, the extent to which these results transfer to other representations depends on the structure of the group and the nature of the problem.

Hardness Transfer from Cayley Table to Other Representations
Hardness results in the Cayley table model can transfer to other group representations under certain conditions. In particular, if a problem remains hard regardless of how the group is specified, then Cayley table hardness serves as strong evidence of intractability in the general case. This occurs when:

•

The problem is combinatorial in nature and does not rely on specific algebraic properties of the group.
Many problems, such as finding a subgroup of a given order, involve searching through subsets of group elements and are unlikely to admit efficient algorithms, regardless of representation.

•

No structural advantage exists in alternative representations.
If no known method exploits algebraic properties to obtain a faster solution, then the complexity in the Cayley table model is expected to be representative of the general case.

•

Known reductions exist between different representations.
If a problem is NP-hard for general groups under the Cayley table model and reductions exist that transform instances into more compact representations (such as permutation groups or groups given by generators and relations), then hardness likely extends to those cases as well.

While the Cayley table is a natural choice for proving worst-case complexity, it is often inefficient in practice. Some group representations
[
6
]
, such as permutations, matrices, or generators with relations, encode the group more compactly and allow for more efficient computations. In these cases, problems that are intractable for general groups may have polynomial-time solutions in structured settings
[
2
,
11
]
. We list the cases where the Cayley table representation is the worst case but not representative of all cases:

•

Permutation group representations often admit efficient algorithms.
For example, the problem of computing a subgroup given a set of generators is intractable for arbitrary groups but can be solved efficiently for permutation groups using the Schreier–Sims algorithm.

•

Matrix groups over finite fields allow algebraic techniques to improve efficiency.
Many computational problems that are difficult in general become tractable when the group elements are matrices, as linear algebra techniques can be applied.

•

Groups given by generators and relations may allow structure-specific optimizations.
The word problem, for instance, is undecidable in some finitely presented groups but trivial in the Cayley table model, as the full operation table allows constant-time lookup.

•

Commutative (abelian) groups often admit efficient solutions.
Many problems that are NP-hard for general groups, such as finding a subgroup of a given order, are solvable in polynomial time when restricted to abelian groups.

Thus, while proving hardness in the Cayley table model provides a strong indication of computational difficulty, additional analysis is required to determine whether the result holds in alternative representations.

1.2
Hardness Transfer Across Group Representations

The complexity of computational problems in group theory can vary depending on how the group is represented. However, for the problem of determining whether a subgroup of a given order exists, we establish that NP-hardness in the Cayley table representation extends to other standard representations.

•

Cayley Table as a General Representation

The Cayley table provides a complete and explicit description of a group, listing all group operations.
It allows no shortcuts—algorithms working on the Cayley table cannot exploit additional structure, making it a natural worst-case representation.

•

Polynomial-Time Transformations

A Cayley table can be converted in polynomial time into other common representations such as:
Generators and relations (via presentation extraction algorithms)
Permutation representation (by representing elements as permutations on themselves)
These conversions ensure that any hardness result for the Cayley table carries over to these representations if no additional structure is exploited.

•

Implication of Efficient Algorithms

Suppose there existed a polynomial-time algorithm for finding a subgroup of a specified order in one of these alternative representations.
Since we can transform the Cayley table into that representation efficiently, we could solve the problem for the Cayley table as well.
This contradicts the established NP-hardness of the problem in the Cayley table representation.

•

When Hardness Does Not Automatically Transfer

In structured groups (e.g., permutation groups, matrix groups), additional algebraic properties can sometimes be leveraged for more efficient algorithms.
For instance, Abelian groups admit polynomial-time solutions, and certain permutation group problems have more efficient algorithms using stabilizer chains and coset enumeration.

Since we have shown the problem to be NP-hard for the Cayley table representation, and since Cayley tables can be transformed into other standard representations in polynomial time, the problem remains NP-hard in all these representations unless additional structure can be exploited. This establishes a broad complexity classification across different models of computation in group theory

3.3
Correctness of the Algorithm

The algorithm iteratively selects an element
a\in G
and computes the subgroup it generates. If the generated subgroup
\langle a\rangle
has order

3.1

If

Lemma 3.1

Let

Remark 3.1
.

The complexity of the subgroup-finding problem exhibits a stark contrast between abelian and non-abelian groups. Our NP-completeness reduction relies on the structure of the symmetric group
S_{n}
, where elements do not necessarily commute, allowing for the encoding of the Hamiltonian Cycle problem. In contrast, for abelian groups, where every element commutes, we present a linear-time algorithm. This contrast highlights how structural properties of groups dramatically impact computational complexity: while the problem remains intractable in the general (non-abelian) case, it becomes efficiently solvable in the abelian setting.

3.5
Complexity Contrast: Abelian vs. Non-Abelian Groups

Our results demonstrate a striking complexity difference in the problem of finding a subgroup of a specified order: the problem is NP-complete in the general case but admits a linear-time algorithm when restricted to abelian groups. This sharp contrast is not unique to our setting and appears in various computational problems in group theory.

•

Group Isomorphism: Isomorphism testing for abelian groups can be done in polynomial time, whereas for non-abelian groups, the best-known algorithms are significantly slower and the problem remains a major open question.

•

Word Problem: In abelian groups, the word problem is solvable in polynomial time using linear algebra techniques. In contrast, for general non-abelian groups, it can be undecidable.

•

Conjugacy Problem: Trivial in abelian groups but can be computationally hard in non-abelian settings.

•

Finding Normal Subgroups: Immediate in abelian groups, where every subgroup is normal, but potentially hard in non-abelian groups.

•

Commutator Problems: Trivial in abelian groups (since all elements commute) but non-trivial and computationally challenging in general.

These examples suggest that the presence of commutativity significantly simplifies computational problems, whereas the added structure in non-abelian groups often leads to increased complexity. Given our results, it would be interesting to explore whether similar complexity gaps exist for other group-theoretic problems under different representations.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the problem of determining whether a finite group contains a subgroup of a given order. We established that this problem is NP-complete in the general case by providing a reduction from the Hamiltonian cycle problem. Additionally, we presented a linear-time algorithm for abelian groups when given their Cayley table representation. This highlights a significant complexity gap between abelian and non-abelian cases, reinforcing the role of group structure in computational tractability.

Our results contribute to the broader understanding of computational group theory, particularly in the context of different group representations. An interesting direction for future work is to explore the parameterized complexity of the problem for various structural restrictions, such as nilpotent or solvable groups. Additionally, understanding the complexity of related problems, such as finding cyclic subgroups of a given order, remains an open avenue for further research.
