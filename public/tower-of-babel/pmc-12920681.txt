Outcomes after treatment with proton pump inhibitors in patients with ineffective esophageal motility: a systematic review
Authors: Fatima Sakr, Maryam Soheilipour, Fatemeh Maghool, Elham Tabesh, Serhat Bor, Peyman Adibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:This systematic review evaluated the effectiveness of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) in improving manometric parameters in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) diagnosed with Ineffective esophageal motility (IEM).Background:IEM represents the most common esophageal motility disorder among patients with GERD. To date, no treatment has conclusively demonstrated efficacy in restoring esophageal motor function in these individuals. Methods:This systematic review was performed according to the PRISMA guidelines. We conducted comprehensive searches of electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, until January 2024 to identify relevant randomized and non-randomized controlled trials. Criteria for study inclusion were based on the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome framework.Results:A total of 9 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising eight non-randomized controlled trials (non-RCTs) and one randomized controlled trial (RCT). Out of 305 recruited patients with a diagnosis of GERD, 185 patients matched the diagnosis of IEM with manometric studies before and after a PPI treatment. PPIs used in the studies included omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, and rabeprazole. The duration of treatment ranged from four to twenty-four weeks. Except for one study, there was no statistically significant improvement in manometric parameters following PPI treatment. Conclusion: Evidence from included studies indicates that although PPIs are effective in healing esophagitis, they do not consistently improve esophageal motility, irrespective of the PPI type, dosage, or treatment duration.

Introduction

Ineffective esophageal motility (IEM) is a common primary motility disorder, reported in approximately 30% of patients undergoing high-resolution manometry (1).

Before the standardization of IEM terminology, the term non-specific esophageal motility disorders (NEMD) was widely used (2). The term "ineffective esophageal motility" was first used in 1997, when Leite et al. proposed it as a more accurate replacement for NEMD, defined manometrically as at least 30% ineffective contractions among 10 test swallows (3).

Since then, the manometric defining criteria of IEM evolved based on conventional, then on high-resolution manometry.

In 2001, Spechler and Castell further classified IEM as a distinct entity among esophageal motility abnormalities. The manometric criteria for IEM were defined as distal esophageal hypocontractility in 30% or more of wet swallows, characterized by contraction amplitude less than 30 mmHg in the distal esophagus (3-8 cm above the LES). These peristaltic waves are not propagated to the distal esophagus or are absent (4).

In 2004, Tutuian and Castell proposed a new manometric threshold for defining IEM, set at 50% or more ineffective wet swallows, which was subsequently adopted by Blonski et al. in 2008. This threshold was found to be more frequently associated with esophageal symptoms such as dysphagia and heartburn, compared with the previous definition (5, 6).

In 2015, the Chicago classification v3.0 introduced an HRM-based definition of IEM, specifying that 50% or more ineffective swallows must have a distal contractile integral (DCI) of less than 450 mmHg · cm · sec (7). The latest update in the Chicago Classification v4.0 revised the criteria for ineffective swallows to include weak esophageal body contraction vigor (DCI of 100-450 mmHg·cm·s), transition zone defects greater than 5 cm measured using a 20-mmHg isobaric contour, or failure of peristalsis (DCI less than 100 mmHg · cm · s). For a conclusive diagnosis of IEM, more than 70% ineffective swallows and/or 50% or more failed swallows are required (8).

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a widespread gastrointestinal disorder that affects an estimated 8% to 33% of the global population (9). Diagnosis of GERD relies on clinical symptoms and endoscopic evidence of esophageal mucosal damage, with typical manifestations including heartburn and acid regurgitation, which often correlate with the severity of endoscopic esophagitis (10, 11). Several studies suggest a notable portion of patients with GERD experience compromised esophageal motility, potentially contributing to the onset of dysphagia (3, 12). The association between IEM and GERD is well-documented, with IEM being the most common motor disorder in patients with GERD, as proven by 24-h pH monitoring (13). In contrast to non-erosive reflux disease (NERD) and physiologic acid exposure, which have a low prevalence of IEM (14), conditions like severe erosive esophagitis (LA C-D) and Barrett's esophagus are consistently associated with a greater likelihood of IEM (15).

IEM is challenging to manage because there is no clear evidence that a pharmacologic intervention consistently restores esophageal smooth muscle contractility or improves symptoms (16). Conventional and most of the new prokinetics are ineffective for IEM (17, 18).

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are the first-line treatment for GERD, offering symptom relief to 70-80% of patients with erosive esophagitis and 50-60% of those with non-erosive reflux disease within four weeks (19). PPIs are also commonly used to manage IEM associated with GERD, alongside dietary and lifestyle modifications (20).

Despite growing interest in esophageal motility disorders, the relationship between GERD and ineffective esophageal motility (IEM) remains unclear. In particular, it is uncertain whether IEM predisposes to reflux or results from chronic acid exposure. Furthermore, the effect of proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy in patients with both GERD and IEM has not been systematically evaluated. This review addresses this gap by summarizing current evidence on the impact of PPI treatment in this subgroup.

Methods

The systematic review adhered to the 2020 PRISMA checklist to ensure optimal reporting of its methodology. Criteria for study inclusion were based on the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome framework. According to existing standards, the study protocol for this systematic review has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024509725).

Types of studies

Included studies investigated the impact of PPI treatment(s) on patients with GERD diagnosed with IEM. The inclusion criteria comprised studies involving adult patients, articles written in English, prospective clinical trials, and studies with comprehensive reporting of results. Conversely, trial registries, conference abstracts, observational studies, case reports, letters, editorials, research involving children, or specific age groups, such as geriatric patients, studies on patients with rheumatologic and systemic disorders known to affect esophageal function (such as scleroderma or diabetes), studies on patients with a history of esophageal surgery (such as antireflux surgery or myotomy) or endoscopic intervention (like transoral fundoplication), studies lacking proper result documentation, and unavailable original articles were excluded.

Participants

Studied patients were diagnosed with both GERD and IEM through various tests (clinical symptoms of GERD, endoscopy showing esophagitis, and esophageal manometry compatible with the diagnosis of IEM).

Outcomes

The primary outcome evaluated the effectiveness of treatment in patients with GERD and IEM, using manometric changes after treatment as the measure—studies reporting only symptomatic relief after treatment were excluded. The secondary outcome was to define a rational approach to managing IEM.

Intervention and comparison

Patients were treated with any PPI intervention. Studies reporting treatments such as surgery, other antiacids, prokinetics, or any other intervention were excluded. The studies had to clearly describe the therapeutic scheme, i.e., doses and duration.

Search strategy

All available years in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched until 31 January 2024, for relevant peer-reviewed human clinical trials, published in English. The search strategy used for the PubMed database is described in Supplementary Figure 1.

References were initially screened based on title and abstract using a citation manager (Mendeley), followed by duplicate elimination. Studies were then screened for abstracts, available full texts, or titles referring to the condition. Literature was reviewed independently by two researchers, and a third independent reviewer resolved discrepancies. The search strategy is detailed in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram (Figure 1).

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted the following information from each included study: the first author, study design, recruited subjects, patients of interest, esophagitis grade/ stage, medication dose, duration, post-intervention manometry, and primary esophageal motor function after treatment. The quality of the included studies was assessed using a standardized form.

Quality assessment

For the randomized controlled trial (RCT) included in this study, the risk of bias was evaluated using version 2 of the Cochrane tool for assessing the risk of bias in RCTs (RoB 2), while the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies-of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was applied to the non-randomized studies. The reviewers independently evaluated publication quality, risk of bias, and evidence grading. In instances of disagreement, a third reviewer reassessed the studies and engaged in discussion with the other reviewers to achieve a consensus. The risk of biases was categorized as low, moderate, serious, critical, or no information.

Results

Following a comprehensive literature search, a total of 1136 records were identified for initial screening. Subsequently, 83 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 74 records were excluded due to various reasons such as study design (22 records), unsuitable participant population (13 records), inappropriate intervention (24 records), and irrelevant outcomes (15 records). Ultimately, 9 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into our review. The search process and results are illustrated in Figure 1.

Eight of the nine included studies were prospective non-RCTs (21-28), while one study was a prospective double-blind RCT (29). The descriptive characteristics and overall quality assessments of these studies are summarized in Table 1 and supplementary Figure 2 and 3. A total of 305 patients with GERD were initially recruited across all studies. The mean age range of participants was 42.6-65 years. Of the 287 patients whose gender was specified, the majority (195) were male. Patients included in these studies were diagnosed with GERD based on one or more of the following criteria: typical clinical symptoms such as heartburn, regurgitation, and endoscopic evidence of erosive esophagitis.

Out of the 305 patients with GERD, 201 underwent manometric studies twice, once before and once after the intervention. The other patients did not undergo a second manometric evaluation due to unmet criteria for peristaltic dysfunction according to the first evaluation (26), intolerance of the procedure or due to a technical failure (25, 27). In another study, of the 100 recruited patients, only those who demonstrated severe reflux esophagitis by endoscopic evaluation were evaluated by a second manometry after PPI treatment (21).

In the only randomized controlled trial included in the review, 4 patients were excluded without specifying the reasons. Of the participants who completed the study, 15 were assigned to the PPI group and 16 to the placebo group (29).

Overall, the review includes 185 patients who met the criteria for a diagnosis of IEM, and who underwent manometric evaluation before and after PPI treatment.

Conventional manometry or 24-hour ambulatory manometry was used to evaluate esophageal motor function. In addition to manometric evaluation, several studies assessed patients clinically before and after treatment or utilized other diagnostic tests such as transit studies and acid clearance tests. These diverse outcomes are summarized in supplementary Figure 4. For our review, we focused on the manometric results

Manometric evaluation of the included patients demonstrated one or more of the following:

- Low mean distal amplitude contractions (<30-40 mmHg, or significantly lower than matched controls)

- ≥ 30% non-propagated contractions

Medication dose and duration

The studies predominantly used Omeprazole at doses of 20-40 mg daily, with variations such as 20 mg twice daily in one study (25). The duration of PPI treatment across the studies ranged from 4 to 24 weeks. The majority of studies administered treatment until there was endoscopic evidence of esophagitis healing (21-23, 25, 26), while some studies had a fixed treatment duration (e.g., 4 weeks) (24, 27-29). For standardization, all PPIs were converted to their Omeprazole equivalent. Thus, the intervention was equivalent to Omeprazole 20-40 mg once daily for 4 to 24 weeks.

Pre- and post-intervention manometry

Manometry was performed using either conventional or 24-hour ambulatory methods. Xu et al., Singh et al., Williams et al., and Howard et al. used conventional manometry (21, 22, 24, 29), while Timmer et al., McDougall et al., Deprez et al., Netzer et al., and Świątkowski et al. used 24-hour ambulatory manometry (23, 25, 26, 27, 28). Post-intervention manometry timing varied across different studies. Some studies conducted manometry on PPI, such as Singh et al., Deprez et al., Netzer et al., and Howard et al. (22, 26, 27, 29), while others like Timmer et al. and Świątkowski et al. waited several days to weeks after stopping PPI therapy (23, 28).

Primary esophageal motor function after treatment

The primary outcome across all studies was the evaluation of esophageal motor function post-PPI treatment. Most studies concluded that healing of esophagitis did not significantly improve esophageal motility.

Xu et al. recruited 100 patients with typical reflux symptoms for endoscopic evaluation, and 25 patients with erosive esophagitis, abnormally low peristaltic amplitude, and a high percentage of failed peristalsis were treated with lansoprazole 30 mg daily for 3-6 months until esophagitis healed. Manometric assessments before and after treatment showed no significant changes in contraction amplitude or the percentage of failed peristalsis (21).

Singh et al. examined 43 patients with confirmed reflux esophagitis who underwent manometric evaluation both before and after treatment with omeprazole 40 mg daily until esophagitis healed. The median distal amplitude in patients was significantly lower compared to controls and did not change after PPI treatment (22).

Timmer et al. recruited 27 patients with reflux esophagitis, conducting manometric evaluations both before and after treatment with omeprazole 40 mg daily until esophagitis healing. The initial manometric studies indicated an abnormally high percentage of non-transmitted contractions. Post-treatment, there was no significant change in mean peristaltic amplitude, nor in the percentage of peristaltic, simultaneous, or non-transmitted contractions (23).

Williams et al. treated 15 patients with severe reflux esophagitis, characterized by abnormally low mean peristaltic amplitude, with omeprazole 40 mg daily for 4 weeks, followed by repeated manometric evaluations. While there was no significant change in propagated swallows post-treatment, the peristaltic amplitude showed significant improvement. However, upon individual analysis, only 3 patients exhibited consistent improvement (24).

In the study by McDougall et al., 26 patients were treated with omeprazole 20 mg twice daily for 8-14 weeks. Of these, 11 patients who showed healing at the end of treatment underwent pre- and post-treatment manometric evaluations. Initially, these patients had abnormally low peristaltic amplitude and a high percentage of simultaneous, dropped, and interrupted contractions. Post-treatment, there was no significant improvement in these parameters (25).

Deprez et al. compared manometric parameters in 16 patients with reflux esophagitis before and after treatment with omeprazole 40 mg daily for 3-6 months until healing of esophagitis. The study found a significant improvement in contraction amplitude and the percentage of peristaltic waves, which were initially abnormally low. Additionally, there was a significant decrease in the percentage of simultaneous contractions (26).

Netzer et al. studied 15 patients with reflux esophagitis treated with pantoprazole 40 mg daily for 4 weeks. The study found no significant changes in peristaltic amplitude or the percentage of propulsive waves before and after treatment, with both parameters remaining lower than normal throughout (27).

Świątkowski et al. examined 20 patients with reflux esophagitis, where manometry initially revealed more than 50% non-effective waves. Patients were treated with rabeprazole 20 mg daily for 8 weeks. Post-treatment manometric evaluations showed no significant improvements in esophageal function parameters, including contraction amplitude, the percentage of peristaltic waves, simultaneous waves, and non-effective peristalsis (28).

Finally, Howard et al. conducted a double-blind RCT with 31 patients with erosive esophagitis, randomized to receive either omeprazole 20 mg daily or a placebo. Manometric assessments were performed before and at 4 weeks of treatment. Patients exhibited a significantly lower mean peristaltic amplitude compared to controls. After the intervention, there was no significant improvement in mean peristaltic amplitude or the percentage of abnormal contractions in patients with healed esophagitis, and mean peristaltic amplitude remained unchanged in the PPI group (29).

Risk of bias

Of the nine studies included in this review, eight were non-randomized and assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Among these, seven studies were rated as having a low risk of bias, whereas one study was judged to have a serious risk of bias. The remaining study was a randomized controlled trial, evaluated using the RoB 2 tool, and was determined to have an uncertain risk of bias. The detailed assessment of risk of bias is illustrated in Supplementary Figures 2 and 3.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated the efficacy of PPI treatment in patients with GERD and IEM for improving esophageal motor dysfunction. The majority of the studies, except for one, did not show a correlation between manometric outcomes and PPI therapy. Overall, indices of esophageal motor function, including distal amplitude, percentage of propagated swallows, peristaltic contractions, simultaneous contractions, and ineffective contractions, did not show significant changes following PPI treatment, regardless of whether esophagitis had healed by the end of the treatment period.

Regarding the clinical impact of PPIs in patients with GERD and IEM, six studies included in this review assessed patients clinically before and after PPI treatment, revealing a significant improvement in reflux symptoms (21, 24, 26-29).

Other non-interventional studies assessed the association between PPI treatment and IEM outcomes.

A case series by Sandhu et al. included 25 patients who underwent HREM at least twice, 6 months apart, and were evaluated between tests for any clinical intervention. The study found no clear link between PPI use and improved motility in patients with esophageal hypomotility (30).

Moreover, Smythe et al. investigated 12 patients with Barrett's esophagus using both conventional and ambulatory manometry, initially while on PPI therapy, and 5 days after, without PPI. They did not find a significant difference in the percentages of successful swallows, peristaltic waves, simultaneous waves, or distal wave amplitude on esophageal motility studies with or without PPI therapy (31).

In a retrospective cohort study, Shetler et al. studied 46 symptomatic patients with IEM who had undergone ambulatory pH monitoring ‘off’ acid suppressive therapy.

The patients were evaluated symptomatically by a validated questionnaire before and 2 months after a twice-daily PPI trial. Evaluated symptoms included dysphagia, heartburn, regurgitation, lower chest pain, and belching.

At the end of the trial, PPI therapy was found to be more effective for IEM patients with abnormal esophageal acid exposure than those with normal acid exposure, as 74% of patients with abnormal acid exposure had a complete PPI response defined by questionnaire values of ‘zero’, in contrast to only 10% of patients with IEM and normal pH studies (32).

In a retrospective study, 138 patients with GERD were analyzed for the development of Barrett's metaplasia or poor esophageal body motility, with or without effective medical treatment by H2 blockers or Omeprazole.

As a result, 41.9% of patients with medication and 59.3% of patients not receiving treatment had impaired esophageal body motility (P < 0.05). Thus, it may be considered that medical treatment partially prevents poor esophageal contractility, but not very effectively. Noticeably, the group with poor esophageal body motility and medical treatment had significantly more altered esophageal contractility and substantially longer history of GERD, suggesting that the duration of reflux is a principal determinant of the occurrence of poor esophageal body motility (33).

In our review, the investigation by Deprez et al. stood out as the sole study to report a noteworthy improvement in esophageal motor dysfunction following the administration of a high-dose PPI regimen over an extended period (Omeprazole 40 mg daily for 12-24 weeks) (26). In contrast, Singh et al., who employed the same high dose for an extended duration, did not find any improvement (22). Additionally, Timmer et al. administered Omeprazole 40 mg daily over an extended period (8-16 weeks) but did not detect any changes in manometric parameters (23). The former study had fewer patients than the 2 later studies, which could, in part, influence the true effect of PPI therapy on variation in manometric parameters.

However, this systematic review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the literature search was restricted to English-language articles, which may have introduced a language bias. Second, most of the included studies had small sample sizes, and the total number of patients analyzed across all studies was only 185. These small samples likely limited statistical power to detect modest treatment effects. Third, the included studies were relatively old, and all used conventional or 24-hour ambulatory manometry rather than high-resolution manometry, which may not reflect current clinical practices and the evolution in the definitions and diagnostic criteria of IEM. In addition, most studies were non-randomized, and only one RCT with uncertain risk of bias was identified, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, heterogeneity in study design and outcome measures prevented a quantitative synthesis. Fifth, motility parameters were measured during PPI therapy in some studies and after PPI therapy in others, which could meaningfully affect outcomes.

Sixth, the patients evaluated across studies did not include cases of non-erosive reflux disease, in which the mechanism of IEM or response to PPI treatment may differ from those with erosive esophagitis.

To sum up, this systematic review found insufficient evidence supporting the efficacy of PPI therapy in ameliorating esophageal motor dysfunction in patients with IEM. This finding holds across various PPIs, dosages, and treatment durations. However, given the limitations discussed above, the findings of this review should be viewed as preliminary. Further well-designed, contemporary studies are warranted to provide more definitive evidence.

Conclusion

Esophageal motor dysfunction in IEM patients associated with GERD cannot be corrected by short to medium-term PPI treatment. Future studies should focus on large, well-designed randomized controlled trials with updated IEM definitions and standardized outcome measures to strengthen the evidence base.
