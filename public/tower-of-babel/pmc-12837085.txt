The biomarker and clinical changes across the Alzheimer’s continuum study (BCAS): rationale, design, and baseline characteristics of the first 1,013 participants
Authors: Hui Chen, Hong Dai, Li Zhang, Yongjian Deng, Kan Zhang, Jie Yu, Guoping Peng, Zexin Guo, Jing Zhang, Changzheng Yuan, Fang Xie, Benyan Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia in China, but deeply phenotyped clinical cohorts remain limited. The Biomarker and Clinical changes across the Alzheimer’s continuum Study (BCAS) was established at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine to capture biological and clinical changes across the AD spectrum.MethodsBCAS is an ongoing, longitudinal memory clinic-based cohort initiated in 2016 in Zhejiang, one of China’s most economically vigorous and rapidly aging regions. Individuals aged ≥ 40 years with cognitive concerns are recruited and undergo standardized clinical evaluation, comprehensive neuropsychological testing, biospecimen collection, and multimodal neuroimaging including MRI and amyloid and tau PET in subsets. Participants are followed every 1–2 years with repeat assessments. This paper reports baseline characteristics and preliminary findings from the first 1,013 participants enrolled up to January 2025.ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 66.5 years (SD 9.6), with 49.8% women and an average of 9.7 years of education. Hypertension (41.4%), diabetes (14.6%), and hypercholesterolemia (12.0%) were the most prevalent comorbidities. The mean MoCA score was 19.2 (SD 6.1). Among risk factors, higher age and diabetes were linked to lower scores, whereas higher education, tea consumption, and higher BMI were associated with better cognitive performance.ConclusionsThe BCAS served as a biomarker-rich and multimodal resource to study the clinical and biological progression of AD in China. Preliminary analyses demonstrate expected associations and support the data quality. Background

With global population aging, dementia represents an increasingly pressing public health issue [1]. This burden is particularly pronounced in China, which has the largest aging population globally. As of 2024, over 200 million people (15.4%) in China were aged 65 years or older, a figure projected to approach 400 million by 2050 [2, 3]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia, imposing a substantial burden on individuals, families, and the healthcare system [4, 5]. Recent estimates suggest that 3.9% of older Chinese adults met criteria for AD, corresponding to ~ 10 million individuals living with AD [6], with an estimated economic burden of US$ 69.0 billion by 2020 [7].

In response, several population-based cohort studies have been established to examine the epidemiology of cognitive outcomes in China [8]. National cohorts such as the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) [9] and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [10], along with regional studies such as the Shanghai Aging Study (SAS) [11], Beijing Aging Brain Rejuvenation Initiative (BABRI) [12], and the Rugao Longevity and Aging Study (RuLAS) [13], have provided valuable evidence on the risk factors for cognitive decline. These studies have identified similar associations of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors with cognitive aging, consistent with primarily U.S./European studies, while also highlighting context-specific exposures in China, such as low educational attainment and rural–urban disparities [14, 15]. However, most existing cohorts rely on brief cognitive screening tools, while biomarkers, neuroimaging, or longitudinal clinical data are limited. Additionally, following individuals with existing cognitive impairment into advanced stages is essential for understanding disease progression and prognostic factors, but remains challenging.

Clinic-based memory cohorts are well-positioned to complement community-based studies with detailed clinical, neuropsychological, and biological data from individuals with cognitive problems [16–18]. While memory clinic cohorts such as the Shanghai Memory Study (SMS) [19], the China Aging and Neurodegenerative Initiative (CANDI) [20], and China Alzheimer's disease and Neurodegenerative Disorder Research (CANDOR) [21], have been established, such cohorts remain scarce in China. In addition, few have comprehensively integrated multimodal data across the AD continuum, such as fluid biomarkers, neuroimaging, and genetic profiles [22]. As diagnostic and therapeutic approaches to AD continue to evolve [23, 24], there is an urgent need for longitudinal, deeply phenotyped clinical cohorts to capture the biological and clinical changes across the AD spectrum.

To address this gap, we established the Biomarker and Clinical changes across the Alzheimer’s continuum Study (BCAS), a longitudinal memory clinic-based cohort since 2016 in Zhejiang, China. BCAS is designed to provide deep clinical and biomarker characterization of individuals across the AD spectrum. Zhejiang province, located on China’s east coast, offers a distinctive and valuable setting for this aim. As one of the country’s most economically dynamic and rapidly aging regions, Zhejiang experienced profound shifts in education, healthcare access, and lifestyle, while also faced with challenges in increasing AD burdens and substantial sub-regional variations. In Zhejiang, traditional lifestyles and strong family-based caregiving norms, remain prevalent, which create a heterogeneous environment that influences risk factor profile and the epidemiology of AD. The BCAS study is thus unique to investigate how regional characteristics intersect with the AD mechanisms to offers insight less captured in other regions of China.

With repeated follow-up assessments and rich clinical and biomarker data, BCAS aims to track AD progression, identify early markers, and refine prognostic models. This cohort will support research on disease mechanisms, inform clinical care, and contribute to the development of precision approaches for diagnosis and intervention. In this study, we outline the rationale, study design, baseline characteristics of the first batch of participants, and future directions of BCAS.

Methods

Overview of study design

The BCAS is an ongoing, longitudinal cohort study established in 2016 at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, embedded within routine clinical practice and leverages the hospital’s multidisciplinary memory clinic infrastructure (Fig. 1). At baseline, participants undergo standardized clinical evaluations, including detailed cognitive assessments, neuroimaging, and collection of biospecimens (blood and optional cerebrospinal fluid). Additional data on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle, medical history, medication use, and caregiver-reported outcomes are collected through structured interviews and self-administered questionnaires.

Data collection and missions of the BCAS. Abbreviations: PRO, patient-reported outcome

The study protocol of BCAS was approved by the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IRB No. initially 2016–0315, renewed to 2025B-0489), and all participants or legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Objectives

BCAS complements existing Chinese cohorts, such as CANDI, CANDOR, and CABLE (Table S1), with its uniqueness from Southeastern China and enrolling participants across the AD continuum. The study population includes both urban residents and individuals from surrounding semi-urban and rural communities. This heterogeneity enables examination of a wide range of risk factors, such as education, occupation, lifestyle behaviors, and environmental exposures.

Another key objective of BCAS is to characterize the clinical heterogeneity and longitudinal trajectories of cognitive decline, and how the cognitive phenotypes differed by pathological features. The memory clinic serves as a diverse group of individuals across the AD continuum from CU, MCI, to dementia due to AD and other etiologies. This spectrum provides opportunities to investigate early markers of disease progression, subtype differentiation, and risk stratification. These findings can inform the development of precision prevention and intervention strategies. Based on this, findings from the BCAS can inform risk prediction and clinical decision-making, and identify subgroups most likely to benefit from targeted interventions.

Study population

Participants are recruited from individuals attending the memory clinic. Eligibility criteria include: (1) age 40 years or older at their first visit; (2) self-reported or informant-reported cognitive symptoms or worries about future cognitive decline; and (3) willingness to participate in longitudinal follow-up, including neuropsychological testing, neuroimaging, and biospecimen collection. Patients with major psychiatric illness, severe hearing or vision impairments, or conditions precluding neuropsychological evaluation are excluded. At enrollment, participants are classified based on standardized clinical and cognitive evaluations into one of the following categories: cognitively unimpaired (CU, including both normal cognition and subjective cognitive decline), MCI, and AD dementia (probable or possible, staged as applicable), other or mixed dementias (e.g., frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body dementia). While the BCAS was designed to recruit participants across the spectrum of AD, we also included other dementia types (e.g., vascular, Lewy body, and frontotemporal dementias, or mixed types) to enable comparative analyses of biomarker and clinical profiles.

We intentionally set a relatively lower inclusion age (≥ 40 years) to capture individuals at earlier stages or preclinical phases of AD. According to the CHARLS 2020 survey, 19.7% of community-dwelling participants aged 40–50 years self-rated their memory as poor, supporting the inclusion of this younger age group in the study. In this study, 63% of participants aged 40–50 years were CU, 22% had MCI, 7% had AD dementia and 7% had other or mixed dementias. A caregiver, study partner, or informant (close relatives) was required for participants with mild cognitive impairment or dementia, to provide supporting information on functional status and medical history. Among CU participants, having an informant was encouraged but not mandatory, and 55.2% had an available informant.

Participants with mild cognitive impairment or dementia, or those showing rapid clinical progression, are scheduled for follow-up every 12 ± 3 months, whereas CU participants are generally scheduled every 24 ± 3 months. Each follow-up includes repeat cognitive testing, clinical reassessment, update of lifestyle and medical history, and event adjudications. For a subset of participants, repeat neuroimaging and biospecimen collection are conducted.

Sample size considerations and enrollment status

As of January 2025, BCAS has enrolled 1,013 participants, with recruitment ongoing toward a target sample size of 2,500. The target sample size was informed by the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) in the United States [25] to provide sufficient statistical power to detect clinically meaningful longitudinal changes in both biomarkers and cognitive outcomes across the AD continuum. While a specific effect size was not pre-specified, this sample size affords the ability to detect a minimum standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of approximately 0.13 (assuming equal allocation between disease and control groups) with 90% power at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. For a scale of 30 points (such as MoCA), this corresponds to a detectable difference of ~ 0.3 points.

Sociodemographic, lifestyle factors, and medical history

At baseline, detailed sociodemographic information is collected through structured interviews conducted by trained research staff, including age, sex, education level, marital status, occupation (current or prior), and place of residence (urban or rural). Education is recorded as total years of schooling and categorized for analysis based on national education standards [26]. Information on key lifestyle variables is obtained via standardized questionnaires. Smoking history included smoking status (current, former, or never), average daily cigarette consumption, total years of smoking, and years since cessation for former smokers. Alcohol history included drinking status (current, former, or never), type of alcoholic beverage intake, average frequency and quantity of alcohol consumption, and years since cessation for former drinkers. To broaden lifestyle assessment, additional data will be collected in future participants, including physical activity and sedentary behavior (using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire, IPAQ), sleep quality (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, PSQI), and dietary behaviors (e.g., frequency of fruit, vegetable, meat, salt, oil, tea, and coffee intake), and social engagement (participation in group activities). Digital cognitive assessments and immersive virtual reality-based paradigms will also be introduced to capture ecologically valid measures of cognition and daily functioning.

Family history of AD and other dementias, as well as age of onset and degree of relationship, is documented based on reports of participants or their caregivers. Trained staff also collect the medical history during the structural interviews, with age of onset and treatment documented, if applicable. At baseline and follow-up, current and past use of medications for AD and other mental and cognitive disorders, such as cholinesterase inhibitors (e.g., donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine) and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist (e.g., memantine), were recorded through structured interviews and verified against medical records. We also record the duration of use, diagnosis for prescription and dosage to facilitate analysis of treatment patterns and associations with clinical outcomes.

MRI and processing

Among the first 1,013 participants, 346 underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at baseline as part of their clinical and research evaluation, performed on a 1.5-T scanner using standardized protocols. The structural imaging protocol includes high-resolution three-dimensional T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequences for volumetric analysis, T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences for assessing white matter hyperintensities (WMH), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) or gradient-echo sequences for detecting cerebral microbleeds. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is acquired in selected participants to evaluate white matter microstructural integrity. In addition, functional MRI (fMRI) is performed, including both resting-state acquisitions and task-based paradigms. The MRI image preprocessing procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and staging

Since 2021, PET imaging has been performed in 215 out of 729 eligible participants, selected according to clinical presentation, research objectives, and informed consent. The protocol includes AV45 amyloid-β (Aβ) PET using 18F-labeled tracers to detect fibrillar amyloid deposition and AV1451 tau PET to detect tau aggregation. For the current study, amyloid and tau positivity are determined visually by two trained nuclear medicine physicians according to established clinical interpretation guidelines [27, 28]. In cases of disagreement, a third senior physician independently reviewed the scans and made the final determination. In the next phase, we plan to derive quantitative SUVR data following the image-processing pipeline of ADNI [29, 30]. With the increased availability of PET scanners, we anticipate a higher imaging rate in subsequent recruitment phases and aim to achieve ≥ 50% PET coverage in the full cohort. The PET image preprocessing procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods.

PET imaging results are integrated according to the AT(N) classification system: “A” (amyloid) based on Aβ-PET positivity, and “T” (tau) based on tau-PET positivity [23]. For “N” (neurodegeneration) markers, we plan to include MRI-based atrophy measures (e.g., hippocampal volume and cortical thickness) and plasma/CSF neurodegenerative biomarkers once ongoing biomarker assays and imaging analyses are completed.

Longitudinal imaging is planned to track biomarker progression.

Bio-sample collection

Biospecimens are collected from eligible and consenting BCAS participants at baseline and follow-up visits. Venous blood is drawn following an overnight fast. Plasma samples are collected in EDTA tubes and serum samples in serum separation tubes, centrifuged at 1500 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and stored in 0.5–1.0 mL aliquots within five hours of collection. Serum/plasma, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and red cells are separated, aliquoted, and preserved at −80 °C for future analyses. Whole blood hematological and biochemical assays, including glucose, lipid profile, liver and kidney function, blood counts, folate, vitamin B12, and ferritin, are performed at the hospital’s central laboratory as standard care procedures. Ongoing biomarker assays (including AD-specific biomarkers and other neurodegenerative biomarkers) will allow us to achieve a more precise biological characterization and staging of participants across the full AD continuum.

CSF collection will be performed in future eligible participants who meet predefined clinical and research criteria and provide written informed consent, using lumbar puncture in the lateral decubitus position at the L3/L4 or L4/L5 interspace, with aliquots stored at −80 °C. With the protocol and infrastructure in place, CSF collection will be piloted among participants enrolled from January 2026 onward and subsequently incorporated into future follow-up waves.

Cognitive and functional phenotyping

BCAS participants undergo comprehensive cognitive assessments conducted by trained neuropsychologists using standardized instruments. The protocol is designed to evaluate multiple cognitive domains and differentiate between AD and other dementia subtypes using cognitive tests that have been previously validated in Chinese. In addition to the MoCA and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) assessing global cognition, we administered Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) [31] and Logical Memory Test (LMT) [32] to assess memory, Trail Making Test (TMT) Parts 1 & 2 [33] and Stroop Color-Word Test [34] to assess executive function and attention, Boston Naming Test (BNT) and Categorical Fluency Test [35] to assess language, and Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCF) and Clock Drawing Test (CDT) to assess visuospatial function [36]. Alternate forms of AVLT are used to reduce practice effects.

Scores for each test are converted to z-scores by dividing the differences between individual scores from the cohort-wide mean at baseline by the cohort-wide standard deviation at baseline and reordered so that positive scores reflect better performance. An average of the contributing z-scores is obtained by cognitive domain and re-normalized to form four domain-specific cognitive z-scores, and the global cognitive composite score will be computed by averaging the domain-specific cognitive z-scores and re-normalized. Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR, global rating) was administered to assess the severity of symptoms. We also include the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) to screen for depressive symptoms and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI-Q) for behavioral and psychological symptoms for patients and informants/caregivers.

Cognitive diagnoses (e.g., CU, MCI, AD dementia) are determined through multidisciplinary consensus meetings involving neurologists, neuropsychologists, and geriatric psychiatrists, following the established NIA-AA criteria for AD, [37] Petersen criteria for MCI, [38] and DSM-5 for major neurocognitive disorder (or dementia) [39].

Outcome follow-up

Longitudinal follow-up will be conducted on an ongoing basis without a predetermined limit, with repeat cognitive testing, neuroimaging (as clinically indicated), clinical diagnosis update, and biospecimens collection. Key outcomes monitored include cognitive changes measured using the above-mentioned strategies, functional decline, diagnostic transitions, and mortality and major clinical events verified via telephone interviews and review of hospital records. To capture the living experience of individuals across the AD continuum, BCAS incorporates a structured set of patient-reported outcome (PRO) and quality of life (QoL) assessments. EuroQol (EQ)−5D-5L is administered both to the patient and to an informant/caregiver, which has been validated in Chinese populations [40, 41]. Participants who decline or are unable to attend in-person assessments will be contacted by telephone.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses in this study aimed to describe the baseline characteristics of the study participants rather than to test new hypotheses. Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and counts and percentages for categorical variables.

Distributions of cognitive test scores (test scores and domain-specific and global cognitive z-scores) at baseline were presented by clinical stage (CU, MCI, AD dementia, and other dementias) and biological stages defined by amyloid and tau PET positivity. Similarly, measures of psychobehavioural status, indicated by Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) activities of daily living (ADL), and neuropsychiatric inventory (NPI) were presented by cliical diagnosis. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess overall group differences in cognitive performance, and t-test for pairwise between-group comparisons. For psychobehavioural performances (non-normal), we applied Kruskal–Wallis test to assess overall group differences, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for pairwise between-group comparisons. Bonferroni corrections were applied for between-group comparisons to address multiplicity. To explore clinical-biological constructs, we assessed cognitive performance stratified by clinical diagnosis and amyloid PET status (A − vs A +), with CU A − group set as reference.

To further examine the data quality and reliability, multivariable linear regression models were used to examine associations between established risk factors and cognitive performance. Effect estimates were presented as beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For each step of analysis, we used a complete-case approach, including only participants with available data on all cognitive domains.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between December 2016 and January 2025, the first 1,013 participants were enrolled in the BCAS (Table 2). The mean age was 66.3 years (SD 9.6), with a balanced sex distribution (49.8% women, 50.2% men). Participants had on average 9.7 years of education (SD 5.6), and most were of Han ethnicity (99.8%). Around one fourth reported a history of smoking (25.3%), and 27.0% reported alcohol consumption history. The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension (41.4%), diabetes (14.6%), and hypercholesterolemia (12.0%). Less common pre-existing conditions included chronic kidney disease (1.2%), asthma (0.5%), and rheumatoid arthritis (1.2%). The mean BMI was 23.0 kg/m2 (SD 3.4), and the mean MoCA score was 19.2 (SD 6.1). Among 37% participants with an education fewer than four years, MoCA-Basic was administered, with a mean score of 17.3 (SD 6.4). Cognitive performance across clinical and pathological stages

Figure 2a shows the distribution of baseline cognitive test scores across clinical diagnostic groups. As expected, participants with AD and other dementias performed worst across all tests, followed by those with MCI, while the CU group had the highest scores. Consistent results were observed for domain-specific and global cognitive function z-scores (Fig. 2b-c), with AD participants performing significantly worse than both MCI and CU groups (all ANOVA P < 0.001; Table S4).

Across clinical diagnostic groups, the prevalence of amyloid and tau PET positivity demonstrated expected gradients along the AD continuum, with A + rates of 42.9% in CU participants, 68.9% in MCI, and 93.2% in AD dementia, and corresponding T + rates of 0%, 27.8%, and 100%, respectively (Table S5). Figure 3 illustrates cognitive test performance stratified by Aβ and tau PET status. Participants with positive PET scans exhibited poorer global and domain-specific cognitive performance compared with those with negative scans. Tau PET positivity showed a numerically larger cognitive z-score difference compared with amyloid PET positivity. (Fig. 3b). For instance, the global cognitive z-score was −0.530 (95% CI −0.794 to −0.266) comparing A + versus A-, and −0.973 (95% CI −1.358 to −0.587) comparing T + versus T- (Table S6). The largest domain-specific differences were observed for visuospatial function: −0.444 (95% CI −0.749 to −0.138) for A + versus A-, and −1.236 (95% CI −1.682 to −0.789) for T + versus T-. Additionally adjusting for clinical group attenuated the associations, but remained in the same direction, and the relationship with the memory domain remained statistically significant. In an exploratory analysis by clinical-biological stage, cognitive performance declined primarily by clinical status, with further differences by amyloid status in select domains, particularly memory and executive function (Figure S2).

Associations between risk factors and cognitive performance

We further explored the associations between established risk factors and cognitive performance. In the multivariable models (Table 3), older age was consistently associated with poorer global cognition by 0.016 z-score (95%CI: 0.023 to 0.010) and most domain-specific scores, except visuospatial function. Each additional year of education was positively associated with all cognitive domains, with the strongest association observed for language. Smoking history was not significantly related to global cognition after adjustment, and alcohol consumption was modestly associated with poorer memory (β = −0.186, 95% CI −0.345 to −0.027). Tea consumption was linked to better performance in all domains, with the largest difference observed for global cognition (β = 0.342, 95% CI 0.211 to 0.474 comparing drinkers versus non-drinkers). Among medical conditions, diabetes was associated with poorer global cognition (β = −0.170, 95% CI −0.336 to −0.004) and executive function (β = −0.184, 95% CI −0.358 to −0.010). Higher BMI was modestly but consistently associated with better cognitive scores across domains.

Discussion

The BCAS is a deeply phenotyped, clinically integrated memory clinic cohort cohort based on a memory clinic to characterize the biological and clinical progression of cognitive impairment in China. This paper reports the design and baseline characteristics of the first batch of participants, spanning CU, MCI, and dementias due to AD and other causes. Ongoing follow-up will enable tracking of disease progression over time.

Compared with previous studies among community-dwelling older adults, such as CHARLS (aged 45 + years) [10] and CLHLS (aged 65 + years) [9], most of which include relatively brief cognitive assessments without biomarker information, BCAS provides more comprehensive data on neuroimaging and biomarkers. It better reflects diagnostic workups, cognitive trajectories, and disease heterogeneity among individuals seeking clinical services, although this clinic-based cohort may be less representative of the general population. A distinctive strength of BCAS is its foundation in a hospital setting in a megacity, which enables the recruitment of participants spanning the full spectrum of the AD continuum and representing diverse educational and socioeconomic backgrounds. The longitudinal structure, with repeated assessments, allows for within-individual comparisons and studying disease progression.

Globally, several deeply phenotyped cohorts have informed understanding of AD mechanisms, including the ADNI [25], the Swedish Biomarkers For Identifying Neurodegenerative Disorders Early and Reliably (BioFINDER) Study [42], and the Stanford Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center cohort [43]. BCAS complements these efforts by focusing on Chinese clinical populations, which have been underrepresented in previous biomarker research. The cognitive performance of participants demonstrate expected patterns: gradient differences in cognitive test scores across clinical stages, tau PET showing stronger associations than amyloid PET, and risk factor associations consistent with prior evidence. These early findings support the reliability of BCAS data and highlight its potential to contribute to AD biomarker validation and risk stratification. As the proportion of participants completing both amyloid and tau PET scans increases in the future, we plan to conduct combined A/T analyses to better characterize biomarker-defined subgroups across the AD continuum.

The BCAS cohort complements existing biomarker-oriented studies in China, including CANDI [20], CANDOR [21], and CABLE [44], with its uniqueness from Southeastern China and enrolling participants across the full AD continuum. Multimodal imaging, including amyloid, tau, and FDG PET, as well as high-resolution structural MRI, enables staging of AD pathology and assessment of neurodegeneration [23]. Comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and validated PROs provide detailed cognitive and functional profiles. Biospecimens are collected and stored under standardized protocols to support biomarker discovery and mechanistic studies. Integration with routine clinical care facilitates the updating of diagnostic classifications and allows feedback to treating clinicians, bridging research and practice. Beyond its depth, the regional context is also a key feature of BCAS. Building on Zhejiang’s rapidly aging, socioeconomically diverse context described earlier, our findings on tea drinking and cognitive performance illustrate how BCAS can capture region-specific risk factor patterns. For example, our baseline analyses suggest a potentially protective association of tea drinking with cognitive performance, a finding that aligns with local drinking habits. Embedding the cohort within this socioeconomic and environmental context strengthens its contribution to understanding AD heterogeneity in diverse populations. This cohort, therefore, supports a wide range of scientific investigations and provides an infrastructure for future ancillary studies.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, given that the study is sited at a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, a well-resourced megacity, there could be potential referral bias and limited generalizability to non-care-seeking or rural populations. Secondly, while procedures such as PET imaging and CSF collection provide valuable data, they are resource-intensive and may not be feasible for all participants, possibly introducing selection bias. Our aim was to achieve > 50% coverage of PET imaging in the cohort. In addition, while we aimed to further expand the scope of lifestyle factor assessments, many of them relied on self-reports and are subject to recall bias and measurement error, especially for the participants with existing cognitive deficits. Finally, because only a small number of CU participants underwent tau PET due to limited resource, the tau PET findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In conclusion, BCAS addresses an important gap in dementia research in China as a biomarker-rich, clinically integrated, and longitudinally followed memory clinic cohort. It complements population-based studies and contributes to global efforts to understand AD pathophysiology, progression, and heterogeneity.
