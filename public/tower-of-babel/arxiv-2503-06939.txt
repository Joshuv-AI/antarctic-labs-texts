Quantization of nonlinear non-Hamiltonian systems
Andy Chia, Wai-Keong Mok, Leong-Chuan Kwek, Changsuk Noh

Abstract
Several important dynamical systems are in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , defined by the pair of differential equations ( x ′ , y ′ ) = ( f ⁡ ( x , y ) , g ⁡ ( x , y ) ) (x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(f(x,y),g(x,y)) . A question of fundamental importance is how such systems might behave quantum mechanically. In developing quantum theory, Dirac and others realized that classical Hamiltonian systems can be mapped to their quantum counterparts via canonical quantization. The resulting quantum dynamics is always physical, characterized by completely-positive and trace-preserving evolutions in the Schrödinger picture. However, whether non-Hamiltonian systems can be quantized systematically while respecting the same physical requirements has remained a long-standing problem. Here we resolve this question when f ⁡ ( x , y ) f(x,y) and g ⁡ ( x , y ) g(x,y) are arbitrary polynomials. By leveraging open-systems theory, we prove constructively that every polynomial system admits a physical generator of time evolution in the form of a Lindbladian. We call our method cascade quantization, and demonstrate its power by analyzing several paradigmatic examples of nonlinear dynamics such as bifurcations, noise-activated spiking, and Liénard systems. In effect, our method can quantize any classical system whose f ⁡ ( x , y ) f(x,y) and g ⁡ ( x , y ) g(x,y) are analytic with arbitrary precision. More importantly, cascade quantization is exact. This means restrictive system properties usually assumed in the literature to facilitate quantization, such as weak nonlinearity, rotational symmetry, or semiclassical dynamics, can all be dispensed with by cascade quantization. We also highlight the advantages of cascade quantization over existing proposals, by weighing it against examples from the variational paradigm using Lagrangians, as well as non-variational approaches.

II.2
Formal statement

Let us denote the generator of time evolution by a linear superoperator
{\cal L}
. The state of the system then evolves according to
\rho^{\prime}(t)={\cal L}\rho(t)
. We have assumed
{\cal L}
to be time independent but it can be made time dependent if necessary (e.g. if the system is driven, or if some parameters acquire time dependence). It is actually more intuitive to define a physically valid quantum evolution by thinking directly in terms
\rho(t)

\rho^{\prime}(t)
. Hence we consider the map generated by
{\cal L}

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t)\equiv e^{{\cal L}\,t}\;,

(10)

for which
\rho(t)={\cal N}(t)\rho(0)

\{{\cal N}(t)\,|\,t\geq 0\}
is called a one-parameter semigroup, because it satisfies the semigroup property

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t)\,{\cal N}(s)={}

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t+s)\;,\quad\forall\;t,s\geq 0\;.

(11)

We now define a valid quantization of (
7

{\cal L}
such that, for any

(i)

\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}_{t}={\rm Tr}\big[\hat{a}\,{\cal L}\,\rho(t)\big]=\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle_{t}
,

(ii)

{\cal N}(t)\,\rho(0)\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{H})
,
\,\forall\,\rho(0)\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{H})
,

(iii)

{\cal N}(t)
is completely positive  .

Here

denotes the set of density operators on the system Hilbert space

While the quantization problem defined by conditions (i)–(iii) is precise, it only constrains the form of
{\cal L}
implicitly. To make the problem more approachable, we leverage a powerful theorem from open-systems theory due to Lindblad
Lin76
, Gorini, Kossakowski, and Sudarshan
GKS76
. It states that (
10
) forms a semigroup of completely-positive trace-preserving maps if and only if
{\cal L}
has the form,

\displaystyle{\cal L}=-i\,[\hat{H},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,\eta_{s}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{s}]\;,

(12)

for some Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}
,
N
arbitrary operators
\hat{L}_{1},\hat{L}_{2},\ldots,\hat{L}_{N}
, and an associated set of non-negative constants
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{N}
. We have defined in (
12
) the dissipator (another superoperator parameterized by
\hat{L}
),

\displaystyle{\cal D}[\hat{L}\,]\equiv\hat{L}\,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,\hat{L}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\,\hat{L}^{\dagger}\,\hat{L}\,\text{\Large$\cdot$}-\frac{1}{2}\,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,\hat{L}^{\dagger}\,\hat{L}\;.

(13)

Note that we are now using a dot to denote where an input operator appears when it is acted on by a superoperator. For example, if
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
are any two operators, and if
{\cal S}=\hat{A}\,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,\hat{A}^{\dagger}
, then
{\cal S}\hat{B}=\hat{A}\hat{B}\hat{A}^{\dagger}
. As advertised in Sec.
I
, we call a generator in the form of (
12
) a Lindbladian. We will also refer to
\hat{L}_{1},\hat{L}_{2},\ldots,\hat{L}_{N}
and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{N}
respectively as Lindblad operators and coefficients. Note that (
12
) includes, as its first term, the Schrödinger equation. Dissipators therefore represent non-Hamiltonian processes. They tend to introduce noise into a quantum system, the effect of which broadens the system’s quasiprobability distribution in quantum phase space. Dissipators can also be difficult to interpret, except for sufficiently simple systems, such as those described by
{\cal D}[\hat{a}]
and
{\cal D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]
(the damped harmonic oscillator and linear amplifier respectively
Aga12
). However, if the Lindblad operator is nonlinear in
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, even a seemingly simple dissipator can be nontrivial to interpret. One such example is
{\cal D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2}]
. Although superficially it can be understood as generalizing one-photon gain to two photons, the noise introduced by
{\cal D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2}]
was shown to be genuinely quantum
Lam67
;
CHN+20
;
CMNK23
.

We have now defined an inverse problem for Lindbladians by (
7
) and (
12
). It is not obvious whether a valid quantization exists for a classical system defined by
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\in\mathbb{P}_{m}
for any
m>0
. Furthermore, knowing the existence of a valid quantization does not imply knowledge of its construction. Our problem is analogous to the inverse problem for Lagrangians in classical mechanics which asks if there exists a Lagrangian whose Euler–Lagrange equation returns,

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}=q(x,x^{\prime})\;,

(14)

where
q(x,x^{\prime})
is given. The answer to this quesion is given by the Helmholtz conditions, which provide the necessary and sufficient criteria for such a Lagrangian to exist
Dou41
;
Eng75
;
Mus09
;
Lop99
. However, to construct the appropriate Lagrangian from
q(x,x^{\prime})
is another question, one that is at least equally important if not more (see e.g. Refs.
Yan72
;
HL02
;
NL07
;
MRS08
;
CN10
;
PM23
and the papers therein). In Sec.
VIII
we prove that the inverse problem for Lindbladians has a solution for all
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\in\mathbb{P}_{m}
, and every
m>0
. We do so constructively, using a strategy that we call cascade quantization (derived in detail in Appendix
C
).

III.2
Generalizations and advantages

The most significant aspect of our quantization method is its exactness. This is so even when we compare our method to other proposals taking an open-systems approach. The literature typically makes one or more approximations to make quantization possible. One such example is to consider the semiclassical limit
ACL21
(or assumptions to the same effect
BGR21
), whereby
\langle{h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\rangle
is replaced by
h(\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}}\rangle)
. Another example is taking a perturbative limit, such as assuming a vanishing nonlinearity
BGR21
;
LS13
;
WNB14
, or vanishing quantumness
HdMPGGZ14
. Our method does not resort to any such approximations, taking only the classical model
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\big(f(x,y),g(x,y)\big)
as input. Below we highlight some difficulties and generalizations that can be handled by our quantization method.

III.2.1
Arbitrary operator orderings and time-dependent systems

Although we have defined the quantum analogue of
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
by a normally-ordered
h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
, this does not restrict the applicability of our method in any way. Given an
h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
for which
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
are ordered arbitrarily, say using anti-normal or symmetric ordering, we can always use
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=\hat{1}
to rewrite
h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
in normal order. Thus, if we can quantize
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
corresponding to normal order, i.e. find an
{\cal L}
such that
\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle
, then we are able to quantize
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
corresponding to any order of
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
. Hence, no loss of generality has incurred by imposing normal ordering in (
7
).

We have so far assumed the Lindbladian to be time independent. This means that only classical systems which are time independent can be quantized. However, the theorem leading to (
12
) can actually be generalized to allow for time-dependent systems. In this case (
10
) is generalized to the time-ordered exponential,

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t,t_{0})={\rm T}_{\triangleleft}\bigg\{\exp\!\bigg[\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\,{\cal L}(\tau)\bigg]\bigg\}\;,

(19)

where
{\rm T}_{\triangleleft}
is defined to be a linear operation such that for any two time-dependent superoperators
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
,

\displaystyle{\rm T}_{\triangleleft}\big\{{\cal A}(t_{1})\,{\cal B}(t_{2})\big\}=\begin{cases}{\cal A}(t_{1})\,{\cal B}(t_{2})\,,\;t_{1}>t_{2}\;,\\
{\cal B}(t_{2})\,{\cal A}(t_{1})\,,\;t_{2}>t_{1}\;.\end{cases}

(20)

The semigroup property in (
11
) is then replaced by the divisibility condition, defined for any
t>t_{1}>t_{0}
, by

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t,t_{1})\,{\cal N}(t_{1},t_{0})={\cal N}(t,t_{0})\;.

(21)

One may then show that an
{\cal L}(t)
is the generator of a divisible set of completely-positive and trace-preserving maps if and only if
RH12
;
Bre12
,

\displaystyle{\cal L}(t)=-i\,[\hat{H}(t),\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+\sum_{s=1}^{N}\,\eta_{s}(t)\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{s}(t)]\;.

(22)

This has the same structure as (
12
) and the same conditions are placed on the parameterizing operators and coefficients as before, but now for all times. In particular, every Lindblad coefficient must now be non-negative for all times, i.e.
\eta_{s}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq t_{0}
and
s=1,2,\ldots,N
.

The generalization to time-dependent systems allows us to probe how quantum nonlinear systems respond to external forcing. Most often, the external force is given by a simple sinusoidal function. However, there is nothing that forbids one to consider an external drive that is stochastic. This means that our quantization method also applies to nonlinear models driven by noise. We will explore such a case in Sec.
V
by quantizing a classical nonlinear system with white noise. However, in doing so, the master equation for the density operator becomes a stochastic differential equation, and the question of how one should interpret such an equation arises (Itô versus Stratonovich)
GZ04
;
Eva13
;
SS19
. Since we have derived our quantization method by assuming normal calculus, the resulting evolution with white noise must be interpreted as a Stratonovich equation. Or stated differently, our quantization rules are derived assuming that
{\cal L}\,dt
is of order
dt
, which can only be consistent with a Stratonovich interpretation.

III.2.2
First-order systems

A significant drawback of the variational approach to quantization is that it relies on Hamiltonians derived from Lagrangians (see Appendix
A
). This is problematic because a Lagrangian, through the Euler–Lagrange equation, is designed to produce (
14
), not (
1
). Lindbladians on the other hand, do not have this defect.

At the core of this problem is the inequivalence between a system defined by a second-order differential equation, and a system defined by a pair of first-order differential equations: Given
x^{\prime\prime}=q(x,x^{\prime})
, it is always possible to find an
f(x,y)
and
g(x,y)
so as to express the second-order equation as a pair of first-order ones. We denote this property as

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}=q(x,x^{\prime})\;\implies\;(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\big(f(x,y),g(x,y)\big)\;.

(23)

All one has to do is let
f(x,y)=y
and
g(x,y)=q(x,y)
. However, the converse is not true:

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}=q(x,x^{\prime})\;\,\quad/\mkern-25.0mu\Longleftarrow\;\;(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\big(f(x,y),g(x,y)\big)\;.

(24)

That is to say, not every first-order system of equations can be written in closed form as
x^{\prime\prime}=q(x,x^{\prime})
, or as
y^{\prime\prime}=p(y,y^{\prime})
for some
p(y,y^{\prime})
. Furthermore, a given second-order equation may correspond to more than one set of first-order equations. Namely, it is possible to find two systems, say
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\big(f(x,y),g(x,y)\big)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\big(r(x,y),s(x,y)\big)
, both satisfying
x^{\prime\prime}=q(x,x^{\prime})
, but for which

\displaystyle\big(r(x,y),s(x,y)\big)\neq\big(f(x,y),g(x,y)\big)\;.

(25)

We can illustrate this point with a simple example. Consider the system

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=f(x,y)=y\;,\quad y^{\prime}=g(x,y)=-x\;.

(26)

This is simply a linear oscillator rotating in phase space in the clockwise direction with constant angular speed. Similarly, a linear oscillator rotating in the counterclockwise direction is given by

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=r(x,y)=-y\;,\quad y^{\prime}=s(x,y)=x\;.

(27)

It is trivial to see that both (
26
) and (
27
) satisfies
x^{\prime\prime}=-x
, but clearly
f(x,y)\neq r(x,y)
and
g(x,y)\neq s(x,y)
.

We have thus shown that a dynamical system can only be defined unambiguously by a set of first-order differential equations. This gives Lindbladians an advantage over Lagrangians for quantization. In Sec.
VII
we give two concrete examples from the literature that are problematic for Lagrangians, but not for the Lindbladian. These are the van der Pol oscillator
SCVC15
, and the so-called unusual Liénard-type oscillator
CSL05
;
CRSL12
. The essential point we wish to make here is that the Lagrangian approach to quantization is flawed at the outset.
10
10

10

One possible reason for using a second-order differential equation to define a system is Newton’s second law. But in nonlinear models,
x
and
y
often represent non-mechanical properties and have no direct relation to Newton’s second law. For example, nonlinear oscillator models are commonly realised using electrical circuits. In this case
x
and
y
obey the familiar circuit laws for voltages and currents. Another example is chemical reactions. In this case one derives the equations of motion for
x
and
y
using the principles of chemical kinetics such as the law of mass action. The resulting model then describes how the concentration of reactant molecules evolve. Even more generally, nonlinear models are sometimes introduced phenomenologically without appealing to any physical principles.

III.2.3
Rotationally-asymmetric systems

Given the Lindblad form to be extremely well known, one might wonder why the inverse problem for Lindbladians had not been solved earlier. Although (
12
) significantly reduces the search space of physical generators of time evolution, finding
\hat{H}
,
\hat{L}_{1},\hat{L}_{2},\ldots,\hat{L}_{N}
, and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{N}
is still difficult. This task is made even more nontrivial by the fact that
{\cal D}[\hat{L}]
depends nonlinearly on
\hat{L}
, and the fact that
\hat{L}
acts on the system state from both the left and right.

There is, however, a special class of systems whose corresponding Lindbladian may be guessed with relative ease. These are systems with rotational symmetry. By this we mean a system
\alpha^{\prime}=h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
for which
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
satisfies
GMK12

\displaystyle h(\alpha\,e^{-i\theta},\alpha^{*}\,e^{i\theta})=h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\,e^{-i\theta}\;,

(28)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant. Such processes can be quantized by dissipators whose Lindblad operator is a function
w(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
such that

\displaystyle{\cal D}\big[w(\hat{a}e^{-i\theta},\hat{a}^{\dagger}e^{i\theta})\big]={\cal D}[w(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})]\;.

(29)

Examples of
w(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
that satisfy (
29
) are
\hat{a}{}^{n}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n}
for all
n>0
. Previous literature using dissipators to quantize non-Hamiltonian terms in
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
has mostly considered rotationally-symmetric processes due to their simplicity.

It helps to see an example of quantization that highlights the difference between systems with rotational symmetry and systems that are rotationally asymmetric. This also puts cascade quantization in the context of ongoing research on quantum nonlinear dynamics. We begin with the simplest rotationally-symmetric processes, given by linear dissipation and amplification with rate
\kappa>0
. These are described by
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\mp\,\kappa\,\alpha
, where the negative sign applies for dissipation, and positive sign for amplification. Rotational symmetry means the system is damped or amplified at equal rates in all directions in phase space. We find, along
x
and
y
,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=\mp\,\kappa\,x\;,\quad y^{\prime}=\mp\,\kappa\,y\;.

(30)

Linear dissipation and amplification can be quantized respectively by
2\kappa\,{\cal D}[\hat{a}]
and
2\kappa\,{\cal D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]
. This example provides another illustration of the inequivalence in defining a system by first- and second-order differential equations. We note that (
30
) gives the familiar second-order equation for a linearly-damped system

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}=\mp\kappa\,x^{\prime}\;.

(31)

This same second-order equation can also be derived from the rotationally-asymmetric process
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=(-i\mp\kappa)(\alpha-\alpha^{*})/2
, which is the same as,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=y\;,\quad y^{\prime}=\mp\kappa\,y\;.

(32)

The difference between (
30

32

;
Gao98a
, which is a linearly-damped system driven by white noise. We will meet noise-driven systems in Sec.
V
.

To give at least one example of a rotationally-symmetric process that is nonlinear, we consider here
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=-\,\gamma\,\alpha^{*}\alpha^{2}
with
\gamma>0
. This generalizes (

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,(x^{2}+y^{2})\,x\;,\quad y^{\prime}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,(x^{2}+y^{2})\,y\;.

(33)

It is straightforward to see that such nonlinear dissipation is quantized by

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}=-i\,\alpha+\kappa\,\alpha-\gamma\,\alpha^{*}\alpha^{2}\;.

We now compare (

IV
Bifurcations

Just as classical bifurcations can be seen to occur in vastly different fields, one can also find bifurcations in a variety of quantum systems. For example, in models motivated by atomic physics (BECs)
STFL06
, solid-state physics (Jahn–Teller and Bose–Hubbard models)
HDMKM04
;
MDMKM10
;
IKV+17
;
YI19
, quantum optics (Jaynes–Cummings and Dicke models)
AM06
;
NFP+06
, nanomechanical systems
MMKM11
, superconducting circuits
MNDM14
;
PBB17
, and recently, in condensed matter physics (the Kitaev chain)
RPR23
. But unlike the classical theory of nonlinear dynamics, there has been no systematic work on quantum bifurcations in the sense of treating their normal forms due to the lack of a quantization method. Here we take a first step towards this end.

An essential point to keep in mind here is that unlike classical systems, quantum systems do not have well-defined paths in phase space due to the inherent noise they possess. Thus, to identify bifurcations in quantum systems we rely on the phase-space quasiprobability representation of quantum states. Amongst the most commonly discussed quasiprobability distributions, the Wigner function is the most favored as it is well-behaved and acts as a witness to nonclassicality when it becomes negative. Given a quantum state
\rho
, the Wigner function is defined by

\displaystyle W(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}ds\;\bra{x+s/2}\rho\ket{x-s/2}\,e^{-isy}\;.

(42)

Thus, long-time properties of a classical system such as attractors are manifested in the quantized system by the peaks of its steady-state Wigner function.

12

The steady-state Wigner function being given by (

This is intuitive since an attractor draws all initial conditions in its basin towards it. By the same token, repellers can be expected to have either zero or relatively low Wigner density at steady state. Low- and high-velocity regions of phase space can also be identified, respectively, with high and low values of the Wigner function. This is because a classical ensemble of initial conditions tend to clump more when travelling through phase-space regions of lower velocity. Bifurcations identified using such a correspondence between a deterministic system and the steady-state distributions of their noisy counterparts are referred to as P-bifurcations in stochastic systems (P being phenomenological)

The phenomenological approach can only explain certain qualitative features of quantum bifurcations. First, information about the quantized system such as the bifurcation point (the parameter value at which the bifurcation occurs) cannot be directly inferred from the classical system. Like classical noise, quantum noise prevents the quantized system from having a well-defined bifurcation point. Of course, it may be possible to formulate an appropriate definition of quantum bifurcations, but that will take us too far from the theme of quantization. Second, and more interestingly, quantum analogues of even very simple bifurcations (the saddle-node and transcritical as we shall see) contain negative values in their Wigner functions. Such features of quantum bifurcations are unidentifiable in the phenomenological approach.

V.2.2
Average effect of noise

We can also get an understanding of how
\Upsilon(t)
affects the system by considering its effect on average. Suppose we are interested in how a function
s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
might change. Then in principle we could use (
75
) to obtain many runs of
\langle{s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\rangle_{\Upsilon}={\rm Tr}[s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})\rho_{\Upsilon}]
and calculate their ensemble average
{\rm E}[\langle{s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\rangle_{\Upsilon}]
. This approach works, but is rather indirect. It does not provide a simple way to capture the average effect of
\Upsilon
on the system. Here we derive an equation that propagates the quantum state of the noise-driven system while averaging over
\Upsilon
. That is, we seek the evolution of a state
\rho
such that, for any
s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})
and any time,

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\big\{s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})\,\rho\big\}={\rm E}\big[\langle{s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\rangle_{\Upsilon}\big]\;.

(81)

Such a state can be found by noting that

\displaystyle{\rm E}\big[\langle{s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\rangle_{\Upsilon}\big]\equiv{}

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\upsilon\,\wp_{\Upsilon}(\upsilon,t)\,{\rm Tr}\big[s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})\,\rho_{\Upsilon}(t)\big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\bigg[s(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})\,\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\upsilon\,\wp_{\Upsilon}(\upsilon,t)\,\rho_{\Upsilon}(t)\bigg]\;,

(82)

where
\wp_{\Upsilon}(\upsilon,t)
is such that
\wp_{\Upsilon}(\upsilon,t)d\upsilon
gives the probability that
\Upsilon(t)\in[\upsilon,\upsilon+d\upsilon]
. Comparing (
81
) to (
82
), we see that

\displaystyle\rho(t)={\rm E}[\rho_{\Upsilon}(t)]=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\upsilon\,\rho_{\Upsilon}(t)\,\wp_{\Upsilon}(\upsilon,t)\;,

(83)

Equation (
83
) implies that an equation of motion for
\rho(t)
can be obtained by averaging (
75
). One way to calculate the ensemble average of (
75

SS19
, given by

\displaystyle d\rho_{\Upsilon}={}

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}\,\rho_{\Upsilon}\,dt+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\,{\cal D}[\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\rho_{\Upsilon}\,dt

\displaystyle+i\,\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2}\,}\,[\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger},\rho_{\Upsilon}]\,dW\;.

(84)

As (
V.2.2
) is now an Itô stochastic differential equation, it obeys Itô calculus for which

has zero mean and
(dW)^{2}=dt
. In particular,
dW
is independent of
\rho_{\Upsilon}
at all times, so the average of (

\displaystyle{\rm E}[\rho_{\Upsilon}\,dW]={\rm E}[\rho_{\Upsilon}]\,{\rm E}[dW]=0\;.

(85)

We thus have, using (

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}={\cal L}_{0}\,\rho+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\,{\cal D}[\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\rho\;.

(86)

Equation (

\Upsilon

according to

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}\;\longrightarrow\;{\cal L}_{\kappa}={\cal L}_{0}+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\,{\cal D}[\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\;.

(87)

Let us therefore consider the effect of
(\kappa^{2}/2)\,{\cal D}[\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=\kappa^{2}\,{\cal D}[\hat{x}]
on the system. Using only

and

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{x}}\rangle_{t}=\langle{\hat{x}}\rangle_{0}\;,\quad\langle{\hat{y}}\rangle_{t}=\langle{\hat{y}}\rangle_{0}\;.

(88)

That is, the noise has no effect on the system’s mean motion. The noise does however introduce diffusion around the system’s mean trajectory. It is straightforward to show that

\displaystyle\langle{(\Delta\hat{x})^{2}}\rangle_{t}=\langle{(\Delta\hat{x})^{2}}\rangle_{0}\;,\quad\langle{(\Delta\hat{y})^{2}}\rangle_{t}=\langle{(\Delta\hat{y})^{2}}\rangle_{0}+\kappa^{2}\,t\;,

(89)

where we have defined

Liénard systems

VI.1
Liénard’s theorem

A major branch of nonlinear dynamics is the study of periodic systems, especially systems with a limit cycle. Such cyclic behavior can be found in a variety of natural and man-made systems, such as the weather, the beating heart, or chemical reactions. Limit-cycle oscillators are also indispensable for the study of synchronization, which examines how multiple such oscillators behave when coupled

PRK01
;
BJPS09
. A fundamental question in the theory of nonlinear oscillators is how stable limit cycles arise. More precisely, given
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\big(f(x,y),g(x,y)\big)
, how should one choose
f(x,y)
and

so that a limit cycle can be obtained? Liénard provided an answer to this question with the following theorem
Str15
. Consider the system defined by

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=y\;,\quad y^{\prime}=-\,u(x)-v(x)\,y\;,

(90)

where
u(x)
and
v(x)
are continuously differentiable functions for all
x
. The system (
90

(i)

u(x)=-u(-x)
,

(ii)

u(x)>0\,,\;\forall\;x>0
,

(iii)

v(x)=v(-x)
,

and if the antiderivative of
v(x)
, i.e.

\displaystyle V(x)=\int^{x}_{0}ds\;v(s)\;,

(91)

is such that
17
17

17

In words,
V(x)
is such that it has exactly one positive root
x_{0}
; is negative for
0<x<x_{0}
; is positive and monotonically increasing for
x>x_{0}
.

(iv)

\exists\;\!!\,x_{0}>0:

V(x_{0})=0
,

(v)

V(x)<0
,
\,\forall\;x\in(0,x_{0})
,

(vi)

V(x_{2})\geq V(x_{1})>0\,,\;\forall\;x_{2}>x_{1}>x_{0}
.

VII
Comparison to literature

VII.1
Van der Pol oscillator

VII.1.1
Variational quantization

It is most appropriate to begin comparing our quantization method against the literature by referring to the van der Pol oscillator. It was defined in (

VII.2

VII.2.1
Variational quantization

Another system which has received some attention in the literature is an oscillator whose position evolution has the following Liénard structure

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}+k\,x\,x^{\prime}+\frac{k^{2}}{9}\;x^{3}+x=0\;,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle f(x,y)=y\;,

\displaystyle y^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}=-k\,x\;.

24
24

24

Nevertheless, there is no contradiction between (

\displaystyle x^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle y^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=-x+\frac{2k}{3}\,x\,y\;.

Relative to variational approaches, there are a lot fewer non-variational techniques, especially ones targeting general systems. Our approach in this work has been to treat the classical model as an open system. This is natural, and not surprisingly, others have also tried to quantize nonlinear systems by following an open-systems approach, though none have prescribed an exact and general way of deriving the quantum generator of time evolution

;
BGR21
;
LS13
;
WNB14

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{x}}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle{G(\hat{y})}\rangle\;,\quad\langle{\hat{y}}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle{F(\hat{x})}\rangle\;.

(118)

The problem defined by (
118

and

25
25

25

A similar technique has been used to quantize a system with a specific

In contrast, our approach does not require anything beyond the results of Gorini, Kossakowski, Sudarshan

Not all non-variational methods follow an open-systems approach and here we discuss one such method due to Tarasov
Tar01
. The idea of Tarasov is to map a classical generator of time evolution to its quantum analogue as a generalized Weyl transform which permits differential operators
Tar01

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}=f(x,y)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+g(x,y)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\;.

(119)

That is, the time evolution of an arbitrary function
s(x,y)
, is described by
s^{\prime}=\mathscr{K}s
. Tarasov’s goal is then to map
\mathscr{K}
to a superoperator
{\cal K}
, which governs the dynamics of an arbitrary operator
\hat{s}
, i.e.
\hat{s}^{\prime}={\cal K}\,\hat{s}

Tar01
. While the method appears to be very general, it does not respect fundamental principles. For example, we can show that Tarasov’s procedure violates the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. To do this, it is sufficient to consider a simple system. Here we take the example of a damped harmonic oscillator
26
26

26

This is the same as (30) of Ref.
Tar01
for

, with
(q_{1},p_{1})=(x,y)

.

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=y\;,\quad y^{\prime}=-x-\gamma\,y\;,

(120)

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}=y\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-(x+\gamma\,y)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\;.

Following Tarasov’s prescription, the quantized version of

can be found to be (setting

\displaystyle{\cal K}=\frac{i}{2}\,[\hat{y}^{2}+\hat{x}^{2},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+i\,\frac{\gamma}{2}\big(\hat{y}\,[\hat{x},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+[\hat{x},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]\,\hat{y}\big)\;.

Consider now the dynamics of

\displaystyle[\hat{x},\hat{y}]^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{\prime}\hat{y}+\hat{x}\,\hat{y}^{\prime}-\hat{y}^{\prime}\hat{x}-\hat{y}\,\hat{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle[\hat{x}^{\prime},\hat{y}]+[\hat{x},\hat{y}^{\prime}]=[{\cal K}\hat{x},\hat{y}]+[\hat{x},{\cal K}\hat{y}]\;.

VIII.1
General strategy

We will split the quantization of
\alpha^{\prime}=h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\in\mathbb{P}_{m}
for any

. To this end, we write a general polynomial of degree

\displaystyle h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\sum_{n=0}^{m}\,h_{n}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(129)

where

is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
, i.e. a linear combination of
\alpha^{*}{}^{k}\alpha^{n-k}
for

. Thus, to quantize a general polynomial system, we only need to quantize an arbitrary homogeneous polynomial. To facilitate the quantization of
h_{n}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
we futher decompose it as

\displaystyle h_{n}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\lambda_{n}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{n}+(1-\delta_{n,0})\sum_{k=0}^{K(n)}\,h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;.

(130)

Note that for
n=0
we have
h_{0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\lambda_{0}
, while for
n\geq 1
we have defined,

\displaystyle h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\mu_{n,k}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{k}\alpha^{n-k}+\nu_{n,k}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{n-k-1}\alpha^{k+1}\;,

(131)

where
\lambda_{n}
,
\mu_{n,k}
, and
\nu_{n,k}
are arbitrary complex constants. The top limit of the sum in (
130
) is defined by

\displaystyle K(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{n}{2}-1\;,\quad\text{$n$ even}\\
\frac{n-1}{2}\;,\quad\text{$n$ odd}\end{cases},

(132)

It is straightforward to check that all homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
are encompassed by (
130
)–(
132
).

We have written

in the form of (
130
) to limit our attention to quantizing
\lambda_{n}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{n}
and

. It is straightforward to see that for all

, the Hamiltonian which quantizes

is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{n}=-\frac{i}{n+1}\,(\lambda_{n}^{*}\,\hat{a}^{n+1}-\lambda_{n}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n+1})\;.

(133)

The problem of quantizing a general polynomial system now boils down to finding a valid quantization
{\cal L}_{n,k}
for

. Since the Lindbladian is a linear superoperator, we may write a valid quantization of

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\sum_{n=0}^{m}\,{\cal L}_{n}\;,

(134)

where
{\cal L}_{n}
quantizes
h_{n}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
, and has the form

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{n}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{n},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+(1-\delta_{n,0})\sum_{k=0}^{K(n)}\,{\cal L}_{n,k}\;,

(135)

assuming
{\cal L}_{n,k}
to quantize
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
. The construct
{\cal L}_{n,k}
we first note a general difficulty in constructing Lindbladians. Given a polynomial system specified by
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
, it is not at all straightforward to find a Lindbladian which generates exclusively
\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle
. In general it is easier to find a Lindbladian which produces
\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle
plus some additional terms. With this in mind, let us denote by
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
the Lindbladian which quantizes all the necessary terms in
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
, but which also generates unwanted byproduct terms. That is,
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
quantizes a system of the form

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}=h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(136)

where
h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
are the byproduct terms not in
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
. These unwanted terms are always of a lower degree than
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
. This will be seen in Sec.
IX
for the case of degree-three systems, and proven in Appendix
C
for an arbitrary polynomial system. Assuming that we can quantize these lower-degree polynomials, we can then cancel them off by adding to
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
another Lindbladian
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}
, which quantizes

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}=-\,h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;.

(137)

A valid quantization of
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
is thus given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{n,k}={\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}+{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}\;.

(138)

We can now see a path towards quantizing a system in
\mathbb{P}_{m}
for any
m
: First, we can already quantize
\mathbb{P}_{3}
with the table in Fig.
1
. Suppose now we want to quantize
\mathbb{P}_{4}
. This means that we require
{\cal L}^{\in}_{4,0}
and
{\cal L}^{\in}_{4,1}
. Since we can quantize
\mathbb{P}_{3}
,
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{4,0}
and
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{4,1}
can be assumed to be at hand. Hence we can quantize
\mathbb{P}_{4}
provided that we have
{\cal L}^{\in}_{4,0}
and
{\cal L}^{\in}_{4,1}
. And if we can quantize
\mathbb{P}_{4}
, then by the same token we can quantize
\mathbb{P}_{5}
if we can find
{\cal L}^{\in}_{5,0}
,
{\cal L}^{\in}_{5,1}
, and
{\cal L}^{\in}_{5,2}
. As with the previous case, having quantized
\mathbb{P}_{4}
, we can assume to have
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{5,0}
,
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{5,1}
, and
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{5,2}
. Below we provide the explicit forms of
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
for all values of
n
and
k
by considering the cases of even and odd
n
in turn.

We refer to our method as cascade quantization—since to quantize
\mathbb{P}_{m}
requires that we know how to quantize
\mathbb{P}_{m-1}
, and to quantize
\mathbb{P}_{m-1}
we need to know how to quantize
\mathbb{P}_{m-2}
, and so forth. Therefore
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is quantized successively, by tacking on (or cascading) Lindbladians from
\mathbb{P}_{m-1}
.

VIII.2
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
for even
n

We prove in Appendix
C.1
that for
n\geq 2
(assumed even),

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{n,k},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{n,k}]+\kappa_{n,k}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}_{n,k}]\;,

(139)

where we have defined the Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{n,k}={}

\displaystyle i\,\frac{(k+2)\,\mu_{n,k}-(k+1)\,\nu_{n,k}^{*}}{(n+2)(k+1)}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k+1}\,\hat{a}^{n-k}

(140)

\displaystyle-i\,\frac{(k+2)\,\mu^{*}_{n,k}-(k+1)\,\nu_{n,k}}{(n+2)(k+1)}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}\;,

and the Lindblad operators and coefficients,

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n}{2}-k-1}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}+\sigma_{n,k}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n}{2}+1}\;,

(141)

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{n,k}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n}{2}+1}\;,\quad\kappa_{n,k}=|\sigma_{n,k}|^{2}\;.

(142)

We have defined for ease of writing,

\displaystyle\sigma_{n,k}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{2\,[(n-k)\,\mu_{n,k}+(k+1)\,\nu_{n,k}^{*}]}{(n+2)(k+1)}\;.

(143)

The explicit form of
h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
is provided in Appendix
C.1
. The important point is that it has degree less than
n
, so it is valid to assume that we have
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}
.

VIII.3
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
for odd
n

VIII.3.1
0\leq k<K

It is shown in Appendix
C.2.1
, that for
n\geq 3
(assumed odd), and
k=0,1,\ldots,K-1
,

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}={}

\displaystyle-i\,[\hat{H}_{n,k}\,,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{n,k}]

\displaystyle+\kappa^{-}_{n,k}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}^{-}_{n,k}]+\kappa^{+}_{n,k}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}^{+}_{n,k}]\;,

(144)

where we have defined the Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{n,k}={}

\displaystyle i\,\frac{(\mu_{n,k}-\nu_{n,k}^{*})}{n+1}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k+1}\,\hat{a}^{n-k}

\displaystyle-i\,\frac{(\mu^{*}_{n,k}-\nu_{n,k})}{n+1}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}\;,

(145)

and the Lindblad operators and coefficients,

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n,k}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}-k}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}+\sigma_{n,k}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,

(146)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{-}_{n,k}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\kappa^{-}_{n,k}=-\frac{4}{n+1}\,\theta(-\zeta_{n,k})\,\zeta_{n,k}\;,

(147)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{+}_{n,k}=\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\kappa^{+}_{n,k}=\frac{4}{n+1}\,\theta(\zeta_{n,k})\,\zeta_{n,k}\;.

(148)

Recall for convenience that we defined the Heaviside step function in (
16
) as

\displaystyle\theta(x)=\begin{cases}0,\;x\leq 0\\
1,\;x>0\end{cases}.

(149)

We have also defined in (
146
)–(
148
) the constants

\displaystyle\sigma_{n,k}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{2\,\big[(n-k)\mu_{n,k}+(k+1)\nu^{*}_{n,k}\big]}{(n+1)(k+1)}\;,

(150)

\displaystyle\zeta_{n,k}={}

\displaystyle\frac{(n-3)-(n+1)|\sigma_{n,k}|^{2}}{4}-k\;.

(151)

As with the case of even
n
, we leave the expression for
h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
to Appendix
C.2.1
and just note here that it is a polynomial of lower degree than
n
. This means that we can again assume to have
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}
.

VIII.3.2
k=K

Finally, for
n\geq 3
(assumed odd) and
k=K=(n-1)/2
, we have from Appendix
C.2.2
,

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,K}=-\,i\,[\hat{H}_{n,K}\,,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+\gamma^{-}_{n,K}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{-}_{n,K}]+\gamma^{+}_{n,K}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{+}_{n,K}]\;,

(152)

where the Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{n,K}=-\,\frac{2\,\Im[\epsilon_{n}]}{n+1}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,

(153)

and the dissipators are defined by

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{-}_{n,K}=\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\gamma^{-}_{n,K}=-\,\frac{4\,\Re[\epsilon_{n}]}{n+1}\,\theta\big(\!-\!\Re[\epsilon_{n}]\big)\;,

(154)

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{+}_{n,K}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\gamma^{+}_{n,K}=\frac{4\,\Re[\epsilon_{n}]}{n+1}\,\theta\big(\Re[\epsilon_{n}]\big)\;.

(155)

Note in (
153
)–(
155
) we have defined

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}=\mu_{n,K}+\nu_{n,K}\;,

(156)

and recall from (
15
) that we are using
\Re[z]
and
\Im[z]
to denote the real and imaginary parts of an arbitrary complex number
z
respectively. As Appendix
C.2.2
shows,
h^{\notin}_{n,K}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
has degree less than
n
, which allows us to construct
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,K}
.

IX
Cascade quantization: Examples

We now illustrate how cascade quantization works by considering the four lowest degree polynomials, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{m}
for
m=0,1,2,3
, considering each case successively. One may also wish to contrast the results here with the table in Fig.
1
.

IX.1
Degree-zero polynomial
(m=0)

Here we have the simplest case of a system defined by a complex-valued constant

\displaystyle h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=h_{0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\lambda_{0}\;.

(157)

This corresponds to an arbitrary displacement in quantum phase space which can be generated by a single Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=-i\,(\lambda_{0}^{*}\,\hat{a}-\lambda_{0}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger})\;.

(158)

The corresponding Lindbladian is simply

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{0},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]\;.

(159)

IX.2
Degree-one polynomial
(m=1)

Since we have quantized
\lambda_{0}
, we only need to focus on a homogeneous polynomial of degree one. Setting
n=1
in (
130
)–(
132
) we have,

\displaystyle h_{1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\lambda_{1}\,\alpha^{*}+h_{1,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(160)

where

\displaystyle h_{1,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\epsilon_{1}\,\alpha\;.

(161)

From (
135
) the quantizing Lindbladian is thus given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{1}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{1},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+{\cal L}_{1,0}\;,

(162)

for which (
133
) gives

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}=-\frac{i}{2}\,(\lambda_{1}^{*}\,\hat{a}^{2}-\lambda_{1}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2})\;.

(163)

To find
{\cal L}_{1,0}
we can now use Sec.
VIII.3.2
with
n=1
and
K=0
. This gives

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{1,0}={}

\displaystyle-i\,[\hat{H}_{1,0},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+\gamma_{1,0}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{-}]+\gamma_{1,0}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{+}]\;.

(164)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1,0}=-\Im[\epsilon_{1}]\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\;,

(165)

and

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{1,0}^{-}=\hat{a}\;,\quad\gamma_{1,0}^{-}=-2\,\theta\big(\!-\!\Re[\epsilon_{1}]\big)\,\Re[\epsilon_{1}]\;,

(166)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{1,0}^{+}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\;,\quad\gamma_{1,0}^{+}=2\,\theta\big(\Re[\epsilon_{1}]\big)\,\Re[\epsilon_{1}]\;.

(167)

Note in this case
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{1,0}
vanishes so that

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{1,0}={\cal L}^{\in}_{1,0}\;.

(168)

Thus we see that a general degree-one polynomial given by

\displaystyle h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=h_{0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h_{1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(169)

is quantized by the Lindbladian

\displaystyle{\cal L}={}

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}+{\cal L}_{1}

(170)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-i\,[\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{1},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]-i\,[\hat{H}_{1,0},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1,0}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{-}]+\gamma_{1,0}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{+}]\;.

(171)

The usefulness of cascade quantization only becomes obvious when quantizing systems of degree two or higher, where (
138
) has a nonvanishing
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}
.

IX.3
Degree-two polynomial
(m=2)

We now encounter the simplest nonlinear system, given by a quadratic polynomial. As we have already quantized a general degree-one system, it is sufficient to quantize a homogeneous quadratic polynomial here. Setting
n=2
in (
130
)–(
132
) gives

\displaystyle h_{2}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\lambda_{2}\,\alpha^{*}+h_{2,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(172)

with

\displaystyle h_{2,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\mu_{2,0}\,\alpha^{2}+\nu_{2,0}\,\alpha^{*}\alpha\;.

(173)

Using (
135
) the Lindbladian corresponding to
h_{2}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
is thus

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{2}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{2},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+{\cal L}_{2,0}\;,

(174)

where (
133
) gives

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{2}=-\frac{i}{3}\,(\lambda_{2}^{*}\,\hat{a}^{3}-\lambda_{2}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{3})\;.

(175)

In contrast to the previous example of a degree-one polynomial, we now have

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{2,0}={\cal L}^{\in}_{2,0}+{\cal L}^{\notin}_{2,0}\;.

(176)

The form of
{\cal L}^{\in}_{2,0}
is given by Sec.
VIII.2
with
n=2
and
k=0
as

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{2,0}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{2,0},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{2,0}]+\kappa_{2,0}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}_{2,0}]\;,

(177)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{2,0}=-\frac{i}{4}\,\big[\,(2\,\mu_{2,0}^{*}-\nu_{2,0})\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2}\hat{a}-(2\,\mu_{2,0}-\nu_{2,0}^{*})\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}^{2}\,\big]\;,

(178)

and

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{2,0}=\hat{a}-\frac{1}{2}\,(2\,\mu_{2,0}+\nu_{2,0}^{*})\,\hat{a}^{2}\;,

(179)

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{2,0}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2}\;,\quad\kappa_{2,0}=\frac{1}{4}\,|2\,\mu_{2,0}+\nu_{2,0}^{*}|^{2}\;.

(180)

It is straightforward to see that
{\cal L}^{\in}_{2,0}
generates

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h_{2,0}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle+\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h^{\notin}_{2,0}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle

(181)

for which

\displaystyle h^{\notin}_{2,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\,\big(|2\,\mu_{2,0}+\nu_{2,0}^{*}|^{2}-1\big)\,\alpha\;.

(182)

We can now construct
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{2,0}
explicitly using the fact that we know how to quantize any degree-one polynomial. It is simple to see that

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\notin}_{2,0}=\eta_{2}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{2}^{-}]+\eta_{2}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{2}^{+}]\;,

(183)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{2}^{-}={}

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\;,

(184)

\displaystyle\eta_{2}^{-}={}

\displaystyle-\theta\big(1-|2\,\mu_{2,0}+\nu_{2,0}^{*}|^{2}\big)\,(|2\,\mu_{2,0}+\nu_{2,0}^{*}|^{2}-1)\;,

(185)

and

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{2}^{+}={}

\displaystyle\hat{a}\;,

(186)

\displaystyle\eta_{2}^{+}={}

\displaystyle\theta\big(|2\,\mu_{2,0}+\nu_{2,0}^{*}|^{2}-1\big)\,(|2\,\mu_{2,0}+\nu_{2,0}^{*}|^{2}-1)\;.

(187)

This is the simplest nontrivial example of cascade quantization. Combining our previous results, an arbitrary degree-two system

\displaystyle h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})={}

\displaystyle h_{0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h_{1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h_{2}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(188)

can be quantized by

\displaystyle{\cal L}={}

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}+{\cal L}_{1}+{\cal L}_{2}

(189)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-i\,[\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]-i\,[\hat{H}_{1,0}+\hat{H}_{2,0},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1,0}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{-}]+\gamma_{1,0}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{+}]

(190)

\displaystyle+{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{2,0}]+\kappa_{2,0}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}_{2,0}]+\eta_{2}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{2}^{-}]+\eta_{2}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{2}^{+}]\;.

IX.4
Degree-three polynomial
(m=3)

Since we have quantized an arbitrary quadratic system, we only have to quantize a homogeneous cubic polynomial in order to quantize any degree-three system. Setting
n=3
in (
130
)–(
132
) we get

\displaystyle h_{3}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\lambda_{3}\,\alpha^{*}+h_{3,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h_{3,1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(191)

in which

\displaystyle h_{3,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})={}

\displaystyle\mu_{3,0}\,\alpha^{3}+\nu_{3,0}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{2}\alpha\;,

(192)

\displaystyle h_{3,1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})={}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{3}\,\alpha^{*}\,\alpha^{2}\;.

(193)

The corresponding Lindbladian is thus

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{3}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{3},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+{\cal L}_{3,0}+{\cal L}_{3,1}\;.

(194)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{3}=-\frac{i}{4}\,(\lambda_{3}^{*}\,\hat{a}^{4}-\lambda_{3}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{4})\;.

(195)

We consider the quantization of
h_{3,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
first. Its Lindbladian can be written as

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{3,0}={\cal L}^{\in}_{3,0}+{\cal L}^{\notin}_{3,0}\;.

(196)

To find
{\cal L}^{\in}_{3,0}
we can use Sec.
VIII.3.1
with
n=3
and
k=0
. This gives

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{3,0}={}

\displaystyle-i\,[\hat{H}_{3,0}\,,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{3,0}]+\kappa^{-}_{3,0}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}^{-}_{3,0}]\;,

(197)

with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{3,0}=\frac{i}{4}\,\big[(\mu_{3,0}-\nu_{3,0}^{*})\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}^{3}-(\mu_{3,0}^{*}-\nu_{3,0})\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{3}\hat{a}\big]\;,

(198)

and the Lindblad operators and coefficients

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{3,0}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\frac{1}{2}\,(3\,\mu_{3,0}+\nu_{3,0}^{*})\,\hat{a}^{2}\;.

(199)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{-}_{3,0}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2}\;,\quad\kappa^{-}_{3,0}=\frac{1}{4}\,|3\,\mu_{3,0}+\nu_{3,0}^{*}|^{2}\;.

(200)

Note that
\kappa^{+}_{3,0}=0
because
\zeta_{3,0}
is negative definite. It can be verified directly that
{\cal L}^{\in}_{3,0}
generates

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h_{3,0}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle+\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h^{\notin}_{3,0}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle\,.

(201)

where

\displaystyle h^{\notin}_{3,0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\frac{1}{2}\,\big(|3\,\mu_{3,0}+\nu_{3,0}^{*}|^{2}-1\big)\,\alpha\;.

(202)

This now determines the form of
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{3,0}
. Having quantized an arbitrary linear system, it is simple to see that

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\notin}_{3,0}=\eta_{3}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{3}^{-}]+\eta_{3}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{3}^{+}]\;,

(203)

with the Lindblad operators and coefficients given by

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{3}^{-}={}

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\;,

(204)

\displaystyle\eta_{3}^{-}={}

\displaystyle-\theta\big(1-|3\,z_{3,0}+z_{3,2}^{*}|^{2}\big)\,\big(|3\,z_{3,0}+z_{3,2}^{*}|^{2}-1\big)\;.

(205)

and

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{3}^{+}={}

\displaystyle\hat{a}\;,

(206)

\displaystyle\eta_{3}^{+}={}

\displaystyle\theta\big(|3\,z_{3,0}+z_{3,2}^{*}|^{2}-1\big)\,\big(|3\,z_{3,0}+z_{3,2}^{*}|^{2}-1\big)\;.

(207)

Turning now to
h_{3,1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
, we find that it may be quantized by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{3,1}={\cal L}^{\in}_{3,1}+{\cal L}^{\notin}_{3,1}\;,

(208)

where
{\cal L}^{\in}_{3,1}
can be found by letting
n=3
and
K=1
in Sec.
VIII.3.2
. This leads to

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{3,1}=-\,i\,[\hat{H}_{3,1}\,,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+\gamma^{-}_{3,1}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{-}_{3,1}]+\gamma^{+}_{3,1}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{+}_{3,1}]\;,

(209)

where the Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{3,1}=-\,\frac{1}{2}\,\Im[\epsilon_{3}]\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2}\,\hat{a}^{2}\;,

(210)

and the dissipators are defined by

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{-}_{3,1}={}

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{2}\;,\quad\gamma^{-}_{3,1}=-\,\Re[\epsilon_{3}]\,\theta\big(\!-\!\Re[\epsilon_{3}]\big)\;,

(211)

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{+}_{3,1}={}

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{2}\;,\quad\gamma^{+}_{3,1}=\Re[\epsilon_{3}]\,\theta\big(\Re[\epsilon_{3}]\big)\;.

(212)

It is then straightforward to show that
{\cal L}^{\in}_{3,1}
generates

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h_{3,1}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle+\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h^{\notin}_{3,1}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle

(213)

where

\displaystyle h^{\notin}_{3,1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=2\,\theta\big(\Re[\epsilon_{3}]\big)\,\alpha\;,

(214)

We should therefore let
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{3,1}
be

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\notin}_{3,1}=\eta_{3}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{3}]\;,

(215)

with

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{3}=\hat{a}\;,\quad\eta_{3}=4\,\theta\big(\Re[\epsilon_{3}]\big)\,\Re[\epsilon_{3}]\;.

(216)

Finally, a general cubic polynomial, given by

\displaystyle h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})={}

\displaystyle h_{0}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h_{1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h_{2}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle+h_{3}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;.

(217)

can now be quantized by combining all our results above to give the Lindbladian,

\displaystyle{\cal L}={}

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{0}+{\cal L}_{1}+{\cal L}_{2}+{\cal L}_{3}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-i\,[\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2}+\hat{H}_{3},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]

\displaystyle-i\,[\hat{H}_{1,0}+\hat{H}_{2,0}+\hat{H}_{3,0},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]-i\,[\hat{H}_{3,1},\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1,0}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{-}]+\gamma_{1,0}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{1,0}^{+}]

\displaystyle+{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{2,0}]+\kappa_{2,0}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}_{2,0}]+\eta_{2}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{2}^{-}]+\eta_{2}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{2}^{+}]

\displaystyle+{\cal D}[\hat{c}_{3,0}]+\kappa^{-}_{3,0}\,{\cal D}[\hat{b}^{-}_{3,0}]+\eta_{3}^{-}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{3}^{-}]+\eta_{3}^{+}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{3}^{+}]

\displaystyle+\gamma^{-}_{3,1}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{-}_{3,1}]+\gamma^{+}_{3,1}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{+}_{3,1}]+\eta_{3}\,{\cal D}[\hat{L}_{3}]\;.

(218)

Note that several dissipators in (
IX.4
) can be grouped together since they have the same Lindblad operator. The examples above illustrate the essence of cascade quantization and how it works in practice.

X
Summary, related works, and outlook

Our main result in this paper is a procedure that we call cascade quantization. It is an exact and effective means of quantizing nonlinear non-Hamiltonian systems for which the dynamical equations are arbitrary polynomials. Defined as an inverse problem for Lindbladians in Sec.
II
, cascade quantization maps a classical system directly and explicitly to completely-positive and trace-preserving evolutions in the Schrödinger picture of quantum theory. To our knowledge, cascade quantization is the first exact analytic result for quantizing a general class of systems while respecting physical requirements. An efficient and user-friendly version of cascade quantization was provided in Sec.
III
, in the form of a table (Fig.
1
). There, we also explained the value of cascade quantization by highlighting the issues that make quantizing a nonlinear non-Hamiltonian system difficult. We then illustrated the power of cascade quantization in Secs.
IV
–
VII
, before delving into its inner workings in Secs.
VIII
,
IX
, and Appendix
C
.

For our first application of cascade quantization we considered the normal forms of some important bifurcations in Sec.
IV
. Despite being textbook examples, they have not been quantized until now. Particularly noteworthy are the saddle-node and transcritical bifurcations which, though dynamically simple, display Wigner negativity in their quantized forms. We then considered the Fitzhugh–Nagumo model and the effects of noise in Sec.
V
. Here we saw how a classical white-noise process can have a resonance effect on the Wigner mode of the quantum Fitzhugh–Nagumo system. This example illustrates how simple classical stochastic systems may be accounted for within cascade quantization. Another interesting class of nonlinear systems are Liénard oscillators. By utilizing Liénard’s theorem and cascade quantization we parameterized an entire family of quantum limit cycles in Sec.
VI
. This now generalizes the earlier results on the quantum van der Pol and Rayleigh oscillators in Ref.
CKN20
.

A major competitor to cascade quantization is the variational paradigm reviewed in Appendix
A
. To highlight the advantages of cascade quantization over variational methods, we examined two examples in Sec.
VII
. These are the van der Pol and unusual Liénard oscillators. For completeness, the comparison has also been supplemented by some additional calculations in Appendix
B
. Section
VII
also compares cascade quantization to other non-variational methods, and explained the problems of these results. Appendix

Despite the range of systems amenable to cascade quantization, its scope is not unlimited. One generalization that is obviously desirable is to extend cascade quantization to systems in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

For instance, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem implies that strange attractors, which are characteristic of chaotic systems, are only possible for autonomous systems with

1
to include systems in
\mathbb{P}_{m}

\mathbb{R}^{2}

Other than enlarging the scope of our quantization method, it would also be interesting to investigate in more detail nonclassical effects in the quantized models. It is important to keep in mind that Wigner negativity is only a sufficient condition on nonclassicality, and that even relatively simple dissipators in uncoupled systems can lead to nonclassical effects

;
MKH20

Beyond purely theoretical interests, this has potential impact on the development of novel quantum technologies. Appendix A
Literature

Arguably the most enduring and influential approach to the quantization of non-Hamiltonian systems is that inspired by Bateman, who, in 1931, proposed two different Lagrangians for the classical damped harmonic oscillator
Bat31
. Assuming the oscillator to have unit mass and damping coefficient
\gamma
, its position
x
satisfies the second-order differential equation

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}+\gamma\,x^{\prime}+\omega_{0}^{2}\,x=0\;.

(219)

Here
\omega_{0}
is the undamped (angular) frequency of the oscillator. Bateman’s publication of Lagrangians leading to (
219
) seems to have been triggered by a rather innocuous remark (at least measured by our current knowledge of open systems), on the impossibility of dissipative dynamical systems to arise from variational principles
Bau31
. He proposed two Lagrangians that formed the basis of intense discussion. In one version, the Lagrangian involves an ancillary oscillator, and is commonly referred to as the Bateman dual-oscillator, or simply time-independent, model. In the other version, the Lagrangian is time dependent, but does not contain any ancillary system. We will briefly review both approaches below. Bateman’s analyses were entirely classical, but spurred a great deal of interest for the corresponding quantum-mechanical description.

A.1
Origins and early work

A.1.1
Bateman, Caldirola, and Kanai

We start with Bateman’s dual-oscillator model. Let us denote by
(x,x^{\prime})
the variables for the damped harmonic oscillator, and
(q,q^{\prime})
the variables for the ancillary system. Bateman’s dual Lagrangian may then be stated as
L=(1/2)[x^{\prime}\,q^{\prime}+\gamma\,(x\,q^{\prime}-x^{\prime}\,q)/2-\omega_{0}^{2}\,x\,q]
. The Hamiltonian corresponding to this Lagrangian is then defined by the Legendre transform
H=y\,x^{\prime}+p\,q^{\prime}-L
, where

and
p=\partial L/\partial q^{\prime}
are the canonical momenta for

and

and

as functions of

\displaystyle H=2\,y\,p+\frac{\gamma}{2}\,(q\,p-x\,y)+\frac{1}{2}\,\bigg(\omega_{0}^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\bigg)\,x\,y\;.

(220)

It is then simple to derive Hamilton’s equations from (

\displaystyle x^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle y\;,

\displaystyle y^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle-\gamma\,y-\omega_{0}^{2}\,x\;,

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}-\gamma\,q^{\prime}+\omega_{0}^{2}\,q=0\;.

(223)

A.1.2
Critiques

Aside from the problems already pointed out above, here we mention two other prominent criticisms associated with (

\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}

If we take the Hamiltonian simply as the generator of time evolution (i.e. simply as a mathematical construct) then the physics of the system should refer to the mechanical variables [as in (
225

[\hat{x},\hat{y}]=i\,\hbar\,\hat{1}
leads to an exponentially decaying commutator for position and mechanical momentum, given by
[\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime}]=i\,\hbar\,\exp(-\gamma\,t)
.
30
30

30

This is a simple consequence of the relationship between the canonical and mechanical momentum already present in the classical theory, given by
x^{\prime}=y\,\exp(-\gamma\,t)

Kan48

Consequently the uncertainty product for position and mechanical momentum is also exponentially damped. This point appears to have been first highlighted by Brittin a year after Kanai’s publication of (

The alternative of interpreting the Hamiltonian to be physical, as the oscillator’s energy, leads to a second critique in the literature. Greenberger has noted that (

Other issues arising from using (

A.2
Open systems and related results

What is now known as an open-systems approach to dissipation was not formulated until the early 1960s. By an open-systems approach, we mean coupling the system of interest to infinitely many ancillary degrees of freedom, collectively called the bath (or reservoir), which are then to be eliminated from the model later. Thus, a dash of open-systems thinking was already present in Bateman’s dual Hamiltonian, if not earlier.

Sticking to the damped harmonic oscillator as a testbed, Senitzky enunciated how one could arrive at a quantum master equation in Lindblad form [recall (
12
)] by coupling an undamped system to a bath
Sen60
;
Sen61
. At around the same time, and pressing on with circuits, Stevens proposed a similar idea by coupling an LC-circuit to a semi-infinite transmission line, which was modelled by chaining units of inductors and capacitors
Ste61
. An important contribution to the quantization literature by Senitzky and Stevens is the preservation of the correct canonical commutation relation in time. They elucidated the role of the bath as a source of noise for the system, and that it was essential for the harmonic oscillator to have the right canonical commutator. Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle is thus maintained in their work. Other than the original publications in Refs.
Sen60
;
Ste61
, Stevens has also written a reflective comment on this issue
Ste80
.

Given the significance of the Lindblad-form master equation for non-Hamiltonian systems, it is worth pointing out at least one approach in the literature using the Lagrange–Hamilton framework that has acknowledged it. Here we refer to the results of Dekker, who showed how the dynamics of the classical damped harmonic oscillator [given by (
221
) and (
222
)] can be generated on adoption of a complex-valued Hamiltonian
Dek75
. To quantize the damped harmonic oscillator, Dekker then appeals to the classical Liouville equation, except now with a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian. To quantize, Dekker turns the classical Liouville equation into an operator equation (where the classical phase-space density is regarded as the classical analogue of the density operator), and imposing canonical commutation relations. The most interesting aspect of Dekker’s procedure for us is that it yields a Lindblad-form equation of motion for the density operator of the damped oscillator
Dek79
.
32
32

32

To preempt our readers who are familiar with quantum-trajectory theory
CSVR89
;
Car93
, Dekker’s approach does not correspond to the non-Hermitian evolution prescribed by the jump trajectories. The evolution under a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian in quantum trajectories is conditioned on null measurements, and therefore does not have a Lindblad form.
Despite this, such a phase-space approach to quantization is not without drawbacks. In view of the results by Senitzky and Stevens, we find Dekker’s equation of motion for the density operator only reproduces the case of a zero-temperature bath from an open-systems perspective. Furthermore, this approach is limited to linear systems
Dek79
. A thorough discussion of noise operators in Dekker’s approach and its relation to Senitzky’s work has been explored in Refs.
Dek77a
;
Dek77b
.

A.3
More recent attempts

A.3.1
Generalisations of Hamilton’s principle

One suggestion put forth by the more modern approaches to non-Hamiltonian systems is to modify Hamilton’s principle of stationary action. In Ref.
Gal13
, Galley critiques the subtle pitfall in the conventional formulation of Hamilton’s principle, which assumes fixed end points for all varied paths in generalized coordinates. This amounts to imposing boundary conditions on the dynamical system which are actually solved with initial conditions. In particular dissipatives systems must be specified as initial-value problems. Galley thus formulates what he referred to as Hamilton’s principle with initial data
Gal13
. To do so, the number of generalized coordinates and velocities is doubled. A new action integral is then introduced, which defines a new Lagrangian (now a function of the doubled coordinates and velocities). The end result being new Euler–Lagrange equations containing nonconservative forces. Equipped with the new formalism, Martínez-Pérez and Ramírez then considered the application of Noether’s theorem to dissipative systems
MPR18
. At around the same time as Galley, a formulation of Hamilton’s principle as an initial-value problem was also proposed by Polonyi, but based on ideas from quantum field theory
Pol14
. The Bateman Lagrangian from Sec.
A.1.1
can then be shown to be consistent with Galley’s approach
MPR18
. That is, the Bateman Lagrangian has the form stipulated by Galley, and not surprisingly, (
219
) and (
223
) can also be derived using Galley’s new Euler–Lagrange equations. Interestingly, Bateman’s dual Langrangian has motivated another modification of the principle of stationary action to include multiple degrees of freedom and nonlinear systems
Lan21
.

There has also been an extension of the principle of least action in Riewe’s fractional-calculus version of variational mechanics that we mentioned at the end of Sec.
A.1.2
. In particular, Lazo and Kumreich fixed certain mathematical inconsistencies in Riewe’s work and proposed a new principle of stationary action which generalizes Riewe’s original version
LK14
.

\hbar\equiv 1

\displaystyle[\hat{x},\hat{y}]=i\,\hat{1}\;,\quad[\hat{q},\hat{p}\,]=i\,\hat{1}\;,

(233)

while all other commutators vanish,

\displaystyle[\hat{x},\hat{q}]=[\hat{x},\hat{p}]=0\;,\quad[\hat{y},\hat{q}]=[\hat{y},\hat{p}]=0\;.

(234)

The form of (
100
) then suggests the following Hamiltonian operator,

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{y}\,\hat{p}+\hat{x}\,\hat{q}+\frac{\mu}{2}\,(\hat{x}^{2}-\hat{1})\,(\hat{q}\,\hat{p}+\hat{p}\,\hat{q})\;.

(235)

To ensure
\hat{H}
is Hermitian, we have symmetrized the product of
\hat{q}
and
\hat{p}
. We have also omitted the symbol for tensor products. An operator like
\hat{x}\otimes\hat{1}
, or
\hat{1}\otimes\hat{q}
, will respectively be denoted simply as
\hat{x}
, or
\hat{q}
. To see that (
235
) makes sense we show that it leads to quantum analogues of (
228
)–(
231
). Using (
235
) we find the following Heisenberg equations of motion on
\mathbb{H}_{\rm pri}\otimes\mathbb{H}_{\rm anc}
:

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle i\,[\hat{H},\hat{x}]=\hat{p}\;,

(236)

\displaystyle\hat{y}^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle i\,[\hat{H},\hat{y}]=-\hat{q}-\mu\,\hat{x}\,(\,\hat{q}\,\hat{p}+\hat{p}\,\hat{q})\;,

(237)

\displaystyle\hat{q}^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle i\,[\hat{H},\hat{q}]=\hat{y}+\mu\,(\hat{x}^{2}-\hat{1})\,\hat{q}\;,

(238)

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle i\,[\hat{H},\hat{p}]=-\hat{x}-\mu\,(\hat{x}^{2}-\hat{1})\,\hat{p}\;.

(239)

As with the classical formulation, the van der Pol oscillator is actually captured by (
236
) and (
239
). Differentiating (
236
) and using (
239
), we arrive at the quantized version of
x^{\prime\prime}+\mu\,(x^{2}-1)\,x^{\prime}+x=0
,

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{\prime\prime}+\mu\,(\hat{x}^{2}-\hat{1})\,\hat{x}^{\prime}+\hat{x}=0\;.

(240)

Again, this is in fact an operator equation on
\mathbb{H}_{\rm pri}\otimes\mathbb{H}_{\rm anc}
. It can be shown explicitly that (
236
)–(
239
) preserves the canonical commutation relations in (
233
) and (
234
). Assuming that (
233
) and (
234
) hold initially at time
t
, then an infinitesimal interval
dt
later, we find

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t+dt),\hat{y}(t+dt)]={}

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t)+\hat{x}^{\prime}(t)\,dt,\hat{y}(t)+\hat{y}^{\prime}(t)\,dt]

(241)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t),\hat{y}(t)]+([\hat{x}(t),\hat{y}^{\prime}(t)]\,dt

\displaystyle+[\hat{x}^{\prime}(t),\hat{y}(t)]\,dt\,,

(242)

where we have neglected terms on the order of
dt^{2}
. Using (
236
) and (
237
) we find

\displaystyle[\hat{x}^{\prime}(t),\hat{y}(t)]=[\hat{x}(t),\hat{y}^{\prime}(t)]=0\;,

(243)

since
\hat{y}(t)
commutes with
\hat{p}(t)
, and
\hat{x}(t)
commutes with
\hat{q}(t)
and
\hat{p}(t)
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t+dt),\hat{y}(t+dt)]=[\hat{x}(t),\hat{y}(t)]=i\,\hat{1}\;.

(244)

The preservation of
[\hat{q},\hat{p}]=i\,\hat{1}
can also be shown in a similar fashion. Next we consider (
234
),

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t+dt),\hat{q}(t+dt)]={}

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t)+\hat{x}^{\prime}(t)\,dt,\hat{q}(t)+\hat{q}^{\prime}(t)\,dt]

(245)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t),\hat{q}(t)]+[\hat{x}(t),\hat{q}^{\prime}(t)]\,dt

\displaystyle+[\hat{x}^{\prime}(t),\hat{q}(t)]\,dt\;,

(246)

where we have again dropped terms on the order of
dt^{2}
. Substituting in (
236
) and (
238
), we find

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t),\hat{q}^{\prime}(t)]+[\hat{x}^{\prime}(t),\hat{q}(t)]={}

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t),\hat{y}(t)]+[\hat{p}(t),\hat{q}(t)]=0\;.

(247)

Hence we have shown

\displaystyle[\hat{x}(t+dt),\hat{q}(t+dt)]=[\hat{x}(t),\hat{q}(t)]=0\;.

(248)

The preservation of the remaining commutators in (
234
) can also be demonstrated by a similar means.

While the above results seem quite sensible, what is ultimately of interest in quantum mechanics, is how a given
\rho(0)\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{H}_{\rm pri}\otimes\mathbb{H}_{\rm anc})
would appear at some future time
t
. Recall from Sec.
II.2
that we have defined
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{H})
to be the set of all valid density operators on
\mathbb{H}
. How states evolve in the Schrödinger picture is also an important practical question, since it is generally difficult, if not impossible to solve the Heisenberg equations of motion. Thus we are interested in

\displaystyle\rho(t)=e^{-i\hat{H}t}\,\rho(0)\,e^{i\hat{H}t}\;.

(249)

However, the instabilities in the original Hamiltonian discussed in Sec.
VII.1
make simulations of (
249
) virtually impossible. Furthermore, neither the ancillary or primary systems can be traced out to provide a simpler description. That is, suppose we only cared about the position of the van der Pol oscillator. Then only density operators in
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{H}_{\rm pri})
are required. But since such states are given by a partial trace of (
249
) over
\mathbb{H}_{\rm anc}
, there is still no possibility of extracting the quantum dynamics of just one degree of freedom.

B.2
Unusual Liénard oscillator

B.2.1
Classical mechanics

As with the van der Pol oscillator, it helps to first work out the classical mechanics of the unusual Liénard oscillator before we quantize it within the variational approach. Recall the classical Hamiltonian for the Liénard-type oscillator was given in (
112
),

\displaystyle H=-\frac{9}{k^{2}}\,S^{\frac{1}{2}}(y)+\frac{1}{2}\,x^{2}-\frac{3}{k}\,y-\frac{k}{3}\,x^{2}\,y\;,

(250)

where for ease of writing we have defined

\displaystyle S^{\beta}(y)=\bigg(1-\frac{2k}{3}\,y\bigg)^{\beta}\;.

(251)

The associated Hamilton equations of motion are then,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}=\frac{3}{k}\,S^{-\frac{1}{2}}(y)-\frac{3}{k}-\frac{k}{3}\,x^{2}\;,

\displaystyle y^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=-x+\frac{2k}{3}\,x\,y\;.

(253)

Note that (
252
) and (
253

x^{\prime}=y
and

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}=S^{-\frac{3}{2}}(y)\,y^{\prime}-\frac{2k}{3}\,x\,x^{\prime}\;.

(254)

We can now use (
252
) and (
253

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}={}

\displaystyle-3\,x\,S^{\!-\frac{1}{2}}(y)+2\,x+\frac{2k^{2}}{9}\,x^{3}

(255)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-3\,x\bigg(1+\frac{k}{3}\,x^{\prime}+\frac{k^{2}}{9}x^{2}\bigg)+2\,x+\frac{2k^{2}}{9}\,x^{3}

(256)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-k\,x\,x^{\prime}-\frac{k^{2}}{9}\,x-x\;.

(257)

B.2.2
Quantum mechanics

To obtain the quantum Hamiltonian based on (

\displaystyle-\frac{9}{k^{2}}\,S^{\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y})+\frac{1}{2}\,\hat{x}^{2}-\frac{3}{k}\,\hat{y}

Note that

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{\prime}=i\,[\hat{H},\hat{x}]={}

The first commutator with the square root may be computed by applying

We also have,

\displaystyle\big[\hat{x}^{2}\,\hat{y}+\hat{x}\,\hat{y}\,\hat{x}+\hat{y}\,\hat{x}^{2},\hat{x}\big]={}

Substituting these into (

Turning now to the Heisenberg equation of motion for

\displaystyle\hat{y}^{\prime}=i\,[\hat{H},\hat{y}]=\frac{i}{2}\,\big[\hat{x}^{2},\hat{y}\big]-i\,\frac{k}{9}\,\big[\hat{x}^{2}\,\hat{y}+\hat{x}\,\hat{y}\,\hat{x}+\hat{y}\,\hat{x}^{2},\hat{y}\big]\;,

The second commutator in the right-hand side may be simplified

\displaystyle\big[\hat{x}\,\hat{y}\,\hat{x},\hat{y}\big]={}

\displaystyle i\,(\hat{x}\,\hat{y}+\hat{y}\,\hat{x})\,.

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{\prime\prime}={}

\displaystyle i\big[\hat{H},\hat{x}^{\prime}\big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle i\,\frac{3}{k}\,\big[S^{\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y}),\hat{x}^{2}\big]+i\,\frac{3}{2k}\,\big[\hat{x}^{2},S^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y})\big]

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{3}\,\big[\hat{x}^{2}\,\hat{y}+\hat{x}\,\hat{y}\,\hat{x}+\hat{y}\,\hat{x}^{2},S^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y})\big]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle i\,\frac{3}{k}\,\Big\{\hat{x}\,\big[S^{\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y}),\hat{x}\big]+\big[S^{\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y}),\hat{x}\big]\,\hat{x}\Big\}

\displaystyle+i\,\frac{3}{2k}\,\Big\{\hat{x}\,\big[\hat{x},S^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y})\big]+\big[\hat{x},S^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y})\big]\,\hat{x}\Big\}

Appendix C
Constructive proof of cascade quantization

For ease of reference, let us recall from Sec.
VIII.1
that our strategy to quantize a system given by
\alpha^{\prime}=h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
, where
h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
is a polynomial of degree
m
, is to first write it as a sum of homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
,

\displaystyle h(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\sum_{n=0}^{m}\,h_{n}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;.

(283)

Thus, to quantize a general polynomial system, we only need to quantize an arbitrary homogeneous polynomial. And to achieve this we futher decompose
h_{n}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
as

\displaystyle h_{n}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\lambda_{n}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{n}+(1-\delta_{n,0})\sum_{k=0}^{K(n)}\,h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(284)

where for
n\geq 1
we have defined,

\displaystyle h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\mu_{n,k}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{k}\alpha^{n-k}+\nu_{n,k}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{n-k-1}\alpha^{k+1}\;,

(285)

with
\lambda_{n}
,
\mu_{n,k}
, and
\nu_{n,k}
being arbitrary complex coefficients. We have also defined the top limit of the sum as

\displaystyle K(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{n}{2}-1\;,\quad\text{$n$ even}\\
\frac{n-1}{2}\;,\quad\text{$n$ odd}\end{cases}.

(286)

We prove constructively in this Appendix that there is always a Lindbladian
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
, i.e. for all values of
n
and
k
, such that it quantizes,

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}=h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;,

(287)

where
h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
are byproduct terms not in
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
, but have lower degree than
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
. Therefore we can always construct another Lindbladian
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}
such that it quantizes

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}=-\,h^{\notin}_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})\;.

(288)

The Lindbladian that quantizes
h_{n,k}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
is then given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{n,k}={\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}+{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}\;.

(289)

C.1
{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,k}
for even
n\geq 2

To make our proof easier to read, we will suppress the subscripts
n
and
k
throughout most of our detailed calculations. The dependence on
n
and
k
will be restored at the end of the subsection, when we come to summarize our results. To begin with, let us assume a Lindbladian of the form

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}=-i\,[\hat{H},\text{\Large$\cdot$}]+{\cal D}[\hat{c}]\;.

(290)

It is then simple show that the evolution of
\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle
, as generated by (

), can be written as

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=i\,\langle{\,[\hat{H},\hat{a}]\,}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\,\langle{\,[\hat{c}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{c}\,}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\,\langle{\,\hat{c}^{\dagger}[\hat{a},\hat{c}]\,}\rangle\;.

(291)

We then seek an
\hat{H}
and a
\hat{c}
such that (

) evaluates to

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\mu\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k}\,\hat{a}^{n-k}}\rangle+\nu\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k-1}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}}\rangle+\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h^{\notin}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle\;,

(292)

where
h^{\notin}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})
will be determined by our construction of
{\cal L}^{\in}
. To this end, we make the ansatz that
\hat{H}
can be put in the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\chi\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k+1}\hat{a}^{n-k}+\chi^{*}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k}\hat{a}^{k+1}\;,

(293)

while the Lindblad operator
\hat{c}
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{c}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n}{2}-k-1}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}+\sigma\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n}{2}+1}\;.

(294)

Here
\chi
and
\sigma
are constants which depend on
\mu
and
\nu

) and (
294
) to evaluate each term in (

\displaystyle i\,[\hat{H},\hat{a}]={}

\displaystyle i\,\chi\,[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k+1}\hat{a}^{n-k},\hat{a}]+i\,\chi^{*}\,[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k}\hat{a}^{k+1},\hat{a}]

(295)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle i\,\chi\,[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k+1},\hat{a}]\,\hat{a}^{n-k}+i\,\chi^{*}\,[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k},\hat{a}]\,\hat{a}^{k+1}

(296)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-i\,\chi\,(k+1)\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k}\,\hat{a}^{n-k}-i\,\chi^{*}\,(n-k)\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k-1}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}\;.

(297)

Now using (

\displaystyle[\hat{c}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]=[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k+1},\hat{a}]\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n}{2}-k-1}+\sigma^{*}\,[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n}{2}+1},\hat{a}]=-(k+1)\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n}{2}-k-1}-\sigma^{*}\,\bigg(\frac{n}{2}+1\bigg)\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n}{2}}\;.

(298)

Multiplying this on the right by
\hat{c}
we get

\displaystyle[\hat{c}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{c}={}

\displaystyle-\bigg[(k+1)\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n}{2}-k-1}+\sigma^{*}\,\bigg(\frac{n}{2}+1\bigg)\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n}{2}}\bigg]\big(\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n}{2}-k-1}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}+\sigma\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n}{2}+1}\big)

) is an unwanted term since it has the wrong powers of
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
[compared to (

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{(n-3)-(n+1)|\sigma|^{2}}{4}-k\;.

(375)

If
\zeta>0
we may consider the Lindblad operator
\hat{b}=\hat{a}^{(n+1)/2}
, with a coefficient
\kappa
to be determined:

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}\;[\hat{b}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{b}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\;\hat{b}^{\dagger}\,[\hat{a},\hat{b}]=\frac{\kappa}{2}\;[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}},\hat{a}]\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}=-\,\kappa\;\frac{(n+1)}{4}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;.

(376)

Comparing this to the first term on the second line in (

) shows that we ought to choose

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{4}{(n+1)}\,\zeta\;,

(377)

If on the other hand,
\zeta<0
, then we may consider
\hat{b}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{(n+1)/2}
, for which

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}\;[\hat{b}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{b}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\;\hat{b}^{\dagger}\,[\hat{a},\hat{b}]={}

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}\;\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}]

(378)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\kappa\;\frac{(n+1)}{4}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}=\kappa\;\frac{(n+1)}{4}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}+\,:\,\mathrel{\hat{R}^{\notin,3}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\;.

(379)

Note that we have used (
C.2.1
) to normally order (
379
), and introduced

\displaystyle R^{\notin,3}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\kappa\,\frac{(n+1)}{4}\,\sum_{p=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\frac{n+1}{2}}\\
{p}\end{array}\right)\frac{\big[(n-1)/2\big]!}{\big[(n-1)/2-p\big]!}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}-p}\,\alpha^{\frac{n+1}{2}-p}\;.

Our choice of
\kappa

\displaystyle\kappa=-\frac{4}{(n+1)}\,\zeta\;.

(382)

Using (
C.2.1
) and whichever choice of
\hat{b}
above depending on the sign of
\zeta
, we get

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}={}

\displaystyle i\,\langle{[\hat{H},\hat{a}]}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\,\langle{[\hat{c}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{c}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\,\langle{\hat{c}^{\dagger}\,[\hat{a},\hat{c}]}\rangle+\frac{\kappa}{2}\,\langle{[\hat{b}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{b}}\rangle+\frac{\kappa}{2}\,\langle{\hat{b}^{\dagger}[\hat{a},\hat{b}]}\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\bigg(i\,\chi+\frac{\sigma}{2}\bigg)(k+1)\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k}\,\hat{a}^{n-k}}\rangle+\bigg[\!-i\,\chi^{*}\,(n-k)+\sigma^{*}\,\frac{(k+1)}{2}\bigg]\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k-1}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}}\rangle+\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h^{\notin}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle\;.

(384)

where

\displaystyle h^{\notin}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=R^{\notin,1}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+R^{\notin,2}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})+\theta(-\zeta)\,R^{\notin,3}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})

(385)

Equation (

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\mu\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{k}\,\hat{a}^{n-k}}\rangle+\nu\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{n-k-1}\,\hat{a}^{k+1}}\rangle\;,

(394)

We also found from (

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{-}_{n,k}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\kappa^{-}_{n,k}=-\frac{4}{n+1}\,\theta(-\zeta_{n,k})\,\zeta_{n,k}\;,

(395)

\displaystyle\hat{b}^{+}_{n,k}=\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\kappa^{+}_{n,k}=\frac{4}{n+1}\,\theta(\zeta_{n,k})\,\zeta_{n,k}\;,

(396)

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{n,k}={}

(397)

The form of
{\cal L}^{\notin}_{n,k}

C.2.2

As before, we begin with the Lindbladian

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}=-i\,[\hat{H},\text{\Large$\cdot$}]+\gamma\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}]\;,

(404)

whose equation of motion for
\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle
is

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=i\,\langle{[\hat{H},\hat{a}]}\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\langle{[\hat{c}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{c}}\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\langle{\hat{c}^{\dagger}[\hat{a},\hat{c}]}\rangle\;.

(405)

Our goal is then to find an
\hat{H}
and a
\hat{c}
so that

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\epsilon\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}\rangle+\langle{\,:\,\mathrel{h^{\notin}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\,}\rangle

\epsilon

\displaystyle\langle{\hat{a}}\rangle^{\prime}=\Re[\epsilon]\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}\rangle+i\,\Im[\epsilon]\,\langle{\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}\rangle\;.

(407)

We first try to generate the second term in (
407
) by using the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-\frac{2\,\Im[\epsilon]}{n+1}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;.

(408)

This gives

\displaystyle i\,[\hat{H},\hat{a}]={}

\displaystyle-i\,\frac{2\,\Im[\epsilon]}{n+1}\,[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}},\hat{a}]\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}=i\,\Im[\epsilon]\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;.

(409)

To generate the first term in (

\Re[\epsilon]

\Re[\epsilon]<0

\displaystyle\hat{c}=\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\gamma=-\,\frac{4\,\Re[\epsilon]}{n+1}\;,

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}\;[\hat{c}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{c}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\;\hat{c}^{\dagger}\,[\hat{a},\hat{c}]=-\frac{2\,\Re[\epsilon]}{n+1}\;[\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}},\hat{a}]\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}=\Re[\epsilon]\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;.

(411)

If instead
\Re[\epsilon]>0

\displaystyle\hat{c}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\gamma=\frac{4\,\Re[\epsilon]}{n+1}\;.

(412)

This gives

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}\;[\hat{c}^{\dagger},\hat{a}]\,\hat{c}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\;\hat{c}^{\dagger}\,[\hat{a},\hat{c}]={}

\displaystyle\frac{2\,\Re[\epsilon]}{n+1}\;\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}]

(413)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Re[\epsilon]\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}=\Re[\epsilon]\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\hat{a}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}+\,:\,\mathrel{R^{\notin}(\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger})}\,:\;,

which is a polynomial of degree

In summary, we are able to quantize exactly

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}=h_{n,K}(\alpha,\alpha^{*})=\epsilon_{n}\,\alpha^{*}{}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\alpha^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad K=\frac{n-1}{2}\;,\quad n=3,5,7,\dots\;,

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{\in}_{n,K}=-i\,[\hat{H}_{n,K}\,,\text{\Large$\cdot$}\,]+\gamma^{-}_{n,K}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{-}_{n,K}]+\gamma^{+}_{n,K}\,{\cal D}[\hat{c}^{+}_{n,K}]\;,

(421)

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{+}_{n,K}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}{}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\;,\quad\gamma^{+}_{n,K}=\frac{4\,\Re[\epsilon_{n}]}{n+1}\,\theta(\Re[\epsilon_{n}])\;.
