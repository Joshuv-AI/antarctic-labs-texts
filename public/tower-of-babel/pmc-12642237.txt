Factors influencing intention to pursue postgraduate studies in nutrition and dietetics among Thai dietitians: a qualitative study
Authors: Thanit Vinitchagoon, Wantanee Kriengsinyos, Aree Prachansuwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe field of nutrition and dietetics is evolving, requiring continuous education and professional development for dietitians to maintain competencies. Pursuing postgraduate studies is a popular method to fulfill these requirements and enhance knowledge and skills. However, several challenges and barriers to postgraduate education exist. Research on these enabling and dissuading factors in a Thai context remains limited. This study aims to explore factors influencing Thai dietitians’ intentions to pursue postgraduate studies in nutrition and dietetics.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted with 30 dietitians working in hospitals and related settings across Thailand. Data were collected through self-administered questionnaires and in-depth interviews guided by the Theory of Planned Behavior. Thematic analysis was performed to identify key themes.ResultsThemes were categorized into four domains: attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and program characteristics. Positive attitudes included personal goals, career advancement, increased income, and skill development. Subjective norms included the influence of colleagues, supervisors, and family. Perceived behavioral control included time, financial, and travel constraints, and language barriers. Preferred program characteristics included relevant content, hybrid learning, and practical research opportunities.ConclusionsThai dietitians are motivated by personal and professional growth, career advancement, and knowledge enhancement, but face challenges such as time constraints, financial burdens, and language barriers. To support their professional growth, postgraduate programs should balance theory and practice, offer flexible learning options, and provide comprehensive support systems. These findings can inform the development of tailored graduate programs and serve as a foundation for similar initiatives in other Southeast Asian countries with developing dietetics professions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08247-8.Keywords: Continuing education, Dietetics, Dietitian, Graduate program, Postgraduate studies

Background

The field of nutrition and dietetics is constantly evolving, driven by advancements in various areas of research, education, and professional practice. To maintain professional competencies, many countries mandate continuous education and development for registered dietitians (RD) to renew the professional license [1]. Pursuing postgraduate studies is a popular means of fulfilling these continuing education requirements [2, 3]. Graduate degrees enhance dietitians' knowledge, skills, and expertise, positioning them for more complex roles in clinical practice, education, and research [4, 5].

Globally, a bachelor’s degree has long been the standard entry-level qualification for dietitians. However, the United States (U.S.), a country with a well-established dietetics profession, has recently implemented stricter educational requirements for RD licensure, recognizing the need for higher competency levels [6]. Effective January 2024, eligibility to take the registration examination for dietitians now requires a minimum of a master’s degree. This elevated standard aims to enhance dietitians’ professional competence and align dietitians with other allied health professionals in terms of knowledge, practical skills, and research capabilities. Such an increase in qualifications is also expected to promote leadership within the profession and potentially improve career advancement and earning potential [7–9]. Nevertheless, raising the educational bar presents challenges, including limited internship placements, variable educational program quality, and the financial burden associated with graduate studies [10]. These challenges highlight the difficulties of advancing educational standards, even in a well-established field within a developed country. It is reasonable to anticipate greater challenges in countries with less developed dietetics professions. Interestingly, comparable issues have been observed in other health professions, such as medicine and nursing, where both motivations and barriers to postgraduate studies have been widely explored in different cultural contexts [11–13]. These studies indicate that such factors are multifaceted, shaped by both individual motivations and contextual influences, and are likely applicable to the field of nutrition and dietetics as well.

Thailand represents a developing country where the dietetics profession is nascent. The field gained official recognition as a legally-approved allied health profession in 2020, with registration exams introduced in 2022 [14]. Currently, a bachelor’s degree in nutrition and dietetics is the minimum qualification for the registered dietitian licensure examination [15]. Although postgraduate studies have recently been approved as a continuing education option for license renewal, research on factors influencing Thai dietitians’ decisions to pursue advanced degrees, including both facilitators and barriers, remains limited.

Understanding factors influencing Thai dietitians’ intention towards pursuing postgraduate education is important to inform the development of graduate programs that aligns with professional and contextual needs. By examining factors influencing intention to pursue postgraduate studies in nutrition and dietetics, we aim to provide insights that can inform the development of graduate programs tailored to the profession’s needs. This research can also serve as a foundation for similar initiatives in other Southeast Asian countries with developing dietetics professions.

Methods

Study design

This study’s objective is to explore factors influencing Thai dietitians’ intentions to pursue postgraduate studies in nutrition and dietetics. We conducted a qualitative study among dietitians employed in hospitals or other settings in Thailand (n = 30). Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires and in-depth interviews. The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) was used as a framework to formulate the interview guide to explore factors affecting the intention to pursue graduate studies, building on previous research focusing on understanding education-related behaviors of students in the field of health sciences [16, 17].

Participants and recruitment strategies

A purposive sample of 30 dietitians was recruited as the target population is relatively specific. The sample size aligned with previous qualitative studies in nutrition and dietetics that utilized a sample size between 10 and 40 participants [18–20]. This sample size range was expected to be sufficient to achieve data saturation. The inclusion criteria to participate in this study are as follows: (1) reside and actively work as a dietitian in a hospital or other nutrition-related institutes in Thailand, (2) hold at least a bachelor’s degree in nutrition and dietetics or related degrees, and (3) able to communicate effectively in Thai. Participants were excluded from the study if they: (1) did not want to pursue a graduate degree in nutrition and dietetics or related areas, and (2) did not provide informed consent. Participants were recruited through announcements posted on widely used social media platforms, including LINE® and Facebook®, targeting communities of nutrition and dietetics professionals, including the official Facebook page of the Thai Dietetic Association, to reach a broad, nationwide audience of dietitians. To enhance coverage, snowball sampling was also employed, as participants were encouraged to share the study link with professional colleagues who might be eligible.

Data collection

Interested individuals completed a screening questionnaire (Appendix A (Suppl.)) to determine eligibility. Those eligible were contacted by the research team through phone or online calls to provide study information and obtain verbal consent. Participants then received an electronic consent form to provide informed consent, along with a link to the demographic questionnaire (Appendix B (Suppl.)). After participants completed the questionnaire and the research team verified the data, participants were contacted to schedule in-depth interviews, conducted either by phone or online. The details of the interview guideline can be found in Appendix C (Suppl.). Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min. Participants received a 300 THB (approximately 8 USD [21]) stipend as compensation. This study was approved by the Mahidol University central Institutional Review Board No. MU-cIRB 2023/285.1309. Participants were informed about the objectives and processes of the study. They voluntarily provided electronic consent before completing the questionnaires, and verbal assent before the interview.

The in-depth interviews were conducted by the first author, who is a registered dietitian and a lecturer in a graduate program in nutrition and dietetics. As an insider within the profession, the interviewer shared a professional background with participants, which could have shaped the dynamics of the interviews. To address this, he engaged in reflexive practices throughout the study. At the start of the interview process, he explicitly informed participants that he would not discuss his workplace or graduate program during the interviews, and that any questions about the institution would be addressed only after the interview session. This approach was intended to emphasize that the interviews sought to explore participants’ general perspectives on pursuing graduate studies in nutrition and dietetics, rather than their views on any specific university. The interviewer also practiced bracketing to minimize the influence of his prior beliefs and professional experiences, and to remain open to the diverse viewpoints expressed by participants.

Data analysis

Demographic data were summarized using descriptive statistics. For continuous variables, mean and standard deviation (SD), or median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported based on the distribution of data. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. For interview data, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and imported into NVivo 14 (Lumivero, 2023) for coding. Thematic analysis was conducted using a mixed inductive and deductive coding approach [22], guided by the TPB. Two researchers independently coded approximately 10% of the transcripts to establish inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s kappa coefficient >0.75). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. The remaining transcripts were coded by the first coder, with subsequent discussions to finalize the codes and formulate a code mapping. Both coders were Thai registered dietitians and lecturers in the graduate programs in nutrition and dietetics, which is expected to enhance contextual understanding.

Results

Thirty dietitians participated in the study, representing 19 provinces across Thailand (Fig. 1.). Descriptive characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 28.2 ± 6.3 years, and the median monthly salary was 20,000 THB (IQR: 17,000–25,750 THB), which is approximately 600 USD [21]. Overall, thirteen themes emerged from the interview data, which could be categorized under four major domains including attitudes towards postgraduate studies, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and program characteristics. Attitudes towards postgraduate studies

Personal goals (n = 25)

Many participants expressed a passion for their profession and a strong desire for continuous learning. Postgraduate education was often viewed as a method to expand their knowledge base for better patient care. For many participants, the pursuit of a graduate degree was driven by intrinsic motivation as they were motivated by a desire to challenge themselves and continue to thrive as professionals.

Career advancement (n = 22)

Participants reported recognizing that a graduate degree could accelerate career progression, opening doors to leadership positions and specialized roles, especially for those with positions in public hospitals and federal/government institutions. It was also perceived as a competitive advantage in the job market and a way to enhance professional credibility. Moreover, the prospect of building new professional relationships and expanding their networks was a significant draw for many participants. Attending graduate school may help connect them with like-minded individuals and gain access to new opportunities.

Increased income (n = 21)

Participants generally recognized the potential for increased earnings associated with a graduate degree. Many described this as a link to higher salaries resulting from promotions or transitions to more specialized roles within the field. However, some participants reported being skeptical as their organizations might not have specific policies in place to reward employees with advanced degrees. Also, some participants expressed concerns about the job market and the possibility that employers might prioritize experience over advanced degrees.

Knowledge and skill development (n = 28)

Participants expressed that the depth of knowledge gained from their undergraduate programs may be inadequate, particularly in relation to the complexities of clinical practice, especially when dealing with challenging cases or specialized populations. Also, the rapid pace of change in the field requires them to keep up with the latest research and evidence-based practices. Furthermore, participants identified specific areas where they felt their knowledge was lacking, such as advanced clinical skills, research methods, or specialized nutrition topics.

Subjective norms

Colleagues/supervisors (n = 15)

Participants reported that witnessing colleagues’ career advancement through higher education served as a significant motivator, mainly through role modeling, peer encouragement, and shared experiences. Supportive supervisors who offered flexibility and encouragement further boosted participants’ motivation. However, concerns about workload distribution emerged as a primary challenge. Participants described fear related to overburdening colleagues due to study-related absences in their shift-based work schedules. Moreover, they expressed potential resistance from supervisors who might be hesitant to support their educational goals especially when scheduling conflicts may arise.

Family members (n = 28)

Family support, both financially and emotionally, appeared to significantly influence participants’ decisions to pursue graduate studies. Many participants reported receiving encouragement from their parents and family members. This support often included verbal affirmations, emotional support, and financial assistance. However, concerns about imposing a financial burden on families, especially those with younger siblings or other dependents, led some participants to prioritize self-funding. This desire to demonstrate their financial independence appeared to coexist with a generational gap in higher education expectations, sometimes resulting in misunderstandings or a lack of parental enthusiasm, as described by some participants.

Perceived behavioral control

Time constraints (n = 25)

Balancing work and graduate studies appeared to present a significant challenge for participants. Juggling demanding work schedules, often including shifts and on-call duties, with rigorous academic requirements was viewed as particularly difficult. Concerns about workload, job performance, and the emotional toll of reduced leisure time and social interactions with friends and family were also prevalent. While supportive supervisors who offered flexible work arrangements could mitigate these challenges, the pressure to meet both professional and academic obligations remained a primary stressor.

Financial limitations/expenses (n = 25)

Financial considerations appeared to significantly impact participants’ decisions to pursue graduate studies. The high costs associated with tuition fees and living expenses were described as a substantial barrier, particularly for participants lacking familial support. Thus, participants expressed a need for financial aid, such as scholarships or grants, to offset these costs and often prioritized spending to accommodate graduate school. Concerns about reduced income due to decreased work hours or fewer part-time jobs further compounded financial challenges. Balancing the potential long-term benefits of a graduate degree against immediate financial sacrifices was a common dilemma.

Language barriers (n = 17)

English language proficiency emerged as a significant barrier for many participants. This concern was particularly relevant for individuals who may not have had extensive exposure to academic English, or who felt that their language skills were not sufficient for advanced study. Participants expressed concerns about their ability to comprehend course materials, particularly in English-medium programs, and to engage effectively in academic discourse. This included worries about reading comprehension, writing assignments, and oral communication. Therefore, many expressed a preference for programs taught in Thai or offering a blend of Thai and English.

Travel constraints (n = 13)

The return to in-person education following the pandemic seemed to pose commuting challenges for participants. While some were adaptable, others strongly preferred flexible learning options. Participants residing in rural or distant areas expressed particular concerns about the time, cost, and logistical difficulties associated with commuting. Balancing work, family, and studies was further complicated by these factors, increasing the risk of burnout. To mitigate these challenges, many participants advocated for online learning, emphasizing its flexibility and accessibility. However, some suggested a hybrid approach combining in-person and online elements to balance social interaction with convenience.

Program characteristics

Program content (n = 17)

Participants reported wanting a graduate program that not only provides theoretical knowledge but also equips them with practical clinical skills and experiences and specialized training in specific areas of dietetics such as sports, pediatrics, geriatrics, and research. Beyond the core academic content, they emphasized the importance of hands-on learning opportunities to bridge the gap between research and practice to be able to apply the knowledge to use in real-world situations, particularly in delivering nutrition counseling and education interventions.

Teaching and learning methods (n = 21)

Most participants favored a hybrid (in-person and online) learning model. While they valued the structured environment, interaction, and hands-on experiences offered by traditional classrooms, they also appreciated the flexibility and accessibility of online learning, especially for those with work or travel commitments. A common preference was for online lectures paired with in-person laboratory or practicum sessions. Additionally, some participants mentioned asynchronous online components to accommodate individual learning paces and better time management.

Research work (n = 14)

Participants expressed interest in thesis topics that were both personally meaningful and practically applicable. Some participants prioritized research that directly addressed challenges within their workplaces or benefited underserved communities. Specific areas of interest included developing innovative health promotion interventions and evaluating existing nutrition programs. Practical considerations such as data accessibility, available resources, and mentorship influenced participants’ research topic selection.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored factors influencing the intention to pursue postgraduate studies in nutrition and dietetics among Thai dietitians. Previous research has investigated factors influencing graduate study intentions among healthcare professionals, mainly physicians and nurses [23–26]. However, this study specifically examined the perspectives of dietitians due to limited knowledge on this topic. Interestingly, participants in this study were primarily early-career dietitians with an average of two years’ work experience. Intention to pursue graduate studies likely varies by age due to factors such as career opportunities, personal circumstances (i.e., family commitments), social circumstances (i.e., financial obligations), and self-efficacy [27]. Therefore, additional factors may influence intentions to pursue postgraduate studies among dietitians with more extensive work experience.

According to the results, there were both enabling and dissuading factors to pursuing postgraduate studies among Thai dietitians, consistent with other studies exploring motivation related to postgraduate studies among Asian population [11, 28]. Both enabling and dissuading factors in this study can be categorized into internal and external domains. Internal enabling factors primarily consisted of intrinsic motivations such as a strong desire for personal and professional growth, and a pursuit of advanced knowledge and skills. External enabling factors included perceived social support from colleagues and family, flexibility of the graduate program, and the perceived value of the program. In contrast, internal barriers primarily include concerns related to workload, time management, and language proficiency, while external barriers included a lack of understanding from family and colleagues, financial constraints, geographical limitations, and inflexible graduate program characteristics. These findings may also reflect broader cultural differences between Eastern and Western populations, as decisions in Eastern collectivist contexts are more strongly shaped by social expectations, family encouragement, and collective values, than in Western individualist contexts [29].

Regarding internal factors, intrinsic motivation has been shown as a key driver for health professionals pursuing postgraduate studies [], especially those in their early careers [30]. This motivation is often linked to enhanced knowledge, clinical decision-making, career advancement, critical thinking, and job satisfaction [25, 31]. To foster a greater intention towards pursuing graduate studies, programs should clearly articulate how students can achieve their professional and personal goals upon completion (e.g., specialization, increased recognition) while aligning these with individual motivations. Moreover, a U.S. study revealed that RDs earning master’s degrees post-licensure reported greater benefits than those earning them beforehand, emphasizing the value of experience in maximizing the program’s impact [32].

Regarding external factors, social support seems to be an equivocal factor, both from family members and peers. A study in doctoral students from the U.S. showed that family members generally provide positive support, more emotionally than financially. However, for those who did not receive such support, the problems were limited time spent with family, family not understanding the value of a graduate degree, and stress related to higher education [33]. Moreover, for people of higher age, balancing family responsibilities and grad school demands is also important, especially for women [34, 35]. However, results could be different based on sociocultural contexts as it is likely contextualized by family characteristics and dynamics, and sociocultural context (individualism vs. collectivism) [36].

Cost is also a significant external barrier to pursuing higher education among health professionals [37]. Despite the importance, the overall cost-effectiveness analysis of continuing professional development, including postgraduate education, in healthcare remains undetermined [38]. While research specifically in Thailand is limited, evidence from other countries, such as Egypt, showed that nurses, despite strong motivations, often delayed postgraduate studies due to financial constraints [39]. Thai nurses, particularly in public hospitals, face heavy workloads, exhaustion, and job dissatisfaction [40]. Financial hardship further impacts their quality of life and life satisfaction [41]. This suggests underpayment relative to workload, potentially driving a shift towards private hospitals and urban areas, mirroring the uneven distribution of Thai physicians [42]. Given these challenges, health professionals, including dietitians, may carefully weigh the costs and benefits of graduate study. Unlike the U.S., limited loan options force many dietitians to fund education personally. Also, the opportunity cost of reduced income during studies among dietitians is not well-established.

Considering the characteristics of graduate dietetics programs, the results from this study indicate a growing demand for specialized curriculum and flexible hybrid learning formats. These findings align with broader trends in dietetics education, particularly in the U.S., where there is a push for recognizing specialist and advanced practice dietitians [43]. The COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated the adoption of distance learning in dietetics, although the efficacy of this approach warrants further study [44]. Furthermore, recent studies showed that effective pedagogies in health professional education often incorporate social participation, reflection, and real-world application [45]. These principles are also consistent with the results from this study. To bridge the gap between academia and practice, collaborative research involving practicing dietitians [46] and service learning experiences [47] are essential for student development and community impact.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include being one among the few studies to systematically explore dietetics education in Southeast Asia, specifically Thailand. Given the predominance of Western-focused research in dietetics education, this study offers a valuable perspective as socioeconomic and cultural factors may differ significantly across continents. Also, this study utilized a behavioral theory (TPB) [48] to examine factors influencing the intention to pursue postgraduate studies, providing concrete hypotheses for future research [49].

While this study has certain strengths, several limitations exist. First, the purposive sampling method in this study limits generalizability to the population of Thai dietitians that may not be representative, particularly those who work in remote areas or specific underserved communities outside of clinics or hospitals. Additionally, while TPB provided a useful framework for understanding intentions to perform behaviors [50], this study did not capture data related to the actual behaviors (applying to graduate program). Future research should explore factors that influence the transition from intention to actual enrollment and track the career trajectories to assess the impact of postgraduate degrees on cost-effectiveness outcomes.

Conclusion

Several factors influenced Thai dietitians’ intentions to pursue postgraduate studies in nutrition and dietetics. Personal and professional growth, career advancement, and knowledge enhancement motivated many, along with supportive social networks. However, time constraints, financial burdens, language challenges, and commuting difficulties posed challenges. Ideal programs should balance theory and practice, offer flexibility, and provide meaningful and practical research opportunities. Comprehensive support systems, including financially accessible and flexible programs, are also essential to encourage postgraduate studies and support professional growth among dietitians in Thailand.
