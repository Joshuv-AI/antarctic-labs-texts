A Polyhedral Perspective on the Perfect Matching Lattice
Olha Silina

Abstract
We study the perfect matching lattice of a matching covered graph G G , generated by the incidence vectors of its perfect matchings. Building on results of Lovász and de Carvalho, Lucchesi, and Murty, we give a polynomial-time algorithm based on polyhedral methods that constructs a lattice basis for this lattice consisting of perfect matchings of G G . By decomposing along certain odd cuts, we reduce the graph into subgraphs whose perfect matching polytopes coincide with their bipartite relaxations (known as Birkhoff von Neumann graphs ). This yields a constructive polyhedral proof of the existence of such bases and highlights new connections between combinatorial and geometric properties of perfect matchings.



1
Introduction

Consider a graph
G=(V,E)
with an even number of vertices. A
perfect matching
is a set of edges
M\subseteq E
incident with each vertex exactly once.
G
is
matching covered
if every edge of
G
belongs to some perfect matching.
The theory of matching covered graphs is a rich area of combinatorics with pioneering results due to Edmonds, Seymour, and Lovász. An object of particular interest is the
perfect matching lattice
, defined as the set of integral linear combinations of the incidence vectors of perfect matchings:

\mathcal{L}(G):=\lat(\mathcal{M})=\{\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}k_{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}:k_{M}\in\mathbb{Z}\},

where
\mathcal{M}
denotes the set of all perfect matchings of
G
.
Lovász
[
10
]
gave a characterization of this lattice based on a structural decomposition of the graph into blocks called
bricks
and
braces
, with the Petersen graph being the only obstruction for this lattice to contain all the integral points in the linear hull of the perfect matchings.

A natural question is whether the generating set of this lattice, i.e. the perfect matchings of
G
, contains a basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
. This question was answered positively by de Carvalho, Lucchesi, and Murty
[
4
]
who proved it through ear-decompositions of a graph. In this paper, we present an algorithmic analogue of this result, mainly:

Theorem 1.1
.

There is a polynomial time algorithm that, given a matching covered graph
G
, finds a lattice basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
consisting of incidence vectors of perfect matchings of
G
.

The main contribution of this paper is a new approach to basis construction for the perfect matching lattice based on polyhedral theory. In addition to the algorithmic result, we identify a class of graphs where a lattice basis can be obtained by a simple inductive process. Moreover, we connect the combinatorial and polyhedral properties of the perfect matchings, bridging our approach and structural methods of de Carvalho, Lucchesi, and Murty. More broadly, our polyhedral techniques offer new tools that may prove valuable in tackling longstanding open problems in matching theory.

To begin with, the
perfect matching polytope

PM(G)
is the convex hull of its perfect matching incidence vectors and is described by the following set of inequalities (Edmonds,
[
7
]
):

PM(G)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{A}_{+}:\begin{array}[]{cc}x(\delta(v))=1&\forall v\in V\\
x(C)\geq 1&\forall C\textmd{ odd cut}\end{array}\right\}

(1)

Here, a
cut

C=\delta(U)
for some
U\subseteq V
is the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in
U
; the sets
U
and its complement
V\backslash U
are the
shores
of
C
. A cut is
odd
if both of its shores have an odd cardinality.

The main idea of the paper is to utilize odd cut decomposition methods to reduce our graph to a number of graphs whose perfect matching polytope coincides with its
bipartite relaxation
:

P(G):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{A}_{+}:x(\delta(v))=1\forall v\in V\right\}.

(2)

Matching covered graphs for which
PM(G)=P(G)
are called
Birkhoff von Neumann (BvN)
graphs. All bipartite graphs and some non-bipartite graphs, in particular certain types of bricks (to be defined later) are BvN. Our algorithm for finding a basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
either finds a set of
\dim(P(G))
linearly independent integral vertices of
P(G)
or finds a facet of
PM(G)
induced by an odd cut constraint
x(C)\geq 1
. We then use this facet to further decompose the graph into smaller ones until eventually the algorithm successfully finds a basis.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we discuss a way to decompose the graph into simple pieces and show how to reduce the basis construction for
\mathcal{L}(G)
to these pieces. In Section
3
we describe a basis construction algorithm for
Birkhoff von Neumann (BvN)
graphs based on polyhedral techniques and discuss what happens when applying this algorithm to a non-BvN graph. In Section
4
we show how to decompose non-BvN graphs into BvN ones using a specific type of odd cuts in a process similar to the tight cut decomposition. In Section
5
we provide an algorithm to find an appropriate cut to be used in the above mentioned decomposition. Finally, in Appendix
A.1
we draw connections between our polyhedral view of the basis and the classical approach based on
ear decompositions
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Tight cut decomposition

Let
G=(V,E)
be a perfect matching covered graph. An odd cut
C
is
tight
if every perfect matching of
G
intersects it exactly once. Polyhedrally, these are the odd cuts whose corresponding constraint in (
1
) is an implicit equality. There are two types of odd cuts of particular interest.

Definition 2.1
.

A pair
\{u,v\}
of vertices in a matching covered graph
G
is a
2
-separation
if
G-u-v
has at least two connected components of even cardinality. A set
B\subseteq V
of at least
2
vertices is a
barrier
if
G-B
has exactly
|B|
odd connected components.

It is quite straightforward to see that if
\{u,v\}
is a
2
-separation in a matching covered
G
with
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
being the (even) components of
G-u-v
, then
C:=\delta(U_{1}\cup\{u\})
and
C^{\prime}:=\delta(U_{1}\cup\{v\})
are non-trivial tight cuts. We refer to these as
2
-separation cuts
.
Similarly, if
B
is a barrier with
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{|B|}
being odd components of
G-B
, then each of
\delta(V_{i})
is a tight cut. We refer to these types of cuts as
barrier cuts
.
The following result highlights the importance of the above definitions:

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem
4.7
in
[
6
]
)
.

If a perfect matching covered graph has a non-trivial tight cut then it has a barrier or
2
-separation tight cut.

This theorem implies that tight cuts can be found efficiently. Indeed, it suffices to check for
2
-separation and barrier cuts. The former can be identified by going over all pairs of vertices
\{u,v\}
and counting the number of components in
G-u-v
. For barrier detection, we use the following characterization for
maximal
barriers, i.e. barriers that are not contained in larger barriers:

Theorem 2.3

(Theorem
3.2
in
[
9
]
)
.

The maximal barriers are exactly the equivalence classes under the equivalence relation on
V
:
x\sim y
iff
G-x-y
has no perfect matching.

Once a tight cut is identified, one could
contract
it: for each of the two shores, consider a graph obtained by shrinking it into a single vertex. Thus, we obtain two smaller graphs. Repeating this process for the new set of graphs, we end up with a list of graphs obtained by contracting each shore of a tight cut into a single vertex. This process is known as
tight cut decomposition
and results in a set of matching covered graphs with no tight cuts. Non-bipartite matching covered graphs with no tight cuts are called
bricks
, and bipartite ones are called
braces
. It is also known that the list of bricks and braces obtained as a result of this decomposition does not depend on the choice of tight cuts. Furthermore, any inclusion-wise maximal laminar family of tight cuts results in a brick and brace decomposition
[
10
]
. Since any laminar family on
|V|
elements has size at most
n-1
(we are interested in non-trivial tight cuts), the total number of bricks and braces is at most
n
, giving the following result:

Corollary 2.4
.

A tight cut decomposition of a graph can be found in polynomial time.

2.2
Lattice theory and dimension results

Given a finite set of vectors
S=\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, their
linear hull
is the set of all possible linear combinations
\lin(S)=\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}:\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}
. An
integral basis
for
\lin(S)
is a set
B
of linearly independent vectors such that any
\mathbf{z}\in\lin(S)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is an integral linear combination of
B
. Assuming all the vectors have rational coordinates, the
lattice
spanned by
S
is the set of all integer linear combinations
\lat(S)=\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}:\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. In this case, we say that
S

spans

\lat(S)
. It is well known that any lattice
\mathcal{L}
has a
basis
, that is, a linearly independent set of vectors that spans
\mathcal{L}
, however, it need not be contained in the original set of generators. The
dimension
of the lattice is equal to the dimension of its linear hull.

Since we are not interested in edges that do not belong to any perfect matching, it is convenient to delete from
G
all edges that are not in any perfect matching, thus making the graph matching covered.
The number of bricks in a tight cut decomposition of a matching covered graph affects the dimension of its perfect matching polytope. In particular, the dimension of the perfect matching polytope is

\dim(PM(G))=|E^{\prime}|-|V|+1-b,

(3)

where
b
denotes the number of bricks in a tight cut decomposition of
G
and
E^{\prime}
is the set of edges that belong to a perfect matching
[
6
]
, with
E=E^{\prime}
for matching covered
G
. Furthermore, the dimension of the perfect matching lattice and its linear hull is

\dim(\lat(G))=|E^{\prime}|-|V|+2-b.

(4)

We will call
near-bricks
all matching covered graphs
G
with exactly one brick.
The following fact due to Lovász connects the linear hull of perfect matchings with
\mathcal{L}(G)
:

Theorem 2.5

(Theorem
6.3
in
[
10
]
)
.

In a matching covered graph
G=(V,E)
, let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of perfect matchings. Then any
x\in\lin(\mathcal{M})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{E}
satisfies
2x\in\mathcal{L}(G)
. Furthermore, if
G
has no Petersen bricks then
\lin(\mathcal{M})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{E}=\mathcal{L}(G)
.

2.3
Petersen graph

In the latter sections, we will reduce lattice basis construction to bricks and braces. In light of
Theorem

2.5
, we describe how to deal with Petersen graph separately.

Suppose that one of the graphs obtained after tight cut decomposition is a Petersen graph
G
(potentially, with parallel edges). To construct the basis for the Petersen graph, first consider the case where
G
has no parallel edges. In this case, there are exactly
6
perfect matchings, as illustrated in
Figure

1
and they form a linearly independent set. Via a simple linear algebraic argument, it can be checked that these vectors form a basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
.

Figure 1:
The Petersen graph. Its perfect matchings are the central one (yellow) and all possible rotations of the one in blue.

Now, if one adds to an edge
e
of
G=(V,E)
a parallel edge
e_{2}
to obtain a new graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
, the new basis can be obtained from a lattice basis
\mathcal{B}
for
\mathcal{L}(G)
as follows.
Pick one perfect matching
M_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
containing
e
and let
M^{*}=M_{1}\backslash\{e\}\cup\{e_{2}\}
. Consider the set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}\cup\{\mathbf{1}_{M^{*}}\}
. We claim that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a lattice basis for
\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})
.
Indeed, it can be readily checked that the set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is linearly independent and
M^{*}
is the only element using
e_{2}
. Furthermore, (
4
) implies that
\dim(\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime}))=\dim(\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime}))
so
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a linear basis. To prove it is integral, consider
y\in\lin(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{E^{\prime}}
, so that
y=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\alpha_{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}
. Since
y(e_{2})
is integral, it means that
\alpha_{M^{*}}
is integral. Hence
y-\alpha_{M^{*}}\mathbf{1}_{M^{*}}\in\lin(\mathcal{B})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{E}
, implying that it is indeed an integral linear combination of
\mathcal{B}
, as wanted.

Repeating this replication process until we reach the correct edge multiplicity results in a lattice basis for the target graph and can be done in polynomial time.

2.4
Tight cut combining

The following approach describes combining bases for the matching lattices of two matching covered graphs into one using an odd cut. See Section 6.3.1 of
[
11
]
for more details.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a matching covered graph
G
, we call an odd cut
C

separating
if both
C
-contractions are matching covered.

Equivalently,
C
is separating if and only if every edge of
G
belongs to a perfect matching
M
with
|C\cap M|=1
. All tight cuts are separating, but there could be other examples.

Let
G
be a matching covered graph with a non-trivial separating odd cut
C
. Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be the two
C
-contractions of
G
. Additionally, say that both
G_{i}
are matching-covered graphs with
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\{\mathbf{x}^{1},\mathbf{x}^{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}=\{\mathbf{y}^{1},\mathbf{y}^{2},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{d_{2}}\}
being lattice bases for
\lat(G_{1})
and
\lat(G_{2})
consisting of perfect matchings.

Definition 2.7

(composition)
.

For two vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{1}}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{2}}
such that
\mathbf{v}_{1}(e)=\mathbf{v}_{2}(e)
for all
e\in E_{1}\cap E_{2}
, define their
composition

\mathbf{w}:=\mathbf{v}_{1}\odot\mathbf{v}_{2}
as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}
whose restriction on
E_{i}
equals
\mathbf{v}_{i}
for
i=1,2
. We define
composition

\mathcal{B}_{1}\odot\mathcal{B}_{2}
as follows. For any edge
e\in C
, define
I(e):=\{i:\mathbf{x}^{i}(e)=1\}
and
J(e):=\{j:\mathbf{y}^{j}(e)=1\}
to be the indices of elements of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
that contain edge
e
. Write
I(e)=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
and
J(e)=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\}
for convenience; notice that
k\geq 1
and
\ell\geq 1
. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{e}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{x}^{i_{1}}\odot\mathbf{y}^{j_{t}}\quad

\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,\ell

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{e}_{\ell+t}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{x}^{i_{1+t}}\odot\mathbf{y}^{j_{1}}

\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,k-1.

We define
\mathcal{B}_{1}\odot\mathcal{B}_{2}=\{\mathbf{z}^{e}_{i}:e\in C,1\leq i\leq|I_{e}|+|J_{e}|-1\}

Compositions of two lattice bases have useful properties, as shown in e.g.
[
2
]
,
[
1
]
:

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
G
be a matching covered graph and let
C
be an odd cut.
Let
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{B}_{1}\odot\mathcal{B}_{2}
. Then:

(i)

\mathcal{B}
consists of linearly independent vectors;

(ii)

any integral vector in
\lin(\mathcal{B})
can be expressed as an integral linear combination of
\mathcal{B}
;

(iii)

\lin(\mathcal{B})=\lin(PM(G)\cap\{x:x(C)=1\})
;

(iv)

|\mathcal{B}|=|\mathcal{B}_{1}|+|\mathcal{B}_{2}|-|C|
.

Since composition of two bases can be done in polynomial time, it suffices to prove Theorem
1.1
for bricks and braces. Then, to obtain a matching lattice basis for an arbitrary graph
G
, it suffices to consider its tight cut decomposition into (linearly many) bricks and braces
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}
and then compose the resulting bases using Theorem
2.8
. The rest of the paper is dedicated to proving the following fact:

Theorem 2.9
.

There is a polynomial time algorithm that, given a brick or a brace
G
, finds a lattice basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
consisting of incidence vectors of perfect matchings of
G
.

3
A polyhedral approach: Birkhoff von Neumann graphs

3.1
An algorithm for BvN graphs

In the following sections we discuss a polyhedral approach to basis construction. To demonstrate the main idea, we first look at the graphs
G
whose perfect matching polytope coincides with its
bipartite relaxation
:

P(G):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{\geq 0}:\begin{array}[]{cc}x(\delta(v))=1&\forall v\in V\end{array}\right\}.

(5)

In other words, all points satisfying non-negativity and degree constraints of (
5
) also satisfy the odd cut constraints of (
1
). Recall that graphs with this property are called
Birkhoff von Neumann (BvN)
.

Bipartite graphs are always BvN (see, e.g. 18.1 of
[
12
]
). Non-bipartite graphs are BvN if and only if there are two vertex-disjoint odd cycles whose complement contains a perfect matching due to the fact that every vertex of the bipartite relaxation is half-integral (30.2 in
[
12
]
). Despite this, the problem of testing BvN in polynomial time is still open.

BvN graphs admit the following polyhedral algorithm that continuously reduces the dimension of the polytope.
At each step
t
, we have a set of current edges of the graph
E^{t}\subseteq E
which is initially equal to
E
. We consider the polytope
P^{t}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{t}}:x(\delta_{G[E_{t}]}(v))=1\forall v\in V,x\geq\mathbf{0}\}
. Its vertices are precisely the perfect matchings of
G[E_{t}]
since
G
was BvN and
G[E_{t}]
is just a restriction of
PM(G)
to the faces defined by
x_{e}=0
for
e\in E\backslash E_{t}
. The algorithm will select a facet of the current polytope
P^{t}
defined by some
x_{e}\geq 0
and go to the facet defined by this inequality. At the same time, augment the current basis
\mathcal{B}
by an incidence vector of any perfect matching
M_{e}
that contains
e
. The total number of steps this algorithm will take is exactly
\dim(PM(G))
as the dimension of the current polytope drops by one each time.

To find a facet-defining inequality, it suffices to identify an edge
e
such that removing the constraint
x_{e}\geq 0
from the polyhedral description creates some new feasible points, which inevitably would have
x_{e}<0
. Therefore, such a point exists if and only if the following linear program has a negative optimal solution:

\min\left\{x_{e}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{t}},\begin{array}[]{cc}x(\delta(v))=1&\forall v\in V\\
x_{f}\geq 0&\forall f\in E_{t}\backslash\{e\}\end{array}\right\}.

(LP1
(t,e)
)

To find an appropriate matching, one can solve a similar linear program, but maximize the value of
x_{e}
instead:

\max\left\{x_{e}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{E_{t}},\begin{array}[]{cc}x(\delta(v))=1&\forall v\in V\\
x_{f}\geq 0&\forall f\in E_{t}\end{array}\right\}.

(LP2
(t,e)
)

Algorithm
1
summarizes this approach.

1:

a BvN graph
G

2:

a set of perfect matchings
\mathcal{B}
that forms a basis to
\mathcal{L}(G)

3:

E^{0}\leftarrow E
,
P^{0}\leftarrow PM(G)=P(G)
,
\mathcal{B}\leftarrow\emptyset
;

4:

for
t=0,1,2,\ldots,\dim(PM(G))-1
:

5:

find
e\in E^{t}
such that
LP1

(
t
,
e
)

has negative objective value;

6:

let
y(e)
be a vertex solution to
LP2

(
t
,
e
)

;

7:

define
E^{t+1}=E^{t}\backslash\{e\}
,
P^{t+1}=P^{t}\cap\{x:x_{e}=0\}
, update
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\cup\{y(e)\}
;

8:

return

\mathcal{B}
.

Algorithm 1

BvN basis construction.

Theorem 3.1
.

The result of Algorithm
1
applied to a matching covered BvN graph is a set
\mathcal{B}
of linearly independent indicator vectors of perfect matchings that forms an integral basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
.

Proof.

Because
G
is BvN it implies that any face of
P(G)
has integral vertices. Therefore, all
y(e)
found as a corner solution to
LP2

(
t
,
e
)

will indeed be incidence vectors of some perfect matchings of
G
(in fact, of
G[E_{t}]
).
It is clear that the final set
\mathcal{B}
will contain exactly
\dim(PM(G))
perfect matchings with the property that each one uses an edge not used in the previous ones. This means that the elements of
\mathcal{B}
are linearly independent and moreover, create an integral basis for
\lin(\mathcal{B})
.
∎

3.2
Non-BvN detection

As mentioned above, the problem of recognizing BvN graphs in polynomial time is still open.
We will avoid directly addressing this problem and instead we will use the following idea which still allows us to use the BvN algorithm for bricks that are non-BvN.

If, given any brick
G
, the above process terminates in
|E|-|V|+1
steps and all the output vectors are integral, then they correspond to a basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
.
Therefore, the only algorithm obstruction in the non-BvN case is
LP2

(
t
,
e
)

having a fractional solution
x^{*}
. This means that
x^{*}
is a fractional vertex of
P(G)
itself, which gives a certificate that
G
is non-BvN.
As
x^{*}
has to be half-integral, its support consists of vertex-disjoint edges of weight
1
and odd cycles of weight
1/2
. Picking any of the odd cycles, we can consider the odd cut
C^{\prime}
whose shore is exactly the vertices of this cycle. Inevitably, we have
x^{*}(C^{\prime})=0
.
In
Section

5
, we describe a combinatorial algorithm to find an odd cut
C
such that the inequality
x(C)\geq 1
is violated by
x^{*}
with more specific properties:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
G
be a brick that is non-BvN. Let
x^{*}
be a fractional vertex of
P(G)
. There is a polynomial time algorithm that finds a separating cut
C
of
G
such that
x^{*}(G)<1
and both
C
-contractions of
G
are near-bricks whose brick is not the Petersen brick.

The following result will prove useful. We will provide the proof of this statement in the appendix.

Theorem 3.3
.

In a near-brick
G
, for an odd cut
C
, the following are equivalent:

•

C
is separating and
x(C)\geq 1
defines a facet of the perfect matching polytope
PM(G)
, and

•

both
C
-contractions are matching-covered near-bricks.

4
General graphs

As in Section
3.2
, consider a non-BvN brick
G
for which the BvN algorithm fails. Theorem
3.2
guarantees the existence of a separating cut
C
both of whose contractions, call them
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, have exactly one brick that is not the Petersen brick.
Since neither
G_{1}
nor
G_{2}
contain the Petersen graph, we can construct lattice bases
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
for the corresponding graphs. Since both contractions do not contain Petersen bricks,
\lin(\mathcal{B}_{i})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{E_{i}}=\mathcal{L}(G_{i})
and hence by
Theorem

2.8
the set
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{B}_{1}\odot\mathcal{B}_{2}
will be an integral basis for its linear hull. However, all elements of
\mathcal{B}
satisfy
x^{T}\mathbf{1}_{C}=1
, so to make it a basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
we need to add at least one perfect matching
M
with
|M\cap C|>1
.
We use the following fact shown in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

In a non-BvN brick that is not Petersen, every separating cut has a perfect matching intersecting it three times.

Such a perfect matching
M^{*}
can be found in polynomial time by considering all possible intersections
M^{*}\cap C
of size three. Finally, we can use
M^{*}
to increment the basis. We can now prove that such a set
\mathcal{B}\cup\{\mathbf{1}_{M^{*}}\}
will be a basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a matching covered graph and let
C
be a non-tight separating cut such that both
C
-contractions are near-bricks whose brick is not the Petersen brick. Let
M^{*}
be a perfect matching with
|M^{*}\cap C|=3
. Then
\mathcal{B}\cup\{\mathbf{1}_{M^{*}}\}
is an integral basis for
\mathcal{L}(G)
consisting of perfect matchings.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, such
C
defines a facet of
PM(G)
. Hence, using part
(iii)
of Theorem
2.8
,
|\mathcal{B}|=\dim(\mathcal{L}(G))-1
. Thus, adding
M^{*}
to
\mathcal{B}
makes it a basis for the linear hull of
\mathcal{L}(G)
, so it suffices to prove that it will be an integral basis.
Indeed, consider any
y\in\mathcal{L}(G)
. We also know it is in the linear hull of
\mathcal{B}\cup\{\mathbf{1}_{M^{*}}\}
. Therefore, we can write it as follows

y=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\alpha_{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}=\sum_{z^{i}\in\mathcal{B}}\beta_{i}z^{i}+\beta_{*}\mathbf{1}_{M^{*}},

(6)

where all
\alpha_{M}
are integers and the goal is to prove that
\beta_{i}
are all integer.
The first equality of (
6
) when multiplied by
\textbf{1}_{E}
gives

\frac{1}{n/2}\textbf{1}^{T}_{E}y=\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\alpha_{M}\textbf{1}^{T}_{E}\mathbf{1}_{M}=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\alpha_{M}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Similarly, multiplying it by
\textbf{1}_{C}
gives

\textbf{1}^{T}_{C}y=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\alpha_{M}\textbf{1}^{T}_{C}\mathbf{1}_{M}\equiv\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\alpha_{M}\mod 2.

Applying the same idea to the last expression of equality (
6
) we obtain

\frac{1}{n/2}\textbf{1}^{T}_{E}y=\sum_{\mathbf{z}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}}\beta_{i}+\beta_{*},\textbf{1}^{T}_{C}y=\sum_{\mathbf{z}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}}\beta_{i}+3\beta_{*}.

Therefore,
\beta_{*}=\frac{1}{2}(\textbf{1}^{T}_{C}y-\frac{1}{n/2}\textbf{1}^{T}_{E}y)
, which is integral by the above.
Finally, consider
y-\beta_{*}\mathbf{1}_{M^{*}}\in\lin(\mathcal{B})
. Since we already showed that
\mathcal{B}
is an integral basis for its linear hull, all the remaining coefficients
\beta_{i}
are also integer.
∎

5.1
Finding a separating cut

If
C

Consider this contraction: call it

We may assume

\cup_{v_{i}\in B\backslash\{u\}}\delta(v_{i})\cup C=\cup_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}\cup 2E^{\prime}

Claim 1
.

For all

Proof of Claim.

For any perfect matching

of

\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}|M\cap\delta(v_{i})|+|M\cap C|=\sum_{i=1}^{t}|M\cap F_{i}|+2|M\cap E^{\prime}|.

This reduces to:

|M\cap C|-1=\sum_{i=1}^{t}(|M\cap F_{i}|-1)+2|M\cap E^{\prime}|.

All terms on the right are nonnegative, so
|M\cap C|\geq|M\cap F_{i}|
for all

\Diamond

Claim 2

At least one of

Proof of Claim.

Suppose all of

Claim 3

\Diamond

Finding a facet defining cut

Let

If both

Case

In the above argument, we require that for the two
