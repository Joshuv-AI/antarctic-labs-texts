Systemic immune inflammatory index (SII) as a predictive marker for the failure of air enema treatment in children with Ileocolic intussusception: a case-control study
Authors: Dong Liu, Hui Cai, Qi-qi He, Shuo Wang, Feng Li, Juan Zhang, Ya-juan Fu, Ze-li Su, Yi-deng Jiang, Li Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe systemic immune inflammatory index (SII) is an emerging inflammatory marker and the effectiveness of air enema treatment may correlate with the degree of intestinal inflammation caused by ileocolic intussusception (ICI). Our goal was to identify the SII as a predictor of air enema failure, so that pediatric surgeons can perform surgical intervention in children with ICI as soon as possible, to minimize complications.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective, case-control study was conducted on children with ileocolic intussusception between 2017 and 2024. The admission demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were analyzed. The specificity and sensitivity of various parameters as predictors of the failure of air enema treatment were established using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsA total of 574 patients were included in the study (469 in the air enema success group and 105 in the air enema failure group). There was an independent positive correlation between SII and the risk of air enema treatment failure in ICI. Additionally, a nonlinear dose–response relationship was observed between SII and air enema treatment failure in ICI within the RCS. Threshold analysis revealed that on the right of this inflection point 332.3, there was a significant 25% increase in the prevalence of air enema treatment failure risk of ICI per 100-unit increase in the SII (OR: 1.25, 95%CI: 1.17–1.32). In the ROC curve analysis, SII demonstrated the highest area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8665 (95%CI: 0.8313–0.9018) compared to other inflammation indices. The critical point for SII was estimated to be 721.5 (sensitivity, 86.67%; specificity, 71.00%).ConclusionsThe SII, a novel, cost-effective, and objective inflammatory marker, can be utilized to predict the risk of air enema treatment failure in children with ileocolic intussusception.

Background

Ileocolic intussusception (ICI) accounts for the majority of pediatric intussusception and is most often idiopathic, although a minority is related to pathological lead points such as Meckel’s diverticulum, polyps, or tumors [1]. It occurs when an ileo-ileal intussusception telescopes further through the ileocecal valve into the right colon [2]. ICI is primarily treated through a non-surgical approach using air enema reduction [3, 4]. If failed, surgical reduction or even intestinal resection is required [5]. Although there is a risk of intestinal perforation during an air enema procedure [6], employing increased pressure settings, extending the duration, and adding extra air insufflation cycles are still necessary to ensure effective treatment outcomes, which in turn increases the risk. Several guidelines and institutional protocols have proposed recommended ranges for pressure, number of attempts and duration, but there is still no universally accepted standard, and considerable variation in practice remains. For example, Japanese guidelines suggest starting pressures of about 60–80 mmHg for gas reduction and limiting the maximum pressure to around 120 mmHg, and national audits and reviews report that most centres use pressures within a similar 80–120 mmHg range [7, 8]. In routine clinical work, specific parameters are often determined according to the individual judgment and experience of the pediatric surgeon. For less experienced surgeons, this reliance on subjective assessment may increase the risk of bowel perforation or exacerbation of the patient’s condition. Accordingly, there is a clear need for an objective index to predict the risk of failure of air enema reduction and to guide pediatric surgeons in their decision-making.

The clinical manifestations of intussusception in children vary and are non-specific. The typical triad includes emesis, intermittent abdominal pain, and currant jelly. However, less than half of children with intussusception exhibit these typical symptoms [9]. Moreover, unlike adults, children are generally less cooperative when providing medical history and undergoing physical examinations. Consequently, before administering an enema, it is challenging for pediatric surgeons to precisely assess the condition in children with intussusception. Owing to this unique characteristic in children, more objective laboratory examinations seem to provide more precise evidence for pediatric surgeons.

Several studies have been conducted to explore potential clinical or radiological predictors of the need for surgical reduction in children with intussusception [10, 11]. Fallon et al. reported that prolonged abdominal symptoms (> 2 days), age < 1 year at reduction, failure of the initial enema, and adverse ultrasound findings such as bowel wall thickening, free or interloop peritoneal fluid, and pathological lead points were associated with an increased likelihood of operative management [10]. In contrast, Tareen et al. found that the length of the history did not significantly influence the success rate of pneumatic reduction, and recommended air enema as first-line treatment regardless of symptom duration [11]. These findings suggest that, although some clinical and ultrasonographic features show promise, their predictive value is inconsistent across studies. Efforts have also been made to predict intestinal necrosis using lactic acid or intestinal fatty acid–binding proteins, yet the outcomes have been inconclusive and the reported sensitivity and specificity remain modest [12]. Overall, no single clinical, radiological, or biochemical marker has been universally accepted as a sensitive and specific predictor for identifying children who will ultimately require surgery.

From a pathophysiological perspective, ileocolic intussusception can cause venous congestion and bowel wall edema, which may progress to ischemia and mucosal barrier injury, thereby triggering a systemic inflammatory response. Consistent with this mechanism, prior studies have reported that higher inflammatory or perfusion-related laboratory markers are associated with more severe disease and poorer nonsurgical outcomes. For example, elevated neutrophil measures have been associated with enema reduction failure, and increased NLR and CRP have been linked to a higher likelihood of requiring surgery in children with ileocolic intussusception [13, 14]. In addition, lactate, reflecting tissue hypoperfusion, has been associated with poor outcomes including air enema failure or early recurrence [15]. These data collectively support the rationale that systemic inflammation severity may be related to reducibility and treatment failure. Therefore, the systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII), a CBC-derived composite marker integrating platelets, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, may provide a practical and objective measure of systemic inflammation in this setting [16, 17]. It has been shown to correlate with a variety of chronic and acute inflammatory conditions and serves as a relatively stable and precise indicator of local or systemic immune and inflammatory responses [17]. Previous studies have demonstrated that SII is associated with the prognostic value of various tumors and diseases caused by acute or chronic inflammation or immune dysfunction [18–20]. In recent years, the application field of the SII has been expanding, and more studies have shown that the SII can also be used to predict the risk of certain diseases and monitor therapeutic effects [21]. Most of these data have been derived from adult populations with malignant and inflammatory diseases, including gastrointestinal disorders. In pediatric populations, emerging evidence suggests that SII is associated with disease severity or adverse outcomes in conditions such as necrotizing enterocolitis, IgA vasculitis with gastrointestinal involvement, and acute appendicitis [22, 23]. However, to our knowledge, SII has not yet been evaluated as a predictor of treatment failure or the need for surgery in children with ileocolic intussusception.

The objective of our study was to examine the predictive value of SII for the risk of air enema treatment failure in pediatric patients with intussusception. We aimed to compare it with various clinical and laboratory indicators to provide insights for the clinical management of ICI, with the goal of reducing or eliminating complications such as intestinal perforation during air enema.

Methods

Study design and population

The study adopted a retrospective research design. Clinical data from patients diagnosed with ICI between January 2017 and June 2024 were systematically collected at the pediatric surgery department of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. This department, being the first pediatric surgery department established in the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, holds the highest level in Ningxia and serves nearly all children with intussusception in the region. Thus, our study effectively reflects the overall situation of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region. All pediatric patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Diagnosis

All children underwent abdominal ultrasonography on admission. The sonographic diagnosis of intussusception was made when typical findings were present, including an intussusception mass with the classic “target”/“doughnut” (concentric ring) appearance on transverse view and/or the “pseudokidney/sandwich” appearance on longitudinal view, and the radiology report indicated intussusception. The diagnosis was subsequently confirmed during air enema reduction, which served as both a diagnostic and therapeutic procedure.

Air enema reduction procedure

Pneumatic reduction was performed under fluoroscopic guidance. Room air was insufflated per rectum, and the progress of reduction was monitored using characteristic fluoroscopic appearances. A “cup-shaped” sign was observed at the apex of the intussusception; with ongoing insufflation, this sign moved proximally along the colon toward the ileocecal region. Successful reduction was defined by disappearance of the cup-shaped sign after passing the right lower quadrant/ileocecal region and visualization of free passage of air into the small bowel.

The intraluminal pressure was strictly limited to a maximum of 13 kPa (97.5 mmHg). The number of attempts was determined according to fluoroscopic progression: if the cup-shaped sign showed no migration and remained fixed at the maximum pressure, the reduction was usually terminated after three attempts; if migration was observed, up to five attempts were allowed. Attempts were separated by intervals of approximately 1–5 min. Failure was defined as reduction not achieved after maximal allowed pressure and attempts, as documented in the medical record (reduction not achieved after maximal attempts and pressure), and these patients proceeded to operative management. No sedatives were used during the procedure.

Data collection

Demographic data, clinical manifestations, onset-to-admission time (OAT), complete blood count (CBC) results, and treatment of intussusception (air enema or operation) were retrieved from the hospital information system (HIS) and are recorded in the case table. Clinical manifestations include abdominal pain, nausea, emesis, bloody stools, and fever (≥ 37.5 °C). Complete blood count (CBC) was obtained after admission and prior to air enema reduction, and included white blood cell (WBC), absolute neutrophil, absolute lymphocyte, absolute monocyte, and platelet counts. SII was calculated based on these pretreatment values (platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count).

Data calculation

In this study, we performed statistical analysis on the retrieved laboratory variables. where P, N, L, and M are platelet counts, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes in the peripheral blood, respectively. From this, we calculated the systemic immune inflammation index [SII, (P*N)/L)] [11] and other CBC-derived inflammatory indicators [24] - system inflammation response index (SIRI, [P*M)/L), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR, N/L), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR, P/L), platelet-to-neutrophil ratio (PNR, P/N), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR, L/M).

Variables

Exposure variable and outcome variable

SII served as the exposure variable. Included children were divided into three categories based on the SII tertiles: Q1 (< 419.95), Q2 (419.95–819.88), and Q3 (> 819.88). The primary outcome was air enema failure, defined as unsuccessful reduction on air enema requiring operative management. Operative management included surgical reduction with or without bowel resection, and all children who proceeded to any operative intervention were classified as “air enema failure”. The decision to proceed to surgery was made by the attending pediatric surgeon based on a combination of (i) failure to achieve reduction on air enema, and/or (ii) clinical deterioration or signs suggestive of complications. Intraoperative findings subsequently determined whether bowel resection was required.

Covariates

Covariates were prespecified a priori based on clinical plausibility and previously reported predictors of failed enema reduction or operative management in pediatric ileocolic intussusception. Accordingly, sex, age, onset-to-admission time (OAT), clinical symptoms at presentation (abdominal pain, fever, nausea, emesis, and bloody stools), and white blood cell count (WBC) were included as covariates [25]. WBC was included as a routinely available inflammatory marker and potential confounder to evaluate the incremental value of SII beyond conventional CBC-based indicators.

Clinical manifestations (fever, nausea, emesis, abdominal pain, and bloody stools) were extracted from the medical record based on the history provided by parents/guardians covering the period from symptom onset to presentation and the physician’s admission assessment. Fever was defined as a documented history of elevated body temperature before presentation (measured at home by caregivers) and/or an elevated temperature recorded by medical staff at presentation.

Statistical analyses

The mean and standard deviation (SD) were used for continuous variables with a normal distribution, whereas the median and inter-quartile range (IQR) were used for skewed distributions. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Frequencies (%) were used to describe the categorical variables. To assess the variations among different groups, we conducted one-way analyses of variance for normal distribution, Kruskal–Wallis tests for skewed distribution, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a VIF threshold of 10 to identify severe multicollinearity. Logistic regression models were used to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the air enema failure risk of ICI connection. Model 1 was an unadjusted rough model, and Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 was adjusted for age + sex + OAT + clinical symptoms (abdominal pain, Fever, Nausea/emesis, Bloody stools); Model 4 was further adjusted for age + sex + OAT + clinical symptoms (abdominal pain, fever, nausea/emesis, and bloody stools) + WBC. In addition, curve fitting using restricted cubic splines (RCS) was performed with four knots at the 5th (127.96), 35th (448.27), 65th (796.35), and 95th (2002.20) percentiles to assess the linearity between SII and ICI surgery intervention [26]. Furthermore, homogeneity among subgroups was evaluated using a stratified logistic regression model that took into account age, sex, clinical symptoms (abdominal pain, Fever, Nausea, emesis, Bloody stools), and WBC count. Ultimately, likelihood ratio tests were used to examine the associations between SII and surgery risk of ICI in the subgroups. A two-piecewise logistic regression model was used to calculate the threshold effect of SII. Finally, we created a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to assess how well the SII can predict ICI surgery by determining the area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curve and the most effective threshold. Given the retrospective design, the sample size was determined by available cases. We did not perform post-hoc power calculations, as they are generally considered uninformative for completed studies; instead, we evaluated sample size adequacy based on events-per-parameter and the precision of estimates. Data processing and analysis were performed using R (4.3.0), along with Zstats v1.0 (www.zstats.net). The tests were two-sided, with a P value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

After screening, from January 2017 to June 2024, a total of 687 patients with intussusception were admitted to our center (Fig. 1). Among them, 574 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The mean age was 22.13 months, including 396 males (69.0%) and 178 females (31.0%).

Assessment and treatment of multicollinearity

A VIF threshold of 10 was used to define severe multicollinearity. Results showed that the VIF values of “nausea” and “emesis” were 13.593 and 13.550, respectively, indicating severe collinearity between the two variables (Table 2). Considering the clinical pathological characteristics of ICI - nausea and emesis are highly correlated accompanying symptoms of gastrointestinal obstruction (emesis often occurs with nausea, and severe nausea is prone to progressing to emesis) - the two variables were merged into a composite variable named “nausea/emesis” to retain comprehensive clinical information while eliminating collinearity. The merging rule was defined as: coded as “positive” if either nausea or vomiting was present, and “negative” if both symptoms were absent. After merging, VIF analysis was re-conducted for all independent variables and Table 2 shows that multicollinearity was effectively resolved (all VIF < 2).

Association between SII and air enema failure risk of ICI

Table 3 presents the results of the univariate analysis. Nausea/Emesis, Bloody stools, OAT, WBC, and SII were positively correlated with the risk of air enema failure in ICI. Table 4 illustrates an independent positive correlation between SII and the risk of air enema treatment failure in ICI across all multivariable logistic regression models, irrespective of adjustments for confounding variables (Model 1: OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.21–1.33; Model 2: OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.22–1.35; Model 3: OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.22–1.36; Model 4: OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.22–1.37). The distribution of confidence intervals further supports the independent association between the SII and the risk of air enema treatment failure in ICI. Notably, after considering potential influencing factors (Model 4), the risk of air enema failure increased by 29% with every 100-unit increment in the SII (Table 4). Additionally, this trend remained significant when SII was analyzed as a categorical variable (tertiles). As indicated in Table 4, the risk of ICI progressively increased with higher tertiles of the SII across all models (P for trend < 0.001). Moreover, a nonlinear dose–response relationship between SII and the risk of air enema treatment failure in ICI was observed in the RCS (Fig. 2).

Threshold analysis

Given the nonlinear relationship between the SII and failure of air enema treatment for ICI, a piecewise logistic regression model was employed to further assess this association. A two-piecewise logistic regression model was used to calculate the threshold effect of SII (Table 5). The model was adjusted for age, sex, OAT, clinical symptoms (abdominal pain, Fever, Nausea/emesis, and Bloody stools), and WBC. The findings indicated that there was a threshold effect on the relationship between SII and the risk of Fair enema treatment failure for ICI (P for the likelihood ratio test < 0.001). Overall, a positive association was observed between the SII and the risk of air enema treatment failure (OR (95%CI): 1.29 (1.22–1.37)]. The SII tangent point was 332.3, and on the left side of this point, there was no correlation between the SII and air enema failure of ICI. Conversely, on the right side of the inflection point, we observed a significant 25% increase in the prevalence of air enema treatment failure risk of ICI per 100-unit increase in the SII (OR, 1.25; 95%CI:1.17–1.32).

ROC analysis

The potential of SII as a predictor for ICI surgery was examined using ROC analysis, as presented in Table 6; Fig. 4, and the AUC was 0.8665 (95% CI 0.8313–0.9018). We compared SII with other inflammatory markers, such as SIRI, NLR, PLR, LMR, and PNR. It is surprising that SII demonstrated the highest AUC, and DeLong’s test showed that the difference was significant. Youden’s index was utilized to identify the the optimal cutoff in our cohort was 721.5, yielding a sensitivity of 86.67% and specificity of 71.00%.

Discussion

In this study, we revealed for the first time the relationship between SII and air enema treatment failure in children treated with ICI. We observed that a higher SII level correlated with a greater probability of air enema failure in children with ICI. An elevated SII may serve as an adjunctive risk-stratification marker to increase awareness of potential air enema failure and support preparedness and counseling; however, specific cutoff values require external validation. Compared with other inflammatory indexes, SII has the highest predictive confidence and value for predicting air enema failure in children with ileocolic intussusception, with an AUC of 0.8665 (0.8313–0.9018). Moreover, the SII is an objective laboratory index derived from routine blood tests and is less dependent on caregiver-reported symptoms; however, it can still be influenced by the timing of blood sampling and the underlying inflammatory state. In our study, CBC was obtained after admission and before pneumatic reduction, and we adjusted for onset-to-admission time to partially account for timing-related variability.

Previous studies have investigated clinical and ultrasonographic predictors of failed enema reduction or operative management in pediatric ileocolic intussusception; however, reported predictors and their performance have been inconsistent across cohorts, as summarized in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [27]. Inflammation- or hypoperfusion-related laboratory markers such as neutrophil-related indices, NLR/CRP, and lactate have also been explored, but none has been universally adopted for routine decision support [13–15]. In this context, our study adds to the literature by evaluating pretreatment SII, a readily available CBC-derived composite marker integrating platelets, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, and by characterizing the exposure–response relationship using both categorical and continuous approaches.

Intestinal injury and inflammation caused by ICI can lead to increased intestinal inflammatory edema. The CBC results of two groups showed that the operation group exhibited notably higher absolute values for inflammatory parameters, including leukocytes and neutrophils, than the non-operation group. These results validated the aforementioned theory, indicating a high degree of intestinal inflammation. This may be related to damage to the intestinal mucosa and disruption of the intestinal barrier, which is associated with systemic inflammation [28]. Such significant intestinal inflammation could lead to substantial edema in the intussusception area and surrounding regions, potentially resulting in the failure of air enema [14]. This reminds us to consider whether inflammatory indicators can serve as predictors of air enema failure in children with ICI.

Children with high level of intestinal inflammation may endure intestinal damage or even perforation, along with higher potential risks of radiation exposure, if air enemas are applied with improper high levels of pressure, frequency, or duration. Thus, exploring a predictor assists clinical pediatric surgeons in conducting a more accurate assessment of a child’s condition prior to an air enema. This may help clinicians, particularly less experienced surgeons, to perform pneumatic reduction with strict adherence to pressure-limited strategies, optimize monitoring and team preparedness, and counsel families appropriately, thereby potentially reducing the risk of complications such as bowel perforation.

SII is a novel, reliable, and stable marker that reflects both the immune response and systemic inflammation at a low cost, making it a useful indicator for clinical applications [29, 30]. This index was developed by Hu et al. [17] and integrates the analyses of neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts. The SII level can provide more clinical information than one or two types of peripheral blood and may be a useful diagnostic biomarker for systemic inflammatory activity. Many studies have demonstrated that the SII has remarkable predictive ability [31].

To the best of our knowledge, no study has focused on the relationship between the SII and air enema failure of ICI. Previous studies on the risk factors for ICI surgery have mostly been based on clinical symptoms. Some scholars have reported that the duration of symptoms exceeding 24 h is a factor related to the surgical treatment of intussusception [32]. However, unlike adults, children cannot accurately describe their physical discomfort, and parents can only judge whether a disease occurs by observing their children’s behavior. For some inexperienced parents, the accuracy of their judgment is low and there may be deviations or errors [33]. We believe that medical history encompasses the information provided by parents and observations collected by doctors, both of which are susceptible to subjective interpretation. In contrast, SII is less dependent on caregiver-reported symptoms, although it may vary with disease stage and timing of sampling.

Ademuyiwa et al. [12] reported the use of intestinal fatty acid-binding proteins as predictors of intestinal necrosis with a sensitivity and specificity of 64% and 88%, respectively. However, owing to the small sample size of this study (25 patients with intestinal necrosis), it is challenging to extrapolate these results. We analyzed 574 children with ICI. Upon studying the relationship between SII and air enema failure in ICI, it has been determined that there is an independent positive correlation between the two and irrespective of adjustments for confounding variables. The risk of air enema failure increased by 29% with every 100-unit increment in the SII. Additionally, this trend remained significant when SII was analyzed as a categorical variable. The risk of air enema failure progressively increased with higher tertiles of the SII across all models. RCS illustrated an nonlinear dose–response relationship between SII and failure risk of air enema. This implies that with the escalation of SII values, children diagnosed with ICI face an elevated likelihood of encountering unsuccessful air enema treatment, ultimately requiring surgical intervention. From results of two-piecewise logistic-regression model, on the right side of SII tangent point 332.3, there was a significant 25% increase in the prevalence risk of air enema failure per 100-unit increase in the SII. The subgroup analyses highlighted the robustness of our results. All the data proves that SII can be used to predict the risk of air enema failure.

Besides SII, there are many other well-known inflammatory markers, such as SIRI, NLR, PLR, LMR, and PNR. We also comprehensively analyzed them. Although all of them were significantly different between the two groups, ROC curve analysis revealed that the SII had the highest AUC, indicating the highest sensitivity and specificity in predicting air enema treatment failure of ICI in children. This marker can be utilized to predict disease progression in children and the necessity for surgical intervention, showing a higher AUC than other CBC-derived indices evaluated in our cohort [28].

In our cohort, an ROC-derived cutoff of 721.5 showed fair discrimination; however, this threshold is data-driven and should be considered exploratory. Rather than serving as a stand-alone trigger for surgery, an elevated SII may prompt closer monitoring, strict adherence to the pressure-limited reduction strategy, and early surgical preparedness when clinically indicated. External prospective validation is required before adopting any specific cutoff in routine protocols.

Furthermore, the calculation of SII is simple and cost-effective, and is available in most hospital laboratories, meaning that children do not need to incur additional expenses for extra tests. With the aid of the SII’s accurate predictive capabilities, pediatric surgeons can manage patients more effectively in various ways, such as planning subsequent steps in advance and communicating the possibility of unsuccessful air enema to parents more clearly. It can also be beneficial for institutions with limited experience or resources to determine whether to refer patients to more experienced physicians at other institutions that can provide more advanced care for intussusception.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center, retrospective analysis; therefore, selection bias, information bias, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded, and the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Second, the tertile cutoffs for SII were derived from the distribution of our cohort and are sample-dependent, which may reduce external validity. Although our primary inference relied on continuous modeling (including spline analyses), the optimal ROC-derived threshold of 721.5 is also data-driven and should be considered exploratory. External validation in independent cohorts and, ideally, prospective multicenter studies is required before SII-based cutoffs can be recommended for routine clinical use. Due to the retrospective design, the specific clinical rationale for terminating reduction was not uniformly documented, and residual confounding by symptom severity cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusions

SII was found to be independently and positively correlated with the risk of air enema failure in the non-operative treatment of ICI in children. The SII is a novel, cost-effective, and objective inflammatory marker capable of predicting the risk of air enema treatment failure in children with ICI. It indicates to the pediatric surgeon the potential need for surgical intervention at an early stage in patients with an SII greater than 721.5. However, these findings are based on retrospective, single-center data, and prospective multicenter validation is necessary before incorporating SII into routine clinical decision-making.
