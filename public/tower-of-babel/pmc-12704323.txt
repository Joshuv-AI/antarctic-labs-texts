Successful treatment of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus osteomyelitis and persistent bacteremia using adjunctive ceftaroline fosamil to vancomycin: A case report
Authors: Sufyan Mohammed Alomair, Khalid Al Sulaiman, Taef Khayyat, Haytham Wali, Mohammad Bosaeed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCeftaroline fosamil is a novel cephalosporin antibiotic effective against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). The FDA approved it in 2010 for the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia and acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections. However, there is a lack of comprehensive data evaluating its effectiveness in treating osteomyelitis. Therefore, we report a case of MRSA osteomyelitis with persistent bacteremia, initially treated with vancomycin, where ceftaroline fosamil was subsequently added due to persistently positive blood cultures.Case reportWe report a case of a 47-year-old male with a medical history of ischemic heart disease, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, schizophrenia, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus who was admitted to the ICU with septic shock. Empiric treatment was initiated with meropenem and vancomycin. Vancomycin was given as 1000 mg IV every 24 h (18.8 mg/kg). Dosing was guided by serum vancomycin concentrations, with adjustments made by critical care clinical pharmacists to maintain target levels of 15–20 µg/mL (10–14 µmol/L). Over the following days, the patient was diagnosed with osteomyelitis, confirmed through CT and MRI imaging. Blood cultures subsequently grew MRSA. Despite 18 days of vancomycin therapy, he had persistent bacteremia, and the infectious disease team recommended the addition of ceftaroline fosamil to vancomycin to address the persistent bacteremia. Blood cultures turned negative within 24 h of initiating combination therapy.ConclusionCeftaroline fosamil shows promise as an adjunct therapy alongside vancomycin for treating osteomyelitis complicated by persistent bacteremia caused by MRSA. Further studies with a larger sample size are needed to refine its application in the management of MRSA osteomyelitis.

Background

Ceftaroline fosamil is a novel cephalosporin antibiotic that has garnered significant attention for its efficacy against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Unlike traditional beta-lactam antibiotics, ceftaroline fosamil possesses a distinct mechanism of action that allows it to bind to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) in MRSA, effectively disrupting bacterial cell wall synthesis. This property makes ceftaroline fosamil a critical option for managing serious infections caused by resistant strains of Staphylococcus aureus[1]. In October 2010, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved ceftaroline fosamil for treating adults with community-acquired pneumonia and acute bacterial skin infections caused by susceptible organisms [2].

Treating osteomyelitis can be difficult due to biofilm formation in patients with implanted devices, frequent relapses, and the need for prolonged treatment to support bone revascularization [3]. Ceftaroline fosamil offers potential benefits, including bone penetration with concentrations in infected bone reaching ∼2.5 µg/g (≈10 % of plasma levels), which exceed the MIC for MRSA, along with high tolerability and ease of use without the need for therapeutic drug monitoring. These properties support its role as an effective treatment option for MRSA osteomyelitis [4].

Insufficient data are available to evaluate the use of ceftaroline fosamil for the treatment of osteomyelitis. Therefore, we report a case of osteomyelitis with persistent bacteremia initially treated with vancomycin, followed by the addition of ceftaroline fosamil due to persistent MRSA-positive blood cultures. The persistent MRSA bacteremia was defined by Fowler et al. as the presence of MRSA-positive blood cultures for seven or more days despite the patient receiving antibiotics to which the isolate was susceptible in vitro [5]. This case report was written in accordance with the CARE guidelines [6].

On arrival, the patient was found to be hypotensive. He was promptly resuscitated, given vasopressors to ensure hemodynamic stability, and admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). On day 3, a pelvis computed tomography (CT) scan showed bilateral hip joint effusions and air locules, with the development of a right obturator space intramuscular collection/abscess and an adjacent area of bony defect of the inferior right pubic ramus, concerning for osteomyelitis. A follow-up pelvic MRI showed an ulcer in the sacrococcygeal region, particularly over the right posterior iliac crest, confirming early osteomyelitis. Moreover, there was a right subcutaneous collection posterior to the right iliac bone opening to the ulcer. A Transthoracic Echocardiogram (TTE) was performed to rule out infective endocarditis, which came back negative. Following the MRI findings, the infectious diseases (ID) service was consulted and recommended continuation of IV vancomycin. Despite ongoing therapy, blood cultures remained persistently positive, likely due to underlying osteomyelitis and/or infected pressure ulcers (Table 3).

MRSA was initially isolated from blood, wound, and respiratory cultures on day 1, with continued positivity in blood cultures on days 6, 10, and 15 despite targeted vancomycin therapy guided by therapeutic drug monitoring. MRSA was also isolated from tissue culture on day 6, suggesting deep-seated infection, likely related to osteomyelitis and infected pressure ulcers. The orthopedic surgery team was consulted, but it was determined that no surgical intervention was needed at that time. Additionally, the plastic surgery team was brought in to manage the patient's pressure ulcers. They recommended removing the existing dressing, cleaning the area, applying Flamazine (silver sulfadiazine cream) with gauze, and securing the area with Meplix or Hypafix.

Based on the ID team recommendations, on day 18, ceftaroline fosamil was added to ongoing vancomycin therapy to address this persistent MRSA bacteremia. The ceftaroline fosamil dose was 600 mg IV Q8hr. One day after starting ceftaroline fosamil (day 19), the blood cultures obtained on that day returned negative (Table 3, Table 4). The patient received three doses of ceftaroline 600 mg IV before the culture on day 19 (i.e., two doses on day 18 and one dose on day 19). The final ID team plan was to continue on both ceftaroline fosamil and vancomycin for a total of six weeks. However, ceftaroline fosamil was continued for a total of two weeks. The clinical success was evident by the eradication of MRSA from the blood and the resolution of the patient’s signs and symptoms of infection. No adverse effects related to ceftaroline were noticed or reported during the treatment course. The patient was subsequently transferred from the intensive care unit to the step-down unit, and then to a regular medical ward, where he continued to receive care. During hospitalization, the patient was designated as do-not-resuscitate (DNR) based on his clinical condition. After treatment for MRSA, the patient did not show any growth of MRSA, as confirmed by several negative blood cultures. However, on day 32, the patient tested positive for Pseudomonas aeruginosa, prompting the initiation of treatment with meropenem and amikacin, in addition to his existing antibiotic regimen. Unfortunately, on day 42 (i.e., 23 days after eradicating the MRSA infection), the patient passed away due to complications related to his underlying cardiovascular comorbidities.

Discussion

In our case report, we presented a case of an adult male admitted to the ICU due to septic shock with persistent bacteremia and osteomyelitis after treatment with vancomycin for 18 days; the addition of ceftaroline fosamil to vancomycin addressed the persistent bacteremia. Blood cultures turned negative within 24 h of initiating combination therapy.

The data on using ceftaroline in combination with vancomycin for persistent bacteremia and/or osteomyelitis is limited. A case report described a patient with persistent MRSA bacteremia secondary to endocarditis, and unresponsive to vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid, and quinupristin/dalfopristin. Clearance of bacteremia was achieved only after initiating combination therapy with ceftaroline fosamil and high-dose daptomycin, with blood cultures turning negative after four days [7]. Additionally, multiple reports have used ceftaroline fosamil in combination with daptomycin to treat bacteremia or endocarditis [7], [8], [9], [10]. One of these reports included 26 patients who used ceftaroline fosamil for treating bacteremia and showed a clinical success rate of 96 % with a median time to culture clearance of two days [8]. In our case, the time to culture clearance was only one day when ceftaroline fosamil was added to the vancomycin. Furthermore, another multicenter cohort study evaluated the effectiveness of ceftaroline fosamil in comparison to daptomycin for treating MRSA bloodstream infections. This study included 270 patients: 83 in the ceftaroline fosamil group and 187 in the daptomycin group. No difference in treatment failure or mortality was observed; however, daptomycin was associated with significantly higher creatine phosphokinase levels. (5.3 % vs 0 %, P = .034) [4].

While most published experiences with ceftaroline combination therapy involve pairing it with daptomycin, our case demonstrates its concomitant use with vancomycin. The rationale for this combination is based on the concept of β-lactams’ synergy effects, in which the ceftaroline can enhance the binding of glycopeptides to S. aureus by altering the cell wall and increasing antibiotic access to target sites [11]. Although the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for infective endocarditis recommend ceftaroline in combination with daptomycin as an alternative regimen for MRSA or MSSA infections in patients with β-lactam allergy, there are no recommendations regarding the use of vancomycin in combination with ceftaroline [12]. However, the pharmacological mechanism is similar: ceftaroline has anti-MRSA activity via high-affinity binding to penicillin-binding protein 2a (PBP2a), and when combined with vancomycin, may improve bacterial killing and reduce the risk of resistance selection [13].

Notably, ceftaroline can also achieve clinically relevant concentrations in bone tissue. Data from the CAPTURE registry support adequate penetration into bone and joint tissues, with reported clinical success rates of around 92.7 % in osteomyelitis cases, including those caused by MRSA (62 %) [3]. This tissue distribution provides an additional mechanistic rationale for including ceftaroline in salvage regimens for treating complicated MRSA bacteremia with an osteomyelitic focus, where vancomycin penetration may be limited. A case series involving five patients with osteomyelitis treated with ceftaroline fosamil found that four of the patients experienced treatment failure, defined as the need for additional antibiotics or surgical intervention within six months after completing therapy [14]. However, none of these patients received ceftaroline fosamil in combination with vancomycin or any other medication that is active against MRSA concurrently. Another case report for a case of persistent bacteremia and osteomyelitis with retropharyngeal abscess that was treated with vancomycin initially and ceftaroline fosamil as an adjunct due to treatment failure. The microbiological cure was achieved, and rash was the only adverse effect documented during the treatment course [15]. Therefore, despite the limited evidence for vancomycin–ceftaroline compared with daptomycin–ceftaroline combination, the mechanistic synergy, bone tissue activity, and our patient’s rapid culture clearance support the use of this combination [16].

The ideal approach to using combination antibiotics for treating MRSA bacteremia is still uncertain. Multiple clinical trials evaluated the use of combination antibiotics in the treatment of MRSA. In the CAMERA2 open-label randomized clinical trial, Tong and his team evaluated the outcomes of using a combination treatment (vancomycin or daptomycin with an antistaphylococcal β-lactam, such as flucloxacillin, cloxacillin, or cefazolin) versus standard treatment (vancomycin or daptomycin) in patients with MRSA bacteremia [17]. They found no difference in all-cause mortality, persistent bacteremia (except on day 5), relapse, or treatment failure. However, the antistaphylococcal β-Lactam that they used had no activity against MRSA, which could explain the lack of difference that they found in their trial [17]. On the other hand, one pilot RCT study recruited 40 patients to receive either daptomycin plus ceftaroline or monotherapy treatment (vancomycin or daptomycin) for the treatment of MRSA bacteremia. The in-hospital, 30-day, and 90-day mortality rates were lower in patients receiving combination therapy (p-values 0.02, 0.02, and 0.03, respectively) [18].

In our patient, the time to blood culture positivity did not show a clear prolongation before the start of ceftaroline, except on day 6 (6.84 days) compared with the first day (3.1 days). However, on the following days, the time to positivity decreased slightly to 2.83 and 2.4 days on days 10 and 15, respectively. Therefore, cultures remained positive during vancomycin therapy. Following the addition of ceftaroline, blood cultures turned negative within 24 h, indicating rapid microbiological clearance. This observation suggests that the combination regimen may have contributed more significantly to culture sterilization than any preceding trend in time to positivity.

Our case report has some limitations. First, the vancomycin trough levels were variable and sometimes below the target due to fluctuating patients’ kidney functions. Therefore, the level of vancomycin was subtherapeutic for 8 days over 39 days during which the patient received vancomycin. However, the vancomycin doses were immediately modified based on the vancomycin levels by critical care clinical pharmacists. Second, we did not utilize the area under the curve (AUC)/minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) ratio for vancomycin monitoring as the AUC/MIC has several challenges, such as requiring two blood samples (peak and trough), higher cost, and higher workload on nurses, which makes it hard to do, especially in high-demand settings with a high number of patients. The 2020 consensus guidelines from the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP), Society of Infectious Diseases Pharmacists (SIDP), Pediatric Infectious Diseases Society (PIDS), and Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) state that AUC/MIC (target 400–600 mg·h/L) should ideally be used for vancomycin therapeutic drug monitoring instead of troughs alone [19]. This is because troughs show only a single exposure point at the end of the dosing interval, which can lead to either overestimating or underestimating target attainment. Therefore, the persistence of bacteremia may have been caused by inadequate vancomycin exposure. This restriction emphasizes how crucial it is, whenever possible, to integrate AUC-guided monitoring techniques into therapeutic practice for vancomycin monitoring. Clinical studies have shown that trough-guided monitoring might result in either underexposure or excessive exposure. For instance, Finch et al. found that a significant percentage of individuals with therapeutic troughs actually had AUCs that were below the recommended threshold, and vancomycin AUC-guided dosing was associated with reduced risk of nephrotoxicity, which was a result of reducing exposure to vancomycin [20]. Lastly, the vancomycin MIC for Staphylococcus aureus was reported for only one sample at 1.5 mg/L, limiting the regular assessment of effectiveness and antibiotic dosing throughout the treatment course.

Our case report illustrates the use of ceftaroline fosamil as an adjunct to vancomycin for treating persistent bacteremia in osteomyelitis. Furthermore, this case underscores the potential role of ceftaroline fosamil as salvage therapy following vancomycin failure in managing osteomyelitis with persistent bacteremia.

Conclusions

Ceftaroline fosamil demonstrated promising results as adjunctive therapy in treating osteomyelitis with persistent bacteremia caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. However, further clinical studies with larger sample size are needed to better assess its efficacy in this setting.
