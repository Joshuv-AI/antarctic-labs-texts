Spatiotemporal evolution and analysis of influencing factors of low-carbon economy in China’s Yangtze River Delta based on nighttime light remote sensing data
Authors: Jianglin Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of a low-carbon economy is essential for current economic growth. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the spatiotemporal evolution of low-carbon economy development in China’s Yangtze River Delta and to clarify its influencing factors, this research employs spatial analysis methods such as kernel density estimation, Moran’s I, spatial Markov chains, and the spatiotemporal geographically weighted regression model. The study draws the following conclusions: (1) The overall development of the low-carbon economy in the region is improving, though there are notable spatial and temporal differences between cities. In recent years, the number of cities with high levels of low-carbon development has steadily increased, yet provincial disparities remain, with Anhui Province facing the most prominent development challenges. (2) Low-carbon economy development in the Yangtze River Delta shows clear spatial clustering and migration patterns. The number of cities in a low-low agglomeration state remains stable, while the number of high-high agglomeration cities has slightly decreased. Spatially, cities with high-high agglomeration are concentrated in the eastern part of the region, while low-low agglomeration is mainly found in the west. (3) Various factors influence the development of the low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta. The average regression coefficients for industrial upgrading, government intervention, technological advancement, education, and economic development are all positive, the effect of human capital shows stage-specific characteristics.Keywords: Nighttime light remote sensing data, Yangtze River Delta, Spatiotemporal evolution, Low-carbon economy, Influencing factors

Introduction

With the ongoing advancement of the global economy and information technology, economic and ecological globalization have become defining features of the 21 st century [1]. Since the start of the 21 st century, rapid economic development and population growth have driven global progress, but also led to significant negative impacts on the environment. The rising prominence of carbon emissions, due to their critical role in global warming, has quickly become a major concern for governments and scholars worldwide [2, 3]. As the world’s largest developing country, China has experienced significant economic prosperity during its transition from a planned to a market economy. However, its sustainable economic development relies heavily on energy consumption [4], and the inefficient use of non-renewable energy sources, along with the sharp rise in greenhouse gas emissions, has become an inevitable challenge in development planning [5].

Since 2008, China has been the world’s largest carbon emitter, a status that places significant pressure on the country to reduce emissions and pursue a low-carbon development path [6]. In 2021, under the core concept of General Secretary Xi’s ecological civilization, the State Council issued the ‘Guiding Opinions on Accelerating the Establishment and Improvement of the Green, Low-Carbon, and Circular Economic System’, which set green development goals for the past decade and outlined the direction for low-carbon economic development.

The Yangtze River Delta, one of the most economically developed and urbanized regions in China, is also one of the world’s six major mega-city regions and serves as a vital engine of China’s economy. Its rapid economic growth and urbanization have led to a high concentration of energy consumption, resulting in a significant increase in CO₂ emissions. The rapid increase in carbon emissions threatens environmental health and constrains the region’s sustainable development [7, 8]. In response, Shanghai Municipality, Jiangsu Province, and Zhejiang Province jointly issued the ‘Implementation Plan for Peak Carbon Achievement in the Yangtze River Delta Eco-Green Integrated Development Demonstration Area’, emphasizing collaborative efforts to enhance green and low-carbon development. In June 2024, responding to national ‘dual-carbon’ targets and promoting the synergistic development of a low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta, central cities in the region jointly launched the ‘Carbon Peak and Carbon Neutrality Initiative of the C9 Alliance of the Yangtze River Delta’. The initiative’s main goal is to create a ‘dual-carbon’ demonstration city, offering practical models for low-carbon development across different regions. In academic research, scientifically and accurately analyzing the spatiotemporal evolution of carbon emissions in the Yangtze River Delta and revealing the intrinsic relationship between carbon emissions and a low-carbon economy have become pressing issues for both scholars and policymakers.

The rapid advancement of remote sensing technology in recent years has been remarkable. As a cutting-edge data source, nighttime light remote sensing has expanded significantly in applications, particularly in assessing human activity intensity, energy consumption patterns, and accurately estimating carbon emissions [9, 10]. Studies both domestically and internationally confirm that nighttime light remote sensing has unique advantages in intuitively and dynamically mapping the spatial distribution and intensity of regional economic activities [11, 12]. This research incorporates nighttime light remote sensing data to assess the current state of low-carbon economic development in the Yangtze River Delta region. A scientific low-carbon economy measurement system is developed by integrating traditional economic and social statistics, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of statistical data while leveraging the spatial resolution advantages of remote sensing. This approach enables comprehensive, high-precision monitoring and analysis of low-carbon economic development in the region.

Literature review

Academic research on the low-carbon economy has been steadily growing since the late 20th century. Across countries, the core concept of low-carbon economic development centers on using carbon emission reduction strategies to promote domestic economic growth [13]. Chen, et al. [14] introduced carbon productivity (CP) and low-carbon economy efficiency (LCEE) to measure the development level of China’s low-carbon economy. They revealed the developmental heterogeneity of China’s GDP and carbon emissions by comparing outcome-oriented performance (actual results) with process-oriented performance (the pathway to those results). Li, et al. [15] innovatively integrated the ecological footprint perspective and used the ultra-efficient SBM model to accurately measure China’s low-carbon economy development, offering clear guidance for related research. Niu, et al. [16] evaluated China’s low-carbon economy development by applying an index weighting algorithm within the fuzzy synthesis framework from a carbon emissions perspective. Yang and Li [17] introduced the PSR model and referred to the LCCI (Low-Carbon City Indicator) index system to build a measurement framework, which was used to assess the actual level of low-carbon economy development in Shanghai. Peng and Deng [18] developed a measurement system using 35 relevant indicators to assess the low-carbon economy development in Guiyang City, providing inspiration for this research.

Leach [19] argues that transitioning to a low-carbon economy will severely challenge Canada’s fossil fuel industry, but also offer new opportunities for low-carbon commodities and industries. Nguyen, et al. [20] demonstrated that renewable and clean energy can diversify the energy system, supporting the development of a low-carbon economy in the region. Zhang, et al. [21] argued that the digital economy will play a central role in advancing a low-carbon economy, primarily through environmental governance, technological innovation, and industrial upgrading, with industrial upgrading being the most significant. Linnenluecke, et al. [22] found that national-level economic and institutional factors positively influence low-carbon economy development, with institutional factors often reinforcing economic ones. Liu [23] explored the trust relationship between government officials and business managers after the introduction of low-carbon policies, arguing that developing a low-carbon economy requires both policy guidance and strengthened trust between government and enterprises for effective implementation. Zhu, et al. [24] employed the spatial Durbin model and Cobb-Douglas production function to explore the impact of green finance on the low-carbon economy. Their results show that green finance influences the low-carbon economy through technology, scale, and structure effects, with green technological innovation being crucial for sustainable and stable development.

Wang, et al. [25] pointed out that most research on low-carbon economies focuses on national and provincial levels, with limited attention to regions and prefectural-level cities. They emphasized the importance of low-carbon development for cities and highlighted that China’s low-carbon efforts are more advanced in the central and eastern regions compared to the west. Zhao, et al. [26] argue that regional heterogeneity is a significant barrier to coordinated low-carbon economic development in China. They suggest that national policies should focus on promoting environmentally friendly technologies in the underdeveloped regions of Northwest China. Yang, et al. [27] argue that China should emphasize the role of the circular economy and low-carbon technologies in driving low-carbon development. They advocate long-term planning and coordination between central and local governments to ensure sustainable progress. Wang and Chang [28] suggest that China’s low-carbon governance should prioritize policy orientation, focusing on laws, regulations, industry standards, and financial taxation. Mittal, et al. [29] analyzed urban transport in China and India, concluding that promoting new energy vehicles and constructing subways are crucial to advancing a low-carbon economy. Busch, et al. [30] found that the core of low-carbon industrial strategies in the U.S. and Europe is integrating industrial development with environmental goals to promote green growth. Fankhauser [31] emphasizes the importance of legal frameworks and carbon pricing in transitioning to a low-carbon economy, arguing that clean energy and policy guidance are essential. Hafeznia, et al. [32] argue that resolving Iran’s natural gas issue is crucial for the country’s transition to a low-carbon society.

The analysis reveals a growing trend in low-carbon economy research in recent years, yet significant room for expansion remains. First, urban carbon emission calculations face precision challenges. Current studies predominantly rely on government-reported energy consumption data, which often contains inaccuracies or inconsistencies that compromise measurement accuracy. Second, existing research predominantly focuses on provincial-level analyses, with limited attention given to city-level perspectives—particularly regarding the spatiotemporal evolution trends and influencing factors of low-carbon economies in the Yangtze River Delta region. Third, while most studies employ linear regression models for high-quality development analysis, there is insufficient literature exploring spatiotemporal pathways for enhancing low-carbon economic performance.

Given this context, this study investigates the spatiotemporal evolution characteristics and influencing factors of regional low-carbon economy in 41 cities along the Yangtze River Delta using nighttime light remote sensing data from 2006 to 2022. The research demonstrates three potential marginal contributions: First, by employing nighttime light remote sensing data to reconstruct carbon emission profiles for Yangtze River Delta cities, we effectively mitigate statistical lag and precision limitations inherent in economic data, thereby enhancing the scientific rigor and accuracy of urban low-carbon economy index estimation. Second, analyzing the spatiotemporal trends in low-carbon economic development across these prefecture-level cities enables local governments to better grasp spatial-temporal patterns and distribution patterns, providing theoretical support for formulating policies tailored to regional conditions. Third, through the application of Geographically and Temporally Weighted Regression (GTWR) method to explore spatiotemporal factors influencing low-carbon economic development in the Yangtze River Delta, this study enriches existing theoretical frameworks. It offers a solid foundation for achieving carbon reduction targets and high-quality economic growth in the region, while providing quantitative support for accelerating the realization of the "dual-carbon" goals.

Data description and model design

Research idea

Based on the aforementioned analysis, it can be observed that the Yangtze River Delta, as a vital component of China’s economy, continues to face significant environmental pressures in its economic development.

To comprehensively understand the spatiotemporal distribution patterns and influencing factors of low-carbon economic development across cities in the Yangtze River Delta, this study proposes three key aspects for exploration: First, we construct a comprehensive evaluation index system for low-carbon economy using eight indicators across four dimensions—low-carbon output, low-carbon consumption, low-carbon environmental protection, and low-carbon development. The entropy method is employed to calculate the comprehensive development index of low-carbon economy for each city from 2006 to 2022. Second, we examine the spatiotemporal evolution trends of low-carbon economic development in Yangtze River Delta cities, specifically analyzing their spatial distribution patterns, agglomeration characteristics, and evolutionary trajectories. Third, we investigate the influencing factors affecting low-carbon economic development in Yangtze River Delta cities, primarily examining their impact effects on urban low-carbon economic growth from both temporal and spatial perspectives. Overview of the study area

The Yangtze River Delta ranks among the top in China for economic development, innovation, openness, and energy transformation, playing a vital role in promoting the nation’s high-quality development. With the region’s focus on economic growth and technological innovation, issues related to energy consumption and carbon emissions have become increasingly prominent. This study selects 41 prefecture-level cities from the three provinces and one city in the Yangtze River Delta to investigate the spatiotemporal evolution of the low-carbon economy and its influencing factors.

Data sources

Nighttime Light Data. This study processes nighttime lighting data in two stages: data collection and data correction. Data collection involves gathering DMSP-OLS data from 2005-2013 and NPP-VIIRS data from 2012-2022, both sourced from the NOAA platform. Data calibration, the core stage, consists of relative calibration, saturation calibration, sensor calibration, and continuous calibration. First, relative, saturation, and sensor corrections are applied sequentially to the DMSP-OLS data for the Yangtze River Delta using the invariant target area method [33]; Next, to build a high-quality continuous imagery set, we apply logarithmic transformation to enhance data distribution characteristics, and use a noise-processing algorithm to effectively reduce noise in the NPP-VIIRS imagery. This approach draws on Pan, et al. [34]. Finally, the optimized NPP-VIIRS data were corrected for time-series continuity alongside the DMSP-OLS data. The key step is ensuring a smooth temporal transition between the two datasets, following the correction strategy of Wu, et al. [35], to create a complete, continuous long-term nighttime lighting dataset.

Kernel density estimation map of low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta region

Analysis of the kernel density curve for the entire region reveals a consistent decline in the height of the main peak over the years, resulting in a significant widening of the peak width. This trend indicates a decentralization of the low-carbon economy distribution within the region, accompanied by an increasing absolute difference between cities. Additionally, the initial kernel density curve exhibits a small tail; however, this curve becomes progressively smoother over time, suggesting that the disparity between cities with higher and lower levels of low-carbon economic development is gradually decreasing. Further, based on the line graph analysis of the average low-carbon economy index in Fig. 6, the peak position of kernel density curves shifts to the high-value area for Jiangsu and Zhejiang Provinces, indicating an increase in their low-carbon economy development levels. In contrast, Anhui Province’s kernel density curve shifts leftward, suggesting a regression in its low-carbon economy development. Jiangsu and Zhejiang Provinces exhibit dragging tails and widening peak widths, indicating a decentralized distribution of the low-carbon economy and a growing absolute difference between cities. Conversely, Anhui Province’s kernel density curve has transitioned from smooth to trailing, with narrowing peak widths and increasing peak heights, revealing a double-peak phenomenon in 2014. This indicates a year-on-year decline in Anhui’s economic development level, accompanied by excessive regional disparities that have resulted in polarization. The significant economic development differences within Anhui Province create a head-heavy inverted triangle structure, suggesting that the imbalance in provincial economic development may contribute to the polarization of low-carbon economy development.

Spatial correlation of low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta region

Table 4 presents the statistical results of the Moran’s I index for the Yangtze River Delta region from 2006 to 2022, as calculated using the global Moran’s index formula in this study. The Z-values range from a maximum of 5.075 to a minimum of 1.756, with P-values less than 0.01 for the period 2006-2018 and less than 0.1 for 2019-2021. These results lead to the rejection of the null hypothesis of no spatial correlation in the low-carbon economy at confidence levels of 90% and 99%. Additionally, the data suggest a clustering effect in the low-carbon economy within the Yangtze River Delta region, where cities with high development levels are geographically concentrated, as are those with low development levels. From an economic development perspective, economically advanced cities typically possess stronger resources and capabilities to invest in low-carbon technologies and sustainable development projects. This, in turn, attracts more investments and talent, further driving their low-carbon economic growth. Moreover, cities that prioritize environmental protection and sustainable development are more likely to formulate and implement policies and practices that promote low-carbon growth. Consequently, the Yangtze River Delta region exhibits significant spatial clustering characteristics in its low-carbon economy.

Furthermore, over the long-term development period from 2006 to 2022, cities in the Yangtze River Delta region exhibiting a high-high agglomeration state of low-carbon economy development are predominantly located in the eastern part, whereas those in a low-low agglomeration state are mainly found in the western part. This phenomenon demonstrates that agglomeration characteristics exhibit significant clustering effects, with high-high agglomeration patterns predominantly concentrated around Suzhou City. The formation of this pattern is closely related to industrial added value. From 2006 to 2022, the annual average growth rate of total industrial output value reached 12.3%, far exceeding the Yangtze River Delta’s average growth rate of 9.8%. Suzhou’s electronic information industry share rose from 35% in 2006 to 48% in 2022, while Nantong’s shipbuilding and offshore engineering industry cluster surpassed 200 billion yuan in scale. These developments have collectively formed a pronounced high-high agglomeration pattern. The competitive dynamics have affected the dominant position of cities originally in the high-high agglomeration state. The pursuit of technological breakthroughs has resulted in increased energy consumption, leading to a temporary stagnation in the development of the low-carbon economy in these cities and weakening their positive influence on the surrounding areas.

Analysis of low-carbon economic impact factors from the perspective of time

After selecting the GTWR model, this research empirically analyzes the influencing factors of the low-carbon economic development level in the Yangtze River Delta region. Figure 8 presents the step-by-step regression results for each variable. Analysis of the results in the figure reveals that the average regression coefficients for Industrial Structure Upgrading, Government Intervention Level, Scientific and Technological Development Level, Education Development Level, and Economic Development Level are all greater than 0. This indicates that these variables positively contribute to enhancing the low-carbon economy level. Amongthem, the average regression coefficient of Human Capital Level exhibits a phased characteristic. Although this coefficient is greater than 0 for most of the period, it is significantly less than 0 during 2011-2013 and 2019-2022. This phenomenon primarily stems from economic structural transformation and delayed policy effects. During 2011-2013, China was in the mid-to-late stages of industrialization, dominated by traditional energy-intensive industries. Short-term human capital development may have exacerbated resource-intensive industry expansion and hindered low-carbon transition. From 2019 to 2022, although low-carbon technologies gradually matured, the COVID-19 pandemic triggered disruptions in global supply chains and domestic growth stabilization pressures. This led to a rebound in high-carbon industries, temporarily overshadowing the driving force of human capital in low-carbon innovation due to short-term economic recovery needs. Consequently, its impact exhibited phased suppression characteristics.

The transition of the industrial structure from high-energy-consuming industries to low-energy-consuming ones, along with a shift from high-tech to the service sector, enhances energy utilization efficiency and promotes the development of a low-carbon economy. Simultaneously, government actions—such as formulating and implementing low-carbon policies, providing financial support, and offering tax incentives—encourage enterprises to reduce carbon emissions and advance the research and development of green technologies. This, in turn, enhances the level of the low-carbon economy. At the same time, the government has proposed regulations such as the Interim Regulations on the Management of Carbon Emission Trading, the Notice on Improving the Financial Subsidy Policy for the Promotion and Application of New Energy Vehicles, and the Implementation Plan for Science and Technology to Support Carbon Peak and Carbon Neutrality (2022-2030), which encourage enterprises to reduce carbon emissions and promote green technology research and development, so as to improve the level of low-carbon economy. Science and technology, along with education, serve as driving forces and guarantees of talent in the low-carbon economy’s development. Advanced science and technology foster innovation in low-carbon technologies and the growth of low-carbon industries, while high-quality talent facilitates the absorption of such technologies and the development of related industries, injecting vigor into the low-carbon economy. However, adjustments in economic structure and changes in Education Development Level impact the role of Human Capital Level in the low-carbon economy. During the early stages of economic transformation in the Yangtze River Delta (e.g., 2011-2013), traditional industries held a significant share, and the development of low-carbon technologies and green industries lagged behind, resulting in insufficient allocation of human capital in the low-carbon sector. Consequently, human capital was predominantly invested in traditional manufacturing and labor-intensive industries, limiting its effectiveness in promoting the low-carbon economy and potentially exerting an inhibitory effect due to uneven resource distribution.

Discussion

This study employs panel data from 41 cities in the Yangtze River Delta region spanning 2006−2022, utilizing spatial econometric models to analyze spatiotemporal evolution patterns and influencing factors of low-carbon economy development. Three key conclusions emerge: First, while cities in the Yangtze River Delta demonstrate steady growth in low-carbon economic performance, significant regional disparities persist. Liu, et al. [44] reveals pronounced regional variations in China’s low-carbon economy, with the number of provinces achieving moderate or higher levels increasing from 11 in 2010 to 22 by 2022, though regional differences remain evident. Second, urban low-carbon economic development exhibits characteristics of spatial agglomeration and migration. Che, et al. [45] study indicates northward expansion of high-emission zones and southward expansion of low-emission zones. From 2000 to 2020, carbon emission fluctuations intensified, aligning with trends linked to central cities and radiating outward from high-emission hubs. Third, multiple factors drive low-carbon economic development in the Yangtze River Delta. Wang, et al. [46] findings suggest that digital economy effectively promotes sustainable urban low-carbon development, with robustness tests confirming the conclusion. He, et al. [47] research highlights market integration’s crucial role in reducing carbon intensity, while average governance effects of industrial and energy structures also positively contribute to emission reduction efforts. However, the degree of market integration and coordination, technical level, and the gap in high-quality development are the inhibiting factors for carbon emission intensity. Therefore, it can be found that the above conclusions are consistent with the existing research conclusions, and the conclusion of this study is verified to be robust.

Given this context, this study explores the spatiotemporal evolution characteristics and influencing factors of urban low-carbon economies in the Yangtze River Delta region, which holds significant theoretical and practical implications. However, three limitations and future research directions remain: First, while the study focuses on 41 prefecture-level cities in the Yangtze River Delta region—more detailed and precise than provincial-level data—it fails to capture the development status of low-carbon economies from a micro perspective. Future research could refine the sample by analyzing county-level cities to examine spatiotemporal evolution characteristics and influencing factors. Second, although the GTWR model was used to explore factors affecting urban low-carbon economies, the analysis only considers six dimensions (human capital, economic development, and educational development) among numerous potential inhibitors. This approach lacks comprehensiveness. Future studies should broaden perspectives by examining digital transformation, intelligent transformation, and new-quality productivity to explore pathways for low-carbon economic enhancement. Third, while the study investigates factors influencing low-carbon economic development, variables typically exhibit significant causal relationships. Examining these factors alone cannot fully grasp the direction of China’s high-quality economic development. Future research could employ bidirectional causality analysis models to reverse-engineer how low-carbon economic development impacts various influencing factors, thereby providing more nuanced recommendations for high-quality economic growth.

Conclusion and suggestion

Research conclusions

Utilizing remote sensing data from nighttime lighting, this study employed various spatial measurement methods—including kernel density estimation, Moran’s index, spatial Markov analysis, and spatiotemporal geographically weighted regression—to conduct a comprehensive spatial analysis of the factors influencing the low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta region. The following conclusions were drawn:

First, the overall development level of the low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta region is improving, although significant disparities exist between cities over time and space. In terms of time dimension, the average index of low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta increased from 0.203 in 2006 to 0.330 in 2022, among which the final level of Jiangsu and Zhejiang was 0.351 and 0.337 respectively, higher than the average level of the research region. The space distribution map in Fig. 4 indicates a year-on-year increase in the number of cities with high levels of low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta region. Over time, Hangzhou and Hefei transitioned from medium or high economic development levels to high low-carbon economy development levels. Additionally, the Kernel Density Estimation map reveals that the low-carbon economy in Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces is generally strong, while that in Anhui province is declining year by year, reflecting regional disparities and polarization.

Second, the development of the low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta region clearly demonstrates characteristics of spatial agglomeration and migration. The global Moran’s I is above 0.15 and passes the significance test at the 1% significance level, indicating that low carbon economy has significant spatial agglomeration characteristics. In terms of spatial evolution, cities in the Yangtze River Delta exhibiting a High-High agglomeration state in low-carbon economy development are primarily located in the eastern part of the region, whereas those in a Low-Low agglomeration state are mainly found in the western part. The Markov results indicate that the state retention probability of low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta in 2014−2022 is 84.1%, and the upward transfer probability (0.185,0.159) is also greater than the downward transfer probability (0.012,0.000), indicating that cities with lower and higher levels have a good trend of upward transfer.

Third, a diverse range of factors influences the development of the low-carbon economy in the Yangtze River Delta region. In this study, the average regression coefficients for the selected variables—industrial structure upgrading, government intervention level, scientific and technological development level, education development level, and economic development level—are all greater than 0, indicating that these variables positively contribute to the advancement of the low-carbon economy. Conversely, the average regression coefficient for human capital level displays stage characteristics, with a particularly pronounced inhibitory effect observed during the period from 2019 to 2022.

Research suggestions

Based on the above research conclusions, this paper puts forward the following three policy suggestions:

First, establish a low-carbon industrial transformation mechanism rooted in structural upgrading. The development of a low-carbon economy must prioritize industrial optimization, driving the transition of traditional energy-intensive industries toward green and low-carbon models. By integrating policy guidance with market mechanisms, we can create a new landscape dominated by low-carbon sectors. Specifically, differentiated industry access standards should be implemented, requiring high-emission industries like steel and chemicals to undergo technological upgrades and mandatory adoption of carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies. Simultaneously, fiscal subsidies and tax incentives should support strategic emerging industries such as renewable energy and environmental protection initiatives.

Second, we should develop carbon reduction pathways through technological innovation to accelerate the growth of a low-carbon economy. On one hand, efforts should focus on adjusting energy structures and improving efficiency by promoting renewable energy sources like photovoltaic and wind power to gradually replace fossil fuels such as coal. Simultaneously, strengthening green technology R&D—such as hydrogen storage and transportation systems and smart grid technologies—can help reduce emission reduction costs. At the management level, we could draw lessons from the EU’s carbon market experience by refining the national carbon trading mechanism, expanding industry coverage, and introducing a paid quota allocation system to incentivize corporate emissions reduction through price signals. For instance, the Yangtze River Delta city cluster could jointly establish a regional carbon budget and minimize emission reduction costs through cross-city pollution rights trading.

Third, advancing the integration of the Yangtze River Delta region and proposing a collaborative low-carbon development model for city clusters. As China’s most economically dynamic area, the Yangtze River Delta must break through administrative barriers to establish coordinated mechanisms among city clusters. Key initiatives include forming a Green Technology Alliance to promote cross-city industry-academia-research collaboration and accelerate the commercialization of low-carbon technologies; creating a unified regional carbon market with standardized quota allocation and trading rules to reduce transaction costs; and jointly planning infrastructure projects such as building a regional new energy charging network and smart microgrids to avoid redundant construction.
