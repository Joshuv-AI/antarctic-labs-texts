Andrea Cernuschi, Thomas Thuillier, Laurent Garrigues

Abstract
The Electron Cyclotron Resonance Ion Plasma ACcelerator (ECRIPAC) is an original accelerator concept proposed in the nineties for the generation of highly energetic pulsed ion beams, suitable for a wide array of applications. The initial studies on the subject were characterized by an important calculation mistake, leading to an incomplete and erroneous literature on the topic. Nevertheless, the simple and well mastered techniques involved in the system (radio frequency and magnetic field), together with the device compactness, are strong motivations for further studies on ECRIPAC. The working principle and physical theory behind the ECRIPAC accelerator concept are thoroughly reviewed in this paper, incorporating significant corrections to the existing literature on the subject, making this work a suitable reference for future studies. A detailed theoretical investigation of the stability condition for the ion acceleration is presented, highlighting more stringent limitations than previously anticipated. Next, the impact of several physical parameters on the accelerator design is analyzed. This study led to the design of a compact demonstrator device able to accelerate He 2+ ions up to 9.5 MeV per nucleon inside a 1.8 m long accelerating cavity. Finally, the results of a Monte-Carlo simulation, developed to model the electron dynamics inside the system, are used to validate the theoretical framework of ECRIPAC.



Introduction –

The Electron Cyclotron Resonance Ion Plasma ACcelerator (ECRIPAC)
Geller et al. 1991
is a novel accelerator concept proposed by R. Geller and K. Golovanivsky, able to produce very energetic pulsed ion beams, theoretically up to hundreds of MeV per nucleon.
Unlike conventional accelerator techniques, such as the typical linear accelerator (Linac) structures and their associated injectors
Ostroumov et al. 2021
;
Stockli and Nakagawa 2013
;
Zhang et al. 2022
, plasma-based accelerator are often distinguished by their compact size. This is true for both well-established methods like plasma wakefield acceleration
Gorbunov et al. 2001
;
Hogan et al. 2005
and more original concepts such as autoresonant accelerators
Sloan and Drummond 1973
and ECRIPAC.
An advantage of ECRIPAC is that it requires simple, robust and well mastered technologies for its operation, leading to possible application in many fields, especially in the medical domain
Schwartz et al. 1995
;
Ishibashi et al. 2006
;
Inoue et al. 2014
.
Due to an important calculation mistake reported in the initial paper
Geller et al. 1991
, the most recent literature on ECRIPAC
Ishibashi et al. 2006
;
Inoue et al. 2014
is based on incomplete and possibly wrong premises. Hence, a careful review of the physical theory underlying the ECRIPAC working principle is deemed necessary to open opportunities for further studies on this promising accelerator concept.
ECRIPAC exploits two known and experimentally verified physical principles: gyromagnetic autoresonance (GA)
Golovanivsky 1980
;
Golovanivsky 1983
;
Golovanivsky 1982
and ion entrainment
Consoli and Hall 1963
;
Bardet et al. 1965
;
Bardet et al. 1964
.

GA
Golovanivsky 1980
;
Golovanivsky 1983
;
Golovanivsky 1982
is a physical mechanism able to increase the energy of an electron population confined in a time-increasing magnetic field
B(t)
by interaction with an injected microwave radiation (HF), whose frequency
\omega_{HF}
satisfies the electron cyclotron resonance (ECR) condition

\Omega(t)=\frac{eB(t)}{m\gamma(t)}=\omega_{HF}\;,

(1)

where
m
is the electron rest mass and
\gamma
the Lorentz factor.
If the magnetic field satisfies some conditions related to a sufficiently smooth time behavior, the electron energy increases quasi-synchronously with the magnetic field growth in a so-called gyromagnetic autoresonance regime:

\gamma(t)\approx\frac{B(t)}{B_{0}}\;,

(2)

where
B_{0}
is the magnetic field satisfying ECR condition for cold electrons.
A magnetic mirror is used to trap and accelerate the charged particles, leading to an anisotropic electron energy gain in the direction perpendicular to the magnetic field lines.

Ion entrainment
Consoli and Hall 1963
;
Bardet et al. 1965
;
Bardet et al. 1964
takes place when the electron population of a plasma is longitudinally accelerated due to a magnetic field gradient, displacing it from the ions. The local difference in ion and electron densities generates a space-charge field, which slows down the electrons and accelerates the ions, leading to a net motion of the latter in the direction of the force acting on the electrons.
This phenomenon has been experimentally observed to yield similar axial velocity for the electrons and ions
Bardet et al. 1965
, determining a net conversion of perpendicular electron energy to parallel ion energy

W_{e\perp}^{in}-W_{e\perp}^{fin}\approx W_{i\parallel}^{fin}.

(3)

The combination of these two phenomena allows building a device theoretically able to accelerate a low pressure plasma by using well establish and commercially available technologies, consisting of static and pulsed coils to generate the overall magnetic field structure and of a microwave injector.
This work presents an in-depth review of the ECRIPAC physical theory, adding important corrections to the current literature, followed by a theoretical study to better comprehend the device limitations and its requirements. This latter investigation led to the proposal of a compact demonstrator device to accelerate He
2+
ions, which has been validated using a Monte-Carlo code able to simulate the electron dynamics inside the accelerator.

ECRIPAC working principle –

An ECRIPAC device
Geller et al. 1991
is composed of three sections (see Fig.
1
):

•

An injector of low pressure highly ionized plasma in free diffusion through differential pumping. Possible candidates for this stage include ECR ion sources
Geller 1996
.

•

A GYRAC section, which includes a reverse field pulsed coil, a resonant cavity and a microwave injection port.

•

A PLEIADE section, constituted by a beam cavity and an axial distribution of coils, generating a long axial magnetic gradient encompassing the GYRAC section.

A typical ECRIPAC axial magnetic field profile is also reported in Fig.
1
inside the cavity: the main static magnetic field gradient (PLEIADE section) is displayed in dark green, with the maximum effect of the reversed pulsed magnetic field (GYRAC section) superimposed in blue.
The ECRIPAC working cycle is composed of three successive phases: the gyromagnetic autoresonance (GA) phase, the plasma compression (c) phase and the PLEIADE phase.

The ECRIPAC process
Geller et al. 1991
starts with the GA phase, by triggering the reverse pulsed coil and hence delivering an axial sinusoidal magnetic field.
When the pulsed coil reaches its maximum intensity (time
t=0
), resulting in a minimum field
B_{min}\leq B_{0}
, both the plasma and the HF power are injected into the cavity. The plasma is then trapped inside the generated transient magnetic mirror, and the electrons are immediately heated by the ECR mechanism.
If the reverse pulsed magnetic field temporal evolution respects the GA hypothesis for a sufficiently slow-varying field in time
Golovanivsky 1983
, the energy of the electrons increases according to Eq.
2
.

The plasma compression phase
Geller et al. 1991
starts as soon as the HF wave injection is stopped (
t=t_{GA}
), and lasts until the transient magnetic mirror well vanishes (
t=t_{c}
), corresponding to the complete restoration of the initial main magnetic field (dark green line in Fig.
1
).
During this period, the energy of the electron population increases by means of the electric field induced by the time variation of the magnetic field. However, the most prominent phenomenon taking place during this phase is the compression of the plasma, leading to increased electron and ion densities, which are crucial factors for the following ion acceleration.
Indeed, under the hypothesis of slowly varying magnetic field, it is possible to demonstrate that the electron motion inside ECRIPAC during this phase is characterized by the adiabatic constants of motion

p_{e}^{2}/B=const\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptr_{e}^{2}B=const\;,

(4)

where
p_{e}
and
r_{e}
are respectively the electron momentum and radial distance from the cavity axis.
Thus, the increasing magnetic field over time leads to a decrease of
r_{e}
, radially compressing the plasma. Moreover, the axial oscillatory motion of the electrons inside the magnetic mirror is damped in time, leading also to an axial compression of the plasma disk.
Starting from the equation of motion for the electrons, it is possible to obtain the time evolution of the Lorentz factor during the plasma compression phase (
t_{GA}\leq t\leq t_{c}
):

\gamma(t)=\sqrt{1+(\gamma_{GA}^{2}-1)\frac{B(t)}{B_{GA}}}\;,

(5)

where
\gamma_{GA}=\gamma(t_{GA})
.

The PLEIADE phase
Geller et al. 1991
, starting at
t=t_{c}
, is the last part of the ECRIPAC working cycle. Its objective is the acceleration of the ion population.
Due to the adiabatic conservation of the magnetic dipole moment, the negative gradient of the magnetic field converts the electron perpendicular velocity into parallel velocity, which leads to the charge separation inside the plasma and the consequential ion acceleration through the ion entrainment mechanism.
An important condition that the PLEIADE magnetic field must satisfy is
d^{2}B/dz^{2}>0
, in order to avoid possible macroscopic instabilities which could prevent the ion acceleration.
Luckily, the diamagnetic character of the plasma helps to mitigate this condition
Geller and Golovanivsky 1992
.
Moreover, an appropriate value for the final minimum field
B_{fin}
of the PLEIADE phase must be used to avoid the spatial divergence of the electron bunch, since the constants of motion of Eq.
4
are still approximately valid in low magnetic field gradients.

Considering a plasma composed of ions with mass
Am_{a}
and electric charge
Q=Ze
(where
m_{a}
is the atomic mass unit,
A
and
Z
the mass and charge numbers respectively), with a number of ions much lower than electrons (
N_{i}\ll N_{e}
), the energy of the ions per nucleon at the end of the accelerating process can be estimated as

\frac{W_{i}}{A}\approx\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{c}-1}{\gamma^{2}_{c}}m_{a}c^{2}\left(1-\frac{B_{fin}}{B_{max}}\right)\;,

(6)

where
c
is the speed of light and
\gamma_{c}=\gamma(t_{c})
.
The accelerator stability during the PLEIADE phase strongly depends on the magnetic field profile, and it is mainly constrained by two phenomena: the stability of the electron bunch and the ion shake out (some ions are not accelerated by the electrons and detach from the plasma).
An ion is entrained by the electrons only if the Coulomb attraction overcome the electrons’ acceleration (non shake out condition), which can also be expressed in the following form by making explicit the PLEIADE field profile

\left|\frac{\nabla B_{z}}{B_{z}}\right|\leq\frac{2e}{m_{a}c^{2}}\frac{Z}{A}E_{sc}\;,

(7)

where
E_{sc}
is the space-charge electric field generated inside the plasma.
Hence, any ion with a ratio
A/Z
not respecting Eq.
7
is not entrained by the electron bunch and is shaken out from the plasma.
Consequently, very large magnetic field gradients can be used to accelerate only the electron population, since all the ions will be shaken out from the plasma.

The stability of the electron bunch during the PLEIADE stage is influenced mainly by two factors: the characteristic time for the spatial divergence of the electron bunch due to Coulomb repulsion and its dampening due to relativistic effects. Thus, the stability condition of the electron bunch can be written as an inequality between the radial and axial forces experienced by the electrons, expliciting the PLEIADE field profile

\left|\frac{\nabla B_{z}}{B_{z}}\right|\geq\frac{2e}{m_{e}c^{2}(\gamma^{3}-\gamma)}E_{sc}\;.

(8)

By equating Eqs.
7
and
8
, it is possible to determine a minimum value for the electron Lorentz factor
\gamma_{min}
at the end of the plasma compression phase to allow the acceleration of ions with a given
A/Z
ratio

\gamma_{min}^{3}-\gamma_{min}=\frac{m_{a}}{m_{e}}\frac{A}{Z}\;.

(9)

Stability conditions of the accelerator –

As already mentioned, a critical point when designing an ECRIPAC device is to ensure a stable acceleration of the ions of interest. This condition depends on the magnetic field profile for the PLEIADE section and presents both an upper and lower limit corresponding to the non shake out condition (Eq.
7
) and the electron bunch stability condition (Eq.
8
) respectively. Combining these two requirements in Eq.
9
provides a lower limit for the electron energy at the end of the plasma compression phase, which however is not sufficient to ensure a stable acceleration over the whole PLEIADE section.
Indeed, the constants of motion of the PLEIADE phase (Eq.
4
) determine a progressive increase in the size of the electron bunch along the cavity due to the negative gradient of the magnetic field. Considering the electron bunch as a thin disk, with radius
r_{e}
and
N_{i}\ll N_{e}
, the peak value for the space-charge electric field can be approximated as
E_{sc}=(eN_{e})/(2\epsilon_{0}S)\propto 1/r_{e}^{2}
, providing progressively more stringent conditions for Eqs.
7
and
8
(Eq.
8
becomes more stringent due to the decrease in electron energy). Thus, it is necessary to have an average electron energy at the end of the plasma compression phase quite larger than the lower limit imposed by Eq.
9
.

This limitation can be better understood from the examples in Fig.
2
, considering an accelerator design for He
2+
(
\gamma_{min}\approx 15.45
): when the electron energy at the beginning of the PLEIADE phase is not large enough (
\gamma_{c}=15.76
, Fig.
2
a), the accelerator loses its stability halfway through the cavity (at 1.8 m), as can be seen by the non shake out (NSO, Eq.
7
) condition becoming lower than the electron bunch stability (EBS, Eq.
8
) condition; the other cases (Fig.
2
b and
2
c) are characterized by a larger electron energy (
\gamma_{c}=16.69
), and hence the stability region (SR, light blue area in Fig.
2
) exists over the whole cavity length.

The stable ion acceleration also requires a magnetic gradient profile
\nabla B/B
strictly located inside this stability region, as illustrated with the black curve in Fig.
2
b. Since the desired ion final energy is explicitly dependent on
B_{fin}
, the control over the
\nabla B/B
range is obtained by adapting the accelerating cavity length
l_{PL}
. The reduction of
l_{PL}
from 5 m (Fig.
2
.b) down to 4.5 m (Fig.
2
.c) results in an inappropriate gradient for the ion acceleration. It is worth noting that the magnetic gradient profile has been tailored to follow the NSO curve shape, to improve the acceleration stability over the whole system.

ECRIPAC parameter analysis –

The physical parameter values admissible for a stable ECRIPAC acceleration have been carefully studied, both for the external fields and the plasma. Two representative parameter studies are presented here for the sake of simplicity: the plasma density
n_{e}
and the ion
A/Z
ratio. Further details can be found in the supplemental material
Sup

The influence of each parameter has been analyzed through a stability map (Fig.

\gamma_{c}

1<\gamma_{c}/\gamma_{min}<1.5

The second y-axis (right side of each plot in red) is the time required to complete the GA phase (

The first x-axis (bottom side of each plot in black) indicates the final magnetic field at the end of the PLEIADE section
B_{fin}

4.5\leq B_{fin}\leq 5

Finally, the second x-axis (top side of each plot in green) corresponds to the final values of the ion kinetic energy per nucleon
W_{i}/A

The nominal values of the accelerator parameters when they are not varied are A/Z = 2 and
n_{e}=10\%\;n_{cr}

n_{cr}=\epsilon_{0}m_{e}\omega_{HF}^{2}/e^{2}
is the critical density,
\epsilon_{0}
the vacuum permittivity and

The ion energy in all stability maps has been evaluated using Eq.

Fig.

t_{GA}
).
Moreover, Fig.

n_{e}
), due to the higher number of electrons and hence a better entrainment of the ions during PLEIADE.
Nevertheless, keeping
n_{e}\ll n_{cr}
appears safer to limit the risk of wave-plasma interaction with the injected HF wave, and hence to prevent a mitigation of the electron energy gain.
A general trend is observed for
B_{fin}
(see Fig.
3

l_{PL}
).
Finally, the electron energy at the beginning of the PLEIADE phase (
\gamma_{c}
) is more a requirement than a parameter for ECRIPAC, since it would be ideal to have the smallest value possible which allows stability. This would allow faster times for the GA phase (
t_{GA}
) and shorter PLEIADE cavity (
l_{PL}
), minimizing the technological requirements for the accelerator and relaxing the conditions needed by the plasma.

To conclude, ECRIPAC is clearly more suited to accelerate ions with low A/Z ratio, by using a dense plasma.

The considered ECRIPAC design uses a 2.45 GHz frequency, very common in industry. The PLEIADE section is 1.8 m long, allowing the He
2+
ion bunches to reach estimated energies in the order of
\sim 9.5MeV/A

As a comparison, a cyclotron capable of producing such an ion beam would require a magnetic pole diameter of approximately 3 m and an external multi-charged ion source, coupled to a 4 m long low-energy beam transport (LEBT) line to inject the beam into the cyclotron.
Otherwise, a linear accelerator would require at least an ion source with a 4 m long LEBT, a 7 m long radio-frequency quadrupole to pre-accelerate the ions up to 1 MeV/A, a 4 m long medium-energy beam transport (MEBT) line, followed by a set of 1 m long RF cavities to reach 9.5 MeV/A.

Monte-Carlo code validation –

A Monte-Carlo particle-tracking code has been used to simulate the electron dynamics, in order to validate the theoretical treatment presented in this paper. The code has been already validated against results from ECR ion source
Cernuschi and Thuillier 2025
and GYRAC accelerators
Andreev et al. 2012
;
Andreev et al. 2016
;
Andreev et al. 2017
. It has been adapted to simulate the prototype design proposed in the previous paragraph.
Figure
4
compares the average evolution in time of the Lorentz Factor (Fig.
4
a) and the radial distance of the electron from the cavity axis (Fig.
4
b) obtained with the Monte-Carlo code and with the theoretical formulation presented earlier.
The Monte-Carlo code uses a statistics of 100000 electrons, with 70% of them undergoing gyromagnetic autoresonance. Further details on the Monte-Carlo code implementation can be found in the supplemental material
Sup
.
The results obtained with the Monte-Carlo code show a strong agreement with the theoretical treatment presented in the paper. The energy gain during GA predicted by Eq.
2
closely follows the average energy of the simulated electrons, while the energy gain during the plasma compression phase (Eq.
5
) is slightly overestimated by less than 4%.
The radial dimension of the electron bunch is also in very close agreement with the theoretical predictions, both during the GA (
r_{e}=v_{e}/\Omega
) and plasma compression phase (Eq.
4
).
The results of the Monte-Carlo code demonstrate that the theoretical treatment for ECRIPAC can be considered reliable, providing confidence in the study presented in this paper.

Conclusion and prospects –

The theoretical study reported in the paper revisited and expanded the current knowledge of the ECRIPAC accelerator concept, highlighting further limitations for the accelerator stability and analyzing the effects that several parameters have on the overall acceleration process.
Errors in the calculations reported in the original paper
Geller et al. 1991
have been addressed. The correct results are presented in this work, providing a suitable reference for future investigations on this innovative accelerator concept.

The minimum energy that the electrons must have at the end of the plasma compression phase, in order to have a stable ion acceleration during the PLEIADE phase, is actually higher than the previously reported limit (Eq.
9
). This is due to the increase in the plasma bunch radius and reduction in energy of the accelerated electron bunch during the PLEIADE phase. The existence of a stable acceleration process is also influenced by many other parameters, including the PLEIADE magnetic field profile (
\nabla B/B
) and the cavity length.

A careful analysis of the effect that several parameters have on the acceleration process has been carried out. It has been concluded that ECRIPAC is more suitable to accelerate low A/Z ions, and that a large electron density is extremely beneficial for the ion acceleration, despite possible problems related to wave-plasma interactions.
Finally, a prototype design for a compact He
2+
accelerator, able to produce ions up to 9.5 MeV/A, has been proposed based on the present theoretical study.

A Monte-Carlo Particle-tracking code, already tested on ECR ion sources and GYRAC accelerator, has been used to validate the theoretical treatment of ECRIPAC using the proposed prototype design, showing a very good agreement and validating the study presented in this paper.
In order to verify the proper working of the ECRIPAC accelerator concept using the proposed compact demonstrator device, a Particle In Cell simulation of the entire system during the full working cycle is currently under development.
