Poison to Detect: Detection of Targeted Overfitting in Federated Learning
Soumia Zohra El Mestari, Maciej Krzysztof Zuziak, Gabriele Lenzini

Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across decentralised clients while keeping local data private, making it a widely adopted privacy-enhancing technology (PET). Despite its privacy benefits, FL remains vulnerable to privacy attacks, including those targeting specific clients. In this paper, we study an underexplored threat where a dishonest orchestrator intentionally manipulates the aggregation process to induce targeted overfitting in the local models of specific clients. Whereas many studies in this area predominantly focus on reducing the amount of information leakage during training, we focus on enabling an early client-side detection of targeted overfitting, thereby allowing clients to disengage before significant harm occurs. In line with this, we propose three detection techniques—(a) label flipping, (b) backdoor trigger injection, and (c) model fingerprinting—that enable clients to verify the integrity of the global aggregation. We evaluated our methods on multiple datasets under different attack scenarios. Our results show that the three methods reliably detect targeted overfitting induced by the orchestrator, but they differ in terms of computational complexity, detection latency, and false-positive rates.

Introduction

Federated Learning (FL) is a distributed training paradigm that enables multiple clients to collaboratively train a model under the coordination of a central orchestrator without the need to share their data either among them or with the orchestrator
[
16
,
22
]
. FL is often considered a privacy-preserving alternative to centralised training. This setting enables the global model to leverage various data sources, thereby overcoming the limitations of sensitive data that cannot be shared across local environments. However, despite these advantages, decentralisation introduces a new attack vector, where both clients and the orchestrator can become malicious actors targeting one another. While the previous research has primarily focused on attacks maintained by malicious clients that manipulate local and global updates and even local data
[
32
]
, less attention has been given to orchestrator-driven attacks. The aggregation process in federated learning, though ostensibly neutral, can be exploited to induce specific learning behaviours in targeted clients– a strategy that we refer to as
targeted aggregation
. In this context, the orchestrator can manipulate model updates to strategically amplify or suppress specific data characteristics, which may inadvertently potentially degrade model performance, or even worse, cause a model to memorise or leak sensitive client data, threatening the integrity and privacy of the system.
[
2
]
. This manipulation can extend beyond aggregation to include the strategic selection of clients, enabling the orchestrator to amplify or suppress particular data patterns through model updates.

Our work focuses on the detection of one of the targeted aggregation processes that we name
targeted overfitting
. In this scenario, the orchestrator performs a double aggregation process where one aggregation is benign, including all updates received from clients, and its output is sent to the non-targeted clients, and a second aggregation is malicious, where the orchestrator aggregates using updates received from a subset of targeted clients, where the result of this malicious aggregation is shared with the targeted clients only. Over iterations, this malicious aggregation leads the targeted clients to overfit on their data. By overfitting the targeted client’s model to its local data, the orchestrator increases the risk of membership inference attacks (MIA)
[
31
]
, where the adversary can infer the presence of specific data points within the training set of a particular targeted client. In addition to that, overfitted models may memorise specific data points
[
24
]
, increasing their vulnerability to data reconstruction attacks
[
3
,
4
,
28
]
and model inversion attacks
[
10
,
13
,
34
]
. This data leakage not only undermines the privacy-preserving goals of federated learning but also exposes the sensitive clients to potential adversaries, other than the orchestrator itself, who may be eavesdropping on the communication channel between the clients and the orchestrator. Over multiple iterations, the targeted client’s model becomes more tailored to its local data distribution, which reduces its performance on unseen data. This causes targeted local models to diverge from the global one. Furthermore, backdoor attacks can be exacerbated by overfitting the targeted client’s model to specific trigger patterns, thereby embedding malicious behaviours more effectively
[
2
]
. Additionally, building on the backdoor attack strategies of Fang
et al.

[
9
]
, targeted overfitting may offer a pathway for training hijacking, wherein the orchestrator manipulates model updates to align with adversarial objectives, effectively steering the targeted client’s model toward specific, potentially harmful patterns

Contribution

Unlike the methods that focus on preventing the results of targeted aggregation behaviours, our study focuses on the detection aspect of the problem. Defence mechanisms like Differential Privacy (DP)
[
18
]
tend to come with a utility-privacy tradeoff that the system may have to deal with even if the potential malicious actors prove to be benign; on the other hand, a detection mechanism may avoid this tradeoff or make the usage of prevention mechanisms like DP more informed. Hence, our study presents different frameworks that enable clients in an FL setting to autonomously identify targeted overfitting induced by the orchestrator. We propose and evaluate three detection mechanisms – label flipping, backdoor trigger, and gradient fingerprinting – for their effectiveness in detecting such targeted manipulations under different scenarios. Our methods operate without the need for communication or cooperation between clients, focusing on timely client-side detection of orchestrator-driven targeted attacks that can cause significant privacy risks, such as membership inference and data reconstruction attacks.

Orchestrator as an attacker

Attacks altering model integrity in federated learning have predominantly focused on client-to-client or client-to-orchestrator threats

Some server-side attacks can also be part of a multi-step attack pipeline aimed at compromising client privacy. Such attacks, namely: membership inference attacks

By manipulating global updates to emphasise certain features or by exaggerating personalisation effects, the orchestrator can increase the generalisation gap, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of inference attacks.

Overfitting

Overfitting itself is a well-recognised challenge in FL and has been attributed to several factors. For instance, a client’s model may overfit its local data, increasing its susceptibility to privacy attacks such as Source Inference Attacks (SIA) from a curious server
[
42
]
. Additionally, data heterogeneity—where client data is non-iid—can lead to local models overfitting their specific distributions, causing divergence from the global objective and hindering convergence
[
36
,
30
]
. Overfitting is also exploited in client-side poisoning attacks, where a malicious client intentionally overfits on poisoned samples to introduce backdoors into the global model
[
39
]
. Moreover, rigid training configurations, such as applying a fixed number of local epochs to all clients regardless of dataset size or complexity, can result in uneven training—causing some clients to be underfitted and others to be overfitted—ultimately degrading the quality of the aggregated global model
[
15
]
.

Detection methods

Client-side detection mechanisms for identifying targeted overfitting and similar malicious behaviours remain largely under-explored. While most existing techniques are not explicitly designed to detect a malicious server, several principles from current research can be adapted by clients to enhance their resilience. For instance, local performance monitoring—a standard machine learning practice—can serve as an early warning system: by maintaining a private test set, a client can compare training and testing performance locally. If the model consistently performs well on training data but poorly on the test set after receiving updates from the server, this persistent overfitting may suggest orchestrator-driven manipulation, although it could also stem from data heterogeneity
[
40
]
. Another promising approach is collaborative validation, as proposed in defences against backdoor attacks
[
1
]
, where randomly selected clients validate the integrity of the global model using their private data and collectively vote on potential anomalies. This mechanism could be generalised to detect other server-side manipulations, especially if multiple clients report similar patterns of degradation or instability. Furthermore, strategies aimed at reducing local overfitting, such as promoting fairness-in-privacy through collaborative regularization
[
42
]
.

Problem statment

We consider a threat model where a malicious aggregator (i.e., the orchestration server in a horizontal FL setting) intentionally performs modified aggregations that aim to make a subset of clients overfit on their local datasets. The targeted clients can be either a single client or a subset of clients among the elected clients.

Suppose the local models obtained from the clients selected at iteration
t
are denoted by the set
\{W_{i}^{t}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
N
is the number of clients participating in that round. Let
\mathcal{S}^{t}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
represent the set of selected clients at iteration
t
. In a benign federated learning setting, the orchestrator aggregates all local updates received from
\mathcal{S}^{t}
using an aggregation function
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
, such as FedAvg. The resulting global model at iteration
t
is then given by:

W_{\text{global}}^{t}=\mathcal{A}\left(\{W_{i}^{t}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{t}}\right).

However, to model the orchestrator’s behaviour in our study, we consider a malicious setting in which the orchestrator may deviate from standard protocol by aggregating updates from only a subset of the selected clients. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\text{mal}}^{t}\subset\mathcal{S}^{t}
denote a subset of clients of size
n<N
, selectively chosen by the malicious server. The corresponding malicious aggregation is defined as:

\tilde{W}_{\text{global}}^{t}=\mathcal{A}\left(\{W_{i}^{t}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{mal}}^{t}}\right).

This biased aggregation may serve various adversarial objectives, such as model manipulation, targeted overfitting, or privacy leakage, by excluding benign updates or overemphasising the influence of certain clients.

In this study, we analyse the targeted overfitting strategies under two distinct scenarios, which arise from two modes of targeting. The modes correspond to the adversary targeting either a single client or a fixed subset of
n
clients.
Formally, the scenarios are defined as follows:

•

Scenario I:
Targeting a specific single client
i^{*}\in\mathcal{S}^{t}
at every iteration
t
, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{\text{mal}}^{t}=\{i^{*}\},\quad\forall t.

•

Scenario II:
Targeting a fixed subset
\mathcal{S}_{\text{mal}}
at every iteration, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{\text{mal}}^{t}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{mal}},\quad\forall t.

We focus on these two scenarios because they capture the fundamental behaviours of targeted overfitting. Scenario I represents the continuous targeting of a single client, while Scenario II generalises this idea to targeting a fixed subset of clients. Other possible scenarios include targeting a single client or a subset of clients periodically during a fixed number of iterations, which can be viewed as special cases or episodic instances of these two scenarios. Thus, studying Scenarios I and II allows us to analyse the core effect of targeted overfitting, as well as the proposed detection mechanisms in a clear and generalisable way.

4
Detecting targeted overfitting

Client-level distance thresholding—comparing received updates to the dispatched global model—is a standard approach for detecting anomalous behaviour. In principle, this can detect targeted overfitting; however, the detection completely fails when the orchestrator uses adaptive optimisers (e.g., Adam, RMSProp), which use internal states like the momentum and the adaptive learning rates. The internal states cause each global update to shift in direction and magnitude depending on the past gradients, leading to non-zero and variable distances between the dispatched and the received models—even in dishonest settings. As a result, distance-based methods become noisy and unreliable. In this section, we propose three methods to detect targeted overfitting that are optimiser-agnostic and can be used autonomously by the clients to detect targeted overfitting early.

Early detection is crucial because the longer targeted aggregation continues, the more the victim clients overfit on their local data. By identifying suspicious updates promptly, clients can take preventive measures such as opting out of the training process or raising an alert in the system to ensure mitigation occurs before the attack fully manifests.

Detection Via Label-Flipping Poisons

We propose a controlled label-flipping mechanism that can be performed by clients as a means to detect targeted overfitting. The core idea is to poison a small subset of data such that its effect remains locally detectable even post-model aggregation. If a client observes that the global model performs almost as well as the local model on the poisoned subset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{flip}}
(evaluated immediately after local training), it may suggest that the server selectively favored the client’s local model during aggregation ( see algorithm
1
). In other words, under honest aggregation, the global model should "correct" the poisoned signal, resulting in a drop in performance on
\mathcal{D}_{\text{flip}}

Specifically, each client selects the least frequent class in its local dataset and flips a fraction
\alpha

\text{flip}(y)=(y+1)\mod K

This cyclic approach ensures that a label

is always mapped to a different class, avoiding self-flipping. This ensures semantic change while avoiding label collisions. In addition to that, targeting the least frequent class minimises disruption to overall training and reduces the likelihood of triggering server-side defences. The flipped samples form a poisoned subset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{flip}}

4.3
Garadient fingerprinting

Our third detection method is the "Gradients/weights fingerprinting". This method relies on either fingerprinting the weights or the gradients, depending on the global optimiser used by the orchestrator. To elaborate, in federated learning, model updates exchanged between clients and the orchestrator depend on the global optimiser: algorithms such as FedAvg
[
26
]
aggregate model weights after local training, whereas methods like FedSGD
[
26
]
or Scaffold
[
17
]
rely on gradient averaging. The client perturbs its local gradient/weight
g
after finishing the local epochs by injecting a small, secret vector
s
, resulting in a fingerprinted update
\tilde{g}=g+\alpha s
, where
\alpha>0
controls the fingerprint strength. The fingerprint vector
s
is client-specific and ideally low-correlation with
g
; common choices include random unit vectors
(s=v/\|v\|,\,v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I))
, sparse binary vectors over client-specific indices, or deterministic vectors derived from the client ID (e.g.,
s=\texttt{HashToVector}(\texttt{ClientID})
). The client sends
\tilde{g}
to the server, which aggregates updates from multiple clients. Upon receiving the global model update
G
, the client evaluates the fingerprint strength as
\text{Strength}_{t}=\langle G_{t},s\rangle
or optionally its cosine similarity. During typical FL rounds, the fingerprint is expected to be diluted by other clients’ updates, resulting in low strength. However, if the client’s update is selectively amplified, the fingerprint will be preserved, leading to an unusually high strength. The value of
\alpha
should be small (
10^{-3}
to
10^{-2}
) to minimize influence on training while maintaining detectability.

Limitations

A key limitation of the label flipping method is that it requires multiple local epochs (typically between 4 and 7) for the model to sufficiently learn the flipped patterns. Furthermore, in heterogeneous (non-IID) data settings, uneven client convergence further weakens the poison signal, reducing detection reliability. Moreover, the method requires computing accuracy on the poisoned subset twice per iteration—before sending the local model and after receiving the aggregated model—making it computationally costly despite its simple design. The threshold
\alpha
is comparably easy to hypertune, as it is always bounded in the interval
\alpha\in[0,1]
, with lower alpha denoting higher sensibility.

5.3.2
Detection using backdoor trigger

From a computational viewpoint, backdoor-based detection can reduce runtime by nearly half compared to label flipping, as the backdoor strength is computed only after receiving the aggregated model, which has similar detection performance. Figure
2(b)
illustrates the iteration-by-iteration progression of the backdoor detection scores
S
(Trigger Influence Score), where higher
S
scores reflect a dishonest aggregation. We clearly observe that the targeted clients score a high S compared to the non-targeted clients. The decision boundary is well separated in Scenario I, reflecting a clear distinction between the cohorts. This decision boundary becomes more subtle in Scenario II. Figure
4(b)
reports similar patterns, where the best detection accuracy in scenario I ranges between 0.95 and 0.98, whereas in scenario II, the detection accuracy ranges between 0.52 and 0.6.

These results indicate that the backdoor method exhibits behaviour similar to that of the label flip method. In the simplest case, where a single client is attacked (Scenario I), the detection is almost immediate (see Table
2(a)
); while in Scenario II, it may require several rounds to detect an attempt by the orchestrator to favour a subset of targeted clients during the aggregation.

Limitations

The backdoor trigger method is twice as computationally efficient as label flipping. However, it still suffers from problems in heterogeneous (non-IID) settings, where uneven client convergence can negatively impact the encoded signal, reducing detection reliability.

5.3.3
Detection using fingerprinting

The fingerprint analysis shows excellent and robust results across all datasets for scenario I, as shown in Table
2(b)
and
2(a)
. Detection is almost immediate across all datasets, with an accuracy ranging from 0.75 to 1.0. Thus for scenario I the fingerprinting method is the most reliable method. However, this method fails drastically in scenario II, where a subset of clients is targeted. This is due to the fact that the fingerprint signal should be stronger to survive an aggregation with the local models of the 2 other clients. However, a stronger fingerprint compromises the model and disrupts the learning process, making this method the least reliable in scenario II.

Limitations

The fingerprinting method described in this paper requires that the orchestrator’s optimiser both receives and sends the same type of updates—either gradients or model weights. Hence, optimisers that receive gradients but return weights (
e.g.
, Fedopt or FedProx) are incompatible with this approach. In addition to that, for scenario II, and since the malicious aggregation targeted a subset of clients, more powerful fingerprints are needed, which may interfere with the learning time, as a stronger fingerprint may degrade the model’s performance.

6
Conclusion

In this article, we studied a potential threat in federated learning that we named "targeted overfitting," in which a malicious orchestrator attempts to make specific clients overfit on their local datasets by performing a malicious aggregation process that singles out those targets. This type of threat may pose disproportionate privacy risks to clients by exposing them to various attacks, such as membership inference attacks. To address this, we proposed three detection mechanisms—label flipping, backdoor triggers, and fingerprinting—that allow clients to autonomously detect the threat without collusion. Our evaluation showed that the three methods can successfully detect the threat when a single client is targeted, with the fingerprinting method being the most reliable. However, when the attack targets multiple clients simultaneously, only the label flipping and backdoor trigger methods maintain effectiveness, whereas the fingerprinting method exhibits substantially reduced performance.

In the subsequent works, we would like to further study this problem from two distinctive angles. Firstly, we would like to amplify the capabilities of the attacker (the orchestrator) by either incorporating more nuanced schemas of aggregation or different types of optimisers. Secondly, we would like to further examine the robustness of the fingerprinting method, which has a clear potential as a validation method, but requires more targeted studies in order to better understand the relevance of the incorporated fingerprint under various aggregation schemas. Given the current lack of similar studies in the literature and the novelty of the introduced methods, we confined ourselves to a fundamental exploration of all of them, which aimed to present their general capabilities, while we hope that further studies into a specific method (such as label flip or backdoor trigger) will further reveal their capabilities and limitations.
