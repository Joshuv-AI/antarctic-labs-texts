Dimer-projection contact and the clock shift of a unitary Fermi gas
Kevin G. S. Xie, Colin J. Dale, Kiera Pond Grehan, Maggie Fen Wang, Tilman Enss, Paul S. Julienne, Zhenhua Yu, Joseph H. Thywissen

Abstract
The time evolution of the contact parameter provides key insights into correlation dynamics in ultracold gases. However, most contact measurements to date have focused on equilibrium systems or slow, global dynamics. Here, we demonstrate that projecting a unitary Fermi gas onto a low-lying dimer state enables rapid probing of the contact. Using 40 K near a broad s-wave Feshbach resonance, we compare the strength of the dimer-projection feature to the strength of the high-frequency tail of radio-frequency spectroscopy. By tuning the correlation strength through temperature, we find that the dimer signal scales proportionally with the contact parameter, in agreement with coupled-channels calculations. Our measurements enable us to constrain the clock shift of the unitary Fermi gas, to which the dimer feature is the dominant contributor. We observe deviations from universal predictions due to finite-range and multichannel effects. Our results establish new universal contact relations and shed light on the structure of the clock shift in strongly interacting Fermi gases. The demonstrated ability to resolve short-range correlations on timescales shorter than the inverse Fermi energy opens new avenues for studying contact correlators, hydrodynamic attractors, and quantum critical behavior.

Discussion and conclusion.

Radio-frequency dimer projection can be used in any system with
a_{13}>0
. We anticipate that for a UFG of
6
Li, both the dimer-projection
\ell_{d}
and the clock shift would approach the universal case. Since the closed-channel fractions are smaller than in the
40
K case,
I_{d}
and
\Delta
should be more successfully handled by the SqW model. An estimate
SM
of the non-universality coefficients
A_{\mathrm{HFT}}
and
A_{c}
indicates they differ from unity by
\lesssim 1\%
, and that
\ell_{d}
is within 10% of the universal limit. Even though
E_{B}
is smaller, using dimer projection would still be the optimal approach for measuring correlations in
6
Li on the scale of
\tau_{F}
.

A natural extension of our work is to generalize the dimer-contact relation beyond unitarity, i.e., to systems with
a_{12}^{-1}\neq 0
, enabling exploration of contact dynamics across the BEC-BCS crossover. Preliminary evidence that
I_{d}
can probe weaker correlations away from unitarity is suggested by the increased drive strengths we found to be required when measuring
\omega_{d}
for Fig.
2
(a).
Another promising direction is the adaptation of rf dimer projection to probe higher-partial-wave correlations, such as the p-wave contact, and to two-dimensional systems where dimer states also exist for
a_{13}<0
.

In summary, we introduce rf dimer projection as a new tool for probing contact dynamics in ultracold gases. Our investigation in
40
K demonstrates that the dimer feature provides a microsecond-scale measure of short-range correlations, due to its spectral isolation and high spectral intensity. This capability opens new avenues for studying nonequilibrium pair-correlation dynamics in strongly interacting systems. Additionally, the dimer feature enabled significant progress towards measuring the clock shift in a unitary Fermi gas.

\alpha_{\mathrm{res}}\approx 2^{-1}\pi t_{\mathrm{rf}}\Omega_{23}^{2}\delta_{t}(\omega)\,,

\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{res}}\approx\frac{\Omega_{23}^{2}}{2\omega^{2}}\,.

(8)

Note that although the numerical factors will depend on the pulse shape, the
\omega^{-2}
scaling here is the generic off-resonant Breit-Rabi oscillation, whose amplitude is the ratio of the Rabi frequency squared to the generalized Rabi frequency squared,
\Omega_{23}^{2}/\omega^{2}
in the far-detuned limit.
The ratio of responses is

\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{HFT}}}{\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{res}}}\approx\frac{t_{\mathrm{rf}}\widetilde{C}\tilde{\omega}^{1/2}}{2^{1/2}\pi\tau_{F}}\,.

(9)

(10)

Now we can consider the rf power required for response

Harmonic trap-averaged contact.
In Fig.
3
we compare the measured, trap-integrated contact with theoretical predictions. Here we describe how these are obtained: We start with Luttinger-Ward calculations for the contact density
\mathcal{C}
of a uniform UFG and then integrate over the inhomogeneous density profile in the trap.

The Luttinger-Ward calculation
Haussmann et al. 2009
;
Enss et al. 2011
is based on a two-channel model in the zero-range limit, where the contact is determined from the density of dimers, or equivalently from the large-momentum tail of the fermion momentum distribution. The dimer Green function is computed via the T-matrix, which captures repeated scattering between two fermions. In the Luttinger-Ward approach the T-matrix is computed self-consistently using dressed Green functions for the fermions in the medium, which acquire a finite lifetime due to scattering off (virtual) dimers; for details on the implementation see
Haussmann et al. 2009
;
Enss et al. 2011
;
Enss 2019
;
Enss 2024
. This computation yields the local contact density
\mathcal{C}=sk_{F}^{4}=s(3\pi^{2}n)^{4/3}
, where both the dimensionless contact
s(\mu)
and the density equation of state
n(\mu)
depend on the chemical potential
\mu
and temperature.

In order to compute the trap-integrated contact
C
, we first determine the density profile of the trapped unitary gas. Within the local density approximation (LDA) it follows as
n(r)=n[\mu(r)]
with the equation of state
n(\mu)
evaluated at the local chemical potential
\mu(r)=\mu-V(r)
for a given external trapping potential
V(r)
. We employ the measured equation of state
Ku et al. 2012a
and construct an all-temperature fitting function that is represented as a barycentric rational approximant, complemented by the virial expansion for high temperatures. For given experimental values of particle number
N
, temperature
T
and potential
V(r)
, we adjust the global chemical potential
\mu
in the density profile
n(r)=n[\mu-V(r)]
until it integrates to the desired particle number
N=\int d^{3}r\,n(r)
. Next, the dimensionless contact
s(\mu)
from the Luttinger-Ward calculation is also encoded into a global fitting function. Then, the trap-integrated contact is obtained as an integral over the trap volume at fixed temperature,

\displaystyle C=\int d^{3}r\,\mathcal{C}(r)=\int d^{3}r\,s[\mu(r)]\,\{3\pi^{2}n[\mu(r)]\}^{4/3}\,,

(16)

with position-dependent local chemical potential
\mu(r)=\mu-V(r)
. The intensive measure for the contact,
C/Nk_{F}
, is shown in Fig.
3
. As a model-independent reference scale, we use the global Fermi momentum of an ideal Fermi gas at zero temperature, as given by the relation
E_{F}=\hbar\bar{\omega}_{\text{trap}}(3N)^{1/3}=k_{F}^{2}/2m
.

The spherical square-well (SqW) model uses an interatomic potential
V_{\sigma}(r)=-V_{\sigma}

0

\sigma=i,f

k_{\sigma}L+\delta_{\sigma}(k)=n\pi

\displaystyle\phi_{\sigma,>}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\sigma,>}\frac{\sin(k_\sigma r + \delta_\sigma)}{r}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\quad r>r_{0}\,,

(S1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\sigma,<}\frac{\sin(s_\sigma\tilde{r})}{r}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\quad r<r_{0}\,,

Scattering T-matrix

The scattering T-matrix is found by summing diagrams to all orders yielding (with
\hbar=1
)

T(z)=\bar{g}\left(1-\frac{\bar{g}}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{p}\frac{1}{z-p^{2}/m}\right)^{-1}\,,

(S26)

where
\bar{g}
is the bare (unrenormalized) coupling constant,
z=k^{2}/m+0^{+}
is the energy,
p
and
k
are collision momenta,
m
is the atomic mass, and
\mathcal{V}
is the volume. Using a momentum cutoff-off
\Lambda
for the sum, one finds a renormalized coupling constant
g
, which we set
=4\pi a/m
where
a
is the measured s-wave scattering length.
\Lambda
is chosen such that the low-

T^{-1}\approx\frac{m}{4\pi}\left(-k\cot\delta+ik\right)\,,

(S27)

with
\cot\delta\approx-a^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}r_{e}k^{2}
, where
r_{e}
is the effective range.
One finds that
\Lambda=4r_{e}^{-1}/\pi
, such that the T-matrix is

T^{-1}(k,a,r_{e})=\frac{m}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{a}+ik+\frac{k}{\pi}\ln\frac{1-\pi 4kr_{e}/4}{1+\pi kr_{e}/4}\right)\,.

(S28)

To find the shallow dimer in the final-state channel, we need to find the pole in its scattering T-matrix. Here, we take
k\to i\kappa
, with
\kappa>0
.
One finds a nonlinear equation

\frac{1}{a_{13}\kappa}=1-\frac{2}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\kappa r_{e,13}\right)\,,

(S29)

whose small-

\kappa\approx a_{13}^{-1}(1+r_{e,13}/a_{13})

S1

We can use the same choice of cutoff to give a range-corrected clock shift.
The clock shift
\Delta
for a balanced gas

Baym et al. 2007

\widetilde{\Delta}=\frac{\Delta}{E_{F}}=\frac{4}{mk_{F}}\frac{\bar{g}_{13}}{\bar{g}_{12}}\left(\frac{1}{g_{12}}-\frac{1}{g_{13}}\right)\widetilde{C}\,,

I.3.3
Inelastic alkali-dialkali collisions

Atom-molecule dynamics in the ultralow collision energy regime have been subject to much study and we refer to
Quemener and Julienne 2012
for the following arguments. For hydrogen-like atoms such as the alkalis, elastic and inelastic threshold cross sections are highly sensitive to the details of the potentials of three-body terms, and it has been shown that inelastic collisions dominate as energy decreases below
\sim 100\,\mu
K. Inelastic collision rates generally increase with increasing vibrational quantum number
\nu
because of the higher number of possible relaxation channels. However, alkali-dialkali systems have shallow minimum potential energy surfaces (relative to hydrogen) and therefore a relatively high molecular state density that leads to strong short-range coupling even for low vibrational states. Consequently, one finds a near-universal inelastic collision rate for alkalis over a large range of initial vibrational states.

After rf association of
\ket{13}
dimers, we hypothesize that they continue to experience a collision process
\ket{13}_{\nu}+\ket{2}\to\ket{13}_{\nu^{\prime}}+\ket{2}+\mathrm{KE}

Quemener and Julienne 2012
where the dimer enters a lower vibrational state
\nu^{\prime}
by giving some kinetic energy
\mathrm{KE}
to the reaction products, which are then lost from the trap. For an inelastic loss rate
\sim 10^{-10}\mathrm{cm}^{3}/\mathrm{s}
and density
n=\sqrt{2}(mE_{F})^{3/2}/(3\hbar^{3}\pi^{2})\sim 10^{13}\mathrm{cm}^{-3}
for our typical
E_{F}
, this process occurs over a timescale
\tau\sim 1
ms. In an experiment cycle, clouds are held for
\sim 26
ms in the optical trap to prepare the cloud for imaging after rf spectroscopy. This is sufficient time for all dimers to undergo this vibrational relaxation process. The net effect is the loss of two
\ket{2}
particles and one
\ket{1}
particle for every dimer formed. This two-to-one loss is immediately apparent in our data, and insensitive to magnetic field (i.e., apparently unrelated to the
a_{12}
Feshbach resonance).

We measure the relaxation rate by “swapping” free-atom
\ket{1}
and
\ket{2}
populations with a variable time delay after dimers are formed. The final populations in
\ket{1}
and
\ket{2}
are then imaged. We find that
\delta N_{2}/\delta N_{1}=1
follows an exponential decay in the delay time of the
\pi
pulse, which can be explained as follows.
If the population swap is applied immediately after the dimer association pulse, the system is left as if we did not apply any pulse at all, and we observe greater loss in
\ket{2}
compared to
\ket{1}
. The opposite occurs in the case of long wait time, as the (initial)
\ket{2}
atoms experience severe atom-dimer decay before being swapped into
\ket{1}
for imaging. The atom-dimer decay rate is extracted from a simple exponential fit, yielding a time constant
\tau=1.4(3)
ms for
T=0.58(2)T_{F}
, and
\tau=0.6(2)
ms for
T=0.32(1)T_{F}
. We attribute this change due to the higher density of a colder gas. Both relaxation times are consistent with a vibrational decay cross-section comparable to the universal rate described in Ref.
Quemener and Julienne 2012
and references therein.

I.3.4
Dimer transfer signal in the spin channel

The resulting two-to-one loss after dimer association described in the previous section has a useful consequence.
Measurements that rely upon the loss of atoms as a signal suffer from shot-to-shot number variation, since one cannot measure both the initial and final atom number in a single experimental cycle.
Measuring instead the ratio
R_{12}\equiv N_{1}/N_{2}
reduces the effect of common-mode fluctuations. To obtain the fraction of atom pairs associated into dimers
\alpha_{d}
from the atom number ratio, we assume the two-to-one loss ratio, such that
N_{1}=N_{1}^{\mathrm{bg}}-\alpha_{d}N^{\mathrm{bg}}_{\mathrm{tot}}/2
and
N_{2}=N_{2}^{\mathrm{bg}}-\alpha_{d}N^{\mathrm{bg}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
where
N^{\mathrm{bg}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
is the initial total atom number.
Then, the ratio can be written
R_{12}=(N_{1}^{\mathrm{bg}}-\alpha_{d}N^{\mathrm{bg}}_{\mathrm{tot}}/2)/(N_{2}^{\mathrm{bg}}-\alpha_{d}N^{\mathrm{bg}}_{\mathrm{tot}})
. By solving for
\alpha_{d}
and defining the fraction of atoms initially in state
\sigma\in{1,2}
as
f_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{bg}}=N_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{bg}}/N^{\mathrm{bg}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
, we obtain

\alpha_{d}=\frac{R_{12}f_{2}^{\mathrm{bg}}-f_{1}^{\mathrm{bg}}}{2R_{12}-1}\,.

(S33)

For a balanced cloud, the background state fractions
f_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{bg}}
are both 0.5. Notice that now the transferred fraction of atoms is written in terms of only fractional measurements of counts of
\ket{1}
and
\ket{2}
atoms.
