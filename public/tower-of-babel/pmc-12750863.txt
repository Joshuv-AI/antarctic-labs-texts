Prevalence and diversity of helminths in three corvid species (Coloeus monedula (Linnaeus, 1758), Garrulus glandarius (Linnaeus, 1758), and Corvus corax (Linnaeus, 1758) from Türkiye
Authors: Mohamed Warsame Dirshe, Hikmet Sami Yıldırımhan, Adel Mohammed Redhwan, Ahmet Onur Girisgin, Yeliz Civil, Ali Adan Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorvidae birds are ecologically significant and host a diverse range of helminth parasites; however, data on their parasitic fauna in Türkiye remain limited. Here, we examined 44 individuals of three corvid species (39 Coloeus monedula, 3 Garrulus glandarius and 2 Corvus corax) from Bursa Province through necropsy and microscopic identification.ResultsWe detected helminth infections in 79.5% of the birds, identifying 12 species across Digenea (Brachylaima fuscata, Brachylecithum lobatum, Lyperosomum longicauda), Cestoda (Choanotaenia macracantha, Passerilepis stylosa), Nematoda (Syngamus trachea, Dispharynx nasuta, Dispharynx sp., Baruscapillaria obsiganata, B. resecta), and Acanthocephala (Sphaerirostris picae, Plagiorhynchus (Prosthorhynchus) cylindraceus).ConclusionsThis study represents the third record for C. monedula, the second for G. glandarius, and the first for Co. corax in Türkiye. Notably, B. fuscata, Br. lobatum, C. macracantha, P. stylosa, and B. resecta helminths were recorded for the first time in Türkiye. These findings enhance the understanding of helminth diversity in regional corvid populations and provide a foundation for future studies on host–parasite dynamics and avian health in Eurasian ecosystems.

Introduction

​The avian family Corvidae, which includes species such as the Eurasian jackdaw (Coloeus monedula Linnaeus, 1758), Eurasian jay (Garrulus glandarius (Linnaeus, 1758)) and common raven (Corvus corax Linnaeus, 1758) and plays a critical role in various ecosystems across Eurasia. These intelligent birds are not only essential for their ecological functions, such as seed dispersal and social foraging, but also harbour a diverse helminth fauna that contributes significantly to our understanding of host-parasite interactions [1]. These birds harbour numerous helminth species, including trematodes, cestodes and nematodes, which may vary significantly depending on geographic location and environmental factors [2]. Understanding these relationships is pivotal, as they can influence bird health, reproductive success, and population dynamics.

Helminthic infections can lead to significant health impairments and behavioural changes in corvids. These parasites may induce stress responses that hinder the cognitive functions of corvids, which are known for their problem-solving abilities and social interactions [2]. Additionally, the presence of helminths can influence population dynamics by influencing reproductive success and survival rates, thus reshaping local ecosystems [1].

Türkiye boasts a rich avifauna, hosting approximately 497 bird species that belong to 76 families. Among these, the Corvidae, known for their intelligence and adaptability, comprise 11 endemic species found throughout the country. These species can be observed in various habitats, including forests, wetlands, and urban areas, portraying the diverse ecosystems that support such a wide array of avian life in Türkiye [3]– [4]. Bursa Province is located in northwest Türkiye (40°11´N; 29°04´E) and is a forested area situated on the northwestern slope of Mount Uludağ. It has a Mediterranean climate and is generally quite humid, with average annual humidity levels ranging from 62% to 74% due to its proximity to the Marmara Sea [5]. The province is home to six corvid bird species: Pica pica, Coloeus monedula, Corvus corax, Garrulus glandarius, Corvus cornix, and Corvus frugilegus [6].

Many helminthological studies have been conducted worldwide on species belonging to the family Corvidae [7–12]. There are also different helminthological studies conducted on species belonging to this family in Türkiye [13–20]. Among the three host species examined in the present study, Coloeus monedula and Garrulus glandarius were previously investigated by Schad [21], Yılmaz [18] and Güven [20]. In the two previous studies on C. monedula, three helminth species were reported, whereas two species were identified in G. glandarius.

This study was conducted to determine the prevalence and diversity of helminth infections in three corvid species (C. monedula, G. glandarius and Co. corax) distributed in Bursa Province, Türkiye.

Methods

Between January 2024 and April 2025, 44 individuals of the family Corvidae [39 Coloeus monedula (17♀, 16♂, 6 juveniles, length range 28.3 ± 4.6 cm), 2 Corvus corax (1 ♀, 1♂, length range 48.5 ± 6.3 cm) and 3 Garrulus glandarius (3 ♂, length range 27.3 ± 5.3 cm)] were examined for helminth parasites. The avian specimens were obtained dead from Bursa Zoo and the Bursa Karacabey Wild Animal Rescue and Rehabilitation Centre, under research permit (No: 10936637) issued by the General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks, Ministry of Forestry and Water Affairs. After the species identification of the birds [22], they were transferred to the Department of Biology, Parasitology Laboratory at Bursa Uludağ University for necropsy. These specimens were stored at − 18 °C until they were dissected. Prior to examination, the specimens were thawed, and their total and body lengths were measured. Subsequently, the mouth, nasal cavity, and eyes were inspected for the presence of ectoparasites. Feathers in the ventral region were then removed, and dissection was initiated.

During the dissection process, the sex of the birds was determined, and the number and location of helminths found in the internal organs were noted. The helminths found were stored in small bottles containing 70% ethyl alcohol. Digenea, Cestoda, and Acanthocephala specimens were stained with Iron Aceto-Carmine, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and then mounted in Entellan→ [23]. Nematodes were cleared with Lactophenol and examined as temporary wet mounts [24]. Species identification was conducted via Leica EZ4 stereo and Olympus CH-2 light microscopes. References consulted for this identification included Yorke and Maplestone [25], Yamaguti [26–28], Merdivenci [13], Khalil et al. [29], Gibson et al. [30], Skrjabin [31] and Anderson [32]. After these procedures, permanent slides were preserved in slide boxes and the remaining specimens were stored in 70% ethanol in the Parasitology Laboratory of the Department of Biology at Bursa Uludag University. The prevalence, mean intensity, and mean abundance values of helminth species are defined as used by Bush et al. [33]. To evaluate the distributional relationships and patterns of the parasite community structure between adult and juvenile crows, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), a multivariate dimension-reduction technique, was conducted using CANOCO version 4.5.

Results

In this study, a total of 44 birds (39 Coloeus monedula, 3 Garrulus glandarius, 2 Corvus corax) from three different species collected in Bursa Province were examined for helminths. Of these, 35 specimens (79.5%) were found to be parasitised.

In Coloeus monedula, we identified three species of Digenea (Brachylaima fuscata (Rudolphi, 1819), Branchylecithum lobatum (Railliet, 1900), and Lyperosomum longicauda (Rudolphi, 1809)), two species of Cestoda (Passerilepis stylosa (Rudolphi, 1809) and Choanotaenia macracantha (Krabbe, 1869)), three species of Nematoda (Syngamus trachea (Montagu, 1811), Dispharynx nasuta (Rudolphi, 1819), and Baruscapillaria obsignata (Madsen, 1945)), and two species of Acanthocephala (Sphaerirostris picae (Rudolphi, 1819) and Plagiorhynchus (Prosthorhynchus) cylindraceus (Goeze, 1782) Schmidt et Kuntz, 1966) (Table 1).

In this study, the helminth species, infection levels, and site of infection in 44 individuals of three bird species belonging to the Corvidae family, which were seized dead in Bursa, were determined. While no parasites were found in 8 (20.5%) of 39 samples of Coloeus monedula, 803 helminths representing 10 species were found in 31 (79.5%) samples. Two different species were found in eight bird samples, four different species were found in five bird samples, and six different species were found in one bird sample. No parasites were found in one of the two specimens of Corvus corax, whereas 19 helminths representing four species were found in the other. In Garrulus glandarius, three specimens were examined, and parasites were found in all of them; 15 helminths representing two species were found. Helminths are not only found in the small intestine but also in places such as the oesophagus, liver and gizzard (Table 2).

According to the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) Biplot statistical evaluation, adult individuals have higher parasite density and number than young individuals.

Discussion

The present study represents the third investigation on Coloeus monedula, with a total of ten helminth species detected. Additionally, this research constitutes the second study on Garrulus glandarius, in which two helminth species were recorded. Furthermore, our study represents the first helminthological investigation on Corvus corax in Türkiye, documenting four helminth species in this host.

Because the diet of birds in the family Corvidae is diverse, birds are exposed to many forms of parasites [1]. Adult crows tend to have higher helminth burdens than juveniles [34, 35]; a similar finding was also observed in our study. Environmental factors and habitat type can affect the prevalence of helminth parasites in corvids. Compared with nematodes and cestodes, acanthocephalans and trematodes are generally found less frequently in corvids [36]. In our study, most of the parasites detected were nematodes. Since the majority of the detected parasites utilise intermediate hosts in their life cycle, these results suggest that their diets are highly varied and depend on the species, location, and season. This situation may also be the reason why some helminth species were observed at a higher rate (79.5%) in samples of Coloeus monedula in our study. The findings of other authors, as presented in Table 3, are consistent with these results.

Some studies on helminths in Corvidae have been conducted in Türkiye, identifying Echinostoma spp., Capillaria spp., Syngamus spp., Porrocaecum spp., Raillietina spp. and Prosthorhynchus spirale in magpies, jackdaws, and rooks [18, 20, 21]. Another study detected Passerilepis sp. and Brachylaima sp. from necropsies of common magpies [37], similar to the genera we found. A study in Iran examined magpies, rooks, and carrion crows and reported that 84.9% harboured helminth parasites [35], a similar infectivity rate to ours. They identified four nematode species, two cestode species, one trematode species, and one acanthocephalan species. Another study, similar to ours in terms of its results, focused on wintering populations of rooks (Corvus frugilegus) in Ukraine and reported that 41 out of the 46 (89.13%) examined rooks were infected with helminths, with a total of 13 species identified [37]. Nematodes were the most abundant group, and Baruscapillaria resecta was the nematode species in which we encountered similar findings.

A study across England, Wales, and Ireland revealed that corvids had some of the highest intestinal parasite prevalence rates among the bird families studied [38]. A similar result was obtained in our study, where 35 (79.5%) out of 44 samples were found to be parasitised.

Several studies have noted that corvids, compared to other passerines, tend to have higher helminth prevalence and diversity. Investigations of rooks, magpies, jackdaws, and various crow species—using post-mortem, fecal, or necropsy surveys with sample sizes ranging from 46 to 755 birds—record nematodes, trematodes, cestodes, and an acanthocephalan. Nematode infections appear repeatedly, with prevalence estimates ranging from 2.32% to 36.9%, cestode infections ranging from 2.5% to 69.7% and trematode rates ranging from 1.9% to 28% [10, 18, 36–38].

Host-specific and regional patterns emerge. In Türkiye, for example, Capillaria spp. infections in rooks and jackdaws account for 9.52–10% [18], whereas British and Irish corvid surveys report Capillaria spp. at 27.2% [38]. In the Czech Republic, trematode prevalence differs between hosts (Brachylecithum lobatum reaching 15% in hooded crows and Lyperosomum petiolatum reaching 28% in magpies), and seasonal trends—such as springtime peaks in British birds—and habitat factors also correlate with infection intensity [36]. Collectively, the published studies document diverse helminth fauna in Eurasian corvids that vary with host species, geography, and environmental conditions. Acanthocephalan parasites were reported in only one region (Iran, Mazandaran), with a prevalence ranging from 18.2% to 64.5% [35].

Corvids play a crucial ecological role in the transmission of parasites to other species. They act as key “spreaders” or “reservoirs” in complex parasite life cycles. For instance, corvids serve as reservoir hosts for Syngamus trachea [39], which we have detected, and as intermediate hosts for certain species of Sarcocystis protozoa [40]. Because corvids are highly synanthropic and often frequent garbage dumps and agricultural areas, they could easily pick up and spread parasites with zoonotic potential. Their droppings can contaminate environments, posing risks to both public health and livestock [41].

In our study, Brachylaima fuscata, Brachylecithum lobatum, Choanotaenia macracantha, Passerilepis stylosa and Baruscapillaria resecta, which are primarily observed in birds belonging to the order Passeriformes, represent the first records of the fauna of Türkiye. The results obtained provide additional data on the helminth fauna associated with the relevant species. Although our findings are similar to those in some other studies, they represent the first records for Türkiye and are significant in that they reveal infected birds to be a potential reservoir for both humans and animals.

Conclusions

These data reveal the helminth burdens in some Corvidae species within the Bursa region, a transit point between Europe and Asia. We believe the results will be valuable for scientists working in this field, enabling them to evaluate their potential infectious agents in further studies. There are still few comprehensive data on the overall prevalence and diversity across all Corvidae species in various regions. More studies are needed to understand the ecological impacts of helminth infections on corvid populations and their role in ecosystems. The study of helminth parasites in Eurasian Corvidae is crucial for understanding host‒parasite dynamics, ecological interactions, and the health of avian populations. Continued research will help fill existing gaps and provide insights into the conservation of these species.
