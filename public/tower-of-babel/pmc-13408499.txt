Agathisflavone as a novel natural inhibitor of ToLCNDV suppressor proteins: insights from computational analyses and in planta validation
Authors: Mehulee Sarkar, Firoz Mondal, Anik Majumdar, Yeluru Mohan Babu, Dharmappa D. Chavan, Aniket A, Dipsikha Mondal, Parimal Sinha, Supradip Saha, Girish Kumar Jha, Mahender Singh, Kajal Kumar Biswas, Sneha Murmu, Anirban Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV) is a major begomovirus in tomato, where suppressor proteins AV2, AC2, and AC4 counteract host RNA silencing and intensify pathogenicity. Current management largely depends on vector control and resistance breeding, both limited by environmental concerns and rapid viral evolution, underscoring the need for targeted antiviral agents. Fifteen plant-derived phytochemicals, previously reported to exhibit antiviral activity against human viruses and representing major phytochemical classes such as flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and phenolics, were screened against the suppressor proteins AV2, AC2, and AC4 encoded by the DNA-A genomic component of ToLCNDV. Based on molecular docking scores, the top-performing candidates comprised six compounds spanning these classes. Among them, agathisflavone consistently displayed the strongest binding affinities, with binding energies of − 8.7, − 9.0, and − 7.9 kcal/mol for AV2, AC2, and AC4, respectively, highlighting it as the most promising inhibitor. Molecular dynamics simulations showed stable protein–ligand interactions with low RMSD and RMSF values and favourable van der Waals and electrostatic contributions. An agathisflavone-enriched extract from cashew leaves was formulated for spray application. In planta silencing reversal assays conducted under agathisflavone-enriched formulation-treated conditions demonstrated strong inhibition of AV2 and AC2 suppressor functions and moderate inhibition of AC4, thereby restoring host RNA silencing. These findings establish agathisflavone as the most potent natural antiviral among the tested phytochemicals and provide a rational framework for developing sustainable antiviral strategies against begomoviruses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54474-4.

Introduction

Leaf curl disease of the tomato crop (Solanum lycopersicum) has emerged as a globally significant disease that causes severe economic losses to its production1. Viruses associated with this disease belong to the family Geminiviridae and are transmitted by whiteflies (Bemisia tabaci) in a persistent and circulative manner. In India, at least 14 distinct begomovirus species are associated with the leaf curl disease of the tomato crop2. Tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV, species: Begomovirus solanumdelhiense) is the most common and prolific among these viruses and has a broad host range across the entire country3. ToLCNDV-infected tomato plants show typical symptoms, such as vein clearing, puckering, blistering, curling (upward or downward), crinkling, reduced leaf size, stunted growth, reduced height, and fewer, smaller fruits4. It is the second most prevalent begomovirus after tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV), with no geographical limitations5.

ToLCNDV is a bipartite begomovirus with two genomic components: DNA-A and DNA-B6. DNA-A encodes six proteins through open reading frames (ORFs) arranged in both orientations. The virion-sense ORFs (AV1 and AV2) encode the coat protein and pre-coat protein, respectively, while the complementary-sense ORFs (AC1, AC2, AC3, and AC4) encode the replication initiator protein (Rep), transcriptional activator protein (TrAp), replication enhancer protein (REn), and a multifunctional protein from the AC4 ORF, which overlaps with AC1. The DNA-B component contains BV1 in the virion sense and BC1 in the complementary sense, which encodes a nuclear shuttle protein (NSP) and a movement protein (MP), respectively4. Among these encoded proteins, those encoded by AV2, AC2, and AC4 ORFs function as viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs) and pathogenicity determinants7–9. VSRs interfere with the host’s cellular silencing machinery and inhibit antiviral responses10. It may interfere with various steps of the RNA-silencing pathway, such as siRNA generation, RISC assembly, or siRNA function, allowing the virus to evade host immunity and establish infection. Hence, targeting VSRs is crucial for managing plant viruses as they play a central role in counteracting host immune responses. Developing artificial microRNAs targeting the genes responsible for viral pathogenicity, replication, and transmission presents a promising method to limit their multiplication and spread11. Exogenous application of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) and hairpin RNAi constructs targeting these symptom determinants of ToLCNDV has been shown to reduce viral titre and limit disease development8,12. However, the practical application of dsRNA faces several limitations, such as rapid degradation by environmental nucleases, inconsistent delivery, and short persistence, which restrict its field utility. Moreover, RNAi operates primarily at the transcript level, providing transient suppression rather than direct inhibition of viral protein function. These limitations highlight the need for alternative or complementary antiviral approaches capable of targeting the viral suppressor proteins directly and offering enhanced stability, specificity, and durability under field conditions.

Natural products, particularly phytochemicals, have long been recognized as valuable resources for drug discovery due to their diverse chemical structures and biological activity. Unlike synthetic antivirals, which often face challenges such as toxicity, resistance development, and high production costs, plant-derived compounds are generally safer, more accessible, and capable of interacting selectively with biological targets13,14. Several classes of secondary metabolites, namely alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenols, have demonstrated potent antiviral activity against a wide range of human viruses, including dengue virus, hepatitis B virus, and SARS-CoV-2, making them promising candidates for novel antiviral applications15–21. These findings suggest that phytochemicals can serve as broad-spectrum antiviral scaffolds, potentially applicable to plant viral systems through a cross-kingdom repurposing approach.

Despite increasing interest in phytochemicals as antiviral agents, their targeted application in the management of plant viral diseases remains underexplored. Though antiviral drug designing and their application are very common for viruses in medical science, only few reports have demonstrated the antiviral efficacy of natural products against plant viruses. For instance, essential oils derived from certain Lamiaceae species were found to reduce the accumulation of tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) and cucumber mosaic virus (CMV); secopregnane steroids isolated from Strobilanthes cusia exhibited antiviral activity against TMV; and pityriacitrin, a natural compound obtained from marine algae, inhibited potato virus X (PVX) replication22–24. More recently, coumarin and its derivatives, as well as rutaecarpine and its analogues have been identified as promising natural inhibitors of TMV25,26. In the context of begomoviruses, however, research has largely been confined to in silico evaluations of natural compounds targeting structural or replication-associated proteins such as Rep and coat proteins27–30. Recently, we have developed an enriched formulation of agathisflavone, a biflavonoid derived from Anacardium occidentale, that effectively inhibits begomovirus infection in planta, likely through targeted suppression of the viral Rep protein31. Comparatively little attention has been directed toward viral suppressor proteins that are pivotal in modulating host RNA silencing and immune responses. Moreover, most existing investigations have primarily focused on flavonoid scaffolds, with limited exploration of other major phytochemical classes, such as alkaloids, terpenoids, and phenolics, that may exhibit distinct antiviral mechanisms. Importantly, previous docking-based studies seldom incorporated molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, a critical step for evaluating the temporal stability and reliability of protein–ligand interactions, which is indispensable for translating computational predictions into biologically meaningful insights. Furthermore, the majority of these computational studies have not been substantiated by in planta validation, leaving a significant gap between virtual screening outcomes and practical antiviral applicability. In view of the above rationale, we hypothesize that phytochemicals with established antiviral activity in mammalian systems can disrupt the suppressor function of ToLCNDV proteins by directly binding to their active or interaction sites, thereby restoring host antiviral silencing. Preference was given to plant-derived antiviral compounds with known low phytotoxicity so that the identified inhibitors could potentially be developed into safe antiviral formulations for field application. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to identify phytochemicals targeting viral suppressor proteins through computational approaches and to functionally validate the inhibition of suppressor activity using a GUS-hairpin based in planta assay. Consistent with this objective, the integrated analysis identified agathisflavone, a biflavonoid from Anacardium occidentale, as a promising inhibitor, that exhibited stable interactions with ToLCNDV VSRs and effectively suppressed their activity in the GUS-hairpin-based in planta assay. These findings support the development of safe, phytochemical-based antivirals and indicate potential broader applicability across plant viruses due to the conserved nature of silencing suppressor proteins.

Materials and methods

Physicochemical characterization of suppressor proteins

The amino acid sequence of AV2/AC2/AC4 of ToLCNDV was deduced from the nucleotide sequence available in the NCBI database (accession number: AJ875157) using the Expasy Translate Tool (https://web.expasy.org/translate/). The physicochemical properties of the three proteins were assessed using Expasy’s ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) (Date accessed 29-4-26).

Construction of 3D protein models and their validation

3D models of AV2/AC2/AC4 proteins were predicted using the Robetta server (https://robetta.bakerlab.org/)32. The server employs a completely automated procedure for predicting protein structures, generating a model for an entire protein sequence regardless of whether there is sequence homology to known protein structures. Robetta analyses input sequences by dividing them into domains, constructing models for domains that have sequence homology to known protein structures through comparative modelling, and for domains that lack such homology, it uses the Robetta ab initio structure prediction approach. The results include predictions of the domains and molecular coordinates of the models covering the entire query length. Among the predicted structures, the best model for each protein was selected based on the lowest energy conformation and the highest model confidence score provided by Robetta. Further, the best model generated was refined using the Galaxy web server (https://galaxy.seoklab.org/)33 to improve structural accuracy and stability. The refined models were validated for their stereochemical quality using PROCHECK34, Verify-3D35, and ERRAT36 modules integrated on the SAVES server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/). Further validation was done using ProQ (https://proq.bioinfo.se/ProQ/ProQ.html) and ProSA-Web (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php)37. The 3D models were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio)38. The active sites of the proteins were identified using the FTSite server (https://ftsite.bu.edu/)39.

Ligand selection, retrieval, and preparation

The phytochemical ligands selected for screening against the suppressor proteins of ToLCNDV were carefully curated based on existing literature evidencing their antiviral efficacy against a range of human pathogenic viruses, such as dengue virus40,41, SARS-CoV42, hepatitis viruses43,44, influenza viruses19, etc., thereby supporting their potential as broad-spectrum antiviral agents. The selection criteria further included plant origin, low phytotoxicity, and structural diversity, ensuring representation of major phytochemical classes. Fifteen phytochemicals were thus selected, including five alkaloids, five flavonoids, three triterpenes, and two phenols (Table 1). The three-dimensional (3D) structures of the ligands were retrieved in simple data format (SDF) from the PubChem server (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)45. They were prepared for docking by adding hydrogens, performing energy minimization, and converting them to the '.pdbqt’ format using the PyRx software package with Autodock Vina (https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/)30.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking studies were conducted using PyRx software package (https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/) (Python Prescription 0.8)30. This tool integrates open-source software, such as AutoDock, AutoDock Vina, and Open Babel, to screen multiple ligands against target proteins61. The selected protein and ligand files were fed as macromolecules and ligands, respectively, to PyRx, and energy minimization was performed using the Universal Force Field (UFF). The chemical compounds and protein structures were exported in '.pdbqt’ format using the Open Babel tool in PyRx. The active binding-site grid box was created by selecting the forward option in PyRx, and the dimensions and coordinates of the grid box were adjusted by modifying its boundary. After docking, the software generated the binding energies for various conformers, which were then saved in a .csv file. The top three ligands of each protein with the lowest binding energies were chosen for further simulations.

Visualization of protein-ligand interaction

The top-scoring docked poses of the drug-like phytoconstituents with the protein model were visualized using Biovia Discovery Studio 2020 (https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download)38. This software was employed to generate 2D and 3D plots illustrating various interactions, such as hydrophobic, hydrogen, alkyl, and electrostatic bonds, between the top three phytoconstituents and their respective protein models.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) is a computational simulation technique used to investigate the dynamic behaviour of biological molecules such as proteins by examining the physical movements of their constituent atoms and molecules. During the simulation, these atoms and molecules interacted over time, providing an understanding of the system dynamics. It mimics the structural changes of biological molecules over a given period, providing atomic-level insights into structural changes62,63. Each protein–ligand complex was subjected to a 100 ns simulation, a duration sufficient to capture equilibrium conformational behaviour of mid-sized protein–ligand systems. The top three ranked compounds in complex with each of the viral suppressor proteins, AV2/AC2/AC4, were employed for MD simulation studies to further assess their interaction in detail. The analysis was performed using GROMACS software package-2021 (https://manual.gromacs.org/)45,64. Ligand topology was constructed using the SwissParam server (https://old.swissparam.ch/)65. The CHARMM36 (Chemistry at Harvard Macromolecular Mechanics) force field was used to create the topology of the protein. All complexes were solvated in a cubic box of TIP3P water models maintaining a 1 nm distance between the protein and the edge of the box. The box dimension along with the number of solvent molecules for each of the system are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The complexes were maintained at a salt concentration of 0.15 M by adding appropriate Na+ and Cl− ions. The steepest descent method was employed for energy minimization with a threshold of 1000 kJ/mol/nm and 500,000 minimization steps, followed by the conjugate gradient method to alleviate conflicting interactions. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) approach was applied to compute long-range interactions66. The system underwent two equilibration in five stages: the initial stage involved NVT ensemble equilibration for 50,000 iterations, with each iteration lasting 2 femtoseconds (fs) to stabilize the temperature (where N represents the constant number of particles, V is the volume, and T refers to the temperature); in the subsequent phase, the pressure was equilibrated at 300 K using the NPT ensemble, employing a V-rescale for temperature regulation and Parrinello-Rahman for pressure maintenance. The equations of motion were integrated using the leap-frog algorithm with a time step of 2 fs. After ensuring that the system met the desired temperature and pressure, the resulting complex was subjected to a production run of 100 ns to observe its motion characteristics.

Trajectory analysis

After performing the MD simulations, the resulting trajectories were analyzed using in-built tools of the GROMACS software package-2021 to compute various parameters, including root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and inter-molecular hydrogen bonds (H-Bond). The RMSD for the protein backbone was calculated using the RMS module, whereas the RMSF for the atomic positions of the Cα backbone of the protein was assessed using the RMSF module. Additional modules such as RG, SASA, and H-Bond were employed to determine RG, SASA, and the number of hydrogen bonds, respectively. Trajectory frames were saved every 10 ps throughout the 100 ns simulation to ensure adequate temporal resolution for RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, and hydrogen bond analyses.

MM-PBSA Analysis

To assess the binding free energy of the docked complexes from 100 ns GROMACS MD trajectories, the Molecular Mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MMPBSA) approach was employed using the gmx_MMPBSA tool67. The binding free energy was calculated using the MM-PBSA approach based on the last 10 ns of the trajectory, with snapshots extracted every 10 ps, resulting in 1000 frames for analyses. The MMPBSA methodology incorporates non-bonded interactions, such as electrostatic energy, polar solvation energy, and van der Waals energy, to evaluate the binding energy (kcal/mol) and relative binding affinity. Extraction and enrichment of agathisflavone for formulation development

Based on the screening results identifying agathisflavone as the most effective compound, cashew (Anacardium occidentale) leaves, a rich natural source of this biflavonoid, were selected for the preparation of an agathisflavone-enriched extract. Leaves were collected from a plantation situated at the Soil Conservation Research Station in Abash, Midnapore, Paschim Medinipur district. 1.2 kg of leaves were air-dried at room temperature for approximately two weeks. The agathisflavone (AGT) extraction procedure followed the method described by68, with slight modifications. Briefly, air-dried cashew leaves were chopped into small pieces, and extraction was carried out using 80% methanol (10 L) by homogenization at room temperature. The resulting crude extract was vacuum-filtered and concentrated to half its original volume using a rotary evaporator (at 40–45 °C). The concentrated extract was subsequently partitioned with ethyl acetate in a separating funnel to obtain the solvent fraction. This was further concentrated using a rotary evaporator (at < 45 °C). A stock (15,000 ppm) agathisflavone-enriched formulation (AGT-F) was developed in 2.5% Triton X-405 and stored at 4 °C. In an earlier experiment, the presence of agathisflavone in the formulation was confirmed by LCMS analysis31.

HPLC profiling

To estimate the relative amount of AGT in crude (methanol extract) and enriched extract (ethyl acetate fraction), chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters Alliance HPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a SupelcoAscentis® C18 analytical column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size; Sigma-Aldrich/MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). The mobile phase consisted of two solvent systems: mobile phase A, comprising ultrapure water containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v), and mobile phase B, comprising acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v). Gradient elution was carried out at a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and column temperature maintained at 25 °C, according to the following program: 0–28 min, 90% A/10% B (isocratic); 28–30 min, linear gradient to 10% A/90% B; held at 10% A/90% B until 30 min. The total run time was 30 min. Sample injection volume was 10 µL. Detection and quantification of AGT were carried out at a wavelength of 336 nm, selected based on the characteristic UV absorption maximum of the target biflavonoid. All chromatographic data were acquired and processed using the Waters Empower software system.

In planta suppressor activity assay

Four-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana plants grown under greenhouse conditions were used for the in planta suppressor activity assay. Fully expanded, non-virus-infected leaves were selected for the experiment. For in planta assays, the stock solution of 15,000 ppm AGT-F was diluted to obtain a final working concentration of 1500 ppm31. After application of the AGT-F, leaves were monitored for any visible phytotoxic symptoms, such as chlorosis, necrosis, or tissue damage. The experimental design consisted of treated and untreated sets for each suppressor protein (AV2, AC2, and AC4). Each treatment included three biological replicates, with three leaves per plant and three technical replicates for image-based quantification. The suppressor activity assay was carried out as standardised in our laboratory9.

For each assay, the agroconstruct expressing GUS alone was infiltrated into the left portion of the leaves, while the agroconstructs of GUS and GUS-hp were infiltrated into the right portion, and the agroconstructs of GUS, GUS-hp and the suppressor gene (AC2/AC4/AV2) were infiltrated into the top portion of both AGT-F-treated and control sets of leaves. The agroconstructs were allowed to express transiently, and the infiltrated leaves from different plants were detached at 3 days post-infiltration and placed in GUS staining buffer (0.1 mM Sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.0, 10 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.1% Triton-X, 2 mM X-Gluc) individually and incubated at 37 °C for overnight with gentle shaking69. The buffer was then replaced with 70% ethanol and incubated at 37 °C for 1–2 h, followed by incubation at 37 °C in 95% ethanol in a slow shaker to remove chlorophyll. For each construct, three biological replicates were taken.

Image analysis for quantitative estimation of suppressor activity

The qualitative expression of suppressor activity was visually monitored through the revival of GUS expression (blue colour) in the suppressor-infiltrated portion. RGB images of GUS expression in all the leaves were captured using a Digital Camera (Nikon, 15 MegaPixel). The camera was fixed on a tripod stand with a height of 25 cm, after adjusting the shutter speed. Images were taken in a dark room with a uniform halogen bulb of 60 watts (4- bulb directed to the inverted plates from a diagonal arrangement). Image-based quantitative assessment of suppressor activity was measured through GUS expression using a previously standardized method9. Briefly, the proportion of blue colour on leaves measuring the area (pixel2) under blue color on leaves through image analysis software- Assess 2.0 (Lamari 2008). For assessment of GUS expression blue areas were separated through threshold limits and areas under dark shades were estimated (89.5% accuracy). Statistical analysis was conducted using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test (p ≤ 0.05) for pairwise comparison of GUS expression intensity under AGT-F treated and untreated conditions for various GUS constructs (GUS + GUS-hp + AV2, GUS + GUS-hp + AC2, GUS + GUS-hp + AC4, GUS and GUS + GUS-hp).

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties of suppressors of RNA silencing of ToLCNDV

Various physical and chemical properties of the three suppressor proteins deduced through Expasy’s ProtParam are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. The molecular weights of the AV2, AC2, and AC4 proteins are 12.9 kDa, 15.7 kDa, and 6.6 kDa, respectively. Among the three proteins, AC4 had the highest isoelectric point at 10.02, followed by AC2 at 9.59 and AV2 at 7.73. Furthermore, the AC4 protein was comparatively more positively charged. All proteins had an estimated half-life of 30 h. The aliphatic index values of AV2, AC2, and AC4 were calculated to be 74.82, 53.31, and 48.79, respectively, indicating their relative thermostability and the proportion of aliphatic amino acids in their sequences. The grand average hydrophobicity (GRAVY) values of the three proteins were calculated as -0.718, -0.941, and − 0.909, respectively, indicating that all three proteins are predominantly hydrophilic, with lower values suggesting a higher affinity for aqueous environments.

The predominance of basic isoelectric points and the hydrophilic character of AV2, AC2, and AC4 supports their functional role as RNA silencing suppressors, as RNA-binding proteins, including viral suppressors, commonly utilize positively charged residues to facilitate electrostatic interactions with negatively charged RNA molecules and associated host factors70,71.

3D model construction and structure validation

In our study, 3D models of AV2, AC2, and AC4 proteins were constructed. The predicted structures of AV2, comprising 112 amino acids, AC2, comprising 139 amino acids, and AC4, comprising 58 amino acids, are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1A–C(i). Structural analysis of the proteins AV2, AC2, and AC4 revealed distinct conformational characteristics. AV2 exhibited six alpha-helical regions interspersed with eight coiled regions. The AC2 protein showed greater structural complexity, with seven alpha helices, two beta sheets, and 11 coiled segments. AC4 displayed a minimal configuration, with its structure limited to a single alpha helix and two coiled regions. The validity of the predicted structures was confirmed through structural assessments and further functional analyses. Analysis of the Ramachandran plot of AV2 revealed that 96% of the residues resided within the most favoured regions and 0% resided in the disallowed regions. In the case of the AC2 protein, 85.5% were in the favourable region and 0% in the disallowed region. For AC4, 96.2% and 0% were in the favourable and disallowed regions, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C(ii)). All predicted structures exhibited PROCHECK G-factor values within the optimal range of − 0.4 to 0.5, suggesting their superior quality. The reliability of the models was determined using the ERRAT and PROSA Z-score metrics, with negative values indicating reliability37. The VERIFY 3D server provided additional validation, confirming model quality by showing that most residues scored above 0.1. The WHATCHECK results further confirm the accuracy of the models. Additionally, ProQ utilizes MaxSub and LGscore to assess the accuracy of the protein model, with an LGscore above 4.0, indicating a highly precise model72. Collectively, these indicate that the predicted structures are of reliable stereochemical quality, consistent with established criteria for validating homology models and assessing their suitability for downstream structural and functional analyses37,73. The details of the quality assessment are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

The analyses indicated that AV2 has three active pockets, and the details of the residues have been presented in Supplementary Fig. S1A–C(iii). Pocket 2 (green) includes Trp2, His14, Cys18, Val48, Leu49, Arg50, Ala51, Lys52, Tyr54, and Ala57. Pocket 3 (purple) is characterized by Trp2, His14, Gly15, and Cys18. Similarly, for the AC2 protein, pocket 1(red) was defined as His53, Arg54, Gly55, His57, His58, Arg66, Tyr68, Leu69, Gly70, Lys73, Ser74, Pro75, and Thr99. Pocket 2 (green) consisted of Arg30, Val32, Val42, His57, Ala91, Arg92, His93, Asn94, Gln95, Ser97, and Asn98. Pockets 3 (purple) included Val42, Ser43, Ile44, Asn45, Cys46, His47, Phe51, His53, Ile56, Ser97, Asn98, and Gln101. In AC4, the residues in pocket 1 (red) include Arg4, Met7, Ser9, Thr25, Trp26, Phe27, Pro28, Gln29, Val30, Gly31, Gln32, His33, Ile34, Ser35, Ile36, and Phe39. Pocket 2 (green) comprises Ser24, Thr25, Trp26, Asn43, Arg46, Met47, and Asn50 residues. Pocket 3 (purple) is defined by Ser23, Ser24, Thr25, Trp26, Phe27, and Gln29.

To assess the effectiveness of molecular interactions between 15 initially selected ligands (Supplementary Fig. S2) and the protein targets, molecular docking analyses was conducted. Ligands with better binding affinity, indicated by lower docking energy, are generally considered desirable drug molecules. In the present study, the top three ligands with the least binding energies were selected for each target protein and used for further analysis. Interestingly, among all the ligands agathisflavone exhibited the highest binding affinity with all three target proteins viz. − 8.7 kcal/mol, − 9.0 kcal/mol, and − 7.9 kcal/mol for AV2, AC2, and AC4, respectively. Other selected ligands were ursolic acid (− 8.3 kcal/mol) and sanguinarium (− 8.2 kcal/mol) for AV2, cudranian 2 (− 8.1 kcal/mol) and sanguinarium (− 8.0 kcal/mol) for AC2, and robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin (− 7.7 kcal/mol) and betulinic acid (− 7.6 kcal/mol) for AC4 (Supplementary Table S4).

MD simulation analysis

The structural stability of the top three docked complexes, namely agathisflavone (PubChem id 5281599), ursolic acid (PubChem CID: 64945), and sanguinarium (PubChem CID: 5154) for AV2; agathisflavone (PubChem id 5281599), cudranian 2 (PubChem CID: 70697747), and sanguinarium (PubChem CID: 5154) for AC2; and agathisflavone (PubChem id 5281599), robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin (PubChem CID: 70697956), and betulinic acid (PubChem CID: 64971) for AC4 protein were evaluated by subjecting them to 100 ns MD simulations. Upon successful completion of the simulations, the obtained trajectories were analyzed to determine RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, and hydrogen bonds as measures of stability for the simulated systems.

Root-mean-square deviation

The RMSD of the unbound AV2 protein (black) fluctuated around an average of 1.272 nm, while in the presence of agathisflavone (red), the RMSD remained steady and consistent throughout the simulation, with an average value of 0.965 nm (Fig. 4a). The average RMSD values for the AV2 complexes with sanguinarine (green) and ursolic acid (blue) were slightly higher at 1.092 nm and 1.126 nm, respectively, indicating relatively weaker binding interactions compared to agathisflavone.

Fig. 4Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of AV2 receptor and its complexes (a, b) AC2 receptor and its complexes and (c, d) AC4 receptor and its complexes (e, f).

Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of AV2 receptor and its complexes (a, b) AC2 receptor and its complexes and (c, d) AC4 receptor and its complexes (e, f).

In case of AC2, significant fluctuations were observed during the initial 10 ns of the simulation (Fig. 4c). However, after 50 ns, the RMSD values stabilized. The AC2-agathisflavone complex (red) eventually stabilized, but its RMSD values were in a higher range compared to AV2 and AC4 complexes, reflecting variations or conformational flexibility within the AC2 structure.

In case of AC4 protein, the RMSD of the unbound protein (black) averaged 1.137 nm (Fig. 4e). In contrast, the AC4 complex with agathisflavone (red) exhibited the lowest average RMSD of 0.945 nm, suggesting a highly stable interaction. The RMSD values for the complexes with betulinic acid (purple) and robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin (pink) were higher at 1.132 nm and 1.226 nm, respectively, indicating less stable interactions compared to agathisflavone.

The RMSD analysis provides significant insights into the stability and interaction dynamics of the AV2, AC2, and AC4 proteins in the presence and absence of their respective ligands. Across all three proteins, agathisflavone consistently exhibited the lowest RMSD values, indicating its strong and stable binding affinity. The relatively higher RMSD observed for the unbound AV2 protein may be attributed to its small size and the presence of flexible regions, which can lead to greater conformational fluctuations during simulation. Additionally, as the structure was derived from ab initio modelling, some degree of structural flexibility is expected. The steady RMSD profiles observed for the AV2-agathisflavone and AC4-agathisflavone complexes highlight the ability of agathisflavone to stabilize these proteins by maintaining consistent interactions throughout the simulation. In contrast, ligands such as sanguinarine (AV2), betulinic acid (AC4), and robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin (AC4) showed slightly higher RMSD values, suggesting moderately stable binding interactions. For the AC2–agathisflavone complex, relatively higher RMSD values were observed during the initial phase of the simulation, followed by stabilization at later stages. This suggests that the complex undergoes conformational adjustments before attaining equilibrium. Such behaviour indicates dynamic adaptation of the ligand within the binding pocket rather than immediate stabilization. This could reflect a less optimal binding pocket or dynamic behavior of AC2 relative to AV2 and AC4. The RMSD results support agathisflavone as the most stable ligand, showing consistent binding and stabilization across all three proteins, as RMSD is a widely accepted metric in which reduced fluctuations reflect conformational stability and reliable protein–ligand interactions during molecular dynamics simulations 80.

Root-mean-square fluctuation

The RMSF plots for AV2 alone and in complex with agathisflavone and sanguinarine largely overlapped, indicating minimal changes in flexibility due to ligand binding. The average RMSF values for AV2 alone (black), AV2-agathisflavone (red), AV2-sanguinarine (green) and AV2-ursolic acid (blue) were 0.363 nm, 0.428 nm, 0.486 nm, and 0.708 nm, respectively (Fig. 4b). These results suggest that agathisflavone caused only minor conformational changes, whereas ursolic acid induced greater flexibility, indicating weaker interaction stability.

For AC2, the average RMSF values for the unbound protein (black), AC2-agathisflavone (red), AC2-sanguinarine (green), and AC2-cudranian (yellow) were 0.477 nm, 0.534 nm, 0.595 nm, and 0.764 nm, respectively (Fig. 4d). The minimal increase in RMSF for the agathisflavone complex indicates relatively stable binding, whereas the higher RMSF for the cudranian complex reflects greater flexibility and less stable interactions.

Similarly, for AC4, the average RMSF values for the unbound protein (black), AC4-agathisflavone (red), AC4-robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin (pink), and AC4-betulinic acid (purple) were 0.511 nm, 0.578 nm, 0.483 nm, and 0.768 nm, respectively (Fig. 4f). These results highlight that AC4 complexes with agathisflavone and robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin exhibited lower flexibility and greater stability, while the betulinic acid complex showed higher RMSF values, reflecting reduced interaction stability.

RMSF analysis provides key insights into the flexibility of protein residues upon ligand binding, with lower RMSF values indicating strong and stable interactions. Among the studied ligands, agathisflavone consistently demonstrated the lowest RMSF values across AV2, AC2, and AC4 proteins, suggesting its superior ability to stabilize binding sites and minimize conformational fluctuations79. For AV2, both agathisflavone and sanguinarine caused minimal conformational changes, as reflected in their low RMSF values. In contrast, ursolic acid induced significant flexibility, reflecting weaker binding efficiency. In AC2, agathisflavone showed relatively stable interactions with modest RMSF increases compared to the unbound protein, while the cudranian complex exhibited higher RMSF values, indicating reduced stabilization. For AC4, robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin and agathisflavone complexes maintained low RMSF values, demonstrating stable interactions. However, betulinic acid displayed greater residue fluctuations, suggesting weaker interactions. These results corroborate RMSD findings, further validating agathisflavone’s potential as a robust ligand15. While robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin (AC4) and sanguinarine (AV2, AC2) also show moderate stabilizing potential, other ligands, such as ursolic acid, cudranian, and betulinic acid, exhibited reduced interaction efficiency.

The lower RMSF values observed for agathisflavone-bound complexes indicate reduced residue-level flexibility and enhanced local stability of the protein–ligand interactions, as RMSF is a reliable measure of atomic fluctuations where decreased mobility reflects stabilization of binding regions during molecular dynamics simulations80.

Radius of gyration and solvent-accessible surface area

The Rg reflects the compactness of a protein-ligand complex, while the SASA quantifies the exposure of the complex to solvent molecules. The lower Rg and SASA values observed for a ligand-bound complexes indicate enhanced structural compactness and reduced solvent exposure, which are commonly associated with stable protein–ligand interactions, as decrease in radius of gyration and solvent-accessible surface area reflect tighter packing and shielding of binding interfaces during molecular dynamics simulations80–82.

The Rg and SASA values for the AV2, AC2, and AC4 proteins were analyzed over a 100 ns simulation period (Fig. 5). For the AV2 protein, the average Rg values during 30–100 ns ranged from 1.563 to 1.937 nm. The complex with sanguinarine (green) exhibited the lowest Rg value of 1.563 nm, indicating a highly compact structure. This was followed by agathisflavone (red) at 1.644 nm and ursolic acid (blue) at 1.937 nm (Fig. 5a-i). The Rg of the unbound AV2 protein (black) was 1.55 nm, similar to the sanguinarine complex, reflecting its natural compactness. For SASA, the average values ranged between 87.197 and 96.389 nm2 (Fig. 5a-ii). The complex with sanguinarine (green) showed the lowest SASA value of 87.197 nm2, followed by agathisflavone (red) at 93.438 nm2 and ursolic acid (blue) at 96.389 nm2. The unbound AV2 protein (black) had a SASA value of 87.851 nm2. These results suggest that sanguinarine and agathisflavone maintain compact and solvent-shielded AV2 complexes.

The compact structures suggest strong protein-ligand interactions that reduce the flexibility and solvent exposure of the binding sites. For AV2, sanguinarine demonstrated the lowest Rg and SASA values, indicating it forms the most compact and stable complex. Agathisflavone showed similar behaviour, reinforcing its versatility as a stable ligand across multiple proteins. Betulinic acid exhibited the highest Rg and SASA values, suggesting weaker binding efficiency. The Rg and SASA results highlight the potential of agathisflavone and sanguinarine as effective ligands for AV2 and AC2, while robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin and agathisflavone are promising for AC4.

Hydrogen bond analysis

The time-resolved intermolecular hydrogen bond profiles of AV2, AC2, and AC4 suppressor proteins and their respective ligands were examined over a 100 ns MD simulation to assess the stability and persistence of interactions (Fig. 6). For the AV2 complexes (Fig. 6a), the number of hydrogen bonds remained relatively low throughout the simulation, with frequent transitions between bonded and non-bonded states. The ursolic acid (blue) complex exhibited the highest average hydrogen bond count (0.441), but these interactions were largely intermittent, indicating that hydrogen bonding contributes minimally to overall stability. The agathisflavone (red) and sanguinarine (green) complexes displayed similarly transient behaviour, suggesting that their binding is likely dominated by hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions rather than persistent hydrogen bonding.

For the AC4 complexes (Fig. 6c), hydrogen bonding was comparatively more stable and persistent throughout the simulation. Hydrogen bonding plays a varying role across the three suppressor proteins. In AV2, interactions are predominantly transient and likely supplemented by non-polar forces. AC2 exhibits dynamic but fluctuating hydrogen bonding, indicative of flexible binding. In contrast, AC4 demonstrates stable and persistent hydrogen bond networks, highlighting a stronger contribution of polar interactions to ligand stabilization. Agathisflavone consistently exhibits balanced interaction profiles, leveraging both hydrogen bonding and non-bonding interactions, making it a strong candidate for further experimental studies. These observations are consistent with the general understanding that protein–ligand stability is governed by a balance of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions rather than hydrogen bond number alone83.

The MM-PBSA results provide critical insights into the binding affinity and stability of the ligand-protein complexes. Across all three proteins, agathisflavone consistently demonstrated the lowest binding free energy, indicating the strongest binding affinity and most stable interactions. This stability is primarily driven by favourable van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, which play a key role in maintaining the protein-ligand complexes. The solvation energy, although partially offsetting these contributions, remained within a manageable range for agathisflavone, further confirming its effectiveness as a ligand. For AV2, the agathisflavone complex displayed the strongest binding affinity, with the lowest binding energy (− 33.85 kcal/mol). This stability was further supported by the low solvation energy and dominant van der Waals contributions. Sanguinarine and ursolic acid exhibited weaker binding, as reflected in their higher binding energies and less favourable interaction profiles. Similarly, for AC2, the agathisflavone complex outperformed cudranian and sanguinarine, with significantly lower binding free energy (− 36.09 kcal/mol). The strong electrostatic interactions observed for this complex highlight the crucial role of charge-based interactions in stabilizing the AC2-agathisflavone complex. The weaker binding observed for cudranian and sanguinarine suggests that these ligands have limited stabilizing effects on AC2. In the case of AC4, agathisflavone again exhibited the strongest binding affinity (− 23.77 kcal/mol), followed by betulinic acid and robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin. While betulinic acid and robinetinidol-(4alpha,8)-gallocatechin showed relatively similar binding energies, agathisflavone’s ability to maintain favorable van der Waals interactions and moderate solvation energy established it as the most stable ligand for AC4. These observations are consistent with the established principle that MM-PBSA-derived binding free energy integrates van der Waals, electrostatic, and solvation contributions to reliably estimate relative ligand affinity and stability in protein–ligand systems84. The results reinforce agathisflavone as the most effective ligand for all three proteins (AV2, AC2, and AC4). Its strong binding affinity, driven by favourable van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, makes it a promising candidate for inhibiting these viral suppressor proteins. Further experimental validation is warranted to confirm its efficacy in managing tomato leaf curl disease caused by begomoviruses.

The findings suggest that agathisflavone can interfere with the functional roles of AV2, AC2, and AC4. For instance, AC2 acts as a transcriptional activator and suppressor of post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS), a key antiviral defence in plants85. Disruption of AC2 by strong ligand binding may impair the virus’s ability to manipulate host gene expression. Similarly, AV2 has been implicated in facilitating cell-to-cell movement and silencing suppression86, while AC4 interacts with host defence pathways and contributes to viral symptom severity87. The disruption of these interactions by phytochemicals like agathisflavone may restore host defence mechanisms and reduce viral pathogenicity.

Interestingly, sanguinarine and robinetinidol-(4α,8)-gallocatechin also demonstrated promising interactions, particularly with AC2 and AC4, respectively. While their RMSD and RMSF profiles indicated moderate stability, their favourable hydrogen bonding and compactness (as seen from Rg and SASA values) suggest potential as adjunct inhibitors. These compounds could be further optimized for combination therapy or structure-based analog development88.

Ethyl acetate fractionation enhances agathisflavone content from cashew leaf extract

Cashew leaves were selected as a natural source of agathisflavone based on their reported antimicrobial properties and abundance68,89, along with the absence of documented viral infections in cashew plants90. Agathisflavone AGT was initially extracted from the leaves using methanol, followed by partitioning of the crude extract into ethyl acetate to obtain an enriched fraction. The efficiency of enrichment was evaluated by comparing chromatographic peak areas between the methanolic extract and the ethyl acetate-partitioned fraction, both analysed at a standardized concentration of 1000 ppm. The ethyl acetate fraction demonstrated a substantially elevated AGT content compared to the crude methanolic extract at equivalent concentration. The relative enrichment, calculated as the percentage increase in peak area following solvent fractionation, yielded an enrichment factor of 91.36%, corresponding to an approximate 1.91-fold increase in AGT concentration relative to the methanolic extract (Fig. 7). These findings indicate that ethyl acetate is an effective solvent system for the selective partitioning and concentration of AGT, consistent with its moderate polarity and known affinity for biflavonoid compounds. The observed enrichment confirms that solvent-guided fractionation represents a viable and efficient strategy for the downstream isolation and standardization of AGT from complex plant matrices.

The observed enrichment is consistent with established solvent-partitioning behaviour, wherein moderately polar flavonoids and phenolic compounds preferentially partition into ethyl acetate fractions during liquid–liquid extraction, resulting in higher concentrations relative to crude methanolic extracts. Such selective enrichment of phenolics and flavonoids in ethyl acetate fractions following fractionation has been widely reported in phytochemical studies91–93.

In planta assay of agathisflavone treatment resulted in inhibition of the suppressor activity of AV2, AC2 and AC4 proteins

The formulation of agathisflavone-enriched extract (AGT-F) was used for the in planta suppressor bioassay to evaluate its inhibitory potential against the suppressor proteins of ToLCNDV. Since plant-derived phytochemicals often act synergistically in enriched fractions rather than as single purified compounds, biological activity was evaluated using the AGT-F instead of a purified compound94.

In the present study, application of the AGT-F into N. benthamiana leaves did not produce any visible phytotoxic symptoms such as chlorosis, necrosis, or tissue damage, indicating that the formulation is non-phytotoxic under the experimental conditions used. A conceptual schematic representation illustrating the principle of the GUS and GUS-hairpin based suppressor activity assay and inhibition of suppressor activity by the phytochemical is shown in Fig. 8a–c. In this system, GUS gene silencing is induced by co-infiltration of a GUS-hairpin construct, which triggers RNA silencing and results in reduced GUS expression. However, when viral suppressor proteins are co-expressed along with the GUS-hairpin construct, they inhibit RNA silencing and restore GUS expression, leading to strong GUS staining. It was hypothesized that if AGT-F inhibits the activity of the viral suppressor protein, RNA silencing will be restored, resulting in a reduction of GUS expression under AGT-F-treated conditions. Under control conditions (leaves treated with Triton-X 405), GUS expression was found to be inhibited when co-inoculated with GUS-hairpin construct. Co-inoculation of AV2/AC2/AC4 overexpression constructs under GUS-hp silenced condition resulted in revival of GUS expression as evidenced through formation of blue colour. In contrast, when AGT-F was sprayed before construct inoculation, there was a significant (p < 0.001) decrease in the revival of GUS expression in all three suppressor inoculated leaves (Fig. 8d and e and Fig. 9). Among the three suppressor constructs, AGT-F application had shown significant interaction (p < 0.001) with the AV2 and AC2 constructs, where GUS expression showed a large drop as compared to suppressor AC4 (Table 3). Based on Tukey HSD (pairwise comparison test) pattern of GUS expression in various sample constructs was noted to be dependent on the presence of AGT. It was evident that AGT is an effective antidote against the viral suppressor proteins.

RNA silencing constitutes a primary antiviral defence mechanism in plants, and plant viruses have evolved viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs) to counteract this host defence system95,96. The objective of this study was to perform structure-based screening of phytochemicals targeting viral suppressor proteins, followed by preliminary experimental validation through in planta suppressor activity assays. Such integrative computational and experimental approaches are widely used as an initial step in antiviral compound discovery before detailed virological and field-level evaluations. Very recently, the inhibition of plant viruses by targeting different viral proteins through phytochemicals has been initiated. A phytochemical, phenothiazine derivative A8, was found to interact directly with TMV CP and restrain viral spread97. A recent study showed that 7-hydroxylathyrol inhibits the C4 suppressor protein, resulting in enhanced plant defence responses98. In comparison, AGT exhibited a greater number of hydrogen bond interactions with AC4 compared to 7-hydroxylathyrol, suggesting stronger and more stable binding. In silico screening of plant secondary metabolites targeting the βC1 suppressor protein encoded by begomovirus-associated betasatellites has demonstrated the potential of phytochemicals for managing begomovirus diseases99. In that study, anonaine exhibited a minimum binding energy of − 6.0 kcal/mol, which is less favorable compared to the stronger binding affinities observed for AGT in the present study.

Phytochemicals are known to inhibit plant viruses through multiple mechanisms, including direct binding to viral proteins and indirect activation of host defence pathways such as RNA silencing, salicylic acid signalling, and systemic acquired resistance. In the present study, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations suggest that AGT may inhibit viral suppressor proteins through direct protein–ligand interactions. However, indirect host defence activation cannot be ruled out. In our earlier study, AGT-F was shown to improve seedling vigour and mitigate oxidative stress due to its antioxidant activity31, suggesting that it may also enhance plant defence responses indirectly. Further studies will be required to determine the exact mechanism of antiviral activity.

It should be noted that transient in planta suppressor assays represent preliminary functional validation and may not fully reflect long-term antiviral efficacy under natural infection conditions. However, in our earlier study, foliar and seed treatment with AGT-F reduced ToLCNDV accumulation, decreased disease severity, and delayed symptom development under glasshouse conditions31, providing additional experimental evidence supporting its antiviral potential. Nevertheless, long-term field evaluation and large-scale validation studies will be required before practical application.

The present work strengthens the recent trend of targeting different viral proteins through antiviral phytochemicals for developing a green strategy for the management of plant viruses. The integrated computational and experimental pipeline used in this study can be extended to other plant viruses possessing RNA silencing suppressor proteins, thereby providing a broader framework for antiviral compound discovery and crop protection strategies.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the antiviral potential of natural phytochemicals by specifically targeting the suppressor proteins of ToLCNDV, a major pathogen that causes significant economic losses in tomato cultivation worldwide. Unlike previous research, which has largely focused on genetic resistance or vector control, this work uniquely employs a comprehensive computational strategy and identifies phytochemical inhibitors of the viral suppressors AV2, AC2, and AC4, the key proteins involved in viral pathogenesis and host immune suppression. Among the 15 phytochemicals previously reported to have antiviral activity against human viruses, AGT emerged as a promising candidate, exhibiting the lowest binding free energies and consistent stability across molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM-PBSA analysis. AGT exhibited strong van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, coupled with favourable solvation energy, which contributed to its robust binding affinity and protein stabilization. These findings were corroborated by RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA analyses, which further highlighted its ability to minimize structural fluctuations and maintain compact, solvent-shielded protein-ligand complexes. Further in planta suppressor activity assay confirmed the inhibitory action of AGT-F against the suppressor proteins of ToLCNDV. The ability of AGT to effectively bind and inhibit multiple suppressor proteins of ToLCNDV positions it as a strong candidate for further experimental validation and development as a natural antiviral agent. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive investigation targeting all three ToLCNDV suppressor proteins using phytochemicals previously reported to possess antiviral activity against human viruses, thereby establishing a new avenue for cross-domain antiviral drug repurposing and advancing the development of sustainable plant virus management strategies. Further studies involving virus infection assays, viral load quantification, and field evaluation will be required to validate the antiviral potential of the identified compounds under natural infection conditions.
