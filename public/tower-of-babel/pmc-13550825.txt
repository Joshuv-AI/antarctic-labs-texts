Health Information-Seeking Behaviors Among Patients with Autoimmune Rheumatic Diseases: A Qualitative Study Guided by the S-O-R Theory
Authors: Beisha Zhu, Lu Yang, Minjie Liu, Huan Jin, Yulan Xu, Shan Fang, Hui Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAutoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) require long-term self-management, making health information-seeking behavior (HISB) essential. Prior studies have largely relied on surveys that identified isolated correlates of HISB without clarifying the underlying mechanisms. This exploratory study used the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework to qualitatively investigate HISB among patients with ARDs, providing theory-informed process-oriented insights into information-seeking behavior in this population.MethodsWe conducted a descriptive qualitative interview study. Seventeen patients with ARDs were recruited using purposive sampling from a tertiary hospital in Wuhan, China. Data were collected through face-to-face, semi-structured, in-depth interviews between September and November 2025 and analyzed using directed content analysis guided by the S-O-R theory.ResultsThree main themes emerged: (1) Stimuli included disease-related events, digital platform information, and social support; (2) Organism comprised personal characteristics, disease cognition and information literacy, and emotional responses; and (3) Response yielded active seeking, passive acceptance, selective acceptance, and distress-driven avoidance. Beyond the conventional linear S-O-R pathway, participants described recurrent disease-related stimuli that appeared to repeatedly reactivate information-seeking processes across the illness trajectory.ConclusionThis study provides qualitative insights into HISB mechanisms among patients with ARDs. Findings suggest that HISB may involve recurrent stimulus-response patterns, which require validation through longitudinal research.

Introduction

Autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) impose a substantial and growing global health burden. A UK cohort study of 22 million individuals found that ARDs and related autoimmune conditions affect 6% to 8% of the population, with rising incidence and prevalence attributable to environmental exposures, improved diagnostics, and aging populations.1 Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), ankylosing spondylitis (AS), Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), dermatomyositis (DM), and systemic sclerosis (SSc) are major contributors to chronic disability, reduced quality of life, and premature mortality worldwide.2,3 ARDs also carry an increased burden of cardiovascular comorbidities: a population-based study of 22 million individuals linked autoimmune diseases to elevated cardiovascular risk,3 and increased subclinical atherosclerosis has been reported in patients with psoriatic arthritis.4 In China, the burden is becoming increasingly acute: the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 estimated RA incidence at 17.38 per 100,000 adults, and a nationwide study reported SLE incidence at 14.09 per 100,000 person-years in 2017.5,6 ARDs are characterized by immune dysregulation, chronic inflammation, progressive tissue damage, and unpredictable disease trajectories. The complexity and chronicity of these conditions require sustained patient engagement in self-care, treatment adherence, and shared decision-making—activities that all presuppose timely access to accurate, relevant health information.

Health information-seeking behavior (HISB) refers to the ways individuals actively or passively search for, access, and engage with health-related information.7 Research across patient populations has identified several determinants of HISB, including disease characteristics, sociodemographic factors, and psychological states.8–11 Prior studies have also mapped the information sources that patients use and the barriers they encounter when seeking information.12 These dynamics are increasingly shaped by the digital environment. In China in particular, short-video and lifestyle-oriented platforms have become central channels for health information, where algorithmic recommendation can rapidly expose patients to both useful guidance and unverified claims, raising distinct challenges for digital health literacy among people managing chronic conditions.13,14 This body of work has advanced understanding of what influences information seeking, but remains fragmented: individual studies have typically examined isolated factors without clarifying how external triggers, internal states, and behavioral outputs connect as a process. Whether and how external events activate cognitive and emotional mechanisms that subsequently shape information-seeking actions are not well understood.

Existing HISB research has identified numerous correlates of information-seeking behavior without adequately explaining the mechanisms linking them. Studies have identified what predicts information seeking, but not the pathway through which external triggers are processed internally to produce behavioral responses. This mechanistic gap is especially pronounced for ARD patients, whose information needs are shaped by disease complexity, fluctuating symptoms, and treatment uncertainty. The Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) theory, originally developed by Woodworth15 to describe how behavioral responses to external stimuli are mediated by the internal state of the organism, was later adapted by Mehrabian and Russell16 to explain how environmental cues influence behavior through internal emotional states.17 Applied to HISB, S-O-R theory posits that external triggers (stimuli) interact with patient-level cognitive and emotional factors (organism) to produce specific patterns of information engagement (responses). Compared with commonly used HISB frameworks, such as the Health Belief Model and Wilson’s model of information behavior, the S-O-R framework places greater emphasis on the cognitive and emotional processes through which external stimuli are translated into behavioral responses. By contrast, the Health Belief Model emphasizes individual perceptual factors as relatively static predictors of behavior, and Wilson’s model focuses on information needs and source use; neither explicitly models the mediational pathway through which external environmental stimuli are translated into behavioral responses via cognitive-emotional states. This feature makes it particularly suitable for examining the complex and emotionally laden information experiences of patients with ARDs. To our knowledge, qualitative applications of S-O-R in health information research remain limited; existing studies have predominantly relied on quantitative designs that test associations between predetermined variables. These approaches capture structural relationships but cannot reveal how the process unfolds from the patient’s perspective—which stimuli are most salient, which internal states mediate the response, and what behavioral patterns result—leaving interventions without a clear mechanistic target.

This study used a qualitative design to explore HISB among patients with ARDs through the lens of S-O-R theory. The research objectives were to identify (1) the external stimuli that trigger health information seeking, (2) the organismic factors that mediate these triggers, and (3) the resulting patterns of information-seeking behavior. We further explored whether participants’ accounts suggested that HISB in ARDs may follow a recurrent process rather than a one-time stimulus-response sequence. By examining these three elements and their interconnections as described by patients, the study yields a theoretically grounded, process-oriented understanding of how ARD patients navigate health information. Because effective information support in ARDs cannot be delivered by any single profession, this process-level understanding is essential for designing team-based, patient-centered interventions in which rheumatologists, specialized nurses, and mental health professionals each contribute to addressing patients’ informational and emotional needs across the illness trajectory.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative design. The Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) theory served as the theoretical framework to guide the research design and data analysis, with the aim of understanding health information-seeking behavior (HISB) among patients with autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) in depth. We followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ),18 and the completed 32-item COREQ checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Theoretical Framework

The S-O-R theory, as adapted by Mehrabian and Russell,16 was used to explain the interplay between individual behavior and the environment. This theory posits that external stimuli (S) influence behavioral responses (R) through an individual’s internal state (O). We operationalized the three dimensions a priori to guide both the interview guide and the coding framework. Stimuli included external triggers preceding information seeking (eg, disease-related events, platform-encountered content, and social-network prompts). Organismic factors were coded separately as cognitive factors (eg, disease knowledge, information literacy) and emotional responses (eg, optimism, distress). Responses comprised reported information-seeking actions (eg, active seeking, passive acceptance, selective acceptance, and avoidance). This framework guided the exploration of the external stimulus factors, internal mechanisms, and behavioral response patterns of HISB among patients with ARDs.

Participants

A total of 17 participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure diversity in gender, age, education level, occupation, household income, type and duration of ARD, and place of residence. The sample included patients with six ARD subtypes (RA, SLE, AS, SS, DM, and SSc) and disease durations ranging from 1 to 20 years. Disease activity and current treatment status were recorded and considered during sampling, so that participants in both flare and relatively stable phases were included, and these factors were attended to when interpreting stimulus-related accounts. Consistent with qualitative methodology, the sample size was determined by data saturation rather than statistical considerations, and recruitment continued until saturation was reached (see Data Collection). Although these conditions differ in clinical presentation and organ involvement, all require long-term self-management, involve substantial information needs, and necessitate ongoing navigation of information sources. We therefore considered that these conditions might share common information-seeking mechanisms while acknowledging potential disease-specific differences. All participants were hospitalized in the rheumatology and immunology department of a tertiary hospital in Wuhan, China, between September and November 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) diagnosed with an ARD and meeting established classification criteria for the respective condition; and (3) able to communicate effectively. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe physical illness or communication barriers precluding participation; and (2) a known history of mental illness or cognitive impairment. A total of 20 patients were approached; 2 declined participation because of time constraints, and 1 withdrew mid-interview because of physical fatigue related to their medical condition. Consequently, 17 participants completed the full interview process and were included in the final analysis.

Data Collection

The interviews were conducted by 2 female researchers (B.Z. and L.Y.) who hold master’s degrees in nursing and have extensive experience in clinical rheumatology care. All researchers received training in qualitative research methods. An interview guide was developed by the research team through literature review and group discussion. The draft guide was tested in 2 preliminary interviews and subsequently revised. The final version was reviewed and approved by 6 experts in nursing, communication studies, and ethics. The main questions included: (1) How do you view health information? What health information do you need to understand? (2) Through which channels and sources do you usually search for health information? (3) Under what circumstances do you actively seek health information? (4) Could you describe your most recent experience of actively seeking health information? (5) What factors motivate you to seek additional health information about your condition? (6) What challenges have you encountered when seeking health information? The sequence of questions was adjusted flexibly according to participants’ responses during the interviews.

No prior relationship existed between the interviewers and the participants before the study commenced. Face-to-face semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese, the participants’ native language, to ensure nuanced expression of illness experiences. For the purpose of international dissemination, representative quotations were later translated into English using a forward-and-backward translation protocol to ensure semantic equivalence, with discrepancies resolved by consensus with a senior bilingual researcher (see Data Analysis). Before each interview, participants were informed about the research purpose, methods, and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained. Because the interviewers were clinically trained nurses, we were attentive to the potential for a power imbalance that might encourage socially desirable responses; to mitigate this, interviewers introduced themselves in a researcher rather than a caregiving role, clarified that participation was independent of the participant’s clinical care, and explicitly invited candid and critical accounts, including negative experiences. Interviews were conducted in comfortable settings chosen by the participants (hospital rooms or education rooms within the department) to ensure a quiet environment. Only the participant and the interviewer were present during each interview. No nonparticipants were present, to ensure privacy and minimize external influence on participants’ responses. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio recorded. During the interviews, researchers maintained an attentive and nonjudgmental attitude, listened carefully, avoided interrupting participants, and used summarizing and questioning techniques to supplement and clarify information, while noting nonverbal cues. Each interview lasted between 20 and 53 minutes. Interview notes and reflexive journals were completed immediately after each interview. Data collection continued until meaning saturation was reached, operationalized as the point at which no new themes, subthemes, or conceptual relationships emerged across the final three consecutive interviews.

Data Analysis

Guided by the S-O-R theoretical framework, we employed a directed content analysis approach to examine the interview data.19 This deductive approach is consistent with recommendations for studies that aim to validate or extend existing conceptual frameworks. Initial coding categories were derived from the 3 core dimensions of the S-O-R model—stimuli, organism, and response—while remaining open to the identification of emergent themes through iterative comparison. The interview recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 hours and supplemented with field notes. Transcripts were returned to participants for verification to ensure data accuracy.

To preserve the original linguistic context and emotional nuances of the participants, the initial data analysis and coding were conducted entirely using the Chinese transcripts. Representative quotes selected for illustration were translated into English following the forward-and-backward translation protocol described in the Data Collection section.

Two researchers independently reviewed the transcripts multiple times to gain a holistic understanding of the content. Using NVivo (version 12; QSR International), we conducted the analysis through the following steps: (1) identifying statements related to HISB as the unit of analysis; (2) immersing in the data through repeated reading; (3) coding relevant content according to the predetermined S-O-R categories; (4) inductively developing subcategories within each main dimension; and (5) refining the connections between textual data and the resulting categories.

To ensure analytical rigor, we held regular team discussions to resolve discrepancies, conducted member checking with participants, maintained a detailed audit trail, and employed researcher triangulation to validate the coding process.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. 2024-1022; Date: December 4, 2024). All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. They were clearly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their medical care. To ensure confidentiality, all data were anonymized by replacing personal identifiers with coded labels. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. Study findings were reported in aggregate form to prevent identification of individual participants.

Trustworthiness

To ensure rigor, this study adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s framework through 4 criteria.20,21 (1) Credibility was strengthened through investigator triangulation, consensus-building discussions to resolve discrepancies, and member checking. Meaning saturation was confirmed when three consecutive interviews yielded no new themes, subthemes, or conceptual relationships. (2) Transferability was supported by purposive sampling to capture clinical and demographic diversity, coupled with thick contextual descriptions in the Results section. (3) Dependability was ensured through a digitally archived audit trail and standardized protocols for application of the S-O-R framework. (4) Confirmability was enhanced through reflexivity memos documenting researchers’ prior assumptions regarding patient information-seeking, weekly peer debriefings to challenge emerging interpretations, and ongoing reflexive discussions among the research team to minimize the influence of researchers’ disciplinary and professional backgrounds on data interpretation.

Themes and Subthemes

This study identified three main themes and ten subthemes based on the S-O-R theory. The themes were: Stimulus (S), encompassing disease-related stimuli, external information stimuli, and social support; Organism (O), which included personal characteristics, disease cognition, and Psychological-emotional responses; and Response (R), characterized by active seeking, passive acceptance, selective acceptance, and avoidance behavior. Theme 1: Stimulus (S)-External Triggers of Health Information Seeking

Participants with ARDs described a range of external stimuli that triggered their HISB. Direct disease-related events—such as initial diagnosis, symptom exacerbations, new symptoms, drug side effects, and changes in treatment—often created an immediate and urgent need to understand their condition or seek reassurance. For example, several participants reported searching online for explanations after experiencing unexpected symptoms, intensified pain, or medication changes.

Beyond their personal disease experiences, many patients emphasized the influence of external information encountered through digital channels. Information discovered on social media platforms (eg, Douyin, a short-video platform known internationally as TikTok, and Xiaohongshu, also known as Little Red Book, a lifestyle-oriented social media platform), health-specific online accounts, and patient forums frequently sparked curiosity about new therapies, prompted concerns, or led to extended searches, regardless of whether the content was authoritative or potentially misleading. Encounters with misinformation or unverified alternative remedies, while sometimes met with skepticism, still motivated further investigation and discussions with peers or healthcare professionals.

Social support systems also played a crucial role. Sharing experiences within patient groups, receiving recommendations from friends or family, and—particularly—suggestions from healthcare providers were all cited as important triggers for seeking additional information. For instance, some participants noted that a doctor’s advice to learn about a new treatment directly prompted them to conduct targeted searches.

Overall, the interplay of these external factors—personal disease experiences, exposure to information (both accurate and misleading), and social support—collectively served as catalysts for most patients to actively seek out health information. This finding highlights the complexity and multidimensionality of health information-seeking behavior in this population. Theme 2: Organism (O)-Internal Mediators of Health Information Seeking

The internal characteristics of patients—encompassing personal attributes, disease cognition, and emotional responses—played a pivotal role in shaping their HISB. Participants’ educational background, professional experience, and age influenced not only their propensity to seek information but also the depth and manner in which they engaged with it. For example, participants with higher educational attainment or occupations involving regular information work tended to conduct more targeted searches, consult more authoritative sources, or adopt a more critical approach to evaluating information. By contrast, older participants or those with limited educational attainment often preferred to rely on the guidance of healthcare professionals, expressing less interest in independent information seeking.

Disease cognition and information literacy further mediated these behaviors. Patients who had lived with their condition for many years generally developed a stronger ability to distinguish between reliable and exaggerated information, enabling them to conduct more purposeful and effective searches. Conversely, those with limited understanding of their disease tended to rely heavily on physicians’ instructions, sometimes expressing confusion or passivity regarding additional information beyond direct medical advice. Notably, the process of learning to critically evaluate health information was often gradual; some participants recounted initial periods of believing unverified claims or attempting unconventional remedies but, over time, learned to filter out misinformation and focus on evidence-based resources.

Psychological and emotional responses also exerted a significant influence. Optimism and hope—often bolstered by exposure to positive stories or new therapies—inspired more active and persistent information seeking. Patients who felt encouraged by others’ recovery experiences reported greater motivation to explore treatment options or new research. Conversely, negative emotions such as distress, anxiety, or shock, particularly following diagnosis, sometimes led to the avoidance of health information altogether. Several patients described deliberately limiting their exposure to disease-related content to protect their emotional well-being, especially during periods of psychological vulnerability.

Together, these organismic factors—personal characteristics, disease cognition, and emotional state—not only mediated patients’ responses to external stimuli but also determined the strategies and extent of their health information engagement. The diversity of these internal motivators underscores the need for tailored support that addresses individual differences in knowledge, skills, and emotional readiness. Theme 3: Response (R)-Behavioral Responses to Health Information Seeking

HISB among patients with ARDs manifested in diverse and dynamic response patterns. Some participants demonstrated active seeking, characterized by proactively utilizing a variety of resources—including online searches, patient groups, and direct consultations with healthcare professionals to obtain the latest information on their condition. These patients often described information as empowering, viewing knowledge as essential to disease management and self-efficacy.

In contrast, others adopted a more passive acceptance approach, relying primarily on information provided by doctors, family members, or content passively encountered through digital platforms. For these individuals, health information seeking was less intentional, often shaped by the guidance and initiative of others rather than their own volition.

A third pattern—selective acceptance—was also observed, in which patients filtered and prioritized information based on personal needs, beliefs, or emotional comfort. Many focused on content that was reassuring or directly relevant to their day-to-day management, while consciously avoiding highly technical, overwhelming, or anxiety-provoking material. This approach allowed patients to maintain emotional stability while still engaging with resources perceived as beneficial. The two patterns are therefore distinguished by the locus of control over information exposure: passive acceptance reflects externally driven exposure shaped by the initiative of others or by platform algorithms, whereas selective acceptance reflects active filtering of available information according to personal relevance and emotional preference.

Finally, avoidance behavior was evident among some participants, who deliberately limited their exposure to disease-related information because of feelings of overwhelm, distress, or skepticism about the reliability of online sources. Concerns about information overload, contradictory advice, and the potential for increased anxiety led these individuals to disengage from active information seeking, instead choosing to place their trust primarily in healthcare providers.

These varied response patterns reflect the complex interplay of external and internal factors shaping patient behavior. Recognizing this spectrum—from active seeking to deliberate avoidance—underscores the importance of personalized patient education and support strategies that accommodate individual informational preferences, emotional needs, and coping styles. Discussion

Main Findings

This study extends previous work by applying the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework qualitatively to health information-seeking behavior (HISB) among patients with autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs). The ten subthemes describe a process in which disease-related and digital stimuli prompt cognitive and emotional responses that shape four behavioral patterns. Crucially, the unpredictable course of ARDs appeared to generate recurrent stimuli, suggesting a temporal dimension to HISB that conventional cross-sectional surveys cannot capture. Taken together, these findings suggest that HISB in ARDs may be conceptualized as an emotionally mediated, potentially recurrent process rather than a single linear decision-making process. Because the data were collected at a single time point, this cyclical interpretation is a conceptual inference drawn from participants’ retrospective accounts rather than an empirically demonstrated behavioral cycle.

Theoretical Integration: Stimulus-Organism-Response as a Process Model

The S-O-R framework may offer particular explanatory value for ARD patients because the fluctuating disease course can generate repeated stimulus exposures that participants perceived as reactivating their information needs. In cancer, diagnosis constitutes a discrete, singular stimulus event that prompts intensive information seeking followed by gradual stabilization.8,9 In contrast, our participants described encountering diagnosis followed by unpredictable flares, medication adjustments, and side effects—each of which they portrayed as a recurrent stimulus that reactivated the organism-response pathway.22 These accounts suggest that, for this population, the S-O-R process may operate not as a one-time transaction but as an iterated loop in which each disease event reshapes the organismic state and potentially alters the behavioral response.

The organismic stage appeared decisive in shaping the response pathway. Participants described a learning trajectory: those with shorter disease duration reported greater uncertainty and reliance on uncritically accepted information, whereas those with longer duration described more targeted searching and selective filtering.23,24 This process—what one participant described as moving from “believing everything” to “knowing what to ignore”—may represent a form of situated health literacy that participants attributed to repeated experience with their condition rather than to formal education alone. Because this was a single-time-point study, the proposed learning trajectory was inferred from participants’ retrospective accounts rather than observed longitudinally. Prior quantitative S-O-R studies using survey methods have identified structural relationships among variables but were less suited to exploring how participants perceived and reconstructed these processes over time.16,17

The mediating role of emotional valence in this process merits emphasis. Participants described optimism and perceived self-efficacy as facilitating active information seeking, whereas distress and anxiety were often associated with avoidance.25 Notably, acceptance-oriented responses can themselves function as a coping strategy rather than mere passivity, which may help explain the selective and passive patterns observed in our sample.26 The S-O-R framework is particularly well suited to capturing these cognitive-emotional dynamics because it conceptualizes the organism as an integrated cognitive-emotional system rather than a purely information-processing unit. Participants further described how similar disease-related events could prompt active seeking in some individuals but avoidance in others, depending on differences in prior knowledge, social support, and emotional responses.

It should also be noted that the original S-O-R framework allows for bidirectional influence, in which behavioral responses may feed back to alter subsequent organismic states. For example, sustained avoidance of health information could reinforce distress-driven cognitive closure, making future engagement more difficult, while successful active seeking might enhance self-efficacy and emotional resilience over time. Our single-time-point interview data were not well suited to capturing these feedback loops, as participants described their typical or most recent behaviors rather than the sequential transitions between stimulus, organism, and response over time.

Comparative Analysis: How ARD Patients Differ from Other Chronic Disease Populations

Several findings align with HISB research in other chronic disease populations.8–10 Social support from peers and healthcare providers consistently catalyzes information engagement, confirming that HISB remains socially embedded rather than individually determined.27,28 Emotional distress has also been associated with information avoidance in cancer and cardiovascular populations, consistent with our findings.29,30

However, several features of ARDs may shape HISB in distinctive ways. The unpredictable flare-remission cycle characteristic of many ARDs may repeatedly reactivate patients’ information needs, even after patients have established information-seeking routines. Compared with some other chronic conditions, ARDs are characterized by greater uncertainty and fluctuation over time.8,9 The immunosuppressant regimens central to ARD management add further informational complexity: patients must simultaneously manage disease activity while considering infection risk, creating competing information demands that some participants described as “balancing two kinds of danger.”

At the same time, disease-specific characteristics across the six included subtypes may have shaped HISB in ways that a pooled analysis cannot fully disentangle. Symptom variability differs markedly—for example, the unpredictable flare-remission course of SLE and RA contrasts with the more insidious, progressive skin and organ fibrosis of SSc—potentially altering the frequency and urgency of disease-related stimuli. Prognostic framing and treatment complexity likewise vary, from conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs to biologic agents and, in SLE, emerging cellular therapies, each generating distinct information needs. Pooling these conditions may therefore have obscured subtype-specific stimulus patterns, and the present findings should be read as reflecting shared mechanisms across ARDs rather than disease-specific characteristics.

The digital information environment also distinguishes this population. The prominence of Douyin and Xiaohongshu as stimulus sources (Table 2) reflects China’s platform ecology, where short-video and lifestyle-oriented social media play an increasingly important role in health information dissemination.31 Short-video formats—common to platforms worldwide—often present complex medical information in brief, algorithmically curated forms that may prioritize engagement over accuracy.13,14,32 Such platforms can function as a double-edged sword, offering accessible peer experiences while also amplifying unverified claims.33 One participant described encountering a video recommending turmeric as a “cure” for rheumatoid arthritis, illustrating how unverified health claims may circulate through social media platforms. The algorithmic curation of these platforms may also reinforce selective acceptance by preferentially surfacing content consistent with users’ prior engagement, potentially narrowing exposure to alternative perspectives and increasing vulnerability to misinformation. Age-related differences in technology preferences may further shape which platforms patients use and how they appraise the information they encounter.34 Within this environment, the shift several participants described—from “believing everything” toward “knowing what to ignore”—may reflect the development of situated digital health literacy.

The influence of educational attainment on information engagement was largely consistent with prior HISB research.23,24 Participants with higher educational attainment generally described more active information seeking and greater capacity to critically evaluate health information.35

Toward a Dynamic Model: Directions for Future Research

The qualitative patterns observed in this study are exploratory but suggest several directions for future research. Participants’ accounts indicate that the relationship between disease-related stimuli and information seeking may not be linear: frequent flares or treatment changes sometimes appeared to shift patients toward selective or avoidant patterns rather than sustained active seeking, raising the possibility that very high stimulus intensity may promote disengagement. Future research could examine whether the relationship between disease-related events and information seeking is nonlinear. Participants also described a learning trajectory from relatively uncritical acceptance toward more selective filtering, suggesting that health literacy and information practices may evolve across the illness trajectory. Longitudinal qualitative or mixed-methods studies that follow patients across the illness trajectory would be valuable for directly examining transitions between information-seeking patterns over time and for testing the proposed cyclical interpretation prospectively.

Implications for Practice and Research

These findings are directly applicable to care delivered by at least two disciplines—rheumatology nursing and mental health practice—and have direct implications for multidisciplinary, team-based care, which is central to supporting patients with ARDs. Supporting effective health information-seeking is not the responsibility of any single profession, and the four response patterns identified here map onto complementary professional roles. In particular, rheumatology nurses may serve as information navigators who help patients identify trustworthy online resources, interpret conflicting information, and manage emotional reactions to disease-related content. For active seekers, rheumatologists can supply accurate, up-to-date treatment information while nurses help patients filter and appraise the large volume of material they gather. For patients showing distress-driven avoidance, mental health professionals can address the anxiety underlying disengagement, with nurses providing emotional support and a low-threshold entry point back to reliable information. For patients exhibiting passive or selective acceptance, health-education nurses can lead structured guidance and, working with health-information teams, push trustworthy content through the short-video and social-media channels these patients already use. Because nurses have the most frequent and sustained contact with patients, they are well positioned to act as gatekeepers who continuously assess each patient’s organismic state—including health literacy and emotional readiness—and to determine, before large-scale information education is delivered, whether a given patient is better served by proactive information provision or by emotional reassurance. Rheumatology nurses are uniquely positioned to assess patients’ evolving information needs during routine care and to deliver tailored guidance aligned with patients’ emotional readiness and health literacy. We therefore suggest that interventions to improve information-seeking be designed and delivered jointly by rheumatology, nursing, and mental health practitioners rather than by any one discipline alone.

Healthcare providers should recognize that ARD patients’ information needs fluctuate with disease activity and adapt their communication strategies accordingly. During disease flares, patients require structured, high-quality information delivered proactively, before distress drives them toward potentially unreliable digital sources.29 Rheumatology clinics might implement tiered information protocols: intensive guided orientation for newly diagnosed patients, followed by gradual transition toward self-directed resources as patients develop disease-specific literacy.24

Digital health platforms serving ARD populations should incorporate algorithmic safeguards against misinformation, particularly for short-video content where compression favors sensational claims over measured medical guidance.32 Curated, quality-controlled video resources offer one promising avenue for delivering trustworthy content in formats patients already use.36 Information interventions should be timed to disease duration, with critical appraisal training introduced during the second year post-diagnosis when patients transition from naive acceptance toward selective engagement.23

Limitations

This single-center study, recruiting participants exclusively from a tertiary hospital in China during hospitalization, limits generalizability to outpatient, rural, and non-Chinese settings. The sample encompassed six ARD subtypes, and although participants shared the core challenges of long-term self-management and a fluctuating disease course—supporting cross-subtype examination of common mechanisms—disease-specific differences in stimulus types and illness trajectories may not have been fully captured; future studies focusing on individual subtypes would allow more granular insights. Relatedly, the sample of 17 participants was determined by data saturation rather than statistical considerations; although saturation was reached, a larger sample stratified by subtype might have surfaced additional disease-specific patterns. Finally, the single-time-point design means that the temporal patterns identified were reconstructed from retrospective accounts rather than observed prospectively, and the proposed learning trajectory and cyclical S-O-R process should be examined through longitudinal research. Despite these boundaries, this study offers a process-level account that can inform hypotheses for future confirmatory work.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, qualitative applications of the S-O-R framework to HISB in ARDs remain limited. The findings identify four behavioral patterns and, as a conceptual inference from participants’ retrospective accounts, suggest an emotionally mediated, potentially recurrent process rather than a single linear stimulus-response sequence. They provide a foundation for multidisciplinary patient education and clinical communication and for longitudinal research on how information-seeking behaviors evolve over time.
