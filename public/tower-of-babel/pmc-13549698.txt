Alterations in Gut Microbiome Diversity and Composition in Patients with Epidermolysis Bullosa: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Suci Widhiati, Hardyanto Soebono, Dewajani Purnomosari, Tri Wibawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) is a rare inherited genodermatosis that affects both the skin and gastrointestinal tract, potentially leading to altered gut microbiota. This study aimed to assess the diversity and composition of the gut microbiome in EB patients compared to healthy controls. Twelve EB patients—six with dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa patients (pDEB) and six with nondystrophic epidermolysis bullosa patients (pNDEB)—and 12 age- and gender-matched healthy controls were included. Fecal samples were collected, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing (V3–V4 region) was performed. Alpha diversity indices revealed a significant reduction in gut microbiota richness in EB patients, particularly in pNDEB, compared to controls. Beta diversity analysis using unweighted UniFrac showed compositional differences between groups, while weighted UniFrac and principal coordinate analysis revealed overlapping clustering patterns. Genus-level comparisons indicated lower relative abundance of Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, and Prevotella in EB subgroups. These findings suggest that EB is associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, warranting further investigation into its potential clinical implications and microbial biomarkers.

Introduction

Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) is a group of inherited mechano-bullous genodermatoses primarily affecting the skin, characterized by recurrent blistering, epithelial fragility, and scarring, with frequent involvement of other epithelial surfaces such as the cornea and gastrointestinal (GI) tract.[1] Clinical manifestations may extend to the oral cavity, pharynx, and GI tract, resulting in erosions and blistering that impair oral intake, nutrient absorption, and visceral protein stores, contributing to malnutrition and growth impairment.[2] Among the four EB subtypes—EB simplex (EBS), junctional EB (JEB), dystrophic EB (DEB), and Kindler EB—GI complications are most commonly observed in DEB and JEB, including dysphagia, microstomia, and anal lesions.[3] These manifestations may disrupt intestinal homeostasis and lead to gut microbiota dysbiosis. Research on gut microbiota has significantly expanded with high-throughput discovery, enabling the identification of previously nonculturable organisms, revealing that over 100 trillion microbes inhabit the human gut, playing essential roles in immune regulation.[4] Gut microbes interact with epithelial and dendritic cells via toll-like receptors, influencing immune effector cells such as macrophages, B cells, and various T-cell subtypes.[5] The balance of gut microbiota can be altered by innumerable factors. Dysbiosis has been linked to increased susceptibility to allergies, autoimmune diseases, and chronic inflammation. While the alteration of gut microbiome has been studied in various inflammatory skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis, acne, urticaria, and psoriasis,[6] its role in EB remains largely unexplored. Evidence from burn patients suggests severe epithelial damage may induce transient gut microbiome imbalances favoring opportunistic pathogens like Enterococcus and Escherichia, followed by gradual microbial recovery.[7] This study aimed to investigate the alterations in gut microbiota among patients with EB compared to healthy individuals.

Material and Methods

We conducted a case-control observational study from 2020 to 2022, approved by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Nursing, and Public Health, Universitas Gadjah Mada (Ref: KE/FK/1489/EC/2019; KE/FK/0480/EC/2021). A total of 24 individuals participated, including 12 patients diagnosed with EB and 12 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. The EB group was subdivided equally into nondystrophic EB (pNDEB) and dystrophic EB (pDEB). Clinical classification was performed using the EB Clinical Diagnostic Matrix Tool,[8] and genetic confirmation was achieved via whole-exome sequencing as previously described.[9] All participants were over one year of age and had completed the weaning phase.

Stool samples were collected in DNA/RNA Shield fecal collection tubes (Zymo Research, CA, USA), containing approximately 2 g of fecal matter, and stored at –80°C until processing. Exclusion criteria for controls included current illness, antibiotic or other oral medication use within the previous week, and recent GI or respiratory infection within two weeks.

DNA was extracted using the DNeasy® PowerSoil® Kit (Qiagen, Germany), quantified using Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and stored at –20°C. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F and 806R, and sequencing libraries were prepared with the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Kit (Illumina, USA). Library quality was verified with Qubit and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2.0, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (250 bp paired-end reads) at Novogene.

Bioinformatic analysis was carried out using FLASH v1.2.7 to merge paired-end reads, followed by quality filtering with quantitative insights into microbial ecology (QIIME) v1.7.0. Chimera sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME against the SILVA database. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% similarity using Uparse v7.0.1090, and taxonomic annotation was performed using the SILVA 16S rRNA database.

Microbial alpha diversity was assessed using Chao1, ACE, Observed Species, Shannon, and Simpson indices. Beta diversity was analyzed using unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances. Diversity analyses and LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) were performed within QIIME and visualized in R (v2.15.3).

Results

A total of 3,150,455 high-quality sequences were obtained from 24 fecal samples, with an average of 1,050,151 reads per group (range: 768,808–1,588,106). After chimera filtering and OTU clustering at a 97% similarity threshold, an average of 1,846 OTUs were identified. OTU richness was highest in healthy controls (2,466), followed by pDEB (1,361) and pNDEB (1,171). Good’s coverage exceeded 99.0% in all samples, indicating sufficient sequencing depth.

The clinical characteristics of participants are presented in Table 1. EB patients showed significantly lower body mass index (BMI) than controls (P = 0.001), indicating poorer nutritional status. The pNDEB group included four patients with JEB, one with EBS, and one with Kindler EB. Cutaneous manifestations varied by subtype, with generalized erosions, nail dystrophy, and syndactyly observed in pDEB, and chronic wounds, acral blistering, and poikiloderma in pNDEB. [Figure 1] Seven out of twelve EB patients reported GI symptoms, including constipation, reflux, and abdominal discomfort.

Beta diversity analysis using unweighted UniFrac showed distinct microbial compositions between groups. Significant differences were found between control and pDEB (P < 0.01), control and pNDEB (P = 0.0095), and between EB subgroups (P < 0.001). However, weighted UniFrac and PCoA did not show clear clustering patterns among groups [Figure 2g and h].

In genus-level comparisons [Supplementary Figure 1], Prevotella and Akkermansia were less abundant in pDEB patients compared to controls, while Corynebacterium, Cetobacterium, and Staphylococcus were reduced in both EB subgroups. These shifts may reflect nutritional differences, gut barrier changes, or host inflammatory states associated with EB.

Discussion

Our findings revealed a marked reduction in gut microbiota diversity and richness among EB patients compared to healthy controls, particularly within the pNDEB group. These results support the hypothesis that EB, a chronic inflammatory condition with systemic involvement, may disrupt gut microbial balance.

Although studies of the skin microbiome in EB have shown reduced diversity, gut microbiota analyses remain limited. Reimer-Taschenbrecker et al.[10] (2022) reported decreased skin microbial richness in recessive dystrophic EB (RDEB) but found no significant differences in gut or oral microbiome. Similarly, Fuentes et al.[11] observed decreased skin microbial diversity in RDEB using shotgun metagenomics. While our findings partially contrast with those of earlier studies, we propose that the reduced gut richness in our cohort may relate to lower BMI and altered GI function, both of which are common in EB. Esophageal blistering, chronic constipation, and reduced food intake may disrupt gut barrier integrity, contributing to dysbiosis.[1213]

Seven of twelve EB patients reported GI symptoms, predominantly constipation and reflux. This clinical burden was more evident in pDEB patients, aligning with previous studies showing a high prevalence of GI involvement in EB, especially in more severe subtypes.[14] Inflammatory changes in the gut epithelium may further influence microbial composition, as low bacterial richness has been associated with proinflammatory states and impaired gut barrier function.[15]

Interestingly, while alpha diversity was reduced, beta diversity analysis showed no significant clustering by group. This may reflect the microbiota’s compensatory capacity to maintain relative abundance and stability over time, a phenomenon explained by the concept of enterotypes.[1617] Still, specific genera differed in abundance between groups. For instance, the observed reduction of Prevotella in EB patients may suggest lower carbohydrate intake or impaired digestion, in contrast to high levels of Prevotella seen in healthy young Indonesians.[18] Reduced Corynebacterium levels, previously linked to stress and depression models,[19] may also have relevance in EB patients experiencing chronic anxiety and psychosocial impact.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not control antibiotic use in patients with EB, nor did we regulate their dietary intake. Notably, excluding patients who had used antibiotics could have significantly reduced our sample size, limiting the inclusion to less severely affected individuals. In our study, two of the twelve EB patients had used antibiotics. Second, the modest sample size limits broader generalizability. Despite these constraints, this study provides new insights into gut microbial shifts in EB and sets the groundwork for future longitudinal and mechanistic studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that gut microbiome diversity and richness are reduced in patients with EB compared to healthy controls. Several bacterial genera showed altered abundance patterns in the EB groups, suggesting gut dysbiosis potentially linked to chronic inflammation and compromised nutritional status. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to clarify the relationship between microbiota composition, GI involvement, and chronic wound severity in EB.
