Pharmacokinetic Drug Interaction Studies of Limnetrelvir with Midazolam and Itraconazole in Healthy Participants
Authors: Janki M Desai, Ekram Ahmed Chowdhury, Izna Ali, Christine M Lee, Jeffrey M Schmidt, Amelia Orejudos, Swarna L Yadlapalli, Shelly Gupta, Christopher Ocampo, Michael G Miller, Ahmed Hamed Salem, Nael M Mostafa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesLimnetrelvir is an oral inhibitor of the SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro) and was developed with the aim of achieving broad-spectrum efficacy against SARS-CoV-2 and emerging variants. The aim of this work was to evaluate potential CYP3A4 drug-drug interactions (DDIs) of limnetrelvir.MethodsIn two phase 1, open-label studies, limnetrelvir was evaluated for DDIs with midazolam (Study 1), a CYP3A4 substrate, and itraconazole (Study 2), a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor. Study 1 (N = 24) consisted of two parts (Studies 1A and 1B), each with two periods (Periods 1 and 2), and Study 2 (N = 12) consisted of one part with two periods. In Study 1A, 12 healthy adult participants received midazolam (1 mg) on Period 1 Day 1 and Period 2 Day 10 and limnetrelvir 400 mg once daily (QD) on Period 2 Days 1–10. In Study 1B, an additional 12 healthy adult participants received midazolam (1 mg) on Period 1 Day 1 and Period 2 Day 5 and limnetrelvir 200 mg QD on Period 2 Days 1–5. In Study 2, 12 participants received limnetrelvir (50 mg) and itraconazole (200 mg twice daily on Day 1 and QD on Days 2 to 6 in Period 2), with limnetrelvir administered as a single dose on Period 1 Day 1 and Period 2 Day 4. All study drugs were administered orally. Serial blood samples were collected and analyzed for drug concentrations. Pharmacokinetic parameters were determined with noncompartmental methods.ResultsCoadministration of limnetrelvir increased the maximum concentration (Cmax) and area under the concentration-time curve from time 0 to infinity (AUCinf) of midazolam by ~ 2.4- to 2.6-fold and ~ 4.6- to 4.3-fold, respectively. The pharmacokinetics of the active metabolite, 1-OH-midazolam, demonstrated a decrease in Cmax by ~ 0.66-fold at Day 5 and ~ 0.96-fold at Day 10, while overall AUC remained stable at both time points. No clinically significant differences in CYP3A4 inhibition after 5 days and 10 days of limnetrelvir administration were observed. Limnetrelvir Cmax and AUCinf increased by ~ 1.7- and ~ 3.6-fold, respectively, when coadministered with itraconazole, suggesting that limnetrelvir is a moderately sensitive substrate of CYP3A4.ConclusionsLimnetrelvir was found to be a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor and substrate, suggesting that dosing adjustment or careful monitoring may be necessary when coadministered with medications highly metabolized by CYP3A4 or with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors to ensure safe and effective treatment.Trial Registration Number (TRN)NCT05691699, NCT05895266. Key Points

Limnetrelvir is an oral small-molecule inhibitor that targets COVID-19’s main protease (Mpro) and has demonstrated broad-spectrum antiviral activity against both SARS-CoV-2 and its emerging variants.Two clinical studies were conducted to investigate the drug-drug interactions of limnetrelvir.Limnetrelvir was found to be a moderate CYP3A inhibitor and substrate.

Introduction

The corona virus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [1], has led to significant global mortality, with nearly 7.1 million deaths worldwide and 1.2 million in the USA as of January 2025 [2]. Primarily affecting the respiratory system, the disease can also impact other organs, with symptoms ranging from mild to severe [3]. The virus undergoes frequent mutations, producing variants that may resist vaccines [4, 5]. Given its high mutation rate, there is an urgent need for additional targeted therapies to support vaccination efforts in controlling COVID-19.

SARS-CoV-2 replicates through five key stages, relying on its main protease (Mpro) for viral protein processing. The virus encodes two polyproteins, pp1a and pp1ab, which are cleaved by Mpro at 11 specific sites to produce nonstructural proteins (nsps) essential for viral replication [6, 7]. These nsps facilitate subgenomic RNA synthesis, enabling host cells to produce viral structural proteins while also suppressing innate immune responses [8]. Mpro presents a promising target for antiviral drug development as it also plays a critical role in viral replication and mutation. Inhibiting Mpro can prevent viral spread while minimizing harm to human cells, thereby reducing side effects associated with many antiviral treatments. As Mpro is highly conserved across coronaviruses, targeting this enzyme could offer broad-spectrum efficacy against SARS-CoV-2 and emerging variants [9].

Limnetrelvir is a direct-acting, orally active small-molecule inhibitor of the SARS-CoV-2 main protease, demonstrating broad-spectrum antiviral activity against both SARS-CoV-2 and its emerging variants [10]. While several antiviral compounds are in development for COVID-19 treatment, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has granted emergency use authorization (EUA) for Paxlovid™ (nirmatrelvir and ritonavir) to treat mild-to-moderate cases in children aged 12 years and older in December 2021. By 2023, Paxlovid™ received full approval in several countries, including the USA, for treating COVID-19 in specific populations [11]. Unlike ritonavir, which acts as a potent mechanism-based (irreversible) inhibitor of cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A4), resulting in profound and sustained suppression of CYP3A4 activity and causing extensive pharmacokinetic drug-drug interaction (DDI) liabilities, limnetrelvir is predicted—based on dynamic victim DDI simulations—to interact moderately with CYP3A4 inhibitors [12]. As a perpetrator, limnetrelvir was expected to be a weak CYP3A4 inhibitor based on physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling (data unpublished), suggesting its DDI liabilities may be lower compared to ritonavir-containing regimens. This moderate inhibitory profile could be clinically significant, as it lowers the risk of severe DDIs, reduces the likelihood of contraindications with commonly prescribed medications metabolized by CYP3A (including statins, antiarrhythmics, and immunosuppressants), and minimizes the need for complex medication management when compared to ritonavir-containing regimens. Thus, limnetrelvir’s favorable DDI profile supports safer integration into COVID-19 antiviral therapy protocols, particularly in populations requiring polypharmacy.

DDIs can lead to changes in systemic exposure, potentially resulting in adverse reactions or loss of efficacy. Midazolam is a clinical index CYP3A4 substrate, and itraconazole is a clinical index CYP3A4 inhibitor [13, 14]. Two studies were conducted to evaluate the effect of multiple doses of limnetrelvir on the PK of midazolam and its active metabolite 1-hydroxy midazolam (1-OH-midazolam). Similarly, a limnetrelvir-itraconazole DDI study was conducted to evaluate the impact of multiple doses of itraconazole (a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor) on limnetrelvir PK. Results from these studies informed the prescribing instructions for concomitant administration of limnetrelvir with CYP3A4 substrates and inhibitors.

Methods

These studies (NCT05895266, NCT05691699) were conducted at the AbbVie Clinical Pharmacology Research Unit (Grayslake, IL, USA) in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki [15, 16]. The protocols and informed consent forms were approved by the institutional review board (Advarra Institutional Review Board, Columbia, MD, USA). Participants provided written informed consent before any study-related procedures were performed.

Study Design

Two open-label phase 1 DDI studies were conducted to evaluate the interactions between limnetrelvir and midazolam (Study 1) or itraconazole (Study 2). Study 1 consisted of two parts (Parts A and B), each part with two periods (Periods 1 and 2), and Study 2 consisted of one part with two periods. Both studies were designed to evaluate the potential for pharmacokinetic (PK) interactions between limnetrelvir and midazolam or itraconazole in healthy participants (Fig. 1). Each part of Study 1 and Study 2 was conducted in 12 healthy adult participants. Study 1A characterized the effect of multiple dosing of limnetrelvir for 10 days on the PK of midazolam and its metabolite, 1-OH-midazolam. Study 1B evaluated midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam PK following 5 days of limnetrelvir dosing (Fig. 1a). In Study 1, a single dose of midazolam 1 mg oral solution was administered alone on Period 1/Day 1 and then after 10 days (Part A; 400 mg once daily (QD)) or 5 days (Part B; 200 mg QD) of limnetrelvir dosing in Period 2. All study drugs were administered orally with ~ 240 mL of water under fasting conditions. In Study 2, a single oral dose of limnetrelvir (50 mg) was administered alone (Period 1/Day 1 and Period 2/Day 4) or in combination with itraconazole (two 100 mg capsules twice daily (BID) on Period 1/Day 1 and QD on Period 2/Days 2–6) under fasting conditions (Fig. 1b).

Participants

Healthy men and women (postmenopausal or permanently surgically sterile), aged 18–65 years with a body mass index of 18–32 kg/m2, were enrolled after screening medical history, physical examination, vital signs assessments, laboratory profile, and a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG). Participants were excluded if they had active or recurrent viral infection, significant illness/infection/major febrile illness, hospitalization, or any surgical procedure within 30 days before the first study drug dose. Participants with a history of epilepsy, significant cardiac, respiratory (except mild asthma as a child), endocrine, renal, hepatic, gastrointestinal, hematologic, or psychiatric disorder, uncontrolled medical illness, significant sensitivity/allergy to medications or foods, diseases aggravated or triggered by ultraviolet radiation, or abnormal photosensitivity were excluded. Tobacco or nicotine use, drug or alcohol use within 180 days before the first study drug dose, or positive drug/alcohol/nicotine screens were excluded. Consumption of grapefruit products, Seville oranges, starfruit products, or quinine/tonic water within 72 h before dosing was prohibited. Use of any medication or vaccine (including over-the-counter or prescription medications, vitamins, and/or herbal supplements) was prohibited during the study except for authorized COVID-19 vaccines administered outside the study or medications prescribed by the study team for adverse event (AEs). Use of any known inhibitors or inducers of drug-metabolizing enzymes within 30 days before and during the study was also prohibited. SARS-CoV-2 vaccine information was recorded.

Pharmacokinetic Sampling and Bioanalysis

Serial blood samples were collected in evacuated potassium-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (K2-EDTA)-containing collection tubes for analysis of plasma concentrations of limnetrelvir, midazolam, and 1-OH-midazolam. In Study 1, midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam samples were collected on Day 1 of Period 1 and Day 10 of Period 2 at 0 (pre-dose), 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h. Limnetrelvir samples were collected on Day 1 and Day 10 of Period 2 at 0 (pre-dose), 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h and prior to dosing on Day 5. In Study 2, limnetrelvir samples were collected starting on Day 1 of Period 1 at 0 (pre-dose), 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, and 15 h post-dose and at 24, 30, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h post-dose on Days 2 through 4. Similarly, on Day 4 of Period 2, limnetrelvir samples were collected at 0 (pre-dose), 0.5, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, and 15 h post-dose and at 24, 30, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h post-dose on Days 5 through 8.

Immediately after sample collection, the blood samples were inverted ~ 8 to 10 times to ensure good mixing of the blood and anticoagulant, placed in an ice bath or chilled cryoblock, and centrifuged within 1 h of collection to separate plasma. Plasma was aliquoted into clean polypropylene tubes and stored in a freezer maintained at − 20 °C or colder until shipped to AbbVie Inc. (North Chicago, IL, USA) for limnetrelvir analysis and Syneos Health (Quebec, Canada) for midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam analysis.

Limnetrelvir quantification was performed with a validated method combining high-performance liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometric detection (LC-MS/MS). Limnetrelvir was extracted from human plasma via liquid-liquid extraction using 96-well plates. Plasma was mixed with a solution containing limnetrelvir-d8, and analytes were isolated using a solution of ethyl acetate and hexanes. The supernatant was diluted with acetonitrile and water then separated chromatographically using an XBridge C8 column (2.1 × 30 mm, 5 microns; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Tandem mass spectrometric detection occurred on a SCIEX API 5500 (Framingham, MA, USA) mass spectrometer in positive ion mode. Concentrations were quantified using a weighted (1/x2) linear regression for plasma concentrations of 5–5000 ng/mL with precision (%CV) below 9.1% and accuracy (%Bias) from − 1.3 to 10.4%. The lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) for limnetrelvir in plasma was 5.00 ng/mL.

For the midazolam assay, plasma was mixed with a solution containing the internal standards midazolam-d4 and 1-OH-midazolam-d4, and the analytes (midazolam, 1-OH-midazolam, and internal standards) were extracted from human plasma by an automated liquid-liquid extraction procedure using a solution of methyl tert-butyl either. Midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam were detected and quantified with a validated LC-MS/MS method. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACE 3 C18 column (50 × 4.6 mm, 3 microns; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) and a mobile phase mixture of water/acetonitrile/methanol (60/15/25) and ammonium formate (1 mM) with 0.2% formic acid. Mass detection was performed on a SCIEX API 5000 in positive ion mode. Concentrations were quantified using weighted (1/x2) linear regressions for midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam plasma concentrations of 10–10,000 pg/mL and 5–5000 pg/mL, respectively. The midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam overall precision (%CV) was less than 3.23% and 4.80%, respectively, and the midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam overall accuracy (%Bias) was between 2.90% to 9.87%, and 2.24 to 8.33%, respectively. The LLOQs for midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam in plasma were 10.0 pg/mL and 5.00 pg/mL, respectively.

Pharmacokinetic Analyses

All drug concentrations that were below the LLOQ were set to 0 and treated as 0 in summary statistics and for the calculation of PK parameters. No participant had incomplete PK profiles. Four PK profiles for 1-OH-midazolam in Study 1A, Period 2 had > 20% of the AUC extrapolated or were removed due to adjusted R-squared values < 0.75. PK parameters for limnetrelvir, midazolam, and 1-OH-midazolam were estimated using noncompartmental methods in Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.4 (Pharsight, A Certara Company, St. Louis, MO, USA). PK parameters included the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to reach Cmax (Tmax), the area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC) from time 0 to the last measurable time point (AUCt) and from time 0 to infinity (AUCinf), and the terminal-phase elimination half-life (t1/2). In Study 1, the exposures (Cmax and AUC) of midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam were compared when administered alone in Period 1 and in combination with limnetrelvir in Period 2. Similarly, limnetrelvir exposures (Cmax and AUC) were compared when administered alone in Period 1 and in combination with itraconazole in Period 2 for Study 2.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.2 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) or higher. The effect of limnetrelvir on midazolam PK was assessed through a repeated-measures analysis for the natural logarithms of Cmax and AUC using data from Period 1/Day 1 and Period 2/Day 10 for Study 1A and from Period 1/Day 1 and Period 2/Day 5 for Study 1B. Each part was analyzed separately, and Dosing Day was included in the model as a fixed effect.

The point estimates of midazolam when administered concomitantly with limnetrelvir (Period 2/Day 10 for Study 1A) relative to midazolam alone (Period 1/Day 1 of Study 1A) were assessed by calculating the difference in least squares means from repeated-measures analyses of the natural logarithms of Cmax and AUC. The 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for the ratio estimates were derived by taking the antilogarithm of the CIs for the difference of the least squares means on the logarithmic scale. The same analysis was performed for 1-OH-midazolam. Similar analyses were conducted for Study 1B using data from Period 1/Day 1 and Period 2/Day 5 for both midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam as well as for limnetrelvir when administered concomitantly with itraconazole (Study 2: Period 2/Days 2–6) relative to limnetrelvir alone (Study 2: Period 1/Day 1).

Safety Monitoring

Throughout the study, routine safety evaluations including AE monitoring, physical examinations, vital sign measurements, 12-lead electrocardiogram assessments, and clinical laboratory tests (hematology, chemistry, and urinalysis) were performed.

Results

Participant Disposition

A total of 24 healthy participants were enrolled in Study 1 (12 participants in each part), and 12 were enrolled in Study 2 (Table 1). The mean age (range) of the participants in Studies 1A, 1B, and 2 were ~41 (26–59), 48 (30–59), and 44 (26–57) years. The mean weight across all three study parts was similar, ranging from 80 to 84 kg. One female was enrolled in Study 1B and Study 2, and all other participants were males. Study 1: Pharmacokinetics of Midazolam and 1-OH-Midazolam

The mean plasma concentration–time profiles for midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam administered alone and with limnetrelvir are shown on a linear and log-linear scale, respectively, in Fig. 2. The PK parameters for midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam following the administration of midazolam with and without limnetrelvir are presented in Table 2. Midazolam was rapidly absorbed with a Tmax ranging from 0.5 to 2 h in Study 1. The harmonic mean t1/2 for midazolam ranged from ~ 3.8 to 5.4 h when dosed alone and from ~ 7.1 to 7.6 h when dosed in combination with limnetrelvir. The point estimates and 90% CIs for the effect of limnetrelvir on the Cmax and AUCinf for midazolam and 1-OH-midazolam are shown in Fig. 4. Following multiple doses of limnetrelvir 400 mg QD for 10 days (Part A), the central values for midazolam Cmax and AUCinf ratios were 2.41 and 4.64, respectively, compared to administration of midazolam alone. Following 5 days of limnetrelvir 400 mg QD dosing (Part B), the point estimates of midazolam Cmax and AUCinf central values were 2.62 and 4.31, respectively, compared to administration of midazolam alone (Fig. 4). Administration of limnetrelvir for 5 or 10 days resulted in an increase in the overall exposure of midazolam by approximately 2.4- to 4.6-fold in healthy participants. Similarly, following 5- or 10-day administration of limnetrelvir with midazolam, the central values of 1-OH-midazolam Cmax and AUCinf ratios were 0.96 and 0.66 and 1.18 and 1.04, respectively, compared to administration of midazolam alone. These findings suggest that coadministration of limnetrelvir and midazolam may inhibit the CYP3A4 mediated metabolism of midazolam.

Study 2: Pharmacokinetics of Limnetrelvir

The mean plasma concentration–time profiles for limnetrelvir when administered alone (Study 2: Period 1/Day 1) and with itraconazole (Study 2: Period 2/Day 4) are shown in Fig. 3. The PK parameters of limnetrelvir in the presence of itraconazole are presented in Table 3. Limnetrelvir was absorbed rapidly, with Tmax ranging from 0.5 to 2.0 h following administration of limnetrelvir alone and in combination with itraconazole. The harmonic mean t1/2 for limnetrelvir was 6.13 h when dosed alone and 11.9 h when dosed in combination with itraconazole. Safety and Tolerability

There were no patterns to the AEs reported, and no new safety findings were identified in these two studies for limnetrelvir, alone or with midazolam or itraconazole (Online Supplemental Material, Tables S1–S3). No participant had a severe or serious AE, discontinued from the study due to an AE, or had a clinically relevant change in vital signs, laboratory values, or ECGs. For both DDI studies, all AEs were mild in severity.

Discussion

These studies were conducted to test the hypothesis that limnetrelvir functions as a weak inhibitor and a moderately sensitive substrate of CYP3A4. Through systematic evaluation of its pharmacokinetic interactions in healthy adult participants, using midazolam (a clinical CYP3A4 probe substrate) and itraconazole (a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor), essential insights into CYP3A4-mediated DDIs were obtained. Clinically, limnetrelvir demonstrated moderate inhibition of CYP3A4 in vivo. These clinical findings inform dosing strategies and drug interaction guidance across diverse patient populations, supporting optimal therapeutic regimens for patients with COVID-19.

Midazolam, a well-established CYP3A4 probe substrate, is highly sensitive to DDIs due to its nearly exclusive CYP3A4 metabolism [17]. A 1 mg dose optimizes clinical DDI studies by enabling precise CYP3A4 activity measurement while minimizing pharmacodynamic effects [18]. Regulatory agencies endorse its use, given its well-characterized metabolism for assessing CYP3A4 inhibition and induction [13, 19].

The limnetrelvir and midazolam DDI study (Study 1) showed an overall increase in the exposure of midazolam. Following the once-daily dosing of limnetrelvir in Studies 1A (for 10 days) and 1B (for 5 days), the Cmax and AUCinf values of midazolam increased as high as ~ 2.4- to 2.6- and ~ 4.6- to 4.3-fold, respectively, compared to administration of midazolam alone. Limnetrelvir administration for either 5 or 10 days produced comparable CYP3A4 inhibition, with no clinically significant differences observed between the two treatment durations. The PK of the active metabolite, 1-OH-midazolam, demonstrated a decrease in Cmax by approximately 0.66-fold at Day 5 and 0.96-fold at Day 10, while overall AUC remained stable at both time points. The t1/2 of midazolam and 1-OH-midazolm was increased by ~ 2 to 3 h when midazolam was combined with limnetrelvir compared to midazolam alone. The modest increases in t1/2 observed for midazolam indicated a limited potential for drug accumulation during repeated dosing. In this study, the relatively minor changes observed in 1-OH-midazolam levels may be attributed to several factors, including extensive pre-systemic (first-pass) metabolism, rapid subsequent metabolism and clearance of 1-OH-midazolam, and the limitation that plasma concentrations reflect only a portion of the total metabolite formed. These factors can collectively diminish the sensitivity of plasma 1-OH-midazolam as an indicator for accurately assessing the extent of CYP3A4 inhibition [20].

As a result, dose adjustments for CYP3A4 substrates may be needed when used with limnetrelvir. This prolongation of half-life is likely due to both CYP3A4 inhibition by limnetrelvir and the use of extended pharmacokinetic sampling in the study. Also, the midazolam metabolite to parent ratio increased by ~ 4- to 5-fold when coadministered with limnetrelvir versus midazolam alone. These findings indicate that limnetrelvir acts as a moderate CYP3A4 inhibitor, with an AUC increase between 2-fold and less than 5-fold [14]. Importantly, there was no clinically significant difference in the extent of CYP3A4 inhibition between 5 and 10 days of limnetrelvir administration. This observed effect contrasts with the initial predictions from PBPK modeling and in vitro studies (data not shown), which anticipated only weak CYP3A4 inhibition. This inconsistency underscores limitations in the original PBPK model, such as potential inaccuracies in input parameters or underestimation of clinical relevance based on preclinical data. This highlights the need to validate model-based forecasts with robust clinical evidence to ensure accurate risk assessment and inform dosing strategies. Dose adjustments may be needed for CYP3A substrates when coadministered with limnetrelvir. This suggests that limnetrelvir may impose a lower CYP3A-mediated DDI burden compared to other agents for COVID [11].

Historically, a high dose of ketoconazole (400 mg by mouth, each day for ≥ 5 days) was used as the gold standard strong CYP3A4 inhibitor to determine the impact of CYP3A4 inhibition on the clearance of CYP3A4 substrates in vivo [21, 22]. In 2013, the FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) advised against using this ketoconazole regimen following review of clinical safety reports [23]. Ketoconazole use in clinical studies in healthy participants had been limited, due in part to evidence demonstrating the potential for liver injury following long dosing periods [21, 22]. The FDA recommended clarithromycin and itraconazole as strong index CYP3A4 inhibitors [24]. Itraconazole is widely used in clinical DDI studies, because it is a potent and reliable inhibitor of the CYP3A4 enzyme. Its predictable inhibitory effects and extensive historical data make it a standard probe for assessing CYP3A4-mediated interactions [25]. A limnetrelvir dose of 50 mg was selected for the itraconazole effect assessments to ensure that a sufficient safety margin for limnetrelvir exposures was maintained in the case of a significant increase in exposures when limnetrelvir was coadministered with itraconazole. Moreover, available clinical data show that limnetrelvir exhibits approximately dose-proportional PK across relevant doses, supporting the extrapolation of the observed DDI at 50 mg to higher, therapeutically relevant doses [26].

In the limnetrelvir and itraconazole DDI study (Study 2), the increase in limnetrelvir exposures (~ 1.7- and 3.7-fold in Cmax and AUCinf, respectively) in the presence of itraconazole confirms that CYP3A4-mediated metabolism is a major route of limnetrelvir elimination [12, 27]. Additionally, the t1/2 of limnetrelvir increased by ~ 6 h when limnetrelvir was coadministered with itraconazole. The increased half-life of limnetrelvir may lead to higher steady-state concentrations and potential accumulation upon repeated administration, which should be considered when determining optimal dosing regimens. While these changes may not warrant substantial dosing interval adjustments, they should still be considered in clinical contexts where accumulation could impact safety or efficacy. This observed increase may be associated with both the extended limnetrelvir PK sample collections during coadministration with itraconazole and the inhibitory effect of itraconazole on CYP3A4. Overall, these studies indicate that limnetrelvir is a moderately sensitive CYP3A4 substrate since the AUCinf increase ranged from ≥ 2-fold to < 5-fold [14].

This article mainly focuses on CYP3A4-mediated metabolism. While other SARS-CoV-2 therapies—like nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, remdesivir, and limnetrelvir—strongly inhibit not just CYP3A4, but also CYP2D6, P-gp, and OATP1B1/3 transporters, which can greatly increase drug exposure through combined effects, these studies center on clinical CYP3A4 DDIs [28, 29]. Although initial in vitro and preliminary PBPK modeling for limnetrelvir considered the involvement of membrane transporters (e.g., P-gp, OATP1B1/3), the present studies were not designed to formally evaluate these pathways. Nevertheless, transporter-mediated effects could contribute to the observed interactions, particularly given that itraconazole is a well-established P-gp inhibitor [30–32]. Future research will address limnetrelvir’s potential for transporter-related DDIs.

Importantly, in real-world settings such as the COVID-19 infection, systemic inflammation can suppress hepatic CYP enzyme activity, including CYP3A4, potentially leading to altered drug metabolism and increased exposure to medications metabolized via these pathways [33, 34]. This may amplify DDI risks beyond what is observed in healthy participant studies. Therefore, caution is needed when extrapolating our findings to patient populations with significant inflammatory states, and additional research in clinical settings is warranted to fully understand the implications of CYP3A4 downregulation in these scenarios. A common limitation of phase 1 DDI studies is a small sample size and limited duration of dosing. These studies were designed to provide statistical power to detect significant differences in PK parameters. The short duration of dosing in this study limited thorough safety assessment of the coadministered drugs. Additionally, the generalizability of these results is limited due to the small number of females (< 6% across both studies) enrolled and the conduct of this study in participants of general good health (e.g., did not enroll participants with COVID-19, hepatic, or renal impairment).

Conclusions

Limnetrelvir can significantly impact CYP3A4-mediated metabolism and may influence the PK of other CYP3A4 substrates. Coadministration with medications highly dependent on CYP3A4 for clearance—such as statins, antiarrhythmics, antiplatelet agents, and sedatives/hypnotics—would be avoided or carefully adjusted to prevent AEs. Strong CYP3A4 inhibition can lead to a substantial increase in limnetrelvir exposure, necessitating dose adjustments when coadministered with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, including antihypertensives (calcium channel blockers), antibiotics, and anti-HIV agents. Additionally, grapefruit-containing food or drinks should be avoided during limnetrelvir treatment due to its potential interaction with CYP3A4 metabolism. Further studies in COVID-19 patients are necessary to better assess the extent and clinical impact of CYP3A4-mediated DDI potential of limnetrelvir.
