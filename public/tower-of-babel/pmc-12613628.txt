Smoking is associated with inferior postoperative outcomes after autologous osteochondral transplantation for osteochondral lesions of the talus: a minimum 5-year clinical follow-up study
Authors: Gokhan Pehlivanoglu, Kadir Ilker Yildiz, Kutalmis Albayrak, Umit Selcuk Aykut, Muhammed Bilal Kurk, Serhat Gurbuz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) are common ankle injuries that can cause chronic pain and reduced function when conservative treatment fails. For larger lesions, autologous osteochondral transplantation (AOT) restores the joint surface with hyaline cartilage while offering reliable outcomes. While factors such as lesion size and age affect outcomes, the impact of cigarette smoking remains unclear. This study aimed to assess whether smoking adversely affects long-term outcomes after AOT for OLTs.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 78 patients who underwent AOT between 2015 and 2019, with a minimum follow-up of five years (mean: 100.4 ± 14.7 months). Patients were categorized as smokers (n = 30) or non-smokers (n = 48). Clinical status was assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Score (AOFAS), Tegner Activity Scale (pre- and post-op), and Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS). Statistical tests included the Mann–Whitney U test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, chi-square test, and Spearman’s correlation analysis, with the significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant improvement in VAS, AOFAS, and Tegner scores postoperatively (p < 0.001). Non-smokers had significantly better outcomes in VAS (p = 0.011), AOFAS (p = 0.008), and FAOS (p = 0.018). Postoperative Tegner scores did not differ significantly (p = 0.498). Baseline characteristics, lesion size, and complication rates were similar between groups. A moderate negative correlation was detected between pack-years and both FAOS (r = − 0.475, p = 0.008) and AOFAS (r = − 0.470, p = 0.009) scores.ConclusionsAutologous osteochondral transplantation leads to statistically significant improvements in pain and functional scores in both smokers and non-smokers. However, smoking is associated with higher residual pain and poorer functional outcomes at midterm follow-up, despite no significant differences in activity levels based on Tegner scores. Preoperative smoking cessation should be encouraged in patients undergoing cartilage repair.Level of evidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: Osteochondral autograft transfer, Osteochondral lesions of the talus, Smoking

Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) are common injuries, often caused by ankle sprains, and can lead to chronic pain and reduced function if not properly treated [1, 2]. Conservative treatment is the first-line option, but surgery is required when symptoms persist or function cannot be restored [3].

Bone marrow stimulation (BMS) is generally preferred for small lesions and has traditionally been applied to defects smaller than 150 mm2 [1, 4]. However, recent literature supports its use primarily for lesions smaller than 100 mm2, as outcomes tend to decline in larger or more complex defects, with a notable proportion of patients experiencing suboptimal results [4–11]. In such cases, autologous osteochondral transplantation (AOT) is a reliable alternative, offering viable hyaline cartilage repair and superior biomechanical integration [5–10, 12]. Other treatment options for larger lesions include autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI), matrix-assisted autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI), and osteochondral allograft transplantation, particularly in cases with extensive cartilage loss or limited autologous donor tissue [9, 12, 13]. However, these methods are limited by higher costs, the need for two-stage procedures (ACI/MACI) and graft availability issues (allograft) [9, 14].

Autologous matrix-induced chondrogenesis (AMIC) has gained attention as an alternative treatment, employing scaffolds such as type I/III bilayer collagen membrane and biomimetic multilayer scaffolds based on collagen-hydroxyapatite or aragonite [13, 15–19]. A systematic review has reported favorable clinical outcomes regarding these biomimetic scaffolds in talar OLTs, although long-term evidence remains limited [15]. Moreover, biologic augmentation strategies, such as bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC), which contains mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs), have been shown to be safe and improve clinical status [20–23].

While several prognostic factors, including lesion size, age, and joint condition are known to affect outcomes, lifestyle factors such as smoking have gained increasing attention [1, 6, 24, 25]. Smoking impairs musculoskeletal healing, delays bone union, and limits cartilage regeneration [25–27]. Clinical evidence suggests that smokers experience inferior outcomes following cartilage repair procedures, including ACI and BMS [1, 24, 25, 28]. Smoking has also been linked to elevated complication rates in forefoot surgery, including delayed union, infection, and prolonged postoperative pain [29].

Despite these insights, the impact of smoking on outcomes following AOT for OLTs remains underexplored. Given the biological demands of graft integration and revascularization in AOT, smoking may be a critical modifiable risk factor influencing postoperative prognosis.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of cigarette smoking on clinical outcomes after AOT in patients with OLTs. We hypothesized that smoking would be associated with inferior clinical outcomes following AOT.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study included 78 patients who met the predefined inclusion criteria, from a total of 102 individuals who underwent AOT for symptomatic OLTs between January 2015 and December 2019 at a tertiary referral center. Inclusion criteria were: (1) focal OLTs measuring ≥ 100 mm2, (2) failure of conservative treatment for at least six months, (3) minimum follow-up of five years with final evaluations conducted between February 2025 and May 2025, (4) patient age between 18 and 60 years at the time of surgery, and (5) lesion located on the medial talar dome. Exclusion criteria included inflammatory joint disease, previous ankle surgery, osteochondral lesions of the contralateral talus, or advanced osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ 2).

Smoking status classification

Patients were categorized as smokers (active cigarette users) or non-smokers (never-smokers), based on self-reported behavior at the time of surgery and follow-up. Lifetime tobacco exposure for smokers was recorded in pack-years. Those who started or quit smoking postoperatively were excluded to ensure group consistency. Smoking status was verified at the final follow-up by an independent clinician blinded to surgical outcomes.

Surgical technique

All patients underwent AOT using a standardized technique. Procedures were performed with patients in the supine position under spinal or general anesthesia. A thigh tourniquet was applied to maintain a bloodless field.

Access to the lesion was achieved via a medial malleolar osteotomy, which was routinely performed due to the typical location of the defect on the medial talar dome. All lesions were confirmed intraoperatively as full-thickness osteochondral lesions consistent with International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) Grade 4. The lesion site was debrided to remove damaged cartilage and sclerotic bone till healthy subchondral bone was exposed. Based on lesion size, one or two cylindrical osteochondral grafts were harvested from the non-weight-bearing lateral femoral condyle using a standardized plug harvesting system (COR™ PRECISION TARGETING SYSTEM; DePuy Mitek, Inc., Raynham, MA, USA).

Recipient sockets were prepared with matching reamers, and grafts were implanted using a press-fit technique, ensuring alignment with the surrounding talar cartilage. Care was taken to maintain surface congruity. Finally, the medial malleolus was anatomically repositioned and secured with cannulated screws.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Patients followed a non-weight-bearing protocol for six weeks, followed by gradual partial weight-bearing under physiotherapy. Full weight-bearing was allowed at eight weeks. Return to sport was individualized, typically permitted after six months based on radiological and functional recovery.

Outcome measures

Data collected included age, sex, BMI (Body Mass Index), operated side, alcohol consumption, lesion size (mm2), cyst presence, shoulder-type lesion status, and number of osteochondral plugs.

Lesion-related parameters—lesion size, cyst formation, and lesion morphology (e.g., shoulder involvement)—were assessed using preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Lesion size was calculated using the ellipse area formula: area = a × b × π ≈ coronal length × sagittal length × 0.79 [30].

Clinical status was measured preoperatively and at final follow-up using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Score (AOFAS), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, and Tegner Activity Scale. The Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS) was assessed at the final follow-up only [31–35]. Postoperative complications were defined as adverse events requiring medical or surgical intervention during follow-up, including anterior impingement, infection, and symptomatic hardware. Donor-site morbidity (anterior knee pain) was recorded and analyzed separately.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics v.26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons (smokers vs. non-smokers) used independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data. Changes from preoperative to final follow-up scores were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between pack-years of smoking and final functional scores (VAS, AOFAS, FAOS, and Tegner), with correlation coefficients classified as follows: 0.10–0.29 weak, 0.30–0.49 moderate, and ≥ 0.50 strong [36].

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Seventy-eight patients were included: 48 non-smokers (Group 1) and 30 smokers (Group 2). The mean follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 100.4 ± 14.7 months (range: 65–120 months). There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of age (p = 0.827), follow-up duration (p = 0.572), sex distribution (p = 1.000), BMI (p = 0.475), or alcohol consumption (p = 0.592) (Table 1).

Both groups showed significant improvements in Tegner, VAS, and AOFAS scores postoperatively compared to preoperative values (p < 0.001 for all intragroup comparisons). Tegner scores did not differ significantly between groups at preoperative (p = 0.426) or postoperative (p = 0.498) assessments.

Preoperative VAS scores were similar (p = 0.983); however, postoperative VAS scores were significantly lower in Group 1 (p = 0.011). AOFAS scores improved significantly in both groups, with higher postoperative scores in Group 1 (p = 0.008). The mean tobacco exposure among smokers, expressed in pack-years, was 23.65 ± 12.59. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a moderate negative correlation between pack-years and both FAOS (r = − 0.475, p = 0.008) and AOFAS (r = − 0.470, p = 0.009) scores. No statistically significant correlations were observed between pack-years and VAS (p = 0.081) or Tegner final scores (p = 0.922).

Postoperative complications occurred in both groups. No cases of osteotomy non-unions or graft failures were detected. One smoker (3.3%) developed a superficial wound infection, which resolved with antibiotics without surgical intervention. Implant-related irritation requiring hardware removal occurred in one smoker (3.3%) and two non-smokers (4.2%). Anterior ankle impingement, either bony or soft tissue, was observed in three smokers (10.0%) and four non-smokers (8.3%), all successfully treated with arthroscopic debridement. Overall complication rates were 5/30 (16.7%) in the smoker group and 6/48 (12.5%) in the non-smoker group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.607).

Donor-site morbidity was observed in 3/30 (10.0%) smokers and 4/48 (8.3%) non-smokers, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.802). All cases were managed conservatively.

Discussion

This study investigated clinical outcomes of AOT for OLTs, with a minimum five-year follow-up and a mean of approximately eight years. Both smokers and non-smokers showed significant improvements in pain and function after surgery, but non-smokers achieved better results in pain (VAS) and functional scores (AOFAS and FAOS).

Smoking disrupts bone and soft tissue healing through several biological mechanisms, including reduced microvascular perfusion, tissue hypoxia, impaired fibroblast activity, and altered collagen synthesis [37, 38]. Additionally, nicotine and other tobacco compounds negatively affect neovascularization and chondrogenic processes that are essential for cartilage and subchondral bone regeneration. These effects may underlie the poorer clinical outcomes observed in smokers. Supporting this, Schmal et al. reported significantly lower intra-articular concentrations of Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF)—key growth factors for osteochondral repair—in smokers [39].

The adverse effects of smoking on musculoskeletal healing have been demonstrated in multiple studies [1, 25, 27, 29, 40, 41]. However, its specific impact on articular cartilage repair remains inconclusive. Jaiswal et al. demonstrated that smokers undergoing ACI for full-thickness knee cartilage defects had worse baseline function and inferior postoperative outcomes, including higher graft failure rates [28]. In a systematic review, Kanneganti et al. reported that three studies found significantly poorer outcomes in smokers after cartilage surgery, while two studies reported no significant differences based on smoking status [27]. In a more recent study, no significant association was observed between smoking and clinical outcomes following MACI for chondral defects of the knee [26].

Previous studies on BMS have highlighted the detrimental impact of smoking on clinical outcomes in patients with OLTs [1, 24]. Cheng et al. [1] identified smoking as an independent risk factor associated with increased postoperative pain and reduced functional recovery following BMS, particularly in older patients and those with larger lesions. The current study reinforces and extends these findings by demonstrating that similar negative effects of smoking are also evident in patients treated with AOT. Additionally, Lee et al. [24] investigated the influence of smoking on outcomes after arthroscopic microfracture for OLTs. Although functional recovery did not differ significantly between smokers and non-smokers, postoperative MRI assessments showed significantly better cartilage repair quality in non-smokers, reflected by higher magnetic resonance observation of cartilage repair tissue (MOCART) scores.

Viehöfer et al. on the other hand, reported a significantly higher incidence of anterior ankle impingement requiring reoperation among smokers treated with AMIC for OLTs [25]. In contrast, our study found comparable reoperation rates due to anterior impingement between smokers and non-smokers following AOT. This difference may result from surgical technique variations: unlike AMIC, AOT does not involve the implantation of a scaffold material, which may influence postoperative tissue responses.

Considering cumulative tobacco exposure, a clear dose–response relationship emerged for functional outcomes. Specifically, higher pack-year values were moderately and significantly associated with lower FAOS and AOFAS scores, whereas no significant correlations were observed with VAS or Tegner scores. These findings suggest that the magnitude of lifetime tobacco exposure may impact postoperative functional recovery. This observation aligns with Jaiswal et al. [28], who reported a strong negative correlation between pack-years and functional outcomes after ACI.

Our findings highlight smoking as a significant and modifiable risk factor influencing clinical recovery after AOT. Despite comparable baseline characteristics—age, BMI, lesion size, and number of osteochondral plugs—smokers experienced higher residual pain and lower functional outcomes. These differences were consistent across patient-reported and clinician-assessed outcomes, supporting established biological mechanisms and prior clinical evidence. They reinforce smoking status as a key modifier of cartilage repair outcomes.

This study had several limitations. Its retrospective nature may introduce inherent selection bias. The lack of radiologic follow-up (e.g., MOCART scores) limits the ability to correlate clinical findings with structural graft incorporation. Furthermore, this single-center study may limit the generalizability of the results. Lastly, donor-site morbidity—while not frequently reported in our cohort—was assessed based on the presence or absence of anterior knee pain and analyzed statistically, without the use of additional quantitative scoring systems. Nonetheless, consistent functional and pain outcomes strengthen the study’s conclusions.

Despite these limitations, our findings have important clinical implications for patient-centered orthopedic care. Smoking status should be part of the preoperative assessment in cartilage repair patients. Incorporating smoking cessation counseling may improve outcomes.

Conclusion

Both smokers and non-smokers showed significant improvement after AOT. Nevertheless, smokers had significantly worse midterm pain and functional outcomes for talar osteochondral lesions, despite no significant difference in activity levels on Tegner scores. While all patients benefited from the procedure, non-smokers demonstrated superior clinical recovery. These results highlight the importance of integrating smoking cessation into the perioperative management of patients undergoing cartilage repair—especially for those with extensive or high-demand lesions.
