Atypical posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome following craniotomy for a metastatic lesion: illustrative case
Authors: Michael Sun, Kaitlyn F Melnick, Raquel Buser, Thiago Carneiro, Ivan Rocha Ferreira Da Silva, Maryam Rahman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAn 80-year-old male was found to have a metastatic lesion in his cerebrum. He underwent uncomplicated resection with prolonged inhalational anesthesia due to issues with intravenous access prior to surgery. Postoperatively, the patient had persistent altered mental status. Immediate postoperative head computed tomography and continuous electroencephalography were negative for possible causes.OBSERVATIONSStandard brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) after metastatic resection demonstrated the development of edema in his pons and thalami, concerning for posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES). Tight blood pressure control was initiated. His neurological examination returned to baseline. Follow-up MRI showed the resolution of edema.LESSONSThis is a report of PRES hypothesized to be attributable to prolonged inhalational anesthetic. PRES is a disorder in which patients present with altered mentation, seizures, visual impairment, or headache. PRES often occurs due to acute uncontrolled hypertension; however, other conditions, including autoimmune disease and cancer chemotherapy, have been linked. In severe cases, permanent brain damage and death have been reported. The authors present a case of PRES as the cause of severe neurological decline following craniotomy without severe or sustained hypertension. The purpose of this report is to present a case of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) as the cause of significant neurological decline following an uncomplicated resection of metastasis to the cerebrum. We hypothesize that PRES could be attributable to blood pressure variability in the perioperative period and prolonged anesthesia. We found no other case reports of PRES following craniotomy.

Illustrative Case

History and Physical Examination

An 80-year-old male with a medical history that included metastatic non–small cell lung cancer, atrial fibrillation, and hypertension presented to an outside hospital with a 1-week history of expressive aphasia. He was transferred to our institution for surgical evaluation after receiving steroids and antiepileptic medication. He denied any weakness, gait issues, or changes in vision. He was found to have a metastatic lesion in his left anterior superior temporal lobe (Fig. 1). The patient and his family elected resection. Surgical Procedure and Postoperative Follow-Up

The patient was brought to the operating room for a left craniotomy for metastatic resection. Upon entering the operating room, the patient’s systolic blood pressure ranged from 180 to 190 mm Hg prior to induction. General anesthesia was induced with rocuronium, propofol, and fentanyl. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane. Following induction, a significant portion of time was spent obtaining vascular access, including an attempt to place a central venous line. This resulted in a total of 3 hours of anesthesia time before starting surgery. The patient was positioned lateral, with the left side up and his head in a Mayfield head holder. Stereotactic registration was performed to identify an appropriate trajectory for resection. During the surgical procedure, the patient’s systolic blood pressure briefly registered 180–190 mm Hg. In response, 50 mg of esmolol was given, and the systolic blood pressure dropped to 75 mm Hg over the course of approximately 15 minutes. For the remainder of the case, the systolic blood pressure ranged from 100 to 150 mm Hg. The remainder of the surgical procedure proceeded without any apparent complications. The time for surgery was slightly less than 4 hours due to careful dissection of the tumor off the middle cerebral artery vessels.

In the immediate postoperative period, the patient was extubated; however, he did not regain consciousness. He was unresponsive to painful stimuli and would only briefly open his eyes. Immediately, a postoperative head computed tomography (CT) scan was obtained, showing no stroke or acute surgical emergency (Fig. 2). FIG. 2.Immediate postoperative head CT without contrast.

He was transferred to the intensive care unit, re-intubated, and placed on continuous electroencephalography (EEG), which showed encephalopathy but no evidence of seizures. Systolic blood pressure at the time of anesthesia handoff was 141 mm Hg and peaked to 189 mm Hg in the immediate postoperative period. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed overnight, revealing the interval development of significant edema in his pons and thalami.

The differential diagnoses based on the postoperative MRI were pseudohypoxic brain swelling (also known as “postoperative intracranial hypotension–associated venous congestion”), osmotic demyelination syndrome, and PRES. Unlike with pseudohypoxic brain swelling, the patient’s postoperative MRI did not have a high diffusion-weighted imaging signal and a low apparent diffusion coefficient signal. Furthermore, given the patient’s intraoperative hypertension, pseudohypoxic brain swelling was thought to be low on the differential. The distribution of edema on postoperative MRI was also concerning for possible osmotic demyelination syndrome, that is, acute demyelination in the setting of osmotic changes, typically with the rapid correction of hyponatremia. Usually associated with central pontine myelinolysis, extrapontine myelinolysis can also occur in the thalami. This was ruled out, as the patient’s preoperative sodium level was 139 mmol/L and his postoperative sodium level was 138 mmol/L. Furthermore, a high diffusion-weighted imaging signal in the pons was not seen. Because of prolonged exposure to sevoflurane and the patient’s elevated blood pressure in the perioperative period, PRES was deemed the most likely etiology.

At this time, he was started on strict blood pressure control, with systolic pressure < 140 mm Hg. His examination subsequently improved, and an interval brain MRI scan was obtained 6 days after his initial postoperative MRI, revealing resolution of the edema in the diencephalon. He was discharged home with family on postoperative day 7, neurologically intact (Fig. 3). Discussion

Observations

PRES is a clinical and radiographic diagnosis. In patients able to provide a history, the presence of a headache, visual impairment or loss, seizures, and medications taken are critical. Imaging is essential as the assessment is the gold standard for the diagnosis of PRES. Standard head CT scans are critical to assess for neurological emergencies that can explain acute changes in mentation, such as intracranial hemorrhages. Brain MRI is the modality of choice for the diagnosis of PRES, as vasogenic edema can be seen as a hyperintense signal on T2-weighted sequences in the parieto-occipital lobes, typically in a symmetric fashion. While edema most commonly occurs in the parieto-occipital lobes, diffuse edema can also occur in the anterior circulation, thalamus, and cerebellum, thus giving an atypical PRES picture.

This is the first case of PRES following craniotomy for resection of a metastatic lesion, although cases of PRES after general anesthesia have been described.1, 2 Classically, PRES is associated with hypertension.3 Our patient had a history of hypertension and had periods of profound hypertension during the procedure, but the majority of blood pressures were in the appropriate range. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane during the 7-hour operation. Sevoflurane is not typically used at our institution for brain tumor resection and could have contributed to the presentation of PRES in this patient. Total intravenous anesthesia target-controlled infusion with propofol and remifen­tanil could have been better suited for this case.

The effect of volatile agents on cerebral blood flow and cerebral autoregulation is still not completely understood. Studies on the use of volatile agents and cerebral blood flow in humans using transcranial Doppler and gas diffuse techniques report dose-dependent increases.4–7 However, MRI-based studies reveal minimal changes in global cerebral blood flow.8–10 Furthermore, studies specifically investigating sevoflurane have reported no change, increases, or decreases in cerebral blood flow.9, 11–17 At the time of the initial workup of severe neurological decline following resection, the initial differentials were intracerebral hemorrhage, stroke, or nonconvulsive status epilepticus. Immediate postoperative head CT and spot EEG were negative. Given that the patient was able to intermittently open his eyes and that there was a positive train of four, no paralytic remained on board. Additionally, basic laboratory values immediately after surgery, including glucose, were within normal limits. Osmotic demyelination could result in a similar fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) signal, but the patient had no changes in his sodium levels intraoperatively or postoperatively. The patient was previously on a chemotherapy regimen for his metastatic cancer, including pembrolizumab, carboplatin, and paclitaxel. Multiple chemotherapy agents have been implicated in the development of PRES. However, this patient was receiving chemotherapy until a hip fracture 4 months prior to craniotomy.

Management of PRES includes identifying possible etiologies such as, but not limited to, hypertension, liver disease, steroid use, cytotoxic medications, or immunosuppressants. After a thorough review of the patient’s medications, the most likely etiology of PRES was deemed to be the patient’s blood pressure fluctuations and prolonged use of sevoflurane. While the patient did receive steroids prior to surgery and there has been evidence that steroid use can cause PRES, steroids were continued due to the amount of vasogenic edema from the patient’s metastatic lesion. After identifying the underlying etiology, reversal or cessation is crucial for the management of PRES. In this patient’s case, his systolic blood pressure goal was decreased from the standard postoperative threshold of < 160 mm Hg to a threshold of < 140 mm Hg. There is no clear, specific, and established antihypertensive regimen for the treatment of acute hypertension in patients with PRES. Due to the rapid reversal of our patient’s PRES with minimal intervention, a combination of blood pressure fluctuations and prolonged exposure to inhaled anesthetic is likely the cause.

Lessons

Prolonged exposure to inhalational anesthesia for intracranial surgical cases should be avoided. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for PRES as a cause of severe neurological decline in the setting of negative head CT and EEG in patients with elevated blood pressure in the perioperative period. Tight blood pressure control and clinical monitoring should be enacted once PRES is recognized. We believe that the development of PRES in this patient was, in part, attributable to prolonged sevoflurane exposure. Protocols to obtain appropriate intravenous access prior to putting the patient under anesthesia would be of benefit and could have avoided this complication. Additionally, other anesthetics, including total intravenous anesthesia target-controlled infusion via propofol and remifentanil, could have been better suited for this case and brain tumor resection in general.
