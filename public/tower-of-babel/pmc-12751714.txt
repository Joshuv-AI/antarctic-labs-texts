Challenges and prospects in integrating abortion care with HIV/family planning services as a one-stop maternal health service in Ethiopia
Authors: Haile Bekele Adane, Lizeth Roets
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChallenges such as work overload, insufficient infrastructure, shortage of logistical supplies and healthcare providers and a standalone health service can hinder access to comprehensive healthcare. Integrated health services, therefore, can potentially improve service quality, lower costs, decrease facility visits, and reduce stigma and discrimination, particularly due to the religious, moral, cultural, and political factors surrounding abortion care.ObjectiveTo describe the challenges and potential advantages of integrating abortion care into HIV and family planning services.MethodA facility-based cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted in public health centers and hospitals. Stratified random sampling methods were used to include 413 abortion care users and 306 healthcare providers, respectively. Data from individuals seeking abortion care were collected by data collectors using a questionnaire, while data from healthcare providers were obtained through a self-administered questionnaire. The collected data underwent verification, cleaning, and entry into EpiData version 3.1 software, followed by exportation and analysis using SPSS version 25.ResultThe study included 413 women receiving abortion care and 306 healthcare providers from 58 public health facilities. Healthcare providers identified factors such as enhancing regulatory documents (n = 271; 65.6%), providing training (n = 271; 65.6%), improving infrastructure (n = 268; 64.9%), ensuring a consistent supply of medical resources (n = 253; 60.8%), program monitoring and evaluation (n = 231; 55.9%) and fostering interaction between healthcare facility units (n = 182; 44.1%) as motivating factors for implementing integrated health services. Abortion care users emphasized that making health facilities available (n = 181; 43.8%) and equipping them with necessary materials, equipment, and supplies (n = 288; 69.7%) can enhance integrated abortion care services. The collected data underwent verification, cleaning, and entry into EpiData version 3.1 software, followed by exportation and analysis using SPSS version 25.ConclusionSuccessful implementation of integrated women’s health services requires a strategic action plan addressing challenges and engaging with motivational factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13735-0.Keywords: Integration, Abortion, Abortion care, Family planning service, HIV service

Introduction

By the late 19th century, legal constraints on abortion services were pervasive globally, impeding women’s access to abortion care and restricting healthcare providers from delivering legally sanctioned services within health facilities [1, 2]. These restrictions were chiefly imposed by colonizing powers like Britain, France, Portugal, Spain, and Italy [1]. Nevertheless, over time, numerous nations, including Ethiopia, have undertaken reforms to their abortion laws [1, 3, 4]. Legislative changes have aimed to promote safer and more accessible abortion care. This is exemplified by the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health’s adoption of comprehensive abortion care in the second Technical and Procedural Guidelines for Safe Abortion Services, which includes offering family planning as part of abortion care [5].

Unsafe abortion practices result in an estimated 44,000 women’s deaths annually and cause permanent or temporary disabilities in approximately 7 million women [6]. Beyond maternal morbidity and mortality, it is important to know the broader impact on individuals, families and communities as well as the violations of women’s rights associated with the lack of safe abortion services. These unfortunate consequences could be substantially diminished with the widespread availability of comprehensive abortion services [7].

Unplanned pregnancies, often resulting in unsafe abortions as reported by the Central Statistics Authority and ICF International in 2016 [8], can be mitigated through effective access to family planning and safe abortion care services. Additionally, a comprehensive approach involves providing integrated services that incorporate abortion care into overall healthcare services. When healthcare services are delivered separately, it can lead to the inaccessibility of specific services, diminished service quality, and unnecessary increases in service costs, as highlighted by the World Health Organization in 2018 [9]. Increased financial burden on clients is frequently associated with multiple visits to health facilities on different days or in separate locations for various healthcare needs [10].

Integrating family planning and HIV services has shown notable benefits for women’s healthcare, particularly in resource-limited countries like Ethiopia, where the risks of unintended pregnancies and HIV transmission are high [11]. Strong evidence supports that such integration enhances the quality and accessibility of women’s health services [12]. Many countries have adopted integrated health service approaches, leading to improved care for women [13]. In settings with limited resources, like Ethiopia, combining abortion care with HIV and family planning services can also be a cost-effective strategy [14].

In modern health systems, integrated healthcare is essential for improving efficiency, aligning vertical programs, making better use of human resources, and enabling clients to access multiple services at one location [15, 16]. Women, in particular, benefit from the integration of family planning, voluntary HIV counselling and testing, and STI screening, which enhances service accessibility and overall care quality [17]. This approach also supports better health outcomes and system performance [15, 18].

Integrating HIV and family planning services contributes to the well-being of women, families, and communities [19, 20]. Expanding access to safe abortion care further helps prevent unsafe abortions, particularly those linked to unintended or unwanted pregnancies [21]. Overall, integrated health services promote comprehensive care delivery, improve health outcomes, boost efficiency, and support sustainable development [17].

However, integrated programs can encounter several challenges, including infrastructure problems, a scarcity of logistics, and a shortage of adequately trained healthcare providers [22]. Additional hurdles in this integrated approach include the lack of sufficient space and time constraints for counselling services [23]. Financial constraints and cultural issues further contribute to the complexity of providing health services [24].

Integrating abortion care with family planning and HIV/AIDS services brings significant benefits for both abortion care users and healthcare providers [22]. Integrating these services directly benefits healthcare providers by promoting stronger collaboration, increasing operational efficiency, and enabling the delivery of higher-quality care while also reducing service costs and reinforcing trust in the healthcare system [10]. Additionally, this integration helps overcome healthcare fragmentation, improves access to essential supplies, prevents redundant decision-making, reduces organizational inefficiencies, and enables more coordinated and timely responses to patient needs [24, 25]. This approach not only improves the delivery of care but also boosts healthcare providers’ job satisfaction by enabling them to offer higher-quality services to the community [22]. Client feedback from integrated services further enhances providers’ insight into their service performance and supports the development of clinical competencies and communication skills, encouraging constructive interactions among healthcare workers [27]. It decreases the number of client visits, potentially generating cost savings at the health system level [25].

At the client level, integrated services help strengthen privacy and confidentiality, thereby reducing stigma and discrimination. Utilising services within the same facility or area, whether delivered by the same or different providers, minimizes the need for multiple referrals [26], ultimately saving patients time, reducing the burden of repeated visits, and limiting time away from work and family responsibilities. Thus, this study aimed to explore the challenges and potential benefits of integrating abortion care into HIV/FP services, based on the perspectives of both healthcare providers and abortion care users, to inform improvement.

Methods

Design

A cross-sectional quantitative study design was employed to collect data at a single point in time, aiming to assess health services integration.

Study setting

The study was carried out in 58 public health facilities, including 8 public hospitals and 50 public health centers, that provided abortion care, HIV and family planning services, but not in an integrated manner, in the former Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPR) of Ethiopia. Among 121 public health facilities in the study areas providing abortion care, HIV and FP services, 58 were proportionally selected according to the number of facilities per zone and randomly within each zone.

SNNPR is one of the largest regions in Ethiopia, covering more than 10% of the country’s land area, with an estimated population of over 20 million, constituting almost a fifth of the country’s total population [28].

Population and sample

The study involved healthcare providers and abortion care users in public health centers and public health hospitals within the designated study areas. The total study population comprised 1860 health service providers, including 104 physicians, 366 midwives, 446 health officers, and 944 nurses. These professionals were actively working in public health facilities within the study region, as indicated by the 2018 annual report of the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region [28]. On the other hand, the population of interest among women of childbearing age (18–49 years old) who received abortion care in the Southern Region in 2018 was reported to be 46,276, as documented in the 2018 report from the SNNPR Health Bureau [28].

The determination of the required sample size followed the single population proportion formula. Given the absence of recorded studies indicating the provision of integrated abortion care with FP-HIV in public health facilities, a 50% proportion was employed to ensure the maximum sample size, aligning with the approach suggested by Bordens and Abbott in 2018 [29].

A simple random sampling method was applied to select 58 public health facilities from the total of 121 facilities existing in the region. Subsequently, a stratified sampling technique was utilized to enrol abortion care users between January 2021 and February 2021, and healthcare providers working in these facilities during the data gathering period. A total of 413 abortion care users and 306 healthcare providers were enrolled in the study. The stratified random sampling method guaranteed that the 306 healthcare providers were selected in proportion to their representation across the different healthcare facilities. Specifically, this included 157 nurses, 72 health officers, 60 midwives, and 17 physicians, based on their respective proportions. Of these proportion of healthcare providers, half were selected from the abortion care unit and the remaining from the HIV-Family planning service providing units. Parts of the administered questions were specific to their respective service-providing units.

Exclusion criteria

All abortion care users who were eligible were interviewed just before their discharge from the health facilities. Those whose age was less than 18 years were excluded from the study for ethical, legal, and methodological considerations. Similarly, healthcare providers who were eligible for the study in the study facilities were included. Those healthcare providers who did not work in an abortion care unit and HIV-FP services were excluded.

Data collection and analysis

The questionnaires were developed by the researcher following an extensive literature review on integrated health services covering abortion, family planning, and HIV. The data were collected from January 10, 2021, to February 15, 2021. Trained data collectors conducted interviews with abortion care users using a developed questionnaire before their discharge from public health facilities. The questionnaire was available in the local language, and each interview, which lasted approximately 35 min, was conducted accordingly. Healthcare providers completed the self-administered questionnaire, focusing on questions based on challenges and opportunities for integrating abortion care with other services. Trained public health officers distributed the questionnaires. Completed questionnaires were then placed in a designated box located in the office of the head of the health facility. All data were entered into EpiData 3.1 software and subsequently exported to Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (SPSS) version 25 for analysis. A descriptive analysis of the frequency and percentages of different variables and the bivariate and multivariate logistic regression was employed. The narrative data from the open-ended questions were also open-coded and thematically analyzed. The results were presented using tables and narratives.

Validity and reliability

Validity and reliability are generally considered complementary, but there are situations where a trade-off may exist, with high validity associated with lower reliability and vice versa [30]. In this context, validity refers to the capacity of an instrument or questionnaire to measure and provide true results [31, 32]. The questionnaire was assessed and approved by the Scientific Review Committee of the custodian university, and a pretest was conducted to enhance the validity and reliability of the questionnaires.

Reliability, on the other hand, refers to the consistency of results yielded by a measuring instrument over time and across different observers [33]. The questionnaires were pretested on 10% of the total sample size. Public health specialists reviewed the data collection tools for both abortion care users and healthcare providers before and after the pretest. Based on the findings, necessary adjustments were made to the pretested questionnaires prior to the actual data collection. The pre-test findings also demonstrated that participants had a uniform understanding of the questions. The main study findings further supported this consistency, indicating the reliability of the questionnaires.

Ethics

Ethics approval for the research was granted by the Health Studies Research Committee of the Department of Health Studies at the University of South Africa (Certificate number: HSHDC/923/2019). Additionally, permission to conduct the research was secured from all selected health facilities, adhering to the South Region Health Bureau Human Research and Technology Transfer Support Core Process (Certificate number: /19/492 12//102012). Prior to commencing data collection, an institutional support letter from the relevant health bureau in the study area was obtained.

To ensure voluntary participation, abortion care users and healthcare providers were provided with a recruitment letter. Respondents were given the option to read the letter themselves or have it read aloud to them if needed. Following this, individuals who volunteered to participate signed written consent forms, affirming their willingness to be part of the study.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Among 422 eligible abortion care users, 413 enrolled in the study, making the response rate 97.9%. The age range of abortion care users varied between 18 and 44 years, with a significant proportion, 275 (66.6%), falling within the 20–29 age bracket. In terms of education, 122 (29.5%) did not have any formal education (no schooling), 207 (50.1%) attended elementary or high school education and 84 (20.3%) held a diploma or degree.

Occupationally, 182 (44.1%) respondents identified as housewives, 59 (14.3%) were self-employed, 56 (13.6%) were public servants, and 53 (12.8%) were students. Regarding marital status, 263 (63.7%) of abortion care users were married, while 150 (36.3%) were single, including those who were divorced, widowed, separated, or unmarried but in a relationship.

The study found that the mean monthly income of respondents was US $96.5, with a standard deviation of + US $65.7 and a median income of US $77.5 (See Table 1).

Service utilization by abortion care users

This study focused on women primarily seeking abortion care at public healthcare facilities. The majority of respondents, 329 (79.7%), specifically requested abortion care only, while 76 (18.4%) sought both abortion care along family planning (FP) or HIV/AIDS services. Only 8 (1.9%) of respondents requested all three services concurrently. During counselling sessions offered by healthcare providers, 139 (33.6%) abortion care users received family planning services, while 162 (39.2%) accessed HIV/AIDS services. Overall, 188 (45.5%) of abortion care users received either family planning, HIV/AIDS or both Family planning and HIV/AIDS integrated services.

Among abortion care users who received integrated services (n = 188), 113 (60%) respondents received the services in abortion care units, 52 (27.6%) in HIV-FP rooms, 12 (6.3%) in delivery rooms, 6 (3.2%) in Antenatal Care (ANC) /prevention of mother to child transmission of HIV (PMTCT) rooms, and 5 (2.6%) in outpatient departments. Regarding the delivery of integrated services, 111 (59%) abortion care users received services in the same room by the same healthcare provider, 46 (24.5%) in the same room but by different healthcare providers (with one providing one service and another offering the next service), and 31 (16.5%) respondents had to move to another room to receive additional care from a different healthcare provider.

While only 188 (45.5%) respondents experienced integrated healthcare services in this study, a significant majority, 332 (80.4%), expressed their wish utilizing integrated healthcare in the future. However, 81 (19.6%) respondents indicated a reluctance to opt for integrated health services even in the future. The reasons given included the desire to receive only abortion care, concerns about long waiting times, fear of stigma and discrimination associated with abortion-related issues, confidentiality concerns, and a perceived decline in the quality of health services.

Healthcare providers

Out of 310 healthcare providers, 306 participated in the study, resulting in a response rate of 98.7%. Among respondents, 164 (53.6%) were female, their ages ranging from 21 to 45 years. More than half, 157 (51.35%) of healthcare providers were nurses by profession, while the remaining were health officers, midwives and medical doctors. Notably, 109 (35.6%) healthcare providers had 36 months or longer work experience, while only 30 (9.8%) respondents had served between 0 and 11 months in their current roles (See Table 2).

Healthcare providers’ capacity

The majority of healthcare providers, 251 (82%), in this study received one or more types of in-service training to enhance their capacity to provide healthcare services. Among those who underwent in-service training (n = 251), 124 (49.4%) received comprehensive abortion care (safe and post abortion care) training, 114 (45.4%) received training in long-acting family planning (FP) services, 81 (32.3%) in short-acting FP training, and 71(28.3%) in HIV counseling in-service training. Of the 251 healthcare providers who received in-service training, 132 (52.6%) received training on integrated health services. HIV-FP integrated training, abortion-FP integrated training, abortion-HIV integrated training and abortion and HIV-FP integrated training were received by healthcare providers 60 (45.5%), 49 (37.1%), 20 (15.2%) and 3 (1.9%), respectively. Regarding the distribution of training, 184 (73.3%) healthcare providers received only one type of in-service training, 58 (23,1%) received two types, 6 (2.4%) received training in three areas, and only 3 (1.2%) respondents received training in all four areas.

In terms of the recency of training, 65 healthcare providers received in-service training within the last 12 months, 74 between 12 and 23 months ago, 47 between 24 and 35 months ago, and 35 respondents received training more than 36 months ago. The organizations responsible for providing in-service training varied, with 181 respondents receiving training from NGOs, 73 from the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region health bureau, 44 from the Federal Ministry of Health, and the remaining 30 respondents receiving training from zonal health departments (see Table 2).

In terms of awareness of regulatory documents, 125 respondents reported knowing of at least one abortion-related regulatory document, 141 were aware of at least one family planning (FP)-related document, 105 were aware of at least one HIV/AIDS-related document, and only 35 indicated awareness of any regulatory document related to integrated health services (see Table 3). It is important to note that this data reflects whether respondents were aware of the presence of such regulatory documents, not their familiarity with or understanding of the specific content. Respondents collectively identified seven different types of abortion-related regulatory documents, nine related to FP, six concerning HIV/AIDS, and seven addressing integrated health services, all of which were perceived as supporting service provision.

Factors influencing receipt of integrated health services

The association of factors with integrated health services was assessed using both bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. In the bivariate analysis, women with a monthly average income of 161–200 USD were three times more likely to receive integrated HIV-FP services compared to those with incomes ranging from 0 to 40 USD. Similarly, abortion care users who received integrated HIV-FP service free of charge were three times more likely, and those who paid out of pocket were four times more likely to use the integrated HIV-FP services compared to those whose payment was covered by health insurance. Those who had previously utilized family planning services demonstrated a statistically significant association with integrated health services, with a COR of 2.804 (95% CI: 1.744 to 4.508). However, the previous history of abortion and place of residence showed no statistical association with integrated services in the bivariate analysis (see Table 6).

In the multivariate logistic regression, abortion care users who had previously used family planning (FP) services were four times more likely to use FP services compared to those who had never used them, with an Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) of 4.299 and a 95% Confidence Interval (CI) of (2.399, 7.704), and a p-value of 0.0001 (see Table 7). However, the place of residence and the previous history of abortion were found to have no statistically significant association with the utilization of integrated services.

Discussion

The standalone or vertical provision of health services may not effectively promote the utilization of integrated health services, as highlighted by UNAIDS in 2015 [17]. Offering integrated health services from the same or different healthcare providers simultaneously and at the same location has been demonstrated to enhance the uptake of various health services, as emphasized by Samuel, Fetters, and Desta in 2016 [34]. In this study, among the 413 abortion care users, 188 received integrated health services, and the majority (n = 332; 80.5%) expressed a preference for utilizing integrated healthcare in the future.

The preference for integrated healthcare is further supported by the efficiency gained when the same healthcare provider offers integrated services in the same room, minimizing waiting times for clients, as suggested by USAID et al. in 2015 [17]. Conversely, receiving healthcare services from different providers in different rooms can potentially increase waiting times as clients move between rooms to receive different services from different healthcare providers [26]. Nonetheless, even when clients visit different rooms, there is still a reduction in the number of visits, thereby decreasing travel time and costs, as noted by Al-Saddique in 2018 [10].

While the advantages of integrated health services are significant, some perceived disadvantages by abortion care users include increased waiting time, fear of stigma and discrimination, and increased workload. However, strategies can be implemented to address these challenges. For instance, using the same consulting room for different services can reduce waiting times, as previously mentioned. Structural integration, effective staff management, enhancing providers’ skills and knowledge, empowering decision-makers, promoting teamwork, and implementing work-sharing are crucial strategies to overcome the challenges associated with integrated healthcare, as highlighted by Mayhew, Hopkins, and Warren in 2017 [35]. These approaches aim to optimize the benefits of integrated services while mitigating potential drawbacks, ensuring a more seamless and patient-friendly healthcare experience.

In delivering integrated health services to clients, healthcare providers, whether they are new or existing staff, require either pre-service or in-service training. In this study, the majority (82%) of healthcare providers received one or more types of in-service training, enabling them to provide integrated healthcare services. However, the findings of a study across five countries raise concerns, as two-thirds of healthcare providers in those settings lacked adequate training and were unfamiliar with guidelines on offering integrated healthcare services [36], even as they attempted to implement integrated services.

Professional development on integrated health services through in-service training is crucial for enhancing the provision of quality healthcare, as emphasized by the WHO in 2016 [37]. Healthcare providers need ongoing knowledge, skills, training, and professional development opportunities to maintain and update their capacity, ensuring they can continuously improve the quality of services they deliver. The government of Ethiopia is actively addressing healthcare providers’ professional development through in-service training [38] and working to improve the integration of women’s health services [8]. These efforts underscore the commitment to enhancing the skills and capabilities of healthcare providers to provide effective integrated healthcare services.

The availability and utilization of integrated regulatory documents in public health facilities play a crucial role, serving as a guide for healthcare workers in providing integrated health services, as highlighted by Borges et al. in 2015 [39]. However, challenges exist, with studies indicating that guidelines are often not readily available in public health facilities. Even when available, healthcare providers may neglect to use these guidelines during their health service consultations, as noted by Tafese, Woldie, and Megerssa in 2013 [40]. This gap highlights the need to improve both the availability of and compliance with integrated regulatory documents to support the effective implementation and delivery of integrated healthcare services in public health facilities.

Supplies such as gloves, antiseptic solutions [39], cotton balls or gauze sponges, analgesics, anaesthetics [5], needles, syringes, pregnancy test kits, blood pressure apparatus, and stethoscopes are essential for providing quality abortion services, as highlighted by Tafese, Woldie, and Megerssa in 2013 [40]. A lack of or shortage of medical supplies was identified as one of the factors hindering the provision of integrated health services [22]. Within this study, the great majority of respondents reported having enough supplies for integrated services. For instance, 96.7% reported plenty of gauze sponges or cotton balls, 95.4% reported having antiseptic solutions and 98% had gloves.

The unavailability of medical supplies not only decreases efficiency and the horizontalization of programs but also leads to the inappropriate use of human resources [15, 16]. Ensuring the availability of necessary medical supplies in health facilities is crucial for enhancing the provision of integrated health services, as emphasized by Kurpas et al. in 2021 [41], and it contributes to promoting the health status of mothers by reducing the risk of unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and sexually transmitted infections like HIV/AIDS [42].

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, a significant statistical association was observed between women who were exempt from service charges and those whose payments were covered by insurance. The average monthly income of the abortion care users was much lower than the national average per capita income of $863 [43]. Therefore, efforts should be made to improve the integration of women’s health services among health insurance users in the community. Abortion care users who had previously utilized family planning (FP) services were four times more likely to use FP-HIV services compared to those who had never used them. This suggests that women who have a history of using FP services are more inclined to utilize FP-HIV services after undergoing an abortion. The increased likelihood among these women can be attributed to their existing knowledge and information about contraceptives, making them more receptive to accessing FP-HIV services following an abortion, as highlighted by Dasa, Kassie, Roba, Wakwoya, and Kelel in 2019 [44]. This underscores the importance of previous FP service utilization in influencing subsequent decisions regarding integrated health services following an abortion.

Conclusion

The findings of this study revealed that integrated health services significantly improve service uptake compared to standalone services. Most abortion care users expressed a preference for integrated healthcare, underscoring its relevance for future service delivery. Providing integrated services under one roof enhances efficiency by reducing client waiting times, minimizing the number of visits required, and saving both time and costs [26]. In this study, 82% of healthcare providers had received in-service training, emphasizing the importance of adequate training in delivering integrated services effectively. However, the unavailability and inconsistent use of integrated regulatory documents were identified as barriers to successful service delivery [40]. The study confirmed the critical role of access to and compliance with these documents within public health facilities. Additionally, the consistent availability of medical supplies is essential for the effective provision of integrated health services and for improving maternal health outcomes [41, 42]. The findings also indicated that abortion care users with a history of family planning use were more likely to utilize integrated FP-HIV services, suggesting that prior exposure to family planning enhances the uptake of integrated care [44]. Overall, integrated maternal health services have the potential to significantly improve community health, particularly the health outcomes of women and children.

Limitations of the study

Not all public health facilities in the study areas were included in this study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, to minimize potential bias and enhance representativeness, the researchers employed a random sampling method and included public health facilities from both rural and urban areas.
