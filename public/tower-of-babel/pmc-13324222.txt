Reference values for relative sit-to-stand power in Chinese middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Fan Zhang
Published: 2026

Introduction

Lower-limb muscle power is a key determinant of functional independence, mobility, and risk of adverse health outcomes in middle-aged and older adults. The sit-to-stand (STS) test has emerged as a simple, practical, and widely used measure to assess lower-extremity function in both clinical and epidemiological settings [1]. Recently, the estimation of relative STS power, which incorporates body size and performance time, has gained attention as a more sensitive indicator of neuromuscular function than traditional strength measures [2]. Despite its growing use, Chinese reference values remain limited. Given potential differences in body composition, physical performance, and aging trajectories, establishing normative data for Chinese adults is essential for accurate assessment and risk stratification. Therefore, this study aimed to develop age- and sex-specific reference values for relative STS power among Chinese middle-aged and older adults to support clinical screening and population-based assessment.

Methods

This analysis was based on data from Wave 3 of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Individual sampling weights provided by CHARLS were applied to all analyses. The CHARLS study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Participants younger than 45 years were excluded. We further excluded individuals with missing data on age, sex, height, or performance in the 5-time STS test. To ensure data quality, observations with implausible values for height or STS test performance were identified and removed according to predefined criteria. After exclusions, 14,715 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. S1).

In CHARLS, lower-extremity function was assessed using the 5-time STS test following a standardized protocol. Participants were instructed to rise from a chair and sit down five times as quickly as possible with arms folded across the chest. Timing began at the initial movement and ended upon completion of the fifth stand. The total time (seconds) was recorded, with shorter times indicating better performance. Relative STS power (W/kg) was calculated as [3]: 0.9 × g × [((0.5 × height) - chair height)/(STS time × 0.1)], where the chair height is 0.47 m, g is 9.81 m/s2.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as counts and weighted percentages. For relative STS power, sex- and age-specific percentile distributions (P5, P10, P20, P30, P40, P50, P60, P70, P80, P90, and P95) were estimated. Percentiles of relative STS power were estimated using weighted empirical quantiles incorporating individual sampling weights provided by CHARLS. These weights account for differential probabilities of selection and non-response, allowing estimates to better reflect the national population distribution. Each observation contributed proportionally to its sampling weight. Age was categorized into 5-year intervals (45–50, 50–55, 55–60, 60–65, 65–70, 70–75, 75–80, 80–85, and ≥85 years). Given the descriptive aim of establishing reference values, no hypothesis testing or regression modeling was performed. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Results

A total of 14,715 participants were included (mean [SD] age, 60 [10] years; 52.3% women) (Table S1). The largest age group was 60–65 years (19.4%), followed by 50–55 years (18.3%). The mean (SD) body mass index was 23.8 (3.7) kg/m², and mean height was 1.58 (0.09) m. The mean (SD) time for the 5-time STS test was 9.6 (3.7) seconds, corresponding to a mean (SD) relative STS power of 3.33 (1.20) W/kg. Most participants resided in urban areas (62.6%) and reported good or better health (78.0%). The majority had no limitations in activities of daily living (81.8%) and had education below junior high school (89.3%). The mean (SD) number of chronic conditions was 1.96 (1.68).

Relative STS power declined progressively with advancing age in both sexes (Table S2 and Fig. 1). Among women, the median (P50) decreased from 3.24 W/kg in those aged 45–50 years to 1.65 W/kg in those older than 85 years. Similarly, in men, the median declined from 4.40 W/kg to 2.07 W/kg across the same age range. Across all age groups, men consistently demonstrated higher relative STS power than women. The distribution of relative STS power showed a gradual leftward shift with age. For example, among women, the 90th percentile decreased from 4.59 W/kg at ages 45–50 years to 2.78 W/kg in those older than 85 years, while the 10th percentile declined from 2.24 W/kg to 0.93 W/kg. Comparable patterns were observed in men, with the 90th percentile decreasing from 6.28 W/kg to 3.51 W/kg and the 10th percentile from 2.94 W/kg to 1.22 W/kg, corresponding to an approximate 49% decline in women and 53% decline in men across the age spectrum.Fig. 1Age- and sex-specific distribution of relative sit-to-stand power.Age-related distributions of relative sit-to-stand (STS) power (W/kg) by sex. Discussion

This study provides the first nationally representative reference values for relative STS power in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Although the age distribution of the sample was uneven, the application of sampling weights mitigates this issue by ensuring that all estimates are representative of the underlying population structure. The observed reduction in STS power with advancing age likely reflects the combined effects of sarcopenia, reduced neuromuscular efficiency, and declines in physical performance. Higher values in men compared with women across all age groups are consistent with known differences in muscle mass and strength [4,5]. These reference values may facilitate the identification of individuals with low muscle power in both clinical and community settings and support early interventions targeting functional decline and sarcopenia.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding age-related changes. Second, although standardized protocols were used, measurement error in the 5-time STS test and height assessment is possible. Third, residual confounding may exist due to unmeasured factors such as comorbidities, physical activity, and direct measures of muscle mass. Finally, the findings may not be generalizable beyond the Chinese population.

In conclusion, we established age- and sex-specific reference values for relative STS power in a nationally representative sample. These findings provide a useful benchmark for assessing lower-extremity function and may inform risk stratification and intervention strategies in aging populations.
