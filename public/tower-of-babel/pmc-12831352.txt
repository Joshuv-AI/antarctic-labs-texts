Trimester-specific association of maternal prenatal physical activities with risk of preterm birth: a prospective pre-birth cohort study
Authors: Jiajin Hu, Yanan Ma, Wenhan Jia, Ningyu Wan, Xiaochuan Wang, Lu Zheng, Zhe Yang, Dongming Zheng, Jun Wei, Deliang Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine the trimester-specific association of maternal prenatal physical activities (PA) with risk of preterm birth (PTB). We recruited 2080 women during the first trimester and conducted follow-ups during the second and third trimesters and at birth. We assessed maternal PA during each trimester and calculated a cumulative PA score range from 0 to 3, with a higher score reflecting higher frequency and a sustained high level of PA over pregnancy. Lower risks of PTB were observed among women who had higher PA levels during the first [tertile 3 (T3) vs. tertile 1 (T1): odds ratio (OR) = 0.48; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.32–0.71, P for trend = 0.003], second (T3 vs. T1: OR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.22–0.52, P for trend < 0.001), and third trimesters (T3 vs. T1: OR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.18–0.45, P for trend = 0.008), respectively. A higher prenatal cumulative PA score significantly correlated with a lower risk of PTB (3 vs. 0: OR = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.11–0.54, P for trend < 0.001) and iatrogenic preterm birth (3 vs. 0: OR = 0.23; 95% CI: 0.09–0.59, P for trend < 0.001) in a dose–response-dependent manner, but not with spontaneous preterm birth. These findings support the necessity of guiding the maternal PA from early pregnancy and highlight the importance of sustained PA to prevent PTB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08518-8.Keywords: Preterm birth, Physical activity, Birth cohort, Iatrogenic preterm birth, Spontaneous preterm birth

Introduction

The global prevalence of preterm births (PTBs) reached 11.1% during the last decade, becoming a challenging clinical and public health problem [1]. In children under 5 years old, PTB complications account for 16% of all deaths and 35% of newborn infant deaths [2]. Even though the mechanism of PTB is unclear, prenatal behavioral factors, such as maternal physical activity (PA), have been shown to contribute to the risk of PTB [3–6]. Compared with genetic and physiological risk factors (e.g., pre-eclampsia), maternal PAs are modifiable, which may provide a practical route for pregnant women to prevent PTB [2].

Data on the relationship between prenatal PA and PTB are conflicting [6, 7]. One meta-analysis illustrated a nonlinear correlation between prenatal PA and PTB risk, with the lowest risk observed among women performing 2–4 h of PA per week [6]; another meta-analysis indicated that pregnant women with higher occupational physical workloads were more likely to develop PTB [7]. One possible reason for such inconsistent results may be that most of the studies in which maternal PA status is assessed are cross-sectional in nature and may therefore not reflect the PA level throughout pregnancy [3, 4, 8–13]. Women may modify their activity or exercise behaviors during different periods of pregnancy [14], however, to our knowledge, only a few studies have examined the association between PA across different trimesters and PTB risks, for example [15], a previous pre-birth cohort study in the United States illustrated sustained low leisure-time PA across pregnancy was associated with lower risk of PTB, however, the study did not examine the sensitive trimester during which prenatal PA influence on PTB risks.

Furthermore, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guideline has recommended 30 min of moderate-intensity exercise [≥ 3 metabolic equivalent (MET)] on most weekdays for pregnant women to achieve better perinatal outcomes, however, the guideline failed to highlight the health benefits of prenatal PA in different trimesters and the role of a sustained high level of PA across three trimesters physical activity on preventing disadvantage perinatal outcomes, such as PTB [16]. This is important because most of pregnant women will reduce physical activity level during the third trimester and are less likely to meet that recommended level of PA [14]. In late pregnancy, a reduction in PA is associated higher incidence of excessive weight gain and gestational diabetes, which may further lead to PTB [15]. To our knowledge, the evidence on whether a sustained high level of PA across all three trimesters reduces the PTB risk is limited.

We aimed to fill these gaps by studying the trimester-specific association between maternal PA and the PTB risk, using data from the China Medical University Birth Cohort (CMUBC) Study. We further examined the protective association of a sustained high level of PA throughout pregnancy with PTB. We hypothesized that pregnant women who maintain a high level of PA throughout pregnancy have the lowest risk of PTB.

Methods

Study sample

The present analysis was based on the CMUBC Study, the design of which was previously described [17]. In short, 2639 pregnant women were enrolled at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University from May 2018 to September 2023. Participants were enrolled during early pregnancy (mean ± standard deviation weeks of gestation: 10.0 ± 2.6 weeks) based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) weeks of gestation < 14 weeks at the time of recruitment; (2) volunteered or agreed to participate in the project; (3) currently lives in Shenyang and has no plan to move away for the next 3 years; and (4) planning to give birth at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University. Follow-ups were conducted at mid pregnancy (24.1 ± 2.5 weeks of gestation), late pregnancy (32.4 ± 2.1 weeks), and at birth. During each visit, face-to-face interviews were conducted to obtain socio-demographic, environmental, and behavioral information. Participants’ clinical information was obtained from their medical records. In the present analysis, we further excluded 289 multiple pregnancies and 270 women with miscarriages before 28 + 0 gestational weeks or no recorded birth outcome. The remaining 2080 pregnant women were included in the final analysis, all of whom had provided written informed consent for inclusion in the study and were not minors or illiterates. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University in 2018 (2018PS381K). Informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study.

Assessment of PA

We used the Chinese version of the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ) to assess participants’ PA at the first-, second- and third-trimester visits. The PPAQ is the most used and recommended self-administered questionnaire to measuring pregnant women’s physical activity, which has higher reliability and validity compared with other physical activity questionnaires such as the Leisure Time Physical Activity Questionnaire and the Recent Physical Activity Questionnaire, according to a previous systematic review study [18]. It has the advantages of being inexpensive and acceptable, compared with objective measures of PA such as accelerometers, which is important in a large population measurement. For Chinese pregnant women, the PPAQ has demonstrated high reliability between total activity and sub-activities according to type and intensity (Intraclass correlation coefficients = 0.77) and moderate validity between the PPAQ data and accelerometer data (Spearman correlation coefficients = 0.35, P < 0.01) [19]. The questionnaire is used to assess a pregnant woman’s total PA volume level based on 31 different PA items, which can be grouped into sedentary-intensity, low-intensity, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity PA subgroups. Research staffs conducted face-to-face interviews to all pregnant women during each visit with the questionnaire, and women were asked to answer according to the PA status of the last week. All activities were converted to METs (1 MET equals 3.5 mL oxygen consumption per kilogram body weight per minute [20], and the total maternal PA volume (MET-h/week) was categorized into tertile groups for subsequent analysis. To further investigate the dose–response association between women’s sustained PA status across three trimesters and the PTB risk, we calculated their prenatal cumulative PA scores (CPASs) following a previous study [21]. Specifically, we computed CPASs by calculating the cumulative number of trimesters that a woman was at high PA volume (reached top tertile of PA volume during the corresponding trimester). The CPAS range from 0 to 3, with a score of 3 indicating a maintenance of high levels of PA throughout the pregnancy (0 means that none of trimesters that the volume of maternal physical activity is in the highest tertile).

Assessment of PTBs

PTB was defined as a live birth giving birth before 37 + 0 gestational weeks [22]. The gestational age was determined using the last menstrual cycle date and was confirmed via ultrasound during the early stages of pregnancy. Experienced gynecologists diagnosed subtypes of PTB. An iatrogenic preterm birth (IPTB) was defined as an induction of labor or cesarean section without labor before 37 gestational weeks according to the maternal and child health status [23], and both spontaneous preterm labor and preterm premature rupture of membranes were diagnosed as spontaneous preterm birth (SPTB).

Assessment of covariates

Using standard questionnaires, we collected sociodemographic and behavioral information of pregnant women, such as age at enrollment, ethnicity (Han vs. others), educational attainment (high school or below vs. college or above), household income per year (< 50 000 Chinese yuan vs. ≥ 50 000 Chinese yuan), current smoking status (no vs. yes), parity (primipara vs. multipara), and child sex (male vs. female). We also measured the calorie intake of the women during each trimester using a previously developed semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire [24]. The questionnaire contains 25 food items, and presented high reproducibility and validity with Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.80 (vegetables) to 0.43 (tubers) between the two repeated FFQ assessments and Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.60 (vegetables) to 0.25 (whole grains) between the FFQ and a three-day food diary. In the present analysis, we excluded three low-consumption items in our study sample (tea, coffee, and alcohol) for pregnant women in our study, leaving 22 food items in the present analysis. Pregnant women reported their consumption of the 22 foods and food groups over the past 3 months based on nine frequency categories ranging from “almost never” to “ ≥ 3 times per day.” The portion sizes of 1260 local pregnant women were calculated using 3-day food diaries as the median amount of food consumed by pregnant women in a single meal. We combined women’s eating frequency and, portion size, and calculated women’s daily energy intake according to the Chinese Food Composition database [25]. Women’s body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. We calculated participants’ gestational weight gain using the measured pre-delivery weight minus the self-reported pre-pregnancy body weight, and categorized their gestational weight gain into three categories (insufficient vs., appropriate vs., and excessive) using the standard of recommendation for weight gain during the pregnancy period for Chinese women [26]. Women’s gestational diabetes mellitus status was assessed with oral glucose tolerance tests at mid pregnancy (median gestational age of 25.1 weeks, interquartile range: 1.9), and diagnosed according to the International Association of Diabetes Pregnancy Study Group Standards: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h plasma glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h plasma glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [27]. We also collected maternal pre-eclampsia/eclampsia status (yes vs. no) from the medical records.

Statistical analyses

We compared the characteristics of women with PTBs with those of women with full-term births using a one-way analysis of variance or chi-square test. We also compared women’s first-trimester PA status between miscarried women and their counterparts. We used Pearson correlation analysis to assess the association between physical activity at different trimesters. Generalized estimating equation models were used to examine the longitudinal relationships between maternal PA level over three trimesters and the risk of PTB and PTB subtypes. We calculated the average level of PA over the three trimesters and use logistic models to examine the association with PTB for sensitivity analysis. In the trimester-specific analysis, multivariable logistic regression models were used to analyze the associations between maternal PA volume level (in tertiles) in the three trimesters and the risk of PTB and PTB subtypes, respectively. In the dose–response analysis, we used multivariable logistic regression models to analyze the relationship of the CPAS with the risk of PTB and PTB subtypes. Crude and adjusted models were used: model I was the crude model; model II adjusted for age at enrollment, household income per year, ethnicity, educational attainment, and parity; model III further adjusted for BMI and energy intake. For sensitivity analyses, we further adjusted for the maternal PA in the previous trimester in the analysis of second and third trimesters to estimate the independent correlation between maternal PA status and PTB, because the association between maternal PA and PTB risk in the later trimesters may be influenced by maternal PA in the previous trimester. We also adjusted for women’s gestational weight gain, gestational diabetes mellitus, and pre-eclampsia/eclampsia status for sensitivity analyses, to examine whether the associations were mediated by the pregnancy complications. We used the median value of each tertile of PA and CPAS as a continuous variable to examine the linear trend. We used median values to impute missing values of the covariates in the generalized estimating equation models and used multiple imputations to impute missing values in the logistic regression models. All statistical analyses were performed with Stata SE (version 16; StataCorp, College Station, TX). A two-side p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the pregnant women included in the final analysis, the incidence of PTB was 8.60% (179/2080). Women with PTBs were older and had a higher pre-pregnancy BMI, higher daily energy intake during the first trimester and lower daily energy intake during the third trimester, lower PA level during all trimesters, higher rates of insufficient gestational weight gain, gestational diabetes mellitus and pre-eclampsia/eclampsia than women with full-term birth. No significant differences in ethnicity, educational attainment, household income per year, parity, smoking status, energy intake during the second trimesters, or child sex was observed between women with PTB and full-term births (Table 1). Maternal PA levels were significantly correlated across the three trimesters (r = 0.49–0.66, P < 0.001) (Table S1). We did not observe significant differences on maternal first-trimester PA volume between women who were miscarriage and their counterparts.

Longitudinal associations of maternal PA with PTB risk and subtypes

The generalized estimating equation models revealed that higher PA levels across pregnancy were associated with a lower risk of both PTB [tertile 3 (T3) vs. tertile 1 (T1): odds ratio (OR) = 0.45; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.30–0.66, P for trend < 0.001] and IPTB (T3 vs. T1: OR = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.23–0.59, P for trend < 0.001). No significant association was observed between maternal PA and SPTB risks (Table 2). Further adjusted for women’s pregnancy complications did not change the results in general (Table S2). Results remained the same in general when analyzing the average physical activity levels over three trimesters with the risk of PTB in logistic regression models (Table S3).

Trimester-specific associations between maternal PA status and risk of PTB

Maternal PA status during all trimesters was negatively associated with the risk of PTB (Table 3). Specifically, compared with pregnant women with a lower PA level, women with a higher PA level in the first (T3 vs. T1: OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.38–0.83, P for trend < 0.001), second (T3 vs. T1: OR = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.29–0.67, P for trend < 0.001), and third (T3 vs. T1: OR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.36–0.86, P for trend < 0.001) trimesters had lower PTB risks. Further adjusting for maternal PA in the previous trimester did not substantially modify the results in the second trimester, while the results in the third trimester did not reach significance (Table S4).

Discussion

In this prospective pre-birth cohort study, we observed that higher levels of prenatal physical activity during the first, second and third trimesters were related to a lower risk of preterm birth and iatrogenic preterm birth, respectively. Women with the highest prenatal cumulative physical activity scores, which indicates a sustained high level of physical activity throughout pregnancy, had the lower risks of preterm birth and iatrogenic preterm birth in a dose–response manner.

To our knowledge, few studies regarding the association of PA in different trimesters with PTB have been conducted [5, 28]. Most across-trimester studies are derived from cross-sectional measurements among different participants that are aggregated to describe PA in each trimester, limiting their ability to reveal trimester-specific associations [29]. In the Brazil Pelotas Birth Cohort study, a higher PA level during the third trimester but not the other trimesters were observed to be negatively associated with PTB risk among 4,139 pregnant women [5, 28], however, the study did not examine the association between PA and PTB subtypes. Our study examined the longitudinal associations of higher levels of PA in all three trimesters with risks of both PTB and PTB subtypes. In our study, the protective association between PA in the second trimester and PTB risk remained stable after adjustment for PA levels in the previous trimester. This result suggested that higher levels of PA in the early trimesters had an independent protective effect on PTB.

In our study, the PA level was related to lower odds of IPTB during all of three trimesters, while the association was not significant with SPTB. Prior research provides clear evidence of reduction of risk for pregnancy-related adverse events, such as pre-eclampsia [30, 31], gestational diabetes mellitus [32], and excessive gestational weight gain [33], all of which are risk factors for IPTB. Although it has been proposed that pregnant women with high levels of inflammation may have a higher risk of SPTB [6, 34], and that PA may reduce inflammation levels [35, 36], our study found no significant association between prenatal PA levels and SPTB risk. In a Dutch multicenter prospective cohort study comprising 2,614 pregnant women, Meertens et al. reported that prenatal PA was not a good predictor for SPTB, which is in line with our study [37]. Since the definitive etiology associated with SPTB is poorly understood [38], further studies are needed to assess the association of maternal PA with spontaneous preterm labor and preterm premature rupture.

Several guidelines such as the ACOG guidelines [39], the Chinese Nutrition Association Guidelines [40] and the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans [41] have consistently suggested women to take moderate- or high-intensity PA during pregnancy, however, these guidelines did not indicate during which period of pregnancy exercise should be maintained. This is important because women usually reduce their PA level in later trimesters, typically because of the weight gain or anxiety about PTB and miscarriage [14]. An important finding of the present study is that our results demonstrated a higher CPAS, which indicates a sustained high level of PA throughout pregnancy, was related to a lower risk of PTB in a dose–response manner. Women who maintained a high level of PA during all three trimesters had the lowest PTB risk. Our result is consistent with a previous prospective cohort study in the United States, which reported a lower level of leisure-time PA throughout pregnancy was associated with higher odds of PTB among 10,038 nulliparous women with singleton pregnancies [15]. These evidences together support the previous guidelines for prenatal PA, which recommends that pregnant women should perform moderate- or high-intensity PA, and further highlight the importance of sustained PA during pregnancy. In addition, our study further revealed the protective association of a sustained high level of PA on PTB was more pronounced for IPTB than it was for SPTB. This is consistent with our previous result that IPTB is more sensitive to maternal PA than SPTB.

The strengths of our study include its prospective design, recruitment of women during early pregnancy, and repeated assessments of their PA status during all three trimesters. These conditions enabled us to examine the trimester-specific association of women’s PA status with PTB risk. Furthermore, we examined the dose–response association between cumulative PA scores, represent sustained high PA levels across pregnancy, and women’s PTB risk, which is less studied. This study also has some limitations. First, the PA status was self-reported rather than measured via exercise accelerators, possibly introducing measurement bias. Although the PPAQ is widely used for the assessment of PA during pregnancy [42–44] and has yielded high reliability and validity among pregnant Chinese women [19], it may not contain all types of PA, thus may underestimated the total PA volume. In addition, the questionnaire only assessed women’s PA status during one week of each trimester, thus may not monitor women’s PA status throughout trimesters. Second, as an observational study, no causal relationship can be inferred from our results, randomized controlled trial study are warranted to examine the causal relationship. Third, the study was conducted in regional setting; thus, the findings may not well generalize to the populations. Fourth, the PTB rate in the present study is relatively high than the average rate of PTB in China, which was 6.9% in the year of 2014 according to a national survey [45]. One possible reason could be due to the rapid increase in PTB rate in China within the context of the universal two child policy in China, while another reason could be that our study participants were enrolled from a top-level hospital; thus, the incidence of PTB and IPTB in the study population was relatively high. Fifth, we were not able to adjust for certain factors that might have decreased participants’ PA levels; for example, their physicians might have recommended that they reduce their PA if they were at risk of PTB. Sixth, the sample size of SPTB in the present analysis in relatively small. This may limit the statistical power to examine the association between PA and SPTB and future studies should expand the sample size to provide adequate statistical power. Finally, we did not assess women’s PA status before pregnancy, which limited us to examine the independent association between PA during pregnancy and PTB risks, given that maternal PA status during pregnancy was highly associated with pre-pregnancy PA status.

Conclusions

Higher levels of PA during each trimester of pregnancy were associated with a lower risk of PTB and IPTB, respectively. Women maintaining high levels of PA across pregnancy had the lowest risks of PTB and IPTB. These findings support the necessity of guiding the maternal PA from early pregnancy and highlights the importance of sustained PA to prevent PTB.
