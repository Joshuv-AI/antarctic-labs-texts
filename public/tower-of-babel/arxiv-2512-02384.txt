Markov Chains Approximate Message Passing
Amit Rajaraman, David X. Wu

Abstract
Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms have long been observed to obtain near-optimal performance in various Bayesian inference settings. However, developing a supporting theory that makes these studies rigorous has proved challenging. In this paper, we study the classical spiked Wigner inference problem, where one aims to recover a planted Boolean spike from a noisy matrix measurement. We relate the recovery performance of Glauber dynamics on the annealed posterior to the performance of Approximate Message Passing (AMP), which is known to achieve Bayes-optimal performance. Our main results rely on the analysis of an auxiliary Markov chain called restricted Gaussian dynamics (RGD). Concretely, we establish the following results: 1. RGD can be reduced to an effective one-dimensional recursion which mirrors the evolution of the AMP iterates. 2. From a warm start, RGD rapidly converges to a fixed point in correlation space, which recovers Bayes-optimal performance when run on the posterior. 3. Conditioned on widely believed mixing results for the SK model, we recover the phase transition for non-trivial inference.

Introduction

Markov chains are fundamental objects of study which model the dynamics of complex systems arising in statistical physics, computational biology, and social networks.
Besides modeling physical systems, they are a highly successful algorithmic tool to sample from high-dimensional distributions in Bayesian inference, statistics, and theoretical computer science.

It has long been empirically observed that canonical Markov chains such as
Glauber dynamics
which attempt to sample from the posterior distribution are quite successful for Bayesian inference. For example,
[
18
]
observed that for community detection in the stochastic block model, the output of the Glauber dynamics is Bayes optimal.
However, there has been a dearth of rigorous results confirming or explaining the algorithmic success of Markov chains in statistical inference.
One fundamental reason for the lack of rigorous progress is that, for many posterior distributions of interest, Glauber dynamics provably suffers from slow worst-case mixing. This leads to the following natural question.

Question 1.1
.

If we run Glauber dynamics for polynomial time on the posterior distribution of a natural Bayesian inference problem, does it achieve Bayes-optimal performance?

To make progress on this, we revisit early empirical studies which compared the performance of Glauber dynamics to that of local
message passing
algorithms.
Our starting point is
[
18
]
, which found that the performance of Glauber dynamics matches that of the Belief Propagation (BP) algorithm which directly estimates the marginals of the posterior in the stochastic block model.

Since then, message passing algorithms such as BP and its dense variant Approximate Message Passing (AMP)
[
22
,
4
]
have been widely applied in a variety of high-dimensional statistics problems, to great success (for a few examples, see
[
19
,
44
,
24
,
30
]
and references therein).
A particularly appealing property of AMP is that its asymptotic performance can be rigorously characterized by a finite-dimensional recursion known as
state evolution
.

For certain inference problems, one can use state evolution to design AMP algorithms to construct Bayes-optimal estimators.
Similarly, we
a priori
know that Glauber achieves the same guarantees in exponential time, since by then the dynamics would be able to produce Gibbs samples.
Motivated by this, we refine our initial question to ask about connecting the performance of these two classes of algorithms if Glauber is only run for polynomial time.

Question 1.2
.

If we run Glauber dynamics for polynomial time, can we prove it performs as well as AMP?

Our main results make progress on
1.2
by proving a formal connection between Markov chains and AMP for the spiked Wigner inference problem, which we define below.
We show that the dynamics of the correlation with the spike—the observable of primary interest—are governed by fixed point relations which also characterize the asymptotic performance of AMP for the same problem. See

1.2
Related work

Spiked Wigner model.

The spiked Wigner model has provided a rich test bed for average-case algorithms and complexity.
Indeed, the basic question of efficient detection and recovery of the spike has been studied through the lens of spectral algorithms
[
3
]
, low-degree polynomials
[
46
]
, and message passing
[
19
]
. Interestingly, these efficient algorithms all succeed in polynomial time once
\lambda>1
. Furthermore, when
\lambda<1
, the mutual information between the observation
\bm{M}
and the signal
{\bm{x}}
turns out to be
o(1)

[
35
]
, indicating that one cannot hope to design an estimator
\widehat{x}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
that is non-trivially correlated with the spike
{\bm{x}}
.

For more general priors beyond
\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\}^{N})
, the story becomes significantly richer.
For example, there exist sparse priors where there exists a statistical-computational gap: there is an intermediate regime of SNRs
(\lambda_{c},\lambda)
where recovery is information theoretically possible, but no known efficient algorithm succeeds at weak recovery.

[
23
]
pin down the information-theoretic threshold at
\lambda=\lambda_{c}
for general bounded i.i.d. priors.
The algorithmic side of special cases such as sparse PCA
[
20
]
and non-negative PCA
[
43
]
have also been extensively studied. The problem has also been generalized to the tensor setting
[
42
,
55
,
7
,
33
]
, where there is an interesting gap in performance between naive local algorithms and methods based on the sum-of-squares hierarchy or the Kikuchi method.

In terms of sampling from the posterior, due to the sign symmetry of the posterior creating a bottleneck in distributions, Glauber suffers an exponential worst-case mixing time.
On the flip side, it has been observed that random initialization or simulated annealing appears to empirically succeed at the goal of sampling from the posterior
[
18
]
. Nevertheless, it has remained challenging to give provable guarantees about the performance of sampling algorithms, even under algorithmic warm starts.
A notable positive sampling result in this direction is
[
45
]
, which uses a different diffusion-based algorithm to sample from the posterior in Wasserstein distance when the SNR
\lambda
is a sufficiently large constant.

Bayes optimal estimation.

There is by now a large body of work characterizing Bayes optimal estimation for mean-squared error. Indeed, past work has designed algorithms that attain mean-squared error within
\frac{\mathrm{polylog}(N)}{\sqrt{N}}
of the MMSE
[
48
,
36
]
, as well as generalizations to constant-rank spikes, and other priors
[
41
,
44
]
. These aforementioned algorithms are based on AMP—one first computes the top eigenvector of
\bm{M}
to obtain a vector that has non-trivial correlation with the spike
{\bm{x}}
, then refines this estimate by iteratively applying the AMP update. To our knowledge, these algorithms based on AMP are the only algorithms that are known to obtain a mean-squared error within
o(1)
of the MMSE in the entire high SNR regime
\lambda>1
.

Typically, one considers the inference setting where knowledge of the full generative model is assumed. Perhaps confusingly, this is also referred to as the “Bayes-optimal setting”.
In
[
2
]
, they study a mismatched setting for spiked Wigner where the knowledge of
\lambda
is not assumed.
In fact, in
Section

2.2
we will show that the performance of their AMP algorithm corresponds with the fixed point equations for RGD.

In certain scenarios, Markov chain-based algorithms (or a sample from the posterior) might be preferred to AMP, since they have the ability to capture second-order information, like the variance of the correlation with the spike. Furthermore, samples can be used more flexibly to compute general posterior expectations.

Dynamics of Markov chains before mixing.

Many physically and algorithmically interesting phenomena manifest only when Markov chain dynamics do
not
mix quickly, such as

[

]
and

[

]

In the context of

For the pure spherical

[

derived a heuristic set of integro-differential equations which govern the behavior of Langevin dynamics when run for

by
[

and the dynamics of the energy observable was eventually established for

by
[

]

In recent work,
[
16
]
rigorously prove that the energy and overlap of the sequential scan block dynamics for the SK model are dictated by an analogous set of integro-difference equations for
O(N)
timescales.

[
37
]
characterized the overlap of Langevin dynamics for spiked matrix posteriors in the large system limit
N\to\infty
for
fixed
times
t
.
The analogous result in discrete time would correspond to asymptotically controlling the overlap for
O(N)
steps of Glauber dynamics.
In contrast, we give control on any sufficiently large polynomial time scale for Glauber.

[
6
]
give an extremely precise characterization of trajectory of Langevin diffusion for the posterior of the multi-spike tensor PCA problem given polynomially many samples of the spiked tensor (the spike being fixed, and the noise tensor i.i.d. across all samples).
Concretely, in the rank-one matrix setting, they establish strong recovery of the spike
{\bm{x}}
when the SNR
\lambda=N^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha>0
.
Their proofs are based on careful analysis of the SDE governing the correlations, which are inaccessible in the discrete setting.

Locally stationary distributions.

Before a recent work of Liu, Mohanty, Raghavendra, Rajaraman, and Wu
[
38
]
, it was not rigorously known whether Glauber dynamics on the spiked Wigner posterior achieves nontrivial recovery of the spike. As mentioned earlier in the introduction, they introduce the framework of locally stationary distributions to analyze the performance of slow-mixing Markov chains on inference problems such as spiked Wigner.

Our results directly improve on theirs in the following concrete ways:

(i)

We achieve tight temperature and SNR thresholds for our analysis, conditional on widely believed mixing conditions for the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model.

(ii)

Wherever SK mixing holds, we nail down the exact correlation that Glauber dynamics achieves for any SNR
\lambda
, as the solution to a simple fixed point equation. In particular, widely believed mixing conditions for the SK model recover the BBP transition
\lambda>1
.

(iii)

In particular, we unconditionally recover weak recovery in the regime
\lambda>\frac{1}{0.295}
for the success of Glauber, based on the rigorous SK mixing results of
[
1
]
.

(iv)

As in
Remark

1.6
, we improve the runtime guarantees by an
N^{2}
factor.

Locally stationary distributions have since been used in other settings to understand the behavior of Markov chains before mixing. Notably,
[
9
]
introduces a definition of local stationarity for quantum Hamiltonians, and uses it to show that all metastable states satisfy certain structural guarantees.
[
47
]
also find an interesting application of locally stationary distributions in the context of stochastic backtracking algorithms for LLM reasoning.

2
Technical Overview

Our main results crucially depend on a very precise understanding of the restricted Gaussian dynamics.
As it turns out, its study transparently clarifies the fixed point structure and its relationship to AMP.
Using the machinery of locally stationary distributions
[
38
, Lemma 3.7]

5.2

The technical overview is organized as follows.
We will first define RGD and describe the high-level reduction showing why one should expect RGD to boil down to a one-dimensional recursion on correlation.
Then, in
Section

2.1

Once we have written down the explicit recursion, we can readily connect it to AMP; this is carried out in
Section

2.2

Finally, we describe some qualitative properties of this recursion, such as the nature of its fixed points, in

2.3

First off, observe that due to the rotational symmetry of the noise matrix and prior, we may assume without loss of generality that the spike is the all-ones vector
\bm{1}

We start by explaining where RGD arises from, and the connection between the posterior and the SK model. It arises from the Hubbard–Stratonovich transform
[

[

Let
\bm{\sigma}\sim\mu_{\beta\bm{W}+\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}}

\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)

\bm{z}=\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{1}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}}\bm{g}

\bm{\sigma}

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\sigma\mid\bm{z}\right]

\displaystyle\propto\mu_{\beta\bm{M}}(\sigma)\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{N}{2\beta\lambda}\left(\bm{z}-\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}\langle\sigma,\bm{1}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\sigma^{\top}\bm{W}\sigma+\bm{z}\langle\sigma,\bm{1}\rangle\right)=\mu_{\beta\bm{W},\bm{z}\bm{1}}\,,

and the quadratic term from the spike in the Hamiltonian is now an external field.
In other words,

\mu_{\beta\bm{W}+\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{z}}\mu_{\beta\bm{W},\bm{z}\bm{1}}\,.

The RGD Markov chain (
Definition

1.3
) is then simply the noising-denoising process associated to the above measure decomposition: if at
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
, draw
\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
, then move to
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{\beta\bm{W},\left(\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}\langle\sigma,\bm{1}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}}\bm{g}\right)\bm{1}}
. It is not difficult to show, given this perspective of a noising-denoising process, that RGD has stationary distribution equal to
\mu_{\beta\bm{M}}
.
The usefulness of RGD is that it brings forth the importance of the correlation with the spike, which is the observable we ultimately care about.

Reduction to one dimension.

So far, we have written down the
N
-dimensional RGD chain, whose evolution explicitly depends on the correlation with the spike.
To make RGD more tractable and eventually recover the AMP update, we would like to reduce the dynamics to a finite-dimensional one.

This motivates us to consider the related
P^{\mathrm{RGD}}
Markov chain on
[-1,1]
, which tracks the (normalized) correlation of the
N
-dimensional RGD iterate with the spike.
In this Markov chain, if starting at
y\in\mathbb{R}
, we draw
\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
, set
\bm{z}=\beta\lambda y+\sqrt{\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}}\bm{g}
, draw
\bm{\sigma}\sim\mu_{\beta\bm{W},\bm{z}\bm{1}}
, and finally move to
\bm{z}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{N}\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{1}\rangle
.
The stationary distribution of this Markov chain is just the (normalized) marginal of
\mu_{\beta\bm{M}}
along the all-ones direction.

So far, this
1
-dimensional chain still goes through an
N
-dimensional SK Gibbs sample
\bm{\sigma}
; in particular, it depends on the
N\times N
matrix
\bm{W}
.
Therefore, to reduce
P^{\mathrm{RGD}}
to an effective 1-dimensional recursion, we must establish two things:

•

Concentration of
\bm{z}^{\prime}
around the mean correlation. More explicitly, we would like to show that with high probability, the disorder
\bm{W}
is such that for all the encountered external fields
\bm{z}
, the correlation of the random draw
\bm{\sigma}
is concentrated around the mean correlation
\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle
.

•

A scalar formula for the mean correlation of a Gibbs sample from an SK model with external field.

Both of these points are tractable using spin glass machinery in the high temperature regime for SK, which we describe shortly.
As we shall soon see, the scalar formula that governs the mean correlation is what crucially leads to state evolution equations.

2.1
The mean recursion and fixed point structure

Let us now see more concretely how the fixed point relation arises from the analysis of
P^{\mathrm{RGD}}
.

Define the mean magnetization
\bm{m}_{h}=\frac{1}{N}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{\sigma}\sim\mu_{\beta\bm{W},h}}\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{1}\rangle
, and for
\bm{\sigma}\sim\mu_{\beta\bm{W},h}
, let
\widetilde{\bm{g}}_{h}
be the random variable
\sqrt{N}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{N}\langle\bm{\sigma},\bm{1}\rangle-\bm{m}_{h}\right)

Under this notation, we can write down the following description of

: from

1.

draw
\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
, set
\bm{h}=\beta\lambda z+\sqrt{\frac{\beta\lambda}{N}}\bm{g}
,

2.

move to

For simplicity of exposition, let us first assume

First, observe by gaussian concentration, with very high probability we have

To simplify the recursion further, we need concentration of

The key observation is that such properties are tractable to prove if the SK model

We first need to introduce some standard spin glass quantities.

2.2
RGD simulates AMP

Let us now draw the explicit connection to AMP fixed points.
The state evolution fixed points (see

for a precise statement) that govern the late-stage performance of Bayes AMP for spiked Wigner

44

read

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\lambda\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\quantity[\tanh(\lambda\bg\sigma+ \lambda^2 \sigma^2)]

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\quantity[\tanh^2(\lambda\bg\sigma+ \lambda^2 \sigma^2)]\,.

Let
(\mu,\sigma^{2})
be the unique fixed point to the SE equations. We claim that
(z,q)=(\frac{\mu}{\lambda},\sigma^{2})
is also a solution to (

\beta=\lambda
.
Indeed, using that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}\tanh(\sqrt{\gamma} \bg+ \gamma)=\bm{\mathrm{E}}\tanh^{2}(\sqrt{\gamma}\bm{g}+\gamma)
for any

, we see that
\mu=\lambda\sigma^{2}
.
Therefore our putative assignment satisfies
z=q
, and some straightforward algebra yields that
(z,q)
is a valid solution to the fixed point equations.

As it turns out, the update equations for RGD given by (

[

]

Specifically, we have
\Delta_{0}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}

\eta_{t}(\cdot)=\tanh(\cdot)

Then some straightforward algebra, combined with

\displaystyle M_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{E}}\quantity[\tanh(\beta\bg\sqrt{Q_t} + \beta\lambda M_t)]

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{E}}\quantity[\tanh^2(\beta\bg\sqrt{Q_t} + \beta\lambda M_t)]\,.

The direct comparison is just identifying

and

The only difference is that

Remark 2.1
.

A subtle point here is that even for

2.3
Qualitative behavior of the fixed point equations

The qualitative behavior of the correlation recursion is determined by the fixed points of the recursion, as well as the convergence to said fixed points.

Convergence is nontrivial to guarantee, and indeed the proof of convergence for Bayes AMP does not immediately generalize to the mismatched setting where
\beta\neq\lambda
.
To this end, in
Proposition

7.1
we establish that so long as
(\beta,\beta\lambda z)
satisfies (
AT
), the map
z\mapsto\bm{m}_{\beta\lambda z}
is strictly increasing in
z
.
For any
\beta<1
, (
AT
) is automatically satisfied, and in this setting we are able to fully characterize convergence for the recursion starting from any
z
.

Turning now to the fixed points, one can immediately identify
z=0
as one fixed point of this recursion. As it turns out, this map
z\mapsto\bm{m}_{\beta\lambda z}
behaves differently depending on
\beta
, as illustrated in
Figure

2
:

(i)

If
\beta
is too small (smaller than
\frac{1}{\lambda}
), the signal is washed out and
0
is the only fixed point of this recursion. Furthermore, it is a stable fixed point.

(ii)

If
\beta
is of intermediate magnitude (between
\frac{1}{\lambda}
and
1
), in addition to an unstable fixed point at
0
, this function has stable fixed points at
\pm\mathsf{OPT}_{\beta,\lambda}
.

(iii)

If
\beta>1
, the formula for the mean correlation
\bm{m}_{\beta\lambda z}
is only valid if
z
is sufficiently large. Indeed, if
z
is close to
0
, (
AT
) is violated, and the corresponding SK model is known to be in the low-temperature replica-symmetry breaking (RSB) regime.
Hence, the recursion is only valid for large
z
, and in this region there are at most two stable fixed points
\pm\mathsf{OPT}_{\beta,\lambda}
.

(iv)

Finally, if
\beta
is very large, the RSB region for
z
is so large that it swallows the fixed points that are present at smaller
\beta
.

(a)

\bm{m}_{\beta\lambda z}
at
\beta<\frac{1}{\lambda}
:
0
is the only fixed point.

(b)

\bm{m}_{\beta\lambda z}
at intermediate
\frac{1}{\lambda}<\beta<1
: non-trivial fixed points appear.

(c)

\bm{m}_{\beta\lambda z}
at
1<\beta<\lambda
: the
0
fixed point is swallowed by intractability.

(d)

\bm{m}_{\beta\lambda z}
at
\beta\gg\lambda
: all fixed points are swallowed by intractability.

Figure 2
:
Behavior of the RGD recursion for different values of
\beta
.

We make the above predictions for the convergence and fixed points rigorous in
Lemma

7.5
.

Figure

2
suggests two kinds of results one could hope to show.

(a)

First, that for any
\beta
, the RGD Markov chain reaches the stable
\mathsf{OPT}_{\beta,\lambda}
fixed point from a sufficiently warm start which puts
z_{0}
in the high-temperature, replica-symmetric (RS) regime. That is, if the initialization
z_{0}
of
P^{\mathrm{RGD}}
is such that
(\beta,\beta\lambda z_{0})
satisfies (
AT
), then the correlation shoots to the stable fixed point
\mathsf{OPT}_{\beta,\lambda}
in a small constant number of steps of RGD. This is essentially immediate from classical analyses of fixed point recursions; see
Lemma

5.4
for details.

(b)

Second, that for
\beta<1
, the RGD Markov chain reaches the stable
\mathsf{OPT}_{\beta,\lambda}
fixed point from an
arbitrary
initialization. In the case that
\beta<\frac{1}{\lambda}
,
0
is the only stable fixed point, so for now we assume that
\beta\in(\frac{1}{\lambda},1)
. Since the previous point implies that the correlation goes to
\mathsf{OPT}_{\beta,\lambda}
once it becomes sufficiently large, the interesting part here is showing that the Markov chain successfully escapes the unstable fixed point at
0
. We establish this in
Lemma

5.6
, where we show that the noise
\bm{g}
and
\widetilde{\bm{g}}
, which are typically
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
, are anticoncentrated enough to cause the correlation to escape
0
.

Remark 2.2
.

As a simple illustrative example, we can carry out the same analysis on the RGD dynamics for the Curie-Weiss model at inverse temperature
\beta
and external field
h
. In this case
\bm{m}_{\bm{h}}
can be computed exactly as
\tanh(\bh)
, and so we see that RGD converges to the stable fixed point of the mean-field recursion
m=\tanh(\beta m + h)
.

3
Conjectures and open questions

One natural question is whether we can extend our analysis to other inference problems.
At least for the case of spiked matrix problems, the general recipe seems fairly adaptable.
For example, consider a more general spiked matrix inference problem with a different prior and noise model (i.e. not
\mathsf{GOE}
).
The main obstacles here are (1) establishing replica symmetry (specifically overlap concentration) for this more general spin glass, and using it to show sample magnetization concentration à la
Lemma

6.2
and (2) proving a mean correlation formula, analogous to
Lemma

6.1
.

Question 3.1
.

Can one establish a kind of universality result for our fixed point characterization that does not rely on model-specific calculations?

It would also be interesting to see if one could push the limits of our analysis to the multi-spike setting and achieve results analogous to
[
6
]
for Glauber dynamics.

Another interesting (but open-ended) question is: what other classes of inference problems can be analyzed using locally stationary distributions?
The success of RGD in the present analysis is rather contingent on the spiked matrix structure; in other inference problems, it is unclear what Markov chain should be used as a proxy to understand Glauber dynamics.

Question 3.2
.

Identify broader inference problems that can be analyzed through the lens of locally stationary distributions.

We next turn to open questions regarding the spiked Wigner problem in particular.
One fundamental barrier to applying our techniques to understand Glauber for
\beta>1
is that the transfer of local stationarity (
Lemma

4.8
) does not work in this setting, even if we assume mixing guarantees in the entire conjectured high-temperature region (
AT
). In particular, it is unclear how to prove a version of
Lemma

4.8
where we only assume that the component measure satisfies an MLSI with high probability, under the assumption that the initialization from which the locally stationary distribution is obtained lives in this high-temperature region.

Question 3.3
.

Assuming SK mixes fast for all
(\beta,h)
satisfying (
AT
), can we extend our analysis to characterize the recovery performance for annealed Glauber dynamics up to
\beta\in\left(1,\beta_{\lambda}\right)
for some
\beta_{\lambda}>\lambda
?

It seems likely that if the above question were answered affirmatively, this would provide an alternate algorithm that attains Bayes-optimal performance in the entire high-SNR regime
\lambda>1
.

We note that
[
2
]
makes some predictions for the behavior of these models in the RSB regime, i.e. beyond
\beta_{\lambda}
.

For technical reasons, the current techniques which involve a transfer of local stationarity between Glauber and RGD necessarily incur some
\mathrm{poly}(N)
overhead in the runtime.
On the other hand, based on physics heuristics and the rigorous results of
[
49
,
16
]
, one would expect the dynamics to achieve nonzero correlation in even
O(N)
steps.

Conjecture 3.4
.

Glauber dynamics succeeds at weak recovery in (near-)linear runtime.

One path towards achieving such a guarantee is to establish exponential contraction of the local stationarity parameter, perhaps by establishing a weak Poincaré inequality
[
29
]
for symmetric test functions.
Indeed, we believe that our techniques to analyze RGD might help in showing that annealed Glauber dynamics succeeds at sampling from the posterior (and potentially even from lower-temperature models, up to the aforementioned threshold
\beta_{\lambda}>\lambda
). Furthermore, while the “conservation of variance” property used to show sampling guarantees for annealed Glauber dynamics is typically reliant on proving certain covariance bounds (e.g.
[
29
, Lemma 6.8]
) which are often technically challenging, perhaps this can be sidestepped if one instead directly tried to establish a weak Poincaré inequality for the (one-dimensional!) RGD Markov chain.

Conjecture 3.5
.

For
\beta\leqslant\beta_{\lambda}
, annealed Glauber dynamics samples from the scaled posterior
\mu_{\beta\bm{M}}
in
\mathrm{poly}(N)
, or even
O(N\log N)
time.

This threshold
\beta_{\lambda}
is defined as the largest temperature
\beta
at which there is a nonzero fixed point
z
to the recursion (
4
) such that
(\beta,\beta\lambda z)
satisfies (
AT
). As shown in
Proposition

7.1
, this threshold is in fact greater than
\lambda
.

For a reversible Markov chain

Definition 4.4

(

6
High-temperature mean magnetization estimates in the SK model

In the RGD recursion for spiked Wigner, the measure decomposition is

\mu_{\beta M,0}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mu_{\beta M,0}\\
\bm{g}\sim N(0,1)\end{subarray}}[\mu_{\beta W,(\frac{\beta\lambda}{n}\expectationvalue{\bone, x}+\sqrt{\beta\lambda/n}\bm{g})\bm{1}}].

The following explicit formula allows us to determine the deterministic scalar recursion which determines the behavior of locally stationary RGD, in the high-temperature regime
\beta<1
.

Lemma 6.1

(High-temperature magnetization concentration for all fields)
.

Let
\beta<1
,
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, and
K>0
large independent of
N
. With probability
1-o(1)
over
W\sim\mathsf{GOE}(N)
, for all
0\leqslant h\leqslant K
,

(i)

if
h<N^{-\left(1/4+\varepsilon\right)}
,

\frac{1}{N}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mu_{\beta W,h\bm{1}}}\langle{\bm{x}},\bm{1}\rangle=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[\tanh\left(\beta\bm{g}\sqrt{q}+h\right)\right]\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{1/2+\varepsilon}}\right)\,.

(ii)

if
h>N^{-\left(1/4+\varepsilon\right)}
,

\frac{1}{N}\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mu_{\beta W,h\bm{1}}}\langle{\bm{x}},\bm{1}\rangle=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[\tanh\left(\beta\bm{g}\sqrt{q}+h\right)\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\right)\,.

where
q=q_{h}
is the functional order parameter defined by

q=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[\tanh^{2}\left(\beta\bm{g}\sqrt{q}+h\right)\right].

Note that for
h>N^{-(1/4+\varepsilon)}
, a bound of the form of (i) is strictly stronger than that in (ii).

The above theorem will be a corollary of two estimates for the mean magnetization. The first is essentially already present in
[
27
]
(also see
[
12
]
).

Lemma 6.2

(essentially
[
27
]
)
.

Let
(\beta,h)
satisfy (
overlap-conc
). Then, for any constant

,

\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[\left(R(\sigma,\bm{1})-q_{1}\right)^{2k}\right]\leqslant O\left(\frac{1}{N^{k}}\right)\,,

where we denote

.

The proof is identical to that of Corollary 4.1, Theorem 5.1, and Theorem 6.1 in
[
27
]
—we observe that all these proofs of upper bounds on the moments go through assuming overlap concentration, even if we do not have a central limit theorem for the overlap (such a CLT
is
required for the stronger Theorem 1.2 in Hanen’s paper, which proves a CLT for the magnetization).

However, this lemma is not useful in the regime where

is tiny, say

itself is

, and the error is at the same scale as the estimate. To get around this, we prove a mean magnetization formula that is more precise in the small

Lemma 6.3

(Magnetization estimates under weak external field)

\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle-q_{1}\right|^{2-\delta}\right]\leqslant O\left(\max\left\{\frac{1}{N},\frac{h}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}\right)^{2-\delta}\,.

We will defer the proof of

Section

Lemma

6.1
.

Proof.

For ease of notation, let
\bm{m}(h)
be the random variable

under external field
h

is the variance of the magnetization in the corresponding model. For

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\left|\bm{m}(h)-q_{1}(h)\right|>\frac{1}{N^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}\right]<\frac{1}{N^{2}}\,.

(11)

On the other hand, for

Proof of

We have by Hölder’s inequality and

Using replicas, we can write

\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[Z^{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle-q_{1}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{\sigma,\rho}\bm{\mathrm{E}}[e^{H_{N}(\sigma)+H_{N}(\rho)}(m(\sigma)-q_{1})(m(\rho)-q_{1})],

where

\bm{\mathrm{Cov}}(H_{N}(\sigma),H_{N}(\rho))=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}NR(\sigma,\rho)^{2}

is distributed as
\mathcal{N}\left(h\left\langle\sigma+\rho,\bm{1}\right\rangle,N\beta^{2}\left(1+R(\sigma,\rho)^{2}\right)\right)

\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[e^{H_{N}(\sigma)+H_{N}(\rho)}\right]=e^{\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}\left(1+R(\sigma,\rho)^{2}\right)+h\left\langle\sigma+\rho,\bm{1}\right\rangle}\,.

Since the summand in fact has no dependence on the disorder, we have reduced the moment calculation to an evaluation of the double sum

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma,\rho\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}}e^{\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}(1+R(\sigma,\rho)^{2})+h\expectationvalue{\sigma+ \rho, \bone}}(m(\sigma)-q_{1})(m(\rho)-q_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=(\bm{\mathrm{E}}Z)^{2}(2\cosh(h))^{-2N}\sum_{\sigma,\rho}e^{\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}R(\sigma,\rho)^{2}+h\expectationvalue{\sigma+ \rho, \bone}}(m(\sigma)-q_{1})(m(\rho)-q_{1})\,,

where we have used that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}[Z]=(2\cosh(h))^{N}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}N}{4}}
. This explains the dependence on
(\bm{\mathrm{E}}Z)^{2}
.

It thus remains to compute the double sum. To this end, we will use the HS transform, followed by the Laplace method.

Fact 6.5

(Hubbard–Stratonovich transform)
.

For any
c>0
, we have

e^{cx^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{c}+2zx}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

Applying the HS transform with
c=\frac{2\beta^{2}}{N}
and
x=\frac{\expectationvalue{\sigma, \rho}}{2}
, we obtain that

e^{\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}R(\sigma,\rho)^{2}}=e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2N}\expectationvalue{\sigma, \rho}^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi\beta^{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{N}{2\beta^{2}}z^{2}+z\expectationvalue{\sigma, \rho}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,

so that the double sum becomes

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi\beta^{2}}}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\frac{N}{2\beta^2} z^2)\sum_{\sigma,\rho}\exp(z\ev{\sigma, \rho} + h\ev{\sigma+ \rho, \bone})(m(\sigma)-q_{1})(m(\rho)-q_{1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

Observe that given
z
, the pairs
(\sigma_{i},\rho_{i})
are independent of each other, and hence this sum over
\sigma,\rho
is tractable to explicitly compute.
In particular, consider the distribution
p
on
\{\pm 1\}^{2}
, with
p(a,b)=\frac{1}{C(z,h)}\exp(zab + ha + hb)
. Then one can check the normalization constant is
C(z,h)=2(e^{z}\cosh(2h)+e^{-z})
, and we have

\sum_{\sigma,\rho}\exp(z\ev{\sigma, \rho} + h\ev{\sigma+ \rho, \bone})(m(\sigma)-q_{1})(m(\rho)-q_{1})=C(z,h)^{N}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{p^{\otimes N}}\left[\left(m(\sigma)-q_{1}\right)\left(m(\rho)-q_{1}\right)\right]\,.

Now, expanding it out, we have

(m(\sigma)-q_{1})(m(\rho)-q_{1})=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}(\sigma_{i}-q_{1})(\rho_{j}-q_{1})+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i}(\sigma_{i}-q_{1})(\rho_{i}-q_{1})\,,

and therefore,

\displaystyle f_{N}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{p^{\otimes N}}[(m(\sigma)-q_{1})(m(\rho)-q_{1})]

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N-1)}{N^{2}}(m(z)-q_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{N}(k(z)-2q_{1}m(z)+q_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle=\left(m(z)-q_{1}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\left(k(z)-m(z)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(m(z)-q_{1}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\left(k(z)-q\right)+\frac{1}{N}\left(q-q_{1}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{N}\left(q_{1}^{2}-m(z)^{2}\right)

where
m(z)\coloneqq\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{p}[a]=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{p}[b]

Thus, our goal is to show that

\displaystyle\left(2\cosh(h)\right)^{-2N}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi\beta^{2}}}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(N\qty[-\frac{z^2}{2\beta^2} + \log(2(e^z \cosh(2h) + e^{-z}))])f_{N}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\leqslant O\left(\frac{h^{2}}{N}\right)\,.

Rearranging, simplifying, and summarizing, we would like to show that

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\beta^{2}}{N}\right)}\left[\left(\frac{e^{z}\cosh(2h)+e^{-z}}{\cosh(2h)+1}\right)^{N}f_{N}(z)\right]\leqslant O\left(\frac{h^{2}}{N}\right)\,,

where an explicit calculation yields that

\displaystyle m(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{z}\sinh(2h)}{e^{z}\cosh(2h)+e^{-z}}\text{ and}

\displaystyle k(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{z}\cosh(2h)-e^{-z}}{e^{z}\cosh(2h)+e^{-z}}\,.

(*)=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\beta^{2}}{N}\right)}\left[g_{N}(\bm{z})^{N}\cdot e^{N\tanh(h)^{2}\bm{z}}\cdot e^{\frac{N\bm{z}^{2}}{2}\left(1-\tanh(h)^{4}\right)}\cdot f_{N}(\bm{z})\right]

Proposition 6.6

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=\tanh(h)=h+O(h^{3})\,,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=O(h)\,,\text{ and }

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=O\left(h^{3}\right)\,,

we have

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[f(\bm{z})\right]

\displaystyle=f(0)+f^{\prime}(0)\mu+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(\mu^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{N}\right)+O\left(\frac{h}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=h+O(h)\cdot\gamma^{2}\tanh(h)^{2}-O(h^{3})\cdot\left(\mu^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{N}\right)+O\left(\frac{h}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

Consequently,

\left|\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[\frac{e^{\bm{z}}\sinh(2h)}{e^{\bm{z}}\cosh(2h)+e^{-z}}\right]-q_{1}\right|=O\left(\frac{h}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\,,

completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.7

(Controlling the overlap)
.

For any constant
k
,

\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\frac{\gamma^{2}}{N}\right)}\left[\left(\frac{e^{\bm{z}}\cosh(2h)-e^{-\bm{z}}}{e^{\bm{z}}\cosh(2h)+e^{-\bm{z}}}-q\right)^{2k}\right]\leqslant O\left(\frac{1}{N^{k}}\right)\,.

Proof.

Our strategy will essentially be identical to that of the previous proof. Again, because

is an

-Lipschitz function, we have

\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[\left(\frac{e^{\bm{z}}\cosh(2h)-e^{-\bm{z}}}{e^{\bm{z}}\cosh(2h)+e^{-\bm{z}}}-q\right)^{2k}\right]\lesssim O\left(\frac{1}{N^{k}}\right)+\left(\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[\frac{e^{\bm{z}}\cosh(2h)-e^{-\bm{z}}}{e^{\bm{z}}\cosh(2h)+e^{-\bm{z}}}\right]-q\right)^{2k}

We have

. As a result,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[k(\bm{z})\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[\bm{z}+h^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{z}^{3}\right]+O\left(h^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu+h^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\cdot\mu^{3}-\mu\cdot\frac{\gamma^{2}}{N}+O\left(h^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu+h^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,.

Let us also figure out the leading terms in the Taylor series of

. We have
q=O(h^{2})
, so suppose that
q=ah^{2}+O(h^{4})
. Expanding out the recursion defining

, using the Taylor series expansion

, we have

q=\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)}\left[\beta\sqrt{q}\bm{g}+h\right]=\beta^{2}q+h^{2}+O\left(h^{4}\right).

It follows that
q=\frac{1}{1-\beta^{2}}\cdot h^{2}+O(h^{4})
. We also have

\displaystyle\mu+h^{2}

\displaystyle=\tanh(h)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}-\left(1-\tanh(h)^{4}\right)}+h^{2}

\displaystyle=h^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}-1}+1\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\beta^{2}}\cdot h^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,.

Therefore,

\left|\bm{\mathrm{E}}\left[k(\bm{z})\right]-q\right|=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.8
.

It holds that
\bm{\mathrm{E}}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\beta^{2}}{N}\right)}\left[g_{N}(\bm{z})^{2N}\right]=1+o_{N}(1)
.

Proof.

Recall

g_{N}(z)=\frac{\cosh(z)+\sinh(h)^{2}e^{z}}{\left(1+\sinh(h)^{2}\right)e^{\tanh(h)^{2}\cdot z}\left(e^{\frac{z^{2}}{2}\left(1-\tanh(h)^{4}\right)}\right)}\,.

Let
\kappa=\tanh(h)^{2}=\frac{\sinh(h)^{2}}{1+\sinh(h)^{2}}
, so we have

g_{N}(z)=\frac{\cosh(z)+\kappa\sinh(z)}{e^{\kappa z+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\cdot(1-\kappa^{2})}}\,.

First note that the terms in the Taylor expansion up to order
2
cancel out—indeed, for small

, we have
\cosh(z)+\kappa\sinh(z)=1+\kappa z+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+O(z^{3})
, and

e^{\kappa z+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\cdot(1-\kappa^{2})}=1+\kappa z+(1-\kappa^{2})\cdot\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\kappa z)^{2}+O(z^{3})=1+\kappa z+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+O(z^{3})\,.

The claim near-immediately follows. For large

term in the denominator dominates, so we may establish a uniform bound of

. We may thus bound

∎
