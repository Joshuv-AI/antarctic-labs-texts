PROM2 exacerbates CCl4-Induced liver fibrosis via NLRP3 inflammasome activation and hepatocyte pyroptosis
Authors: Jiashi Guo, Yan Lin, Xia Gong, Ge Kuang, Jun Hu, Hui Du, Hao Liu, Jiao Zhang, Li Zhang, Jingyuan Wan, Ting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver fibrosis is a progressive disorder resulting from chronic liver injury, and its molecular mechanism remains incompletely elucidated.MethodsThe role of PROM2 was assessed by integrating transcriptomic datasets with in vivo liver fibrosis models. Functional analyses were performed using PROM2 knockdown or overexpression strategies, along with NLRP3 knockout models, to investigate its mechanistic involvement in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and inflammasome activation.ResultsTranscriptomic analysis revealed that PROM2 expression was significantly upregulated in fibrotic livers and positively correlated with fibrosis- and EMT-related gene signatures. PROM2 knockdown significantly attenuated hepatic inflammation, inhibited EMT, and reduced fibrotic remodeling. In contrast, PROM2 overexpression aggravated these pathological features. Notably, in NLRP3-deficient mice, PROM2 overexpression failed to induce inflammation, EMT, or fibrosis, indicating that the pro-fibrotic effects of PROM2 are mediated through NLRP3 signaling.ConclusionPROM2 contributes to liver fibrosis by promoting EMT and activating NLRP3-dependent inflammatory pathways, suggesting it as a potential therapeutic target for liver fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05920-5.

Introduction

Liver fibrosis represents a reparative process triggered by persistent liver damage, characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix and fibrotic scar formation [1–3]. Cirrhosis is the end stage of progressive hepatic fibrosis and affects nearly 800 million people worldwide and is responsible for around 2 million annual deaths [4]. Without effective intervention, hepatic fibrosis may eventually progress to hepatocellular carcinoma and liver failure. Currently, no effective therapies are available except liver transplantation, which remains the only curative option.

The pathological mechanisms of organ fibrosis are complex. Epidemiological and pathological studies have revealed a close association between fibrosis and epithelial cancers. Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) represents a key mechanistic bridge between fibrosis and tumor progression. It entails the breakdown of epithelial polarity, morphological transformation into a fibroblast-like phenotype, loss of epithelial marker expression, and upregulation of mesenchymal markers. Consequently, cells gain the capacity to degrade the basement membrane and migrate through the extracellular matrix [5–7].

Fibrosis and epithelial cancers also share overlapping features at both the cellular and molecular levels. Fibrosis is an unregulated response of various tissues to injury, marked by fibroblast and immune cell activation, which collectively drive persistent extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation and inflammation. Tumor stromal constituents—including fibroblasts, immune cells, and ECM—play a central role in the initiation and advancement of tumors by establishing a pro-mutagenic microenvironment that fosters cancer cell survival, proliferation, and invasion. Other shared features include overproduction of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), increased oxidative stress, dysregulation of the Hippo signaling pathway, and enhanced cellular senescence [8–10]. Our previous bioinformatics analysis further confirmed a positive correlation between EMT activity and liver fibrosis severity.

Prominin family proteins, especially PROM1 and PROM2, are pentaspan transmembrane glycoproteins enriched in plasma membrane protrusions such as microvilli and cilia. PROM2 is a 112 kDa protein structurally homologous to PROM1 (CD133), featuring five transmembrane domains and two large glycosylated extracellular loops that mediate cholesterol binding and membrane curvature regulation [11, 12]. PROM2 is predominantly expressed in epithelial cells and is involved in membrane organization, vesicular trafficking, and microdomain stability.

Under pathological conditions, PROM2 may translocate from the plasma membrane to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Current research on PROM2 is limited, mainly focusing on metastasis burden and ferroptosis evasion in epithelial tumor cells [13, 14]. PROM2 is involved in regulating vesicular endocytosis, and its overexpression induces notable alterations in the structure and function of the plasma membrane, leading to the formation of additional protrusions and boosting invasive potential. Additionally, as a component of intracellular vesicles, PROM2 can participate in cell-cell signaling via exosomes, promoting metastatic spread. During vascular migration, PROM2 overexpression promotes ferroptosis resistance by forming microvesicles that export Fe²⁺ from cells. Hepatocytes, derived from primitive gut epithelial cells, also express PROM2 highly on their plasma membranes. Unfortunately, there is currently no research on PROM2 in liver diseases. Notably, our previous work showed that chronic hepatic injury induces ER stress, a key driver of liver fibrosis. Prolonged ER stress, resulting from excessive activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR), disrupts lipid metabolism, induces insulin resistance, and impairs mitochondrial function, thereby promoting liver fibrosis advancement [15]. During this process, PROM2 expression may be upregulated in parallel with ER stress activation. Bioinformatic analyses from our group revealed a significant correlation between PROM2 expression and liver fibrosis.

Furthermore, both tumor progression and fibrosis are regulated by EMT. PROM2-overexpressing hepatocytes may undergo EMT, serving as central drivers of fibrotic remodeling. The association between PROM2 and EMT has also been supported by previous bioinformatics results. Together, these findings led us to hypothesize that PROM2 may play a previously unrecognized role in liver fibrogenesis.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J wild-type mice and NLRP3 knockout (NLRP3⁻/⁻) mice were acquired from the Experimental Animal Center of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China) for subsequent experimental investigations. PROM2 expression was modulated via lentiviral delivery of full-length PROM2 cDNA (Lv‑PROM2) or PROM2 shRNA (Lv‑PROM2 shRNA); the viral vectors were supplied by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Guangzhou, China). A total of 1 × 10⁹ vg in 100 µL suspension was injected via the tail vein into 8-week-old mice. Control groups received Lv‑EGFP or Lv‑Scramble. Mice were then allowed to recover and establish stable gene expression for 14 days before initiation of CCl₄ treatment. All animal-related experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the ethical guidelines and protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Chongqing Medical University.

Liver fibrosis was induced in 8-week-old mice by intraperitoneal injection carbon tetrachloride (CCl4; Xianding Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) diluted in olive oil (1 mL/kg), administered three times per week for 8 weeks. Control groups received equivalent volumes of olive oil or vehicle alone. This CCl₄-induced model was used to evaluate hepatic fibrotic remodeling and assess the functional consequences of PROM2 modulation.

Serum enzyme analysis

Following serum collection, the levels of the hepatic enzymes, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), were assessed using commercially available assay kits (Bioengineering Research Institute, Nanjing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hydroxyproline assay

Liver tissues were accurately weighed after sectioning, and hydroxyproline content were subsequently quantified with a hydroxyproline quantification kit (Bioengineering Research Institute, Nanjing, China).

Liver histology

At selected time points, mice were euthanized, and liver tissues were fixed, embedded, and sectioned (4 μm). H&E, Sirius Red, and Masson’s trichrome staining (Sigma‑Aldrich, USA) were used to assess fibrosis. Fibrosis was scored using the Ishak system, and stained areas were quantified in five random ×200 fields using ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded liver Sect. (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking, sections were incubated with anti–α-SMA antibody (Abcam, UK) overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB staining (Dako, USA). α-SMA expression was quantified as IOD in five random ×200 fields using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence

Tyramine signal amplification (TSA) was performed using a TSA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA). Liver tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and then underwent antigen retrieval using citrate buffer. After blocking with 10% goat serum for 30 min, sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against F4/80 or CD11b (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA) at 4 °C, followed by a 60-minute incubation with goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody. Alexa Fluor 488 Tyramide was applied for 6 min, and cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer containing PMSF, and quantified with a BCA assay (Thermo Scientific, USA). Equal protein amounts were denatured, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1000). Details of the antibodies used are listed in Table 1. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 1 h. Results

PROM2 is upregulated in fibrotic patients and mice

Liver fibrosis is a progressive and evolving condition that arises from continuous liver injury caused by various factors, including viral infections, alcoholic liver disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [16]. PROM2 mRNA expression was examined in normal, hepatitis, and cirrhotic liver samples using two separate microarray datasets obtained from the GEO database (GSE143318 and GSE33814) to investigate the role of PROM2 in liver fibrosis. The analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of PROM2 mRNA expression in hepatitis and cirrhotic liver tissues relative to normal tissues (Fig. 1A and B). Elevated PROM2 expression at both mRNA and protein levels was demonstrated in fibrotic livers by RT-qPCR and Western blotting after establishment of a CCl₄-induced hepatic fibrosis model in mice to explore the alterations in PROM2 expression during liver fibrosis (Fig. 1D-F). Consistent with these findings, liver tissue analysis from both healthy individuals and liver fibrosis patients revealed a substantial increase in PROM2 protein expression in fibrotic tissues (Fig. 1G-I). These results highlight the essential role of PROM2 in hepatic fibrosis development.

PROM2 expression under fibrotic conditions was analyzed using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the GSE174748 dataset to identify specific liver cell types expressing PROM2. The analysis revealed predominant localization of PROM2 in cholangiocytes, with minimal expression in hepatocytes and other non-parenchymal cells during fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. S5A-C).

Collectively, these findings—derived from bulk transcriptomic profiling, in vivo validation, and scRNA-seq analysis—indicate that PROM2 is upregulated in liver fibrosis and suggest a potential role for cholangiocyte-derived PROM2 in the progression of hepatic fibrogenesis.

PROM2 correlates with fibrosis and EMT markers

Multiple liver fibrosis-related datasets were further examined, revealing that hepatic PROM2 transcript levels in patients with hepatitis and cirrhosis from GSE143318 and GSE33814 were significantly positively correlated with fibrosis-associated genes, including COL1A2, COL3A1, COL4A1, and COL5A1 (Fig. 2A and B). Moreover, hepatic PROM2 expression showed a positive association with NLRP3 pathway activation, based on analyses of GSE143318, GSE33814, and GSE121248 datasets (Fig. 2C-E). Notably, in the GSE143318 and GSE33814 datasets, hepatic NLRP3 expression was also positively correlated with EMT marker genes, including CDH1, SNAI1, VIM, and CDH2 (Fig. 2F-G). Dual immunofluorescence staining for PROM2 and NLRP3 was performed on liver tissues from CCl₄-treated mice to validate transcriptomic associations at the protein level and assess their spatial distribution. Co-expression of PROM2 and NLRP3 proteins was observed in fibrotic regions, with partial colocalization in non-parenchymal cells (Supplementary Fig. S5D), supporting a spatial link between PROM2 and inflammasome-mediated fibrosis.

Finally, we investigated whether PROM1, a related prominin family gene, could be co-regulated or compensate for PROM2. However, PROM1 expression remained unchanged across disease stages and showed no significant correlation with PROM2 in both human (GSE89632) and murine (GSE222576) fibrosis datasets (Supplementary Fig. S6A-B). These findings indicate that PROM1 is unlikely to compensate for PROM2 dysregulation under fibrotic or inflammatory conditions.

In summary, PROM2 functions as a key fibrosis-associated regulator, closely linked to EMT progression and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, independent of PROM1.

PROM2 knockdown attenuated CCl4-induced inflammatory response and EMT

To investigate the protective role of PROM2 deficiency in liver fibrosis, an efficient PROM2 knockdown (KD) mouse model was successfully established (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Compared with control mice, PROM2-KD mice exhibited markedly alleviated liver inflammation following CCl₄ exposure. Specifically, levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, CCL2, and CXCL1 were significantly reduced in the PROM2-KD group (Fig. 3B). Additionally, PROM2 knockdown also led to reduced hepatic infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages (Fig. 3C and D). Discussion

Liver fibrosis is a complex process involving interactions between resident and non-resident heterogeneous hepatocyte populations, ultimately leading to extracellular matrix deposition and organ failure [17]. Previous research indicates that EMT and impaired epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk are key drivers of tissue fibrosis and tumor progression. Notably, PROM2 has been linked to the initiation and metastasis of various cancers across different tissues and organs. Given the shared EMT dysregulation underlying both tissue fibrosis and cancer, and the high expression of PROM2 on hepatocyte plasma membrane protrusions, it is conceivable that PROM2 could partake in the onset of hepatic fibrosis. In addition, combined with previous bioinformatics findings, PROM2 may serve as a crucial instigator in the establishment of liver fibrosis. Building on these insights, we investigated the possible role of PROM2 in liver fibrosis. In this study, we demonstrate that PROM2 expression is markedly upregulated in both CCl₄-induced fibrotic mouse livers and in human liver fibrosis samples. Elevated PROM2 levels show strong positive correlations with EMT markers and components of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Functional experiments further reveal that PROM2 overexpression exacerbates liver inflammation, promotes EMT, and induces hepatocyte pyroptosis, thereby accelerating fibrotic progression. Conversely, silencing PROM2 significantly attenuates these pathological processes. Notably, the pro-fibrotic effects of PROM2 are abolished in NLRP3-deficient mice, indicating that PROM2 promotes fibrosis predominantly through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. These findings identify PROM2 as a key upstream regulator that simultaneously drives hepatocyte EMT and inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, contributing to chronic inflammation, hepatocyte loss, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition. Together, our results establish a mechanistic link between PROM2 and NLRP3 signaling in liver fibrosis and suggest that targeting PROM2 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating fibrotic liver disease.

Numerous studies have emphasized the crucial role of EMT in the development of key organs such as the kidney, liver, lung, and intestine [18–21]. For instance, research using transgenic rat models has demonstrated that EMT contributes to liver fibrosis, with over 30% of newly formed fibroblasts arising from local EMT [22]. EMT is also pivotal in tumor initiation and metastasis. It comprises three subtypes: type 1 (embryogenesis), type 2 (repair-related fibrosis), and type 3 (tumor-associated, driven by genetic/epigenetic changes) [23–25]. Among these, type 2 EMT events are part of a repair process, where epithelial cells transform into fibroblast-like cells to repair tissue damage caused by injury or inflammation. These cells retain epithelial markers such as cytokeratin and E-cadherin, while expressing mesenchymal markers, including α-SMA [8, 26]. Under acute injury, type 2 EMT can support effective tissue repair. However, during chronic inflammation, persistent EMT activation leads to excessive ECM accumulation and progressive fibrosis, eventually impairing organ function [27–29]. Undoubtedly, EMT is a key pathological process in both fibrosis and cancer, with its molecular mechanisms playing a crucial role in the progression of organ fibrosis and cancer.

The upregulation of PROM2 has been demonstrated to play a pivotal part in the onset and advancement of various cancers. Previous expression profiling studies have revealed that PROM2 levels are elevated in various cancer types, such as breast, ovarian, lung, pancreatic, bladder, and melanoma [14, 30–34]. PROM2 may promote tumorigenesis by enhancing ferroptosis resistance and facilitating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Studies in lung squamous cell carcinoma showed that PROM2 knockdown decreased mesenchymal marker N-cadherin and increased epithelial marker E-cadherin, indicating EMT reversal [35]. Similarly, transcriptome and functional assays in breast cancer, renal clear cell carcinoma, and melanoma confirmed that reducing PROM2 expression inhibits EMT and tumor cell migration [14, 36]. Additionally, experiments conducted on renal clear cell carcinoma and melanoma confirmed that reduction in E-cadherin expression with shPROM2 validated the capability of PROM2 knockdown to reverse the EMT process [14]. EMT is a critical developmental process in which epithelial cells gain fibroblast-like properties, including diminished intercellular adhesion and enhanced motility. It is regulated by transcription factors such as SNAIL1/2, ZEB1/2, and TWIST, which repress epithelial markers like E-cadherin while activating mesenchymal genes, thus driving development, fibrosis, and cancer progression [37, 38].

Previous studies have accentuated the key function of hepatocyte apoptosis and necrosis in liver fibrosis [39, 40]. Recent investigations reveal that pyroptosis contributes to chronic liver disease progression by promoting local inflammation via inflammatory cytokine release and immune cell recruitment [41–43]. While the majority of studies on pyroptosis in liver diseases have concentrated on monocytes and macrophages, a recent study indicates that despite hepatocyte-resistance to inflammasome-activated pyroptosis, excessive inflammasome activation can still lead to irreversible cell pyroptosis in hepatocytes, accompanied by severe intrahepatic inflammation primarily characterized by neutrophil infiltration and sustained inflammasome-driven fibrogenesis [44–47]. Classical inflammasomes consist of nod-like receptors (NLRs), ASC, and Caspase-1 [48, 49]. The NLRP3 inflammasome, activated by bacteria, viruses, or endogenous DAMPs, triggers pyroptosis by cleaving gasdermin D (GSDMD), causing membrane pore formation and release of IL-1β and IL-18 [50–52]. Growing evidence indicates that hepatocyte pyroptosis mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome plays a significant role in the development of metabolic-associated fatty liver disease and the progression of liver fibrosis [53, 54]. Hepatocyte pyroptosis activates myofibroblasts, and the resulting cell loss is progressively compensated by myofibroblast accumulation, thereby promoting the aggravation of liver fibroplasia. Furthermore, studies indicate that hepatic cell-derived NLRP3 inflammasome assemblies, along with intercellular crosstalk among hepatic cell populations, constitute key orchestrators in propagating hepatic fibrogenesis. Hepatocytes along with other non-parenchymal cells release IL-1β and IL-18 which directly induce stellate cell activation upon binding to their respective receptors derived from hepatic stellate cells. Conversely, DAMPs released from apoptotic or necrotic hepatocytes transform stellate cells into an activated phenotype involved in scar formation while also accelerating stellate cell activation leading to further progression of liver fibrosis [55–59]. In acute BDL models, NLRP3 gene knockdown mice exhibit increased inflammation and hepatocyte apoptosis/necrosis. Conversely, in chronic BDL models, NLRP3 deletion significantly alleviates both hepatic cell death-induced inflammatory responses and the severity of liver fibrogenesis caused by chronic cholestatic injury [60, 61]. Recent investigations employing differential gene expression profiling alongside KEGG pathway analysis have identified a strong association between NOD-like receptor signaling pathways and downstream cascades triggered by NLRP3 inflammasomes, which are closely linked to pyroptosis-mediated processes. These inflammatory responses appear to be regulated by PROM2, suggesting a potentially critical role in both tumorigenesis and fibrogenesis [62–64]. Our research results indicate that, on the one hand, PROM2 might accelerate the pathological alterations of liver fibrosis by enhancing the EMT process; on the other hand, it is likely to exacerbate the intrahepatic inflammatory cascade and increase hepatocyte death through activating NLRP3-mediated hepatocyte pyroptosis, ultimately promoting the irreversible progression of liver fibrosis (Fig. 9). However, the precise mechanistic details remain elusive at present juncture. While the carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄) model offers a reproducible and well-established system for studying core mechanisms of liver fibrosis—including hepatocyte necrosis, inflammation, hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition—it primarily induces centrilobular damage via oxidative stress. This contrasts with the multifactorial etiology (viral, metabolic, alcoholic, autoimmune) and sustained low-grade inflammatory/metabolic drivers characteristic of human disease. Therefore, although invaluable for investigating fundamental pathways like epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), pyroptosis, and inflammasome activation, direct extrapolation of findings requires caution due to mechanistic differences. To enhance translational relevance, our study integrated human fibrotic liver datasets alongside the CCl₄ model. Future work employing complementary models (e.g., MCD/CDAA diets, humanised mice) will be essential to further validate findings across contexts more representative of diverse human fibrogenesis.

Conclusion

EMT is a crucial pathological mechanism responsible for chronic fibrotic diseases and is integral to the aggravation of liver fibroplasia. Meanwhile, excessive activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome can result in irreversible pyroptosis of hepatocytes, inducing severe intrahepatic inflammatory responses and persistent inflammasome-driven hepatic fibrosis alterations. Our findings suggest that PROM2 serves as a critical regulator in this process by promoting both EMT and inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, thereby exacerbating liver fibrogenesis.
