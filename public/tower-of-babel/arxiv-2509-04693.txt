Emergent odd viscoelasticity in chiral soft glassy materials
Debarghya Banerjee, Peter Sollich

Abstract
Rheological properties of chiral active materials have been an important area of research in the recent past, in particular regarding odd terms in their mechanical response. While much progress has been made in the study of odd viscous fluids and odd elastic solids, there is still a lack of understanding of odd viscoelastic responses. We introduce a chiral soft glassy rheology model to understand the emergence and nature of such odd viscoelastic responses in a class of amorphous solids. We use this model, which effectively considers an ensemble of actively rotating inclusions in a glassy matrix, to study the linear stress response to steady and oscillatory shear flows. For steady shear we find an odd viscosity that, non-trivially, grows as the active rotation frequency Ω \Omega decreases. In oscillatory shear we find an odd viscoelastic spectrum with a non-trivial dependence on the driving frequency ω \omega , combining resonance effects around ω = 2 ​ Ω \omega=2\Omega with glassy power laws at larger ω \omega .

To adapt the SGR model to chiral active matter, we again consider mesoscopic elements of the system, each containing a rotating “inclusion” surrounded by a glassy “matrix”. Fig.

As in the original SGR model, we assume that the mesoscopic elements are large enough for local stresses and strains to be defined, yet small enough compared to the system size that we can eventually describe the macroscopic behaviour by suitable averaging.
The key parameter for the chiral activity of the system will be the angular frequency
\Omega
with which the inclusions rotate. We construct below the full master equation for the chiral SGR model, which requires us to track tensorial strains of both the inclusion and the matrix part of each mesoscopic element. A pre-averaging approximation that becomes exact for small deformations

\Omega

Finally we consider oscillatory flows and derive the viscoelastic spectrum, which again contains an odd component with a frequency dependence exhibiting both (modified) glassy power laws and resonance-like effects when the driving frequency is of the order of
\Omega

Matrix and inclusion elasticity
– As in the original SGR model we assign an elastic (free) energy to deformations of the matrix and inclusion parts of each mesoscopic element (Fig.

\displaystyle E^{\rm elastic}=\frac{k_{1}}{2}\,l_{ij}^{I}l_{ij}^{I}+\frac{k_{2}}{2}\,l_{ij}^{M}l_{ij}^{M}+k_{3}\,l_{ij}^{M}l_{ij}^{I}

(1)

Chiral SGR model
–
To construct the chiral SGR model, we consider the dynamics of the deformation of a single mesoscopic element in an externally imposed flow described by a velocity field with components
v_{i}(x,y,t)
. As in the original SGR model we assume that the deformations of the matrix and inclusion part of the mesoscopic element follow the externally imposed velocity gradient
\partial_{k}v_{i}
. In addition, the
inclusion rotates actively with angular frequency
\Omega
. To account for this, we need to work explicitly with the deformation tensors, which we denote by

F_{ij}
. Their dynamics is, by the above assumptions,

\frac{d}{dt}F_{ij}^{I}=(-\Omega\epsilon_{ik}+\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{I},\quad\frac{d}{dt}F_{ij}^{M}=(\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{M}

(2)

where
\epsilon_{ij}
is the two-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol (
\epsilon_{12}=-\epsilon_{21}=1
,
\epsilon_{11}=\epsilon_{22}=0
). In addition to these elastic deformations, we assume as in the original SGR model that plastic yield events can take place once the local elastic energy
E^{\rm elastic}
gets sufficiently close to a local yield energy
E
. The rate for such yield events is taken to have an activated form,

\Gamma_{0}e^{-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}}

Sollich et al. 1997
;
Sollich 1998
;
Cates and Sollich 2004
, with attempt frequency
\Gamma_{0}
. The effective temperature
\mathcal{X}
represents mechanical noise arising by stress propagation (see e.g. Refs.
Nicolas et al. 2018
;
Picard et al. 2004
) from yield events taking place elsewhere in the material. After a yield event, the local yield energy
E
is assumed to be drawn randomly from a distribution
\rho(E)
, which plays the role of a density of states or prior distribution. The deformations
\bm{F}^{I}=\{F^{I}_{ij}\}
,
\bm{F}^{M}=\{F^{M}_{ij}\}
of inclusion and matrix in the mesoscopic element are similarly taken to be reset in a yield event, to values drawn from residual deformation distributions

\rho^{I}\!(\bm{F}^{I})
and
\rho^{M}\!(\bm{F}^{M})
, respectively. The analogue of the conventional SGR picture of full relaxation by yielding corresponds to the choice
\rho^{I}\!(\bm{F}^{I})=\prod_{ij}\delta(F_{ij}^{I}-\delta_{ij})
and
similarly for
\rho^{M}\!(\bm{F}^{M})
.

Putting everything together, the statistics
P(E,\bm{F}^{I},\bm{F}^{M},t)
of mesoscopic elements, in a local region where the velocity gradient
\partial_{k}v_{i}
can be taken as approximately spatially constant, evolves according to the master equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{I}}\left(P(-\Omega\epsilon_{ik}+\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{I}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{M}}(P(\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{M})

\displaystyle-\Gamma_{0}e^{-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}}P

\displaystyle+\Gamma(t)\rho(E)\rho^{I}\!(\bm{F}^{I})\rho^{M}\!(\bm{F}^{M})\ .

(3)

The last term, which represents the statistics of mesoscopic elements
after a yield, contains as prefactor the overall local yield rate

\Gamma(t)=\Gamma_{0}\langle e^{-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}}\rangle_{P}
, thus ensuring probability conservation.
To complete the description, one requires expressions for the strains
l^{I}_{ij}
and
l^{M}_{ij}
that feature in

. We assume for these the Green-Lagrange form

l_{ij}^{I}=\frac{1}{2}(F_{ik}^{I}F_{jk}^{I}-\delta_{ij}),\qquad l_{ij}^{M}=\frac{1}{2}(F_{ik}^{M}F_{jk}^{M}-\delta_{ij}).

(4)

Other choices could be made but will give identical results in the regime of small deformations that we will concentrate on.

In analysing the chiral SGR model (
3
) we will initially keep the density of states

arbitrary but eventually take it to be of the exponential form used in the original SGR model. For the residual deformation distributions we will assume that the average residual strain tensor after a yield vanishes,

\int d{\bm{F}}^{I}\,l_{ij}^{I}\,\rho^{I}\!({\bm{F}}^{I})=0
, and similarly for

\rho^{M}\!({\bm{F}}^{M})
.
This property holds if, for example, the residual deformations are isotropically distributed – making the average of
l_{ij}
proportional to
\delta_{ij}

– and area-preserving so that the trace of the average has to vanish.
It should be noted that in the chiral SGR model (
3

\Omega
characterising the rotation of the inclusions. This rotation could alternatively be considered as arising from an active torque, leading in an overdamped setting to the torque-balance equation
\tau^{\rm active}=\gamma\Omega
and hence

– From (
3

The relevant local averages are over

In addition to the stresses, the hydrodynamic equations involve the fraction

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(E,t)=-\frac{G(E,t)}{\tau}+\Gamma(t)\rho(E),

(5)

Multiplying the master equation by

Conclusions

\sim\Omega\omega^{\mathcal{X}-3}
. This regime of higher frequencies is separated from the low frequency range
\omega\ll\Omega
by a resonance-like peak at
\omega=2\Omega
, where the active rotation reinforces extensional and compressional deformations in each period.

In future work it would be interesting to explore the full nonlinear response of the chiral SGR model, for example to large amplitude oscillatory shear, which in recent years has been studied extensively in glassy systems
Ewoldt et al. 2008
;
Parley et al. 2022
;
Radhakrishnan and Fielding 2016
;
Radhakrishnan and Fielding 2018
. We expect nonlinear effects to be particularly important around the resonance peaks we have identified, or generally in settings where large residual deformations remain after yield events.
It will also be interesting to compare the model predictions to realizations in particle systems, e.g. in dense mixtures of passive particles and active spinners.

Overall, the combination of glassy effects as captured by SGR and chirality in active matter provides a rich ground for the theoretical study of
odd viscoelastic
responses and a new approach to the modelling of materials with active torques
Banerjee et al. 2021
;
Surówka et al. 2023
;
Duclut et al. 2024
;
Soni et al. 2019
;
Hargus et al. 2024
. Given the prevalence of
chirality both at biomolecular level
Compton and Pagni 2002

and on larger scales in the motion of microorganisms in active glasses
Lama et al. 2024
,
we believe that studies in this direction will provide a deeper understanding of experimentally realizable chiral active systems
Huang et al. 2020
;
Fürthauer et al. 2012
;
Fürthauer et al. 2013

in the near future, for which the chiral SGR model can provide paradigmatic theoretical insights.

I
Elastic description of mesoscopic elements

We give here details of the properties of the mesoscopic elements in chiral SGR, in a slightly generalized form compared to the main text. To model the elastic energy that describes the mechanics of the mesoscopic units we first define two tensorial strains: one gives the strain of the inclusion
l_{ij}^{I}
and the other describes the strain of the matrix
l_{ij}^{M}
.
We then write the associated elastic energy in the generic quadratic form

\displaystyle E^{\rm elastic}=\frac{k_{1}}{2}l_{ij}^{I}l_{ij}^{I}+\frac{k_{2}}{2}l_{ij}^{M}l_{ij}^{M}+k_{3}l_{ij}^{I}l_{ij}^{M},

(14)

where the last term arises from mismatch between the inclusion and the matrix strains. As is commonly done in elastoplastic approaches
Nicolas et al. 2018
we will take our systems as incompressible so that all strains will be volume-preserving. The elastic constants
k_{1,2,3}
therefore relate to shear deformations and we have omitted terms that would result from local volume changes.

Using the above expression for the elastic energy we one compute the corresponding stress tensors:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l_{ij}^{I}}=k_{1}l_{ij}^{I}+k_{3}l_{ij}^{M}

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l_{ij}^{M}}=k_{2}l_{ij}^{M}+k_{3}l_{ij}^{I}

(16)

We then assume that the total stress of the local material element, consisting of an inclusion and a local region of matrix around it, is given by

\sigma_{ij}=\xi\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l_{ij}^{I}}+\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l_{ij}^{M}}=(\xi+k_{3}/k_{1})\sigma_{ij}^{I}+(1+\xi k_{3}/k_{2})\sigma_{ij}^{M}

(17)

where

\sigma_{ij}^{I}=k_{1}l_{ij}^{I},\qquad\sigma_{ij}^{M}=k_{2}l_{ij}^{M}\ .

(18)

The dimensionless parameter
\xi
above characterises the compliance between the inclusion and the matrix. This parameter effectively allows for some slip between inclusion and matrix; quantitatively,
\xi
describes how much of an applied external strain is reflected in the deformation of the inclusion.
The expressions in the main text correspond to the special case
\xi=1
.

To define the dynamics of local elements, we will work with deformation gradient tensors
F_{ij}^{I}
for the inclusion and
F_{ij}^{M}
for the local matrix region. These give the linear transformation of embedded material vectors from an unstrained reference configuration, with
F_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
indicating an undeformed state. We assume that the appropriate strains entering the local elastic energy and stresses are the Green-Lagrange tensors

l^{I}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(F^{I}_{ik}F^{I}_{jk}-\delta_{ij}),\qquad l^{M}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(F^{M}_{ik}F^{M}_{jk}-\delta_{ij})

(19)

as stated in Eq. (
4
) in the main text.

II
Chiral SGR model

We next define the chiral SGR model, which is based on the original version for passive systems
Sollich et al. 1997
;
Sollich 1998
as extended to tensorial stresses and strains
Cates and Sollich 2004
. The model describes the dynamics of local elements in an external flow field with velocity components
v_{i}
and velocity gradient tensor
\partial_{k}v_{i}
. It assumes that, as long as no local yielding takes place, the deformation tensor of the local matrix region follows the externally imposed deformation

\frac{d}{dt}F^{M}_{ij}=(\partial_{k}v_{i})F^{M}_{kj}

(20)

where as in the main text we use the Einstein summation convention. The inclusion deformation likewise follows the external deformation but moderated by the softness factor
\xi
; in addition it rotates actively with angular velocity
\Omega
so that

\frac{d}{dt}F^{I}_{ij}=(-\Omega\epsilon_{ik}+\xi\partial_{k}v_{i})F^{I}_{kj},

(21)

In addition to this deterministic dynamics, plastic yielding is assumed to take place at a rate
\Gamma_{0}e^{-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}}
, with
\mathcal{X}
an effective temperature resulting from mechanical noise generated by propagation of stresses from plastic events elsewhere in the material. The activation barrier is the difference between a yield energy
E
and the elastic energy
E^{\rm elastic}
already stored in the element. After a yield event, we assume as in the original SGR model that a new yield energy
E
is assigned to the local element, drawn randomly from a prior distribution
\rho(E)
.
At the same time, the elastic deformations are relaxed. As explained in the main text we do allow for a feature called “frustration” in conventional SGR
Sollich 1998
, which corresponds to local deformations after a strain not being reset to zero but instead drawn from some isotropic distribution of residual deformations.
Starting from these deformations further strain is accrued by external deformation and active rotation, and the elastic energy
E^{\rm elastic}
is calculated accordingly.

Combining the above ingredients of the dynamics one can write the dynamics of the chiral SGR model as a master equation for the distribution
P\equiv P(\bm{F}^{I},\bm{F}^{M},E,t)
across local elements, where
\bm{F}^{I}=\{F^{I}_{ij}\}
,
\bm{F}^{M}=\{F^{M}_{ij}\}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{I}}\left(\left(-\Omega\epsilon_{ik}+\xi\partial_{k}v_{i}\right)F_{kj}^{I}P\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{M}}\left((\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{M}P\right)

\displaystyle-\Gamma_{0}e^{-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}}P+\Gamma(t)\rho(E)\rho^{I}\!(\bm{F}^{I})\rho^{M}\!(\bm{F}^{M})

(22)

Here
\Gamma(t)=\Gamma_{0}\langle\exp[-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}]]\rangle_{P}
is the average yield rate
and
\rho^{I}\!(\bm{F}^{I})
,
\rho^{M}\!(\bm{F}^{M})
are the distributions of the residual deformations after a yield event.
Eq. (
22
) can usefully be rewritten in a form that isolates the strain dependence of the yielding processes, as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{P}+\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{I}}\left(\left(-\Omega\epsilon_{ik}+\xi\partial_{k}v_{i}\right)F_{kj}^{I}P\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{M}}((\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{M}P)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\tilde{\tau}}f(\{l_{ij}^{I}\},\{l_{ij}^{M}\})P+\Gamma(t)\rho(E)\rho^{I}\!(\bm{F}^{I})\rho^{M}\!(\bm{F}^{M})

(23)

with the definitions

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}

\displaystyle=\tau_{0}\exp\left(\frac{E}{\mathcal{X}}\right)

\displaystyle f(\{l_{ij}^{I}\},\{l_{ij}^{M}\})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{E^{\rm elastic}}{\mathcal{X}}\right)

(24)

and
\tau_{0}=1/\Gamma_{0}
.

III
Stress dynamics and pre-averaging approximation

We give details here of how to analyse the master equation (
23
) within a pre-averaging approximation introduced for SGR in Ref.
Fielding 2020
. This gives exact results to linear order in local strains.

To construct this approximation we separate the full probability
P(\bm{F}^{I},\bm{F}^{M},E,t)
into
the marginal distribution over yield energies,

G(E,t)=\int d\bm{F}^{I}\,d\bm{F}^{M}P(\bm{F}^{I},\bm{F}^{M},E,t)
, and the remaining conditional probability distribution

P_{1}(\bm{F}^{I},\bm{F}^{M}|E,t)=P(\bm{F}^{I},\bm{F}^{M},E,t)/G(E,t)
of deformations of local elements with a given yield energy
E
. Accordingly we have
P=G(E,t)P_{1}
and the master equation (
23
) takes the form

\displaystyle P_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(E,t)+G(E,t)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{P}_{1}+G(E,t)\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{I}}\left(\xi(\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{I}P_{1}\right)-G(E,t)\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{I}}\left(\Omega\epsilon_{ik}F_{kj}^{I}P_{1}\right)

\displaystyle+G(E,t)\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{ij}^{M}}\left((\partial_{k}v_{i})F_{kj}^{M}P_{1}\right)=-\frac{G(E,t)}{\tilde{\tau}}f(\{l_{ij}^{I}\},\{l_{ij}^{M}\})P_{1}+\Gamma(t)\rho(E)\rho^{I}\!(\bm{F}^{I})\rho^{M}\!(\bm{F}^{M})

(25)

We now integrate over the local deformation variables
\bm{F}^{I}
and
\bm{F}^{M}
to obtain Eq. (
5
) from the main text for the distribution of yield energies:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(E,t)=-\frac{G(E,t)}{\tilde{\tau}}\langle f(\{l_{ij}^{I}\},\{l_{ij}^{M}\})\rangle_{E,t}+\Gamma(t)\rho(E)

(26)

where the average on the r.h.s. is over the conditional distribution
P_{1}(\bm{F}^{I},\bm{F}^{M}|E,t)
, as indicated by the conditioning variables in the subscript.
As expected, terms in (
25
) corresponding to local deformation changes do not contribute here, with
G(E,t)
evolving only due to plastic yielding.

In a similar fashion we can first multiply (
25
) by
l_{ij}^{I}
or
l_{ij}^{M}
and then integrate over
\bm{F}^{I}
and
\bm{F}^{M}
, to get equations of motion for the averages of the local strain tensors. We recall that they are related to the deformation gradient tensors by

\displaystyle l_{ij}^{I}=\frac{1}{2}(F_{ik}^{I}F_{jk}^{I}-\delta_{ij}),

(27)

\displaystyle l_{ij}^{M}=\frac{1}{2}(F_{ik}^{M}F_{jk}^{M}-\delta_{ij}).

(28)

We begin with the somewhat simpler
l^{M}_{ij}
. Multiplying by this and integrating at fixed
(E,t)
, the first term on the l.h.s. of (
25
) becomes
(\partial G/\partial t)\langle l^{M}_{ij}\rangle_{E,t}
and the second one
G(\partial/\partial t)\langle l^{M}_{ij}\rangle_{E,t}
. Using the decomposition
P=GP_{1}
introduced above, the average matrix stress can be written as

\sigma^{M}_{ij}=k_{2}\langle l^{M}_{ij}\rangle=\int dE\,\sigma^{M}_{ij}(E,t)

(29)

where

\sigma^{M}_{ij}(E,t)=k_{2}G(E,t)\langle l^{M}_{ij}\rangle_{E,t}

(30)

is the contribution to the stress from elements with yield energy
E
. The first two terms on the l.h.s. of (
25
) that we have just worked out therefore combine to
(\partial/\partial t)\sigma^{M}_{ij}(E,t)/k_{2}
.
The third and fourth terms give zero after integrating by parts. On the r.h.s., the first term gives
-G\tilde{\tau}^{-1}\langle l_{ij}^{M}f\rangle_{E,t}
and the second one
\Gamma(t)\langle l^{M}_{ij}\rangle_{\rho}
where the subscript
\rho
indicates an average over the post-yield distribution.
But the average
\langle l^{M}_{ij}\rangle_{\rho}
actually vanishes, provided the residual strains after yield are volume-preserving on average and randomly oriented (see discussion in main text). We make this assumption throughout both for the matrix and the inclusion.

The nontrivial fifth term on the l.h.s. of (
25
) gives after multiplication by

(F_{ik}^{M}F_{jk}^{M}-\delta_{ij})/2
and integration

\displaystyle\frac{G(E,t)}{2}\int d\bm{F}^{I}d\bm{F}^{M}(F_{ik}^{M}F_{jk}^{M}-\delta_{ij})\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{lm}^{M}}\left((\partial_{n}v_{l})F_{nm}^{M}P_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{G(E,t)}{2}\left\langle(\partial_{n}v_{l})F_{nm}^{M}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial F_{lm}^{M}}F_{ik}^{M}F_{jk}^{M}\right)\right\rangle_{E,t}

(31)

\displaystyle=-\frac{G(E,t)}{2}\left\langle\left[(\partial_{n}v_{i})F_{nk}^{M}F_{jk}^{M}+(\partial_{n}v_{j})F_{ik}^{M}F_{nk}^{M}\right]\right\rangle_{E,t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{G(E,t)}{2}\left\langle\left[(\partial_{n}v_{i})(2l_{nj}^{M}+\delta_{nj})+(\partial_{n}v_{j})(2l_{ni}^{M}+\delta_{ni})\right]\right\rangle_{E,t}

\displaystyle=-\left[(\partial_{k}v_{i})\sigma_{kj}^{M}(E,t)/k_{2}+(\partial_{k}v_{j})\sigma_{ki}^{M}(E,t)/k_{2}+G(E,t)v_{ij}\right]

with
v_{ij}=(\partial_{i}v_{j}+\partial_{j}v_{i})/2
the symmetrised velocity-gradient tensor. Combining then all terms and multiplying by
k_{2}
gives the equation of motion for
\sigma^{M}_{ij}(E,t)
as

(\partial/\partial t)\sigma^{M}_{ij}-\left[(\partial_{k}v_{i})\sigma_{kj}^{M}+(\partial_{k}v_{j})\sigma_{ki}^{M}+k_{2}Gv_{ij}\right]=-k_{2}G\tilde{\tau}^{-1}\langle l_{ij}^{M}f\rangle_{E,t}

(32)

where we have dropped all arguments (
E,t
) for brevity. A fully analogous calculation gives for the contribution to the inclusion stress of local elements with yield energy
E
,

\sigma^{I}_{ij}(E,t)=k_{1}G(E,t)\langle l^{I}_{ij}\rangle_{E,t}\ ,

(33)

the equation of motion

(\partial/\partial t)\sigma^{I}_{ij}-\left[\xi(\partial_{k}v_{i})\sigma_{kj}^{I}+\xi(\partial_{k}v_{j})\sigma_{ki}^{I}+k_{1}\xi Gv_{ij}\right]=-k_{1}G\tilde{\tau}^{-1}\langle l_{ij}^{I}f\rangle_{E,t}-\Omega(\epsilon_{ik}\sigma_{kj}^{I}+\epsilon_{jk}\sigma_{ik}^{I})\ .

(34)

So far everything is an exact consequence of the master equation (
25
), but the equations for
G(E,t)
,
\sigma^{I}_{ij}(E,t)
,
\sigma^{M}_{ij}(E,t)
are not closed because of the averages over
P_{1}
featuring on the r.h.s. of Eqs. (
26
,
32
,
34
). To close the equations, we make the pre-averaging approximation from Ref.
Fielding 2020
, in which
f(\{l_{ij}^{I}\},\{l_{ij}^{M}\})
is replaced by
f(\{\langle l_{ij}^{I}\rangle\},\{\langle l_{ij}^{M}\rangle\})
everywhere, the averages being conditional on
(E,t)
. In this way
f
becomes just a function of
\{\sigma^{I}_{ij}\}
,
\{\sigma^{M}_{ij}\}
and can be pulled out of all the averages so that e.g. the r.h.s. of (
32
) becomes
-G\sigma^{M}_{ij}f\tilde{\tau}^{-1}=-G\sigma^{M}_{ij}/\tau
, with
\tau
evaluated in the same pre-averaging approximation as
f
.
In this way we obtain the following set of closed equations of motion:

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{G}{\tau}+\Gamma(t)\rho(E)

(35)

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)\sigma_{ij}^{I}-\xi\sigma_{ik}^{I}\partial_{k}v_{j}-\xi(\partial_{i}v_{k})\sigma_{kj}^{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{1}\xi Gv_{ij}-\frac{\sigma_{ij}^{I}}{\tau}-\Omega(\epsilon_{ik}\sigma_{kj}^{I}+\epsilon_{jk}\sigma_{ik}^{I}),

(36)

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)\sigma_{ij}^{M}-\sigma_{ik}^{M}\partial_{k}v_{j}-(\partial_{i}v_{k})\sigma_{kj}^{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{2}Gv_{ij}-\frac{\sigma_{ij}^{M}}{\tau}\ .

(37)

Our considerations so far have related to a local element at a fixed position in space; in a flow we need to replace
(\partial/\partial t)
in the equations above by the advective derivative
D_{t}\equiv(\partial/\partial t)+({\bm{v}}\cdot\nabla)
, giving equation (
8
) in the main text.

Note that as explained above but not written explicitly in Eqs. (
35
,
36
,
37
),
G(E,t)
,
\sigma^{I}_{ij}(E,t)
,
\sigma^{M}_{ij}(E,t)
all depend on
E,t
. The quantities for different yield energies
E
are coupled via the overall yield rate

\Gamma(t)=\Gamma_{0}\int dE\,G(E,t)\langle f\rangle_{E,t}\tilde{\tau}^{-1}\to\Gamma_{0}\int dE\,\frac{G(E,t)}{\tau}

(38)

where pre-averaging has been used again in the last step.

IV
Linearized theory: steady state and oscillatory flows

We consider the stress response to steady state, spatially uniform flows
v_{i}
, within a linear theory where convective and co-rotational terms can be discarded so that Eqs. (
35
,
36
,
37
) simplify to

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)G=-\frac{G}{\tau}+\Gamma(t)\rho(E)\ ,

(39)

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)\sigma_{ij}^{I}=k_{1}\xi Gv_{ij}-\frac{\sigma_{ij}^{I}}{\tau}-\Omega(\epsilon_{ik}\sigma_{kj}^{I}+\epsilon_{jk}\sigma_{ik}^{I})\ ,

(40)

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)\sigma_{ij}^{M}=k_{2}Gv_{ij}-\frac{\sigma_{ij}^{M}}{\tau}\ .

(41)

We recall that
G
,
\{\sigma^{I}_{ij}\}
and
\{\sigma^{M}_{ij}\}
depend on
E
and
t
. In the linear regime, given that
E^{\rm elastic}
is quadratic in strains, we have
f=1
to linear order so that
\tau=\tilde{\tau}=\tau_{0}e^{E/\mathcal{X}}
. We drop the tilde on
\tilde{\tau}
for brevity below.

In a steady state the right hand sides of Eqs. (
39
,
40
,
41
) need to vanish, giving for
G

G(E)=\Gamma\tau(E)\rho(E),\qquad\Gamma=\int dE\,\tau(E)\rho(E)\ .

(42)

Here the steady state yield rate
\Gamma
follows from the normalization of
G(E)
, which we recall is the distribution of yield energies across local elements. The results (
42
) are, as expected, identical to those for the original SGR model
Sollich et al. 1997
;
Sollich 1998
.

For the steady state stresses one finds the conditions

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ij}^{I}=\tau\left[k_{1}\xi Gv_{ij}-\Omega(\epsilon_{ik}\Sigma_{kj}^{I}+\epsilon_{jk}\Sigma_{ik}^{I})\right],

(43)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ij}^{M}=\tau k_{2}Gv_{ij}.

(44)

Eq. (
43
) is a linear system of equations for the three independent components of
\Sigma_{ij}^{I}
and can be solved straightforwardly to give

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{xx}^{I}\\
\Sigma_{yy}^{I}\\
\Sigma_{xy}^{I}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{k_{1}\xi G\tau}{1+4\Omega^{2}\tau^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1+2\Omega^{2}\tau^{2}&2\Omega^{2}\tau^{2}&-2\Omega\tau\\
2\Omega^{2}\tau^{2}&1+2\Omega^{2}\tau^{2}&2\Omega\tau\\
\Omega\tau&-\Omega\tau&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{xx}\\
v_{yy}\\
v_{xy}\end{pmatrix}

(45)

One can ask about the stability of this steady state: small perturbations
\tilde{\sigma}^{I,M}_{ij}=\sigma^{I,M}_{ij}-\Sigma^{I,M}_{i,j}
around it evolve as

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)\tilde{\sigma}_{ij}^{I}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}_{ij}^{I}-\Omega(\epsilon_{ik}\tilde{\sigma}_{kj}^{I}+\epsilon_{jk}\tilde{\sigma}_{ik}^{I}),

(46)

\displaystyle(\partial/\partial t)\tilde{\sigma}_{ij}^{M}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\tilde{\sigma}_{ij}^{M}\ .

(47)

This shows that
\sigma_{ij}^{M}
is always stable and relaxes back to the steady state at rate
1/\tau
. For
\sigma_{ij}^{I}
, one finds by writing (
46
) as a linear equation for the vector
(\tilde{\sigma}^{I}_{xx},\tilde{\sigma}^{I}_{yy},\tilde{\sigma}^{I}_{xy})
that the three eigenvalues are
-1/\tau
and
-1/\tau\pm 2i\Omega
, giving two additional damped oscillatory relaxation modes arising from the active rotation frequency
\Omega
.

For the case of oscillatory flows one replaces
v_{ij}
by
v^{0}_{ij}e^{i\omega t}
. As the yield rate time evolution (
39
) does not couple to the flow at linear order,
G(E)
remains time-independent and given by Eq. (
42
). The stress responses, on the other hand, will (after possible transients) have the same oscillatory time dependence as the imposed flow,
\sigma^{I}_{ij}=\sigma^{I,0}_{ij}e^{i\omega t}
and similarly for
\sigma^{M}_{ij}
. Dropping the 0-superscripts for brevity leads to the equations

\displaystyle i\omega\sigma_{ij}^{I}=k_{1}\xi Gv_{ij}-\frac{\sigma_{ij}^{I}}{\tau}-\Omega(\epsilon_{ik}\sigma_{kj}^{I}+\epsilon_{jk}\sigma_{ik}^{I}),

(48)

\displaystyle i\omega\sigma_{ij}^{M}=k_{2}Gv_{ij}-\frac{\sigma_{ij}^{M}}{\tau}\ .

(49)

Bringing the terms on the l.h.s. to the right, one finds the same equations as in steady shear, except for the replacement of
1/\tau
by
1/\tau_{\omega}=1/\tau+i\omega
; explicitly, one has
\tau_{\omega}=\tau/(1+i\omega\tau)
. Translating the steady state results to the current setting then gives

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{xx}^{I}\\
\sigma_{yy}^{I}\\
\sigma_{xy}^{I}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{k_{1}\xi G\tau_{\omega}}{1+4\Omega^{2}\tau_{\omega}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1+2\Omega^{2}\tau_{\omega}^{2}&2\Omega^{2}\tau_{\omega}^{2}&-2\Omega\tau_{\omega}\\
2\Omega^{2}\tau_{\omega}^{2}&1+2\Omega^{2}\tau_{\omega}^{2}&2\Omega\tau_{\omega}\\
\Omega\tau_{\omega}&-\Omega\tau_{\omega}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{xx}\\
v_{yy}\\
v_{xy}\end{pmatrix}

(50)

and

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{xx}^{M}\\
\sigma_{yy}^{M}\\
\sigma_{xy}^{M}\end{pmatrix}=k_{2}G\tau_{\omega}\begin{pmatrix}v_{xx}\\
v_{yy}\\
v_{xy}\end{pmatrix}

(51)

From the last expressions one sees that, at the level of linear response, the stresses in the matrix exhibit a simple Maxwell behaviour (for, we recall, fixed
E
), with a complex viscosity proportional to
\tau_{\omega}=\tau/(1+i\omega\tau)
.

For the inclusion stresses, the viscosity tensor in (
50
) has a more complicated form. However, this can be decomposed using partial fractions and written in terms of three frequency dependent viscosity terms,
\eta_{0}
,
\eta_{+}
and
\eta_{-}
defined as

\displaystyle\eta_{0}=\frac{k_{1}\xi G\tau}{1+i\omega\tau},\qquad\eta_{\pm}=\frac{k_{1}\xi G\tau}{2(1+i\omega\tau\pm 2i\Omega\tau)}.

(52)

corresponding to the three eigenvalues determining the stability of the unsheared steady state.
Rewriting Eq. (
50
) in terms of these functions we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{xx}^{I}\\
\sigma_{yy}^{I}\\
\sigma_{xy}^{I}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{0}+\eta_{+}+\eta_{-})&\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{0}-\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})&-i(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})\\
\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{0}-\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})&\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{0}+\eta_{+}+\eta_{-})&i(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})\\
\frac{i}{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})&-\frac{i}{2}(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})&\eta_{+}+\eta_{-}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{xx}\\
v_{yy}\\
v_{xy}\end{pmatrix}

(53)

From this we can now extract the frequency-dependent even shear
and odd viscosities
Avron 1998
, which we define as

\eta_{\text{shear}}=\eta_{xyxy},\qquad\eta_{\text{odd}}=\eta_{xyxx}

(54)

where
\eta_{xyxy}
etc are the relevant entries of the matrix in Eq. (
53
). Explicitly we find

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{shear}}=\eta_{+}+\eta_{-}

(55)

\displaystyle\eta_{\text{odd}}=i(\eta_{+}-\eta_{-})

(56)

We note as an aside that one could also extract from the above calculation the bulk viscosity
\eta_{\text{bulk}}=\eta_{xxxx}=\eta_{yyyy}
, which in our analysis of incompressible flows plays no role. This bulk viscosity comes out here as
\eta_{\text{bulk}}=\eta_{0}+\eta_{+}+\eta_{-}
, but as explained in the main text a consistent determination of the prefactors would require introducing separate elastic constants for shear and for compression in
E^{\rm elastic}
.

So far all the calculations in the calculations were for local elements with a given yield energy
E
, whose value enters via
G(E)
and
\tau(E)=\tau_{0}\exp(E/\mathcal{X})
. Using the standard SGR assumption that the prior yield energy distribution is exponential,
\rho(E)=e^{-E}
for
E>0
, one has from (
42
) that

G(E)=(1-1/\mathcal{X})e^{-E(1-1/\mathcal{X})}
. Integrating
\eta_{0}
from (
52
) over all
E
gives then

\eta_{0}^{*}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\,\eta_{0}(E,\omega)=k_{1}\xi\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\,\frac{G(E)\tau(E)}{1+i\omega\tau(E)}=k_{1}\xi\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\infty}d\tau\,P_{\rm ss}(\tau)\frac{\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}

(57)

Here we have explicitly written the arguments
E
and
\omega
omitted above, and added an asterisk as superscript on the integrated version to conform with standard notation for complex viscosities. In the last step we have also changed integration variable from
E
to
\tau
and identified the steady state distribution of
\tau
,

P_{\rm ss}(\tau)=(\mathcal{X}-1)\tau_{0}^{\mathcal{X}-1}\tau^{-\mathcal{X}}\ .

(58)

One observes that the result (
57
) is exactly the frequency-dependent viscosity of the standard SGR model
Sollich et al. 1997
;
Sollich 1998
, except that in the latter the modulus prefactor and the timescale
\tau_{0}
are normally set to unity and the result is expressed in terms of the corresponding viscoelastic modulus
G^{*}(\omega)=i\omega\eta^{*}(\omega)
. The latter is seen from (
57
,
58
) to be a superposition of Maxwell modes with different relaxation times
\tau
, which are distributed according to the power law spectrum
P_{\rm ss}(\tau)
.

For the viscosities
\eta_{\pm}
from Eq. (
52
) involving the active rotation velocity
\Omega
, one finds by an exactly analogous calculation

\eta_{\pm}^{*}(\omega)=k_{1}\xi\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\infty}d\tau\,P_{\rm ss}(\tau)\frac{\tau}{2(1+i\omega\tau\pm 2i\Omega\tau)}=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{0}^{*}(\omega\pm 2\Omega)

(59)

We can finally use the above expressions to write down the overall shear and odd viscosities as a function of frequency, in the form

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{\text{shear}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[\eta^{*}_{0}(\omega+2\Omega)+\eta^{*}_{0}(\omega-2\Omega)]

(60)

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{\text{odd}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}[\eta^{*}_{0}(\omega+2\Omega)-\eta^{*}_{0}(\omega-2\Omega)]\ .

(61)

So far we have evaluated the contribution to the dynamical viscosity from the inclusion. From Eq. (
51
) one reads off that the role of the matrix is rather simpler: it makes no contribution to the odd viscosity, while to the shear viscosity in adds a term proportional to
\eta_{0}^{*}(\omega)
. As this is the same as in the original SGR model, we will not discuss it further.

Returning to the more interesting inclusion stress, to understand the implications of the expressions (
60
,
61
) we can exploit existing results for
G^{*}(\omega)
from the original SGR model
Sollich et al. 1997
;
Sollich 1998
to deduce the behaviour of
\eta_{0}^{*}(\omega)
. To simplify the expressions we set
\tau_{0}=1
in the following. We also focus on the effective temperature regime above but close to the glass transition,
1<\mathcal{X}<2
, and on frequencies and active rotation frequencies below the fastest yield rate of
\Gamma_{0}=1/\tau_{0}=1
, i.e.
\omega\ll 1
and
\Omega\ll 1
.
In this regime we have
G^{*}(\omega)\propto(i\omega)^{\mathcal{X}-1}

Sollich et al. 1997
;
Sollich 1998
and therefore
\eta^{*}_{0}(\omega)\propto(i\omega)^{\mathcal{X}-2}
. For negative frequencies we deduce from (
57
) that
\eta^{*}_{0}(-\omega)=\overline{\eta^{*}_{0}(\omega)}
, where the overline indicates complex conjugation.

Consider now first the case of steady shear,
\omega=0
. With
\eta^{*}_{0}(-2\Omega)=\overline{\eta^{*}_{0}(2\Omega)}
we then get, using the notation
\eta_{0}^{\prime}(\omega)=\real[\eta_{0}(\omega)]
,
\eta_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\imaginary[\eta_{0}(\omega)]
,

\eta^{*}_{\rm shear}(\omega=0)=\eta_{0}^{\prime}(2\Omega)\sim\Omega^{\mathcal{X}-2},\qquad\eta^{*}_{\rm odd}(\omega=0)=-\eta_{0}^{\prime\prime}(2\Omega)\sim\Omega^{\mathcal{X}-2}

(62)

as stated in the main text. Both viscosities for steady flows are thus real-valued. The shear viscosity is
smaller
than in conventional SGR, and thus also smaller than the matrix contribution, which actually diverges because
\eta_{0}(\omega\to 0)\to\infty
. (This is related to the full nonlinear flow curve having, in the regime we are considering, a power law onset at small shear rates, with exponent less than unity
Sollich et al. 1997
;
Sollich 1998
.) The physical reason for this reduction in shear viscosity is that the active motion rotates slowly relaxing (large
\tau
) local elements into the flow before they have time to relax by yielding, thus effectively cutting off the spectrum of relaxation times at
\tau\sim 1/\Omega
. While the active rotation thus reduces the shear viscosity, it simultaneously causes an odd viscosity to appear, with the same scaling with
\Omega
and so, non-trivially,
growing
as
\Omega
decreases.

For small nonzero frequencies
\omega\ll\Omega
, we can expand (
60
,
61
) in
\omega
around
\eta_{0}(2\Omega)
to find up to linear order

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{\rm shear}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}^{\prime}(2\Omega)+i\omega\frac{d\eta_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{d\omega}(2\Omega)\sim\Omega^{\mathcal{X}-2}+i\omega\Omega^{\mathcal{X}-3}

(63)

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{\rm odd}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\eta_{0}^{\prime\prime}(2\Omega)+i\omega\frac{d\eta_{0}^{\prime}}{d\omega}(2\Omega)\sim\Omega^{\mathcal{X}-2}-i\omega\Omega^{\mathcal{X}-3}

(64)

Both viscosities thus acquire imaginary (elastic) contributions that are smaller by
\omega/\Omega
than the leading viscous (real) terms.

In the opposite limit
\omega\gg\Omega
of shear frequencies larger than the active rotation frequency (but still
\omega\ll 1
), it is the
2\Omega
-terms in (
60
,
61
) that can be treated as small corrections, and expanding around
\eta_{0}(\omega)
gives

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{\rm shear}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(\omega)+O(\Omega^{2})\sim\omega^{\mathcal{X}-2}-i\omega^{\mathcal{X}-2}

(65)

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{\rm odd}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2i\Omega\,\eta_{0}^{\prime}(\omega)+O(\Omega^{3})\sim-\Omega\,\omega^{\mathcal{X}-3}-i\Omega\,\omega^{\mathcal{X}-3}

(66)

where as before we have omitted all prefactors on the r.h.s. Active effects are thus small in the shear viscosity in this regime and enter only in higher order corrections in
\Omega
. The odd viscosity, on the other hand, is proportional to
\Omega
.
It thus vanishes when
\Omega\to 0
, and this has to be true generally for
\omega>0
as can be seen from (
61
).

As one might expect, the two expansions performed in (
63
–
66
) match in terms of order of magnitude when extrapolated to their crossover point
\omega\sim\Omega
, all terms then being of
O(\Omega^{\mathcal{X}-2})
. On the other hand, the signs of two of the terms, namely the imaginary part
\eta^{\prime\prime}_{\rm shear}
of the shear viscosity and the real part
\eta^{\prime}_{\rm odd}
of the odd viscosity, change between the two regimes. This is because there is in fact a divergence at
\omega=2\Omega
in Eqs. (
60
,
61
), which is “inherited” from the divergence of
\eta_{0}(\omega)
for
\omega\to 0
. Physically, the external shear frequency is in effect in resonance here with the active rotation and can thus generate large stresses. This is a non-trivial qualitative prediction of our theory. The fact that stresses become large, however, means that our linear theory should not be trusted to capture the resonance effects quantitatively: for this a fully nonlinear treatment would be required. Note finally that, in the regime where active effects from the rotating inclusion are subleading, i.e. (
65
), the signs of the real and imaginary parts of
\eta_{\rm shear}(\omega)
are as physically required in a passive system, i.e. with viscous part
\eta_{\rm shear}^{\prime}(\omega)>0
and elastic part
\eta_{\rm shear}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)<0
.

We comment briefly on the regime of high frequencies, although we expect in practice that other rheological contributions including e.g. solvent viscosity will be dominant in this regime. For
\omega\gg 1
, SGR predicts effectively Maxwell behaviour with a relaxation time of
\tau_{0}=1
, i.e.
G^{*}(\omega)\sim 1+i\omega^{-1}
. Correspondingly,
\eta_{0}(\omega)\sim\omega^{-2}-i\omega^{-1}
and one finds from Eqs. (
60
,
61
) by using
\Omega\ll\omega

\eta^{*}_{\rm shear}(\omega)\sim\omega^{-2}-i\omega^{-1}+O(\Omega^{2}),\qquad\eta^{*}_{\rm odd}(\omega)\sim-\Omega\omega^{-2}-i\Omega\omega^{-3}+O(\Omega^{3})

(67)

Chiral activity thus again gives only small perturbations to the shear viscosity, while the odd viscosity is proportional to
\Omega
. For
\omega\sim 1
these expressions connect smoothly to Eqs. (
65
,
66
) as expected.

V
Energy Balance

We comment briefly on the energy balance in the chiral SGR model defined above. To find this we can multiply Eq. (
22
) by
E^{\rm elastic}
and integrate over
\bm{F}^{I}
,
\bm{F}^{M}
and
E
. After appropriately integrating by parts this gives

(d/dt)\langle E^{\rm elastic}\rangle=(\partial_{k}v_{i})\left\langle F^{M}_{kj}\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial F^{M}_{ij}}\right\rangle+(-\Omega\epsilon_{ik}+\xi\partial_{k}v_{i})\left\langle F^{I}_{kj}\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial F^{I}_{ij}}\right\rangle-\Gamma_{0}\langle E^{\rm elastic}e^{-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}}\rangle+\Gamma(t)\langle E^{\rm elastic}\rangle_{\rho}

(68)

where the last term represents the average elastic energy stored locally after a yield,
which can be nonzero due to residual deformations.
The derivatives w.r.t. the deformation tensors can be expressed as ones w.r.t. the strains as follows:

F^{M}_{kj}\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial F^{M}_{ij}}=(2l^{M}_{km}+\delta_{km})\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l^{M}_{im}}

(69)

and similarly for
F^{I}_{ij}
. This gives for the first term on the r.h.s. of (
68
)

(\partial_{k}v_{i})\left\langle 2l^{M}_{km}\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l^{M}_{im}}+\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l^{M}_{ik}}\right\rangle

(70)

For small strains the first part can be neglected. Proceeding similarly for the second term in (
68
) gives

(d/dt)\langle E^{\rm elastic}\rangle=(\partial_{k}v_{i})\left\langle\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l^{M}_{ik}}+\xi\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l^{I}_{ik}}\right\rangle-\Omega\epsilon_{ik}\left\langle\frac{\partial E^{\rm elastic}}{\partial l^{I}_{ik}}\right\rangle-\Gamma_{0}\langle E^{\rm elastic}e^{-(E-E^{\rm elastic})/\mathcal{X}}\rangle+\Gamma(t)\langle E^{\rm elastic}\rangle_{\rho}

(71)

One recognizes in the first term
(\partial_{k}v_{i})\sigma_{ik}=v_{ik}\sigma_{ik}
, which is the rate of mechanical work expressed in terms of total stress and (symmetrized) velocity gradient. The last two terms give the dissipation rate from yield events. Generalizing slightly from the original SGR model
Sollich 1998
, one sees that the model effectively assumes that the dissipated energy in a yield is the difference between elastic energy before the yield (penultimate term) and after the yield (from residual deformations, last term). The second term from the active rotation vanishes because of the antisymmetry of
\epsilon_{ik}
, so that the overall energy balance relation has the same structure as in SGR, i.e. the rate of change of elastic energy (l.h.s.) is the difference of mechanical work (first term on r.h.s.) and dissipated energy (last two terms on r.h.s.).
