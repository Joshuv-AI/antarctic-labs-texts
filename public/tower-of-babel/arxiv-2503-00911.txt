Two characterizations of Bass orders via branches
Luis Arenas-Carmona

Abstract
. It has been known for some time that the orders in the four dimensional matrix algebra over a local field that can be written as a finite intersection of maximal orders are precisely those whose Gorenstein closure is Eichler. In this paper, a similar characterization is given for orders whose Gorenstein closure is a Bass order. A second characterization, this time for the Bass orders themselves, is given in terms of their branches, i.e., maximal subgraphs of the Bruhat-Tits tree whose vertices are orders containing them.



1.
Introduction

In all of this work,
\mathfrak{A}
denotes the matrix algebra

\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
over a local field
K
.
The element
\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{A}}\in\mathfrak{A}

is the matrix with

1
’s in the main diagonal and
0
’s elsewhere, which is the
multiplicative identity of the ring
\mathfrak{A}
.
The matrix algebra
\mathfrak{A}
is a particular
example of a quaternion algebra, i.e., a central simple

K
-algebra of dimension
4
. It is called the split
quaternion algebra. Any non-split quaternion algebra
is a division algebra. There is actually a unique
quaternion division algebra over the local field
K
.
In general,
\mathfrak{L}
denotes a commutative
subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}
. Other upper case Frankfurter letters,
like
\mathfrak{D}
,
\mathfrak{E}
,
\mathfrak{R}
or
\mathfrak{H}
, denote orders in
\mathfrak{A}
,
i.e., lattices over the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq K

that are unitary subrings of
\mathfrak{A}
. In particular, they are
close under multiplication and contain
\mathbf{1}
.
Some of these letters will be restricted
to particular families of orders as we progress. In general,

\mathfrak{R}
(or variations of this symbol) is assumed to be
a full order, in the sense that it spans the whole algebra

\mathfrak{A}
, while
\mathfrak{H}
might span a smaller algebra.

Gorenstein orders can be defined as orders
\mathfrak{R}
whose
codifferent, defined by

\mathrm{codiff}(\mathfrak{R})=\{a\in\mathfrak{A}|\textnormal{ tr}(a\mathfrak{R})\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{A},

is invertible as a two-sided fractional
\mathfrak{R}
-ideal.
There is a list of properties that can be shown to be equivalent
to being Gorenstein. For instance,
\mathfrak{R}
is Gorenstein if
and only if
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}(\mathfrak{R},\mathcal{O}_{K})
is proyective
as an
\mathfrak{R}
-bimodule. See
[
9
, Prop. 24.2.3]
for
details. It is shown in
[
9
, Prop. 24.2.15]
that for
every full order
\mathfrak{R}
in a quaternion algebra, there exists a
unique Gorenstein order
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}=\mathrm{Gor}(\mathfrak{R})

and a unique ideal
I\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
satisfying

(1)

\mathfrak{R}=\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{1}+I\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}.

In particular,

\mathfrak{R}
is Gorenstein if and only if
I=\mathcal{O}_{K}
and

\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}=\mathfrak{R}
. Equivalently, a Gorenstein order
is one for which no non-trivial expression of the form
(
1
), for an order
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}
and an ideal

I
, is possible. This equivalent definition
is the only one needed in all that follows.
In current literature the order
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}

is called the Gorenstein closure of
\mathfrak{R}
,
although the reader must be warned that
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}

is not, in general, the smallest Gorenstein order
containing
\mathfrak{R}
. The term “Gorenstein saturation”
is also used in literature.
An order
\mathfrak{B}
is Bass when every order
\mathfrak{R}
containing

\mathfrak{B}
is Gorenstein. Both Gorenstein and Bass orders play
a central role in the classification of quaternion orders.
See
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
9
, §24.2]

for more details on the general theory.

In
[
8
]
, it is proved that an order is an intersection of
maximal orders if and only if its Gorenstein closure is an Eichler
order. We define a weak intersection of maximal orders as an
order of the form
\mathfrak{R}=\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}
where

\mathfrak{D}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{D}_{n}
are
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-maximal orders
in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
for some finite field extension
L/K
.
The main result of this work is a characterization of
Bass orders along the same lines as in the reference:

Theorem 1.1
.

An order
\mathfrak{R}
of maximal rank is a weak intersection
of maximal orders if and
only if its Gorenstein closure is a Bass order.

Since an Eichler order
\mathfrak{E}
is completely determined by the
set
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{E})
of maximal orders containing it, we
can often study its properties in terms of the set

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{E})
. This set can be given
additional structure
that can be used as a tool in this study. In fact, the set

\mathfrak{t}(K)
of maximal orders in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)

can be endowed with a graph structure by defining
a neighbor relation on this set.
The precise definition is recalled in next section.
The set
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
of maximal orders containing
a given order
\mathfrak{H}
can be seen as a subgraph with the induced
relation, and it fact it turns out to be a connected subgraph.
In these terms, we can give a purely geometrical
characterization of Bass orders that is essential in the sequel
and we record it here as it is an important result on its
own right:

Theorem 1.2
.

An order
\mathfrak{H}
is Bass if and only if the graph

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
is a path.

The importance of Theorem
1.1
is that we can use graphs
over field extensions of
K
to study Bass orders, pretty much
in the same fashion as we have done for Eichler orders in
previous works. For instance, the
set of Bass orders containing
a particular matrix
\mathbf{m}
can be studied by computing
the branch of the order
\mathcal{O}_{L}[\mathbf{m}]
in the
Bruhat-Tits tree
\mathfrak{t}(L)
, for suitable extensions

L/K
. In fact, the proofs in this work show that we can
retrict ourselves to working on quadratic extensions.

2.
Trees and branches

In this work, by a graph
\mathfrak{g}
we mean a
set
V=V_{\mathfrak{g}}
whose elements are called vertices,
together with a symmetric relation

A_{\mathfrak{g}}=A\subseteq V\times V

containing no diagonal element
(v,v)
.
In what follows, lower case frankfurter letters like

\mathfrak{t}
,
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{s}
and the like
are used to denote graphs.
The set

\bar{A}
of geometrical
edges is the set of unordered pairs
\{v,w\}
of vertices
for which
(v,w)\in A
, and therefore also
(w,v)\in A
.
The valency of a graph is the number of geometrical edges
having it as a vertex. The realization

\mathrm{real}(\mathfrak{g})
is defined as the topological
space obtained by identifying the endpoints
(0,a)

and
(1,a)
of a marked interval

\tilde{a}=[0,1]\times\{a\}
to the vertices
v
and
w

for every geometrical edge
a=\{v,w\}
. For instance, we can
identify
v
with
(0,a)
and
w
with
(1,a)
. The resulting
space, up to homeomorphism, is independent of choices involved
in this construction. A full subgraph
\mathfrak{h}

of a graph
\mathfrak{g}
is a subset

V_{\mathfrak{h}}\subseteq V_{\mathfrak{g}}
provided
with the induced relation
A_{\mathfrak{h}}=A_{\mathfrak{g}}\cap(V_{\mathfrak{h}}\times V_{\mathfrak{h}})
.
No other type of subgraph needs to be considered here.
The realization
\mathrm{real}(\mathfrak{h})

is naturally a subspace of
\mathrm{real}(\mathfrak{g})
.

A graph is said to be connected if so is its realization.
An important example of connected graph is the real line
graph
\mathfrak{r}

that have a vertex
v_{n}
for every integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The vertex
v_{n}
is only a neighbor of the vertices

v_{n+1}
and
v_{n-1}
. The realization

\mathrm{real}(\mathfrak{r})
is homeomorphic to the real line

\mathbb{R}
. A integral interval is a connected subgraph of

\mathfrak{r}
. Its realization is an interval in the topological
sense. A path in a graph
\mathfrak{g}
is an injective
map from an integral interval to
\mathfrak{g}
that sends
neighbors to neighbors. As expected, we have finite paths,
i.e., paths defined on a finite interval,
ray-like paths, defined on integral intervals whose
realization is homeomorphic to the positive real line

[0,\infty[
, and maximal paths, defined on
the whole of
\mathfrak{r}
. A finite path has precisely
two vertices of valency one
v,w
called the endpoints.
We call it the finite path connect
v
to
w
.
In a connected graph, for every pair
(v,w)
of vertices,
there is a path connecting
v
to
w
.

A tree is a graph whose realization is simply connected.
In a tree, for every pair of vertices
\{v,w\}
,
there is a unique finite path with
v
and
w
as
endpoints. A path in a tree is an isomorphism between
an interval and its image, which fails to hold for more
general graphs. The integral interval on which a path
in a tree is defined is determined by its image,
up to a shift. In this context, the image of a path
is caled a line, and the image of a ray-like paths is
called a ray.
Two rays in a tree are said to have the
same visual limit
if their intersection is a ray. Visual limits are
equivalent classes under this relation. In a tree,
there is a unique ray joining a vertex to a visual limit,
meaning a unique ray in a given equivalence class
with a given initial vertex. Along similar lines,
there is a unique maximal line joining two visual limits.

Vertices in a tree are classified according to their
valencies. Vertices of valency
1
are called leaves.
Vertices of valency
2
are called bridges. Finally,
vertices of valency
3
or more are called nodes.

Our main example of graph is the Bruhat-Tits tree

\mathfrak{t}(K)
for a local field
K
. The vertices
of
\mathfrak{t}(K)
are the maximal orders
\mathfrak{D}

in the matrix algebra
\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
.
Two such orders
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
are neighbors if, in some
basis, they have the form

\mathfrak{D}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{O}_{K}&\mathcal{O}_{K}\\
\mathcal{O}_{K}&\mathcal{O}_{K}\end{array}\right),\qquad\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{O}_{K}&\pi_{K}^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{K}\\
\pi_{K}\mathcal{O}_{K}&\mathcal{O}_{K}\end{array}\right).

Note that this is indeed a symmetric relation.
It is well known that this graph turns out to be a tree
whose visual limits are in correspondence with
the
K
-points of the projective line,
see
[
7
, §II.1]
.

For any order
\mathfrak{H}
, not necessarily of maximal rank,
the branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
is defined as the full
subgraph whose vertices are precisely the maximal orders
containing
\mathfrak{H}
. The branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u})
for a
matrix
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
is defined analogously.
Note that

(2)

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),

for any set
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{n}\}

that generates
\mathfrak{H}

as a
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order. The branches
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})

and
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u})
are known to be connected,
see for example
[
1
, Prop. 2.3]
.

To compute the branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
we use
relation (
2
) above. Furthermore,
it can be assumed that none of the generators
\mathbf{u}_{i}

is central, since central integers are contained in
every maximal order. To compute the
branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u})
, for a non central
integral element
\mathbf{u}
, we note that the
order
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathbf{u}]
, generated by
\mathbf{u}
,
has rank two.
It spans the algebra
\mathfrak{L}=K[\mathbf{u}]
, which is
isomorphic to one of the following:

K[x]/(x^{2})
,
K\times K
, or a quadratic extension

L
of
K
. Case by case, computations are as
follows:

(1)

If
\mathfrak{L}\cong K[x]/(x^{2})
, then

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u})
is what we call an infinite
foliage. It can be defined as the union of a
sequence of balls
\mathfrak{b}_{i}
, of radius

i
and center
v_{i}
, for

i=1,2,\dots
, where
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},\dots

is the ordered sequence of vertices in a ray.
See
[
1
, Prop. 4.4]
.

(2)

If
\mathfrak{L}\cong K\times K
, then
its integral elements are all in a ring

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{K}\times\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
and
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathbf{u}]=\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{1}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
,
for some
r\geq 0
. Then
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u})
is
the set of orders at distance
r
or less
from a maximal line, see
[
1
, Prop. 2.4]

and
[
1
, Prop. 4.2]
.

(3)

If
\mathfrak{L}\cong L
is a quadratic
extension, then again
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathbf{u}]=\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{1}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}

for some
r\geq 0
. Then
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u})
is
the set of orders at distance
r
or less
from unique vertex, if
L/K
is unramified,
or an edge otherwise, see
[
1
, Prop. 2.4]

and
[
1
, Prop. 4.2]
.

Another important property, which is critical for us
and can be found in
[
1
, Prop. 2.4]
,
is given in the following proposition:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{H}
be an arbitrary order in
\mathfrak{A}
, and consider
the order
\mathfrak{H}^{[r]}=\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{1}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathfrak{H}
.
Then the branch

\mathfrak{s}_{K}\left(\mathfrak{H}^{[r]}\right)
contains precisely
the maximal orders at distance
r
or less from the
branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})

of
\mathfrak{H}
.∎

Any order that can be written as an intersection of maximal orders can be written as the intersection of
the maximal orders containing it. In other words, if

\mathfrak{H}
is an intersection of maximal orders, then

\mathfrak{H}=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{D}\in\mathfrak{s}}\mathfrak{D}
, where

\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
. The map

\mathfrak{s}\mapsto\mathfrak{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{D}\in\mathfrak{s}}\mathfrak{D}

defines a bijection between subgraphs that
are branches of orders on one hand and intersections
of maximal orders on the other. The intersection

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
is the largest order whose branch
is
\mathfrak{s}
. For a full order
\mathfrak{R}
, and for any
maximal order
\mathfrak{D}
containing
\mathfrak{R}
, there is an integer

r
satisfying
\mathfrak{D}^{[r]}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}
, and therefore

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{R})\subseteq\mathfrak{s}_{K}\left(\mathfrak{D}^{[r]}\right)
.
Hence, there is a finite
number of maximal orders
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
satisfying
\mathfrak{R}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
,
as the graph
\mathfrak{s}_{K}\left(\mathfrak{D}^{[r]}\right)
is a ball
of radius
r
.
In particular, a full order is an intersection of maximal
orders if and only if it is a finite intersection of
maximal orders. It is proved in
[
8
]

that the finite intersections of maximal orders are precisely
the orders whose Gorenstein closure is Eichler. In fact,
for an Eichler order
\mathfrak{E}=\mathfrak{D}\cap\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
, the branch

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{E})
consists precisely on the maximal orders
in the line from
\mathfrak{D}
to
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
. Since an order whose
Gorenstein closure
\mathfrak{E}
is Eichler has the form

\mathfrak{E}^{[r]}
for
r\geq 0
, its branch consists precisely on
the orders at distance
r
from a line. It follows that
the branch of any order equals the branch of an order
of the form
\mathfrak{E}^{[r]}
, as above. Next result is now
immediate:

Proposition 2.2
.

The branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{R})
of every full order

\mathfrak{R}
consists of
the maximal orders at distance
r
or less from a finite
line
\mathfrak{p}
called the stem of the branch.∎

The subgraph of
\mathfrak{t}(K)
consisting precisely of
the orders at distance
r
or less from a line
\mathfrak{p}
is denoted

\mathfrak{p}^{[r]}
and called the
r
-th tubular neiborhood of
\mathfrak{p}
.
There is a stronger version of Proposition
2.2
that is shown
in
[
1
, Prop. 5.3]
and
[
1
, Prop. 5.4]
:

Proposition 2.3
.

The branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
of every order
\mathfrak{H}

is either an infinite foliage, or a tubular
neighborhood
\mathfrak{p}^{[r]}
of a line

\mathfrak{p}
, which could be a ray
or a maximal line. The former holds
precisely when
\mathfrak{H}
is generated by a nilpotent element.
∎

Example 2.4
.

If
\mathfrak{b}
is a ball of radius
r
in

\mathfrak{t}(K)
, i.e., the set of maximal orders
at distance
r
or less from a single vertex
\mathfrak{D}
,
then
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathfrak{b}}=\mathfrak{D}^{[r]}
. Choose
three maximal orders
\mathfrak{D}_{1},\mathfrak{D}_{2},\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{b}
, such that the lines from
\mathfrak{D}

to any pair of the

\mathfrak{D}_{i}
intersect in a single point. These vertices
are said to be spread out in the ball. In this case,
it is easy to see that no smaller tubular neighborhood
of a line contains all three,
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
and

\mathfrak{D}_{3}
, as vertices. It follows that

\mathfrak{D}^{[r]}=\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{2}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{3}
.

One last consequence that we use throughout, and is proved by a case
by case inspection of the branches described above, is the following:

Proposition 2.5
.

Every node of the branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
, for every order

\mathfrak{H}
, has valency
q+1
where
q
is the cardinality of
the residue field
\mathbb{K}
of
K
. The graph

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
has bridges precisely when
it is a line of length
2
or larger.∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

If
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
is a line, every overorder of it has
the same property. If one of them, say
\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}

were not Gorenstein,
by the characterization of Gorenstein orders given in the
introduction, we could write it in the form

\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}=\tilde{\mathfrak{H}}^{[r]}
for
r>0
. Then Proposition

2.1
applies and the branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})

contains every order at a distance
r
or smaller
from
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\tilde{\mathfrak{H}})
. This contradicts the fact
that
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H}^{\prime})
is a line. We conclude that
every overorder
\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{H}
is Gorenstein, and therefore

\mathfrak{H}
is Bass. Sufficiency follows. For the necessity, we
observe that, if
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
is not a
line, then
\mathfrak{H}
is contained in the order

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{D}\in V(\mathfrak{s})}\mathfrak{D}
,
whose Gorenstein closure
\mathfrak{E}
is Eichler, as it is an
intersection of maximal orders. Furthermore, the branch of

\mathfrak{E}
is the stem of
\mathfrak{s}
, which does not coincide
with
\mathfrak{s}
, as
\mathfrak{s}
is not a line.
This implies that
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
is not Gorenstein.
We conclude that
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
is an overorder
of
\mathfrak{H}
that fails to be Gorenstein.
∎

If
L/K
is an extension of local fields, and if

\mathfrak{D}
is a maximal
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order in

\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
, then

\mathfrak{D}_{L}=\mathcal{O}_{L}\mathfrak{D}
is a maximal
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-order
in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
. The map
\mathfrak{D}\mapsto\mathfrak{D}_{L}

identifies
\mathfrak{t}(K)
with a subgraph of

\mathfrak{t}(L)
, whenever
L/K
is an unramified
extension. In the general case, however, we can only
identify the realization

\mathrm{real}\big(\mathfrak{t}(K)\big)
with
the realization of a subgraph
\mathfrak{t}(L/K)

of
\mathfrak{t}(L)
where the extensions

\mathfrak{D}_{L}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}_{L}
of two neighboring vertices

\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{t}(K)
lie
at a distance
e=e(L/K)
. Recall that
e(L/K)

denote the ramification index of the extension
L/K
.
The description of the branch

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}})
,
when
\mathfrak{L}
is isomorphic to a quadratic extension

L
of
K
can be refined in terms of the tree

\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
,
as described in next results, see
[
2
, §5]

for the case of odd
characteristic and
[
3
, §4]
for the case
of characteristic
2
:

Proposition 2.6
.

Assume
\mathfrak{L}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
is
isomorphic to an unramified
extension
L
of
K
.
Let
\mathfrak{L}_{L}=L\mathfrak{L}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
, which is
isomorphic to
L\times L
,
be the extension of scalars of
\mathfrak{L}
to
L
,
and let
\mathfrak{H}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}_{L}}
be its ring of
integers. Then the line
\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H})\subseteq\mathfrak{t}(L)
contains a single
vertex of
\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
, namely the vertex

\mathfrak{D}_{L}
, where
\mathfrak{D}
is the only maximal order
containing
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be any matrix satisfying

\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathbf{u}]=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
. Then, since
\mathfrak{L}/K

is an unramified extension, the minimal polynomial
of
\mathbf{u}
has two eigenvalues in

\mathcal{O}_{L}
with different
images in the residue field of
L
. In particular,

\mathbf{u}
generates
\mathfrak{H}\cong\mathcal{O}_{L}\times\mathcal{O}_{L}

as an

\mathcal{O}_{L}
-order. In particular
\mathfrak{D}_{L}
contains

\mathfrak{H}
. Furthermore, the line
\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H})

must be preserver by the Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)
,
since so is the algebra
\mathfrak{L}
. If the line

\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H})
contained a second vertex

\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}_{L}
where
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
is a
maximal
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order, then the Galois group,
preserving two points in a line, must preserve its
visual limits, but then those visual limits, as
Galois-invariant elements in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(L)

should actually belong to
\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
,
and therefore every vertex in the line must
belong to
\mathfrak{t}(K)
. This
contradicts the fact that
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(u)

has a single vertex, and therefore it has no visual
limit.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Assume
\mathfrak{L}
is isomorphic to a ramified
separable extension
L
of
K
.
Let
\mathfrak{L}_{L}=L\mathfrak{L}\cong L\times L

be the extension of
\mathfrak{L}
, and let

\mathfrak{H}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}_{L}}
its ring of integers.
Then the line
\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H})\subseteq\mathfrak{t}(L)
contains no
vertex of
\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
,
and the vertex of
\mathfrak{t}(L/K)

that is closest to
\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H})

is the midpoint of the line connecting
the two maximal orders containing

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
.

Proof.

Here the matrix
\mathbf{u}
satisfying

\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathbf{u}]=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
can be assumed to be a uniformizing
parameter of the discrete valuation domain

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
. It suffices to see that
conjugation by
\mathbf{u}
leaves every vertex in the line

\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H})
invariant, while it permutes
the two maximal orders containing
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
,
and therefore leaves no point of the realization of

\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
invariant, except for the midpoint
of that particular edge. Now, conjugation by

\mathbf{u}
must
preserve the branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{u})

which consists
precisely of that edge alone. Furthermore, the normalizer
of a maximal order
\mathfrak{D}
is
K^{*}\mathfrak{D}^{*}
, which contains
only elements whose determinant has even valuation.
Since the determinant of the uniformizer
\mathbf{u}

has odd valuation, it must permute the endpoints of the edge.
On the other hand, in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
, the matrix
u

can be factored as
\mathbf{u}=\pi_{L}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
,
where
\pi_{L}
is a
uniformizer, and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
is a unit in
\mathfrak{H}
. It follows
that conjugation by
\mathbf{u}
leaves invariant every
maximal order containing
\mathfrak{H}
.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Assume
\mathfrak{L}
is isomorphic to an
inseparable extension
L
of
K
.
Let
\mathfrak{L}_{L}=L\mathfrak{L}\cong L[x]/(x^{2})
.
Let
z
be the visual limit of the branch of
any non-trivial order in
\mathfrak{L}_{L}
.
Then the vertex of
\mathfrak{t}(L/K)

that is closest to
z
is the midpoint of the
line connecting the two maximal orders containing

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
.

Proof.

In this case
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}_{L}}
is not defined,
since
\mathfrak{L}_{L}=L\mathfrak{L}\cong L[x]/(x^{2})

has no maximal order. However, we can still write

\mathbf{u}=\pi_{L}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, as above,
where
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{L}_{L}

generates an order
\mathfrak{H}
, and it is a unit.
The preceding proof
carries over if
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}_{L}}
is replaced
by the order
\mathfrak{H}
thus defined. Note that all orders
spanning the algebra
\mathfrak{L}_{L}
have a branch with
the same visual limit, as one is always contained in the
other.
∎

Although invariant visual limits are
those with representatives in the sub-tree

\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
, the reader must be warned
that the analogous statement on vertices is false
for ramified extensions. For instance, the maximal
orders in the smallest path joining
\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H})
and

\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
, in the context of Prop.
2.7
,
are Galois invariant.

3.
The residual representation

In all that follows, we let
\mathfrak{H}
be an arbitrary order in

\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
and let

\mathfrak{D}
be a maximal order containing
\mathfrak{H}
. We let
\mathbb{D}=\mathfrak{D}/\pi\mathfrak{D}
and

\mathbb{H}=\mathfrak{H}/\pi\mathfrak{H}
. Note that there is an induced map
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}\cong\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{K})

which we call the residual representation of
\mathfrak{H}
into
\mathfrak{D}
.
The neighbors of
\mathfrak{D}
in the Bruhat-Tits tree are in natural
correspondence with the one dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{K}^{2}
.
In fact, assume
\mathfrak{D}=\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
,
which is the set of matrices satisfying

m(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{2})\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}^{2}
. This order
is identified with the endomorphism ring

\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{2})
, and we use,
analogously, the notation

\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}(\Lambda)

for the ring of matrices
\mathbf{m}
satisfying

\mathbf{m}\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda
, for any full lattice

\Lambda\subseteq K^{2}
. Then, a subspace

\mathbb{K}\bar{\mathbf{v}}
with

\bar{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{K}^{2}

corresponds to the maximal order

\mathrm{End}_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}(\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{O}_{K}^{2})
, for
any lifting
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{2}
of
\bar{\mathbf{v}}
.
It follows that the lattice

\Lambda=\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{v}+\mathcal{O}_{K}^{2}
satisfy
\boldsymbol{\eta}\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda

precisely when
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\eta}})(\mathbb{K}\bar{\mathbf{v}})\subseteq\mathbb{K}\bar{\mathbf{v}}
, for the reduction

\bar{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{H}
of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.
This proves next Lemma, which is essential in what follows:

Lemma 3.1
.

In the preceding notations, the neighbors of
\mathfrak{D}
in
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})

are naturally in correspondence with the one-dimensional eigenspaces
of the algebra

\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})\subseteq\mathbb{D}\cong\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
.∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{H}
is a non-maximal order.
The maximal order
\mathfrak{D}
is the only vertex in

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})

if and only if the image of
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}
contains a quadratic
extension of
\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

For the sufficiency, we note that a matrix generating a quadratic
extension has, by definition, no invariant
subspace. For the necessity, we split the proof in three cases
according to the dimension of

\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
. If the dimension is four,
i.e., if
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})=\mathbb{D}
, then Nakayama’s Lemma
implies that
\mathfrak{H}=\mathfrak{D}
is maximal,
which contradicts the assumption.
If the dimension is two or one,
it is generated by a single element
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.
Then,
\mathfrak{D}
is the only vertex in
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})

precisely when
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
has no eigenvalues, i.e.,
when
\mathbb{K}[\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}]
is a quadratic extension
of
\mathbb{K}
.
Assume, therefore, that
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
is
three dimensional. We claim that every three dimensional
subalgebra of the four dimensional matrix algebra contains
a non-trivial idempotent. Therefore, it can be assumed,
through a change of basis, that the algebra

\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
contains
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)
.
Then
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})=\textnormal{\scriptsize$\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{K}&V\\
W&\mathbb{K}\end{array}\right)$\normalsize}
,
where either
V
or
W
is the null space.
This algebra always has an invariant subspace.
To prove the claim we consider the two dimensional
space
Z
of matrices of the form
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
0&0\end{array}\right)
.
Since
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
has dimension
3

and
Z
has dimension
2
, the intersection

Z\cap\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
is non-trivial.
If the intersection contains no non-trivial idempotent,
it must be the space generated by the nilpotent matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
0&0\end{array}\right)
. An analogous reasoning with
matrices of the form
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
b&0\end{array}\right)
show that, if

\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
does not contain a non-trivial
idempotent, then it must contain
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)
.
However,
\textnormal{\scriptsize$\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
0&0\end{array}\right)$\normalsize}\textnormal{\scriptsize$\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)$\normalsize}=\textnormal{\scriptsize$\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)$\normalsize}

is also an idempotent. The result follows.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume the graph
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
contains more
than one vertex.
The maximal order
\mathfrak{D}
is a leaf of

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
if and only if
the image
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})

contains a non-trivial nilpotent element.

Proof.

Since the branch is connected, if
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})

contains more than one vertex, any of its vertices must
have neighbors. For the sufficiency, we note that non-trivial
nilpotent elements have a unique invariant subspace. Next
we prove the necessity. By hypotheses, there is a unique
invariant subspace. If there is no non-trivial nilpotent
element, there must exist two diagonalizable elements

\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}
with
only one common invariant subspace. By suitably choosing the basis
B
,
we assume the common invariant subspace
is spanned by the first vector in
B
,
and also that the matrix of
\boldsymbol{\tau}
with respect
to the basis
B
is diagonal. In other words,
we have a matrix representation of the form

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&b\end{array}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a^{\prime}&c^{\prime}\\
0&b^{\prime}\end{array}\right).

Since the second element in
B
is not an eigenvector of

\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}
, we have
c^{\prime}\neq 0
.
Then there is a linear combination of
\mathbf{1}
,

\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}
that is
non-trivial and nilpotent, as claimed.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

If a maximal order
\mathfrak{D}
is a leaf of the branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
then the extension

\mathfrak{D}_{L}
is a leaf of the branch

\mathfrak{s}_{L}(\mathfrak{H}_{L})
for any field extension

L/K
.

Proof.

The residual algebra
\mathbb{D}
injects into

\mathbb{D}_{L}=\mathfrak{D}_{L}/\pi_{L}\mathfrak{D}_{L}
, as both algebras
are naturaly isomorphic to a matrix ring over the
corresponding finite field.
If
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is a non-trivial nilpotent element in

\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
, the corresponding image
in the residual representation on
L
remains non-trivial
as an element of
\mathbb{D}_{L}
,
and it is immediate that remains an idempotent.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume the graph
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
contains more
than one vertex.
The maximal order
\mathfrak{D}
is a bridge of

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{H})
if and only if
the image of
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}
contains a
non-trivial idempotent and
no nilpotent element.

Proof.

Sufficiency is immediate since an idempotent has precisely
two eigenspaces and we know from the preceding results that
the valency of
\mathfrak{D}
cannot be smaller than
2
.
For the necessity, the absence of nilpotent elements follow
again from the preceding lemma, so we prove only the
existence of a non-trivial idempotent.
By the general theory, there is a basis
B=\{\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{2}\}

of
\mathbb{K}^{2}
whose elements are eigenvector for every element in

\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
. In particular, every element
in
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
is diagonal with respect to
B
,
with the eigenvalues in the diagonal. If the eigenvalues are
equal for every element in
\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})
, then
every subspace is also an eigenspace, and therefore

\mathfrak{D}
would be a node of the branch. Since this is not the case by
hypothesis, we conclude the existence of an element

\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\rho_{\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{D}}(\mathbb{H})

with different eigenvalues,
i.e., we can write

\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&b\end{array}\right),

in the basis
B
, with
a\neq b
. Then

\frac{1}{a-b}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-b\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{D}})

is a non-trivial idempotent.
∎

4.
On the classification of Bass orders

The results in this section are well known, but we include them here for
two reasons. To show that they can be deduced from the geometry
of branches, and to give a complete proof of Theorem 1.2 in next section,
as they are essential there.
It is well known (c.f.
[
9
, Prop. 23.4.3]
) that the conjugates of the orders of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{O}_{K}&I\\
J&\mathcal{O}_{K}\end{array}\right)
, where
I
and
J
are

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-ideals, or equivalently
those containing a copy of the ring of integral diagonal matrices,
are precisely the Eichler orders.

Proposition 4.1
.

A Bass order contained in three different maximal orders is Eichler.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be such an order. By Theorem
1.2
,
the branch
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{B})

is a line, and since it contains three different maximal orders,
it is a line of length
2
or more.
We conclude that
\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathfrak{B})
has a bridge.
By Lemma
3.5
the corresponding
residual algebra contains a non-trivial idempotent. By the Idempotent Lifting Theorem
(c.f.
[
6
, Theorem 6.18]
) we conclude that
\mathfrak{B}
contains an idempotent, and the result
follows from the characterization of Eichler orders quoted above.
∎

All modules considered here are left modules,
and also ideals are left ideals unless otherwise stated.
Next result is immediate from the correspondence theorem, and the fact that
R
-submodules of a ring
R
are precisely the ideals.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
R
be a ring, and let

\Lambda_{0}\subseteq\Lambda_{1}
be two
R
-modules satisfying

\Lambda_{1}/\Lambda_{0}\cong R
as
R
-modules. Then
every
R
-submodule of
\Lambda_{1}
containing
\Lambda_{0}

equals
I\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{0}
for some ideal
I
of
R
.
In particular, if
R
is a principal ideal domain, then
every
R
-submodule of
\Lambda_{1}
containing
\Lambda_{0}

equals
a\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{0}
for some element
a\in R
.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

A Bass order
\mathfrak{B}

contained in a unique maximal order
\mathfrak{D}
has the form
\mathfrak{B}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathfrak{D}
,
for some integer
r\geq 0
, and
for some two-dimensional subalgebra

\mathfrak{L}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}

that is isomorphic to an unramified quadratic extension

L/K
.

Proof.

If follows from Lemma
3.2
that the residual algebra contains a quadratic extension of the residue field

\mathbb{K}
.
Any generator of this extension lifts to a generator of a
subalgebra
\mathfrak{L}
, isomorphic to an unramified quadratic
extension, satisfying
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}
. Since
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
contains all elements in
\mathfrak{L}

that are integral over
\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
the quotient module
\mathfrak{D}/\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}

is a torsion free
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
-module of rank one.
Since
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
is principal,
it follows that the preceding lemma applies.
Note that every ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
has
a generator of the form
\pi_{K}^{r}
.
∎

If
\mathfrak{L}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
is a subalgebra
isomorphic to a ramified quadratic extension
L/K
, we denote
by
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}
an arbitrary uniformizing
parameter in
\mathfrak{L}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

If
\mathfrak{E}
is an Eichler order contained in precisely
two maximal orders,
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
,
and if
\mathfrak{L}
is a subalgebra, isomorphic to a ramified
quadratic extension
L/K
, satisfying
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}\subseteq\mathfrak{E}
, then
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{r}\mathfrak{E}
is a Bass order for
any integer

r\geq 0
. Furthermore, any Bass order
\mathfrak{B}
contained precisely
in the maximal orders
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{2}

has an expression of the form

\mathfrak{B}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{r}\mathfrak{E}
,
as described above.

Proof.

We can write
\mathfrak{E}=\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{2}
as an intersection of
maximal orders. These are the only maximal orders containing

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
, by the description of branches in §2.
Since
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{-1}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
, conjugation by

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}

preserves the set
\{\mathfrak{D}_{1},\mathfrak{D}_{2}\}
, and therefore

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}\mathfrak{E}=\mathfrak{E}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}
. We conclude
that
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{r}\mathfrak{E}
is a bilateral
ideal in
\mathfrak{E}
, so

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{r}\mathfrak{E}

is indeed a ring, and therefore an order.
Since it contains
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
, it
is contained in precisely two maximal orders,
and therefore it is a Bass order.

Now let
\mathfrak{B}
be any Bass order contained in
precisely two maximal ideals, say
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
and

\mathfrak{D}_{2}
. Note that
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
are neighbors as
branches are connected graphs.
If
\mathfrak{B}
is Eichler, there is nothing to prove, so
we assume this is not the case.
Take a generating set
\{\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\dots\}

for
\mathfrak{B}
. Note that the image of
none of these generators can have different eigenvalues in
any residual algebra, or the
residual algebra would have an idempotent,
and therefore, reasoning as in the proof of
Proposition
4.1
, we would conclude that
the order
\mathfrak{B}
is Eichler.
In other word, for every
\mathbf{q}_{i}
,
and for every
j\in\{1,2\}
,
the order
\mathfrak{D}_{j}
is either a leaf or a node in

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{q}_{i})
.
In what follows, we say that
\mathfrak{D}_{j}
is a leaf for a matrix

\mathbf{q}
if it is a leaf of the branch

\mathfrak{s}_{K}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
, and apply the same
convention to nodes.
We conclude that there is some generator,
say
\mathbf{q}_{1}
, for which
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
is a leaf,
and some generator, say
\mathbf{q}_{2}
, for which
\mathfrak{D}_{2}

is a leaf. If for some
\mathbf{q}_{i}
, with
i\in\{1,2\}
,
both maximal orders are leaves, we set

\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}_{i}
.
Otherwise we set
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2}
.
Note that in the later case

\mathfrak{D}_{1}
is a node for
\mathbf{q}_{2}
and

\mathfrak{D}_{2}
is a node for
\mathbf{q}_{1}
. We claim that
both
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
are leaves for
\mathbf{q}
.
In fact, if
\mathbf{q}
is contained in a neighbor

\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
, since
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
contains
\mathbf{q}_{1}
, as

\mathfrak{D}_{1}
is a node of
\mathbf{q}_{1}
, then
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
contains

\mathbf{q}_{2}=\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}_{1}
,
which contradicts the fact that
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
is a leaf for

\mathbf{q}_{2}
. The same holds for the neighbors of
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
.
We conclude that
\mathbf{q}
, thus defined, is contained
in precisely two maximal orders, whence

\mathcal{O}[\mathbf{q}]
is the ring of integers

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
of a ramified quadratic extension

\mathfrak{L}
of
K
, by the description of
branches for matrices in §2, and we have
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}
.
In particular,
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{E}
are

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
-modules, and the result follows
reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
4.3
.
∎

5.
Proof of the main result

Proposition 5.1
.

Every Bass order is a weak intersection of maximal orders.

Proof.

We use the classification in last section to give a case
by case proof. The result is well known for
Eichler orders, for which the field
L
on which the orders
are defined can be assumed to be
L=K
. Therefore,
we can assume that the Bass order
\mathfrak{B}
is in one of
the cases considered in Proposition
4.3
or
Proposition
4.4
.

Firstly we let
\mathfrak{B}
be a Bass order of the form

\mathfrak{B}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathfrak{D}
,
where
L/K
is an unramified quadratic extension.
This order in contained in
a unique maximal order, say
\mathfrak{D}
. Then the extension

\mathfrak{L}_{L}=L\mathfrak{L}\cong L\times L

has a ring of integers

\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}_{L}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{L}\times\mathcal{O}_{L}
,
which is contained precisely in the maximal orders
in an infinite line
\mathfrak{p}
, passing through
\mathfrak{D}
,
and whose visual limits
z
and
\bar{z}

satisfy
\bar{z}=\sigma(z)
,
as elements in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(L)
,
where
\sigma
is the
generator of the Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)
.
Let
\mathfrak{D}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
be a
maximal order in
\mathfrak{p}
at distance
r
from
\mathfrak{D}
. Set

\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{D}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cap\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
.
This is an order in

\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
that contains
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}
and
it is contained
in
\mathfrak{D}
. It follows that

\mathfrak{B}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\pi_{K}^{r^{\prime}}\mathfrak{D}
for
some integer

r^{\prime}
. In fact,
r^{\prime}
is the smallest integer
s
satisfying

\pi_{K}^{s}\mathfrak{D}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{1}
, which is equivalent
to
\mathfrak{D}_{L}^{[s]}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{1}
. Since

\mathfrak{s}_{L}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{L}^{[s]}\right)
is a ball of radius
s

around the vertex
\mathfrak{D}_{L}
, it follows that
r=r^{\prime}
,
and the result follows.

Finally, assume that
\mathfrak{B}
is a Bass order of the form

\mathfrak{B}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{r}\mathfrak{E}
,
where
\mathfrak{E}=\mathfrak{D}\cap\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
is an Eichler order of level
1

containing
\mathfrak{B}
, the subalgebra

\mathfrak{L}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
is isomorphic
to the ramified quadratic extension
L
of
K
, and

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}

is a uniformizer. Let
\mathfrak{E}_{L}=\mathcal{O}_{L}\mathfrak{E}
be the extension,
which is an Eichler order, as it contains a non-trivial
idempotent. The order
\mathfrak{E}_{L}
is contained precisely
in the maximal orders between
\mathfrak{D}_{L}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{L}^{\prime}

in the tree
\mathfrak{t}(L)
, as follows from Cor.

3.4
. In fact,
\mathfrak{E}_{L}
is contained
in exactly
3
maximal orders in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
,
since
\mathfrak{D}_{L}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{L}^{\prime}
lie at distance
2
in

\mathfrak{t}(L)
. The third order is denoted
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
.
It is located between
\mathfrak{D}_{L}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{L}^{\prime}
in

\mathfrak{t}(L)
, and it is not defined over
K
.
Note that we can write

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}=\pi_{L}\boldsymbol{\tau}
in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(L)
, where
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is a unit in
the algebra
\mathfrak{L}_{L}=L\mathfrak{L}
.
Assume first that
L/K
is separable. Then
the matrix
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}
has two
Eigenvectors in the vector
space
L^{2}
, which correspond to visual limits
z
and
\bar{z}

of the tree
\mathfrak{t}(L)
, and the generator
\sigma

of the Galois group
\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)

satisfies
\sigma(z)=\bar{z}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}

be the infinite line with visual limits
z
and
\bar{z}
.
Let
\mathfrak{D}_{0}
be the point of the line
\mathfrak{p}

that is closest to
\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
.
By Prop.
2.7
, the vertex in

\mathfrak{t}(L/K)
that is closest to
\mathfrak{D}_{0}

is
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. Consider a maximal order
\mathfrak{D}_{1}

in the ray from
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
to
z
at distance

r
from
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
. In the inseparable case
we use the ray joining
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
to
the visual limit
z
of the branch of any non-trivial
order in
\mathfrak{L}_{L}
, as described in Prop.

2.8
, but the reasoning is similar.
Note that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}

is contained in every maximal order in the line
\mathfrak{p}
,
and also in
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
, so it must be contained in

\mathfrak{D}_{1}
. We claim that

\mathfrak{B}=\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{E}\cap\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
. Again,
set
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{E}\cap\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
.
Then
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
is an order containing
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}

and contained in
\mathfrak{E}
, so that

\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}+\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{r^{\prime}}\mathfrak{E}
,
for some positive integer
r^{\prime}
, which is
the smallest value of
s
satisfying

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{s}\mathfrak{E}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{1}
.
Since
\mathfrak{E}
spans
\mathfrak{E}_{L}
as a
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-module,
we can replace
\mathfrak{E}
by
\mathfrak{E}_{L}
and require that

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{s}\mathfrak{E}_{L}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{L}^{s}\boldsymbol{\tau}^{s}\mathfrak{E}_{L}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{1}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{L}}^{*}\subseteq\mathfrak{E}_{L}^{*}
, we can ignore it and just require
that
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{L}^{s}\mathfrak{E}_{L}\subseteq\mathfrak{D}_{1}
, which is
equivalent to
s\geq r
by the general theory. The result
follows by reasoning as in the previous case.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume
\mathfrak{R}
is a weak intersection of maximal orders, and
let
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{1}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathfrak{R}
. Then
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}

is a weak intersection of maximal orders.

Proof.

Assume
\mathfrak{R}=\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}
,
where
\mathfrak{D}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{D}_{n}
are maximal
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-orders for
some extension
L
of
K
. Since
\mathfrak{R}
is contained
in some maximal
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
,
it does not hurt to assume that at least one of the

\mathfrak{D}_{i}
is defined over
K
, say
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
to fix ideas.
We claim that

\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}=\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{[er]}
,
where
e=e(L/K)
is the ramification index. Since

\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{[er]}
is an intersection of maximal orders,
the result follows. If
\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{D}_{i}
then

\pi_{K}^{r}\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{[er]}
, since the normalized valuation
\nu_{L}
on the local field
L
satisfies

\nu_{L}\left(\pi_{K}^{r}\right)=er
. It follows that

\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{\prime\prime}:=\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{[er]}
.
On the other hand, assume
\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{R}^{\prime\prime}
. Write

\mathfrak{D}_{1}=\mathfrak{D}_{L}
, where
\mathfrak{D}
is an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order
in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
. Since
\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}^{[er]}
,
then
\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}_{L}
for every maximal
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order

\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
at distance
r
from
\mathfrak{D}
, according to Prop.

2.1
. Since

\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
, then
\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}

for every such maximal
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
,
i.e.,
\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{D}^{[r]}
. It follows that

\mathbf{h}=z\mathbf{1}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathbf{u}
,
where
z\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{D}
. Since

\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{[er]}
, then
\mathbf{h}=z^{\prime}\mathbf{1}+\pi_{K}^{r}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
with

\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{i}
, but then
\mathbf{u}
and

\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
differ by a scalar, which must be integral, hence
\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{D}_{i}
. As

\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
, then
\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{R}
and
therefore
\mathbf{h}\in\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}
, as claimed.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume
\mathfrak{R}
is a weak intersection of maximal orders, and
its branch contains a ball
\mathfrak{b}
of radius
r
.
Then there is
an order
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}
that is a weak intersection of maximal
orders and satisfies
\mathfrak{R}=\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{[r]}
.

Proof.

Assume
\mathfrak{R}=\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}
,
where
\mathfrak{D}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{D}_{n}
are maximal
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-orders for
some extension
L
of
K
. As before, it does not hurt
to assume that at least three of these orders,
say
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
are spread out
leaves of the ball
\mathfrak{b}
, in the sense
defined in Example
2.4
. It follows that the
branch
\mathfrak{s}_{L}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}\right)

has width
er
or larger, so we can write

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}(\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}_{j})^{[er]}

for some collection
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}_{m}
.
Furthermore, we can assume that one of these orders
is the center of the ball
\mathfrak{b}
. In particular it is defined over
K
, so the proof of
\mathfrak{R}=\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{[r]}
,
where
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}=\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}_{j}
, can be
written by copying the proof of last proposition.
The result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
\mathfrak{R}
be an order that is a weak intersection
of maximal orders,
and let
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}
be its Gorenstein closure.
If
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}
is not a Bass order, then its branch contains
a ball of positive radius by Theorem
1.2
and
Prop.
2.2
.
Then Prop.
5.3
shows that
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}
is not
Gorenstein, contradicting its definition.
It follows that
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}
is indeed a Bass order.
On the other hand, if
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}
is a Bass order,
then Prop.
5.1
shows that
\tilde{\mathfrak{R}}

is a weak intersection of maximal orders, and
so is
\mathfrak{R}
by Prop.
5.2
, as claimed.
∎
