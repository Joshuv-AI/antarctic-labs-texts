Infinite temperature at zero energy
Matteo Ippoliti, David M. Long

Abstract
We construct a family of static, geometrically local Hamiltonians that inherit eigenstate properties of periodically-driven (Floquet) systems. Our construction is a variation of the Feynman-Kitaev clock—a well-known mapping between quantum circuits and local Hamiltonians—where the clock register is given periodic boundary conditions. Assuming the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) holds for the input circuit, our construction yields Hamiltonians whose eigenstates have properties characteristic of infinite temperature, like volume-law entanglement entropy, across the whole spectrum—including the ground state. We then construct a family of exactly solvable Floquet quantum circuits whose eigenstates are shown to obey the ETH at infinite temperature. Combining the two constructions yields a new family of local Hamiltonians with provably volume-law-entangled ground states, and the first such construction where the volume law holds for all contiguous subsystems.



I
Introduction

Tools from quantum information have found increasing application in many-body physics, both in and out of equilibrium.
Many processes are now understood in terms of
entanglement entropy
—an observable-agnostic quantification of correlations.
The spatial scaling of entanglement entropy sharply distinguishes different types of states.
At high energy density, the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) characterizes eigenstates as effectively random, and relates their entanglement entropy to the thermodynamic entropy. This gives rise to extensive (“volume law”) scaling of entanglement entropy
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Srednicki 1999
;
Rigol et al. 2008
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
.
At low energies, different structures of entanglement are possible. Gapped ground states obey the “area law” (proven in one dimension
Hastings 2007
and for some models in higher dimension
Anshu et al. 2022
): the entanglement entropy of a subsystem scales in proportion to its boundary, representing a limited amount of correlations.
Gapless ground states, on the contrary, can violate the area law—for example, one-dimensional quantum critical states exhibit logarithmic scaling of entanglement
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
;
Calabrese and Cardy 2009
.

Surprisingly, violations of the area law in gapless ground states can be maximal: families of local, one-dimensional spin Hamiltonians were constructed which have volume-law entangled ground states
Gottesman and Hastings 2010
;
Irani 2010
;
Vitagliano et al. 2010
;
Ramirez et al. 2014
, notably including translationally invariant models known as (colored, area-deformed) Motzkin and Fredkin chains
Bravyi et al. 2012
;
Movassagh and Shor 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2017a
;
Salberger and Korepin 2018
.
This shows that ground states can have an amount of entanglement comparable to that of highly excited states, challenging prior beliefs about the nature of quantum correlations at low energy.
At the same time, despite their high entanglement, these ground states are very different from genuine ETH-obeying, highly excited eigenstates. The structure of their parent Hamiltonians, which enables their exact solution, also imparts a “rainbow” structure to the state
Alexander et al. 2019
;
Alexander et al. 2021
: the entanglement is dominantly shared between pairs of qubits related by a reflection about the mid-point of the chain. While the half-chain entropy is high, local reflection-symmetric subsystems can have much lower entanglement.
It thus remains unclear how closely the entanglement structure of a (gapless) ground state can resemble that of a highly excited state, with a thermal, “random-looking” character and in particular volume-law scaling for all local subsystems.

In this work,
we construct a large family of geometrically local static Hamiltonians which display properties associated to infinite temperature across almost all eigenstates. In particular, the eigenstates—including the ground state—are volume-law entangled for all contiguous subsystems.
Our construction is based on the Feynman-Kitaev clock (FK clock) model
Kitaev et al. 2002
, a well-known model which embeds the output of a quantum circuit into the ground state of a static Hamiltonian.
By modifying the FK clock model to include periodic boundary conditions—so that the final “time” on the clock is the same as the initial time—we embed
Floquet states

Bukov et al. 2015
;
Rudner and Lindner 2020
of an input staircase circuit in eigenstates of the Hamiltonian.
Almost all Floquet states of generic quantum circuits are expected to obey the ETH at infinite temperature, and we prove that this implies volume-law entanglement of the clock Hamiltonian eigenstates.

While eigenstates of generic circuits are widely believed to obey the ETH, in order to remove this physical assumption, we construct a
specific
family of Floquet circuits whose eigenstates provably obey ETH at infinite temperature.
Used as an input for the FK clock Hamiltonian, this produces ground states which are provably volume-law entangled.
These circuits are a quantum implementation of linear feedback shift registers (LFSRs)
Tausworthe 1965
;
Massey 1969
;
Klein 2013
—classical circuits that play a role in pseudorandom number generation.
Independently of their application to our construction, we expect these Floquet models to be very useful in the study of ergodicity, thermalization, and chaos in quantum systems
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
.
While there are several solvable models of thermalization based on random
Chan et al. 2018
;
Fisher et al. 2023
;
Liao and Galitski 2022
or dual unitary
Bertini et al. 2025a
circuits, and proofs of thermalization in a large class of translationally-invariant Hamiltonians
Huang and Harrow 2020
;
Pilatowsky-Cameo and Choi 2025
, this solvability does not extend to a closed-form expression for the eigenstates, and only a proof of a weak form of ETH
Biroli et al. 2010
;
Mori 2016
;
Łydżba et al. 2024
;
Vikram 2025
.
The only class of prior models known to us in which ETH is provably obeyed are random matrices
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
;
Khaymovich et al. 2020
;
Sugimoto et al. 2023
, which have no locality structure.
Further, the LFSR dynamics conform with many statements of quantum chaos, but fail many others.
Indeed, while we show that their eigenstates obey ETH, in the sense that off-diagonal matrix elements of local operators are small, their spectra are nothing like those of a Haar random unitary.
As an exactly solvable edge case, they clarify the implications of different criteria of chaos and thermalization, and should be broadly useful in sharpening our understanding of many-body dynamics.
We note that LFSR quantum circuits also recently appeared in Ref.
Kim 2025
in an unrelated context.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In
Sec. II
we review the standard Feynman-Kitaev clock construction, introduce its modifications with periodic boundary conditions on the clock, and prove that its eigenstates exhibit volume law entanglement whenever a corresponding eigenstate of the input circuit obeys the ETH (Theorem
II.3
).
Then, in
Sec. III
, we introduce Floquet quantum circuits based on classical LFSRs and prove that almost all eigenstates thereof obey the ETH at infinite temperature (Theorem
III.2
), which in combination with the previous result yields a rigorous construction of local Hamiltonians with volume-law entangled ground states (Theorem
III.3
).
We discuss the implications and possible extensions of our work in
Sec. IV
.

Our construction of volume-law-entangled ground states is based on the Feynman-Kitaev clock (FK clock) model
(
Kitaev et al. 2002
, Chapter 14)
.
This model is designed to embed the result of a quantum computation in a Hamiltonian ground state. In its original context, it was intended to show that finding the ground state of a
k
-local Hamiltonian is, in general, hard. (It is QMA complete
Kitaev et al. 2002
.)

By introducing periodic boundary conditions to the FK clock model, we demonstrate that it can be used to embed any Floquet eigenstate of a quantum circuit into the ground state of a local Hamiltonian.

In
Sec. II.1
we review the FK clock construction, which takes as input a quantum circuit, and produces a Hamiltonian whose ground state contains the output of the quantum circuit.
Then we introduce periodic boundary conditions in
Sec. II.2
, and demonstrate that the new Hamiltonian eigenstates are related to Floquet states of the input circuit.
In
Sec. II.3
, we extend the clock model to a geometrically local many-body Hamiltonian.
Finally, in
Sec. II.4
we prove that eigenstates of the clock model have volume-law entanglement provided the Floquet states of the input circuit obey (diagonal) ETH.

II.1
Open clocks

The FK clock model is defined on a tensor product Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{clock}}

(1)

with factors
\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\text{clock}}
of dimension
2^{n}
and
T
respectively.
\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}
represents a system of
n
qubits (referred to as spins) while
\mathcal{H}_{\text{clock}}
contains states of an auxiliary
clock
degree of freedom.
Denote an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{\text{clock}}
by
\ket{t}
with
t\in\{0,...,T-1\}
. Given any quantum circuit consisting of
T-1
local gates on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}
,
\{u_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-2}
, the FK clock Hamiltonian is

H=\Pi^{\text{(init)}}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-2}\Pi_{t}^{\text{(tick)}},

(2)

where

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}-\Pi^{\text{(init)}}=

\displaystyle\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0},

(3a)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}-\Pi_{t}^{\text{(tick)}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big[u_{t}\otimes\ket{t+1}-\mathbbm{1}\otimes\ket{t}\big]

\displaystyle\quad\times\big[u^{\dagger}_{t}\otimes\bra{t+1}-\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{t}\big],

(3b)

and
\ket{\psi_0}
is a state in
\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}
.
We refer to this model as the open FK clock model, to indicate that it has open boundary conditions—there are no terms in the Hamiltonian which couple
\ket{T-1}
to
\ket{0}
.

The interpretation of the Hamiltonian (
2
) is as follows.
The projector
\Pi^{\text{(init)}}
picks out an initial state
\ket{\psi_0}
on which some quantum computation
\prod_{t=0}^{T-2}u_{t}
is to be performed.
Then each of the projectors
\Pi_{t}^{\text{(tick)}}
tries to connect each “tick” of the clock with the application of a gate
u_{t}
to the spins [
Fig. 1
(a)]. That is, it gives an energy penalty unless a state is of the form
u_{t}\ket{\psi_t}\otimes\ket{t+1}+\ket{\psi_t}\otimes\ket{t}
.
In fact, this Hamiltonian is frustration free, with a unique zero-energy ground state given by the
history state

\ket{\Psi_{\text{hist}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}U_{t:0}\ket{\psi_0}\otimes\ket{t},

(4)

where we define the propagator
U_{t_{2}:t_{1}}
in terms of a partial product of gates from the circuit,

U_{t_{2}:t_{1}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}u_{t_{2}-1}\cdots u_{t_{1}}&\text{for }t_{2}>t_{1},\\
\mathbbm{1}&\text{for }t_{2}=t_{1},\\
u^{\dagger}_{t_{2}}\cdots u^{\dagger}_{t_{1}-1}&\text{for }t_{2}<t_{1}.\end{array}\right.

(5)

Measuring the clock in the history state Eq. (
4
) gives a uniformly random outcome
t\in\{0,\dots T-1\}
. In particular it gives the outcome
\ket{T-1}
with probability
1/T
. In this case, the spins are projected into a state corresponding to the result of applying the quantum computation
U_{T-1:0}
to
\ket{\psi_0}
,

[\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{T}]\ket{ \Psi_{\text{hist}}}\propto U_{T-1:0}\ket{\psi_0}.

(6)

Provided the quantum circuit size
T
is polynomial in
n
, this outcome can be postselected efficiently. This is what allows the reduction of any problem in QMA to an instance of the local Hamiltonian problem, giving QMA-completeness
Kitaev et al. 2002
;
Kempe et al. 2006
.

II.2
Periodic clocks

The FK clock model is designed such that the ground state encodes the time evolution of a state under
U_{t:0}
.
The finite nature of this process (with an input state, a computation, and an output) translates to open boundary conditions on the clock register: states
t=0
and
t=T-1
are not connected by ticks.
By changing the clock’s boundary conditions to be periodic [
Fig. 1
(b)], we obtain Hamiltonians that try to energetically enforce
time-periodic
history states, where the output of the computation is equal to the input.
That is, the eigenstates of
H
encode Floquet states of the circuit, interpreted as a fixed unitary evolution applied periodically in time.

The periodic FK clock model is defined in the same spin-clock Hilbert space as in
Sec. II.1
,
but with a circuit on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}
with one more gate,
U_{T:0}=\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}u_{t}
, and an associated tick projector that replaces the initialization term,

H=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Pi_{t}^{\text{(tick)}}.

(7)

The final tick projector connects states
\ket{0}
and
\ket{T-1}
,

\mathbbm{1}-\Pi_{T-1}^{\text{(tick)}}=\frac{1}{2}\big[u_{T-1}\otimes\ket{0}-\mathbbm{1}\otimes\ket{T-1}\big]\\
\times\big[u^{\dagger}_{T-1}\otimes\bra{0}-\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{T-1}\big].

(8)

The periodic FK clock model is typically no longer frustration free.
One can satisfy all tick projectors for
t=0,\dots T-2
with a history state
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\ket{\psi_t}\otimes\ket{t}
as in the open case, but the added tick projector across the boundary imposes the additional constraint that
U_{T:0}\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{\psi_0}
.
This can only be satisfied if the spectrum of
U_{T:0}
contains
+1
, which is usually not the case.

Even though
H
is frustrated, its eigenstates can be obtained exactly assuming that we are given a complete set of Floquet states
\{\ket{\phi}\}
for
U_{T:0}
, such that

U_{T:0}\ket{\phi}=e^{i\phi}\ket{\phi}.

(9)

We observe that
H
has Krylov sectors given by

\mathrm{span}\left\{\ket{K_{t,\phi}}:=U_{t:0}\ket{\phi}\otimes\ket{t}:t\in\{0,...,T-1\}\right\}.

(10)

Indeed, noting that

H=\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(u_{t}\otimes\outerproduct{t+1}{t}+\text{h.c.})

(11)

(where we identify
\ket{T}\equiv\ket{0}
)
H
acts on the Krylov states
\ket{K_{t,\phi}}
as

H\ket{K_{t,\phi}}=\ket{K_{t,\phi}}-\frac{1}{2}\ket{K_{t+1,\phi}}-\frac{1}{2}\ket{K_{t-1,\phi}},

(12)

where we defined
\ket{K_{T,\phi}}=U_{T:0}\ket{\phi}\otimes\ket{0}
.
The final tick at
t=T-1
takes
\ket{K_{T-1,\phi}}
to
\ket{K_{T,\phi}}
. However, by the assumption that
\ket{\phi}
is an eigenstate of
U_{T:0}
, we have that

\ket{K_{T,\phi}}=U_{T:0}\ket{\phi}\otimes\ket{T}=e^{i\phi}\ket{K_{0,\phi}}.

(13)

The final tick returns the
\ket{K_{T-1,\phi}}
state to the first state in the Krylov sector, but with a twisted phase.
Indeed, the matrix for
H
in the
\ket{K_{t,\phi}}
basis of the sector is just that for a single particle hopping on a
T
-site circle, with a twist of the periodic boundary conditions by
\phi
,

[H]_{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0&\cdots&-e^{i\phi}\\
-1&2&-1&\cdots&0\\
0&-1&2&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-e^{-i\phi}&0&0&\cdots&2\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

The eigenstates in this Krylov sector are

\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}e^{i(k-\phi/T)t}\ket{K_{t,\phi}}

(15)

where
k\in\frac{2\pi}{T}\{0,1,...,T-1\}
represents the momentum of the clock’s hand. The corresponding eigenvalues are

E_{k,\phi}=1-\cos(k-\phi/T).

(16)

Some remarks about this energy spectrum are in order. First,
simple counting shows that the energies
E_{k,\phi}
account for all
T2^{n}
eigenvalues of
H
, so we have the entire spectrum. Notably, the spectrum is bounded:
\mathrm{sp}(H)\subseteq[0,2]
.
This is despite the Hamiltonian
H
ostensibly being a many-body Hamiltonian. Effectively,
H
splits into exponentially many single-particle models, each with a bounded energy, and
H
inherits the spectral properties of these single-particle models.
However, the eigenstates making up these single-particle sectors involve the infinite-temperature Floquet states
\ket{\phi}
.

Secondly, note that the phase
\phi
appearing in
E_{k,\phi}
can be modified by introducing a phase twist
U_{T:0}\mapsto e^{i\chi}U_{T:0}
.
Such a twist can be introduced, for instance, by multiplying
u_{T-1}
by
e^{i\chi}
. Inspecting Eq. (
11
), this is physically a twist of the periodic boundary conditions by
\chi
, or equivalently a flux through the clock.
In particular, we can set
\chi=-\phi
for any
\phi
, which tunes the energy of
\ket{\Psi_{0,\phi}}
to
0
. That is, by introducing a phase twist, any Floquet state can be embedded in the frustration-free ground state of the periodic FK clock model. It follows also that the FK clock Hamiltonian (assuming a nondegenerate spectrum for
U_{T-1:0}
) is frustration-free for
2^{n}
distinct choices of
\chi
in the interval
[0,2\pi)
. In this sense the Hamiltonian is typically exponentially close to frustration-free.

Related to this, we also see that the finite-size gap of the periodic FK clock model is extremely small.
For typical choices of circuit, the values of
\phi
will have typical level spacings of
2\pi\cdot 2^{-n}
.
However, the energies
E_{k,\phi}
have a van Hove singularity near
E_{k,\phi}=0
, so that the density of states is enhanced even further, and the gap above the ground state is smaller still.
From
E_{0,\phi}\approx(\phi/T)^{2}/2
, it will be
O(4^{-n})
.

Many-handed clocks

0\leq t\leq n-2

V(\mathbbm{1}\otimes c^{\dagger}_{t+1}c_{t})V^{\dagger}=u_{t}\otimes c^{\dagger}_{t+1}c_{t}

(27)

and for
t=n-1
, recalling
u_{n-1}\propto\mathbbm{1}
,

V^{\dagger}(u_{n-1}\otimes c^{\dagger}_{0}c_{n-1})V=U_{n:0}\otimes c^{\dagger}_{0}c_{n-1}.

(28)

Thus under the change of basis
V
, the FK clock Hamiltonian of Eq. (
17
) reads

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}HV

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\left(\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{n-2}(c^{\dagger}_{t+1}c_{t}+\mathrm{h.c.})\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}U_{n:0}\otimes c_{0}^{\dagger}c_{n-1}+\mathrm{h.c.}

(29)

Here the only operator acting nontrivially on the spin system is the Floquet unitary
U_{n:0}
(and its adjoint), which can be diagonalized separately from the clock. Projecting on a Floquet state
\ket{\phi}
such that
U_{n:0}\ket{\phi}=e^{i\phi}\ket{\phi}
returns a tight binding Hamiltonian for the clock fermions with a twisted boundary condition
\phi
, the same as in Eq. (
21
).
It follows that the eigenstates
\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf k, \phi}}
are obtained as

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf k, \phi}}

\displaystyle=V(\ket{\phi}\otimes c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}\ket{\Omega}),

(30)

as can be also verified by direct calculation.
This form illustrates more explicitly the structure of the eigenstates [Eq. (
23
)].
It also shows that, given an eigenstate
\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf k,\phi}}
, it is possible to efficiently prepare the corresponding Floquet state
\ket{\phi}
of the spin system by acting with
V^{\dagger}
(a polynomial-depth circuit) and discarding the clock.

II.4
Volume-law eigenstate entanglement entropy

In this section, we show that the eigenstates of the periodic FK clock Hamiltonian are volume-law entangled whenever the eigenstates of the input quantum circuit obey the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH)
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
.

As we have seen, the eigenstates of the periodic FK clock Hamiltonian
H
[Eq. (
17
)] are expressed in terms of eigenstates of a unitary circuit
U_{n:0}
.
The vast majority of these Floquet eigenstates are generically expected to obey the ETH at infinite temperature:

Definition II.1

(Floquet ETH)
.

Let
\{\ket{i}\}
be eigenstates of a Floquet operator on
n
qubits without conservation laws and let
O
be a local operator with operator norm
\norm{O}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
. The states
\{\ket{i}\}
are said to obey the
eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
(ETH) at infinite temperature, or
Floquet ETH
, if

\bra{i}O\ket{j}=\delta_{ij}\frac{{\rm Tr}(O)}{2^{n}}+2^{-n/2}R_{ij}^{O},

(31)

where
|R_{ij}^{O}|
is of order 1.
If Eq. (
31
) holds with
i=j
and all local operators
O
, the state
\ket{i}
is said to obey
diagonal ETH
at infinite temperature, or
diagonal Floquet ETH
.

The statement of ETH usually demands that the factors
R_{ij}^{O}
be erratically varying or random-like in some way which is rarely quantified precisely. They cannot be completely uncorrelated in actual local models
Pappalardi et al. 2022
;
Pappalardi et al. 2025
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Hahn et al. 2024
. We will only demand that they are
O(1)
in system size when the support of the operator
O
is fixed.
Also note that Definition
II.1
gives no condition on the eigenvalues
e^{i\phi}
of the Floquet unitary, only on the eigenstates
\ket{i}
. To relate ETH to equilibration (smallness of fluctuations in expectation values about their steady state value), additional non-resonance conditions on such eigenvalues are typically required
Srednicki 1999
;
Linden et al. 2009
;
Pilatowsky-Cameo and Choi 2025
;
Huang 2024
.
We separate these non-resonance conditions from the condition on eigenstates, and refer only to the latter as ETH.

A consequence of diagonal Floquet ETH is that each Floquet eigenstate is highly entangled. One can show that local reduced density matrices are extremely close to maximally mixed:

Lemma II.2
.

Suppose
\ket{i}
obeys diagonal Floquet ETH and that for all operators
O
supported in a local subsystem
A
of size
\ell
with
\norm{O}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
we have
|R^{O}_{ii}|\leq C_{\ell}
. Then

\displaystyle\norm{\Tr_{\bar{A}}\ketbra{i}{i} - 2^{-\ell} \unit_A}_{\Tr}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ell}2^{-n/2},

(32a)

\displaystyle\norm{\Tr_{\bar{A}}\ketbra{i}{i}}_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\ell}+C_{\ell}2^{-n/2},

(32b)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{\Tr}
is the trace norm and
\norm{\cdot}_{\mathrm{op}}
is the operator norm.

Proof.

For Eq. (
32a
), use
\norm{\Delta}_{\Tr}=\sup_{\norm{O}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1}|\Tr(O \Delta)|
and directly apply Definition
II.1
.
Then Eq. (
32b
) follows from the triangle inequality and
\norm{\Delta}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\norm{\Delta}_{\Tr}
.
∎

This implies a lower bound on entanglement entropy:
for any local subsystem
A
, we have
S_{A}(\ket{i})\geq|A|\ln(2)+O(2^{-n/2})
.
It is natural to expect that this volume-law scaling of entanglement entropy is inherited by the Hamiltonian eigenstates
\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\phi}}
[Eq. (
23
)], as we show is indeed the case. Note that in
Sec. III
we introduce a model where Floquet ETH can be proven rigorously for almost all eigenstates, without any physical assumptions. All the results of this section hold for that choice of
\{u_{t}\}_{t}
.

Here we focus on the case of a one-handed clock, while the many-handed case (conceptually analogous but technically more involved) is discussed in Appendix
A
.
We assume diagonal Floquet ETH not just for the Floquet state
\ket{\phi}
, but also for the partially time-evolved states
U_{t:0}\ket{\phi}
for all
t<n
; note that these are eigenstates of
U_{t:0}U_{n:t}
, the same periodic staircase circuit with a different choice of starting time for the period. Thus, if
\ket{\phi}
obeys diagonal Floquet ETH, all of
\{U_{t:0}\ket{\phi}\}_{t}
typically will as well.
We focus on contiguous subsystems
A
of the spin-clock ladder:
A=A_{s}\cup A_{c}
where
A_{s}
and
A_{c}
describe the same interval
\{t_{1},t_{1}+1,\dots t_{1}+\ell-1\text{ mod }n\}
in the spin and clock chains respectively.
4
4

4

These are also the relevant contiguous subsystems when viewing the spin-clock ladder as a single chain of 4-level qudits.

Theorem II.3

(Volume-law entanglement from Floquet ETH)
.

If
U_{t:0}\ket{\phi}
obeys diagonal Floquet ETH (Definition
II.1
) for all
t<n
, then all eigenstates
\ket{\Psi_{k, \phi}}
of the one-handed periodic FK clock Hamiltonian [Eq. (
11
)] which are built from
\ket{\phi}
are volume-law entangled:
the entanglement entropy of any interval
A
of length
\ell\leq n/2
obeys

S_{A}(\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}})\geq\ell\ln(2)

(33)

provided that

\frac{\ell\ln(2)}{n/2}\geq C_{\ell}2^{-n/2}+C_{\ell}^{2}2^{\ell-n},

(34)

where
C_{\ell}
is as in Lemma
II.2
.
Eq. (
34
) holds in particular for any constant
\ell
at sufficiently large
n
.

Proof.

We lower-bound the entanglement entropy by the second Rényi entropy,

S_{A}(\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}})\geq S^{(2)}_{A}(\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}}):=-\ln\Tr(\rho_A^2),

(35)

where
\rho_{A}
is the reduced density matrix of
\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{k,\phi}(t)\varphi^{\ast}_{k,\phi}(t^{\prime})\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t^{\prime})}\otimes{\rm Tr}_{\bar{A}_{c}}(\outerproduct{t}{t'}),

(36)

with
A_{s}
and
A_{c}
the subsystems of the spin and clock chains,
\varphi_{k,\phi}(t)=n^{-1/2}e^{i(k-\phi/n)t}
are the free fermion eigenfunctions, and

\displaystyle\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t^{\prime})}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}_{\bar{A}_{s}}(U_{t:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{t^{\prime}:0}^{\dagger}).

(37)

For the operator on the clock Hilbert space,
we have
{\rm Tr}_{\bar{A}_{c}}(\outerproduct{t}{t'})=\outerproduct{t}{t'}_{A_{c}}
if both
t,t^{\prime}
belong to
A_{c}
, otherwise
{\rm Tr}_{\bar{A}_{c}}(\outerproduct{t}{t'})=\delta_{t,t^{\prime}}\outerproduct{\Omega}{\Omega}_{A_{c}}
, with
\ket{\Omega}
the vacuum state. This gives

\displaystyle\rho_{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t,t^{\prime}\in A}e^{i(k-\phi/n)(t-t^{\prime})}\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t^{\prime})}\otimes\outerproduct{t}{t'}_{A_{c}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\notin A}\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t)}\otimes\outerproduct{\Omega}{\Omega}_{A_{c}}.

(38)

The purity of
\rho_{A}
is calculated straightforwardly using orthogonality of the clock states (
\innerproduct{t}{t'}=\delta_{t,t^{\prime}}
,
\innerproduct{\Omega}{t}=0
), giving

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{t,t^{\prime}\in A}\left\|\sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}_{A_{s}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left\|\sum_{t\notin A}\sigma^{(t,t)}_{A_{s}}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(39)

with
\|O\|_{F}=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}(O^{\dagger}O)}
the Frobenius norm. Here we used the fact that
\sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}=(\sigma^{(t^{\prime},t)})^{\dagger}
.

Let us focus on the “diagonal” terms
\sigma^{(t,t)}
first. Note that these are simply the reduced density matrices of the states
U_{t:0}\ket{\phi}
, which we assume obey diagonal Floquet ETH (Definition
II.1
).
Applying Lemma
II.2
to
U_{t:0}\ket{\phi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}[\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t)}\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})}]

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}[\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t)}(\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})}-2^{-\ell}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s}})]+2^{-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\ell}+\|\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\sigma^{(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})}_{A_{s}}-2^{-\ell}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s}}\|_{\Tr}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\ell}+C_{\ell}2^{-\ell-n/2}+C_{\ell}^{2}2^{-n}

(40)

for some constant
C_{\ell}
as in Lemma
II.2
. We also used the triangle inequality and
|\Tr(O_1 O_2)|\leq\norm{O_1}_{\mathrm{op}}\norm{O_2}_{\Tr}
.

For the “off-diagonal” terms
\sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}_{A_{s}}
with
t\neq t^{\prime}
, we must distinguish two cases, depending on whether or not
A
contains the origin,
t=0
.

(i)

0\notin A
. Using the composition property of propagators
U_{t:0}=U_{t:t^{\prime}}U_{t^{\prime}:0}
, we can write write
\sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}_{A_{s}}=\sigma^{(t,t)}_{A_{c}}U_{t:t^{\prime}}
, noting that
U_{t:t^{\prime}}
is entirely supported in
A_{s}
and thus can be taken outside the partial trace.

(ii)

0\in A
. In this case
t
and
t^{\prime}
may lie on opposite sides of the origin, making
U_{t:t^{\prime}}
a nonlocal string that spans
\bar{A}_{s}
.
To avoid this issue we use the composition property in a different way. Assuming
t>t^{\prime}
without loss of generality, we have
U_{t:0}\ket{\phi}=U_{t:n}U_{n:0}\ket{\phi}=e^{i\phi}U_{n:t}^{\dagger}\ket{\phi}
, using the fact that
\ket{\phi}
is a Floquet eigenstate. Thus
\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(t,t^{\prime})}=e^{i\phi}U_{n:t}^{\dagger}\sigma_{A_{s}}^{(0,0)}U_{t^{\prime}:0}^{\dagger}
.

In both cases we managed to reduce
\sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}_{A_{s}}
to a product of a diagonal term,

\sigma^{(t,t)}_{A_{s}}\approx 2^{-\ell}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s}}
,
and unitary operators supported inside
A_{s}
. It follows that

\|\sigma^{(t,t^{\prime})}_{A_{s}}\|_{F}^{2}=\Tr[\sigma^{(t,t)}_{A_{s}}\sigma^{(t,t)}_{A_{s}}]\leq 2^{-\ell}+C_{\ell}2^{-\ell-n/2}+C_{\ell}^{2}2^{-n},

(41)

as before.

Adding all terms in Eq. (
39
), we get

{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}^{2})\leq\frac{\ell^{2}+(n-\ell)^{2}}{n^{2}}\left[2^{-\ell}+C_{\ell}2^{-\ell-n/2}+C_{\ell}^{2}2^{-n}\right],

(42)

and thus

\displaystyle S_{A}(\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}})

\displaystyle=-\ln{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}^{2})

(43a)

\displaystyle\geq\ell\ln(2)+\ln\left[\frac{n^{2}}{\ell^{2}+(n-\ell)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\ln\left[1+C_{\ell}2^{-n/2}+C_{\ell}^{2}2^{\ell-n}\right],

(43b)

\displaystyle\geq\ell\ln(2)+\frac{\ell\ln(2)}{n/2}-C_{\ell}2^{-n/2}-C_{\ell}^{2}2^{\ell-n},

(43c)

where we used
x\geq\ln(1+x)
, and used
\ell\leq n/2
so that
\ln[n^{2}/(\ell^{2}+(n-\ell)^{2})]\geq 2\ln(2)\ell/n
(which follows from concavity of the logarithm).
When the conditions of the theorem hold, the combination of the latter three terms in Eq. (
43c
) is nonnegative, and we simply have
S_{A}(\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}})\geq\ell\ln(2)
.
∎

Theorem
II.3
applies to all eigenstates
\ket{\Psi_{k,\phi}}
, and thus in particular to the ground state
\ket{\Psi_{0,\phi}}
, provided the associated Floquet state
\ket{\phi}
meets the theorem’s assumptions. If such an eigenstate exists anywhere in the spectrum of
U_{n:0}
, then it can always be embedded in the ground state of
H
by introducing a phase twist
\chi=-\phi
in the clock, e.g., by changing
u_{n-1}=c\mathbbm{1}
to
u_{n-1}=ce^{i\chi}\mathbbm{1}
.
Thus Theorem
II.3
shows that the ground state of the periodic FK clock model can be volume-law entangled.

We note that Eq. (
42
) has a physically intuitive structure: the purity factors into two contributions,
2^{-\ell}
(up to a small error term) from the spin chain, and
(\ell/n)^{2}+(1-\ell/n)^{2}
from the clock. The latter comes from the probability,
\ell/n
, of finding the clock hand in region
A_{c}
in an eigenfunction
\varphi_{k,\phi}(t)
. This factorization in fact holds more generally. In Appendix
A
we prove a generalization of Theorem
II.3
to the many-handed case:

Theorem A.1
.

Let
A
be any subinterval of the spin-clock system.
Then the second Réyni entropy of any many-hand clock eigenstate
\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\phi}}
is bounded below as

S^{(2)}_{A}(\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\phi}})\geq S^{(2)}_{A}(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}\ket{\Omega})+\min_{\mathbf{t}}S^{(2)}_{A}(U_{\mathbf{t}:0}\ket{\phi}),

(44)

where the minimum is over hand configurations with no more hands than
\ket{\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\phi}}
, and

c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}=\sum_{\mathbf{t}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k},\phi}(\mathbf{t})c_{\mathbf{t}}^{\dagger}=\prod_{k\in\mathbf{k}}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}e^{i(k-\phi/T)t}c_{t}^{\dagger}\right]

(45)

is the creation operator for a Slater determinant state.

Provided all
\{U_{\mathbf{t}:0}\ket{\phi}\}_{\mathbf{t}}
are volume-law entangled, then this theorem generalizes our result of volume-law entanglement to many-handed clock eigenstates as well. We note again that the contributions to the entanglement lower bound from the clock and the spin system add up. In particular the clock contributes the entanglement of a free-fermion Slater determinant state of momenta
\mathbf{k}
, which can be itself volume-law (in highly excited states)
Lai and Yang 2015
or logarithmic (in the Fermi sea ground state)
Wolf 2006
;
Gioev and Klich 2006
.

III
Floquet circuits with provably thermal eigenstates

So far, our results rely on the assumption of ETH for the input Floquet circuit. While the ETH is widely believed to hold for generic choices of circuit
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Liao and Galitski 2022
, it is desirable to have models where our results are provable. In fact, there are few (if any) local circuit or Hamiltonian models which provably obey the ETH. Beyond our application, it is broadly beneficial to the study of ergodicity and chaos in quantum systems to have an exactly solvable model of eigenstate thermalization.

To this end, we introduce a class of Floquet circuits whose eigenstates are provably thermal. These circuits are based on linear feedback shift registers (LFSRs)
Tausworthe 1965
;
Massey 1969
;
Klein 2013
, classical circuits that play a role in pseudorandom number generators. Below, we introduce LFSRs and their implementation as staircase quantum circuits, derive their eigenstates (
Sec. III.1
), prove they obey infinite-temperature ETH (
Sec. III.2
), and use this to prove volume-law entanglement in the corresponding FK clock Hamiltonian ground state (
Sec. III.3
).

While our results are phrased in terms of a staircase circuit, this is not essential to the construction. The same eigenstate thermalization properties can be shown to hold for a brickwork circuit constructed from the same gates.
5
5

5

The staircase and brickwork versions of the circuit are related by a Clifford similarity transformation, so they share the same spectrum and the same set of Pauli matrix elements. The latter is all we use to conclude eigenstate thermalization.

III.1
LFSR circuits

An LFSR is a reversible update rule for a classical state of
n
bits,
(z_{0},...,z_{n-1})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, given by
6
6

6

For convenience we use a backward shift, instead of the more standard forward shift, to obtain a quantum staircase circuit with the same layout used in
Sec. II
.

z_{i}^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z_{i+1}&\text{for }i<n-1,\\
z_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}z_{i}\bmod 2&\text{for }i=n-1.\end{array}\right.

(46)

The system size
n
and binary coefficients
a_{i}
completely specify the model. In words, the LFSR shifts all bits by one place, then the
output
bit (
i=0
) is fed back into the
input
position (
i=n-1
) along with a linear function of the other bits.

As a reversible finite-state machine, each LFSR partitions the state space into a set of orbits. An LFSR is called
maximal
if it splits the state space into just two orbits: the all-zero state (which always forms a trivial orbit by itself, due to linearity) and all
2^{n}-1
non-zero states. Maximal LFSRs are known to exist for all values of
n
, can be constructed efficiently, and are in one-to-one correspondence with primitive polynomials of degree
n
over the binary field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
; see Appendix
B
for more details.

The LFSR update rule, Eq. (
46
), is naturally implemented as a staircase circuit of
\mathsf{SWAP}
and
\mathsf{CNOT}
gates,

U_{n:0}=(\mathsf{CNOT}_{n-2,n-1})^{a_{n-1}}\mathsf{SWAP}_{n-2,n-1}\times\cdots\\
\cdots\times(\mathsf{CNOT}_{0,1})^{a_{1}}\mathsf{SWAP}_{0,1},

(47)

corresponding to
u_{i}=(\mathsf{CNOT}_{i,i+1})^{a_{i}}\mathsf{SWAP}_{i,i+1}
for
0\leq i\leq n-2
and
u_{n-1}=\mathbbm{1}
. Here

\mathsf{CNOT}_{i,j}
is the controlled NOT gate with control bit
i
and target bit
j
and the
a_{i}
are the binary coefficients from Eq. (
46
). This is sketched in
Fig. 2
(a).
The staircase circuit structure makes these models well-suited to our FK clock Hamiltonian construction.

Equation (
47
) belongs to several special classes of quantum circuits:
reversible classical circuits (also known as “automaton” or “permutation” circuits
Gopalakrishnan 2018
;
Gopalakrishnan and Zakirov 2018
;
Iaconis 2021
;
Bertini et al. 2025b
), which do not generate coherence;
Clifford circuits, which do not generate “magic” (non-stabilizerness)
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
;
Veitch et al. 2014
;
dual-unitary circuits
Bertini et al. 2025a
, which are unitary along both the time and space directions.
Despite these special features,
U_{n:0}
has eigenstates that behave similarly to random states, as we show next.

Let us consider a maximal LFSR with update rule
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=\alpha\mathbf{z}
, with
\alpha
a
n\times n
binary matrix implementing Eq. (
46
).
The Floquet unitary in Eq. (
47
) acts on the compuational basis as
U_{n:0}\ket{\mathbf z}=\ket{\alpha\mathbf z}
. From this, it is clear that the all-zero state
\ket{\boldsymbol{0}}
is an eigenstate with eigenvalue
+1
. The remaining
2^{n}-1
eigenstates span the nontrivial orbit and are given by

\ket{\psi_q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}-1}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-2}\omega^{qj}\ket{\alpha^j \boldsymbol{1}},

(48)

where
\bm{1}=(00\dots 01)
,
\omega=e^{2\pi i/(2^{n}-1)}
, and
q\in\{0,1,...,2^{n}-2\}
.
The eigenstate
\ket{\psi_q}
has eigenvalue
\omega^{-q}
.
For
q=0
, Eq. (
48
) gives a nearly disentangled state,
\ket{\psi_0}\propto\ket{+}^{\otimes n}-2^{-n/2}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
, with eigenvalue
+1
. So the
+1
eigenspace of
U_{n:0}
is spanned by two product states,
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
and
\ket{+}^{\otimes n}
. All other eigenspaces,
\omega^{q}
with
q\neq 0
, are non-degenerate.
Hereafter we refer to all
q\neq 0
eigenstates in Eq. (
48
) as the
nontrivial eigenstates
of the maximal LFSR.

It is useful at this point to introduce the discrete logarithm on the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, defined as follows: for all bitstrings
\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0}
,
\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})
is the unique element
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}-1}
such that
\alpha^{j}\bm{1}=\mathbf{z}
. With this definition, the LFSR eigenstates Eq. (
48
) may be written as

\ket{\psi_q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}-1}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0}}\omega^{q\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})}\ket{\mathbf z}.

(49)

These states were introduced in Refs.
van Dam and Seroussi 2002
;
van Dam 2003
under the name of “chi states” in the context of quantum algorithms for algebraic problems, and also recently appeared in Ref.
Kim 2025
in the context of magic gate catalysis for quantum computing.

The discrete logarithm
\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})
is known to behave ‘‘pseudorandomly’’, without obvious patterns or correlations across inputs.
7
7

7

Computing
\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})
is also thought to be a hard problem for classical computers, where quantum computers may have exponential advantage
Shor 1994
;
Shor 1997
;
van Dam 2003
.

As a consequence, the phases
\{\omega^{q\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})}:\mathbf{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}\}
vary erratically between different basis states. This suggests a similarity between the nontrivial (
q\neq 0
) eigenstates of Eq. (
48
) and pseudorandom phase states
Ji et al. 2018
;
Brakerski and Shmueli 2019
, which in turn behave similarly to Haar-random states. We make this intuition precise in the following.

Figure 2:
(a) Circuit diagram for a LFSR with
n=8
qubits, implementing the update rule Eq. (
46
) with
a_{2}=a_{3}=a_{4}=1
,
a_{i}=0
otherwise.
(b-c) Numerical tests of Lemma
III.1
.
(b) Distribution of Pauli expectation values on the nontrivial eigenstate
\ket{\psi_1}
of an
n
-bit maximal LFSR, normalized by the value of the upper bound in Lemma
III.1
, for various system sizes. All
4^{n}-1
non-idenity Pauli operators are included. The dashed line represents a semicircle distribution,
p(\mu)=(2/\pi)\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}
for
|\mu|\leq 1
. All computed values respect the bound (
|\mu|\leq 1
).
(c) Distribution of Pauli matrix elements between nontrivial eigenstates
\ket{\psi_1}
and
\ket{\psi_q}
of an
n
-bit maximal LFSR, normalized as in (b). All
4^{n}-1
nontrivial Pauli operators and all
2^{n}-2
values of
q\neq 0
are included. The inset shows that the matrix elements come exponentially close to saturating the bound,
\max|\mu|\approx 1-\mathrm{const.}\times 4^{-n}
.
There is a visible spike in
p(|\mu|)
above the semicircle background at
|\mu|=1/2
; this arises from Pauli operators that are either purely
X
-type or
Z
-type, which are a fraction
\sim 2^{1-n}
of the total (Appendix
D
).

III.2
Eigenstate thermalization

We begin by showing that all matrix elements of non-identity Pauli operators between nontrivial LFSR eigenstates are exponentially small.
We then use this fact to establish a precise statement of eigenstate thermalization, from which near-maximal entanglement entropy follows.

Lemma III.1

(Bound on Pauli matrix elements)
.

For any maximal
n
-bit LFSR and any Pauli operator
P\neq\mathbbm{1}
, the eigenstates in Eq. (
49
) obey

|\bra{\psi_q}P\ket{\psi_{q'}}|\leq\frac{2^{1+n/2}}{2^{n}-1}

(50)

for all
q,q^{\prime}\neq 0
.

We remark that this holds for
all
non-identity Pauli operators, not just local ones.
The proof of Lemma
III.1
, reported in Appendix
C
, uses tools from algebraic number theory (bounds on “mixed-character sums”

Castro and Moreno 2000
) that formalize the “pseudorandom” nature of the discrete logarithm.

We test Lemma

numerically by computing Pauli matrix elements between nontrivial eigenstates, shown in

\mu:=\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{1+n/2}}\bra{\psi_q}P\ket{\psi_{q'}}

p(|\mu|)\propto\sqrt{1-|\mu|^{2}}
, very nearly saturating the bound
|\mu|\leq 1
.

Lemma
III.1
also implies a precise statement of eigenstate thermalization for maximal LFSRs.

Theorem III.2

(LFSR eigenstate thermalization)
.

For any maximal
n
-bit LFSR, all nontrivial eigenstates obey the infinite-temperature ETH:

\bra{\psi_q}O\ket{\psi_{q'}}=2^{-n}{\rm Tr}(O)\delta_{q,q^{\prime}}+2^{-n/2}R^{O}_{qq^{\prime}},

(52)

for all
q,q^{\prime}\neq 0
and all local operators
O
, where
|R^{O}_{qq^{\prime}}|\leq 2^{2+\ell}\|O\|_{\rm op}
and
\ell
is the size of the support of
O
.
It follows that nontrivial eigenstates have entanglement entropy

S_{A}(\ket{\psi_q})\geq\ell\ln(2)-2^{4+2\ell-n},

(53)

which is near-maximal for
\ell=o(n)
.

Proof.

First let us consider the case of a Pauli operator,
O=P
. Lemma
III.1
immediately gives Eq. (
52
) with
|R^{P}_{qq^{\prime}}|\leq 4
.
For a general operator
O
, let us decompose in the Pauli basis:
O=\sum_{P\in A}o_{P}P
, with
o_{P}={\rm Tr}(PO)/2^{n}
, with
A
the support of
O
and
P\in A
denoting Pauli operators supported in
A
.
Plugging into the left hand side of Eq. (
52
) yields the correct
\delta_{q,q^{\prime}}
term, as well as
R^{O}_{qq^{\prime}}=2^{n/2}\sum_{P\in A}^{\prime}o_{P}\bra{\psi_q}P\ket{\psi_{q'}}
with the primed sum denoting
P\neq\mathbbm{1}
.
The triangle inequality and Lemma
III.1
give

\displaystyle\left|R^{O}_{qq^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n/2}\sum_{P\in A}{}^{{}^{\prime}}|o_{P}||\bra{\psi_q}P\ket{\psi_{q'}}|\leq 4\sum_{P\in A}|o_{P}|.

(54)

Then Cauchy-Schwarz gives

\sum_{P\in A}|o_{P}|\leq 2^{\ell}\left(\sum_{P\in A}|o_{P}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=2^{\ell-n/2}\|O\|_{F}

(55)

where
\|O\|_{F}=[{\rm Tr}(O^{\dagger}O)]^{1/2}

is the Frobenius norm. The norm inequality
\|O\|_{F}\leq 2^{n/2}\|O\|_{\rm op}
then gives

\left|R^{O}_{qq^{\prime}}\right|\leq 2^{2+\ell}\|O\|_{\rm op},

(56)

which concludes the proof of Eq. (
52
).

Next we prove Eq. (
53
). Volume-law entanglement is a standard consequence of ETH, since Eq. (
52
) implies that local reduced density matrices must be very close to the fully mixed state (Lemma
II.2
). In this case we can use the slightly more powerful result of Lemma
III.1
.
Since the von Neumann entropy is lower-bounded by the second Rényi entropy, we have

\displaystyle S_{A}(\ket{\psi_q})

\displaystyle\geq-\ln\left[\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}\sum_{P\in A}\bra{\psi_q}P\ket{\psi_q}^{2}\right],

(57)

where
P\in A
denotes Pauli operators supported in region
A
, and we have used the fact that
{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}^{2})=2^{-\ell}\sum_{P\in A}[{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}P)]^{2}
for any density matrix
\rho_{A}
on
A
.
We then invoke Lemma
III.1
with

to bound
|\bra{\psi_q}P\ket{\psi_{q}}|^{2}\leq 2^{4-n}
for
P\neq\mathbbm{1}
.
Splitting the Pauli sum into
P=\mathbbm{1}
and
P\neq\mathbbm{1}
(
4^{\ell}-1

\displaystyle S_{A}(\ket{\psi_q})

\displaystyle\geq\ell\ln(2)-\ln\left[1+\frac{4^{\ell}-1}{2^{n-4}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\ell\ln(2)-2^{4-n}(4^{\ell}-1),

(58)

where we also used
\ln(1+x)\leq x
. Eq. (
53
) follows immediately.
∎

We note that Theorem

does not require the subsystem

III.3
Volume-law entangled ground states

In

we proved that periodic FK clock Hamiltonians have volume-law entangled ground states (Theorem

), or at least volume-law entanglement of the states

(Theorem

Here we show that, when the gate sequence

(Volume-law entangled ground states)

For any maximal
n

at least

away from 0 (modulo

Proof.

The Krylov sectors of the trivial “scar” LFSR eigenstates

Then we can apply Theorem

Inspecting the proof of Theorem

∎

Theorem

Irani 2010
;

Notably, the volume-law scaling of entanglement entropy is established for all contiguous intervals up to the half cut.

IV
Discussion

We have introduced two families of solvable models: a local many-body variant of the Feynman-Kitaev clock (FK clock) with periodic boundary conditions, and a class of Floquet circuits based on linear feedback shift registers (LFSRs).
The former provides a way of embedding Floquet states throughout the spectrum of a static local Hamiltonian, while the latter provides an analytically tractable instance of ETH-obeying many-body Floquet states.
Using the LFSR circuits in the periodic FK clock construction, we obtain a one-dimensional Hamiltonian where all but a vanishing fraction of eigenstates are provably volume law entangled.
The volume law scaling of entanglement can be realized by the ground state, and applies to arbitrary intervals of up to one half the global system size.

Both classes of models we introduced are valuable as exactly-solvable counterexamples or edge-cases for conventional lore regarding the behavior of entanglement in many-body systems.
First, the periodic FK clock Hamiltonians give rise to a qualitatively new type of area-law violation in local one-dimensional models.
While other examples of volume-law entangled ground states are known
Gottesman and Hastings 2010
;
Irani 2010
;
Bravyi et al. 2012
;
Movassagh and Shor 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2017a
;
Salberger and Korepin 2018
, they display a special “rainbow-like” entanglement structure
Alexander et al. 2019
, such that not all subsystems actually obey the volume law.
The eigenstates of the FK clock model, being descendants of Floquet states, are closer to resembling infinite-temperature eigenstates.
With that said, the FK clock eigenstates do not obey ETH at any temperature.
They retain some of the observable physics of the free fermion clock hands
Łydżba et al. 2023
, despite the presence of nontrivial entanglement between these degrees of freedom and the spin system.
For instance, eigenstates of the same energy but opposite net hand momentum can be distinguished by measuring local “dressed” current operators,

i(c_{t+1}^{\dagger}c_{t}\otimes u_{t}-\textrm{h.c.})
, as these act like ordinary current operators on a free-fermion state [see Eq. (
30
)].
Meanwhile, observables supported only on the spin system appear essentially featureless in FK clock eigenstates.
Having such structure in the ground state is unconventional, even in a gapless system.
If one believes that low-energy and long-wavelength degrees of freedom of local Hamiltonians in one-dimension are described by quantum field theory (QFT), then the QFT for this model must simultaneously account for a zero-temperature free-fermion piece and a featureless infinite-temperature piece, with nontrivial entanglement between them.

The utility of the LFSR circuits as solvable case studies for ETH and chaos
is likely even further reaching.
While the ETH is well-established numerically
Rigol et al. 2008
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
, and has been understood through heuristics for decades
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
, we are not aware of any other local models which have been rigorously shown to satisfy it.
At best, a variety of models have been shown to satisfy
weak ETH
, where eigenbasis matrix elements vanish slower than exponentially in system size
Biroli et al. 2010
;
Mori 2016
;
Łydżba et al. 2024
;
Vikram 2025
.
The LFSR circuits thus provide a unique handle on understanding ETH and its implications as we continue to move from a qualitative understanding of ergodicity and chaos in quantum systems to a quantitative one.
Indeed, LFSR circuits meet many characterizations of quantum chaos and thermalization, but drastically fail others.
Beginning with thermalization: as we have proved, they satisfy ETH in the sense of Definition
II.1

D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
.
Nonetheless, the statistics of their quasienergies are nothing like a random matrix
Potters and Bouchaud 2020
—they are evenly spaced, maximally violating the “no-resonance condition” that is often used to show equilibration
Srednicki 1999
;
Linden et al. 2009
;
Pilatowsky-Cameo and Choi 2025
;
Huang 2024
.
As a consequence, despite satisfying ETH and being ergodic—in the sense that time-averaged expectation values of local observables reproduce infinite temperature expectation values—LFSR dynamics need not equilibrate
Srednicki 1999
. As LFSR circuits map
Z
-basis states to
Z
-basis states, the expectation value of a local Pauli
Z
operator only ever jumps between
\pm 1
when applying the circuit, and never approaches the equilibrium value of
0
.
Another consequence of the uniform level spacing is that the Heisenberg times and Poincaré recurrence times coincide for LFSR circuits, whereas generically the latter is exponentially larger
Bocchieri and Loinger 1957
;
Oszmaniec et al. 2024
.
The relation of LFSR circuits to quantum chaos seems even more contentious.
Conforming with a notion of quantum chaos, they have exactly maximal Krylov complexity
Nandy et al. 2025
up to order
2^{n}-1
when acting on either
X
or
Z
basis product states (except
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
or
\ket{+}^{\otimes n}
) or Pauli operators (except the identity).
8
8

8

This makes them concrete examples of what Ref.
Vikram and Galitski 2023
called “quantum cyclic ergodicity”. They also exhibit “quantum cyclic aperiodicity”, but over too short a time scale for there to be nontrivial consequences on the spectrum, as studied in Ref.
Vikram and Galitski 2023
.

However, they are at the intersection of permutation circuits and Clifford circuits, generating no coherence and no magic, and thus no state or operator entanglement when acting on physically relevant classes of input states or operators
Prosen and Pižorn 2007
;
Prosen and Žnidarič 2007
.
Altogether, the LFSR circuits represent a useful edge case to disentangle and discriminate between inequivalent
Harrow and Huang 2023
—but often conflated—ideas in the study of chaos and thermalization.
It would be enlightening to make a more comprehensive assessment of how they may or may not be considered chaotic
Pandey et al. 2020
.

Much of the phenomenology we have discussed should be highly unstable to generic perturbations.
Indeed, the single-hand sector of the FK clock model has a bandwidth which is independent of system size.
Typical extensive perturbations will result in a bandwidth which is extensive, presumably completely rearranging the eigenstates in the thermodynamic limit.
Introducing local impurities to the model will maintain the
O(1)
bandwidth, but may still drastically change ground state properties, especially as the density of states is very large near the ground state.
The many-hand sectors already have extensive bandwidth, but this does not guarantee that volume law entanglement in the ground state will persist.
Generally, we expect typical extensive perturbations will open a gap in the FK clock model. (Similar instability was numerically observed in the Fredkin chain
Adhikari and Beach 2019
.)
Perturbations to the LFSR circuits should make them more in line with conventional expectations for chaotic quantum circuits, but it is not clear that any analytic control can be maintained as such a perturbation is introduced.

Despite this fragility to generic perturbations, the class of periodic FK clock models is still quite large. Perturbations of the Hamiltonian which maintain the form of Eq. (
17
), only changing the
u_{t}
gates, are expected to typically maintain the infinite-temperature properties of the FK clock eigenstates, including volume law entanglement.
This gives a manifold of Hamiltonians with extensive dimension which are all expected to have volume law entangled ground states. While still fine-tuned, this is a much larger family than those obtained by prior constructions
Bravyi et al. 2012
;
Movassagh and Shor 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2017a
;
Salberger and Korepin 2018
.

Indeed, the precise form of Eq. (
17
) can even be slightly relaxed.
A simple extension of the FK clock model which does not spoil the volume law entanglement of eigenstates is to give the
c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}
and
c^{\dagger}_{t+1}c_{t}
terms in Eq. (
17
)
t
-dependent amplitudes. For instance, adding arbitrarily weak random disorder will localize the clock hands
Anderson 1958
, but nonetheless retain volume law entanglement in the system’s eigenstates.
Even including density-density interactions
c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}c^{\dagger}_{t^{\prime}}c_{t^{\prime}}
only introduces interactions among the dressed fermions [Eq. (
30
)], and so preserves the decomposition into Krylov sectors (though it makes the sectors interacting, spoiling solvability).

More significant extensions to the FK clock model may include variants in higher dimensions
Gosset et al. 2015
;
Zhang and Klich 2023
;
Balasubramanian et al. 2023
, or with other input circuits.
Indeed, there are many important classes of models which can be realized as Floquet circuits, including discrete time crystals
Khemani et al. 2019
;
Else et al. 2020
and topological Floquet phases
Harper et al. 2020
;
Long and Else 2024
.
These Floquet circuits generate intrinsically driven phenomena which are impossible to realize with static, local Hamiltonians, so the embedding of their eigenstates in the clock model must greatly affect their properties.
How, then, might the nontrivial Floquet dynamics of these circuits appear in either the dynamics or spectrum of the periodic FK clock model? Can these exhibit any remnants or signatures of intrinsically driven physics?
We leave these questions for future work.

Finally, we comment on another possible implication of our embedding of Floquet states in static Hamiltonian spectra, relating to the preparation of quasi-stationary states of Floquet circuits.
There are well-developed computational techniques for finding ground (or low-energy) states of local Hamiltonians, particularly in one dimension, including variational methods based on tensor networks
White 1992
;
Catarina and Murta 2023
or neural quantum states
Lange et al. 2024
. Targeting Floquet states tends to be more challenging.
Our construction of periodic FK clock Hamiltonians presents an interesting opportunity to map the latter problem into the former and thus take advantage of ground-state numerical methods.
While it will typically be very difficult to find the exact ground state, due to the gap in the periodic FK clock Hamiltonian being exponentially small (
\propto 4^{-n}
), even a superposition of low-energy eigenstates is sufficient for some purposes.
By applying the circuit in Eq. (
30
), a low-energy state of the spin-clock ladder can then be efficiently converted to a state of the spin system which is quasi-stationary under Floquet dynamics.
Provided that the variational energy of the spin-clock state is much smaller than the finite-size energy gap of the free-fermion sector (
\propto n^{-2}
), the resulting spin state has a narrow quasi-energy distribution and is correspondingly long-lived under the Floquet dynamics.
An interesting direction for future work will be to compare the efficiency, accuracy, and achievable system sizes in this approach against existing techniques
Khemani et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2017b
;
Yu et al. 2017
;
Sierant et al. 2020
;
Luitz 2021
.

\Tr_{B}\outerproduct{K_{\mathbf{t},\phi}}{K_{\mathbf{t}',\phi}}=\Tr_{B}[U_{\mathbf{t}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}]\\
\otimes\Tr_{B}[c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{t}}\outerproduct{\Omega}{\Omega}c_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}}].

(76)

Write
\mathbf{t}_{A}
for the ordered vector of hand positions consisting of elements of
\mathbf{t}
which lie inside
A

Then, denoting
\ket{\mathbf{t}}=c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{t}}\ket{\Omega}

\Tr_{B}\outerproduct{\mathbf{t}}{\mathbf{t}'}=\delta_{\mathbf{t}_{B}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{B}}\outerproduct{\mathbf{t}_{\Aclock}}{\mathbf{t}'_{\Aclock}},

(77)

where
\ket{\mathbf{t}_{\Aclock}}
is a state on the subsystem.
This gives us an expression for the purity

\Tr\left[(\rho^{A}_{\mathbf{k},\phi})^{2}\right]=\sum_{\mathbf{t}_{1},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1},\mathbf{t}_{2},\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k},\phi}(\mathbf{t_{1}})\varphi^{*}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}(\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1})\varphi_{\mathbf{k},\phi}(\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2})\varphi^{*}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}(\mathbf{t}_{2})\delta_{\mathbf{t}_{1,B}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1,B}}\delta_{\mathbf{t}_{2,B}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2,B}}\delta_{\mathbf{t}_{1,A}\mathbf{t}_{2,A}}\delta_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1,A}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2,A}}\\
\times\Tr\left[\Tr_{B}[U_{\mathbf{t}_{1}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1}}]\Tr_{B}[U_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{t}_{2}}]\right].

(78)

The key parts of Eq. (
78

\delta
-functions and the trace involving
\ket{\phi}
. Equation (
66

\Tr\left[\Tr_{B}[U_{\mathbf{t}_{1}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1}}]\Tr_{B}[U_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{t}_{2}}]\right]\\
=\exp\left[-S^{(2)}_{A}(U_{\mathbf{s}:0}\ket{\phi})\right]

for some
\mathbf{s}

\delta

Indeed, bounding each of these terms in Eq. (

\mathbf{s}

\Tr\left[(\rho^{A}_{\mathbf{k},\phi})^{2}\right]\leq\exp\left[-S^{(2)}_{A}(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}\ket{\Omega})-\min_{\mathbf{s}}S^{(2)}_{A}(U_{\mathbf{s}:0}\ket{\phi})\right].

(80)

Taking the logarithm gives the lower bound for the second Réyni entropy stated in Eq. (

In proving Eq. (
79

1.

the subsystem
A
does not contain the sites
\{0,T-1\}
, and

2.

A
does contain
\{0,T-1\}

In case

does not straddle the final bond completing the periodic boundary conditions, and any
\mathbf{t}

In case

\mathbf{t}=(\mathbf{t}_{A1},\mathbf{t}_{B},\mathbf{t}_{A2})
.

First consider case
1
, and apply Lemma

\displaystyle U_{\mathbf{t}_{1}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1}}

\displaystyle=V_{1}U_{\mathbf{s}_{1}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{s}_{1}}W_{1}^{\dagger},

(81)

\displaystyle U_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{t}_{2}}

\displaystyle=W_{2}U_{\mathbf{s}_{2}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{s}_{2}}V_{2}^{\dagger},

(82)

for
V_{j}
,
W_{j}
, and
\mathbf{s}_{j}
as appearing in the lemma.
Due to the
\delta

\delta_{\mathbf{t}_{1,B}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1,B}}\delta_{\mathbf{t}_{2,B}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2,B}}

\delta

\delta_{\mathbf{t}_{1,A}\mathbf{t}_{2,A}}\delta_{\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{1,A}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{2,A}}

and

\Tr\left[\Tr_{B}[U_{\mathbf{s}_{1}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{s}_{1}}]\Tr_{B}[U_{\mathbf{s}_{2}:0}\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}U_{0:\mathbf{s}_{2}}]\right],

(83)

where we must have

and we observe that

where “

It is also possible to obtain a loose lower bound on the free-fermion contribution to the second Réyni entropy.

Lemma A.3
.

The second Réyni entropy of any
M
-fermion plane-wave state obeys

S^{(2)}_{A}(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}\ket{\Omega})\geq\frac{\ell^{2}}{n^{2}}

(89)

for any interval
A
of length
\ell\leq n/2
.

Proof.

We use the result from Ref.
Muth et al. 2011
that
S^{(2)}_{A}\geq 2\Delta N_{A}^{2}
, where
\Delta N_{A}^{2}
is the number variance in the subsystem
A
.
Let
\langle O\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}:=\bra{\Omega}c_{\mathbf{k},\phi}Oc^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\phi}\ket{\Omega}
; we have

\displaystyle\Delta N_{A}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\langle\left(\sum_{t\in A}c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}-\left\langle\sum_{t\in A}c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}\in A}\langle c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}c^{\dagger}_{t^{\prime}}c_{t^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}-(\ell M/n)^{2}.

(90)

Using the change of basis
c_{t}:=n^{-1/2}\sum_{q}e^{i(q-\phi/n)t}c_{q,\phi}
(with
q\in\tfrac{2\pi}{n}\{0,1,...,n-1\}
) we get

\langle c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}c^{\dagger}_{t^{\prime}}c_{t^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{q_{1}^{(\prime)},q_{2}^{(\prime)}}e^{i(q_{1}-q_{2})t+i(q_{1}^{\prime}-q_{2}^{\prime})t^{\prime}}\\
\times\langle c^{\dagger}_{q_{1},\phi}c_{q_{2},\phi}c_{q_{1}^{\prime},\phi}^{\dagger}c_{q_{2}^{\prime},\phi}\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}.

(91)

Then using Wick’s theorem and
\langle c^{\dagger}_{q,\phi}c_{q,\phi}\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}=\delta_{q\in\mathbf{k}}
,

\displaystyle\langle c^{\dagger}_{t}c_{t}c^{\dagger}_{t^{\prime}}c_{t^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathbf{k},\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{2}}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{q\in\mathbf{k}}\sum_{q^{\prime}\notin\mathbf{k}}e^{i(q-q^{\prime})(t-t^{\prime})}

(92)

and thus, assuming without loss of generality that
A=\{0,1,...,\ell-1\}
,

\displaystyle\Delta N_{A}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{q\in\mathbf{k}}\sum_{q^{\prime}\notin\mathbf{k}}\left|\sum_{t\in A}e^{i(q-q^{\prime})t}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{q\in\mathbf{k}}\sum_{q^{\prime}\notin\mathbf{k}}\left|\frac{e^{i(q-q^{\prime})\ell}-1}{e^{i(q-q^{\prime})}-1}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{q\in\mathbf{k}}\sum_{q^{\prime}\notin\mathbf{k}}\frac{\sin^{2}[(q-q^{\prime})\ell/2]}{\sin^{2}[(q-q^{\prime})/2]}.

(93)

To get a loose lower bound, we can restrict the sum to the boundary of the ocuupied states: pairs
q,q^{\prime}
such that
|q-q^{\prime}|=2\pi/n
. There are at least two such pairs provided that
0<M<n
. This gives

\displaystyle\Delta N_{A}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\frac{\sin^{2}[\pi\ell/n]}{\sin^{2}[\pi/n]}\geq\frac{\ell^{2}}{2n^{2}}

(94)

when
\ell\leq n/2
—we can upper-bound the denominator by
(\pi/n)^{2}
and lower-bound the numerator by
(\pi\ell/n)^{2}/4
in the relevant range.
∎

Appendix B
Review of LFSRs

Here we provide a brief review of the mathematical tools necessary to understand LFSRs, specifically the algebra of finite fields
Lidl and Niederreiter 1996
.

First we recall that
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is the binary field—the set
\{0,1\}
equipped with addition and multiplication. This meets the axioms of a field (commutativity, associativity, distributivity, existence of additive and multiplicative identity elements, existence of additive and multiplicative inverses). Crucially the field has characteristic 2, i.e.,
1+1=0
.

We can then define the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
as follows.
Consider
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
, the ring of polynomials in one variable
x
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. This is a ring, not a field, as multiplication is not invertible. Next, take a polynomial
p(x)
of degree
n
and consider the quotient
\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle p(x)\rangle
, comprising equivalence classes of polynomials modulo multiples of
p(x)
:
q_{1}(x)\equiv q_{2}(x)
if and only if
q_{1}(x)-q_{2}(x)=p(x)r(x)
with
r(x)\in\mathbb{F}[x]
.
There are
2^{n}
such equivalence classes, each represented by a polynomial of degree
\leq n-1
:

\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/\langle p(x)\rangle\ni[c_{0}+c_{1}x+\dots c_{n-1}x^{n-1}],\quad c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}.

(95)

A standard result in algebra states that the quotient
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/p(x)
forms a field if and only if
p(x)
is
irreducible
, i.e., is not divisible by any polynomials other than
1
and itself. This is the finite field of
2^{n}
elements,
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
[any irreducible
p(x)
gives the same result up to isomorphism]. This construction is analogous to how one builds the finite field
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
as a quotient of
\mathbb{Z}
modulo a prime number
p
; irreducibility of
p(x)
plays a role analogous to primality of
p
in ensuring the existence of multiplicative inverses.

A degree-
n
polynomial
p(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
is
primitive
if (i) it is irreducible, and (ii) the minimum nonzero
l
such that
x^{l}\equiv 1\mod p(x)
is
l=2^{n}-1
. Condition (ii) can be rephrased as stating that all
2^{n}-1
non-zero elements of the field can be generated as powers of
[x]
:

\{[x]^{l}\}_{l=1}^{2^{n}-1}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{[0]\}
.

Primitive polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
play an important role in the theory of maximal LFSRs
Tausworthe 1965
;
Golomb 2017
. Consider forming
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}=\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/\langle p(x)\rangle
with
p(x)=1+x^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}x^{i}
a primitive polynomial of degree
n
. Note that the coefficient of
x^{n}
is fixed to 1 because
p
has degree
n
, and the coefficient of
x^{0}
is fixed to
1
because
p
is irreducible (thus not divisible by
x
).
Now consider the map
[q(x)]\mapsto[x]\cdot[q(x)]\equiv[xq(x)]
of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
into itself.
In the representation of each element of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
as a binary vector of coefficients
(c_{i})_{i=0}^{n-1}
, Eq. (
95
), we can write the action of this map as

[x]\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}x^{i}\right]=\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}x^{i+1}\right]=\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}^{\prime}x^{i}\right],

(96)

defining new coefficients
(c^{\prime}_{i})_{i=0}^{n-1}
. To obtain the new coefficients, note that multiplication by
x
generates a degree-
n
term,
x^{n}
, that can be eliminated by using the equivalence relation
p(x)\equiv 0
as
x^{n}\equiv 1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}x^{i}
, giving

c^{\prime}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}c_{n-1}&\text{for }i=0,\\
c_{i-1}+c_{n-1}a_{i}&\text{for }i>0.\end{array}\right.

(97)

This rule is known as a LFSR in
Galois form
. It corresponds to a circuit like the one in
Fig. 2
(a) up to exchanging the control and target qubits in each CNOT gate (i.e., conjugating by a Hadamard gate on each qubit).
The more commonly used form of LFSRs, which we adopt in
Sec. III
, is known as
Fibonacci form
. The two are equivalent, being related by a unitary transformation (global Hadamard).
Note our convention in
Sec. III
also applies a spatial inversion, i.e., bits shift backward rather than forward—this is so that the resulting staircase circuit [
Fig. 2
(a)] has the same layout as the one used in
Sec. II
.

This construction of LFSRs from multiplications in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
allows us to use algebraic tools to study their orbits.
In particular, the sequence of states of the LFSR starting from
[1]
(equivalence class of the constant polynomial) and iterating the update
l
times is given by
\{[x]^{l}\}
;
if
p(x)
is primitive, then by definition this contains all of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{[0]\}
, i.e., all non-zero states of the LFSR. Thus the LFSR is maximal (contains an orbit of size
2^{n}-1
).
As a result, the search for
n
-bit maximal LFRSs reduces to the search for primitive polynomials of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Primitive polynomials of any degree
n
can be constructed efficiently [in time
O(n)
]
Rifà and Borrell 1995
.
The number of primitive polynomials of degree
n
can be expressed in terms of
Euler’s totient function

\varphi
as
\varphi(2^{n}-1)/n
. In general
\varphi
is bounded as

\frac{x}{c\log\log x}\leq\varphi(x)\leq x

(98)

for some constant
c
at sufficiently large argument
x
. It follows that fraction of choices for the coefficients
\{a_{i}\}
such that the resulting LFSR is maximal is
\Omega(1/n\log n)
when
n
is large.

We conclude by mentioning two more results on primitive polynomial that are relevant to our physical application.
First, Theorem 1 in Ref.
Cohen 2004
states that it is always possible to find a primitive polynomial of degree
n
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
with its first
n/4
coefficients
a_{1},...,a_{n/4}
fixed to arbitrary values. This implies the ability to grow any given
n
-bit maximal LFSR into a
4n
-bit maximal LFSR, thus taking a thermodynamic limit along a family of system sizes
n_{k}=n_{0}4^{k}
.
Secondly, it is known that many (though not all) system sizes
n
admit
primitive trinomials

Zierler and Brillhart 1968
,
p(x)=x^{n}+x^{k}+1
for some
k
. These correspond to LFSR circuits with a
single

\mathsf{CNOT}
gate, at position
k
.
Furthermore, every
n\leq 660
admits a maximal LFSR with at most three
\mathsf{CNOT}
gates
Rajski and Tyszer 2003
;
Kim 2025
.
Plugged into our periodic FK clock construction, this gives Hamiltonians that are nearly translationally invariant (where all but a constant number of local Hamiltonian terms are identical up to translation).

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
III.1

Here we prove Lemma
III.1
, which is one of the main technical results of this work, underpinning the construction of provably thermal Floquet eigenstates in
Sec. III
.

Let us take a Pauli operaor
P=X_{\mathbf{a}}Z_{\mathbf{b}}
(we neglect phase factors), where
X_{\mathbf{u}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{u_{i}}
and
Z_{\mathbf{v}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{v_{i}}
,
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_q}X_{\mathbf{u}}Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0}\\
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\neq\bm{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\bra{\mathbf z}X_{\mathbf{u}}Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\mathbf z'}}{2^{n}-1}\omega^{q^{\prime}\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})-q\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\neq\bm{0},\mathbf{u}}\frac{(-1)^{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}}{2^{n}-1}\omega^{q^{\prime}\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})-q\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u})}

(99)

where we used
Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\mathbf z'}=(-1)^{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\ket{\mathbf z'}
and
\bra{\mathbf z}X_{\mathbf{u}}\ket{\mathbf z'}=\delta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u}}

Now we invoke the following properties of the discrete logarithm:

(i)

r\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})=\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}^{r})\text{ mod }2^{n}-1
for all
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}-1}
;

(ii)

\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})-\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})=\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{-1})\text{ mod }2^{n}-1
.

Here the power
\mathbf{z}^{r}
and the multiplicative inverse
\mathbf{y}^{-1}
are both defined in the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
(Appendix
B
).
Using these facts, we may rewrite

\displaystyle\omega^{q^{\prime}\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})-q\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{u})}

\displaystyle=\omega^{\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}^{q^{\prime}})-\log_{\alpha}[(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{u})^{q}]}

\displaystyle=\omega^{\log_{\alpha}\left[\mathbf{z}^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{u})^{-q}\right]}.

(100)

At this point it is helpful to define the following functions:

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{z}},

(101)

\displaystyle\chi(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\omega^{\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})},

(102)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{z}^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{u})^{-q}.

(103)

Here
\varphi
is an

for
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, i.e. it satisfies

\varphi(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime})=\varphi(\mathbf{z})\varphi(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}),

(104)

while
\chi
is a
multiplicative character
, i.e. it satisfies

\chi(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\prime})=\chi(\mathbf{z})\chi(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}).

(105)

The latter follows from property (ii) of the discrete logarithm above.

g
is a rational function with a
q

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}
(recall the field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}

) and a
(q^{\prime}-q)
-fold pole at infinity, assuming without loss of generality that
q^{\prime}\geq q
.

In terms of these functions, our Pauli matrix element Eq. (

\bra{\psi_q}X_{\mathbf{u}}Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\bm{0},\mathbf{u}\}}\varphi(\mathbf{z})\chi(g(\mathbf{z})).

(106)

Sums of this type are known as

, as they combine additive and multiplicative characters. They are widely studied in algebraic number theory where they arise in connection with the Riemann hypothesis

Mixed character sums often obey bounds of the form

\left|\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}}\varphi(\mathbf{z})\chi(\mathbf{z})\right|\leq O(2^{n/2}),

(107)

which is the same bound one gets with high probability when summing

\varphi(\mathbf{z})=(-1)^{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{z}}

Many bounds on mixed character sums along the lines of Eq. (

9

is the total order of the poles of
f
, and
r
is the number of points that are simultaneously poles of
f
and zeros or poles of
g
.

Since in our case
f(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{z}
, we have
l=1
(a single pole at
\mathbf{z}=\infty
) and
d=1
(the order of the pole is 1).
For the coefficients
s
and
r
we need to distinguish two cases (recall we assumed without loss of generality
q^{\prime}\geq q
):

•

q=q^{\prime}
:
g(\mathbf{z})=[\mathbf{z}/(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{u})]^{q}
does not have a pole at infinity. We have
s=2
(a zero at
\mathbf{z}=\bm{0}
and a pole at
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}
, the multiplicity
q
does not count) and
r=0
(no poles in common between
f
and
g
).

•

q<q^{\prime}
:
g(\mathbf{z})
has a pole at infinity. We have
s=3
(a zero at
\mathbf{z}=\bm{0}
, poles at
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{z}=\infty
) and
r=1
(the pole at infinity is common to
f
and
g
).

Either way, we obtain
s-r=2
, and thus
C=2
:

|\bra{\psi_q}X_{\mathbf{u}}Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}|\leq\frac{C2^{n/2}}{2^{n}-1}=\frac{2^{1+n/2}}{2^{n}-1}

(110)

which concludes the proof for the case of
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\neq\bm{0}
(where both
\varphi
and
\chi
are nontrivial characters and Theorem
C.1
applies). In the case where either character is trivial the calculation can be carried out explicitly, see App.
D.3
, and the results also obey the bound.

Appendix D
Details on calculations of Pauli matrix elements

D.1
Construction of eigenstates

Here we provide details on the method used to obtain the data in
Fig. 2
(b-c).
The LFSRs used for each system size are specified by the coefficients in Table
1
.
For each one we generate the maximal orbit
\{\alpha^{j}\bm{1}:\ j=0,\dots 2^{n}-2\}
by starting from
\bm{1}=00\dots 01
and iterating the update
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=\alpha\mathbf{z}
until the orbit closes; we verify the orbit length is indeed
2^{n}-1
.
We then numerically create all nontrivial eigenstates one component at a time as

\innerproduct{\alpha^j\boldsymbol{1}}{\psi_q}=\frac{\omega^{qj}}{\sqrt{2^{n}-1}}

(111)

with
\omega=e^{2\pi i/(2^{n}-1)}
, for
j\in\{0,...,2^{n}-2\}
and
q\in\{1,...,2^{n-2}\}
.

D.2
Pauli orbits

Due to the Clifford nature of the LFSR circuit, the
4^{n}-1
nontrivial Pauli operators break up into orbits,
\{U_{n:0}^{-l}PU_{n:0}^{l}\}_{l}
. It is easy to see that each orbit has length
2^{n}-1
, same as the orbit of bitstring states—for example one can show that

\displaystyle\bra{\mathbf z}U_{n:0}^{\dagger}X_{\mathbf{u}}U_{n:0}\ket{\mathbf z'}

\displaystyle=\bra{\alpha\mathbf z}X_{\mathbf{u}}\ket{\alpha\mathbf z'}=\delta_{\alpha\mathbf{z},\alpha\mathbf{z}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}+\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{u}}=\bra{\mathbf z}X_{\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{u}}\ket{\mathbf z'},

(112)

implying
U_{n:0}^{\dagger}X_{\mathbf{u}}U_{n:0}=X_{\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{u}}
; since
\alpha^{-1}
has the same orbits as
\alpha
(traversed in inverse order), this shows that the
X
part of any Pauli string has periodicity
2^{n}-1
. The same conclusion applies to the
Z
part:

\displaystyle\bra{\mathbf z}U_{n:0}^{\dagger}Z_{\mathbf{v}}U_{n:0}\ket{\mathbf z'}

\displaystyle=\bra{\alpha\mathbf z}Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\alpha\mathbf z'}=(-1)^{\mathbf{v}\cdot(\alpha\mathbf{z})}\delta_{\alpha\mathbf{z},\alpha\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(\alpha^{T}\mathbf{v})\cdot\mathbf{z}}\delta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}=\bra{\mathbf z}Z_{\alpha^{T}\mathbf{u}}\ket{\mathbf z'},

(113)

which implies
U_{n:0}^{\dagger}Z_{\mathbf{v}}U_{n:0}=Z_{\alpha^{T}\mathbf{v}}
;
\alpha^{T}
represents the circuit obtained by a spatial inversion and global Hadamard which has the same orbit structure as
\alpha
.
So the
4^{n}-1
nontrivial Pauli strings split into
2^{n}+1
orbits of length
2^{n}-1
.
Up to a phase, we can reduce Pauli matrix elements between eigenstates to a canonical representative of each Pauli orbit:

\bra{\psi_q}X_{\mathbf{u}}Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}\omega^{l(q-q^{\prime})}\bra{\psi_q}Z_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}&\text{ if }\mathbf{u}=\bm{0},\\
\omega^{l(q-q^{\prime})}\bra{\psi_q}X_{\mathbf{1}}Z_{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}&\text{ otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(114)

for some integer
l
. The
\mathbf{u}=\bm{0}
specifies one orbit, while
\mathbf{u}\neq\bm{0}
specifies the remaining
2^{n}
orbits, one for each
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
.
Thus, up to phase factors, there are only
2^{n}+1
inequivalent Pauli operators for the purpose of computing matrix elements.

D.3
Special cases

For certain Pauli operators the calculation of matrix elements is analytically tractable. These Pauli operators account for the visible spike in the data of
Fig. 2
(c) at
|\mu|=1/2
.

First, consider Pauli operators
P=Z_{\mathbf{v}}
, made only of
Z
Pauli matrices.
Matrix elements of these operators between nontrivial LFSR eigenstates can be written as

|\bra{\psi_q}Z_{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}|=|\bra{\psi_q}Z_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}|

(115)

using the Pauli orbit structure discussed previously to choose a convenient representative [Eq. (
114
)].
Then we can write

\displaystyle(2^{n}-1)\bra{\psi_q}Z_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0}}\chi^{q-q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z})\varphi(\mathbf{z}),

(116)

where we introduced the multiplicative character
\chi(\mathbf{z})=\omega^{\log_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})}
and the additive character
\varphi(\mathbf{z})=(-1)^{\bm{1}\cdot\mathbf{z}}=(-1)^{z_{n-1}}
.

•

If
q=q^{\prime}
, the sum involves only the additive character, and we get

(2^{n}-1)\bra{\psi_q}Z_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q}}=\left(\sum_{\mathbf{z}}\varphi(\mathbf{z})\right)-1=-1;

(117)

•

If
q\neq q^{\prime}
, then
\chi^{q-q^{\prime}}
is a nontrivial multiplicative character, and the sum is known as a
Gauss sum

G(\chi^{q-q^{\prime}})
.
A standard result in finite field algebra states that, for any nontrivial multiplicative character, the absolute value of the Gauss sum is fixed to
Ireland and Rosen 1982

|G(\chi)|=2^{n/2}

(118)

(generally
\sqrt{q}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
). It follows that

|\bra{\psi_q}Z_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}|=\frac{2^{n/2}}{2^{n}-1}.

(119)

Next, consider Pauli operators
P=X_{\mathbf{u}}
, made only of
X
Pauli matrices.
We have

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_q}X_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\neq\bm{0}}\chi^{q}(\mathbf{z})\chi^{-q^{\prime}}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\delta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}+\bm{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0},\bm{1}}\chi^{q}(\mathbf{z})\chi^{-q^{\prime}}(\bm{1}+\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}J(\chi^{q},\chi^{-q^{\prime}}).

(120)

Here we introduced the
Jacobi sum
defined for two multiplicative characters
\chi_{1,2}
as

J(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0},\bm{1}}\chi_{1}(\mathbf{z})\chi_{2}(\bm{1}+\mathbf{z}).

(121)

Provided each of
\chi_{1}
,
\chi_{2}
, and
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}
are nontrivial, then another standard result in finite-field algebra relates the Jacobi sum to Gauss sums
Ireland and Rosen 1982
:

J(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})=\frac{G(\chi_{1})G(\chi_{2})}{G(\chi_{1}\chi_{2})},

(122)

which in particular implies
|J(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})|=2^{n/2}
.

•

If
q=q^{\prime}
, then
\chi^{q}\chi^{-q^{\prime}}
is trivial (the identity character) and we cannot use Eq. (
122
). However in that case we can compute the sum directly:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0},\bm{1}}\chi^{-q}(\mathbf{z})\chi^{q}(\bm{1}+\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\neq\bm{0},\bm{1}}\chi^{q}(\bm{1}+\mathbf{z}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\neq\bm{0},\bm{1}}\chi^{q}(\mathbf{y})=-\chi^{q}(\bm{1}),

(123)

where we changed variable to
\mathbf{y}=\bm{1}+\mathbf{z}^{-1}
in the sum and used the fact that the sum of all roots of unity vanishes:
\sum_{\mathbf{y}\neq\bm{0}}\chi^{q}(\mathbf{y})=0
. Since
-\chi^{q}(\bm{1})
is a phase factor we conclude

|\bra{\psi_q}X_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q}}|=\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}.

(124)

•

If
q\neq q^{\prime}
(and
q,q^{\prime}\neq 0
) then each of
\chi^{q}
,
\chi^{q^{\prime}}
,
\chi^{q-q^{\prime}}
is nontrivial, and Eq. (
122
) implies

|\bra{\psi_q}X_{\bm{1}}\ket{\psi_{q'}}|=\frac{2^{n/2}}{2^{n}-1}.

(125)

Normalized by the upper bound of Lemma
III.1
, the diagonal matrix elements (expectation values) of
X_{\mathbf{u}}
and
Z_{\mathbf{v}}
yield
|\mu|=2^{-n/2}
,
while the off-diagonal matrix elements yield
|\mu|=1/2
.
The latter explains the “spike” clearly visible in
Fig. 2
(c) at
|\mu|=1/2
. Since the special value
|\mu|=1/2
is attained in two out of
2^{n}+1
Pauli orbits, the size of the feature in the normalized distribution of matrix elements is
\propto 2^{-n}
.
