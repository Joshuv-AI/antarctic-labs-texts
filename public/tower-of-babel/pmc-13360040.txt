The association between team-based model of care characteristics and patient outcomes in community health centres: a systematic review
Authors: Li-Anne Audet, Pablo Galvez Hernandez, Ifeyinwa Chizoba Akamike, Jennifer Rayner, Ruth Martin-Misener, Walter P. Wodchis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity Health Centres (CHCs) are community-governed organizations often located in communities facing significant challenges with social determinants of health. To date, no review has summarized the evidence on the effects of team-based models of care on patient outcomes. The objective of this systematic review was to synthesize the evidence on the characteristics of interprofessional teams practicing in CHCs and their associations with patient outcomes.MethodsA systematic review was conducted following the guidelines of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. In October 2024, an electronic search was performed in five databases and the grey literature using a combination of descriptors and keywords. No restrictions were applied regarding language or year of publication. Screening and data extraction were independently conducted by two reviewers. The included papers focused on interprofessional teams composed of at least three different types of providers (e.g., physician, nurse, and nutritionist) practicing in CHCs and addressing at least one patient outcome (e.g., satisfaction, diabetes management). The risk of bias was assessed by two independent reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist.ResultsFrom 6,309 identified papers, 36 papers were included. Most papers focused on team composition (n = 18) and staffing patterns (n = 8). Adjustments to staffing patterns—such as increasing full-time equivalents, expanding team size, and diversifying skill mixes—were associated with improved patient satisfaction and increased number of visits. Adding a chiropractor reduced opioid prescriptions and patient pain, while including a pharmacist improved hepatitis management and patient satisfaction. Inconsistent findings were observed regarding the inclusion of nurse practitioners and physician assistants in interprofessional teams and their impact on the number of patients seen in CHCs. Specialized interprofessional teams addressing diabetes, pain management, and childhood obesity had better health outcomes and care management compared to standard care, highlighting the value of tailored interprofessional collaboration in achieving improved health outcomes for specific populations.ConclusionsSpecific team compositions are associated with improved health outcomes for the populations served by CHCs. Future research is needed to deepen the understanding of the associations between team composition, the type of care provided, and patients' clinical and psychosocial needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03286-6.

Background

Community Health Centres (CHCs) are community governed organizations typically located in areas where residents face significant challenges related to social determinants of health and limited access to healthcare. In 2021, Nagel et al. conducted a concept analysis and identified six key attributes that define CHCs: primary care, accessibility, preventive care, a defined population, health promotion, and comprehensive care [54]. Firstly, CHCs often serve as the primary point of entry for healthcare among vulnerable populations, including low-income communities, individuals with mental health or substance use issues, immigrants, refugees, and visible minorities. Secondly, the care philosophy of CHCs emphasizes culturally sensitive care, health promotion and prevention, adopting a holistic approach that takes into account both the clinical and psychosocial contexts of patients [54].

Internationally, CHCs are most commonly found in North America and China. In Canada and the U.S. (in the U.S., they are known as Federally Qualified Health Centers, or FQHCs), CHCs share similar governance, organizational structures, and models of care. They are typically governed by community boards with consumer representation and operate as non-profit organizations, funded primarily through government support [1, 10, 78]. CHCs provide a broad range of services, including medical, dental, psychosocial, and educational care, offering services either free of charge or at reduced cost for individuals without insurance or not registered with a primary care physician. The defining features of CHCs, compared with other primary care settings, are their accessibility to uninsured or unregistered patients, community-based governance, and salary-based clinician compensation [73]. In China, CHCs are the main providers of urban primary care, while township hospitals and village clinics serve rural areas. Chinese CHCs operate under three models, government-managed, hospital-managed, or private, and patients are generally expected to pay for services regardless of the model [42].

To support these purposes, a team-based model of care (TBMC) has been introduced in CHCs since their conception. A TBMC is an evidence-based approach where at least three different types of healthcare professionals (HCPs) collaborate with patients and their families or caregivers to achieve shared treatment goals [36, 37, 72]. In 2017, Baik conducted a concept analysis to define and examine team-based care in clinical practice, identifying three core attributes: interprofessional collaboration, a patient-centered approach, and an integrated care process. Current literature agrees on the effectiveness of TBMC for patients, families, interprofessional teams, and healthcare organizations. Compared to traditional physician-focused care, TBMC has shown success in improving health outcomes, enhancing patient experience, reducing healthcare utilization, improving continuity of care, and lowering costs [36, 37, 72].

Several systematic reviews have been published examining the effectiveness of TBMC in various primary healthcare settings [16, 32, 43]. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Hinneh et al. [32] found that TBMC for managing hypertension in Africa led to reductions in blood pressure. Furthermore, their study indicated that the inclusion of specific providers, such as community health workers and nurses, within interprofessional teams contributed to these improvements in blood pressure management [32]. Other reviews have also explored various interprofessional team characteristics and their impact on patient, team, and organizational outcomes. For example, the umbrella review by Wei et al. [79] suggested that team-level characteristics such as size and adequate staffing can enhance team collaboration, improve care quality, and positively affect patient outcomes [79]. Most of these reviews encompass a broad range of primary care settings, such as clinics, family health teams, and home-visit programs, yet it remains unclear whether CHCs were included. Considering that CHCs have a distinct governance structure and serve a higher proportion of patients affected by social determinants of health (e.g., low-income individuals, newcomers) compared to other primary care settings [41], this unique context warrants dedicated attention.

Although several literature and systematic reviews have been conducted on primary healthcare, less attention has been paid to synthesizing the literature specifically on TBMC in CHCs, particularly regarding how different team characteristics influence care quality and patient outcomes. Therefore, we argue that a new systematic review is needed and this is supported by two key points. First, existing reviews, including for example, the systematic reviews published by Marten et al. (2021) [47, 64] and Wang et al. [77] conducted in CHC settings focus on a specific provider or type of care, such as pharmacist-led interventions or telemedicine [47, 64, 77]. However, prior studies have highlighted the value of interprofessional collaboration across a broader range of providers to improve the accessibility and quality of care for patients and families. Second, while some reviews [13, 55] have examined CHC performance and productivity, such as cost and visit volume, less attention has been given to patient- and family-centered outcomes, including patient-reported outcomes, clinical outcomes, and care experiences. To the best of our knowledge, there is no systematic review that assesses the direct benefits of TBMC for patients and families in CHCs.

To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review aimed at building upon previous work and examining the effects of different characteristics of TBMC in CHCs for communities, patients, and families. Our review specifically focused on TBMC involving at least three different types of providers, consistent with the conceptualization of team-based care proposed by Baik [5], captures a broad range of TBMC models and characteristics (e.g., team size, composition, dynamics, utilization of e-health, etc.), and assesses patient outcomes. The objectives of this systematic review were to: 1) examine the association between TBMC characteristics and models of care and patient outcomes in CHCs, 2) identify the team characteristics that significantly impact patient outcomes, and 3) discuss future research directions in this area.

Methods

Design

A systematic review of the literature was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions guidelines [30]. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO [3, 4].

Search strategy

In October 2024, a search was performed across five databases: 1) MEDLINE, 2) CINHAL, 3) Embase, 4) Cochrane Library, and 5) PsycINFO, to identify papers examining TBMC characteristics and patient outcomes in CHCs. Multiple queries were executed in each database by combining two independent sets of keywords and descriptors: 1) CHCs and 2) TBMC (see Table 1). Additionally, a search of the grey literature was conducted in November 2024, following the Cochrane recommendations [30], to retrieve pre-publication works, reports, and conference proceedings. The grey literature search included: 1) Opengrey, 2) ClinicalTrials.gov, 3) Google Scholar, 4) National Library of Medicine, and 5) the Virginia Henderson Database. No restrictions on language or publication date were applied. For each included paper, reference lists (backward search) and publications citing the included papers (forward search) were reviewed for potential additional relevant papers. An academic librarian was consulted to help develop and execute the search strategy. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All published and unpublished quantitative papers addressing TBMC in CHCs were eligible for inclusion. Mixed-method papers were included as well, provided that the quantitative component was presented separately and could be independently extracted from the qualitative portion. Based on Baik’s [5] concept analysis of team-based care, papers were included if they described teams involving interprofessional collaboration among three or more types of healthcare providers and provided direct patient care under a collaborative and integrated approach. All aspects of team characteristics and models of care, such as size, credentials and accreditations, provider types, team dynamics (e.g., relationships, workload), staffing patterns, integration of e-health (e.g., telemedicine, electronic health records), and skill mix, were considered [5]. Similarly, based on Liu et al. [44] concept analysis on patient outcomes, all types of patient outcomes, including clinical and psychosocial outcomes, patient-reported outcome and experience measures (PREMS and PROMs), and healthcare resource utilization, were included [44]. To be eligible, papers had to focus on one or more team characteristics and their association with one or more patient outcomes. CHCs were defined broadly to include any health center meeting the description provided by Nagel et al. [54] and were required to meet the following three criteria: 1) be labeled as a CHC, community health clinic, federally qualified health center (FQHC), Centre local de services communautaires (CLSC), or other related terms, 2) be based in primary healthcare settings; and 3) be publicly funded [54]. All types of patient populations were included, and there were no restrictions on geographical location, language, or publication date. Research protocols were included if they were linked to a subsequent result publication.

Exclusion criteria included qualitative papers, discussion papers, letters to the editor, and all types of reviews. Review articles were excluded to ensure inclusion of primary quantitative studies only and to avoid overlap with primary studies identified through the literature search. Conference proceedings that did not report study findings and research protocols without a corresponding results publication were excluded, as were studies conducted in primary care settings other than CHCs. Papers focusing on the practice of a single provider (e.g., physician-led care) or a dyadic team (e.g., physician-nurse) were excluded, as well as papers examining specific interventions or programs (e.g., addition of weekly meetings for staff or smoking cessation programs for patients) within CHCs. Papers involving the pilot testing or implementation of specific new interventions that were not related to teams’ day-to-day and routine characteristics, structures, models of care, or practices were excluded. Papers that measured organizational outcomes (e.g., cost) or interprofessional team outcomes (e.g., clinician communication) without assessing patient-level outcomes were also excluded.

Study selection

A standardized chart for paper selection was developed for the assessors. The selection process was carried out by two independent assessors (LAA, PGH). A training session was conducted using 25 titles to review the inclusion/exclusion criteria and familiarize assessors with the screening platform. Inter-rater agreement was calculated at each stage of the study selection process. Interpretation of agreement percentages (0–100%) was guided by prior work by Belur et al. [7] and Park and Kim [58], with thresholds defined as follows: 0–20% slight agreement, 21–40% fair, 41–60% moderate, 61–80% substantial, and 81–100% near-perfect agreement.

A calibration session with 300 titles resulted in an inter-rater agreement of 76.28% (substantial agreement) [7, 58]. Titles and abstracts were screened, and full texts were retrieved if abstracts were unavailable. Duplicates were removed using the Bramer et al. technique [9]. The inter-rater agreement for title and abstract screening was 79.30% (substantial agreement) [7, 58]. The primary area of disagreement centered on determining whether or not a study focused on TBMC characteristics. Full-text screening was also carried out by the same assessors (LAA, PGH), resulting in an 87.88% inter-rater agreement (near perfect agreement) [7, 58]. Final decisions about paper inclusion were made through consensus, with a third assessor (WPW) resolving any remaining discrepancies. Ongoing meetings and discussions between the two assessors (LAA, PGH) and a tie-breaker (WPW) helped strengthen consensus and resolve divergences. Data extraction

A standardized extraction form was created and pilot-tested by the research team before use. The following data were extracted from each included paper: 1) title, 2) first author, 3) year of publication, 4) location, 5) study design, 6) research objective/question, 7) CHC description, 8) population/sample size, 9) team characteristics, 10) team composition, 11) comparison, 12) data sources, 13) tools used, 14) data collection timeline, 15) outcome measures, 16) participant-level covariates, 17) team-level covariates, 18) organizational-level covariates, 19) additional qualitative data collection, and 20) key findings. Data from secondary analyses and papers related to the same research project were extracted alongside the primary study and color coded in the extraction chart. Extraction was performed by one reviewer (LAA) and reviewed for accuracy by a second reviewer (ICA).

Data analysis

Following the Cochrane methodological guidance for descriptive analysis in systematic reviews, descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the included papers [15]. In line with the recommendations of Popay et al. [61], a narrative synthesis of the available evidence was conducted, and a thematic analysis was used to group findings based on TBMC characteristics (e.g., provider types, staffing patterns, electronic health record utilization, etc.) [61]. Themes were identified by the research team through an inductive process based on findings reported from the included papers. A p-value of 0.05 was set for identifying statistically significant associations [15]. Meta-analysis was not attempted due to the methodological heterogeneity of the included papers [31].

Study quality appraisal

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Checklists were used to assess the methodological quality of the included papers [6, 53]. These checklists were selected because they accommodate various quantitative study designs. The JBI checklists for cohort, cross-sectional, economic evaluation, quasi-experimental, and RCT studies were applied, with the appropriate checklist selected based on each study’s design. For mixed-methods studies, the quantitative component was evaluated using the appropriate JBI checklist, as JBI does not provide a checklist specifically for mixed-methods designs. An overall score was assigned to each paper, calculated as the percentage of "yes" responses relative to the total possible points. The risk of bias and the assignment of each score were assessed by two independent reviewers (LAA, ICA). A calibration session was conducted with five full-text papers, yielding an inter-rater agreement of 92% (near-perfect agreement) [7, 58]. Divergences were discussed between the reviewers to reach consensus.

Results

Out of the 6,309 records initially retrieved, 5,418 were excluded after reviewing their titles and abstracts, as outlined in Fig. 1. A total of 581 papers were further screened during the full-text review, of which 548 were excluded, resulting in 33 papers being included. An additional three papers identified through other sources were also added (n = 36). The primary reasons for exclusion were: 1) the study was not conducted in a CHC, and 2) it did not focus on the characteristics of TBMC. Specifically, many of the excluded papers explored the implementation of interventions involving interprofessional teams, such as introducing weekly team meetings or creating online discussion forums.

The final selection comprised 36 papers, which included 27 distinct research studies. Two papers were related to the same overarching study, another two were linked to a second overarching study, and five papers were published from a third study. The characteristics of all included papers are summarized in Table 2 and Appendix B. These papers were published between 1994 and 2022, with 19 (52.7%) published between 2010 and 2019, and 12 (33.3%) between 2020 and 2024. Most of the papers were conducted in the United States (n = 27) and were based on a cross-sectional design (n = 15). Team-based models of care characteristics and composition

TBMC characteristics were grouped into five main themes: 1) types of providers, 2) specialized interprofessional teams, 3) staffing patterns and team dynamics, 4) accreditations, and 5) e-health. The following counts are not mutually exclusive, as some papers were categorized under more than one main theme. A total of 18 papers focused on adding specific types of providers to teams. Five papers explored specialized interprofessional teams, including social service liaison teams (n = 1), weight clinics (n = 1), diabetes-specialized teams (n = 1), pain teams (n = 1), and hepatitis C teams (n = 1). Seven papers examined staffing patterns and team dynamics. Three papers examined accreditations: two focused on CHCs with Patient-Centered Medical Home Model (PCMH) certification, and one on CHCs with National Health Service Corps (NHSC) certification. Six papers examined e-health, including four investigating telemedicine utilization and two exploring the use of EHRs within CHCs.

Each included paper described one team. On average, team compositions included 5 to 6 types of providers, ranging from a minimum of 3 (inclusion criteria) to a maximum of 16 different provider types per team. Based on our inductive process and the types of providers reported in the included papers, most teams included primary care providers, which were mostly physicians (n = 33), nurse practitioners (NPs), and physician assistants (PAs) (n = 21), and nurses (n = 20). Other providers included mental health/substance abuse/behavioral health specialists (n = 12), dieticians (n = 11), social workers (n = 8), non-clinical/administrative/enabling staff (n = 8), pharmacists (n = 7), chiropractors (n = 7), occupational therapist and physiotherapist (n = 6), case managers or coordinators (n = 5), massage therapist (n = 5), medical assistants (n = 4), psychologists (n = 3), health promoters (n = 3), vision and dental care (n = 3), psychologist (n = 2), midwives (n = 2), interpreter (n = 1), and laboratory technicians (n = 1), and community health worker (n = 1).

Patient outcomes

A total of six themes of patient outcomes were identified: (1) population and disease management, (2) number of visits, (3) provider outcomes, (4) experience of care and satisfaction, (5) medication intake, and (6) pain and disability. The following counts are not mutually exclusive, as some papers examined more than one type of outcome. Fifteen papers examined population and disease management, 15 examined number of visits, six examined experience of care and satisfaction, four examined provider outcomes, four examined pain and disability, and two examined medication intake.

Sample sizes

Average sample sizes were calculated for papers with the same unit of analysis. Among 16 papers that included patient samples, the average sample size was 267.14 (SD = 310.24), ranging from 30 to 945 participants. For the 11 papers that reported the sample size of CHCs, the average sample size was 904.82 (SD = 389.51), ranging from 16 to 1,384 CHCs. Four papers included the total number of patient encounters with an average sample size of 7,848.33 encounters (SD = 7,796.90). Two papers examined the number of annual patient encounters per CHCs, with sample sizes of 3,139 and 4,096 encounters per year per CHCs. One paper focused on audit calls and had a sample size of 21,302 calls. Most of the included papers (n = 22) used electronic health data for their data collection, and half (n = 18) employed surveys. A total of 23 papers included a comparison group, while 13 did not. Types of comparison groups included pre- and post-measures (n = 7), usual care (n = 4), different TBMC models of care compared to each other (n = 2), different types of providers compared to each other (n = 2), different measures compared to each other (n = 1), funding status (n = 1), medication intake by patient (n = 1), private physician office compared to CHC (n = 1), teaching status (n = 1), PCMH (patient-centered medical home) status (n = 1), case–control (n = 1) and telemedicine usage (n = 1).

Risk of bias

The risk of bias was assessed for 35 included papers. No risk of bias assessment was performed on the included study protocol. On average, the risk of bias score was 65.30% (SD: 24.52), with scores ranging from 24.51% to 100%. Two papers [26, 67] achieved a top score of 100%, while four papers [34, 48, 68, 80] scored 25% or lower. A total of 18 papers [12, 14, 18, 25, 26, 28, 38, 39, 50–52, 59, 60, 67, 68, 70, 76, 80] were assessed using the JBI cross-sectional checklist, including the fifteen cross-sectional studies and three mixed-method studies in which the quantitative analysis were based on cross-section design (average score = 70.13%, SD = 23.14), 11 [19, 20, 22, 27, 29, 34, 45, 48, 62, 68, 69] using the cohort checklist (average score = 57.01%, SD = 29.05), three [17, 63, 74] using the economic evaluation checklist (average score = 72.72%, SD = 18.18), two [23, 65] using the randomized controlled trial checklist (average score = 65.30%, SD = 24.51), and one [56] using the quasi-experimental checklist (score = 77.7%).

The following section reports the overall scores as well as strengths and weakness of papers per study design from the JBI checklists. The JBI checklists do not specify threshold scores for quality ranking; therefore, it is not possible to assign a quality category (for example, low, moderate, high risk of bias) based on the overall scores [6, 53]. Overall, most papers identified, accurately measured, and considered confounding variables in their statistical analyses. More than half of the papers assessed with the cross-sectional JBI checklist scored 75% or higher. However, several papers lacked detailed descriptions of the study subjects and settings. Papers assessed with the cohort studies checklist showed a wide range of scores, with half scoring above 80% and several scoring below 50%. Most of those papers did not provide information on loss to follow-up or strategies to address incomplete follow-up. All three economic evaluation papers scored higher than 50%. RCTs and quasi-experimental papers showed mixed risk of bias scores. Two papers scored above 60%, while one had a score below 40%. RCTs were particularly associated with bias risks due to the absence of concealed allocation of participants to treatment and control groups, lack of blinding to group assignment, and insufficient information on attrition and loss to follow-up. Type of providers

A total of 18 papers examined the addition of specific types of providers within interprofessional teams and their association with a wide range of patient outcomes. Seven papers examined the inclusion of a chiropractor in interprofessional teams. Five papers were generated from the same research study, resulting in chiropractors being studied in three distinct settings. Garner et al. [26] and Prater et al. [62] identified significant associations between the addition of a chiropractor and reductions in pain and disability (p > 0.01), as well as improvements in participants' overall health (p < 0.05 Emary et al [17–19, 21] published five papers on the same overarching research study and found significant associations between the inclusion of a chiropractor and decreases in opioid prescriptions and refills (hazard ratio: 0.48, 99% CI: 0.29–0.77), as well as reductions in pain, discomfort, and disability, and improvements in patient satisfaction and mobility [17–21].

Four papers examined the inclusion of NPs and PAs, also known as advanced practice providers, in interprofessional teams, which is a small number considering that these providers were included in 21 teams and the findings were mixed. In these papers, NPs and PAs managed their own patient panels under autonomous (NPs), collaborative (NPs and PAs), or supervisory (PAs) models of care involving physicians. In all papers, NPs and PAs practiced within a team-based model of care, aligned with this review’s inclusion criteria, and worked in partnership with other healthcare providers. Gold et al. found a significantly higher likelihood of angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) prescriptions for patients with diabetes when managed by NPs or PAs, compared to those followed by physicians (p < 0.05) [27]. Morgan, Park, and Saloner also explored the impact of NPs and PAs on visit volume within interprofessional teams. Saloner suggested that CHCs with NPs, PAs, and physicians had a higher number of employed primary care providers compared to private offices, resulting in greater appointment availability (p < 0.01) and shorter wait times until appointment (p < 0.01) for uninsured patients [67]. Morgan suggested that physicians see a higher volume of patients per day compared to NPs and PAs (p < 0.05) [51, 52]. Park examined the scope of practice for NPs and PAs and found that the number of visits per NP or PA increased with greater prescription authority and more support staff (p < 0.05). Also, the number of visits per full-time equivalent (FTE) physician decreased as NP prescription authority increased. Finally, in states with broader PA scopes of practice, there was a significant decrease in NP visits [59].

The remaining papers examined the addition of a wide range of provider types within interprofessional teams, including aboriginal health workers, medical assistants, community health workers, behavioral health staff, nurse coordinators, physician residents, interpreters, and pharmacists. Aboriginal health workers, medical assistants, and community health workers were associated with improved delivery of diabetes care and management, as well as better control of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels (p < 0.001) [65, 69]. Behavioral health staff were associated with an increase in visits for patients with depression and anxiety, particularly those living in rural areas [28]. For high-users and patients with severe chronic conditions, the addition of a nurse coordinator was associated with increased visit frequency and better continuity of care (p < 0.001) [56]. The inclusion of physician residents within interprofessional teams was significantly associated with lower rates of low birth weight in newborns (p < 0.01) [12], while the addition of a Spanish interpreter was linked to higher patient satisfaction and a better care experience for patients living in the U.S. and coming from South America or Mexico [39].

Staffing patterns and team dynamics

A total of six papers examined staffing patterns. Staffing patterns were defined by the mix and/or number of full-time equivalent (FTE) employees across various categories of providers. The most frequently reported provider categories in the included papers were primary care providers, advanced practice nurses, allied health professionals, and behavioral and mental health providers. Most of the papers identified that an increase in FTE employees was associated with a higher number of visits [28, 38, 45, 74]. Luo et al. [45] and Ku et al. [38] suggested that higher proportions of FTE physicians within interprofessional teams were associated with increased productivity and a higher adjusted volume of patients seen (p < 0.001). Thorsen et al. [74] and Han et al. [29] found that higher proportions of FTE behavioral health staff within the entire healthcare team were linked to better management of patients with depression, anxiety, and diabetes. Chuang et al. [14] found that lower provider-to-patient ratios were associated with higher cancer screening rates (p < 0.05). Miharti et al. [50] suggested that interprofessional teams with a lower staff mix (e.g., variety of different types of healthcare providers within a team) outperformed those with a higher staff mix, being associated with increased care delivery to mothers and infants (e.g., vaccination rates, contraception use) (p < 0.05). One paper examined the relationship between staff workload, staff relationships, and the management of diabetes. Vargas Bustamante et al. [76] found that a high workload alone was associated with a lower likelihood of having optimal LDL-C levels, while a high workload combined with good staff relationships was associated with higher volumes of LDL-C, blood pressure (BP), and hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) testing (p < 0.05).

Specialized interprofessional teams

A total of five papers explored specialized interprofessional teams (i.e., teams that are part of standard practice and fully integrated into daily care within the CHC) focused on social liaison service inclusion [80], weight management for children with obesity [2], diabetes [34], pain management [62], and hepatitis C [70]. Young et al. (1994) [80] found that participants followed by a specialized social liaison team reported feeling a greater sense of responsibility and engagement in their care (p < 0.05). Anand et al. [2] investigated a weight management clinic for children with obesity and found that, out of 402 participants, nearly 80% reported making changes to their lifestyle, physical activity, or diet, and about half showed a decrease in body mass index (BMI) after a 12-weeks follow-up. Katz et al. [34] reported that, after intervention by a diabetes-specialized team, 66% of participants were managing their glucose at home (p < 0.001). Additionally, most participants who had experienced hypoglycemic episodes at home saw resolution post-intervention. Among patients without hypoglycemia, mean HbA1C levels were significantly better controlled after the intervention compared to baseline measures (p = 0.02). Prater et al. [62] compared a specialized pain team to chiropractor care and found reductions in reported pain and disability in both groups. Participants receiving chiropractic care reported lower pain levels compared to those seen by the pain clinic (p < 0.01). Sims et al. [70] evaluated a hepatitis-C specialized team and found that 85% of patients under the team's care completed therapy (with no missed appointments), and 85% achieved a sustained virologic response.

Accreditations

Two papers in the US compared CHCs with and without the Patient-Centered Medical Home Model (PCMH) accreditation [14, 60] and one paper the National Health Service Corps (NHSC) [29] accreditation. The PCMH is an accreditation available for healthcare centers in the US that focuses on the delivery of team-based, patient-centered care, continuity of care, care collaboration and transitions, and quality improvement. To be certified, healthcare centers must meet all 40 core criteria (e.g., after-hours access to appointments, involving patients in treatment discussions) and at least 25 of the elective criteria. Park et al. [60] found that in PCMH-CHCs, an increase of one FTE advanced practice staff (e.g., advanced practice nurse, NP, PA) was associated with an approximate incremental increase of 539 visits annually (p = 0.03) compared to CHC without the PCMH accreditation. No other significant associations were identified for any other types of providers. Elsewhere, Chuang et al. [14] did not find any significant association between PCMH accreditation and cancer screening rates.

Han et al. [29] compared CHC participating in the NHSC program with CHC not enrolled in program and examined whether NHSC accreditation was associated with higher number of visits. The NHSC serves as a hiring program for CHCs, promoting the hiring of recent graduates into CHCs. Han et al. [29] did not find any significant association between NHSC-affiliated CHCs and non-NHSC CHCs regarding the total number of visits for all types of providers.

E-health

A total of six papers examined e-health including the utilization of EHR and telemedicine in CHCs. A total of four papers examined the practice of interprofessional teams via telemedicine. McCarthy et al. [48] explored the implementation of a telepharmacy sub-team within an on-site interprofessional team. Shin et al. [68], Pyne et al. [63], and Fortney et al. [23] examined the delivery of care by interprofessional teams via telemedicine, including both on-site and off-site providers. Compared to on-site care provided by interprofessional teams alone, all papers identified significant associations between the addition of telemedicine care and improvements in several outcomes: higher monthly mean number of patient seen [48], better control of HbA1C for patients with diabetes [68], a greater reduction in depression symptoms, an increase in depression-free days, and overall improved wellbeing for patients with depression (p < 0.001; p < 0.009) [23, 63].

Two papers examined the utilization of electronic health records (HER) by interprofessional teams. Frogner et al. [25] categorized interprofessional teams by the number of years since they adopted EHR within their practice (ranging from no live EHR to seven or more years) and found that CHCs where EHR had been adopted 3 or 4 years earlier were associated with the highest physician productivity. Chuang et al. [14] measured EHR utilization in three subcategories: absence of EHR, basic use (e.g., capturing and sharing clinical data), and advanced use (advanced clinical processes and benefits perceived by healthcare providers) and found that EHR use was associated with higher cervical and colorectal cancer screening rates (p < 0.01). Frogner et al. [25] suggested that a 10% increase in physicians’ FTE was associated with double the increase in visits in CHCs with EHR compared to those without.

Discussion

The aim of this systematic review was to examine the association between TBMC characteristics and patient outcomes in CHCs. A total of 36 papers were retrieved, focusing on five themes of interprofessional teams’ characteristics and six themes of patient outcomes. Most of the papers identified benefits for patients and families resulting from specific TBMC characteristics.

The findings from this review emphasize the significance of provider types within interprofessional teams and their impact on care delivery and patient outcomes. Some papers indicate that physicians are the most productive providers, with the highest volume of visits per day and per year, underscoring their key role within interprofessional teams. In recent years, the literature has identified several factors that contribute to the high productivity and patient volume of primary care physicians, including their skills and experience, the types of patients they see, and the regulations in their workplace. For instance, appointment durations may vary depending on the patient's condition and needs. Physicians also often treat both recurring patients and individuals with low-complexity needs, which leads to shorter appointments and higher visit volumes [33, 52]. Additionally, factors such as time pressure (i.e., the need for physicians to see patients in less time) and compensation may further influence their high volume and productivity [24, 40].

This review also highlights the value of a diverse range of providers and specialized interprofessional teams in delivering high-quality, targeted care to patients and families managing complex clinical conditions. Previous studies have emphasized the significance of aligning the scope of practice of specific providers with the unique needs of patients based on their conditions. Patient conditions often require specialized care, and each provider type brings specific skills, training, and expertise to address these needs [35]. For instance, several studies have highlighted the benefits of chiropractic treatment for patients with pain, disability, and musculoskeletal disorders, which can be attributed to chiropractors' targeted approach to these issues [75]. Specialized interprofessional teams were associated with improved clinical indicators for patient with diabetes, hepatitis C and childhood obesity, as the approach, provided by a diverse set of providers, addresses the multifaceted needs of patients. These teams highlight the value of a variety of provider types having specialized services, each contributing complementary expertise to care for patients with complex conditions [8].

Some of the retrieved papers focused on telemedicine and EHR utilization, highlighting the role of electronic platforms in improving care provided by interprofessional teams. The telehealth model of care expanded significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic and has remained widespread due to its convenience. Previous studies indicate that telehealth is particularly effective for appointments where in-person interactions are not necessary. It helps eliminate barriers such as travel and geographic distance, making healthcare appointments more accessible and convenient for patients. This increased accessibility allows for easier meetings between patients and clinicians, leading to stronger continuity of care, more follow-up appointments, and ultimately better management of patient conditions, with a reduction in negative outcomes and deterioration [46, 71]. EHRs have been in use by clinicians for decades, even before the pandemic. Research suggests that healthcare centers with EHR systems benefit from features like appointment reminders, follow-up coordination with specialists, improved communication between providers, and enhanced administrative support. These functions contribute to better clinician workflows, higher-quality care, more effective follow-up, and improved continuity of care [57, 66].

Finally, the association between accreditations and patient outcomes showed mixed results. Two out of three papers focusing on the PCMH and NHSC found no significant impact of these accreditations on patient outcomes. These findings emphasize the importance of bridging the gap between policies and actual practice in healthcare settings. Previous research has highlighted that for policies to drive meaningful change, they must have a direct impact on bedside practice [11]. To explain the lack of clear findings, other studies have suggested that certain policies and organizational structures, such as accreditation, are often reflected in other team characteristics like provider-to-patient ratios and team size. As a result, the overarching accreditation itself may not be a strong enough variable to show a direct link to patient outcomes [29, 60].

The findings presented here should be interpreted with caution for four important reasons. First, several papers presented descriptive data, comparative groups, or pre- and post- findings without testing whether these changes were statistically significant. The absence of statistical validation raises concerns about the robustness of the findings. Second, five of the retrieved papers were linked to the same overarching research project, which could also have led to an overestimation of the findings and conclusions drawn by the authors, as well as our summary of the results. Third, in teams where certain provider types (e.g., interpreter, community health worker) or categories (e.g., behavioral and mental health provider) might be represented by small FTE proportions, relatively small additions to FTE capacity may be enough to produce measurable effects on outcomes. Finally, limited information was reported in the included studies regarding the context of the CHCs, as well as the regulatory frameworks and scopes of practice of the healthcare providers within their teams. For instance, variations in provider roles and authority (e.g., NP, PAs) across urban versus rural CHCs and teaching versus non-teaching CHCs may influence clinical practice and, consequently, patient outcomes.

Future research should focus on four key directions. First, more empirical data is needed to examine the types of providers within interprofessional teams and their association with patient profiles, including clinical, mental health, socioeconomic, and equity-related factors. The findings from this review highlight the impact of provider types on patient outcomes, but most of the included papers have focused on the general population or patients with a narrow set of conditions (e.g., diabetes). Less attention has been given to subpopulations with shared characteristics, such as individuals with low income, multimorbidity, or psychiatric disorders. More research is needed to identify the most effective provider types for these subgroups, as well as how specific providers can address the unique needs of each subgroup.

Second, while this review identified several papers that focused either on a specific type of provider or on a mix of providers, less attention has been paid to understanding the value of varied healthcare provider types, such as specialized professions, and which combinations of providers are most effective for managing patients based on their conditions. For instance, specialized interprofessional teams are designed to collaborate on treating patients with specific conditions, but models like TBMC involve a broader range of providers, and patients may see different types depending on their needs. It remains unclear which combinations of providers are most appropriate for patients with particular conditions.

Third, future research should examine outcomes related to healthcare resource utilization. Surprisingly, the papers included in this review did not examine outcomes like emergency room (ER) visits, hospital admissions, or primary care visits outside of CHCs. This may be due to the focus of the included studies on team characteristics at the individual level, assessing direct benefits for patients, clinicians, and CHCs. However, future research exploring healthcare resource utilization could provide deeper insights into how TBMC models affect access to care and whether the care provided within CHCs is sufficient. Previous research has suggested that metrics like ER visits and hospital admissions could serve as proxies for the quality and continuity of care delivered by interprofessional teams, if measured accurately [3, 4, 49].

Finally, future research should explore a broader range of emotional and psychosocial indicators. While several studies have examined various clinical and health promotion outcomes, and some have assessed PROMs related to satisfaction and care experience, few have investigated the long-term emotional, mental, and psychosocial outcomes associated with TBMC in CHCs. For example, future studies should consider outcomes such as distress, perceived readiness and satisfaction in self-managing care, feelings of isolation and burden, and other psychosocial factors experienced by individuals living with chronic conditions or affected by social determinants of health and inequities. Investigating these outcomes would enhance our understanding of how TBMC influence the well-being of patients, caregivers, and families.

This review has several key strengths, as well as some limitations. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to comprehensively examine a broad range of TBMC characteristics and patient outcomes specifically within CHC settings. In our work, we captured a variety of TBMC characteristics, including provider types, staffing, EHR and telemedicine usage, and health center accreditations. Additionally, we retrieved a wide array of outcomes, covering clinical indicators, lifestyle promotion, access to care, patient experience, and mental health indicators. Second, for this review, we conducted an extensive search across multiple sources (e.g., databases, grey literature, and secondary searches), with no restrictions on language, location, or publication year. Furthermore, we included a broad spectrum of quantitative designs and types of CHCs and similar organizations. This thorough search enabled us to gather studies from diverse geographical areas, spanning different time periods, and employing various research methodologies. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. Despite our extensive search strategy across several databases and grey literature, there is still a possibility that some relevant studies were missed. Additionally, the high heterogeneity of the results prevented us from conducting a formal meta-analysis. Finally, the aim of this review was to retrieve objective TMC characteristics; thus, we did not conduct an analysis related to team effectiveness or team functioning.

Conclusion

CHCs cater to a wide range of populations with complex and multifaceted needs, underscoring the significance and relevance of TBMC in these settings. This review emphasizes the importance of TBMC characteristics and their influence on patient experience and satisfaction, the management of both clinical and psychosocial conditions, and access to care. However, to accurately assess these impacts and strengthen the evidence base, studies must employ robust methodological designs and rigorous analyses.
