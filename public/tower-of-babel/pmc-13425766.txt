Factors influencing the reimbursement decisions of cancer drugs in Spanish National Health System
Authors: Pelayo Nieto-Gómez, Celia Castaño-Amores, Antonio José García-Ruiz, Juan Oliva-Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the influence of several factors, including added clinical benefit, cost-utility, availability of therapeutic alternatives, innovation level, and evidence uncertainty, on public reimbursement decisions for cancer drugs targeting solid tumours in Spanish National Health System between 2010 and 2023.Methods220 drug indications for solid tumours authorised between January 2010 and December 2023 were included. Clinical benefit was assessed using the European Society for Medical Oncology- Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS). Cost-utility was estimated based on pharmacological costs and Quality Adjusted Life Years (QALY), using data from Health Technology Assessment Agencies (HTAAs) reports and list prices. Other variables included innovation, presence of therapeutic alternatives, quality of clinical trial evidence, and availability of overall survival (OS) and quality of life data (QoL). This influence was also controlled for by the year of the reimbursement decision. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics and several probit regression models to estimate the impact of each factor on reimbursement likelihood.ResultsOf the 220 indications analysed, 68.2% received public reimbursement. Substantial clinical benefit was observed in 50% of cases and was significantly associated with reimbursement (OR = 2.7, 95% CI: 1.4–5.3; p < 0.01). Median incremental cost-utility ratios (ICUR) were €86,770/QALY (list prices) and €47,190/QALY (reimbursed prices), with no significant differences between reimbursed and non-reimbursed indications. The probit results indicate that substantial clinical benefit significantly increases the probability of public reimbursement by between 17.5 and 20.1% points (pp). Another relevant factor is the absence QoL data, which decreases the probability of reimbursement by between 13.9 and 14.8 pp. Drugs with reimbursement decisions made between 2020 and 2023 had a 19.4 pp lower probability of being reimbursed.ConclusionSubstantial clinical benefit and absence of QoL uncertainty significantly increases the likelihood of public reimbursement for oncology drugs in Spain, while cost-utility estimates apparently show no influence under current decision-making processes. Decisions made between 2020 and 2023 had a lower probability of receiving public reimbursement compared to the 2010–2019 period possibly due to the accumulation of drugs approved by European Commission in recent years. These findings highlight the need for clearer integration of economic evaluations and transparent criteria as Spain updates its P&R framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13561-026-00804-1.

Introduction

The treatment of cancer has evolved significantly in recent decades, with therapeutic approaches shifting from conventional chemotherapy to precision medicine with targeted therapies or immunotherapy [1]. In some cases, the clinical benefit of these therapies has been remarkable, increasing overall survival (OS), limiting treatment toxicity, or improving quality of life (QoL). However, in other cases, this clinical benefit has been modest compared to the standard of care [2, 3].

In recent decades there has been a remarkable development of scoring systems to assess clinical benefit in oncology, such as, the widely used European Society of Medical Oncology-Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO-MCBS) in Europe, or others including the French Amélioration du Service Médical Rendu (ASMR) or the added benefit scale of the German Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care (IQWIG) [4–6]. Having scoring systems like these, adapted to each national context, is crucial for making informed decisions, ensuring reimbursement, and selecting therapies that offer the greatest benefit, while avoiding the reimbursement of less relevant therapies and repositioning cancer drugs within therapeutic algorithms to maximize health outcomes for the global population. In fact, added clinical benefit is explicitly mentioned in Spanish legislation and appears in the 2023 OECD Policy Survey on Cancer Care Performance covering the EU-27 countries and Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland [7, 8].

A relevant added clinical benefit should be the primary factor to consider when introducing a new therapy into therapeutic arsenal, whether public or private. For orphan drugs without a comparator, it can be difficult to establish the added clinical benefit. However, even scales such as the ESMO-MCBS can assess the clinical benefit of these drugs based on their efficacy compared with the natural course of the disease in the absence of treatment. Nevertheless, it should not be the only criterion considered. For example, the availability of therapeutic alternatives, the degree of innovation, and the quality of the evidence used to estimate both the clinical benefits and cost-effectiveness are factors frequently cited in the literature regarding the reimbursement of cancer and other drugs in Europe [9–11].

Another important factor considered in several European countries is cost-effectiveness [9, 11]. In Spain, cost-effectiveness is a criterion for the reimbursement of new medicines, which appears in current legislation since 2011, but it is not sufficiently developed [12]. In recent years, there have been some efforts to develop economic evaluations (EE) in our country. In this sense, in 2020, there was a proposal to incorporate EE into the drug assessment process [13]. However, in 2023, this initiative encountered a setback when the Spanish courts invalidated the 2020 Plan [14]. Recently, the Advisory Committee on the Financing of Pharmaceuticals for the National Health System (CAPF, Comité Asesor para la Financiación de la Prestación Farmacéutica del Sistema Nacional de Salud) has developed a methodological guideline for conducting EE for the price and reimbursement (P&R) process [15]. However, as of the writing of this article, information regarding EE in the context of medicines reimbursement is not publicly available. A review of the EEs included in the Spanish Health Technology Assessment reports (IPTs, for their acronym in Spanish) between 2020 and 2023, a period during which EEs were incorporated prior to the National Court judgement, found that only 19 out of 181 IPTs (10.5%) included an EE and with a great heterogeneity in their methodological quality [16]. The project of Royal Decree regulating the procedures for the P&R of medicines specifies that the regulation will develop ‘the analysis of EE and the budgetary impact of the medicines to meet the efficiency criterion,’ although it is still unclear how this will be implemented or whether this information will be public [17].

This Royal Decree is expected to further develop the remaining criteria to consider for establishing P&R. Some of these include the availability of therapeutic alternatives, the degree of innovation of the medication, and the severity of the condition [7, 8].

In the context of the legislative change in our country regarding P&R, the objective of this study is to assess the influence of factors such as added clinical benefit, cost-utility, the availability of therapeutic alternatives, the degree of innovation of medications, and the presence of uncertainty on the decisions regarding the reimbursement of oncology drugs for solid tumours in Spain over the last decade. We aim to determine the role each factor included in the legislation has played in decision-making for cancer drugs up to the present moment, so it can serve as a baseline knowledge and support thoughtful reflection before the imminent change in the P&R legislation.

Methods

Selection of drugs and indications

On previous research, data on all indications for medicines targeting solid tumours authorised between January 2010 and October 2022 were collected [2]. We expanded this original selection to December 2023 and we gathered a list of every drug classified under groups L01 (antineoplastic agents) and L02 (endocrine therapy) according to the Anatomical-Therapeutic-Chemical classification (ATC) from the AEMPS-CIMA website [18]. Data for each indication were collected and stored in a Microsoft Excel® database. We applied several exclusion criteria for this study: paediatric indications, indications for benign tumours, biosimilar drugs, generic drugs, and different doses of the same drug.

Selection of variables and data extraction

For each indication, we obtained the reimbursement decision using the Search Engine for Information on the Reimbursement Status of Medicines (BIFIMED) on the Spanish Ministry of Health (MoH) website [19].

To understand which factors have influenced the decision-making regarding cancer drugs in recent years, we selected the variables detailed in Table 1. We selected clinical benefit, cost-utility (see Sect. 2.3), degree of innovation and availability of therapeutic alternatives because these criteria are included in the actual Spanish P&R legislation. Moreover, these criteria are commonly applied in P&R decisions throughout the European Union [8, 9].

To determine the clinical benefit, the ESMO-MCBS scale version 1.1 was considered [4]. This tool was selected to evaluate the clinical benefit of each indication from the standpoint of medical oncologists within our context. It is a validated and widely adopted instrument in clinical assessments, supporting decision-making in routine practice as well as in Health Technology Assessment Agencies (HTAA) reports. Notably, the ESMO-MCBS is incorporated into IPTs as a reference for clinical benefit assessment. The scale measures the benefit of each drug by comparing it to a control, typically the one used in the pivotal study. Scores range from A to C for treatments in the adjuvant setting, and from 1 to 5 for therapies targeting advanced or metastatic disease. High scores (A, B, 4, or 5) represent substantial clinical benefit, while a score of 3 reflects moderate benefit. The score assigned to each indication was based on ESMO’s official scorecards [20]; when no scorecard was available, we performed the assessment using the appropriate form.

To assess the potential impact of uncertainty and the quality of the submitted evidence, we included three variables: the quality of the trial evidence (high quality: phase III vs. low quality: phase I, II and/or single-arm clinical trials), availability of OS data, and availability of QoL data. The absence of OS and QoL data, as well as trials with small patient populations or lacking a comparator arm, represent significant limitations when making P&R decisions. These factors, although they are not the only sources of uncertainty, limit the ability to accurately quantify the clinical benefit expected once the drug is available and introduce uncertainty into cost-utility modelling and budget impact analysis.

To control for the effect of time, we introduced a time variable based on the reimbursement decision date for each indication, which was obtained from the Nomenclátor Alcántara database (available only to civil servants) [23]. The periods were selected so that the number of decisions would be comparable between periods. Finally, two periods were chosen: 2010 to 2019 and 2020 to 2023.

Cost-utility estimation

As shown in Table 1, cost-utility was one of the variables included in the analysis. Because cost-utility data for authorised medicines are not publicly available in Spain we had to estimate them. We estimated the incremental cost-utility ratios considering only the pharmacological costs of the cancer drugs. We support this decision based on the fact that pharmacological cost is the most important health care costs item in the treatment of advanced or metastatic cancer. We verified this fact by means of a preliminary analysis of the extracted data (see Table A1, online resource 1), where we obtained disaggregated costs for 47 indications from HTAA reports and the Tufts CEA Registry. Of the 47 indications included in this cost-proportion analysis, only 3 were sourced from Tufts. One study was French, and the other two were American. We found that pharmacological costs accounted for an average of 66% (SD 19.5%) and a median of 70% (IQR 49–81) of the total costs [24–26]. Excluding these two American studies (n = 45), the mean and the median proportion of drug costs over the total costs and remain unchanged. Therefore, although we are aware that this estimate could overestimate the drug costs over the total healthcare costs, we consider that our approach is reasonable for estimating cost-utility of a large number of indications in the specific case of oncology drugs in the Spanish framework (healthcare payer perspective) [15]. We performed estimations using both official list prices (‘precios notificados’ in Spanish) and reimbursed prices. However, given the confidentiality of reimbursed prices, incremental cost-utility ratios (ICUR) estimated with this price will only be reported in an aggregated manner.

List prices (PVL/laboratory selling price) were sourced from the BOT PLUS database provided by the General Council of Official Pharmacists’ Associations, applying the mandatory discount set out in Royal Decree 8/2010 [27, 28]. We calculated treatment acquisition costs for both experimental and control arms in each pivotal clinical trial (PCT). When the control arm included multiple regimens, we used the average cost across them. Treatment duration for each drug was estimated based on the most recent available publication, prioritizing information in the following order: number of cycles, mean treatment duration, and median duration. For consistency, we used year and month conversions of 365.25 days and 30.44 days, respectively. Vial optimization strategies were also considered. Dosing calculations based on weight or body surface area used anthropometric data from the Spanish Health Atlas, assuming a standard weight of 70.5 kg and a body surface area of 1.78 m² [29]. For ease of analysis, the cost of drugs common to both treatment arms were excluded, provided there were no relevant differences in treatment duration or overall cost.

Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) reported by HTAAs were used as the measure of effectiveness when available, with a focus on data from the Canadian Drug Agency (CDA-AMC) and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [24, 25]. When possible, information from Economic Guidance Panels was given precedence over that submitted by manufacturers. In cases where no such data were available, the Tufts University Cost-Effectiveness Analysis Registry was consulted [26]. Treatment costs were discounted starting from the first year. The discount rate applied corresponded to that used in the source of effectiveness data. Generally, a 1.5% discount rate was used, aligning with CDA-AMC guidelines [24]. For estimates based on NICE data, a 3.5% discount rate was applied [24]. When data came from alternative sources, their respective discount rates were used. Finally, the calculated ICURs were grouped into three categories.

In principle, we have considered ICURs as a continuous variable. However, we have also reconfigured the variable in our analyses according to the threshold values discussed in the Spanish context. Since there are no official cost-utility thresholds in Spain, for the list prices, the ICURs were classified based on their distribution into: less than 50,000 €/QALY, between 50,000 and 100,000 €/QALY, and greater than 100,000 €/QALY [30]. For the reimbursed prices, thresholds available in the literature were used: less than 25,000 €/QALY, between 25,000 and 60,000 €/QALY, and more than 60,000 €/QALY [31].

Lastly, since the costs were estimated and the effects were adapted from the HTAA reports, there is no specific time horizon. As mentioned earlier, the time horizon for costs was the treatment duration according to the PCTs, while the time horizon for effects depended on that considered in each HTAA report.

Statistical analysis and econometric models

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the different variables. Proportions were calculated for categorical variables, while means and medians were estimated for continuous variables.

Several models were developed to estimate the effect of each variable on the likelihood of reimbursement. The reimbursement decision (yes/no) was the dependent variable. The explanatory variables are defined in Table 1. Several probit regressions were conducted, and marginal effects were estimated for each variable in every model. The models with the best fit were ultimately selected. For the statistical analysis, we used the STATA software. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Additionally, we conducted a multinomial probit model including the following categories: reimbursed, conditionally reimbursed, and not reimbursed. The results of this analysis are presented in Table A2 of Online Resource 2.

Results

Descriptive analysis of the indications and variables selected

Figure 1 presents the flowchart outlining the process for selecting drugs and indications. In total, 78 drugs and 220 indications were ultimately included in the analysis. A complete list of all the drugs and indications can be found in online resource 2.

Fig. 1Flowchart of drugs and indications selection

Flowchart of drugs and indications selection

Considering the tumour type, the most common indications were for lung cancer (n = 55, 25%), breast cancer or gynaecological tumours (n = 46, 20.9%), gastrointestinal tumours (n = 42, 19.1%), genitourinary tumours (n = 41, 18.6%), and melanoma or other tumours (n = 36, 16.4%). Most of the indications were for advanced or metastatic stages (n = 201, 91.4%), while only 19 indications were for adjuvant setting (8.6%). 88 indications (40%) corresponded to new authorisations by the European Commission (EC), while 132 (60%) were indication extensions.

Regarding clinical benefit, half of the indications showed substantial clinical benefit. Specifically, 14 indications received an ESMO-MCBS score of A (6.3%), 23 received a score of 5 (10.5%), and 73 received a score of 4 (33.2%).

In terms of therapy availability, 150 of them (68.2%) were available at the data cut-off date. Of these, 33 (15% of the total) had conditional reimbursement and only 2 of them were subject to a pay-for-performance agreement.

Table 2 summarizes the descriptive results for the variables of the drugs and indications included in the analysis. The mean ICUR (list prices) was 117,784 €/QALY (SD: 98,086), and the median was 86,770 €/QALY (IQR: 55,227–148,433). For the ICUR (reimbursed prices), the mean was 60,350 €/QALY (SD: 49,032), and the median was 47,190 €/QALY (IQR: 24,415–77,435). No statistically significant differences were found between the reimbursed and non-reimbursed indications. The non-reimbursed indications had a median ICUR (list prices) of 84,233 €/QALY (IQR: 65,351 − 185,118) and the reimbursed indications had a median ICUR of 86,557 €/QALY (IQR: 52,720 − 145,506). Of the indications classified as pay-for-performance and conditional reimbursement, n = 33, 21 (63.6%) demonstrated substantial clinical benefit and had an average ICUR (list price) of €100,660 (SD; €55,520) per QALY. Considering reimbursed prices these indications had an average ICUR of €48,300 (SD; €30,010) per QALY. Drugs without substantial clinical benefit (n = 12) showed a mean ICUR of €97,995 per QALY (SD: €55,760). When considering reimbursed prices, the mean ICUR was €49,147 per QALY (SD: €29,821). Complete information of incremental costs (list price), incremental QALY, ICUR and sources can be found in online resource 2.

In total, there were data on reimbursement decisions dates for 199 indications. The number of reimbursement decisions changed over time, with the highest number in 2023 (n = 51, 25.6%), 2021 (n = 30, 13,6%) and 2022 (n = 25, 11,4%).

Association between clinical benefit and reimbursement decision

There is a statistically significant association between substantial clinical benefit and the reimbursement decision. Excluding indications with no reimbursement decision, 82 (83.7%) of the 98 indications with substantial clinical benefit were reimbursed, while 68 (65.4%) of the 104 indications without substantial clinical benefit were reimbursed (OR = 2.7; 95%CI (1.4–5.3); p < 0.01).

Association between cost-utility and clinical benefit

The total costs of treating indications with substantial clinical benefit (list prices) averages €75,557 (SD: €46,072), whereas for those without substantial clinical benefit, the mean total cost is €59,085 (SD: €53,918). This difference was statistically significant according to the Kruskal–Wallis test (p < 0.01). Nevertheless, there were no statistically significant differences in median ICUR according to clinical benefit (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.87). The median ICUR for indications with substantial clinical benefit was 87,967 €/QALY (IQR: 62,861–141,809), while for those without substantial clinical benefit it was 85,344 €/QALY (IQR: 51,294–158,607). The distribution of these ICURs can be seen in Fig. 2. It shows the cost-utility plane of the different indications estimated using list prices. As shown, ICURs are not unevenly distributed according to the level of substantial clinical benefit. 16 indications (45.7%) with an ICUR below 50,000 €/QALY had substantial clinical benefit. Similarly, 39 (60.9%) and 42 (53.2%) of the indications with an ICUR between 50,000 and 100,000 €/QALY and over 100,000 €/QALY, respectively, had substantial clinical benefit.

Models 2 to 3 provide the best explanation of the reimbursement decision, with an adequate sample size (202 and 199 observations, respectively). In Model 1 and 2, there is a reduction from 220 indications to 202 due to 18 indications with pending reimbursement decision at the time of the analysis. There is a drop in observations in Model 3, down to 199, as 3 indications did not have a reimbursement date available.

As can be seen, the key factor explaining reimbursement decisions is the presence of substantial clinical benefit, which was significant across all models. Model 1 to 3 shows that, for a given indication, providing a substantial clinical benefit compared to the treatment selected in the pivotal clinical trial increases the probability of reimbursement by between 17.5 and 20.1% points (pp).

Another relevant factor is the uncertainty regarding QoL. As shown in Models 2 and 3, the absence of any QoL data reduces the probability of reimbursement by between 13.9 and 14.8 pp.

The period in which the reimbursement decision was made also helps explain the probability of receiving positive reimbursement. In this regard, Model 3 shows that during the 2020–2023 period, the probability of receiving positive reimbursement was 19.4 pp lower.

On the other hand, the inclusion of the efficiency dimension substantially reduces the sample size, due to the impossibility of obtaining ICURs for all the cases included in the previous models. Therefore, the results are not directly comparable with models 1 to 3. The most important result to highlight in models 4 and 4bis is that the ICUR, whether based on list prices or reimbursed prices, was not a relevant variable in explaining reimbursement decisions. The results did not change when the variable was introduced into the model as a continuous variable, a categorical variable (thresholds), or when transformed to logarithms.

The remaining variables, such as the presence of therapeutic alternatives, innovation, uncertainty regarding the quality of the evidence, or the availability of OS data, were also not statistically significant.

An interesting point to consider is the special treatment of those drugs and indications that received a favorable reimbursement decision, albeit on a conditional basis. Due to the limited number of cases, this analysis was excluded from the main results and has been included in a supplementary appendix instead (table A2 of online resource 2). The primary findings of our analysis remain essentially unchanged: substantial clinical benefit continues to be the main significant variable, and the lack of statistical significance for the economic evaluation outcomes persists. The only noteworthy change occurs within the variables capturing uncertainty in clinical trials, where the presence of QoL outcomes would only achieve significance under a 90% confidence interval.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the main factors that explain the reimbursement decision for cancer drugs in Spain. It is not surprising that one of the most important factors in the reimbursement decision is the presence of substantial clinical benefit. As we mentioned earlier, this is a criterion established in Spanish legislation and was also stated in the 2023 OECD Policy Survey on Cancer Care Performance [7, 8]. It is important to highlight that these results confirm and extend those obtained in previous work [2, 9]. A recent publication analysing the association between the reimbursement decisions and reimbursement criterions in Spain concludes that for oncology drugs there is a positive association between criterion (c) (the social and therapeutic value of the medicines and incremental clinical benefit, also considering cost-effectiveness) and the reimbursement decision, corroborating our analysis [32]. However, this criterion includes the added therapeutic value taking cost-effectiveness into account, making it impossible to determine which specific factor is influencing the reimbursement decision. Moreover, the authors conclude that there is no clear association between the content of the IPTs and the reimbursement decisions. For cancer drugs, we consider the inclusion of clinical benefit as measured by the ESMO-MCBS in the IPTs to be a valuable approach that may help explain the strong association observed between added clinical benefit and reimbursement decisions. The availability of a standardized European scale facilitates the assessment of clinical benefit in a way comparable to France’s ASMR system, thereby enabling more consistent, value-based P&R decisions.

Notwithstanding the above, although a part of indications without substantial clinical benefit are excluded from reimbursement, there is still a high proportion of reimbursed drugs with limited therapeutic value. Specifically, 24 indications (10.9% of the total) were reimbursed with an ESMO-MCBS score of 1, 2, or C. These drugs offer a rather low incremental benefit. In terms of utility, they offered an average of 0.33 incremental QALYs and an estimated ICUR (list price) of 114,895 €/QALY. If we consider the reference thresholds estimated by Vallejo-Torres and other proposed in the literature, this represents a very high opportunity cost for Spanish National Health System [31, 33]. This is particularly relevant, especially considering that 20 of these 24 indications (83.3%) had an alternative therapeutic option available. While it seems appropriate to reimburse new medicines or indications even if the clinical benefit of these is not substantial, the condition for this is that the price should be similar to that of existing therapeutic alternatives. This principle does not seem to have been applied in many situations in the Spanish context.

The second factor that appears to be relevant in the reimbursement decision is the availability of QoL data. QoL data are particularly important in cancer drugs, since, as previously noted, most of the indications analysed target advanced or metastatic stages (91.4%). Uncertainty regarding QoL makes it difficult to evaluate each indication, both in terms of clinical benefit and EE, as estimating utilities becomes challenging. The positive valuation of QoL data is a good sign that decision-makers’ choices are aligned with patient preferences. There is evidence that cancer patients place high value on QoL, sometimes even prioritizing it over life prolongation [34–37].

In contrast, this highlights the relatively limited presence of comparative QoL data submitted by marketing authorisation holders to the EMA, with such data available in only around 46% of the indications analysed. Given that QoL information is important both for decision-makers and patients, efforts should be made to include this type of data in both clinical trials, marketing, and reimbursement submissions.

Taking into account the relevance of these two clinical factors in decision-making, this is something that could be reinforced by the new Joint Clinical Assessment (JCA) framework. In this regard, the JCA may reduce uncertainty in the evaluation of outcomes by providing a more thorough and homogeneous assessment that addresses the most relevant Patient-Intervention-Comparator-Outcome (PICO) question for each national context. However, when it comes to setting price and reimbursement, there will always be a negotiation at the Member State level which, even taking the JCA into account, will depend on many other factors. The JCA will likely have a greater impact on the organization of health technology assessment, particularly on the timelines for conducting the clinical evaluation, potentially shortening this initial phase. However, it is less clear that it will facilitate or accelerate the reimbursement of oncology drugs, given the existence of other factors beyond the clinical domain.

The last factor that appears to influence reimbursement decisions is the time-related variable. As shown in the results, decisions made between 2020 and 2023 were more likely to be negative compared to those made between 2010 and 2019. For example, between 2010 and 2015, all 23 oncology indications submitted for reimbursement received a favourable decision, regardless of whether the clinical benefit was relevant or not. In contrast, between 2020 and 2023, 68.6% received public reimbursement (79.4% of those with relevant clinical benefit and 56.4% of those without). This may be due to the COVID-19 pandemic, during which there may have been issues in terms of drug evaluation and a possible reduction in reimbursement in the following years. Another possible reason may be related to the accumulation of P&R submissions during that four-year period, as around 60% of the decisions analysed were made between 2020 and 2023. Moreover, 95 of the 220 indications (43.2%) included in our analysis were authorised by the EC between 2020 and 2023. This highlight that a high number of submissions concentrated in a short period of time may make decision-makers less inclined to a positive reimbursement, especially if the cost of these therapies is high. The decisions made during this period had an estimated average incremental cost of €71,336 (3,717 − 226,954) per patient, which could result in a high budgetary impact. In fact, of the 58 drugs with positive reimbursement, 42 (72.4%) were subject to the Medicines Supply Management System (SEGUIMED), 5 to an additional discount, 3 to a price-volume agreement, 3 to a spending cap, 1 to a maximum cost per patient, 1 to a pay-for-performance arrangement, and 1 to supply based on sales and/or age. Additionally, 33 indications had a conditional reimbursement that limits the use of the medicines to those patients who are most likely to benefit from them. These measures aim to limit the budgetary impact in various ways and highlight the relevance of budget impact in this type of decision-making. To demonstrate the significance of budget impact within the context of the Spanish NHS, a recent review of reimbursement decisions in our country [32] shows that criterion (d) of the legislation ‘rationing of public spending on medicines and budget impact from the perspective of the Spanish NHS’ was used either alone or in combination with other criteria in 101 out of 143 (70.6%) non-reimbursed decisions. Furthermore, it was the sole criterion for non-reimbursed drugs in 36 out of 143 cases (25.2%).

It is also important to consider the broader regulatory and methodological context. In particular, the 2020 methodological consolidation through REvalMed, together with the legal uncertainty introduced by the 2023 Spanish Court judgement, contributed to a period of significant disturbance. In this sense, REvalMed represented a more complex and comprehensive evaluation framework, providing greater methodological robustness and incorporating expert working groups that collaboratively contributed to the development of IPTs in order to enhance their usefulness for decision-making. However, the model was not sufficiently supported in terms of resources and depended largely on voluntary participation. Consequently, the preparation of IPTs was frequently delayed, which may also have contributed to longer decision-making timelines. Conversely, it is difficult to determine whether the introduction of EE into the IPTs under REvalMed was a serious attempt to incorporate the efficiency dimension in the evaluation process or merely a learning pilot to identify the strengths and weaknesses of including economic evaluations within a limited framework. The Spanish Court ruling disrupted this process. At present, there is no available information regarding the role that EE play, how this information, supposedly generated by pharmaceutical companies, is submitted, which public bodies assess the submitted information, and through which processes. The ongoing legislative reform in the pharmaceutical field in Spain should clarify these aspects.

On the other hand, in a complementary manner, this lower probability of reimbursement during this period may be also related to possible changes in drug policies depending on the management teams responsible for reimbursement decisions in the MoH. For example, in 2020, significant methodological changes were introduced under the Plan for the Consolidation of the IPTs for Medicines in Spanish NHS [13]. This plan incorporated EE and established a drugs evaluation network called REvalMed, with the aim of improving efficiency in the assessments and reimbursement of medicines within the Spanish NHS.

However, our results show that the efficiency dimension does not appear to significantly influence the reimbursement decision in Spain. These results are in line with what was previously stated, that no statistically significant differences were found between the ICURs for reimbursed and non-reimbursed indications. This situation contrasts with those found by other authors, who did find a significant influence between cost-effectiveness and the reimbursement decision in other countries; in other words, the lower the ICUR, the higher the probability of reimbursement [38–41]. As previously mentioned, this is particularly relevant when considering the opportunity cost of indications with the highest ICURs, especially when their clinical benefit is marginal. A recent article published in The Lancet concluded that the reimbursement of new medicines by NHS England resulted in a net loss of 1.51 million QALYs in oncology [42]. When reallocating resources to reimburse new drugs or technologies, it is crucial to consider what we are foregoing by doing so. As Vallejo-Torres et al. stated: “Taking into account these health opportunity costs when making funding and price-setting decisions is needed to avoid future health losses, and to ensure equal consideration for every patient—whether visible or invisible” [43].

In this sense, it is surprising that, despite being the fifth largest pharmaceutical market in Europe by sales volume, and ranking among the top ten globally [44], Spain still lacks a robust regulatory and organizational framework that effectively incorporates efficiency considerations into drug evaluation procedures [12, 15]. Such a framework would be valuable for providing relevant data to inform P&R decisions. Ensuring that access to innovative medicines and other health care technologies aligns with the optimal use of public healthcare resources is crucial for the sustainability of the Spanish NHS. Additionally, due to the societal impact of access to these therapies, especially for conditions with high rates of mortality and disability, decision-making processes must be transparent and accountable.

Encouragingly, the MoH has recently released EE guidelines developed by the Advisory Committee on Pharmaceutical Provision of the NHS [15]. Furthermore, as of this writing, a significant regulatory overhaul is in progress, aimed at aligning national evaluation systems with the requirements set forth by the European Health Technology Assessment reform [45]. Key priorities for Spain include the establishment of new evaluation bodies, improved coordination with existing HTAAs, and the seamless integration of EE into P&R decision processes challenges that demand prompt and decisive action.

The availability of therapeutic alternatives and the degree of innovation also do not appear to be key factors in the reimbursement decisions for cancer drugs in Spain. Although the presence of therapeutic alternatives was negatively associated with the probability of reimbursement, this association was not statistically significant. Similar findings for this factor have been reported for cancer drugs by other authors in Sweden [38]. Dakin et al. found similar results in their review and models of the decisions made by NICE [46]. However, in neither Sweden nor England the absence of therapeutic alternatives is not a criterion for drug reimbursement; both focus on cost-effectiveness, and in the case of Sweden, also on solidarity, equity, and the severity of the condition [11]. In recent research, Pinilla-Dominguez et al. found that criterion (e) ‘availability of alternative therapeutic options for the condition at the same or inferior price to that of the medicine under consideration’ was used alone in only 24 out of 143 (16.8%) of the negative recommendations [32]. Moreover, innovation was not used in any of the 253 decisions analysed [32]. One possible explanation for these non-significant results could be the lack of clear definition of these criteria. Unlike for clinical benefit, for which we have the ESMO-MCBS that is included in the IPTs, the degree of innovation and the absence of therapeutic alternatives are not formally defined. It is possible that, in some cases, the absence of therapeutic alternatives is considered simply because there is no drug approved for a specific indication, even though other drugs may be used off-label. In other instances, the absence of alternatives may be claimed when an existing option delivers poor outcomes. However, as we have seen, some of these more innovative drugs offer only a few months of survival, do not always provide a substantial or even moderate clinical benefit nor any benefit in terms of quality of life (80.4% of indications without QoL benefit were reimbursed) [2, 3]. An example of a country where there are indeed scales to assess therapeutic need is Italy, where both therapeutic need and clinical benefit are valued on a five-level scale [47]. For our analysis, we used the information available in the IPTs and applied our best judgment to define these variables, but it is possible that they do not strictly align with the spirit of the law.

Orphan drug status could also be a variable to consider given that these are, in principle, indicated for the treatment of rare diseases. However, we believe that this is adequately reflected within the variables of innovation and availability of therapeutic alternatives. Furthermore, in our sample, there were only 8 orphan drug indications out of the 220, of which 5 were considered innovative and another 5 had therapeutic alternatives, either authorised indications or off-label regimens.

Finally, the fact that the drug is a targeted therapy could also be considered as a factor influencing reimbursement. However, targeted therapies do not necessarily, in and of themselves, yield better outcomes for patients in terms of OS, QoL or reduction of toxicities. The clinical value of a drug, whether it is a targeted therapy or not, is best captured by the variable of substantial clinical benefit.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several limitations, mainly related to the estimation of cost-utility. One of these is that the ICURs were calculated solely based on the drug’s acquisition cost. This approach was to ease the estimations over numerous indications. Aware of this limitation, we conducted a review of all CDA-AMC and NICE reports, as well as every study from the Tufts CEA Registry used to extract outcomes data, to assess the impact of the acquisition cost on total costs (see supplementary material). As mentioned in the Materials and Methods section, for 47 indications where costs were disaggregated, the average pharmacological cost accounted for approximately two-thirds of the total cost. This highlights the substantial impact that acquisition costs have on the overall costs in cancer indications.

Another limitation is that the discount rate applied for the calculation of the ICUR was not consistent, as it depended on the rate used in each report utilized for extracting outcomes. However, when comparing the ICUR results with and without the discount, we found that the average ratio was 0.99 (SD 0.01). Moreover, discount rate was not applied in 129 indications because the treatment duration did not exceed one year. This is consistent with the rapid progression of advanced or metastatic cancers.

It is also worth mentioning that the estimations relied on several assumptions. First, adapting QALYs from different sources can lead to misinterpretation in the estimates due to the heterogeneity of the assumptions used in each model. However, of the 177 indications from which we adapted the QALYs, 147 (83%) were obtained from CDA-AMC. On the other hand, although patient preferences and utility values may vary between countries, we believe that adapting QALYs primarily from CDA-AMC may be a reasonable approach, although it would ideally be preferable to have primary data from Spain, this information is unfortunately not available, which constitutes a limitation of the study. For the ICUR calculations, mean or median values were used for parameters such as weight, height, body surface area, treatment duration, and so on. Potential dose reductions were not factored in, and costs were calculated based on standard doses. All these elements could potentially impact the ratios. However, the goal of this study was to provide an estimate of cost-utility and examine its potential link to reimbursement decisions. In addition, for drugs targeting single indications that were not reimbursed, price data were not available (11 out of 52). This gap could introduce a considerable bias when analysing the results. Unfortunately, the only available prices are those that have been definitively agreed upon, and without a P&R decision, there is no available price for consultation.

Unlike the list price, which remains stable once set, the reimbursed price varies over time as new indications are reimbursed or the price is revised according to different agreements. This could bias the results of Model 4 bis, and it is advisable to interpret these results with caution.

It has not been possible to incorporate another variable that could be relevant in the analyses, such as the estimated budgetary impact. The temporal variable can provide us with indirect information about the potential weight of the budgetary impact on the reimbursement decision. As we have seen, a high volume of submissions in a short period of time can decrease the likelihood of reimbursement, especially if the incremental costs are high. However, this does not seem to have influenced the prices of medicines that, without demonstrating relevant clinical benefit, have been reimbursed during the period 2020–2023. Therefore, future analyses providing information that is currently not available will be necessary to check whether the budgetary impact has played a proxy role for the efficiency dimension. Unfortunately, the opacity of the procedure prevents us from determining the budgetary impact of each drug included in the analysis. This is a limitation of our study, and future research should address the effect that budget impact has on reimbursement decisions. However, achieving this would require greater transparency in the information provided by decision-makers.

A key strength of this study is that, to the best of our knowledge, it is the first to explore the factors influencing the reimbursement decisions of cancer drugs in Spain through a comprehensive analysis of a large sample of indications over a 14-year period. This work, although imperfect, aims to contribute to the transparency and accountability of reimbursement decisions in our country, which without a clear framework, as we mentioned earlier, lack the desirable consistency and transparency [32].

Conclusion

The main factors influencing reimbursement decisions for cancer drugs in Spain are clinical benefit and the level of uncertainty regarding QoL. Cancer drugs that demonstrate substantial clinical benefit and include available QoL data are significantly more likely to be reimbursed. In contrast, the presence of therapeutic alternatives, the degree of innovation, and the efficiency dimension do not appear to play a significant role. Temporal differences are observed in reimbursement outcomes, with a lower likelihood of positive reimbursement decisions during the 2020–2023 period, possibly related to the sharp increase in submissions during that time. Our analysis highlights the need to clarify the P&R criteria in the new legislative framework to ensure that decisions are more transparent, technically grounded, and accountable.
