A systematic review of breastfeeding prevalence, duration and determining factors in women with type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Fiona L. Britten, Emma Shipton, Madeline Duke, Emma L. Duncan, Leonie K. Callaway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThere is evolving evidence of the metabolic benefit of breastfeeding for women with diabetes and their infants but little data about breastfeeding prevalence and duration in women with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. This is the first systematic review to examine prevalence and duration of breastfeeding in women with pregestational diabetes and variables associated with breastfeeding establishment.MethodsA systemic literature review was conducted to assess breastfeeding prevalence in women with pregestational diabetes, and to describe variables reported to affect prevalence. The study was registered with the PROSPERO database (CRD42023447299).ResultsWomen with type 1 diabetes had lower prevalence and shorter duration of breastfeeding compared with normoglycaemic women. In contrast, there was insufficient evidence regarding prevalence and duration of breastfeeding in type 2 diabetes. Multivariate analysis identified variables associated with reduced breastfeeding prevalence or duration in pregestational diabetes. These included elevated body mass index, reduced maternal education, and less frequent early breastfeeding. Only two studies found an independent association with diabetes. No studies controlled for all likely confounders.ConclusionsBreastfeeding prevalence and duration are likely reduced in type 1 diabetes; however, evidence in type 2 diabetes is inconclusive. A significant knowledge gap remains in this area; further research is required to establish the prevalence of breastfeeding and associated variables in women with pregestational diabetes. Given the long‐term metabolic benefit of lactation in women with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and the increasing prevalence of pregestational type 2 diabetes, further data are needed to design effective interventions to support breastfeeding in this population.

INTRODUCTION

The World Health Organisation strongly recommends breastfeeding all babies wherever possible, for 2 years or longer. 4 Commonly listed evidence‐based benefits include reduced neonatal infection risk, due to innate immune components and immunoglobulins present in breastmilk. 5 Breastfeeding also has multiple metabolic benefits for the infant, including reduced risk of obesity, type 1 and type 2 diabetes, 6 , 7 of particular relevance to infants of women with pregestational diabetes, who have an increased lifetime risk of diabetes. 8 , 9 Whilst mechanisms remain unproven, reduced risk of type 1 diabetes is thought due to reduced early exposure to foreign antigens and exposure to biologically active antibodies, cytokines, and hormones in breastmilk. 10 Reduced risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes may be mediated by breastmilk components (i.e. long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids) 11 microbiome alternations 12 and optimal volume transfer at the breast. 13

Breastfeeding also bestows long‐term metabolic benefits for the mother, reducing the risk of progression to type 2 diabetes, 1 and reducing cardiovascular risk, 2 specifically in women with a history of gestational diabetes and type 2 diabetes. 3 Proposed mechanisms include glucose transfer into breastmilk (approximately 50–75 g/day), improved insulin sensitivity, and increased beta cell mass. 14 Enhanced lipolysis and endogenous insulin production, potentially mediated by prolactin, have also been described. 15 Oxytocin may also improve cardiovascular outcomes. 16

Despite these documented benefits, to date few studies have assessed breastfeeding in women with pregestational diabetes. Several small‐moderate sized prospective studies have conflicting results. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Two large studies (>500 participants with type 1 diabetes) suggest breastfeeding duration is shorter in type 1 diabetes 21 , 22 but may be due to medical or social confounders rather than diabetes itself. No prior systematic reviews assessing breastfeeding prevalence in women with pregestational type 1 and type 2 diabetes have been published.

Given the documented benefits of breastfeeding and the suggestion in the limited literature of lower rates of breastfeeding in women with diabetes as outlined above, we hypothesised that breastfeeding prevalence in women with pregestational diabetes would be lower than normoglycaemic women and that this may be due to confounding factors rather than diabetes itself. If so, this may represent a missed opportunity to improve maternal and infant health outcomes. Thus, the primary aim of this systematic review was to examine breastfeeding prevalence and duration in women with pregestational diabetes. A secondary aim was to examine variables associated with breastfeeding establishment in women with pregestational diabetes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A systematic literature review was conducted to assess breastfeeding prevalence in women with pregestational diabetes post‐partum and to describe variables reported to affect prevalence. This question had not been previously addressed in the PROSPERO database, and the study was registered (CRD42023447299).

Definitions, inclusion and exclusion criteria

Cohort definitions

Women were considered to have pregestational diabetes if diagnosed before pregnancy with T1DM, type 2 diabetes, maturity‐onset diabetes in the young (MODY), or diabetes otherwise undifferentiated.

Breastfeeding definitions

Definitions of ‘exclusive’, ‘full’, ‘predominant’, and/or ‘partial’ breastfeeding have differed between authors and over time. 5 For this review, the following definitions were used, based on descriptions given by authors in their articles, regardless of the term used within the paper itself: Exclusive breastfeeding [exclusive‐BF]: babies fed only breastmilk (via breast or bottle if expressed breast milk) or medicine/water since birth.Full breastfeeding [full‐BF]: babies fed only breastmilk (via breast or bottle) or medicine/water at time of survey who may have received formula at some point previously.Any breastfeeding [any‐BF]: babies fed breastmilk at time of survey who may also have been receiving formula. Embase and CINAHL were also searched using the same MeSH terms modified as required for each database. Duplicate articles were identified and deleted using the Deduplicator. 23 Abstracts were screened by two reviewers independently (FB and ES). Full text was retrieved for studies meeting inclusion criteria, or where it was unclear from the abstract if they met inclusion criteria. Full texts of selected articles were then reviewed; articles not meeting inclusion criteria were excluded. Reference lists of identified relevant articles were further screened to ensure inclusion of all relevant studies. Where there was disagreement regarding study inclusion after independent screening, a third reviewer adjudicated (MD).

Inclusion criteria

All studies published in English that quantitatively examined breastfeeding in women with pregestational diabetes (i.e. breastfeeding prevalence assessed at specified time points from hospital discharge onwards; or where breastfeeding duration was quantified mean/median as appropriate) were included. A normoglycaemic comparator group was not required for study inclusion (see Figure 1 for inclusion/exclusion criteria). Studies included cohort, observational, and randomised controlled trials (RCTs), and rate or prevalence of breastfeeding may have been a primary or secondary aim.

Outcome definition

The principal outcome of interest was the prevalence of breastfeeding in women with pregestational diabetes at different timepoints, compared to women without diabetes. A secondary objective was to examine factors associated with breastfeeding in women with type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes specifically. Relevantly, none of the studies that listed breastfeeding simply as ‘ever’ (or similar non‐quantitative duration) assessed factors associated with breastfeeding; thus, their exclusion from our analysis did not bias the secondary objective.

Data extraction

Data from each study were tabulated into Table S1, then further summarised in Tables 2, 3, 4, 5.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment was performed using the validated Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS) 24 or the Janet Briggs Institute checklist for Case Series 25 by two reviewers independently. Based on this adapted tool, studies were classified as good (7–9), fair (5–7), or poor (4 or less). Lower total scores or no stars in any domain were considered to have a higher risk of bias. Results for each domain of Selection, Comparability, and Outcome are listed in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and the adapted tool in Table S2. However, studies ranked as poor quality and case series were included to fulfil the second aim of the current study, as these papers were still able to provide information regarding variables associated with breastfeeding. Studies were categorised as small if there were 100 participants or fewer with a diabetes subtype, moderate if >100 participants, or large if >500 participants.

RESULTS

1031 unique articles were identified; 120 full texts were scrutinized; and 28 articles, published over a forty‐year period, were included in this review (See Figure 1). Quality scores ranged from good to poor; most poorly scored studies were due to lack of an adequately controlled comparator group. Studies were reported as per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA). 26 Data for studies reporting cohorts of women with type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes are reported in Tables 1 and 2 respectively. Studies comparing type 1 and type 2 diabetes (but without normoglycaemic controls) are reported in Table 3. Studies with only aggregated type 1 and type 2 diabetes data are reported in Table 4. A summary of all study results is reported in Table S1.

Breastfeeding prevalence in women with pregestational diabetes

Most studies were small and lacked an appropriate control group. In studies that considered type 1 and type 2 diabetes separately, the median number of participants with type 1 diabetes was 42 (range 2–1096), and with type 2 diabetes was 30 (range 15–212). Only four prospective studies had more than 100 participants with pregestational type 1 diabetes 20 , 21 , 22 , 32 ; and only one prospective study had more than 100 women with pregestational type 2 diabetes. 18

Results from six studies reporting breastfeeding at hospital discharge, 19 , 27 , 31 , 35 , 37 , 39 and three studies reported breastfeeding at 3 days post‐partum 21 , 28 , 29 are presented in Table 1. Breastfeeding prevalence varied widely, ranging from 47% to 100%. Considering women with type 2 diabetes:

Breastfeeding at hospital discharge/three days post‐partum

Studies comparing women with type 2 diabetes and controls are presented in Table 2. Only two studies of breastfeeding in women with type 2 diabetes had a normoglycaemic control. These are presented in Figure 5. The only large prospective study of breastfeeding prevalence in women with type 2 diabetes vs. normoglycaemic women was the PANDORA (Pregnancy and Neonatal Outcomes in Remote Australia) study, conducted in rural Australia, assessing 144 women with type 2 diabetes (123 [85%] Indigenous women), 684 with gestational diabetes (261 [38%] Indigenous women), and 222 normoglycaemic controls (111 [50%] Indigenous women). Exclusive‐BF at hospital discharge was lower in women with type 2 diabetes vs. controls overall in a fully adjusted model (49% vs. 85%, odds ratios [OR] 0.4 [confidence interval] [CI (0.2–0.8)]). Indigenous women with type 2 diabetes were more likely to exclusive‐BF than non‐indigenous women with type 2 diabetes (52% vs. 29% p = 0.047). 18

One small prospective urban study compared breastfeeding rates at hospital discharge in women with type 2 diabetes against BMI‐matched normoglycaemic women and normoglycaemic women with normal‐range BMI. Women with type 2 diabetes had lower prevalence of full‐BF at discharge compared with normoglycaemic women, whether elevated or normal BMI (43% vs. 90% [p = 0.01] and vs. 92% [p = 0.03], respectively). 17

Breastfeeding beyond hospital discharge

Duration

A large retrospective Australian study considering duration of BF after hospital discharge (N = 5650) included 108 pregnancies in women with type 2 diabetes. 34 It reported shorter duration of any‐BF in women with type 2 diabetes, compared to all other study participants (noting the comparator population could include women with gestational diabetes or type 1 diabetes), with median breastfeeding duration of 6 (range: 0–24) months vs. 9 (0–84) months for women with vs. without type 2 diabetes respectively. 34

Prevalence

Data for studies of BF prevalence in women with type 2 diabetes that include normoglycaemic controls are presented in Figure 6. It is unclear whether BF prevalence is lower in women with type 2 diabetes vs. normoglycaemic women.

Considering the PANDORA study, prevalence of full‐BF did not show a clear difference in women with type 2 diabetes vs. normoglycaemic women at either 6 weeks or at 6 months in a multivariate model fully adjusted for confounders. However, confidence intervals were wide and there was substantial loss to follow up. 18 The small prospective urban study described above showed full‐BF at 4 months post‐partum was lower in women with type 2 diabetes compared to normoglycaemic women with normal BMI (23% vs. 67% p = 0.047), but similar to matched‐BMI normoglycaemic women (23% vs. 22% p = >0.99). 17

Breastfeeding in women with type 2 diabetes compared to women with type 1 diabetes or gestational diabetes

Seven studies compared breastfeeding prevalence and/or duration in women with type 2 diabetes vs. type 1 diabetes, and two studies compared these parameters in women with type 2 diabetes vs. gestational diabetes. Results are presented in Table 3.

Breastfeeding at hospital discharge in women with type 2 vs. type 1 diabetes

Three studies reported on breastfeeding at discharge. Two showed significantly lower full‐ or exclusive‐BF in women with type 2 diabetes vs. type 1 diabetes (72% vs. 45% p = <0.01 37 and 23% vs. 34% p = 0.02 35 respectively). However, there was no difference in any‐BF (93% vs. 86% p = 0.17 37 and 46% vs. 47% p = 0.92 35 ). A third study reported any‐BF of 100% in both groups but had only 21 type 1 participants and two type 2 participants. 39

Breastfeeding beyond hospital discharge in women with type 2 vs. type 1 diabetes

Five studies examined breastfeeding beyond hospital discharge in these women. The largest of these (44 women with type 2 diabetes, 105 with type 1 diabetes) reported lower breastfeeding prevalence in women with type 2 diabetes vs. type 1 diabetes at 4 months post‐partum (for full‐BF: 23% vs. 49%, p = <0.01; for any‐BF: 34% vs. 61%, p < 0.01). 37 None of the other four studies (with sizes 15–44 women with type 2 diabetes and 11–81 with type 1 diabetes) reported a difference. 36 , 38 , 40 , 49

Breastfeeding in women with type 2 diabetes vs. gestational diabetes

Two studies reported these data. Any‐BF prevalence was lower at discharge 42 and at 2, 4, and 6 weeks 38 in women with type 2 diabetes vs. gestational diabetes.

Women with undifferentiated pregestational diabetes

Studies considered breastfeeding prevalence in pregestational diabetes (i.e. in which the study analysis considered women with type 1‐ and type 2 diabetes as a single group) vs. controls at various timepoints. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 48 , 49 , 50 Results are presented in Table 4.

Breastfeeding at hospital discharge

Considering any‐BF: Two large studies had conflicting results. One found any‐BF at discharge was slightly, but significantly, lower in women with undifferentiated pregestational diabetes (61% vs. 65%, p < 0.05) (pregestational diabetes: n = 6323; normoglycaemic women: n = 792,730). 46 However, a slightly smaller study did not replicate these findings with any‐BF at discharge 62% vs. 68%, p = 0.13 (pregestational diabetes: n = 138, normoglycaemic women: n = 1283). 48

Considering exclusive‐BF: a third study (women with pregestational diabetes: n = 159; normoglycaemic women: n = 23,291) found exclusive‐BF was lower at hospital discharge in women with pregestational diabetes vs. normoglycaemic women (OR 0.43 [CI: 0.29–0.63]), with lowest prevalence in insulin‐treated women vs. non‐insulin‐treated women with pregestational diabetes (OR 0.38 [CI 0.24–0.6]). 45

Breastfeeding beyond hospital discharge

Duration

The studies listed above did not address this question.

Prevalence

Post hospital discharge, two studies reported that any‐BF was lower in women with pregestational diabetes vs. normoglycaemic controls (assessed at 2 months 50 and 3–12 months 44 ). One study reported no difference (BF assessed at 28 days 43 ).

One small study of 38 women with pregestational diabetes and 52 normoglycaemic controls did not find any difference in prevalence of any‐BF or full‐BF at 3, 6, or 12 months. 49

Most studies assessing variables associated with breastfeeding included all study participants in their initial univariate analysis without differentiating by glycaemic status (i.e. women with pregestational diabetes, gestational diabetes, and without diabetes). DISCUSSION

This systematic review is the first to examine breastfeeding prevalence and duration in women with pregestational diabetes and variables associated with breastfeeding establishment. In line with our hypothesis, there was a moderate degree of evidence to suggest decreased breastfeeding prevalence in type 1 diabetes compared to normoglycaemic controls. However, there was insufficient evidence and disparate results regarding breastfeeding prevalence in women with type 2 diabetes.

Even when considering the data for women with type 1 diabetes, there was pronounced variation in breastfeeding prevalence within studies (between diabetes and control groups) and between studies even when similar time points were considered. This may be due to variation in study design or to the possible effect of multiple variables on breastfeeding prevalence, which may have changed over time. For example, routine nursery admission for babies of mothers with type 1 diabetes in previous decades may have resulted in less early breastfeeding. Variations in sociocultural breastfeeding practices over time and geographically may have also affected breastfeeding prevalence reported in the studies we examined.

Multivariate analysis of variables affecting breastfeeding beyond 6 weeks included all participants including normoclycaemic controls in many studies. This limited our ability to derive variables specifically affecting breastfeeding in women with pregestational diabetes to four studies, all of which analysed type 1 and type 2 diabetes as a single group. In addition, all studies omitted key variables. Despite this limitation, three variables were associated with breastfeeding continuation in both diabetes‐specific subgroup analysis and multivariate analysis in three or more studies (analysis including normoglycemic controls). These were early breastfeeding, elevated BMI, and reduced maternal education. As only one study performed multivariate analysis for type 2 diabetes exclusive of type 1 diabetes, it is difficult to separate variables differentially affecting diabetes subtypes with currently available evidence.

Importantly only two studies found diabetes was independently associated with reduced breastfeeding prevalence, and neither considered early feeding frequency, which may be an important confounding variable.

None of the identified factors are specific to diabetes; however, some of them are more common in women with pregestational diabetes, including earlier gestation at delivery and elevated BMI (in type 2 diabetes). 53 In addition, peripartum pregestational diabetes management is associated with events that may reduce the likelihood of early breastfeeding in the general population, including birth type [vaginal birth, caesarean section, etc.], separation of mother and infant, infant nursery admission, and prelacteal formula introduction (i.e. in the first three days of life). 54 , 55

Early breastfeeding

First breastfeed timing and more frequent feeds prior to lactogenesis have consistently been associated with breastfeeding establishment in the general population. 4 Two large studies of women with type 1 diabetes in our review identified these factors as important variables. 20 , 21

Elevated BMI

An important consideration, particularly in type 2 diabetes, is the effect of obesity, rather than diabetes, on breastfeeding rates. Breastfeeding prevalence is reduced in women with obesity, 56 and obesity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes in women of reproductive age. 53 Whether obesity and type 2 diabetes have independent effects on breastfeeding is unknown. The only large prospective study that assessed this question (in a rural population with a high proportion of indigenous women) showed an effect of BMI on breastfeeding at 6 weeks but not an independent effect of type 2 diabetes. 18 Insulin resistance has been hypothesised to affect milk production 57 but conclusive evidence that this independently affects breastfeeding establishment and/or duration is lacking. Only two studies one in type 1 diabetes, 32 and one including both type 1 and type 2 diabetes 37 assessed maternal insulin dose or diabetes control and associations with breastfeeding beyond 6 weeks. These variables were not significantly associated with breastfeeding prevalence in either study on multivariate analysis (see Table 5). Thus, the role of BMI and insulin resistance in breastfeeding prevalence in women with type 2 diabetes remains an important research question.

Reduced maternal education

Similarly to other variables, maternal education and socioeconomic status have also been reported as important variables in breastfeeding establishment in women without diabetes. 58 Thus, it is unsurprising that this variable may be associated with breastfeeding outcomes in women with pregestational diabetes.

Cultural factors

Cultural factors that affect breastfeeding practice may influence breastfeeding prevalence geographically (within and between countries) and temporally, affecting women with pregestational diabetes along with the general community. For example, Denmark has high breastfeeding rates, and breastfeeding prevalence was similarly high in Danish women with type 1 diabetes. 32 In Germany, increased institutional support for breastfeeding increased breastfeeding rates in both women with type 1 diabetes and in normoglycaemic controls. 59 , 60 The PANDORA study provides a good example of the complexity of cultural interactions, where women with type 2 diabetes (85% of whom were Indigenous) had similar breastfeeding rates to normoglycaemic controls, and increased breastfeeding was independently associated with more remote location. 18 Collectively, these studies suggest local community factors affect breastfeeding and may be more relevant than the presence of pregestational diabetes. 61

Perceived Milk supply

The most commonly cited maternal reason for breastfeeding cessation is perceived insufficient supply of breastmilk. 62 This was not adequately explored in the reviewed studies and represents an important omission. Whether women with pregestational diabetes have actual or perceived insufficient breastmilk requires further exploration.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

This study is the first to comprehensively review the literature regarding the breastfeeding outcomes in women with pregestational diabetes. This systematic review also used standardised definitions for breastfeeding, mitigating a common problem in breastfeeding studies and allowing more effective comparisons. All studies that reported prevalence or duration of breastfeeding in type 1, type 2, or pregestational diabetes were considered, regardless of the presence of controls or study size, allowing a thorough examination of the topic whilst recognising the impact of study quality on the capacity to draw robust conclusions.

The major limitation of our review proved to be the paucity of adequately sized, appropriately controlled, and sufficiently detailed studies of breastfeeding in the literature. Breastfeeding was often a secondary outcome, or an incidentally reported outcome. Studies often failed to describe breastfeeding adequately (including type [exclusive/formula‐supplemented/etc] and breastfeeding prevalence was measured at varying times [i.e. at discharge, 3 months, 6 months etc]) between studies.

Most large studies only considered breastfeeding at discharge which is an important limitation because timing of breastfeeding measurement may be important in pregestational diabetes. Full breastfeeding at discharge is strongly correlated with breastfeeding establishment in women without diabetes. 63 However early use of formula to manage neonatal hypoglycaemia is common in infants of mothers with pregestational diabetes. This may result in formula use at discharge that does not reflect ultimate breastfeeding outcome. 17

Studies were also small. For women with type 1 diabetes, the median study size was 30 participants, and for women with type 2 diabetes, the median size was 40 participants. These studies are too small to detect modest differences in breastfeeding prevalence between diabetic and normoglycaemic controls and between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Fifty participants per group would be required to see a difference in breastfeeding prevalence of 15%, and very few studies had this number of participants.

Few studies were prospective and few studies – whether prospective or retrospective – controlled for confounding by comparing breastfeeding prevalence or duration in women with pregestational diabetes to appropriately matched controls. Few studies adequately controlled for the many confounding variables relevant to type 1 and type 2 diabetes perinatal care. Most studies could only consider a few confounding variables, due to small size, though even larger studies (500+ participants) did not include all variables known to affect breastfeeding prevalence and duration. Larger studies also tended to be retrospective and to combine type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes data. Indeed, whether large or small, very few studies specifically compared clearly delineated type 1 and type 2 groups, despite these diseases having very different pathogenesis, and thus likely different impact on breastfeeding.

Some studies were unrepresentative of the general population. For example, the only large prospective study assessing breastfeeding in type 2 diabetes, the PANDORA study, was in rural Australia with a large number of Indigenous women. 18 Whilst an important study, this population does not reflect national or global populations.

Substantial heterogeneity precluded metanalysis, especially as few studies had adequate normoglycaemic controls and breastfeeding prevalence measurements occurred at differing timepoints and by different methods, making it difficult to combine data in an appropriate way.

For the outlined reasons, it is difficult to draw conclusions regarding breastfeeding prevalence and duration in women with pregestational diabetes. It appears that breastfeeding in women with type 1 diabetes is lower than for women without diabetes. However, this may not necessarily relate to type 1 diabetes itself, but to other factors associated with lower breastfeeding rates. For women with type 2 diabetes, there are too few studies to draw conclusions. Historically most women with pregestational diabetes had type 1 diabetes, but women with pregestational diabetes are now more likely to have type 2 diabetes, due to increasing obesity and older maternal age. 64 Considering the importance of breastfeeding for infant nutrition and maternal metabolic benefits, there is a startling dearth of knowledge in this area.

Our findings suggest large, adequately controlled studies are urgently required to characterise contemporary breastfeeding behaviours in women with pregestational diabetes, particularly type 2 diabetes, and the variables contributing to breastfeeding continuation. If diabetes itself does not independently affect breastfeeding it is important to identify which variables are most strongly associated with breastfeeding in women with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, to improve peripartum management to mitigate these risks if needed.

CONCLUSION

Breastfeeding prevalence and duration may be reduced in women with type 1 diabetes, but there is insufficient evidence to comment on breastfeeding prevalence in type 2 diabetes. Variables associated with reduced breastfeeding prevalence are similar to those described in the general population, but may be more common in women with pregestational diabetes. Given the evidence of long‐term metabolic benefit of lactation in women with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and the rapidly rising rates of diabetes in women of reproductive age, particularly type 2 diabetes, 64 this area of research is an important priority. Failure to identify opportunities to increase breastfeeding in this population represents a missed opportunity to improve maternal and infant health outcomes.
