Sugar profile, phenolic compounds, and antioxidant capacity of the most preferred commercial fruit juices in a sample of school-age children: a combined laboratory and survey-based study
Authors: Damla Unlu, Merve Ozdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESThis study evaluated the commercial fruit juice consumption habits and laboratory-analyzed nutritional quality (sugar profile, phenolics, antioxidant capacity) in school-age children (6–12 yrs) relative to the body mass index (BMI) z-score and dietary Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity (ORAC) density.SUBJECTS/METHODSThis cross-sectional study with 183 parents evaluated the dietary intake and dietary ORAC density using the Food Frequency Questionnaire and BEBIS 9.0. Juice sugar profiles (HPLC), total phenolics, and antioxidant capacity (ABTS assay) were measured spectrophotometrically.RESULTSEnergy intake was negatively correlated with the dietary ORAC density (r = –0.284, P < 0.001). Unadjusted bivariate analysis showed that every 1 mL increase in daily fruit juice intake was associated with a 0.389 g increase in total sugar intake (B = 0.389, P < 0.001). This association lost its statistical significance after adjusting for the daily total energy intake, age, sex, and maternal education level (B = 0.055, β = 0.081, P = 0.093, ΔR2 = 0.004). The mean daily intake was 53.3 mL/day (99.5% adherence to AAP guidelines), showing no correlation with the BMI z-scores (r = –0.030, P = 0.683) or a significant difference across BMI categories (P = 0.335). Laboratory analyses of the children’s most consumed commercial juices revealed total sugar concentrations of 20.2–21.4 g/100 mL.CONCLUSIONDespite commercial fruit juices having a high sugar concentration, consuming them in small amounts may limit the total sugar intake. Provided portion control is maintained, 100% fruit juice should be evaluated as a nutrient-dense, polyphenol-rich matrix rather than as mere free sugar. Public health strategies should prioritize portion management, food literacy, and clear front-of-pack labeling (such as Traffic Light and Nutri-Score) to help consumers distinguish 100% juices from added-sugar nectars rather than eliminating fruit juices from pediatric diets.

INTRODUCTION

Childhood obesity continues to be a significant public health challenge globally [1], and sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption is considered one of the primary drivers of this condition [23]. Commercial fruit juices, with a sugar content (ranging from 5 to 17 g per 100 mL) generally resembling that of SSBs [4], present a serious “nutritional paradox,” despite being traditionally perceived as a healthy source of nutrients such as vitamin C and potassium [5]. Compared to whole fruits, they contain less dietary fiber and vitamin C, but they still retain a high concentration of polyphenols [67]. Despite their natural image, the absorption rate of liquid sugars from the gastrointestinal tract is less effective in inducing satiety than whole fruits; this situation can lead to excessive energy intake and high body mass index (BMI) z-scores in children [8].

The literature presents conflicting evidence on this matter. While some studies associate fruit juice consumption with increased dietary quality and antioxidant intake [910], others emphasize that these beverages are a concentrated source of free sugars that replace healthier options like water or whole fruit, linking them to weight gain and type 2 diabetes [1112]. Nevertheless, there are also meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials showing that no relationship exists between 100% fruit juice consumption and weight gain or obesity [131415]. In addition, an epidemiological study evaluating SSB consumption and cardiovascular disease risk factors reported that after a 17-yrs follow-up of children aged 9–16 yrs, SSB consumption was associated with hypertension in boys, but no association was reported for fruit juice or diet soda [16].

According to the definition of the European Union Fruit Juice Directive [17], fruit juice cannot contain added sugar, preservatives, colorants, or flavorings. Nevertheless, the lack of a globally unified consensus regarding its natural sugar content causes some dietary guidelines to classify fruit juice under the fruit category, while others place it in the SSB category [18]. This results in inconsistencies in public policies [719]. The contradictions among the recommendations provided by major health authorities, such as the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the UK’s Eatwell Guide, and the Nutrition Committee of the European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN), complicate the interpretation of scientific evidence regarding whether fruit juice should be treated as a form of processed fruit or an SSB [20212223].

Although fruit juice consumption is controversial in terms of quantity in the literature, the role of parameters such as the nutritional quality, sugar profile, and antioxidant capacity of the consumed beverage on metabolic outcomes in children is unclear [24]. In particular, studies investigating the relationship between phytochemical differences among commercial 100% fruit juices and fruit nectars on the market and children’s daily nutrient intake and BMI z–scores remain limited. Given the variability in dietary guidelines and commercial product formulations, analyzing these beverages at the laboratory level has become necessary. This study evaluated the “healthfulness” of commercial fruit juices consumed by school-age children (aged 6–12 yrs) on a scientific basis by revealing their nutrient density through laboratory data and providing concrete data to aid dietary recommendations in school-age nutrition.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between July and December 2024 in Türkiye. The study population consisted of employees from 3 different factories who had children aged 6–12 yrs. The participants were selected using a convenience sampling method based on voluntary participation. The required sample size was determined using G*Power software (v3.1.9.4). The calculated minimum sample size was 134 based on a power (1 − β) of 0.95 and a significance level (α) of 0.05. The target was adjusted to 147 to account for a potential 10% attrition rate. During recruitment, 219 individuals were reached. After data quality control, 36 participants were excluded based on the following pre-established exclusion criteria: 1) providing incomplete fruit juice consumption frequency or type data despite completing the general dietary assessment (n = 15); 2) missing portion sizes or quantitative volume details for foods and beverages that prevented dietary software entry (n = 13); and 3) completely blank or physiologically implausible dietary records (n = 8). Consequently, the final cross-sectional analysis included 183 parents (Fig. 1).

Ethical statement

The Başkent University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee approved the study protocol (Date: June 26, 2024; Project No: KA24/193; Decision No: 24/135). All participants were informed of the objectives, and written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection.

Dietary assessment and anthropometric measurements

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire. Socio-demographic and health information, including the child’s age, sex, and family socioeconomic status, was recorded. For the dietary assessment, a semi-quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire comprising 77 food and beverage items grouped into nine main categories (meat and meat products, legumes, milk and dairy products, selected vegetables, fresh and dried fruits, bread and cereals, fats/oils, sugars and sweets, nuts and seeds, and beverages) based on an eight-point frequency scale (ranging from ‘per meal’ to ‘never’), covering a retrospective one-month reference period, was administered. The instrument was adapted from the validated Turkish National Nutrition Survey guidelines, and the authors intentionally expanded the beverage section to distinguish between 100% commercial fruit juices and specific fruit nectar variants (e.g., peach, apricot, and cherry nectars). An open-ended section was incorporated to identify the specific commercial fruit juice brands and exact product variants most frequently consumed by the children. This purpose-designed adaptation provided high matrix resolution while avoiding the excessive respondent burden typical of exhaustive dietary inventories. A panel of 3 expert pediatric nutritionists verified the content and face validity of this adapted instrument independently before implementation.

Regarding portion size estimation and quantification, parents reported habitual amounts (g/mL) for each food and beverage item based on standardized household measures (e.g., standard cups and glasses) and specific commercial packaging volumes (e.g., 200 mL Tetra Pak units). All reported frequencies and portion sizes were systematically converted into daily milliliter or gram equivalents (mL/day or g/day). These standardized daily intake values were then processed using the Nutrition Information System software (BEBIS 9.0, Stuttgart, Germany) to determine the daily nutrient and total antioxidant intakes. Within the reporting modules of the software, the dietary antioxidant parameters were evaluated and presented under the Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity (ORAC) nomenclature, using the reference values integrated from the comprehensive 3,100 foods antioxidant database developed by Carlsen et al. [25].

The age- and sex-specific z-scores for weight-for-age, height-for-age, and BMI-for-age were calculated using WHO Anthro Plus software, based on the WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study classifications.

Laboratory analysis of fruit juices

The commercial fruit juice samples selected for laboratory analysis were driven directly by the survey outcomes to ensure ecological validity and reflect real-world exposure. Specifically, the 4 analyzed products (two 100% mixed fruit juices and 2 peach nectars) were identified from Food Frequency Questionnaire data as the top 4 most frequently and systematically consumed commercial beverage variants among the participating children, collectively accounting for the vast majority of the packaged juice exposure of the cohort. The brand names were kept anonymous and coded (e.g., Brand A or Brand B).

Sugar component analysis

Individual sugar components, specifically sucrose, glucose, and fructose, were quantified using a Shimadzu LC-20AT HPLC system (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a Refractive Index Detector. The fruit juice samples (peach and mixed juices) were diluted with ultra-pure water and filtered through a 0.45 μm PTFE membrane filter prior to injection. The separation was performed on a specialized carbohydrate column, and identification was based on a comparison of the retention times with authentic standards. The results are expressed as g/100 mL of juice.

Total phenolic content and total antioxidant capacity (ABTS)

The samples were first centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 20 min using an Eppendorf 5810 R centrifuge (Hamburg, Germany) to obtain a clear supernatant. The total phenolic content and total antioxidant capacity were measured using a PerkinElmer Lambda 25 UV/Vis Spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Total phenolic content: Determined spectrophotometrically at 765 nm using the Folin–Ciocalteu method. The results were calculated from a gallic acid standard curve and are expressed as the mg gallic acid equivalent/L (mg GAE/L).

Total antioxidant capacity (ABTS): Determined spectrophotometrically using the ABTS [2,2'-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)] radical scavenging assay. The results were calculated using the calibration curves derived from Trolox standards and consistently expressed as µmol Trolox equivalent/L (µmol TE/L).

All analyses were performed in technical triplicates to ensure reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The descriptive statistics are expressed as the frequencies and percentages [n (%)] for the categorical variables and as the m ± SD for the continuous variables. The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests alongside visual inspections of histograms and Q–Q plots. The group comparisons for normally distributed continuous variables were conducted using an Independent-Samples t-test. The linear relationships between the continuous variables were evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficients. A hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the independent association between daily fruit juice consumption and the total daily sugar intake while controlling for potential confounders. Model 1 included the baseline covariates (total energy intake, age, sex, and maternal education level), and Model 2 introduced the daily commercial fruit juice intake (mL/day). The multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with values < 2.5 considered acceptable. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Fruit juice consumption patterns and adherence to recommended limits

The daily dietary patterns and the energy contribution of macronutrient sources were examined. BEBIS analysis showed that 13.4%, 2.2%, and 0.5% of the daily total energy intake was derived from whole fruits, commercial fruit juices, and other SSBs or soft drinks, respectively. This low energy contribution of commercial fruit juices aligns with the children’s high adherence to portion size limits. According to the study results, the vast majority of the children (99.5%, n = 182) consumed fruit juice below the daily portion limits recommended by the AAP, with an average consumption of 53.3 mL (less than a traditional teacup) (Table 3). When evaluated across BMI categories, the daily fruit juice consumption averaged 67.1 ± 20.4 mL for normal-weight children and 71.0 ± 32.3 mL for overweight and obese children. An independent-samples t-test showed no significant difference in the mean consumption levels between the BMI categories (P = 0.335) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The mean daily commercial fruit juice intake among school-age children was 53.3 mL, reflecting the high rate of adherence (99.5%) to the upper limits established by the AAP (118–177 mL/day for ages 4–6 and ≤ 236 mL/day for ages 7–12) [21]. Although Western population-based surveillance datasets, such as National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) III, historically reported substantially higher daily intakes (210–240 mL/day) with a considerable proportion of children exceeding health guidelines [26], the present findings revealed a markedly lower volumetric consumption pattern. Far from being an isolated sample-specific finding, this lower volume aligns with the global beverage mapping reported by Singh et al. [27], which highlighted pronounced regional heterogeneity and lower standardized fruit juice intakes across Middle Eastern and Southern European clusters compared to North America. Choi and colleagues in the US reported that the primary factors associated with how often caregivers/parents give their children fruit-flavored SSBs were a “misperception that the product is healthy” and marketing claims on the packaging such as “Source of Vitamin C” and “Convenience of box/pouch packaging.” National data from the Ministry of Health Guide to Reducing Sugar Consumption in Türkiye and national nutrition surveys show that when purchasing packaged products, parents in Türkiye first look at the “Best Before Date” (40.6%) and “Price/Brand Reliability” (55.5%) criteria; the nutritional value or health claims were secondary priorities [2829]. In addition, although fruit juice has become a staple of children’s breakfasts and daily hydration in Western societies, children’s fluid intake in Türkiye is traditionally centered around water, tea/herbal tea, or milk/ayran [2628]. The fact that children access fruit juice not as a “daily primary fluid source” but as an “occasional/specific snack” may provide a cultural-behavioral explanation for the significantly lower average consumption volume (53.3 mL) in the study group compared to Western cohorts such as NHANES [26]. When benchmarked against national surveillance data, the observed daily intake prevalence parallels the consumption profile reported in the Türkiye Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative Report [30]. According to national data, 14.6% of children nationwide consume freshly squeezed fruit juice every day, whereas this rate was found to be 10.9% in the present study. Similarly, although the daily consumption rate of commercial fruit juice nationwide is 10.4%, this rate was determined to be 14.8% in the present sample.

Importantly, when evaluating the body weight status across BMI categories, an independent-samples t-test showed no statistically significant difference in the mean daily fruit juice intake between normal-weight children and overweight/obese children (67.1 ± 20.4 mL/day vs. 71.0 ± 32.3 mL/day, respectively; P = 0.335). Hence, within the observed portion-controlled intake range, the fruit juice volume did not differ across BMI categories. These findings closely mirror the comprehensive meta-analysis conducted by Auerbach et al. [31], who showed that the routine consumption of 100% fruit juice within the recommended limits does not lead to clinically significant weight gain or adverse changes in body composition among school-aged children. This finding is also supported by cross-sectional baseline data from the Growing Up Today Study cohort [32]. Consequently, the low consumption levels observed in the present study group (an average of 53.3 mL/day) likely explain the absence of a direct association between the consumption of commercial fruit juice and children’s BMI z-scores.

Despite the lack of an association with anthropometric measures, a significant positive correlation was identified between the daily fruit juice intake (mL) and the daily total sugar intake (g) (r = 0.572, P < 0.001), highlighting that fruit juice is a notable contributor to the overall dietary sugar load in children. A notable finding of this study relates to the nuanced relationship between fruit juice consumption, total sugar intake, and anthropometric outcomes. Although unadjusted bivariate analysis initially suggested a positive association between fruit juice intake and total sugar load (B = 0.389, P < 0.001, R2 = 0.327), this association lost statistical significance after adjusting for the total daily energy intake, age, sex, and maternal education level (B = 0.055, β = 0.081, P = 0.093, ΔR2= 0.004). This attenuation suggests that commercial fruit juice intake was not independently associated with the total dietary sugar intake once the total energy intake and demographic covariates were accounted for in the model.

Although this finding may appear, at first glance, to contrast with studies establishing direct links between sugary beverage consumption and metabolic risks [3334], it suggests that the relationship between moderate fruit juice intake and overall sugar load should be evaluated within the broader context of the total dietary intake. Further studies will be needed to clarify how the physiological impact of fruit juice on children’s daily sugar intake compares to that of SSBs. From a biological perspective, liquid calories generally do not elicit the same degree of satiety as solid foods, so that they can contribute to higher total daily energy intake [8]. Consequently, the independent contribution of portion-controlled fruit juice becomes statistically non-significant when the total energy intake is controlled for.

Therefore, this study highlights the importance of considering the impact of specific beverage choices on diet quality (such as dietary ORAC density) when formulating pediatric nutritional recommendations by integrating laboratory-based beverage profile analyses with overall dietary intake patterns—rather than evaluating individual products in isolation [24].

Quantitative laboratory measurements indicated distinct phytochemical differences between the beverage matrices, with the independent-samples t-tests confirming that 100% mixed fruit juices possessed significantly higher total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity compared to fruit nectars (P < 0.001). In contrast, peach nectars exhibited an approximately threefold higher sucrose content and 57% lower antioxidant capacity (P < 0.001). This phenomenon can be explained by the “fruit juice matrix” theory proposed by Hutchins and Ruxton [7]. The matrix is a biochemical and structural entity representing the potential of vitamins, minerals, and polyphenols found within a food structure to modulate sugar metabolism and glycemic responses [7]. These researchers show that 100% fruit juices are not merely simple sugar solutions, but complex biochemical matrices containing components that distinguish them from SSBs. In the present laboratory analyses, this nutrient-dense phytochemical matrix was evident in the 100% fruit juices. In contrast, in nectar formulations, the diluted fruit content and added sucrose substantially diminished this bioactive profile, shifting their chemical characteristics closer to those of conventional SSBs. Mavadiya et al. [19] reported that there is an urgent need for consumer guidelines to distinguish 100% fruit juices clearly from other fruit-flavored beverages in this context.

The negative correlation observed between the total energy intake and dietary ORAC density (r = −0.284, P < 0.001) indicates a dietary shift where children with higher energy intakes tend to consume foods with lower antioxidant capacities. This shows that evaluating isolated foods or food groups is insufficient to capture complex dietary habits, as metabolic outcomes are driven by intricate dietary patterns [24]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding moderate intake thresholds (≈100 mL/day) [3]—alongside the higher antioxidant capacity revealed in the present laboratory analyses—suggests that incorporating portion-controlled 100% fruit juice could favorably contribute to the overall diet quality and antioxidant intake in specific populations, particularly those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds [35].

Within the present study, school-age children consumed commercial fruit juice in strictly portion-controlled amounts (averaging 53.3 mL/day, with 99.5% adherence to AAP guidelines) [21]. In line with the longitudinal observations from the Framingham Children’s Study, maintaining such moderate, controlled exposure during childhood may foster favorable dietary habits without promoting excessive energy intake [36]. Wan et al. [36] reported that children who consume controlled amounts of fruit juice are 3.8 times more likely to consume whole fruits in later childhood, suggesting that the appropriate fruit juice intake may serve as a dietary bridge toward healthy long-term eating behaviors. This portion-controlled intake pattern must also be interpreted through the socioeconomic lens of the present sample, which comprised children of factory workers predominantly representing working-class and lower-to-middle socioeconomic backgrounds. Families with lower socioeconomic status are often more price-sensitive and may systematically substitute more expensive whole fruits or 100% commercial fruit juices with lower-cost, sugar-added fruit nectars. Consequently, targeted food literacy programs and clear front-of-pack labeling are urgently needed to protect these pediatric groups from excessive sugar exposure and the associated potential metabolic risks. Specifically, implementing standardized Front-of-Pack Labeling systems, such as the Traffic Light system or Nutri-Score, could serve as practical public health tools to help consumers immediately visually distinguish between nutrient-dense 100% fruit juices and sugar-added fruit nectars at the point of purchase [373839].

Although public health initiatives such as the National Salt and Sugar Reduction Initiative target a voluntary 40% sugar reduction in packaged beverages [40], the inability of the general public to distinguish between 100% fruit juices and sugar-added nectars remains a major challenge for food literacy. Specifically, the Ministry of Health’s ‘Guide to Reducing Sugar Consumption in Türkiye (2021)’ established a target of a 10% voluntary sugar reduction across packaged foods and beverages by 2025. Nevertheless, the present laboratory data indicate that these industry reformulation goals have not yet effectively translated into retail product formulations because commercial fruit juices and nectars still exhibit a substantial sugar load (20.2–21.4 g/100 mL). Consequently, children remain exposed to a high portion-based sugar concentration, even under low volumetric exposure. Therefore, monitoring beverage-derived sugar intake during childhood represents an important window for establishing healthy dietary patterns and mitigating potential dietary risks [41]. Although the ESPGHAN guidelines [23] recommend strict limitations on all sugar-containing beverages, the present antioxidant and polyphenol findings suggest a more balanced perspective. Rather than completely banishing fruit juices from pediatric diets, promoting the portion-controlled consumption of nutrient-dense 100% fruit juices within established AAP limits provides a realistic, balanced, and practical public health strategy.

This study had certain methodological limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the data on the children’s dietary intake and anthropometric measurements (weight and height) were based on parental self-reports. Regarding dietary data, parental self-reports may introduce recall bias, the underreporting of unhealthy foods, or the overreporting of healthy options. Similarly, relying on the parent-reported height and weight introduces a potential source of information or reporting bias in pediatric obesity research, which could affect the precision of BMI z-score classifications. Nevertheless, parent-reported anthropometric data in this pediatric age group have been validated extensively in the literature, showing strong correlations (weight r ≥ 0.87, height r ≥ 0.82) with actual physical measurements [424344].

Second, although the laboratory analyses targeted the 4 products most frequently consumed by the children in the study sample, the chemical profiling process was limited to a specific subset of commercially available beverages (n = 4 products). This may not fully reflect the market-wide diversity of all commercial fruit products. Despite these limitations, a major strength of this study is that it analyzed the volume of fruit juice consumed, the specific sugar profiles, and the antioxidant capacities of these beverages, providing laboratory data to determine the dietary nutrient density. By integrating individual-level dietary survey metrics with laboratory-level biochemical analysis, this dual-methodological framework provides a comprehensive evaluation of the volumetric intake and chemical quality. Moreover, focusing on the 6–12 age group, a critical developmental period during which dietary habits are actively shaped and become difficult to alter later in life, offers practical insights for public health policies, the importance of food literacy for parents, and pediatric nutrition guidelines.

CONCLUSION

Commercial fruit juice consumption among the school-age children evaluated in this study was low (mean 53.3 mL/day) and characterized by a high adherence (99.5%) to AAP portion guidelines, showing no significant differences across BMI categories. Laboratory analyses confirmed the distinct chemical profiles across beverage matrices. The 100% commercial fruit juices had substantially higher phenolic content and antioxidant capacity, characterized by naturally occurring sugars, whereas fruit nectars contained markedly lower antioxidant capacities and higher sucrose concentrations. Furthermore, the dietary ORAC density was inversely correlated with the total energy intake, suggesting that the overall diet quality decreases as the daily caloric intake rises. Provided that strict portion control is maintained within established AAP limits, 100% fruit juice can provide dietary polyphenols and antioxidants, distinguishing it from sugar-added beverages. Nevertheless, future longitudinal and intervention studies evaluating diverse food matrices will be needed to understand the broader metabolic implications of liquid sugar sources in pediatric populations.
