Best Evidence Summary for Volume Management of Patients With Heart and Kidney Comorbidity
Authors: Huang Aoli, Chen Xia, Zhu Xujing, Yang Qiaozhen, Yang Yeyao, Feng Jiehui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo summarise the best evidence of volume management of patients with coexisting cardiac and renal disorders.DesignAn evidence summary, a form of rapid review.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in guideline websites, professional association websites and six databases included Web of Science, Embase, CIHAHL, PubMed, CNKI, SinoMed from their inception to 1 April 2024 on volume management for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. We used the standard that the Fudan University Center for Evidence‐based Nursing set based on the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI) methods of generating an evidence summary, which includes problem establishment, literature retrieval, literature screening, literature evaluation, evidence summary and grading and the formation of practice suggestions. Two researchers independently carried out the screening, quality appraisal, classification and extraction of the included literature and evidence.ResultsThis study selected 36 articles, including two systematic reviews, six randomised controlled trials, four quasi‐experimental studies, six cohort studies, four cross‐sectional studies, six expert consensus documents and eight guidelines. A total of 37 evidence items were summarised, covering assessment of volume management, strategies of volume management, education, selection of dialysis modality, patients' preferences and cooperation between multidisciplinary teams and patients.ConclusionCultural and regional differences should be considered in volume management of patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. Future research should focus on investigating the impact of various volume management strategies—including dialysis‐related education for healthcare providers, dialysis modalities, initiation timing, dialysis dose, treatment duration, patient‐reported outcomes and interdisciplinary collaboration—on volume‐related indicators and symptoms of volume overload in patients.Keywords: cardiac volume, cardio‐renal syndrome, comorbidity, evidence‐based nursing, heart failure, renal insufficiency

Introduction

The heart and kidney regulate cardiac output, volume status and vascular tone to maintain hemodynamic stability (Lim and Kim 2021). The bidirectional abnormalities of heart and kidney functions can be influenced by the relationship between these two organs, in which one organ may induce or worsen disease in the other (Lim and Kim 2021). The global prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) was 9.1%, affecting 697.5 million people in 2017 (GBD 2020). Additionally, the global prevalence of end‐stage renal disease was 0.07% (GBD 2020), accounting for 6.3% of the Medicare budget (Assimon et al. 2016). From 1990 to 2017, the mortality of CKD patients increased by 41.5% worldwide, which was mainly associated with diabetes and impaired kidney dysfunction (GBD 2020). In end‐stage renal disease patients, approximately 30% have chronic heart failure on initiation of dialysis and mainly died from cardiovascular causes (Uduman 2018). Additionally, renal dysfunction plays an important role in the progression of cardiovascular disease (Uduman 2018). Cardiovascular disease (CVD) caused by CKD or impaired kidney function accounted for 7.6% of global CVD deaths in 2017 (GBD 2020). Approximately one‐fifth of patients with acute heart disease have experienced acute kidney injury (AKI) or renal dysfunction during the course of the disease, and AKI has been proven to be associated with a five‐fold increased risk of death and prolonged length of stay (Jentze et al. 2020). Renal dysfunction is also an independent risk factor for morbidity and mortality of heart failure patients (Uduman 2018).

The features of heart failure reflect a syndrome characterised by the renal retention of sodium and water with resulting intravascular and interstitial fluid volume expansion and redistribution. The kidney acts as an early responder to the myocardial dysfunction and resulting the reduction in effective circulating blood volume, which further promote renal sodium and water retention (Wayne 2016). In the United States and Europe, there are over 1 million annual hospitalisations for heart failure, more than 90% of which are due to symptoms and signs of fluid overload (FO) (Ambrosy et al. 2014). FO is associated with worse outcomes, such as acute kidney dysfunction in heart failure patients (Costanzo et al. 2017) and is independently associated with increased mortality in patients after cardiac surgery (Anja et al. 2017). In addition, AKI patients with volume overload also experience complications such as cognitive dysfunction and malnutrition (Dogukan et al. 2009; Canaud et al. 2022). The mortality risk of AKI patients with FO is 1.38 times higher than patients without FO (Jhee et al. 2020). Meanwhile, for every 1% increase in overhydration/extracellular water, the all‐cause mortality or cardiovascular morbidity of CKD patients increased by 1.08 times (Tsai et al. 2015).

For patients with heart and kidney comorbidity, healthcare providers face challenges in balancing various risk factors, clinical factors, comorbid conditions and patient preferences to develop personalised volume management strategies for maintaining volume balance, due to the complexity of their comorbid conditions. The volume management of patients with heart and kidney disease presents several issues: a lack of awareness regarding volume management among both healthcare providers and patients; insufficient communication between healthcare providers and patients during the management process; disparities in dialysis medical resources between different countries and regions; a lack of standardised criteria for dialysis indications and modalities in volume management; and inadequate attention given to symptoms self‐reported by patients (Chan et al. 2019; Flythe et al. 2020). We have to address the complexity of kidney and heart diseases and decision‐making discrepancy such as patients with CKD and heart failure often present with similar symptoms such as fatigue, shortness of breath and swelling in the legs or ankles due to fluid overload. However, despite these overlapping signs, the underlying causes differ: CKD‐related symptoms stem from impaired waste and fluid excretion, while heart failure symptoms arise from the heart's inability to pump blood effectively. This similarity can complicate diagnosis and treatment, especially when considering diuretics, as both conditions require careful fluid balance but carry different risks with over‐ or under‐diuresis. With these existing challenges in volume management for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity, it is imperative to systematically retrieve, synthesise and summarise the available evidence, thereby establishing a clinical foundation for addressing the aforementioned issues.

In an information‐driven society, obtaining authentic and reliable evidence to guide clinical decision‐making has become the biggest challenge faced by practitioners (Cheng et al. 2018). The aim of an evidence summary is to transform original studies, guidelines and expert consensus into evidence that is reliable, comprehensive, concise, easy to understand and easy to disseminate, so that it can help clinical practices to improve patient outcomes (Jordan et al. 2018). There are some studies on volume management of patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. However, there is a lack of evidence summary on this topic to provide references for healthcare professionals in addressing existing issues in volume management.

Methods

We used standard that the Fudan University Center for Evidence‐based Nursing set based on the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI) methods of generating an evidence summary, which includes problem establishment, literature retrieval, literature screening, literature evaluation, evidence summary and grading, the formation of practice suggestions (Munn et al. 2015). This study has been registered in the Fudan University Center for Evidence‐based Nursing; The registered number was ES20245614.

Problem Establishment

For the recommendation that the Fudan University Center for Evidence‐based Nursing proposed, we used the PIPOST model to establish the problem (Zhu et al. 2020). (i) Population (P): patients with coexisting cardiac and renal disorders necessitating volume management (including cardio‐renal syndrome). Heart disease: for cardiac disorders requiring volume management, common examples include cardiac decompensation and heart failure. Renal diseases: for renal disorders, typical instances involve renal insufficiency, renal failure and patients on dialysis therapy. (ii) Intervention (I): measures related to volume management including assessment of risk factors of volume overload or depletion, specific intervention measures of it, education project for relevant patients (iii) Professional (P): healthcare workers (iv) Outcome (O): objective volume relevant outcomes such as blood volume, output, weight, hemodynamic indicators, electrolyte level, subjective outcomes such as patients' self‐reported symptoms, self‐management level and volume management compliance (v) Setting (S): specialist wards or ICU (vi) Type of evidence (T): systematic reviews and meta‐analysis, randomised controlled trials, quasi‐experimental studies, cohort studies, cross‐sectional studies, guidelines, expert consensuses.

Evidence Retrieval

According to ‘6S’ pyramid model, we searched Web of Science, Embase, CIHAHL, PubMed, CNKI, SinoMed six databases and Guidelines International Network (GIN), National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE), BMJ Best practice, the Registered Nurses Association of Ontario (RNAO), National Guideline Clearinghouse (NGC), the American heart association website. The search included literature in English or Chinese published from the inception of each database up to April 1, 2024. In addition, hand searching of list references and citation tracking was used to find extra articles.

Based on the research theme of volume management in patients with heart and kidney comorbidity, we have categorised the search terms into five key blocks: cardiac volume, kidney disease (such as renal insufficiency, renal failure, kidney injury and dialysis, etc.), heart disease (such as heart decompensation and heart failure, etc.), cardiorenal syndrome and comorbidities. We performed a comprehensive search using a combination of medical subject headings (MESH) and free‐text terms across all five blocks. Due to the extensive number of search terms, the MESH and free‐text terms for each block, along with the detailed search strategies employed in the three major databases, have been provided in the Supporting Documents.

Study Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

(i) Study population was adult with coexisting cardiac and renal disorders (including cardio‐renal syndrome); (ii) interest of intervention focused on practice and risk assessment of volume management; (iii) literature types were systematic reviews and meta‐analyses, randomised controlled trials, quasi‐experimental studies, cohort studies, cross‐sectional studies, guidelines, expert consensus; (iv) English and Chinese literature.

Exclusion Criteria

(i) Duplicate, plan, translation, conference, letter, case, editorial and abstracts‐only literature; (ii) guideline interpretations; (iii) full text is not visible; (iv) failed quality evaluation.

Literature Screening

Two researchers independently screened all included studies. H.A. conducted initial searching and removed duplicates using ENDNOTE. Then titles and abstracts were reviewed during the initial screening. Z.X. reviewed full texts for re‐screening. Discussions about the literature were conducted with a third researcher when there was disagreement in the evaluation of included studies. When a third researcher could not make a decision, we conducted group discussions to reach a consensus.

Quality Appraisal

Two researchers (Y.Q. and Y.Y.) were trained in systematic evidence‐based methodologies and independently evaluated studies quality. A third researcher was invited to discuss when disagreement occurred. The JBI Evidence‐Based Evaluation Criteria was applied to evaluate systematic reviews and meta‐analysis, randomised controlled trials, quasi‐experimental studies, cohort studies, cross‐sectional studies, expert consensus (Aromataris et al. 2024). We research team derived the scoring thresholds through extensive consultation and by reference to established practices in the existing literature (Martin et al. 2020). Assigned a value: yes = 1; no or not applicable = 0. Studies were assigned to three quality tiers (high, medium, low) defined by their total score: high for > 2/3, medium for > 1/3 to ≤ 2/3 and low for ≤ 1/3. The 2017 version of the appraisal of guidelines for research and evaluation II (AGREE II) evaluation tool was adopted to evaluate guidelines. Each item is scored from 1 to 7 points: 1 is strongly disagree and 7 is strongly agree. All six domains with scores ≥ 60% are A‐level recommendations. Guidelines with scores ≥ 30% in at least three domains but < 60% are B‐level recommendations. Guidelines with scores < 30% in at least three domains are C‐level recommendations and were excluded (AGREE Next Steps Consortium 2017).

Evidence Extraction and Summary

A structured approach was implemented wherein: (1) literature categorisation was performed using ENDNOTE; (2) volume management‐related contents were extracted from included literature through evidence extraction forms; and (3) synchronous tabulation was conducted for systematic classification and synthesis of the evidentiary materials. Two researchers (H.A. and C.X.) who were trained in evidence‐based methodologies conducted the extraction, classification and translation of included literature and evidences independently, one of whom had over 10 years of work experience. The third investigator was assigned the role of adjudicating conflicts and resolving questions that arose during the process. Additionally, this investigator was responsible for verifying and reviewing the evidence that had been extracted, categorised and summarised by the other two researchers. The researchers should read the included literature one by one, then respectively extract the content and sources of evidence according to the original text and summarise the evidence according to the topic. The sources should be marked, and the theme setting should focus on the key links in practice (Xing et al. 2020). The following principles were followed for evidence integration: (i) when evidence from different sources contradicted each other, the high‐level, high‐quality and newer evidence were adopted; (ii) when the contents from multiple sources were the same or complementary, a single piece of combined evidence was formed. The 2014 version of the JBI Evidence Level System was applied to classify evidence into levels 1 to 5 (The Joanna Briggs Institute Levels of Evidence and Grades of Recommendation Working Party 2014).

Results

Search Results

A total of 9891 articles were identified through the search. Of these, 4544 duplicates were removed using ENDNOTE. After screening titles and abstracts, 202 articles remained for full‐text screening. During full‐text screening, 127 articles did not address the phenomenon of interest, 11 articles did not report the result of interest, 28 articles were of low quality and finally 36 articles were included in this study. There were two systematic reviews (Horowitz et al. 2023; Scotland et al. 2018), six randomised controlled trials (Al‐Hwiesh et al. 2019; Griva et al. 2018; Lanqing et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2020; Qing et al. 2023; Zhuanfang et al. 2024), four quasi‐experimental studies (Aşci et al. 2006; Ronco et al. 2001; Usui et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2018), six cohort studies (Booth et al. 2010; Feng et al. 2023; Hong et al. 2019; Maarek et al. 2017; Valle et al. 2011; Trezzi et al. 2013), four cross‐sectional studies (El‐Shehaby et al. 2011; Cader et al. 2014; Caiping et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022), six expert consensuses (Chan et al. 2019; Flythe et al. 2020; Rong et al. 2018; Mehrotra et al. 2023; Xuxi et al. 2023; Expert Group of Chinese Society of Nephrology 2024) and eight guidelines (Blake et al. 2011; Cardiorenal Syndrome Guidelines Working Group of Chinese Nephrologist Association of Chinese Medical Doctor Association 2023; Emergency Medicine Branch of China Association for the Promotion of International Exchange in Healthcare et al. 2022; Ezekowitz et al. 2017; Heidenreich et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2015; Woodrow et al. 2017). Quality Evaluation of Systematic Reviews

Two systematic reviews were included in this study (Horowitz et al. 2023; Scotland et al. 2018). Both were conducted within the past five years. For the fourth item, which assessed whether the sources and resources used to search for studies were adequate and the ninth item, which assessed whether the likelihood of publication bias was assessed, both items were rated as ‘no’. The remaining items were rated as ‘yes’. Therefore, both systematic reviews received a total score of 9, which exceeds two‐thirds of the maximum score and were therefore rated as high quality and included.

Discussion

Literature Quality Appraisal

The JBI Evidence‐Based Evaluation Criteria was applied to evaluate the included literature. Two included systematic reviews were rated as high quality. A consistent limitation identified across them was the inadequate sources and resources used to search for studies, as well as a lack of description regarding the retrieval of grey literature. Furthermore, neither review employed statistical methods to assess publication bias or its potential impact on the results. According to our criteria, six RCTs were rated as high quality despite shared shortcomings in allocation concealment and blinding—a common methodological concern in all but one study (Griva et al. 2018). Those RCTs failed to provide specific details regarding allocation concealment or the implementation of blinding procedures. Consequently, we were unable to make definitive judgments on these methodological aspects and could only classify these studies as having a high risk of selection bias. This limitation necessitates cautious interpretation of the reliability of their subjective outcomes. Nevertheless, they provided comprehensive descriptions and demonstrated proper implementation in areas including randomisation, outcome measurement, follow‐up reporting and intervention delivery. These aspects represent commendable methodological strengths. All four quasi‐experimental studies were rated as high quality, with a common limitation identified as the lack of repeated measures of study outcomes. This methodological gap may introduce potential biases, including the inability to verify and account for natural temporal trends, as well as unstable estimates of the effects of volume management interventions in patients with heart and kidney comorbidities. Future research should focus on conducting high‐quality randomised controlled trials to address this limitation. All six cohort studies and four cross‐sectional studies were rated as high quality, demonstrating robust methodologies in outcome measurement and follow‐up procedures, which effectively controlled for measurement and attrition bias. However, a common limitation was observed in the identification and control of confounding variables, as most studies failed to adequately address potential confounders or describe strategies to mitigate their influence. This methodological gap may introduce some heterogeneity in the reported findings across studies. The five consensus documents were rated as medium‐to‐high quality. The primary limitations identified include the lack of description regarding the specific processes for evidence retrieval and synthesis, as well as failure to report potential competing interests. These omissions may introduce associated biases into the findings. AGREE II evaluation tool was adopted to evaluate the quality of guidelines. Among the eight guidelines, one was rated as A, while the remaining seven received B. The identified limitations primarily include: the failure to incorporate perspectives or preferences of the target population in the participant composition; the absence of descriptions regarding barriers or facilitators to the implementation of recommended measures; and insufficient disclosure of competing interests in the editorial independence section. These shortcomings may compromise the applicability assessment of the guidelines and potentially introduce bias.

Volume Assessment

Evidence 1–9 summarises the assessment of volume management for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. On this topic, the largest body of evidence supports a recommendation of dynamically evaluating the volume status based on the clinical symptoms and signs of patients, combined with the use of relevant laboratory indicators and imaging examinations such as BNP, NT‐proBNP, carbohydrate antigen 125, chest X‐ray and cardiovascular ultrasound. These evidences are derived from a cohort study (Booth et al. 2010) expert consensus (Flythe et al. 2020) and guidelines (Heidenreich et al. 2022; Cardiorenal Syndrome Guidelines Working Group of Chinese Nephrologist Association of Chinese Medical Doctor Association 2023; Chinese Society of Nephrology et al. 2022; Ezekowitz et al. 2017). NT‐proBNP has been widely used in the screening, diagnosis and differential diagnosis of heart failure. CA125 has found to be elevated in chronic HF patients with pericardial, pleural and peritoneal effusions. This elevation is not only as a consequence of mesothelial cells irritation by serosal effusion but, perhaps, by mesothelial cell interaction with inflammatory mediators present in HF. Besides, serum albumin and plasma adrenal medulla are impact factors for cardiac dysfunction and volume overload founded by some cohort studies and cross‐sectional studies (Yu et al. 2022; Caiping et al. 2020; Cader et al. 2014; El‐Shehaby et al. 2011). In volume overload, the patient's gastrointestinal mucosa becomes edematous, which impairs protein absorption and exacerbates hypoalbuminemia. Volume overload leads to left ventricular dysfunction through an increase in blood pressure, left ventricular hypertrophy and left ventricular dilatation. Plasma adrenomedullin levels were increased in left ventricular failure, myocardial infarction and peripheral arterial occlusive disease. Thus, the concomitant cardiovascular disease in haemodialysis patients may have led to cardiovascular dysfunction, resulting in increased plasma adrenomedullin level (El‐Shehaby et al. 2011).

In addition, evidence supporting the importance of monitoring weight and blood pressure in patients with heart and kidney disease to prevent volume overload is also cited from multiple sources, including cross‐sectional study, expert consensus and guideline (Caiping et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022; Ezekowitz et al. 2017; Xuxi et al. 2023). Weight monitoring is one of the simplest methods to assess volume load, and any rapid weight gain (i.e., a sudden increase of more than 2 kg within 3 days) should prompt immediate medical attention (Ezekowitz et al. 2017; Xuxi et al. 2023). Blood pressure has been proven to be a risk factor for dialysis patients with volume overload in two cross‐sectional studies (Caiping et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022). Peridialytic blood pressure measurements have clinical importance for assessing and managing hemodynamic stability during dialysis session. Clinical practitioners can combine these biomarkers with patients' symptoms in clinical practice to achieve early identification of volume overload.

A cohort study showed that the fluorescent dye dilution technique can be applied to patients receiving haemodialysis with simple instrumentation and minimal changes to their conventional treatment (Maarek et al. 2017). It uses the fluorescence of a small bolus of indocyanine green (ICG) dye injected into the haemodialysis circuit to non‐invasively estimate cardiac output and blood volume based on indicator dilution principles. The dye clears completely from the blood in < 10 min (half‐life < 3 min), allowing for a comprehensive description of cardiac hemodynamics during haemodialysis treatment. Analysis of the ICG curves yielded the central blood volume, which can help interpret changes in cardiac function during haemodialysis in terms of preload changes (Maarek et al. 2017). However, large‐scale, multi‐centre randomised controlled trials are needed to thoroughly explore its effectiveness in volume management for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity.

Strategies of Volume Management

Evidence 10–19 summarises volume management strategies for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. In this theme, the largest body of evidence supports the use of BIVA and lung ultrasonography. BIVA is a non‐invasive method for volume detection. Two systematic reviews indicated that bioimpedance‐guided volume management was associated with reduced all‐cause mortality, decreased overhydration, improved blood pressure and increased cost‐effectiveness among dialysis patients (Horowitz et al. 2023; Scotland et al. 2018). Additionally, some cohort studies (Hong et al. 2019; Valle et al. 2011) showed that combining bioimpedance with clinical evaluation of biochemical indicators contributes to maintaining fluid balance. Combining BIVA with clinical evaluation of biochemical indicators is recommended to maintain fluid balance.

Two cohort studies conducted by Feng et al. (2023) and Trezzi et al. (2013) were found that ultrasound performed at the bedside can detect lung water and intravascular overload and their reduction after dialysis in yet asymptomatic patients with using same outcome indicators, such as number of B‐lines, the diameter of the inferior vena cava and weight. A quasi‐randomised controlled trial (Usui et al. 2022) found that low‐intensity discontinuous exercise at less than the anaerobic threshold (AT), compared with high‐intensity discontinuous exercise above the AT, was shown not to induce changes in blood volume while increasing the removal of small molecules such as potassium, phosphate and urea nitrogen during haemodialysis exercise. The exercise was started 60 min after the start of dialysis, and 20 min of exercise was performed thrice at 10‐min intervals (first, second and third time, 60–80, 90–110 and 120–140 min, respectively) for a total of 60 min. However, a notable limitation of this study is its small sample size of only 31. Future research should validate these findings in larger cohorts of patients with heart and kidney comorbidity.

Sodium and fluid restriction is a critical component of daily volume management for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. It is recommended that peritoneal dialysis patients with heart failure should limit their daily salt intake to < 2 g of sodium or 5 g of sodium chloride, and patients with volume overload should limit their daily sodium intake to < 1.5 g in a quasi‐experimental and guidelines. Recording input and output is the basis of volume assessment for patients with heart and kidney disease. Maintaining a balance of intake and output is safe, provided there is no rapid increase in body mass or worsening of heart failure symptoms/signs in the short term. Nevertheless, for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity, there are no exact unified limit standards for input and output to maintain volume balance in guidelines or expert consensus, which represents a knowledge gap we have identified. The 2017 Canadian cardiovascular society guidelines for the management of heart failure suggested that restriction of daily fluid intake to approximately 2 L/day should be considered for patients with fluid retention or congestion that is not easily controlled with diuretics (Ezekowitz et al. 2017). However, the Chinese nursing expert consensus on volume management in patients with chronic heart failure suggested the following: for normal volume status, a fluid intake of 1.5–2 L/day; for volume overload, a fluid intake of 1–1.5 L/day; and for volume depletion, an increase in fluid intake based on normal drinking, with attention to sodium supplementation (Xuxi et al. 2023). It is necessary to address the lack of unified standards for intake and output restrictions in patients with heart and kidney comorbidity.

Education

Evidence 20–22 summarises the content of education for healthcare workers and patients. It is an indispensable component of volume management. In this theme, the largest body of evidence supports the education of healthcare workers and patients about volume management. A Chinese expert consensus on the nursing practice of ultrafiltration therapy for heart failure recommended that medical staff involved in dialysis should grasp the relevant knowledge of cardiac dysfunction, skills in managing dialysis access and team collaboration in emergency situations to handle adverse events of volume imbalance (Rong et al. 2018). The skills in managing dialysis access include routine maintenance of arteriovenous fistula, arteriovenous graft and central venous catheter; preparation of central venous catheter before dialysis (checking for patency); and measures to prevent blood clotting in the circuit during dialysis. In a meanwhile, it was also advised that patients should be fully prepared before dialysis, including receiving relevant knowledge education delivered by experienced members of a multidisciplinary team in a global expert consensus about dialysis patients with kidney disease (Chan et al. 2019) and a British renal association clinical practice guideline on peritoneal dialysis in adults (Woodrow et al. 2017). However, there is a lack of high‐quality interventional research on health education related to dialysis for healthcare professionals to explore its effect on FO, including specific volume‐related indicators. Several randomised controlled studies have indicated that targeted education on volume management related to home monitoring indicators (weight and blood pressure), symptom recognition (edema, dyspnea, chest distress, shortness of breath) and basic coping skills (salt and water restriction, weight control) for patients with heart and kidney disease could improve patients' dry weight and volume self‐management levels (Griva et al. 2018; Lanqing et al. 2023; Qing et al. 2023; Zhuanfang et al. 2024). These four studies, conducted in China and Singapore, employed similar research designs wherein the control groups received usual care while the intervention groups received targeted health education interventions focused on volume management. Nurses can implement the above targeted education to engage patients in volume management, thereby making the management process more efficient.

Selection of Dialysis Modality

Evidence 23–32 summarises the selection of dialysis modality. It should be based on the patients' condition, hemodynamic indicators and existing comorbidity. It is recommended to consider continuous renal replacement therapy for AKI patients with unstable hemodynamics, as stated in an expert consensus (Expert Group of Chinese Society of Nephrology 2024). FO is one of the important indicators for initiating CRRT. However, due to its high operational difficulty, it is more challenging for medical staff to initiate CRRT. In addition, there is a lack of exploration regarding the effects of different initiation timings, modalities, haemodialysis volume and duration of CRRT on outcomes in patients with heart and kidney comorbidity.

Early ultrafiltration also plays a critical role in the management of volume overload in patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. In a quasi‐experimental study, Ronco et al. 2001 randomly assigned 22 heart failure patients with fluid overload and acute renal failure to two different treatment schedules: (1) a 4‐h acute ultrafiltration session (UF) and (2) 24‐h slow continuous ultrafiltration (SCUF). Both treatments were monitored with continuous arterial blood pressure recording and continuous blood volume measurement. The results indicated that slow continuous ultrafiltration is more beneficial for stabilising intradialytic blood pressure than intermittent ultrafiltration (Hu et al. 2020). In another randomised controlled trial, Hu et al. (2020) randomly assigned 100 acute decompensated heart failure patients into early ultrafiltration or torasemide plus tolvaptan groups. The primary results showed that early ultrafiltration was superior to diuretics in promoting weight loss and increasing fluid output.

For patients with insufficient ultrafiltration or difficult‐to‐control volume, it is suggested to choose peritoneal dialysis combined with haemodialysis to prevent cardiac congestion in a guideline for the management of chronic heart failure in dialysis patients in China (Chinese Society of Nephrology et al. 2022). In addition, in a prospective randomised study, 88 patients with type 1 acute cardiorenal syndrome were randomly assigned into a strategy of ultrafiltration therapy or tidal peritoneal dialysis. The results proved that tidal peritoneal dialysis was beneficial for preserving cardiac and renal function and promoting fluid loss when compared with ultrafiltration. Clinical practitioners could choose a suitable dialysis modality for patients based on this evidence. Nevertheless, there remains a need for high‐quality experimental studies to compare the effects of peritoneal dialysis with CRRT on specific volume‐related indicators, clinical symptoms and signs, patient complaints and self‐reported outcome.

Patients' Preference

Evidence 33–35 summarises patients' preferences in volume management. A global expert consensus (Mehrotra et al. 2023) of managing the symptom burden associated with maintenance dialysis concluded that patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) play a vital role in identifying patient‐prioritised symptoms. They may enhance the patient‐provider relationship, improve communication and support shared decision‐making. However, symptoms are seldom formally assessed on a frequent basis and patients describe underreporting their symptoms (Flythe et al. 2018). For medical staff, it is essential to not only engage with patients and assess their symptoms but also to integrate these insights into a comprehensive clinical assessment that is accessible to both the patient and the multidisciplinary team, extending beyond cardiology and nephrology. However, limited data exist showing that PROMs improve patient outcomes or facilitate volume management. This gap necessitates further high‐quality experimental studies.

It is suggested to incorporate patients' preferences and local medical resources into dialysis prescription, as stated in a global expert consensus of blood pressure and volume management in dialysis (Flythe et al. 2020). The heterogeneity of the dialysis population with heart and kidney comorbidity, such as treatment setting (in‐centre vs. home therapies, medication use, etc.), must be considered when prescribing dialysis. Besides, since all other health conditions are relative contraindications for dialysis, it is recommended for clinical practitioners to inform patients and caregivers of the challenges, considerations and trade‐offs of the different dialysis modalities so that dialysis selection can be tailored to their preferences, health and social circumstances (Chan et al. 2019). Given the scarcity of evidence, it remains difficult to provide robust objective evidence demonstrating improvements in patient outcomes, cost‐effectiveness or healthcare resource utilisation from integrating patient preferences and local resources into volume management.

Cooperation

Evidence 36–37 summarises cooperation in volume management. On this topic, the largest body of evidence supports the multidisciplinary collaboration in volume management. The heart and kidneys regulate cardiac output, volume status and vascular tone to maintain hemodynamic stability. Given its complexity and the need for comprehensive and precise volume management, a multidisciplinary team involving nursing, medical, pharmaceutical, cardiac and renal healthcare workers is required, as described in several expert consensus documents and guidelines (Ezekowitz et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2015; Chan et al. 2019; Heidenreich et al. 2022; Mehrotra et al. 2023). Multidisciplinary team follow‐up was associated with reduced HF hospitalisations, all‐cause hospitalisations and mortality (Heidenreich et al. 2022). Cardiac or renal specialists need not be the sole authority for prescribing. The initiation and discontinuation of medications can be shared by a multidisciplinary team to comprehensively address the risks of polypharmacy in patients with cardiorenal comorbidities. However, more high‐quality, multiteam collaborative research on volume management is needed for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity to further explore its efficacy and cost‐effectiveness.

Evidence Application

Evidence application is a systematic and complex process that involves all aspects of evidence‐based practice. Therefore, a conceptual framework or theoretical model (e.g., The Knowledge to Action Framework, The Ottawa Model of Research Use, The Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Service Framework) is needed as a guide to promote the effective and ethical transformation of evidence and to formulate scientific change strategies and appropriate outcome evaluation indicators.

Preparations for evidence application should be conducted across three levels: the system level, the practitioner level and the patient level (Zhang et al. 2020). At the system level, develop context‐appropriate volume management protocols and institutional guidelines based on the synthesised best evidence for volume management in patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. Refine management processes and establish a quality control system. At the practitioner level, form a multidisciplinary team dedicated to volume management in patients with heart and kidney comorbidity. Conduct targeted training programs and foster effective collaboration among team members to facilitate evidence implementation. At the patient level, implement comprehensive health education initiatives. Following formal evidence implementation, regularly monitor the evidence application process, provide periodic feedback to investigators and develop tailored intervention strategies to promote evidence application. This iterative cycle continues until positive clinical outcomes are achieved.

Limitations

First, our study only included literature published in English and Chinese, so that we did not search literature in other languages. Second, we did not include unpublished studies and some other published studies may be missed in our retrieval, so there may still be a risk of publication bias. Third, the evidence of volume management of patients with heart and kidney comorbidity should periodically update in the future to provide medical staff latest reference. Fourth, methodological limitations in the included studies may introduce potential risks of publication and selection bias into the evidence base. Future research should address this gap by implementing more rigorous methodological designs.

Conclusion

This study extracted a total of 37 best evidence items on volume management for patients with heart and kidney comorbidity, covering six themes: assessment of volume management, strategies of volume management, education, selection of dialysis modality, patients' preferences and cooperation between multidisciplinary teams and patients. Since we only included literature published in English and Chinese, cultural and regional differences should be taken into consideration in clinical application. Future high‐quality research should focus on investigating the impact of various volume management strategies—including dialysis‐related education for healthcare providers, CRRT modalities, initiation timing, dialysis dose, treatment duration, patient‐reported outcomes and interdisciplinary collaboration—on volume‐related indicators and symptoms of volume overload in patients.
