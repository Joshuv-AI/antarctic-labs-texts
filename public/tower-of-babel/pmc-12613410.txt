Enhancement of CAR-T cell efficacy and persistence via CD30 costimulation and NF-κB signaling
Authors: Yang Su, Guang Yang, Bi Chen, Yiguan Qian, Wenjie Ma, Xiaotian Jiang, Qing Yu, Yang Li, Luwei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-redirected T lymphocytes have achieved unprecedented clinical responses in hematologic neoplasms, their role in solid tumors still needs to be improved. To overcome current limitations, we rationally designed a next-generation CAR-T cell construct incorporating autonomous costimulatory signaling pathways that operate independently of tumor antigen engagement, thus faithfully emulating physiological T cell activation paradigms. We screened seven costimulatory receptors from the TNF receptor superfamily and identified that CD30 is the most effective enhancer of CAR-T cell function. Subsequent structural analysis revealed that the intracellular domain (ICD) of CD30 is primarily responsible for its costimulatory activity. Our data showed that these CAR-T cells co-expressing CD30 ICD have stronger proliferation ability and cytokine secretion function. The results of in vitro experiments showed that CD30 signaling improved the cytotoxicity of CAR-T cells and reduced the expression of exhaustion-related markers. In mouse models of orthotopic renal cancer, lung metastasis, and hematologic malignancies, these CAR-T cells showed better expansion capability and superior antitumor activity. Sequencing results suggest that CD30 signaling may enhance the function of CAR-T cells by increasing the activation of the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway. Further mechanistic experiments confirmed that the NF-κB pathway is significantly activated in CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells compared to CAIX CAR-T cells. Reversal experiments demonstrated that NF-κB pathway inhibitors can reverse the effector function of CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells, while CAIX-IKKβ CAR-T cells with self-activated NF-κB pathway also exhibit significant functional advantages. By integrating autonomous costimulatory signaling, we demonstrated improved CAR-T cell persistence, proliferation, and antitumor activity across multiple preclinical models, highlighting the therapeutic potential of this approach for both hematologic malignancies and solid tumors.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02446-z.Keywords: Tumor immunology, Immunotherapy, CAR-T, CD30, NF-κB pathway

Introduction

As central mediators of adaptive anti-tumor immunity, T lymphocytes encounter profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironments in solid malignancies, which significantly suppress their cytotoxic activity and effector potential [1, 2]. While various immunotherapeutic approaches have been developed to rejuvenate endogenous anti-tumor T cell responses, the large-scale generation of functionally competent, tumor-specific T lymphocytes capable of mediating potent tumor regression continues to pose substantial clinical obstacles [3]. Currently, alternative methods have been developed to modify tumor-specific T cells in vitro, and a large number of tumor-reactive T cells have been adoptively reinfused into patients, which has demonstrated positive results in clinical settings [4]. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered T lymphocytes have emerged as a cornerstone cellular immunotherapy, functioning through ex vivo expansion of tumor-specific T-cell clones for precise neoplastic targeting [5]. Canonical 2nd-generation CAR constructs comprise of an antigen-binding single-chain variable fragment (scFv) extracellular unit connected via hinge region to a transmembrane anchor, intracellularly coupled with tandem signaling motifs, the primary CD3ζ activation domain, and a secondary costimulatory domain (CSD) [6, 7].

As an essential functional module, the costimulatory domain constitutes a defining feature of 2nd-generation CAR architectures. Among various available options, the co-stimulatory domains originating from 4-1BB (TNFRSF9) and CD28 (T-cell-specific surface glycoprotein CD28) have emerged as the most extensively characterized and clinically validated molecular components [8, 9]. Consistent with prior studies, CD28-costimulated CAR-T cells (CD28-CAR-T) products exhibit potent short-term tumoricidal effects, but are constrained by their transient functional durability and diminished survival kinetics in physiological microenvironments [8, 10]. CAR-T cells incorporating the 4-1BB costimulatory domain (4-1BB-CAR-T) demonstrate modest initial cytotoxic potential but superior resistance to T-cell exhaustion [11]. Although CD28- and 4-1BB-co-stimulated CAR-T constructs achieve durable responses in hematologic malignancies, their therapeutic efficacy becomes significantly compromised within immunosuppressive solid tumor niches [12, 13]. The clinical application of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors confronts multiple established biological limitations. These include well-documented challenges such as T-cell exhaustion mechanisms, suboptimal persistence of engineered lymphocytes, inefficient tumor infiltration by adoptively transferred cells, and functional suppression within immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments (TME) [13–15]. Preclinical and clinical studies have investigated tandem integration of CD28 and 4-1BB costimulatory domains in CAR-T constructs to potentiate solid tumor responses. Despite these engineering efforts, objective response rates remain below clinically meaningful thresholds in most solid malignancies [16, 17]. Therefore, improving the function of CAR-T based on costimulatory molecules is crucial for the future treatment of solid tumors.

Co-stimulatory signaling cascades serve as essential regulators of T lymphocyte clonal expansion and effector differentiation [18]. T cells of different stages and subtypes utilize different costimulatory signals [19]. The tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) superfamily constitutes critical immunomodulatory elements, including TNFR2, 4-1BB, CD30, HVEM, CD27, and CD40 [20]. These receptors orchestrate immune responses through TRAF protein-mediated assembly of signaling complexes, principally activating nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) transcriptional networks [21, 22]. CD30, a type I transmembrane protein within the TNFR superfamily, functions as a co-stimulatory immune checkpoint molecule that modulates T cell receptor (TCR) signaling thresholds and effector differentiation [20, 23], especially in the process of CD4+ T cell differentiation and cytokine secretion [24, 25]. At pathological expression levels, overexpression of CD30 leads to self-aggregation, initiating TRAF-dependent IκB kinase (IKK) phosphorylation cascades that drive NF-κB nuclear translocation independent of CD30L engagement [26]. These findings suggest that the CD30 ICD (CD30 intracellular domain) encodes structural determinants essential for initiating NF-κB pathway activation through ligand-independent mechanisms. In natural T cells, TNFR superfamily-derived costimulation is physically independent of the antigen recognition structure, whereas in CAR-T cells, these structures are coupled with it. In this study, we employed P2A to express costimulatory signaling proteins in tandem, aiming to explore their impact on CAR-T cell functionality independent of antigen-mediated activation.

We engineered a CAR-T construct incorporating a CD28 domain and an intracellular domain of constitutively expressed costimulatory receptors. Clinically approved CARs incorporate a CD28-derived costimulatory domain that is spatially juxtaposed with the CD3ζ signaling module within the T cell receptor (TCR) complex, enabling simultaneous activation upon antigen engagement [27]. For another co-stimulatory domain in CD28-CAR-T, TNFR superfamily members are commonly selected [28–30]. Through systematic screening of seven TNFR superfamily co-stimulatory receptors, we identified that integrating CD30 into clinically prevalent CD28-based CAR architectures significantly enhanced CAR-T functionality. Through further structural optimization, we found that the CD30 ICD is the critical functional domain to enhance the function of CAR-T cells. In our engineered CAIX.CD30(ICD) construct, the CD30 intracellular domain was incorporated into a second-generation CAR targeting carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX). The CAIX.CD30(ICD) structure includes the transmembrane region of CD30 but does not include the extracellular segment. Compared to conventional CAIX CAR-T cells utilizing CD28 costimulation alone, the CAIX.CD30(ICD) variant demonstrated enhanced proliferative capacity, elevated cytokine secretion, improved tumoricidal activity, and reduced exhaustion markers. In orthotopic renal carcinoma models, the modified CAR-T cells exhibited prolonged persistence and superior antitumor efficacy. These findings suggest that strategic integration of co-stimulatory domains may provide a viable approach to enhance CAR-T cell functionality against solid tumors.

Methods

Cell culture

The human renal carcinoma cell line OS-RC-2 and the gastric adenocarcinoma cell line N87 were procured commercially from Pricella (Wuhan, China). Both lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, maintained at 37 °C under 5% CO₂.

To generate luciferase-expressing OS-RC-2 cells, parental cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding luciferase and selected via puromycin resistance. Stable OS-RC-2 Luc⁺ cells were propagated under identical culture conditions as the parental line, with bioluminescence imaging routinely performed to confirm luciferase expression.

Human T lymphocytes were expanded in X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% FBS and 200 IU/mL recombinant human interleukin-2 (rhIL-2; PrimeGene, Cat. #101-02). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, with cell density and viability quantified using a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) per manufacturer guidelines.

CAR-T formulation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from healthy donors via density gradient centrifugation (Lymphoprep™, StemCell Technologies, #07851/07861) were cryopreserved in vapor-phase liquid nitrogen for subsequent CAR-T manufacturing. Post-thaw recovery involved activation with anti-CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Dynabeads™, Thermo Fisher, #11132D) at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio. At 24 h post-stimulation, T lymphocytes were transduced with γ-retroviral vectors encoding CAIX-specific CAR constructs incorporating distinct costimulatory domains. Magnetic bead-based stimulation was discontinued on day 5 by physical bead removal, followed by ex vivo expansion in IL-2-supplemented X-VIVO 15 medium (200 IU/mL) for 5 additional days. Final cellular products underwent quality control verification prior to functional evaluation through standardized in vitro cytotoxicity assays or preclinical in vivo modeling. The scFv sequence against CAIX was from the antibody G250 [31], and the HER2-specific scFv sequence, FRP5, was from a previous literature report [32]. The positive rate of CAR-T cells was detected using PE G4S antibody (38907, CST, Shanghai, China) or APC G4S antibody (68718, CST, Shanghai, China). G4S is an antibody for the linker part of the CAR construct.

ELISA

CAR-T cells underwent three repetitive cycles of in vitro antigen exposure to recombinant CAIX protein (48 h/cycle). The concentration of recombinant protein used is 1ug/mL. Following each exposure phase, cells were harvested via centrifugation, quantified via trypan blue exclusion, and re-challenged with CAIX at equivalent effector cell densities to maintain consistent stimulation pressure. After the three-round co-incubation, the culture medium in the co-culture wells was collected. Before detection, the supernatant was diluted 5-fold. Human ELISA kits (Dakewe Biotech Co., Ltd., China) were used to detect cytokines in the supernatant. The HIL2-E96.1 kit was used to detect IL-2, and the RD-IFN-γ-Hu kit was used to detect IFN-γ. The detection operations were performed according to the instructions of the kit manufacturers, and the absorbance was measured using a Cytation3 (BioTek Instruments, Inc., USA). A standard curve was drawn based on the standards provided in the kit, and the cytokine concentration was calculated by multiplying the dilution factor.

Flow cytometry

For surface marker analysis, cultured CAR-T cells or isolated mouse PBMCs were resuspended as single-cell suspensions and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting specific surface markers. Appropriate isotype-matched antibodies served as negative controls for the gating strategy establishment. Immunophenotypic profiling was executed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), with acquired datasets subjected to computational interrogation via FlowJo software v10 (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA). To enable absolute T cell quantification, 5 µL of fluorescent counting beads (Catalog #424902, BioLegend, Shanghai, China) were incorporated into each sample. The absolute T cell count was calculated using the following calculation: Total T cells = (Counting bead volume × Bead concentration × Acquired T cell events × Dilution factor) / Acquired bead events. The antibody panel included the following validated reagents from BioLegend (Shanghai, China): anti-CD8 (980902, BioLegend), anti-Granzyme B (515405, BioLegend), anti-Perforin (308117, BioLegend), anti-PD-1 (329917, BioLegend), and anti-CD3 (981012, BioLegend).

Multi-round co-culture killing model

Tumor cells were plated in 24-well plates at 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/well 24 h prior to T cell addition, with three duplicate wells per group. After overnight adherence, the tumor cells in one of the duplicate wells were trypsinized and counted. Subsequently, CAR-T cells were co-cultured with tumor cells in the other two duplicate wells at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 1:5. We selected the E: T ratio of 1:5 specifically because we did not want CAR-T cells to rapidly clear the tumor. Instead, we aimed to observe reduced CAR-T cell exhaustion and enhanced effector persistence under sustained tumor antigen stimulation. In the co-culture system, we used IL-2-free X-VIVO 15 medium (Lonza). On day 5 of co-culture, cells from one replicate well were harvested, and residual CD3⁺ T cells and EGFP⁺ tumor cells were quantified by flow cytometry. The T cells in the other replicate well were collected, divided equally into two parts, and added to the wells pre-seeded with 2.5 × 10⁵ tumor cells one day in advance. Five days later, the cells in one of the wells were collected, and the number of residual CD3⁺ T cells and EGFP⁺ tumor cells was detected by flow cytometry. The T cells in the other well were used for the next round of co-culture. When CAR-T cells were collected for the next round of co-culture, cytokines and tumor debris in the medium were removed via centrifugation, and the CAR-T cells were resuspended in fresh medium. In this process, we did not perform a cell count of the CAR-T cells. The above steps were repeated until the T cells lost their tumor-killing ability, and then the experiment was terminated.

Carcinoma in situ

Male immunodeficient NCG mice (6-week-old) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. and housed under standard feeding conditions. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with protocols approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Huaian No.1 People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. DW-P-2024-038-01). To establish an orthotopic renal cancer model, 1 × 10⁶ OS-RC-2Luc⁺ cells were injected under the renal capsule of NCG mice. Seven days post-implantation, mice were randomized into three treatment groups (n = 5/group): untransduced T cells (control), CAIX-targeted CAR-T cells, and CAIX.CD30(ICD)-engineered CAR-T cells. Each group received 4 × 10⁶ corresponding CAR-T cells via tail vein injection. Tumor progression was longitudinally monitored using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) through bioluminescence detection. Peripheral blood samples were collected on days 11 and 18 post-treatment for flow cytometric quantification of circulating CAR-T cells. Survival outcomes were recorded until study termination at day 20. After the mice died, the kidney tissues were collected and photographed. The small intestine, lung, liver, brain, spleen, heart, and stomach tissues of the mice were obtained for hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining.

To establish a pulmonary metastasis model, 1 × 10⁶ OS-RC-2Luc⁺ cells were intravenously inoculated into NCG mice via tail vein injection. Five days post-inoculation, animals were randomized into three experimental cohorts (n = 5/group): (1) Untransduced T cell control, (2) CAIX-targeted CAR-T cells, and (3) CAIX.CD30(ICD)-engineered CAR-T cells. Each cohort received 4 × 10⁶ respective CAR-T cells through the same administration route. Longitudinal tumor progression was quantitatively monitored via in vivo bioluminescence imaging using an IVIS platform.

For the hematologic malignancy model, 1 × 10⁶ Nalm6Luc⁺ cells were intravenously engrafted into NCG mice via tail vein injection. Five days post-engraftment, animals were randomized into three experimental cohorts (n = 5/group): (1) Untransduced T cell control, (2) CD19-targeted CAR-T cells, and (3) CD19.CD30(ICD)-engineered CAR-T cells. Each cohort received 4 × 10⁶ respective CAR-T cells through the same administration route. Longitudinal tumor progression was quantitatively monitored via in vivo bioluminescence imaging using an IVIS platform.

RNA sequencing

CAIX and CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells from three donors were co-cultured with OS-RC-2 cells at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 1:5 in IL-2-free X-VIVO 15 medium (Lonza) using 10 cm culture dishes for defined durations (24 h/72 h). Cells were stained with anti-human AF488 anti-CD3 antibody (Product No. 300454, BioLegend, Shanghai, China) and sorted using a FACS Aria III flow cytometer (BD, NJ, USA). The sorted cells, along with two baseline CAR-T cell groups prior to co-culture, were subjected to transcriptomic profiling via RNA sequencing conducted by Novogene Corporation (Beijing, China).

Blockade of the NF-κB signaling pathway

Prior to initiating multi-round cytotoxicity assays, CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells underwent 1-hour preconditioning in serum-free medium containing 20 µM NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai). CAR-T effector cells were subsequently introduced into tumor-preloaded wells at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 1:5. To maintain sustained pathway inhibition, BAY 11-7082 was replenished in the culture medium at 24-hour intervals throughout the experimental duration.

Western blot

CAR-T cells and OS-RC-2 cells were co-cultured in IL-2 free X-VIVO 15 medium at a ratio of 1:5 (E: T) in 10 cm culture dishes for 72 h. Cells were stained with anti-human AF488 anti-CD3 antibody (Product No. 300454, BioLegend, Shanghai, China) and sorted using a FACS Aria III flow cytometer (BD, NJ, USA). Isolated CAR-T cells (post-co-culture) and pre-co-culture CAR-T cells were lysed in lysis buffer. After centrifugation to clarify the lysate, supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA kit (Beyotime, Nantong, China), with 10 µg protein loaded for gel electrophoresis and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Beyotime, Nantong, China). Membranes were blocked, incubated overnight with primary antibody at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at room temperature, and imaged using a Tanon 4600 chemiluminescence system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). The protein expression levels were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (Ashland, OR, USA). Information on the antibodies used in the immunoblotting assay is displayed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Flow cytometry data were processed with FlowJo software (version 10.9.0), while in vivo imaging data were analyzed using IndiGo software. For comparisons between two experimental groups, either the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test or the two-tailed Student’s t-test was selected based on data distribution characteristics. Multiple group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. Experimental designs involving two independent variables were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Survival analyses were performed with the Kaplan-Meier method, and statistical differences between survival curves were determined by the log-rank test. In all analyses, a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of CD30 co-expression as a functional enhancer of CAR-T cells

In current clinical CAR designs, costimulatory signaling cascades are co-activated with CAR-derived signals following antigen engagement. This contrasts with physiological T cell activation, where costimulatory pathways operate in parallel with canonical TCR signaling. To investigate whether CAR-T cell efficacy could be improved through antigen-independent costimulation, we conducted a systematic screening of seven costimulatory receptor candidates for their functional enhancement potential.

We constructed a retroviral vector encoding the following modular components in sequential order: (i) a humanized scFv targeting CAIX, (ii) the hinge and transmembrane domains derived from CD8α, (iii) an intracellular signaling module consisting of tandem CD28 costimulatory and CD3ζ activation domains, (iv) a self-cleaving P2A peptide (ATNFSLLKQAGDVEENPGP), followed by (v) one of seven alternative full-length costimulatory receptors (4-1BB, OX40, CD27, CD40, HVEM, GITR, or CD30) (Fig. 1A). P2A-based polycistronic protein expression is mediated through ribosomal skipping [33]. The fused DNA is transcribed into a full-length messenger RNA (mRNA) through classical transcription mechanisms. During subsequent translation, the CAIX polypeptide is cleaved between glycine and proline residues in the P2A region, separating it from the specified co-stimulatory receptor. This process generates two independent proteins: the CAIX CAR and the full-length (FL) costimulatory receptor. We transduced human T cells with these retroviral vectors and compared their proliferative capacity in vitro cell culture. We observed significantly enhanced growth of CAIX.CD30 CAR-T cells compared to control CAIX (FL) CAR-T cells (Fig. 1B). As a result, we focused our characterization on CAR-T cells containing CD30.

Following three rounds of in vitro co-incubation with CAIX protein, CAIX (FL) CAR-T cells exhibited significantly enhanced proliferative capacity compared to baseline controls (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, antigen-specific stimulation induced elevated secretion of effector cytokines in CAIX (FL) CAR-T cells, including interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (Fig. 1D) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) (Fig. 1E), relative to conventional CAIX CAR-T cells.

Co-expression of the intracellular domain of CD30 is central to enhancing the function of CAR-T cells

To further clarify the functional domain of the CD30 molecule, we constructed a costimulatory receptor containing only the intracellular domain of CD30 (CD30 ICD), and a costimulatory receptor containing only the TRAF-binding site in the CD30 intracellular region (CD30 ICD-TBD) (Fig. 2A-B). Comparative analysis of donor-derived CAR-T cells revealed higher CAR⁺ cell frequencies in CAIX constructs than in CAIX.CD30 counterparts, with CAR⁺ proportions demonstrating an inverse correlation with CD30 costimulatory domain length (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 2). This might be because the shorter the CAR plasmid is, the higher its expression efficiency in T cells. We introduced these retroviruses into human T cells via transduction and subsequently assessed the comparative proliferation rates of the transduced T cells in culture. Following retroviral transduction of human T cells, comparative proliferation analysis revealed significantly enhanced growth kinetics in CAIX.CD30 and CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells relative to control CAIX CAR-T cells, whereas CAIX.CD30(ICD-TBD) variants exhibited no significant proliferative improvement (Fig. 2D).

Through three rounds of in vitro co-incubation with CAIX protein, we observed that CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells exhibited significantly higher expression levels of the cytotoxic markers granzyme B (GB) and perforin (Fig. 2E), along with reduced expression of the exhaustion marker PD-1, LAG-3, and Tim-3 (Fig. 2F), compared to control CAIX CAR-T cells. CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells demonstrated significantly enhanced proliferative capacity compared to control CAIX CAR-T cells (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, upon antigen-specific stimulation with CAIX, these modified cells secreted elevated levels of IFN-γ (Fig. 2H) and IL-2 (Fig. 2I) relative to the control group. Whereas CAIX.CD30(ICD-TBD) did not exhibit the same advantages as CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells (Fig. 2E-I). Therefore, we focused on the characterization of CAR-T cells containing CD30 ICD.

To access tonic signaling without antigen, we detected the pCD3ζ, pZAP70, pERK, pAKT, basal IL-2 or IFN-γ secretion in CAIX CAR-T and CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). The results indicate that there is no significant difference in the basal activation state between CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells and CAIX CAR-T cells under antigen-free conditions.

CAR-T cells co-expressing CD30 ICD exhibited stronger effector functions against target cells in vitro

The cytotoxic efficacy of CAIX-CAR-T cells was evaluated in vitro using CAIX-positive human renal carcinoma OS-RC-2 cells. Through repeated co-culture killing assay cycles (Fig. 3A) conducted at an effector-to-target (E: T) ratio of 1:5, CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells demonstrated significantly enhanced tumor-lytic activity compared to both non-targeting CD19 CAR-T controls and conventional CAIX CAR-T cells (Fig. 3B-C, Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, the superior in vitro performance of CAR-T cells co-expressing CD30 ICD was also confirmed using HER2 CAR-T targeting erb-b2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (HER2) in the human gastric cancer cell line N87 (Fig. 3D-E, Supplementary Fig. 5). The gating strategies of repeated co-culture killing assays were showed in Supplementary Fig. 6. In addition, we verified the specificity of CAIX CAR-T cells in MDA-MB-231 cells that do not express the CAIX antigen (Supplementary Fig. 7A-C). Moreover, we used CAIX CAR-T and CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells to target PANC-1 cells with low expression of the CAIX antigen (Supplementary Fig. 7A). The results confirmed that CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells still had a killing advantage in cells with low CAIX expression (Supplementary Fig. 7D-E). These findings demonstrate that the intracellular costimulatory domain of CD30 enhances the cytolytic activity of CAR-T cells in vitro, and this functional enhancement is broadly applicable across CAR constructs targeting different tumor-associated antigens.

As CD30 is related to CD4+ T cell differentiation and can affect the proportion of memory T cells. We conducted multiple rounds of killing experiments again (Supplementary Fig. 8A) and focused on the changing characteristics of cell phenotypes as the number of killing rounds increased. The results showed that as the number of rounds increased, the proportions of CD4-positive T cells and memory phenotypes both increased in CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells, and the levels in CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells were consistently higher than those in CAIX CAR-T cells (Supplementary Fig. 8B-C). Additionally, in the multiple-round killing experiments, the activation indicators in the CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T group were consistently higher than those in the CAIX CAR-T group, while the exhaustion indicators were consistently lower than those in the CAIX CAR-T group (Supplementary Fig. 8D-E).

CAR-T cells co-expressing CD30 ICD exhibit excellent therapeutic effects on orthotopic human renal cancer xenografts

Orthotopic xenografts effectively model patient-like tumor growth and metastasis, providing clinically relevant predictions of anti-cancer treatment efficacy [34]. To assess the therapeutic efficacy of CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells against orthotopic renal carcinoma, 1 × 10⁶ OS-RC-2 Luc⁺ cells were surgically implanted beneath the renal capsule of NCG mice, followed by intravenous administration of 4 × 10⁶ CAR-T cells on day 7 post-engraftment (Fig. 4A). Longitudinal bioluminescence imaging revealed superior tumor regression in CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T-treated mice compared to non-transduced T cell (UTC) and CAIX CAR-T control groups (Fig. 4B-C). In addition, no significant weight loss was observed during CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T therapy, indicating no serious adverse reactions (Fig. 4D). Peripheral blood monitoring on days 11 and 18 demonstrated significantly enhanced CAR-T cell persistence in the CAIX.CD30(ICD) cohort (Fig. 4E-F), correlating with prolonged overall survival (Fig. 4G). Histopathological evaluation through H&E staining of major organs (small intestine, lung, liver, brain, spleen, heart, stomach) showed comparable tissue integrity between CAIX.CD30(ICD) and CAIX CAR-T groups, indicating no exacerbation of therapy-related toxicity (Fig. 4H). Additionally, we used mouse serum (day 15) to assess liver function indices, and the results indicated that, compared to the CAIX CAR-T group, the CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T group showed no significant changes in ALT, AST, or ALP levels (Fig. 4I). Collectively, these findings establish that CD30 intracellular costimulatory domain-incorporated CAR-T cells achieve potent antitumor efficacy against orthotopic renal cancer xenografts while maintaining favorable biosafety profiles.

CAR-T cells co-expressing CD30 ICD exhibit therapeutic efficacy in other in vivo tumor models

The CD30 ICD costimulatory molecule enhances the function of CAR-T cells through the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway

To characterize the mechanism underlying CD30 costimulatory domain-mediated enhancement of CAR-T cell cytotoxicity, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on CAIX and CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells. Comparative transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct gene expression signatures between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 9A-B), with pathway enrichment analysis demonstrating sustained NF-κB pathway activation in CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells (Fig. 6A-C). Time-resolved pathway analysis pre- and post-OS-RC-2 stimulation (0/24/72 h) showed differential signaling dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 10A-C), while NF-κB-associated gene clusters exhibited constitutive activation in CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells even under basal conditions (Fig. 6D). Western blot validation confirmed elevated phosphorylation of NF-κB signaling intermediates (p65, IκBα) in CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T cells both pre- and post-antigen exposure (Fig. 6E), corroborating the RNA-seq findings.

To clarify the role of NF-κB signaling in the enhanced killing function mediated by the CD30 ICD co-stimulatory domain in CAR-T cells, we conducted reversal experiments using the NF-κB signaling pathway inhibitor BAY 11-7082. Subsequently, we independently expressed the key NF-κB signaling pathway protein IKKβ in CAIX CAR-T cells to construct CAR-T cells with self-activated NF-κB signaling (Fig. 7D). Discussion

While CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated clinical success in treating hematological malignancies, including leukemia, multiple myeloma, and lymphoma [35–37], its therapeutic potential remains limited in solid tumor contexts due to biological barriers such as immunosuppressive microenvironments and inefficient tumor infiltration [38]. All currently approved CAR-T cell therapies—including Breyanzi, Yescarta, Kymriah, Tecartus, and Abecma rely exclusively on a single costimulatory domain derived from either 4-1BB or CD28 [39–43]. To broaden the clinical application scope of CAR-T therapy and improve its efficacy, it is essential to develop new types of CAR-T cells. In this study, we generated a CAIX-targeted CAR-T cell that independently expresses CD30 ICD and exhibits enhanced anti-tumor activity. Our experimental data demonstrate that the CD30 signaling, stimulated independently, plays a vital role in sustaining CAR-T cell persistence. This advanced CAR-T construct exhibits stronger proliferation ability and cytotoxicity upon repeated antigen challenge compared to conventional 2nd-generation CAR-T cells. CD30 is produced in a protein form independent of CAR. This design enables signal transduction without relying on the interaction between the CAR and the antigen. During the activation of natural T cells, multiple costimulatory receptors are induced to be expressed in a time-dependent manner [19], and these receptors provide persistent and comprehensive costimulatory signals for T cell survival and activation [19]. Consequently, through the activation of endogenous costimulatory pathways, the CD30-expressing CAR-T cell acquires additional costimulatory signals that can enhance its in vivo persistence.

The molecular weight of the vector affects its transduction efficiency in cells [44, 45]. The smaller the molecular weight, the more favorable it is for its expression in cells. Truncating CD30 to exclude its extracellular domain (CD30 ICD) retained its ability to enhance CAR-T cell function, indicating that CD30-mediated signaling is ligand-independent [26]. When we further reduced CD30 ICD to only include its TRAF-binding domain, we found that CD30 lost its ability to promote the function of CAR-T cells. This means that the complete intracellular segment and transmembrane segment is needed to ensure its activation function. Mechanistic investigations revealed that CD30-mediated NF-κB pathway activation enhances CAR-T cell persistence while mitigating exhaustion phenotypes. Notably, our engineered NF-κB-constitutively active CAR-T cells recapitulated these functional attributes, demonstrating the pathway’s central role in modulating T cell activation, differentiation, proliferative capacity, and survival. The NF-κB signaling axis directly regulates IL-2 transcription, a master regulator of T cell clonal expansion. Secreted IL-2 engages high-affinity IL-2 receptors (CD25/CD122/CD132) through autocrine/paracrine mechanisms, initiating JAK-STAT5 signaling cascades that drive metabolic reprogramming and survival factor upregulation, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing proliferation loop in activated T cells [46]. Furthermore, NF-κB signaling drives Th17 cell lineage specification by transcriptionally upregulating master regulators and effector cytokines, while simultaneously amplifying Th17-mediated inflammatory responses through STAT3-dependent transcriptional circuits [47]. In addition, NF-κB activation can also inhibit T cell apoptosis and promote T cell survival [48]. An intriguing observation is that the NF-κB activation level in the CAIXCD30(ICD) CAR-T group did not increase further after antigen stimulation but was instead lower than before activation. We hypothesize that this could be due to a negative feedback mechanism. When encountering antigen stimulation, the effector functions of CAR-T cells are activated, which in turn feedback inhibits the activation level of the NF-κB pathway. Similarly, when the activation level of CAR-T cells decreases, the NF-κB pathway in the CAIXCD30(ICD) CAR-T group is reactivated. We also observed that the GSEA pathway analysis results showed the cell cycle gene set enriched at the top of the ranked list in the CAIX CAR-T group, yet this pathway was not among the differentially enriched pathways identified by KEGG analysis. These are very interesting phenomena, and we plan to conduct in-depth research on this result in the future.

Among these costimulatory receptors, CD30 was selected in our screening system after many rounds of antigen stimulation. While all costimulatory receptors within the TNFR superfamily share the common mechanism of recruiting TRAFs to activate both NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, they exhibit distinct variations in their TRAF binding specificity, signal transduction potency, and downstream effector functions [20]. Moreover, during the process of T cell activation, these costimulatory receptors exert their functions at distinct stages of T cell activation and differentiation [19]. Future investigations should prioritize elucidating the unique signaling topology of the CD30 costimulatory axis and specific mechanisms of CD30 ICD autoreactivation to inform rational engineering strategies for next-generation CAR-T cells. Meanwhile, in future studies, we plan to construct murine-derived CAR-T cells and further explore the impact of CAIX.CD30(ICD) CAR-T on endogenous immunity or the tumor microenvironment in immunocompetent mice, while also analyzing the activation, exhaustion, and phenotypic characteristics of CAR-T cells. While our current study validates CD30 costimulation in clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and gastric adenocarcinoma models, its generalizability across other solid tumors remains unaddressed. Furthermore, the utilization of TAAs as CAR targets inherently carries on-target/off-tumor toxicity risks, which may be exacerbated by CD30’s restricted expression profile in normal tissues. Systematic evaluation of CD30-incorporated CAR-T cell specificity through multi-organ toxicology studies and single-cell epitope mapping, therefore, represents a critical translational prerequisite.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a CAR construct that can constitutively express the costimulatory receptor CD30 ICD. Compared with the commonly utilized 28Z-CAR-T cells, this strategy endows T cells with unique functional characteristics, including stronger proliferation ability and effector function (Fig. 8). These characteristics have improved the anti-tumor efficacy of CAR-T in preclinical mouse models and in vitro co-culture models. Our CAR design can be applied to CAR-T cells targeting different targets to enhance their anti-tumor efficacy, providing a new strategy to improve the efficacy of CAR-T cells. These findings are of great significance for the therapy of solid tumors with CAR-T cells.
