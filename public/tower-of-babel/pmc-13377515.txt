Using Dose-Additive Models To Predict Exposure and Effects of a Military Site Risk-Relevant PFAS Mixture in Mice
Authors: Andrew G. East, Allison M. Narizzano, Suma S. Thomas, Bharat Chandramouli, Matthew A. Bazar, Lindsay A. Holden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are widespread environmental contaminants that are largely observed as mixtures. PFAS-focused ecological risk assessments largely assess individual PFAS, but understanding PFAS mixture exposure and potential ecological and biological effects is critical to characterizing real-world risk. To address this, we exposed CD-1 mice to individual PFAS and mixtures of PFAS that are relevant to surveyed environmental media and aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) used on Department of Defense (DoD) sites. Our study was performed in two parts: (1) assess the whole-body concentration of PFAS and (2) assess tissue compartment concentrations of PFAS (i.e., serum, liver, kidney, and brain). Organ weights and clinical chemistry were collected in the tissue compartment study to measure potential toxicological effects of individual and mixtures of PFAS. Mixed-effect models were used to evaluate how well relationships between dose and body compartments predicted exposure with and without the influence of mixtures. A PFOS-relative weighting factor was evaluated for the potential to predict select effects from dose and tissue concentrations. We apply the laboratory-based finding to a desktop ecological risk assessment scenario and find that while changes in PFAS dietary concentrations may alter exposure and effect estimates, differentiating reference vs impacted sites is difficult. Our results indicate that dose addition methods are successful predictors of PFAS exposure and liver effects and, importantly, provide efficient predictive screening tools for ecological risk assessors evaluating sites’ PFAS risks.

Introduction

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are commonly observed in the environment as mixtures, some of which are highly complex. In the case of aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) use on military installations, the bulk of measured PFAS mass in environmental media proximal to or following AFFF use may be composed of only a few PFAS. − To support ecological risk assessments at locations where there is known or suspected PFAS contamination due to historical AFFF use, simplified approaches such as indicator PFAS or relative potency factors may be a reasonable approach to reduce the complexity of PFAS hazard and exposure assessment data requirements. One of the challenges of assessing sites with detectable PFAS mixtures in environmental media is accurately quantifying the uncertainty associated with potentially interactive chemical-to-chemical exposure or toxicity relationships. This requires knowledge of both well-studied PFAS and less-studied PFAS. Further, site-specific field sample data, compared to lab-generated data, often have higher uncertainty due to the inherent variability associated with receptor behavior, natural history, lifespan, or other biological characteristics. This elevated variation and uncertainty may be misattributed to the identity and physicochemical characteristics of the PFAS at a specific site and may incorrectly influence the interpretation of PFAS mixtures as additive or nonadditive.

Data to support ecological risk assessments (exposure and effect data) in aquatic systems is available for many PFAS, but terrestrial systems have substantially fewer data to support ecological risk assessment-relevant analyses. For example, Zodrow et al. found sufficient data to develop risk-based screening levels for 2 of 6 highly relevant PFAS in birds and 6 of 6 highly relevant PFAS in terrestrial mammals; Grippo et al. identified toxicity data for mammals exposed to perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) in 19 sources, perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) in 17 sources, and “Other PFAS” in 13 sources; and Reinikainen et al. suggest that PFOS is the primary driver of risk; however, the lack of availability of terrestrial exposure and effect studies across the large PFAS chemical family may unintentionally result in the conclusion that the PFAS that are driving risk are simply the most well-studied. The stark difference in availability of terrestrial data between PFOA or PFOS and all other PFAS presents a major challenge to risk assessments on sites with PFAS mixtures.

To provide relevant and appropriate site-specific ecological risk assessments, there is a need to define specific terrestrial food web exposure parameters. As an example, predators of small mammals often consume substantial portions of individual prey animals, and an optimal food-web model would rely on whole animal concentrations. Estimating whole animal body burden concentrations through dynamic/kinetic models , or solubility/activity models , from field data is plausible, but laboratory-based empirical observations have the fewest causal factors and, subsequently, the least uncertainty. In short, the observations are directly tied to the PFAS-organism system only. Similarly, there is a need to define dose–response relationships concurrent to exposure measures in order to assess the hypothesis of additive toxicity of mixtures of PFAS. − Translating laboratory study findings to apical organismal effect estimates can support efficient risk assessment decision-making. As such, this study was designed to assess exposure and effects of individual and mixtures of PFAS within the controlled laboratory space using field-relevant PFAS mixtures representing soil or water substrates on military sites , at toxicologically relevant concentrations. ,− This study is also downstream of a series of works from the same study team exploring internal concentrations and kinetics in wild mice (Peromyscus), , toxicity of single PFAS in wild mice (Peromyscus) − and internal concentrations and kinetics in amphibians and rabbits as part of a “food-web in the lab” project. , In short, addressing the influence of military site mixtures of PFAS at risk-relevant concentrations can likely be approached from a simplified study design. We acknowledge the specificity of this approach and do not assert that this methodology speaks for all PFAS, mixtures, or risk assessment scenarios.

The three main objectives of this study were to (1) quantify individual and mixtures of PFAS in multiple tissue compartments of a small mammal (CD-1 mice) exposed for 28 days; (2) evaluate additivity or nonadditivity of PFAS concentrations and effects across tissue compartments using hierarchical models; and (3) provide PFAS-specific predictive models for ΣPFAS exposure and effects. These objectives address the hypothesized additivity of PFAS in exposure and effects, with the goal of supporting ecological risk assessments for terrestrial small mammals on military sites by providing a useful, accurate, and accessible approach to predicting PFAS mixtures exposure and effects across similar terrestrial systems. To demonstrate the applicability of this approach and offer specific insights into these types of systems, we provide a worked example of a desktop screening ecological risk assessment.

Methods

Assumptions and Hypotheses

Several important considerations of this work are explicitly clarified. We refer to the word “additivity” in both exposure and effect measures, following the statistical definition. Each PFAS makes a proportional contribution to a sum. , This statistical model is downstream of a “full-factorial” model in which the interaction between PFAS would be considered. Accordingly, a definition of “additivity” can be applied to both effects and exposure measures if the dose predicts the response data (effects or exposure data) via an additive model that ignores PFAS:PFAS interaction(s).

We also designed this study to efficiently use animals, limit the substantial analytical chemistry burden (note there were ∼900 samples in this analysis), and maximize concurrent exposure and effect data on relevant mixtures. Given the amount of literature indicating PFAS likely solicit additive effects, ,, we did not pursue full single PFAS dose response data. This lab has contributed several single PFAS exposure and effects studies in mice to the body of literature. − This lab has also contributed analyses of environmental media on military sites that define the PFAS mixtures used here. , Note that substantial literature that is driving the PFAS relative potency discussion does not contain mixture test cases and simply asserts additivity based on single PFAS studies. Similarly, the contrasting perspective of nonadditivity is also based on single PFAS studies and lacks a mixture test case. Literature considered proximal to our scenario of interest (terrestrial vertebrates and military site PFAS) that includes concurrent toxicological evaluation of dose additivity with single and mixtures of PFAS is binary in nature , and supports our approach by demonstrating dose additive approaches. A simplified design as “all cases” is likely an inefficient use of animals, given the success of these binary mixture studies and the specificity of the scenario we intend to inform (screening risk assessments of PFAS mixtures on military sites).

However, due to our approach, we are unable to provide a textbook relative potency factor (RPF), defined as a dose adjustment that relates one PFAS to another PFAS at an equivalent effect magnitude (e.g., LD50 vs LD50). We utilized a PFOS relative factor (λi) which is a weighting factor that relates one PFAS to another PFAS at an equivalent dose. As we only used a linear dose response function, a change on the x-axis (dose) is related to a change on the y-axis (effect or exposure measure) via the slope (β). Accordingly, this factor provides a comparative basis similar to a relative potency factor, but due to the limited scope of treatments tested, we do not label it as such. We do not assert that these factors will be relevant nor correct for PFAS, doses, or mixtures substantially outside those covered here. The goal is to demonstrate that mixtures from military sites at risk-relevant concentrations are more likely to behave additively as compared to interactively.

A third assumption of note is that this study design is powered to detect an interaction, defined as a lack of additivity. If there was a mixture-driven interaction, then single PFAS treatments would be poorly predicted by a mixture-based model. Hence, our goal was largely to demonstrate that models based on trends from mixture treatment groups and y-intercept adjustments for specific PFAS would suffice as estimation tools.

PFAS and Dosing Solutions

PFAS selected for the study were based on mixtures representative of AFFF-impacted surface waters or mixtures representative of AFFF-impacted surface soils. , Individual PFAS exposures to perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA), perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorobutanesulfonic acid (PFBS), 6:2 fluorotelomer sulfonate (FTS), or 8:2 FTS represent baseline single PFAS data analogous to prior work and were all nominally at 2.5 mg/kg-d to avoid mortality observed at the next highest dose administered with PFOS, 20 mg/kg-d. − PFAS mixtures were delivered at 3 concentrations, all described relative to PFOS (Table ). Concentrations of ΣPFAS in the mixture treatments bracket the nominal single PFAS doses (2.5 mg/kg-d) for a comparative basis. The surface water C6–8 mixture contained PFOS, PFHxS, PFHxA, and PFOA by a nominal ratio of 1:0.62:0.25:0.17 (Table ) based on previous characterization of PFAS-impacted surface water at military sites. The surface soil FTS mixture contained PFOS, 6:2 FTS, and 8:2 FTS by a nominal ratio of 1:0.1:0.05 (Table ) based on previous characterization of PFAS-impacted soil at military sites. , PFAS were purchased from commercial suppliers, with the goal of obtaining the potassium salt (K+) or anionic form (see details in Table SI2). All chemicals were stored at room temperature and in the dark, as suggested by the manufacturer.

Dosing solutions were prepared by weighing neat PFAS as purchased (either as acid or as salt) using a calibrated and verified scale and dissolving them in a known volume of deionized water in HDPE bottles. Weights of salts were not adjusted to account for cations, based on the small influence of cations on target concentration (e.g., 39.1 g/mol out of 538.22 g/mol for PFOS K+ salt is a smaller relative influence on error than the 70–130% error expected in analytical methods). Dissociated anion concentrations from analytical measures were used in all analyses, whereas nominal concentrations were used as guides for dosing and study design.

Two batches of dosing solutions were prepared 72 h prior to the study start and were maintained on orbital shaker plates, constantly running throughout the study at 60–100 rpm, as per sampling and storage validation exercises. Batches were alternated daily for daily dosing aliquots, sampled for analytical verification on study days 0 and 28, and pooled in equal volumes from each batch by a calibrated and verified pipet. Control dosing solutions (deionized water) were bottled, shaken, and sampled similarly.

Mice Husbandry, Dosing, and Sampling

CD-1 mice (Crl:CD1­(ICR)) arrived from Charles River Laboratories at 42 days old in two cohorts (whole-body study and serum study) consisting of 300 mice (10 per sex per treatment; 15 treatments: 1 control, 8 individual analytes, 6 mixtures). The animal facility at the Defense Centers for Public Health-Aberdeen (DCPH-A) Toxicology is AAALAC International-accredited and supported by a Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)-trained Quality Assurance team. Animal use was approved under protocol #26-22-02-01 by the DCPH-A Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted according to the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and all applicable federal and DoD regulations.

Male mice were individually housed due to recurrent observations of fighting that precluded confidence in animal health; female mice were group-housed at 5 per cage. Animals were housed in temperature-, relative humidity-, and light-controlled rooms with automated data recording and 24-h monitored out-of-range alarm systems. Target conditions in the room were 20–22 °C, 30–70% humidity, and 12:12 h light:dark cycle. A certified pesticide-free rodent chow (Envigo Teklad 2020X Certified Rodent Diet) and filtered tap water were available ad libitum. Feeding, bedding, and enrichment materials were plastic-free (e.g., steel, paper, wood) to minimize uncontrolled PFAS contamination. Mice were dosed daily (every 22–26 h) by trained staff via oral gavage at 10 mL/kg using a 1 mL disposable syringe and 16-gauge 1.5 in. stainless steel ball-end oral gavage needle. Animals were weighed weekly.

On the 29th day (∼24 h after receiving 28 consecutive daily doses), mice in the whole-body study were euthanized by inhalation of CO2 and cervical dislocation. The entire skin was removed, and bodies were placed in a clean, labeled Whirl-Pak. Whirl-Paks were then placed in a −80 °C freezer in small batches throughout sample collection and stored at −80 °C until shipping for analysis. On the 29th day (∼24 h after receiving 28 consecutive daily doses), mice in the serum study were rendered insensate by inhalation of CO2 and euthanized by decapitation and exsanguination. Whole trunk blood was collected into a clean, labeled 1.5 mL polypropylene disposable microcentrifuge tube, allowed to clot, centrifuged, and the serum was decanted into a clean, labeled 0.8 mL microcentrifuge tube and transferred to −80 °C for storage until shipping for analysis. Serum was also aliquoted for clinical chemistry analysis from specific treatments (see below). The liver, kidneys (paired), brain, and ∼1 cm2 of skin were collected by trained necropsy staff (including a board-certified veterinary pathologist to ensure notation of gross lesions or other observations), weighed, and transferred into clean, labeled cryovials (1.5 and 5 mL) and stored at −80 °C until shipping for analysis.

PFAS Quantification in Tissues and Dosing Solutions

Dosing solution, whole-body, and serum samples from all treatments and animals were shipped on dry ice with a chain of custody to SGS Axys (Sidney, BC, Canada). Liver, kidneys, and brain samples from select treatments and animals (logistically constrained to 13 treatmentsexcluded PFNA and PFBS, as they were not in either mixtureand 7 animals per sex) were shipped on dry ice with a chain of custody to SGS Axys (Sidney, BC, Canada). All tissue samples were homogenized, and tissue, serum, and dosing solution samples were prepared, extracted, and analyzed for 40 PFAS via ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS) following the procedures of the draft method (4th) EPA 1633. Briefly, a portion of each sample was accurately weighed into a tube, spiked with isotopically labeled quantification standards (surrogate compounds), and extracted with 50% formic acid. The resulting extract was cleaned by solid-phase extraction (SPE) using a disposable cartridge containing a weak anion exchange sorbent. The SPE cartridge was eluted by basic methanol. The extract for each sample was spiked with labeled recovery (internal) standards and analyzed by UHPLC-MS/MS. UHPLC–MS/MS is utilized with a reversed-phase C18 column using a solvent gradient. The column was coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer run at unit mass resolution in the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, using negative electrospray ionization. The instrument was calibrated after every 10 samples or every 12 h, whichever came first, and at the end of every run. Summarized reporting limits (determined per batch) are included in the SI Tables SI3–SI8. Data flagged as “beyond the standard curve” were included as quantitative measures based on pilot studies that indicated dilution and undiluted runs were within 10% of each other. Data flagged as below reporting but above detection (J-flag, “trace”) were included as quantitative measures due to consistent overlap with measures in batches where given samples were above reporting vs below reporting (see Figure SI1). In the brain tissues, J-flagged data were excluded as substantially less than 50% of sample sizes were above detection limits, and the overlap between J-flag data and quantitative data could not be confirmed (see Figure SI1). This approach was determined based on a trade-off between censoring methods , and including maximal individual data. Summarization techniques for “trace environmental samples” lose individual-level resolution, and our laboratory studies with many individuals and high concentrations are intended to express variation as a combination of individuals and instrumental analysis.

Clinical Chemistry Quantification

Standard serum chemistry parameters were evaluated on select thawed serum samples using the DiaSys Response 910 Vet Analyzer (DiaSys Diagnostic Systems, MI, USA) with proper calibration and verification. The nine parameters analyzed were albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), cholesterol (CHOL), glucose (GLUC), total protein (TP), creatinine (CREA), and triglycerides (TRIG). The three calculated parameters were globulin (GLOB), the ALB/GLOB ratio, and the BUN/CREA ratio. Samples were selected randomly from mice exposed to the C6–8 mixture (low, medium, and high), PFOS, PFOA, PFHxS, PFHxA, and control treatments. Samples from other treatments (e.g., FTS mixture) were excluded due to cost constraints.

Statistical Modeling of Exposure and Effects

All model development, evaluation, and figure generation utilized R (version 4.4.0). A selection of relevant code is provided in the Supporting Information for a reproducible example. The overarching approach is based on the hypothesis: If PFAS exposure or effects data from mixture treatment groups are predictive of single PFAS treatment groups, then mixtures behave additively (see Figure SI2). PFAS concentration data (individual PFAS and ΣPFAS) that were used to address exposure include the dose, whole body, serum, liver, kidney, and brain. PFAS effect data that were used to explore effects were relative liver weight and ALT concentration, as they had consistent dose–response relationships. Other PFAS effect data that did not have a consistent dose–response relationship included body weight, brain and kidney weights, and the remaining clinical chemistry parameters (see Tables SI18 through SI21). Some differences were identified in per-contrast to control ANOVAs, but they were in singleton PFAS treatments and less directly contributory to the observed liver toxicity pattern. For instance, ALB, AST, CREA, TP, GLU, and CHOL differences were detected, but not in the mixture treatments (with increasing concentrations), and all with either weaker biological ties to liver impacts (i.e., TP) or weaker levels of response to liver impacts (i.e., AST).

Modeling Additivity in Exposure

To explore the additivity in exposure hypothesis, a mixed-effects model (i.e., hierarchical model) was fit with all dose:tissue or tissue:tissue combinations for each individual PFAS, where dose or sample and sex were the independent variables (fixed effects in Model 1). Random effects in Model 1 were PFAS-specific and allowed varying both y-intercept and slope (see Figure SI2), which provided a measure of the variance of interactive effects in relation to individual-to-individual variability. Model 1 was fit to compare the variance of the random effects against overall residuals to determine whether to reject the hypothesis of additivity. Specifically, if the variance associated with the random effects is greater than the residuals, then any one animal’s concentrations require accounting for a nonadditive mixture relationship. If the variance associated with the random effects is less than the residuals, then any one animal’s concentrations do not require accounting for a nonadditive mixture relationship, and an additive mixture relationship is inferred (see Figure SI2). If this additive mixture relationship was observed, mixed-effect models with varying intercepts only were fit to the same predictor and estimate pair (Model 2). These mixed-effects models (Model 1 and Model 2) also accounted for sex to more completely describe the relationship between dose and tissue or sample and tissue.

Modeling Additivity in Effects

A dose addition relative effect approach was expected to perform well for these data. , Data from single-PFAS exposures were utilized as PFOS-relative factors (λ i ) specific to each individual PFAS. This approach of comparing effect levels at common doses was taken to minimize animal use, power the study to detect interactions, and account for the bulk of literature that is indicative of additive toxicity and exposure in these PFAS, while retaining a comparative basis at toxicologically relevant doses of toxicologically driving PFAS (i.e., PFOS or PFOA near 2.5 mg/kg-d). ,,

These PFOS-relative factors (λ i ) were then used to weight the individual PFAS doses in mixture treatments to calculate a ΣPFAS that predicted an effect. Effects require a common normalization factor for this approachhere, as PFOS is the most relevant PFAS at the sites of interest, the mean relative liver weight of the PFOS-alone treatment (by sex) was used to generate a proportional relative liver weight value for each animal (relative to the PFOS treatment mean). The interpretation is then that the proportional relative liver weight change (log10 transformed to ensure equivalent up/down comparison) is a function of ΣPFAS dose. The mixture treatments proportional to PFOS treatment relative liver weights were used to estimate the overall slope (beta in Model 3). The single PFAS treatments proportional to PFOS treatment relative liver weights were used to estimate the PFOS-relative factors (λ i ) of each PFAS and scale the dose addition. To evaluate the model performance, if a regression fit to the λ i -adjusted proportion PFOS treatment relative liver weights were inside the confidence intervals of the original, unadjusted proportion PFOS treatment relative liver weights linear regression, then additivity should not be rejected.

As ALT showed a dose–response effect and is often utilized as a measure of hepatocyte damage, a linear regression was fit to log10 relative liver weight proportion change and log10 ALT proportional change to generate a slope (unit change per unit change in relative effects with equal up/down directionality). Modeling the ALT endpoint provides an example of translation from organ weight effect (determined via lethal sample collection) to apical organismal health effect (determined via nonlethal sample collection).

Benchtop Risk Assessment Example

To demonstrate the utility of these models, we provide an example “benchtop” risk assessment. We assume all other things being equal, with a reference site being 10-fold reduced compared to a risk assessment site, or a risk assessment site being 10-fold increased compared to a reference site. We used a C6–8 mixture of PFOS, PFHxS, PFOA, and PFHxA due to the relevance of this mixture − , and the similarity in the relative effect magnitude of these PFAS at common doses. We report individual PFAS and sum PFAS whole animal and serum concentrations using the models presented in this paper. We report the estimated relative liver weight increase per the 10-fold increase in exposure and the increase in ALT per that increase in liver weight.

Results

PFAS Concentrations

The studies were conducted in two cohorts, and between the two cohorts, the dosing solutions were comparable (Table SI9). As such, only measured data are incorporated into statistical models and figures. There were consistent impurities that appear to be associated with the commercially available PFAS salts and anions used in this study. Specifically, perfluoropentanesulfonic acid (PFPeS), perfluoroheptanesulfonic acid (PFHpS), and perfluorononanesulfonic acid (PFNS) were detected in all treatments that contained PFOS (Table SI9). PFAS concentrations in mouse tissues (Figures and ) indicated that relative PFAS concentrations across tissues were largely consistent (see complete summarized data set in SI Table SI17). In PFOS-, PFOA-, and PFHxS-singly exposed animals, common relative concentrations were consistently observed across the tissues analyzed with [liver] > [serum] > [whole-body], [kidney] > [brain] (Figures and ). PFNA samples were not available for analysis, but it is expected that the same relative concentration across tissues would be observed in the PFNA-singly exposed animals. Note that PFHxS-singly exposed animals had serum concentrations that were approximately equivalent to liver concentrations (Figure ) and that in the C6–8 mixture-exposed animals, PFHxS appears to be the dominant PFAS in serum, but not in other compartments, despite PFOS being the dominant analyte in the dosing solution (Figure and Table SI3). PFHxA, PFBS, and 6:2 FTS had wider distributions within and across compartments and lower concentrations than the other PFAS, likely due to known elimination kinetics. , 8:2 FTS-singly exposed animals generally followed the pattern of relative tissue compartment concentrations observed with animals exposed to PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS. Effects Summary

Effects data are summarized in detail in the Tables SI18–SI21, and we note that the concentrations selected were intended to avoid inducing overt effects and toxicodynamic confounders. Bodyweight trajectories of the mice were only impacted by exposure to PFNA across all groups (Table SI20), and this impact was not weight loss but less weight gained. Organ weights were sometimes impacted by PFAS exposure, and liver weights from PFAS-exposed mice were often greater than those of controls (Table SI21). Specifically, relative liver weights (liver weight (g)/(terminal body weight (g) – liver weight (g))), as a measure of effect, differed across PFAS by 1.03 to 2.80 vs control treatment relative liver weight (Table ). Our individual PFAS doses were quantified at 1.39 to 2.46 mg/kg-d, which is comparable to the commonly reported LOAELs near 1 mg/kg-d in the literature, − and concurs with our observed increases in relative liver weights for those soliciting effects (not PFHxA, PFBS, and 6:2 FTS in females).

Several clinical chemistry parameters were impacted by individual PFAS and mixtures of PFAS, but the ALT data showed a consistent dose–response and are appropriate to consider for total liver health based on the observed increases in relative liver weight (SI). Specifically, ALT values (Units/L) increased by as much as 2- to 6-fold in the highest ΣPFAS treatment (C6–8 High) and 4- to 5-fold in the PFOA-alone treatment (SI) (Table ).

Evidence of Additivity in Exposure Measures

The loge-linear relationship between dose and tissue concentrations was qualitatively similar across PFAS, regardless of whether animals were exposed to individual PFAS or mixtures of PFAS (Figure , Figure SI3, Figure SI4). When evaluated quantitatively using a median dose and median y-intercept, the tissue- and sex-specific slopes produced mean predicted concentrations that fell entirely within the maximal confidence intervals estimated by the regression residuals (Figure SI5). In short, the difference in tissue- and sex-specific slopes was equal to or smaller than the difference in individual animals’ tissue- and sex-specific concentrations, which means that nonadditive trends are unlikely to be detectable given the expected individual-driven variation. If the PFAS-specific linear models were less parallel, it would indicate that there was a different relationship between dose and tissue concentration for PFAS singletons (higher doses, rightward on the x-axis) and PFAS mixtures (lower doses, leftward on the x-axis) (Figure , Figure SI3, Figure SI4) and would indicate that PFAS were not additive in this system for the measured endpoints. Some PFAS were introduced as impurities and were not part of the intended dosing schemes (for example, PFPeS (Figure SI6)), but they appear regularly and along similar linear trajectories.

A further demonstration of additivity in exposure measures was that the linear model fit to the dose (mg/kg-d) and whole-body (ng/g) data from mixture treatments and impurities (lower doses, leftward on the x-axis) captured the mean observed singleton treatment data (higher doses, rightward on the x-axis) or had confidence intervals that contained a linear model fit to most available data (including singleton treatments; Figure , other dose and tissue combinations, Figure SI9–SI13). This successful prediction suggests that the accumulation of specific PFAS is not impacted by coexposure to other PFAS, and an additive model of exposure can be assumed. Whole-body PFOA dosed as a singleton appears to be lower than expected in the all-data model than the mixture- and impurity-data model, implying that PFOA may not fit a strict additive model or that uptake/deposition is concentration-dependent; however, we note the extensive range of data, subsequent narrow confidence intervals, and qualitatively minor overprediction.

Using a mixed-effect (hierarchical) model (Model 1) that includes analyte-specific correlated varying slopes and intercepts, the variation around the model that was associated with individual animals and general “noise” (residuals, Table , σ ij ) was, in all cases, larger than the variation associated with a potential mixture effect (nonadditivity effect variation, Table , σ βi ). The interpretation is that a mixture-driven interaction is unlikely, as the nonadditive effect would be unlikely to be detected given the variability in individual data. In mixed-effect modeling parlance, the mixture interaction parameter is not informative and should be trimmed from the model. Subsequent interpretation was that the less complicated model (Model 2), with analyte-specific intercepts only, sufficiently represents the system. To evaluate this assertion, a comparison between Model 1 (Figure SI14) and Model 2 (Figure SI15) suggests that the simplification of the model from accounting for a mixture effect (Model 1) to strictly additive (Model 2) only minorly reduces the precision or accuracy. Comparing the Residuals columns (σ ij ) in Table , simplifying the model increases dose-to-tissue predictions’ residuals by zero to 0.01 loge units in 3 of 5 cases. All other Model 1 vs Model 2 σ ij comparisons have residual increases of 0.1 loge units or smaller. On an untransformed scale, this translates to approximately a 10% increase in variation that would then be attributed to individuals and not (mis-)­attributed to a nonadditive mixture relationship. Further, as individual-driven tissue concentration variability was larger than the difference between slopes in Model 1, if an interactive nonadditive mixture effect existed (e.g., synergism, potentiating, etc.), it would be challenging to detect in a field setting.

Evidence of Additivity in Effects

Relative liver weights from singleton PFAS exposures, proportional to PFOS, were used as PFOS-relative factors (λ i ) (Table , Figure SI16, Table SI14) because PFOS is expected to be representative of PFAS in surface soil and surface water on contaminated military sites. , These PFOS-relative factors weight the PFAS-specific contribution to the dose–response function. Model 3 describes the ΣPFAS predictor of relative liver weight (proportional to PFOS) to a per-PFAS dose-additive predictor of relative liver weight (Model 3, Figure , Table , SI Table SI15). Because the proportional increase in relative liver weight predicted by the additive model was within the confidence intervals of the original dose-response model and the λ i -adjusted means moved closer to the dose-response models (Figure ), the weighted dose-additive response does not influence the expected dose-response function, and any given PFAS-specific prediction is sufficiently normalized to a ΣPFAS prediction. PFOA appears to be the only PFAS that may not follow the expected additive influence on liver weight because the λ i -adjusted toxicity estimate fell outside the mixture model confidence intervals. See SI for other combinations of predictors and liver effects, as well as model performance plots (Figure SI16 through SI25) and parameter estimates (Table SI14 through SI16). Overall, the prediction of PFAS-mixture toxicity using a PFOS-relative weighting (λ i ) were adequate for these environmentally relevant PFAS, and this risk-relevant endpoint, and the dose window.

Because ALT is often utilized as a measure of hepatocyte damage, ALT measures were also relativized to the PFOS treatment and a linear model was then fit with the relative liver weight (proportional to PFOS treatment mean relative liver weight) as the predictor and ALT value (proportional to PFOS treatment mean ALT value) as the response (Table SI16, Figure SI26). Benchtop Risk Assessment Example

To demonstrate the utility of our observations, we applied the models to a desktop ecological risk assessment. Results indicate that a 10-fold increase in ΣC6–8PFAS in dose (i.e., dietary concentration) was predicted to lead to overlapping individual PFAS measures in whole-body concentration and serum concentration (Figure , top). This 10-fold increase in exposure resulted in an increase in relative liver weight (∼20%), but this health effect may be challenging to confidently detect given individual variability and substantial reference vs impacted confidence interval overlap (Figure , bottom left). The estimated ALT increase, given the increase in liver weight, however, appeared more detectable, and a 10-fold increase in diet ΣC6–8PFAS yielded a 20–30% increase in ALT measures (Figure , bottom right) that was less likely to overlap zero and more likely to be detected.

Using additive approaches, a hypothetical reference (ref.) vs exposed (ERA) site results in some overlap of estimated PFAS in trophic transfer (top left) and internal concentrations (top right), and this increase in exposures leads to overlapping relative (Rel.) liver weight increases (bottom left), but that increase in liver weight is likely detectable via ALT (bottom right) due to the predicted increase in ALT unlikely to include zero.

Discussion

CD-1 mice were exposed via oral gavage to individual PFAS and mixtures of PFAS that are relevant to environmental media concentrations at sites where PFAS-containing AFFF products have been used. Whole-body, serum, liver, kidney, and brain concentrations were determined using EPA Method 1633 for 40 PFAS analytes. Body weights, organ weights, and clinical chemistry were collected to evaluate effects. Evidence of additivity in exposure measures and select effect measures is demonstrated by exploring the prior hypothesis that single-PFAS data would be predicted by PFAS mixture relationships. We found that a hierarchical model approach using exposure data does not require an interaction term, and a PFOS-relative factor (λ i ) sufficiently predicts relative liver weights. Taken together, results suggest that a dose-additive interpretation should not be rejected for these PFAS and that a nonadditive interpretation (e.g., interactive) is less likely to capture the underlying process.

In this study, we have shown that the assessment of military site-relevant PFAS mixtures and risk-relevant doses can be reasonably simplified by the assumption of exposure additivity and demonstrated the application of relative exposure and effect approaches for specific endpoints. This means that ΣPFAS is predictive of tissue concentrations and liver effects for these military site-relevant mixtures and that individual PFAS observations are not influenced by being in a mixture. So, even if exposure or effects details about individual PFAS (i.e., less studied or less observed PFAS) are not known, a central estimate can be attained for ΣPFAS through average parameters. This estimate should be accompanied by the broad uncertainty indicated by the layered variance parameters that capture PFAS-to-PFAS differences and residuals driven by individuals about the mean (see Models 2 and 3). As such, it would be a coarse estimate and would be unlikely to have utility for PFAS that have drastically different properties (e.g., volatile, labile, biodegradable, etc.).

This work is broadly motivated by the desire to understand risk at military sites where potential impacts to mammals are of interest and a specific suite of prevalent PFAS is expected. − Further, as this study is based on 28 days of exposure and “grab samples” and ignores kinetics or toxicodynamics, it is most aligned with scenario-relevant short-term toxicological studies , and short, but ecologically relevant time spans of animals’ time on potentially impacted sites. Nonetheless, in an ecological assessment, the length of exposure on a site may not be constant, and a timeline of one month may be reasonable. Further, in a screening assessment, protective approaches may be taken, and a risk assessor may be interested in calculating the highest potential concentrations or highest potential impacts. While some PFAS (e.g., PFOS) have slow elimination resulting in nearly continuous uptake, some PFAS have high elimination rates, and trajectories of tissue concentrations may have high early peaks and low steady-state concentrations. , As an example, in CD-1 mice exposed to a 6:2 FTS-containing AFFF for 28 days vs 42 (male) to ∼63 (female) days, liver weight effects were not observed in the long-term exposures , and the hypothesis is that this was due to a reduction in serum concentrations. , While we do not have time course data to verify this hypothesis specific to this study, the left-skewed female 6:2 FTS serum distributions in the present study do provide some indication that concentrations may be dropping near 28 days of exposure in CD-1 mice. This facility does have time course data with a different mouse species (Peromyscus spp.) that indicates 6:2 FTS concentrations decrease between 21 and 28 days of exposure. , We also note that known 6:2 FTS metabolites were not detected in Peromyscus spp. after 28 days of exposure. , In other time course data with more diverse taxa (toads, rabbits) and more diverse PFAS mixtures (>10 PFAS), no negative correlations were observed across PFAS (using targeted analyses), , so if there was metabolism of 6:2 FTS, we are relatively confident that the metabolites were not known risk-relevant PFAS like PFOS or PFOA. We do note the widely reported degradation pathways of FTS associated with a combination of biological and physicochemical activity (see Harding-Marjanovic et al. as an example and summaries in Evich et al.) and the downstream potential for PFAS generation that are not measured in the methods used here.

Our successful dose-additive prediction of mixture exposure and effects suggests dose–response functions are likely parallel in the dose ranges tested, and with relative liver weight as an endpoint. Notably, dose-additive exposure and effects are expected across mammalian and avian taxa reproductive and survival endpoints. ,,, In this study, only 6:2 FTS (females only) and PFHxA-exposed animals had nonsignificant liver effects compared to control; all other individual and mixtures of PFAS tested resulted in increased relative liver weights in exposed animals vs control. We note that animals exposed to 6:2 FTS (females only) and PFHxA have some of the lowest, per-dose, internal concentrations, and 6:2 FTS is not expected to impact liver weights in longer exposures. , In decreasing order of fold-change relative liver weights vs control treatment, PFOA > PFOS > PFHxS > 8:2 FTS ≥ 6:2 FTS (depends on comparator sex) ≥ PFHxA (depends on comparator sex) at approximate mean relative effect magnitudes of 2.6, 1.6, 1.6, 1.2, 1.2, 1.1 (Table ). As we use a linear dose-response model, the order of effect magnitude is comparable to the order of dose potency in other works. While our data are somewhat in alignment, a key comparison to Bil et al. , suggests that we see higher PFOA toxicity than expected and lower PFHxA toxicity than expected. We expect this difference is a function of detailed toxicokinetic accounting and determining relative potency based on internal dosimetry vs external oral dosing, as well as differences between rats and mice. , This is a key observation that corroborates our workwhile there are generalizable trends across risk-relevant PFAS, there are some taxa- and PFAS-driven differences that are critical to consider in specific scenarios.

Importantly, based on our ALT observations, detection of liver effects may be better served, both practically (i.e., survival sampling) and protectively (i.e., sensitivity), by this clinical chemistry measure, as the log10 unit change in ALT vs log10 unit change in relative liver weight is 1.5 in males and 2.1 in females (linear slope in Table SI16, Figure SI26). It is also important to note that hepatotoxicity is only one type of effect associated with PFAS exposure in mammals, and it is not consistently the most protective effect across PFAS (e.g., 6:2 FTS was associated with immune effects, and PFNA was associated with liver effects in Peromyscus spp.). In conclusion, we have demonstrated an additive model of exposure and liver effects in mice exposed to military site-relevant PFAS. The study design provides simple predictions of whole-body concentration, serum and tissue concentrations, and simple relative effect measures. While time-course data or other PFAS may expand the window of possible predictions, the observations reported and methods used herein are highly relevant to DoD sites, highly approachable, and efficiently address concerns associated with PFAS mixtures. We demonstrate the utility of our data via a worked example, where a 10-fold difference between PFAS levels at a reference site and an exposed site may lead to overlapping PFAS-specific tissue concentrations, overlapping but potentially adverse relative liver weights, and detectable increases in measurable liver enzymes.
