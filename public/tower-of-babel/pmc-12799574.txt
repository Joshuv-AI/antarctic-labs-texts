Effects of Hibiscus sabdariffa calyx extract on IL-10, TNF-α, and woven bone during orthodontic relapse
Authors: Evie L Pakpahan, Ida B Narmada, Retno P Rahayu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTooth relapse occurs after orthodontic treatment. Bioactive chemicals in Hibiscus sabdariffa calyx extract (HS-CE) can protect bones and prevent inflammation. This study evaluated the effects of HS-CE on interleukin-10 (IL-10), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) expression, and woven bone formation during orthodontic relapse.MethodsSixteen male Wistar rats underwent orthodontic tooth movement of the left maxillary first molar using a 10 g nickel–titanium closed-coil spring for 14 days, followed by a 7-day retention phase. Rats were divided into two groups (n = 8): phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and HS-CE. During retention and the subsequent 7- and 14-day relapse phases, each group received daily intrasulcular applications of PBS or HS-CE (0.01 mL). Relapse distance was measured as the percentage return of tooth movement relative to initial separation. Four animals per group were euthanized at each time point, and maxillary tissues were processed for histological evaluation of woven bone and immunohistochemical staining of IL-10 and TNF-α. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance and independent samples t-tests, and Tukey's post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05).ResultsHS-CE significantly reduced relapse distance at days 7 and 14 (p < 0.05). IL-10 expression was elevated on day 7 (p < 0.05) and day 14 (p < 0.001), whereas TNF-α was suppressed on both days (p < 0.01; p < 0.001). The woven bone area was greater in the HS-CE group (p < 0.001).ConclusionsHS-CE enhanced IL-10 expression, suppressed TNF-α, increased woven bone formation, and reduced the relapse distance, highlighting its potential as a natural adjuvant to improve post-treatment stability.Keywords: Hibiscus sabdariffa, Interleukin-10, Orthodontic tooth movement, Osteogenesis, Relapse, Tumor necrosis factor-alpha

Introduction

The primary objective of orthodontic treatment is attaining correct tooth alignment and functional occlusal relationships, thereby enhancing masticatory efficiency and improving facial aesthetics, as well as ensuring long-term treatment stability.1 Long-term stability of tooth alignment is a key goal, but various studies have shown that the predictability of treatment outcomes can vary and there is a risk of relapse following the completion of treatment.2, 3, 4

Relapse refers to the return of teeth to their original positions after orthodontic treatment and it remains a complex challenge in the field of orthodontics.5,6 Relapse may continue until the full resolution of alveolar bone resorption. Decreasing alveolar bone resorption and enhancing bone formation following orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) are expected to be effective strategies for preventing tooth relapse.7, 8, 9 Current strategies rely on fixed or removable retainers, but both have limitations, with relapse rates of up to 40 % within two years for removable retainers, and although fixed retainers are more effective, they can increase periodontal risks and oral hygiene complications.10,11 Previous studies explored the use of pharmacological agents such as simvastatin and bisphosphonates to prevent orthodontic relapse,12 but these medications may lead to adverse effects with prolonged use.13,14

Cytokines and bone remodeling are critical determinants of orthodontic relapse.15 Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) promotes osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption, and interleukin-10 (IL-10) exerts protective effects by suppressing osteoclastogenesis and supporting bone homeostasis.16,17 In addition, woven bone formation is the initial stage of bone regeneration, providing early structural stability that is essential for long-term periodontal support and reducing relapse.18,19

Hibiscus sabdariffa calyx extract (HS-CE) is rich in anthocyanins, polyphenols, and vitamin C, and exhibits antibacterial activity against multiple bacterial strains, but with no cytotoxic effects on osteoblasts.20,21 Previous studies have shown that HS-CE promotes bone formation, modulates inflammatory pathways, and exhibits osteoprotective effects relevant to orthodontics.22,23 However, its effects on woven bone formation and the expression of IL-10 and TNF-α during the critical retention phase have not been explored. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the potential of using HS-CE for modulating bone remodeling and inflammatory mediators, thereby assisting in the prevention of orthodontic relapse.

Materials and methods

All experimental procedures were approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dental Medicine, Airlangga University, Indonesia (ethical approval no. 0823/HRECC.FODM/VIII/2024).

Experimental animal models

The experimental protocol is depicted in Figure 1A. Male Wistar rats (n = 16) aged 12–14 weeks and weighing 250–400 g were obtained from the Institute of Tropical Disease, Airlangga University (Surabaya, Indonesia). Before the experiment commenced, all rats underwent an acclimatization period for 1 week under standardized laboratory conditions. The ambient temperature was controlled within the range of 25 °C–30 °C, with daily assessments of environmental parameters such as temperature, humidity, and cage hygiene. Cages were sanitized on a weekly basis. Animals received 25 g of standard laboratory food (Charoen Pokphand Indonesia, Sidoarjo, Indonesia) twice daily and had unrestricted access to mineral water.

Experimental design and timeline of orthodontic tooth movement (OTM), retention, and relapse in rats. (A) Schematic timeline for the experimental protocol. OTM was applied to the left maxillary first molar using a 10 g nickel–titanium closed-coil spring for 14 days. On day 14, the orthodontic appliance was removed, and a composite resin retainer was placed for 7 days (retention period). Intrasulcular application of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or Hibiscus sabdariffa calyx extract (HS-CE) was performed daily from day 14 until the end of the relapse phase. On day 21, the retainer was removed, initiating the relapse phase. Rats were euthanized at days 28 and 35 (n = 4 per group per time point). (B) Intraoral photograph and illustration showing the placement of the orthodontic appliance.

Rats were randomly assigned to two treatment groups, where one group was treated by intrasulcular application of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and the other group received HS-CE. Starting on day 14 after OTM, both treatments (0.01 mL/day) were delivered using a 10 μL Hamilton microsyringe fitted with a 30-gauge detachable needle (Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA). The injections were precisely delivered into the distal gingival sulcus of the left maxillary first molar of the rats. After injection, the rats were kept in a cage without food for approximately 15 min to enhance absorption.24 The treatments continued throughout the 7-day retention period and subsequent relapse phase. Four animals from each group were euthanized on days 28 and 35, which corresponded to days 7 and 14 of relapse, respectively. Maxillary tissues were collected for analysis.

Preparation of HS-CE

HS-CE was produced and extracted according to a previously established protocol, with modifications.25 Fresh HS calyx was cultivated in Kediri, East Java, Indonesia, and then dried at 50 °C for 24 h in a vacuum drying oven (Memmert, Schwabch, Germany) until it reached a crushable state. Next, 1000 g of desiccated calyx was ground into a fine powder and filtered through a 60-mesh sieve (Retsch, Duesseldorf, Germany). To obtain the plant extract, 1000 g of calyx powder was mixed with 5000 mL of 96 % ethanol (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and subjected to maceration for 72 h, where the solvent was replaced every 24 h. After each interval, the mixture was filtered through a Whatman filter paper (Cytiva, Shanghai, China) to obtain the filtrate, which was subsequently concentrated at around 40 °C and 100 mBar pressure in a vacuum rotary evaporator (Buchi, St. Gallen, Switzerland) until no solvent was visibly dripping from the apparatus. The extraction yield was 31.72 %. The concentrated extract was subsequently diluted to 10 % using sterile NaCl for intrasulcular application to rats.23

Phytochemical screening

Preliminary phytochemical screening was conducted for HS-CE based on modified standard procedures.26,27

Experimental OTM and relapse in rats

OTM was performed as described in a previous study.16 All procedures were conducted under general anesthesia to ensure optimal access and minimize animal discomfort. Anesthesia was administered via intramuscular injection of ketamine (1 mL/100 g body weight; Ketamine 10 %, Alfasan, Woerden, The Netherlands) and xylazine (0.03 mL/100 g body weight; Xylazin 2 %, Bayer, Jakarta, Indonesia). A nickel–titanium closed-coil spring (Ormco, Orange, CA, USA) was placed between the maxillary first molar and maxillary incisor on the left side. The coil spring was secured to the maxillary left first molar and incisor using a 0.1 mm stainless steel wire ligature (Ortho Organizers, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The wire was coated with flowable composite resin to enhance retention (FGM Dental Group, Joinville, Brazil). As described in a previous study, a preliminary force of 10 g was applied to enable mesial displacement of the left maxillary first molars (Figure 1B).16 A dynamometer (MedKraft Orthodontics, Miami, FL, USA) was utilized to calibrate the orthodontic force.

OTM was maintained for a period of 14 days across all groups. After completing this phase, the coil spring was removed under general anesthesia. Composite resin (FGM Dental Group) was employed to maintain the interdental space between the maxillary left first and second molars for a retention period of 7 days. The retaining substance was subsequently removed to facilitate relapse migration.

Relapse distance

Impressions of the upper jaw were taken using a hydrophilic vinyl silicone impression (3M, St. Paul, MN, USA). This material was injected using mixing and intraoral tips around the maxillary molars. A rigid wire, shaped and covered with thick bandages, was used as a tray to hold the impression, thereby facilitating removal from the oral cavity of a rat under general anesthesia. The impression obtained was photographed. The distance between the distal cervical region of the maxillary first molar and mesial cervical region of the second molar was measured using ImageJ software. Histological analysis of woven bone formation

At each experimental time point, rats were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). The left maxillae were dissected, fixed in 10 % neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, and decalcified in 10 % ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) at pH 7.4 for 21–30 days at room temperature, with regular solution changes every 3 days. Following decalcification, samples were rinsed in PBS and embedded in paraffin after dehydration through a graded ethanol series and xylene clearing.

Vertical sectioning was performed at a thickness of 4–6 μm using a rotary microtome (Indogen Intertama, Jakarta, Indonesia) to produce mesiodistal sagittal slices. Sectioning was directed from the buccal aspect, ensuring that each slice included the left maxillary first and second molars. The periodontal ligament tissue surrounding the left maxillary first and second molars was excised using an excisional biopsy technique by following the normal tissue margin approximately 2 mm around the area of interest. The tissue was oriented in the cassette according to the cutting surface to maintain anatomical consistency across specimens.

Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The region of interest (ROI) for woven bone analysis was defined as the interradicular and interalveolar trabecular bone between the roots of the first and second molars on the left maxilla, excluding the periodontal ligament, cementum, and cortical bone margins.

Woven bone was identified histologically as immature bone characterized by irregular trabecular architecture, disorganized collagen arrangement, abundant osteoid matrix, and numerous osteocyte lacunae lacking lamellar organization. Quantitative analysis of the woven bone area was performed using NIS-Elements D software (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan) based on high-resolution images captured under a light microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) at standardized magnification ( × 100).

For each specimen, three non-overlapping fields within the defined ROI were analyzed per section and three sections per sample were used. The woven bone area (μm2) was quantified by threshold-based segmentation of the eosinophilic osteoid matrix. All measurements were conducted in triplicate by a blinded examiner to minimize observer bias.

Immuhistochemical analysis of IL-10 and TNF-α expression

Consecutive sections from the same left maxillary molar region were used for immunohistochemical analysis to ensure anatomical consistency with the histological evaluation. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with a peroxidase blocking solution to eliminate endogenous enzyme activity. The slides were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with monoclonal antibodies against TNF-α (Bio X Cell, Lebanon, NH, USA) and IL-10 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Signals were detected using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine chromogen (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Positive immunoreactivity was indicated by brown cytoplasmic staining in macrophage cells. The evaluation was carried out within the same ROI used for woven bone analysis by employing the same imaging and analysis settings to ensure comparability among samples.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All data were represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilk's test was used to confirm normality (p > 0.05) and Levene's test to assess homogeneity of variance (p > 0.05). One-way analysis of variance was conducted to detect significant differences, which were analyzed using Tukey's post hoc test (p < 0.05).

Discussion

OTM promotes bone resorption on the compression side and stimulates bone growth on the tension side in reaction to mechanical pressure. The applied force results in localized hypoxia and fluid displacement within the compression zone, leading to an aseptic inflammatory response.30 Phytochemical screening of HS-CE confirmed the presence of flavonoids, phenolics, alkaloids, terpenoids, and tannins, which are widely recognized for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic activities.31, 32, 33 These constituents provide a mechanistic basis for the observed effects of HS-CE in modulating immune responses and promoting bone regeneration during the relapse phase following OTM. Previous studies have examined the impacts of HS flower extracts on inflammatory markers and bone apposition during the active phase of tooth movement, but the effects of HS-CE during post-treatment relapse have not been studied.22

In this study, HS-CE administration resulted in increased IL-10 expression, reduced TNF-α levels, and enhanced woven bone formation during the relapse phase, suggesting that these phytochemical components of HS-CE created a local microenvironment that was conducive to bone stability. Consistent with these molecular and histological changes, HS-CE also significantly decreased the relapse distance compared with PBS, with reductions evident on both day 7 and day 14. This functional improvement highlights the translational relevance of cytokine modulation and bone regeneration by HS-CE in limiting tooth displacement after treatment.

IL-10 is an anti-inflammatory cytokine with vital roles in regulating immune responses and maintaining bone homeostasis by inhibiting osteoclast differentiation and reducing bone resorption.15,17 The significant upregulation of IL-10 expression in the HS-CE group on both day 7 and day 14 of the relapse phase suggests that HS promoted an anti-inflammatory environment during this time. IL-10 expression peaked on day 14 of the relapse phase, and this temporal increase may suggest sustained activation of anti-inflammatory pathways by the extract. These results are consistent with previous studies of the antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties of HS-CE, which were largely attributed to its high anthocyanin content.34, 35, 36, 37 Delphinidin-3-sambubioside (Dp3Sam), a key anthocyanin in HS-CE, has been shown to inhibit the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways.38 Dp3Sam exerts immunomodulatory effects by enhancing IL-10 production, which leads to downregulation of receptor activator of NF-κB ligand expression and reductions in the levels of IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic 1, while simultaneously upregulating osteoprotegerin. Their combined effects inhibit inflammation-induced bone resorption.23,39,40

By contrast, HS-CE significantly inhibited TNF-α. The observed increase in TNF-α in the PBS group from days 7–14 of relapse agrees with previous findings, highlighting the heightened inflammatory response during the relapse phase.41 TNF-α is a proinflammatory cytokine produced by various cell types, such as macrophages and periodontal ligament cells. Elevated TNF-α levels have been observed in gingival crevicular fluid during OTM, highlighting its role in inflammation and bone remodeling.16,42 In this study, the lowest TNF-α concentrations were observed in the HS-CE group on day 14 of the relapse phase. Similarly, a previous study demonstrated that quercetin-3′-o-glucoside, an anthocyanin component of HS, can inhibit TNF-α expression.42 In addition, the polyphenol content of HS has been shown to effectively impede the activity of TNF-α, which is a known trigger of inflammation.43

Histological evaluations indicated significantly greater woven bone growth in the HS-CE group compared with the PBS group at both time points in the relapse phase. The augmented bone area observed in the HS-CE group aligns with the favorable cytokine profile characterized by increased IL-10 and reduced TNF-α levels promoting the formation of a localized microenvironment that was conducive to osteoblast-mediated bone deposition.44

Previous studies have demonstrated that the highest rate of orthodontic relapse occurs immediately after appliance removal and continues through day 7 of the relapse phase. Subsequently, the rate of relapse gradually declines, stabilizing by approximately day 21.45, 46, 47 In the present study, administration of HS-CE significantly increased IL-10 expression, reduced the TNF-α levels, and promoted woven bone formation at day 7. These findings indicate that HS-CE effectively modulated the early inflammatory response and enhanced bone regeneration during the critical initial phase of relapse. In addition, the significant increase in bone area from day 7 to day 14 of the relapse phase indicates ongoing bone remodeling and stabilization.

In addition to cytokine modulation, woven bone formation was significantly enhanced in the HS-CE group. Woven bone represents an essential stage of early bone repair, providing provisional structural support and a framework for subsequent lamellar bone maturation. The increase in the woven bone area observed in this study reflects an active osteoblast response, facilitated by phytochemicals in HS-CE such as vitamin C, which promotes collagen synthesis and osteoblast differentiation.48, 49, 50 Strengthening the anchorage zone through woven bone deposition is a key factor for reducing the relapse risk because it stabilizes the periodontal ligament–bone interface during the critical retention phase.51,52

These findings highlight the dual mechanistic effect of HS-CE on preventing relapse by upregulating IL-10 and suppressing TNF-α, while also facilitating early bone regeneration through the formation of woven bone. The observed reduction in relapse distance further emphasizes the importance of these effects.

HS-CE has potential as a natural therapeutic adjuvant for enhancing post-treatment stability and reducing orthodontic relapse. In this study, the administration of HS-CE significantly increased IL-10 expression, reduced the TNF-α levels, promoted woven bone formation, and decreased the relapse distance day 7, indicating its ability to modulate early inflammation and accelerate bone regeneration to mechanically stabilize the tooth position during the critical initial phase of relapse. The continued increase in the bone area from day 7 to day 14 further suggests that HS-CE supports sustained bone remodeling and consolidation. Evaluating these two key time points in the present study provided a more comprehensive understanding of the temporal biological responses associated with relapse prevention. The combined use of histological and immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated the consistent biological effects of HS-CE within alveolar bone tissues, and the intrasulcular route of administration is a minimally invasive and clinically applicable approach for local therapy.

However, extended observation periods, varying dosages, and molecular level studies are required to further elucidate the mechanisms and optimize the clinical potential of HS-CE in maintaining post-orthodontic stability.

Conclusions

This study examined the effectiveness of HS-CE in modulating immune responses and aiding bone regeneration during the relapse phase after OTM. The phytochemical components of HS-CE enhanced IL-10 expression, reduced the TNF-α levels, and promoted woven bone formation. These outcomes correlated with a significant decrease in the relapse distance, suggesting improved post-treatment stability. HS-CE has potential as a natural adjuvant for preventing orthodontic relapse, but further research is needed for quantitative profiling, dosage optimization, and clarifying molecular pathways.
