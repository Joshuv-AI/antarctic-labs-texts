Identification and characterization of the Quinoa AP2/ERF gene family and their expression patterns in response to salt stress
Authors: Bahlanes Bakhtari, Hooman Razi, Abbas Alemzadeh, Ali Dadkhodaie, Ali Moghadam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The APETALA2/ETHYLENE RESPONSIVE FACTOR (AP2/ERF) transcription factors play crucial roles in plant growth, development, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. This study was performed to comprehensively identify and characterize the AP2/ERF gene family in quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.), a highly resilient pseudocereal crop known for its salinity tolerance. A total of 150 CqAP2/ERF genes were identified in the quinoa genome; these genes were unevenly distributed across the chromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis divided the CqAP2/ERFs into five subfamilies: 71 ERF, 49 DREB, 23 AP2, 3 RAV, and 4 Soloist. Additionally, the DREB and ERF subfamilies were subdivided into four and seven subgroups, respectively. The exon-intron structure of the putative CqAP2/ERF genes and the conserved motifs of their encoded proteins were also identified, showing general conservation within the phylogenetic subgroups. Promoter analysis revealed many cis-regulatory elements associated with light, hormones, and response mechanisms within the promoter regions of CqAP2/ERF genes. Synteny analysis revealed that segmental duplication under purifying selection pressure was the major evolutionary driver behind the expansion of the CqAP2/ERF gene family. The protein-protein interaction network predicted the pivotal CqAP2/ERF proteins and their interactions involved in the regulation of various biological processes including stress response mechanisms. The expression profiles obtained from RNA-seq and qRT-PCR data detected several salt-responsive CqAP2/ERF genes, particularly from the ERF, DREB, and RAV subfamilies, with varying up- and downregulation patterns, indicating their potential roles in salt stress responses in quinoa. Overall, this study provides insights into the structural and evolutionary features of the AP2/ERF gene family in quinoa, offering candidate genes for further analysis of their roles in salt tolerance and molecular breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81046-1.

Introduction

Soil and water salinization are critical constraints on crop production and increasingly threaten the sustainability of agricultural systems worldwide. Approximately 20% of the world’s cultivated land and 33% of irrigated agricultural land are adversely affected by high salinity1. High salinity commonly has harmful effects on the physiological functions of crop plants, leading to growth slowdown and yield loss2.

A promising strategy for coping with high soil salinity is the cultivation of alternative salinity-tolerant crop species on saline soils. Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is a typical example of a so-called “cash crop halophytes”3. Quinoa, belonging to the Amaranthaceae family, is a facultative halophytic pseudocereal crop originating from the Andean region of South America4. Quinoa has drawn increasing attention in recent years because its seeds are rich in high-quality protein, minerals, vitamins, and several health-beneficial phytochemicals4. Additionally, quinoa is recognized as a candidate crop for coping with increasing environmental stresses under the scenario of global climatic change due to its high tolerance to salinity and other abiotic stresses, including frost, drought, and poor soils5. The publication of the quinoa genome sequence6 has provided the foundation for comprehensive molecular analysis of the genes underlying abiotic stress tolerance mechanisms.

Plants utilize complex cellular and molecular regulatory mechanisms that trigger various biochemical and physiological responses to environmental challenges7. Transcription factors (TFs) are key regulatory proteins involved in modulating plant biological processes through the regulation of the transcription of target genes8. Several transcription factor families, such as APETALA2/Ethylene Responsive Factor (AP2/ERF)9, bHLH10, MYB11, Dof12and WRKY13, have been shown to be crucial in plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses14.

The AP2/ERF superfamily is a broad group of plant-specific transcription factors. The AP2/ERF proteins are characterized by a highly conserved AP2/ERF DNA binding domain comprising approximately 60–70 amino acid residues15. The AP2/ERF family members can be classified into the AP2, DREB, ERF, RAV, and Soloist subfamilies based on the number of AP2/ERF domains and the amino acid sequence similarity16. The AP2 subfamily contains two AP2/ERF domains and is further subdivided into AP2 and AINTEGUMENTA monophyletic groups17. The DREB and ERF subfamilies possess a single AP2/ERF domain as well as a conserved WLG motif18. While the DREB proteins normally bind to the cis-acting dehydration-responsive element/C repeat (DRE/CRT) with a core motif of CCGAC, the ERF proteins interact with the ethylene-responsive element (ERE) with a core sequence of AGCCCGCC19. Members of the RAV subfamily have two DNA binding domains, one AP2/ERF domain and one B3 domain, which is a plant-specific protein domain exclusively identified in transcription factors20. The remaining AP2/ERF family members with an AP2-like domain and different gene structures are frequently known as Soloists21.

AP2/ERF transcription factors are essential regulators involved in plant organ development22, hormone-signaling pathways23, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses24,25. In particular, the involvement of the DREB and ERF subfamilies in salinity tolerance has been revealed in various crop species such as soybean26 (Glycine max), rice27 (Oryza sativa), common bean28 (Phaseolus vulgaris), maize29 (Zea mays), and wheat30,31 (Triticum aestivum). Several AP2/ERFmutants exhibited altered sensitivity to hormones and abiotic stresses, implying that this family of transcription factors mediates responses to environmental and endogenous stimuli and participates in different regulatory processes32.

With the increasing availability of plant genome sequence data, many AP2/ERFencoding genes have been discovered and characterized in several plant species such as Arabidopsis33 (Arabidopsis thaliana), grapevine34 (Vitis vinifera), rapeseed35 (Brassica napus), sugarcane36 (Saccharum spontaneum), wheat37, peanut38 (Arachis hypogaea), and maize39. Recently, transcriptome analysis illustrated the differential expression of several quinoa AP2/ERFencoding genes in response to high-temperature stress40, phosphorous levels41, and relative humidity stress42, however, little is known about the characterization of the AP2/ERF gene family in quinoa and their expression profiles under salt stress conditions. The discovery of quinoa AP2/ERF (CqAP2/ERF) genes at a genome-wide scale and exploration of their functions, target genes, and molecular pathways can aid in better elucidating the molecular mechanisms of tolerance to abiotic stresses in quinoa. In the present study, members of the AP2/ERF gene family were identified in the quinoa genome. Subsequently, the CqAP2/ERF genes were subjected to various computational approaches to determine gene structure, conserved protein domains, phylogenetic relationships, cis-regulatory elements, chromosomal locations, synteny, and duplication events. Furthermore, the expression profiles of several CqAP2/ERF genes under salinity conditions were determined via transcriptome and qRT-PCR analyses. The findings of this study enhance our insights into the evolution and biological functions of CqAP2/ERF genes and provide useful information for further investigations aimed at improving salinity tolerance in quinoa.

Materials and methods

Genomic identification and classification of CqAP2/ERF genes

The quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa v1.0) genome database was obtained from the Phytozome v13 (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/pz/portal.html) and ChenopodiumDB (https://www.cbrc.kaust.edu.sa/chenopodiumdb/). The Arabidopsis AP2/ERF sequences were retrieved from the Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) (http://www.arabidopsis.org) and were used as queries by searching against the quinoa genome databases using the BLASTP program with default parameters43. Thereafter, all the candidate CqAP2/ERF protein sequences were checked and verified for the presence of the AP2 (PF00847) and B3 (PF02362) domains by PFAM (http://pfam.xfam.org/) and the Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool (SMART) (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de). The molecular weights (MWs) and the theoretical isoelectric points (pIs) of the identified CqAP2/ERF proteins were obtained by using Protein Calculator v3.4 (https://protcalc.sourceforge.net/). The online tool BUSCA (https://busca.biocomp.unibo.it/) was utilized to predict the subcellular locations of the CqAP2/ERF proteins.

Phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, and conserved motif analysis

Multiple sequence alignment of the deduced amino acid sequences of the Arabidopsis and quinoa AP2/ERF proteins was performed using ClustalW in the MEGA-X program44 under default parameters. The phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the MEGA-X program based on the neighbor-joining method with the following settings: a bootstrap value of 2,000, pairwise deletion, and Poisson correction. The web program iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/)45 was used to further process the obtained phylogenetic tree. Conserved motifs of the CqAP2/ERF proteins were identified using the online tool Multiple EM for Motif Elicitation (MEME) version 5.4.1 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme), with the following parameters: (1) the number of occurrences of a single motif among the sequences within the model was zero or one per sequence; (2) the maximum number of motifs detected was set to 30; (3) the optimum motif width was set to ≥ 6 and ≤ 50; and (4) motifs with E-values less than 0.05 were selected. Finally, TBtools software46 was used to combine and visualize the results of the phylogenetic tree, the gene structure analysis, and the conserved motif identification.

Chromosomal localization, gene duplication, and syntenic analysis

The chromosomal location information of the CqAP2/ERF genes was searched against the Oma browser (https://omabrowser.org/oma/genome/CHEQI/info/) and the locations obtained were mapped using MG2C (MapGene2Chromosome v2.1; http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_ v2.1/)47. To detect gene duplication, the gene pairs with greater than 70% coding sequence identity were regarded as duplicated CqAP2/ERFgenes48. A duplicated gene pair found across distinct chromosomes is referred to as a segmentally duplicated gene pair, while a duplicated gene pair located close to each other on the same chromosome is classified as a tandemly duplicated gene pair49. TBtools software V2.02646 was employed to analyze and visualize the duplication events of the CqAP2/ERF genes. Accordingly, the non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka) and synonymous substitution rate (Ks) were calculated, and the Ka/Ks ratios were obtained to determine the selection pressure for each pair of duplicated CqAP2/ERFgenes. The Ka/Ks < 1, Ka/Ks = 1, and Ka/Ks > 1 indicate the purifying, neutral, and positive selection, respectively50. The divergence time (million years ago, Mya) of duplicated gene pairs was estimated using the formula T = Ks/(2λ) × 10−6, where λ = 6.5 × 109,51. The syntenic relationships of AP2/ERF genes between quinoa and three plant species from the Amaranthaceae family (Sugar beet (Beta vulgaris), Amaranth (Amaranthus hypochondriacus), and Spinach (Spinacia oleracea)) as well as Arabidopsis were analyzed by TBtools software V2.02646.

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements in the promoter regions of CqAP2/ERFs

To further study the functions of the CqAP2/ERF genes, the 2 kb upstream regions of the CqAP2/ERF genes were extracted from the quinoa genome sequence for promoter analysis. These sequences were searched against the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to detect putative cis-regulatory elements within the promoter regions.

Protein-protein interaction network and gene ontology

The protein-protein interaction network among CqAP2/ERF proteins was predicted based on the Arabidopsis AP2/ERF orthologous proteins using STRING v12 software (http://string-db.org). Subsequently, the Cytoscape software v3.8.2 (https://cytoscape.org/) was used to find and visualize protein-protein interaction networks comprising highly interactive AP2/ERF proteins. To better understand the functions of the CqAP2/ERFgenes, Gene Ontology (GO) annotation, and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes)52 pathway enrichment analysis were performed with OmicsBox software v3.1.2 (https://www.biobam.com/omicsbox).

RNA-seq data analysis

The publicly available RNA-Seq data generated from shoots and roots of the quinoa genotype Q30 under control and salinity-treated conditions (NCBI Bioproject number: PRJNA605324)53 were utilized to identify the CqAP2/ERF genes with differential expression in response to salinity. Reads were trimmed based on a minimum score of 0.05 to remove low-quality sequences and a maximum number of two ambiguous nucleotides. Moreover, adapter sequences and reads shorter than 15 nucleotides were excluded. The clean reads were then mapped to the quinoa reference genome obtained from Phytozome V13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Cquinoav1.0). The expression levels of the genes were calculated using the TPM (transcript per million) method. The quantile method54 was used to normalize the expression data. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected using Baggerley’s statistical test, implemented in CLC Genomics Workbench 21 software, with a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.05 and a fold change ≥ |1.5|. The expression profiles of the CqAP2/ERFgenes were visualized as a heatmap using TBtools46.

qRT-PCR analysis

The salinity experiment was conducted with three replicates under controlled greenhouse conditions (16 h daylight; 25 °C/15°C day/night temperature) at the School of Agriculture, Shiraz University, Shiraz, Iran. Seeds of the quinoa genotype Bouchane-4 (kindly supplied by Professor Christian Jung and Dr. Nazgol Emrani, Kiel University, Germany) were sown in plastic pots (diameter: 21 cm; height: 18 cm) filled with a sterilized mixture of silty loam soil: leaf mold (2:1 v: v). The soil mixture had an electrical conductivity (EC) of 1.6 ds/m. Quinoa plants with four pairs of fully developed leaves [growth stage code: 14 according to BBCH scale55) were subjected to salinity treatment (300 mM NaCl). Leaf and root samples were collected at 0 (non-stress control), 6, and 24 h after applying salinity treatment. The plant materials were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80ºC.

Real-time quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was used to explore the expression patterns of six CqAP2/ERF family genes, CqRAV02, CqERF54, CqERF50, CqDREB12, CqERF37, and CqDREB02, in the leaves and roots of the quinoa genotype, Bouchane-4 in response to salinity. Total RNA was isolated from the samples using a column RNA isolation kit (DENA Zist Asia, Iran) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and quality were determined using Nanodrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). DNA contamination was removed from RNA using DNaseI treatment (Thermo Scientific, USA). Next, the first strand of cDNA was synthesized with an Easy cDNA Synthesis kit (Parstous, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems™ StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System using the RealQ Plus 2x Master Mix Green with high ROX™ (Ampliqon, Denmark) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The sequences of primer used are listed in Supplementary File S1. According to Zhu et al.56, the expression level of quinoa elongation factor1 alpha (CqELF1α; GenBank accession number: AUR62027945) was used as the internal control gene. The expression assays were conducted with three biological and two technical replicates for each gene. The 2−∆∆CTmethod57 was used to determine the relative expression of the genes. The data were subjected to an analysis of variance, and statistical significance among mean values was determined by Tukey’s test (p ≤ 0.05).

Results

Genomic identification and classification of CqAP2/ERF genes

A total of 150 putative AP2/ERF genes were identified in the quinoa genome. The CqAP2/ERF genes were named according to their classification of the AP2/ERF superfamily and chromosomal locations. Based on the amino acid sequence similarity and the number of the conserved AP2/ERF domains, the CqAP2/ERF genes could be classified into five subfamilies: the AP2 subfamily (23 genes; CqAP2-01 to CqAP2-23), the DREB subfamily (49 genes; CqDREB01 to CqDREB49), the ERF subfamily (71 Genes; CqERF01 to CqERF71), the RAV subfamily (3 genes, CqRAV01 to CqRAV03) and the Soloist subfamily (4 genes, CqSoloist01 to CqSoloist04). The CqAP2/ERF genes exhibited a wide range of coding sequence lengths from 285 bp (CqDREB19) to 1851 bp (CqERF52) resulting in proteins with 94 to 616 amino acids. The molecular weights of the predicted CqAP2/ERF proteins varied from 10.82 kDa (CqDREB19) to 67.46 kDa (CqERF52). Moreover, the theoretical isoelectric points (pIs) of the CqAP2/ERF proteins broadly ranged from 4.03 (CqDREB47) to 10.9 (CqDREB04). The results of subcellular localization analysis revealed that the vast majority of CqAP2/ERFs (132 members) were localized in the nucleus, while 16 members were predicted to be localized in chloroplast, and the remaining two members were recognized to be in the extracellular space. Detailed information on the CqAP2/ERF genes is provided in Supplementary File S2.

Phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, and conserved motif analysis

To understand the evolutionary relationships of AP2/ERF transcription factors in quinoa, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the deduced amino acid sequences of 150 CqAP2/ERF genes and 147 A. thaliana AP2/ERF genes (Fig. 1). The phylogenetic tree classified the CqAP2/ERF genes into ERF, DREB, AP2, RAV, and Soloist clades, which are analogous to those in Arabidopsis. According to the classification system of Arabidopsis AP2/ERFs16, the DREB subfamily of CqAP2/ERF was divided into four subclades: I (10 members), II (6 members), III (22 members), and IV (11 members). The ERF subfamily, the largest group of CqAP2/ERF proteins with 71 members was further divided into seven subclades: V (11 members), VI (8 members), VII (7 members), VI-L (2 members), VIII (14 members), IX (20 members), and X (9 members). No proteins belonging to the Xb-L subgroup of Arabidopsis ERFs were found in quinoa. There was only the subgroup V of ERFs for which quinoa substantially had a greater number of members than Arabidopsis (11 vs. 5). The phylogenetic tree also revealed that 23 CqAP2/ERFs together with 18 Arabidopsis AP2/ERFs constituted the AP2 subfamily, while the RAV and Soloist clades comprised 3 and 4 members from quinoa as well as 6 and 1 members from Arabidopsis, respectively.

Phylogenetic tree of the AP2/ERF family members in Chenopodium quinoa (Cq), and A. thaliana (At). ERF, DREB, AP2, RAV, and Soloist subfamilies were presented in different colors.

To perceive the structural diversity of the CqAP2/ERF genes, we determined the exon-intron organization of the CqAP2/ERF genes. Gene structural analysis revealed that the number of exons in the CqAP2/ERF genes varied from 1 to 12, and 43.3% of the genes had no introns in their open reading frame regions (Fig. 2). The quinoa AP2 subfamily genes exhibited complex structures; that is, all the members, had introns ranging from 3 to 11 (Fig. 2). CqAP2-11 was the gene with the greatest number of exons (12 exons). The majority of members of the RAV, DREB, and ERF subfamilies had only one exon which encompasses a sequence region encoding the AP2 domain (Fig. 2). The results also showed that two Soloist members had one exon, while the other two members had six exons (Fig. 2). Most of the 120 CqDREB and CqERF genes had zero or one intron; only eight genes, namely, CqERF17, CqERF15, CqERF35, CqERF63, CqERF52, CqERF33, CqERF09, CqERF57 and CqERF47 were found to have two introns, while CqERF12 contained three introns (Fig. 2). In general, the phylogenetically closely related members from the same subfamily had similar exon-intron structures in terms of the number of introns and lengths of exons. Noticeably, very large introns (> 5421 bp) were identified in a few DREB, and ERF members, including CqERF18, CqERF12, CqDREB06, CqERF64, CqERF52, and CqDREB14.

The phylogenetic relationships (A), compositions of conserved protein motifs (B), positions of protein domains (C), and the exon-intron structures (D) of quinoa AP2/ERFs.

To further explore the evolutionary connections and functional classification of CqAP2/ERF proteins, 30 conserved motifs, designated motifs 1–30, were analyzed using the MEME tool (Fig. 2). In addition to the domains present in AP2/ERF proteins, the CqAP2/ERF proteins contain some conserved motifs that may play roles in transcriptional activity, protein-protein interactions, and DNA localization. The size of the motifs ranged from 11 to 50 amino acids. Motif analysis revealed that the motif 1 and the motif 3 were common in almost all CqAP2/ERF proteins. Motif 2 was also highly conserved among the members of the ERF, and DREB subfamilies. Indeed, these conserved motifs were present within the AP2 domain of CqAP2/ERF proteins. The AP2 domain is generally characterized by the conserved elements identified within the motifs 1–3. Motif 1 contained RAYD and WLG elements, the latter of which is highly conserved between C. quinoaand other species, such as Arabidopsis. Furthermore, the conserved elements YRG and EIR were detected within motif 2, while motif 3 had the conserved amino acid sequence LNFP, located in the C-terminal region of the AP2 domain, which is also present in some proteins involved in disease resistance58,59. The results also uncovered that some protein motifs were specific for members of particular subfamilies or groups, suggesting functional conservation in different groups. For instance, motif 28 was identified as a conserved element of the B3 DNA-binding domain in RAV subfamily proteins (Fig. 2). Outside the AP2 and B3 domains, motifs 5, 7, 9, 14, and 4 existed only in the AP2 subfamily proteins (Fig. 2). Moreover, motif 12 was specifically detected in the members of ERF subgroup V, while motif 19 containing a small block of the conserved amino acid sequences, MCGGAI, was only observed in the members of ERF subgroup VII. DREB proteins also acquired specific conserved motifs. Motif 10 was discovered in the members of DREB subgroup IV, while motif 8, which contains two blocks of the conserved residues, LPR[P/A] and [L/R]AAA, was detected in DREB subgroup III. The sequences of the conserved motifs are listed in Supplementary File S3.

Chromosomal localization, gene duplication, and syntenic analysis

The chromosomal locations of the CqAP2/ERF genes were determined and mapped onto the corresponding chromosomes (Fig. 3). The 144 CqAP2/ERF genes were unevenly distributed on the 18 quinoa chromosomes, while six CqAP2/ERF genes were not mapped to any chromosome and were therefore assigned to chromosome zero. The chromosome distribution pattern of the CqAP2/ERF genes showed that they were dispersed individually or in clusters formed in different regions of the quinoa chromosomes. The most CqAP2/ERF genes were located on chromosome 7 (22 genes) followed by chromosome 16 (12 genes), and chromosome 1 (13 genes). On the other hand, the minimum number of the CqAP2/ERF genes were located on chromosome 6 (2 genes). Interestingly, at least one of the 71 CqERF genes could be found on each of the 18 chromosomes. Except for chromosomes 5, 12, and 17; at least one of the 49 CqDREB genes could be detected on the other chromosomes (Fig. 3).

Chromosomal distributions of the quinoa AP2/ERF (CqAP2/ERF) genes. The ERF subfamily genes are represented in black; the DREB subfamily genes in blue; the AP2 subfamily genes in green, the RAV subfamily genes in red, and the Soloist subfamily genes in brown.

Due to the significant role of genome-wide duplication, either tandem or segmental, in the expansion and subsequent potential functional diversification of plant gene families during evolution60, we investigated duplication events among CqAP2/ERF genes across the quinoa genome. A total of 66 pairs of duplicated genes were identified, of which two pairs were regarded as tandemly duplicated genes including CqAP2-13/CqAP2-16 located on chromosome 9 and CqERF02/CqERF03 located on the unassigned fragment of the quinoa genome designated chromosome zero (Fig. 4). Segmental duplication events greatly contributed to the expansion of the AP2/ERF family in quinoa. Eleven pairs of AP2 subfamily members, 30 pairs of ERF subfamily members, 22 pairs of DREB subfamily members, one pair of RAV subfamily members, and two pairs of Soloist subfamily members were the consequences of segmental duplications of the quinoa genome (Fig. 4). Furthermore, all chromosomes of quinoa contained duplicated AP2/ERF family members (Fig. 4). Chromosomes 7, 1, 16 and 9 contained more duplicated CqAP2/ERF gene pairs than the other chromosomes. In addition, the Ka/Ks ratio was calculated for each duplicated CqAP2/ERF gene pair to determine which type of selection pressure was prevalent during the evolution of the CqAP2/ERF gene family. The Ka/Ks values for all the duplicated CqAP2/ERF gene pairs were less than 1 (except for CqERF14/CqERF04), ranging from 0 to 2.28, with an average of 0.27 (Supplementary File S4). Thus, these genes might be under intense purifying selection pressure, leading to the maintenance of their original functions. Furthermore, it was estimated that the tandem and segmental duplication events among the 66 CqAP2/ERF duplicated gene pairs might have occurred 3.25 to 52.2 million years ago (mya), while the segmentally duplicated genes CqDREB46/CqDREB06 and CqERF62/CqERF15 were much more ancient, diverging 162.59 and 227.44 mya, respectively (Supplementary File S4).

A comparative syntenic analysis was carried out to discover orthologous relationships of AP2/ERF genes between quinoa and three other Amaranthaceae plant species, Sugar beet (Beta vulgaris), Amaranth (Amaranthus hypochondriacus), and Spinach (Spinacia oleracea) as well as the model species A. thaliana (Fig. 5). In total, 125, 114, 81, and 71 CqAP2/ERF genes were found to be orthologous to genes in sugar beet, Amaranth, Arabidopsis, and spinach, respectively (Supplementary File S5). Orthologous genes were identified across all chromosomes of quinoa. Some CqAP2/ERF genes such as CqERF64, CqERF54, CqERF40, CqERF09, and CqDREB47 were associated with at least three orthologous gene pairs (particularly AP2/ERF genes in Beta vulgaris and Amaranthus hypochondriacus), indicating that these genes potentially played important roles in CqAP2/ERF superfamily evolution. Analysis of orthologous relationships (Fig. 5) revealed a syntenic bias toward particular genomic regions in Amaranth and Spinach, implying that chromosomal rearrangement events might influence the distribution of AP2/ERF genes in the genomes of these species. Notably, 19 CqAP2/ERF genes (CqERF29, CqERF37, CqDREB48, CqERF08, CqDREB20, CqSoloist03, CqAP2-06, CqDREB26, CqAP2-17, CqERF14, CqDREB24, CqDREB02, CqERF03, CqERF02, CqERF67, CqDREB03, CqERF49, CqSoloist02, and CqERF04) were absent in the other species examined (Supplementary File S5).

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements in the promoter regions of CqAP2/ERFs

To explore the regulatory functions of CqAP2/ERF genes, 2000 bp sequences upstream of the initiation codon were analyzed to identify putative cis-regulatory elements. The identified cis-regulatory elements within the promoter regions of the CqAP2/ERF genes were classified into 21 groups according to their functions associated with growth and development, hormones, and stress responses (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figure S1). The results showed that light-responsiveness elements were common in the promoters of all the CqAP2/ERF genes. The cis-regulatory elements recognized by the MYB and MYC transcription factors were present in the promoters of 84.6% (127 members) and 80% (120 members) of the CqAP2/ERF genes, respectively. Twelve different types of hormone-responsive elements were discovered, including those related to abscisic acid (ABRE), auxin (TGA-element, AuxRR-core, and TGA-box), methyl jasmonate (MeJA) (TGACG-motif, CCGTCC-box and CGTCA-motif), gibberellin (TATC-box, P-box and GARE-motif) and salicylic acid (TCA-element and SARE). A total of 67.3% (101 members), 66% (99 Members), 24% (36 members), 36% (54 members), and 28.6% (43 members) of the CqAP2/ERF genes contained abscisic acid-, MeJA-, auxin-, gibberellin-, and salicylic acid-responsive elements within their promoter regions, respectively. Furthermore, 27.3% (41 members), 28% (42 members), 15.3% (23 members), and 60% (90 members) of the CqAP2/ERF genes had stress-related cis-regulatory elements within their promoter regions involved in low-temperature responsiveness (LTR), drought induction (MBS), defense responsiveness (TC-rich repeats), and anaerobic induction (ARE), respectively (Fig. 6). These results revealed that the quinoa AP2/ERF superfamily genes might contribute to modulating biotic or abiotic stress responses as well as hormone signaling mechanisms.

Protein-protein interaction network and gene ontology

To infer protein-protein interactions between CqAP2/ERF and other proteins in quinoa, an interaction network was constructed based on Arabidopsis orthologs using the STRING database (Fig. 7a). The network encompassed 50 nodes comprising 40 Arabidopsis AP2/ERF and 10 other proteins that strongly interact with AP2/ERF proteins. According to the predicted network, 85 CqAP2/ERF proteins appeared in the known Arabidopsis protein-protein interaction network indicating that similar protein interactions may occur in quinoa. Notably, the network revealed that many Arabidopsis AP2/ERF proteins (29 out of 40) showed high similarity with multiple CqAP2/ERF proteins (up to 4 quinoa AP2/ERF for each Arabidopsis AP2/ERF), suggesting similar and synergistic regulatory functions of some CqAP2/ERF members. Furthermore, the highly interactive AP2/ERF and related proteins were identified as hub proteins and an interaction network was visualized in Arabidopsis (Fig. 7b). As a result, the AP2/ERF proteins including AP2 (CqAP2-11 and CqAP2-15), RAV1 (CqRAV02), F25G13.130 (Cqsoloist01 and Cqsoloist04), DREB1B (CqDREB49, CqDREB17, CqDREB16, and CqDREB48), RAP2-2 (CqERF71 and CqERF32), DREB1D (CqDREB24 and CqDREB43), ERF4 (CqERF12), ERF5 (CqERF65, CqERF53, and CqERF48), ERF017 (CqDREB20 and CqDREB02), ERF053 (CqDREB40 and CqDREB03), ERF109 (CqERF37), and RAP2-3 (CqERF24 and CqERF41) were known as the key AP2/ERF genes which their encoded proteins play important roles in the protein-protein interaction network involved in the regulation of the relevant biological processes. Altogether, protein interaction analysis provided an intuitive way to predict the functions of quinoa AP2/ERF proteins based on the known functions of Arabidopsis AP2/ERF proteins.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed to better understand the functional relevance of the AP2/ERF gene family in quinoa. 117 CqAP2/ERF genes were ontologically annotated and enriched in various functional categories of the GO terms “biological process”, “molecular function”, and “cellular component”. A total of 117 genes of the annotated genes were discovered to have DNA-binding transcription factor activity, according to the GO molecular function. Within the biological process ontology, the functional categories of “Regulation of transcription DNA-templated transcription” (101 genes), “Positive regulation of gene expression” (33 genes), “Ethylene-activated signaling pathway” (31 genes), “Response to xenobiotic stimulus” (27 genes), “Response to chitin” (22 genes), and “Response to water deprivation” (18 genes) were the most highly represented functional categories (Fig. 8). Moreover, the category “Response to salt stress” which included seven genes (CqDREB03, CqDREB09, CqDREB25, CqDREB32, CqDREB40, CqDREB41, and CqERF59) was among the significantly enriched categories. According to the cellular component ontology, the annotated CqAP2/ERF genes were associated with the nucleus (59 genes), cytoplasm (four genes), plasma membrane (four genes), and chloroplast (two genes) (Fig. 8). In addition, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that some CqAP2/ERF genes were significantly linked to three pathways, namely, “plant hormone signal transduction”, “MAPK signaling pathway”, and “Plant-pathogen interaction” (Fig. 8). Taken together, these results suggested the involvement of CqAP2/ERF genes in crucial cellular activities in quinoa.

RNA-seq data analysis

The expression patterns of CqAP2/ERF genes in salt-stressed shoots and roots of quinoa were analyzed using publicly available transcriptome data. Consequently, 59 CqAP2/ERF genes, represented in a heatmap (Fig. 9), were significantly differentially expressed in response to salt stress. Most of the CqAP2/ERF genes (54 genes) showed differential expression in the root transcriptome, of which 50 genes were upregulated and four genes were downregulated in response to salt stress. Additionally, 22 differentially expressed CqAP2/ERF genes were detected in the shoot transcriptome including 18 up- and four downregulated genes. The results identified 17 common CqAP2/ERF genes between root and shoot transcriptomes, while the other CqAP2/ERF genes exhibited organ-specific expression including five shoot-specific and 37 root-specific genes, implying the functional divergence of CqAP2/ERF genes among different organs. There was only one common gene, CqRAV02, which showed an opposite expression regulation between the roots (upregulated) and shoots (downregulated) in response to salt stress conditions. The genes CqDREB36, CqDREB02, and CqERF29 were upregulated to the greatest extent in the roots, while the genes CqERF37, CqDREB02, and CqERF30 were most upregulated in the shoots under salt stress conditions (Fig. 9). On the other hand, CqERF44 and CqERF40 showed the highest downregulation levels in the roots and shoots, respectively, in response to salt stress (Fig. 9).

qRT-PCR analysis

Among the differentially expressed CqAP2/ERF genes identified by quinoa transcriptome analysis, we selected six genes including CqDREB02, CqDREB12, CqERF37, CqERF50, CqERF54, and CqRAV02 from three different AP2/ERF subfamilies (DREB, ERF, and RAV) to further evaluate their expression profiles in response to salt stress using qRT-PCR (Fig. 10). Moreover, these genes were selected for qRT-PCR verification because, based on the RNA-seq results, they showed different expression patterns in response to salt stress. Accordingly, CqDREB12 and CqERF54 were exclusively expressed in the shoots while exhibiting contrasting expression change patterns. The expression of CqRAV02 differed between shoots and roots in response to salinity, and the remaining three genes (CqDREB02, CqERF37, and CqERF50) showed increased expression in both the shoots and roots of quinoa plants. Additionally, five out of the six selected genes (CqRAV02, CqDREB02, CqERF37, CqDREB12, and CqERF50) were present in the protein-protein interaction network, indicating their relative importance. The qRT-PCR findings were generally in accordance with the results obtained from the RNA-seq data. The genes CqRAV02, CqDREB12, and CqERF37 showed differential expression patterns between leaves and roots during the salt stress period. The expression of CqRAV02 in leaves was significantly (p ≤ 0.05) downregulated in response to salt treatment and reached a minimum at 24 h after imposing salinity. In contrast, the CqRAV02 expression in roots peaked at 6 h, and then dramatically (p ≤ 0.05) decreased at 24 h, after salinity induction (Fig. 10). The results uncovered that salt treatment caused a rapid and significant (p ≤ 0.05) upregulation in the expression of CqDREB12 and CqERF37 in leaves, however, their expression levels at 24 h after salt application were much lower than those under non-saline conditions (Fig. 10). On the other hand, the expression of these genes significantly (p ≤ 0.05) decreased in the roots of quinoa plants under salt stress conditions. Compared with that under non-saline conditions, the expression of CqERF54 exhibited a significant (p ≤ 0.05) and progressive decrease in the leaves and roots of quinoa plants at 6 and 24 h after salt application, whereas the expression of CqERF50 and CqDREB02 rapidly increased and then decreased at 24 h post-salt treatment (Fig. 10), however, no significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) was observed between the CqDREB02 expression in the roots of quinoa plants at 6 and 24 h after imposing salt stress.

Discussion

The AP2/ERF proteins constitute a large plant transcription factor family involved in regulating multiple biological processes that participate in growth and development as well as responses to biotic and abiotic stresses14. AP2/ERFs are known to be important candidate genes contributing to flower development14, and tolerance to a variety of stress conditions such as drought61, low temperature62, heat63, and salinity29,64 in different plant species. Therefore, the discovery and characterization of AP2/ERF genes and their encoded proteins at the genome-wide level will aid in clarifying the molecular pathways underlying plant growth, development, and adaptation to environmental stresses. The structure and functions of the AP2/ERFgenes have been extensively investigated in many plant species such as wheat65, maize39, rice58, sugarcane36, and peanut38, however, there is still limited knowledge regarding the characteristics of these genes and their expression profiles under saline conditions in quinoa.

In this study, a search for AP2/ERF genes in the quinoa genome resulted in the identification of 150 nonredundant members, while a recent report detected 105 AP2/ERFgenes in quinoa66. The increase in the number of identified CqAP2/ERF genes was mainly due to the identification of more genes from the ERF and DREB subfamilies. The number of AP2/ERFgenes in quinoa was comparable to that in Arabidopsis, although the quinoa genome size (~ 1.5 Gb)67 is much larger than that of Arabidopsis (~ 125 Mb). The number of genes in this gene superfamily is highly diverse among different plant species with various genome sizes. The total AP2/ERF genes in quinoa were greater than those in barley (Hordeum vulgare) (121; genome size: ~5.3 Gb)68, peach (Prunus persica) (131; genome size: ~227 Mb)69, grapevine (132; genome size: ~500 Mb)34, and Tartary buckwheat (Fagopyum Tataricum) (134; genome size: ~540 Mb)70, but less than those in peanut (188, genome size: ~2.7 Gb)71, maize (214; genome size: ~2.4 Gb)39, sugarcane (218; genome size: ~3.36 Gb)36, lettuce (Lactuca sativa) (224; ~2.5 Gb)72, sunflower (Helianthus annuus) (288, genome size: ~3.5 Gb)73, and bread wheat (517; genome size: ~17 Gb)65. The discrepancy between the AP2/ERF gene number and the genome size of plants implies that the AP2/ERF gene superfamily expanded in a species-specific manner during long-term evolution.

The CqAP2/ERF genes varied considerably in the size and physicochemical properties of their encoded proteins, comparable to AP2/ERFsfrom other plant species16,36,74. This diversity among AP2/ERF proteins likely reflects a broad range of their functional capabilities, enabling plants to regulate their growth and response mechanisms for adaptation in diverse habitats. Meanwhile, analysis of the phylogenetic tree illustrated the well-separated clades of the CqAP2/ERF subfamilies, and also further classifications of ERF and DREB members of quinoa into subclades consistent with the classification system of these subfamilies in Arabidopsis16 (Fig. 1). Indeed, some members of the CqAP2/ERFs were present in each subclade together with their Arabidopsis homologs, suggesting that they may have shared ancestry. Additionally, most of the AP2/ERF proteins may have preceded species divergence. Notably, no member of the ERF proteins in quinoa fell into the Xb-L subgroup of Arabidopsis ERFs, implying that gene loss events may have occurred during the genetic evolution of quinoa. It is worth mentioning that the abundance of quinoa ERF members assigned to the subclade V was substantially greater than that of Arabidopsis ERF members (11 vs. 5). Interestingly, five pairs of segmentally duplicated ERF genes in quinoa (CqERF05 and CqERF13; CqERF16 and CqERF19; CqERF17 and CqERF18; CqERF25 and CqERF42; CqERF61 and CqERF69) existed in this subclade. This redundancy may be related to potential species-specific adaptations, which could be crucial for quinoa’s resilience in harsh environments.

Consistent with the typical pattern of AP2/ERFgene structure observed in other plant species39,73,75,76, the number of introns in the AP2 subfamily genes was greater than that in the genes of the ERF, DREB, and RAV subfamilies in quinoa. This diversity in gene structure may serve as an evolutionary marker in gene families. Although the evolutionary pressures influencing intron retention or loss are multifaceted and context-dependent, it can be speculated that the fewer introns in the ERF and DREB subfamilies than in the AP2 subfamily might represent an evolutionary adaptation for rapid and efficient gene expression in response to environmental stresses77,78. Moreover, relatively conserved intron-exon structures among the quinoa genes of ERF, DREB, and RAV members may indicate evolutionary constraints and functional significance. In contrast, the AP2 subfamily members have retained a more complex structure which may be related to adaptive changes or the emergence of new functions associated with their involvement in a wider array of developmental processes. However, further functional studies are needed to better understand the intricate relationships between intron numbers and the functional diversity within the AP2/ERF gene family.

The analysis of the conserved motifs of the CqAP2/ERF proteins revealed that each subfamily and subgroup of CqAP2/ERF proteins shared similar motifs, which might be related to their specific functions. However, some motifs were common among all the CqAP2/ERF subfamilies. Accordingly, motifs 1–3, detected within the AP2 domain, were common among CqAP2/ERF proteins, as well as among AP2/ERF proteins in other plant species such as Triticum durum37, Erianthus fulvus74, and Brassica napus35. These universally conserved motifs likely indicate a core functional requirement, possibly involved in fundamental processes such as general DNA binding affinity. The conserved amino acid sequence LNFP, located within motif 3, is also present in some proteins that contribute to disease resistance in plants59. On the other hand, motif 8, which was specifically detected in DREB subgroup III, contained two blocks of conserved residues, namely, LPR[P/A] and [L/R]AAA. These are the essential signatures in Arabidopsis for CBL-interacting serine/threonine protein kinase-12 and auxin response factor-19, respectively58. Motif 19 was found only in the members of ERF subgroup VII and contains a small block of a conserved amino acid sequence, MCGGAI, which plays a role in ethylene transcriptional activation79. Motif 24, containing the conserved EDLL amino acid residues, was observed in six members of quinoa ERF proteins (CqERF07, CqERF10, CqERF34, CqERF39, CqERF52, and CqERF66). The EDLL element was previously found to be a transcriptional activation domain in AP2/ERF proteins80. Similar protein motifs and gene structures within a subgroup of AP2/ERF proteins imply a shared evolutionary history and likely functional similarities.

The expansion of CqAP2/ERFgenes could be attributed to whole-genome duplication, segmental duplication, and tandem duplication events. The quinoa genome underwent a whole genome duplication event approximately 4.3 million years ago81, creating a polyploid state that may have contributed to increasing the abundance of CqAP2/ERF genes. This study found evidence indicating that segmental duplications, rather than tandem duplications, were the primary evolutionary driver behind the expansion of the AP2/ERF gene family in quinoa, accounting for 64 AP2/ERFgenes (42.6%) in the species. The retention of a large number of repeated chromosome segments in the quinoa genome during chromosome doubling could explain this phenomenon82. The key role of segmental duplication events was also manifested in the expansion of several other gene families in quinoa83–85. However, the uneven distribution of CqAP2/ERF genes across quinoa chromosomes suggested that gene loss events may have also occurred during the quinoa evolution. Despite the extensive variation in AP2/ERF gene numbers among plant species, the composition of AP2/ERF subfamilies follows to a consistent pattern across different species, as the ERF is the most abundant subfamily, followed by DREB, AP2, and RAV or Soloist. It is postulated that the gene duplication process might have been more prominent in the ERF and DREB subfamilies, leading to their increased number of genes. Accordingly, approximately 45% (30 pairs) and 33% (22 pairs) of the duplicated CqAP2/ERFgenes, detected in this study, belonged to the ERF and DREB subfamilies, respectively (Supplementary File S4). Moreover, the prevalence of the ERF and DREB subfamilies in quinoa may reflect their crucial roles in a complex regulatory network involved in stress response and adaptation. Both the ERF and DREB subfamilies are involved in the regulation of plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses86,87. Plants encounter various stresses frequently throughout their lifecycle, and having a large pool of genes in these subfamilies provides more components for this intricate regulatory system and allows for more coordinated and adaptable responses.

Gene duplication processes not only play important roles in the expansion of gene families but also may contribute to functional diversification or subfunctionalization of genes, in which each copy acquires a specialized role. The prevalence of purifying selection pressure among the duplicated quinoa CqAP2/ERFgenes aligns with the strong evolutionary force of purifying selection observed in duplicated genes within the AP2/ERF families of other plant species such as maize39, wheat65, peanut38, and lettuce72. Indeed, this selective pressure restricts the evolutionary divergence of the duplicated AP2/ERF proteins, by eliminating deleterious mutations, to preserve their essential functions ensuring their effectiveness in regulating gene expression. Functional redundancy arising from such duplications may provide advantages in plant adaptation to environmental stresses. A potential mechanism is the gene dosage effect, where increased gene product levels can facilitate a rapid and efficient response to environmental stresses.

The presence of orthologs of CqAP2/ERF genes across the genomes of the three other Amaranthaceae species (B. vulgaris, A. hypochondriacus, and S. oleracea) and (A) thaliana suggested their evolutionary conservation and functional importance. Some CqAP2/ERF genes such as CqERF64, CqERF54, CqERF40, CqERF09, and CqDREB47 were assumed to be orthologs of at least three pairs of corresponding genes in other plant species (particularly AP2/ERF genes in (B) vulgaris and A. hypochondriacus), suggesting that these genes play essential roles in regulating the expression of genes involved in various biological processes across different plants. These orthologous genes likely share a common ancestry and have been retained across divergent plant lineages. Interestingly, 19 CqAP2/ERFgenes were found only in quinoa with no orthologous genes detected in the investigated species. This finding pointed out that the evolution of plants not only involves gene retention but is also accompanied by gene loss and mutation88. While some CqAP2/ERFgenes may be conserved as orthologs across plant species, others may have undergone divergence and functional diversification. Variations in gene regulation, expression patterns, and protein function may have contributed to different levels of plant adaptation to diverse environmental conditions and developmental programs89.

The identification of diverse CREs associated with hormonal responses, stress signals, and developmental cues within the promoters of CqAP2/ERF genes unravels the complex regulatory mechanisms governing their expression. This finding suggested a potential role for the ABA and MeJA signaling pathways, as well as the MYB and MYC transcription factors, in regulating CqAP2/ERFgene expression. MYB and MYC transcription factors play crucial roles in plant responses to stresses such as drought, salt, and low temperature through ABA-dependent signaling pathways90. Our findings are generally in agreement with the results obtained from promoter analysis of AP2/ERFgenes in other plant species36,74,91. The interplay between the predicted CREs and their corresponding transcription factors likely facilitates a dynamic and responsive transcriptional program, ensuring that the expression of CqAP2/ERF genes is finely tuned to enable quinoa to adapt its growth and developmental processes in response to internal and external cues.

The AP2/ERFgenes, particularly those members in the ERF, DREB, and RAV subfamilies are widely involved in regulating the response to salt stress in various plant species32. They are considered prominent candidates for genetic engineering to improve crop abiotic stress tolerance92. Thus, the expression profiles of salt stress-responsive CqAP2/ERF genes were analyzed to elucidate their possible functions. Several salt-responsive CqAP2/ERF genes, mostly ERF and DREB genes, were identified with varying expression levels and tissue specificity, suggesting that these genes may have diverse functions, acting as components of complex regulatory networks governing quinoa’s response to salt stress. The downregulation of CqRAV02expression observed in quinoa plants exposed to salt stress was consistent with the changes in the expression of its orthologs in Arabidopsis93, rice94, and maize95 under similar circumstances. The overexpression of AtRAV1 resulted in reduced plant size and increased sensitivity to drought and salt indicating that AtRAV1may be a negative regulator of growth and abiotic challenges96. Therefore, the downregulation of CqRAV02may be involved in activating stress-responsive pathways. It is noteworthy that the protein-protein interaction network shed light on RAV1 (CqRAV02) as a hub protein interconnected with two key ABA signaling transcription factors, ABI3 and ABI5, which underlie the regulation of seed maturation and the response to various stresses96,97.

Substantial upregulation of CqDREB12 was detected specifically in the leaves of quinoa plants subjected to salt stress, implying that tissue-specific regulation may control the functions of this gene. In agreement with our findings, the Arabidopsis ortholog of CqDREB12, namely DREB2C, showed greater expression in leaves than in roots under salt stress conditions19. It was previously demonstrated that DREB2Cmay regulate ABA and stress responses, most likely by controlling the expression of a subset of ABA/stress-responsive genes through binding to DRE/CRT sequences in the promoters of target genes98. As shown in the protein-protein interaction network (Fig. 7a), DREB2C, coupled with DREB1A and DREB2A, interacts with ABF transcription factor members such as ABF2 to eventually activate the transcription of downstream ABA-responsive genes98. The expression of CqDREB02 was significantly induced in the leaves and especially in the roots of quinoa. This gene was assumed to be an ortholog of Arabidopsis ERF017, recognized as a core component of the protein-protein interaction network (Fig. 7a), indicating the possible involvement of CqDREB02 in a similar stress-response network that enhances tolerance to high salinity in quinoa. Previous studies also reported the upregulation of ERF017 in response to salt stress99as well as drought, ABA, and jasmonic acid treatments100. Interestingly, this gene is known to be specifically upregulated by salt stress in the roots of Arabidopsis101.

The expression of the CqERF37 gene exhibited a differential early response to salt stress between the leaves (upregulation) and roots (downregulation) of quinoa, however, a sharp decrease in the leaf expression of CqERF37 was subsequently observed. Similarly, the expression of ERF109 in trifoliate orange (Poncirus trifoliata(L.) Raf.) displayed an early slight increase in response to salt, followed by a dramatic decrease, and remained steady during subsequent sampling times102. ERF109, the Arabidopsis ortholog of CqERF37, is known as a key regulatory gene in conferring salt tolerance in Arabidopsis103. This gene regulates various downstream genes involved in reactive oxygen species scavenging, tryptophan biosynthesis, and plant hormone signal transduction pathways104. Notably, CqERF37 (ERF109) was identified as an important regulatory protein in the protein-protein interaction network (Fig. 7a), consistent with the findings in Pinus Massoniana105. However, further investigations are needed to extensively explore the functions of CqERF37 in relation to quinoa’s salt tolerance. The increased CqERF50 transcripts in both the leaves and roots of quinoa plants under salt-stress conditions suggested that CqERF50 is involved in the mechanisms by which plants counteract the adverse effects of high salinity. Accordingly, the upregulation of the CqERF50 ortholog in Arabidopsis (AtERF8) under saline106and drought107 conditions has been reported. Furthermore, AtERF8plays important roles in ABA signaling and pathogen defense108. Syntenic analysis revealed that CqERF54 is likely an ortholog of the Arabidopsis CRF2 (Cytokinin Response Factor 2) gene. It has been demonstrated that CRF2is involved in modulating salt stress responses associated with photosynthesis109. Thus, the significant downregulation of CqERF54in response to salt stress may be due to the negative effect of salinity on the cytokinin transduction pathway, potentially by increasing the degradation of some cytokinin signaling components110.

Conclusions

In this study, a total of 150 genes encoding CqAP2/ERF proteins were identified in quinoa and classified into the AP2 (23), ERF (71), DREB (49), RAV (3), and Soloist (4) subfamilies. These genes were unevenly distributed across the quinoa chromosomes. We extensively elucidated various structural and evolutionary features of CqAP2/ERF genes, including their genomic organization, conserved protein domains and motifs, cis-regulatory elements, phylogenetic relationships, and synteny. The results revealed a significant contribution of segmental duplication events to the expansion of the AP2/ERF family in quinoa. The protein-protein interaction network predicted the highly interactive CqAP2/ERF proteins regulating the biological processes associated with this transcription factor family. Additionally, the gene expression analyses by RNA-seq and qRT-PCR under control and salt stress conditions identified several differentially expressed CqAP2/ERF genes, implying their potential roles in response to salt stress. Overall, our findings provided valuable information for better understanding the characteristics and evolution of the AP2/ERF gene family in quinoa. Moreover, this study identified several candidate salt-responsive CqAP2/ERF genes, suggesting that further functional exploration of these transcription factors and their regulatory networks will be beneficial for developing salt-tolerant crop varieties.
