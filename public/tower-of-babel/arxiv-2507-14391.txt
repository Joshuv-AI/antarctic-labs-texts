Policy relevance of causal quantities in networks


Abstract
In settings where units’ outcomes are affected by others’ treatments, there has been a proliferation of ways to quantify effects of treatments on outcomes. Here we describe how many proposed estimands can be represented as involving one of two ways of averaging over units and treatment assignments. The more common representation often results in quantities that are irrelevant, or at least insufficient, for optimal choice of policies governing treatment assignment. The other representation often yields quantities that lack an interpretation as summaries of unit-level effects, but that we argue may still be relevant to policy choice. Among various estimands, the expected average outcome — or its contrast between two different policies — can be represented both ways and, we argue, merits further attention.

How should we quantify the effects of an intervention?

In the case of evaluating the effects of a binary treatment, such as through a randomized experiment, the average treatment effect (ATE) is often the primary quantity of interest (or
estimand
). It is sometimes sufficient for decision making.
Consider a setting where the outcome of each unit
i\in[n]
depends on its own treatment
Z_{i}
, so that we observe
Y_{i}:=y_{i}(Z_{i})
; that is, there is “no interference”
(
Cox, 1958
)
.
1
1

1

We use upper case letters to denote (observed) random variables and boldface letters to denote vectors.
The decision maker has utility that is linear in the average outcome,
U(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}(z_{i})
. Then, when deciding whether to apply a costless treatment to an entire population or not, the decision maker can simply look at the sign of the
\text{ATE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}(1)-y_{i}(0)
.
Even with a costly treatment, the ATE may remain a sufficient summary of the potential outcomes for decision-making, such as choosing among policies governing randomized rollout of a treatment. Given known costs of treatment (which may be nonlinear in how many units are treated), the ATE tells the decision-maker what proportion of the population to treat at random.
2
2

2

Of course, we typically do not know the ATE, but rather have sample data, such as that arising from a randomized experiment or observational data.
Then we have to decide how to make choices under uncertainty and/or ambiguity; see, e.g.,
Manski, 2004
. In this article, we focus solely on what quantities are of interest, neglecting estimation and inference.

By contrast, in settings where the units’ outcomes are affected by others’ treatments, how to quantify effects has been less clear.
This includes how to study important questions about how changes to communication technologies affect the spread of information, how recommendation and matching algorithms affect market outcomes, and how environmental policy affects public health.

Consider the case of interventions in a network. In a social network where the outcome for one unit might depend on the treatment of all other units, there are many possible comparisons to make: with a binary treatment, the table of all potential outcomes is
n\times 2^{n}
(Figure
1
, top left), and there are many ways to summarize and contrast these entries. Naturally, there has been a proliferation of proposed estimands — often intended to capture various notions of direct and indirect (or spillover) effects; that is, the effects of a unit’s own treatment on itself and various versions of how treatment of some units might affect other units.

Many of these estimands can be seen as involving one of two different ways of averaging over units and possible treatment assignments. Here we will describe these two ways of averaging, noting that one often results in quantities that are irrelevant, or at least insufficient, for optimal choice of policies. The other sometimes results in quantities that lack an interpretation as summaries of unit-level effects, but we argue that additional attention is due here as they may even then be applicable for policy choice. In particular, both ways of averaging generally coincide for one estimand — namely the expected average outcome — that we argue is both policy-relevant and causally interpretable.

Averaging over assignments, then over units

Perhaps most common are estimands that can be represented as first averaging over treatment assignments within units and then over units. For example, we might first separately average all the potential outcomes for unit
i
where it is treated or not treated; then we can average these two cases across units. Or we might first average all the potential outcomes for unit
i
where it is treated and at least one of its network neighbors is treated (and separately all the potential outcomes where it is not treated and neither are any of its neighbors); then we again average these cases across units.

These quantities can be motivated through the choice of an exposure mapping
(
Aronow and Samii, 2017
)
, which specifies which changes in treatment assignments can affect a unit’s outcome; that is, it specifies level sets in the potential outcome function whereby two treatment vectors
\boldsymbol{z}\neq\boldsymbol{z^{\prime}}
result in the same outcome for that unit,
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z^{\prime}})
, if they correspond to the same exposure,
d_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=d_{i}(\boldsymbol{z^{\prime}})
. If there is no interference, then each unit’s outcome only depends on its own treatment, so
d_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}):=z_{i}
just indicates whether
i
is treated in
\boldsymbol{z}
. Similarly, we might suppose that each unit’s outcome only depends on their own treatment and whether any of their neighbors are treated, or more flexibly, the number of their neighbors who are treated.
Under such an assumed exposure mapping, there is really only one averaging step — averaging over units — as all the potential outcomes pooled in the first step are identical.

However, researchers have been concerned about the consequences of misspecifying the exposure mapping,
3
3

3

One concern is that if a unit’s outcomes are affected by their own treatment and neighbors’ outcomes, this leads to potentially global dependence
(
Manski, 2013
;
Leung, 2024
;
Eckles et al., 2017
, e.g.)
.

so then we should think about these quantities as potentially involving two steps of averaging indeed.
More specifically, considering the case of something we might label a “direct effect”, we can represent the first averaging step as marginalizing over
i
’s potential outcomes according to a distribution over the treatment of all other units
\boldsymbol{Z}_{-i}
(Figure
1
, top right) separately for cases where
i
is treated or not. This could be the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{-i}
given that
i
receives treatment (or not). In that case, the difference in these expectations defines an expected exposure effect of the unit’s own treatment
(
Sävje, 2023
)
.
4
4

4

Related quantities appear in many other papers
(
Hudgens and Halloran, 2008
;
VanderWeele and Tchetgen, 2011
;
Forastiere et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
.

Under many other experimental designs, these quantities are averages of unit-level causal effects, though it can be ambiguous exactly what they are effects
of
.
Under independence of the entries of
\boldsymbol{Z}
(i.e. a possibly heterogeneous Bernoulli design), the expected exposure effect for a unit’s own treatment coincides with the expected ATE
(
Sävje et al., 2021
, EATE;)
, which marginalizes using the
unconditional
distribution on
\boldsymbol{Z}_{-i}
.
This is a design-specific quantity, but it has a clear interpretation as the average effect of a unit’s own treatment, given how other units are being treated.

Now for a way these estimands might not be what we want.
Many of these seem to have little to do with decisions, and they will typically be insufficient for policy choice. First, if the exposure mapping that motivated the interest in these estimands is wrong, these quantities may not be interpretable as averages of unit-level causal effects
(
Leung, 2024
)
and can be misleading for policy choice
(
Auerbach et al., 2024
)
. Here we show how, even when the exposure mapping is correct, they may not allow us to know which policy is better.

Let us assume that each unit’s outcome only depends on whether 0, 1, or at least 2 of its neighbors are treated; it is unaffected by its own treatment. Thus, the true exposure mapping for each unit just has three levels,
d_{i}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1,2+\}
.
Estimands defined by this exposure mapping are then the average potential outcome under each of these exposures,
(\overline{Y}(0),\overline{Y}(1),\overline{Y}(2+))
; where
\overline{Y}(d):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}(d)
and we abuse the notation to denote potential outcomes under exposure
d
. Differences between these can be seen as indirect (or spillover) effects.
These quantities are obviously a limited guide to policy. Say average outcomes are maximized with a single treated neighbor, i.e.
\overline{Y}(1)>\max(\overline{Y}(0),\overline{Y}(2+))
. Unlike the average potential outcome under treatment (in the absence of interference), we typically cannot select a policy that directly corresponds to this average potential outcome: for many graphs, it will not be possible to assign treatments so that all units have exactly one treated neighbor.

Thus, if these average potential outcomes by exposure are to be relevant to policy choice, only some of them will be relevant (i.e. for the choice of all-or-none-treated policy,
\overline{Y}(0)
and
\overline{Y}(2+)
are relevant) or that they will need to be combined to choose among policies that treat some units at random — though their usefulness here may also be limited, as we now describe.

Consider a network given by Figure
2A
, or a network consisting of many copies of this graph. Say we are choosing between two (homogeneous Bernoulli) policies for treating units:
\pi
treats each unit with probability
p
, while
\pi^{\prime}
treats each unit with probability
p^{\prime}>p
. Can we use the average potential outcomes to make the optimal choice? No. These estimands have ‘‘compressed’’ the potential outcomes in a way that is not very useful for this purpose. The same three averages can arise from tables of potential outcomes that have very different implications for policy choice.
5
5

5

For example, say all outcomes are zero, except in some cases when a unit has exactly one treated neighbor, it may have a positive outcome (i.e.
y_{i}(d)\neq 0
only if
d=1
). In one setting, it is the higher-degree units who are helped by having one treated neighbors; in another, it is the lower-degree units who are so helped. Both of these settings can yield identical average potential outcomes. However, for a decision-maker with utility linear in the average outcome, the optimal choice of
p
or
p^{\prime}
is different.

Attempts to use these averages for policy choice, such as through a plug-in estimator of the expected average outcome under each policy, will be misleading. Here one problem is that we have discarded information about how likely different units are to receive different exposures under relevant policy changes.
Sometimes these averages will be sufficient for the average outcome under
some
policy, other than all-or-none-treated, but it may not have been of interest. If such a policy exists, it will be one that induces homogeneous probability of exposures across all units. In the biclique example of Figure
2A
, however, there is no heterogeneous Bernoulli policy with different probabilities for units with different degrees (other than treating no or all units); see Figure
2C
, and the Appendix for further details.

Moving beyond this particular example, consider the causal relationships between policies, treatments, exposures, and outcomes when the exposure mapping is correctly specified — represented by the directed acyclic graph in Figure
2B
. This illustrates that it can be easy to end up considering interventions on the exposures
D_{i}
that cannot be induced by any distribution for
\boldsymbol{Z}
(that is, any choice of policy
\pi
). Or, even if such a policy exists that induces that distribution on
D_{i}
, that policy may not be of interest at all.

Thus, while one might have thought that at least
correctly-specified
exposure mappings make the average potential outcomes under each exposure sufficient for policy choice;
however, we have seen is that is not in general the case.
6
6

6

In noting that misspecified exposure mappings can be irrelevant to policy choice,
Auerbach et al., 2024
use an example in which the correctly-specified mapping is sufficient, but this depends on other characteristics of that example.

Further confusion is possible when attempting to use misspecified exposure mappings for policy choice.

To summarize, these assignments-then-units averages are often interpretable as averages of unit-level effects, though some of the clarity here can depend on correctly specifying an exposure model. But, even in some of the best cases — that is, even when they have a clear interpretation of this kind — these quantities are often not useful for policy choice.

Averaging over units, then over assignments

We can also represent some estimands as first averaging over units and then over possible treatment assignments. In the simplest case, we can, for each possible treatment assignment, average the potential outcomes of all units; then we marginalize over those average potential outcomes according to the probability of each under a policy of interest. This
expected average outcome
(EAO) directly addresses the question of what happens in expectation under that policy. And, if the decision-maker’s utility is linear in the average outcome, it is sufficient for policy choice. In the context of causal inference in networks, the EAO (or the difference in EAO under two policies) has been studied in some recent work
(
Chin et al., 2022
;
Viviano, 2025
, e.g.,)
.
One observation is simply that EAOs under policies of interest should continue to receive attention from researchers.

It is also possible to define other quantities less familiar in the causal inference literature.
To again consider something like a “direct effect”, we can, for each possible treatment assignment
\boldsymbol{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, summarize the potential outcomes of all units that were treated or not in that vector (Figure
1
, lower left). Then we can average these
2\times{2^{n}}-2
entries to produce average outcomes for treated and untreated units under some distribution for
\boldsymbol{Z}
, some policy of interest
\pi
. We call these
expected focal average outcomes
(EFAOs), as they are the expected averages of different focal sets defined by treatment (and potentially indirect exposure to treatment, say from units at a distance
\ell
away in the network).
A decision-maker could combine these two averages in a (possibly weighted) average welfare computation.

We might even contrast the treated and untreated EFAOs.
There are some special cases where an EFAO contrast coincides, exactly or asymptotically, with an intuitively related quantity represented via the other route. For example, this comparison of average outcomes for treated and untreated units is the expected exposure effect of the unit’s own treatment when
\pi
is a completely randomized (i.e.
m
-out-of-
n
) design, it is the EATE when
\pi
is a homogeneous Bernoulli policy, and it is the global average treatment effect
(
Ugander et al., 2013
, GATE;)
when
\pi
is an all-or-none-treated policy. These are all cases where the policy induces homogeneous probabilities of each exposure (see Appendix for further details and spillover effects).

In general, however, EFAOs may not be interpretable as averages of unit-level causal effects. Under a heterogeneous Bernoulli policy and no interference, for example, this kind of treated–untreated comparison has little relationship to the ATE, as even with no treatment effects whatsoever, such a policy might produce large average treated–untreated differences.

If these quantities are not interpretable as averages of unit-level causal effects, are they useful?
We contend that these quantities are often nonetheless causally interpretable, in a different way, and useful for policy choice, at least when decision-makers have preferences that go beyond average outcomes.

First, a contrast of these quantities
between
two policies
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
is still a causal effect of policy on aggregate outcomes. Perhaps
\pi
, compared with
\pi^{\prime}
, increases the expected average outcomes of treated units, or increases how much better treated units’ outcomes are compared with untreated units’ outcomes. These are causal quantities, describing how the policy chosen affects aggregate outcomes, even if they are not averages of
unit-level
effects.

Second, these quantities still may be useful for policy choice, as policymakers may have preferences about the outcomes of units that end up treated and untreated. Decision-makers are often evaluated, at least in part, based on naive comparisons (e.g., of treated and untreated units) or on myopic summaries (e.g., only looking at treated units).
Thus, rational (if perhaps cynical) decision-makers will have preferences that separately consider outcomes for treated and untreated units.
They may prefer treated units to have good outcomes; or they may prefer treated units to not have outcomes that are
too good
, in the case of social programs that they want to appear to be
ex post
targeted to the less well-off.
Furthermore, in the context of causal inference in networks, they may have preferences about the outcomes of units with various exposures to treatment.
In a social network a decision-maker may have preferences about the outcomes of people within one hop of units selected for an intensive marketing intervention
(
Cai et al., 2015
, as in, e.g.,)
.
While some of these preferences might arise in settings without interference, positing interference often means we expect that units will continue to interact, such that unit
i
’s intermediate outcome
Y_{i}
should be interpreted in the context of aggregate outcomes as they will, e.g., be competing for scarce resources with the income it represents.

Thus, particularly in the presence of interference, it will be natural for decision-makers to have complex aggregations of outcomes in mind when choosing among policies.
This is represented in Figure
2B
, where knowing these EFAOs could enable quantifying how policy choice affects the decision-maker’s payoff, i.e.,
\Pr\left(\widetilde{Y}\,\middle\lvert\,\text{do}\,(\Pi=\pi)\right)
. And, for a decision-maker simply choosing among policies according to maximizing their expected utility, they only need effects of
\Pi
on
\widetilde{Y}
.

Conclusion

How then should we quantify outcomes and effects of interventions in networks?
Decision-makers need quantities that are relevant for policy choice, and often in the causal inference literature we want quantities that are summaries of unit-level causal effects.
The expected average outcome (EAO) is a quantity that coincides under both kinds of averaging we have described, under
any
treatment policy, regardless of whether it induces different exposures homogeneously or not. Contrasts of EAOs are thus both policy-relevant — since we marginalize over the treatment of all units under the same policy and therefore can compare this quantity across different policies — and is causally interpretable at the unit-level — since we average over outcomes of all units.
Rather than fixating on specific unit-level causal contrasts that are informative only about policies that are homogeneous in the exposures they induce, we argue that estimating EAOs — for all policies within a larger relevant policy space — will be more directly decision-relevant.

Some other estimands in the literature may be able to be combined to yield a difference in EAOs for pairs of policies while also each being interpretable as summaries of unit-level causal effects. For example, for comparing policies that treat
m
versus
m-1
units, we could decompose this difference in EAOs into a direct effect and an average (or total) effect of treating a random unit on all other units
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
. We encourage further attention to decompositions of EAOs and differences in EAOs that may provide additional insight into the mechanisms underlying aggregate consequences of a treatment policy.

Next, say
\mathcal{N}_{S}
encodes a structure over which we wish to compute some kind of “indirect” effects: for example, let
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}
denote a “network neighborhood” for unit
i
, and
\mathcal{N}_{S}:=\bigcup_{i\in S}\mathcal{N}_{i}
. Then, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\left\langle Y_{i}\right\rangle_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{S}}\,\middle|\,\left|\mathcal{N}_{S}\right\rvert>0\right]=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{Y_{i}\{1-\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}(1-Z_{j})\}}{1-\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}(1-Z_{j})\right\rangle_{k\in[n]}}\,\middle|\,\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}(1-Z_{j})\right\rangle_{k\in[n]}<1\right]\right\rangle_{i\in[n]},

which, when
\pi
is a completely randomized policy
at the neighborhood level
for every unit
i
, or an all-or-none-treated policy, is the same as
\left\langle\left\langle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Y_{i}Z_{j}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z_{j}\right]}\right\rangle_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\right\rangle_{i\in[n]}=\left\langle\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Y_{i}\,\middle|\,Z_{j}=1\right]\right\rangle_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\right\rangle_{i\in[n]}.
Such policies could be given by, for instance, the independent-set design
(
Cai et al., 2024
)
. A scenario where this holds for any completely randomized policy or all-or-none-treated policy
\pi
is when the neighborhood structure is of unique pairs, i.e.
\forall i\in[n],\exists j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}:\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{j\},\nexists k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}:\mathcal{N}_{k}=\{j\}
. This could be done, say, by uniquely assigning every unit to another unit that we might think it is the most influential on. Similarly, if averaging outcomes over
\mathcal{N}_{S}^{\prime}:=\bigcap_{i\in S}[n]\setminus\mathcal{N}_{i}
, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\left\langle Y_{i}\right\rangle_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{S}^{\prime}}\,\middle|\,\left|\mathcal{N}_{S}^{\prime}\right\rvert>0\right]=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{Y_{i}\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}(1-Z_{j})}{\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}(1-Z_{j})\right\rangle_{k\in[n]}}\,\middle|\,\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}(1-Z_{j})\right\rangle_{k\in[n]}>0\right]\right\rangle_{i\in[n]},

which under the same assumptions on the policy
\pi
as above yields the estimand

\left\langle\left\langle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Y_{i}(1-Z_{j})\right]}{1-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z_{j}\right]}\right\rangle_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\right\rangle_{i\in[n]}=\left\langle\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Y_{i}\,\middle|\,Z_{j}=0\right]\right\rangle_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\right\rangle_{i\in[n]}.

Therefore, the difference in expected average outcomes of the out-neighbors of treated units and out-neighbors of control units gives the average indirect causal effect
(
Hudgens and Halloran, 2008
)
when
\pi
is a completely randomized policy at the neighborhood level for every unit, and the global average treatment effect when
\pi
is an all-or-none-treated policy.

Disjoint union of bicliques

Let
K_{u,v}
be a biclique or a complete bipartite graph (see Figure
2A
), and
K_{u,v}^{n}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{u+v}}K_{u,v}
be the disjoint union of bicliques with
n
nodes, wherein
u\leq v\ll n,n\mod(u+v)=0
. Say the true exposure model
d_{i}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1,\dots,u\}
has
u+1
levels, corresponding to the number of treated neighbors—where the level
u
corresponds to
u
or more treated neighbors. Say the individual-level potential outcomes are given by
Y_{u}(d)
or
Y_{v}(d)
for every node respectively in the left or right half of a biclique. A decision-maker might be interested specifically in homogeneous Bernoulli policies with treatment probability
p\in(0,1)
. Then the average potential outcomes under each of these exposures is given by

\left\langle Y_{i}(d)\right\rangle_{i\in[n]}=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[Y_{i}\,\middle|\,D_{i}=d\right]\right\rangle_{i\in[n]}=\frac{u\,Y_{u}(d)+v\,Y_{v}(d)}{u+v}.

This quantity may not be directly useful for policy implementation, since a homogeneous Bernoulli policy may induce exposures heterogeneously across the units.

On the other hand, consider the second kind of (units-then-assignments) averaging:

\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\left\langle Y_{i}\right\rangle_{i\in[n],D_{i}=d}\,\middle|\,\left\langle\left[D_{i}=d\right]\right\rangle_{i\in[n]}>0\right]\approx\frac{u\,f_{p}(v,d)\,Y_{u}(d)+v\,f_{p}(u,d)\,Y_{v}(d)}{u\,f_{p}(v,d)+v\,f_{p}(u,d)},

where
f_{p}(u,d):=\binom{u}{d}p^{d}(1-p)^{u-d}
encodes the probability of
d
of
u
neighbors being treated, and define
f_{p}(v,u):=\sum_{d=u}^{v}\binom{v}{d}p^{v}d(1-p)^{v-d}
for the exposure of
u
or more neighbors of nodes in the left half of the clique. Evidently, if
u=v
(i.e. exposures are homogeneous) or
Y_{u}(d)=Y_{v}(d)
(i.e. outcomes under an exposure are homogeneous), then both of the above expressions are equal to one another. However, if the biclique has heterogeneous degrees and the true potential outcomes are heterogeneous—even if the average potential outcomes are held constant—then these two quantities can be quite different. Furthermore, the sign of their difference is given by
\sgn[Y_{v}(d)-Y_{u}(d)][f_{p}(u,d)-f_{p}(v,d)]
, i.e. for some policies
p
the sign will be different, and swapping the potential outcomes of the left- and right-half nodes flips the sign for the same policy
p
. Considering heterogeneous Bernoulli policies where treatments probabilities may vary with degree, it might be possible that
\forall p\in(0,1)\,\exists p^{\prime}\in(0,1)
such that
f_{p}(u,d)=f_{p^{\prime}}(v,d)
. However, there may nonetheless be no
(p,p^{\prime})\in(0,1)^{2}
where this holds
\forall d\in\{0,1,\dots,u\}
. In other words, there may not be a policy class of interest for which the average outcomes by exposure are relevant (see Figure
2C
).
