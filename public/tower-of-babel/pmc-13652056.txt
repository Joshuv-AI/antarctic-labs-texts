From gut colonization to recurrence: E. coli secretion systems at the host-microbiota interface
Authors: Marcin Równicki, Maciej Obrębski, Jakub P. Piwowarski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Escherichia coli occupies a unique position between commensal colonization and infection. It is a common member of the intestinal microbiota, yet selected pathogenic or opportunistic lineages can persist in mucosal niches, interact with resident microbial communities, and in some cases seed recurrent extraintestinal diseases. The mechanisms enabling long-term colonization, survival under host and antibiotic pressure, and relapse are diverse, depending on pathotype, infection site, host environment, and microbial community structure. Here, we critically examine how T3SS, T5SS, and T6SS may contribute to persistence-associated biology in specific E. coli contexts. T3SS primarily mediates epithelial interaction in enteric pathotypes, T5SS drives adhesion, biofilm formation, mucosal colonization, and immune modulation, and T6SS functions as a microbiota-facing mechanism of interbacterial competition. We distinguish experimentally demonstrated roles from speculative links to chronicity and recurrence, framing these systems as context-dependent interaction modules rather than a unified network, while highlighting evidence gaps relative to established persistence determinants.

Introduction

Escherichia coli is one of the most versatile bacterial species associated with humans and animals. It is a frequent member of the intestinal microbiota, but selected lineages have acquired virulence traits that allow them to cause disease in intestinal or extraintestinal sites. Pathogenic E. coli are commonly grouped into intestinal pathogenic E. coli (IPEC) and extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC). 1 IPEC include enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC, including enterohemorrhagic E. coli, EHEC), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC), diffusely adherent E. coli (DAEC), and enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC). 2 ExPEC include uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), neonatal meningitis-associated E. coli (NMEC), sepsis-associated E. coli (SePEC), and avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC). 1 , 3 These groups differ substantially in ecological niche, infection route, tissue tropism, virulence repertoire, and clinical outcome. 3 Therefore, mechanisms described in one pathotype or infection site should not be directly extrapolated to another without careful qualification. In this review, we focus primarily on EPEC, EHEC/STEC, EAEC, DAEC, and UPEC, for which secretion-system functions are directly relevant to the persistence-associated processes discussed below. The remaining pathotypes are included here to define the broader IPEC and ExPEC groups and are mentioned subsequently only where they provide mechanistically informative examples.

Long-lasting E. coli-associated conditions are biologically heterogeneous and differ across pathotypes and anatomical niches. For example, EAEC has been associated with persistent diarrhea, 4 EHEC can show prolonged fecal shedding for weeks or, in some patients, months after symptomatic infection, 5 DAEC has been linked to chronic mucosal inflammation, 6 and UPEC can cause recurrent urinary tract infection or chronic bacterial prostatitis. 7 These examples show that E. coli persistence can involve distinct processes, including prolonged colonization, chronic inflammation, and recurrent infection. 8

In this review, we therefore use persistence-related terminology in a restricted sense (see Box 1). Persistent infection and recurrent infection are distinguished by whether bacterial presence continues with ongoing or intermittent pathology 9 or a new symptomatic episode occurs after apparent clinical resolution, respectively. 7 Relapse is distinguished from reinfection according to whether recurrence originates from the same strain or reservoir or represents a new infection episode. Chronic infection involves ongoing bacterial presence or repeated reactivation, 10 whereas chronic disease may persist after bacterial clearance because of host-driven inflammation, tissue remodeling, or immune dysregulation. Persister cells, biofilm-associated survival, and intracellular reservoirs are considered individually. These processes may overlap in some settings, but they are mechanistically distinct and should be interpreted separately unless direct evidence connects them.

Secretion systems are relevant to E. coli pathobiology because they mediate interactions with host cells, neighboring microbes, and the extracellular environment. 11 They can deliver or export proteins that alter epithelial signaling, immune recognition, adhesion, aggregation, biofilm development, nutrient acquisition, or interbacterial competition. 12 In this context, secretion systems should be viewed not only as acute virulence devices, but also as host-microbiota interaction modules that may support persistence and recurrence.

E. coli encodes or acquires multiple secretion and export pathways, including Type I, Type II, Type III, Type V, and Type VI secretion systems, as well as T4SS-like elements, outer membrane vesicles, and non-classical secretion processes. 13-15 Type I and Type II secretion systems contribute to the export of several proteins relevant to bacterial physiology and virulence, and outer membrane vesicles can deliver toxins, enzymes, and immunomodulatory molecules. These mechanisms are important but are not the primary focus of this review. We focus on T3SS, T5SS, and T6SS because they map onto three major interaction axes at the host-microbiota interface: epithelial manipulation, mucosal adhesion or biofilm formation, and interbacterial competition.

T3SS is discussed primarily in the context of enteric pathotypes such as EPEC and EHEC, where it mediates intimate epithelial attachment, cytoskeletal remodeling, barrier disruption, and modulation of inflammatory or phagocytic responses. These activities can support colonization and mucosal niche formation, but direct evidence that T3SS drives chronic or recurrent E. coli infection remains context-dependent and limited. T5SS is discussed as a broad family of surface-associated and secreted proteins, including autotransporters, SPATEs, trimeric autotransporter adhesins, inverse autotransporters, and two-partner secretion systems. These proteins are strongly linked to adhesion, aggregation, mucin interaction, complement resistance, epithelial damage, and biofilm formation, all of which may contribute to mucosal persistence depending on pathotype and niche. T6SS is discussed mainly as a microbiota-facing system that mediates contact-dependent interbacterial competition, antibacterial effector delivery, immunity-dependent survival, and niche fitness in polymicrobial environments. Its contribution to persistence or recurrence in E. coli should be interpreted cautiously because it depends on strain background, effector repertoire, microbial community structure, and host environment.

The aim of this review is to provide a critical, context-separated synthesis of how secretion systems contribute to specific processes that may support long-term colonization, reservoir stability, antibiotic survival, or recurrence in defined E. coli pathotypes and infection models. We discuss evidence gaps that remain central to the field, including whether secretion systems are enriched in recurrent clinical isolates, whether they are expressed or active during chronic infection, whether they remain functional after antibiotic exposure, and how their contribution compares with established persistence determinants such as fimbriae, capsule, LPS remodeling, iron acquisition, toxins, stress-response pathways, c-di-GMP signaling, biofilm regulators, and metabolic adaptation.

Secretion systems as host-microbiota interaction modules in E. coli

In the context of gut colonization and recurrent infection, secretion systems are relevant not simply because they deliver virulence factors, but because they mediate interactions with three major selective environments: host epithelial cells, immune defenses, and the resident microbiota. T3SS primarily shapes epithelial manipulation and host-cell interactions, T5SS contributes to adhesion, aggregation, mucosal persistence and biofilm formation, whereas T6SS supports interbacterial competition and niche fitness in polymicrobial environments.

(1)The Type III Secretion System (T3SS)

(1)The Type III Secretion System (T3SS)

The Type III Secretion System (T3SS)

The Type III Secretion System (T3SS) is a multiprotein nanomachine used by certain pathogenic strains of E. coli to directly interface with host cells. 16 This system enables the single-step transport of proteins across three barriers: the bacterial inner membrane, outer membrane, and host cell membrane (Figure 1). 17 , 18 Proteins secreted by the T3SS, termed effector proteins, exhibit diverse functions aimed at subverting host cell processes to benefit bacterial survival and replication 19-22 (Table 1). Effector proteins are recognized and secreted by the T3SS through an N-terminal signal sequence, which acts as a molecular tag to direct them to the secretion machinery. 23 , 24 Prior to secretion, these effectors remain in their native, folded conformation. However, to traverse the narrow T3SS needle, they must undergo ATP-dependent unfolding—a prerequisite for their passage into the host cell. 25 Many, but not all, T3SS effectors rely on dedicated bacterial chaperones during secretion to bind unfolded substrates, prevent premature folding and aggregation, and guide effectors to the injectisome. 26 , 27 Once translocated into the host cytoplasm, effectors are intrinsically optimized to refold autonomously, owing to a unique balance of mechanical lability that enables their unfolding by the injectisome’s ATP-driven unfoldase and facilitates efficient spontaneous refolding within the host cell. Furthermore, pH-dependent folding switches in effectors promote conformational recovery after translocation without reliance on host chaperones, although some effectors may later hijack these chaperones to modulate host pathways. 28-30

Schematic representation of Type III (T3SS), Type V (T5SS), and Type VI (T6SS) Secretion Systems. T3SS delivers effectors through an injectisome that spans the bacterial envelope and interfaces with the host membrane. T6SS is a contractile phage-tail-like apparatus that propels Hcp-VgrG-PAAR-associated effectors into neighboring cells or releases them extracellularly. In contrast, T5SS secretes proteins outside the cell through a two-step Sec-dependent mechanism. These proteins cross the inner membrane with the help of the Sec secretion pathways and then, after folding their translocator ß-domain in the outer membrane involving BAM complex, transport their own passenger α-domain to the extracellular space.

T3SSs are structurally and functionally similar to a syringe and are often referred to as “injectisomes” and “needle and syringe”-like apparatuses because of their structure. At its core, the T3SS can be divided into three primary structural elements: a basal body, a needle component, and a translocon complex. 37 , 38 The basal body, spanning both bacterial membranes, integrates cytoplasmic elements with a multi-ring architecture. 37 That architecture bridges the inner and outer membranes, creating an anchor-like formation with a central rod. In most T3SSs, the basal body is comprised of over 15 distinct proteins. 39 Extending outwards from the basal body, a hollow needle-like filament projects beyond the bacterial surface, passing through the secretin pore. This needle features a channel in the outer membrane, known as the secretin, and secretes effector proteins into the extracellular environment. 40 Recent structural studies provide the first direct proof that translocation occurs via passage through the needle's hollow core by imaging a “trapped” effector protein within the needle using cryo-EM and single-particle techniques. 18

At the needle tip resides the translocon complex, a critical component that detects host cells, mediates interaction, and regulates effector release. 41 When bacteria contact a host cell, translocons assemble and form a pore that delivers and allows effector proteins to enter and act on the host cell. 42 , 43 Once assembled, the T3SS functions as a molecular syringe, injecting effector proteins into the host cell cytoplasm. 44

The translocon is crucial for this delivery step, but it is not involved in the effectors' initial release from the bacteria. 45 Some studies, however, challenge this view, proposing a revised two-step model instead. In this model, the effectors and the translocon components are released prematurely and stored near the bacterial surface, potentially within lipid vesicles, before host contact. 42 , 43

Contact of bacteria with the host cell, possibly mediated by the needle, triggers pore formation by these pre-secreted components, allowing effectors to enter the host. 42 , 43 However, further research is necessary to determine the exact mechanism driving this process.

Interestingly, the T3SS injectisome shares a conserved transmembrane export apparatus with the flagellum, including homologous basal body rings and a protein secretion mechanism driven by ATP hydrolysis and proton motive force. 46 Phylogenetic evidence indicates that injectisomes evolved from flagellar systems through gene loss and horizontal transfer, adapting the motility machinery for effector protein translocation into host cells. 47 Moreover, there is evident regulatory interplay between these two components. Shulman et al. demonstrated that functional T3SS influences flagellar motility through transcriptional regulation of flagellar genes and secretion of flagellar proteins. 48 Notably, wild-type strains maintain motility through T3SS-mediated flagellar protein secretion, while T3SS mutants exhibit reduced transcription of flagellar genes and impaired motility. Degenerate T3SS loci, although no longer functioning as canonical secretion systems, may contribute to pathogenesis by altering bacterial surface properties, adhesion, or host interactions. 48 Within the gut ecosystem, T3SS activity is therefore best understood as an epithelial interaction module. Its major contribution to persistence may not be chronicity per se, but the creation of a permissive mucosal interface through intimate attachment, barrier disruption, immune modulation, and altered epithelial signaling.

The Type V Secretion System (T5SS)

Type V secretion systems (T5SS) are among the most prevalent mechanisms used by Gram-negative bacteria to display or secrete surface-associated proteins, including many E. coli colonization and virulence factors. 14 , 49 In E. coli, T5SS proteins generally follow a Sec-Bam-dependent pathway: precursor polypeptides cross the inner membrane through the Sec pathway, are stabilized in the periplasm, and are assembled in the outer membrane as β-barrel translocator domains that deliver passenger domains to the bacterial surface or extracellular space (Figure 1). 50 , 51

T5SS proteins are structurally diverse, comprising several subfamilies from Type Va to Type Ve. Because detailed T5SS classification and transport mechanisms have been reviewed extensively elsewhere, including in T5SS-focused reviews by Fan et al. 14 and Meuskens et al. 52 , we discuss architecture here only where it is directly relevant to adhesion, immune modulation, biofilm formation, mucosal colonization, or interbacterial competition. Accordingly, we focus on the T5SS subfamilies most relevant to E. coli persistence-associated biology: classical autotransporters (Va), two-partner secretion systems (Vb), trimeric autotransporters (Vc), and inverse autotransporters (Ve). 49 , 53

Within the Va group, self-associating adhesins such as Ag43, AIDA-I, TibA, and Upa family proteins promote autoaggregation, epithelial or extracellular-matrix adhesion, and biofilm formation in pathotypes including DAEC, EAEC, STEC, and UPEC (Table 2). 54-56 Serine protease autotransporters of Enterobacteriaceae (SPATEs), including EspP, Pic/PicU, Sat, Vat, and Hbp, act on host substrates such as mucins, complement components, coagulation factors, and leukocyte glycoproteins, depending on the protein (Table 2). 57-59 Through these activities, SPATEs can modulate mucus barriers, serum killing, epithelial integrity, and inflammatory responses. These functions may support mucosal niche formation or prolonged colonization in specific contexts, but their contribution to long-term carriage or recurrence remains pathotype- and model-dependent and has not yet been directly demonstrated in most E. coli systems.

A distinct functional axis of T5SS is interbacterial competition. Among non-classical Type V systems, two-partner secretion systems such as CdiA/B mediate contact-dependent inhibition of neighboring bacteria and have been linked to increased levels of antibiotic-tolerant persister cells in UPEC under defined experimental conditions, suggesting a possible density-dependent survival strategy in competitive niches (Table 2). 84 , 85 T5SS proteins can also reinforce physical retention at host surfaces. Trimeric autotransporters (Vc), exemplified by EhaG and related adhesins, form multimeric surface structures specialized for strong adhesion to host tissues and extracellular matrix, and can contribute to biofilm formation in EHEC and UPEC. 86

Likewise, inverse autotransporters (Ve) contribute to host-surface attachment. These include intimin, a key adhesin of EPEC and EHEC that mediates intimate attachment to intestinal epithelium through binding to the T3SS-delivered Tir receptor, 88 , 89 and YeeJ, which supports mucosal adhesion and biofilm formation (Table 2). 53 Unlike T3SS and T6SS, many T5SS proteins are shared between commensal and pathogenic E. coli, and their biological impact depends strongly on regulatory and ecological context. This is particularly relevant for ExPEC lineages, which can reside in the gut as commensals but cause disease after translocation to extraintestinal sites. The examples above illustrate several distinct functional routes by which T5SS proteins may support niche establishment: surface-associated adhesins promote aggregation, epithelial or extracellular-matrix attachment, and biofilm-associated retention; SPATEs modify host-facing barriers and immune components; and CdiA/B primarily targets neighboring bacteria, linking T5SS activity to interbacterial competition. Regulatory plasticity further shapes these functions, as illustrated by phase-variable Ag43, context-dependent Upa proteins, and CdiA/B-mediated competition. 56 , 91 , 92 Thus, T5SS contributions to persistence-associated biology arise from mechanistically distinct activities rather than from a single conserved persistence function.

Taken together, T5SS proteins act at the boundary between commensal fitness and virulence. Their roles in adhesion, aggregation, mucin interaction, complement modulation, interbacterial competition, and biofilm formation provide plausible routes toward mucosal persistence or protected-niche survival. However, these activities should not be interpreted as direct evidence for chronic or recurrent infection unless tested in appropriate long-term colonization, antibiotic-survival, reservoir, or relapse models.

The Type VI Secretion System (T6SS)

The Type VI Secretion System (T6SS), identified after the classical Type I-V secretion systems, is a contractile phage-tail-like apparatus widely distributed among Gram-negative bacteria. 93 These gene clusters, averaging 20 kb in size, often exist in multiple chromosomal copies. 94 Initially hypothesized to act as a virulence mechanism targeting eukaryotic host cells—a role supported by its involvement in pathogenesis—only a subset of T6SSs have been linked to host infection. 95 Instead, evidence highlights its critical role in bacterial competition: the T6SS delivers antibacterial effectors directly into neighboring cells during cell-to-cell contact. Target-cell survival depends largely on whether the recipient encodes cognate immunity proteins or other protective mechanisms. In E. coli, several T6SS effector proteins have been identified. 96-98

In addition to its antibacterial activity, the T6SS can target eukaryotic cells, including fungal cells, expanding its role in inter-kingdom interactions (Table 3). 15 , 95 In some bacteria, T6SS-associated effectors have been implicated in nutrient acquisition, including acquisition of metal ions such as iron and manganese. Whether this mechanism broadly contributes to E. coli persistence remains less well established. 99 This functional versatility highlights the adaptability of the T6SS and its importance in bacterial survival and fitness.

The T6SS apparatus is a double-membrane-spanning structure that delivers bacterial cytoplasmic substrates directly into a target cell or to the extracellular space (Figure 1). 106 This transport mechanism is a one-step process where substrates are secreted across both bacterial membranes simultaneously. Its architecture comprises three primary structural modules: a membrane complex, a baseplate complex, and a tail complex. 107

The membrane complex acts as an anchor, facilitating the integration of the T6SS into the bacterial cell envelope. This complex is specific to the T6SS and is minimally composed of three proteins: TssJ (an outer membrane lipoprotein), TssL (an inner membrane protein), and TssM (an inner membrane connector). Together, these proteins form a channel through which the tail complex can pass. The membrane complex is also crucial for maintaining the integrity of the bacterial cell during the secretion process. TssM is a key component that connects the outer membrane protein TssJ and the inner membrane protein TssL. 107 The next component, the baseplate, is composed of five cytoplasmic proteins—TssE, TssF, TssG, TssA, and TssK—and connects the membrane complex (TssJ–TssL–TssM) to the tail components. This assembly platform anchors the tail and initiates its polymerization. The sixth protein, VgrG, forms a needle-like spike in the center of the baseplate complex and is required for proper assembly of the T6SS tail tube. VgrG, located at the tip of the tail, helps to penetrate the target cell. 96 , 108

Extending from the baseplate is the contractile tail sheath, a tubular structure formed by TssB and TssC proteins. 109 This sheath spans from the bacterial inner membrane into the cytoplasm, encapsulating an inner tube composed of hemolysin co-regulated protein (Hcp). The Hcp tube, assembled as stacked hexameric rings, serves as an effector carrier or scaffold for transporting effector proteins—either folded or partially unfolded—into target cells during sheath contraction. 102 , 103 Together, the sheath and inner tube form a dynamic, phage-like apparatus optimized for precise effector delivery. 110

The assembly and function of the T6SS rely on several other proteins. These include ClpV ATPase (also known as TssH), which facilitates the depolymerization of TssB/TssC complexes within the cytoplasm; TssE, which initiates sheath polymerization; and PAAR, which sharpens the tip of the VgrG spike and may connect VgrG to effector proteins. 97 , 111 The structural architecture of the T6SS apparatus has been described in detail by structural and imaging studies 106 , 108 , 112 and reviewed for pathogenic E. coli. 113

The T6SS operates through a series of steps. First, the components assemble at the bacterial membrane, with the sheath forming around the inner tube and the baseplate attaching to the sheath. Upon firing, the extended sheath rapidly contracts, propelling the Hcp tube and VgrG spike out of the bacterial cell and into the target cell. 112 After contraction, the ATPase ClpV disassembles the contracted TssB/TssC sheath to recycle its components for another firing cycle. 97 , 111

The VgrG spike helps penetrate the target cell membrane, and the effector proteins are delivered through the inner tube. Some VgrG proteins have extensions that act as effector domains, allowing the T6SS to deliver a variety of different proteins. This versatility makes the T6SS a powerful tool used by bacteria for both offense and defense. It can inject toxins into competing bacteria or deliver proteins that help the bacteria survive in harsh environments. 105 , 112 , 114

The T6SS displays structural and mechanistic similarities to bacteriophage tail structures, with VgrG proteins mimicking the gp5-gp27 puncturing complex of phage T4 and Hcp hexamers forming tubes analogous to viral tail tubes. 106 Cryo-electron tomography reveals that T6SS sheaths adopt extended and contracted states nearly identical to phage tails, with contraction propelling Hcp-VgrG complexes into adjacent cells. 112 While phages use this machinery for DNA delivery, bacteria repurposed it for interbacterial competition and host manipulation, retaining core structural motifs but adapting regulatory elements for environmental responsiveness. 93 In the gut, the relevance of T6SS is therefore primarily ecological. By mediating contact-dependent interactions with neighboring microbes, T6SS may influence niche occupation, competition within polymicrobial communities, and the stability of E. coli reservoirs. Changes in the relative abundance and spatial occupancy of competing bacterial populations may in turn modify the microbial environment encountered at the mucosal surface, providing an indirect route through which T6SS-mediated competition can affect the host-microbiota interface. Its contribution to host virulence appears more strain-dependent and should therefore be interpreted more cautiously than its role in interbacterial competition.

This ecological role is illustrated in vivo by the interaction between the murine pathogen Citrobacter rodentium and commensal E. coli Mt1B1. A study by Serapio-Palacios et al. showed that C. rodentium uses T6SS-1 to target commensal E. coli during intestinal colonization, whereas E. coli Mt1B1 employs two T6SSs of its own to counter this competition. 105 This interaction demonstrates how T6SS activity can shape gut niche occupancy by determining which bacterial populations retain access to colonized intestinal niches. It also provides an intriguing example of how E. coli can use T6SS not only for offensive competition but also for defense against competing bacteria, highlighting reciprocal competitive dynamics within the gut microbiota.

Although T3SS, T5SS, and T6SS differ in architecture, substrates, and mechanisms of transport, their activities can affect related infection-associated processes, including epithelial interaction, immune modulation, biofilm formation, and interbacterial competition. More specifically, T3SS primarily acts at the epithelial interface through host-cell manipulation, T5SS supports surface retention and host-facing interactions through adhesion, aggregation, biofilm formation, and secreted or surface-associated proteins, whereas T6SS primarily shapes the microbial neighborhood through contact-dependent competition and niche occupancy. Together, these distinct activities provide complementary routes by which E. coli can establish and maintain colonized niches in a pathotype- and context-dependent manner. However, overlap at the phenotypic level should not be interpreted as evidence that these systems are co-regulated or function as a coordinated persistence network. The following sections therefore discuss these functions by biological context and distinguish experimentally demonstrated mechanisms from indirect or speculative links to persistence-associated biology.

Secretion-system functions at the gut-host interface

E. coli can establish long-term colonization or persistent infection in specific pathotypes, niches, and host contexts. Secretion systems may contribute to several processes associated with long-term survival within the host. In contexts where persistence-associated biology is relevant, three broad processes are particularly relevant: (i) modulation or evasion of host immune defenses, (ii) mucosal niche establishment and maintenance, and (iii) survival of antibiotic exposure. Below we explore the complex interplay between E. coli T3SS, T5SS, and T6SS and these steps, focusing on how these systems may contribute to chronic infection, recurrence or persister cell-associated survival.

(1)Modulation of the host immune defenses

Modulation of the host immune defenses

The host immune system is a main barrier against bacterial pathogens, hindering their ability to establish infection and spread. E. coli has evolved mechanisms to modulate innate immune responses, including strategies to evade phagocytosis and autophagy, which ultimately allows E. coli to survive and proliferate within the host.

T3SS

Phagocytosis serves as a key defense mechanism of the innate immune system, enabling the engulfment and destruction of invasive pathogens. E. coli, mainly EHEC and EPEC, employ a T3SS to subvert this process, evading both cis-phagocytosis (direct uptake of the bacterium itself) and trans-phagocytosis (phagocytosis of unrelated IgG-opsonized particles) 115 through modulation of Rho family of small GTPases and other actin regulators. These effectors primarily illustrate how T3SS can modulate phagocytic uptake and epithelial responses during intestinal infection. The T3SS-dependent effectors—EspF, EspB, EspJ, and EspH—have been characterized as key inhibitors of phagocytosis. 19 , 20 , 115 EspF inhibits PI3K (phosphatidyl inositol 3-kinase) activation, 115 and EspJ inhibit the Src signaling, both of which are required for phagocytosis. 20 In addition, studies have shown that EspF inhibits phagocytosis by macrophages 116 by disrupting mitochondrial activity, and nucleolar function (Figure 2). 21 EspB affects myosin function to inhibit pseudopod extension and phagosome closure (Figure 2), 117 EspH disrupts the host-cell actin cytoskeleton by suppressing Rho GTPases, 34 crucial regulators of actin polymerization. This disruption impairs the ability of phagocytes to engulf bacteria by targeting Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), which activate Rho GTPases. 19 By inhibiting RhoGEFs, EspH prevents the activation of Rho GTPases and subsequent actin polymerization, preventing bacterial internalization. 19 Of note, EspT expressed by atypical invasive EPEC, exhibits GEF activity that induces the formation of actin-based plasma membrane ruffles, thereby facilitating the bacterium’s invasion of nonphagocytic cells. 36 T3SS effectors can also modulate inflammatory signaling pathways, including nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK); these mechanisms are not discussed in detail here because they have been extensively reviewed elsewhere. 118 , 119

T5SS

Two well-described autotransporters (ATs) from the Va subtype of the SPATE group: extracellular serine protease P (EspP) and the protein involved in colonization (Pic), have been shown to inactivate host immune system components. 67 , 72 EspP is a class-1 serine protease AT described in EPEC and related enteric E. coli backgrounds. 58 In STEC, a related EspP homolog has been described as PssA (protease secreted by STEC). 68 Like other SPATEs, EspP undergoes proteolytic processing during secretion, releasing its passenger domain to act extracellularly. One of the key functions of the EspP passenger domain is the cleavage of host substrates, including pepsin A, and human coagulation factor V. 68 Cleavage of factor V can impair coagulation and has been linked to the pathogenic effects of EspP in enteric infection. Moreover, EspP cleaves the complement system components: C3, C3b, and C5, interfering with the complement cascade and disrupting the first line of host immune defenses. 67 At the same time, EspP does not target complement regulatory proteins like Factor H (FH) and Factor I (FI), preserving their functionality, which allows intact FH and FI to assist bacteria in evading complement activation via the alternative pathway. 67 Beyond its effects on host substrates, EspP contributes to bacterial adherence and biofilm establishment by promoting the formation of rope-like adhesive aggregates, which may enhance colonization and potentially support persistence. 69 Studies show that EspP also exhibits proteolytic activity against T3SS translocator components. 70 Downregulation of T3SS in this mechanism leads to further promotion of adherence to host tissues. 70

Pic is a class-2 SPATE described in EAEC and, as PicU, in selected UPEC strains. 71 , 120 Following secretion and passenger-domain release, Pic contributes to serum resistance and protection from complement-mediated bacteriolysis by degrading key complement proteins involved in all three activation pathways (classical, lectin, and alternative), including C2, C3, C3b, C4, and C4b (Figure 3). 72 In contrast, Pic does not cleave C1q, C5, or factor H, demonstrating a degree of substrate specificity. 72 Pic-mediated cleavage of C3 generates a C3b-like fragment that is further degraded by host regulators (factor I and factor H), reducing complement activation synergistically with host complement molecules. 72 These activities may favor mucosal colonization in specific pathotype- and model-dependent contexts, but a direct contribution to chronic or recurrent infection has not been established. In addition to its complement-targeting activities, Pic exhibits mucinase activity facilitating the degradation of mucins such as MUC2 and MUC5AC, allowing E. coli to penetrate the mucus layer. 57 Surprisingly, a study of Flores-Sanchez et al. revealed that Pic stimulates rapid mucin secretion in goblet-like cells by intracellular Ca-signaling pathway activation mediated by PLC signal transduction pathway activation, DAG production and IP3 release. 59 Therefore, this dual activity of Pic, functioning both as a mucus secretagogue and a mucinase, facilitates the generation and penetration of the mucus layer niche, enabling EAEC to reside within the mucus layer and simultaneously access epithelial cells. 59 Pic also specifically targets O-linked glycan-rich glycoproteins on leukocyte surfaces, disrupting proteins involved in adhesion, migration, and inflammatory responses. 79 A key example is CD43 expressed across nearly all hematopoietic cell lineages, including those in the bone marrow, thymus, and peripheral lymphoid tissues. In its unprocessed form, CD43 functions to prevent leukocyte adhesion to neighboring cells, thereby enabling effective migration in response to chemokine signaling. However, cleavage of CD43 alters its normal regulatory function, thereby promoting leukocyte adhesion to adjacent cells. 79 This highlights the complexity and multitargeted character of pathogenic strategy facilitating Pic functionality, potentially paralyzing local leukocyte-mediated functions: trafficking, migration, and chemoattractant responding.

Another example of a class-2 SPATE is the non-cytotoxic hemoglobin protease, also known as hemoglobin-binding protein (Hbp), which is expressed in ExPEC and APEC strains and contributes to evasion of host immune responses. 80 Hbp is closely related to temperature-sensitive hemagglutinin (Tsh); the two homologs differ by only two amino acids and exhibit nearly identical functions. Following Otto et al. 80 we use the designation Hbp throughout this review.

Similarly to Pic AT, Hbp exhibits proteolytic properties towards mucin, coagulation factor V, and O-glycosylated proteins (CD43, CD45, CD93, CD162, and CXCL1), thereby modifying leukocyte physiology and functionality (Figure 2). 80 , 81 Furthermore, the most distinctive function of Hbp relates to survival and the acquisition of nutrients critical for growth during infection in a challenging environment. Hbp facilitates iron acquisition for bacterial cells through heme release from hemoglobin. 81 Iron availability in host cells is tightly regulated and this element is often sequestered to limit bacterial proliferation. By acquiring iron through Hbp, E. coli can sustain its survival and growth even in iron-limited environments like the urinary tract or during infection-associated inflammation. This correlates with literature reports of the isolation of Hbp protein from clinical strains of UPEC, 82 but it remains unclear whether this form of iron acquisition contributes directly to persistent or recurrent infection in vivo.

An example of ATs interacting with host immune responses is the Eib family of immunoglobulin-binding proteins, which belong to the group Vc - trimeric autotransporter adhesins (TAA). 121 Besides playing a role in adherence to epithelial cells, autoagglutination and biofilm formation, these proteins bind the Fc portion of IgG and IgA in a non-immune manner, effectively interfering with normal antibody functions such as opsonization and activation of the complement cascade (Figure 2). 121 All Eib family members including EibA, EibC, EibD, EibE, EibF, and the newly identified EibG share several features, such as the ability to impart resistance to human serum complement and a tendency to exist as stable multimers. 122 , 123 By masking the bacterial surface with bound immunoglobulins, Eib proteins may prevent immune recognition and phagocytosis, thereby contributing to recurrence or persistence of infection, but further studies are necessary to prove this conclusion. 124

T6SS

While research on T6SS primarily focuses on its role in interbacterial competition, there is at present no direct evidence that the T6SS enables the bacterium to evade phagocytosis. Rather than mediating immune evasion in the strict sense, the available studies point instead to roles in immune modulation and intracellular survival. 15 , 95

A study provides insights into the T6SS's role in intracellular survival. 95 This study demonstrates that EHEC secretes a catalase enzyme, KatN, via its T6SS. This catalase neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced by macrophages, which are essential for killing phagocytosed bacteria (Figure 2). By reducing ROS levels, EHEC can survive within macrophages and potentially evade phagocytic killing. This finding supports a role for T6SS-mediated KatN secretion in intramacrophage survival under oxidative stress. 95

Further research is needed to determine whether T6SS-associated effects on immune-cell function or intracellular survival extend beyond specific experimental models. At present, available evidence supports a role for selected T6SS effectors in immune modulation or oxidative-stress survival. Investigating the potential role of T6SS effectors in phagocytosis evasion, chronic infection, or recurrence will be crucial for clarifying how T6SS contributes to immune evasion in E. coli.

Mucosal niche establishment and reservoir formation

Adherence to host tissues

E. coli T3SS effectors, besides blocking phagocytosis, manipulate cytoskeletal components to form intracellular pedestals, which promote bacterial attachment and colonization. 36 , 89 This process, mediated by the translocated effector Tir (translocated intimin receptor), binds the bacterial surface adhesin intimin, triggering a signaling cascade that recruits host actin-nucleating factors (e.g., Nck, N-WASP, and the Arp2/3 complex) (Figure 3). 88 , 89 The resulting pedestal anchors the bacterium to the host cell surface while maintaining its extracellular localization, thereby avoiding internalization and lysosomal destruction. 36 , 89 Interestingly, EPEC and EHEC utilize distinct mechanisms for pedestal formation, with EPEC relying on Tir tyrosine phosphorylation and EHEC employing the effector protein EspFU to bypass this step. 125 This dual strategy—blocking phagocytic uptake while securing extracellular adhesion—promotes colonization of the intestinal epithelium and could contribute to persistence under some conditions, but has not been directly linked to chronic or recurrent infection. 19 , 116 In addition, Esp proteins can act as adhesins, mediating the binding to epithelial cells. 16 , 126 For example, the EspA, EscF, and the translocon pore proteins EspB and EspD are thought to help the bacteria overcome the thick glycocalyx layer that covers the intestinal epithelium. These components work together to form a channel that connects the bacterial cytosol with the host cell membrane, allowing for the translocation of effector proteins. 17 , 18 , 42 Studies have shown that E. coli strains with a nonfunctional T3SS are unable to adhere to enterocytes and are avirulent in infection models. This highlights the importance of T3SS in E. coli pathogenesis and its role in establishing infection. 126 , 127

In addition, T5SS can also mediate host tissue attachment in diverse E. coli pathotypes. 55 Classical ATs like AIDA-I, TibA and Ag43 promote diffuse adherence to epithelial cells and extracellular matrix components, thereby stabilizing intimate attachment of EHEC, EPEC and UPEC to mucosal surfaces. 60,63–65,128 Serine protease autotransporters like Sat enable immune evasion by cleaving complement proteins, protecting bacteria from bloodstream clearance. 129 In different E. coli pathotypes, T3SS-driven pedestal formation and T5SS-mediated adhesins can each support extracellular colonization of the intestinal or urinary tract epithelium. Any functional cooperation between these systems during persistent or recurrent infection remains to be demonstrated. 130–132 The role of T6SS in E. coli adherence to host tissues is not well established. However, some studies suggest that it may contribute to this process by modulating bacterial interactions with host cells and the surrounding environment. 103 , 104 , 133 For example, T6SS effectors can modify host cell adhesion molecules or promote bacterial internalization. 103 , 133 In EHEC, the T6SS has been shown to be involved in intracellular survival and virulence in mice. 95 This suggests that the T6SS may contribute to EHEC colonization and survival in specific host niches and underscores its importance for competition with other bacteria in the gut microbiome, whereas any direct role in persistence or recurrence remains speculative. 105 , 134 , 135

Biofilm-associated persistence and intracellular reservoirs

Biofilms are complex communities of bacteria encased in an extracellular matrix that provides protection against environmental stresses and host defenses. 54 , 136 Numerous secretion systems have been implicated in biofilm formation and maturation or at least play an adjuvant role in the structural and physiological development of biofilms (Tables 1–3). However, despite this vast knowledge, many specific aspects of the role of secretion systems in persistent and recurrent infections, particularly in the context of intracellular bacterial communities (IBC) established by diverse E. coli pathotypes other than UPEC, remain insufficiently explored.

Although the T3SS is classically associated with acute host-cell manipulation in enteric E. coli pathotypes, persistence in E. coli infections is more clearly linked to biofilm formation, intracellular bacterial communities, quiescent intracellular reservoirs, and persister-cell development. This is best established in UPEC, where bacteria can form intracellular bacterial communities within bladder epithelial cells and later establish quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs) that survive antibiotic exposure and immune clearance, thereby contributing to recurrent infection. 7 , 137 , 138 These intracellular biofilm-like communities are supported by adhesion, aggregation, stress tolerance, and regulatory pathways that promote a sessile or low-metabolic state. In this context, c-di-GMP signaling is particularly relevant because elevated c-di-GMP levels promote biofilm-associated physiology, extracellular matrix production, and persister formation, which may link surface attachment and intracellular persistence to antibiotic tolerance and relapse. 139 Rather than assigning persistence to a single secretion system, current evidence suggests that secretion-system-associated adhesins, toxins, and competition factors can influence biofilm-associated survival, intracellular maintenance, or reactivation in defined models. These effects depend on broader regulatory and stress-response pathways, including c-di-GMP signaling, metabolic adaptation, and host-niche conditions. Although intracellular bacterial communities and quiescent intracellular reservoirs are well described in UPEC bladder infection models, whether similar reservoir-based strategies operate in other E. coli pathotypes remains less well established. 140

Several T5SS proteins contribute to adhesion, autoaggregation, and biofilm-associated phenotypes in E. coli, including AIDA-like adhesins, self-associating autotransporters, and trimeric autotransporter adhesins. 141 These proteins can promote bacterial–bacterial interactions, epithelial attachment, and extracellular matrix binding, thereby supporting surface-associated colonization and biofilm formation. However, biofilm formation should be interpreted as a persistence-associated phenotype only when linked to long-term colonization, antibiotic survival, reservoir maintenance, or relapse in an appropriate model. The most well-characterized autotransporters contributing to biofilm establishment are Self-Associating Auto Transporters (SAATs): Antigen 43 (Ag43), AIDA-I, and TibA (Figure 3). Extensive research has been conducted on their contribution to biofilm maturation, intracellular survival, and host colonization which has been comprehensively described in the literature over the past two decades. 141 Ag43, encoded by the flu gene, is one of the best-characterized autotransporters involved in autoaggregation and biofilm development. This self-associating adhesin enhances bacterial clustering and biofilm maturation, reinforcing the structural integrity of biofilms in both extracellular and intracellular environments. 54 , 60 , 142 Notably, Ag43 expression has been associated with UPEC intracellular bacterial communities in bladder epithelial cells, suggesting a role in aggregation or community structure within this niche; its direct contribution to recurrence should be interpreted in the context of UPEC-specific reservoir models. 138 In addition, Ag43-mediated cell aggregation enhances resistance to oxidative stress, shielding E. coli from host immune mechanisms such as neutrophil-derived hydrogen peroxide. 143

AIDA-I and TibA are SAATs that also contribute to E. coli biofilm formation, but are rather secreted by diarrheagenic and enterotoxigenic strains. 61 , 62 These glycosylated adhesins mediate bacterial aggregation and adhesion to host epithelial cells, supporting colonization-associated phenotypes; direct links to long-term persistence or recurrence remain less well defined. Their ability to modulate interspecies interactions suggests an important role in polymicrobial infections, further complicating treatment strategies. Several studies have indicated that SAATs are induced specifically during biofilm growth, and their expression enhances E. coli biofilm formation. 143-145 Studies on clinically isolated strains of UPEC have demonstrated that E. coli isolates from recurrent UTIs exhibit enhanced biofilm-forming ability compared to isolates from acute infections, further supporting the role of biofilms in infection relapse. 146

Among the autotransporters engaged in adhesion, the Upa family of autotransporters functions primarily as adhesins, mediating bacterial attachment to host extracellular matrix (ECM) components. 56 , 66 , 86 This role appears complementary to SAATs, which primarily mediate cell-to-cell adhesion and self-recognition 147 rather than being involved in non-specific initial adhesion. Type V autotransporters such as UpaB and UpaH facilitate adhesion to fibronectin and laminin and may support bladder colonization in UPEC models (Figure 3). 66 In murine UTI models, deletion of the upaB gene significantly reduces early colonization. 56 Trimeric adhesin (T5cSS) UpaG further contributes to biofilm stability and interbacterial aggregation, enhancing resistance to hydrodynamic forces within the bladder. 86 , 87 While their direct involvement in intracellular biofilms remains under investigation, their role in promoting strong bacterial attachment to ECM may influence UPEC colonization and biofilm-associated phenotypes, but direct links to IBC maturation or recurrent infection require model-specific evidence.

In parallel to IBCs, QIRs represent a distinct survival strategy in which E. coli enters a quiescent state within deeper layers of bladder epithelial cells. Unlike actively growing IBCs, QIRs consist of non-replicating bacteria enclosed within membrane-bound vacuoles. 7 Host cell differentiation and actin cytoskeletal rearrangements appear to regulate the transition of QIRs into active infection states, triggering bacterial release and subsequent reinfection. 7 , 60 , 148 This mechanism may contribute to antibiotic treatment failure because bacteria within QIRs can be protected from antibiotic exposure and immune clearance and may later resume growth under permissive conditions. These intracellular reservoir states should be distinguished from phenotypic persister cells, which are defined by antibiotic tolerance without genetic resistance. 7 , 137

Among T5SS-associated proteins, the vacuolating autotransporter toxins Vat and Sat can modify epithelial-cell physiology. These toxins, members of the SPATEs, induce vacuolation and cytoskeletal remodeling in host epithelial cells, which can promote intracellular survival or dissemination in experimental models. 75 , 78 These activities may create host-cell conditions that favor bacterial survival or dissemination, but direct roles in QIR formation or recurrent infection require cautious interpretation. Vat, expressed by UPEC, disrupts the urothelial monolayer and increases epithelial permeability (Figure 2). By altering the actin cytoskeleton and tight junctions, it facilitates bacterial invasion and intracellular survival. 76 , 78 Similarly, Sat exhibits vacuolating activity in bladder and kidney epithelial cells, leading to epithelial damage and bacterial dissemination (Figure 3). 75 , 76 The ability of these toxins to create intracellular niches further complicates bacterial clearance, suggesting their potential contribution to prolonged or relapsing infections. However, further research into the precise molecular interactions governing these processes is essential to fully understand their contribution and to develop novel treatment strategies aimed at preventing reinfection.

The T6SS has been implicated in biofilm formation in several bacterial species, including E. coli. 113 Evidence from other Gram-negative bacteria illustrates how T6SS activity can influence biofilm-associated competition. In Vibrio cholerae, T6SS-mediated killing within biofilms is associated with localized extracellular DNA release, whereas the response regulator VxrB coordinates T6SS gene expression and biofilm matrix production. 149 These findings illustrate a possible link between T6SS-mediated competition and biofilm architecture, but their relevance to E. coli biofilm-associated persistence remains to be demonstrated. Another example is that T6SS-mediated killing of neighboring bacteria can alter the structure and composition of the biofilm. This can be beneficial for the bacteria possessing the T6SS, allowing them to dominate the biofilm and access more resources. 150

In addition, T6SS has an important role in intermicrobial interactions. Comparative studies in other Gram-negative pathogens, including Vibrio cholerae and Salmonella enterica, show that T6SS-mediated antibacterial activity can shape intestinal colonization by targeting competing members of the gut microbiota. 134 , 135 In E. coli, available evidence suggests that T6SS may similarly contribute to strain-dependent interbacterial competition, niche fitness, and persistence-associated interactions within polymicrobial environments, although the ecological relevance of these activities remains less well defined.

Persistence in the host

Persister cells represent phenotypically altered bacterial subpopulations that can survive otherwise bactericidal antibiotic exposure without acquiring genetic resistance. 151 This bet-hedging survival strategy allows a small fraction of cells to enter a transient growth-arrested or low-activity state, tolerate antibiotic stress, and resume growth once antibiotic pressure is removed. 152 In E. coli, persister-cell biology should be distinguished from biofilm-associated protection, intracellular reservoirs, poor antibiotic penetration, and clinical recurrence. These processes may overlap in some infection settings, but they are not equivalent.

Among secretion-system-linked mechanisms, the clearest experimental connection is contact-dependent inhibition (CDI). Ghosh et al. demonstrated that CDI systems in the commensal gut isolate EC93 and the UPEC strain 536 contribute to antibiotic-tolerant persister-cell formation through direct cell-cell interactions. 85 CDI-mediated growth inhibition can induce a persister-like state in susceptible cells within dense clonal populations, and this effect depends on the balance between toxin delivery and cognate immunity. 85 Notably, CDI-mediated persister formation is population density-dependent, with higher persister fractions arising in dense, clonal communities. 85 These findings suggest that CDI can link interbacterial interaction to antibiotic-tolerant survival under defined high-density conditions, although broader roles in intracellular reservoirs, treatment failure, or recurrent infection remain indirect and require model-specific validation.

In vitro studies of antibiotic-induced persisters have traditionally emphasized dormancy, but this assumption may not apply to all intracellular antibiotic-tolerant populations. 151 This assumption is not necessarily absolute in case of intracellular persisters, as shown by Staples and colleagues on the example of non-growing, antibiotic-tolerant Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium persister cells which actively subverted macrophage host cells. 153 During macrophage infection, S. enterica antibiotic persisters retain transcriptional, translational, and metabolic activity and, moreover, translocate pathogenicity island 2 (SPI-2) type III secretion system effectors into the eukaryotic cytosol to reprogram the host cell into a non-inflammatory and infection-permissive state. 153 Although similar behavior has not been demonstrated for E. coli, this finding raises the possibility that secretion-system activity may also contribute to the host-modulating capacity of intracellular E. coli persisters. This remains speculative and should be tested directly in E. coli infection models.

A related, but distinct, question concerns factors that support intracellular survival of E. coli in phagocytic cells. OmpA-positive E. coli can bind, enter, and survive within murine and human macrophages and monocytes, suppress selected chemokine or cytokine responses, and inhibit macrophage apoptosis through induction of the anti-apoptotic factor Bcl-xL. 154 , 155 OmpA has also been reported to influence secretion of T3SS proteins such as EspA and EspB in EHEC without reducing expression of LEE-encoded T3SS genes. 156 These findings suggest a possible link between envelope-associated factors, secretion activity, and intracellular host-cell modulation, but they should be interpreted as an open research question rather than evidence that E. coli persisters actively reprogram host cells during antibiotic exposure.

Potential functional interplay and open questions on secretion-system co‑regulation

The interactions discussed in this subsection are presented as hypothesis-generating observations rather than as evidence of a coordinated secretion-system program. Each has been documented individually, but their integration into a single regulatory network has not been demonstrated. Evidence from separate studies suggests possible functional convergence between secretion-system-associated processes or parallel responses to shared host cues. Whether these observations reflect direct cross-regulation, coordinated expression, or independent context-dependent responses remains unknown. Nevertheless, comparative analysis of distinct secretion systems suggests that their activity may be context-dependent and modulated by host or environmental cues, potentially allowing E. coli to adjust its interaction strategies across different colonization and infection settings.

Particularly interesting is the “pseudo-antagonistic” interaction between two major virulence systems, the T5SS and T3SS, mentioned above. The proteolytic activity of the extracellular serine protease EspP against T3SS translocon components constitutes a form of negative regulation that can dampen T3SS‑dependent host-cell interactions. 70 However, this effect may be offset by EspP’s additional functions, including rope‑like aggregate formation and complement cleavage, which facilitate adherence, biofilm formation, and immune evasion. 67 , 69 In this way, EspP activity could shift the balance from strongly invasive, T3SS‑dominated interactions toward more colonization‑oriented strategies in specific niches, although any contribution to chronic or recurrent infection in E. coli remains to be demonstrated. Conversely, EspP appears to exhibit functional synergy with the autotransporter Pic in the degradation of key complement cascade components, especially C3 and C3b, thereby protecting bacteria from complement-mediated lysis. 67 EspP activity preserves the integrity of the complement regulators Factor I and Factor H, which are essential for the degradation of Pic-derived C3b-like fragments and for co-opting host regulatory molecules to suppress complement activation and enhance inhibition of the alternative pathway. 72 , 157 Thus, both autotransporters enhance immune evasion not only through direct proteolysis, but also via synergistic collaboration with host immune regulators.

Beyond transcriptional control, one possible model is that T3SS and T6SS activities could, in principle, act in a complementary fashion to promote intracellular survival. As described above, the T6SS enhances survival within macrophages by secreting the catalase KatN, which neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during the oxidative burst in specific EHEC infection models. 95 By analogy, the T3SS activity has been implicated in maintaining persister-like intracellular cells in Salmonella 158 and could, in principle, modulate host trafficking or inflammatory signaling in ways that prevent bactericidal responses in E. coli. Together, these systems might collectively resemble an intracellular protection module in some infection settings. Although few studies have explicitly linked these mechanisms, their coexistence across multiple infection models is consistent with, but does not establish, such a coordinated strategy, which remains to be tested directly. At present, however, these mechanisms have been documented separately, and there is no direct evidence that they operate as a coordinated program in E. coli.

Overall, the cited studies suggest that secretion-system activities can be context-dependent and may respond to overlapping host or microbial cues. However, evidence that T3SS, T5SS, and T6SS operate as a coordinated persistence program in E. coli remains lacking. Future studies should directly test whether these systems are co-regulated, co-expressed, or functionally linked during long-term colonization, antibiotic exposure, or recurrence.

From gut colonization to recurrence: evidence limits and future directions

E. coli secretion systems contribute to several biological processes that can influence long-term colonization, mucosal niche formation, microbiota competition, antibiotic survival, and recurrent infection. However, the evidence does not currently support a single unified model in which T3SS, T5SS, and T6SS act as a coordinated secretion-system network driving persistence across all E. coli pathotypes. Their roles are better understood as pathotype-specific, niche-specific, and evidence-dependent.

T3SS is best established as an epithelial interaction system in enteric pathotypes such as EPEC and EHEC, where it promotes intimate attachment, cytoskeletal remodeling, barrier disruption, and modulation of host signaling. These activities may facilitate colonization and create a mucosal interface favorable to bacterial persistence, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence for chronic infection or recurrence without appropriate model-specific support. T5SS provides one of the clearest links to mucosal persistence-associated phenotypes because many autotransporters and related proteins promote adhesion, aggregation, biofilm formation, mucin interaction, complement resistance, and epithelial damage. These functions are particularly relevant to reservoir formation and tissue colonization, especially in ExPEC and UPEC contexts, but they also overlap with general colonization and virulence mechanisms. T6SS is most strongly supported as an ecological and microbiota-facing system. Its antibacterial effectors, immunity proteins, and contact-dependent killing activity can influence interbacterial competition and niche occupation within polymicrobial environments. Links between T6SS and recurrence or chronic infection in E. coli remain more indirect and should be tested in strain- and niche-defined models.

A major challenge for the field is to separate persistence-associated mechanisms from general virulence traits. Adhesion, immune modulation, epithelial injury, biofilm formation, and interbacterial competition can all support colonization, but they do not automatically demonstrate persistence, persister-cell formation, or clinical relapse. Future studies should therefore define the biological endpoint carefully: persistent colonization, persistent infection, phenotypic antibiotic tolerance, biofilm-associated survival, intracellular reservoir formation, relapse, or reinfection. This distinction is especially important when comparing enteric E. coli pathotypes with UPEC and other ExPEC lineages, because mechanisms established in bladder reservoir models cannot be directly transferred to intestinal carriage or systemic infection.

Several key evidence gaps remain. It is still unclear whether T3SS, T5SS, or T6SS genes are consistently enriched in recurrent clinical isolates, whether these systems are expressed during long-term colonization, whether secretion activity persists after antibiotic exposure, and whether secretion-system mutants show reproducible defects in relapse or reservoir reseeding in vivo. It is also unclear how secretion systems interact with better-established persistence determinants, including type 1 fimbriae, curli, capsule, LPS modification, iron acquisition systems, toxins, stringent and general stress responses, c-di-GMP signaling, biofilm matrix production, and metabolic adaptation. Addressing these questions will require longitudinal clinical isolate collections, pathotype-resolved comparative genomics, in vivo expression analyzes, polymicrobial gut models, and infection systems that distinguish relapse from reinfection.

Overall, T3SS, T5SS, and T6SS should be viewed as context-dependent interaction modules rather than universal drivers of E. coli persistence. Their importance lies in the fact that they connect bacterial physiology to host epithelial responses, immune pressures, microbial competition, and reservoir stability. A critical, evidence-calibrated understanding of these systems may help identify when secretion-system activity is merely associated with colonization and when it causally contributes to persistent or recurrent infection. This distinction will be essential for developing future anti-virulence or reservoir-disrupting strategies that complement, rather than replace, conventional antimicrobial approaches.
