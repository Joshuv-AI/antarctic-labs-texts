Dupilumab in Epidermolysis Bullosa Pruriginosa: “All That Glisters Is Not Gold for Every Age”
Authors: Michela Magnano, Riccardo Balestri, Giulia Rech, Andrea Bassi, Carlo Mazzatenta
Published: 2026

Introduction

Epidermolysis bullosa pruriginosa (EBP) is a very rare clinical variant of dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, characterized by intense pruritus, prurigo-like nodular lesions, and skin fragility. The itch is severe and poorly responsive to traditional therapies. Recently, dupilumab has been proposed as a promising therapeutic option for EBP, especially for controlling pruritus [1,2]. However, incomplete responses have been observed, particularly among adults over middle age [1].

Case Presentation

We report a pediatric and an adult case of EBP treated with dupilumab, with different outcomes.

The first patient was a 12-year-old female with a long-standing history of pruritic dermatitis localized to the limbs, initially diagnosed as eczema and treated with steroids, without improvement (Figure 1A). Suspecting an EB, we performed a biopsy, revealing a subepidermal bulla, with a negative direct immunofluorescence. Genetic testing identified a COL7A1mutation, confirming the diagnosis of EBP.

A) 12-year-old patient before dupilumab; B)12-year-old patient after four weeks of treatment; C) 62-year-old patient before dupilumab; D) 62-year-old patient after 14 weeks of treatment.

Dupilumab was initiated at 400 mg, followed by 200 mg every 14 days. The patient showed marked improvement after the second dose (Figure 1b). At the 14-week follow-up, she reported significant pruritus reduction and near-complete resolution of skin lesions. After one year, the dose was tapered to 200 mg every four weeks, maintaining good control of symptoms and clinical signs.

The second patient was a 62-year-old female with a lifelong history of EBP and confirmed heterozygous COL7A1 mutation. She had undergone multiple unsuccessful treatments, including systemic steroids and advanced wound care (Figure 1C). Given her history of breast cancer and pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma syndrome type 1, systemic immunosuppressive therapies were contraindicated. Dupilumab was started at the standard dosing (600 mg loading, then 300 mg every 14 days). However, at the 14-week follow-up, she showed no clinical improvement, therefore the treatment was discontinued after six months (Figure 1D).

Conclusion

The mechanism of dupilumab’s efficacy in EBP remains unclear. Some authors hypothesize a Th2-mediated pathogenesis, although the roles of IL-4 and IL-13 are not fully defined.

To date, including our patients, 22 cases of EBP treated with dupilumab have been reported (Table 1), with mostly favorable outcomes. Of these, only three patients failed to respond. Notably, the mean age of non-responders was significantly higher (60.33±1.57 years) than that of responders (24.37±14.41 years) [1]. On the other hand, our pediatric case responded rapidly to dupilumab, and disease control was maintained after dose tapering.

Although the exact mechanism of pruritus in EBP has not been elucidated, we can speculate on the role of age in the response to therapy. Dupilumab has been shown to help disrupt the itch–scratch cycle, leading to symptomatic and clinical improvement [3]. This supports the hypothesis of a possible involvement of intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD). The role of IENFD has gained increasing relevance in the diagnostics and management of chronic pruritus.

In atopic dermatitis and bullous pemphigoid, dupilumab, like other immunomodulatory medications, significantly increased IENFD to levels similar to those of healthy subjects, suggesting a restoration of neuroanatomy [4,5]. Since that cutaneous nerve fiber appears to undergo continuous remodeling, we can speculate that in EBP, aging and a long-standing history of scratching may impair neuronal plasticity, thereby reducing the potential for nerve restoration and, consequentially, the effectiveness of dupilumab.

However, because efficacy appears more limited in older individuals [6–8], further studies are needed to clarify the pathogenic mechanism and which therapeutic strategies are most effective in this population.
