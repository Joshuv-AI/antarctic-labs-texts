Residue-free peeling of EGaIn oxide enabled by contact angle control
Authors: Sangyun Jung, Jeong Gon Son, Michael D. Dickey, Wonjung Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gallium-based liquid metals (GBLMs) are widely used in soft machines and stretchable electronics, but their nanometer-thick surface oxide layer induces strong adhesion to substrates, making residue-free removal challenging. Conventional methods rely on harsh chemical treatments, limiting practical applications. Here, we show that the oxide layer of eutectic gallium–indium (EGaIn) can be cleanly peeled from a substrate when a sufficiently large contact angle is maintained during slow delamination. Through experimental and theoretical analysis, we show that peeling fails at small contact angles due to mechanical fracture of the oxide skin at the oxide–air interface, leaving behind residue. By examining the contact line at the nanoscale, we identify the failure mechanism governing this process. This work provides fundamental insights into the interfacial mechanics of GBLMs and establishes a framework for improving their controllability in liquid metal-based technologies.

Introduction

Gallium-based liquid metals (GBLMs), such as eutectic gallium–indium (EGaIn) and Galinstan, have garnered significant attention for their unique combination of fluidity, high electrical conductivity, and low toxicity1. These properties make them ideal for soft electronics2, flexible circuits3,4, and biomedical devices5, where they integrate with gels, elastomers, and biological tissues to form functional components. Their motion can be precisely controlled through electrochemical modulation of surface tension6,7 and electromagnetic forces8,9, enabling applications in microfluidic systems10, and actuators11.

Building on their fluidity, electrical conductivity, and tunable interfacial properties, GBLMs have recently enabled more advanced and integrated systems. GBLMs have enabled reconfigurable architectures with real-time responsiveness to shape and strain12, deformable circuits that conform to 3D freeform surfaces13, and fully integrated standalone platforms that combine sensing, actuation, and power in a single autonomous system14.

A defining characteristic of GBLMs is their rapid oxidation in ambient conditions, resulting in the formation of a nanometer-thick gallium oxide (Ga₂O₃) skin15. This oxide layer stabilizes at approximately 3 nm under laboratory conditions16 and significantly alters interfacial behavior. It imparts non-Newtonian rheological properties17, allowing for stable patterning18, controlled extrusion19, and microfabrication of liquid metal structures20. However, it also induces strong adhesion to solid surfaces, pinning the contact line and hindering smooth motion21,22. Once adhered, GBLMs resist detachment, and their removal often leaves residual oxide deposits23,24, posing challenges for applications that require clean separation24,25.

Various approaches have been explored to mitigate adhesion, including surface roughening26–28, nanoparticle coatings22,29, and chemical treatments such as phosphonic acid passivation24 and interfacial lubrication layers30. While these methods can reduce adhesion under specific conditions, they do not provide a general solution for residue-free removal when GBLMs are in direct contact with smooth solid surfaces.

A key but poorly understood factor in this process is the receding motion of the contact line. Unlike advancing contact angles, which are well characterized, receding contact angles are difficult to define because the receding contact line remains pinned due to strong adhesion31,32. When the liquid metal retracts, the oxide layer remains adhered to the substrate, leaving behind an oxide membrane33,34.

Encapsulated liquid systems, such as vesicles and elastic capsules, can be detached from surfaces by controlling membrane mechanics35,36, suggesting that a similar approach may enable residue-free detachment of oxide-coated GBLMs. Previous studies have reported a case where printed GBLM micro-structures could be removed under tensile forces19, but peeling is not always successful, especially at larger length scales. Instead of detaching cleanly, the residue remains on the surface, highlighting the complexity of the delamination mechanism.

In this study, we investigate the mechanics of residue-free peeling of the oxide layer surrounding EGaIn and demonstrate that the local contact angle dictates successful peeling. Peeling occurs only at slow speeds and large contact angles, while at small angles, the oxide remains adhered to the substrate. Through microscopic analysis of the contact line, we identify the relationship between the oxide layer’s radius of curvature and the contact angle, explaining why peeling fails at small angles. Based on these findings, we propose a roll-to-roll transfer concept that maintains a large contact angle while applying tension, offering a potential strategy for residue-free patterning of liquid metal structures via transfer. These insights advance the fundamental understanding of GBLM interfacial mechanics and provide a framework for controlling adhesion and detachment, with implications for microfabrication, flexible electronics, and liquid metal processing.

Repeated experiments revealed two key factors governing the peeling behavior. First, a sufficiently low peeling speed is critical. At high speeds, dynamic impacts on the oxide layer can lead to failure, as seen in Fig. 1b and Supplementary Movie 1. This sensitivity to speed may stem from local dynamic effects near the contact line, such as stick-slip oscillations similar to those observed during tape peeling38, which can be triggered by rapid motion. While we note the importance of peel speed, the focus of this paper is on the role of contact angle. When the peeling speed is sufficiently reduced to a quasi-static regime (<100 μm/s, as in the successful case shown in Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1), the outcome is governed by the contact angle (θ) between the oxide layer and the lower substrate. Peeling consistently fails when θ < 40° (Fig. 3a), whereas successful peeling is reliably observed when θ ≥ 40° (Fig. 3b).

Energy analysis of thin-film delamination

The separation of the oxide layer from a substrate can be considered analogous to the peeling of thin solid films adhered to surfaces. Previous studies have shown that peeling behavior depends on peeling angle39,40. To explore the relationship between peeling and the contact angles observed in our experiments, we interpret our findings within the established framework of thin-film peeling mechanics.

To estimate the mechanical energy required to peel the oxide layer from the substrate, we may first note that in systems without polymeric adhesives (i.e. systems without viscoelastic dissipation), the delamination energy G is considered equivalent to the thermodynamic work of adhesion W, which represents the reversible energy needed to break intermolecular interactions per unit area42. However, peeling the oxide layer of EGaIn also involves the local motion of liquid EGaIn, encapsulated by the oxide skin near the contact line, which may introduce additional energy dissipation43. Thus, the delamination energy can be expressed as the sum of the work of adhesion W and the viscous dissipation Ψ∗ arising near the contact line.

The van der Waals interaction is the primary contributor to W44, which can be expressed as W = A/(12πd2), where A is the Hamaker constant for the interacting surfaces (oxide and substrate) and d is the cut-off length representing their effective separation45. Based on Lifshitz theory, we estimate the Hamaker constant for gallium oxide interacting with glass or PMMA to be ~A ~ 10−19 J (Supplementary Note 2). Using a universal cut-off length of d = 0.2 nm45,46, the work of adhesion is calculated as W ≈ 0.1 J/m2. This analysis neglects hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups on the oxide surface and the substrate, which may be present34. Although abundant hydrogen bonding could enhance adhesion, our previous work suggests that van der Waals forces dominate, rendering the contribution of hydrogen bonds negligible32. Therefore, we consider our estimation to provide a reasonable order-of-magnitude approximation for scaling analysis.

We next consider the contribution of viscous dissipation to G. The energy dissipation associated with the delamination of EGaIn per unit area is given by Ψ* ≈ μUl(θ – sinθcosθ)/(θ2 – sin2θ), where μ is the viscosity of EGaIn, U is the EGaIn flow speed, and l is a dimensionless coefficient associated with the length scale43 (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Using μ ~ 10-3 Pa·s and l ~ 1043, we estimate Ψ* ~ 0.01U(θ – sinθcosθ)/(θ2 – sin2θ), indicating that dissipation increases with higher speed and lower contact angle. The upper bound of Ψ* is approximately 10-5 J/m2 for the smallest contact angle of 10° and a maximum peeling speed of U ≈ 0.1 mm/s in our experiments. Since Ψ* is much smaller than W, the effect of viscous dissipation can be considered negligible.

We now assess the work required to delaminate the oxide layer by pulling. According to Eq. (1), peeling occurs when the applied tension equals W. While W remains constant at 0.1 J/m², the required peeling tension decreases as the contact angle decreases, suggesting that peeling should become easier at lower angles. During delamination, the maximum observable angle is the advancing contact angle, reported to be approximately 150° for EGaIn on a glass or PMMA plate32. At this angle, the required peeling tension is given by T ~ W/(1+cosθadv) ≈ 0.7 N/m, where θadv is the advancing contact angle. Since the yield limit of the oxide skin in tension has been measured as TY ≈ 0.73 N/m47, this suggests that the oxide membrane can be delaminated without tearing and that peeling should occur more readily at smaller contact angles. For instance, at a contact angle of 10°, the required peeling tension is reduced to 0.05 N/m.

However, contrary to this energy-based prediction, our experiments revealed that peeling consistently failed at smaller contact angles. This discrepancy with conventional delamination theory suggests that additional factors, beyond energy-based mechanics, govern the peeling process and warrant further investigation.

SEM and AFM analysis of the contact line

We hypothesize that the solid oxide skin undergoes significant deformation during the peeling process at small contact angles, leading to localized stress, particularly near the contact line (Fig. 4). Given the inherent brittleness of gallium oxide on EGaIn20,48, such deformation may induce local mechanical failure at the oxide-air interface, ultimately preventing successful peeling. To investigate this deformation, we examined microscopic regions near the contact line, where the oxide layer experiences the highest curvature, in both successful and failed peeling cases (see white boxes in Fig. 3a, b) using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). Figure 5a presents a magnified view of the contact line in the failed peeling case, revealing a distorted interfacial morphology, including an uneven EGaIn interface and surface irregularities on the substrate, suggesting the oxide has partially collapsed around the circumference of the EGaIn. To further analyze the interfacial shape, we conducted atomic force microscopy (AFM) scans along a path from Points A to B, as indicated by the white dashed arrows in Fig. 5a, b. Figure 5c shows the height profile for the failed peeling case. The profile remained nearly constant until an elevation of approximately 10 nm appeared at x ≈ 25 nm. Given that the oxide skin is approximately 3 nm thick31, this elevated region likely represents a multilayered oxide structure, possibly incorporating some liquid EGaIn (lower right schematic in Fig. 5c). The profile maintained a height of y ≈ 10 nm until x ≈ 100 nm, beyond which the height increased linearly with a slope of approximately 30°, as observed in Fig. 3a.

The interfacial profile for the successful peeling case is shown in Fig. 5d. Unlike the failed case, the elevation began abruptly at x ≈ 100 nm, forming a steep slope of approximately 85°. As x increased, the slope decreased, eventually stabilizing at around 40°, matching the contact angle observed in Fig. 3b. The shaded region along the contact line in Fig. 5b indicates an area where electron beam reflection was obstructed, suggesting a smoothly curved surface49,50. Since AFM cannot precisely measure steep cliffs and the observed slope (~85°) closely matches the sidewall slope of the AFM scanning tip51, we infer that the EGaIn interface has a convex curvature, as illustrated by the black dashed line in Fig. 5d.

Comparison of the interfacial profiles suggests that mechanical failure of the oxide skin, induced by tension along the oxide layer, occurred in the failed peeling case. Since the primary difference between the two cases is the contact angle at which the oxide skin was pulled, this finding raises further questions about the role of contact angle in fracture mechanics and its influence on the microscopic shape of the oxide layer near the contact line.

Force analysis for oxide skin fracture

We analyze the microscopic shape of the oxide skin near the contact line using two-dimensional beam bending theory with a rectangular cross-section. This simplification is justified by our experimental observations. In the peeling tests reported in this study, the curvature in the xz-plane is much smaller than that in the xy-plane (Fig. 2), and the contact line remains nearly straight (Fig. 3). These features support the use of a two-dimensional approximation for the interfacial region where peeling occurs. Based on the interfacial profile observed in successful peeling cases (Fig. 5d), we consider a bent oxide skin in the region near the contact line where 0 ≤ θ ≤ 90°, as depicted in Fig. 6a. The leftmost point is defined as Point O, where the oxide skin is assumed to have the minimum radius of curvature, as inferred from Fig. 5d. The radius of curvature for a two-dimensional solid layer is given by R = EI/M, where E is the Young’s modulus of gallium oxide, I is the second moment of area per unit depth, and M is the bending moment per unit depth. We assume a uniform oxide layer thickness along the interface. This is supported by the known self-limiting growth behavior of gallium oxide, which naturally restricts further thickening beyond a critical value15, making significant variation over nanoscale regions unlikely. As a result, the moment of inertia can reasonably be treated as constant (see Fig. 6), and the bending stiffness EI is assumed to remain uniform along the interface. To formulate the balance of moments around the left end of this section, we consider the forces acting on the oxide skin. At x∞, the oxide skin experiences tension T, but no bending moment, as the curvature is effectively zero. Additionally, the oxide skin is subjected to a pressure difference ΔP from the encapsulated EGaIn liquid. At x, the oxide experiences a local tension T(x, y) and bending moment M(x, y). The liquid pressure is associated with local flow and can be estimated as ΔP ~ μU/(x∞tan2θ) where μ is the viscosity of EGaIn and U is the flow speed (Supplementary Note 4). The tension T is on the order of G/(1+ cosθ), as given by Eq. (1). Therefore, the ratio of the pressure force to the tension can be approximated as ΔP(x∞−x)/T ~ μU(1+cosθ)/(Gtan2θ). The upper bound of this ratio occurs at the smallest contact angle of 10° in our experiments and is on the order of 10-4, indicating that the contribution of EGaIn liquid pressure to the bending moment is negligible. Thus, the balance of moments around the left end of the oxide section simplifies to M(x, y) = −(x∞ − x)Tsinθ + (y∞ − y)Tcosθ.

To assess the maximum allowable bending deformation of the oxide skin, we consider the stress distribution near the contact line, where the outermost convex region experiences the highest tensile stress (Fig. 6a). The maximum stress at Point O is given by σ = Eϵ, where ϵ is the strain at the outermost part of the convex side. In the thin-film bending limit (t ≪ RO), the local strain is approximated as ϵ ~ t/RO, leading to σ ~ Et/RO52. When σ approaches the yield stress σY, the oxide layer is expected to fracture. we estimate RY ≈ 60 nm using E ≈ 4 GPa17 and t ≈ 3 nm31. This implies that the oxide layer can sustain a curved shape only if RO exceeds 60 nm; below this threshold, bending-induced stress leads to fracture near Point O (Fig. 6a).

This prediction aligns with our experimental observations. When RO fell below the threshold, smoothly curved profiles were no longer observed; instead, wedge-like shapes formed in the oxide skin (Fig. 7a, b). In Fig. 7c, the black dashed line represents the theoretical threshold from Eq. (4), and data points below this line lie along the x-axis, as their radii of curvature effectively vanished due to fracture.

These findings elucidate the role of contact angle in the peeling behavior of EGaIn. To overcome adhesion forces, tension must be applied to the oxide skin near the contact line, inducing local bending deformation. As θ decreases, the bending deformation increases. When this deformation exceeds the fracture threshold, the oxide layer breaks near the contact line. Once fractured, the oxide skin loses its smoothly curved morphology, resulting in an irregular interfacial shape (Fig. 5a). At such small contact angles, the oxide is known to collapse on itself31,33,34, forming the folded region observed in Fig. 5a and illustrated in Fig. 7c. This collapsed region appears to adhere strongly to the substrate, so that continued pulling of the oxide induces localized yielding in the oxide skin (Fig. 3a). Consequently, the applied tension is no longer sufficient to sustain delamination, preventing successful peeling.

Transfer printing of EGaIn line arrays

Our understanding of oxide layer peeling mechanics has enabled the development of a transfer printing technique for EGaIn line arrays. While transfer printing is widely used for pattern replication on flexible substrates, achieving complete delamination without damage remains a challenge, particularly for liquid metal structures.

To address this, we introduce a roll-to-roll transfer method that ensures complete and residue-free printing by preventing oxide layer fracture via a sufficiently large contact angle throughout the process. Figure 8a illustrates the system, which employs a receiver film coated with an adhesive, wound onto three rotating rollers. This motion applies tension along the oxide layer while maintaining a large contact angle on the donor substrate. A sufficiently high contact angle facilitates clean detachment, enabling continuous and uniform transfer of the line arrays onto the receiver film.

Figure 8c demonstrates the process. The left column presents real images from an x-direction view, while the right column provides corresponding side-view illustrations. The EGaIn lines are prepared by injecting liquid metal into a sacrificial mold. The peeling process begins when the edge of the EGaIn line arrays on the donor substrate (glass) is anchored to the receiver film (polyimide) (Fig. 8c(i)). As the rolling motion progresses, the arrays detach from the donor and adhere to the receiver (Fig. 8c(ii)). Throughout the process, the contact angle remains large, ensuring that the oxide skin retains its smooth, curved shape (inset in Fig. 8b). After complete transfer, no residual EGaIn remains on the donor (Fig. 8c(iii) and Supplementary Movie 6).

Discussion

This study presents a detailed analysis of the peeling mechanics of the oxide layer surrounding EGaIn, highlighting the critical role of the contact angle between the oxide and the substrate. Our experiments, combined with microscopic observations near the contact line, reveal that successful peeling occurs when the oxide layer maintains a smooth, curved shape at large contact angles, whereas wedge-shaped profiles at small contact angles lead to peeling failure. These findings suggest that severe deformation at small angles induces mechanical fracture, indicating that maintaining a large contact angle is crucial for successful peeling.

However, increasing the contact angle alone does not ensure successful delamination. As shown in Fig. 1b and Supplementary Movie 1, high-speed pulling of the oxide-coated metal fails to achieve clean peeling. Dynamic effects disrupt control over the contact angle, making it difficult to sustain a sufficiently large value throughout the process. These transient reductions increase the risk of oxide fracture and ultimately prevent successful peeling. Additionally, dynamic motion may introduce impulsive loads along the oxide skin, raising the likelihood that tensile stress exceeds the yield limit and causes mechanical failure. Minimizing such dynamic influences is therefore essential. Further investigation is needed to quantify their microscale effects and to identify the velocity range within which peeling remains reliable.

Methods

Separation of an EGaIn droplet adhered to a solid plate

The experimental setup is schematically shown in Fig. 1a. Before testing, a slide glass substrate (Microscope slides, Marienfeld) was rinsed with 99.9% ethyl alcohol to remove dust and then dried. An EGaIn droplet was slowly dispensed onto the glass plate using a syringe to ensure strong adhesion. To remove the droplet, a metal rod (2.5 mm or 1 mm in diameter) with double-sided adhesive tape (Scotch, 3 M) applied to its tip was brought into contact with the upper surface of the droplet. Once the upper region was firmly anchored to the tape, the rod was raised quasi-statically using a motorized stage, applying tension to the oxide layer. The stage velocity was set to 0.3 cm/s for the test in Figs. 1b and 3 μm/s for Fig. 1c, d. The entire process was recorded using a digital single-lens reflex camera (EOS-70D, Canon).

Peel test for an EGaIn film

1) Preparation of the upper plate. The upper plate, composed of three different materials, is schematically shown in the inset of Fig. 2a. A slide glass substrate (Microscope slides, Marienfeld) was entirely covered with 20,000-grit lapping film (Norton), and a portion of the sandpaper was overlaid with double-sided adhesive tape (Scotch, 3 M). 2) Preparation of the EGaIn film. EGaIn was injected into a gap between two parallel plates at a controlled rate of <0.1 μl/min using a syringe pump (Fusion 200X, Chemyx). 3) Peeling test observation. The entire peeling process was recorded using a high-speed camera (Fastcam SA3, Photron) equipped with a long-distance microscope lens (12× Zoom, Navitar). The oxide skin was illuminated by an LED light source (Cyclops I-120 W, KOMI) to enhance contrast during imaging. All images were analyzed using Photron Fastcam Viewer software (Photron, Ver. 3).

Visualization of the microscopic contact line

1) Sample preparation. The preparation process for SEM and AFM imaging is schematically shown in Fig. 2b. After partial peeling of the oxide layer (corresponding to stage (vi) in Fig. 2b), the position of the upper plate was fixed. The entire setup, including the upper and lower plates and the EGaIn film, was carefully placed in the vacuum chamber of the SEM and on the platform of the AFM for imaging. 2) SEM imaging of the contact line. Microscopic observations were conducted along the contact line using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-7100F, JEOL) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. 3) Interfacial profile measurement by AFM. The surface profile was measured using an atomic force microscope (AFM, NX-10, Park Systems) with a scanning tip (PPP-NCHR, Park Systems), which has a tip radius of approximately 10 nm. The length of scanned path was 1 μm with a resolution of 512 pixels, and the scan rate was 0.05–0.1 Hz. 4) Data analysis. All data were processed using XEI software (Park Systems, Ver. 1.8.2), MATLAB (MathWorks, Ver. 2025b), and ImageJ (Ver. 1.47).

Transfer printing of EGaIn line arrays

1) Preparation of the donor substrate. A glass slide (Microscope slides, Marienfeld) was used as the donor substrate. Before use, it was rinsed with 99.9% ethyl alcohol to remove dust and dried thoroughly. 2) Preparation of EGaIn line arrays. EGaIn line arrays were prepared by injecting EGaIn into a 3D-printed sacrificial mold (Form 3, Formlabs) placed on a donor substrate. The mold contained grooves with a square cross-section of 0.7 × 0.7 cm². To ensure strong adhesion to the donor substrate, EGaIn was injected at a controlled rate of <0.1 μl/min using a syringe pump (Fusion 200X, Chemyx). To facilitate separation from the mold, the groove walls were coated with Teflon solution (601S2, DuPont) and Fluorinert Electronic Liquid (FC40, 3 M), allowing the EGaIn arrays to adhere preferentially to the donor substrate while detaching from the mold. 3) Preparation of the receiver film. A polyimide (PI) tape (Alphaflon) was used as the receiver film. 4) Preparation of the roller apparatus. Three acrylic cylinders with a diameter of 5 mm and a length of 7 cm were used. A rubber ring with a thickness of 1 mm was used to provide friction and prevent slipping on the donor substrate. 5) Transfer printing condition. The detachment of EGaIn line arrays was performed by rotating the rollers at a speed of approximately 0.1 mm/s.
