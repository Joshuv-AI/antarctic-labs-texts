A Systematic Review of Psychosocial Interventions for Enhancing Resilience in Medical Sciences Students
Authors: Sahar Ghanbari, Sara Hosseini, Sara Zamiran, Somaye Kavousipor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsUndergraduate medical sciences students experience substantial psychological distress due to demanding academic workloads and early clinical exposure, increasing their vulnerability to anxiety, depression, and burnout. This systematic review evaluates randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining psychosocial interventions—including mindfulness-based programs, cognitive-behavioral strategies, acceptance-based therapies, and structured stress-management training—designed to enhance resilience among undergraduate medical sciences students.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, ISI Web of Science, ProQuest, Google Scholar, and Semantic Scholar identified studies published between January 2000 and April 2025.Results11 RCTs met inclusion criteria. Data extraction included participant characteristics, intervention type and duration, delivery format, and outcome measures. Methodological quality was assessed using the PEDro scale, and risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool.ConclusionAcross the included trials, psychosocial interventions demonstrated consistent positive effects on resilience, perceived stress, coping flexibility, and psychological well-being. Mindfulness-based programs—ranging from brief sessions to full 8-week MBSR courses—generally reduced distress, though shorter or peer-led formats produced mixed results. Cognitive-behavioral and stress-management interventions improved coping skills, emotional regulation, and self-efficacy. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy enhanced psychological flexibility, and structured resilience curricula such as the Penn Resilience Program yielded significant gains in resilience scores. Variability in intervention design, duration, and measurement tools limited comparability across studies. Overall, psychosocial interventions appear effective in enhancing resilience among undergraduate medical sciences students. Integrating structured, theory-based approaches into medical education may strengthen students’ psychological health and academic functioning. Introduction

Undergraduate medical sciences students are exposed to substantial psychological stress due to demanding academic workloads, high performance expectations, and early involvement in emotionally challenging clinical environments. Evidence consistently shows elevated rates of anxiety, depression, and burnout among healthcare students.1–4 These pressures intensify during the transition from classroom learning to clinical practice, where students must simultaneously acquire foundational scientific knowledge and develop emerging professional competencies.5 This combination of academic rigor and early patient-care responsibilities creates a unique vulnerability compared with postgraduate trainees.

Resilience—commonly defined as the capacity to adapt positively to adversity and recover from stress—has been identified as a key protective factor for maintaining mental health and academic functioning in student populations. Contemporary perspectives emphasize that resilience is not a fixed trait but a dynamic process that can be strengthened through targeted training.6,7 Psychosocial interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT),8 mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR),9 and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT),10 which have shown promise in promoting psychological well-being in student populations.11,12 These approaches strengthen students’ capacity to manage stress and support both mental health and occupational performance.13–16

Despite growing interest, resilience-building programs for healthcare students remain inconsistent in design, theoretical grounding, and implementation. Many interventions lack standardization, vary widely in duration and content, and are not tailored to the specific stressors faced by undergraduate medical sciences students.17,18 Cultural and contextual factors also influence intervention effectiveness,19–23 yet these elements are often insufficiently addressed. A Cochrane review further highlights that resilience training is frequently conflated with other psychosocial approaches, and its effectiveness depends heavily on the targeted outcomes and training environment.24

Given these gaps, a systematic synthesis of randomized controlled trials is needed to clarify which psychosocial strategies effectively enhance resilience in undergraduate medical sciences students. This review evaluates the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions—including mindfulness-based programs, cognitive-behavioral approaches, acceptance-based therapies, and structured stress-management training—and identifies key components associated with improved resilience and psychological well-being in this at-risk population.

Methods

Study Design and Protocol

This systematic review was conducted by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and transparency. The review aimed to identify and evaluate interventions designed to enhance psychological resilience among medical science students. The protocol for this review was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO).

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, ISI Web of Science, ProQuest, Google Scholar, and Semantic Scholar for studies published from January 2000 to April 2025. This timeframe was selected to capture contemporary psychosocial and resilience-based interventions. Search terms combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords related to resilience, psychosocial interventions, and medical sciences students. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the search, and filters were applied to restrict results to peer-reviewed English-language articles.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The review included randomized controlled trials involving undergraduate medical sciences students. Studies were required to evaluate psychosocial interventions aimed at enhancing resilience, such as mindfulness-based programs, cognitive-behavioral approaches, acceptance-based therapies, or structured stress-management training. Eligible studies compared these interventions with control conditions such as no intervention, standard curriculum, or alternative non-psychosocial activities. Outcomes needed to include resilience, stress, coping, psychological well-being, or related mental-health indicators.

Studies were excluded if they involved postgraduate trainees, non-medical student populations, non-psychosocial interventions, qualitative designs, non-RCT methodologies, or insufficient outcome reporting.

Study Selection Process

Following the removal of duplicates using reference management software (EndNote X9), two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently evaluated by the same reviewers. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when consensus could not be reached. Data Synthesis

Due to anticipated heterogeneity in intervention types and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis was planned. Where appropriate, random-effects meta-analyses will be conducted to pool effect sizes for resilience outcomes. Heterogeneity will be assessed using the I2 statistic, and subgroup analyses will explore moderators such as intervention type and duration.

Results

Study Characteristics and Quality Assessment

The final review included 11 randomized controlled trials evaluating psychosocial interventions aimed at enhancing resilience among undergraduate medical sciences students (Table 1). These studies spanned diverse health-related disciplines, including medicine, nursing, physical therapy, and allied health fields. Sample sizes ranged from small cohorts of 21–60 participants to large university-wide trials, which enrolled more than 600 students.28,30

Methodological quality, assessed using the PEDro scale, demonstrated overall moderate to high rigor. Nine studies were rated moderate to high quality, and two were moderate. Risk-of-bias evaluation using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool indicated common challenges typical of psychosocial trials, including limited blinding and reliance on self-report measures.

Intervention Characteristics

Interventions varied substantially in duration, content, and delivery format. Programs were delivered individually or in groups and commonly incorporated psychoeducation, role-play, guided discussions, homework, and practical exercises.

Mindfulness-Based Interventions

Mindfulness-based approaches were the most frequently implemented, appearing in most of the studies.9,26,28–30,33 These ranged from brief two-session formats to full eight-week MBSR courses with weekly 75–120-minute sessions and home practice. Most studies reported reductions in perceived stress and improvements in well-being,26,30,33 although shorter or peer-led formats produced mixed or nonsignificant results.28,29

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

One study implemented an eight-session ACT protocol and reported significant improvements in psychological flexibility, supporting ACT’s relevance for adaptive coping in student populations.31

Cognitive-Behavioral and Stress-Management Approaches

CBT-based and stress-management interventions were evaluated in 3 studies.25,27,34 These programs included multicomponent curricula addressing communication skills, crisis management, anger regulation, cognitive reframing, and coping strategies delivered over 7–10 weeks. Findings consistently demonstrated improvements in coping skills, emotional regulation, and self-efficacy.

Structured Resilience-Training Programs

Three studies implemented structured resilience curricula: Penn Resilience Program,27 Stop Running on Empty! (26 sessions)32 and Transforming Lives Through Resilience Education.25 All three reported significant increases in resilience scores, suggesting that multisession, theory-driven programs may yield more robust outcomes than brief workshops.

Outcome Measures

Resilience and stress outcomes were assessed using validated instruments. The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) demonstrated significant reductions in stress in several studies.26,30 The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) was frequently used to evaluate resilience.9,33 Additional measures included the Resilience Scale (RS), Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), Dispositional Resilience Scale (DRS), Brief COPE, Cognitive Flexibility Inventory, Coping Flexibility Scale, DASS-21, GAD-7, TICS, SSCS, BSI, CORE-OM, and PTSD Checklist.

Across the 11 included RCTs, psychosocial interventions consistently demonstrated positive effects on resilience, perceived stress, coping flexibility, and psychological well-being.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes evidence from eleven randomized controlled trials evaluating psychosocial interventions designed to enhance resilience among undergraduate medical sciences students. Across these studies, interventions such as mindfulness-based programs, cognitive-behavioral approaches, acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), and structured resilience-training curricula demonstrated varying degrees of effectiveness in improving stress, coping, psychological flexibility, and resilience. However, interpretation of these findings requires caution due to substantial heterogeneity in intervention design, delivery, and measurement approaches.

Mindfulness-based interventions were the most frequently implemented.25,26,28–30,33 These programs ranged from brief peer-led sessions to full eight-week MBSR courses. Several studies reported reductions in perceived stress and improvements in well-being,9,26 while others—particularly shorter or peer-led formats—showed no significant changes.28,29 These mixed results suggest that mindfulness effectiveness may depend on program fidelity, duration, participant engagement, and contextual factors such as academic pressure and cultural expectations.

Beyond mindfulness, ACT demonstrated positive outcomes in improving psychological flexibility,31 highlighting the value of acceptance-based approaches for enhancing adaptive functioning. Cognitive-behavioral and stress-management interventions also contributed to resilience enhancement. Mansuroğlu (2025) reported improvements in coping and emotional regulation following a multi-component CBT-based program,34 while Peng (2014) and Mejia-Downs (2020) demonstrated consistent gains in resilience through structured resilience-training curricula such as the Penn Resilience Program and Stop Running on Empty!25,27,32 Yan (2024) further supported the effectiveness of structured resilience education in improving students’ adaptive capacities.9

Despite these encouraging findings, methodological limitations across studies constrain the strength of conclusions. Many trials employed small sample sizes, reducing statistical power and increasing the likelihood of Type II error. Most relied heavily on self-reported outcomes—including the PSS, CD-RISC, DASS-21, and coping inventories—which are subject to response bias and may not fully capture objective changes in resilience or stress. Blinding was not feasible in psychosocial interventions, increasing the risk of performance and detection bias. Additionally, follow-up periods were generally short or absent, limiting understanding of long-term intervention effects.

Generalizability of the findings is also limited. The included studies were conducted across diverse healthcare disciplines (medicine, nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy) and cultural contexts (eg, Iran, Turkey, Germany, the United States). Cultural norms, academic pressures, and institutional resources may influence both stress experiences and responsiveness to psychosocial interventions.19–23 A Cochrane review further noted that resilience training is often not clearly distinguished from other psychosocial approaches and that effectiveness depends heavily on specific outcomes and training environments.24 These contextual differences underscore the need for culturally sensitive, discipline-specific adaptation of resilience-building programs.

Occupational Therapy Perspective on Resilience

Occupational therapy concepts provide a complementary lens for understanding resilience in healthcare students. Within occupational therapy, resilience is closely tied to flexible engagement in daily occupations—such as self-care, education, rest, social participation, and leisure—which collectively support health and well-being.35,36 Occupational resilience refers to an individual’s ability to navigate stressors by modifying patterns of occupational participation and adapting daily routines.37 These principles help explain why activity-based strategies—such as mindfulness practice, goal-setting, and problem-solving—may enhance resilience. They also suggest potential avenues for future intervention development, particularly in designing programs that integrate meaningful occupations and activity-oriented coping strategies into medical curricula.

Overall Interpretation

Taken together, the evidence suggests that psychosocial interventions can enhance resilience and mental well-being among undergraduate medical sciences students. However, the considerable heterogeneity across studies, methodological limitations, and contextual variability indicate that no single approach can be universally recommended. Future research should prioritize multisite randomized trials, standardized outcome measures, culturally sensitive program design, and long-term follow-up to clarify which strategies most effectively strengthen resilience in this at-risk population.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Restricted access to the full text of some relevant studies limited the depth of analysis. Many resilience interventions also focus on generally healthy student populations rather than individuals experiencing adversity, which is central to resilience theory. Additionally, most included trials relied heavily on self-reported outcomes and often lacked adequate baseline mental-health data, reducing the precision and interpretability of findings.

Future Directions for Occupational Therapy

Future work should prioritize occupational therapy–led, student-focused research to develop standardized resilience interventions grounded in OT principles. Technology-based supports, such as mental-health applications, may enhance accessibility and engagement. Occupational therapists can play a key role in integrating early resilience training into healthcare curricula through workshops and interdisciplinary collaborations aimed at reducing burnout. By incorporating CBT, MBSR, and ACT within occupation-centered frameworks, OT practitioners can help students manage academic and professional challenges. Future initiatives should emphasize objective outcome measures, diverse cultural contexts, and tailored intervention designs.

Stress Management Theory underscores the importance of identifying stressors, applying strategies such as relaxation, exercise, and time management, and adjusting these techniques over time with support from strong social networks.38 These principles can guide educational workshops for occupational therapists and inform evaluations of intervention effectiveness among students and healthcare professionals across different cultural settings. Embedding resilience training and wellness programs within medical curricula may strengthen coping skills and support overall student well-being.

Conclusion

Across the eleven included trials, psychosocial interventions—mindfulness-based programs, cognitive-behavioral and stress-management approaches, acceptance and commitment therapy, and structured resilience-training curricula—show potential to improve resilience and psychological well-being in undergraduate medical sciences students. However, substantial heterogeneity in intervention design, populations, and outcome measures requires cautious interpretation. Small samples, reliance on self-report, limited blinding, and short follow-up periods further constrain the strength of the evidence. Occupational therapy concepts offer a complementary theoretical perspective, though direct empirical support within the included studies is limited. Overall, psychosocial interventions appear promising, but more rigorous, standardized, and context-sensitive research is needed to determine which approaches most effectively enhance resilience in this student population.
