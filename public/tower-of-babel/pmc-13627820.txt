Classic Ataxia–Telangiectasia associated with a homozygous missense ATM variant: A case report
Authors: Zyad Al-Frejat, Leen Azzam, Grace Hanna, Doaa Abo Hamza, Ghuroub Al Khayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ataxia–telangiectasia (A-T) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the ATM gene, characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, telangiectasias, immunodeficiency, and increased cancer risk Reporting the phenotypic spectrum of ATM variants is essential to refine genotype–phenotype correlations.We report a 6-year-old boy presenting with progressive gait ataxia, recurrent respiratory infections, chronic otorrhea, and conjunctival telangiectasias. Laboratory studies revealed immunodeficiency with reduced IgG and IgA, and elevated IgM. Brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy, while chest X-ray revealed bilateral interstitial infiltrates. Whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous missense variant in ATM (c.4940 T > G; p.Leu1647Arg), currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS); this represents only the second reported family with this variant.The patient improved with intravenous antibiotics and IVIG. Family genetic testing was recommended given parental consanguinity and an affected sibling.This case illustrates the classical phenotype of A-T with immunodeficiency and neurological dysfunction. Such cases reinforce genotype–phenotype correlations and may guide diagnosis and counseling. Although the variant's pathogenicity remains to be functionally validated, its clinical correlation supports disease relevance.Careful clinical and familial correlation of ATM variants is crucial for advancing diagnostic precision, genetic counseling, and future therapeutic strategies in A-T.

Introduction

Ataxia-telangiectasia (A-T) is a rare, autosomal recessive multisystem disorder characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, oculocutaneous telangiectasias, immunodeficiency, radiosensitivity, and increased cancer predisposition [1]. The disease is caused by pathogenic variants in the ATM gene, which encodes a serine/threonine kinase central to the DNA damage response and cell-cycle control [2]. Loss of ATM function leads to genomic instability and contributes to the wide spectrum of clinical manifestations observed in affected individuals [3].

More than 1000 variants in the ATM gene have been reported to date, including nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, and missense changes, with considerable heterogeneity in both genotype and phenotype [4]. While truncating variants typically result in the classic early-onset form of A-T, missense and leaky splice variants may be associated with atypical or milder presentations [5]. Continuous identification of novel ATM variants is therefore essential to improve understanding of the mutational spectrum, refine genotype–phenotype correlations, and support accurate genetic counseling.

we describe a child with a classic A-T phenotype associated with a homozygous missense ATM variant (c.4940 T > G; p.Leu1647Arg), emphasizing the clinical relevance of such exceptions and contributing to the understanding of genotype–phenotype variability in A-T.

Case presentation

A 6-year-old male was admitted to the hospital with a primary complaint of gait ataxia, accompanied by fever, non-productive cough, and purulent discharge from the right ear. Neurological symptoms had progressively worsened over recent weeks.

The patient's past medical history was notable for recurrent lower respiratory tract infections, intermittent abdominal pain, and chronic right sided otorrhea and ataxia noted approximately three years ago.

Vital signs on admission

Temperature: 38 °C.

Respiratory rate: 35 breaths/min.

Heart rate: 110 beats/min.

Blood pressure: 95/60 mmHg.

On physical examination, the child was conscious and responsive, with evidence of failure to thrive, pallor, and generalized weakness. A productive cough was noted, along with bilateral conjunctival telangiectasias. Purulent discharge was observed from the right ear. Chest auscultation revealed coarse crackles over both lung fields. Abdominal examination was unremarkable, with no tenderness or palpable organomegaly; a cutaneous nevus was noted on the abdominal wall.

Neurological examination demonstrated cerebellar signs, including gait ataxia and impaired coordination on finger–nose testing, while the Romberg test was negative. Deep tendon reflexes were preserved and symmetric, and no pyramidal signs were detected. The patient exhibited dysarthria and difficulty with visual pursuit and fixation. Involuntary movements were observed, consisting of intermittent choreiform movements involving the lower lip and, occasionally, the limbs. No dystonia or oculomotor apraxia was identified.

Laboratory investigations revealed

White blood cell count: 13,200/μL (60% neutrophils).

Hemoglobin: 10.3 g/dL.

Platelet count: 319,000/μL.

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR): 28 mm/h.

C-reactive protein (CRP): elevated (10× upper limit of normal).

Renal function: within normal limits.

Immunoglobulin panel:

IgG: 200 mg/dL (decreased).

IgA: 50 mg/dL (decreased).

IgM: 452 mg/dL (elevated).

IgE: 20 IU/mL.

alpha-fetoprotein: 47.7 ng/mL (elevated; reference <8 ng/mL)[.

Radiological imaging showed:

Chest X-ray: bilateral interstitial infiltrates.

Abdominal ultrasound: absence of the left kidney (suggestive of renal agenesis).

Whole-exome sequencing (CentoXome®, Centogene GmbH, Germany) identified a homozygous missense variant in ATM (NM_000051.4:c.4940 T > G; p.Leu1647Arg).

This variant was first reported by Kılıç et al. [7] in three affected members of a single consanguineous Turkish family with ataxia–telangiectasia; the present case represents the second reported family. It is absent from population databases (gnomAD, ESP, 1000 Genomes) and is currently classified in ClinVar as a variant of uncertain significance (ACMG/AMP criteria PM2, PP3, PP4); no functional studies are available. Given parental consanguinity, both parents are presumed heterozygous carriers.

The patient was treated with intravenous antibiotics targeting pneumonia and otitis media, along with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), which led to significant improvement in infectious symptoms. Partial neurological improvement was observed, with improvement in gait ataxia while limb ataxia persisted. He was discharged on oral antibiotic with close clinical follow-up planned.

Genetic testing of family members was strongly recommended, given the positive history of cerebellar ataxia in a 4-year-old sibling and the presence of parental consanguinity.

Discussion

Ataxia–telangiectasia (A-T) is a rare autosomal recessive multisystem disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the ATM gene, leading to defective DNA damage response and genomic instability. Clinically, A-T is characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, oculocutaneous telangiectasias, immunodeficiency with recurrent infections, and increased cancer susceptibility [6].

In this patient, whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous ATM missense variant, c.4940 T > G (p.Leu1647Arg), located in exon 33.This variant was first described by Kılıç et al. [7] in a consanguineous Turkish family; our patient represents the second reported family and, to our knowledge, provides the first neuroimaging and detailed neurological characterization associated with this genotype. The variant is absent from population databases and meets ACMG/AMP criteria PM2 (absence from population databases), PP3 (concordant deleterious in-silico predictions), and PP4 (a highly specific A-T phenotype); it is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance. Its recurrence in two unrelated families with classic A-T (criterion PS4) provides additional evidence supporting reclassification toward likely pathogenic, although functional confirmation of impaired ATM kinase activity would be required for a definitive classification. Confirmatory genotyping of the clinically affected 4-year-old sibling would add segregation evidence (PP1) supporting this interpretation.

Neurologically, classic A-T is defined by early-onset progressive cerebellar dysfunction, with truncal and gait ataxia typically appearing in early childhood and worsening over time. Brain MRI commonly demonstrates cerebellar atrophy involving the vermis and hemispheres with relative preservation of supratentorial structures. The neurological presentation of our patient—including progressive gait ataxia, cerebellar signs, dysarthria, oculomotor abnormalities, and choreiform movements—together with MRI evidence of cerebellar atrophy, is consistent with the classic A-T phenotype and reflects widespread cerebellar and extrapyramidal involvement described in advanced disease [8].

Immunodeficiency is present in approximately two-thirds of individuals with classic A-T and represents a major contributor to morbidity. Our patient exhibited recurrent otorrhea and lower respiratory tract infections, supported by radiological evidence of pulmonary involvement and laboratory findings of hypogammaglobulinemia with elevated IgM, a pattern frequently reported in A-T. These immunological abnormalities likely contributed to the patient's recurrent infections and clinical deterioration [9].

Ataxia–telangiectasia is classically associated with biallelic truncating ATM variants, whereas missense variants are more often linked to variant or attenuated phenotypes. Notably, in this case, a homozygous missense variant was associated with a clinical picture indistinguishable from classic A-T. This observation adds to growing evidence that certain missense variants can result in severe functional impairment of ATM kinase activity, leading to classic disease manifestations.The independent occurrence of this same missense variant with a classic phenotype in two unrelated families reinforces this conclusion. Such cases underscore that genotype–phenotype correlations in A-T are not absolute and that missense variants should not be assumed to confer milder disease [10], [11], [12].

Although no malignancy was detected at the time of evaluation, individuals with biallelic pathogenic ATM variants carry a well-established increased risk of malignancy. In addition, heterozygous carriers are known to have an elevated cancer risk, particularly for breast cancer [13]. Accordingly, genetic counseling was recommended for the family, along with long-term oncological surveillance for the patient and appropriate cancer risk counseling for heterozygous relatives.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights a homozygous missense ATM variant associated with classic A-T, reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation of ATM missense variants and comprehensive clinical correlation. As the second reported family with this variant, this report also contributes evidence toward its reclassification. Recognition of such presentations has important implications for diagnosis, prognosis, genetic counseling, and long-term management.
