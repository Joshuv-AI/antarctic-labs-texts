Extending Memorization Dynamics in Pythia Models from Instance-Level Insights Journal: Nuclear Physics B
Jie Zhang, Qinghua Zhao, Lei Li, Chi-ho Lin

Abstract
Large language models have demonstrated a remarkable ability for verbatim memorization. While numerous works have explored factors influencing model memorization, the dynamic evolution memorization patterns remains underexplored. This paper presents a detailed analysis of memorization in the Pythia model family across varying scales and training steps under prefix perturbations. Using granular metrics, we examine how model architecture, data characteristics, and perturbations influence these patterns. Our findings reveal that: (1) as model scale increases, memorization expands incrementally while efficiency decreases rapidly; (2) as model scale increases, the rate of new memorization acquisition decreases while old memorization forgetting increases; (3) data characteristics (token frequency, repetition count, and uncertainty) differentially affect memorized versus non-memorized samples; and (4) prefix perturbations reduce memorization and increase generation uncertainty proportionally to perturbation strength, with low-redundancy samples showing higher vulnerability and larger models offering no additional robustness. These findings advance our understanding of memorization mechanisms, with direct implications for training optimization, privacy safeguards, and architectural improvements.

The verbatim memorization of large language models (LLMs) refers to the ability of LLMs to reproduce entire sequences from their training set

(

)
, this phenomenon has since been documented in models like LLaMA and GPT-3.5
(

)
, raising concerns about copyright, data privacy
(

;
Wu et al. 2023
)
, and influencing factual knowledge
(

)
, reasoning
(
Prashanth et al. 2025
)
, and task performance

Prior works found that memorization increases with model scale, context length, and data frequency
(
Carlini et al. 2023
;
Tirumala et al. 2022
)
, with preferential memorization of nouns, numbers, and unique samples. Lower temperature settings enhance memorization while learning rates have minimal impact
(

)
.
Studies have also shown that memorization scaling scales linearly with model size and exponentially with training epochs
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
. While its relationship with reasoning abilities is non-linear
(

)
, it improves performance on tasks such as reading comprehension
(

)
.

Recent interpretability studies have located memorization mechanisms within model architectures, identifying specific neurons in MLP layers responsible for storing factual knowledge

;
Bayazit et al. 2024
)
and revealing layer-specific functions in the memorization process
(
Haviv et al. 2023
)
.
Despite these advances, several critical aspects of memorization remain insufficiently explored.

This paper answers four specific questions. RQ1: How does memorization evolve with model scale? RQ2: How do models balance new memorization acquisition and forgetting across scales? RQ3: How do data properties influence memorization? RQ4: How robust is memorization against perturbations?

Specifically, consistent with prior studies, we conduct a systematic investigation using
Pythia
family models. First, we develop finer-grained
n
-gram-based memorization metrics to analyze the relationship of memorization score and memorization efficiency with model scale and training steps. Second, we examine memorization evolution by quantifying
Both Memorized, Both Unmemorized, Small-Only Memorized, Large-Only Memorized, Newly Forgotten, Newly Memorized
samples. Third, we investigate the impacts of average token frequency, the repetition count, prompt perplexity, compressibility degree, and sequence uncertainty on memorization. Finally, we evaluate memorization robustness through controlled token shuffling and editing perturbations.

Our work yields several significant findings and we provide a comprehensive, deep analysis of each phenomenon, for example:

1.

Larger models memorize more training data but with consistently decreasing memorization efficiency as parameters increase. This phenomenon may be attributed to the observation that as model scale increases, more allocation is directed to other capabilities rather than solely proportionally increasing memorization efficiency.

2.

Models memorize 1-gram samples at smaller scales but 32-gram samples only at larger scales (
\geq
1B), suggesting prioritization of lexical content over sequential ordering.

3.

Memorization overlap decreases between increasingly dissimilar models (e.g., at different scales or training stages) due to continuous new memorization acquisition and old memorization forgetting, reducing shared sequences across these model variations.

4.

Average token frequency has a mild impact on memorized samples, but a strong positive impact on non-memorized samples. This is likely because token frequency functions as a secondary memorization factor.

5.

Higher prompt perplexity significantly decreases memorization score. This may be due to greater complexity and unpredictability making memorization more difficult.

6.

Higher compressibility significantly reduces memorization score. This is likely because low redundancy and high information density provide fewer contextual cues, making data harder to memorize.

These findings provide new insights into memorization in LLMs, explaining some of the existing findings and offering a more detailed understanding of memorization evolution and response to external perturbations.

Related Work

Extensive works on memorization in LLMs have been conducted, and can be grouped into four categories: data properties, model configurations, relationships with other abilities, and interpretability.

Data Properties

Existing works established that memorization varies with data properties.
Tirumala et al. 2022
found that models preferentially memorized nouns and numbers, with unique samples exhibiting higher memorization probability. Similarly,
Chen et al. 2022
observed elevated memorization for atypical samples.
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Carlini et al. 2021
;
Carlini et al. 2023
demonstrated that repetition, longer prefixes, longer prompts, and sufficient pretraining enhanced memorization.
Wang et al. 2024
identified the influence of dynamic soft prompting, while
Prashanth et al. 2025
correlated memorization with text repetition, semantic similarity, and perplexity.

Model Configurations

Model architecture and training dynamics also influenced memorization.
Tirumala et al. 2022
;
Kiyomaru et al. 2024
reported that larger models memorized more before overfitting, retained more during training, and memorized faster, with learning rate having limited impact on these processes.
Chang et al. 2024
demonstrated that memorization occurred as a distributed process across model layers.
(
Li et al. 2024
)
found that aligned models predicted memorized samples with greater confidence, while
(
Menta et al. 2025
)
observed that early layers amplified token predictions and upper layers enhanced prediction confidence.

Relationships with Other Abilities

Zhu et al. 2024
asserted that memorization underpinned other abilities in LLMs.
Zhang et al. 2024
suggested that graph reasoning relied heavily on memorized patterns rather than genuine reasoning abilities.
Prabhakar et al. 2024
indicated that complex reasoning benefited from memorization, while
Huang et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
argued that verbatim memorization was inherent to language modeling and fundamentally intertwined with reasoning processes.

Interpretability

From an interpretability perspective, studies have investigated the internal mechanisms of memorization in LLMs, revealing the activation of specialized modules
(
Pawlicki et al. 2024
;
Zarei and Komeili 2025
)
.
Geva et al. 2021
;
Geva et al. 2023
;
Stoehr et al. 2024
identified feed-forward layers as key-value memory stores, with complexity increasing across depths.
Dai et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2023
;
Bayazit et al. 2024
located subnetworks critical for factual knowledge retrieval.
Gurnee et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
;
Hintersdorf et al. 2024
isolated specific neurons driving memorization in
Pythia
models, distinguishing between unary neurons (feature-specific) and multary neurons (context-active).
Lu et al. 2024
demonstrated that fact memorization scaled linearly with model size and exponentially with training epochs.

Despite these insights, fundamental questions remain at the instance level regarding both memorization evolution with model scale and its robustness to perturbations. This work addresses these gaps by investigating: (1) the relationship between model scale, training steps, and memorization efficiency; (2) the dynamics of newly memorized and forgotten samples across model sizes; (3) memorization patterns across diverse data characteristics; (4) sensitivity to prefix perturbations (shuffling, insertion, deletion, replacement) on tokens of varying frequency; and (5) a novel multi-granularity memorization scoring method based on exact
n
-gram matching.

(1)

where

represents the set of all
n
-grams in sequence
S
, and
|\{N_{n}(\text{G})\cap\mathcal{N}_{n}(\text{T})\}|
denotes the number of
n
-grams in the generated sequence that exactly match
n
-grams in the ground truth sequence. When

(
Biderman et al. 2023
;
Prashanth et al. 2025
)

RQ1: How does Memorization Evolve with Model Scale?

Numerous studies have investigated the correlations between model scale and memorization.
Carlini et al. 2023
;
Tirumala et al. 2022
demonstrated that LLMs exhibit stronger memorization and reduced forgetting during training, and
Biderman et al. 2023
observed consistent patterns of memorized samples across different checkpoints of the same model. However, current memorization metrics, based on either unigram exact matches or full-sequence exact matches, present limitations in their generalizability as they only consider token content while neglecting token order.

To examine memorization dynamics across model scales and trained steps, we replicate established methodologies, identifying memorized instances as 1-gram scores exceeding 0.5. Our analysis focuses on dynamic memorization patterns under
n
-gram metrics and assesses memorization efficiency.

RQ4: How Robust is Memorization Against Perturbations?

Stoehr et al. 2024
has revealed that LLMs can maintain memorization even under significant text modifications, challenging our intuitive understanding. However, these findings lack quantitative measures of perturbation intensity, making it difficult to systematically analyze the relationship between text alterations and memorization robustness. To address this limitation, we design two perturbation strategies with measurable disruption levels: token shuffling and editing. The results are shown in Figure
5
.

7.1
Perturbation Strategies

To investigate the impact of text noise on model memorization, we propose two perturbation strategies: Shuffling Perturbation and Editing Perturbation. The two strategies simulate common real-world text errors, such as word order changes, typos, or edits. These strategies are designed to abstractly model and quantify disruptions in a controlled manner, enabling systematic exploration of how varying degrees of noise affect memorization. By using tokens as the unit of analysis—the direct input to large language models (LLMs)—we ensure that perturbations are precise and relevant to model behavior. We detail each strategy and their quantifiable metrics.

Shuffling Perturbation.
This strategy simulates real-world text noise, such as word order variations due to human error or stylistic changes, by systematically perturbing token positions while preserving the token set. The perturbation process is controlled by a ratio parameter
r\in[0,1]
, which determines the number of random token swaps as
r*\text{T}
, where
T
is the sequence length. This allows precise control over the degree of disruption: a higher ratio results in more extensive shuffling, mimicking greater deviations from the original sequence.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

This work presents a detailed analysis of memorization dynamics in
Pythia
models across scales, training steps, and perturbation conditions. Our findings reveal that memorization capacity increases with model scale but with declining parameter efficiency. Continuity patterns emerge across model sizes, as larger models typically inherit samples memorized or unmemorized by smaller variants. Furthermore, our analysis demonstrates that data characteristics, including token frequency, repetition, and entropy, exert differential effects on memorized versus non-memorized samples. Additionally, we found that prefix perturbations significantly reduce memorization performance, with these effects particularly pronounced on low-redundancy samples. These insights enhance our understanding of memorization mechanisms in large language models.

Extending our quantitative framework for analyzing memorization under controlled input perturbations, our future research has two primary thrusts. First, we will investigate the critical relationship between memorization and the generation of model hallucinations. Second, we will examine the effects of dynamic prompting strategies on memorization. A central challenge is developing methods to effectively measure these characteristics to rigorously assess their influence on memorization and model behavior.
