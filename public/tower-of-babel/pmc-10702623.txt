Comparison of Aesthetic Outcomes of Epidermal Staples vs Sutures in Deep Epigastric Perforator Flap Breast Reconstruction
Authors: Kyle M Ockerman, Sabrina H Han, Nhan S Trieu, Markos Mardourian, Dan Neal, Suhail Kanchwala, Lisa Spiguel, Heather J Furnas, Sarah Sorice-Virk
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA secondary benefit of abdominally based autologous breast reconstruction may be improving the abdominal contour; however, poor scaring can lead to aesthetic dissatisfaction and complications. Although studies have demonstrated favorable aesthetic results and decreased operative time using dermal or subcuticular stapling (Insorb), no reports exist regarding epidermal stapling.ObjectivesThe aim of this study is to compare the aesthetic abdominal scar outcomes, closure time, and postoperative complications of abdominally based breast reconstruction patients who have undergone suture closure vs epidermal staple closure.MethodsA total of 217 patients who underwent abdominally based autologous breast reconstruction from 2011 to 2022 were included and retrospectively analyzed (staples = 41, suture = 176). Twenty-four patients’ postoperative abdominal scar photographs were randomly chosen (staples = 12, sutures = 12) and assessed by 3 board-certified plastic surgeons using a modified patient observer scar assessment scale (POSAS) and visual analog scale (VAS). Closure time (minutes per centimeter) using staples or sutures was also analyzed.ResultsThe assessment of abdominal scars closed by epidermal staples revealed significant improvements in thickness (P = .033), relief (P = .033), surface area (P = .017), overall opinion (P = .033), POSAS score (P = .034), and VAS scar score (P = .023) in comparison with scars closed by sutures. Closing the abdominal wound with staples was significantly faster than closing with sutures (P < .0001). Staple and suture closure had similar postoperative complication rates.ConclusionsAbdominal donor-site scar quality may be superior and faster using the epidermal staple compared to traditional suture closure.Level of Evidence: 2

Breast reconstruction after mastectomy continues to increase as a result of the ever-growing evidence of positive and restorative psychological outcomes and the innovations in reconstructive techniques.1,2 From implant-based to autologous procedures, patients have a wide variety of breast reconstruction options. The deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap has been firmly established as one of the preferred techniques in autologous breast reconstruction technique.3 This option offers women a more natural-feeling breast in addition to the potential for resensation4 by utilizing the patient's lower abdominal skin and adipose tissue to reconstruct the breast.5 In addition, a favorable byproduct is the resultant enhanced abdominal contour.5

In comparison with other reconstructive options, this modality has the highest level of patient-reported satisfaction.6 However, abdominally based breast reconstruction also has the most extensive abdominal donor-site scar, which can negatively affect the overall patient's satisfaction and health-related quality of life after breast reconstruction.7-9

Typically, the abdominal donor skin incision is closed with a combination of interrupted and running sutures. However, for large surgical wounds after an already lengthy operation such as free flap breast reconstruction, suture closure can often be time-consuming, leaving wounds open longer and increasing the risk of infection.10 Several technical innovations to decrease closure times have been brought to market and reported upon for a multitude of indications. Chief among these are dermal and epidermal staplers, which are widely used in a variety of general surgery, gynecologic, orthopedic, and plastic surgery procedures.11,12 While prior studies have demonstrated favorable aesthetic results and decreased operative time using absorbable dermal stapling (ie, subcuticular staples; Insorb) in autologous breast reconstruction,13 to our knowledge, no reports exist regarding epidermal stapling. The purpose of this study was to compare the aesthetic abdominal donor scar quality, postoperative complications, and closure time in patients who have undergone suture or epidermal staple closure of the abdominal donor site in autologous abdominally based breast reconstruction.

METHODS

The University of Florida Institutional Review Board approved this single-site, retrospective study (IRB202200637). The inclusion criteria for the study were adult female patients 18 years or older who underwent free, abdominally based autologous breast reconstruction between December 2011 and December 2022 at an academic institution. Exclusion criteria were patients under the age of 18 and patients who did not undergo abdominally based autologous breast reconstruction during the study period. Written informed consent was obtained by the patients for the publication of their images. All procedures were followed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. Patients were divided into 2 groups for comparative analysis: patients whose abdominal incisions were closed by running 3-0 smooth or barbed Monocryl suture (Stratafix Spiral Monocryl Plus Device; or Monocryl [poliglecaprone 25] sutures; Ethicon, Raritan, NJ) and patients whose abdominal incisions were closed by standard epidermal staples (Ethicon PXW35 [Resterilized] past expiration Proximate PX Fixed-Head Skin Stapler). Of note, in all patients in the staple group, dermal staples (CooperSurgical 2030 INSORB Subcuticular Stapler; CooperSurgical, Inc., Trumbull, CT) were utilized for the deep dermal layer of the closure, while some patients in the suture group underwent dermal closure using 3-0 Monocryl (Ethicon) and others dermal/subcuticular stapling.

Surgical Procedure

Patients underwent either immediate or delayed abdominally based free tissue transfer for breast reconstruction following mastectomy by a fellowship-trained microsurgeon. The choice of whether a muscle-sparing free transverse rectus abdominis flap (MS-TRAM) vs a free DIEP flap was performed depended on the patient’s anatomy. The abdominal flap harvest was performed in standard fashion. All patients underwent fascial closure with retro rectus mesh placement on a case-by-case basis as deemed appropriate by the attending surgeon. The Scarpa's fascia was closed with interrupted 2-0 Vicryl sutures (Ethicon). One or two 19 French Blake drains were placed in all patients. For the suture group, the subcuticular layer was closed with a running 3-0 Monocryl Stratafix or simple 3-0 Monocryl suture, while in the staple group, an epidermal stapler was used. Staples were removed on an average of 5 to 7 days after surgery to avoid train track marks. The drains were removed when output was <30 cc per 24 h for 2 consecutive days. Some patients received closed incision negative pressure therapy (ciNPT) using 3M™ Prevena Plus Customizable Incision Management System (KCI USA, Inc., San Antonio, TX) over the abdominal incision providing a continuous −125 mm Hg pressure for an average of 7 days (range: 7-14).

The patient's abdominal wound was closed by either plastic surgery fellows (postgraduate years [PGY] 6-11), residents (PGY 1-6) under close attending surgeon supervision, or by the attending surgeon. Patients were assessed by the attending surgeon or physician assistants at regularly scheduled follow-up visits (or more frequently as dictated by clinical findings) for 1-year postoperation and yearly thereafter. No patients were lost to follow-up.

Assessment and Outcome Measures

Patient characteristics were retrospectively collected, including age, race, BMI, comorbidities, surgical technique, preoperative radiation therapy (XRT), and preoperative chemotherapy. The number of abdominal drains placed, time to drain removal, prophylaxis abdominal ciNPT use and duration, days to dermal staple removal, and postoperative abdominal incision complications within 90 days postsurgery were also recorded. Patients were noted to have abdominal incision complications if at least one of the following occurred: infection, hematoma, seroma, skin necrosis, or wound dehiscence. Infection was defined as erythema, signs or symptoms of systemic infection, and the clinical need for antibiotics as determined by the senior author.

Time to closure and wound length using staples and sutures were also measured for 20 patients (10 per group). Wound closure time was defined as the time (measured in minutes) it took to complete the dermal and epidermal components of the abdominal wound closure over the entire length of the wound (in centimeters). Individual closure times were then compared in cm/min.

Twenty-four patients’ postoperative abdominal scar photographs were randomly chosen (staples = 12, sutures = 12). Anteroposterior and bilateral oblique photographs of the abdomen were chosen at least 4 months after surgery and were cropped consistently from below the abdominal scar to the beginning of the breast. Patient identifiers, such as tattoos, were removed to protect patient privacy. The photographs were randomly displayed in a consistent order using Google Slides (Alphabet Inc., Mountain View, CA). Three board-certified plastic surgeons independently reviewed the abdominal scars using a modified patient and observer scar assessment scale (POSAS) and visual analog scale (VAS). The VAS is a validated and widely used method for scar assessment, while the modified POSAS data provide a more comprehensive assessment of the wound.14 The 3 surgeons were asked to use both systems in order to reinforce the validity of their assessments. The reviewers did not participate in the procedures and were blinded to the type of abdominal incision closure. Answers were recorded using a Qualtrics (Seattle, WA) survey and exported to Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) for data analysis.

Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale

The POSAS is a validated scar assessment tool that comprises an observer and a patient scale.15,16 The observer scale was only used in this study. The evaluation contains 6 parameters and an overall rating.15 As pliability could not be assessed on pictures, it has been deleted from the scale, resulting in the “modified POSAS” scale. Scales range from 1 for normal skin to 10 for the worst scar imaginable.15

Visual Analog Scale Scar Scoring System

The VAS scar scoring system is an assessment that consists of a 10-cm straight line representing scar quality, with 1 end labeled 0 that represents normal skin and the other end labeled 10 that represents a poor scar.17

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and R statistical software package (V.4.1.1). The VAS scar and POSAS scoring results are expressed as mean values of scores for the staples and suture closures. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and range. Categorical variables were expressed as number of patients and their proportion to the group under study. Postoperative complication P-values are the results of Mann–Whitney tests for continuous variables or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. VAS and POSAS scores were analyzed using t-tests. Interrater reliability was expressed by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC values below 0.5 indicated poor reliability, between 0.5 and 0.75 indicated moderate reliability, between 0.75 and 0.9 indicated good reliability, and above 0.9 indicated excellent reliability.18 Closure time P-values are the results of a 2-tailed paired t-test. For all analyses, statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Scar Outcomes

A random cohort of 24 patients abdominal scar images were obtained at least 4 months postoperation (Figures 1-3; staples = 7.8 months, sutures = 17.5 months; P = .0029). Each patient’s abdominal scar image was evaluated independently in a blinded fashion by 3 board-certified plastic surgeons using the POSAS and VAS scar score (Table 2). The mean VAS scar score for scars closed with staples was significantly less than scars closed with sutures (staples = 2.0, sutures = 3.4; P = .023). Staple-closed scars showed significant improvements in relief (P = .033), surface area (P = .017), thickness (P = .033), overall opinion (P = .033), and POSAS score (P = .034). There was no significant difference in pigmentation (P = .078) and vascularity (P = .262).

DISCUSSION

Although most patients are satisfied with their breast reconstruction, one of the most significant points of dissatisfaction often cited is the abdominal donor-site scar.13,19 Here, we compare the aesthetic outcomes of the abdominal scar after abdominally based free tissue transfer for breast reconstruction between epidermal staples and traditional subcuticular sutures. To our knowledge, this study represents the first to report on epidermal staple closure in abdominally based autologous breast reconstruction donor sites.

Skin staples have a poor reputation with regards to their aesthetic outcomes, as they can be associated with unsightly scarring and “train tracking.”20 Unsurprisingly therefore, most plastic surgeons, chiefly concerned with aesthetic outcomes, have an almost innate reluctance to employ skin staples as a definitive closure modality. Surprisingly, however, results of this study suggest that using staples in lieu of a traditional subcuticular closure with suture results in a superior abdominal donor-site scar in autologous breast reconstruction.

To examine the aesthetic outcomes, photographs of scars of patients who underwent abdominally based breast reconstruction with staple or suture abdominal donor-site closure were evaluated using the POSAS and VAS by 3 independent and blinded board-certified plastic surgeons after at least 4 months postoperation. At 3 months postoperation, the original skin regains its maximum amount of salvageable tensile strength.21 The use of photographs for scar evaluation has previously been shown to be largely equivalent to live patient assessment22 and is a widely accepted evaluation tool in the literature.23-25

Both the VAS and POSAS data showed a significant difference in scar quality between staples and suture abdominal closures. When comparing the degrees of scar healing, the data obtained using the VAS scoring system indicated superior aesthetic outcomes in stapled surgical wounds. Moreover, the surgeons’ assessment of surface area, thickness, and overall opinion showed a significant difference in favor of staple closure. No significant difference was found in the surgeons’ scoring of pigmentation and vascularity between staple and suture closure. This is unsurprising as vascularity is traditionally measured clinically by manually inducing blanching,15 a feature that is not replicable in a 2-dimensional format.

While no authors have specifically investigated the aesthetics of epidermal staples, dermal/subcuticular staples (Insorb) on their own lead to better scar outcomes and faster closure times.13,26 Using the Vancouver scar scale, Han et al demonstrated better donor-site scar quality using absorbable dermal staples in comparison with sutures in autologous breast reconstruction.26 Similarly, in a randomized control trial using the Manchester scar scale, Chung et al showed overall better results and more favorable patient-reported satisfaction using the dermal absorbable stapler compared to sutures.13 All patients in the staple cohort also underwent deep dermal closure with dermal staples, while only some were in the suture group, which may therefore have contributed to the more favorable aesthetic outcome at donor sites in the staple group.

While the least technically demanding and rarely considered a critical component of the operation, the results of the donor-site wound closure are among the most visible results of the surgical intervention for the patient.27,28 Potential complications and aesthetic outcomes may therefore be given disproportionate attention by the patient with regard to judging surgical success.12,29 Treating the abdominal donor-site closure with particular attention instead of an afterthought after a long and often arduous operation is therefore important for optimal patient-reported outcomes.28

Despite staples being removed between Days 5 and 7, the differences in the rate of complications between the groups in our cohort were insignificant. Our results compare similarly with other studies conducted in general surgery, cesarean section, and autologous breast reconstruction that indicate no significant differences in rates of complication when employing staples vs sutures.11-13 These results further underscore the legitimacy of staples as a safe and potentially superior method of donor-site wound closure in abdominal-based autologous breast reconstruction. Notably, a crucial component to optimizing aesthetic success and avoiding dreaded train tracking is the timing of staple removal. In this study cohort, all staples were removed on average between postoperative Days 5 and 7. This is particularly imperative in darker pigmented individuals or those with darker pigmented individuals or those with histories of poor scarring. Indeed, 1 patient was not able to return for staple removal until postoperative Day 19 resulting in permanent train tracks (Figure 4). Research on staple closure in head and neck cancer surgery and scalp wounds showed similarly that removal of staples between 7 and 10 days avoids train track scars.30,31

In addition to potential aesthetic benefits, epidermal stapling may be more cost-effective compared to barbed sutures favored by many plastic surgeons for this indication (staples = $10 per stapler vs Stratafix® sutures = ∼$80 per 30 cm suture). Similarly, dermal staples were previously shown to be cheaper than conventional sutures in autologous breast reconstruction.26

As shown by others,26,32,33 using epidermal staples also led to significantly shorter closure times than using subcuticular sutures. Our results compare similarly with other studies that found reduced operating time using staples. While this may not be the primary motivating factor for implementing this closure technique, it holds tremendous benefit in its own right. Total time under anesthesia has been linked to an increased risk for complications including infection, venous thromboembolic events, and wound-healing issues.34-37 This is of particular concern to patients with medical comorbidities which render them less favorable operative candidates. Additionally, from a cost perspective, every minute under anesthesia amounts to $62.38 Thus, saving an average of 0.074 min using staples may be of fiscal benefit as well. Of note, in order to facilitate a true comparison of closure time between epidermal sutures, the dermal layer in this subset of patients was closed using dermal staples in both groups. In order to definitively compare all groups, future comparisons should include dermal suturing as well. However, intuitively 1 can likely safely deduce that any degree of suturing will be associated with longer time.

While this study is limited by its small sample size and lack of randomization, it is meant to provide the investigators’ preliminary findings to introduce epidermal staples as a potential tool for donor-site closure after abdominal-based breast reconstruction that yields favorable aesthetic outcomes and decreased closure times. In addition, the validated scar assessment tools used in this study were not developed specifically for judging linear scars, do not specifically evaluate the presence of staple or suture marks, and were originally designed for in-person scar assessment. Another limitation is that the aesthetic scar assessments had moderate interrater reliability, indicating some differences in ratings between reviewers. Unsurprisingly, this was most pronounced in the subscales that evaluate aspects of scar assessment most suited for evaluation by physical examination. Another limitation to this study and technique is that stapling requires a patient to reliably have access to and return to a follow-up appointment for staple removal or be educated on staple removal. Some patients in both groups underwent incisional negative pressure therapy which has also been shown to improve scar quality.39 An additional limitation is that even though the 2 cohorts did not significantly differ in their baseline characteristics, such as race, age, and BMI, there are other factors such as skin color specifically that were not controlled for in this study and may affect the aesthetic outcome of the scar. Photographs of the scars also included the surrounding abdominal contour, which may have impacted raters’ opinion. Lastly, as the closure for both staples and sutures was performed by a variety of surgeons at different skill levels (ie, residents and fellows), the aesthetic scar outcome may vary based on skill, leading to staples yielding a potentially better result than sutures. However, this speaks to the versatility of staples, especially when working in a team, as it may obviate the need for the highest skilled member to perform every portion of this already long and complex operation without negatively impacting aesthetic outcome or procedure duration. However, in future studies, it would be important to control for surgeon skill and experience when evaluating the aesthetic scar outcome. Randomized controlled trials are in preparation and will address these shortcomings as well as skin pigment and patient-reported outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

Epidermal staples may lead to improved abdominal donor-site aesthetics and faster closure times in patients after abdominally based autologous breast reconstruction.
