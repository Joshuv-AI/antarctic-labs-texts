CUL2 confers ferroptosis resistance in pancreatic cancer by disrupting KEAP1-mediated NRF2 degradation
Authors: Kui Yu, LiLi Chen, Yong Zhong, Yang Wang, Na Zhu, Daoheng Wang, Wenyan Yao, Haoran Bai, Lijuan Yang, Dachuan Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer (PC) is characterized by high chemoresistance and poor prognosis. CUL2, a scaffold protein of E3 ubiquitin ligases, has been implicated in tumor progression, but its role in PC remains unclear.MethodsCUL2 expression was analyzed in PC tissues and cell lines using TCGA and GEO datasets, qRT-PCR, Western blot, and immunohistochemistry analysis. The biological functions of CUL2 were investigated through gain- and loss-of-function studies in vitro. The impact of CUL2 overexpression on gemcitabine sensitivity was evaluated in xenograft models. Molecular mechanisms were explored using proteasome inhibitor MG132, co-immunoprecipitation, subcellular fractionation, and ferroptosis assessment.ResultsCUL2 was significantly upregulated in PC tissues and correlated with poor prognosis. CUL2 promoted PC cell proliferation, migration, and EMT. Mechanistically, CUL2 competed with NRF2 for KEAP1 binding, thereby preventing KEAP1-mediated NRF2 degradation and promoting NRF2 nuclear translocation. CUL2 promoted oxidative stress while activating NRF2-dependent antioxidant response. The CUL2-NRF2 axis suppressed ferroptosis and conferred gemcitabine resistance in PC cells. In xenograft models, CUL2 overexpression enhanced tumor growth and attenuated gemcitabine sensitivity through NRF2-mediated ferroptosis inhibition.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a novel mechanism whereby CUL2 promotes PC progression and ferroptosis resistance through regulation of the KEAP1-NRF2 axis. CUL2 overexpression enhances cellular antioxidant capacity and maintains mitochondrial integrity, thereby conferring broad resistance to ferroptosis-inducing conditions. This study suggests that targeting the CUL2-NRF2 axis to enhance ferroptosis sensitivity might represent a promising therapeutic strategy for PC treatment.Graphical abstract Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, CUL2, NRF2, Ferroptosis, Gemcitabine resistance

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a malignancy of the gastrointestinal system with high immunosuppression that tends to be asymptomatic at an early stage [1]. Therefore, it is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide with a 5-year survival rate of only approximately 13% [2]. Based on clinical staging, PC is classified into four categories: resectable, borderline resectable, locally advanced/unresectable, and metastatic disease [3]. Although surgical resection combined with adjuvant chemotherapy remains the only curative treatment for PC, only 15–20% of patients present with resectable disease at diagnosis. Approximately 30–40% of patients show locally advanced disease, and another 40% have distant metastases [3, 4]. For patients with unresectable or metastatic disease, systemic chemotherapy is the primary treatment option, with gemcitabine-based regimens being the standard first-line therapy [5]. Despite these treatment strategies, the therapeutic efficacy is largely limited by intrinsic and acquired drug resistance.

The Cullin-2 protein (CUL2) is a core component of the multiple Cullin-RING-based E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase complex, which functions in protein ubiquitination [6]. CUL2 enables ubiquitin protein ligase binding and is involved in proteasomal ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic processes and protein ubiquitination through a conserved Cullin homology domain at the C-terminus [7]. Several cancers are associated with CUL2, including colorectal [8], gastric [9], and cervical cancers [10]. CUL2 has been reported to be involved in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) through interaction with circSTX6 and HIF1A signaling [11]. However, the detailed molecular mechanisms of CUL2 in PC progression and chemoresistance remain largely unknown.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a crucial role in chemotherapy-induced cancer cell death, as many chemotherapeutic drugs increase ROS levels to promote cancer cell apoptosis [12, 13]. The Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1)-NRF2 system serves as a major cellular defense mechanism against oxidative stress, where KEAP1 acts as a stress sensor and regulates the stability and nuclear translocation of NRF2, a key transcription factor that activates antioxidant response elements (AREs) [14, 15]. Under normal conditions, KEAP1 mediates NRF2 degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. However, oxidative stress disrupts KEAP1-NRF2 binding, leading to NRF2 nuclear translocation and the activation of antioxidant genes [16]. Notably, dysregulation of the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway has been implicated in cancer progression and chemoresistance [17, 18]. In PC, enhanced NRF2 activity, such as through the GRP78-mediated unfolded protein response, has been shown to contribute to gemcitabine resistance [16, 19], suggesting that modulation of the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway and ROS levels plays a critical role in PC chemoresistance.

Ferroptosis, a recently identified form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a crucial mechanism in cancer therapy [20]. This unique cell death pathway is distinct from apoptosis and is regulated by multiple factors, including the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway [21]. Recent studies have shown that ferroptosis plays a significant role in chemotherapy response, particularly in PC [22]. Notably, ferroptosis has been associated with gemcitabine resistance in PC [22], suggesting that targeting ferroptosis might be a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance.

Recent studies have suggested that Cullin family members, including CUL4A, play important roles in cancer chemoresistance by regulating chemosensitivity to gemcitabine through TGFBI in lung cancer [23, 24]. Moreover, CUL2 has been reported to regulate chemoresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma through modulation of MAF1-mediated transcription [25]. Gemcitabine remains the first-line chemotherapy for PC treatment, but its therapeutic efficacy is largely limited by drug resistance [26]. Although CUL2 has been associated with certain types of cancer and chemoresistance, its specific role in PC and gemcitabine resistance remains to be elucidated.

Given that CUL2 functions as a critical component of E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes and the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway is tightly regulated by protein ubiquitination, combined with their respective roles in chemoresistance and ferroptosis regulation, we hypothesized that CUL2 might modulate gemcitabine resistance in PC through regulation of the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway and ferroptosis. In this study, we have assessed the interactions between CUL2, NRF2, and KEAP1 in the progression of PC and gemcitabine resistance through in vitro molecular techniques and an in vivo xenograft animal model of PC. Our findings indicate that CUL2 regulates the KEAP1–NRF2 pathway to enhance the growth of PC and gemcitabine resistance by modulating ferroptosis. Given that CUL2 functions as a critical component of E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes and the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway is tightly regulated by protein ubiquitination, combined with their respective roles in ferroptosis regulation, we hypothesized that CUL2 might modulate ferroptosis sensitivity in PC through regulation of the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway. In this study, we assessed the interactions between CUL2, NRF2, and KEAP1 in PC progression and ferroptosis resistance through in vitro molecular techniques and an in vivo xenograft animal model. Our findings indicate that CUL2 regulates the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway to enhance PC growth and confer ferroptosis resistance, suggesting a novel therapeutic target for overcoming treatment resistance in PC.

Materials and methods

Patients and clinical tumor samples

Twenty pairs of tumor and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from treatment-naïve patients with PC treated at Shanghai Fourth People’s Hospital between August 2022 and May 2023. Patients who had received prior chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included other malignancies, severe systemic diseases, and pregnancy. The tissue samples were used for quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR), western blot, and immunohistochemical analysis. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of Longhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number:2022-068ID="MN1">−001) and was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

PC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. For subcutaneous xenograft tumors, tissues were harvested, fixed, embedded, and sectioned using the same procedure. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min in a microwave oven. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. After blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CUL2 (1:200, Abcam, ab133180), Ki-67 (1:800, Cell Signaling Technology, #9449), GPX4 (1:2,000, Cell Signaling Technology, #59735) and NRF2 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, bs-1074R) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200, Vector Laboratories) for 1 h at room temperature. The immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB substrate kit (Vector Laboratories), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The staining intensity was scored as: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). The percentage of positive cells was scored as: 0 (0%), 1 (1–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), or 4 (76–100%). The final immunoreactivity score was calculated by multiplying the intensity score by the percentage score (range 0–12). Five random fields were evaluated for each section by two independent pathologists who were blinded to the clinical information.

Regents

The NRF2 inhibitor ML385 was purchased from Cayman Chemical Company (Ann Arbor, MI, USA) and dissolved in DMSO. Ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and ferroptosis activator Erastin were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Gene expression data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-PAAD) dataset and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE16515. For TCGA-PAAD, RNA-seq data (FPKM values) were downloaded and log2-transformed. The GSE16515 dataset, containing microarray expression profiles of PC and adjacent normal tissues, was downloaded and normalized. GSEA was performed using GSEA software (version 4.2.3, Broad Institute) with MSigDB hallmark gene sets as the reference. Samples were divided into high and low CUL2 expression groups based on the median expression value. Gene sets with nominal P-value < 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched. The normalized enrichment score (NES) was calculated to determine the degree and direction of enrichment. All analysis were performed using default parameters with 1,000 permutations.

Cell culture

The human PC cell lines CAPAN2, PanC-1, AsPC-1, and BxPC-3 and the human pancreatic ductal epithelial cell line HPDEC1 were obtained from the Type Culture Collection Committee of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested for mycoplasma contamination. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, CA, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Construction of vectors and transfection

The CUL2 gene (NM_001198777.2) and NRF2 gene (NM_006164.5) were inserted into vector pcDNA 3.1 for lentiviral-mediated overexpression in PC cells using restriction enzymes and PCR. The vectors were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfection efficiency was verified by Western blot and qRT-PCR analysis 48 h post-transfection. Cells with stable overexpression were selected using G418 (400 µg/mL) for 2 weeks. The expression levels of target genes were regularly monitored to ensure stable transfection throughout the experiments.

ShRNA knockdown

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting CUL2 and KEAP1 were designed and cloned into the pLKO.1-puro lentiviral vector. The shRNA sequences were as follows: sh-CUL2#1 (targeting NM_001198777.2): 5’-GCAAGGGTGCAGACTATATGG-3’; sh-CUL2#2: 5’-GGTGATTCATGAATGTCAACA-3’; sh-KEAP1: 5’-GCCTCATTGAATTCGCCTACA-3’; and a non-targeting scrambled control shRNA (sh-NC): 5’-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG-3’. Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the pLKO.1 constructs, psPAX2 packaging plasmid, and pMD2.G envelope plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Viral supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection, filtered through 0.45 μm filters, and used to infect PC cells in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. Infected cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 1 week to establish stable cell lines. The knockdown efficiency was verified by Western blot and qRT-PCR analysis.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion capabilities were evaluated using 24-well Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Corning, NY, USA). For migration assays, AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells (2 × 104) were suspended in 200 µL serum-free DMEM after respective treatments for 48 h. The cells were then seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with 600 µL DMEM containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed with cotton swabs. The migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. For invasion assays, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) diluted 1:8 in serum-free DMEM and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 6 h. The subsequent steps were identical to the migration assay except that 5 × 104 cells were seeded and the incubation time was extended to 48 h. For both assays, five random fields per chamber were photographed under a light microscope (200× magnification), and cells were counted using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Wound healing assay

Cell migration ability was assessed using wound healing assay. AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured until reaching 90% confluence. After transfection with sh-NC or sh-CUL2 for 24 h, a straight scratch was made across the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Cell debris was removed by washing with PBS, and fresh serum-free medium was added. Images of the wounds were captured at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera. The wound closure area was measured using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) and calculated as follows: wound closure (%) = [(original wound area - remaining wound area)/original wound area] × 100%.

EDU proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell-Light EdU Apollo567 In Vitro Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. After respective treatments for 48 h, cells were incubated with 50 µM EdU for 2 h at 37 °C. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with Apollo reaction cocktail for 30 min in the dark. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and five random fields were selected for each well. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated as the number of EdU-positive cells divided by the total number of Hoechst-positive cells.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence analysis of EMT markers, AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were seeded on glass coverslips in 24-well plates. Following the respective transfections, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against E-cadherin (#3195, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:2,000) and N-cadherin (#13116, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1,000) overnight at 4 °C. For co-localization analysis, cells were incubated with mouse anti-CUL2 (1:200, Novus, H00008453-M03) and rabbit anti-NRF2 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, #12721) primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The fluorescence intensity and co-localization analysis were quantified using ImageJ software (from at least five random fields per sample.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitations (co-IPs) were performed using a method described previously (29). Briefly, HEK293T cells transfected with FLAG-tagged CUL2 and Myc-tagged KEAP1 were lysed by sonication in 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, and Igepal (0.5%, pH 7.5). The lysates were incubated with anti-FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), anti-CUL2 (Santa Cruz), and anti-Myc (Cell Signaling) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitates were resolved in SDS-PAGE and probed with anti-FLAG, anti-CUL2, and anti-Myc antibodies. Protein interactions were determined using western blotting.

Ubiquitination assay

AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 48 h. Prior to harvest, cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 for 6 h to inhibit proteasomal degradation. Cells were collected and lysed in denaturing buffer (2% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate, 50 mM sodium fluoride) with protease inhibitors and boiled for 10 min. The lysates were diluted 10-fold with dilution buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100) and subjected to immunoprecipitation with anti-NRF2 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #12721, 1:100) overnight at 4 °C. The immunoprecipitates were washed four times with wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40) and analyzed by Western blot using anti-ubiquitin antibody to detect ubiquitinated NRF2. Total cell lysates were also analyzed by Western blot as input controls.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo, Japan). AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and allowed to attach overnight. Cells were then treated with increasing concentrations of gemcitabine (0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 µM) for 48 h. Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). Cell viability was calculated as the percentage relative to untreated control cells. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software through nonlinear regression analysis. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated three times independently.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo, Japan). AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and allowed to attach overnight. For CUL2 functional analysis, cells were transfected with CUL2 overexpression plasmid, sh-CUL2#1, or corresponding controls for 48 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with ferroptosis inducers (Erastin 5 µM) for CUL2 overexpression groups, or ferroptosis inhibitors (Ferrostatin-1, 10 µM) for CUL2 knockdown groups, with vehicle controls for an additional 24 h.

For gemcitabine sensitivity analysis, cells were treated with increasing concentrations of gemcitabine (0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 µM) for 48 h. Following treatments, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). Cell viability was calculated as the percentage relative to corresponding control cells. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated three times independently.

Colony formation assay

The long-term proliferative capacity of cells was evaluated using colony formation assay. AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well and allowed to attach overnight. After respective treatments, cells were cultured in complete medium for 14 days, with medium containing fresh compounds replaced every 3 days. At the end of the experiment, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS and air-drying, plates were scanned and the number and size of colonies were quantified using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell fraction

Cell fractions were obtained as described previously [27]. Briefly, cells were lysed by vortexing in a buffer containing 10 mM HEPES pH7.9,10 mM KCl,1.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.5 mM b-mercaptoethanol. Then, 5 mL of 10% NP-40 (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the mixture. After a further round of vortexing, cell lysates were subjected to centrifugation at 16,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cytoplasmic cell fraction was obtained from the supernatant and the cell pellet was lysed with a buffer containing 10 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 1 mM DTT, 7.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.3 M NaCl, 1 M urea, and 1% NP-40. The nuclear fraction was obtained from the supernatant after further centrifugation at 16,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C.

Cell apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested and washed twice with cold PBS, then resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/mL. 100 µL of the cell suspension was transferred to a 5 mL culture tube, followed by addition of 5 µL of FITC-conjugated Annexin V and incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, 5 µL of propidium iodide (PI) was added and incubated for an additional 5 min in the dark. Finally, 400 µL of 1× binding buffer was added to each tube. The samples were analyzed immediately using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences). Early apoptotic cells (Annexin V-positive/PI-negative) and late apoptotic cells (Annexin V-positive/PI-positive) were quantified. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc.). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mitochondrial morphology was examined using TEM. AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were transfected with vector or oe-CUL2 plasmids for 24 h, followed by treatment with or without ML385 (10 µM) for an additional 24 h. Cells were then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C. After washing with phosphate buffer, cells were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at 4 °C, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7), stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a TEM (JEOL JEM-1400) operating at 80 kV. Mitochondrial morphology was assessed in at least 20 cells per condition, focusing on characteristics such as mitochondrial size, shape, and cristae integrity.

C11-BODIPY staining for lipid ROS detection

Cells were transfected with sh-NC or sh-CUL2#1 for 48 h, then treated with Ferrostatin-1 (10 µM) or vehicle control for an additional 24 h. To analyze lipid peroxidation, cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS, then incubated with 10 µM BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10445) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After staining, cells were washed once with PBS and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur). Lipid peroxidation was assessed by measuring the shift from red fluorescence (FL2 channel, 590 nm) to green fluorescence (FL1 channel, 510 nm), with increased green fluorescence indicating elevated lipid ROS levels. Gates were set to determine the percentage of cells with high lipid ROS (green fluorescence-positive cells). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software, and results were expressed as percentage of lipid ROS-positive cells in bar graphs.

FerroOrange staining for intracellular iron detection

Intracellular iron levels were measured using FerroOrange (Dojindo Molecular Technologies). Cells transfected with sh-NC or sh-CUL2#1 were treated with Ferrostatin-1 (10 µM) or vehicle control for 24 h, then incubated with 1 µM FerroOrange in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing with PBS, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using the PE channel. For fluorescence microscopy, cells were seeded on coverslips, stained with FerroOrange, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope with appropriate filters.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe (Sigma-Aldrich). AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells were transfected with vector or oe-CUL2 plasmids for 24 h, followed by treatment with or without ML385 (10 µM) for an additional 24 h. Cells were then incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After washing three times with PBS, the fluorescence intensity was measured using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc.).

Oxidative stress measurements

Lipid peroxidation and glutathione levels were assessed to evaluate oxidative stress. MDA levels were measured using a Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, treated cells were collected and homogenized in MDA lysis buffer containing butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). The lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatants were reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) for 1 h at 95 °C. The MDA-TBA adduct was measured colorimetrically at 532 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). For GSH/GSSG ratio determination, a GSH/GSSG Ratio Detection Assay Kit (Abcam) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were harvested in ice-cold PBS, and samples were processed for both GSH measurement (deproteinized with 5% sulfosalicylic acid) and GSSG measurement (treated with GSH quencher before deproteinization). The GSH and GSSG contents were determined using a kinetic assay, and the ratio was calculated. All measurements were performed in triplicate and normalized to protein concentration.

Subcutaneous xenograft tumor model

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Longhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. PanC-1 and AsPC-1 cells stably expressing Vector or oe-CUL2 were harvested and resuspended in PBS. Cells (1 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. When tumors reached approximately 80–100 mm3 (five days after implantation), mice were randomly divided into treatment groups (n = 6 per group). Mice received either PBS or gemcitabine (50 mg/kg/week) via intraperitoneal injection. Tumor size was measured weekly using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (length × width2)/2. Body weight was also monitored weekly. After four weeks of treatment, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Tumors were harvested, weighed, photographed, and either fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for immunohistochemical analysis or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for biochemical assays including MDA, GSH levels and iron content measurements.

Measurement of ferroptosis-related parameters in tumor tissues

Tumor tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS and processed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Iron content was determined using an Iron Assay Kit (MAK025, Sigma-Aldrich). Briefly, the tissue homogenates were incubated with Iron Reducer for 30 min at room temperature to transform Fe3+ to Fe2+, followed by reaction with Iron Probe to produce a colorimetric (593 nm) product proportional to the iron content.

Lipid peroxidation was assessed by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using a Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (MAK085, Sigma-Aldrich). The tissue homogenates were reacted with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 95 °C for 60 min to generate a MDA-TBA adduct, which was measured colorimetrically at 532 nm.

Glutathione (GSH) levels were measured using a Glutathione Assay Kit (CS0260, Sigma-Aldrich). The samples were first deproteinized with 5% 5-sulfosalicylic acid solution, then the supernatant was used for GSH determination based on the reaction of 5,5’-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) with GSH to generate 2-nitro-5-thiobenzoic acid, measured at 412 nm. All measurements were normalized to protein concentration determined by BCA assay.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from PC cells using Trizol (Invitrogen) and cDNA was generated from RNA using a cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied BioSystems, Waltham, MA, USA). SYBR Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) was used to quantify the expression levels of CUL2 with the following primers: CUL2 Forward: 5′-TTGCAAAGCACCTGAACTGC-3′, Reverse: 3′-AAGCTCGTGAGCCTGTCTTC-5′; GAPDH Forward: 5’-TGTGGGCATCAATGGATTTGG-3’, Reverse: 5’- ACACCATGTATTCCGGGTCAAT-3’. The GAPDH gene was used for normalization and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer and equal amounts of protein, determined by a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), were separated using SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes and blocked for 2 h in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CUL2 (ab133180, 1:1,000, Abcam), KEAP1 (sc-514914, 1:1,000, Santa Cruz), NRF2 (ab137550, 1:1,500, Abcam), NQO1 (ab80588, 1:1,000, Abcam), HMOX1 (ab189491, 1:1,000, Abcam), FTH1 (ab75972, 1:1,000, Abcam), P62 (ab207305, 1:1,000, Abcam), NRF2 (#127121, 1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology), ACSL4 (ab155282, 1:1,500, Abcam), NOX1 (ab121009, 1:1,000, Abcam), GPX4 (ab125066, 1:1,000, Abcam), SLC7A11 (ab300667, 1:2,000, Abcam), and β-actin (ab7817, 1:2,000, Abcam). Membranes were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Immunoreactive protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the protein levels relative to β-actin were determined by Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparison between two groups, normal distribution was first verified using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and then paired or unpaired Student’s t-test was performed to assess statistical significance. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between continuous variables. For all statistical analysis, P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed at least three times independently.

Results

CUL2 is overexpressed in PC tissues and correlates with poor prognosis

To investigate the role of CUL2 in PC, we first analyzed its expression in PC tissues and cell lines and explored its correlation with clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis. Analysis of the TCGA-PAAD dataset using the GEPIA web tool revealed that CUL2 mRNA levels were significantly higher in PAAD tissues compared to normal pancreatic tissues (Fig. 1A). Similar results were observed in the GSE16515 dataset, which also showed significant upregulation of CUL2 expression in PC tissues compared to normal pancreatic tissues (Fig. 1B). To validate this finding, we performed qRT-PCR on 20 pairs of PC and adjacent normal tissues from our own cohort. Consistent with the TCGA data, CUL2 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in PC tissues (Fig. 1C). Western blot analysis of 10 pairs of PC and adjacent normal tissues further confirmed the elevated CUL2 protein levels in PC tissues (Fig. 1D). Moreover, CUL2 expression significantly correlated with tumor grade in the TCGA-PAAD dataset (Table 1). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that CUL2 was overexpressed in PC tissues, and its expression positively correlated with higher tumor stages (Fig. 1E). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that high CUL2 expression was associated with worse overall survival in PAAD patients (Fig. 1F). To investigate CUL2 expression in PC cell lines, we performed qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis on CAPAN2, PanC-1, AsPC-1, BxPC-3, and the control cell line HPDEC1. CUL2 mRNA and protein levels were higher in all PC cell lines compared to HPDEC1, with the highest expression observed in AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells (Fig. 1G, H). These results suggest that CUL2 may play an oncogenic role in PC progression and could serve as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

Knockdown of CUL2 inhibits cell proliferation, migration, and invasion and regulates EMT markers in PC cells

To explore the biological function of CUL2 in PC, we established stable CUL2 knockdown cell lines using shRNA. The efficiency of knockdown was confirmed by both qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis (Fig. 2A, B). GSEA using both GSE16515 dataset and TCGA-PAAD dataset revealed significant positive correlations between CUL2 expression and EMT-related gene signatures as well as proliferation-related gene sets (Fig. 2C). Functionally, EdU incorporation assay showed that CUL2 knockdown significantly decreased cell proliferation ability (Fig. 2D) and impaired both migration and invasion capacities as assessed by transwell assays (Fig. 2E). Wound healing assays further confirmed the inhibitory effect of CUL2 knockdown on cell migration (Fig. 2F). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining showed that CUL2 knockdown increased E-cadherin while decreasing N-cadherin expression, indicating suppressed EMT (Fig. 2G). These findings collectively demonstrate that CUL2 acts as an oncogenic factor by promoting pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, migration, and EMT, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target.

CUL2 regulates oxidative stress and NRF2 nuclear localization in PC

Previous studies have shown that CUL2 can promote oxidative stress through stimulating hydroxyl radical formation from molecular oxygen by redox cycling [28]. However, the exact role of CUL2 in oxidative stress regulation in PC remains unclear. To investigate the role of CUL2 in oxidative stress regulation, we first established CUL2-overexpressing PC cell lines. Both qRT-PCR and western blot analysis confirmed successful CUL2 overexpression (Fig. 3A, B). Pearson correlation analysis using TCGA-PAAD dataset revealed strong positive correlations between CUL2 and multiple oxidative stress-related genes, including HMOX1, SOD1, SOD2, CAT, and NOX4 (Fig. 3C). Consistently, DCFH-DA staining showed elevated ROS levels in CUL2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3D), accompanied by decreased GSH/GSSG ratio and increased MDA levels (Fig. 3E, F). The accumulation of ROS in cells leads to varying degrees of oxidative stress-induced damage, which can promote tumorigenesis [29]. Cancer cell survival depends on maintaining a delicate balance between ROS generation and elimination, primarily regulated by the master antioxidant transcription factor NRF2 [30]. Dual immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that while CUL2 was predominantly cytoplasmic, NRF2 was distributed in both cytoplasm and nucleus, with notable co-localization in the cytoplasmic region (Fig. 3G). Western blot analysis showed that CUL2 overexpression significantly increased the expression of NRF2 target genes, including NQO1, HMOX1, and FTH1 (Fig. 3H, I). Moreover, subcellular fractionation revealed that CUL2 overexpression primarily enhanced nuclear accumulation of NRF2 while maintaining relatively stable cytoplasmic levels (Fig. 3J). These findings demonstrate that CUL2 promotes oxidative stress in PC cells and reveals a previously unknown mechanism involving NRF2 nuclear translocation.

NRF2 is required for CUL2-mediated aggressive phenotype in PC cells

To determine whether NRF2 mediates the oncogenic effects of CUL2, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing NRF2 in CUL2-knockdown cells. The efficiency of NRF2 overexpression was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis (Fig. 4A). Notably, NRF2 overexpression significantly reversed the inhibitory effects of CUL2 knockdown on cell migration and invasion, as demonstrated by transwell assays (Fig. 4B, C). EdU incorporation assays revealed that the reduced proliferation capacity caused by CUL2 knockdown was largely restored upon NRF2 overexpression (Fig. 4D, E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis showed that NRF2 overexpression rescued the EMT phenotype in CUL2-knockdown cells, evidenced by decreased E-cadherin and increased N-cadherin expression (Fig. 4F, G). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that the enhanced apoptosis induced by CUL2 knockdown was significantly attenuated by NRF2 overexpression (Fig. 4H). These findings demonstrate that NRF2 is a crucial downstream effector mediating the oncogenic functions of CUL2 in PC. This suggests that the CUL2-NRF2 axis plays a crucial role in PC progression.

CUL2 prevents NRF2 degradation through competitive binding with KEAP1

After establishing that the overexpression of CUL2 could lead to higher levels of NRF2 in the nucleus, we next tried to determine the mechanism involved. To investigate how CUL2 regulates NRF2, we first examined the effects of CUL2 knockdown on NRF2 expression. Western blot analysis showed that CUL2 knockdown significantly decreased NRF2 protein levels in both AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells. However, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that NRF2 mRNA levels remained unchanged, suggesting that CUL2 regulates NRF2 at the post-transcriptional level (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, Pearson correlation analysis of TCGA-PAAD dataset demonstrated a significant positive correlation between CUL2 and NRF2 mRNA expression (r = 0.675, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). This discrepancy between in vitro knockdown experiments and clinical data might reflect the complex regulatory network in the tumor microenvironment, where stabilized NRF2 protein could potentially create a positive feedback loop to enhance its own transcription.

Given that NRF2 protein levels, but not mRNA levels, were affected by CUL2, we next examined whether CUL2 regulates NRF2 through protein stability. Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 rescued the reduction of NRF2 protein levels caused by CUL2 knockdown, indicating that CUL2 regulates NRF2 through the proteasome-dependent degradation pathway (Fig. 5C). Levels of ubiquitinated NRF2 (Ub-NRF2) were measured by IP in AsPC-1 cells. We found that the levels of Ub-NRF2 were significantly lower than the control in cells overexpressing CUL2 whereas they were significantly higher in cells inhibiting the expression of CUL2 in Panc-1 cells (Fig. 5D). Next, we assessed levels of CUL2, NRF2, KEAP1, and p62 proteins in PC cells overexpressing CUL2 with or without KEAP1 knockdown. When CUL2 was overexpressed the relative level of the NRF2 protein increased significantly compared to the control. However, when the expression of KEAP1 was inhibited the NRF2 protein reached the same level as the cells overexpressing CUL2. No significant differences were obtained with p62. Both CUL2 overexpression and KEAP1 knockdown significantly increased NRF2 protein levels to a similar extent, suggesting that CUL2 and KEAP1 regulate NRF2 through the same pathway (Fig. 5E). Co-IP assays were then performed to detect the interaction between Flag-tagged CUL2 and Myc-tagged KEAP1 in HEK293T cells. The results indicate an interaction between CUL2 and KEAP1 (Fig. 5F). Finally, we examined the interactions using increased concentrations of CUL2 (0, 2, 4, or 8 µl) and constant levels of NRF2 (4 µl) and KEAP1(6 µl) in a co-IP in vitro translation system (Fig. 5G). We found that increasing concentrations of CUL2 resulted in higher binding of CUL2 to KEAP1 whereas the simultaneous binding of NRF2 to KEAP1 decreased. From these results, we concluded that CUL2 competes for KEAP1 binding with NRF2, leading to the accumulation and activation of NRF2 and inhibiting the KEAP1-dependent ubiquitination of NRF2.

CUL2 suppresses ferroptosis through NRF2 activation in PC cells

GSEA of both GSE16515 and TCGA-PAAD datasets revealed significant negative correlations between CUL2 expression and ferroptosis-related gene signatures (Fig. 6A). Cell viability assays demonstrated that CUL2 overexpression increased cell viability, while erastin treatment reversed this protective effect. Conversely, CUL2 knockdown decreased cell viability, which was rescued by Ferrostatin-1 treatment, confirming that CUL2 protects cells from ferroptotic death (Fig. 6B). CUL2 knockdown significantly increased lipid ROS levels detected by C11-BODIPY staining and elevated iron accumulation measured by FerroOrange staining. Ferrostatin-1 treatment effectively rescued the lipid peroxidation and partially reversed iron accumulation (Fig. 6C-D). At the molecular level, CUL2 knockdown increased ACSL4 and NOX1 expression while decreasing GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels. Ferrostatin-1 treatment partially reversed these changes (Fig. 6E, Fig. S1A). Given our findings that CUL2 stabilizes NRF2 and previous reports linking NRF2 to ferroptosis regulation [31, 32], we investigated whether CUL2 regulates ferroptosis through NRF2. To confirm the NRF2-dependency of CUL2’s anti-ferroptotic effects, we used CUL2-overexpressing cells treated with the NRF2 inhibitor ML385 as a rescue approach. TEM analysis showed that CUL2 overexpression maintained normal mitochondrial morphology, while ML385 treatment induced ferroptotic mitochondrial changes (Fig. 6F). CUL2 overexpression decreased MDA levels and increased the GSH/GSSG ratio, effects that were abolished by ML385 treatment (Fig. 6G). Western blot analysis confirmed that CUL2 overexpression modulated ferroptosis-related proteins, and these effects were reversed by NRF2 inhibition (Fig. 6H, Fig.S1B). These results demonstrate that CUL2 suppresses ferroptosis through NRF2 activation.

CUL2-NRF2 axis promotes gemcitabine resistance by suppressing ferroptosis in PC cells

Gemcitabine resistance remains a major challenge in PC treatment [5]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that NRF2-mediated antioxidant response contributes to gemcitabine resistance [33, 34]. Notably, recent research has revealed that ferroptosis inhibition promotes gemcitabine resistance in PC [35], highlighting the critical role of ferroptosis suppression in chemoresistance. Given our findings that CUL2 stabilizes NRF2 and suppresses ferroptosis, we hypothesized that the CUL2-NRF2 axis might contribute to gemcitabine resistance through ferroptosis regulation.

To investigate this mechanism, we evaluated cell viability under various conditions. CUL2 overexpression significantly increased gemcitabine IC50 values, and this protective effect was abolished by either NRF2 inhibition (ML385, 10 µM) or ferroptosis activation (Erastin, 5 µM). Importantly, ferroptosis inhibition by Ferrostatin-1 (10 µM) produced similar gemcitabine resistance as CUL2 overexpression (Fig. 7A). Colony formation and flow cytometry analysis further validated these findings. Under PBS treatment, CUL2 overexpression enhanced colony formation and decreased apoptotic rate, which were reversed by ML385 treatment, suggesting that CUL2 promotes cell survival through NRF2 pathway. Notably, neither Erastin nor Ferrostatin-1 affected cell survival in PBS-treated cells. When treated with gemcitabine, although overall colony formation decreased and apoptosis increased in all groups, CUL2 overexpression cells showed higher colony formation and lower apoptotic rate compared to control cells. These effects of CUL2 overexpression were abolished by either ML385 or Erastin treatment, while Ferrostatin-1 showed similar effects as CUL2 overexpression (Fig. 7B, C). These results indicate that CUL2-NRF2 axis promotes cell survival under normal conditions, while mediates gemcitabine resistance through ferroptosis regulation under drug treatment.

CUL2 promotes tumor growth and gemcitabine resistance through NRF2-mediated ferroptosis regulation in vivo

To investigate the role of CUL2 in PC growth and chemoresistance in vivo, we established xenograft models using PanC-1 and AsPC-1 cells with CUL2 overexpression. In PBS-treated groups, CUL2 overexpression significantly increased tumor volume and weight compared to the Vector group (Fig. 8A-C). Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed that CUL2 overexpression promoted Ki-67 expression under both PBS and gemcitabine conditions, although gemcitabine treatment led to an overall decrease in Ki-67 levels. Similarly, GPX4 expression was elevated by CUL2 overexpression in both PBS and gemcitabine groups, while gemcitabine treatment caused a general reduction in GPX4 levels. CUL2 overexpression significantly promoted NRF2 expression, and this upregulation remained unchanged regardless of gemcitabine treatment (Fig. 8D-F). To further investigate the involvement of ferroptosis regulation, we measured iron content, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant levels in tumor tissues. In PBS-treated groups, CUL2 overexpression decreased iron accumulation and MDA levels while maintaining higher GSH levels compared to Vector controls. Under gemcitabine treatment, Vector group tumors showed significantly increased iron content and MDA levels, along with depleted GSH levels. However, CUL2 overexpression effectively counteracted these changes, maintaining relatively lower levels of iron and MDA, while preserving GSH levels (Fig. 8G). These findings demonstrate that CUL2 promotes tumor growth and gemcitabine resistance in vivo by enhancing NRF2-mediated antioxidant response and suppressing ferroptosis.

Discussion

PC remains one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide, characterized by its aggressive nature and poor response to conventional chemotherapy. In this study, we reveal a previously unrecognized role of CUL2 in PC progression and chemoresistance. Our findings demonstrate that CUL2 is significantly upregulated in PC tissues and correlates with poor clinical outcomes. Through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experiments, we established that CUL2 functions as an oncogenic factor by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and EMT in PC cells. More importantly, we uncovered a novel molecular mechanism whereby CUL2 regulates the KEAP1-NRF2 axis through direct protein-protein interaction. Specifically, CUL2 competes with NRF2 for KEAP1 binding, thereby preventing KEAP1-mediated NRF2 degradation and promoting NRF2 nuclear translocation. This stabilization of NRF2 by CUL2 leads to enhanced antioxidant response and suppressed ferroptosis. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the CUL2-NRF2 axis plays a crucial role in gemcitabine resistance. Our findings not only establish CUL2 as a potential prognostic biomarker for PC but also provide mechanistic insights into how CUL2 contributes to chemoresistance through regulation of the KEAP1-NRF2 pathway and ferroptosis.

The Cullin family proteins serve as scaffold components of multisubunit E3 ubiquitin ligases, playing crucial roles in protein degradation and various cellular processes [36]. These proteins have been extensively studied in cancer development, with different family members showing distinct functions. For instance, CUL3 has been shown to promote tumor progression through multiple mechanisms, including the inhibition of autophagy via BECN1 degradation and regulation of the NRF2 pathway [37, 38]. Similarly, CUL5 has been implicated in cancer development through its role in protein degradation, as demonstrated in colorectal cancer where CUL5 neddylation mediates NOXA degradation [39]. Despite the well-established roles of many Cullin family members, the function of CUL2 in cancer, particularly in PC, remains largely unexplored. Previous studies have primarily positioned CUL2 as a downstream effector in cancer progression. For instance, in cervical carcinogenesis, CUL2 was identified as a target of miR-154-5p in regulating the HPV16 E7-pRb pathway [40]. Similarly, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, CircSTX6 was found to promote cancer progression partially through its interaction with CUL2 [11]. However, these studies did not fully elucidate the intrinsic function and regulatory mechanism of CUL2 in cancer development. In this study, we demonstrate that CUL2 functions as a key upstream regulator in PC progression. We identified that CUL2 significantly promotes cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in PC cells. Moreover, CUL2 was found to regulate EMT, a crucial process in cancer metastasis. These findings provide new insights into CUL2’s function beyond its traditional role as a scaffold protein in E3 ligase complexes.

We also found a high expression of CUL2 in AsPC-1 and PanC-1 cells. A lower expression of CUL2 was discovered in CAPAN2 and CFPAC1 cells but this was still higher than in non-cancerous pancreatic cells. AsPC-1 cells lack the KRAS mutations normally associated with PC but the reason why this cell line should overexpress CUL2 the most is unclear. There is some evidence that the KEAP1–NRF2 pathway may interact with KRAS to promote PC and drug resistance [41] but whether CUL2 influences KRAS through the same pathway requires further investigation.

To investigate the mechanism by which CUL2 regulates NRF2, we examined protein degradation and ubiquitination using MG132 and co-IP assays. The proteasome inhibitor MG132 increased NRF2 levels regardless of CUL2 status, while CUL2 overexpression decreased Ub-NRF2 levels and increased total NRF2 protein to levels comparable with KEAP1 knockdown. Co-IP assays revealed a direct interaction between CUL2 and KEAP1, suggesting a competitive binding mechanism. This finding aligns with previous studies where proteins such as ATDC in PC [42] and FAM129B in breast and colon cancer [43] were shown to activate NRF2 through competitive binding with KEAP1, leading to reduced ROS and increased chemoresistance.

In addition to its role in promoting PC progression, our study revealed a novel regulatory mechanism of CUL2 in oxidative stress and chemoresistance. Previous studies have shown that antioxidant mechanisms can contribute to both cell survival and chemotherapy resistance. For instance, GSH-generating enzymes induced by NRF2 nuclear translocation not only protect cells against ROS but also prevent gemcitabine-mediated apoptosis in PC [34, 44]. The NRF2/GSH antioxidant system has been implicated in drug resistance across various cancer types [45], as GSH acts as a crucial reducing agent against oxidative stress [46]. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis suppression, which is regulated by NRF2 through iron metabolism and GSH synthesis, contributes significantly to therapy resistance [47]. Our findings demonstrate that CUL2-mediated NRF2 activation not only enhances the antioxidant defense system but also suppresses ferroptosis, thereby promoting chemoresistance in PC cells.

Although our study can be substantiated by similar results in the literature some limitations need to be addressed. First, there was a limited number of patients in our study from one institute. Further studies are needed to confirm our results using a larger cohort from a more diverse population. Second, we observed an interesting discrepancy between our in vitro findings and TCGA-PAAD data analysis. Although CUL2 manipulation did not affect NRF2 mRNA levels in PC cells, TCGA-PAAD dataset showed a strong positive correlation between CUL2 and NRF2 mRNA expression. This suggests the existence of additional regulatory mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment that coordinate CUL2 and NRF2 transcription. Future studies should focus on identifying these potential common upstream regulators or feedback loops that might explain this clinical observation. Understanding these mechanisms could provide new insights into the regulation of the NRF2 pathway in PC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study reveals novel insights into the role of CUL2 in PC progression and ferroptosis resistance. We demonstrate that CUL2 functions as a key upstream regulator promoting PC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT. More importantly, we uncovered a previously unknown mechanism whereby CUL2 modulates the KEAP1-NRF2 axis through competitive binding with KEAP1, leading to increased NRF2 nuclear translocation and activation of downstream antioxidant pathways. Through activation of the NRF2/GSH antioxidant system, CUL2 creates a protective environment that enhances cancer cell survival and suppresses ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent programmed cell death. This CUL2-mediated ferroptosis resistance not only promotes tumor progression but also contributes to therapeutic resistance, including resistance to ferroptosis-inducing agents and chemotherapeutics such as gemcitabine. Our findings demonstrate how CUL2 orchestrates both antioxidant defense and ferroptosis regulation, expanding its function beyond the traditional role as a scaffold protein in E3 ligase complexes. Targeting the CUL2-KEAP1-NRF2 axis to enhance ferroptosis sensitivity might represent a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome treatment resistance in PC.
