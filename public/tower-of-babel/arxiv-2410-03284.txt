uniINF: Best-of-Both-Worlds Algorithm for Parameter-Free Heavy-Tailed MABs
Yu Chen, Jiatai Huang, Yan Dai, Longbo Huang

Abstract
In this paper, we present a novel algorithm, uniINF , for the Heavy-Tailed Multi-Armed Bandits (HTMAB) problem, demonstrating robustness and adaptability in both stochastic and adversarial environments. Unlike the stochastic MAB setting where loss distributions are stationary with time, our study extends to the adversarial setup, where losses are generated from heavy-tailed distributions that depend on both arms and time. Our novel algorithm uniINF enjoys the so-called Best-of-Both-Worlds (BoBW) property, performing optimally in both stochastic and adversarial environments without knowing the exact environment type. Moreover, our algorithm also possesses a Parameter-Free feature, i.e. , it operates without the need of knowing the heavy-tail parameters ( σ , α ) (\sigma,\alpha) a-priori. To be precise, uniINF ensures nearly-optimal regret in both stochastic and adversarial environments, matching the corresponding lower bounds when ( σ , α ) (\sigma,\alpha) is known (up to logarithmic factors). To our knowledge, uniINF is the first parameter-free algorithm to achieve the BoBW property for the heavy-tailed MAB problem. Technically, we develop innovative techniques to achieve BoBW guarantees for Parameter-Free HTMABs, including a refined analysis for the dynamics of log-barrier, an auto-balancing learning rate scheduling scheme, an adaptive skipping-clipping loss tuning technique, and a stopping-time analysis for logarithmic regret.

Introduction

Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB) problem serves as a solid theoretical formulation for addressing the exploration-exploitation trade-off inherent in online learning. Existing research in this area often assumes sub-Gaussian loss (or reward) distributions
(
Lattimore & Szepesvári, 2020
)
or even bounded ones
(
Auer et al., 2002
)
. However, recent empirical evidences revealed that
Heavy-Tailed
(HT) distributions appear frequently in realistic tasks such as network routing
(
Liebeherr et al., 2012
)
, algorithm portfolio selection
(
Gagliolo & Schmidhuber, 2011
)
, and online deep learning
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
. Such observations underscore the importance of developing MAB solutions that are robust to heavy-tailed distributions.

In this paper, we consider the Heavy-Tailed Multi-Armed Bandits (HTMAB) proposed by
Bubeck et al. (2013)
. In this scenario, the loss distributions associated with each arm do not allow bounded variances but instead have their
\alpha
-th moment bounded by some constant
\sigma^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\in(1,2]
and
\sigma\geq 0
are predetermined constants. Mathematically, we assume
\mathbb{E}[\lvert\ell_{i}\rvert^{\alpha}]\leq\sigma^{\alpha}
for every arm
i
. Although numerous existing HTMAB algorithms operated under the assumption that the parameters
\sigma
and
\alpha
are known
(
Bubeck et al., 2013
;
Yu et al., 2018
;
Wei & Srivastava, 2020
)
, real-world applications often present limited knowledge of the true environmental parameters. Thus we propose to explore the scenario where the algorithm lacks any prior information about the parameters. This setup, a variant of classical HTMABs, is referred to as the
Parameter-Free
HTMAB problem.

In addition to whether
\sigma
and
\alpha
are known, there is yet another separation that distinguishes our setup from the classical one.
In bandit learning literature, there are typically two types of environments, stochastic ones and adversarial ones.
In the former type, the loss distributions are always stationary,
i.e.
, they depend solely on the arm and not on time. Conversely, in adversarial environments, the losses can change arbitrarily, as-if manipulated by an adversary aiming to fail our algorithm.

When the environments can be potentially adversarial,
a desirable property for bandit algorithms is called
Best-of-Both-Worlds
(BoBW), as proposed by
Bubeck & Slivkins (2012)
. A BoBW algorithm behaves well in both stochastic and adversarial environments without knowing whether stochasticity is satisfied. More specifically, while ensuring near-optimal regret in adversarial environments, the algorithmic performance in a stochastic environment should automatically be boosted, ideally matching the optimal performance of those algorithms specially crafted for stochastic environments.

In practical applications of machine learning and decision-making, acquiring prior knowledge about the environment is often a considerable challenge
(
Talaei Khoei & Kaabouch, 2023
)
. This is not only regarding the distribution properties of rewards or losses, which may not conform to idealized assumptions such as sub-Gaussian or bounded behaviors, but also about the stochastic or adversarial settings the agent palying in. Such environments necessitate robust solutions that can adapt without prior distributional knowledge. Therefore, the development of a BoBW algorithm that operates effectively without this prior information – termed a parameter-free HTMAB algorithm – is not just a theoretical interest but a practical necessity.

Various previous work has made strides in enhancing the robustness and applicability of HTMAB algorithms. For instance,
Huang et al. (2022)
pioneered the development of the first BoBW algorithm for the HTMAB problem, albeit requiring prior knowledge of the heavy-tail parameters
(\alpha,\sigma)
to achieve near-optimal regret guarantees.
Genalti et al. (2024)
proposed an Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) based parameter-free HTMAB algorithm, specifically designed for stochastic environments. Both algorithms’ regret adheres to the instance-dependent and instance-independent lower bounds established by
Bubeck et al. (2013)
.
Though excited progress has been made, the following important question still stays open, which further eliminates the need of prior knowledge about the environment:

Can we design a BoBW algorithm for the Parameter-Free HTMAB problem?

Addressing parameter-free HTMABs in both adversarial and stochastic environments presents notable difficulties:

i)
although UCB-type algorithms well estimate the underlying loss distribution and provide both optimal instance-dependent and independent regret guarantees, they are incapable of adversarial environments where loss distributions are time-dependent;

ii)
heavy-tailed losses can be potentially very negative, which makes many famous algorithmic frameworks, including Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL), Online Mirror Descent (OMD), or Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader (FTPL) fall short unless meticulously designed; and

iii)
while it was shown that FTRL with
\beta
-Tsallis entropy regularizer can enjoy best-of-both-worlds guarantees, attaining optimal regret requires an exact match between
\beta
and
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
— which is impossible without knowing
\alpha
in advance.

Our contribution.

In this paper, we answer this open question affirmatively by designing a single algorithm
uniINF
that enjoys both Parameter-Free and BoBW properties — that is, it
i)
does not require any prior knowledge of the environment,
e.g.
,
\alpha
or
\sigma
, and
ii)
its performance when deployed in an adversarial environment nearly matches the universal instance-independent lower bound given by
Bubeck et al. (2013)
, and it attains the instance-dependent lower bound in stochastic environments as well.

Related Work

Due to space limitations, we defer the detailed comparison with the related works into
Appendix

A
and only discuss a few most related ones here.

Heavy-Tailed Multi-Armed Bandits.
HTMABs were introduced by
Bubeck et al. (2013)
, who gave both instance-dependent and independent lower and upper bounds under stochastic assumptions. Various efforts have been devoted in this area since then. To exemplify,

Wei & Srivastava (2020)
improve the instance-independent upper bound;

Yu et al. (2018)
developed a pure exploration algorithm for HTMABs;

Medina & Yang (2016)
;
Kang & Kim (2023)
;
Xue et al. (2024)
considered the linear HTMAB problem; and
Dorn et al. (2024)
investigated the symmetric reward distribution.

Best-of-Both-Worlds Algorithms.

Bubeck & Slivkins (2012)
pioneered the study of BoBW bandit algorithms, and was followed by various improvements including
EXP3
-based approaches
(
Seldin & Slivkins, 2014
)
, FTPL-based approaches
(
Honda et al., 2023
)
, and FTRL-based approaches
(
Wei & Luo, 2018
;
Zimmert & Seldin, 2019
)
. When the loss distribution can be heavy-tailed,
Huang et al. (2022)
achieves best-of-both-worlds property under the known-
(\alpha,\sigma)
assumption.

Parameter-Free HTMABs.
Another line of HTMABs’ research aimed at getting rid of the prior knowledge of
\alpha
or
\sigma
, which we call Parameter-Free HTMABs. Along this line,
Kagrecha et al. (2019)
presented the GSR method to identify the optimal arm in HTMAB without any prior knowledge. In terms of regret minimization,
Lee et al. (2020b)
and
Lee & Lim (2022)
considered the case when
\sigma
is unknown.
Genalti et al. (2024)
were the first to achieve the parameter-free property while maintaining near-optimal regret. However, all these algorithms fail in adversarial environments.

We use

\operatorname{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}}
to additionally suppress all logarithmic factors.
We usually use bold letters

to denote a vector, while

denotes an entry of the vector. Unless mentioned explicitly,
\log(x)
denotes the natural logarithm of

. Throughout the text, we will use

Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB) is an interactive game between a player and an environment that lasts for a finite number of

T>0
rounds.
In each round
t\in[T]
, the player can choose an action from
K>0
arms, denoted by
i_{t}\in[K]
.
Meanwhile, a
K
-dimensional loss vector
\bm{\ell}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is generated by the environment from distribution
\bm{\nu}_{t}
, simultaneously without observing
i_{t}
.
The player then suffers a loss of
\ell_{t,i_{t}}
and observe this loss (but not the whole loss vector
\bm{\ell}_{t}
).
The player’s objective is to minimize the expected total loss, or equivalently, minimize the following (pseudo-)regret:

\mathcal{R}_{T}:=\max_{i\in[K]}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,i_{t}}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,i}\right],

(1)

where the expectation is taken
w.r.t.
the randomness when the player decides the action
i_{t}
and the environment generates the loss
\bm{\ell}_{t}
. We use
i^{\ast}=\mathrm{argmin}_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t,i}\right]
to denote the optimal arm.

In Heavy-Tailed MABs (HTMAB), for every
t\in[T]
and
i\in[K]
, the loss is independently sampled from some heavy-tailed distribution
\nu_{t,i}
in the sense that
\mathbb{E}_{\ell\sim\nu_{t,i}}[\lvert\ell\rvert^{\alpha}]\leq\sigma^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\in(1,2]
and
\sigma\geq 0
are some pre-determined but
unknown
constants.
A Best-of-Both-Worlds (BoBW) algorithm is one that behaves well in both stochastic and adversarial environments
(
Bubeck & Slivkins, 2012
)
, where stochastic environments are those with time-homogeneous
\{\bm{\nu}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
(
i.e.
,
\nu_{t,i}=\nu_{1,i}
for every
t\in[T]
and
i\in[K]
) and adversarial environments are those where
\nu_{t,i}
’s can depend on both
t
and
i
. However, we do not allow the loss distributions to depend on the player’s previous actions.

Assumption 2

(Unique Best Arm)
.

In stochastic setups, if we denote the mean of distribution

\mu_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\ell\sim\nu_{1,i}}[\ell]

4.1
Refined Log-Barrier Analysis

We adopt the

\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}):=-S_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log x_{i}

While log-barrier regularizers were commonly used in the literature for data-adaptive bounds such as small-loss bounds
(
Foster et al., 2016
)

(
Wei & Luo, 2018
)
, and second-order bounds

Precisely, it is known that log-barrier applied to a loss sequence

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{y},\bm{c}_{t}\rangle\lesssim\sum_{t=1}^{T}((S_{t+1}-S_{t})K\log T+\textsc{Div}_{t})

\textsc{Div}_{t}\leq S_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}x_{t,i}^{2}}c_{t,i}^{2}

\textsc{Div}_{t}\leq S_{t}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}x_{t,i}}c_{t,i}^{2}

5.1
, we will decompose the regret into skipping error
\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t,i_{t}}-\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{{\text{skip}}})\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]
and main regret which is roughly
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{y},\bm{\ell}^{{\text{skip}}}_{t}\rangle
.
The skipping errors already include the indicator
\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]
, so the exclusion of
i^{\ast}
is automatic. However, for the main regret, we must manually introduce some
(1-x_{t,i})
to exclude
i^{\ast}
— which means the previous bounds mentioned above do not apply, while our novel
Equation

5
is instead helpful.

4.2
Auto-Balancing Learning Rate Scheduling Scheme

We design the our auto-balancing learning rate
S_{t}
in
Equation

4
. The idea is to balance a Bregman divergence term
\textsc{Div}_{t}
and a
\Psi
-shifting term
\textsc{Shift}_{t}
that arise in our regret analysis (roughly corresponding to the terms on the RHS of
Equation

5
). They allow the following upper bounds as we will include as
Lemmas

5
and
9
:

\underbrace{\textsc{Div}_{t}\leq\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\left(S_{t}^{-1}\left(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{clip}}\right)^{2}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\right)}_{\text{Bregman Divergence}},\quad\underbrace{\textsc{Shift}_{t}\leq{\mathcal{O}}\left((S_{t+1}-S_{t})\cdot K\log T\right)}_{\text{$\Psi$-Shifting}}.

(6)

Thus, to make
\textsc{Div}_{t}
roughly the same as
\textsc{Shift}_{t}
, it suffices to ensure
(S_{t+1}-S_{t})S_{t}\approx(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{clip}})^{2}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\cdot(K\log T)^{-1}
. Our definition of
S_{t}
in
Equation

4
follows since
(S_{t+1}-S_{t})S_{t}\approx S_{t+1}^{2}-S_{t}^{2}
.

4.3
Adaptive Skipping-Clipping Loss Tuning Technique

For heavy-tailed losses, a unique challenge is that
\mathbb{E}[\ell^{2}_{t,i_{t}}]
, the squared incurred loss appearing in the Bregman divergence term (as shown in
Equation

6
), does not allow a straightforward upper bound as we only have
\mathbb{E}[\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}\rvert^{\alpha}]\leq\sigma^{\alpha}
. A previous workaround is deploying a
skipping
technique that replaces large loss with
0

(
Huang et al., 2022
)
. This technique forbids a sudden increase in the divergence term and thus eases the balance between
\textsc{Div}_{t}
and
\textsc{Shift}_{t}
.
However, this skipping is not a free lunch: if we skipped too much, the increase of
S_{t}
will be slow, which makes the skipping threshold
C_{t,i}
grow slowly as well — therefore, we end up skipping even more and incurring tremendous
skipping error
! This eventually stops us from establishing instance-dependent guarantees in stochastic setups.

To address this limitation, we develop an
adaptive skipping-clipping
technique. The idea is to ensure that every loss will influence the learning process — imagine that the green
\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{clip}}
in
Equation

4
is replaced by
\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}}
, then
S_{t}
will not change if the current
\ell_{t,i_{t}}
is large, which risks the algorithm from doing nothing if happening repeatedly.
Instead, our clipping technique induces an adequate reaction upon observing a large loss, which both prevents the learning rate
S_{t}
from having a drastic change and ensures the growth of
S_{t}
is not too slow. Specifically, from
Equation

4
, if we skip the loss
\ell_{t,i_{t}}
, we must have
S_{t+1}=\Theta(1+(K\log T)^{-1})S_{t}
. This is crucial for our stopping-time analysis in
Section

5.2
.

While clipping is used to tune
S_{t}
, we use the skipped loss
\bm{\ell}^{\text{skip}}_{t}
(more specifically, its importance-weighted version
\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}
) to decide the action
\bm{x}_{t}
in the FTRL update
Equation

2
.
Utilizing the truncated non-negativity assumption (
Assumption

1
), we can exclude one arm when controlling the skipping error, as already seen in
Equation

5
. We shall present more details in
Section

5.4
.

5
Main Results

The main guarantee of our
uniINF
(
Algorithm

1
) is presented in
Theorem

3
below, which states that
uniINF
achieves both optimal minimax regret for adversarial cases (up to logarithmic factors) and near-optimal instance-dependent regret for stochastic cases.

Theorem 3

(Main Guarantee)
.

Under the adversarial environments,
uniINF
(
Algorithm

1
) achieves

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}=\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\right).

Moreover, for the stochastic environments,
uniINF
(
Algorithm

1
) guarantees

{\mathcal{R}}_{T}={\mathcal{O}}\left(K\left(\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\log T\cdot\log\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\Delta_{\min}}\right).

The formal proof of this theorem is provided in
Appendix

F
. As shown in
Table

1
, our
uniINF
automatically achieves nearly optimal instance-dependent and instance-independent regret guarantees in stochastic and adversarial environments, respectively. Specifically, under stochastic settings,
uniINF
achieves the regret upper bound
{\mathcal{O}}\left(K\left(\nicefrac{{\sigma^{\alpha}}}{{\Delta_{\min}}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha-1}}}\log T\cdot\log\nicefrac{{\sigma^{\alpha}}}{{\Delta_{\min}}}\right)
.
Compared to the instance-dependent lower bound
\Omega\left(\sum_{i\neq i^{\ast}}(\nicefrac{{\sigma^{\alpha}}}{{\Delta_{i}}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha-1}}}\log T\right)
given by
Bubeck et al. (2013)
, our result matches the lower bound up to logarithmic factors
\log\nicefrac{{\sigma^{\alpha}}}{{\Delta_{\min}}}
which is independent of
T
when all
\Delta_{i}
’s are similar to the dominant sub-optimal gap
\Delta_{\min}
.
For adversarial environments, our algorithm achieves an
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\right)
regret, which matches the instance-independent lower bound
\Omega\left(\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\right)
given in
Bubeck et al. (2013)
up to logarithmic terms. Therefore, the regret guarantees of
uniINF
are nearly-optimal in both stochastic and adversarial environments .

5.1
Regret Decomposition

In
Sections

5.1
,
5.2
,
5.3
, and
5.4
, we sketch the proof of our BoBW result in
Theorem

3
.
To begin with, we decompose the regret
\mathcal{R}_{T}
into a few terms and handle each of them separately. Denoting
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
as the one-hot vector on the optimal action
i^{\ast}\in[K]
,
i.e.
,
y_{i}:=\mathbbm{1}[i=i^{\ast}]
, we know from
Equation

1
that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}\rangle\right].

As the log-barrier regularizer
\Psi_{t}
is prohibitively large when close to the boundary of
\Delta^{[K]}
, we instead consider the adjusted benchmark
\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}
defined as
\widetilde{y}_{i}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{T}&i\neq i^{\ast}\\
1-\frac{K-1}{T}&i=i^{\ast}\end{cases}
and rewrite
{\mathcal{R}}_{T}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\widetilde{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]}_{\textsc{I. Benchmark Calibration Error}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]}_{\textsc{II. Main Regret}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}-{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]}_{\textsc{III. Skipping Error}}.

(7)

We now go over each term one by one.

Term I. Benchmark Calibration Error.

As in a typical log-barrier analysis
(
Wei & Luo, 2018
;
Ito, 2021b
)
, the Benchmark Calibration Error is not the dominant term. This is because

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\widetilde{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{K-1}{T}\mathbb{E}[|\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}|]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{K-1}{T}\mathbb{E}[|\ell_{t,i_{t}}|]\leq\sigma K,

which is independent from
T
. Therefore, the key is analyzing the other two terms.

Term II. Main Regret.

By FTRL regret decomposition (see
Lemma

29
in the appendix; it is an extension of the classical FTRL bounds
(
Lattimore & Szepesvári, 2020
, Theorem 28.5)
), we have

\displaystyle\quad\textsc{Main Regret}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{y}},\widetilde{{\bm{\ell}}}_{t}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[D_{\Psi_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t})]+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Psi_{t+1}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})-\Psi_{t}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})\right)-\left(\Psi_{t+1}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})-\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\right)\right],

where
D_{\Psi_{t}}(\bm{y},\bm{x})=\Psi_{t}(\bm{y})-\Psi_{t}(\bm{x})-\langle\nabla\Psi_{t}(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle
is the Bregman divergence induced by the
t
-th regularizer
\Psi_{t}
, and
{\bm{z}}_{t}
denotes the posterior optimal estimation in episode
t
, namely

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}:=\argmin_{{\bm{z}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\langle\widetilde{{\bm{\ell}}}_{s},{\bm{z}}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{z}})\right).

(8)

For simplicity, we use the abbreviation
\textsc{Div}_{t}:=D_{\Psi_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t})
for the Bregman divergence between
{\bm{x}}_{t}
and
{\bm{z}}_{t}
under regularizer
\Psi_{t}
, and let
\textsc{Shift}_{t}:=\left[\left(\Psi_{t+1}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})-\Psi_{t}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})\right)-\left(\Psi_{t+1}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})-\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\right)\right]
be the
\Psi
-shifting term. Then, we can reduce the analysis of main regret to bounding the sum of Bregman divergence term
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}]
and
\Psi
-shifting term
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Shift}_{t}]
.

Term III. Skipping Error.

To control the skipping error, we define
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}:=\ell_{t,i_{t}}-\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}}=\ell_{t,i_{t}}\mathbbm{1}[\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}\rvert\geq C_{t,i_{t}}]
as the loss incurred by the skipping operation at episode
t
. Then we have

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}-{\bm{\ell}}_{t}^{\text{skip}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[K]}(x_{t,i}-y_{i})\cdot(\ell_{t,i}-\ell_{t,i}^{\text{skip}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\neq i^{\ast}}x_{t,i}\cdot\left|\ell_{t,i}-\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i}\right|+(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)\cdot\left(\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]+(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)\cdot\left(\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}}\right).

Notice that the factor
(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)
in the second term is negative and
{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}
-measurable. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}[\lvert\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}\rvert\geq C_{t,i^{\ast}}]\cdot\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}\right]\geq 0,

where the inequality is due to the truncated non-negative assumption (
Assumption

1
) of the optimal arm
i^{\ast}
. Therefore, we have
\mathbb{E}[(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)\cdot(\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 0
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}-{\bm{\ell}}_{t}^{\text{skip}}\rangle\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\lvert\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}],

(9)

which gives an approach to control the skipping error by the sum of skipping losses
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}
’s where we pick a sub-optimal arm
i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}
. Formally, we give the following inequality:

\displaystyle\textsc{Skipping Error}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right].

To summarize, the regret
{\mathcal{R}}_{T}
decomposes into the sum of Bregman divergence terms
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}]
,
\Psi
-shifting terms
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Shift}_{t}]
, and sub-optimal skipping losses
\mathbb{E}[|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]]
, namely

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textsc{Div}_{t}\right]}_{\textsc{Bregman Divergence Terms}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\textsc{Shift}_{t}\right]}_{\textsc{$\Psi$-Shifting Terms}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]}_{\textsc{Sub-Optimal Skipping Losses}}+\sigma K.

(10)

In
Sections

5.2
,
5.3
, and
5.4
, we analyze these three key items and introduce our novel analytical techniques for both adversarial and stochastic cases. Specifically, we bound the Bregman divergence terms in
Section

5.2
(all formal proofs in
Appendix

C
), the
\Psi
-shifting terms in
Section

5.3
(all formal proofs in
Appendix

D
), and the skipping error terms in
Section

5.4
(all formal proofs in
Appendix

E
). Afterwards, to get our
Theorem

3
, putting
Theorems

7
,
10
, and
13
together gives the adversarial guarantee, while the stochastic guarantee follows from a combination of
Theorems

8
,
11
, and
14
.

5.2
Analyzing Bregman Divergence Terms

From the log-barrier regularizer defined in
Equation

2
, we can explicitly write out
\textsc{Div}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\textsc{Div}_{t}

\displaystyle=D_{\Psi_{t}}(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{z}_{t})=S_{t}\sum_{i\in[K]}\left(-\log\frac{x_{t,i}}{z_{t,i}}+\frac{x_{t,i}}{z_{t,i}}-1\right).

To simplify notations, we define
w_{t,i}:=\frac{x_{t,i}}{z_{t,i}}-1
, which gives
\textsc{Div}_{t}=S_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{K}(w_{t,i}-\log(w_{t,i}+1))
. Therefore, one natural idea is to utilize the inequality
x-\log(x+1)\leq x^{2}
for
x\in[-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
. To do so, we need to ensure
-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\leq w_{t,i}\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
,
i.e.
,
{\bm{z}}_{t}
is multiplicatively close to
{\bm{x}}_{t}
. If the losses are bounded, this property is achieved in previously papers
(
Lee et al., 2020a
;
Jin & Luo, 2020
)
. However, it is non-trivial for heavy-tailed (thus unbounded) losses. By refined analysis on the calculation of
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{z}}
by KKT conditions, we develop novel technical tools to depict
{\bm{z}}_{t}
and provide the following important lemma in HTMABs. We refer readers to the proof of
Lemma

15
for more technical details.

Lemma 4

(
{\bm{z}}_{t}
is Multiplicatively Close to
{\bm{x}}_{t}
)
.

\frac{1}{2}x_{t,i}\leq z_{t,i}\leq 2x_{t,i}
for every
t\in[T]
and
i\in[K]
.

Lemma

4
implies
w_{t,i}\in[-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]
. Hence
\textsc{Div}_{t}=S_{t}\sum_{t=1}^{K}(w_{t,i}-\log(w_{t,i}+1))\leq S_{t}\sum_{t=1}^{K}w_{t,i}^{2}
.
Conditioning on the natural filtration
{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}
,
w_{t,i}
is fully determined by feedback
\widetilde{{\bm{\ell}}}_{t}
in episode
t
, which allows us to give a precise depiction of
w_{t,i}
via calculating the partial derivative
{\partial w_{t,i}}/{\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t,j}}
and invoking the intermediate value theorem. The detailed procedure is included as
Lemma

16
in the appendix, which further results in the following lemma on the Bregman divergence term
\textsc{Div}_{t}
.

Lemma 5

(Upper Bound of
\textsc{Div}_{t}
)
.

For every
t\in[T]
, we can bound
\textsc{Div}_{t}
as

\textsc{Div}_{t}={\mathcal{O}}\left(S_{t}^{-1}(\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\right),\quad\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\textsc{Div}_{\tau}={\mathcal{O}}\left(S_{t+1}\cdot K\log T\right).

Compared to previous bounds on
\textsc{Div}_{t}
,
Lemma

5
contains an
(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}
that is crucial for our instance-dependent bound and serves as a main technical contribution, as we sketched in
Section

4.1
.

Adversarial Cases.

By definition of
S_{t}
in
Equation

4
, we can bound
S_{T+1}
as in the following lemma.

Lemma 6

(Upper Bound for
S_{T+1}
)
.

The expectation of
S_{T+1}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{T+1}]\leq 2\cdot\sigma(K\log T)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}.

(11)

Combining this upper-bound on
\mathbb{E}[S_{T+1}]
with
Lemma

5
, we can control the expected sum of Bregman divergence terms
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textsc{Div}_{t}]
in adversarial environments, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 7

(Adversarial Bounds for Bregman Divergence Terms)
.

In adversarial environments, the sum of Bregman divergence terms can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textsc{Div}_{t}\right]=\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\right).

Stochastic Cases.

For
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(\log T)
bounds, we opt for the first statement of
Lemma

5
. By definition of
\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}}
, we immediately have
|\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}}|\leq C_{t,i_{t}}=\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(S_{t}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{-1})
. Further using
\mathbb{E}[\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}\rvert^{\alpha}]\leq\sigma^{\alpha}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]={\mathcal{O}}(S_{t}^{1-\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}(1-x_{t,i^{\ast}}))
(formalized as
Lemma

20
in the appendix).

We can now perform a
stopping-time argument
for the sum of Bregman divergence terms in stochastic cases. Briefly, we pick a fixed constant
M>0
. The expected sum of
\textsc{Div}_{t}
’s on those
\{t\mid S_{t}\geq M\}
is then within
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(M^{1-\alpha}\cdot T)
according to
Lemma

5
. On the other hand, we claim that the sum of
\textsc{Div}_{t}
’s on those
\{t\mid S_{t}<M\}
can also be well controlled because
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]={\mathcal{O}}(\mathbb{E}[S_{t}^{1-\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}(1-x_{t,i^{\ast}})\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}])
.
The detailed analysis is presented in
Section

C.6
, and we summarize it as follows. Therefore, the sum of Bregman divergences in stochastic environments is also well-controlled.

Theorem 8

(Stochastic Bounds for Bregman Divergence Terms)
.

In stochastic settings, the sum of Bregman divergence terms can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textsc{Div}_{t}\right]={\mathcal{O}}\left(K\sigma^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Delta_{\min}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\log T+\frac{{\mathcal{R}}_{T}}{4}\right).

5.3
Analyzing
\Psi
-Shifting Terms

Since our choice of
\{S_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
is non-decreasing, the
\Psi
-shifting term
\Psi_{t+1}({\bm{x}})-\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}})\geq 0
trivially holds for any
{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{[K]}
. We get the following lemma by algebraic manipulations.

Lemma 9

(Upper Bound of
\textsc{Shift}_{t}
)
.

For every
t\geq 0
, we can bound
\textsc{Shift}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\textsc{Shift}_{t}={\mathcal{O}}\left(S_{t}^{-1}(\ell^{{\text{clip}}}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\right),\quad\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\textsc{Shift}_{\tau}={\mathcal{O}}\left(S_{t+1}\cdot K\log T\right).

Similarly,
Lemma

9
also contains a useful
(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}
term — in fact, the two bounds in
Lemma

9
are extremely similar to those in
Lemma

5
and thus allow analogous analyses. This is actually an expected phenomenon, thanks to our auto-balancing learning rates introduced in
Section

4.2
.

Adversarial Cases.
Again, we utilize the
\mathbb{E}[S_{T+1}]
bound in
Lemma

6
and get the following theorem.

Theorem 10

(Adversarial Bounds for
\Psi
-Shifting Terms)
.

In adversarial environments, the expectation of the sum of
\Psi
-shifting terms can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\textsc{Shift}_{t}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[S_{T+1}\cdot(K\log T)]=\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\right).

Stochastic Cases.

Still similar to
\textsc{Div}_{t}
’s, we condition on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}
and utilize
\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{{\text{clip}}}\rvert\leq C_{t,i_{t}}
to get
\mathbb{E}\left[\textsc{Shift}_{t}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}\right]={\mathcal{O}}(S_{t}^{1-\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}(1-x_{t,i^{\ast}}))
(formalized as
Lemma

24
in the appendix). Thus, for stochastic environments, a stopping-time argument similar to that of
\textsc{Div}_{t}
yields
Theorem

11
.

Theorem 11

(Stochastic Bounds for
\Psi
-shifting Terms)
.

In stochastic environments, the sum of
\Psi
-shifting terms can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\textsc{Shift}_{t}\right]={\mathcal{O}}\left(K\sigma^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Delta_{\min}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\log T+\frac{{\mathcal{R}}_{T}}{4}\right).

5.4
Analyzing Sub-Optimal Skipping Losses

This section controls the sub-optimal skipping losses when we pick a sub-optimal arm
i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}
,
i.e.
,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\rvert=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}-\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}\rvert\geq C_{t,i_{t}}]\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}].

For skipping errors, we need a more dedicated stopping-time analysis in both adversarial and stochastic cases.
Different from previous sections, it is now non-trivial to bound the sum of
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}
’s on those
\{t\mid S_{t}<M\}
where
M
is the stopping-time threshold. However, a key observation is that whenever we encounter a non-zero
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}
,
S_{t+1}
will be
\Omega(1)
-times larger than
S_{t}
.
Thus the number of non-zero
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}
’s before
S_{t}
reaching
M
is small.
Equipped with this observation, we derive the following lemma, whose formal proof is included in
Section

E.1
.

Lemma 12

(Stopping-Time Argument for Skipping Losses)
.

Given a stopping-time threshold
M
, the total skipping loss on those
t
’s with
i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]\leq M\left(\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{M^{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]+2\cdot\frac{\log M}{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{16K\log T}\right)}+1\right).

Equipped with this novel stopping-time analysis, it only remains to pick a proper threshold
M
for
Lemma

12
. It turns out that for adversarial and stochastic cases, we have to pick different
M
’s. Specifically, we achieve the following two theorems, whose proofs are in
Sections

E.2
and
E.3
.

Theorem 13

(Adversarial Bounds for Skipping Losses)
.

By setting adversarial stopping-time threshold
M^{\text{adv}}:=\sigma(K\log T)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\right).

Theorem 14

(Stochastic Bounds for Skipping Losses)
.

By setting stochastic stopping-time threshold
M^{\text{sto}}:=4^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\sigma^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Delta_{\min}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]={\mathcal{O}}\left(K\log T\cdot\sigma^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Delta_{\min}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)+\frac{{\mathcal{R}}_{T}}{4}\right).

6
Conclusion

This paper designs the first algorithm for Parameter-Free HTMABs that enjoys the Best-of-Both-Worlds property. Specifically, our algorithm,
uniINF
, simultaneously achieves near-optimal instance-independent and instance-dependent bounds in adversarial and stochastic environments, respectively.
uniINF
incorporates several innovative algorithmic components, such as
i)
refined log-barrier analysis,
ii)
auto-balancing learning rates, and
iii)
adaptive skipping-clipping loss tuning. Analytically, we also introduce meticulous techniques including
iv)
analyzing Bregman divergence via partial derivatives and intermediate value theorem and
v)
stopping-time analysis for logarithmic regret. We expect many of these techniques to be of independent interest. In terms of limitations,
uniINF
does suffer from some extra logarithmic factors; it’s dependency on the gaps
\{\Delta_{i}^{-1}\}_{i\neq i^{\ast}}
is also improvable when some of the gaps are much smaller than the other. We leave these for future investigation.

Reproducibility Statement

This paper is primarily theoretical in nature, with the main contributions being the development of
uniINF
, the Best-of-Both-Worlds algorithm for Parameter-Free Heavy-Tailed Multi-Armed Bandits, and the introduction of several innovative algorithmic components and analytical tools. To ensure reproducibility, we have provided clear and detailed explanations of our methods, assumptions, and the development process of the algorithm throughout the paper. Complete proofs of our claims and results are included in the appendices. We have made every effort to present our research in a transparent and comprehensive manner to enable other researchers to understand and replicate our results.

Appendix A
Additional Related Works

In this section, we present a detailed version of literature review to include more discussion and related works.

A.1
Multi-Armed Bandits

The Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB) problem
(
Thompson, 1933
;
Slivkins et al., 2019
;
Lattimore & Szepesvári, 2020
;
Bubeck et al., 2012
)
is an important theoretical framework for addressing the exploration-exploitation trade-off inherent in online learning. Various studies discovered different setups for the loss (or reward) signals. For example, most works focus on standard bounded losses where the loss of each arm belongs to a bounded range such as
[0,1]

(
Auer et al., 2002
;
Wei & Luo, 2018
;
Lee et al., 2020a
;
Jin et al., 2023
)
.
Srinivas et al. (2009)
;
Wu et al. (2015)
;
Deng et al. (2022)
assumed the losses following Gaussian or sub-Gaussian distribution. Another branch of works called scale-free MABs consider the case when the losses’ range is unknown to the agent
(
Putta & Agrawal, 2022
;
Chen & Zhang, 2023
;
Chen & Zhang, 2024
;
Hadiji & Stoltz, 2023
)
. In this paper, we consider the heavy-tailed loss signals where each arm’s loss do not allow bounded variances but instead have their
\alpha
-th moment bounded by some constant
\sigma^{\alpha}
, which is called the Heavy-Tailed Multi-Armed Bandits (HTMABs).

A.2
Heavy-Tailed MABs

HTMABs were introduced by
Bubeck et al. (2013)
, who gave both instance-dependent and instance-independent lower and upper bounds under stochastic assumptions. Various efforts have been devoted in this area since then. To exemplify,

Wei & Srivastava (2020)
removed a sub-optimal
(\log T)^{1-1/\alpha}
factor in the instance-independent upper bound;

Yu et al. (2018)
developed a pure exploration algorithm for HTMABs;

Medina & Yang (2016)
,
Kang & Kim (2023)
, and
Xue et al. (2024)
considered the linear HTMAB problem; and
Dorn et al. (2024)
specifically investigated the case where the heavy-tailed reward distributions are presumed to be symmetric. Nevertheless, all these works focused on stochastic environments and required the prior knowledge of heavy-tail parameters
(\alpha,\sigma)
. In contrast, this paper focuses on both-of-both-worlds algorithms in parameter-free HTMABs, which means
i)
the loss distributions can possibly be non-stationary, and
ii)
the true heavy-tail parameters
(\alpha,\sigma)
remain unknown.

A.3
Best-of-Both-Worlds Algorithms

Bubeck & Slivkins (2012)
pioneered the study of Best-of-Both-Worlds bandit algorithms, and was followed by various improvements including
EXP3
-based approaches
(
Seldin & Slivkins, 2014
;
Seldin & Lugosi, 2017
)
, FTPL-based approaches
(
Honda et al., 2023
;
Lee et al., 2024
)
, and FTRL-based approaches
(
Wei & Luo, 2018
;
Zimmert & Seldin, 2019
;
Jin et al., 2023
)
. Equipped with data-dependent learning rates,
Ito (2021a)
;
Ito (2021b)
;
Tsuchiya et al. (2023)
;
Ito & Takemura (2023)
;
Kong et al. (2023)
achieve Best-of-Both-Worlds algorithms in various online learning problems. When the loss distribution can be heavy-tailed,
Huang et al. (2022)
gave an algorithm
HTINF
that achieves Best-of-Both-Worlds property under the known-
(\alpha,\sigma)
assumption. Unfortunately, without access to these true parameters, their alternative algorithm
OptHTINF
failed to achieve near-optimal regret guarantees in either adversarial or stochastic environments.

A.4
Parameter-Free HTMABs

Another line of research aimed at getting rid of the prior knowledge of
\alpha
or
\sigma
, which we call Parameter-Free HTMABs. Along this line,
Kagrecha et al. (2019)
presented the GSR method to identify the optimal arm in HTMAB without any prior knowledge. In terms of regret minimization,
Lee et al. (2020b)
and
Lee & Lim (2022)
considered the case when
\sigma
is unknown.
Genalti et al. (2024)
were the first to achieve the parameter-free property while maintaining near-optimal regret. However, all these algorithms fail in adversarial environments – not to mention the best-of-both-worlds property which requires optimality in both stochastic and adversarial environments.

Lemma 15

(Restatement of

.

For every

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x_{t,i}\leq z_{t,i}\leq 2x_{t,i}.

Proof.

Positive Loss.

Below we first consider
\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}>0
, the other case
\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}<0

\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}=0

The left side
\frac{1}{2}x_{t,i}\leq z_{t,i}
:

According to the KKT conditions, given
S_{t}
and
L_{t-1}
(
L_{t}
), the goal of computing
x_{t}
(
z_{t}
) can be reduced to find a scalar multiplier
Z_{t}
(
\widetilde{Z}_{t}
), such that we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}x_{t,i}(Z_{t})=1

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}z_{t,i}(\widetilde{Z}_{t})=1

where

\displaystyle x_{t,i}(\lambda)=\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i}-\lambda},\quad z_{t,i}(\lambda)=\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t,i}-\lambda}=\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i}+\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i}-\lambda}.

(12)

Note that each
z_{t,i}(\lambda)
is an increasing function of
\lambda
on
(-\infty,L_{t,i}]
. Since
\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}>0
, it is easy to see that
z_{t,i_{t}}(Z_{t})<x_{t,i_{t}}
. Thus in order to satisfy the sum-to-one constraint, we must have
\widetilde{Z}_{t}>Z_{t}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{t}-Z_{t}<\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}
. Moreover, we have
z_{t,i_{t}}<x_{t,i_{t}}
and
z_{t,i}>x_{t,i}
for all

. Therefore, we only need to prove that

. Thanks to the monotonicity of

, it suffices to find a multiplier
\overline{Z}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}}{2},

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 1.

(14)

With the above two conditions hold, we can conclude that
\widetilde{Z}_{t}\geq\overline{Z}_{t}
,
i.e.
,
\overline{Z}_{t}
is a lower-bound for the actual multiplier
\widetilde{Z}_{t}
making
z_{t}\in\Delta

z_{t,i_{t}}=z_{t,i_{t}}(\widetilde{Z}_{t})\geq z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})\geq\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}}{2}
.

For our purpose, we will choose
\overline{Z}_{t}
as follows

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}=\begin{cases}Z_{t}&\text{if }x_{t,i_{t}}<\frac{1}{2},\\
\text{the unique solution to }z_{t,i_{t}}(\lambda)=\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}}{2}&\text{if }x_{t,i_{t}}\geq\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

We will verify
Equation

13

14

\overline{Z}_{t}

x_{t,i_{t}}<\frac{1}{2}

S_{t}=4C_{t,i_{t}}\cdot(1-x_{t,i_{t}})\geq 2\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t,i_{t}}-\overline{Z}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i_{t}}+\frac{\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}}{x_{t,i_{t}}}-Z_{t}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i_{t}}+\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,i_{t}}}-Z_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{t}}{(L_{t-1,i_{t}}-Z_{t})\cdot(1+\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,i_{t}}(L_{t-1,i_{t}}-Z_{t})})}

\displaystyle=x_{t,i_{t}}\cdot\left({1+\frac{1}{2x_{t,i_{t}}}\cdot x_{t,i_{t}}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}x_{t,i_{t}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}x_{t,i_{t}}.

Thus, we have
\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t,i_{t}}-\overline{Z}_{t}}\geq\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}}{2}
holds regardless
x_{t,i_{t}}\geq 1/2
or not.

Then, we verify the other statement
Equation

14
. When
x_{t,i_{t}}<\frac{1}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle=z_{t,i_{t}}(Z_{t})+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}z_{t,i}(Z_{t})

\displaystyle=z_{t,i_{t}}(Z_{t})+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}

\displaystyle<x_{t,i_{t}}+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}=1.

For the other case
x_{t,i_{t}}\geq\frac{1}{2}

\overline{Z}_{t}

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2S_{t}}{x_{t,i_{t}}}+L_{t-1,i_{t}}+\frac{\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}}{x_{t,i_{t}}}

x_{t,i_{t}}<x_{t,j^{*}}

\displaystyle z_{t,j^{*}}(\overline{Z}_{t})-x_{t,j^{*}}=x_{t,j^{*}}>x_{t,i_{t}}>x_{t,i_{t}}-z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t}).

(19)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})-1

\displaystyle=\left[z_{t,j^{*}}(\overline{Z}_{t})-x_{t,j^{*}}\right]+\left[z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})-x_{t,i_{t}}\right]+\sum_{i\neq i_{t},j^{*}}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})-x_{t,i}

\displaystyle>\sum_{i\neq i_{t},j^{*}}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})-x_{t,i}

\displaystyle\geq 0

hence
\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})>1
.
In all, we show that
\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})\geq 1
, and we are done.

Negative Loss.

The proof of case
\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i}<0
is very similar. In this case, we have
z_{t,i_{t}}(Z_{t})>x_{t,i_{t}}
, which shows that
\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(Z_{t})>1
. Therefore, we have
\widetilde{Z}_{t}<Z_{t}
, which implies
z_{t,i}<x_{t,i}
for
i\neq i_{t}
and
z_{t,i_{t}}>x_{t,i_{t}}
. Therefore, we only need to verify that
z_{t,i_{t}}\leq 2x_{t,i_{t}}
and
z_{t,i}\geq\frac{1}{2}x_{t,i}
for
i\neq i_{t}
. We apply similar proof process as above statement.

For the first inequality, we just need to verify that there exists a multiplier
\overline{Z}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 2x_{t,i_{t}},

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\geq 1.

(21)

We set
\overline{Z}_{t}
as the unique solution to
z_{t,i_{t}}(\lambda)=2x_{t,i_{t}}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,i_{t}}}+L_{t-1,i_{t}}+\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,i_{t}}}+\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,i_{t}}}+L_{t-1,i_{t}}+\frac{\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}}{x_{t,i_{t}}}

\displaystyle\geq Z_{t}+\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,i_{t}}}-\frac{C_{t,i_{t}}}{x_{t,i_{t}}}

\displaystyle=Z_{t}+\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,i_{t}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}

\displaystyle=Z_{t}-\frac{S_{t}(2x_{t,i_{t}}-1)}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}

If
x_{t,i_{t}}\leq 1/2
, we have
\overline{Z}_{t}\geq Z_{t}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})\geq z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}z_{t,i}(Z_{t})=2x_{t,i_{t}}+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}\geq 1.

For the other case, if
x_{t,i_{t}}>1/2
, we have for any
i\neq i_{t}

\displaystyle z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i}-Z_{t}+\frac{S_{t}(2x_{t,i_{t}}-1)}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}}

\displaystyle=x_{t,i}\cdot\left(1+x_{t,i}\cdot\frac{2x_{t,i_{t}}-1}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq x_{t,i}\cdot\left(1+\frac{2x_{t,i_{t}}-1}{4x_{t,i_{t}}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=x_{t,i}\cdot\frac{4x_{t,i_{t}}}{6x_{t,i_{t}}-1},

where the second inequality holds by
2x_{t,i_{t}}-1>0
and
x_{t,i}\leq 1-x_{t,i_{t}}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})\geq 2x_{t,i_{t}}+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}\cdot\frac{4x_{t,i_{t}}}{6x_{t,i_{t}}-1}=2x_{t,i_{t}}+\frac{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}{6x_{t,i_{t}}-1}\geq 1.

For the second inequality, we need to verify that there exists a multiplier
\overline{Z}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}x_{t,i}\quad\forall i\neq i_{t},

(22)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 1.

(23)

Let
j^{*}=\argmax_{j\neq i_{t}}x_{t,j}
. Then we set
\overline{Z}_{t}
as

\displaystyle\overline{Z}_{t}=Z_{t}-\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}.

Hence, we have for any
i\neq i_{t}
,

\displaystyle z_{t,i}\left(\overline{Z}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{t}}{(L_{t-1,i}-Z_{t})\cdot\left(1+\frac{S_{t}}{(L_{t-1,i}-Z_{t})x_{t,j^{*}}}\right)}

\displaystyle=x_{t,i}\cdot\left(1+\frac{x_{t,i}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq x_{t,i}\cdot\frac{1}{2},

where the inequality holds by
x_{t,j^{*}}>x_{t,i}
. Therefore, we have

z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})\geq\frac{1}{2}x_{t,i}.

Then we verity
Equation

23
. Since we have
\overline{Z}_{t}\leq Z_{t}
, then
z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})\leq x_{t,i}
for any
i\neq i_{t}
. Thus we only need to prove
z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})\leq x_{t,i_{t}}
. Notice that

\displaystyle z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})=\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i_{t}}-Z_{t}+\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}+\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}}\leq\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i_{t}}-Z_{t}+\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}},

where the inequality is due to
\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}=\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}/x_{t,i_{t}}\geq-C_{t,i_{t}}/x_{t,i_{t}}
and the definition of
C_{t,i_{t}}
in
Equation

3
. If we have
x_{t,i_{t}}\geq 1/2
, then

\displaystyle\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{2(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{2\sum_{j\neq i_{t}}x_{t,j}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,j^{*}}}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Therefore, we have
z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})\leq x_{t,i_{t}}
, which implies that
\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})\leq\sum_{i\in[K]}x_{t,i}=1
. For the case when
x_{t,i_{t}}<1/2
, we also have

\displaystyle\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{2x_{t,i_{t}}}.

If
x_{t,j^{*}}\leq 2x_{t,i_{t}}
, we still have
z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})\leq x_{t,i_{t}}
. Otherwise, for
x_{t,j^{*}}>2x_{t,i_{t}}
, we can write

\displaystyle z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i_{t}}-Z_{t}+\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}+\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t-1,i_{t}}-Z_{t}+\frac{S_{t}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{S_{t}}{4x_{t,i_{t}}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}}

\displaystyle=x_{t,i_{t}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{1}{4(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}\right)^{-1}

Notice that here
x_{t,i_{t}}\in[0,1/2]
, which implies that
1+\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}}{x_{t,j^{*}}}-\frac{1}{4(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}\geq 1/2
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle z_{t,i_{t}}(\overline{Z}_{t})\leq 2x_{t,i_{t}}\leq\frac{1}{2}x_{t,j^{*}}+x_{t,i_{t}}.

Since
z_{t,j^{*}}(\overline{Z}_{t})=\frac{1}{2}x_{t,j^{*}}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}z_{t,i}(\overline{Z}_{t})\leq\sum_{i\in[K]\setminus\{i_{t},j^{*}\}}x_{t,i}+\frac{1}{2}x_{t,j^{*}}+\frac{1}{2}x_{t,j^{*}}+x_{t,i_{t}}=1.

In all, we conclude
Equation

23
and prove this lemma.
∎

C.2
Calculating
w_{t,i}
via Partial Derivatives

By inspecting the partial derivatives of the multiplier
\widetilde{Z}_{t}
with respect to the feedback vector
\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}
, we can derive the following lemmas on
w_{t,i}
.

Lemma 16
.

We have

\displaystyle w_{t,i}=\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i}}{S_{t}}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=i_{t}]-\frac{{\zeta_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}{\zeta_{t,k}^{2}}}\right),

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}:=\argmin_{{\bm{z}}\in\Delta}\langle{\bm{L}}_{t-1}+\lambda\widetilde{\ell}_{t},{\bm{z}}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{z}}).

Here
\lambda
is some constant ranged from
(0,1)
, implicitly determined by
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
.

Proof.

Notice that conditioning on the time step before
t-1
,
z_{t,i}
only depends on
\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i}
. Then we denote

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}_{t,i}(\theta):=\left.\frac{\partial\widetilde{Z}_{t}}{\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i}}\right|_{\widetilde{{\bm{\ell}}}_{t}=\theta{\bm{e}}_{i}},

where
{\bm{e}}_{i}
is the unit vector supported at the
i
-th coordinate, and
\theta
is a scalar.

Notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[K]}z_{t,j}=\sum_{j\in[K]}\frac{S_{t}}{L_{t,j}-\widetilde{Z}_{t}}=1,

(24)

Then partially derivate
\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i}
on both sides of
Equation

24
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[K]}-\frac{S_{t}}{(\ell_{t,j}-\widetilde{Z}_{t})^{2}}\cdot(\mathbbm{1}[j=i]-\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}_{t,i})=-\frac{z_{t,i}^{2}}{S_{t}}+\widetilde{Z}_{t,i}^{\text{skip}}\sum_{j\in[K]}\frac{z_{t,j}^{2}}{S_{t}}=0,

which solves to

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}_{t,i}=\frac{z_{t,i}^{2}}{\sum_{j\in[K]}{z_{t,j}^{2}}}.

Similarly, we denote

\displaystyle z^{\prime}_{t,ij}(\theta):=\left.\frac{\partial z_{t,i}}{\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t,j}}\right|_{\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}=\theta{\bm{e}}_{j}}

Then according to the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle z^{\prime}_{t,ij}=\frac{z_{t,i}^{2}}{S_{t}}\cdot\left(z_{t,j}^{2}/\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)-\mathbbm{1}[i=j]\right).

We denote

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{t,ij}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\left.\frac{\partial w_{t,i}}{\partial\widetilde{\ell}_{t,j}}\right|_{\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}=\theta{\bm{e}}_{j}}.

Recall that
w_{t,i}=\frac{x_{t,i}}{S_{t}}(\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i}-(\widetilde{Z}_{t}-Z_{t}))
, hence

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{t,ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{t,i}}{S_{t}}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=j]-\left(\frac{z_{t,j}^{2}}{S_{t}}\right)/\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{z_{t,k}^{2}}{S_{t}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{t,i}}{S_{t}}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=j]-\left({z_{t,j}^{2}}\right)/\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{z_{t,k}^{2}}\right)\right).

Thus according to the intermediate value theorem, we have

\displaystyle w_{t,i}=\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i}}{S_{t}}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=i_{t}]-\frac{{\zeta_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}{\zeta_{t,k}^{2}}}\right),

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}:=\argmin_{{\bm{z}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\langle{\bm{L}}_{t-1}+\lambda\widetilde{\ell}_{t},{\bm{z}}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{z}}),

and
\lambda
is some constant ranged from
(0,1)
, implicitly determined by
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}
. Furthermore,
Lemma

4
guarantees that

\displaystyle x_{t,i}/2\leq\zeta_{t,i}\leq 2x_{t,i}\quad\forall i\in[K].

(25)

∎

C.3
Bregman Divergence before Expectation: Proof of
Lemma

5

Lemma 17

(Formal version of
Lemma

5
)
.

We have

\textsc{Div}_{t}\leq 2048\cdot S_{t}^{-1}(\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}.

Therefore, the expectation of the Bregman Divergence term
\textsc{Div}_{t}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}]\leq 2048\sum_{i\in[K]}S_{t}^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i})^{2}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}]x_{t,i}(1-x_{t,i})^{2}.

Moreover, for any
{\mathcal{T}}\in[T]
, we can bound the sum of Bregman divergence term by

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\textsc{Div}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 4096\cdot S_{{\mathcal{T}}+1}K\log T.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
w_{t,i}
, we have

\displaystyle\textsc{Div}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[K]}S_{t}(w_{t,i}-\log(1+w_{t,i}))

By
Lemma

4
, we have
w_{t,i}=x_{t,i}/z_{t,i}-1\in[-1/2,1/2]
. Therefore, since
x-\log(1+x)\leq x^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\textsc{Div}_{t}\leq\sum_{i\in[K]}S_{t}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=i_{t}]w_{t,i}^{2}+\mathbbm{1}[i\neq i_{t}]w_{t,i}^{2}\right).

(26)

By
Lemma

16
, we have

\displaystyle w_{t,i}=\widetilde{\ell}_{t,i_{t}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i}}{S_{t}}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=i_{t}]-\frac{{\zeta_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}{\zeta_{t,k}^{2}}}\right),

and
\zeta_{t,i}
satisfying
x_{t,i}/2\leq\zeta_{t,i}\leq 2x_{t,i}
by
Equation

25
.
Then, for
i\neq i_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle w_{t,i}

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i}}{S_{t}}\cdot\frac{\zeta_{t,i_{t}}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}{\zeta_{t,k}^{2}}},

\displaystyle w_{t,i}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}})^{2}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i}^{2}}{S_{t}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\zeta_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{\zeta_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}\leq 16\cdot(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}})^{2}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i}^{2}}{S_{t}^{2}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}.

And for
i=i_{t}
,

\displaystyle w_{t,i_{t}}

\displaystyle=\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}\cdot\frac{1}{S_{t}}\cdot\frac{\sum_{j\neq i_{t}}\zeta_{t,j}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}{\zeta_{t,k}^{2}}},

\displaystyle w_{t,i_{t}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}})^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{S_{t}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left(\sum_{j\neq i_{t}}{\zeta_{t,j}^{2}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{\zeta_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}\leq 256\cdot(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}})^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{S_{t}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left(\sum_{j\neq i_{t}}{x_{t,j}^{2}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[K]}S_{t}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=i_{t}]w_{t,i}^{2}+\mathbbm{1}[i\neq i_{t}]w_{t,i}^{2}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle\leq 256\cdot(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{{\text{skip}}})^{2}\cdot S_{t}^{-1}\cdot\left(\frac{\left(\sum_{j\neq i_{t}}{x_{t,j}^{2}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}\frac{x_{t,i}^{2}\cdot x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}\right)

Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\sum_{j\neq i_{t}}{x_{t,j}^{2}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}\frac{x_{t,i}^{2}\cdot x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}{x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}}=\left(1-\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}.

For
x_{t,i_{t}}\leq 1/2
, we have
4(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\geq 1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\leq 1+1\cdot 1\leq 8(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}.

(28)

For
x_{t,i_{t}}\geq 1/2
, we denote

\displaystyle\widetilde{x}_{t,i}:=\frac{x^{2}_{t,i}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x^{2}_{t,k}},\quad\forall i\in[K],

which satisfies
\widetilde{x}_{t,i}\leq x_{t,i}^{2}/x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}\leq 4x_{t,i}^{2}
for every
i\in[K]
. Since
x_{t,i_{t}}\geq 1/2
, we also have
1/2\leq x_{t,i_{t}}\leq\widetilde{x}_{t,i_{t}}\leq 1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1-\widetilde{x}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}+\widetilde{x}_{t,i_{t}}\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}\widetilde{x}_{t,i}

(29)

\displaystyle\leq(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}+\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}4x_{t,i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}+4\left(\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 5(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}

Combine these cases, we get

\displaystyle\textsc{Div}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[K]}S_{t}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}[i=i_{t}]w_{t,i}^{2}+\mathbbm{1}[i\neq i_{t}]w_{t,i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 256\cdot(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{{\text{skip}}})^{2}\cdot S_{t}^{-1}\cdot\left(\left(1-\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\sum_{i\neq i_{t}}x_{t,i}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\cdot\frac{x_{t,i_{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}x_{t,k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2048\cdot(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{{\text{skip}}})^{2}\cdot S_{t}^{-1}\cdot(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2},

where the first inequality is due to
Equation

26
, the second inequality is due to
Equation

27
, and the last inequality is due to
Equation

28
for
x_{t,i_{t}}\leq 1/2
and
Equation

29
for
x_{t,i_{t}}\geq 1/2
.

Moreover, taking expectation conditioning on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}
, we directly imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}]\leq 2048\sum_{i\in[K]}S_{t}^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i})^{2}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}]x_{t,i}(1-x_{t,i})^{2}.

Consider the sum of
\textsc{Div}_{t}
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\textsc{Div}_{t}\leq 2048\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}S_{t}^{-1}\cdot(\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\cdot(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}.

Notice that by definition of
S_{t+1}
in
Equation

4
, we have

\displaystyle(K\log T)\cdot(S_{t+1}^{2}-S_{t}^{2})=\left(\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}}\right)^{2}\cdot(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}.

Moreover, since
|\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}}|
is controlled by
C_{t,i_{t}}
, we have

\displaystyle S_{t+1}^{2}\leq S_{t}^{2}+C_{t,i_{t}}^{2}(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\cdot(K\log T)^{-1}=S_{t}^{2}\left(1+(4K\log T)^{-1}\right)\leq 2S_{t}^{2},

(30)

where the first inequlity is due to
|\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}}|\leq C_{t,i_{t}}
and the definition of
C_{t,i_{t}}
in
Equation

3
, and the second inequlity holds by
T\geq 2
.
Therefore, as
\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}}\rvert\leq\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{clip}}\rvert
trivially, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\textsc{Div}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2048\sum_{t=1}^{T}S_{t}^{-1}\cdot(\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\left(1-{x}_{t,i_{t}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2048\cdot K\log T\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\frac{S_{t+1}^{2}-S_{t}^{2}}{S_{t}}

\displaystyle=2048\cdot K\log T\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}\frac{(S_{t+1}+S_{t})(S_{t+1}-S_{t})}{S_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq 2048(1+\sqrt{2})K\log T\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{T}}S_{t+1}-S_{t}

\displaystyle=4096\cdot S_{{\mathcal{T}}+1}K\log T,

where the first ineuqality is by
Lemma

5
and
|\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}}|\leq|\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{clip}}|
, the second inequality is due to the definition of
S_{t+1}
in

and

, and the last inequality is due to the
S_{t+1}\leq\sqrt{2}S_{t}
by
Equation

30
.
∎

C.4
Bounding the Expectation of Learning Rate
S_{T+1}

6

Recall the definition of
S_{t}
in

, we have

\displaystyle S_{T+1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4+\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{clip}})^{2}\cdot(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\cdot(K\log T)^{-1}}

which bounds

Lemma 18

(Restatement of

)

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{T+1}]\leq 2\cdot\sigma(K\log T)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}T^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(32)

Proof.

By clipping operation, we have

\displaystyle|\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}}|\leq C_{t,i_{t}}=S_{t}\cdot\frac{1}{4(1-x_{t,i_{t}})}\leq S_{t}\leq S_{T+1}\leq S.

Therefore, we know

\displaystyle 4+\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\cdot(K\log T)^{-1}\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\ell_{t,i_{t}}|^{\alpha}\cdot S^{2-\alpha}\cdot(K\log T)^{-1},

where the inequality is given by

,
|\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}}|\leq S
and
|\ell^{\text{clip}}_{t,i_{t}}|\leq|\ell_{t,i_{t}}|

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}\leq\frac{4}{3}(K\log T)^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}\rvert^{\alpha}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{1/\alpha}(K\log T)^{-1/\alpha}\cdot\sigma\cdot T^{1/\alpha}

The conclusion follows from the fact that

.
∎

E.1
Constant Clipping Threshold Argument: Proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
12
.

For a given clipping threshold constant
M
, we first perform clipping operation
\operatorname{Clip}(\ell_{t,i_{t}},M)
, which gives a
universal
clipping error

\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{Univ}}(M):=\begin{cases}\ell_{t,i_{t}}-M&\ell_{t,i_{t}}>M\\
0&-M\leq\ell_{t,i_{t}}\leq M\\
\ell_{t,i_{t}}+M&\ell_{t,i_{t}}<-M\end{cases}.

If
C_{t,i_{t}}\geq M
, then this clipping is ineffective. Otherwise, we get the following
action-dependent
clipping error

\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{ActDep}}(M):=\begin{cases}\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}-\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{Univ}}&C_{t,i_{t}}<M\\
0&C_{t,i_{t}}\geq M\end{cases}.

Therefore, we directly have
|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\leq|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{Univ}}(M)|+|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{ActDep}}(M)|
. Another important observation is that if
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{ActDep}}(M)\neq 0
, we have
S_{t}\leq M
,
(\ell^{{\text{clip}}}_{t,i_{t}})^{2}=C_{t,i_{t}}^{2}
, and
|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{ActDep}}(M)|\leq M
, which implies that

\displaystyle S_{t+1}^{2}=S_{t}^{2}+C_{t,i_{t}}^{2}\cdot(1-x_{t,i_{t}})^{2}\cdot(K\log T)^{-1}=S_{t}^{2}\left(1+(16K\log T)^{-1}\right).

Thus the number of occurrences of non-zero
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{ActDep}}(M)
’s is upper-bounded by
\lceil\log_{\sqrt{1+(K\log T)^{-1}/4}}M\rceil
. Then we can bound the sum of the sub-optimal skipping loss by

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{Univ}}(M)|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}^{\text{ActDep}}(M)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\ell_{t,i_{t}}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[|\ell_{t,i_{t}}\geq M]\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]+M\cdot\mathbb{E}[\log_{\sqrt{1+(16K\log T)^{-1}}}M]+M

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{\alpha}M^{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]+2M\cdot\frac{\log M}{\log(1+(16K\log T)^{-1})}+M.

∎

,
i.e.
,
y_{i}:=\mathbbm{1}[i=i^{\ast}]
. By definition of
\mathcal{R}_{T}
in
Equation

1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}\rangle\right].

Consider the adjusted benchmark
\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}
where

\widetilde{y}_{i}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{T}&i\neq i^{\ast}\\
1-\frac{K-1}{T}&i=i^{\ast}\end{cases}.

By standard regret decomposition in FTRL-based MAB algorithm analyses, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\widetilde{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]}_{\textsc{Benchmark Calibration Error}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]}_{\textsc{Main Regret}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}-{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]}_{\textsc{Skipping Error}}.

(36)

As in a typical log-barrier analysis, the Benchmark Calibration Error is not the dominant term. This is because we have, by definitions of
{\bm{y}}
and
\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\widetilde{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{K-1}{T}\mathbb{E}[|\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i_{t}}|]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{K-1}{T}\mathbb{E}[|\ell_{t,i_{t}}|]\leq\sigma K,

which is independent from
T
. Therefore, the key is analyzing the other two terms.

By the FTRL decomposition in
Lemma

29
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\textsc{Main Regret}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}^{\text{skip}}_{t}\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{\bm{y}},\widetilde{{\bm{\ell}}}_{t}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[D_{\Psi_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t})]+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Psi_{t+1}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})-\Psi_{t}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})\right)-\left(\Psi_{t+1}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})-\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\right)\right],

where

D_{\Psi_{t}}(\bm{y},\bm{x})=\Psi_{t}(\bm{y})-\Psi_{t}(\bm{x})-\langle\nabla\Psi_{t}(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle,

given the Bregman divergence induced by the
t
-th regularizer
\Psi_{t}
, and
{\bm{z}}_{t}
denotes the posterior optimal estimation in episode
t
, namely

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}:=\argmin_{{\bm{z}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\langle\widetilde{{\bm{\ell}}}_{s},{\bm{z}}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{z}})\right).

As mentioned in
Section

5
, we denote
\textsc{Div}_{t}:=D_{\Psi_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t})
for the Bregman divergence between
{\bm{x}}_{t}
and
{\bm{z}}_{t}
under regularizer
\Psi_{t}
,
\textsc{Shift}_{t}:=\left[\left(\Psi_{t+1}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})-\Psi_{t}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})\right)-\left(\Psi_{t+1}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})-\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})\right)\right]
be the
\Psi
-shifting term, and
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}:=\ell_{t,i_{t}}-\ell_{t,i_{t}}^{\text{skip}}=\ell_{t,i_{t}}\mathbbm{1}[\lvert\ell_{t,i_{t}}\rvert\geq C_{t,i_{t}}]
be the sub-optimal skipping losses. Then, we can reduce the analysis of main regret to bounding the sum of Bregman divergence term
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}]
and
\Psi
-shifting term
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Shift}_{t}]
. Moreover, for sub-optimal skipping losses, we have

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}-{\bm{\ell}}_{t}^{\text{skip}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[K]}(x_{t,i}-y_{i})\cdot(\ell_{t,i}-\ell_{t,i}^{\text{skip}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\neq i^{\ast}}x_{t,i}\cdot\left|\ell_{t,i}-\ell^{\text{skip}}_{t,i}\right|+(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)\cdot\left(\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]+(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)\cdot\left(\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}}\right).

Notice that the factor
(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)
in the second term is negative and
{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}
-measurable. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}[\lvert\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}\rvert\geq C_{t,i^{\ast}}]\cdot\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}\right]\geq 0,

where the inequality is due to the truncated non-negative assumption (
Assumption

1
) of the optimal arm
i^{\ast}
. Therefore, we have
\mathbb{E}[(x_{t,i^{\ast}}-1)\cdot(\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}-\ell_{t,i^{\ast}}^{\text{skip}})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 0
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{\ell}}_{t}-{\bm{\ell}}_{t}^{\text{skip}}\rangle\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\lvert\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}],

which gives an approach to control the skipping error by the sum of skipping losses
\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}
’s where we pick a sub-optimal arm
i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}
. Formally, we give the following inequality:

\displaystyle\textsc{Skipping Error}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}\rvert\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right].

To summarize, the regret
{\mathcal{R}}_{T}
decomposes into the sum of Bregman divergence terms
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Div}_{t}]
, the
\Psi
-shifting terms
\mathbb{E}[\textsc{Shift}_{t}]
, and the sub-optimal skipping losses
\mathbb{E}[|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]]
, namely

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textsc{Div}_{t}\right]}_{\textsc{Bregman Divergence Terms}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\textsc{Shift}_{t}\right]}_{\textsc{$\Psi$-Shifting Terms}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]}_{\textsc{Sub-optimal Skipping Losses}}+\sigma K.

We discuss the regret upper bound under adversarial and stochastic environments separately.

Adversarial Cases.
According to
Theorems

19
,
23
, and
26
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textsc{Div}_{t}\right]}+{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\textsc{Shift}_{t}\right]}+{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]}+\sigma K

\displaystyle\leq 8192\cdot\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}(\log T)^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}+2\cdot\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}(\log T)^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\cdot\left((\log T)^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}+\frac{2}{\alpha}(\log T)^{2-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}+2\log\sigma-\frac{2}{\alpha}\log K-\frac{2}{\alpha}\log\log T\right)+\sigma K

\displaystyle=\sigma K^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}\cdot\left(8195(\log T)^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}+\frac{2}{\alpha}(\log T)^{2-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}+2\log\sigma-\frac{2}{\alpha}\log K-\frac{2}{\alpha}\log\log T\right)+\sigma K.

Stochastic Cases.
According to
Theorems

21
,
25
, and
27
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textsc{Div}_{t}\right]}+{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\textsc{Shift}_{t}\right]}+{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\textsc{SkipErr}_{t}|\cdot\mathbbm{1}[i_{t}\neq i^{\ast}]\right]}+\sigma K

\displaystyle\leq 8192\cdot 16384^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\cdot K\Delta_{\min}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\sigma^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\log T+\frac{1}{4}{\mathcal{R}}_{T}

\displaystyle\quad+2K\sigma^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Delta_{\min}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\log T+\frac{1}{4}{\mathcal{R}}_{T}

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\Delta_{\min}^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\cdot K\log T\cdot\frac{4^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}{\alpha-1}\left(2\log 4+\alpha\log\sigma+\log(1/\Delta_{\min})\right)+\frac{1}{4}{\mathcal{R}}_{T}+\sigma K

\displaystyle\leq K\left(\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\log T\cdot\left(8192\cdot 16384^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}+2+\frac{4^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}{\alpha-1}\left(2\log 4+\log\left(\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)\right)\right)+\frac{3}{4}{\mathcal{R}}_{T},

which implies that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{T}\leq 4\cdot K\left(\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\log T\cdot\left(8192\cdot 16384^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}+2+\frac{4^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}}{\alpha-1}\left(2\log 4+\log\left(\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)\right)\right).

Therefore, we finish the proof, which shows the BoBW property of
Algorithm

1
.
∎

The following lemma characterizes the FTRL regret decomposition, which is the extension of the classical FTRL bound
(
Lattimore & Szepesvári, 2020
, Theorem 28.5)
.
Dann et al. (2023a, Lemma 17)
also gave a similar result, but we include a full proof here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 29

(FTRL Regret Decomposition)
.

In
uniINF
(
Algorithm

1
), we have (set
S_{0}=0
for simplicity),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}\rangle\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[D_{\Psi_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t})]+\mathbb{E}[(\Psi_{t}(\widetilde{{\bm{y}}})-\Psi_{t-1}(\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}))-(\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\Psi_{t-1}({\bm{x}}_{t}))],

where
D_{\Psi_{t}}
is the Bregman divergence induced by
\Psi_{t}
, and
{\bm{z}}_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}:=\argmin_{{\bm{z}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\langle\widetilde{{\bm{\ell}}}_{s},{\bm{z}}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{z}})\right).

Proof.

We denote
{\bm{L}}_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{s}
. Denote
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
as the Frenchel conjugate of function
f:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
, where

\displaystyle f^{*}({\bm{y}}):=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\left\{\langle{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}\rangle-f({\bm{x}})\right\}.

Moreover, denote
\overline{f}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
as the restriction of
f:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
on
\Delta^{[K]}
, i.e,

\displaystyle\overline{f}({\bm{x}})=\begin{cases}f({\bm{x}}),&{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{[K]}\\
\infty,&{\bm{x}}\notin\Delta^{[K]}\end{cases}.

Therefore, by definition, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}=\nabla\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t}),\quad{\bm{x}}_{t}=\nabla\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1}).

Then recall the properties of Bregman divergence, we have

\displaystyle D_{\Psi_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t})=D_{\Psi_{t}}(\nabla\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1}),\nabla\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t}))=D_{\overline{\Psi}_{t}^{*}}(-{\bm{L}}_{t},-{\bm{L}}_{t-1}).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t}-\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},-\widetilde{\bm{\ell}}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t})-\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1})-\langle\nabla\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1}),-{\bm{L}}_{t}+{\bm{L}}_{t-1}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t})-\overline{\Psi}_{t}^{*}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\overline{\Psi}_{t}^{*}}(-{\bm{L}}_{t},-{\bm{L}}_{t-1})+\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t})-\overline{\Psi}_{t}^{*}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\Psi_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t})+\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t})-\overline{\Psi}_{t}^{*}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1})\right).

For the second and third terms, we have

\displaystyle\quad\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t})-\overline{\Psi}_{t}^{*}(-{\bm{L}}_{t-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\overline{\Psi}^{*}_{t}(-{\bm{L}}_{t})-\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},-{\bm{L}}_{t-1}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\left\{\langle{\bm{x}},-{\bm{L}}_{t}\rangle-\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}})\right\}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},-{\bm{L}}_{t-1}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\langle{\bm{x}}_{t+1},-{\bm{L}}_{t}\rangle-\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t+1})-\langle{\bm{x}}_{t},-{\bm{L}}_{t-1}\rangle+\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\left\{\langle{\bm{x}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\Psi_{T}({\bm{x}})\right\}+\langle{\bm{x}}_{T},-{\bm{L}}_{T-1}\rangle-\Psi_{T}({\bm{x}}_{T})

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\Psi_{t-1}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\left\{\langle{\bm{x}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\Psi_{T}({\bm{x}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{{\bm{y}}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\Psi_{T}(\widetilde{{\bm{y}}})-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{[K]}}\left\{\langle{\bm{x}},-{\bm{L}}_{T}\rangle-\Psi_{T}({\bm{x}})\right\}+\Psi_{T}(\widetilde{{\bm{y}}})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\Psi_{t-1}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\Psi_{t}(\widetilde{{\bm{y}}})-\Psi_{t-1}(\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}))-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\Psi_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})-\Psi_{t-1}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right),

which finishes the proof.
∎
