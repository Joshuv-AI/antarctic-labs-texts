C-Reactive Protein-Triglyceride Glucose Index and Mortality in Patients with Ventricular Septal Rupture After Acute Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Yifeng Chen, Zebin Lin, Xinlong Di, Qingwang Hou, Fujian Zhou, Yu Xia, Shengfang Wang, Hejia Wan, Yuhao Liu, Yiqiang Yuan, Jianmin Tang, Yipin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVentricular septal rupture (VSR) is a life-threatening complication of acute myocardial infarction (AMI). The C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) integrates insulin resistance (IR) and inflammation. The present study was designed to investigate the correlation between CTI and 1-year all-cause mortality in VSR patients after AMI.Methods284 VSR patients were consecutively enrolled at Fuwai Central China Cardiovascular Hospital between January 2018 and April 2025. According to the median CTI value (6.408), the 284 VSR patients were divided into two groups. The relationship between CTI and clinical outcomes was evaluated using multivariate logistic regression analysis, followed by mediation analysis to explore indirect statistical associations.Results1-year all-cause mortality in VSR patients was 71.8% (204/284). The mortality of the high CTI group was significantly higher than that of the low CTI group (87.3% vs 56.3%, P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that CTI was associated with mortality (OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.85–4.51, P < 0.001), with mortality showing an upward trend as CTI levels increased (P < 0.001). Mediation analyses identified indirect statistical associations involving neutrophil count and NT-proBNP, with mediation proportions of 65.8% and 35.3%, respectively (both P < 0.05).ConclusionCTI is associated with mortality but not independently, with indirect statistical associations involving inflammation and increased cardiac load.Keywords: acute myocardial infarction, inflammation, insulin resistance, ventricular septal rupture

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency. In essence, it is myocardial ischemic necrosis caused by the sudden interruption of coronary blood flow, posing one of the major global public health burdens.1 Despite advances in the diagnosis, risk stratification, and management of AMI, post-infarction complications remain major determinants of disability, impaired quality of life, and mortality.2 While ventricular septal rupture (VSR) occurs infrequently as a complication of AMI, it stands among the most life-threatening mechanical complications with regard to prognostic outcomes.3 Myocardial ischemic necrosis disrupts the integrity of the ventricular septum, resulting in the formation of a pathological defect. The interventricular pressure gradient drives a left-to-right shunt through the defect, and progressive shunt enlargement may result in hemodynamic instability.4,5 Advances in transcatheter interventional therapy, surgical treatment, and related technologies have reduced the incidence of VSR; however, outcomes remain poor once it occurs.4,6

C-reactive protein (CRP) mainly reflects the systemic inflammatory state and constitutes an independent cardiovascular risk factor.7 The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index focuses on evaluating metabolic disorders and insulin resistance (IR).8,9 There may be limitations to assessing VSR patients’ prognosis with a single biomarker. CTI integrates inflammation and insulin resistance, providing a more comprehensive risk assessment than either component alone.10,11 CTI was calculated as 0.412 × ln(CRP [mg/L]) + [ln(TG [mg/dL] × FPG [mg/dL])]/2.12 Multiple clinical studies have shown that CTI has a good application prospect in cardiovascular risk assessment and stratification. For example, Cheng et al found that CTI was related to heart failure;13 Chen et al found a correlation between CTI and newly developed coronary heart disease.14 However, the correlation between CTI and the prognosis of this high-risk group of VSR patients has not been systematically evaluated.

This study aims to explore the association between CTI and 1-year all-cause mortality of VSR patients after AMI.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study of 284 VSR patients at Fuwai Central China Cardiovascular Hospital (January 2018–April 2025) was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (approval date: 22 May 2025; approval number: 39). Informed consent was waived because this retrospective analysis of existing clinical records posed minimal risk. Patient confidentiality was maintained throughout via complete data de-identification. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥18 years; (2) Definite diagnosis of AMI, including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI); (3) The interruption of ventricular septal continuity was confirmed through echocardiography, or a left-to-right shunt was verified by left ventriculography. Exclusion criteria: (1) Ventricular septal rupture caused by non-ischemic reasons (eg, congenital heart disease, infective endocarditis, trauma); (2) Comorbidities of malignant tumors, end-stage liver disease, or autoimmune diseases. The outcome was defined as all-cause mortality during the 1-year follow-up. A large proportion of follow-up was conducted through outpatient visits and telephone. This study was reported in accordance with the RECORD statement.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analysed using R software (version 4.2.2) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-sided). The missForest procedure based on R was employed to impute the missing covariates. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). Group comparisons were conducted using either independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test, with test selection determined by data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage). When the anticipated frequency was less than 5, Fisher’s exact test was employed for intergroup comparisons; otherwise, the chi-square test was utilized. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. Subsequent to variable selection using the Boruta algorithm, multivariable logistic regression was employed to assess the relationship between CTI and mortality, while its correlations with other indicators were assessed through Spearman analysis. Mediation analysis with 1,000 bootstrap resamples was employed to explore the indirect statistical associations connecting CTI to mortality.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Based on the median CTI (6.408), 284 VSR patients were stratified into low (CTI < 6.408, n = 142) and high (CTI ≥ 6.408, n = 142) CTI groups (Table 1). For demographic characteristics, no significant differences existed between groups in age, gender, history of hypertension, diabetes, or drinking (P > 0.05). Regarding laboratory indicators, the levels of NT-proBNP, neutrophils, and myoglobin in the high CTI group were significantly higher than those in the low CTI group (P < 0.001), while albumin levels were significantly lower (P < 0.001). No significant differences existed between groups in TC, LDL-c, or UA (P > 0.05). Concerning clinical treatment characteristics, the high CTI group had higher usage rates of IABP (P < 0.001), CRRT (P < 0.001), and mechanical ventilation (P = 0.004) than the low CTI group. Transcatheter closure (66.2% vs 50.0%) and surgical repair (17.6% vs 9.9%) were more frequently performed in the low CTI group (both P < 0.001). Notably, the mortality was higher in the high CTI group (87.3% vs 56.3%, P < 0.001).

Association Between CTI and Mortality

Multivariate logistic regression (Table 2) showed that a significant link with mortality was identified for CTI in the unadjusted Model 1 (OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.85–4.51, P < 0.001). After adjustment for clinical treatment-related covariates in Model 2, the association was attenuated but still statistically significant (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.05–2.88, P = 0.033). However, in Model 3, after further adjusting for laboratory indicators, when CTI was considered as a continuous variable, the association was not significant (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 0.75–2.29, P = 0.341). But when analyzed by quartile classification in Model 3 (with the Q1 group as the reference group), the death risk of patients in the Q3 group was still significantly increased (OR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.06–6.73, P = 0.036), while there was no significant difference in the Q4 group (OR = 1.83, 95% CI: 0.60–5.55, P = 0.286). In Models 1, 2, and 3, the Q3 or Q4 groups showed a significantly increased risk of death relative to the Q1 reference group, and the trend test found an increasing trend in mortality with higher CTI levels. In Model 4, where both clinical treatment-related variables and laboratory indicators were adjusted, the association completely disappeared. The effects were not statistically significant for either continuous variables (P = 0.983) or quartiles (all P > 0.05), and the trend test was also not statistically significant (P for trend = 0.444). Collectively, the association between CTI and mortality was attenuated after adjustment for treatment-related factors and laboratory markers, indicating that these covariates may account for the observed association.

Correlation Analysis Between CTI and Laboratory Indicators

Spearman correlation analysis (Table 3) showed that CTI had a moderate positive correlation with inflammatory indicators (neutrophils, rho = 0.467, P < 0.001), liver function indicators (AST, rho = 0.373; ALT, rho = 0.351, both P < 0.001), cardiac function markers (NT-proBNP, rho = 0.369; myoglobin, rho = 0.308, both P < 0.001), and coagulation function indicators (INR, rho = 0.320, P < 0.001). However, CTI was inversely linked with eGFR (rho = −0.173, P < 0.05). The above results suggest that CTI is closely related to the multi-system pathophysiological disorders of VSR patients.

Mediation Analysis of the Association Between CTI and Mortality

Mediation analyses showed (Figure 2 and Table 4) that the indirect association involving neutrophils and NT-proBNP was statistically significant (both P < 0.05). The mediation proportions were 65.8% (95% CI: 17.5–250.2%) for neutrophils and 35.3% (95% CI: 7.0–144.9%) for NT-proBNP. No statistically significant indirect associations were observed for the other organ function or injury markers (all P > 0.05). These findings suggest that CTI is related to mortality in VSR patients, involving inflammation and increased cardiac load.

Discussion

Our study focused on VSR patients after AMI, a high-risk population with an extremely poor prognosis whose 1-year all-cause mortality was as high as 71.8%. In the single-center retrospective studies in this field, our study included the largest sample size so far (to our knowledge). CTI was associated with mortality, although this association was not independent. Rather, it reflected indirect statistical relationships involving inflammation and increased cardiac load.

Mortality in the high CTI group (87.3%) markedly exceeded that in the low CTI group. The risk of death in the highest tertile or quartile of CTI was higher than that in the reference group. This suggests that patients with higher CTI levels have a poorer prognosis, which is consistent with reports from other studies linking higher CTI levels to adverse outcomes.15–17 Recent studies in broader cardiovascular populations have similarly linked higher CTI levels to new-onset atrial fibrillation after STEMI and recurrent cardiovascular events after percutaneous coronary intervention.18,19 The odds ratio decreased from 2.89 in Model 1 to 1.74 in Model 2, indicating that clinical treatment strategy is an important confounding factor. Advanced life support therapies (such as IABP, CRRT, and mechanical ventilation) are more commonly used in the management of critically ill patients.20,21 The high CTI group received these supportive interventions at a higher frequency than the low CTI group. As responses to severe pathological conditions, these interventions themselves also affect patient outcomes, thereby attenuating the apparent association between CTI and mortality to some extent.22,23 The 2013 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association guidelines stated that surgical repair is necessary for VSR patients.22 Nevertheless, VSR patients in the high CTI group received transcatheter interventional therapy and surgical repair less frequently than those in the low CTI group. This may be due to the intense inflammatory response, severe insulin resistance, and even hemodynamic instability observed in VSR patients with high CTI levels.4,5,10 Despite efforts to maintain vital signs through advanced life support, critically ill patients may not survive long enough to undergo transcatheter interventional therapy or surgical repair.24,25 No significant association was observed in Model 3, which modeled CTI as a continuous variable. However, when CTI was analyzed by quartile grouping, the Q3 group was significantly associated with mortality, and the trend test was statistically significant. This seemingly contradictory result stems from the inclusion of important variables (neutrophils and NT-proBNP) that affect prognosis in Model 3. Notably, the Q3 group showed statistical significance compared to the Q1 group. Given that the OR for Q4 was lower than that for Q3, and the confidence intervals for Q3 and Q4 were wide and overlapping, coupled with the fact that adjustments in Model 4 eliminated the quartile association and linear trend, the results for Q3 are more likely to reflect sampling variability, data sparsity, and model instability rather than a reproducible biological threshold or a nonlinear dose-response relationship.

Neutrophils ranked first in Boruta variable importance, and CTI showed the strongest correlation with neutrophils in laboratory examinations. Mediation analysis observed an indirect statistical link involving neutrophils. These findings suggest that CTI is closely related to the inflammation at the time of VSR diagnosis, but they do not confirm that CTI triggers or causes neutrophil-mediated damage. Neutrophils play a major role in the inflammatory response.26,27 After AMI, the necrotic tissue in the infarct area releases cell debris and inflammatory mediators as chemical signals to induce neutrophil migration and recruitment to the ischemic lesion. These recruited granulocytes initiate the inflammatory response by synthesizing and releasing mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, oxygen-free radicals, and proteases, with their excessive release often leading to severe damage in surrounding normal tissues.28–30 Studies have confirmed that myocardial infarction is often accompanied by the activation of the inflammatory system and the occurrence of IR, and the inflammatory response and IR can aggravate each other.31,32 Monomeric C-reactive protein (mCRP) drives macrophages to transform into pro-inflammatory phenotypes by activating the JNK signaling pathway. This mechanism not only enhances and perpetuates the inflammatory response after myocardial infarction, but also further hinders the repair process of myocardial tissue.33 IR damages myocardial structure and function through mechanisms including abnormal lipid accumulation in cardiomyocytes, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress.34 Defined as a chronic low-grade inflammatory state, IR exacerbates systemic inflammation through the release of inflammatory mediators at the tissue level, which in turn further impairs insulin sensitivity, ultimately establishing a vicious cycle.35 Beyond CTI, mendelian randomization evidence regarding N,N,N Trimethyl-L-alanyl-L-proline further highlights the potential relevance of metabolic perturbations in myocardial infarction.36 A persistent inflammatory state induces microcirculatory dysfunction, leading to vasospasm and embolism, which exacerbate ischemic injury and exert sustained adverse effects on the cardiac remodeling process after myocardial infarction.37,38 The inflammatory response and IR, along with their interactions, collectively contribute to the poor prognosis.

Mediation analysis also identified a statistically significant indirect association through NT-proBNP. The left-to-right shunt causes an increase in right ventricular volume load, and the inflammatory response and IR further damage cardiomyocytes, thereby significantly increasing NT-proBNP levels. Elevated NT-proBNP levels accurately reflect increased ventricular wall stress.39 According to Laplace’s law, elevated ventricular wall stress increases myocardial oxygen consumption, accelerating cardiac dysfunction in VSR patients and ultimately elevating the risk of adverse cardiovascular events.40

CTI, a comprehensive biomarker reflecting inflammation and insulin resistance, is calculated using routine biochemical indicators, offering both convenience and cost-effectiveness. The CTI value of VSR patients at admission can be used as an auxiliary risk assessment indicator, working in conjunction with other clinical assessment tools to identify high-risk patients, thus providing clinicians with a reference for developing individualized treatment strategies. For VSR patients, in addition to correcting hemodynamic abnormalities, reducing the inflammatory response and improving IR may help improve prognosis.

This study has limitations. First, the retrospective design inevitably carries risks of selection bias and information bias, and failed to include key clinical variables (such as the location and diameter of the ventricular septal rupture and the target vessel lesion). Second, although this cohort is relatively large for VSR research, only 80 patients survived; the limited sample information may reduce the precision of multivariable regression and mediation analysis estimates, increase the risk of overfitting and coefficient instability, and result in a wide 95% confidence interval for the neutrophil mediation proportion. Third, CTI, neutrophils, and NT-proBNP were all measured within 24 hours of VSR diagnosis. Due to the lack of time series data, the observed indirect associations may merely reflect a synchronous covariation between these indicators and the overall severity of the disease. Mediation analysis can only suggest potential statistical associations, but it cannot establish causal relationships. Finally, CTI was measured at a single time point, preventing assessment of whether longitudinal changes in CTI were associated with disease progression, treatment response, or subsequent clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

CTI is associated with mortality but not independently, with indirect statistical associations involving inflammation and increased cardiac load. Future research should conduct multicenter prospective cohort studies and integrate dynamic CTI monitoring data to evaluate CTI’s associations with disease progression, treatment response, and long-term prognosis.
