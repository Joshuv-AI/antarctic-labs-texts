Randomized Controlled Trial Comparing AI-Generated, Clinician-Edited to Human-Generated After-Visit Summaries
Authors: Jonah Zaretsky, Christopher Kim, Vincent Major, Benjamin Verplanke, Christopher Sonne, William Robert Small, Priyanka Solanki, Lucille Fenelon, Nilufar Tursunova, Alyssa Gutjahr, Yunan Zhao, Saul Blecker, Jonathan Austrian, Paul Testa, Jonah Feldman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo test whether AI-generated, clinician-edited after-visit summaries (AVS) are more patient-friendly than clinician-generated AVS, without compromising safety, when implemented in a live inpatient setting.Patients and MethodsPatients discharged from inpatient medicine units between January 22, 2025, and May 23, 2025, were randomized to the intervention arm, which included AI-generated, clinician-edited AVS, or the control arm, which included usual care of clinician-only AVS. Outcomes include text between 6th and 8th grade reading levels, and presence of a “simplified” description of both the diagnosis and treatment/interventions. Our composite outcome was positive if all 3 of these outcomes were positive. We also surveyed patients, nurses, and clinicians on perceptions of safety, empathy, and understandability.ResultsAmong 61 patients enrolled in the trial, the AI-generated, clinician-edited AVS were significantly more likely to have a mean reading level closer to 6th to 8th grade than the control group (8.5 vs 10.6, P <.001). The AI-generated, clinician-edited AVS were also more likely to contain simplified explanations of diagnoses (96.7% vs 12.9%, P <.001) and hospital interventions (86.7% vs 12.9%, P <.001). Our composite outcome favored AI-generated, clinician-edited summaries (13.3% vs 6.5%, p = 0.637).ConclusionIn this small, randomized trial, exploratory and component measures suggest improved patient-friendliness in AI-generated, clinician-edited AVS, although no significant difference was observed in the primary outcome.ClinicalTrials.gov numberNCT06711458

Introduction

Patients’ understanding of their own health is important for both health-related outcomes and improved satisfaction with their own care.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 This is of particular concern at the time of discharge from an inpatient hospital stay, which can be a vulnerable time for patients.6 Studies have shown that patients frequently have a limited understanding of their own hospital stay.7,8 Furthermore, written materials such as after-visit summaries at the time of discharge that are meant to address patient understanding are often not interpretable by patients.9, 10, 11

Small efforts to make medical notes patient-friendly have been piloted, such as creating letters or having volunteer students explain notes to patients.12,13 However, these are limited in scalability. Artificial Intelligence (AI) has the capability to scale this translation and transformation of medical notes. AI has already shown success in creating summarization of hospital courses for clinical purposes.14 Furthermore, AI often conveys language that may be judged more empathetic than language written by clinicians and has also had success in simplifying educational materials for patients.15, 16, 17, 18 Generative AI has been used on hypothetical patient records to simplify patient materials.19

While there is a need for simplified, patient-friendly versions of patients’ own medical records, there has been limited study of AI-generated patient-friendly notes in a real-world clinical setting. In a first effort, our group tested this in a study looking at 50 real inpatient discharge summaries from medicine units at our health system.20 We found that AI-generated patient-friendly discharge summaries were significantly more readable and understandable than the originals. In our study, we looked at balancing metrics to assess accuracy and completeness. The majority of the patient-friendly discharge summaries had perfect accuracy scores. Primarily, discharge summaries graded as imperfect were due to omissions. We concluded that future iterations of our work would be launched with physician-AI partnerships to bolster improved readability and understandability while having extra layers of support for safety.

While our study showed that AI improved the understandability and readability of discharge summaries, there were several limitations. First, our original study was cross-sectional and not implemented in a live clinical setting or integrated within the electronic health record. Furthermore, the patient-friendly language was compared with discharge summaries as they appear in the electronic medical record, which are often intended for other clinicians, rather than materials specifically created to be patient-facing, such as after-visit summaries. Finally, given our intent to use AI in a clinician-AI partnership, where final texts could still be edited by providers, our original study only included unedited AI output.

In this study, we address these limitations by conducting a randomized controlled trial. Our objective was to evaluate whether AI-generated, patient-facing after-visit summaries were more patient-friendly than clinician-generated, patient-facing after-visit summaries. Importantly, we wanted to test this implemented as a workflow in a live clinical setting. In order to do this, we designed workflows in our electronic health record. To test “patient friendliness” we created a primary outcome that was a composite of three components: readability, the presence of a simplified explanation of diagnosis, and the presence of a simplified explanation of interventions during the hospital stay. We also evaluated the impact of the discharge instructions on perceptions of safety, understandability, and empathy based on survey responses from patients, primary nurses, and attending physicians.

Methods

Setting and Population

This randomized controlled single-blind superiority trial was conducted at NYU Langone Health. Participants were eligible for the trial if they were 18 years old or older, able to read and understand English, and admitted to the inpatient medicine unit at one of the NYU Langone Health campuses. The study was preregistered with ClinicalTrials.gov with the number NCT06711458. The protocol was approved by our institutional review board. No patients were involved in the study design. Our work complies with the CONSORT reporting guidelines.

Randomization and Interventions

Patients planned for discharge were identified at morning interdisciplinary rounds by study facilitators. All providers in study units were given a training video before recruitment, familiarizing them with the AI-integrated discharge workflow. If identified patients were agreeable to participate, consent was obtained after which patients were randomized using simple randomization. Some identified patients could not be enrolled because they met the exclusion criteria, being discharged prior to enrollment or on a different day, or— in three cases—technical issues prevented the generation of AI text from the medical record.

(Figure 1). Randomization was triggered by facilitators in Epic, once per eligible encounter, and stored in the chart to determine whether the AI-generated content is displayed or not. Only the patients remained blind to allocation. For those randomized to the control group, the standard discharge process was followed. For those randomized to the intervention arm, the AI-integrated discharge process was followed. This process included a preview of the AI discharge content within the electronic health record (EHR), and an option to populate the discharge instructions text box with AI-generated text. The study was intention-to-treat, although in all cases the AI-generated text was inserted. Decision support encouraged providers to modify the text as needed. We also included hard stops reminding providers to review the AI-generated text (Supplementary Figure).

CONSORT Flow Diagram.

Once modifications were finished on AI-generated text, the usual discharge process was followed. This text, along with any other text or information added, appeared on the patient’s printed after-visit summary, which was given to the patient and reviewed with the patient by the primary nurse. After this process, the patient, primary nurse, and attending physician were asked to complete a brief survey in REDCap.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite, conjunctive outcome that included three components: a simplified description of the patient’s diagnosis (eg, “heart attack” rather than “myocardial infarction”), a simplified description of the hospital interventions (what was done to treat the patient’s condition), and a reading level of the text between 6th and 8th grade as measured by the Flesch-Kincaid grade level. Only cases where all three of these components were met were considered positive outcomes.

All text contained in the after-visit summary was evaluated for readability using the Flesch-Kincaid Grade level21, including any text that might have been added outside of the generative AI workflow by other services involved in the patient’s care.

The after-visit summaries were deidentified and reviewed by authors P.S. and N.T. for the presence (yes or no) of a simplified description of the diagnosis and a simplified description of hospital interventions. To operationalize our review, we defined “simplified descriptions” as those written in non-technical language and understandable to a typical patient without a medical background. Specifically, we assessed: (1) the presence of a simplified description; (2) whether the description met minimum intelligibility criteria for patients, as defined by Horwitz7 and colleagues and (3) intelligibility to medical professionals, also based on Horwitz et al.

In cases where there were discrepancies between the reviews, a third reviewer, author J.Z., was the tie-breaking vote. To assess agreement between reviewers for the simplified diagnosis and simplified intervention, we calculated Cohen’s Kappa. As far as timing, the review was completed at the end of the study, after all patients had been enrolled. The role of the reviewer was to make the determination in accordance with the definitions described above.

As secondary outcomes, we included results of the patient, primary nurse, and attending physician surveys which had three questions: (1) Do you feel safe with these discharge instructions? (2) Were these instructions easy to understand? (3) Were these instructions written in an empathetic manner? For each of these three questions, participants could answer: Yes, definitely; Yes, somewhat; or No. Only “Yes, definitely” was considered a positive response, which we refer to as “top box.” Facilitators were instructed to report any safety concerns that arose in the process of ascertaining safety perceptions. Respondents had the opportunity to free-text comments in cases where top-box safety scores were not achieved.

We evaluated edits made to the AI-generated text using Levenshtein distance14, which calculates the minimum number of single-character edits required to change one block of text into another. We also calculated the normalized Levenshtein distance, which divides the Levenshtein distance by the longer character length between the pre- and postedited documents and helps account for the fact that longer text is expected to have more edits. We considered there to be minimal or no change in text when the normalized Levenshtein distance was less than one percent.

LLM Prompt Design and EHR Integration

We used the same prompt to transform discharge summaries into patient-friendly discharge summaries as in our previous work.20 The prompt was engineered using an iterative process. Our final prompt contained few-shot learning examples as well as enumerations of various features we would expect in a patient-friendly output, such as aiming for a 6th-grade reading level and limiting sentence length. We used GPT-4 with a temperature setting of 0.

A detailed summary of our prompt engineering and text from the prompt are not included here but are described in prior work. The supplementary figures from that prior work contain the text of the prompt.

In order to integrate our prompt into our electronic health record system, we created a data pipeline that transferred discharge summaries from the electronic health record to a private and secure instance of GPT-4. Signing of the discharge summaries triggered an outbound web request to an internal server endpoint, which initiated the process for that patient.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was determined based on feasibility and availability of facilitators to conduct the study. We did not do power calculations to determine the sample size. With regard to subject-level data, we did not have any missing information. We used Fisher’s exact test to compare categorical outcomes (eg, primary composite outcome, presence of simplified diagnosis, top box safety). For continuous outcomes such as Flesch-Kincaid (FK), we used t-tests to compare the differences between groups. We used a paired t-test to compare the AI-generated Flesch-Kincaid grade score with the clinician-edited Flesch-Kincaid grade scores. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We report absolute differences in the proportion of positive results for the effect size of the composite outcome and component outcome. We use the difference in mean to report the effect size of differences in mean Flesch-Kincaid grade level.

Results

There were 30 patients who were randomized to the intervention arm and 31 who were randomized to the control arm. All patients completed the study. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Each participant’s after-visit summary was reviewed for the presence of a simplified description of diagnosis and a simplified description of hospital interventions. There were 4 (6.6%) cases in which there was disagreement between reviewers regarding the simplified diagnosis. There were 7 (11.4%) cases in which there was disagreement regarding simplified intervention. Agreement was assessed using Cohen's Kappa, showing a strong level of agreement for simplified diagnosis (percentage of agreement = 93.4%, κ = 0.869) and a moderate agreement for simplified intervention (percentage of agreement = 88.5%, κ = 0.771).22

For our primary composite outcome, the intervention had a total of 4 (13.3%) patients that met the composite outcome compared with 2 (6.5%) in the control arm, for an absolute difference of 0.07 (95% CI -0.08 – 0.22; p = 0.425) (Figure 2). We found that a simplified diagnosis was present in 29 (96.7%) patients in the intervention arm compared with 4 (12.9%) in the control arm for a difference of 0.84 (95% CI 0.70 – 0.97; P <.001). The presence of a simplified intervention was found in 26 (86.7%) of patients in the intervention arm compared with 4 (12.9 %) patients in the control arm for a difference of 0.74 (95% CI 0.57 – 0.91; P <.001). A total of 7 (23.3%) patients had a Flesch-Kincaid grade level between 6th and 8th grade reading levels in the intervention arm compared with 3 (9.7%) in the control arm for a difference of 0.14 (95% CI -0.05 – 0.32 p = 0.182). The average Flesch-Kincaid grade level was 8.5 in the intervention arm compared with 10.6 in the control arm for a difference of -2.09 (95% CI -3.04 - -1.14; p < 0.001). (Figure 3). Neither group had any reported adverse events or serious adverse events.

Surveys of patients, nurses, and attending physicians revealed no major differences between the intervention and control groups (Table 2). Attending physicians and patients reported nearly identical perceptions of safety across both arms. Among nurses, there was a trend toward higher perceptions of safety in the control group (26/30 vs 30/31; absolute difference, −0.10; 95% CI, −0.18 to −0.02). However, 3 out of 4 cases in which safety was not rated as “top box” were attributable to factors unlikely to be related to the AI-generated text itself and instead reflected broader aspects of the discharge process or clinician-edited content. Lightly edited free-text responses describing these cases included: (1) mention of a neck clot that had been discussed with providers; (2) initially illegible instructions that were subsequently clarified by providers; (3) a last-minute medication prescribed prior to discharge; and (4) the addition of medical terminology by clinicians to otherwise plain-language AI-generated instructions.

Notably, attending physicians also demonstrated a trend toward perceiving greater empathy in responses in the intervention arm (20/30 vs 14/31; absolute difference, 0.22; 95% CI, 0.07 to 0.36).

We looked at the effect of changes made to the AI-generated text in the treatment arm. The total Levenshtein distance was 143 (SD 157), and the mean normalized Levenshtein edit distance, which is the percent of text that was edited, was 15% (SD 16%). A total of 9 (30%) of cases had minimal to no change. The Flesch-Kincaid grade level, limited to just the AI content (excluding other content placed in the discharge instructions), had a mean of 4.82 (+/- 0.87 SD) before editing, which increased to 5.27 (+/- 1.04 SD) after editing (p = 0.002).

Discussion

In this small randomized controlled trial, implementation of AI in a live clinical workflow was associated with improved patient-friendliness on exploratory and component measures, although no statistically significant difference was observed in the primary outcome. We demonstrated statistically significant improvements in simplified diagnoses, descriptions of interventions, and average readability scores in the intervention group. Our composite primary outcome, although improved in the intervention group, was not found to be statistically significant. In part, this may have been related to its restrictive nature. To achieve a primary outcome, a Flesch-Kincaid grade level between the 6th and 8th grade levels needed to be achieved in conjunction with the other 2 outcomes. This may have led to a decrease in the total number of positive outcomes, making comparisons between intervention and control groups difficult.

Our previous research demonstrated the potential for incorporating AI-generated, patient-friendly text into the discharge process.20 This study adds to this in showing exploratory evidence for improved patient-friendliness (1) compared with human-generated patient-facing after-visit summaries and (2) using a novel, EHR-integrated workflow. While other randomized studies have incorporated simplified, AI-generated language into a clinical workflow23, and done patient-facing studies of AI-generated language24,25 this project is a first to be patient facing and in a live clinical setting. This project also gathers feedback from both patients and healthcare providers on AI-generated content tailored to individual patient charts. While existing studies have explored patient perspectives on AI in healthcare25,26, this specific context is novel in that feedback was obtained from real patients in real time. We did not find any major differences in the perceptions of safety related to the discharge instructions between intervention and control groups, although objective measures of safety were not undertaken.

Our study has several limitations. First, our low number of patients with an FK grade level between 6 and 8 led to a low number of primary outcome results, which in turn makes detecting differences between two groups unlikely. We feel that this is a limitation as it may obscure notable differences between our intervention group and control group. We therefore had to resort to outcome variables that were not prespecified. Given that our outcomes that are not pre-specified are intuitive statistics to look at in composite studies, the risk of unreported comparisons is minimal.27

Another major limitation is the risk of confirmation bias in 2 of the 3 primary endpoints: simplified diagnosis and simplified intervention. Given notable differences between AI-generated text and clinician-generated text, reviewers who determined these two endpoints might have been able to tell which outputs were from AI and thus be biased. These results must be considered in this context.

Other limitations include its restriction to English-speaking patients, representing a drawback but also an opportunity for leveraging AI to broaden accessibility of patient-friendly summaries in the future.28,29 The study’s relatively small sample size was guided by feasibility; this contributed to fewer outcome events and reduced the likelihood of detecting significant differences in the primary outcome. Patients were identified at morning interdisciplinary rounds, which means enrollment may have been affected by the timing of discharge rather than consecutive sampling. We also included decision support and hard-stop reviews in the intervention arm that were not mirrored in the control arm, which might mean part of the observed effect of AI itself might be obscured by the effect of increased clinician attention.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial builds on our previous work and shows that using AI tools in real clinical settings can enhance the patient-friendliness of the after-visit summaries when compared with the current standard of care using human-generated after-visit summaries. The study revealed significant improvements in mean readability scores and increased presence of simplified diagnosis and interventions. These results underscore the potential of generative AI to craft patient-friendly materials directly from a patient's chart at scale, aligning with the broader healthcare goals of improving patient engagement and comprehension. Given the lack of randomized controlled trials in the growing field of AI research30, this study is also notable for its trial design. Future studies with larger participant pools, objective measures of safety, and continuous patient feedback are essential to explore and validate this AI use case further.
