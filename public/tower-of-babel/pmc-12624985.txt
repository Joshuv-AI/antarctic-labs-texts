Isolation and identification of two highly virulent Glaesserella parasuis serovar 7 strains in China
Authors: Wang Zhendong, Cai Yuchen, An Jiahui, Chao Lumen, Li Yufeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlaesserella parasuis (G. parasuis), the etiological agent of Glässer’ s disease, is a gram-negative bacterium and a common commensal in the upper respiratory tract of pigs, which can lead to fibrinous polyserositis, meningitis and polyarthritis. In this study, two highly virulent G. parasuis strains belongs to serovar 7, which is normally considered as an avirulent serovar of G. parasuis, were isolated from diseased pigs in China, named as vHPS7 and aHPS7. Animal experiments revealed that vHPS7 and aHPS7 were highly virulent and could lead the death of mice as that of Nagasaki. Cell adhesion and invasion assays showed that the ability of adhesion and invasion of these two serovar 7 isolates is significantly higher than that of non-virulent serovar 7 strains. To further investigate the phylogenic evolution of G. parasuis, a pan-genome analysis was performed and we found that the G. parasuis strains have high variable “open” pan-genome, especially, these two serovar 7 isolates belongs to different clade with classic virulent strains. In addition, the virulence factors of these isolates were predicted in VFDB and we found these two isolates have a similar profile with standard virulent serovar 5 strains rather than non-virulent serotype 7 isolates. Our data indicating that the “non-virulent” serovar 7 of G. parasuis is becoming a potential risk, and these genomic characterization and potential virulence factors will provide ways to further understand the mechanisms underlying pathogenicity of G. parasuis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04452-z.Keywords: Glaesserella parasuis, Virulence variation, Serovar 7, Whole genome sequencing, Virulence factors, Drug resistance

Backgroumd

Glaesserella parasuis, a gram- negative bacteria commonly located in the upper respiratory tract of porcine as an opportunistic pathogen, can lead to Glässer’s disease (GD), which characterized by fibrinous polyserositis, meningitis and polyarthritis [1]. It has caused huge economic losses in swine industry worldwide [2].

G. parasuis are classified into 15 standard serovars and many non-typable isolates [3]. Among fifteen standard serovars of G. parasuis, serovars 1, 5, 10, 12, 13, and 14 are considered as highly virulent and cause high mortality in pigs, serovars 2, 4, 8, and 15 are moderately virulent, and 3, 6, 7, 9, and 11 are considered as non-virulent [4, 5]. Serovar 5 and 4 are widely considered as the most common serovars existed in clinically sick pigs worldwide [2]. However, no absolute relationship between virulence and serovars have been found [6].

Bacterial pathogenesis is a multifactorial process, which include entry into the host, evasion of host defenses, bacterial multiplication and damage to tissues, especially, virulence factors play critical roles in the interactions between pathogens and hosts [7]. There are a number of virulence factors of G. parasuis have been described including transferrin, trimeric autotransporter (VtaA), lipooligosaccharide (LOS), cytolethal distending toxin (CDT) etc [7, 8]. LOS can induce the release of IL-6 and IL-8 from PBMEC and NPTr, but serve as only a minor role in invasion and adhesion [9]. G. parasuis could produce a novel IgA protease and escape the innate immune system by degradation of IgA [10]. And capsular polysaccharide had been associated with G. parasuis virulence as well as nasal colonization in pigs [11]. Most of the researches had focused on the potential virulent factors and pathogenic mechanism of classic highly virulent serovars, but there are few studies on the isolates with highly virulent belonged to non-virulent serovars [12, 13].

G. parasuis isolates belonged to serovar 7 from diseased pigs with characteristics of GD were identified. To determine the virulence of these isolates, mice infection, PK-15 cell adhesion and invasion were performed. Further, genomic analysis was performed to study the phylogenic and virulent formation of G. parasuis.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animals were purchased from the Comparative Medicine Center of Yangzhou University. All animal experiments were performed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Nanjing Agricultural University with the approval of the Department of Science and Technology of Jiangsu Province. All experimental procedures were conformed to institutional guidelines in accordance with international law.

Bacteria isolation and identification

The samples collected from lungs in a farm with more than 200 diseased pigs in Jiangsu Province, China. The bacteria were streaked in tryptic soy agar (TSA; OXOID, Hampshire, ENGLAND), supplemented with 5% bovine serum and 0.01% nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and incubated at 37 for 48 h. After culturing the bacteria, Gram staining and transmission electron microscopy were performed. The Gram- negative bacteria were identified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for the genus and serovars of G. parasuis [14, 15]. Genomic DNA was extracted with TIAN amp Bacteria DNA Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH) and used as templates, amplification was carried out according to the procedure of pre-denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min, pre-denaturation at 95 ℃ for 15 s, annealing at 56 d ℃ for 1 min, and extension at 72 ℃ for 1 min. The identified G. parasuis bacteria pellet were adsorbed to polyvinyl formal-carbon-coated grids (EMS) for 2 min, fixed with 2.5% gluteraldehyde for 1 min, respectively washed twice with PBS and water, and then negatively stained with 0.5% phosphotungstic acid for 20 s and observed with electronic microscope.

Bacterial adhesion and invasion assay

PK-15 cells were seeded into 12-well tissue culture plates. The monolayer was rinsed with sterile PBS to remove antibiotics and infected with G. parasuis at MOI of 100. The plates were centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min and incubated at 37 ℃ in 5% CO2. For adherence assay, after 1 h, cells were washed with PBS to remove non-specifically attached bacteria and treated by ddH2O, then the cells were harvested by scraping and transferred to sterile centrifuge tubes. The collected cells were smeared onto Trypticase Soy Agar plates and incubated at 37 ℃ for 48 h. For invasion assay, cell plates infected with G. parasuis were incubated at 37 ℃ in 5% CO2 for 2 h. After this treatment, non-specifically attached bacteria were removed by washing three times with PBS. Following antibiotics treatment for 1 h, cells were washed three times with PBS. Cells were broken by ddH2O and harvested by scraping. The bacteria were then diluted and smeared onto the TSA agar plates for colony forming. Adhesion and invasion assay were repeated independently for three times.

Virulence assessment using a BALB/c mice model

Mouse model has been used to determine the protective efficacy of experimental vaccine candidates [16]. So, mice could be used as a model to evaluate the virulence of G. parasuis strains. In our study, 65 female BALB/c mice at six weeks were used and randomly divide into seven groups with 5 for control, the experiment mice were infected using Nagasaki (HPS5), vHPS7, aHPS7 with two different doses of 5 × 109 and 1 × 1010, respectively. After infection by abdominal cavity, the symptoms and death of mice were recorded at 24 h, 48 h, and 96 h, respectively. After the experiment, all mice in the control group and the experimental group were euthanized by cervical dislocation, all experimental procedures were conformed to institutional guidelines in accordance with international law.

Genome sequencing

The genome DNA integrity was detected by the agarose gel electrophoresis and quantified by Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific). The whole genome of aHPS7 and vHPS7 were sequenced using PacBio Sequel platform and Illumina NovaSeq PE150. The tRNAscan-SE 1.31 [17] and RNAmmer 1.2 [18] were used to predicted the tRNAs and rRNAs.

Bioinformatic analysis

All genome data of G. parasuis published in Genbank were downloaded and used to perform the pan-genome analysis (until 2021 Apr) (Table. S1). Prokka was used to annotate the genome sequences [19], pan-genome analysis was performed by using Roary [20], the core-genome alignment phylogenic analysis was using Fasttree [21] and the phylogenic tree was constructed by iTOL [22]. Pairwise synteny comparisons among the three genomes were performed using MUMmer [23]. Multiple genome alignments were analyzed and visualized with Progressive Mauve [24]. Potential virulence factors were predicted using the Virulence Factor Database (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) [25]. Respectively Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) sequences were predict by CRISPR digger [26]. NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP) were performed to annotate the genome of G. parasuis. The circular genomes were visualized by Circos [27] For Multilocus sequence typing (MLST), allele numbers and sequence types (STs) were ssigned based on the PubMLST database [28]. Population structure and putative clonal complexes were inferred using the eBURST algorithm [29].

Statistical analysis

Three biological replicates were carried out for every experiment and results were recorded as mean value ± SD. GraphPad Prism 5 was used to analyze differences between groups (one-way ANOVA; Tukey’s post-hoc test, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05).

Results

Isolation and preliminary characterization of vHPS7and aHPS7 from pigs with characteristics of GD

The G. parasuis strains in this study (designated vHPS7 and aHPS7) were isolated from diseased pigs with characteristics of GD in different areas of Jiangsu Province, China, in 2017. The genus specific PCR assay indicated that these two strains are all belong to serovar 7 and designated as vHPS7 and aHPS7 (Fig. S1A). When cultured on TSA, they formed small brown- to- gray colonies. Gram staining showed that they are Gram- negative small bacterium (Fig. S1B). Moreover, the capsular substance of vHPS7 and aHPS7 could be clearly observed through a transmission electron microscopy (Fig. S1C).

vHPS7 and aHPS7 showed high adhesion and invasion ability in vitro

To evaluate the virulence of vHPS7 and aHPS7 in vitro, the bacterial adhesion and invasion assays were performed with PK-15 cells at MOI of 1:100. We found that the adhesion and invasion of vHPS7and aHPS7 to cells are significantly higher than that of reference serovar 7 strain 174 (Fig. 1).

vHPS7 and aHPS7 exhibited high virulence in BALB/c mice

Six weeks old BALB/c mice were infected with Nagasaki, vHPS7 and aHPS7 using two different doses. Some symptoms including depression, messy hair and acute death appeared in all three groups after 10 hours infection. Especially, the mice in groups infected using Nagasaki and vHPS7 were all dead at 24 h for both two doses (Fig. 2A and B). However, the mice survival infected using aHPS7 infection with 5 × 109 CFU/mL) and 1 × 1010 CFU/mL can be only last for 72 h and 48 h (Fig. 2A and B), respectively. At the same time, we found the BALB/c mice in control group are all in health status. Importantly, we also reisolated target vHPS7 and aHPS7 in the lungs from infected mice (data not shown).

General structure and features of the vHPS7 and aHPS7 genomes

vHPS7 contains a single, circular chromosome that is 2,275,841 base pairs (bp) in length, with genomic GC content of 39.93%. The chromosome encodes 1963 protein-coding genes, 58 tRNA genes and 19 tRNA genes (Fig. S2A). The complete circular genome of aHPS7 is found to be 2,355,923 bp in length, encodes 2051 protein-coding genes, with GC content of 39.92%, 58 tRNAs and 19 rRNAs (Fig. S2B). Pan-genome analysis of G. parasuis

Through pan-genomics analysis of the 109 G. parasuis isolates, we found that the G. parasuis strains have high variable “open” pan-genome. With the number increasing of genomics, the total genes of G. parasuis are also increased and the conserved gene numbers (core genome) is decreased (Fig. 3A). Also, the pan-genome of G. parasuis contains 8055 genes, but only 612 genes belonging to core genome, suggestion that there is a big diversity within this genus (Fig. 4B).

Pan-genome analysis of G. parasuis

Based on the gene presence and absence, a phylogenic analysis was performed, and we found that the whole G. parasuis genus could be clearly separated to two clades (Fig. S3), and each clade is relatively conserved (red box of Fig. S3). Further, a phylogenic tree based on the core gene alignment and integrated analysis with serovars was built, we found that the relative virulent serovars isolates is concentrated in one clade and the relative avirulent serovars isolates in another, indicating that two clades of G. parasuis is associated with virulence (Fig. 5).

MLST analysis demonstrate the diversity of G. parasuis

MLST is an important method in studying the epidemiology of prokaryote and be widely used. Until 2021 Apr, a total 498 STs were identified based on seven housekeeping genes (atpD, infB, mdh, rpoB, 6pgd, g3pd, frdB) in G. parasuis. Based on their allelic profiles, a population view of G. parasuis was diagramed by eBURST (Fig. S4). Despite there are some CCs were found, CC324, CC309 and CC246, etc., the virulent G. parasuis isolates were sporadic, such as Nagasaki (ST 24), SH0165 (ST31), ZJ0906 (ST19) and SC1401 (ST346), no link between virulence and MLST was found. In this study, aHPS7 belong to ST366 and vHPS7 belong to ST370, they are also sporadic STs. These finding also indicated the diversity within the virulent G. parasuis.

Comparative genomic analysis between aHPS7, vHPS7 and Nagasaki

Through the phylogenic analysis, we found two clades of G. parasuis, surprisingly, we found that our isolates, aHPS7 and vHPS7 belong to the relative avirulent clade. To further analysis the phylogenic difference between these isolates and classic virulent strains, we compared aHPS7 and vHPS7 with a standard serovar 5 virulent strain Nagasaki. Through X-alignment, we found that there are obviously different between these three isolates, even between aHPS7 and vHPS7 (Fig. S5A), which is consistent with the phylogenetic analysis. Synteny analyze show that despite there are some homologous regions between these isolates (red box in Fig. S5B), they also show a number of exchanges, rearrangement, insertion and deletion of chromosomes (Fig. S5B).

To further analysis the virulence formation of these serovar 7 isolates, we predicted the putative virulence factors in VFDB. Through compare the putative virulence factors profiles of aHPS7, vHPS7 with Nagasaki and an avirulent serovar 7 strains sHPS7, we found that two high virulent serovar 7 isolates have a similar virulence factor profiles with Nagasaki than sHPS7 (Table. S2). Also, a type IV pili cluster and an iron transport locus were identified in aHPS7 and vHPS7, which is presence in Nagasaki and absence from sHPS7.

Discussion

G. parasuis is an important bacterial pathogen in swine industry and caused great ecnomic loss to nursing pigs. The majority of previous studies is focus on the serovar 5, which is the most virulent and prevalent serovar of G. parasuis. However, in this study, two highly virulent isolates belonged to serovar 7, which is always be considered as non-virulent serovar, were isolated, from diseased pigs with characteristics of GD. BALB/c mice were infected with different dose of G. parasuis, we found that the mice infected with these two serovar 7 isolates showed similar symptoms and mortality with that of serotype 5. Compared with avirulent strain, adhesion and invasion of two serovar 7 isolates to PK-15 cells were significantly up-regulated. Further, the genomic analysis shows that two virulent serovar 7 isolates cluster into different branches with classic virulent G. parasuis in phylogenetics.

Genomic analysis is widely used to study the genetic diversity and molecular epidemiology. Through pan-genome analysis, we found that G. parasuis have a very “open” pan-genome and the core-genome is very small, indicating that G. parasuis is very diversity. The MLST analyzes also showed the same results. Also, based on the gene presence and absence and core genome alignment, we found G. parasuis could clearly separate into two clades, which are associated with serovars and virulence. These finding is accordance with Howell et al. [30]. Interestingly, we found that two virulent serovar 7 isolates clustered into the relative avirulent clade, our finding indicating that the virulent serovar 7 isolates may have the different pathogenesis mechanism.

The horizontal transfer of virulence factors from virulent strains is an important way to convert virulence. To analyze the virulence formation of these serovar 7 isolates, we predicted the virulence factor profiles in VFDB, we found that the virulence factor profiles of these two isolates is more similar with that of standard serovar 5 virulent strain Nagasaki rather than an avirulent serovar 7 isolates. Some of them were previously identified to be associated with adhesion, invasion and virulence, for example, type IV pili was the only virulence factor in Neisseria meningitidis, and it also could mediate the adhesion in epithelial cells of pathogenic Escherichia coli [31]. The hitABC genes of Haemophilus iron transport locus were proved to be essential for Haemophilus influenzae iron transport [32]. Interestingly, type IV pili cluster and an iron transport locus were identified in two isolates and Nagasaki, which is absence from sHPS7.

However, there are many other ways associated with the virulence. A study demonstrated that Glässer’ s disease is being caused by strains with capsular types, but there are strains with uncharacterized polysaccharide capsules capable of causing GD [33]. Another novel Glaesserella sp. strain with virulence factors putatively acquired by horizontal transfer was recently described in Australia [34]. And these could potentially be explained that acquisition of virulence factors from more virulent strains by natural transformation system present in these species [35]. In addition, another reference has proposed that virulence can be weaked due to passage in vitro [36]. Combined with our study, these data assumed that the virulence of G. parasuis is multi-factor and not highly linked with serovars [12, 37].

However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, the virulence assessment was conducted in a murine model, which, while useful for preliminary evaluation, may not fully recapitulate the complex host-pathogen interactions occurring in the natural porcine host. Differences in immune response, tissue tropism, and disease progression between mice and pigs could influence the translational relevance of our findings. Second, the conclusions regarding the pathogenic potential of serovar 7 are drawn from only two isolates, which limits the generalizability of our results to the broader population of G. parasuis serovar 7 strains. Future studies involving a larger number of isolates and experimental infections in pigs are necessary to validate these observations and to better understand the epidemiological significance of virulent serovar 7 strains in the field.

Our results demonstrated that the non-virulent serovar 7 of G. parasuis also have the pathogenesis potential. Several researches already reported that serovar 7 is prevalence in China and some other regions with a certain level of isolation rate [38, 39]. Considering our previously research, which demonstrated that the serovar 7 is a multi-drug resistant reservoir [40], these data indicating that some isolates of the “non-virulent” serovar 7 of G. parasuis are becoming a potential risk not only because of antimicrobial resistance but also as a pathogenic factor.
