Operators on complemented posets
Michal Botur, Ivan Chajda, Helmut Länger

Abstract
Given a complemented poset 𝐏 = ( P , ≤ , 0 , 1 ) \mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1) , we can assign to every x ∈ P x\in P the set x + x^{+} of all its complements. We study properties of the operator + on P P , in particular, we are interested in the case when x + x^{+} forms an antichain or when + is involutive or antitone. We apply + to the set Min ⁡ U ⁡ ( x , y ) \Min U(x,y) of all minimal elements of the upper cone U ⁡ ( x , y ) U(x,y) of x , y x,y and to the set Max ⁡ L ⁡ ( x , y ) \Max L(x,y) of all maximal elements of the lower cone L ⁡ ( x , y ) L(x,y) of x , y x,y . By using + we define four binary operators on P P and investigate their properties that are close to adjointness. We present an example of a uniquely complemented poset that is not Boolean. In the last section we study the orthogonality relation induced by complementation. We characterize when two elements of the Dedekind-MacNeille completion of 𝐏 \mathbf{P} are orthogonal to each other. Finally, we extend the orthogonality relation from elements to subsets and we prove that two non-empty subsets of P P are orthogonal to each other if and only if their convex hulls are orthogonal to each other within the poset of all non-empty convex subsets of P P .

Introduction

An element
a
of a complemented lattice
\mathbf{L}=(L,\vee,\wedge,0,1)
may have more than one complement if
\mathbf{L}
is not distributive. However, R. P. Dilworth
[
6
]
showed that in a distributive complemented (i.e. Boolean) lattice the complementation is unique. An element of a modular lattice may have several complements and it may happen that for an appropriate choice of these complements the lattice in question becomes orthomodular and for another choice it need not become orthomodular. One can overcome the problem of choosing an appropriate complement by considering for each
a\in L
the set
a^{+}
of all complements of
a
without preferring any single of them. Hence,
+
is in fact a unary operator on
\mathbf{L}
, see e.g.
[
2
]
. For some reasons concerning so-called unsharp logics, we are interested in complemented posets instead of lattices, see e.g.
[
5
]
. In posets we usually work with the sets
\Min U(x,y)
and
\Max L(x,y)
of all minimal elements of the upper cone
U(x,y)
of the elements
x
and
y
and of all maximal elements of the lower cone
L(x,y)
of
x
and
y
, respectively. These sets substitute the missing elements
x\vee y
and
x\wedge y
in the case when the supremum or infimum of
x
and
y
, respectively, do not exist. Of course, in the case when
x\vee y
exists it coincides with
\Min U(x,y)
and a similar situation occurs for
x\wedge y
. The operators
\Min U
and
\Max L
serve as logical connectives disjunction and conjunction, respectively, in certain kinds of unsharp logics, see again
[
5
]
.

Now it is a question whether also for a complemented poset
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
it makes sense to define and study the operator
+
assigning to every element
a
of
P
the set of all its complements, and moreover, whether this operator shares similar properties with the corresponding operator in lattices. It makes no sense to study
+
for Boolean posets since in this case every element
a
has a unique complement
a^{\prime}
, thus
a^{+}=\{a^{\prime}\}
like in Boolean lattices. However, we show that a finite uniquely complemented poset need not be distributive and hence Boolean. We demonstrate the properties of
+
in several examples of complemented posets being neither Boolean nor lattices. Finally, using this operator we define other derived operators having similar properties like
t
-norms and we investigate corresponding adjointness properties.

Basic concepts

Basic concepts on posets are included e.g. in
[
2
]
. Let us recall some well-known concepts and let us define some additional ones.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
be a poset,
a,b\in P
,
A,B\subseteq P
and
\,{}^{\prime}
a unary operation on
P
. Then
\Max A
and
\Min A
denote the set of all maximal and minimal elements of
A
, respectively. We define

\displaystyle A\leq B

\displaystyle\text{ if }x\leq y\text{ for all }x\in A\text{ and all }y\in B,

\displaystyle L(A)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in P\mid x\leq A\},

\displaystyle U(A)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in P\mid A\leq x\},

\displaystyle A\leq B

\displaystyle\text{ if }x\leq y\text{ for all }x\in A\text{ and }y\in B,

\displaystyle A\leq_{1}B

\displaystyle\text{ if for every }x\in A\text{ there exists some }y\in B\text{ with }x\leq y,

\displaystyle A\leq_{2}B

\displaystyle\text{ if for every }y\in B\text{ there exists some }x\in A\text{ with }x\leq y.

Hence
(U,L)
forms the Galois correspondence between the posets
(2^{P},\subseteq)
and
(2^{P},\subseteq)
induced by the binary relation
\leq
on
P
.

Here and in the following we identify singletons with their unique element. Instead of
L(\{a\})
,
L(\{a,b\})
,
L(A\cup\{a\})
,
L(A\cup B)
and

we simply write
L(a)
,
L(a,b)
,
L(A,a)
,
L(A,B)
and
LU(A)
. Analogously we proceed in similar cases.

The subset
A
of
P
is called an

•

upset
if
x\in A
,
y\in P
and
x\leq y
imply
y\in A
,

•

down set
if
x\in A
,
y\in P
and
y\leq x
imply
y\in A
.

The smallest upset containing
a
is
\uparrow a:=\{x\in P\mid a\leq x\}
and the smallest down set containing
a
is
\downarrow a:=\{x\in P\mid x\leq a\}
. The smallest upset including
A
is

A^{\uparrow}:=\bigcup_{x\in A}(\uparrow x)=\{y\in P\mid\text{there exists some }x\in A\text{ with }x\leq y\}

and the smallest down set including
A
is

A^{\downarrow}:=\bigcup_{x\in A}(\downarrow x)=\{y\in P\mid\text{there exists some }x\in A\text{ with }y\leq x\}.

The mappings
A\mapsto A^{\uparrow}
and
A\mapsto A^{\downarrow}
are closure operators on the poset
(2^{P},\subseteq)
, i.e. we have

•

A\subseteq A^{\uparrow}
;
A\subseteq B\Rightarrow A^{\uparrow}\subseteq B^{\uparrow}
;
(A^{\uparrow})^{\uparrow}=A^{\uparrow}
,

•

A\subseteq A^{\downarrow}
;
A\subseteq B\Rightarrow A^{\downarrow}\subseteq B^{\downarrow}
;
(A^{\downarrow})^{\downarrow}=A^{\downarrow}
.

Moreover, it is easy to see

•

A\leq_{1}B\Leftrightarrow A\subseteq B^{\downarrow}\Leftrightarrow A^{\downarrow}\subseteq B^{\downarrow}
,

•

A\leq_{2}B\Leftrightarrow B\subseteq A^{\uparrow}\Leftrightarrow B^{\uparrow}\subseteq A^{\uparrow}
.

The
poset

\mathbf{P}
is called

[
8

) if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:

\displaystyle L\big(U(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx LU\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle UL\big(U(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx U\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle U\big(L(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx UL\big(U(x,z),U(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle LU\big(L(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx L\big(U(x,z),U(y,z)\big).

The
poset

\mathbf{P}
is called

0

. Such a poset will be denoted by

and in the whole paper we assume that

is

A reflexive and transitive relation on a set is called a

, in particular, the relations
\leq_{1}
and
\leq_{2}
are quasiorders on the power set
2^{P}
of
P
.

If the supremum or infimum of
A
exists in the poset
\mathbf{P}
, we denote it by
\bigvee A
and
\bigwedge A
, respectively.

Now let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded poset. Recall that
b
is called a

of

if
a\vee b=1
and
a\wedge b=0
. We write

in this case. This relation is considered as

. The
poset

\mathbf{P}
is called

if every element of

has at least one complement. A distributive complemented poset is called a

. A unary operation
\,{}^{\prime}
on

is called an

if it satisfies the identity

, it is called

if
x,y\in P

and it is called a

if

for all

[

]
. For the reader’s convenience we repeat the proof.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded distributive poset and

. Then

and hence the complementation in a Boolean poset is a unary operation that is an antitone involution
(

]
)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle L(b)

\displaystyle=L(1,b)=L\big(U(c,a),b\big)=LU\big(L(c,b),L(a,b)\big)=LU\big(L(c,b),0\big)=LU\big(L(b,c),0\big)=

\displaystyle=LU\big(L(b,c),L(a,c)\big)=L\big(U(b,a),c\big)=L(1,c)=L(c).

∎

The operator
+

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded poset. We define the following unary operator on subsets of
P
:

A^{+}:=\{x\in P\mid x\perp y\text{ for all }y\in A\}

for
A\subseteq P
. Hence, if
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
is a Boolean poset then
x^{+}=x^{\prime}
for all
x\in P
. Obviously,
(^{+},^{+})
is the Galois correspondence between the posets
(2^{A},\subseteq)
and
(2^{A},\subseteq)
induced by the symmetric relation
\perp
. From this fact we obtain immediately

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset and
A,B\subseteq P
. Then

(i)

A\subseteq(A^{+})^{+}
,

(ii)

A\subseteq B
implies
B^{+}\subseteq A^{+}
,

(iii)

\big((A^{+})^{+}\big)^{+}=A^{+}
,

(iv)

A\subseteq B^{+}
is equivalent to
B\subseteq A^{+}
.

Since
\mathbf{P}
is non-trivial, we have
\emptyset^{+}=P
and
P^{+}=\emptyset
.

A
subset

A
of
P
will be called
closed
if
(A^{+})^{+}=A
. Let
\Cl(\mathbf{P})
denote the set of all closed subsets of
P
. Then clearly
\Cl(\mathbf{P})=\{A^{+}\mid A\subseteq P\}
. Because of
A^{+}\cap A^{++}=\emptyset
for all
A\subseteq P
we have that
\BCl(\mathbf{P}):=\big(\Cl(\mathbf{P}),\subseteq,{}^{+},\emptyset,P\big)
forms a complete ortholattice with orthocomplementation
+
and

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i\in I}A_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}\right)^{+}\right)^{+},

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i\in I}A_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i\in I}A_{i}

for all families
(A_{i};i\in I)
of closed subsets of
P
.

Observe that
A^{+}=\bigcap\limits_{x\in A}x^{+}
for all
A\subseteq P
and that in case that
\mathbf{P}
is uniquely complemented, in particular if
\mathbf{P}
is Boolean, we have
\Cl(\mathbf{P})=\{\emptyset,P\}\cup\{x^{+}\mid x\in P\}
.

.

Let

(i)

(ii)

(iv)

Let

A
poset
is said to be
of finite length
if it has no infinite chains. Let
(P,\leq)
be a poset of finite length and
A\subseteq P
. Then
\Max A\neq\emptyset
,
\Min A\neq\emptyset
,

,

,

and
\Min A\leq_{2}A
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
be a poset of fine length and
A,B\subseteq P
with
A\subseteq B
. Then the following holds:

(i)

\Max A\leq_{1}\Max B
and
\Max L(B)\leq_{1}\Max L(A)
,

(ii)

\Min B\leq_{2}\Min A
and
\Min U(A)\leq_{2}\Min U(B)
.

According to the remark at the beginning of this section we have
A\leq_{1}\Max A
. Now
\Max A\subseteq A\subseteq B\leq_{1}\Max B
and therefore
\Max A\leq_{1}\Max B
. Finally we conclude

and therefore
\Max L(B)\leq_{1}\Max L(A)

.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length and consider the following statements:

(i)

,

(ii)

for all

(iii)

(iv)

\big(\Min U(x,y)\big)^{+}\leq_{1}\Max L(x^{+},y^{+})

,

(v)

\Min U(x^{+},y^{+})\leq_{2}\big(\Max L(x,y)\big)^{+}
for all

.

(i)
implies
(ii)
and
(iii)
. Moreover,
(iv)
implies
(ii)
, and
(v)
implies
(iii)
. If for all

,

has a greatest element
x_{2}
then

(iv)
. If, finally, for all

,

has a smallest element

then
(i)

(v)
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in P
.

That (i) implies (ii) and (iii) is clear.

If
\mathbf{P}
satisfies (iv) and

If
\mathbf{P}

Now assume (i) and that for all

has a greatest element

.

x\in P

has a smallest element

and

k^{+}\not\leq_{2}f^{+}
.

That the generalized De Morgan’s laws do not hold in general is shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length that is not uniquely complemented. Then the generalized de Morgan’s laws

\displaystyle\big(\Min U(x,y)\big)^{+}

\displaystyle\approx\Max L(x^{+},y^{+}),

\displaystyle\big(\Max L(x,y)\big)^{+}

\displaystyle\approx\Min U(x^{+},y^{+})

do not hold.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{P}
is complemented, but not uniquely complemented, there exists some
a\in P
with
|a^{+}|>1
. Let
b,c\in a^{+}
with
b\neq c
. Suppose
\big(\Min U(x,y)\big)^{+}\approx\Max L(x^{+},y^{+})
holds. Then

a^{+}=\big(\Min U(a,0)\big)^{+}=\Max L(a^{+},0^{+})=\Max L(a^{+})\subseteq L(a^{+}).

Since
b,c\in a^{+}
we have
b\in a^{+}\subseteq L(a^{+})\subseteq L(c)
, i.e.
b\leq c
. From symmetry reasons we have also
c\leq b
and hence
b=c
, a contradiction. Now suppose
\big(\Max L(x,y)\big)^{+}\approx\Min U(x^{+},y^{+})
holds. Then

a^{+}=\big(\Max L(a,1)\big)^{+}=\Min U(a^{+},1^{+})=\Min U(a^{+})\subseteq U(a^{+}).

Since
b,c\in a^{+}
we have
c\in a^{+}\subseteq U(a^{+})\subseteq U(b)
, i.e.
b\leq c
. From symmetry reasons we have also
c\leq b
and hence
b=c
, a contradiction.
∎

Example 3.10
.

The complemented poset
(P,\leq,0,1)
visualized in Fig. 8

does not satisfy the generalized De Morgan’s laws since

\displaystyle\big(\Min U(a,0)\big)^{+}

\displaystyle=a^{+}=\{c,d,g,h\}\neq\{0\}=\Max L(a^{+})=\Max L(a^{+},0^{+}),

\displaystyle\big(\Max L(a,1)\big)^{+}

\displaystyle=a^{+}=\{c,d,g,h\}\neq\{1\}=\Min U(a^{+})=\Min U(a^{+},1^{+}).

4
Derived operators

Using the operator
+
we define other important operators in complemented posets of finite length as follows:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length and
a,b\in P
. Define

\displaystyle a\circ b

\displaystyle:=\Max L(a,b),

\displaystyle a\to b

\displaystyle:=\Min U(a^{+},b),

\displaystyle a\odot b

\displaystyle:=\Max L\big(b,U(a,b^{+})\big),

\displaystyle a\hookrightarrow b

\displaystyle:=\Min U\big(a^{+},L(a,b)\big).

Observe that e.g.

\displaystyle\Min U\big(a^{+},L(a,b)\big)

\displaystyle=\Min\big(U(a^{+})\cap UL(a,b)\big)=\Min\Big(U(a^{+})\cap U\big(\Max L(a,b)\big)\Big)=

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(a^{+},\Max L(a,b)\big).

The following can be easily seen:

\displaystyle x\circ y=y\circ x,

\displaystyle\text{ and }x\circ y=x\text{ if }x\leq y,

\displaystyle 1\to x=x,

\displaystyle\text{ and }x\to y=1\text{ if }x\leq y,

\displaystyle x\odot 1=x,

\displaystyle\text{ and }y\odot x=x\text{ if }x\leq y,

\displaystyle 1\hookrightarrow x=x,

\displaystyle\text{ and }x\hookrightarrow y=1\text{ if }x\leq y.

The couple
(\circ,\to)
is said to form an
adjoint pair
if

x\circ y\leq z\text{ is equivalent to }x\leq y\to z

for all
x,y,z\in P
.

Definition 4.2
.

We call a binary operator
*
on a complemented poset
(P,\leq,0,1)
of finite length

•

monotone from the left if
x\leq y
implies
x*z\leq_{1}y*z
,

•

monotone from the right if
x\leq y
implies
z*x\leq_{2}z*y
,

•

weakly antitone from the left if
x*y\geq 1*y
,

•

weakly monotone from the right if
x*y\leq x*1

(
x,y,z\in P
)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite height. Then the following holds:

(i)

\circ
and
\odot
are monotone from the left,

(ii)

\to
and
\hookrightarrow
are monotone from the right.

Proof.

Let
a,b,c\in P
with
a\leq b
.

(i)

We have
L(a,c)\subseteq L(b,c)
and hence

a\circ c=\Max L(a,c)\leq_{1}\Max L(b,c)=b\circ c.

Moreover, we have
\{c\}\cup U(b,c^{+})\subseteq\{c\}\cup U(a,c^{+})
and hence

L\big(c,U(a,c^{+})\big)\subseteq L\big(c,U(b,c^{+})\big)

whence

a\odot c=\Max L\big(c,U(a,c^{+})\big)\leq_{1}\Max L\big(c,U(b,c^{+})\big)=b\odot c.

(ii)

We have
U(c^{+},b)\subseteq U(c^{+},a)
and hence

c\to a=\Min U(c^{+},a)\leq_{2}\Min U(c^{+},b)=c\to b.

Moreover, we have
\{c^{+}\}\cup L(c,a)\subseteq\{c^{+}\}\cup L(c,b)
and hence

U\big(c^{+},L(c,b)\big)\subseteq U\big(c^{+},L(c,a)\big)

whence

c\hookrightarrow a=\Min U\big(c^{+},L(c,a)\big)\leq_{2}\Min U\big(c^{+},L(c,b)\big)=c\hookrightarrow b.

∎

On the other hand, weak monotonicity from the right of the operator
\odot
yields that the complementation of the poset in question is unique.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length and assume
\odot
to be weakly monotone from the right. Then
\mathbf{P}
is uniquely complemented.

Proof.

Let
a\in P
and
b,c\in a^{+}
. Then
U(c,b^{+})=1
since
a\in b^{+}
and
c\vee a=1
. Hence

b=\Max L(b,1)=\Max L\big(b,U(c,b^{+})\big)=c\odot b\leq c\odot 1=c.

Now
c\leq b
follows from symmetry reasons. Together we obtain
b=c
. ∎

The next two results show how the properties of the pair
(\odot,\hookrightarrow)
influence the behavior of the pair
(\circ,\to)
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length and
a,b,c\in P
. Then the following hold:

(i)

If
\hookrightarrow
is weakly antitone from the left then
a\circ b\leq c
implies
a\leq b\to c
.

(ii)

If
\odot
is weakly monotone from the right then
a\leq b\to c
implies
a\circ b\leq c
.

Proof.

The following are equivalent:
a\circ b\leq c
,
\Max L(a,b)\leq c
,
L(a,b)\leq c
. Moreover, the following are equivalent:
a\leq b\to c
,
a\leq\Min U(b^{+},c)
,
a\leq U(b^{+},c)
.

(i)

If
\hookrightarrow
is weakly antitone from the left and
a\circ b\leq c
then

a=1\hookrightarrow a\leq b\hookrightarrow a=\Min U\big(b^{+},L(b,a)\big)

and
L(a,b)\leq c
and hence

U(b^{+},c)\subseteq U\big(b^{+},L(a,b)\big)\subseteq U(a),

i.e.
a\leq U(b^{+},c)
showing
a\leq b\to c
.

(ii)

If
\odot
is weakly monotone from the right and
a\leq b\to c
then

\Max L\big(b,U(c,b^{+})\big)=c\odot b\leq c\odot 1=c

and
a\leq U(b^{+},c)
and hence

L(a,b)\subseteq L\big(U(b^{+},c),b\big)\subseteq L(c),

i.e.
L(a,b)\leq c
showing
a\circ b\leq c
.

∎

Remark 4.6
.

In a Boolean poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
the operator
\hookrightarrow
is weakly antitone from the left and the operator
\odot
is weakly monotone from the right since

\displaystyle x\hookrightarrow y

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(x^{\prime},L(x,y)\big)=\Min UL\big(U(x^{\prime},x),U(x^{\prime},y)\big)=\Min ULU(x^{\prime},y)=

\displaystyle=\Min U(x^{\prime},y)\geq y=1\hookrightarrow y,

\displaystyle x\odot y

\displaystyle=\Max L\big(y,U(x,y^{\prime})\big)=\Max LU\big(L(y,x),L(y,y^{\prime})\big)=\Max LUL(y,x)=

\displaystyle=\Max L(y,x)\leq x=x\odot 1.

Corollary 4.7
.

If
\mathbf{P}
is a complemented poset of finite length,
\hookrightarrow
is weakly antitone from the left and
\odot
is weakly monotone from the right then
(\circ,\to)
form an adjoint pair.

Of course, in a Boolean poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
the operator
\hookrightarrow
is weakly antitone from the left since in such a poset we have

\displaystyle x\hookrightarrow y

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(x^{\prime},L(x,y)\big)=\Min U\big(L(x,y),x^{\prime}\big)=\Min UL\big(U(x,x^{\prime}),U(y,x^{\prime})\big)=

\displaystyle=\Min ULU(y,x^{\prime})=\Min U(y,x^{\prime})\geq y=1\hookrightarrow y.

However, we can present an example of a non-uniquely complemented poset that is not a lattice and where
\hookrightarrow
is weakly antitone from the left.

Example 4.8
.

The complemented poset
(P,\leq,0,1)
from Figure 8 is neither distributive since

L\big(U(e,f),b\big)=L(1,b)=L(b)\neq 0=LU(0,0)=LU\big(L(e,b),L(f,b)\big)

nor a lattice since
a\vee b
does not exist. Now let
x,y,z\in P
. Then

\displaystyle U(x^{+})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P&\text{if }x=1,\\
1&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\circ 1

\displaystyle=\Max L(x,1)=x,

\displaystyle x\to y

\displaystyle=\Min U(x^{+},y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Min U(0,y)=y&\text{if }x=1,\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle x\hookrightarrow y

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(x^{+},L(x,y)\big)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Min U\big(0,L(1,y)\big)=y&\text{if }x=1,\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The assumption of
(i)
of Theorem
4.5
is satisfied since
x\hookrightarrow y\geq y=1\hookrightarrow y
. Accordingly, the conclusion of
(i)
of Theorem
4.5
holds:

If
x\circ y\leq z
and
y=1
then
x=x\circ 1=x\circ y\leq z=1\to z=y\to z
.

If
x\circ y\leq z
and
y\neq 1
then
x\leq 1=y\to z
.

We now turn our attention again to the adjointness of the operators
\circ
and
\to
. In the proof we use similar arguments as in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length. If

U\big(x^{+},L(x,y)\big)\subseteq U(y)\text{ for all }x,y\in P

(1)

then for all
x,y,z\in P

x\circ y\leq z\text{ implies }x\leq y\to z.

If

L\big(x,U(x^{+},y)\big)\subseteq L(y)\text{ for all }x,y\in P

(2)

then for all
x,y,z\in P

x\leq y\to z\text{ implies }x\circ y\leq z.

Proof.

Let
a,b,c\in P
. Then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle a\circ b

\displaystyle\leq c,

\displaystyle\Max L(a,b)

\displaystyle\leq c,

\displaystyle L(a,b)

\displaystyle\leq c,

\displaystyle L(a,b)

\displaystyle\subseteq L(c),

\displaystyle U(c)

\displaystyle\subseteq UL(a,b).

Moreover, the following are equivalent;

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq b\to c,

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq\Min U(b^{+},c),

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq U(b^{+},c),

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\in LU(b^{+},c),

\displaystyle L(a)

\displaystyle\subseteq LU(b^{+},c),

\displaystyle U(b^{+},c)

\displaystyle\subseteq U(a).

If
a\circ b\leq c
and (1) then
U(c)\subseteq UL(a,b)
and hence

U(b^{+},c)=U(b^{+})\cap U(c)\subseteq U(b^{+})\cap UL(a,b)=LU\big(b^{+},L(a,b)\big)\subseteq U(a)

showing
a\leq b\to c
. If, conversely,
a\leq b\to c
and (2) then
L(a)\subseteq LU(b^{+},c)
and hence

L(a,b)=L(a)\cap L(b)\subseteq LU(b^{+},c)\cap L(b)=L\big(U(b^{+},c),b\big)\subseteq L(c)

showing
a\odot b\leq c
.
∎

Example 4.10
.

The poset from Figure 8 satisfies
(1)
since

U\big(x^{+},L(x,y)\big)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}=U(y)&\text{ if }x=1\\
\subseteq U(x^{+})=\{1\}\subseteq U(y)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 4.11
.

Every Boolean poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
satisfies
(1)
and
(2)
since

\displaystyle U\big(x^{\prime},L(x,y)\big)

\displaystyle=UL\big(U(x^{\prime},x),U(x^{\prime},y)\big)=ULU(x^{\prime},y)=U(x^{\prime},y)\subseteq U(y),

\displaystyle L\big(x,U(x^{\prime},y)\big)

\displaystyle=LU\big(L(x,x^{\prime}),L(x,y)\big)=LUL(x,y)=L(x,y)\subseteq L(y).

In the next theorem we prove a similar assertion as in Theorem
4.9
, but for the operators
\odot
and
\hookrightarrow
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length. If

U\Big(x^{+},L\big(x,U(x^{+},y)\big)\Big)\subseteq U(y)\text{ for all }x,y\in P

(3)

then for all
x,y,z\in P

x\odot y\leq z\text{ implies }x\leq y\hookrightarrow z.

If

L\Big(x,U\big(x^{+},L(x,y)\big)\Big)\subseteq L(y)\text{ for all }x,y\in P

(4)

then for all
x,y,z\in P

x\leq y\hookrightarrow z\text{ implies }x\odot y\leq z.

Proof.

Let
a,b,c\in P
. From the proof of Theorem
4.12
we have that
a\odot b\leq c
is equivalent to
L\big(b,U(a,b^{+})\big)\subseteq L(c)
and that
a\leq b\hookrightarrow c
is equivalent to
U\big(b^{+},L(b,c)\big)\subseteq U(a)
. If
a\odot b\leq c
and (3) then
L\big(b,U(a,b^{+})\big)\subseteq L(c)
and hence

\displaystyle U\big(b^{+},L(b,c)\big)

\displaystyle=U\big(b^{+},L(b)\cap L(c)\big)\subseteq U\Big(b^{+},L(b)\cap L\big(b,U(a,b^{+})\big)\Big)=

\displaystyle=U\Big(b^{+},L\big(b,U(a,b^{+})\big)\Big)\subseteq U(a)

showing
a\leq b\hookrightarrow c
. If, conversely,
a\leq b\hookrightarrow c
and (4) then
U\big(b^{+},L(b,c)\big)\subseteq U(a)
and hence

\displaystyle L\big(b,U(a,b^{+})\big)

\displaystyle=L\big(b,U(a)\cap U(b^{+})\big)\subseteq L\Big(b,U\big(b^{+},L(b,c)\big)\cap U(b^{+})\Big)=

\displaystyle=L\Big(b,U\big(b^{+},L(b,c)\big)\Big)\subseteq L(c)

showing
a\odot b\leq c
.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

Every Boolean poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
satisfies
(3)
and
(4)
since

\displaystyle U\Big(x^{\prime},L\big(x,U(x^{\prime},y)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=U\Big(x^{\prime},LU\big(L(x,x^{\prime}),L(x,y)\big)\Big)=U\big(x^{\prime},LUL(x,y)\big)=

\displaystyle=U\big(x^{\prime},L(x,y)\big)=UL\big(U(x^{\prime},x),U(x^{\prime},y)\big)=ULU(x^{\prime},y)=

\displaystyle=U(x^{\prime},y)\subseteq U(y),

\displaystyle L\Big(x,U\big(x^{\prime},L(x,y)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=L\Big(x,UL\big(U(x^{\prime},x),U(x^{\prime},y)\big)\Big)=L\big(x,ULU(x^{\prime},y)\big)=

\displaystyle=L\big(x,U(x^{\prime},y)\big)=LU\big(L(x,x^{\prime}),L(x,y)\big)=LUL(x,y)=L(x,y)\subseteq

\displaystyle\subseteq L(y).

The poset from Figure 8 satisfies (3) since

U\Big(x^{+},L\big(x,U(x^{+},y)\big)\Big)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}=L(y)&\text{ if }x=1\\
\subseteq U(x^{+})=\{1\}\subseteq U(y)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Recall that a poset
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
is called
modular
if one of the following equivalent conditions is satisfied:

\displaystyle U\big(L(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle=UL\big(x,L(y,z)\big)\text{ for all }x,y,z\in P\text{ with }z\leq x,

\displaystyle L\big(U(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle=LU\big(x,U(y,z)\big)\text{ for all }x,y,z\in P\text{ with }x\leq z.

The next result shows that stronger versions of modularity imply adjointness if
\odot
and
\hookrightarrow
.

Corollary 4.14
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a complemented poset of finite length. If

U\big(L(A,y),C\big)=UL\big(A,U(y,C)\big)\text{ for all }y\in P\text{ and all }A,C\in 2^{P}\setminus\{\emptyset\}\text{ with }C\leq A

(5)

then for all
x,y,z\in P

x\odot y\leq z\text{ implies }x\leq y\hookrightarrow z.

If

L\big(U(A,B),z\big)=LU\big(A,L(B,z)\big)\text{ for all }z\in P\text{ and all }A,B\in 2^{P}\setminus\{\emptyset\}\text{ with }A\leq z

(6)

then for all
x,y,z\in P

x\odot y\leq z\text{ implies }x\leq y\hookrightarrow z.

Proof.

We have that (5) implies

\displaystyle U\Big(x^{+},L\big(x,U(x^{+},y)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=U\Big(L\big(U(x^{+},y),x\big),x^{+}\Big)=UL\big(U(x^{+},y),U(x,x^{+})\big)=

\displaystyle=ULU(x^{+},y)=U(x^{+},y)\subseteq U(y)

for all
x,y\in P
, i.e. (3). Moreover, (6) implies

\displaystyle L\Big(x,U\big(x^{+},L(x,y)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=L\Big(U\big(L(x,y),x^{+}\big),x\Big)=LU\big(L(x,y),L(x^{+},x)\big)=LUL(x,y)=

\displaystyle=L(x,y)\subseteq L(y)

for all
x,y\in P
, i.e. (4). Now Corollary
4.14
follows from Theorem
4.12
.
∎

5
Orthogonality, Dedekind-MacNeille completion and convex subsets

In this section we investigate the binary relation
\perp
of orthogonality introduced in Section 2 within the Dedekind-MacNeille completion (see e.g.
[
2
]
) of a poset and within the poset of non-empty convex subsets of a bounded poset.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
be an arbitrary poset. Put

D(\mathbf{P}):=\{L(A)\mid A\subseteq P\}=\{B\subseteq P\mid LU(B)=B\}.

Then
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P}):=(D(\mathbf{P}),\subseteq)
is a complete lattice, called the
Dedekind-MacNeille completion
of
\mathbf{P}
. It is well-known that in
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i\in I}A_{i}

\displaystyle=LU\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i\in I}A_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i\in I}A_{i}.

Moreover, it is well-known that if
a,a_{i}\in P
for all
i\in I
then

\displaystyle L(a)=\bigvee_{i\in I}L(a_{i})

\displaystyle\text{ if and only if }a=\bigvee_{i\in I}a_{i},

\displaystyle L(a)=\bigwedge_{i\in I}L(a_{i})

\displaystyle\text{ if and only if }a=\bigwedge_{i\in I}a_{i}.

Further, the mapping
x\mapsto L(x)
is an embedding of
\mathbf{P}
into
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})
, i.e. for all
x,y\in P
,
x\leq y
if and only if
L(x)\subseteq L(y)
. If
\mathbf{P}
has a smallest element
0
and a greatest element
1
then
L(0)
and
L(1)
are the smallest, respectively greatest element of
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})
. Hence, if
\mathbf{P}
is bounded and
a,b\in P
then
a\perp_{\mathbf{P}}b
if and only if
L(a)\perp_{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})}L(b)
.

We can characterize the orthogonality of two elements of the Dedekind-MacNeille completion of a bounded poset as follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded poset and
A,B\in D(\mathbf{P})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

A\perp_{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})}B
,

(ii)

\bigvee_{\mathbf{P}}(A\cup B)=1
and
x\wedge y=0
for all
x\in A
and all
y\in B
.

Proof.

We have
A\perp_{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})}B
if and only if
A\vee_{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})}B=L(1)
and
A\wedge_{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})}B=L(0)
. Now the following are equivalent:
A\vee_{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})}B=L(1)
,
LU(A,B)=L(1)
,
U(A,B)=U(1)
,
\bigvee_{\mathbf{P}}(A\cup B)=1
. Moreover
A\wedge_{\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})}B=L(0)
if and only if
A\cap B=\{0\}
. Now assume
A\cap B=\{0\}
. If
a\in A
,
b\in B
,
c\in P
and
c\leq a,b
then
a\in A=LU(A)
and
b\in B=LU(B)
. Hence
c\in LU(A)\cap LU(B)=A\cap B=\{0\}
, i.e.
c=0
showing
a\wedge b=0
. If, conversely
x\wedge y=0
for all
x\in A
and all
y\in B
and
d\in A\cap B
then
d=d\wedge d=0
showing
A\cap B=\{0\}
. So we finally obtain that
A\cap B=\{0\}
is equivalent to the fact that
x\wedge y=0
for all
x\in A
and all
y\in B
.
∎

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
be a poset,
a,b,c\in P
and
A,B\subseteq P
. Recall that
A
is called
convex
if
x,z\in A
,
y\in P
and
x\leq y\leq z
together imply
z\in A
. Let
\Conv\mathbf{P}
denote the set of all convex subsets of
\mathbf{P}
. We define a binary relation
\sqsubseteq
on
2^{P}
by

A\sqsubseteq B\text{ if and only if }A\leq_{1}B\text{ and }A\leq_{2}B.

Since the intersection of convex subsets of
\mathbf{P}
is again a convex subset of
\mathbf{P}
, there exists a smallest (with respect to inclusion) convex subset
\overline{A}
of
\mathbf{P}
including
A
, the so-called
convex hull
of
A
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(P,\leq)
be a poset and
A\subseteq P
. Then

\overline{A}=\{x\in P\mid\text{there exist }y,z\in A\text{ with }y\leq x\leq z\}.

Proof.

Let
a,c\in\overline{A}
and
b\in P
and assume
a\leq b\leq c
. Then there exist
d,e\in A
with
d\leq a
and
c\leq e
. Now
d\leq b\leq e
which shows
b\in\overline{A}
. We have proved
\overline{A}\in\Conv\mathbf{P}
. Now let
B\in\Conv\mathbf{P}
with
A\subseteq B
and let
f\in\overline{A}
. Then there exist
g,h\in A
with
g\leq f\leq h
. Since
g,h\in B\in\Conv\mathbf{P}
we conclude
f\in B
. This shows
\overline{A}\subseteq B
completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Observe that
\overline{A}=A^{\downarrow}\cap A^{\uparrow}
. Moreover, let
\Convs\mathbf{P}
denote the set of all non-empty elements of
\Conv\mathbf{P}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
be a poset. Then the following holds:

(i)

\BConv\mathbf{P}:=(\Conv\mathbf{P},\sqsubseteq)
is a poset,

(ii)

if
\mathbf{P}
has a smallest element
0
and a greatest element
1
then

\BConvs\mathbf{P}:=(\Convs\mathbf{P},\sqsubseteq,\{0\},\{1\})

is a bounded poset.

Proof.

(i)

Since
\leq_{1}
and
\leq_{2}
are quasiorders, the same is true for
\sqsubseteq
. Let
A,B\in\Conv\mathbf{P}
, assume
A\sqsubseteq B
and
B\sqsubseteq A
and let
a\in A
. Because of
B\leq_{2}A
there exists some
b\in B
with
b\leq a
, and because of
A\leq_{1}B
there exists some
c\in B
with
a\leq c
. Since
b\leq a\leq c
and
b,c\in B\in\Conv\mathbf{P}
we conclude
a\in B
. This shows
A\subseteq B
. From symmetry reasons we have also
B\subseteq A
, i.e.
A=B
.

(ii)

According to (i),
(\Convs\mathbf{P},\sqsubseteq)
is a poset. It is easy to see that
\{0\}\sqsubseteq C\sqsubseteq\{1\}
for all
C\in\Convs\mathbf{P}
.

∎

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded poset and
A,B\subseteq P
. Then we define

A\perp_{\mathbf{P}}B\text{ if and only if }x\perp_{\mathbf{P}}y\text{ for all }x\in A\text{ and all }y\in B.

Finally, we show that two non-empty subsets of a bounded poset
(P,\leq,0,1)
are orthogonal if and only if their convex hulls are orthogonal in the bounded poset of non-empty convex subsets of
P
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded poset and
A,B
non-empty subsets of
P
. Then
A\perp_{\mathbf{P}}B
if and only if
\overline{A}\perp_{\BConvs\mathbf{P}}\overline{B}
.

Proof.

First assume
A\perp_{\mathbf{P}}B
. Let
C\in\Convs\mathbf{P}
with
\overline{A},\overline{B}\sqsubseteq C
. Assume
a\in C
. Then there exist
b\in\overline{A}
and
c\in\overline{B}
with
b,c\leq a
. Hence there exist
d\in A
and
e\in B
with
d\leq b
and
e\leq c
. Since
d,e\leq a
and
d\vee e=1
we conclude
a=1
. Hence
\overline{A}\vee_{\Convs\mathbf{P}}\overline{B}=\{1\}
. Now let
D\in\Convs\mathbf{P}
with
D\sqsubseteq\overline{A},\overline{B}
. Assume
f\in D
. Then there exist
g\in\overline{A}
and
h\in\overline{B}
with
f\leq g,h
. Hence there exist
i\in A
and
j\in B
with
g\leq i
and
h\leq j
. Since
f\leq i,j
and
i\wedge j=0
we conclude
f=0
. Hence
\overline{A}\wedge_{\Convs\mathbf{P}}\overline{B}=\{0\}
. This shows
\overline{A}\perp_{\BConvs\mathbf{P}}\overline{B}
. Conversely, assume
\overline{A}\perp_{\BConvs\mathbf{P}}\overline{B}
. Let
k\in A
,
l\in B
and
m,n\in P
with
m\leq k,l\leq n
. Then
\downarrow m\sqsubseteq\overline{A},\overline{B}\sqsubseteq\uparrow n
and hence
\downarrow m=\{0\}
and
\uparrow n=\{1\}
whence
m=0
and
n=1
. This shows
k\perp_{\mathbf{P}}l
. Since
k
and
l
were arbitrary elements of
A
and
B
, respectively, we get
A\perp_{\mathbf{P}}B
.
∎
