Taylor expansions over generalised power series
Vincent Bagayoko, Vincenzo Mantova

Abstract
We study the existence of formal Taylor expansions for functions defined on fields of generalised series. We prove a general result for the existence and convergence of those expansions for fields equipped with a derivation and an exponential function, and apply this to the case of standard fields of transseries, such as log \log - exp \exp transseries and ω \omega -series.



Introduction

In classical analysis, given a ring of real (or complex) smooth germs at a
point
a\in\mathbb{R}
, computing the Taylor series

f\mapsto f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)X+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(a)X^{2}+\cdots

yields a differential ring homomorphism into the ring of power series

\mathbb{R}\llbracket X\rrbracket
. For analytic functions,
by
definition
, this is an
embedding
whose image is contained in the
subring
\mathbb{R}\left\{\!\!\!\{X\}\!\!\!\right\}
of the

convergent
power series: in this case, the power series of
f

encodes all the local information about
f
, and by uniqueness of analytic
continuations, also its behaviour on the maximal interval over which
f

extends to an analytic function.

More general power series can be used to study real analytic functions at
points where they fail to be analytic.
Transseries
in particular
allow writing expansion that include the symbols
\exp
,
\log
in order to
account for essential singularities of respectively exponential and
logarithmic type. For instance, Stirling’s formula for the
\Gamma(x)

function can be read as the transserial expansion

\Gamma(x)=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{x\log x-x-\frac{1}{2}\log x}+\frac{B_{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}e^{x\log x-x-\frac{7}{2}\log x}+\cdots

Transseries come in different flavours: grid-based transseries

[
17
,
22
]
and log-exp transseries
[
12
,
17
,
15
]
are
contained in the class-sized field
\mathbb{R}{\langle\!\langle}\omega{\rangle\!\rangle}
of

\omega
-series
[
8
]
, and larger systems of so-called hyperseries

[

5
]
including transexponential terms, and even
surreal numbers
[
7
,
8
,
4
]
, fit in that picture. For the
purposes of this note, we deal with transseries in an abstract way. First, let
us fix an algebra
\mathbb{A}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket

of
Noetherian series
(see
Section

1.3
),
where
\mathfrak{M}
is a partialy ordered monoid and
\mathbf{K}
is a field,
both of which can be proper classes. Noetherian series come with a natural
formal notion of infinite sum
\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}
whenever the family

(f_{i})_{i\in I}
is
summable
. We then fix a
derivation

\partial
which is
strongly (
\mathbf{K}
-)linear
, meaning it
commutes with infinite sums and it vanishes on
\mathbf{K}
.

Since
\mathbb{A}
is a differential ring, the Taylor series of some
f\in\mathbb{A}
is defined as

f+f^{\prime}X+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{2!}X^{2}+\cdots=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{f^{(n)}}{n!}X^{n}.

This induces
[
29
]
a differential ring homomorphism from

\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
.

Since
\mathbb{A}
allows some infinite sums, one may ask for which
\delta\in\mathbb{A}
the family
\left(\frac{f^{(k)}}{k!}\delta^{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, in which case its sum is an element of
\mathbb{A}

Given a power series
P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}\in\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
with coefficients in
\mathbb{A}
, let the
convergence locus
of

\operatorname{Conv}(P)\coloneqq\left\{\delta\in\mathbb{A}:(P_{k}\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ is summable}\right\}.

One may easily verify that
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
is always convex,

0\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
, and for instance
\operatorname{Conv}(P+Q)\supseteq\operatorname{Conv}(P)\cap\operatorname{Conv}(Q)
(Lemma
2.6
). We write

\operatorname{Conv}(f)
for the convergence locus of the Taylor series of
f
.

The main result of this paper are explicit and almost sharp bounds for

\operatorname{Conv}(f)
when
\mathbb{A}
is an algebra of transseries. More
precisely, we say that
\mathbb{A}
is a
differential pre-logarithmic
Hahn field
if
\mathbf{K}
is an ordered field and we are given a morphism
of ordered monoids
\ell:(\mathfrak{M},\cdot,1,\prec)\rightarrow(\mathbb{A},+,0,<)
, which we call
pre-logarithm
, satisfying the
following assumptions:

1.

\ell(\mathfrak{M})
is closed under
truncation
(see
Definition
1.10
);

2.

for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
,
\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\coloneqq\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}}{\mathfrak{m}}=(\ell(\mathfrak{m}))^{\prime}
.

If
\mathbf{K}
is an ordered exponential field
[
24
]

\ell

\log:\mathbb{A}^{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}

Our conditions and results are stated in terms of valuation theory, using the
dominance relations
\preccurlyeq

\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\preccurlyeq x^{-1}\Rightarrow(\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})^{{\dagger}}\preccurlyeq x^{-1}\text{\quad and\quad}\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\succ x^{-1}\Rightarrow(\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})^{{\dagger}}\asymp\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}.

(1)

This condition, as arbitrary it may seem, is satisfied in most contexts of

we obtain:

Theorem 1

(
Theorem

5.2
for
\triangle=\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{S}}
)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a differential
pre-logarithmic Hahn field satisfying (
1

Then, for all

, we have

\operatorname{Conv}(f)=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)}\operatorname{Conv}(\mathfrak{m})\supseteq\left\{\delta\in\mathbb{A}:\delta\prec x,\mathfrak{m^{{\dagger}}}\delta\prec 1\text{ for all }\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)\right\}.

The bound is often sharp, meaning that
\operatorname{Conv}(f)
is often equal to the
right hand side: if
\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\delta\succcurlyeq 1
for some

\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)

, then
\delta\notin\operatorname{Conv}(f)
(see Remark

where the Taylor series of

converges on some
\delta\asymp x
, in which

We shall actually prove an even stronger statement. Many fields of
transseries admit a
composition law
: a function that takes
f,g\in\mathbb{A}
with
g\in\mathbb{A}^{>\mathbf{K}}
and
return
f\circ g\in\mathbb{A}
, such that

1.

the map
f\mapsto f\circ g
is strongly
\mathbf{K}
-linear;

2.

(\log f)\circ g=\log(f\circ g)
;

3.

(f\circ g)=(f^{\prime}\circ g)g^{\prime}
;

4.

(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ(g\circ h)
.

We are then interested in whether the identity

f\circ(g+\delta)=f\circ g+(f^{\prime}\circ g)\delta+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}\circ g}{2!}\delta^{2}+\cdots

holds, and for which
\delta
. We formalise this notion by fixing an arbitrary
strongly linear operator
\triangle:\mathbb{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
.
For
P\in\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
, let
\operatorname{Conv}_{\triangle}(P)
be the convergence locus of the power series
\triangle(P)
(meaning
that we apply
\triangle
on each coefficient of
P
) and

\operatorname{Conv}_{\triangle}(f)
be the same for the Taylor series of
f\in\mathbb{A}
in place of

. We shall prove the following:

Theorem 2

(
Theorem

5.2
)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a differential pre-logarithmic Hahn field satisfying (
1
) and let
\triangle:\mathbb{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
be strongly linear morphism of algebras. For all
f\in\mathbb{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Conv}_{\triangle}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcap_{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)}\operatorname{Conv}_{\triangle}(\mathfrak{m})

\displaystyle\supseteq

\displaystyle\left\{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{B}:\varepsilon\prec\triangle(x),\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger})\varepsilon\prec 1\text{ for all }\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)\right\}.

We also show (Theorems
5.5
and
5.10
) that

\triangle
commutes with families of analytic functions on
\mathbb{S}

and
\mathbb{T}

. We then
apply these results in the case of
\omega
-series, taking
\triangle:f\mapsto f\circ g
, and show:

Theorem 3
.

Let

with
g>\mathbb{R}
and

\delta\prec g

(\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\circ g)\delta\prec 1

\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}f

f\circ(g+\delta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{f^{(k)}\circ g}{k!}\delta^{k}.

The same conclusion applies for other maps
\triangle
. For instance, we could
take

to be surreal numbers using the composition of
[

]
.

\bullet

Écalle
[

\bullet

Van den Dries, Macintyre and Marker
[
16

showed

\bullet

Schmeling
[
30

showed that
\omega

on transserial fields that are closed under exponentiation, and that they
have Taylor expansions with optimal convergence locus. Unfortunately, his
proof is incomplete.

\bullet

Van der Hoeven
[

showed that the
theorem above is valid in the subfield of
\mathbb{R}{\langle\!\langle}\omega{\rangle\!\rangle}
of grid-based transseries.

\bullet

Berarducci and Mantova defined
[
8
, Theorem 6.3]
a

on Conway’s

, and showed

that a series

f\circ(\xi+\delta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{f^{(k)}\circ\xi}{k!}\delta^{k}

at every

for small enough (but

depending on

and
\xi
.

\bullet

Van den Dries, van der Hoeven and Kaplan

[

showed that the theorem above is valid
in their field of so-called logarithmic hyperseries.

In particular, there is no proof in the literature of the optimal result for

\omega
-series or even
\log

\exp
transseries. Our method is designed to
be as general as possible, and we will use it subsequently in order to derive

[

]
of the field of logarithmic

We prove the main theorem by switching perspective. Instead of fixing
f
and
looking at which
\delta
’s make the Taylor series of
f
around
g

convergent, we fix
\delta
and
g
and study which series in
\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
converge around
g
at least as far as
\>\delta
.
Fixing
(g,\delta)\in\mathbb{A}^{>\mathbf{K}}\times\mathbb{A}
, the
operator

\mathbb{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}\>;\>f\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{f^{(k)}\circ g}{k!}\delta^{k}

can be obtained in the following three steps:

\mathbb{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket\>;\>f\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{f^{(k)}}{k!}X^{k},

(2)

\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket\>;\>\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(P_{k}\circ g)X^{k},

(3)

\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}\hskip 8.00003pt;\>\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}\delta^{k},

(4)

each of which defines a strongly linear morphism of algebras. The first step
itself can be seen as an infinite sum of iterated operators

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{k!}(X\cdot\overline{\partial})^{k}

(5)

evaluated at
a\in\mathbb{K}
, where
\overline{\partial}
is the operator

\overline{\partial}:\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket\>;\>\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}^{\prime}X^{k}.

(6)

As a consequence of van der Hoeven’s implicit function
theorem
[
21
]
, the summability of (
5
) only
requires the operator
X\cdot\overline{\partial}
to commute with infinite
sums and to be contracting in a valuation theoretic sense. What makes
convergence of Taylor series difficult is the fact that the operation
(
4
) is
not
defined, in general, on the whole algebra

\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
. In order to obtain the largest
subalgebra on which all operations can be performed, we are to find the domain
of (
4
), then its preimage under (
3
), and then
the preimage of the latter under (
2
). This in turn leads us
to study conditions under which
X\cdot\overline{\partial}
is contracting
and commutes with sums, and under which the infinite sum
(
5
) ranges in that last domain.

We determine the subalgebra of Noetherian series of
\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
on which (
4
) is defined in

Section

3.1
. A large part of the problem then reduces to
finding subalgebras of Noetherian series of
\mathbb{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
between which endomorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
and derivations on

\mathbb{A}
can be extended as in (
3
,

6

Sections
3.2
and
4.3
.
We then combine our findings in
Section

5

main theorems and apply them to
\omega
-series.

1.1
Noetherian orderings

We first introduce the types of ordered sets that will be the supports of our
formal series throughout the paper.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(\mathbf{X},<)
be a partially ordered class. A

chain
in
\mathbf{X}
is a linearly
ordered subclass of
\mathbf{X}
. A
decreasing
chain
in
\mathbf{X}
is chain
\mathbf{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{X}
without minimal element, i.e. with

\forall y\in\mathbf{Y},\exists z\in\mathbf{Y},(z<y).

An
antichain
in
\mathbf{X}
is a
subclass
\mathbf{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{X}
, no two distinct elements of
which are comparable, i.e. with

\forall y,z\in\mathbf{Y},y\leqslant z\Longrightarrow y=z.

We say that
(\mathbf{X},<)
is

Noetherian
if there are
no infinite decreasing chains and no infinite antichains in
(\mathbf{X},<)
.

Noetherianity is a strengthening of well-foundedness (no decreasing chains),
and a weakening of well-orderedness (the conjunction of linearity and
well-foundedness). Noetherian orderings are sometimes called
well-partial-orderings. It will be convenient to rely on the notion of bad
sequence and minimal bad sequence of
[
28
]
. If
(\mathbf{X},<_{\mathbf{X}})
is an ordered class, then a
bad sequence
in
\mathbf{X}
is a sequence
u:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbf{X}
such that there are
no
numbers
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with

i<j
and
u_{i}\leqslant_{\mathbf{X}}u_{j}
.

Lemma 1.2
.

[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
Let

(\mathbf{X},<_{\mathbf{X}})
be a partially ordered class. The following
statements are equivalent

a)

(\mathbf{X},<_{\mathbf{X}})
is
Noetherian.

b)

There is no bad sequence in

(\mathbf{X},<_{\mathbf{X}})
.

c)

Every sequence in
\mathbf{X}
has a
weakly increasing subsequence.

1.2
Noetherian subsets in ordered monoids

An
ordered monoid
is a tuple
(\mathfrak{M},\cdot,1,\prec)
where
(\mathfrak{M},\cdot,1)
is a monoid and

is
a partial ordering on

(7)

We fix an ordered monoid
(\mathfrak{M},\cdot,1,\prec)
. For

As in

[

]

1.4
Dominance relation and valuation

Let
\mathbb{A}=\textbf{{{K}}}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket

be an algebra of Noetherian series. Given
s,t\in\mathbb{A}
, we
write
s\preccurlyeq t
if for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}s
, there is an
\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}t
with
\mathfrak{m}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{n}
. Then

\preccurlyeq
is a linear quasi-ordering on
\mathbb{A}
. We write
\prec

for the corresponding (strict) ordering
s\prec t\Longleftrightarrow(s\preccurlyeq t\wedge t\mathrel{\not\preccurlyeq}s)
. We have
s\prec t
if and only if

t\neq 0
, and for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}s
, there is an

\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}t
with
\mathfrak{m}\prec\mathfrak{n}
. Note
that the inclusion
\mathfrak{M}\subseteq\mathbb{A}\setminus\{0\}

preserves the orderings on
(\mathfrak{M},\prec)
and
(\mathbb{A}\setminus\{0\},\prec)
respectively. We define

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{\preccurlyeq}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{s\in\mathbb{A}:\operatorname{supp}s\preccurlyeq 1\}=\{s\in\mathbb{A}:s\preccurlyeq 1\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{\prec}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{s\in\mathbb{A}:\operatorname{supp}s\prec 1\}=\{s\in\mathbb{A}:s\prec 1\}\text{, \quad and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{\prec s}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{t\in\mathbb{A}:t\prec s\}

for all
s\in\mathbb{A}
. Series in
\mathbb{A}^{\prec}
are said

infinitesimal
, whereas series in

\mathbb{A}^{\preccurlyeq}
are said
bounded
.
Note that
\mathbb{A}^{\preccurlyeq}=\mathbf{K}\oplus\mathbb{A}^{\prec}
.
The algebra
\mathbb{A}^{\preccurlyeq}
is always local with maximal ideal

\mathbb{A}^{\prec}
(
[
6
, Proposition 2.8]
).

Suppose that
(\mathfrak{M},\prec)
is a linearly ordered Abelian group. Then
it is well-known
[
19
]
that
\mathbb{A}
is a field and

[
23
]
that
\mathbb{A}^{\preccurlyeq}
is a valuation ring of

\mathbb{A}
. In that case, for all
s,t\in\mathbb{A}
, we have
s\preccurlyeq t
if and only if
t\prec s
is false. We write
write
s\asymp t
if
s\preccurlyeq t
and
t\preccurlyeq s
. Then
\preccurlyeq
is a dominance relation as per

[
2
, Definition 3.1.1]
. For
s\in\mathbb{A}^{\times}
, so

\operatorname{supp}s\neq\varnothing
, we write

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{s}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\max\operatorname{supp}s\in\mathfrak{M},

\displaystyle\tau_{s}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle s(\mathfrak{d}_{s})\mathfrak{d}_{s}\in\mathbf{K}^{\times}\mathfrak{M},\text{\quad and}

\displaystyle c_{s}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle s(\mathfrak{d}_{s})\in\mathbf{K}^{\times}.

respectively for the
dominant monomial
,

dominant term
and
leading
coefficient
of
s
. When
s,t
are non-zero,
we have
s\prec t
(resp.
s\preccurlyeq t
, resp.
s\asymp t
) if and only
if
\mathfrak{d}_{s}\prec\mathfrak{d}_{t}
(resp.
\mathfrak{d}_{s}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{d}_{t}
, resp.
\mathfrak{d}_{s}=\mathfrak{d}_{t}
). Moreover, any
s\in\mathbb{A}^{\times}
can be written
uniquely as

s=c_{s}\mathfrak{d}_{s}(1+\varepsilon_{s})

(9)

where
\mathfrak{d}_{s}\in\mathfrak{M}
,
c_{s}\in\mathbf{K}^{\times}
and

\varepsilon_{s}\prec 1
.

Assume finally that
\mathbf{K}
is an ordered field, while
(\mathfrak{M},\prec)
is still a linearly ordered Abelian group. Then we have a positive
cone

\mathbb{A}^{>}\coloneqq\{s\in\mathbb{A}:s\neq 0\wedge c_{s}>0\}
.

on
\mathbb{A}
, that is, defining
s<t\Longleftrightarrow t-s\in\mathbb{A}^{>}
, the structure
(\mathbb{A},+,\times,<,\prec)
is an
ordered valued field with convex valuation ring
\mathbb{A}^{\preccurlyeq}
.
We write

\mathbb{A}^{>,\succ}\coloneqq\{s\in\mathbb{A}:s>\mathbf{K}\}=\{s\in\mathbb{A}:s\geqslant 0\wedge s\succ 1\}

Series in
\mathbb{A}^{>,\succ}
are called
positive
infinite
.

Remark 1.8
.

If
\mathbf{K}
is algebraically closed and

\mathfrak{M}
is divisible, then
[

]
the field

is algebraically closed. If

1.5
Summable families

We fix an algebra
\mathbb{A}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket

of Noetherian series.

Definition 1.9
.

Let
\mathbf{I}
be a class. A family
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
in

\mathbb{A}
is said summable if

i.

\bigcup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\operatorname{supp}s_{i}

is a Noetherian subset of
(\mathfrak{M},\succ)
, and

ii.

\{i\in\mathbf{I}:\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}s_{i}\}

is finite for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
.

Then we may define the sum
\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}s_{i}
of
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
as the series

\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}s_{i}\coloneqq\mathfrak{m}\longmapsto\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}(\mathfrak{m})\in\mathbb{A}.

If only
i
holds, then we say that
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
is
weakly summable
. For
s\in\mathbb{A}
, the family
of terms
(s(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m})_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}
is
summable with sum

\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}s(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m}=s.

Definition 1.10
.

A
truncation
of
s
is a series
of the form
\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{I}}s(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m}
where
\mathfrak{I}
is a subclass of
\mathfrak{M}
which is
initial for the ordering
\prec
. A subclass
\mathbf{C}
of
\mathbb{A}
is
said
closed under truncation
if any truncation of an
element in
\mathbf{C}
lies in
\mathbf{C}
.

As a consequence of Lemma
1.2
, we obtain:

Lemma 1.11
.

Let
\mathbf{I}
be a class and let

(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a family in
\mathbb{A}
. Then
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
is summable (resp. weakly summable) if and only if for each
injective sequence (resp. sequence)
i:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbf{I}
and each sequence
(\mathfrak{m}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\mathfrak{m}_{k}\in\operatorname{supp}s_{i(k)}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there
are a
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
with
k<l
and
\mathfrak{m}_{k}\succ\mathfrak{m}_{l}
(resp.
\mathfrak{m}_{k}\succcurlyeq\mathfrak{m}_{l}
).

Proposition 1.12
.

[
21
, Proposition 3.1(e)]

Let
\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J}
be classes and let
(\mathbf{I}_{j})_{j\in\mathbf{J}}
be a family of subclasses of
\mathbf{I}
such that

\mathbf{I}
is the disjoint union
\mathbf{I}=\bigsqcup_{j\in\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{I}_{j}
. Let
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a summable
family. Then for all
j\in\mathbf{J}
, the family
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{j}}
is summable, the family
(\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{j}}s_{i})_{j\in\mathbf{J}}
is summable, and

\sum_{j\in\mathbf{J}}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}_{j}}s_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}s_{i}.

We have the following corollary:

Lemma 1.13
.

Let
\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J}
be classes and let
(s_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in\mathbf{I}\times\mathbf{J}}
be a summable family in

\mathbb{A}
. For each
i_{0}\in\mathbf{I}
and for each
j_{0}\in\mathbf{J}
, the families
(s_{i_{0},j})_{j\in\mathbf{J}}
and
(s_{i,j_{0}})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
are summable. Moreover families
\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbf{J}}s_{i,j}\right)_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
and
\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}s_{i,j}\right)_{j\in\mathbf{J}}
are summable, with

\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbf{J}}s_{i,j}\right)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbf{I}\times\mathbf{J}}s_{i,j}=\sum_{j\in\mathbf{J}}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}s_{i,j}\right).

We leave it to the reader to check that the sum of two summable families is
summable:

Lemma 1.14
.

Let
\mathbf{I}
be a class and let
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
and
(t_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be summable families in

\mathbb{A}
and let
c\in\mathbf{K}
. The family
(s_{i}+ct_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
is summable with
\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}(s_{i}+ct_{i})=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}s_{i}+c\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}t_{i}
.

Let

be summable

Proof.

where the

∎

Proposition 1.17
.

Let
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a summable family
and let
(t_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a weakly summable family. Then the

(s_{i}\cdot t_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
is summable.

Proof.

Set
\mathfrak{S}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\operatorname{supp}s_{i}
and

\mathfrak{T}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\operatorname{supp}t_{i}
. The class

\bigcup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}(\operatorname{supp}s_{i})\cdot(\operatorname{supp}t_{i})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}\cdot\mathfrak{T}
is Noetherian by
Lemma
1.3
. For
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
, the classes

\mathfrak{S}_{0}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{S}:\exists\mathfrak{t}\in\mathfrak{T},\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}\}
and

\mathfrak{T}_{0}=\{\mathfrak{t}\in\mathfrak{T}:\exists\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}\}
are
both finite. Thus the class
\mathfrak{X}\coloneqq\{i\in\mathbf{I}:\exists\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{S}_{0},\mathfrak{s}\in\operatorname{supp}s_{i}\}
is finite by summability of
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
.
We deduce that
\{i\in\mathbf{I}:\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}s_{i}t_{i}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{X}
is finite. Therefore
(s_{i}t_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
is summable.
∎

Proposition 1.18
.

Let
\mathbf{I}
be a class and let
f:\mathbf{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
be an arbitrary function. Let

(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a summable family in
\mathbb{A}
and let

\delta\in\mathbb{A}^{\preccurlyeq}
. The family
(s_{i}\cdot\delta^{f(i)})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}

Proof.

The family
(\delta^{f(i)})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
is weakly summable by
Proposition
1.4
, so this follows from Proposition
1.17
.
∎

Lemma 1.19
.

Let
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a family
in
\mathbb{A}
. Assume that there are Noetherian subclasses
\mathfrak{S}

and
\mathfrak{T}
of
(\mathfrak{M},\succ)
with
\mathfrak{T}\prec 1

and a function
f:\mathbf{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that for
all
i\in\mathbf{I}
, we have

\operatorname{supp}s_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}^{f(i)}\cdot\mathfrak{S}.

If
(s_{j})_{j\in\mathbf{J}}
is summable whenever
\mathbf{J}\subseteq\mathbf{I}
and

is finite, then
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}

is summable.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
is not summable. So
there is an injective sequence
(i_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence
(\mathfrak{m}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathfrak{m}_{0}\nsucc\mathfrak{m}_{1}\nsucc\cdots
and
\mathfrak{m}_{k}\in\operatorname{supp}s_{i_{k}}
for all

. We have
\{\mathfrak{m}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}^{\infty}\cdot\mathfrak{S}
where

\mathfrak{T}^{\infty}\cdot\mathfrak{S}

by Lemma
1.3
and Proposition
1.4
. So

is Noetherian and we may

(\mathfrak{m}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is constant. Fix

\mathfrak{t}\in\mathfrak{T}^{\infty}
and
\mathfrak{s}\in S
with

\mathfrak{m}_{k}=\mathfrak{t}\mathfrak{s}
for all

. We
have
\mathfrak{t}\in\mathfrak{T}^{f(i_{k})}
for all

.
By Proposition

, this implies that

∎

1.7
Strongly linear functions

Let
\mathbb{A}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
and

\mathbb{B}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{N}\rrbracket
be algebras of
Noetherian series over
\mathbf{K}
. Consider a function
\Phi:\mathbb{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
which is
\mathbf{K}
-linear. Then
\Phi
is said

strongly linear
if for every
summable family
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
in
\mathbb{A}
, the family
(\Phi(s_{i}))_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
in
\mathbb{B}
is summable, with

\Phi\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}s_{i}\right)=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\Phi(s_{i}).

Definition 1.20
.

A function
\Phi:\mathfrak{M}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is said

Noetherian
if for all
Noetherian subsets
\mathfrak{S}
of
(\mathfrak{M},\succ)
, the family

(\Phi(\mathfrak{m}))_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{S}}
is summable in

\mathbb{B}
.

Proposition 1.21
.

[
21
, Proposition 3.5]

Assume that
\Phi:\mathfrak{M}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is
Noetherian. Then
\Phi
extends uniquely into a strongly linear function

\hat{\Phi}:\mathbb{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
. Furthermore, if

\Phi
is a morphism of monoids, then
\hat{\Phi}
is a morphism of
algebras.

It follows that a linear function
\Phi:\mathbb{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is strongly linear if and only if
\Phi\upharpoonleft\mathfrak{M}
is Noetherian and
\Phi(s)=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}s(\mathfrak{m})\Phi(\mathfrak{m})
for all
s\in\mathbb{A}
.

Corollary 1.22
.

Any embedding of ordered monoids
f:\mathfrak{M}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{N}
extends uniquely to a strongly
linear embedding of algebras
\mathbb{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
.

Lemma 1.23
.

Let

be strongly linear. Let

be a weakly

. Then

Write

(\mathfrak{s})_{\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{S}}
is summable, so

(\Phi(\mathfrak{s}))_{\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{S}}
is summable. So the

\mathfrak{T}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{S}}\operatorname{supp}\Phi(\mathfrak{s})

, we have

(\Phi(s_{i}))_{i\in\mathbf{I}}

2.1
Elementary analysis on valued fields

Let
(\mathbf{F}_{0},v_{0}),(\mathbf{F}_{1},v_{1})
be (possibly class-sized)
valued fields with non-trivial valuations. For
x_{0},\rho_{0}\in\mathbf{F}_{0}

and
x_{1},\rho_{1}\in\mathbf{F}_{1}
we write

\displaystyle B_{0}(x_{0},\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{y\in\mathbf{F}_{0}:v_{0}(y-x_{0})\geqslant v(\rho_{0})\}\text{\quad and}

\displaystyle B_{1}(x_{1},\rho_{1})

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{y\in\mathbf{F}_{1}:v_{1}(y-x_{1})\geqslant v(\rho_{1})\}.

Then
\mathbf{F}_{0}
and
\mathbf{F}_{1}
have a natural topology called the

valuation topology
. We say that a subclass
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{0}
is a
neighborhood
of
x\in\mathbf{X}
if there is a
\rho\in\mathbf{F}_{0}^{\times}
with
B_{0}(x,\rho)\subseteq\mathbf{X}
. We say that
\mathbf{X}
is
open

if it is empty or if it is a neighborhood of each of its points.

The standard definition of differentiable real-valued function can be
formulated for functions between
\mathbf{F}_{0}
and
\mathbf{F}_{1}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
x\in\mathbf{F}_{0}
and let
\mathbf{X}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{0}
be a
neighborhood of
x
. Then a function
f:\mathbf{X}\longrightarrow\mathbf{F}_{1}
is said differentiable at
x
if there is an
l\in\mathbf{F}_{1}
such that

\forall\varepsilon\in\mathbf{F}_{1}^{\times},\exists\delta\in\mathbf{F}_{0}^{\times},\forall y\in B_{0}(x,\delta),f(y)\in B_{1}(f(x)-(y-x)l,(y-x)\varepsilon),

i.e.
l
is a limit at
0
of the function
\mathbf{F}_{0}^{\times}\longrightarrow\mathbf{F}_{1};h\mapsto\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
.

Then
l
is unique, we write
l=f^{\prime}(x)
and we call
f^{\prime}(x)
the derivative
of
f
at
x
. If moreover
\mathbf{X}
is open and
f
is differentiable at
each
x\in\mathbf{X}
, then we say that
f
is

differentiable
and we write
f^{\prime}
for
the function
\mathbf{X}\longrightarrow\mathbf{F}_{1};y\mapsto f^{\prime}(y)
.

Many elementary properties of differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}
are
retained in the more general context of valued fields. In particular, the sum
and product of differentiable functions at a point is differentiable at this
point. Moreover, for
f,g
differentiable at
x
(resp. on
\mathbf{O}
), we
have

(fg)^{\prime}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)g(x)+f(x)g^{\prime}(x).

In other words, the derivation operator
f\mapsto f^{\prime}(x)
behaves as a
derivation on the ring of differentiable functions at
x
. We also have a
chain rule: if
f:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbf{U}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{1}
is differentiable at
x
where
\mathbf{U}
is a neighborhood of

f(x)
, and
g:\mathbf{U}\longrightarrow\mathbf{F}_{2}
is differentiable
at

2.2
Power series

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a field and let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra over

\mathbf{F}
. Seeing
\mathbb{N}
as the ordered monoid
(\mathbb{N},+,0,>)
, we have an algebra
\mathbf{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket\coloneqq\mathbf{A}\llbracket X^{\mathbb{N}}\rrbracket
of Noetherian series corresponding to
the algebra of formal power series over
\mathbf{A}
. It is equipped with a
standard derivation

P=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}P_{k}X^{k}\longmapsto P^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(k+1)P_{k+1}X^{k}.

Moreover, for
P,Q\in\mathbf{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
with
Q_{0}=0
(in
other words,
Q\in X\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}\llbracket X\rrbracket
), we have a

composite power series

P\circ Q\coloneqq P_{0}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\sum_{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}=k}P_{n}Q_{m_{1}}\cdots Q_{m_{n}}\right)X^{k}\in\mathbf{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket.

For
P\in\mathbf{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
and
Q,R\in X\mathbf{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
, we have
Q\circ R\in X\mathbf{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket
and

P\circ(Q\circ R)=(P\circ Q)\circ R.

2.3
Convergence of power series

We fix a linearly ordered Abelian group
\mathfrak{M}
, a field
\mathbf{K}
,
and consider the field
\mathbb{S}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a power series

P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket,

and
s\in\mathbb{S}
, we say that
P

converges at

s
if the family
(P_{k}s^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable. We then set

\tilde{P}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}s^{k}.

We write
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
for the class of series
s\in\mathbb{S}
at which
P
converges.

Example 2.3
.

Any power series
P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}c_{k}X^{k}\in\mathbf{K}\llbracket X\rrbracket
converges on
\mathbb{S}^{\prec}
by
Lemma
1.15
. In fact, since the sequence
(s^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\preccurlyeq
-increasing whenever
s\succcurlyeq 1
, we
have
\operatorname{Conv}(P)=\mathbb{S}^{\prec}
unless
P
is a polynomial, in
which case
\operatorname{Conv}(P)=\mathbb{S}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

[
30
, Corollary 1.5.8]
For
all
P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
, and
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathbb{S}
with
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
, we have
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta\Longrightarrow\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
.

Proof.

Write
P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}
and
u\coloneqq\varepsilon/\delta\preccurlyeq 1
. By Proposition
1.18
for

I=\mathbb{N}
and
f=\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{N}}
, the family
(P_{k}\delta^{k}u^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=(P_{k}\varepsilon^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is
summable.
∎

In particular,
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
is a union of balls, hence the following.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
. Then

\operatorname{Conv}(P)
is an open additive subgroup of
\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\!\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
.

We say that a
P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
is

convergent
if
\operatorname{Conv}(P)\neq\{0\}
, and we write
\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
for the class of convergent power
series.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
.
Then
\operatorname{Conv}(P)\cap\operatorname{Conv}(Q)\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(P+Q)
,
with equality if
\operatorname{Conv}(P)\neq\operatorname{Conv}(Q)
. Moreover,

\operatorname{Conv}(P)\cap\operatorname{Conv}(Q)\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(PQ)
.

Proof.

For
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)\cap\operatorname{Conv}(Q)
, the families
(P(k)\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Q(k)\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are
summable, so by Proposition
1.14
so is
((P_{k}+Q_{k})\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. So
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P+Q)
.

The family
(P_{k}Q_{n}\delta^{k+n})_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable by
Proposition
1.16
. Therefore
(\sum_{k+n=m}\binom{m}{k}P(k)Q(n)\delta^{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable by
Proposition
1.12
. So
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(PQ)
. If
\operatorname{Conv}(P)\neq\operatorname{Conv}(Q)
, then by
Proposition
2.4
, we may assume that
\operatorname{Conv}(P)\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(Q)
, so
\operatorname{Conv}(P)\cap\operatorname{Conv}(Q)=\operatorname{Conv}(P)
. For
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(Q)\setminus\operatorname{Conv}(P)
, if
((P_{k}+Q_{k})\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, were summable, then so would be
(P_{k}\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=((P_{k}+Q_{k})\delta^{k}-Q_{k}\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
by Proposition
1.14
: a contradiction. So

\operatorname{Conv}(P+Q)=\operatorname{Conv}(P)
.
∎

Corollary 2.7
.

The class
\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
containing
\mathbb{S}\cup\{X\}
.

In the sequel, we assume that
\mathbf{K}
has characteristic zero.

Lemma 2.8
.

For all
P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\operatorname{Conv}(P)=\operatorname{Conv}(P^{(n)})
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for
n=1
. We have
0\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)\cap\operatorname{Conv}(P^{\prime})
. Recall that
P^{\prime}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(k+1)P_{k+1}X^{k}
. For
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{S}^{\times}
, the family
(P_{k}\varepsilon^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable if and only if
(P_{k+1}\varepsilon^{k+1})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable. Since
\mathbf{K}
has
characteristic zero, this is equivalent to
((k+1)P_{k+1}\varepsilon^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
being summable. We deduce that

\operatorname{Conv}(P)=\operatorname{Conv}(P^{\prime})
.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

The algebra
\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
is a differential subalgebra
of
(\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket,\mathord{\>{}^{\prime}})
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
be a power series and let

\varepsilon,\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
. Write
P_{+\varepsilon}
for the
power series

P_{+\varepsilon}\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\widetilde{P^{(k)}}(\varepsilon)}{k!}X^{k}.

We have
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\varepsilon})
and

\widetilde{P_{+\varepsilon}}(\delta)=\tilde{P}(\varepsilon+\delta).

Proof.

Note that
P_{+0}=P
and that
P_{+\varepsilon}(0)=\tilde{P}(\varepsilon)
, so we may assume that
\varepsilon
and
\delta
are
non-zero. The power series
P_{+\varepsilon}
is well-defined by
Lemma
2.8
. We have trivially that

\bigcup_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{supp}(P_{k+i}\varepsilon^{k+i})=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{supp}(P_{j}\varepsilon^{j}),

where the right hand set is well-based since
(P_{j}\varepsilon^{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable. For each monomial
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
,
the set
I_{\mathfrak{m}}\coloneqq\{(i,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(P_{k+i}\delta^{k+i})\}
is contained in

\{(i,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}:i+k\in J_{\mathfrak{m}}\}
where

J_{\mathfrak{m}}\coloneqq\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(P_{j}\delta^{j})\}.

Since
(P_{j}\varepsilon^{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, we deduce that

J_{\mathfrak{m}}
, and hence
I_{\mathfrak{m}}
are finite. This shows that

(P_{k+i}\varepsilon^{k+i})_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable.
Likewise,
(P_{k+i}\delta^{k+i})_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\frac{\widetilde{P^{(k)}}(\varepsilon)}{k!}\delta^{k}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\binom{k+i}{k}P_{k+i}\varepsilon^{i}\delta^{k}.

(11)

Therefore it suffices to show that the family
(P_{k+i}\varepsilon^{i}\delta^{k})_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable in order to prove that
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\varepsilon})
. For
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
, write

\varepsilon^{i}\delta^{k}=u^{i+k}v^{k}

where
(u,v)=\left(\varepsilon,\delta/\varepsilon\right)
if
\delta\preccurlyeq\varepsilon
and
(u,v)=\left(\delta,\varepsilon/\delta\right)
if
\varepsilon\prec\delta
. In any case, we have
v\preccurlyeq 1
and the family
(P_{k+i}u^{k+i})_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable.
Applying Proposition
1.18
for
I=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and
f=(a,b)\mapsto a+b
, we see that the family

(P_{k+i}u^{k+i}v^{k})_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}=(P_{k+i}\varepsilon^{i}\delta^{k})_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable.

On the other hand we have
\delta+\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\varepsilon
or

\delta+\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta
, so
\delta+\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
and
(P_{k}(\delta+\varepsilon)^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is
summable. By Lemma
1.13
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\widetilde{P^{(k)}}(\varepsilon)}{k!}\delta^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\binom{k+i}{k}P_{k+i}\varepsilon^{i}\delta^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k\in\mathbb{N}}\binom{k+i}{k}P_{k+i}\varepsilon^{i}\delta^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{l\leqslant j}\binom{j}{l}P_{j}\varepsilon^{j-l}\delta^{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}P_{j}(\varepsilon+\delta)^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(\varepsilon+\delta),

as desired.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
. The function
\tilde{P}
is infinitely differentiable on

\operatorname{Conv}(P)
with
\tilde{P}^{(n)}=\widetilde{P^{(n)}}
on

\operatorname{Conv}(P)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Recall by Corollary
2.5
that
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
is open. We
first prove that
\tilde{P}
is differentiable on
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
with

\tilde{P}^{\prime}=\widetilde{P^{\prime}}
. Let
s\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{S}^{\times}
. For all
h\in\mathbb{S}
with
h\preccurlyeq s
, Proposition
2.10
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{P}(s+h)-\tilde{P}(s)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k>0}\frac{\widetilde{P^{(k)}}(s)}{k!}h^{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{P^{\prime}}(s)+hu,

where
u\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\widetilde{P^{(k+2)}}(s)}{(k+2)!}h^{k}
. We have
u\preccurlyeq\widetilde{P^{(k+2)}}(s)s^{k}\eqqcolon v
for a
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Seting
\delta\coloneqq\varepsilon/v
, we obtain
\frac{\tilde{P}(s+h)-\tilde{P}(s)}{h}-\widetilde{P^{\prime}}(s)=hu\preccurlyeq\varepsilon
whenever
h\preccurlyeq\delta
. So
\tilde{P}
is differentiable at
s
with
\tilde{P}^{\prime}(s)=\widetilde{P^{\prime}}(s)
. The result for all
n
follows by induction.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\mathbf{U}\subseteq\mathbb{S}
be
open. Let
P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}P_{k}X^{k}\in\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
and
Q=\sum_{k>0}Q_{k}X^{k}\in X\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
. Let
\varepsilon_{P}\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
and

\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(Q)
with

\forall m>0,Q_{m}\varepsilon^{m}\prec\varepsilon_{P}.

(12)

Then
\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(P\circ Q)
, and
(\widetilde{P\circ Q})(\varepsilon)=\tilde{P}(\tilde{Q}(\varepsilon))
.

Proof.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{>}
, set
X_{n,k}\coloneqq\{v\in(\mathbb{N}^{>})^{n}:|v|=k\}
and

c_{n,k}\coloneqq\sum_{v\in X_{n,k}}P_{n}Q_{v_{[1]}}\cdots Q_{v_{[n]}},

so
P\circ Q=P_{0}+\sum_{k>0}\left(\sum_{n\geqslant 0}c_{n,k}\right)X^{k}
. Note that since
\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(Q)
, the set

\mathfrak{S}_{Q}\coloneqq\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{supp}(Q_{m}\varepsilon^{m})

is well-based. We have
\mathfrak{S}_{Q}\prec\varepsilon_{P}
by
(
12
). Let
\mathfrak{m}\coloneqq\mathfrak{d}_{\varepsilon}
. The set
\mathfrak{S}_{P}\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{supp}(P_{n}\mathfrak{m}^{n})
is well-based. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{>}
, we have

\operatorname{supp}c_{n,k}\varepsilon^{k}\subseteq(\mathfrak{S}_{Q}\cdot\mathfrak{m}^{-1})^{n}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{P},

where
\mathfrak{S}_{Q}\cdot\mathfrak{m}^{-1}
is well-based and
infinitesimal, and
\mathfrak{S}_{P}
is well-based. Since each family
(c_{n,k}\varepsilon^{k})_{k>0}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
is summable with sum

\tilde{Q}(\varepsilon)^{n}
. Applying Lemma
1.19
for

f(n,k)=n
, we conclude that
(c_{n,k}\varepsilon^{k})_{n\geqslant 0,k>0}
is summable. We deduce by Lemma
1.13
that

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(\tilde{Q}(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}P_{n}\tilde{Q}(\varepsilon)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}P_{n}\left(\sum_{k>0}Q_{k}\varepsilon^{k}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{0}+\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\sum_{k>0}c_{n,k}\varepsilon^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{0}+\sum_{k>0}\left(\sum_{n\geqslant 0}c_{n,k}\right)\varepsilon^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widetilde{P\circ Q})(\varepsilon).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
and let
\delta,\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
. We have
\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\delta})=\operatorname{Conv}(P)
and
P_{+(\delta+\varepsilon)}=(P_{+\delta})_{+\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\delta\neq 0
.
Proposition
2.10
shows that
\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\delta})\supseteq\operatorname{Conv}(P)
and that
(P_{+\delta})_{+\varepsilon}
is well-defined. Since
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\delta})
, Propositions
2.4
and

2.10
yield

\widetilde{(P_{+\delta})_{+\varepsilon}}(\iota)=\widetilde{P_{+\delta}}(\varepsilon+\iota)=\tilde{P}(\delta+\varepsilon+\iota)

for all
\iota\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
. We deduce by
Proposition
2.14
that
P_{+(\delta+\varepsilon)}=(P_{+\delta})_{+\varepsilon}
. Applying
Proposition
2.10
, this time to
(P_{+\delta},-\delta)
, we get
\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\delta})\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(P)
, hence the equality.
∎

2.4
Zeroes of power series

We next consider zeros of power series functions. A
zero
of a power series
P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
is an element
s\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
with
\tilde{P}(s)=0
. We
still assume that
\mathbf{K}
has characteristic zero.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{S}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}
and let
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)\setminus\{0\}
. If
\tilde{P}(\varepsilon)=0
for all
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta
then
P=0
.

Proof.

We have
(\tilde{P})^{(n)}(0)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
since

\tilde{P}
is constant around
0
. It follows by
Lemma
2.11
that
\widetilde{(P^{(n)})}(0)=0

for all

, so
P=P_{+0}=0
.∎

2.5
Analytic functions

Assume that
\mathbf{K}
has characteristic zero. Let
\mathbb{S}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
be a fixed field of well-based series over

\mathbf{K}
where
\mathfrak{M}
is non-trivial. We also fix a non-empty open
subclass
\mathbf{O}
of
\mathbb{S}
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
f:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}
be a function and let
s\in\mathbf{O}
. We say that
f
is
analytic
at

s
if there are a convergent power series
f_{s}\in\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
and a
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(f_{s})\setminus\{0\}
such that for all
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta
, we have

s+\varepsilon\in\mathbf{O}\Longrightarrow f(s+\varepsilon)=\widetilde{f_{s}}(\varepsilon).

We say that
f_{s}
is a
Taylor series
of
f
at
s
. We say that
f
is
analytic
if
it is analytic at each
s\in\mathbf{O}
.

Example 2.16
.

The function
\tilde{P}
induced by a convergent power series
P\in\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
is analytic on
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
, by definition.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
f:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}
be analytic at
s\in\mathbf{O}
. Then
f_{s}
is the unique Taylor series of
f
at
s
.

Proof.

Let
P\in\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
and
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)\setminus\{0\}
with
s+\varepsilon\in\mathbf{O}
and

f(s+\varepsilon)=\tilde{P}(\varepsilon)
for all
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta
. Then the function
\widetilde{f_{s}-P}
is zero on the
class of series
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta
, so we have
f_{s}=P
by
Proposition
2.14
.
∎

If
f:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}
is analytic at
s\in\mathbf{O}
where
\mathbf{O}
is open, then we can define

\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{s}\coloneqq\{t\in\mathbf{O}:t-s\in\operatorname{Conv}(f_{s})\wedge f(t)=\widetilde{f_{s}}(t-s)\}.

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
. Then
\tilde{P}
is analytic on
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
with

\tilde{P}_{\delta}=P_{+\delta}
and
\operatorname{Conv}(\tilde{P})_{\delta}=\operatorname{Conv}(P)
for all
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
.

Proof.

Let
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
. The class
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
is open by
Corollary
2.5
, with
\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\delta})=\operatorname{Conv}(P)
. By Proposition
2.10
, we
have
\tilde{P}(\delta+\varepsilon)=\widetilde{P_{+\delta}(\varepsilon)}
for all
\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(P)
, so
\tilde{P}
is
indeed analytic on
\operatorname{Conv}(P)
with
\operatorname{Conv}(\tilde{P})_{\delta}\supseteq\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\delta})=\operatorname{Conv}(P)
. But we also have

\operatorname{Conv}(\tilde{P})_{\delta}\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(P_{+\delta})=\operatorname{Conv}(P)
by definition, hence the result.
∎

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
f:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}
be analytic at
s\in\mathbf{O}
and let
\mathbf{U}\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{s}
be a non-empty open subclass containing
0
. Then
f
is

, with

Let
\delta\in\mathbf{U}
and set

. Since

\rho\neq 0
with

\delta+\varepsilon\in\mathbf{U}
for all
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\rho
. Thus
f(t+\varepsilon)=\widetilde{f_{s}}(\delta+\varepsilon)

whenever
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\rho
. But given such

, we

\widetilde{f_{s}}(\delta+\varepsilon)=\widetilde{(f_{s})_{+\delta}}(\varepsilon)
by Proposition
2.10
, whence

f(t+\varepsilon)=\widetilde{f_{s}}(\delta+\varepsilon)=\widetilde{(f_{s})_{+\delta}}(\varepsilon).

So
f
is analytic at
t
with
f_{t}=(f_{s})_{+(t-s)}
.
∎

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
f:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}

be analytic at
s\in\mathbf{O}
. Then
f
is infinitely differentiable at

s
, and each
f^{(n)}
for

is analytic at
s
with

\operatorname{Conv}(f^{(n)})_{s}\supseteq\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{s}
. Moreover, we have

f_{s}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\tfrac{f^{(k)}(s)}{k!}X^{k}.

Proof.

Recall that
\widetilde{f_{s}}
is infinitely differentiable on
\operatorname{Conv}(f_{s})
. It follows since
\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{s}
is a neighborhood of
s
that

f
is infinitely differentiable at
s
. By
Lemma
2.11
, each derivative

\widetilde{f_{s}}^{(n)}
for

is a power series function on

\operatorname{Conv}(f_{s})
, and is thus analytic on
\operatorname{Conv}(f_{s})
by
Proposition
2.18
. By
Lemma
2.11
, given
\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{s}-s

f^{(n)}(s+\delta)=\widetilde{f_{s}}^{(n)}(\delta)=\widetilde{(f_{s})^{(n)}}(\delta)
. Therefore

is analytic at
s

and
\operatorname{Conv}(f^{(n)})_{s}\supseteq\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{s}
. Write

. We deduce that
f_{s}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\tfrac{f^{(k)}(s)}{k!}X^{k}
.

∎

Proposition 2.21
.

Let
\mathbf{O}\subseteq\mathbb{S}
be
open and non-empty and assume that
\mathbf{O}=\bigsqcup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{O}_{i}
where each
\mathbf{O}_{i}
is open and non-empty.
Let
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a family where
s_{i}\in\mathbf{O}_{i}
for
all
i\in\mathbf{I}
. Let
(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{I}}
be a family of
convergent power series in
\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}

with
(s_{i}+\operatorname{Conv}(P_{i}))\supseteq\mathbf{O}_{i}
. The function
f:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}
such that for all
i\in\mathbf{I}

and
s\in\mathbf{O}_{i}
, we have
f(s)=P_{i}(s-s_{i})
is well-defined
and analytic.

Proof.

Let
s\in\mathbf{O}
and let
i\in\mathbf{I}
with
s\in\mathbf{O}_{i}
.
We have
s-s_{i}\in\mathbf{O}_{i}-s_{i}\subseteq\operatorname{Conv}(P_{i})
so

\widetilde{P_{i}}(s-s_{i})
is defined. In particular
f
is well-defined.
The class
\mathbf{O}_{i}-s_{i}
is a neighborhood of
0
, so there is a

\delta\in\operatorname{Conv}(P_{i})\setminus\{0\}
such that
s_{i}+\varepsilon\in\mathbf{O}_{i}
whenever
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta
.
Given
\varepsilon\preccurlyeq\delta
, we have

f(s+\varepsilon)=P_{i}(s+\varepsilon-s_{i})=(P_{i})_{+(s-s_{i})}(\varepsilon)

by Proposition
2.10
. Therefore
f
is
analytic at
s
with
f_{s}=(P_{i})_{+(s-s_{i})}
.
∎

We leave it to the reader to check that analyticity, at a point or on an open
class, is preserved by sums and products. The following result can be used to
show that the compositum of analytic functions is analytic. As a corollary of
Proposition
2.12
, we obtain:

Corollary 2.22
.

Let
\mathbf{U}\subseteq\mathbb{S}
be open.
Let
f:\mathbf{U}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S},g:\mathbf{O}\longrightarrow\mathbf{U}
and let
s\in\mathbf{O}
such that
g
is
analytic at
s
and
f
is analytic at
g(s)
. Write
g_{s}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{k}X^{n}
. Let
\varepsilon_{f}\in\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{g(s)}-g(s)
and
\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(g)_{s}-s
with
\forall k>0,a_{k}\varepsilon^{k}\prec\varepsilon_{f}
. Then function
f\circ g
is analytic at

s
with
s+\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Conv}(f\circ g)_{s}
, and
(f\circ g)_{s}=f_{g(s)}\circ(g_{s}-g(s))
.

Remark 2.23
.

A well-known type of analytic functions is that of
restricted real-analytic functions of
[
13
,
15
]
. Given a
non-empty interval
I
of
\mathbb{R}
and
f:I\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

is an analytic function, then
f
extends into a function
\overline{f}:I+\mathbb{S}^{\prec}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}+\mathbb{S}^{\prec}

by

\forall r\in I,\forall\varepsilon\prec 1,\overline{f}(r+\varepsilon)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{f^{(k)}(r)}{k!}\varepsilon^{k}.

We say that
\overline{f}
is a restricted real-analytic function on

\mathbb{S}
. The function
\overline{f}
is in fact analytic.

Remark 2.24
.

Our notion of analyticity is local, which makes it subject to pathologies
(see Proposition
2.21
). A stronger version of analyticity
would be to impose that a function
f
is analytic at
s\in\mathbb{S}
if
there is a power series
f_{s}\in\mathbb{S}\left\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\right\}
such that
f(s+\varepsilon)=\widetilde{f_{s}}(\varepsilon)

for
\varepsilon
ranging in the
whole locus of convergence

\operatorname{Conv}(f_{s})
of
f_{s}
.

3
Algebras of Noetherian series given by cuts

In this section, we assume that
\mathbf{K}
is a field and that

\mathfrak{M}
is a linearly ordered Abelian group, so
\mathbb{S}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
is a field. Our main tool for
proving the strong linearity of operators involved in Taylor expansions is the
construction in
Section

3.1
of algebras of formal series
over
\mathbb{S}
related to a convergence condition given by a final segment

\mathfrak{S}
of
(\mathfrak{M},\prec)
. A typical example would be the
interval
\mathfrak{S}=\{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}\}
for some fixed
\mathfrak{n}
. For instance, we can
construct a subalgebra of
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
whose elements
converge for all
\delta\prec\mathfrak{S}
.

3.1
Algebras of formal power series given by
cuts

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a final segment of
(\mathfrak{M},\prec)
. We will
define a partial ordering
\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}
on the direct product

\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}:\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}\wedge k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

It will extend to the smallest ordering on this product such that
X\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathfrak{S}
in
\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}}\rrbracket
. Consider the subclass

(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}\coloneqq(\mathfrak{M}^{\prec}\times\{X^{0}\})\sqcup\{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}:k>0\wedge\exists\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{m}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{u}^{-k}\}.

(13)

So for
(\mathfrak{m},k)\in\mathfrak{M}\times\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}1\Longleftrightarrow\mathfrak{m}\nsucc\mathfrak{S}^{-k}
. Recall that a strictly positive cone on an
Abelian, torsion-free group
(\mathcal{G},\cdot,1)
is a subset
P\subseteq\mathcal{G}\setminus\{1\}
which is closed under products and such that
P\cap P^{-1}=\varnothing
. Such a cone induces a partial ordering
<_{P}
on

\mathcal{G}
given by
f<_{P}g\Longleftrightarrow gf^{-1}\in P
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The class
(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
is a
strictly positive cone on
\mathfrak{M}\cdot X^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

By definition, the class
(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
does not contain
1=1X^{0}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}X^{k},\mathfrak{n}X^{k^{\prime}}\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
. We may assume without loss of generality that
k\leqslant k^{\prime}
. If
k=k^{\prime}=0
, then
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\prec 1
so

\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\mathfrak{n}X^{k^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}\prec 1
. If

k=0
and
k^{\prime}\neq 0
, then
\mathfrak{m}\prec 1
,
k^{\prime}>0
and there is
a
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{S}
with
\mathfrak{n}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{u}^{-k^{\prime}}
. We then have
\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\mathfrak{n}X^{k^{\prime}}=(\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n})X^{k^{\prime}}
where
\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}\prec\mathfrak{n}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{u}^{-k^{\prime}}
. We deduce that

\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\mathfrak{n}X^{k^{\prime}}\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
. Otherwise, we must have
k,k^{\prime}>0
,
and there are
(\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{w})\in\mathfrak{S}
such that

\mathfrak{m}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{v}^{-k}
and
\mathfrak{n}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{w}^{-k^{\prime}}
. Taking
\mathfrak{p}\coloneqq\max(\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{w})\in\mathfrak{S}
, we have

\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{p}^{-(k+k^{\prime})}
, so

\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\mathfrak{n}X^{k^{\prime}}\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
. Thus
\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\mathfrak{n}X^{k^{\prime}}\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
is closed under products.

It remains to show that we cannot have
\mathfrak{m}^{-1}X^{-k}\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
. If
k=0
,
then this follows from the fact that
\mathfrak{M}^{\prec}
is a strictly
positive cone on
\mathfrak{M}
. Otherwise, we have
k>0
so

\mathfrak{m}^{-1}X^{-k}\not\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
.
∎

We thus obtain a partial ordering
\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}
on

\mathfrak{M}\cdot X^{\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}\cdot X^{\mathbb{Z}}
by setting

\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathfrak{n}X^{k^{\prime}}\Longleftrightarrow\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}^{-1}X^{k-k^{\prime}}\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}.

Mind that this is the
reverse
ordering of the ordering
<_{P}
given by
the positive cone
P=(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\mathfrak{S}}
. We write
\mathfrak{M}\times_{\mathfrak{S}}X^{\mathbb{N}}

for the corresponding partially ordered monoid. We may consider the algebra
of Noetherian series

\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\times_{\mathfrak{S}}X^{\mathbb{N}}\rrbracket

for this ordering.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have a natural inclusion
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
given by

s\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\operatorname{supp}s}s(\mathfrak{m}X^{k})\mathfrak{m}\right)X^{k}.

Proof.

The identity is an embedding of
(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}},\prec_{\mathfrak{S}})
into the lexicographic power
(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}},\prec_{\operatorname{lex}})
with prevalence on
X^{\mathbb{N}}
, so
Corollary
1.22
yields the inclusion.
∎

Under this inclusion, we have
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}=\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
if and only if

\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{M}
and
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}=\mathbb{S}[X]
if and only if
\mathfrak{S}=\varnothing
. In the divisible case, this generalises as follows:

Lemma 3.3
.

Given a final segment
\mathfrak{T}
of

\mathfrak{M}
, we have

\mathfrak{S}\subsetneq\mathfrak{T}\Longleftrightarrow\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}\subsetneq\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathfrak{S}\subsetneq\mathfrak{T}
. Then the identity
(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}},\prec_{\mathfrak{S}})\longrightarrow(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}},\prec_{\mathfrak{T}})
is an
embedding,
whence
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}\subseteq\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}
by
Corollary
1.22
. Now let
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{T}\setminus\mathfrak{S}
, so
\mathfrak{u}\prec\mathfrak{S}
. We claim that
the power series
P\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{u}^{-k}X^{k}

lies in
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}\setminus\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Indeed, we have

\mathfrak{u}^{-k}(\mathfrak{u}^{-(k+1)})^{-1}=u\in\mathfrak{T},\text{\qquad whereas\qquad$\mathfrak{u}^{-k}(\mathfrak{u}^{-(k+1)})^{-1}=\mathfrak{u}\prec\mathfrak{S}$}.

Thus
\mathfrak{u}^{-k}X^{k}\succ_{\mathfrak{T}}\mathfrak{u}^{-(k+1)}X^{k+1}
, but the same terms are not comparable for

\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Hence the support of
P
is Noetherian for

\succ_{\mathfrak{T}}
but not for
\succ_{\mathfrak{S}}
, i.e.
P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}\setminus\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Recall that inclusion is a linear
ordering on the collection of final segments of
\mathfrak{M}
, so this
concludes the proof.
∎

The main feature of
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}
is
that its elements can be evaluated at series
\delta
in extensions of

\mathbb{S}
such that
\delta\prec\mathfrak{S}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{N}\supseteq\mathfrak{M}
be an
Abelian, linearly ordered group extension and write
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{N}\rrbracket
, so we have a natural
inclusion
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
. Let
\mathfrak{I}
be a
Noetherian subset of
(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}},\succ_{\mathfrak{S}})
and let
\delta\in\mathbb{T}
with
\delta\prec\mathfrak{S}
. Then the family
(\mathfrak{m}\delta^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{I}}
is summable in
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Write
\mathfrak{v}=\mathfrak{d}_{\delta}
. Let
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}X^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an injective sequence in
\mathfrak{I}
. Since

\mathfrak{I}
is Noetherian, there are
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i<j

and
\mathfrak{m}_{j}X^{k_{j}}\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathfrak{m}_{i}X^{k_{i}}
. If

k_{i}=k_{j}
, then this means that
\mathfrak{m}_{j}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{i}
, so

\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{v}^{k_{j}}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{v}^{k_{i}}
.
Otherwise, we must have
k_{i}<k_{j}
, and
\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{-1}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{u}^{k_{i}-k_{j}}
for a
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{S}
. Since
\mathfrak{v}\prec\mathfrak{S}
, we have

\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{-1}\prec\mathfrak{v}^{k_{i}-k_{j}}
, so

\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{v}^{k_{j}}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{v}^{k_{i}}
.
We conclude with Lemma
1.11
that

(\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{v}^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{I}}
is
summable. Since
\mathfrak{I}
is Noetherian, the set
\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\exists\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M},\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{I}\}
must be well-ordered in
(\mathbb{Z},<)
. It follows by
Proposition
1.18
that
(\mathfrak{m}\delta^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{I}}
is summable.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

In the same notations as above, for all
\delta\in\mathbb{T}^{\times}
with
\delta\prec\mathfrak{S}
, the function

\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}\>;\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\mapsto\mathfrak{m}\delta^{k}
extends uniquely into a
strongly linear morphism of algebras
\operatorname{ev}_{\delta}:\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

The function preserves products, so the result follows from
Proposition
1.21
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{M}
is divisible. Let

\mathfrak{N}\supseteq\mathfrak{M}
be an Abelian linearly ordered group
extension and set
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{N}\rrbracket
. For
P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}P_{k,\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\right)X^{k}
in
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket
and
\delta\in\mathbb{T}^{\times}
, we have

P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\mathfrak{m}\succ\delta\}}\Longleftrightarrow(P_{k,\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\delta^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{M}\cdot X^{\mathbb{Z}}}\text{ is summable in $\mathbb{T}$.}

Proof.

We write
P_{k}=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}P_{k,\mathfrak{m}}

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If
P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\mathfrak{m}\succ\delta\}}
, then
(P_{k,\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\delta^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{M}\cdot X^{\mathbb{Z}}}
is
summable by Proposition
3.5
. Assume conversely that
(P_{k,\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\delta^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{M}\cdot X^{\mathbb{Z}}}
is summable. Write
\mathfrak{d}\coloneqq\mathfrak{d}_{\delta}
and
\mathfrak{T}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{d}\}
. Assume for
contradiction that the support of
P
is not Noetherian in
(\mathfrak{M}\times_{\mathfrak{S}}X^{\mathbb{Z}},\succ_{\mathfrak{T}})
. So there is a
bad sequence
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}X^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\mathfrak{m}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}P_{k_{i}}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. If

(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
were constant, then the sequence

(\mathfrak{m}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
would witness that
\operatorname{supp}P_{k}

is not Noetherian in
\mathfrak{M}
. So we may assume that
(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly increasing. For all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i<j
, we have
\mathfrak{m}_{i}X^{k_{i}}\mathrel{\not\succcurlyeq}\mathfrak{m}_{j}X^{k_{j}}
.
This implies that
\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{-1}\succ\mathfrak{T}^{k_{i}-k_{j}}
, whence
\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{-1}\in(\mathfrak{M}\setminus\mathfrak{T})^{k_{i}-k_{j}}
by divisibility of

\mathfrak{M}
. Thus
\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{-1}\succcurlyeq\mathfrak{d}^{k_{i}-k_{j}}
. Now the family
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\delta^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable. Therefore there are
i<j
with

\mathfrak{m}_{i}\delta^{k_{i}}\succ\mathfrak{m}_{j}\delta^{k_{j}}
, whence

\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{d}^{k_{i}}\succ\mathfrak{m}_{j}\mathfrak{d}^{k_{j}}
:
a contradiction. ∎

Remark 3.7
.

We do not have
P\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{M}\setminus\operatorname{Conv}(P)}
in general. For instance if
\mathfrak{M}=x^{\mathbb{Q}}
is a multiplicative copy of
(\mathbb{Q},+,0,<)
, then
the series
P=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}x^{-2^{k}}X^{k}
satisfies

\operatorname{Conv}(P)=\mathbb{S}
but
P\not\in\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\varnothing}=\mathbb{S}[X]
.

3.2
Cut extensions of algebra
morphisms

Fix a non-trivial,
linearly
ordered
Abelian group

\mathfrak{N}
and write
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{N}\rrbracket
. Let
\triangle:\mathbb{S}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}
be a strongly linear morphism of algebras. Let
\mathfrak{T}\subseteq\mathfrak{N}
be a non-empty final segment, and write

\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\exists\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{T},\mathfrak{m}\succcurlyeq\mathfrak{d}_{\triangle(\mathfrak{n})}\}.

Then
\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})
is a final segment of
\mathfrak{M}
,
so we have orderings
\prec_{\mathfrak{T}}
and
\prec_{\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})}
on
\mathfrak{N}\times X^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}}
respectively, and two corresponding algebras of
Noetherian series
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})}
and
\mathbb{T}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}
.

Note that
\prec_{\mathfrak{T}}
and
\prec_{\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})}

extend the orderings on
\mathfrak{M}
and
\mathfrak{N}
respectively, so

\triangle
is an embedding
\mathbb{S}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}
. Consider the function

\displaystyle\overline{\triangle}:\mathfrak{M}\times_{\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})}X^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}X^{k}

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\triangle(\mathfrak{m})X^{k}

Proposition 3.8
.

The function
\overline{\triangle}
is
Noetherian.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{I}
be a Noetherian subset of
(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}},\succ_{\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})})
. We want to prove
that the family
(\overline{\triangle}(\mathfrak{m}X^{k}))_{\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{I}}
is summable. Let
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}X^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an injective sequence in
\mathfrak{I}
and let

(\mathfrak{n}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a
sequence with
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}\triangle(\mathfrak{m}_{i})

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
1.11
, it suffices
to show that there are
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
with
i<j
and

\mathfrak{n}_{j}X^{k_{j}}\prec_{\mathfrak{T}}\mathfrak{n}_{i}X^{k_{i}}
. This
condition is preserved under taking subsequences, so we may assume that

(\mathfrak{m}_{i}X^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly decreasing for the
ordering
\prec_{\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})}
. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the relation

\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{i+1}}{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}X^{k_{i+1}-k_{i}}\in(\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{Z}})^{\prec,\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})},

(14)

implies in particular that
k_{i+1}\geqslant k_{i}
. Taking a subsequence
if necessary, we may assume that
(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is either
constant or strictly increasing.

In the constant case, the condition (
14
) reduces to

\mathfrak{m}_{i}\succ\mathfrak{m}_{i+1}
, i.e.
(\mathfrak{m}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly decreasing. But then since
\triangle
is strongly
linear, the family
(\triangle(\mathfrak{m}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is
summable. By Lemma
1.11
, there are
i\in\mathbb{N}

and
l>0
with
\mathfrak{n}_{i+l}\prec\mathfrak{n}_{i}
, whence

\mathfrak{n}_{i+l}X^{k_{i+l}}=\mathfrak{n}_{i+l}X^{k_{0}}\prec_{\mathfrak{T}}\mathfrak{n}_{i}X^{k_{0}}=\mathfrak{n}_{i}X^{k_{i}}
.

In the strictly increasing case, the condition (
14
)
translates as
\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{i+1}}{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{-(k_{i+1}-k_{i})}
for some
\mathfrak{u}_{i}\in\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})
. Rewriting this as

\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}{\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{-k_{i}}}\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{i+1}}{\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{-k_{i+1}}},

we have the following weakly decreasing sequence in
\mathfrak{M}
:

\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{0}}{\mathfrak{u}_{0}^{-k_{0}}}\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{1}}{\mathfrak{u}_{0}^{-k_{1}}}\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{2}}{\mathfrak{u}_{0}^{-k_{1}}\mathfrak{u}_{1}^{-(k_{2}-k_{1})}}\succcurlyeq\cdots\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{i+1}}{\mathfrak{u}_{0}^{-k_{1}}\mathfrak{u}_{1}^{-(k_{2}-k_{1})}\cdots\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{-(k_{i+1}-k_{i})}}\succcurlyeq\cdots.

Write
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\coloneqq\mathfrak{u}_{0}^{-k_{1}}\mathfrak{u}_{1}^{-(k_{2}-k_{1})}\cdots\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{-(k_{i+1}-k_{i})}
for each
i>0
. Since

\triangle\upharpoonleft\mathfrak{M}
is Noetherian,
Lemma
1.23
and Lemma
1.11
for the
sequence
\mathfrak{n}_{i}(\mathfrak{d}_{\smash{\triangle(\mathfrak{p}_{i})}})^{-1}\in\operatorname{supp}\triangle(\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}{\mathfrak{p}_{i}})
gives
i,j>0
with
i<j
and

\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{i}}{\triangle(\mathfrak{p}_{i})}\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}{\triangle(\mathfrak{p}_{j})},

whence

\mathfrak{n}_{i}\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}{\mathfrak{d}_{\triangle(\mathfrak{u}_{i})}^{-(k_{i+1}-k_{i})}\cdots\mathfrak{d}_{\triangle(\mathfrak{u}_{j-1})}^{-(k_{j}-k_{j-1})}}.

Taking
\mathfrak{u}\coloneqq\min(\mathfrak{u}_{i},\ldots,\mathfrak{u}_{j-1})
, we obtain

\mathfrak{n}_{i}\succcurlyeq\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}{\mathfrak{d}_{\triangle(\mathfrak{u})}^{-(k_{j}-k_{i})}},

whence
\frac{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}{\mathfrak{n}_{i}}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{d}_{\triangle(\mathfrak{u})}^{-(k_{j}-k_{i})}
. But

\mathfrak{d}_{\triangle(\mathfrak{u})}\in\mathfrak{T}
, so this means
that
\mathfrak{n}_{j}X^{k_{j}}\prec_{\mathfrak{T}}\mathfrak{n}_{i}X^{k_{i}}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

The function
\overline{\triangle}

extends into a strongly linear morphism of algebras
\overline{\triangle}:\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\triangle^{\ast}(\mathfrak{T})}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{T}}
.

4
Differential algebra

We fix a field
\mathbf{K}
, and we recall that our algebras
(\mathbf{A},+,\cdot,0,.)
over
\mathbf{K}
are always associative, but not necessarily
commutative or unital.

4.1
Differential algebra

We first recall standard and basic notions in differential algebra. The
results here are folklore and we give proofs for the sake of completion. Let

\mathbf{B}
be an algebra over
\mathbf{K}
and let
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathbf{B}
be a subalgebra. A function
\partial:\mathbf{A}\longrightarrow\mathbf{B}
is called a

derivation
if it is
\mathbf{K}
-linear and
satisfies the Leibniz product rule

\forall a,b\in\mathbf{A},\partial(a\cdot b)=\partial(a)\cdot b+a\cdot\partial(b).

Example 4.1
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a
\mathbf{K}
-algebra,
a\in\mathbf{A}
,
\delta,\partial:\mathbf{A}\longrightarrow\mathbf{A}
are derivations and

\sigma:\mathbf{A}\longrightarrow\mathbf{A}
is an automorphism of
algebra, then the following functions are derivations
\mathbf{A}\longrightarrow\mathbf{A}
:

\bullet

\partial+a\cdot\delta\coloneqq\>b\mapsto\partial(b)+a\cdot\delta(b)
,

\bullet

[\partial,\delta]\coloneqq\partial\circ\delta-\delta\circ\partial
,

\bullet

[a,\cdot]\coloneqq b\mapsto a\cdot b-b\cdot a
,

\bullet

\sigma\circ\partial\circ\sigma^{\operatorname{inv}}\coloneqq b\mapsto\sigma(\partial(\sigma^{\operatorname{inv}}(b)))
.

Lemma 4.2

(
[
21
, Corollary 3.9]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{A}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
and

\mathbb{B}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{N}\rrbracket
are algebras
over
\mathbf{K}
of Noetherian series and that
\partial:\mathbb{A}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a strongly linear function with

\partial(\mathfrak{m}\cdot\mathfrak{n})=\partial(\mathfrak{m})\cdot\mathfrak{n}+\mathfrak{m}\cdot\partial(\mathfrak{n})

for all
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then
\partial
is a
derivation.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{A}
. We have

\displaystyle\partial(a\cdot b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}a(\mathfrak{m})b(\mathfrak{n})\mathfrak{m}\cdot\mathfrak{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}a(\mathfrak{m})b(\mathfrak{n})\partial(\mathfrak{m}\cdot\mathfrak{n})\text{{\hskip 0.0pt}(by strong linearity)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}a(\mathfrak{m})b(\mathfrak{n})(\partial(\mathfrak{m})\cdot\mathfrak{n}+\mathfrak{m}\cdot\partial(\mathfrak{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}a(\mathfrak{m})b(\mathfrak{n})\partial(\mathfrak{m})\cdot\mathfrak{n}+\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}a(\mathfrak{m})b(\mathfrak{n})\mathfrak{m}\cdot\partial(\mathfrak{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}\partial(a(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m})\cdot(b(\mathfrak{n})\mathfrak{n})+\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}(a(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m})\cdot(b(\mathfrak{n})\partial(\mathfrak{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}\partial(a(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m})\right)\cdot b+a\cdot\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}}\partial(b(\mathfrak{n})\mathfrak{n})\right)\text{{\hskip 0.0pt}(by
Proposition\penalty\ \ref{prop-sum-prod})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial(a)\cdot b+a\cdot\partial(b).\text{{\hskip 0.0pt}(by strong linearity)}

This concludes the proof.
∎

The following result is folklore. We prove it for completion.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that
\mathbf{K}
has characteristic
0
.
Let
\mathbf{A}
be a
\mathbf{K}
-algebra and let
\partial:\mathbf{A}\longrightarrow\mathbf{A}
be a derivation. Then the function

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\partial}:\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\llbracket X\rrbracket

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\partial^{[k]}(a)}{k!}X^{k}

(15)

is a morphism of algebras.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in\mathbf{A}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, an easy induction using
the Leibniz product rule shows that

\partial^{[k]}(a\cdot b)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\partial^{[i]}(a)\cdot\partial^{[k-i]}(b).

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\partial}(a\cdot b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\partial^{[k]}(a\cdot b)}{k!}X^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{k!(k-i)!}\partial^{[i]}(a)\cdot\partial^{[k-i]}(b)\right)X^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\sum_{m+p=k}\frac{1}{m!p!}\partial^{[m]}(a)\cdot\partial^{[p]}(b)\right)X^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{m!}\partial^{[m]}(a)X^{m}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{p\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{p!}\partial^{[p]}(b)X^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\partial}(a)\cdot\mathcal{T}_{\partial}(b).

The function
\mathcal{T}_{\partial}
is clearly
\mathbf{K}
-linear, so we
are done.
∎

4.2
Differential pre-logarithmic Hahn fields

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a non-trivial, linearly ordered Abelian group and let

\mathbf{K}
be an ordered field. We write
\mathbb{S}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
. Recall that
\mathbb{S}
is an ordered field
extension of
\mathbf{K}
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\ell:(\mathfrak{M},\cdot,1,<)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{S},+,0,<)
be an embedding of ordered groups. Then we say that
(\mathbb{S},\ell)

is a
pre-logarithmic Hahn field
.

This is a weaker version of the notion of pre-logarithmic section

[
25
, Definition 2.7]
on
\mathbb{S}
.

Remark 4.5
.

Given an embedding of ordered groups

\log_{\mathbf{K}}:(\mathbf{K}^{>},\cdot,1,<)\longrightarrow(\mathbf{K},+,0,<)
, the function
\ell
extends
[
24
, Lemmas 4.12 and
5.1 and Theorem 4.1]
into an embedding of ordered groups
\log:(\mathbb{S}^{>},\cdot,1,<)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{S},+,0,<)

with

\log(c\mathfrak{m}(1+\varepsilon))=\ell(\mathfrak{m})+\log_{\mathbf{K}}(c)+\sum_{k>0}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\varepsilon^{k}

for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
,
c\in\mathbf{K}^{>}
and

\varepsilon\prec 1
. As a consequence of Proposition
2.21
,
the function
\log
is analytic on
\mathbb{S}^{>}
with
\operatorname{Conv}(\log)_{s}=s+\mathbb{S}^{\prec}
and
\log^{(k)}(s)=(-1)^{k}(k-1)!s^{-k}
for all
s\in\mathbb{S}^{>}
and
k>0
.

Definition 4.6
.

Assume that
(\mathbb{S},\ell)
is a pre-logarithmic Hahn field. Let

\partial:\mathbb{S}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}
be a strongly linear
derivation. We say that
(\mathbb{S},\ell,\partial)
is a

differential pre-logarithmic Hahn field
if we have

\partial(\ell(\mathfrak{m}))=\frac{\partial(\mathfrak{m})}{\mathfrak{m}}
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
.

Remark 4.7
.

Given
s,t,u\in\mathbb{S}
, we will write
s=t+o(u)
if
s-t\prec u
. Suppose that
s
and
t
have maximal common truncation
u
, and
write
s=u+c_{1}\mathfrak{m}_{1}+o(\mathfrak{m}_{1})
,
t=u+c_{2}\mathfrak{m}_{2}+o(\mathfrak{m}_{2})
where
c_{i}\in\mathbf{K}
and

\mathfrak{m}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
. Then
s>t
if and only if

c_{1}\mathfrak{m}_{1}+o(\mathfrak{m}_{1})>c_{2}\mathfrak{m}_{2}+o(\mathfrak{m}_{2})
if and only if
c_{1}\mathfrak{m}_{1}>c_{2}\mathfrak{m}_{2}
.

Our next technical result Proposition
4.9
is a version of
Proposition
3.8
for derivations, where a final segment

\mathfrak{S}
of
\mathfrak{M}
, we extend a strongly linear derivation on

\mathbb{S}
into a strongly linear derivation on
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}
. To that end, we need to “prepare”
\mathfrak{S}

with respect to some weak summability condition on its boundary. This is why
we need the following general lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
C
be a convex subset of some truncation
closed
L\subseteq\mathbb{S}
. Then there is a series
\varphi
and a
coinitial sequence in
C
of the form
(\psi_{i}+c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i})_{i<\kappa}
, where each
\psi_{i}
is a truncation of
\varphi
,
c_{i}\in\mathbf{K}
,
\mathfrak{o}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
. Moreover, we
may assume that either
(\psi_{i})_{i<\kappa}
is injective, in which case

\mathfrak{o}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)\cup\{0\}
(in particular,

(\psi_{i}+r_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i})_{i<\kappa}
is weakly summable), or
\psi_{i}=\varphi
for all
i<\kappa
.

Proof.

The conclusion is trivial if
C
has a minimum, so assume
C
does not. Let

\varphi_{0}\coloneqq 0
. By induction, consider the set
T_{i,I}
of the
dominant terms of
\gamma-\varphi_{i}
for
\gamma
in some initial segment

I\subseteq C
: if there is
I
so that
T_{i,I}=\{t_{i}\}
is a
singleton, we let
\varphi_{i+1}\coloneqq\varphi_{i}+t_{i}
, otherwise we
stop. At the limit stage, let
\varphi_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j<i}t_{j}
. The
procedure stops at some ordinal
i=\lambda
and we set
\varphi\coloneqq\varphi_{\lambda}
.

For any
\gamma_{i}\in C
, let
\psi_{i}
be the maximal common truncation of

\varphi
and
\gamma_{i}
and write
\gamma_{i}=\psi_{i}+c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i}+o(\mathfrak{o}_{i})
where
c_{i}\in\mathbf{K}
and
\mathfrak{o}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
. By maximality of
\psi_{i}
, there is
\gamma_{i+1}\in C
such that
\gamma_{i+1}<\gamma_{i}
and the maximal common
truncation of
\gamma_{i+1}
and
\gamma_{i}
is exactly
\psi_{i}
. By
definition of the ordering, we must have
\psi_{i+1}+c_{i+1}\mathfrak{o}_{i+1}+o(\mathfrak{o}_{i+1})<\psi_{i}+c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i}+o(\mathfrak{o}_{i})
.

It follows that there is a coinitial sequence
(\gamma_{i})_{i<\kappa}
such
that for all
i<j
we have
\psi_{i}+c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i}+o(\mathfrak{o}_{i})>\psi_{j}+c_{j}\mathfrak{o}_{j}+o(\mathfrak{o}_{j})
.
Therefore, the sequence
(\psi_{i}+c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i})
is also coinitial
with
C
. It ranges in
L
because
L
is truncation closed, thus it ranges
in
C
too.

After extracting a subsequence, we may further assume that
(\psi_{i})_{i<\kappa}
is either constant or injective. In the former case, we must have

\psi_{i}=\varphi
, and we are done. In the latter, write
\psi_{i+1}=\psi_{i}+d_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}+o(\mathfrak{p}_{i})
. We must have
\gamma_{i}=\psi_{i}+c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i}>\psi_{i}+d_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}+o(\mathfrak{p}_{i})\ni\gamma_{i+1}
; if
\mathfrak{o}_{i}\succ\mathfrak{p}_{i}
, then
\psi_{i}+d_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i}>\psi_{i}+|2d_{i}|\mathfrak{p}_{i}>\gamma_{1}
; if
\mathfrak{o}_{i}\prec\mathfrak{p}_{i}
, then

s_{i}<0
and so
\psi_{i}>\gamma_{1}
. Hence after possibly replacing
c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i}
with
|2d_{i}|\mathfrak{p}_{i}
or with
0
, we may assume
that
\mathfrak{o}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}(\psi_{i+1})\cup\{0\}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)\cup\{0\}
, and we are done. ∎

4.3
Cut extensions of derivations

Let
(\mathbb{S},\partial,\ell)
be a differential pre-logarithmic Hahn
field with
\mathbb{S}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
, such
that
\ell(\mathfrak{M})
is truncation closed in
\mathbb{S}
. Let

\mathfrak{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
be a final segment and consider the
corresponding algebra
\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}}\rrbracket
for the
ordering
\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}
of
Section

3.1
. Note that

\partial
is a derivation
\mathbb{S}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Consider the function

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}:\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}X^{k}

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}X^{k}.

Proposition 4.9
.

The function
\overline{\partial}
is Noetherian.

Proof.

Let
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}X^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly

\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}
-decreasing sequence in
\mathfrak{M}\times X^{\mathbb{N}}
. This means, by definition, that
(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

is weakly increasing and that there are monomials
\mathfrak{u}_{i}\succ\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathfrak{m}_{i+1}\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{k_{i+1}-k_{i}}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{i}
. Letting
\mathfrak{p}_{i+1}\coloneqq\mathfrak{u}_{0}^{k_{1}-k_{0}}\cdots\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{k_{i+1}-k_{i}}
,

\mathfrak{p}_{0}\coloneqq 1
, we find that
\mathfrak{m}_{i+1}\mathfrak{p}_{i+1}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}
.

Now pick some arbitrary
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}
for

i\in\mathbb{N}
. We claim that after taking a subsequence, the monomials

\mathfrak{n}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}
appear in the supports of some summable
family. This implies that there are
i<j
such that
\overset{}{}\mathfrak{n}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}\succ\mathfrak{n}_{j}\mathfrak{p}_{j}
, and so

\mathfrak{n}_{i}\succ(\min_{i\leqslant n<j}\mathfrak{u}_{n})^{k_{j}-k_{i}}\mathfrak{n}_{j}
, thus
\mathfrak{n}_{i}X^{k_{i}}\succ_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathfrak{n}_{j}X^{k_{j}}
, proving that
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}X^{k_{i}})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, and so that
\overline{\partial}
is Noetherian.

As a warm-up, observe that if
(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is constant, then

\mathfrak{n}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}=\mathfrak{n}_{i}
is in the support of

\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}
, and
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable by
strong linearity of
\partial
.

In the general case, observe that by strong linearity of
\partial
, the
family

(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i})^{\prime}=\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}\mathfrak{p}_{i}+\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{p}_{i}(\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{{\dagger}})

is summable. Note moreover that
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}\mathfrak{p}_{i}
. We also have

\mathfrak{p}_{i+1}^{\dagger}=(k_{1}-k_{0})\mathfrak{u}_{0}^{\dagger}+\cdots+(k_{i+1}-k_{i})\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{{\dagger}}.

If the sequence
(\ell(\mathfrak{u}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is weakly
summable, then
(\mathfrak{u}_{i}^{\dagger})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is weakly
summable, so
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=((\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i})\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\dagger})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, hence
(\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}\mathfrak{p}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, and we are done.

If the sequence
(\ell(\mathfrak{u}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is not
coinitial in
\ell(\mathfrak{M}\setminus\mathfrak{S})
, then there is

\mathfrak{u}\succ\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathfrak{u}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{u}_{i}
for all
i
. So we may assume that
\mathfrak{u}_{i}=\mathfrak{u}
for all
i
, in which case
(\ell(\mathfrak{u}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is weakly summable, and we are done. Otherwise, we may replace

(\mathfrak{u}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with a subsequence of any other
coinitial sequence in
\ell(\mathfrak{M}\setminus\mathfrak{S})
. Since

\ell(\mathfrak{M})
is truncation closed and
\ell(\mathfrak{M}\setminus\mathfrak{S})
is a convex subset of
\ell(\mathfrak{M})
, by
Lemma
4.8
, we may choose the sequence so that
(\ell(\mathfrak{u}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is either weakly summable, or of the
form
(\varphi+c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and not weakly
summable. Thus after taking a subsequence with
(\mathfrak{o}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
strictly increasing and with
r_{i}<0
.

In the former case, we are done. In the latter, note that for any choice of
non-zero
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, there is another coinitial sequence

(\mathfrak{v}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\ell(\mathfrak{M}\setminus\mathfrak{S})
such that
\ell(\mathfrak{v}_{i})=\varphi+n_{i}c_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{i}
. If there are
j\in\mathbb{N}
and infinitely many
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\varphi^{\prime})\cup\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{j}^{\prime})
, we note that

(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}(\varphi^{\prime}+\mathfrak{o}_{j}^{\prime}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, and we are done. Otherwise, we can choose
n_{i}
so
that
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{o}_{j}^{\prime})

implies that
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{m}_{i}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{{\dagger}})
and in particular
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i})^{\prime}
. With this
choice, for each
i
we have
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}\in(\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}_{i}\varphi^{\prime})\cup(\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i})^{\prime})
, and we are done.
∎

We thus have a strongly linear extension
\overline{\partial}:\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}
of
\overline{\partial}
. It follows from
Lemma
4.2
that
\overline{\partial}
is a derivation. Define

\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{\small{\textdagger}}}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\mathfrak{d}_{\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}}\in\mathfrak{S}^{-1}\}.

Lemma 4.10
.

The class
\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{{\small{\textdagger}\/}}}

is a subgroup of
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

For
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{\small{\textdagger}}}
, we have

\mathfrak{d}_{(\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}^{-1})^{{\dagger}}}=\mathfrak{d}_{\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}-\mathfrak{n}^{{\dagger}}}\preccurlyeq\max(\mathfrak{d}_{\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}},\mathfrak{d}_{\mathfrak{n}^{{\dagger}}})
.
We deduce since
\mathfrak{S}^{-1}
is an initial segment of
\mathfrak{M}

that
\mathfrak{d}_{(\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}^{-1})^{{\dagger}}}\in\mathfrak{S}^{-1}
, whence
\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}^{-1}\in\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{\small{\textdagger}}}
.
∎

We write
\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\left\llbracket\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{\small{\textdagger}}}\right\rrbracket
, so
\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}
is a subfield of

\mathbb{S}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose that
\partial(\mathfrak{S}^{-}\;{}^{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{${\dagger}$}})\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}
. Then the function

\displaystyle X\cdot\overline{\partial}:\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}(P)X

is a strongly linear and contracting derivation.

Proof.

This is the restriction of a strongly linear function, so it is strongly
linear. Since
\overline{\partial}
is a derivation, so is
X\cdot\overline{\partial}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}X^{k}\in\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{\small{\textdagger}}}\times X^{\mathbb{N}}
,
and let
\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}\left((X\cdot\overline{\partial})(\mathfrak{m}X^{k})\right)
. So
\mathfrak{m}\neq 1
, and

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{q}X^{k+1}
for a
\mathfrak{q}\in\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
. We want to show that
\mathfrak{n}\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathfrak{m}X^{k}
. We have
\mathfrak{q}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
, so

\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}
. We deduce
since
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{\small{\textdagger}}}
that

\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{m}^{-1}\in\mathfrak{S}^{-1}
, so

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{q}X^{k+1}\prec_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathfrak{m}X^{k}
.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Assume that
\partial(\mathfrak{S}^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{{\small{\textdagger}\/}}})\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}
. Then the function

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{s^{(k)}}{k!}X^{k}

is a well-defined and strongly linear morphism of algebras.

Proof.

We apply Proposition
1.25
to
X\cdot\overline{\partial}
.
Since
\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}\llbracket X\rrbracket_{\mathfrak{S}}
, this shows that the restriction of

\mathcal{T}_{X\cdot\overline{\partial}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(X\cdot\overline{\partial})^{[k]}}{k!}
to

\mathbb{S}_{[\mathfrak{S}]}

5
Taylor expansions

Our goal in this section is to study the convergence of Taylor expansions. We
fix an ordered field
\mathbf{K}
. Let
\mathfrak{M},\mathfrak{N}
be
non-trivial, linearly ordered Abelian groups. Let
(\mathbb{S},\ell,\partial)
be a differential pre-logarithmic Hahn field with
\mathbb{S}=\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}\rrbracket
, write
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\mathbf{K}\llbracket\mathfrak{N}\rrbracket
and let
\triangle:\mathbb{S}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}
be a strongly linear morphism of
ordered rings. We also fix an
x\in\mathbb{S}^{\times}
, such that for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
the
following holds:

(\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\preccurlyeq x^{-1}\wedge(\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})^{{\dagger}}\preccurlyeq x^{-1})\text{ or $(\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\succ x^{-1}\wedge(\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})^{{\dagger}}\asymp\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}})$)}.

(16)

Remark 5.1
.

The condition (
16
) is satisfied for differential fields of
transseries, including surreal numbers (Proposition
5.12
), that
are built upon a variable
x
in a constructive way (see
Lemma
5.13
). We expect it is valid in most reasonable
differential fields of transseries.

Given
s\in\mathbb{S}
, and
\delta\in\mathbb{T}
, we study the
convergence of the Taylor series
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle(s^{(k)})}{k!}X^{k}\in\mathbb{T}\llbracket X\rrbracket
at
X=\delta
.
That is, we want to find conditions under which the family
(\triangle(s^{(k)})\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable. Our summability result is
as follows:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
be a
well-based subset. For all
\delta\in\mathbb{T}
with
\delta\prec\triangle(x)
and
\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}})\delta\prec 1
whenever

\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{S}
, the family
(\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{(k)})\delta^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{S}\wedge k\in\mathbb{N}}
is
summable.

Remark 5.3
.

Subject to the condition
\delta\prec\triangle(x)
, the
domain of summability of the family
(\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{(k)})\delta^{k})_{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}s\wedge k\in\mathbb{N}}
is
optimal. Indeed, let
f\in\mathbb{S}
. If each element
\mathfrak{m}
in
the support of
f
is flat in the sense that
\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\preccurlyeq x^{-1}
, then the condition
(\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}))\delta\prec 1
is already implied by
\delta\prec\triangle(x)
. Suppose
now that there is an
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}f
which is not flat.
Then (
16
) implies that we have
(\mathfrak{m}^{(k)})^{{\dagger}}\asymp\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, for all

\delta
with
\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}})\delta\succcurlyeq 1
, we
have
\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{(k)})\preccurlyeq\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{(k+1)})\delta
, whence

\triangle(\mathfrak{m})\preccurlyeq\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})\delta\preccurlyeq\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime})\delta^{2}\preccurlyeq\cdots.

This implies that the family
(\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{(k)})\delta^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is not summable.

In transseries and surreal numbers (taking
x
as the unique monomial with
derivative
1
), a slightly stronger version of (
16
) applies:
in the case
\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\preccurlyeq x^{-1}
, we get
(\operatorname{supp}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})^{{\dagger}}\asymp x^{-1}
. This entails that

(\mathfrak{m}^{(k)})^{{\dagger}}\asymp x^{-1}
, thus as in the previous
argument
\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{(k)})\preccurlyeq\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{(k+1)})\delta
whenever
\delta\succcurlyeq\triangle(x)
. So in surreal numbers and transseries, the bound
\delta\prec\Delta(x)
is also sharp. In order to prove the stronger version of
(
16
), it suffices to use the fact that if

\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}}\preccurlyeq x^{-1}
, then there are an
r\in\mathbb{R}
and a
\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}
such that

\mathfrak{n}^{{\dagger}}\prec 1
and
\mathfrak{m}=x^{r}\mathfrak{n}
.

Given a fixed
\delta\in\mathbb{T}
with
\delta\prec\triangle(x)
, let

\mathfrak{S}_{\delta}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{N}:\mathfrak{n}\succ\delta\}
, and write

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{\triangle,\delta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left({\triangle^{\ast}}(\mathfrak{S}_{\delta})\right)^{-\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{\small{\textdagger}}}=\{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\triangle(\mathfrak{m}^{{\dagger}})\delta\prec 1\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{\triangle,\delta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}_{\triangle,\delta}\rrbracket.

We call the partial map

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\triangle):\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle(s^{(k)})}{k!}\delta^{k}.

a Taylor deformation of
\triangle
.
Theorem

5.2
follows from
the following result:

Proposition 5.4
.

The class
\mathfrak{M}_{\triangle,\delta}

is a subgroup of
\mathfrak{M}
and
\mathbb{S}_{\triangle,\delta}

\mathbb{S}

We show that Taylor deformations of

Let

Taylor expansions and analytic functions

We next prove
Theorem

5.5
. In order to do that, we rely on the
following formal result:

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{N}_{0}

be non-trivial, linearly ordered Abelian groups and set
\mathbb{S}_{0}=\mathbb{R}\llbracket\mathfrak{M}_{0}\rrbracket
and
\mathbb{T}_{0}=\mathbb{R}\llbracket\mathfrak{N}_{0}\rrbracket
. Let
\triangle_{0}:\mathbb{S}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}_{0}
be a strongly linear morphism of
rings and let
\mathrm{d}:\mathbb{S}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}_{0}\>;\>s\mapsto s^{\prime}
be a strongly linear derivation.

Let
s\in\mathbb{S}_{0}
, let
f:\mathbb{S}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}_{0}
and
g:\mathbb{T}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}_{0}
be
analytic at
s
and
\triangle_{0}(s)
respectively, with
\operatorname{Conv}(f)_{s}\supseteq s+\mathbb{S}_{0}^{\prec s}
and
\operatorname{Conv}(g)_{\triangle_{0}(s)}\supseteq\triangle_{0}(s)+\mathbb{T}_{0}^{\prec\triangle_{0}(s)}
. Assume
that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(f^{(k)}(s))^{\prime}=f^{(k+1)}(s)s^{\prime}

(17)

and

\triangle_{0}(f^{(k)}(s))=g^{(k)}(\triangle_{0}(s)).

(18)

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{T}_{0}
such that the family
(\triangle_{0}(s^{(k)})\varepsilon^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable with

\forall k>0,\triangle_{0}(s)\succ\triangle_{0}(s^{(k)})\varepsilon^{k}.

(19)

Then the family
(\triangle_{0}(f(s)^{(k)})\varepsilon^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, with

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle_{0}(f(s)^{(k)})}{k!}\varepsilon^{k}=g\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle_{0}(s^{(k)})}{k!}\varepsilon^{k}\right).

In other words, the relation
\triangle_{0}\circ f=g\circ\triangle_{0}
is
also satisfied for the Taylor deformation
T_{\varepsilon}(\triangle_{0}):s\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle_{0}(s^{(k)})}{k!}\varepsilon^{k}
of
\triangle_{0}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\varepsilon\neq 0
. By
Proposition
2.19
, the function
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{S}^{\preccurlyeq\varepsilon}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}_{0}
given
for
\delta\preccurlyeq\varepsilon
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\delta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle_{0}(s^{(k)})}{k!}\delta^{k}

is analytic on
\mathbb{S}^{\preccurlyeq\varepsilon}_{0}
. Our goal is to
show that
g(\mathcal{A}(\varepsilon))=\tilde{P}(\varepsilon)
where

P\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle_{0}(f(s)^{(k)})}{k!}X^{k}\in\mathbb{T}\llbracket X\rrbracket.

The function
g
is analytic at
\triangle_{0}(s)
with
\operatorname{Conv}(g)_{\triangle_{0}(s)}\supseteq\triangle_{0}(s)+\mathbb{T}_{0}^{\prec\triangle_{0}(s)}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k>0
, we set

\displaystyle X_{n,k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\{v\in(\mathbb{N}^{>})^{n}:|v|\coloneqq v_{[1]}+\cdots+v_{[n]}=k\}\text{\quad and}

\displaystyle c_{k,n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in X_{n,k}}\frac{g^{(n)}(\triangle_{0}(s))}{n!}\frac{\triangle_{0}(s^{(v_{[1]})})}{v_{[1]}!}\cdots\frac{\triangle_{0}(s^{(v_{[n]})})}{v_{[n]}!}.

We have
\mathcal{A}(\delta)-\triangle_{0}(s)\prec\triangle_{0}(s)
by
(
19
), so we may apply
Proposition
2.22
and see that
g\circ\mathcal{A}

is analytic on
\mathbb{S}_{0}^{\preccurlyeq\varepsilon}
. Moreover, the
family
(c_{k,n}\varepsilon^{k})_{n\in\mathbb{N},k>0}
is summable,
with

g\circ\mathcal{A}(\varepsilon)=g(\triangle_{0}(s))+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N},k>0}c_{n,k}\varepsilon^{k}.

(20)

So by Lemma
1.13
, the family
\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{k,n}\varepsilon^{k}\right)_{k>0}
is summable, and

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N},k>0}c_{n,k}\varepsilon^{k}=\sum_{k>0}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{k,n}\right)\varepsilon^{k}.

Since
g(\triangle_{0}(s))=\triangle_{0}(f(s))
and in view of
(
20
), it suffices to show that
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{k,n}=\frac{f(s)^{(k)}}{k!}
for all
k>0
. By
(
18
), we have
\triangle_{0}(f^{(n)}(s))=g^{(n)}(\triangle_{0}(s))
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that we
have a chain rule (
17
) at
s
. An induction gives
Faà di Bruno’s formula, i.e.

\frac{(f(s))^{(k)}}{k!}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{v\in X_{n,k}}\frac{f^{(n)}(s)}{n!}\frac{s^{(v_{[1]})}}{v_{[1]}!}\cdots\frac{s^{(v_{[n]})}}{v_{[n]}!}.

Therefore

\frac{\triangle_{0}(f(s)^{(k)})}{k!}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{v\in X_{n,k}}\frac{g^{(n)}(\triangle_{0}(s))}{n!}\frac{\triangle_{0}(s^{(v_{[1]})})}{v_{[1]}!}\cdots\frac{\triangle_{0}(s^{(v_{[n]})})}{v_{[n]}!}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}c_{n,k}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem

5.5
follows from Proposition
5.9

for
(\mathbb{S}_{0},\mathbb{T}_{0},\triangle_{0},\mathrm{d})=(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{T},\triangle,\partial)
and
g\coloneqq\Psi(f)
for each

.

Assume that
x^{\prime}=1
. Let

The relation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(T_{\delta}(\triangle)(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\triangle(s^{(k)})}{k!}\delta^{k}\right)
