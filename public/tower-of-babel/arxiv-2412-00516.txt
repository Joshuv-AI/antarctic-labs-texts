Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theory for the Hessian
Karol Bołbotowski and Guy Bouchitté

Abstract
. The classical Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem establishes a significant connection between Monge optimal transport and maximization of a linear form on the set of 1-Lipschitz functions. This result has been widely used in various research areas. In particular, it unlocks the optimal transport methods in some of the optimal design problems. This paper puts forth a similar theory when the linear form is maximized over C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} functions whose Hessian lies between minus and plus identity matrix. The problem will be identified as the dual of a specific optimal transport formulation that involves three-point plans. The first two marginals are fixed, while the third must dominate the other two in the sense of convex order. The existence of optimal plans allows to express solutions of the underlying Beckmann problem as a combination of rank-one tensor measures supported on a graph. In the context of two-dimensional mechanics, this graph encodes the optimal configuration of a grillage that transfers a given load system.



Keywords:
Hessian-constrained problem, Monge optimal transport, tensor-valued measures,
second-order Beckmann problem, convex order, stochastic dominance, optimal grillage.

1.
introduction

The classical Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem plays a fundamental role in the Monge optimal transport theory
[
29
,
32
]
.
In the Euclidean framework, it states that,
for given probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite first-order moments, the
L^{1}
Monge-Kantorovich distance

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\left\{\iint|x-y|\,\gamma(dxdy)\ :\ \gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\right\}\

coincides with the maximum in the following linear programming problem

{\mathcal{I}_{1}(f)}=\sup\left\{{\int u\,df}\ :\ u\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ {\mathrm{lip}}(u)\leq 1\right\}

(1.1)

for the signed measure
f=\nu-\mu
. Above,
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
stands for the set of probability measures on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
having the first and second marginal
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively.

The equality
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)={\mathcal{I}_{1}(f)}
is a key ingredient for deriving a
PDE approach to the optimal transport problem, see e.g.
[
8
,
17
]
. It also allows to interpret the Monge distance as the total variation of a vector measure
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
which solves the so-called
Beckmann problem:

\min\left\{\int|\sigma|\ :\ {-{\mathrm{div}}\,\sigma=f}\ \ \text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}.

(1.2)

The geometric insights into the optimal transport interpretation are
very meaningful. Using the notion of transport rays or, more generally, geodesics, it is possible to recast the solutions to Beckmann problem from the optimal transport plans
\gamma
via the decomposition formula:

\sigma=\iint\lambda^{x,y}\,\gamma(dxdy),\qquad\lambda^{x,y}:=\frac{y-x}{{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}}\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}[x,y].

(1.3)

In particular, it follows that every optimal measure
\sigma
is supported on the convex (geodesic) hull of the support of
f
.

In the late 1990s, a bridge between Monge optimal transport and optimal design was proposed in
[
8
]
. It was found that an optimal measure
\sigma
for (
1.2
) represents the heat flow in a conductor to be optimally designed for a given source
f
. The optimal transport approach can be applied accordingly to derive the best distribution of the conductive material. In particular, this approach covers the case of concentrated source terms
f
, which cannot be treated by the classical PDE methods.

Later on, the paper
[
7
]
has shown that a larger class of optimal design problems can be tackled by solving
a Beckmann-type problem, see also the recent work
[
6
]
. This includes compliance minimization of elastic bodies where
\sigma
is tensor-valued, while the potential
u
is vectorial, and they represent stress and displacement, respectively.
However, the optimal transport strategy does not automatically extend to these cases.
In view of the important underlying applications, there is an urge to study possible extensions of the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality principle. Such a question could be formulated as follows.
Let
A
be a linear differential operator on smooth vector-valued functions
u:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
Au:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for
\varrho
take a semi-norm on real
n\times d
matrices. Is there an optimal transport formulation that we can employ to address the following maximization problem:

\sup\Big\{{\left\langle u,f\right\rangle}:\ u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{n}),\ \ \varrho(Au)\leq 1\ \text{in $\Omega$}\Big\},

(1.4)

where
\Omega
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
f
is a suitable source term supported on
{\overline{\Omega}}
? By standard convex duality, the above supremum can be written as an infimum in the following Beckmann-type problem:

\min\left\{\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\ :\ A^{*}\sigma=f\ \text{ in $\big(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)^{n}$},\ \spt{\sigma}\subset{\overline{\Omega}}\right\},

(1.5)

where
\varrho^{0}
is the polar of
\varrho
given by

\varrho^{0}(S):=\sup\big\{{\left\langle Q,S\right\rangle}\,:\ \varrho(Q)\leq 1\big\}.

(1.6)

In the classical Rubinstein-Kantorovich framework,
f
is a scalar measure (
n=1
),
\varrho
is the Euclidean norm, and
A
is the gradient operator. In order that the supremum (
1.4
) is finite,
f
must be
balanced
, that is
\mu=f_{+}
and
\nu=f_{-}
must have the same mass. Note that if
\Omega
is not convex, then the Euclidean distance appearing in the definition of
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)
should be replaced by the geodesic distance induced by
\Omega
. For a detailed study see
[
7
]
where a number of explicit examples is given.

When
A
is no longer the gradient operator, there are very few results regarding a possible optimal transport approach.
In the recent work
[
4
]
, the present authors put forward a formulation where
the Monge-Kantorovich distance emerges and is maximized with respect to a suitable class of metrics
d(x,y)
on
\Omega
.
Therein, the potential
u
becomes a pair
(v,w):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
, and it must meet the constraint
e(v)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla w\otimes\nabla w\leq\mathrm{Id}
where
e(v)
is the symmetrized gradient. The non-linear operator on the left hand side defines the strain tensor in the Föppl’s membrane model
[
13
]
, rendering the formulation in
[
4
]
an optimal membrane problem. Despite the non-linearity, the problem admits the form (
1.5
) upon the right choice of
A
and
\varrho
, see
[
4
]
for more details.
Prior to the latter work, an attempt to treat the case where
A
is simply the symmetrized gradient (i.e.
Au=e(u)
for
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
) was made in
[
10
]
where (
1.5
) is nothing else but the famous Michell problem.
However, to our knowledge, the bridge with optimal transport in this case has not yet been established, and challenging open problems remain.

The aim of the present paper is to provide an optimal transport approach in the case of the Hessian operator
Au=\nabla^{2}u
and with
\varrho
being the spectral norm. We will limit ourselves to the case when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we will assume that
f
is a measure, more accurately

f=\nu-\mu

for two probabilities
\mu,\nu
. The special choice of
\varrho
makes it possible to rewrite (
1.4
) as the second-order counterpart of (
1.1
):

\mathcal{I}(f):=\sup\left\{\int u\,d\nu-\int u\,d\mu\ :\ u\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ {\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1\right\}.

(1.7)

The supremum
\mathcal{I}(f)
is finite and is attained if and only if
\mu
and
\nu
share the barycentre
[\mu]=[\nu]
while exhibiting finite second-order moments:
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This way
\mathcal{I}(f)
defines a distance between such
\mu
and
\nu
. We should point out that similar distances (called
ideal metrics
) were introduced years ago by V.M. Zolotarev
[
34
]
with the aim of studying continuity and stability of stochastic models in probability theory.

Under the foregoing assumptions on the data
\mu,\nu
, we will see that the classical duality theory leads to a well-posed second-order Beckmann formulation:

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f):=\min\biggl\{\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\ :\ \sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}),\ \ {\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=\nu-\mu\ \ \text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\biggr\}

(1.8)

with the zero-gap equality
\mathcal{I}(f)=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f)
.
Here,
\varrho^{0}
is the Schatten norm on symmetric matrices
S\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
given by
\varrho^{0}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}(S)|
, and

\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)
is intended in the sense of convex one-homogeneous functionals on measures

[
19
]
.
Through the classical methods, the optimality conditions involving pairs
(u,\sigma)
can be derived, even in the case of singular measures
\sigma
and for general semi-norms
\varrho
. For a detailed study we refer to e.g.
[
9
]
where optimal design problems for plates
are considered.

At the core of our work lies a connection between the pair (
1.7
), (
1.8
) and a newly proposed
three-marginal optimal transport problem.
Let us introduce the cost function defined for each triple
(x,y,z)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3}
by

c(x,y,z):=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Bigr).

(1.9)

We are looking for probabilities
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3})
(three-marginal transport plans) to solve

\mathcal{J}(\mu,\nu):=\inf\biggl\{\iiint c(x,y,z)\,\pi(dxdydz)\ :\ \pi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)\biggr\}.

(1.10)

The set
\Sigma(\mu,\nu)\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3})
consists of 3-plans
\pi
whose first and second marginal is

\mu
and
\nu
, respectively, and which satisfy the following equations

\iiint\!{\left\langle z-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,\pi(dxdydz)=\iiint\!{\left\langle z-y,\Psi(y)\right\rangle}\,\pi(dxdydz)=0

(1.11)

for any smooth test functions
\Phi,\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It will unravel that these relations have a natural interpretation in probability theory (via convex order) as well as
in structural mechanics (moment equilibrium at junctions of a grillage).
The main result of the paper reads as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Take
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
sharing the barycentre
[\mu]=[\nu]
, and let
\mathcal{J}(\mu,\nu)
be defined by (
1.10
).
For
f=\nu-\mu
the value
\mathcal{I}(f)
is given by (
1.7
). Then,

(i) it holds that

\mathcal{I}(f)=\mathcal{J}(\mu,\nu),

(1.12)

while there exist optimal pairs
(u,\pi)
solving (
1.7
) and (
1.10
), respectively;

(ii) an admissible pair
(u,\pi)
is optimal if and only if the following three-point equality is satisfied
\pi
-a.e.,
with
c
defined by (
1.9
),

\displaystyle[u(y)+{\left\langle\nabla u(y),z-y\right\rangle}]-[u(x)+{\left\langle\nabla u(x),z-x\right\rangle}]=c(

\displaystyle x,y,z)

(1.13)

for
\pi
-a.e.
(x,y,z)
.

It is worth mentioning that the equality (
1.13
) is in close relation with the admissibility of
u
in (
1.7
). Indeed, following
[
21
]
, the condition
{\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1
is equivalent to the existence of a continuous vector function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that:

\displaystyle[u(y)+{\left\langle\Phi(y),z-y\right\rangle}]-[u(x)+{\left\langle\Phi(x),z-x\right\rangle}]\leq c(

\displaystyle x,y,z)

(1.14)

\displaystyle\forall\,(x,y,z)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3}.

In addition, the inequality (
1.14
) can be satisfied only for
\Phi=\nabla u
.

With the equality (
1.12
) at hand, we are in a position to propose the tensor counterpart of the decomposition (
1.3
) that was useful in the first-order gradient case. We show that optimal measures for the second-order Beckmann problem (
1.8
)
can be decomposed to rank-one tensor measures supported on polygonal lines. More precisely, for any triple
(x,y,z)
let us define the following measure valued in the space of symmetric matrices, i.e. an element of
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})
:

\sigma^{x,y,z}(d\xi):={\left\lvert\xi-z\right\rvert}\,\Big(\sigma^{z,x}(d\xi)-\sigma^{z,y}(d\xi)\Big),

(1.15)

where we denote

\sigma^{a,b}=\frac{b-a}{|b-a|}\otimes\frac{b-a}{|b-a|}\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}[a,b].

(1.16)

The measure
\sigma^{x,y,z}
(see Fig.
1
) is supported on the set
[x,z]\cup[z,y]
. Its second distributional divergence is
{\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma^{x,y,z}=f^{x,y,z}
, where

f^{x,y,z}:=\delta_{y}-\delta_{x}-{\mathrm{div}}\big((z-y)\,\delta_{y}-(z-x)\,\delta_{x}\big)

(1.17)

includes a first-order term. The key observation is that
\sigma^{x,y,z}
solves (
1.8
)
for
f=f^{x,y,z}
with the equality
\mathcal{I}(f^{x,y,z})=c(x,y,z)
provided that
z
belongs to the ball
B\big(\frac{x+y}{2},\frac{|x-y|}{2}\big)
(see Proposition
4.6
).
Accordingly, our strategy for solving (
1.8
) for
f=\nu-\mu
is to search for optimal tensor measures

\sigma
in the form

\sigma=\iiint\sigma^{x,y,z}\,\pi(dxdydz),

where
\pi
is a suitable three-marginal plan, see the convention (
1.22
) below. Satisfying the admissibility condition
\pi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
in the three-marginal optimal transport formulation (
1.10
) guarantees that
{\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=\iiint f^{x,y,z}d\pi=\nu-\mu
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\overline{\pi}
be an optimal 3-plan for (
1.10
). Then,

(i) the tensor measure
\overline{\sigma}=\iiint\sigma^{x,y,z}\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)
solves the second-order Beckmann problem (
1.8
) for
f=\nu-\mu
;

(ii) let
\overline{\gamma}:=\Pi_{1,2}^{\#}(\overline{\pi})
be the marginal of
\overline{\pi}
with respect to the first two variables, and let
\overline{u}

be any solution of (
1.7
). Then, for any
\overline{\pi}
-integrable test function
\varphi:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have the disintegration formula:

\iiint\varphi\,d\overline{\pi}=\iint\varphi\big(x,y,z_{\overline{u}}(x,y)\big)\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy),

where

z_{\overline{u}}(x,y):=\frac{x+y}{2}+\frac{\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)}{2}.

(1.18)

As a result, the optimal measure
\overline{\sigma}
is supported on the closed subset

\mathcal{B}(\spt\mu,\spt\nu):=\bigcup\left\{B\Big(\frac{x+y}{2},\frac{|x-y|}{2}\Big)\,:\,(x,y)\in\spt\mu\times\spt\nu\right\},

(1.19)

where
B(x_{0},r)
is the closed ball of radius
r>0
and centred at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let us recall that, in the first-order case (
1.2
), a geometric bound on the support of any optimal measure can be expressed as in (
1.19
) if we replace the ball on the right-hand side by the line segment
[x,y]
. Contrarily, in the Hessian case, a larger set is required to cover the support of the possible optimal measures. This will be confirmed on a number of examples.
Thus, we refute the conjecture in
[
9
]
where, assuming mild conditions on the norm
\varrho^{0}
entering (
1.8
), it was suggested that optimal measures are supported on the convex hull of the source
f
.

The proof of the central equality (
1.12
), that we will present here, passes through an unexpected link between our three-marginal optimal transport formulation (
1.10
) and optimization under the convex order dominance constraints. This connection is based on the observation that the existence of a 3-plan
\pi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
which admits
\rho
as the third marginal
is equivalent to the convex order conditions
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu,\ \rho\succeq_{c}\nu
, that is:

\int\varphi\,d\rho\ \geq\ \max\left\{\int\varphi\,d\mu,\int\varphi\,d\nu\right\}\quad\text{for all convex \ $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$}.

(1.20)

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be probability measures satisfying
[\mu]=[\nu]
, and set

{\mathcal{V}}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\Big\{{\rm{var}}(\rho)\ :\ \rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\mu,\ \ \rho\succeq_{c}\nu\Big\},

(1.21)

where
{\rm{var}}(\rho)
is the variance of the measure
\rho
.

(i) With
f=\nu-\mu
, the following equalities hold true:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(f)={\mathcal{V}}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{{\rm{var}}(\mu)+{\rm{var}}(\nu)}{2}=\mathcal{J}(\mu,\nu).

Moreover, an admissible
3
-plan
\pi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
is optimal for (
1.10
) if and only if its third marginal
\rho

is a minimizer in (
1.21
).

(ii) The infimum in (
1.21
) is achieved. Moreover, to any minimal
\rho
we can associate at least one 3-plan
\pi
that solves (
1.10
) and whose three marginals are
\mu,\nu
, and
\rho
, subsequently.

Here, several comments are in order. When proving the relation between
\mathcal{I}(f)
and
{\mathcal{V}}(\mu,\nu)
, we shall
pass through a duality result (Proposition
3.3
) which allows to identify
{\mathcal{V}}(\mu,\nu)
as the supremum in the following auxiliary problem:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\mu,\nu):=\sup\bigg\{\int\varphi\,d\mu+\int\psi\,d\nu\,:\,\varphi,

\displaystyle\,\psi\ \text{are convex $C^{1,1}$ functions},

\displaystyle\varphi(z)+\psi(z)\leq|z-z_{0}|^{2}\ \ \ \forall\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigg\},

where
z_{0}=[\mu]=[\nu]
.
Then, the crucial equality
\mathcal{I}(f)={\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{1}{2}\big({\rm{var}}(\mu)+{\rm{var}}(\nu)\big)
will be derived directly in Section
3.3
by exploiting a smoothing effect due to convexification of semi-concave functions
[
12
,
14
,
3
]
, see
Lemma
3.5
where we restate this property.

On another note, the construction of an optimal 3-plan
\overline{\pi}
announced in the assertion (ii) will use two martingale transports: one between
\mu
and
\rho
, and the other between
\nu
and
\rho
. Their existence follows from the classical theorem of Strassen. An optimal
\overline{\pi}
can be then constructed via a gluing argument, see the statement and the proof of Lemma
3.7
. In general, such
\overline{\pi}
is not unique.

Let us also point out that the problem (
1.21
) falls within a larger class of stochastic optimization problems under dominance constraints, for which there exist many applications in mathematical finance, statistical decision theory, or economics. For instance, see
the papers
[
15
,
26
,
33
]
.

We close the introduction with a comment on the close relation between the results presented herein and an optimal design problem in mechanics when
d=2
. Any measure
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2})
that satisfies the equation
{\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=f
represents a
bending moment tensor
in a plate that is subject to a load
f
. If the measure
\sigma
is of the form
\iiint\sigma^{x,y,z}d\pi
, then we speak of a
grillage
– a particular plate that decomposes to straight bars. The bars exhibit linearly varying rank-one bending moments, see Fig.
1
depicting the basic two-bar measure
\sigma^{x,y,z}
. A natural issue studied in the literature
[
28
,
27
,
5
]
consists in finding an optimal configuration of the grillage, i.e. a coupling
\overline{\pi}
that minimizes a certain total energy functional. To date, however, the existence result was not available, and neither were the criteria for the finite support of potential solutions
\overline{\pi}
, which corresponds to practical designs in the form of finite systems of bars.

In this work we show that, when the load
f
is a measure, an optimal grillage can be recast by solving the new three-marginal optimal transport formulation (
1.10
). As a byproduct, we get the bound
\mathcal{B}(\spt f_{+},\spt f_{-})
on the support of the associated tensor measure
\overline{\sigma}
. Moreover, a finitely supported optimal 3-plan
\overline{\pi}
can be selected provided that
f
is also finitely supported. In this case, the induced measure
\overline{\sigma}
concentrates on a graph. It should be noted that, despite a similarity to the optimal grillage problem, there is no such OT reformulation for the more popular
optimal truss problem
. In fact, it has been known for 120 years that optimal trusses do not exist even for the simplest load data
[
23
]
. In this case, a relaxation in the form of the famous Michell formulation
[
10
,
22
]
is essential. On top of that, a geometric bound on the support of its solutions is still pending.

Finally, we stress that our results concerning optimal grillages do not immediately extend to the case of a source
f
containing a first-order distribution term, or to the case when the support of the induced stress
\overline{\sigma}
is confined within a given domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Such extensions are beyond the scope of this paper and are worthy of future study.

The paper is organized as follows.
In the preliminary Section
2
we adapt the classical duality theory to show the no-gap equality between (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) as well as the existence of an optimal pair
(u,\sigma)
. Besides, in view of the forthcoming connection with the stochastic optimization, we give a short background on convex order and its relation with martingale transport.
The Section
3
presents the proofs of Theorem
1.1
, Corollary
1.2
,
and Theorem
1.3
.
In Section
4
, we give a series of examples where optimal configurations are determined explicitly. The Section
5
is devoted to the underlying 2D optimal design formulation. Numerical examples of optimal grillages are given and discussed along with the related open questions. Readers who are less interested in the applications should feel free to skip this final section.

Notations

Throughout the paper we will use the following notations.

•

The Euclidean norm of
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
|z|
.

•

By
{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
we shall denote the space of
d\times d
symmetric matrices, while
{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}_{+}}
will be its subset whose elements are positive semi-definite.
Given
A,B\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}
, we will write
A\leq B
if
B-A\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}_{+}}
. Moreover,
{\mathrm{Tr}}A
stands for the trace of
A
, while
{\mathrm{Id}}
is the identity matrix.

•

For natural
k\in\mathbbm{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
,
C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the spaces of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are continuously differentiable up to order
k
, while
\nabla u
and
\nabla^{2}u
are the gradient and the Hessian of a function
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, respectively. Moreover,
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the subset of continuous functions that vanish at infinity.

•

\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the space of
C^{\infty}
functions that are compactly supported, and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(the dual of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
).

•

For a function
v:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\mathrm{lip}}(v)
stands for the Lipschitz constant equal to
\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{{\left\lvert v(x)-v(y)\right\rvert}}{{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}}
.

•

By

The classical duality framework

The duality theory involving the linear constrained problem

)
and the general Beckmann formulation (

is well understood in the case of the Hessian operator as far as we are confined in a bounded domain

and when
\varrho

{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}

[

. Since we are concerned with the case

Therefore, in addition to the general notations given in the introduction, for

-

{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

p

is a finite dimensional normed vector space.

-

X_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\|u\|_{X_{p}}:=\sup\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{p}}<+\infty
.
The closed subspace
X_{p,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that
\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{p}}=0
, is a separable
Banach space.

A pairing between
X_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by
{\left\langle u,\mu\right\rangle}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,d\mu
.
Noticing that
X_{p,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})={(1+|{\,\cdot\,}|^{p})}\,{C}_{0}
, it is easy to see that the topological dual of
X_{p,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be identified with
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
through this duality bracket.
As a consequence of the dominated convergence theorem, we have a useful convergence criterium for a sequence
(v_{n})
in

, namely:

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\|v_{n}\|_{X_{p}}<+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}

\displaystyle v_{n}(x)=0\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\ \ {\left\langle v_{n},\mu\right\rangle}\to 0\quad\ \ \forall\,\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The next result applies to general first-order distributional source terms of the kind
f=f_{0}-{\mathrm{div}}F
, where

(f_{0},F)
is any pair in
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that the following balance condition is met:

\int f_{0}=0,\qquad{\int xf_{0}+\int F=0}.

(2.2)

The two conditions mean that
f
is orthogonal to affine functions, which is clearly necessary for the finiteness of

\mathcal{I}(f)=\sup\Big\{{\left\langle u,f\right\rangle}:\ u\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ {\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1\Big\}.

(2.3)

We recall the dual problem that we have named the second-order Beckmann formulation:

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f)=\inf\biggl\{\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\,:\,\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}),\ {\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=f\ \text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\biggr\},

(2.4)

where
\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)
is intended in the sense of convex one-homogeneous functionals on measures

[
19
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
f
given as above satisfies (
2.2
). Let
\varrho
be any norm on
{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}

and
\varrho^{0}
its polar defined by (
1.6

2.3
) and the infimum in (
2.4

Furthermore, we have the equality

\mathcal{I}(f)=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f).

(2.5)

Proof.

We begin by proving the existence of a maximizer for (
2.3
). By the orthogonality conditions (
2.2
), we may restrict the supremum to functions
u
belonging to the subset

K_{0}:=\Big\{u\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ :\ {\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1,\ u(0)=0,\ \nabla u(0)=0\Big\}.

Let
(u_{n})
be a maximizing sequence in
K_{0}
. Then,
|u_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
and
|\nabla u_{n}(x)|\leq|x|
.
By applying Arzela-Ascoli theorem, we can assume that

uniformly on compact subsets, where

is a suitable element of
K_{0}

To prove that

is optimal, we only need to check that

, which, due to the particular form of

{\left\langle u_{n}-u,f_{0}\right\rangle}\to 0,\qquad{\left\langle\nabla(u_{n}-u),F\right\rangle}\to 0.

Put

. Then,
(v_{n}\nabla v_{n})\to(0,0)
pointwisely, while
|v_{n}(x)|\leq|x|^{2}
and
|\nabla v_{n}(x)|\leq 2|x|
.
Therefore,

is bounded in

The existence of a minimal

in (
2.4
) follows from the direct method. Indeed, the convex functional

\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})\mapsto\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)

), while the distributional constraint

is weakly-* closed.

We now prove the equality (
2.5
) within two steps.

Step 1:
\mathcal{I}(f)\leq\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f).
It is enough to prove the following inequality:

{\left\langle u,f\right\rangle}\leq\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\qquad\text{for every $(u,\sigma)\in K_{0}\times{{S}_{f}}$},

(2.6)

where

by the convolution

Then,

Let us now consider an element

Then, our claim (

This concludes Step 1.

Step 2:

We are going to show the equality

\mathcal{I}_{\rm reg}(f):=\sup\Big\{{\left\langle u,f\right\rangle}\ :\ u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ {\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1\Big\}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f).

Clearly,

is not larger than

and, thanks to Step 1, the equality above will imply that the three quantities coincide

. We introduce the value function

2.2.
Convex order and martingale transport

Stochastic ordering plays an important role in probability theory as a tool for comparing random variables through their probability laws.
Here, we are concerned specifically with the convex order between measures in
{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+})
(
p\in\{1,2\}
).
To any measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+})
we associate its total mass
\|\mu\|
and its barycentre
[\mu]
given by:

\|\mu\|=\int\mu,\qquad[\mu]=\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}\int x\,\mu(dx).

Definition 2.2
.

Given two non-negative measures
\mu,\nu
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we say that
\nu
dominates
\mu
in the sense of convex order, in short
\nu\succeq_{c}\mu
, if for every
convex
function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
there holds the inequality

\int\varphi\,d\nu\geq\int\varphi\,d\mu.

(2.9)

By the Moreau-Yosida infimal convolution technique, we know that any convex lower semi-continuous function

\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup{+\infty}
is the non-decreasing limit
of a sequence of convex Lipschitz continuous functions.
Therefore, in order to show that
\nu\succeq_{c}\mu
, the inequality (
2.9
) needs to be checked for convex Lipschitz continuous functions only. If it is the case, then (
2.9
) automatically extends to any convex lower semi-continuous
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
.

The following properties are straightforward.

•

By testing (
2.9
) with affine functions (which are integrable), we see that

\nu\succeq_{c}\mu\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\|\mu\|=\|\nu\|,\quad\text{and}\quad[\mu]=[\nu].

•

\mu\succeq_{c}\delta_{[\mu]}
(Jensen’s inequality).

The next characterization of the convex order is crucial. Beforehand, let us recall that for any coupling measure
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
there exists a unique disintegration of the form
\gamma=\mu\otimes\gamma^{x}
(see the notation (
1.22
)), where
x\mapsto\gamma^{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
\mu
-measurable mapping, cf. for instance Section 2.5 in
[
2
]
. By the set of martingale transport plans
MT(\mu,\nu)
we understand the family of those couplings
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)

whose disintegration
satisfies the condition
[\gamma^{x}]=x

\mu
-a.e. It turns out that the set
MT(\mu,\nu)
is non-empty if and only if
\nu\succeq_{c}\mu
. This fact is a direct corollary of the Strassen theorem
[
31
]
:

Theorem 2.3

(
Strassen
)
.

The convex order

\nu\succeq_{c}\mu
holds true if and only if there exists a
\mu
-measurable map

x\mapsto p^{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that:

(i)

[p^{x}]=x

\mu
-a.e.,

(ii)

\nu(B)=\int p^{x}(B)\,\mu(dx)
for any Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Some straightforward consequences of Strassen theorem for measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are listed below:

(p1)

Assume that
\nu\succeq_{c}\mu
. Then,
{\rm{var}}(\nu)\geq{\rm{var}}(\mu)
, while
strict inequality holds unless
\mu=\nu.

Indeed, assuming that
[\mu]=0
, for
p^{x}
such that
\nu=\int p^{x}\,\mu(dx)
with
[p^{x}]=x

\mu
-a.e.,
we have

{\rm{var}}(\nu)-{\rm{var}}(\mu)=\int\big({\left\langle|{\,\cdot\,}|^{2},p^{x}\right\rangle}-|x|^{2}\big)\,\mu(dx),

which is positive unless the Jensen’s inequality
{\left\langle|{\,\cdot\,}|^{2},p^{x}\right\rangle}\geq|x|^{2}
is an equality for
\mu
-a.e.
x
. By the strict convexity of
|\cdot|^{2}
, this is possible only if
p^{x}=\delta_{x}
, hence, if
\nu=\mu
.

(p2)

Assume that
[\nu]=0
, and take the convolution
\rho=\mu\star\nu
. Then, it holds that
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu.
Indeed,
\rho=\int p^{x}\,\mu(dx)
where
p^{x}:=({\mathrm{id}}+x)^{\#}\nu
satisfies the condition
[p^{x}]=x
(
\mathrm{id}
is the identity map).
Thanks to this property, one can check (see
[
25
]
) that, for centred Gaussian distributions
\rho,\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the respective covariance matrices
R,M\in{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}_{+}}
, the condition
\rho\succeq_{c}\mu
reduces to the order relation
R\geq M
in the sense of quadratic forms.

Finally, we point out that optimal transport problems under the martingale constraint of the kind

\inf\left\{\iint c(x,y)\,\gamma(dxdy)\,:\,\gamma\in MT(\mu,\nu)\right\}

are considered in the literature, most often for the cost
c(x,y)=|x-y|^{p}
with
p\geq 1
, see for instance
[
1
,
33
,
20
,
18
]
.
A peculiarity of the quadratic cost
p=2
is
that, for
\nu\succeq_{c}\mu
, the infimum above is reached by any
\gamma\in MT(\mu,\nu)

since the total cost remains constant and equal to
{\rm{var}}(\nu)-{\rm{var}}(\mu)
on this subset. This fact will manifest itself in the proof of Theorem 1.3.

3.1.
Dualization of the minimal variance problem and optimality conditions

Let us rewrite

(3.1)

By the properties (p1), (p2) that conclude Section

3.2.

The proof of Theorem

Let

3

\pi_{2,3}\in MT(\nu,\rho).\end{cases}

(3.7)

Accordingly, we obtain the equality

\mathcal{A}(\mu,\nu)=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ :\ \exists\,\pi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu),\ \ \Pi_{3}^{\#}(\pi)=\rho\right\}.

(3.8)

Proof.

Upon recalling the equilibrium conditions (
1.11

\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
,
checking the equivalence (
3.7

\displaystyle\iiint{\left\langle z-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,\pi(dxdydz)=0\quad\forall\,\Phi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \pi_{1,3}\in MT(\mu,\rho),

\displaystyle\iiint{\left\langle z-y,\Psi(y)\right\rangle}\,\pi(dxdydz)=0\quad\forall\,\Psi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ \pi_{2,3}\in MT(\nu,\rho).

\pi_{1,3}(dxdz)=\mu(dx)\otimes p^{x}(dz)
, which gives:

\displaystyle\iiint{\left\langle z-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,\pi(dxdydz)

\displaystyle=\iint{\left\langle z-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,\pi_{1,3}(dxdz)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\int{\left\langle z-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,p^{x}(dz)\right)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\int{\left\langle[p^{x}]-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,\mu(dx).

Clearly, these integrals vanish for every

if and only if

holds

Let us now prove the equality (

By (

with

\displaystyle=\int\big(p_{1}^{z}(dx)\otimes\delta_{\xi}(dz)\big)\,\rho(d\xi),

\displaystyle\gamma_{2,3}(dydz)

\displaystyle=\int\big(p_{2}^{z}(dy)\otimes\delta_{\xi}(dz)\big)\,\rho(d\xi).

Then, it is easy to check that the measure

3.3.
Prove of the equality
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{I}(\nu-\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\big({\rm{var}}(\mu)+{\rm{var}}(\nu)\big)

First, we recall a classical result that establishes a connection between the bounds on the Hessian and the convexity properties.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any continuous function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

u\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
{\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1
;

(ii)

u\in W^{2,\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
-\mathrm{Id}\leq\nabla^{2}u\leq\mathrm{Id}
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(iii)

both functions
\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}+u
and
\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-u
are convex.

Next, we consider the subclass
\mathcal{G}\subset{C}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consisting of continuous functions
\varphi
such that
|{\,\cdot\,}|^{2}-\varphi
admits an affine minorant. Note that
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}
if and only if
\varphi(x)\leq{\left\lvert x-x_{0}\right\rvert}^{2}+b
for a suitable pair
(x_{0},b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Then, we introduce the transform
\mathcal{L}:\varphi\in\mathcal{G}\to\hat{\varphi}\in\mathcal{G}
defined by:

\mathcal{L}\varphi=\hat{\varphi}\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{\varphi}(x):={\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}-({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}(x).

(3.9)

Below we show that
\mathcal{L}
preserves convexity while adding smoothness. This is a rephrase of the fact that taking convex envelope preserves semi-concavity. The following result essentially reproduces Theorem 2.3 in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

The transform
\mathcal{L}
enjoys the following properties:

(i)

\mathcal{L}\varphi\geq\varphi
, while
\mathcal{L}\varphi\equiv\varphi
if and only if
{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi
is convex;

(ii)

\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}
(idempotence);

(iii)

If
\varphi
is convex, then
\hat{\varphi}:=\mathcal{L}\varphi
is convex and
C^{1,1}
, whilst
u:=\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\hat{\varphi}
satisfies
{\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1
.

Proof.

The first two properties are straightforward. In order to show that
\hat{\varphi}
is convex, we need only to check the Jensen’s inequality
\int\hat{\varphi}(z+\xi)\,p_{0}(d\xi)\geq\hat{\varphi}(z)
for every centred finitely supported
probability
p_{0}
and for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In view of the particular form of
\hat{\varphi}
given in (
3.9
), this amounts to showing that

\int({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}(z+\xi)\,p_{0}(d\xi)\ \leq\ ({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}(z)+{\rm{var}}(p_{0}).

(3.10)

To prove (
3.10
), we fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose a finitely supported probability
p^{z}
such that
[p^{z}]=z
and

({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}(z)\geq\int\big({\left\lvert\zeta\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi(\zeta)\big)\,p^{z}(d\zeta)-\varepsilon.

Then, by applying Jensen’s inequality to
({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}
(which is majorized by
{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi
), we infer that for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\displaystyle({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-

\displaystyle\varphi)^{**}(z+\xi)-({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\int\left(\big({\left\lvert\zeta+\xi\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi(\zeta+\xi)\big)-\big({\left\lvert\zeta\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi(\zeta)\big)\right)p^{z}(d\zeta)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle={\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert}^{2}-2{\left\langle z,\xi\right\rangle}-\int\big(\varphi(\zeta+\xi)-\varphi(\zeta)\big)\,p^{z}(d\zeta)+\,\varepsilon\,.

By integrating with respect to the centred measure
p_{0}(d\xi)
and by Fubini theorem, we deduce that:

\displaystyle\int({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-

\displaystyle\varphi)^{**}(z+\xi)\,p_{0}(d\xi)-({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}(z)

\displaystyle\leq{\rm{var}}(p_{0})+\varepsilon-\iint\big(\varphi(\zeta+\xi)-\varphi(\zeta)\big)\,p^{z}(d\zeta)\otimes p_{0}(d\xi)

\displaystyle={\rm{var}}(p_{0})+\varepsilon-\int\left(\int\big(\varphi(\zeta+\xi)-\varphi(\zeta)\big)\,p_{0}(d\xi)\right)p^{z}(d\zeta)

\displaystyle\leq{\rm{var}}(p_{0})+\varepsilon.

Let us point out that, in order to reach the last line above, we used the convexity of
\varphi
, which, by Jensen’s inequality, renders the integral with respect to
p_{0}(d\xi)
non-negative. Since
\varepsilon
can be chosen arbitrarily small, we get our claim (
3.10
), hence the convexity of
\hat{\varphi}
.

To complete the proof of the assertion (iii), we observe
that the function
u=\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\hat{\varphi}
is such that
\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-u=\hat{\varphi}
and

\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}+u=({\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}-\varphi)^{**}
, which are convex functions. By virtue of Lemma
3.4
, it follows that
u
(hence also
\hat{\varphi}
) is
C^{1,1}
, and there also holds
{\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1.

∎

Proposition 3.6

4.2.
Gaussian measures

In this example we assume the data to be two centred Gaussian distributions on

are two positive semi-definite covariance matrices.
Note that if these matrices are ordered, we find ourselves in the framework of the former example (see the comment after
(p2) in Section

In the general case, at the core of the solution lies the spectral decomposition of the difference of the covariance matrices:

N-M=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\,a_{i}\otimes a_{i},

where
a_{i}
are mutually orthogonal vectors on the unit sphere

. Let us define the projection matrices

P_{-}:=\sum_{\{i\,:\,\lambda_{i}<0\}}a_{i}\otimes a_{i},\qquad P_{+}:=\sum_{\{i\,:\,\lambda_{i}\geq 0\}}a_{i}\otimes a_{i}=\mathrm{Id}-P_{-}.

The following symmetric positive semi-definite matrices will prove to be essential:

\displaystyle M\vee N

\displaystyle:=M+(N-M)_{+}=N+(M-N)_{+},

\displaystyle M\wedge N

\displaystyle:=M-(M-N)_{+}=N-(N-M)_{+},

where

(N-M)_{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\lambda_{i})_{+}a_{i}\otimes a_{i},\qquad(M-N)_{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\lambda_{i})_{-}\,a_{i}\otimes a_{i}.

According to Remark
4.3
,
M\vee N
can be seen as the least majorant of the matrices
M,N
, and
M\wedge N
as their greatest minorant.

We are going to now show that an optimal pair
(u,\pi)
is given by

u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda_{i}){\left\langle a_{i},x\right\rangle}^{2},\qquad\pi=\gamma(dxdy)\otimes\delta_{z_{u}(x,y)}(dz),

where we agree to the convention that
\mathrm{sgn}(0)=1
, while

-

the transport plan
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a normal distribution

\gamma=\mathcal{N}\left(0,G\right),\qquad G=\begin{bmatrix}M&M\wedge N\\
M\wedge N&N\end{bmatrix};

-

the function
z_{u}
is computed according to (
1.18
), which here leads to

z_{u}(x,y)=P_{-}x+P_{+}y.

The positive semi-definiteness of

is clear since

is a minorant for both
M
and
N
.
Since
\nabla^{2}u=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda_{i})\,a_{i}\otimes a_{i}

is also straightforward. In view of the disintegrated form of

) holds for every triple

(x,y)
ranges in the whole
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}
. This reduces to a tedious but elementary computation.

The more involved part is showing the admissibility
\pi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
. As the first and second marginals of
\pi
coincide with those of

\mu
and
\nu

, it is enough to show that the marginals
\pi_{1,3}:=\Pi^{\#}_{1,3}(\pi)

Integrating against a test function

\displaystyle\iint\phi(x,z)\,\pi_{1,3}(dxdz)=\iiint\phi(x,z)\,\pi(dxdydz)

\displaystyle=\iint\phi\big(x,z_{u}(x,y)\big)\,\gamma(dxdy)

\displaystyle\iint\phi(

Remark 4.3
.

It is possible to show directly that

is a solution to the minimal variance problem (

satisfies the dominance constraints (cf. (p2) in Section

), we have

In the opposite direction, any admissible

admits a covariance matrix

such that

. Therefore, since
{\rm{var}}(\rho)={\mathrm{Tr}}\,R
, we have:

It is not difficult to check that the right hand side above is a semi-definite program which admits a unique solution given by

. The optimality of

follows.
Notice that, similarly, the matrix

is the least majorant of the matrices

, whilst

is their greatest minorant.

4.3.
Two-point measures

The simplest non-trivial data possible is when both measures are supported by two points:

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mu_{i}\,\delta_{x_{i}},\qquad\nu=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\nu_{j}\,\delta_{y_{j}}.

As the barycentres must coincide, the problem is virtually planar. We can thus
a priori
assume that

. In addition, we enforce that the four points are not aligned so that 1D scenario is avoided.

As before, we assume that the measures are centred, i.e.
[\mu]=[\nu]=0
. In this case
x_{1}=-\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}x_{2}
,
y_{1}=-\frac{\nu_{2}}{\nu_{1}}y_{2}
. Note that the weights follow automatically from the positions:

\mu_{i}=\frac{{\left\lvert x_{i^{\prime}}\right\rvert}}{{\left\lvert x_{1}\right\rvert}+{\left\lvert x_{2}\right\rvert}},\qquad\nu_{j}=\frac{|y_{j^{\prime}}|}{{\left\lvert y_{1}\right\rvert}+{\left\lvert y_{2}\right\rvert}},

(4.3)

where
i^{\prime}=3-i
,
j^{\prime}=3-j
.

The main challenge lies in the fact that the type of the solution switches depending on the geometrical property of the convex quadrilateral formed by the points
x_{1},y_{2},x_{2},y_{1}
. Indeed, the two cases below must be considered:

(A)

the pairs of opposite edges of the quadrilateral are inclined at an angle non-greater than
\pi/2
;

(B)

the angle between one of the pairs of opposite edges exceeds
\pi/2
.

The pairs of lines extending the edges in questions are drawn in Fig.
3
(a). In fact, being in the scenario (A) is equivalent to the system of two inequalities:

\displaystyle{\left\langle x_{2}-y_{2},y_{1}-x_{1}\right\rangle}\geq 0,

(4.4a)

\displaystyle{\left\langle x_{1}-y_{2},y_{1}-x_{2}\right\rangle}\geq 0.

(4.4b)

It is worth emphasizing that at least one of those inequalities is always met.

Case (A)

To extent, this case is similar to the Gaussian example as again the spectral decomposition of the difference of the covariance matrices will play the central role. Defining
M=\int x\otimes x\,\mu(dx)
and
N=\int y\otimes y\,\nu(dy)
we can make use of (
4.3
) to show that

M=-x_{1}\otimes x_{2}=-x_{2}\otimes x_{1},\qquad N=-y_{1}\otimes y_{2}=-y_{2}\otimes y_{1}.

(4.5)

Since we assumed that the four points are not collinear, the difference always has two eigenvalues of opposite signs:

N-M=\lambda_{a}\,a\otimes a+\lambda_{b}\,b\otimes b,\qquad\lambda_{a}<0,\ \ \lambda_{b}>0,

where
a\perp b
, and
a,b\in S^{1}
.
In what follows we prove that in the case (A) the problems
\mathcal{I}(\nu-\mu)
and
\mathcal{J}(\mu,\nu)
are solved by, respectively,

u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\big({\left\langle b,x\right\rangle}^{2}-{\left\langle a,x\right\rangle}^{2}\big),\qquad\pi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\gamma_{ij}\,\delta_{(x_{i},y_{j},z_{ij})},

(4.6)

where

\gamma_{ij}=\mu_{i}\frac{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle}{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-y_{j}\rangle},\qquad z_{ij}={\left\langle a,x_{i}\right\rangle}\,a+{\left\langle b,y_{j}\right\rangle}\,b.

(4.7)

We observe that
z_{ij}=z_{u}(x_{i},y_{j})=P_{-}x_{i}+P_{+}y_{j}
for
P_{-}=a\otimes a
,
P_{+}=b\otimes b
. Accordingly, both admissibility of
u
and the three-point optimality condition (
1.13
) can be shown identically as in Example
4.2
. The biggest challenge consists in showing that
\pi\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
. In fact, it is the positivity of
\gamma_{ij}
that is the most delicate. The following result shows that it characterizes the case (A):

Lemma 4.4
.

The inequalities (

) hold true if and only if

for all

As the proof is rather long and technical, it is moved to Appendix

. We can readily check that

\displaystyle\pi_{1,3}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\gamma_{ij}\,\delta_{(x_{i},z_{ij})}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mu_{i}\,\delta_{x_{i}}\!\otimes p^{i},\qquad p^{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle}{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-y_{j}\rangle}\,\delta_{z_{ij}}.

Noting that

[p^{i}]=x_{i}
, rendering
\pi_{1,3}
a martingale.

To show that
\pi_{2}=\nu

is martingale as well, we derive an alternative formula for

\mu_{i}=\frac{{\left\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle}}{\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle}
thanks to (

and formulas (

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\left\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle}}{\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle}\frac{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle}{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-y_{j}\rangle}=\frac{{\left\langle a\otimes b,-x_{i^{\prime}}\otimes x_{i}\right\rangle}+{\left\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle}\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-y_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\left\langle a\otimes b,-y_{j}\otimes y_{j^{\prime}},\right\rangle}+{\left\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}\right\rangle}\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-y_{j}\rangle}=\frac{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle b,y_{j^{\prime}}-y_{j}\rangle}\frac{{\left\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-y_{j}\right\rangle}}{\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\nu_{j}\,\frac{{\left\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-y_{j}\right\rangle}}{\langle a,x_{i^{\prime}}-x_{i}\rangle}.

4.4.
The basic first-order distribution data

Unlike in the previous examples, here we shall consider a source which is not a measure but the
first-order distribution
f^{x,y,z}
defined in the introduction. It is supported on the two points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and parametrized by the third point
z
:

f^{x,y,z}=\delta_{y}-\delta_{x}-{\mathrm{div}}\big((z-y)\,\delta_{y}-(z-x)\,\delta_{x}\big).

To focus attention we shall assume that the vectors
x-z
and
y-z
form an angle ranging in
(0,\pi]
. That is to say that
x\neq y
, while
z
cannot lie on the line crossing
x,y
except on the open segment
]x,y[
.

As announced in the introduction,
f^{x,y,z}={\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma^{x,y,z}
. Namely, it is the source term induced by the measure
\sigma^{x,y,z}
that serves as an elementary block for building solutions
\sigma
of the second-order Beckmann problem (
1.8
) for sources that are measures.
Since
\sigma^{x,y,z}
is a competitor in the problem (
1.8
) for the source
f=f^{x,y,z}
, it is natural to ask if it is optimal for such a basic first-order distribution data. This short subsection is to settle this issue.

To that aim we exploit the construction of
u
put forth in Example
4.3
, case (B). With the polar coordinate system satisfying
\varrho(z)=0
,
\vartheta(x)=0
,
\vartheta(y)\in(0,\pi]
, we repeat the construction of
u
with the parameter
\alpha=\pi/\big(2\,\angle(x-z,y-z)\big)
. By the property that is analogous to (
4.10
), one obtains

\displaystyle{\left\langle u,f^{x,y,z}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\big[u(y)+{\left\langle\nabla u(y),z-y\right\rangle}\big]-\big[u(x)+{\left\langle\nabla u(x),z-x\right\rangle}\big]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}{\left\lvert x-z\right\rvert}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\left\lvert y-z\right\rvert}^{2}=c(x,y,z).

On the other hand, from the proof of Corollary
1.2
we also know that
\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma^{x,y,z})=c(x,y,z)
. Owing to the duality result in Proposition
2.1
, optimality of the pair
(u,\sigma^{x,y,z})
will follow provided that
u
is admissible. In view of Lemma
4.5
, it is the case only if
x-z
and
y-z
form an obtuse angle, which is to say that
z
lies in the disk of the diameter
[x,y]
. We have arrived at the following result:

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume that
z\in B\big(\frac{x+y}{2},\frac{|x-y|}{2}\big)
, then
\sigma=\sigma^{x,y,z}
solves the second-order Beckmann problem (
1.8
) for the first-order distribution data
f=f^{x,y,z}
. Accordingly, we have the equality

\mathcal{I}(f^{x,y,z})=c(x,y,z).

Remark 4.7
.

The result above is valid for
z=x
(or for
z=y
). In this case,

\sigma^{x,y,z}
is negative (or positive) semi-definite, while the optimal potential is given by
u=-\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
(or
u=\frac{1}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}
).

On the other hand, we stress the fact that
\sigma^{x,y,z}
is no longer optimal if
z
is outside of the disc
B\big(\frac{x+y}{2},\frac{|x-y|}{2}\big)
. Indeed, in this case we can show that
\mathcal{I}(f^{x,y,z})=|z-\frac{x+y}{2}|{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}
which is strictly less than
c(x,y,z)
for such
z
. An exception occurs when
z
lies on the extension of the segment
[x,y]
. This is due to the cancelling effect between the positive and negative parts of
\sigma^{x,y,z}
.

5.
The optimal grillage

We conclude with a section devoted to an application of the results developed in this paper to optimal design in mechanics. Classically, by a
grillage
one understands a planar multi-junction structure whose components are 1D straight bars. Although geometrically identical to
trusses

[
10
]
, a grillage – typically constituting a bearing structure of a ceiling – lies in a horizontally oriented plane and it is loaded vertically at its junctions. The load causes the bars to bend rather than stretch, ultimately resulting in different equilibrium configurations for the two types of structures.

The optimal design of trusses is famously known to be ill-posed, calling for relaxation in the form of the
Michell problem

[
10
,
22
]
. We will utilize Corollary
1.2
to prove that, in contrast, optimal grillages do exist provided that the load is a measure.
Despite the vast literature on grillage optimization initiated in
[
28
]
, it seems to be the first result of its kind. Before stating the theorem, we will briefly recall the topic of truss optimization. We will finish with two open problems, including the extension of the existence result to data that are first-order distributions.

5.1.
Review on truss optimization and Michell problem

A truss is a particular case of a 2D or 3D elastic solid that decomposes to one-dimensional straight bars. In general, the stress tensor in a solid can be described as a matrix valued measure
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})
. It must satisfy the equilibrium equation
-{\mathrm{div}}\,\sigma=F
in
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{d}
for a system of forces
F\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
\sigma
to exist, the load
F
has to be balanced in the following sense:
\int{\left\langle v_{0},F\right\rangle}=0
whenever
v_{0}(x)=Ax+b
for
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a skew-symmetric
d\times d
matrix
A
. By a truss we can understand the stress tensors that are of the form:

\sigma_{\lambda}=\iint\sigma^{x,y}\,\lambda(dxdy),\qquad\lambda\in{\mathcal{M}}\big((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2};\mathbb{R}\big),

(5.1)

where
\sigma^{x,y}=\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\otimes\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}[x,y]
for
x\neq y
, and
\sigma^{x,x}=0
. The positive and negative part
\lambda_{+}(dxdy)
,
\lambda_{-}(dxdy)
represent, respectively, the tensile and compressive forces in the bars
[x,y]
.

Optimizing trusses amounts to looking for a measure
\lambda
that, under the condition of equilibrating
F
, minimizes the total energy, cf.
[
10
]
. Energy of a single bar
[x,y]
that is subject to a unit tensile/compressive force is the total variation
\int{\left\lvert\sigma^{x,y}\right\rvert}={\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}
. Accordingly, the optimal truss problem reads:

\inf\left\{\iint{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}\,{\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}(dxdy)\,:\,\lambda\in{\mathcal{M}}\big((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2};\mathbb{R}\big),\ \ -{\mathrm{div}}\,\sigma_{\lambda}=F\right\}.

(5.2)

Note that the support of
\lambda
can exceed the set
(\spt F)^{2}
, which is to say that we can add junctions that are not loaded.

In (
5.2
) the total mass of
\lambda
is not controlled, raising the issue of existence. Moreover, in practice
engineers expect that for a finitely supported load
F
there is a solution
\overline{\lambda}
that is finitely supported. This means that the structure can be manufactured as a junction of a finite number of bars.
Meanwhile, already at the dawn of the 20th century, A.G.M. Michell observed that an optimal truss does not exist even for the simplest loads. In his celebrated paper
[
23
]
he considered the
bridge problem
where the data is the system of three vertical forces in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

F=\frac{e_{2}}{2}\delta_{e_{1}}+\frac{e_{2}}{2}\delta_{-e_{1}}-e_{2}\delta_{0},

(5.3)

where
e_{1}=(1,0)
,
e_{2}=(0,1)
. Then, looking for finitely supported solutions of (
5.2
) leads to construction of minimizing sequences
\lambda_{h}
with the number of points in
\spt\lambda_{h}
going to infinity. When taking the weak-* limit
\overline{\sigma}
of the sequence
\sigma_{\lambda_{h}}
one discovers that it is not representable through (
5.1
), see Fig.
4
(a).
The measure
\overline{\sigma}
is a solution of what today is known as the Michell problem:

\min\left\{\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\,:\,\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}),\ \ -{\mathrm{div}}\,\sigma=F\right\}.

(5.4)

Recall that
\varrho^{0}
is the Schatten norm:
\varrho^{0}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}(S)|
. In the modern measure-theoretic setting the Michell problem was first formulated in
[
10
]
. Therein, it was proved that
\inf\eqref{eq:optimal_truss}=\min\eqref{eq:Michell}
.
Once a compactly supported
F
satisfies the balance condition, the minimum in the Michell problem is attained. From Fig.
4
(a) one can discern that solutions may charge curved curves (the thick lines in the figure). It rules out representing solutions through (
5.1
). To address this, the work
[
10
]
put forward another formulation where one seeks a signed measure on the space of regular curves, thus allowing for curved bars. To date, the existence issue remains open.

5.2.
Optimal grillage via the three-marginal optimal transport

The other example of a structure that is built from 1D bars is a grillage, and it is a special case of a
plate
. By definition, plates are two-dimensional bodies occupying a horizontal plane
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the case of plates, the measure
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})
represents the
bending moment tensor
. The out-of-plane equilibrium of the plates is governed by the equation
{\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=f
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
f=f_{0}-{\mathrm{div}}\,F
is a first-order distribution. The measure
f_{0}
models out-of-plane forces. One can think of the positive part
f_{0,+}
as of the gravity pull, whilst
f_{0,-}
plays the role of the upward reaction forces. The term
F
represents torques that act about in-plane axes. The balance condition for the load
f
reads as in (
2.2
).

With the second-order equilibrium equation, the decomposition of the measure
\sigma
to segments allows for adding affinely varying density. One of the ways of achieving this is through using
\sigma^{x,y,z}
as the basic measure. It concentrates on the union of segments
[x,z]\cup[z,y]
, see Fig.
1
. Thus, by a grillage we will understand the bending moment tensor of the form

\sigma_{\pi}=\iiint\sigma^{x,y,z}\,\pi(dxdydz),\qquad\pi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{+}\big((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3}\big).

(5.5)

In the case of grillages,
\pi(dxdydz)
enjoys the interpretation of the
transverse shear force
in the two-bar structure.
Assuming that the segments
[x,z]
and
[z,y]
do not overlap, the energy of this structure is
\int{\left\lvert\sigma^{x,y,z}\right\rvert}=\frac{1}{2}\big({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\big)=c(x,y,z)
. Accordingly, the optimal grillage problem can be formulated as follows:

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f):=\inf\left\{\iiint c(x,y,z)\,\pi(dxdydz)\,:\,\pi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{+}\big((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3}\big),\ \ {\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma_{\pi}=f\right\}.

Note that
\pi
is a positive Borel measure that is not necessarily finite. In fact, the condition
\iiint c\,d\pi<\infty
is sufficient for
\sigma_{\pi}
to be a well defined element of the space
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})
.

A priori
, the optimal grillage problem shares the issues of non-compactness that are known for truss optimization. A natural candidate for relaxation is the second-order Beckmann problem (
1.8
):

\mathcal{I}(f)=\min\biggl\{\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\ :\ \sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}),\ \ {\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=f\biggr\}.

The solution is guaranteed to exist provided that
f_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
F\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy the balance condition (
2.2
), see Section
2.1
.
Utilizing the subadditivity of the functional
\sigma\mapsto\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)
we can show that
\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma_{\pi})\leq\iiint c\,d\pi
, which furnishes the inequality

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f)\geq\mathcal{I}(f).

(5.6)

Historically, the systematic study of optimal grillages was initiated in the engineering paper
[
28
]
. Inspired by the theory of Michell structures, the author has tackled the Beckmann problem

from the outset. However, in the numerous analytical examples worked out in
[

]
and subsequent works, e.g.
[
27

, one can discern the grillage-like structure (

. Unlike in the Michell problem, the curved bars are not exhibited at optimality.

The next result lays out a foundation for the foregoing observations. Exploiting the novel three-marginal optimal transport formulation developed in this paper, we show that the optimal grillage problem
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f)
admits a solution when

is a measure, i.e. when the first-order term

is absent. On top of that, we prove that the optimal grillage consists of a finite number of bars once the load

is discrete.

Theorem 5.1
.

If the load distribution
f
is a measure in
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then the equality
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f)=\mathcal{I}(f)
holds true together with the following statements.

(i)

There exists a solution
\overline{\pi}
of the optimal grillage problem
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f)
, and, for any such solution,

solves the Beckmann problem
\mathcal{I}(f)
. Moreover,
\overline{\pi}
can be chosen such that
\overline{\pi}\big((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{3}\big)<\infty

\spt\sigma_{\overline{\pi}}\subset\mathcal{B}(\spt f_{+},\spt f_{-}),

(5.7)

where

is the Jordan decomposition to the positive and negative part.

(ii)

If, in addition, the measure

\sigma_{\overline{\pi}}\ll\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}G

\spt f_{+}
,
\spt f_{-}

Proof.

It is not restrictive to assume that

5.3.
Open problems

5.3.1.
Loads that are general first-order distributions

Generalization of Theorem
5.1
towards general first-order distributions
f=f_{0}-{\mathrm{div}}\,F
is not straightforward. Unlike
f_{0}=\nu-\mu
, the vector measure
F
does not admit a natural decomposition to a pair of measures. It makes it difficult to propose a generalization of the set
\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
, and thus to find the right optimal transport formulation like (
1.10
) whose solution is guaranteed to exist.

The situation improves when the supports of the measures
f_{0},F
are finite. It is then possible to prove that there exists a finitely supported solution of the optimal grillage problem
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f)
. The main argument is using
the minimal extensions of jets
put forth in
[
21
]
. We skip the details here, and, instead, in Fig.
4
(b) we show the optimal grillage for
f_{0}=0
and
F
as in the bridge problem, see (
5.3
). Note that the mechanical nature of
F
differs for trusses (
F
are forces) and grillages (
F
are torques).

If the measure
F
is not finitely supported, the issue of existence is more subtle. The authors found examples of
F
that charge a curved curve for which existence of solutions
\overline{\pi}
to the optimal grillage problem
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(-{\mathrm{div}}F)
must imply that
\overline{\pi}\big((\mathbb{R}^{2})^{3}\big)=\infty
. The infinite mass of
\overline{\pi}
makes it possible to construct infinite chains of straight bars whose lengths tend to zero, while their thickness is bounded from below by a positive constant. Such chains seem to open the door to forming solutions
\overline{\pi}
for such data
F
.
Ultimately, the optimal grillage problem for data that are general first-order distributions is not well understood at the moment, and it remains to leave the reader with the following question:

Problem 5.4
.

Assume that
f=f_{0}-{\mathrm{div}}\,F
where
(f_{0},F)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and that the support of
F
is infinite. Does the optimal grillage problem
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f)
admit a solution?

5.3.2.
Domain confinement

In practical applications, engineers often work within a prescribed design domain
\Omega
, a bounded open and connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For instance, a natural choice for
\Omega
in Example (
5.3
) is the square
Q=\spt\mu
being the outline of a ceiling.
The domain confinement can be easily accounted for in the optimal grillage problem
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{OG}}(f)
by adding the constraint
\spt\sigma_{\pi}\subset{\overline{\Omega}}
. Assuming that the load
f
is a measure, from assertion (i) of Corollary
5.1
we can see that the whole result holds true provided that

\mathcal{B}(\spt f_{+},\spt f_{-})\ \subset\ \overline{\Omega}.

(5.8)

In this case, the constraint
\spt\sigma_{\pi}\subset{\overline{\Omega}}
is not binding. If the inclusion (
5.8
) is not satisfied, then one should work within the framework of the second-order Beckmann problem, whose modification now reads

\mathcal{I}(f,\Omega):=\min\biggl\{\int\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\ :\ \sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}}),\ \ \spt\sigma\subset{\overline{\Omega}},\ \ {\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=f\biggr\}.

Numerical experiments in 2D indicate that, with the condition (
5.8
) violated, there might not be solutions of
\mathcal{I}(f,\Omega)
which take the form
\sigma_{\pi}
. It appears that optimal
\overline{\sigma}
may charge subsets of the boundary
\partial\Omega
with the density being a full-rank matrix. In terms of mechanics, it corresponds to 1D bars (possibly curved) subject not only to bending moments but also to torsion. In the interior
\Omega
, however, the solution seems to decompose to straight bars
\sigma^{x,y,z}
. These observations lead to the following open problem:

Problem 5.5
.

Assume a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Lipschitz regular boundary and a load
f\in{\mathcal{M}}({\overline{\Omega}})
. Do there exist
\sigma_{\partial\Omega}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})
concentrated on
{\partial\Omega}
and
\pi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{+}(\overline{\Omega}^{3})
such that

\overline{\sigma}=\iiint\sigma^{x,y,z}\,\pi(dxdydz)+\sigma_{\partial\Omega}

solves the confined second-order Beckmann problem
\mathcal{I}(f,\Omega)
?

Appendix A
Convex analysis

Let
X
be a normed space and let
h:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a convex function.
Recall that the Moreau-Fenchel conjugate of
h
is defined on the dual space
X^{*}
by:

h^{*}(x^{*}):=\sup_{x\in X}\big\{{\left\langle x,x^{*}\right\rangle}-h(x)\big\}\qquad\forall\,x^{*}\in X^{*}.

Clearly,
h^{*}
is convex and lower semi-continuous with respect to the weak-* topology on
X^{*}
.
Next, we define the biconjugate of
h
on
X
by:

h^{**}(x):=\sup_{x^{*}\in X^{*}}\big\{{\left\langle x,x^{*}\right\rangle}-h^{*}(x^{*})\big\}\qquad\forall\,x\in X.

The following classical result (due to J.J.
Moreau
[
24
]
in the infinite dimensional case) is used several times in this paper.

Proposition A.1
.

Assume that there exists
r>0
such that

\sup\{h(x)\,:\,\|x\|\leq r\}<+\infty.

Then:

(i)

h
is continuous at
0
, while
h^{*}
is coercive and attains its minimum on
X^{*}
;

(ii)

we have the equalities:
h(0)=h^{**}(0)=-\min h^{*}.

Appendix B
Integration by parts

We give here the justification of the integration by parts formula on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that was required in Section
2
(see (
2.7
)) and in the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
f=f_{0}-{\mathrm{div}}\,F
, where

(f_{0},F)
is any pair in
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
2.2
). Let
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}})

satisfy
{\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=f
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then, for every
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
{\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)<+\infty
, we have:

\int{\left\langle\nabla^{2}u,\sigma\right\rangle}={\left\langle u,f\right\rangle}={\left\langle u,f_{0}\right\rangle}+{\left\langle\nabla u,F\right\rangle}.

(B.1)

Proof.

By the orthogonality conditions (
2.2
), (
B.1
) is valid for affine functions. Therefore, it is not restrictive to assume that
u
and
\nabla u
vanish at
0
; we may also assume that
{\mathrm{lip}}(\nabla u)\leq 1
.
On the other hand, the equality
{\mathrm{div}}^{2}\sigma=f
in the sense of distributions implies that (
B.1
) holds true if
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By using smooth convolution kernels, this can be extended to
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that are compactly supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In order to remove the latter condition, we consider a sequence of radial cut-off functions
\eta_{k}(x):=\eta(\frac{|x|}{k})
where

\eta\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R};[0,1]),\qquad\eta(t)=1\quad\text{if\quad$|t|\leq k$},\qquad\eta(t)=0\quad\text{if\quad$t\geq 2k$}.

Then, we set
u_{k}:=u\,\eta_{k}
. Since
u_{k}
satisfies (
B.1
), we have only to check that the sequence
(v_{k})
, given by
v_{k}:=u-u_{k}=(1-\eta_{k})u
, satisfies:

{\left\langle v_{k},f_{0}\right\rangle}\to 0,\qquad{\left\langle\nabla v_{k},F\right\rangle}\ \to 0,\qquad{\left\langle\nabla^{2}v_{k},\sigma\right\rangle}\to 0.

(B.2)

Since
v_{k}(x)
vanishes for
|x|\leq k
, while
|v_{k}(x)|\leq|(u(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
elsewhere, we infer that

(v_{k})
is bounded in
X_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence,
{\left\langle v_{k},f_{0}\right\rangle}\to 0
by applying (
2.1
) with
\mu=f_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
In the same way,
\nabla v_{k}
is supported on the subset
\{k\leq|x|\leq 2k\}
, where it satisfies the upper bound

\displaystyle|\nabla v_{k}(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\big(1-\eta_{k}(x)\big)\nabla u(x)-\frac{1}{k}u(x)\,\eta^{\prime}\Big(\frac{|x|}{k}\Big)\frac{x}{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\nabla u(x)|+\frac{{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)}{k}|u(x)|\leq\big(1+{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)\big)\,|x|.

In the last inequality we used the fact that
|\nabla u(x)|\leq|x|
, and
|u(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}\leq k|x|

on
\spt(\nabla v_{k})
.
It follows that
(\nabla v_{k})
is bounded in
X_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and, recalling that
F\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we may apply
(
2.1
) to infer that
{\left\langle\nabla v_{k},F\right\rangle}\to 0
.
Next, we see that
\nabla^{2}v_{k}=(1-\eta_{k})\nabla^{2}u-(\nabla\eta_{k}\otimes\nabla u+\nabla u\otimes\nabla\eta_{k}+u\nabla^{2}\eta_{k})
where

\displaystyle\nabla\eta_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\eta^{\prime}\Big(\frac{|x|}{k}\Big)\frac{x}{|x|},

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\eta_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\eta^{\prime\prime}\Big(\frac{|x|}{k}\Big)\frac{x\otimes x}{|x|^{2}}+\frac{1}{k|x|}\eta^{\prime}\Big(\frac{|x|}{k}\Big)\left(\mathrm{Id}-\frac{x\otimes x}{|x|^{2}}\right).

We notice that:

-

for any
x\neq 0
there hold the bounds
\varrho(\nabla\eta_{k}\otimes\nabla u+\nabla u\otimes\nabla\eta_{k})(x)\leq 2|\nabla u(x)|\frac{{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)}{k}
and
\varrho\big(\nabla^{2}\eta_{k}(x)\big)\leq\frac{{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta^{\prime})}{k^{2}}+\frac{{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)}{k{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}}
;

-

\nabla\eta_{k}
and
\nabla^{2}\eta_{k}
are supported on
\{k\leq|x|\leq 2k\}
, where it holds that
|u(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}\leq 2k^{2}
and
|\nabla u(x)|\leq|x|\leq 2k
.

All in all, we obtain a uniform upper bound for
\varrho(\nabla^{2}v_{k})
whose support is contained in
\{k\leq|x|\leq 2k\}

\displaystyle\varrho\big(\nabla^{2}v_{k}(x)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\varrho\big(\nabla^{2}u(x)\big)+\varrho\big(\nabla\eta_{k}\otimes\nabla u+\nabla u\otimes\nabla\eta_{k}\big)(x)+|u(x)|\,\varrho\big(\nabla^{2}\eta_{k}(x)\big)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\,|\nabla u(x)|\frac{{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)}{k}+|u(x)|\Big(\frac{{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta^{\prime})}{k^{2}}+\frac{{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)}{k{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\,{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)\frac{|x|}{k}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta^{\prime})\frac{|x|^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)\frac{|x|}{k}

\displaystyle\leq C:=1+5\,{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta)+2\,{\mathrm{lip}}(\eta^{\prime}).

By virtue of the inequality
|{\left\langle\nabla^{2}v_{k},\sigma\right\rangle}|\,\leq\,\varrho(\nabla^{2}v_{k})\,\varrho^{0}(\sigma)\leq C\varrho^{0}(\sigma)

holding in the sense of measures,
it follows that:

|{\left\langle\nabla^{2}v_{k},\sigma\right\rangle}|\leq C\int_{\{k\leq|x|\leq 2k\}}\varrho^{0}(\sigma).

Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varrho^{0}(\sigma)<+\infty
, we conclude that
{\left\langle\nabla^{2}v_{k},\sigma\right\rangle}\to 0

as required in (

).

\mathrm{sgn}(\gamma_{12})=\mathrm{sgn}({\left\langle b,y_{1}-x_{1}\right\rangle})
and
\mathrm{sgn}(\gamma_{21})=\mathrm{sgn}({\left\langle b,x_{2}-y_{2}\right\rangle})
. Defining the two functions:

\displaystyle f_{1}(t):={\left\lvert v_{2}\right\rvert}{\left\langle b,y_{1}-t\,x_{1}\right\rangle}={\left\langle\tilde{x}_{2}(t_{2})-y_{2},y_{1}-\tilde{x}_{1}(t)\right\rangle},

(C.4)

\displaystyle f_{2}(t):={\left\lvert w_{1}\right\rvert}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{t}\,x_{2}-y_{2},b\right\rangle}={\left\langle\tilde{x}_{2}(t)-y_{2},y_{1}-\tilde{x}_{1}(t_{1})\right\rangle}

(C.5)

we see that
\mathrm{sgn}(\gamma_{12})=\mathrm{sgn}(f_{1}(1))
, and
\mathrm{sgn}(\gamma_{21})=\mathrm{sgn}(f_{2}(1))
.
Due to (
C.1
), the function
f_{1}
is strictly decreasing, and
f_{2}
is strictly increasing on
(0,\infty)
. The alternative formulas for
f_{1},f_{2}
given above follow by (
C.3
). They provide the equalities
f_{1}(t_{2})=f_{2}(t_{1})=0
since
g(t_{1})=g(t_{2})=0
.

Let us now assume that the inequality (
4.4a
) is satisfied or, equivalently,
g(1)\geq 0
. By the properties of
g
, there holds
t_{1}\leq 1\leq t_{2}
. Since
f_{1}
is decreasing, we have
f_{1}(1)\geq f_{1}(t_{2})=0
. Similarly, because
f_{2}
is increasing,
f_{2}(1)\geq f_{2}(t_{1})=0
. This gives
\gamma_{12}\geq 0
and
\gamma_{21}\geq 0
.

Contrarily, assume that (
4.4a
) does not hold, which gives
g(1)<0
. Then, either
1<t_{1}<t_{2}
or
t_{1}<t_{2}<1
. In the first case, we have
f_{2}(1)<f_{2}(t_{1})=0
, which yields
\gamma_{21}<0
. In the second case
f_{1}(1)<f_{1}(t_{2})=0
, and thus
\gamma_{12}<0
by the same token. The proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Let us observe that
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is
C^{1}
and
2\pi
-periodic on
\mathbb{R}
. We thus immediately infer that
u
is
C^{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{z_{0}\}
. However, thanks to the factor
r^{2}
in the definition of
\upsilon
, it can be showed that
\nabla u
is also continuous at
z_{0}
with
\nabla u(z_{0})=0
, that is
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The
function
h
is also piecewise
C^{2}
. More precisely,
h^{\prime\prime}
has discontinuity points
2k\pi
and
2k\pi+\pi/(2\alpha)
for integer
k
if and only if
\alpha\neq\beta
. As a result,
u
is not of class
C^{2}
except for the case
{\left\langle x_{2}-y_{2},y_{1}-x_{1}\right\rangle}=0
, which corresponds to the condition
\alpha=\beta
exactly. Nonetheless, the piecewise continuity of
h^{\prime\prime}
is enough to deduce that

\displaystyle u\in C^{2}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}),\qquad

\displaystyle V_{i}=\big\{(\varrho,\vartheta)^{-1}(r,\theta)\,:\,r>0,\ \theta\in A_{i}\big\},

\displaystyle A_{1}=]0,\pi/(2\alpha)[,\quad\ \ A_{2}=]\pi/(2\alpha),2\pi[.

Moreover, on each open set
V_{i}
there holds
\nabla^{2}u(x)=(Q(x))^{\top}H_{i}\big(\varrho(x),\vartheta(x)\big)\,Q(x)
, where
Q(x)
is a rotation matrix, and

H_{i}(r,\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\,\frac{\partial^{2}\upsilon}{\partial r^{2}}&-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial\upsilon}{\partial\theta}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^{2}\upsilon}{\partial r\partial\theta}\,\\
-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial\upsilon}{\partial\theta}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^{2}\upsilon}{\partial r\partial\theta}&\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\upsilon}{\partial^{2}\theta}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\upsilon}{\partial r}\,\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\,h_{i}(\theta)&\frac{1}{2}h_{i}^{\prime}(\theta)\,\\
\,\frac{1}{2}h_{i}^{\prime}(\theta)&\frac{1}{2}h^{\prime\prime}_{i}(\theta)+h_{i}(\theta)\,\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash(V_{1}\cup V_{2})
is Lebesgue negligible and
h_{i}
are cosine functions, we infer that
u\in W^{2,\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, which establishes the first part of the assertion.

To prove the second part, it is enough that we check that for
i=1,2
the eigenvalues
of
H_{i}(r,\theta)=H_{i}(\theta)
remain in the regime
[-1,1]
if and only if
{\left\langle x_{2}-y_{2},y_{1}-x_{1}\right\rangle}\leq 0
. Starting from
i=1
, we obtain

H_{1}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\,\cos(2\alpha\theta)&\alpha\sin(2\alpha\theta)\\
\,\alpha\sin(2\alpha\theta)&(1-2\alpha^{2})\cos\big(2\alpha\theta\big)\,\end{bmatrix},

and, after using the Pythagorean trigonometric identity, formulas for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}
follow:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}(\theta)=(1-\alpha^{2})\cos(2\alpha\theta)\pm\alpha\sqrt{1-(1-\alpha^{2})\cos^{2}(2\alpha\theta)}.

Assume first that
{\left\langle x_{2}-y_{2},y_{1}-x_{1}\right\rangle}>0
, which gives
\alpha>1
. Then, clearly
\lambda_{-}(0)=1-2\alpha^{2}<-1
.
It remains to check the case when
{\left\langle x_{2}-y_{2},y_{1}-x_{1}\right\rangle}\leq 0
, for which
\alpha,\beta\leq 1
. Thanks to elementary computations we get the estimate:

\displaystyle\Big(\pm 1-

\displaystyle(1-\alpha^{2})\cos(2\alpha\theta)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(1-\alpha^{2})\big(1\mp\cos(2\alpha\theta)\big)^{2}+\alpha^{2}\big(1-(1-\alpha^{2})\cos^{2}(2\alpha\theta)\big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,\Big(\alpha\sqrt{1-(1-\alpha^{2})\cos^{2}(2\alpha\theta)}\Big)^{2},

where we acknowledged that
\alpha\leq 1
.
Since the term
(1-\alpha^{2})\cos(2\alpha\theta)
ranges in
[-1,1]
, from the estimate above we can deduce that indeed
\lambda_{\pm}(\theta)\in[-1,1]
. Handling the matrix
H_{2}(\theta)
amounts to replacing
\alpha
with
\beta
. However, since
\beta\leq 1
as well, the same reasoning stands.
∎
