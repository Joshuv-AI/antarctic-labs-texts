Mechanistic insight into low-temperature alkylation of thiophene with styrene over zeolites MCM-22 and β
Authors: Xiaopan Li, Sensen Shang, Chenyu Liu, Jingjing Diao, Xiang Li, Xiaojun Lv, Qingbo Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFriedel-Crafts alkylation reactions are generally accepted to proceed by an electrophilic aromatic substitution (SEAr) mechanism. Here, we observed that MCM-22 exhibited a high activity in the alkylation of thiophene with styrene at temperatures near ambient, while β was almost inactive. Three species were identified in the adsorption of thiophene on HMCM-22 and Hβ: the hydrogen-bonded thiophene, the protonated thiophene, and the thiophene oligomers. Styrene was chemically adsorbed on the two zeolites through its vinyl group. The infrared studies of the consecutive adsorption of thiophene and styrene on MCM-22 and β reveal that the major difference between the thiophene species on the two zeolites was the disappearance of the protonated thiophene on MCM-22. These results indicate that the reaction over MCM-22 occurs by the attack of a protonated thiophene molecule on the side chain of styrene that chemically adsorbed on the zeolite but not the conventional SEAr mechanism. Subject terms: Catalytic mechanisms, Heterogeneous catalysis, Porous materials

Introduction

The alkylation of aromatic compounds with alkenes is an important process in chemical industry1–4. For instance, ethylbenzene and cumene are mainly produced by the alkylation of benzene with ethene and propene, respectively2–4. Ethylbenzene is used for the production of styrene (a critical monomer for making various plastics and polymers), and cumene is an essential raw material for manufacturing phenol and acetone. Such alkylation reactions are examples of the Friedel-Crafts alkylation reaction, a textbook reaction that occurs by an electrophilic aromatic substitution (SEAr) mechanism. Strong acids like AlCl3 and zeolites (ZSM-5, β, Y, and MCM-22) are used as catalysts. Zeolites are the preferred industrial catalysts. Over zeolites, the protonation of an alkene molecule by a Brønsted acid site creates a positively charged carbonium ion (an electrophile), which then attacks an aromatic ring to yield an alkylated aromatic product5. Corma et al. studied the alkylation of benzene with ethylene and propene under liquid-phase reaction conditions (160–240 °C, 3.5 MPa, and WHSV 1.5–640 h−1) over zeolites MCM-22, β, and ZSM-56. They showed that the alkylation of benzene with propene on MCM-22 followed an Eley-Rideal-type mechanism. The coverage of benzene on active sites was much lower than that of propene.

As an aromatic compound, thiophene is more reactive in Friedel–Crafts reactions than aromatic hydrocarbons due to the presence of the sulfur atom7,8. The sulfur atom donates two electrons into the aromatic sextet, increasing the electron density on the carbon atoms and making them more susceptible to electrophilic attack7,8. This makes the alkylation of thiophenic compounds with olefins an attractive technique for the removal of sulfur (typically several hundred ppm) from gasoline, because it does not need H2 and can be carried out at mild conditions with high desulfurization efficiency as well as low loss in octane number9–11. The alkylation reactions yield alkylthiophenes with higher boiling points that can be removed from gasoline by subsequent distillation. Thus, it can also be a potential approach to remove thiophenic compounds from highly reactive unsaturated hydrocarbons, such as the styrene manufactured by extraction from pyrolysis gasoline.

Styrene is an important monomer widely used in the production of various plastics and polymers. It is industrially manufactured by direct dehydrogenation of ethylbenzene with excess superheated steam over iron oxide-based catalysts12,13. About 80–90% of worldwide styrene production is accomplished by this process12,13. The second most used process for the production of styrene is the so-called propylene oxide/styrene co-production method (also known as the co-oxidation method), which contributes to 10~15% of the total styrene manufacturing13,14. Sulfur contamination is not a concern in either process, because their raw materials are essentially sulfur-free. Besides these two major processes, styrene can also be obtained by extraction from pyrolysis gasoline. Pyrolysis gasoline is a byproduct in the production of ethylene and propylene by steam cracking of naphtha or light gas oil15. Since naphtha and light gas oil contain significant amounts of sulfur, sulfur compounds are present in pyrolysis gasoline and thus the extracted styrene. The presence of sulfur significantly degrades the quality of styrene and its downstream products. We determined that the sulfur content of a fraction with eight carbon atoms extracted from pyrolysis gasoline (the C8 fraction) provided by the Xinjiang Tianli Petrochemical Co., Ltd. (Xinjiang, China) was 321 ppm. The sulfur compounds in the C8 fraction can be roughly classified into two types: (1) mercaptans, thioethers or sulfides, and disulfides; (2) thiophene and its alkylated derivatives (thiophenes). Mercaptans, sulfides, and disulfides are less problematic than thiophenic compounds. They can be relatively readily removed by several approaches under mild conditions such as adsorptive desulfurization16–18, the sweetening process using aqueous alkanolamine and alkaline18, or the MeroxTM process19–21. The removal of thiophene and thiophenes from styrene is very challenging because styrene is highly reactive and can polymerize even without an initiator22. For pure styrene system, the adiabatic polymerization onset temperature is 106 °C23. The desulfurization of the C8 fraction or styrene must be carried out at low temperatures. The conventional techniques like hydrodesulfurization and oxidative desulfurization are not applicable for the desulfurization of styrene. Considering the vinyl group of styrene and the aromatic nature of thiophenic compounds, the alkylation of thiophenic compounds with styrene can be a potential solution. Herein, we studied the alkylation of thiophene with styrene at low temperatures over zeolites MCM-22 and β in their H form (denoted as HMCM-22 and Hβ, respectively). MCM-22 is one of the most widely investigated MWW zeolites24. It consists of two different pore structures. One is identified by two-dimensional sinusoidal 10-membered ring slightly elliptical channels, and the other is 12-membered ring interlayer supercages25–27. Zeolite β has a complex structure. It is an intergrowth of several distinct but closely related polymorphs, including chiral polymorph-A, achiral polymorph-B, and polymorph-C28. Both MCM-22 and β zeolites are important alkylation catalysts2–4. We observed that HMCM-22 and Hβ behaved very differently in the alkylation of thiophene with styrene. HMCM-22 exhibited a high activity at temperatures near ambient, while Hβ was almost inactive. The infrared studies of consecutive adsorption of thiophene and styrene on HMCM-22 and Hβ enabled a mechanistic insight into the low-temperature alkylation of thiophene with styrene. It is demonstrated that the reaction over the HMCM-22 catalyst follows a different mechanism than the conventional SEAr mechanism.

Results and discussion

Catalyst characterization

The XRD patterns of the HMCM-22 and Hβ samples agreed well with the standard XRD patterns of the corresponding zeolites in JCPDS cards (see Supplementary Fig. S1). The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of HMCM-22 and Hβ are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. HMCM-22 showed the typical platelet-like morphology29,30. In agreement with previous observations31,32, Hβ exhibited a morphology of aggregates of nanosized particles. The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of HMCM-22 and Hβ are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3. Both isotherms are type I in shape. As a large-pore zeolite, the specific surface area and the micropore volume of Hβ were larger than those of the medium-pore HMCM-22 zeolite (see supplementary Table S1). A marked hysteresis at high p/p0 values was observed in the isotherm of HMCM-22 (see supplementary Fig. S3a), suggesting that the crystal sizes of HMCM-22 are small and it presents a relatively high external specific surface area33. As indicated in Supplementary Table S1, the external specific surface area of HMCM-22 was ca. 1.5 times as high as that of Hβ. The Si/Al ratios of the HMCM-22 (12.3) and Hβ (13.1) samples measured by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy were about equal. The 27Al and 29Si MAS NMR spectra of HMCM-22 and Hβ were consistent with those reported in literature29,34,35. Two peaks at around 0 and 60 ppm were detected in the 27Al MAS NMR spectra of the two zeolite samples (see Supplementary Fig. S4), which can be assigned to the octahedrally-coordinated nonframework Al3+ species and the tetrahedrally-coordinated framework Al3+ species, respectively36,37. The tetrahedrally-coordinated Al3+ species was the dominant Al3+ species in HMCM-22, whereas the number of the octahedrally-coordinated Al3+ species in Hβ was comparable to that of the tetrahedrally-coordinated Al3+ species (see Supplementary Fig. S4). More nonframework Al3+ species were found in Hβ. The 29Si MAS NMR spectra demonstrate that the Si species in the HMCM-22 and Hβ catalysts were different (Fig. 1). Multiple peaks were found in the spectrum of HMCM-22. The spectrum of Hβ was characterized by a strong peak at −112 ppm with two shoulders at ca. −105 and −116 ppm. Zeolites are microporous crystalline aluminosilicates in which the frameworks are composed of SiO4 and AlO4 tetrahedra. There are five types of SiO4 units in silicates, which are usually denoted as Q0, Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4. The Q stands for the tetrahedrally coordinated Si by four oxygens and the superscripts for the number of bridging oxygens or the nearest neighboring SiO4 units38,39. For instance, the Q4 silicon atom has no adjacent hydroxyl group, while the Q3 silicon atom is only bound to one hydroxyl group40. Depending on the number of aluminum atoms in the second coordination sphere, five possible Q4 silicon environments can be found in the framework of zeolites. They are abbreviated as Si(nAl), where n (0 ≤ n ≤ 4) is the number of aluminum atoms connected to a silicon atom through oxygen atoms41,42. In the 29Si MAS NMR spectrum of HMCM-22 (Fig. 1a), the Q3 sites including Si(OSi)3OH and Si(1Al) are identified by peaks at −98 ppm and −101 ppm, respectively34,35. The other five peaks from −106 ppm to −120 ppm correspond to the inequivalent Si(0Al) sites with Si–O–Si angels vary from 138° to 164°29,34. The small peak in the 29Si MAS NMR spectrum of Hβ (Fig. 1b) at −105 ppm is due to the Si(1Al) sites, whereas the major peak at −112 ppm and the small one at −116 ppm can be attributed to the inequivalent Si(0Al) sites43,44.

The acid properties of the two zeolite samples were studied by means of Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy of pyridine adsorption (Py-IR, see Supplementary Fig. S5). As revealed by the Py-IR measurements (see Supplementary Fig. S5) as well as the numbers of Brønsted acid sites and Lewis acid sites in the HMCM-22 and Hβ samples estimated based on their Py-IR spectra at 150 °C (see Supplementary Table S2), the numbers of both Brønsted acid sites and Lewis acid sites in HMCM-22 were lower than those in Hβ. Additionally, the number of the Brønsted acid sites in the HMCM-22 sample was much larger than that of the Lewis acid sites, while the Hβ sample contained about equal numbers of Brønsted acid sites and Lewis acid sites (see Supplementary information for details). This is consistent with the 27Al MAS NMR results that Hβ possessed more nonframework Al3+ species. The Al3+ species in nonframework structures of zeolites function as Lewis acid sites45. The acid sites on the external surfaces of HMCM-22 and Hβ were measured by infrared spectroscopy of 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine adsorption (DTBPy-IR). Two bands at 1615 and 1535 cm−1 corresponding to the 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine bonded to Brønsted sites were observed in the DTBPy-IR spectra measured at 200 °C (see Supplementary Fig. S6)46,47. The intensities of these two bands in the spectrum of Hβ were higher than those in the spectrum of HMCM-22. The numbers of acid sites on the external surface of HMCM-22 and Hβ estimated based on their DTBPy-IR spectra were 64 μmol g−1 and 172 μmol g−1, respectively. Hβ possessed more external surface acid sites than HMCM-22.

Alkylation activity

HMCM-22 exhibited a high activity in the alkylation of thiophene with styrene. The reaction already started at temperature as low as 15 °C, and the conversion of thiophene over HMCM-22 increased markedly with temperature (see Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8). At 30 °C, the conversion of thiophene was more than 45% after 120-min reaction and reached 91% after 720-min reaction (Fig. 2). The alkylation of thiophene with styrene yielded the following sulfur-containing compounds, one monoalkylated thiophene (mono-AT), two dialkylated derivatives of thiophene (di-ATs), and trace amounts of trialkylated derivatives of thiophene (tri-ATs) (see Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10). At 30 °C, mono-AT was the dominant sulfur-containing compound with its selectivity above 79% (see Supplementary Fig. S11). On the contrary, Hβ showed almost no activity. The conversion of thiophene over Hβ was less than 1% at 30 °C (Fig. 2).

Conversions of thiophene over HMCM-22 and Hβ at 30 °C.

The red solid line with filled squares (■) shows conversions of thiophene over HMCM-22 as a function of reaction time, the blue solid line with filled circles (●) displays conversions of thiophene over Hβ as a function of reaction time. Source data are provided as Supplementary Data 1.

The spent HMCM-22 catalyst was first washed with methanol at 25 °C for 8 h, and then calcined in air at 550 °C for 8 h to obtain the regenerated HMCM-22 catalyst (HMCM-22-R). The activity of HMCM-22-R was almost identical to that of the fresh HMCM-22 (Fig. S12). The contents of sulfur in the spent HMCM-22 catalyst, the HMCM-22-R catalyst, and the spent HMCM-22-R catalyst are listed in Table S3. The contents of sulfur in the spent HMCM-22 and the spent HMCM-22-R catalysts were close to each other, which were about 20 times as high as that in HMCM-22-R. The results indicate that the sulfur compounds deposited on the surface of HMCM-22 after the reaction can be removed by the regeneration procedure.

We also studied the reactions of thiophene with styrene at initial styrene/thiophene (ST/T) molar ratios around or not that far from stoichiometric values. The same conclusion was reached. HMCM-22 was active in the reaction of thiophene with styrene at temperatures near ambient, while Hβ was almost inactive. Hence, we only showed the data over HMCM-22 in Supplementary Fig. S13. The conversion of thiophene over HMCM-22 increased significantly with the increase in the ST/T ratio from 0.5 to 2. The further increase in the ST/T ratio from 2 to 20 showed very minor influence on the conversion of thiophene.

Infrared studies

The distinctly different catalytic performances of the HMCM-22 and Hβ catalysts cannot be attributed to their different specific surface areas and acid properties. Otherwise, the Hβ catalyst that possessed higher specific surface area and more acid sites (particularly more accessible external surface acid sites) should exhibit higher activity. Therefore, we carried out infrared studies of the adsorption of thiophene (T-IR) and styrene (ST-IR) on HMCM-22 and Hβ for an understanding of the surface species and reaction mechanism. The T-IR spectra of the two zeolite catalysts exhibited the major bands at 3116, 3075, 2937, 2859, 1504, 1454, 1438, 1405, and 1396 cm−1 (Fig. 3). The assignments of these bands are as follows. (1) The bands at 3116, 3075, 1405, and 1396 cm−1 (the group I bands) correspond to thiophene hydrogen bonded to zeolites by π-electronic interaction48–53. (2) The bands at 2859, 2937, and 1504 cm−1 (the group II bands) are assignable to thiophene oligomers48,52–55. (3) The bands at 1454 and 1438 cm−1 (the group III bands) can be attributed to protonated thiophene53,54. Our computational results suggest that the two group III bands are likely due to two different conformations of protonated thiophene (see Supplementary Fig. S14). A strong band at about 1500 cm−1 was observed in the computed infrared spectra of the two protonated thiophene species as well (see Supplementary Fig. S14), suggesting that the 1504 cm−1 band could be associated with both protonated thiophene and thiophene oligomers. The T-IR spectra (Fig. 3) demonstrate that the protonation of thiophene over HMCM-22 and Hβ occurred at temperatures near ambient. The intensities of the group III bands in the T-IR spectra of HMCM-22 and Hβ decreased with temperature and became too low to be detected at the same temperature of 200 °C, indicating that the strength of the adsorption of the protonated thiophene on HMCM-22 is as strong as that on Hβ. Additionally, the group III bands in the T-IR spectra of HMCM-22 were weaker than those in the T-IR spectra of Hβ. This is expected because the numbers of acid sites of HMCM-22 were lower than those of Hβ.

Figure 4 shows the ST-IR spectra of HMCM-22 and Hβ. The infrared spectrum of styrene was also measured for comparison. In the spectrum of styrene (Fig. 4a), the bands at 1460, 1500, and 1600–1610 cm−1 correspond to the skeletal C–C stretching modes of the aromatic ring56. The C = C stretching mode of the vinyl group occurs at 1640 cm−1, and the 1820 cm−1 band is the overtone of the 910 cm−1 band due to the C–C out-of-plane bending mode56. The bands between 1700 and 2000 cm−1 are characteristic of the absorption of monosubstituted aromatics, resulting from combination bands that are independent of the nature of substituent56–58. In the C–H vibration region, only the bands between 3000 and 3100 cm−1 corresponding to the vinyl and aromatic C–H stretching vibrations were observed. The ST-IR spectra of HMCM-22 and Hβ were basically the same, which differed from the IR spectrum of styrene (Fig. 4). In the ST-IR spectra of the two zeolite samples, the bands at 1820 and 1640 cm−1 corresponding to the vinyl group of styrene disappeared. Two new bands at 1450 and 1375 cm−1 associated with the C–H bending vibrations of saturated hydrocarbons appeared. Meanwhile, significant absorption bands in the 2800~3000 cm−1 alkane region were observed. The band at 1600 cm−1 agrees well with the bands due to the skeletal C–C stretches of the aromatic rings of the ethylbenzene molecules adsorbed on catalyst surfaces48,56. The presence of the bands corresponding to saturated hydrocarbon groups demonstrates that styrene is chemically adsorbed on the zeolite catalysts through its vinyl group (Scheme 1). Similar infrared spectra were observed in the chemisorption of styrene on silica59,60. It is proven that the adsorption occurs in a highly selective way, almost entirely favoring bonding through the external vinyl group and leaving the aromatic ring unperturbed59,60. This also rules out the formation of protonated styrene. The protonation of styrene leads to the formation of a Wheland structure, resulting in the loss of aromaticity. Hence, the alkylation of thiophene with styrene over HMCM-22 is unlikely to follow the conventional SEAr mechanism. The ST-IR spectra (Fig. 4) indicate that the adsorption of styrene on the two zeolite catalysts took place at ambient temperature and the chemisorbed styrene species did not undergo desorption at temperature up to 250 °C, demonstrating that the chemisorption of styrene on HMCM-22 and Hβ is strong and easy to occur. Therefore, the adsorption of styrene cannot be the determining factor for the reaction of thiophene with styrene.

The infrared measurements of the consecutive adsorption of thiophene and styrene on the two zeolite catalysts at 25 °C were carried out in two manners, the adsorption of thiophene followed by the adsorption of styrene (T-ST-IR) and the reversed sequence (ST-T-IR). The T-ST-IR spectrum of Hβ (Fig. 5) was just an overlap of the corresponding T-IR (Fig. 3b) and ST-IR spectra (Fig. 4b). Almost all the characteristic bands of the adsorbed thiophene species were detected in the T-ST-IR spectrum of Hβ. The 1454 cm−1 band in the T-IR spectra was overlapped with the 1450 cm−1 band in the ST-IR spectra. The 1504 cm−1 band in the T-IR spectra (Fig. 3b) was detected as a weak shoulder near the 1500 cm−1 band in the T-ST-IR spectra (Fig. 5). In the T-ST-IR spectrum of HMCM-22 (Fig. 5), the band at 1438 cm−1 due to the protonated thiophene species disappeared, while all the other characteristic bands of the adsorbed thiophene species were still visible.

Based on the different catalytic performances of HMCM-22 and Hβ as well as the results of the above infrared studies, we propose that the alkylation of thiophene with styrene over HMCM-22 occurs by the attack of a protonated thiophene molecule on the side chain of styrene that chemically adsorbed on the zeolite, followed by deprotonation to yield an alkylated thiophene molecule (Scheme 1). Except for a decrease in the intensities of the bands attributed to the adsorbed thiophene species, the ST-T-IR spectrum of HMCM-22 was similar to the T-ST-IR spectrum (see Supplementary Fig. S15), suggesting that the alkylation reaction takes place between the adsorbed thiophene and styrene species. We further carried out a kinetic analysis based on the data of the reactions of thiophene with styrene at 30 °C and different initial ST/T ratios. The initial rates of these reactions (-rini) were calculated based on the corresponding initial concentrations of thiophene and the slopes of conversion-t curves at t = 0 (see Supplementary Fig. S13). According to Corma et al.6, if the reaction of thiophene with styrene follows an Eley-Rideal (E-R) mechanism then the inverse of the initial rate (−1/rini) should vary linearly with the ST/T ratio. If the Langmuir-Hinshelwood (L-H) mechanism holds true, then a convex curve should be obtained when (−1/rini) is plotted against the ST/T ratio. The plot of (−1/rini) versus the initial ST/T ratio is shown in Supplementary Fig. S16, and a convex curve was observed. Combined with the results of the infrared measurements, it is likely that the reaction of thiophene with styrene over HMCM-22 at temperatures near ambient follows an L-H mechanism, or at least we cannot rule out the L-H mechanism. To test this hypothesis, we conducted two series of experiments using n-hexane as the solvent at 30 °C: (1) the initial concentration of thiophene was fixed at 3.3 mol∙L−1 while those of styrene were varied from 0.3 to 4.3 mol∙L−1, and (2) the initial concentration of styrene was fixed at 3.3 mol∙L−1 while those of thiophene were varied from 0.3 to 4.3 mol∙L−1. The results (see Supplementary Figs. S17–S20) suggest that the reaction of thiophene with styrene at low temperatures is more likely to follow an L-H mechanism than an E-R mechanism and that the adsorption of thiophene on HMCM-22 may be much stronger than that of styrene (see Supplementary information for details). It must be mentioned that further studies are needed for a thorough understanding of the reaction of thiophene with styrene on the surface of HMCM-22. As pointed out by Prins61, proof for the mechanism was in most cases circumstantial based on kinetics. Under many circumstances, the L-H mechanism and the E-R mechanism have been claimed for the same or related reactions by different authors.

In the alkylation of thiophene with styrene over HMCM-22, the selectivities of di-ATs and tri-ATs were low and the selectivities of the three sulfur-containing products were almost independent of the reaction time or the conversion of thiophene (see Supplementary Fig. S11), indicating that the formation of di-ATs and tri-ATs is difficult. Hence, the alkylation of thiophene with styrene may follow a concerted mechanism rather than a stepwise mechanism. That is, mono-AT, di-ATs, and tri-ATs are formed synchronously over HMCM-22 during the alkylation of thiophene with styrene. The distinct different catalytic performance of HMCM-22 and Hβ in the alkylation of thiophene with styrene remains an important question for our future study. The interaction of the protonated thiophene species with zeolites can be a factor. Too strong interaction leads to weaker desorption and slower release of active sites, while too weak interaction results in less adsorbed species. However, this is unlikely to be the case for HMCM-22 and Hβ, because the strengths of their acid sites and the adsorption of the protonated thiophene species on the two zeolite catalysts were about equally strong. Hence, other factors such as the different structures of HMCM-22 and Hβ as well as different coordination states of Si and Al species in the two zeolites should be considered. We then studied the reaction of thiophene with styrene over an MCM-56 zeolite and an MCM-49 zeolite in their H form (denoted as HMCM-56 and HMCM-49, respectively) at 30 °C. The structures of the HMCM-56 and HMCM-49 zeolites were confirmed by their XRD patterns (see Supplementary Fig. S21). All these MWW-type zeolites were active in the reaction of thiophene with styrene, although their activities were lower than that of HMCM-22 (see Supplementary Fig. S22). Therefore, the ability to catalyze the alkylation of thiophene with styrene at temperatures near ambient seems to be a catalytic property of the MWW-type zeolites. The different coordination states of the Si and Al species in HMCM-22 and Hβ revealed by 27Al and 29Si MAS NMR spectroscopy may also be responsible for their different catalytic performance, because the locations and coordination states of Al and Si species in zeolites play important roles for activating molecules62.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated that the HMCM-22 catalyst with a Si/Al ratio of 12.3 is a promising catalyst for the alkylation of thiophene with styrene. It was able to catalyze the reaction at temperatures near ambient. On the contrary, the Hβ catalyst having a similar Si/Al ratio of 13.1 showed almost no activity. Three types of thiophene species were identified in the adsorption of thiophene on HMCM-22 and Hβ: the hydrogen-bonded thiophene, the protonated thiophene, and the thiophene oligomers. Styrene was chemically adsorbed on the two zeolite catalysts through its vinyl group. Such chemisorption was strong and easy to occur. The T-ST-IR spectrum of Hβ was just an overlap of the corresponding T-IR and ST-IR spectra. In the T-ST-IR and ST-T-IR spectra of HMCM-22, the bands due to the protonated thiophene species disappeared. These results indicate that the alkylation of thiophene with styrene over HMCM-22 at low temperatures occurs by a different mechanism than the conventional SEAr mechanism. The mechanism involves the attack of a protonated thiophene molecule on the side chain of styrene that chemically adsorbed on HMCM-22, and the subsequent deprotonation to yield an alkylated thiophene molecule. According to the selectivities of the sulfur-containing products, the further alkylation of mono-AT with styrene is slow.

Methods

Materials

Styrene (98%), thiophene (98%), hexamethylenimine (HMI ≥ 98%), NH4Cl (99.5%), pyridine (99%), 2,6-di-tert-butyl-pyridine (99%), and n-hexane (99%) were provided by Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). NaOH (98%) and sodium aluminate (40–45% Na2O and 50–56% Al2O3) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Silicon dioxide (99.99%, nanopowder, average particle size 20 nm) was purchased from Beesley New Materials (Suzhou) Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). High purity Ar (99.999%) was the product of Feilin Gases (Tianjin) Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). The MCM-22 and β zeolites were purchased from Tianjin Yuanli Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). The MCM-49 zeolite was provided by Zhuoran Environmental Protection Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). An MCM-56 zeolite was synthesized following the method described by Roth et al.63,64: 0.90 g of 50 wt.% NaOH aqueous solution was mixed with 1.05 g of sodium aluminate in 38.85 g H2O. A quantity of 4.07 g HMI was added dropwise to the solution. The mixture was stirred for 5 min, followed by the addition of 9.33 g of silicon dioxide. The final mixture was further stirred for another 5 min and subsequently transferred into a Teflon-lined steel autoclave. Crystallization was conducted at 145 °C for 33 h under autogenous pressure with continuous stirring. The solid product was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with distilled water, dried at 60 °C, and finally calcined at 550 °C to obtain the MCM-56 zeolite.

The MCM-22, β, MCM-56, and MCM-49 zeolites in their H form were obtained by ion-exchange with an aqueous solution of NH4Cl (1.0 M). Before the ion-exchange process, all the zeolite samples were first calcined in air at 550 °C for 5 h. Then the zeolite sample was ion-exchanged with the NH4Cl solution (1.00 g sample in 20.0 mL of 1.0 M NH4Cl solution) at 80 °C under stirring. The NH4Cl solution was renewed every 4 h for total 12 h. The solid was filtered, washed with deionized water, dried at 80 °C for 12 h, and calcined at 550 °C for 5 h.

The alkylation of thiophene with styrene

The alkylation reactions were carried out in a 100 mL three-neck round-bottom flask with a magnetic stir bar under atmospheric pressure. Prior to reaction, 50 mL solution of 1 wt.% thiophene in styrene was charged into the flask. After the flask was heated or cooled to the desired reaction temperature in a water bath, about 0.3 g of zeolite catalyst was added in batches under stirring, and the product sample was taken at a regular interval of 1 h. The reactions of thiophene with styrene at initial ST/T molar ratios varying from 0.5 to 20 over HMCM-22 and Hβ were conducted at 30 °C for kinetic analysis. Characterization and theoretical calculations

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) patterns of the zeolite samples were collected on a Rigaku D/Max-2400 X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation source and Ni filter. Nitrogen physisorption was performed on a BSD-PM1/2 analyzer at the temperature of liquid nitrogen. Prior to the measurement, about 0.10 g of catalyst sample was degassed at 300 °C for 6 h and finally outgassed to 10−5 Pa. The Si/Al ratios of the zeolite catalysts were determined by a Thermo Fisher ARL ADVANT’X Intellipower TM3600 X-ray fluorescence spectrometer. SEM images were captured on a Regulus 8100 electron microscope. The 27Al solid-state magic-angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (27Al MAS NMR) spectra were recorded on an Avance III WB 400 spectrometer at 104.26 MHz with a short 0.6 µs pulse. The rotation rate and the cycle delay were 10 kHz and 0.5 s, respectively. The 29Si solid-state magic-angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (29Si MAS NMR) spectra were measured at 79.4 MHz with a spin rate of 5 kHz and a recycle time of 10 s.

The Fourier transform infrared spectra of pyridine (Py-IR), 2,6-di-tert-butyl-pyridine (DTBPy-IR), styrene (ST-IR), and thiophene (T-IR) adsorbed on the zeolite samples were obtained using the same method on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet 6700 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer equipped with a deuterated triglycine sulfate detector and an operando-tetra high-temperature diffuse reflection cell (Beijing Operando Technology Co., Ltd.). There are two BaF2 infrared windows and a Sharp X diffuse accessory in the cell. Taking Py-IR as an example, the samples were first dried at 350 °C under a pressure of 10⁻3 Pa for 1 h. Subsequently, the samples were cooled to room temperature and further evacuated to a pressure of 10⁻5 Pa. After this pretreatment, the background spectrum was recorded at room temperature. Pyridine vapor was then gradually introduced at room temperature until the sample surface reached a saturated state. Thereafter, the samples were desorbed under a pressure of 10⁻5 Pa to ensure the complete removal of physically adsorbed pyridine. The samples with chemically adsorbed pyridine were subjected to heat treatments at different temperatures under vacuum, followed by the recording of Py-IR spectra. The concentrations of Lewis acid sites and Brønsted acid sites were calculated from the intensities of the band at 1454 cm−1 (for Lewis acid sites) and the band at 1540 cm−1 (for Brønsted acid sites) using extinction coefficients, ε(L) = 2.22 cm2 μmol−1, and ε(B) = 1.67 cm2 μmol−1, respectively64,65. 2,6-Di-tert-butylpyridine is used for determining the external surface acidity. The measurement is conducted at 200 °C, and the extinction coefficient is the same as that of pyridine in the infrared spectrum.

The infrared measurements of the consecutive adsorption of thiophene and styrene on the zeolite catalysts at 25 °C were carried out in two manners, the adsorption of thiophene followed by the adsorption of styrene (T-ST-IR) and the reversed sequence (ST-T-IR). After the zeolite sample was pretreated following the method described in the Py-IR, ST-IR, and T-IR measurements, the cell was cooled to 25 °C and evacuated to 10−5 Pa. Then, a background spectrum was recorded. The vapor of thiophene (or styrene) was first added in doses at 25 °C until the sample surface was saturated. The sample was again evacuated to a pressure of 10−5 Pa, and the vapor of styrene (or thiophene) was added in doses at 25 °C. After being evacuated to 10−5 Pa at 25 °C to make sure that there was no physisorbed species, the T-ST-IR (or ST-T-IR) spectra were recorded.

The infrared spectra of the protonated thiophene species were calculated using the restricted Hartree-Fock method with a 6–311 + + G(d,p) basis set. The calculations were performed with the GAMESS software package (version 2023 R1)66.
