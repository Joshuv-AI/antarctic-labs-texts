The Editor,

We thank the author for his thoughtful commentary on our study. His observations merit a focused, point-by-point response.

On The Intrinsic Limitations of Lung Ultrasound (LUS) as a “Regionally Dependent” Modality

The correspondent suggests that expanding scanning zones does not overcome LUS's inability to detect non-subpleural pathology. We agree, and this is precisely what our data demonstrate and openly acknowledge. The negative predictive value (NPV) for consolidation in the 12-zone protocol (0.61), though superior to the 6-zone (0.47), explicitly does not reach the rule-out power of high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT). Our conclusion was never that LUS supplants HRCT, rather that a 12-zone approach closes the diagnostic gap significantly, moving Cohen's kappa from moderate (0.52) to substantial (0.81), a clinically meaningful distinction when HRCT transport carries real hemodynamic risk for unstable patients. The argument that LUS cannot detect deep consolidations does not invalidate zone expansion; it merely defines the residual indication for HRCT, which we clearly articulate.

On The 24-hour Temporal Window

The concern about disease evolution within 24 hours is methodologically valid and was acknowledged as a limitation in our paper. However, a 24-hour window is consistent with and in several cases more stringent than landmark LUS diagnostic accuracy studies in the intensive care unit (ICU) setting.1–4 Critically, all LUS examinations were performed as close in time to HRCT as feasible, with operators blinded to CT findings, minimizing systematic incorporation bias. Interobserver reliability was further validated in a pilot cohort of 20 patients, yielding a κ of 0.86. A shorter window would have reduced sample feasibility without materially changing the core finding.

On Outcome-based Validation and the “physiological Integration” Argument

The correspondent proposes that LUS evaluated within outcome-based and physiological frameworks, for instance, combined with mechanical power indices. We find this intellectually stimulating, but it represents a separate research question from ours. A diagnostic accuracy study using HRCT as the gold standard is the methodologically appropriate design to characterize sensitivity, specificity, and agreement, the foundational step required before integration into complex physiological frameworks is possible. Proposing that our study should have simultaneously measured outcomes conflates distinct phases of clinical evidence development.

Furthermore, the suggestion that “multiple LUS protocols exist without clear evidence of superiority” actually underscores the necessity of precisely the kind of head-to-head, HRCT-referenced comparison we conducted. Our data provide that missing comparative evidence, demonstrating that zone expansion yields a statistically and clinically significant improvement in sensitivity (0.84 vs 0.76) and area under the curve (AUC) (0.92 vs 0.87) for consolidation specifically, findings that are novel within the South Asian critical care context.

Conclusion

Our study was designed with a clearly scoped objective: To quantify the incremental diagnostic yield of the 12-zone over the 6-zone LUS protocol using HRCT as the definitive reference standard. It achieves this with rigorous methodology, validated interobserver reliability, and transparent acknowledgment of limitations. We welcome the future research agenda proposed by the correspondent, and submit that our work provides the necessary diagnostic foundation upon which such physiological integration studies must be built.
