Development and validation of a predictive model for delayed emergence from anesthesia in elderly patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: a retrospective study
Authors: Ruirong Chen, Yue Zhang, Xiongpeng He, Min Liu, Wenjia Shen, Chunhua Xi, Guyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelayed emergence from anesthesia is a common perioperative complication in elderly patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), which may lead to prolonged recovery and increased healthcare burden. However, predictive tools specifically developed for this population remain limited. This study aimed to develop and validate a practical model to identify patients at increased risk of delayed emergence from anesthesia.MethodsThis retrospective study included 1,400 elderly patients who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) at a tertiary hospital in Beijing between January 2020 and April 2026. An additional temporally independent cohort of 300 patients was used for temporal validation. Perioperative variables, including baseline characteristics, comorbidities, intraoperative parameters, and laboratory indicators, were collected. Predictors were selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, followed by multivariable logistic regression to construct the model. A nomogram was developed and internally validated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Model performance was assessed using discrimination, calibration, decision curve analysis (DCA), and temporal validation with subgroup analyses.ResultsDelayed emergence occurred in 210 patients (15.00%). Six independent predictors were identified: myocardial infarction, colloid solution use, bradycardia, end-tidal CO2 < 35 mmHg, hypothermia, and APTT. The model showed excellent discrimination in the derivation cohort (AUC = 0.909, 95% CI 0.888–0.929) with good calibration and clinical utility. In the temporal validation cohort, it maintained good discrimination (AUC = 0.863) and clinical usefulness, although calibration showed some deviation from ideal agreement. Subgroup analysis suggested better performance in patients aged ≥ 80 years.ConclusionsThe nomogram demonstrated good discrimination and clinical utility for predicting delayed emergence from anesthesia in elderly patients undergoing VATS. It may facilitate perioperative risk assessment and individualized perioperative management, although further recalibration may be required before external application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04128-9.Keywords: Delayed emergence from anesthesia, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, Elderly patients, Nomogram, Risk prediction, Perioperative factors

Background

Lung cancer remains one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Although substantial advances have been achieved in early detection, molecularly targeted therapies, and immunotherapy in recent years, the overall prognosis of lung cancer remains unsatisfactory [2]. Its persistently high mortality rate continues to impose a significant global public health burden. Surgical resection is widely regarded as the standard treatment for patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [3]. Research has shown that both the incidence and mortality of lung cancer increase progressively with advancing age, with older individuals representing a particularly high-risk population [4]. As population aging continues to accelerate, the diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer will face substantial challenges.

In thoracic surgery, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has been widely adopted as a primary surgical approach for the treatment of mediastinal tumors and lung cancer [5, 6]. Compared with conventional thoracotomy, VATS is associated with reduced surgical trauma, shorter duration of chest tube placement, less postoperative pain, and a shorter length of hospital stay [7]. It has also been associated with decreased healthcare expenditures [8], reduced perioperative complications and mortality, improved postoperative quality of life, and enhanced recovery [9]. Although VATS offers well-recognized advantages such as reduced surgical trauma and faster recovery, it may still be associated with an increased risk of delayed emergence from anesthesia in clinical practice [10]. This phenomenon is not solely related to the use of double-lumen endotracheal tubes and one-lung ventilation [11], but may also be associated with the inherent complexity of thoracic procedures [12]. which can prolong operative duration. In addition, perioperative hypothermia [13–15] may further contribute to delayed emergence from anesthesia. The reported incidence of delayed emergence varies substantially across different surgical populations, likely due to differences in patient characteristics, anesthetic techniques, surgical complexity, and diagnostic criteria [10, 16, 17]. Current evidence does not demonstrate that thoracic surgery has a consistently higher or lower incidence of delayed emergence compared with laparoscopic or other surgical procedures. For example, a recent study of elderly patients undergoing VATS radical resection reported a delayed emergence incidence of 16.34%, which was higher than that reported in elderly patients undergoing non-cardiac general anesthesia but lower than that observed after more complex procedures such as radical biliary surgery [10]. These findings suggest that the risk of delayed emergence after VATS may be influenced by the specific patient population and perioperative conditions rather than surgical type alone. Nevertheless, thoracic surgery represents a distinct clinical setting because of its unique anesthetic requirements and physiological challenges, and the risk factors associated with delayed emergence following VATS remain insufficiently characterized.

Delayed emergence from anesthesia is defined as the failure to regain consciousness within 90 [10] to 120 [16] minutes after discontinuation of general anesthesia, along with an inability to appropriately respond to external stimuli and verbal commands. In the present study, delayed emergence was defined as an anesthesia emergence time > 90 min. Delayed emergence from anesthesia is mainly related to three categories of factors, including anesthetic drug related factors, patient related conditions, and surgery related factors [17]. These mechanisms may act individually or in combination to prolong the recovery of consciousness after general anesthesia. Delayed emergence from anesthesia is associated with increased postoperative complications, prolonged recovery room or ICU stay, and higher perioperative resource utilization [18–20]. It may also delay the recognition of serious neurological or metabolic complications, thereby potentially worsening patient outcomes [21].

Currently, older adults already account for a substantial proportion of patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery. With ongoing population aging and advances in medical care, the proportion of elderly surgical patients continues to rise [22]. Elderly patients are characterized by distinct pathophysiological features [23–26], including a higher burden of comorbidities, reduced multi-organ function, and diminished physiological reserve, which collectively increase the risk of delayed emergence from anesthesia during the perioperative period [27–29]. Therefore, identifying elderly patients at high risk of delayed emergence from anesthesia is of important clinical significance.

The reported incidence of delayed emergence from anesthesia ranges from 9% to 25% across different surgical populations [10, 16, 17], including elderly patients undergoing non-cardiac procedures and those receiving thoracoscopic or laparoscopic surgery. Advanced age, anesthetic depth, intraoperative hemodynamic instability, pulmonary disease, operative factors, fluid management, and body temperature have all been identified as important contributors to delayed emergence from anesthesia [10, 17, 21].

Although numerous studies have reported delayed emergence from anesthesia, most have focused on identifying associated risk factors, while research on practical predictive tools remains in its early stages. Moreover, few models have been specifically developed for elderly patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery, and heterogeneity within this population, particularly in terms of age-stratified risk, has not been adequately addressed. Existing models are also limited by relatively small sample sizes and may not fully incorporate key perioperative variables, such as comorbidities and intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations [10]. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a practical nomogram specifically for predicting delayed emergence from anesthesia in elderly patients undergoing VATS, providing an individualized tool for dynamic perioperative risk assessment before emergence from anesthesia and supporting personalized perioperative management.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD statement. A total of 1,700 elderly patients who underwent VATS at a tertiary hospital in Beijing were consecutively enrolled. Patients treated between January 2020 and December 2025 (n = 1,400) were used as the development cohort for model construction, while those treated between January and April 2026 (n = 300) served as a temporal validation cohort. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) elective VATS resection; (2) age ≥ 65 years; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification I–IV; (4) complete medical records; and (5) receipt of combined intravenous and inhalational general anesthesia with planned postoperative admission to the post-anesthesia care unit. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of severe neurological or psychiatric disorders, including stroke with hemiplegia, Parkinson’s disease, or advanced dementia; (2) long-term preoperative use of sedative, hypnotic, antidepressant, or opioid medications; (3) severe preoperative cognitive impairment or hearing or communication disorders precluding cooperation with instructions; (4) intraoperative conversion to thoracotomy; (5) major intraoperative adverse events, including massive bleeding requiring substantial transfusion, cardiac arrest, or anaphylactic shock; (6) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, defined as Child–Pugh class C or dialysis-dependent renal failure; and (7) unplanned direct postoperative admission to the intensive care unit with ongoing sedation. A flowchart of the study design is presented in Fig. 1.

Patient selection flowchart. A total of 1,851 potential participants were initially screened. After exclusions for not meeting inclusion criteria (n = 32) and declining to participate (n = 2), 1,817 patients were assessed for eligibility. Further exclusions included intraoperative conversion to thoracotomy (n = 39), major intraoperative adverse events (n = 12), and direct postoperative ICU admission with ongoing sedation (n = 66), resulting in 1,700 eligible patients. These were divided by time period into a development cohort (January 2020- December 2025, n = 1,400) and a temporal validation cohort (January 2026 - April 2026, n = 300). In the development cohort, 1,190 patients had non-delayed emergence and 210 had delayed emergence. In the temporal validation cohort, 260 patients had non-delayed emergence and 40 had delayed emergence

Anesthesia management

All patients received general anesthesia according to a standardized institutional protocol for video assisted thoracoscopic surgery, involving induction with propofol, sufentanil, and a non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent, followed by double lumen endotracheal intubation for one lung ventilation. Anesthesia was maintained with a combination of intravenous and volatile anesthetics together with continuous remifentanil infusion, titrated according to standard clinical signs of anesthetic depth and supplemented by Bispectral Index monitoring in patients for whom this modality was available. Specific anesthetic and analgesic agents, including supplemental analgesics and antiemetics, were adjusted according to individual patient characteristics and clinical judgment. Intermittent neuromuscular blockade was maintained throughout surgery and reversed with neostigmine and atropine at the end of the procedure. A warming blanket was routinely used for all patients, with additional warming measures, including intravenous fluid warming and forced air warming devices, applied as needed according to each patient’s intraoperatively monitored core temperature. Ventilation parameters were adjusted to maintain standard oxygenation and end tidal carbon dioxide targets. While Bispectral Index values, individual anesthetic and opioid dosages, and the precise timing of neuromuscular blockade reversal were not systematically extracted as discrete variables in the retrospective dataset, the physiological consequences of temperature and ventilatory management most directly relevant to delayed emergence are represented in the final predictive model through the inclusion of hypothermia and etCO2 < 35 mmHg, and the standardized nature of this institutional protocol is expected to reduce, though not eliminate, heterogeneity related to the underlying anesthetic techniques themselves.

Sample

Candidate predictors were prespecified based on published evidence and multidisciplinary expert opinion (Table 1). A multidisciplinary panel comprising 3 senior thoracic surgeons, 3 senior anesthesiologists, and 2 senior perioperative nurses, each with at least 10 years of clinical experience, participated in discussions to identify clinically relevant predictors of delayed emergence. No formal Delphi process or structured consensus rating was used. Candidate variables were selected according to three predefined criteria: (1) biological and clinical plausibility based on the literature; (2) routine availability in the electronic medical record before or during surgery; and (3) clinical relevance to elderly patients undergoing thoracic surgery. Variables for which expert opinions diverged were retained for subsequent statistical evaluation rather than excluded at this stage. The complete list of candidate predictors, definitions, timing of assessment, clinical rationale, and supporting references is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Sample size estimation was guided by the events-per-variable (EPV) principle for multivariable prediction modeling [30]. Assuming that approximately 10 predictors would be retained in the final logistic regression model and that at least 10–15 outcome events per predictor were required to ensure stable estimation31, the minimum number of events was estimated to be 100–150. Given an anticipated incidence of delayed emergence from anesthesia ranging from 9% to 25%, the required sample size for model development was estimated to be approximately 400-1,667 patients. After accounting for a potential 10%−20% rate of incomplete data, the adjusted sample size requirement was approximately 445-2,083 patients. The development cohort included 1,400 patients, which satisfied these requirements and was considered adequate to support model development using penalized regression and internal validation with bootstrap resampling to mitigate overfitting. In addition, an independent temporal validation cohort of 300 patients was included to evaluate model performance over time. Although no formal sample size calculation was performed for the validation cohort, this sample size was considered acceptable for temporal validation, as recommended by previous methodological studies.

Ethical consideration

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval No. TREC2026-KY123), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Given the retrospective nature of the study and the inclusion of elderly patients, some individuals had impaired cognitive or communication abilities or could not be reached at the time of follow-up. Therefore, informed consent was obtained from patients via telephone or electronic communication (e.g., text messaging or WeChat), or from their legally authorized representatives when the patients were unable to provide consent themselves.

For patients who could not be contacted despite repeated attempts or were deceased, the requirement for informed consent was partially waived by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval No. TREC2026-KY123), in accordance with ethical regulations for retrospective studies. In such cases, only anonymized data were included to ensure patient confidentiality.

Measures

This study developed a structured data collection form to identify demographic characteristics and preoperative and intraoperative risk factors associated with delayed emergence from anesthesia. Variables collected included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification (ASA). Comorbidities recorded were hypertension, coronary heart disease (CHD), myocardial infarction, valvulopathy, arrhythmia, cerebral infarction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus, and smoking history. Intraoperative variables included hypothermia, estimated blood loss, colloid and crystalloid infusion volumes, urine output, blood products infusion (BPI), intraoperative hypertension, hypotension, tachycardia, bradycardia, oxygen saturation (SpO2) below 90%, end-tidal carbon dioxide (etCO2) below 35 mmHg or above 45 mmHg, and anesthesia duration. Laboratory parameters included hemoglobin, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), serum Ca2+, serum K+, neutrophil ratio, albumin, uric acid (UA), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL). Anesthesia-related technique variables were not included to reduce heterogeneity and potential confounding in the predictive model.

Data collection

Clinical information was retrieved retrospectively from the hospital information system. Preoperative laboratory indicators were selected from the most recent examinations conducted within 72 h before surgery. If multiple laboratory measurements were available during this period, the value obtained from the latest examination before surgery was used for the final analysis. Data extraction and verification were carried out independently by two researchers to ensure consistency and accuracy. Incomplete records were managed using listwise deletion, with mean substitution applied where appropriate. Following data screening and quality control, a total of 1400 patients were retained for final analysis.

Intraoperative hemodynamic abnormalities were classified using predefined clinical thresholds. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) > 140 mmHg or an increase exceeding 20% from baseline persisting for at least 1 minute [31]. Hypotension was defined as SBP < 90 mmHg or a reduction greater than 20% from baseline lasting ≥ 1 minute [31]. Tachycardia was defined as a heart rate (HR) > 100 beats per minute sustained for ≥ 1 min, whereas bradycardia was defined as HR < 50 beats per minute over the same duration [31]. Intraoperative hypothermia was defined as a core body temperature below 36 °C at any recorded time point [32].

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was delayed emergence from anesthesia, defined as an anesthesia emergence time > 90 min, in accordance with previously established definitions of delayed emergence [10, 16]. Anesthesia emergence time was calculated from the discontinuation of all general anesthetic agents to recovery of consciousness. Recovery of consciousness and extubation were documented separately in the anesthesia record. Recovery of consciousness was assessed and recorded in real time by attending anesthesiologists or anesthesiologists of a higher professional rank, based on the patient’s ability to spontaneously open the eyes or open the eyes in response to verbal command, and to appropriately follow simple verbal instructions (e.g., squeezing the examiner’s hand or nodding on request). Although extubation was routinely performed after patients met standard clinical extubation criteria, extubation time was not used in the definition of the primary outcome. Patients with an emergence time > 90 min were classified as the delayed emergence group, whereas those with an emergence time ≤ 90 min were classified as the non-delayed emergence group.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons between groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages, and differences were evaluated using the chi-square (χ2) test. For model development, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to identify potential predictors and reduce multicollinearity. Variables selected by LASSO were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to construct the prediction model. Potential nonlinear relationships between continuous predictors and the outcome were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions. A nomogram was developed based on the final model. Model performance in the development cohort was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration was evaluated using calibration plots, calibration intercept (CITL), calibration slope, and the Brier score. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA). Internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism-corrected performance. Temporal validation was conducted in an independent cohort. Model performance in the validation cohort was assessed using the same metrics, including ROC curves with AUC, calibration analysis (calibration plots, CITL, calibration slope, and Brier score), and DCA. In addition, subgroup analyses were performed in the temporal validation cohort according to age (< 80 and ≥ 80 years). The discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility of the model were further evaluated within each subgroup, and compared with those of the overall validation cohort. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Use of large language models

No large language models (e.g., ChatGPT) were used in the preparation of this manuscript.

Results

A total of 1,400 patients were included, of whom 210 (15.0%) experienced delayed emergence from anesthesia. Patients with delayed emergence were more likely to have a history of myocardial infarction, valvular disease, and cerebral infarction, and exhibited a higher incidence of intraoperative hypothermia, bradycardia, and reduced end-tidal CO2. They also had longer anesthesia duration and hospital stay. Most other baseline and laboratory variables were comparable between groups. Detailed characteristics are presented in Supplementary Tables 2–4.

Univariate analysis of delayed emergence from anesthesia

In univariate logistic regression analysis, variables with p < 0.10 were considered for further evaluation. Among baseline characteristics, male sex, lower BMI, smoking history, CHD, valvular heart disease, myocardial infarction, and cerebral infarction were associated with delayed emergence, while age and CKD showed borderline associations. Intraoperative factors significantly associated with delayed emergence included BPI, prolonged anesthesia duration, increased colloid administration, greater blood loss, higher urine volume, hypothermia, bradycardia, and reduced etCO2 (< 35 mmHg). Among laboratory parameters, prolonged APTT and decreased eGFR were significantly associated with delayed emergence, whereas PT, LDH, and serum Ca2+ showed borderline effects. Restricted cubic spline analysis was further performed to explore potential non-linear associations. eGFR showed approximately linear negative associations with delayed emergence, indicating that higher levels were associated with a reduced risk of delayed emergence. In contrast, APTT demonstrated a positive linear relationship with delayed emergence, suggesting that prolonged coagulation time increases the risk. Variable selection and multivariable logistic regression analysis

All 23 perioperative variables that met the inclusion threshold in the univariate analysis were entered into a penalized logistic regression model using the LASSO regression with tenfold cross-validation. As illustrated in Fig. 2, regression coefficients progressively approached zero with increasing values of the tuning parameter (λ), indicating effective shrinkage and variable selection. The optimal λ values were determined via cross-validation (Fig. 3), yielding λmin and λ1se. The λ_min criterion retained 14 variables with nonzero coefficients, whereas λ1se resulted in a more compact model comprising 6 predictors. Considering the trade-off between model parsimony and stability, λ1se was selected for the final model. Accordingly, six variables were retained: colloid solution use, APTT, myocardial infarction, bradycardia, etCO2<35 mmHg, and hypothermia.

In the multivariable analysis, delayed emergence from anesthesia was modeled as a binary outcome, coded such that the absence of delayed emergence was assigned a value of 0 and its occurrence a value of 1. The six predictors identified by LASSO regression were entered into the logistic regression model, with variable definitions provided in Table 3. All variables remained statistically significant (all p < 0.001). The findings indicate that intraoperative administration of colloid solutions and impaired coagulation function were associated with an increased risk of delayed emergence. In addition, a history of myocardial infarction, as well as the occurrence of intraoperative bradycardia, reduced etCO2(< 35 mmHg), and hypothermia, were all linked to a higher likelihood of delayed emergence from anesthesia.

The EPV of the multivariable logistic regression model was 35, calculated as the number of delayed emergence events divided by the six included predictors. This value exceeds commonly recommended thresholds, indicating an acceptable model stability and a low risk of overfitting.

Development of the delayed emergence nomogram model

Based on the results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis, a predictive equation for delayed emergence was constructed as follows: Logit(P) = −11.493 + 2.265 × myocardial infarction + 0.002 × colloid solution use + 1.970 × bradycardia + 2.862 × etCO2 <35 mmHg + 1.832 × hypothermia + 0.113 × APTT. Using this regression model, a corresponding nomogram was developed to facilitate individualized prediction of delayed emergence risk in clinical practice. The final nomogram is presented in Fig. 4.

Nomogram for predicting delayed emergence from anesthesia. The nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariable logistic regression model to provide an individualized estimation of delayed emergence risk in elderly patients undergoing Video-assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS). The model includes six independent predictors: myocardial infarction, intraoperative colloid solution volume, bradycardia, end-tidal CO2< 35 mmHg, hypothermia, and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). Binary predictors were coded as No = 0 and Yes = 1. Colloid solution was entered in milliliters (mL) and APTT in seconds (s). For each predictor, a corresponding score is assigned according to the upper “Points” scale. The total score is obtained by summing all individual scores and then projected vertically to the bottom “Predicted Probability” axis to estimate the risk of delayed emergence

Evaluation of prediction efficiency of nomogram model

Internal validation of the predictive model was conducted using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. The model demonstrated strong discriminative ability, with an original AUC of 0.909 (95% CI, 0.888–0.929) and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.904, suggesting minimal overfitting (Fig. 5). The corresponding Somers’ Dxy was 0.817 for the original model and 0.820 after bootstrap correction, further supporting robust discrimination. Calibration performance showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 6). The calibration-in-the-large was − 0.024, indicating negligible systematic prediction bias, while the calibration slope was 1.056, reflecting near-ideal calibration. The Hosmer - Lemeshow test was not statistically significant (χ2 = 10.089, P = 0.259), supporting good model fit. Overall prediction accuracy was supported by a Brier score of 0.073. The optimal probability threshold was 0.189, yielding a sensitivity of 0.814 and a specificity of 0.855, indicating balanced classification performance. Further calibration diagnostics demonstrated acceptable prediction error, with a mean absolute error of 0.147 and a 90th percentile error of 0.498. The reliability component was 0, resolution was 0.052, and uncertainty was 0.127, indicating appropriate calibration structure and moderate outcome variability.

Evaluation of clinical utility of nomogram model

Decision curve analysis was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the prediction model. As shown in Fig. 7, the net benefit of the model remained consistently above zero across a wide range of threshold probabilities (0–1.0). In addition, the model demonstrated superior net benefit compared with alternative decision strategies across the entire threshold spectrum. These results indicate that the model provides stable clinical value and may offer meaningful support for perioperative risk stratification and individualized decision-making under varying clinical preference settings.

Temporal validation and subgroup analysis of the prediction model

In the temporal validation cohort (n = 300), the model demonstrated good discriminative performance, with an overall area under the curve (AUC) of 0.863, indicating strong ability to distinguish between patients with and without delayed emergence (Supplementary Fig. 4). Subgroup analyses showed that the model achieved an AUC of 0.826 in patients aged < 80 years and 0.945 in those aged ≥ 80 years (Supplementary Fig. 4). Calibration analysis revealed substantial miscalibration in the temporal validation cohort, with a calibration-in-the-large (CITL) of − 3.177 (95% CI, − 3.810 to − 2.631) and a calibration slope of 5.706 (95% CI, 4.265–7.344), indicating marked deviation from the ideal calibration (intercept = 0 and slope = 1). The Brier score was 0.077, suggesting acceptable overall prediction accuracy despite poor calibration. The calibration curve (Supplementary Fig. 5) further demonstrated systematic disagreement between predicted and observed event probabilities across most risk ranges, consistent with the calibration metrics. In the age subgroup analyses, the < 80 years group showed a CITL of − 3.204 (95% CI, − 3.864 to − 2.638), a calibration slope of 5.074 (95% CI, 3.525–6.810), and a Brier score of 0.073. For patients aged ≥ 80 years, the corresponding values were − 1.621 (95% CI, − 4.490 to 0.351), 6.504 (95% CI, 1.944–14.400), and 0.149, respectively. Although the model maintained good discrimination in both age groups (AUC 0.826 and 0.945, respectively), calibration remained suboptimal, particularly given the substantial deviation of the calibration slope from the ideal value and the wide confidence intervals observed in the ≥ 80 years subgroup. Decision curve analysis demonstrated positive net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities in both subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 6). After logistic recalibration of the intercept and slope in the temporal validation cohort, the calibration curve demonstrated substantially improved agreement between predicted and observed risks. The recalibrated model retained the same discriminative ability (AUC = 0.863), while the mean absolute error decreased to 0.026 and the 90th percentile absolute error was 0.066, indicating improved calibration accuracy.

Discussion

In this study, delayed emergence from anesthesia was observed in 210 of 1,400 elderly patients (15.00%) undergoing VATS, indicating that it remains a clinically relevant perioperative issue consistent with previous reports [10, 16, 17]. Based on these findings, a nomogram was established to facilitate individualized estimation of delayed emergence risk in routine clinical practice.

In the multivariable analysis, delayed emergence from anesthesia was independently associated with a history of myocardial infarction, prolonged APTT, greater intraoperative colloid administration, intraoperative bradycardia, reduced etCO2, and intraoperative hypothermia. These results align with prior evidence [10, 16, 17], underscoring the role of pre-existing cardiac pathology, coagulation impairment, and intraoperative physiological derangements in the delayed recovery of consciousness. Importantly, the persistence of these associations under routine perioperative management suggests an inherent vulnerability in a subset of patients, who remain at elevated risk of delayed emergence despite standard anesthetic care.

Myocardial infarction is defined as an irreversible necrotic state of myocardium [33]. Patients with a prior history of myocardial infarction typically exhibit reduced cardiac reserve and an increased susceptibility to perioperative hemodynamic instability [34, 35], including hypotension and arrhythmias. These alterations may compromise cerebral perfusion, thereby delaying anesthetic clearance and ultimately prolonging recovery of consciousness [17]. Therefore, greater attention should be paid to elderly patients with a history of myocardial infarction, with emphasis on thorough preoperative evaluation and optimization of cardiovascular function to reduce perioperative risk. Notably, this pathophysiological vulnerability may interact with other perioperative disturbances identified in the present study. Intraoperative bradycardia may contribute to delayed emergence from anesthesia through its effect on cardiac output. Reduced heart rate can decrease cardiac output and systemic perfusion, leading to impaired drug clearance and reduced cerebral blood flow [36]. Such hemodynamic compromise has been recognized as a contributing factor to delayed recovery of consciousness after general anesthesia [17]. In addition, bradycardia is a readily detectable early hemodynamic signal of reduced cardiac output and may therefore serve as an important warning sign of perioperative circulatory instability. During anesthesia, reductions in cardiac output are typically multifactorial. In addition to bradycardia, they may result from hypovolemia due to blood loss, myocardial depression caused by anesthetic agents, or reduced venous return associated with positive pressure ventilation. Under stable ventilatory conditions, etCO2 can serve as a surrogate for cardiac output, as decreased systemic blood flow limits the transport of carbon dioxide to the lungs, leading to lower etCO2 [37, 38]. These hemodynamic changes may further reduce cerebral blood flow and aggravate cerebral hypoperfusion. In contrast, APTT reflects the integrity of the intrinsic and common coagulation pathways [39], with prolongation indicating deficiencies or dysfunction of clotting factors. Such abnormalities may signify a dysregulated hemostatic state, which can disrupt microvascular integrity and ultimately impair tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery [40]. Accordingly, in elderly patients, greater attention should be paid to preoperative coagulation assessment, and appropriate optimization should be undertaken to reduce the risk of perioperative hemodynamic and microcirculatory complications. In addition, greater intraoperative colloid administration has been associated with adverse outcomes in previous studies, particularly in critically ill or high-risk surgical patients [41, 42]. Increased colloid use in this context most likely reflects the underlying severity of intraoperative blood loss and hemodynamic instability, rather than representing a modifiable factor that directly causes delayed emergence [41]. That said, previous studies have also suggested that excessive colloid infusion may independently contribute to hemodilution and impaired coagulation function [41–43], as well as endothelial dysfunction and tissue edema [44, 45], which could theoretically disrupt microcirculatory perfusion and oxygen diffusion. Given the observational design of this study, this association is more likely to reflect the severity of underlying intraoperative blood loss and hemodynamic instability than a direct causal effect of colloid volume itself. Reducing colloid administration should therefore not be expected, on its own, to lower the risk of delayed emergence. Rather, greater colloid use may serve as a clinically useful marker prompting closer attention to the hemodynamic instability that it reflects, particularly in elderly patients with reduced physiological reserve. Intraoperative hypothermia has been associated with prolonged recovery from anesthesia, which is consistent with previous studies [10, 46]. In elderly patients, several factors may contribute to the development of hypothermia, including a reduced basal metabolic rate [23] and an impaired vasoconstrictive response to cold [47]. Hypothermia may decrease the activity of drug-metabolizing enzymes, thereby slowing the biotransformation and elimination of anesthetic agents [46, 48]. As a result, anesthetic drugs may accumulate in the body, ultimately leading to delayed emergence from anesthesia. These findings highlight the importance of careful perioperative temperature monitoring and active warming strategies in elderly patients.

The final model integrates both preoperative and intraoperative variables. Myocardial infarction, a preoperative variable, reflects baseline cardiovascular vulnerability, while prolonged APTT, also assessed preoperatively, reflects underlying coagulation status. The remaining intraoperative variables, including colloid solution use, bradycardia, etCO2 < 35 mmHg, and hypothermia, are not merely statistical predictors but also clinically actionable warning signs that may emerge at any point during surgery. Their occurrence should prompt heightened vigilance for an increased risk of delayed emergence as soon as they are observed, rather than only after the completion of surgery. Accordingly, the model does not require all intraoperative variables to be finalized before it can inform clinical judgment; as each intraoperative variable becomes available, it can be incorporated to update the risk estimate in real time, allowing clinicians to integrate the patient’s preoperative vulnerability profile with evolving intraoperative physiological signals throughout the procedure. This supports individualized perioperative management, including enhanced monitoring during emergence, targeted optimization of fluid and temperature management, and closer postanesthesia care unit observation for high-risk patients, before anesthetic agents are discontinued. From a clinical perspective, the model is not intended to guide specific therapeutic interventions, but rather to identify elderly patients at increased risk of delayed emergence, thereby facilitating closer perioperative observation and more timely recognition of physiological deterioration in vulnerable individuals. Although the model demonstrated good discrimination in the temporal validation cohort, the initial calibration analysis revealed substantial deviation from ideal agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, with confidence intervals for both the calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope excluding the ideal values. A calibration slope well above 1 indicates that predicted probabilities were more extreme than warranted, while the markedly negative calibration-in-the-large reflects systematic overestimation of absolute risk. This finding should not be underestimated, as it indicates that the model’s original absolute risk estimates could not be considered reliable in this population, despite preserved discrimination. To address this, we performed logistic recalibration of the intercept and slope, which markedly improved agreement between predicted and observed risks while fully preserving the model’s discriminative ability. This indicates that the miscalibration reflected a correctable, population level shift in absolute risk rather than a loss of the model’s underlying ability to rank patients by relative risk. Nonetheless, this recalibration was derived and evaluated within the same institution, and prospective validation, together with recalibration where necessary, in independent multicenter cohorts remains warranted before the model’s absolute risk estimates can be considered generalizable to other clinical settings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study conducted in a tertiary hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the model to other institutions and patient populations. Temporal validation using multicenter, prospective cohorts is therefore required to confirm the robustness and applicability of the nomogram in different clinical settings. Second, although a relatively comprehensive set of demographic, clinical, intraoperative, and laboratory variables was included, several anesthesia related factors were not specifically assessed, including depth of anesthesia, anesthetic and opioid dosage, neuromuscular blockade and its reversal, and postoperative sedative or analgesic exposure. These factors may influence recovery from anesthesia by affecting hypnotic burden, respiratory function, neuromuscular recovery, and postoperative level of consciousness [17, 49]. Because of the retrospective design, detailed and standardized data on these variables were not consistently available across all patients. In addition, anesthesia management for VATS at our institution follows relatively standardized protocols, which may reduce but does not eliminate the contribution of these factors to heterogeneity in delayed emergence. Some of these factors may also be partially and indirectly captured through correlated variables already included in the model, such as surgical duration and intraoperative hemodynamic parameters, which are generally associated with cumulative anesthetic and opioid exposure. Their absence is therefore more likely to affect the precision of individualized risk estimation in patients whose anesthetic management deviated from standard protocols, rather than to substantially compromise the overall discriminative performance of the model. We focused on variables that were routinely recorded and readily applicable in clinical practice. Nonetheless, the potential influence of these anesthesia specific factors on model interpretation cannot be entirely ruled out, and future prospective multicenter studies with detailed anesthetic exposure data are warranted to determine whether incorporating them can further improve the predictive accuracy and clinical applicability of the model. Third, although the model demonstrated excellent discrimination in the derivation cohort (AUC = 0.909), this performance reflects apparent, internally validated discrimination only. To further evaluate model performance, a single-center temporal validation cohort was subsequently used to assess the model’s temporal transportability. While this approach reduces the risk of overfitting associated with pure internal validation, it does not constitute external validation in the strict sense, as it was obtained from the same institution and therefore may not fully capture variations in patient characteristics, anesthetic practices, and perioperative management across different clinical settings. In addition, the validation cohort included only a relatively limited number of delayed emergence events, which may reduce the stability of the performance estimates. Therefore, prospective multicenter external validation is warranted to further evaluate the generalizability, calibration, and clinical utility of the nomogram before widespread implementation. Another limitation of this study is the inability to completely distinguish delayed emergence from hypoactive postoperative delirium. Although these conditions are conceptually distinct, with delayed emergence reflecting failure to regain an adequate level of consciousness and hypoactive delirium reflecting fluctuating attentional and cognitive disturbances after consciousness has been regained, prior studies using the Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS) have shown that the majority of emergence delirium cases in the PACU are of the hypoactive subtype, typically corresponding to RASS scores between − 1 and − 3, whereas hyperactive presentations (RASS ≥ 1) are comparatively rare [50]. However, a formal RASS assessment was not systematically used in our study, and postoperative delirium was not routinely assessed using validated screening instruments such as the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU), the Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (Nu-DESC), or the 3-Minute Diagnostic Interview for Confusion Assessment Method (3D-CAM), because of the retrospective nature of the study. This distinction is clinically important, as hypoactive delirium is the most common subtype among older surgical patients and can be difficult to distinguish from residual anesthetic effects at the bedside [51]. Because our outcome definition required only a basic threshold of eye opening and appropriate response to simple verbal commands, rather than a comprehensive assessment of attention and cognition, this may have partially reduced, but does not eliminate, the risk of misclassifying hypoactive delirium as delayed emergence. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized assessments, such as RASS for sedation and wakefulness and validated delirium screening tools for the postanesthesia care unit (PACU) setting, are warranted to improve outcome classification and further enhance the predictive accuracy and clinical applicability of the model. Finally, the clinical impact of the nomogram may be limited, as it primarily reflects perioperative physiological and laboratory vulnerability rather than providing guidance for additional therapeutic interventions beyond routine anesthetic management.

Conclusion

This study developed a nomogram with good discriminative performance and clinical utility for predicting delayed emergence from anesthesia in elderly patients undergoing VATS. By integrating preoperative and intraoperative variables, the model facilitates perioperative risk assessment before emergence from anesthesia, helping identify patients who remain vulnerable despite standard perioperative management. It may assist clinicians in optimizing perioperative management and preparing individualized postoperative monitoring. Temporal validation initially showed calibration deviation, which was substantially corrected through logistic recalibration; prospective multicenter validation remains necessary before broader clinical application.
