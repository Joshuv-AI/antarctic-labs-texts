Risk of Psychiatric Worsening With GLP‐1 Receptor Agonist Use in Patients With Type 2 Diabetes and Treated Depression
Authors: Yu Chang, Ming‐Hong Hsieh, Po‐Chung Ju, Cheng‐Chen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo compare 1‐year psychiatric outcomes after GLP‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1 RA) versus sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) initiation in patients with type 2 diabetes and treated depression.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective active‐comparator, new‐user cohort study using electronic health records from the TriNetX network. Patients with type 2 diabetes, depression and antidepressant treatment within 6 months before index were included. After 1:1 propensity score matching, outcomes were compared using Cox proportional hazards models. Primary outcomes were suicidality and antipsychotic use. All‐cause mortality was a secondary outcome.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 34 761 patients were included in each group. GLP‐1 RA initiation was not associated with a higher risk of suicidality than SGLT2i initiation (hazard ratio [HR] 0.88, 95% CI 0.74–1.05). GLP‐1 RA initiation was associated with a lower risk of antipsychotic use (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.82–0.90; absolute risk difference −1.4%) and lower all‐cause mortality (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.58–0.71). Findings were consistent across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsIn patients with type 2 diabetes and treated depression, we did not detect increased short‐term documented suicidality after GLP‐1 RA initiation compared with SGLT2i initiation and observed less subsequent treatment intensification. These observational findings suggest that treated depression alone should not lead to routine avoidance of GLP‐1 RAs in this population.Keywords: antidiabetic drug, antiobesity drug, GLP‐1 analogue, real‐world evidence, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) have become an important treatment option for type 2 diabetes [1]. However, their effects on psychiatric outcomes have been a subject of growing concern and debate [2]. Some studies have suggested potential benefits on mood‐related outcomes [3, 4, 5, 6], whereas pharmacovigilance reports and several observational cohort studies have raised concerns about adverse neuropsychiatric events, including depression and suicidality [7, 8, 9]. This conflicting evidence complicates clinical decision‐making, particularly when GLP‐1 RAs are prescribed to patients who may already be vulnerable to psychiatric worsening.

Depression and type 2 diabetes share inflammatory and metabolic features, including cytokine dysregulation and chronic low‐grade inflammation [10, 11]. This biological overlap provides additional rationale for evaluating psychiatric outcomes after GLP‐1 RA initiation in patients with type 2 diabetes and depression. Our prior cohort study examined incident depression among patients with newly diagnosed diabetes who initiated GLP‐1 RAs versus sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) [12]. In that study, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with a modestly increased risk of depression, and the signal appeared more evident during the subacute period after treatment initiation. This prior finding raises a distinct question: whether GLP‐1 RA initiation worsens outcomes in patients with established depression. Because incident disease risk and worsening of pre‐existing illness represent fundamentally different clinical processes [13, 14], this question warrants separate investigation.

Evidence addressing this question remains limited [15]. In patients with pre‐existing depression, clinically relevant indicators of worsening include suicidality and treatment intensification, including augmentation of antidepressant therapy [16]. A recent Swedish within‐individual cohort study reported no worsening risk for depression or anxiety and suggested possible improvement during GLP‐1 RA exposure [17]. Additional evidence is needed to replicate and extend these findings in independent populations and clinical settings.

In the present study, we used an active‐comparator, new‐user design to compare GLP‐1 RA initiators with SGLT2i initiators among patients with type 2 diabetes, depression and recent antidepressant treatment. We focused on suicidality and antipsychotic augmentation of antidepressant therapy as clinically relevant primary outcomes. Our goal was to clarify the psychiatric risk profile of GLP‐1 RAs in patients with pre‐existing depression.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX US collaborative network [18], a federated platform that enables analysis of electronic health records across participating institutions without centralising or transferring individual‐level patient data [19]. Reporting was guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cohort studies, as summarised in Table S1.

The study compared patients with type 2 diabetes and pre‐existing treated depression who initiated a GLP‐1 RA with those who initiated an SGLT2i. SGLT2is were selected as the active comparator because they are used in overlapping cardiometabolic treatment settings and represent a clinically relevant therapeutic alternative [20]. To reduce confounding by indication and prevalent‐user bias [21, 22], we used an active‐comparator, new‐user design [23], assessed baseline covariates before treatment initiation and applied propensity score matching. Because treatment was not randomised, findings were interpreted as observational associations.

Patient Selection and Cohort Definition

The primary analysis included patients who initiated a GLP‐1 RA or an SGLT2i between 1 January 2018 and December 31, 2024. This window was selected to reflect the period of broader clinical adoption of both drug classes. Eligible patients had both type 2 diabetes and depression recorded before the index treatment and had evidence of antidepressant treatment within 6 months before index. We required recent antidepressant treatment to focus the analysis on patients with clinically treated depression and plausible risk of short‐term psychiatric worsening. The GLP‐1 RA cohort included dulaglutide, semaglutide, tirzepatide and liraglutide initiators. The SGLT2i cohort included canagliflozin, dapagliflozin and empagliflozin initiators.

Patients were excluded if they had suicidality, antipsychotic use, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia‐spectrum disorders or prior exposure to either study drug class within the year before index. These exclusions were intended to reduce baseline heterogeneity in severe psychiatric instability and to make subsequent events more interpretable as worsening after treatment initiation. Patients with pregnancy within the year before index and those with end‐stage renal disease or dialysis were also excluded to reduce treatment‐selection bias related to contraindications or major prescribing constraints. The index date was defined as the first qualifying prescription date for either a GLP‐1 RA or an SGLT2i during the study period. Eligibility criteria, exclusion criteria and baseline covariates were assessed before the index date. Follow‐up began on the index date, and person‐time before the index date was not included in the outcome follow‐up. The cohort definition is provided in Table S2, and the cohort assembly process is summarised in Figure S1.

Covariates and Propensity Score Matching

To reduce confounding, propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression [24]. Patients were matched 1:1 using nearest‐neighbour matching with a caliper of 0.1 on the propensity score scale, as implemented in the TriNetX platform. Baseline covariates were assessed before index and included demographic characteristics, psychiatric burden, diabetes severity and complications, cardiometabolic comorbidity, healthcare utilisation and concomitant medication use, including psychotropic and antihyperglycemic therapies. Detailed definitions and codes are provided in Table S2. We adjusted for available medication‐class indicators. Patient‐level counts of distinct medications and medication doses were not available as covariates in the aggregate analytic environment used for this study. Within the platform, patients with missing covariate data were excluded from the platform‐generated analysis. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using absolute standardised mean differences (aSMDs), with post‐match values below 0.1 considered acceptable. The detailed baseline characteristics are shown in Table S3.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were suicidality and antipsychotic use. Suicidality was defined as suicidal ideation (ICD‐10 R45.851) or suicide attempt (ICD‐10 T14.91). Antipsychotic (ATC Code N05A) use was selected as a clinically relevant indicator of treatment augmentation in patients with established depression. Secondary outcomes included all‐cause mortality, suicidality plus mortality and antipsychotic use plus mortality. Mortality was included as a secondary outcome both alone and in composite with the primary outcomes to account for the competing risk of death [25, 26]. The primary follow‐up window was 1 year after treatment initiation. For nonfatal outcomes, follow‐up ended at the earliest of the outcome event or 365 days after the index date. We selected a 1‐year window to capture the near‐term period after treatment initiation while maintaining comparable follow‐up across cohorts.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analysis was conducted in the 1‐year matched cohort, with follow‐up not censored for post‐index treatment discontinuation, switching or crossover. We summarised event counts and compared outcomes using Cox proportional hazards models with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), Kaplan–Meier‐based cumulative incidence curves and log‐rank tests. For the main analysis, we also reported fixed‐time absolute risk, risk difference and risk ratio at 365 days. Because proportional hazards were not uniformly satisfied across outcomes as assessed by Schoenfeld residuals [27], HRs were interpreted as summary measures over follow‐up, and fixed‐time risk measures were emphasised in the main presentation.

To provide a complementary time‐to‐event summary that was less dependent on proportional hazards [28], we reconstructed event‐time data from the exported Kaplan–Meier curves and estimated restricted mean survival time (RMST) at 365 days [29, 30]. We calculated E values for the main HR estimates as a sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding. Prespecified sensitivity analyses examined extended follow‐up at 2 years and 3 years, a 3‐month‐to‐1‐year landmark analysis to reduce concerns about early‐event and protopathic bias, and a cohort beginning in 2016 to assess robustness to the cohort entry period. We performed a stricter exclusion sensitivity analysis that extended baseline exclusions for suicidality, antipsychotic use, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia‐spectrum disorders and prior exposure to either study drug class to any time before index. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using a narrower antipsychotic definition restricted to quetiapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole and brexpiprazole. As an additional sensitivity analysis to address baseline antidepressant treatment complexity, Cox models were further adjusted for individual commonly used antidepressants recorded before the index date, including trazodone, mirtazapine, citalopram, paroxetine, sertraline, venlafaxine, bupropion, fluoxetine and duloxetine. Additional analyses compared outcomes against dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4is) as an alternative active comparator, examined acute appendicitis as a negative‐control outcome to probe residual bias and evaluated the unmatched cohort to compare results before matching. Subgroup analyses by age and sex addressed clinically relevant sources of heterogeneity; agent‐specific analyses for individual GLP‐1 RAs served as an exploratory assessment of within‐class heterogeneity. Because only aggregate data was available, interaction p values in subgroup and agent‐specific analyses were estimated based on subgroup‐specific log HRs and standard errors derived from the reported confidence intervals. RMST estimation, E‐value calculation and figure generation were performed in R version 4.5.2.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Before matching, 64 653 GLP‐1 RA initiators and 44 218 SGLT2i initiators met the eligibility criteria for the primary analysis. After 1:1 propensity score matching, each cohort comprised 34 761 patients (Figure S1). Prior to matching, GLP‐1 RA users were younger, more likely to be female and more likely to have obesity, whereas SGLT2i users had a higher burden of cardiovascular and renal comorbidities. After matching, all covariates were well balanced (aSMDs < 0.1; Figure 1 and Table S3). The matched cohorts had a mean age of 61.6 years, and approximately 62.9% of participants were female in both groups. The propensity score density plot also showed improved overlap after matching (Figure S2). Follow‐up duration was also similar between groups, with median follow‐up of 365 days in both cohorts and mean follow‐up of 351.2 days in the GLP‐1 RA group and 344.8 days in the SGLT2i group (Table S4).

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

In the matched cohort, no clear between‐group difference was observed for suicidality (Table 1 and Figure 2). The 1‐year cumulative incidence was 0.7% in the GLP‐1 RA group and 0.7% in the SGLT2i group, corresponding to an absolute risk difference of −0.1% (95% CI, −0.2% to 0.0%). The HR was 0.88 (95% CI, 0.74–1.05; log‐rank p = 0.169), and the RMST difference at 365 days was 0.3 days (95% CI, 0.0–0.6; p = 0.079).

For antipsychotic use, GLP‐1 RA initiation was associated with a lower risk than SGLT2i initiation (Table 1 and Figure 3). The cumulative incidence was 10.8% versus 12.2%, corresponding to an absolute risk difference of −1.4% (95% CI, −1.9% to −1.0%) and a risk ratio of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.85–0.92). The HR was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.82–0.90; log‐rank p < 0.001), and the RMST difference at 365 days was 4.9 days (95% CI, 3.5–6.2; p < 0.001). As a secondary outcome, all‐cause mortality was also lower in the GLP‐1 RA group, with a 1‐year cumulative incidence of 2.0% versus 3.0%, an absolute risk difference of −1.0% (95% CI, −1.3% to −0.8%), and a HR of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.58–0.71; p < 0.001). Lower risks were also observed for the composite outcomes of suicidality plus mortality and antipsychotic use plus mortality (Table 1). Point‐estimate E values were 1.60 for antipsychotic use and 2.50 for mortality, with confidence‐limit E values of 1.46 and 2.17, respectively (Table S5).

Sensitivity, Subgroup and Agent‐Specific Analyses

The main findings were generally consistent across prespecified sensitivity analyses (Tables 2 and S6). For antipsychotic use, the lower risk associated with GLP‐1 RA initiation was consistent across all prespecified sensitivity analyses, with HRs ranging from 0.84 to 0.94. Notable analyses included the 3‐month‐to‐1‐year landmark analysis (HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.88–0.97) and the DPP‐4i comparator analysis (HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.80–0.89). For suicidality, the primary analysis remained near the null, but lower risk estimates were seen in some extended or alternative‐comparator analyses, including the 2‐year analysis (HR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.74–0.97) and the DPP‐4i comparator analysis (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.65–0.98).

Subgroup analyses by age showed no clear evidence of heterogeneity in treatment effects, with approximate interaction p values of 0.846 for suicidality and 0.357 for antipsychotic use. In contrast, sex‐based heterogeneity was observed for antipsychotic use (interaction p = 0.013) and antipsychotic use plus mortality (interaction p = 0.005), although the direction of association favoured GLP‐1 RA use in both sexes. Agent‐specific analyses suggested heterogeneity for suicidality and mortality across individual GLP‐1 RAs, with lower suicidality HR estimates for semaglutide and tirzepatide, but these analyses were exploratory and based on aggregate‐data approximations. The negative‐control outcome of acute appendicitis showed no significant association with treatment group. Unmatched analyses yielded larger effect estimates (Table S6).

Discussion

In this active‐comparator, new‐user analysis of patients with type 2 diabetes and recently treated depression, initiation of a GLP‐1 RA was associated with a lower 1‐year risk of antipsychotic use than initiation of an SGLT2i, while the risk of suicidality was similar between groups. The composite outcomes incorporating mortality also favoured GLP‐1 RA use, and all‐cause mortality was lower in the GLP‐1 RA group. These findings suggest that, in a clinically vulnerable population with established treated depression, GLP‐1 RA initiation did not indicate increased psychiatric worsening and may reflect less subsequent treatment intensification.

The contrast between suicidality and antipsychotic augmentation is clinically informative. Suicidality is uncommon, multifactorial and less directly tied to day‐to‐day depressive symptom burden. By comparison, antipsychotic use may better reflect clinical decisions made when depressive symptoms remain insufficiently controlled despite ongoing antidepressant treatment, although it remains an imperfect proxy for symptom severity. The composite outcomes including mortality were directionally consistent with the main findings, which matters because mortality can act as a competing event in this population. A lower mortality rate in GLP‐1 RA users could keep more patients under observation for psychiatric outcomes, which would tend to attenuate the observed difference in psychiatric event rates. The mortality finding should be interpreted cautiously because mortality was a secondary outcome and the magnitude of the association may reflect residual confounding or treatment channelling in addition to possible cardiometabolic benefit.

The temporal sensitivity analyses further support the primary findings. The RMST results remained closely aligned with the primary findings, showing little separation for suicidality and a modest but consistent advantage for antipsychotic use. The 3‐month‐to‐1‐year landmark analysis preserved the direction of association for antipsychotic use, which reduces concern that the main finding was driven entirely by early prescribing dynamics or protopathic bias. The longer 2‐year and 3‐year follow‐up analyses also preserved the direction of association, although effect sizes were smaller than in the primary analysis. This pattern suggests that the association may be strongest soon after treatment initiation and may attenuate over time.

Subgroup analyses were exploratory. We found no clear age‐based effect modification. For antipsychotic use, point estimates suggested a stronger association in men (HR, 0.80) than in women (HR, 0.90). This pattern may reflect sex‐related differences in the psychiatric effects of GLP‐1 RA, although differences in prescribing practices and residual confounding remain possible explanations [31]. Agent‐specific analyses suggested heterogeneity for suicidality, with lower HRs for semaglutide and tirzepatide, whereas the reduction in antipsychotic use was more consistent across agents. The more favourable suicidality estimates for semaglutide and tirzepatide may reflect pharmacologic differences, including dual incretin activity in the case of tirzepatide, but the tirzepatide estimates in particular should be interpreted cautiously. Tirzepatide entered clinical use later than other agents, and channelling is possible given its potent weight‐loss effects, which may lead to preferential prescribing in patients with greater weight‐loss motivation or lower psychiatric comorbidity burden.

These findings are complementary to the recent Swedish within‐individual study [17], which examined periods of GLP‐1 RA exposure and worsening mental illness in people with depression or anxiety. The within‐individual design is well suited to reducing stable between‐person confounding, whereas the present study uses an active‐comparator framework more directly aligned with clinical prescribing decisions. The compatible findings across both studies are supportive of a similar overall pattern across different populations and analytic approaches. The agent‐level pattern is also qualitatively similar, with semaglutide showing more favorable signals than dulaglutide in both studies.

Depression and type 2 diabetes share overlapping pathophysiology, including chronic low‐grade inflammation [32, 33, 34]. GLP‐1 receptors are also expressed in brain regions involved in mood regulation and reward processing [35], raising the possibility that GLP‐1 RAs may exert central effects beyond their peripheral metabolic actions [36]. Whether these central effects differ by baseline psychiatric status is a hypothesis that cannot be tested in the present data. Such effects may potentially contribute to anhedonia‐like states in individuals without depression while being neutral or beneficial in those with established illness. Future studies with prospectively measured psychiatric phenotypes and longitudinal weight data are needed to clarify whether psychiatric outcomes after GLP‐1 RA initiation are influenced by weight loss, metabolic improvement, central mechanisms or other pathway‐specific effects.

Clinically, these findings support considering GLP‐1 RAs when otherwise indicated for patients with type 2 diabetes and treated depression. The reassurance is population‐level and focused on documented short‐term psychiatric outcomes, so individualised psychiatric assessment and follow‐up remain important, particularly for patients with severe, unstable or recently worsening depression.

Limitations

First, despite the active‐comparator design and propensity score matching, residual confounding may remain because depression severity and other clinical factors were incompletely captured, including PHQ‐9 scores, depression duration, treatment response, antidepressant dose, patient‐level counts of distinct medications and psychosocial factors that may reflect baseline psychiatric vulnerability. Second, laboratory and physical measures were not incorporated into the covariate set or subgroup analyses. Because availability of these variables was incomplete and likely non‐random across patients, conditioning on them could introduce selection or collider bias. Third, the outcomes were defined using diagnostic coding and treatment proxies. Code‐based suicidality outcomes may have low sensitivity and may underestimate true events [37], and the low event rate limited precision. Antipsychotic use reflects treatment intensification more than depressive severity itself, and newly added antidepressant agents or other post‐index antidepressant treatment changes were not fully captured as competing or intercurrent events. Fourth, because only aggregate exports were available, formal competing‐risk modelling was not feasible, subgroup interaction p values were approximate and RMST estimates were reconstructed from Kaplan–Meier curves rather than estimated from person‐level survival data. Finally, the TriNetX network in this study primarily reflects healthcare organisations in the United States, which may limit generalisability to other settings.

Conclusion

In this active‐comparator, new‐user cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes and actively treated depression, we did not detect a higher short‐term risk of documented suicidality after GLP‐1 RA initiation compared with SGLT2i initiation, while GLP‐1 RA initiation was associated with a lower risk of subsequent antipsychotic use. These observational findings do not support an increased short‐term psychiatric worsening in this population [38], and suggest that a history of treated depression alone should not preclude consideration of these agents when clinically indicated. Randomised trials with prospective psychiatric endpoints are needed to establish causality more definitively.
