Expired apheresis platelet-derived exosomes promote cutaneous wound healing
Authors: Zhongwei Xiong, Yuhan Wang, Chuan Jiang, Ling Jiang, Jiasen Ye, Shan Hu, Fujiang Xu, Chengcen Luo, Yuanshuai Huang, Zhi Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes derived from apheresis platelets (AP-Exos) can regulate cellular functions and promote tissue regeneration; however, the characteristics and therapeutic efficacy of exosomes from expired apheresis platelets remain unclear. This study evaluated the properties of exosomes derived from apheresis platelets stored for 7 days (D7-Exos) and their role in skin wound repair. Exosomes were isolated from apheresis platelets stored for 3 days (D3-Exos) and 7 days, and their morphology, particle size, growth factor content, and biological effects on keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells were examined. The potential involvement of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in HUVEC was investigated. A full-thickness skin defect model was established in rats to compare the therapeutic efficacy of D3-Exos, D7-Exos, and platelet lysate (PL). D7-Exos showed higher particle concentration and elevated levels of TGF-β1 and PDGF-BB compared with D3-Exos. They significantly promoted cell proliferation, enhanced collagen secretion in HSF, and increased VEGF secretion and angiogenic capacity in HUVEC. D7-Exos activated the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in HUVEC and upregulated VEGF expression. In vivo, D7-Exos markedly accelerated wound closure, re-epithelialization, and collagen deposition, with better efficacy than D3-Exos and PL. D7-Exos are enriched with multiple growth factors, promote VEGF secretion in HUVEC via activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, and accelerate wound healing, suggesting potential as a candidate therapeutic approach for skin injury, pending further preclinical validation and safety assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-64768-2.Keywords: Expired platelets, Exosomes, Wound healing, Cell proliferation, AngiogenesisSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

The treatment of skin wounds includes conventional conservative therapy and surgical intervention. While conservative treatments have limited efficacy and prolonged healing times, surgical repair offers better outcomes but is constrained by high costs, donor shortages, and the risk of immune rejection1. Platelet concentrates, such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and PL, have been widely applied in wound healing due to their abundance of bioactive repair factors2–6. However, the lack of standardized preparation and application protocols, coupled with considerable individual variability in therapeutic outcomes, limits the broader use of PRP and PL7. Recent studies have revealed that platelet-derived exosomes (P-Exos) play a pivotal role in the wound healing process mediated by platelet concentrates8–10. Compared to PRP and PL, P-Exos are enriched with higher concentrations of growth factors and exhibit lower immunogenicity11,12. Therefore, P-Exos represent a promising optimized alternative to PRP or PL for wound healing applications13.

The storage period for platelets is limited to only five days, which is a major factor contributing to their substantial wastage14. Although platelet products lose some efficacy after exceeding the storage deadline, they still retain abundant growth factors, cytokines, chemokines, and exosomes15. Successful extraction and application of exosomes from expired platelets could not only harness their biological functions but also reduce the waste of valuable blood resources. Studies have shown that exosomes derived from expired platelets exhibit favorable bioactivity in hemostasis and drug delivery, with effects comparable to those of exosomes from platelets within the storage period16,17. A recent clinical trial has further confirmed the safety and efficacy of platelet-derived exosomes in chronic wound healing18. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that, provided the expired platelets are free from contamination, their derived exosomes also possess the potential to promote wound healing. However, systematic investigations to verify this hypothesis are still lacking.

This study aims to isolate exosomes from expired apheresis platelets (APs), quantify the expression levels of their contained growth factors, and systematically evaluate their potential role in tissue repair through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. On one hand, this research is expected to provide a novel therapeutic strategy for tissue regeneration, thereby advancing the field of regenerative medicine and improving patients’ quality of life. On the other hand, subject to the confirmation of safety and the establishment of standardized processing protocols, it may also provide a theoretical basis for the reutilization of expired APs, thereby contributing to the conservation and optimization of blood resources.

Methods

Sample preparation

With approval from the Luzhou Municipal Health Commission, apheresis platelets (APs) collected on the same day were obtained from the Luzhou Central Blood Bank and immediately stored in a platelet agitator. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, which waived the requirement for written informed consent. A total of five bags of APs were acquired and sampled on storage day 3 (D3) and day 7 (D7). Under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet, samples were aseptically collected, aliquoted into 50 mL centrifuge tubes, and heat-sealed to ensure sterility.

Exosome Isolation

Exosomes were isolated using a combination of differential centrifugation and a commercial kit. Briefly, samples were sequentially centrifuged at 3,000 × g, 300 × g, 2,000 × g, and 10,000 × g for 10 min each at 4 ℃ to progressively remove cells, cell debris, and larger particulate contaminants. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and exosomes were subsequently extracted from 4 mL of filtrate according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the exoEasy Maxi Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The eluate was concentrated using Amicon® Ultra-2 centrifugal filter units and subjected to buffer exchange with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Preparation of platelet lysate (PL)

A volume of 10–30 mL of APs stored for 7 days was transferred into 50 mL centrifuge tubes. The samples were frozen at − 80 °C until completely solidified, then thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min until fully liquefied. The tubes were gently inverted five times to ensure thorough mixing. This freeze–thaw cycle was repeated three times to achieve complete platelet lysis and the release of intracellular bioactive factors. After returning to room temperature, the lysates were centrifuged at 6000 × g for 30 min to remove cell debris and particulate matter. The resulting supernatants were collected, transferred to new tubes, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent experimental analysis.

Characterization of exosomes

The morphology and concentration of exosomes were assessed using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; ZetaView, Germany) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi, Japan). For protein extraction, 100 µL of exosomes suspension was mixed with an equal volume of RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1 µL protease inhibitor and 1 µL phosphatase inhibitor, followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatant was collected and transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, China) for subsequent normalization analyses. Western blot analysis was performed to detect typical exosomal marker proteins, including CD63, TSG101, and CD9. The dilution ratios of the corresponding primary antibodies are detailed in Table 1.

Quantification of growth factors in exosomes by ELISA

Exosome samples were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors before analysis. PL samples were thawed and centrifuged to remove membrane debris. The concentrations of TGF-β1, PDGF-BB, and VEGF in PL and AP-Exos were quantified using a double-antibody sandwich ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Standard curves were generated using recombinant human TGF-β1 (Absin, China), PDGF-BB (Cloud-Clone Corp, China), and VEGF (HUABIO, China). Specifically, 100 µL of standard solution or test sample was added to 96-well microplates pre-coated with monoclonal antibodies against human TGF-β1, VEGF, or PDGF-BB. After incubation, biotinylated detection antibodies were added to specifically bind the captured growth factors. Following washing to remove unbound antibody-enzyme conjugates, tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was added for color development. The reaction was terminated with stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader. The concentrations of the growth factors were calculated using specialized analysis software.

Cell culture

HaCaT cells were purchased from Shandong JieluoXuan Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA). HSF were obtained from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China) and maintained in primary fibroblast basal medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% primary fibroblast culture supplement. HUVEC were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO₂.

Exosome labeling and uptake assay

A total of 100 µL of D3-Exos and D7-Exos were thawed on ice, respectively. According to the manufacturer’s instructions of the PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker Mini Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 100 µL of Diluent C was added to each exosome sample to obtain a 200 µL mixture. Separately, 4 µL of PKH67 dye was diluted in 200 µL of Diluent C to prepare the staining solution, which was mixed thoroughly in the dark. The 204 µL dye solution was then added to the 200 µL exosome mixture, resulting in a total volume of 404 µL. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 10 min to complete the labeling reaction. Labeled exosomes were subsequently purified using Exosome Purification Mini-Columns (Enze Kangtai, China) to remove excess free dye. HaCaT, HSF, and HUVEC cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded onto sterile coverslips in 24-well plates. After cell attachment, PKH67-labeled exosomes were added and incubated for 24 h under light-protected conditions. After incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and washed again with PBS three times. To increase membrane permeability, cells were treated with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by three PBS washes. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI for 3–5 min. Finally, the uptake of exosomes was observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell proliferation assay

HaCaT, HSF, and HUVEC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL of their respective culture media. Preliminary dose-response studies (utilizing 5, 50, and 100 µg/mL of D3-Exos or D7-Exos) identified 50 µg/mL as the optimal concentration for promoting cell proliferation; therefore, this concentration was selected for all subsequent experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1). Following cell adhesion, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium containing either 50 µg/mL exosomes (D3-Exos or D7-Exos) or PL. To ensure consistency, all exosomal and PL dosages were standardized based on protein concentrations determined by a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in the dark. The absorbance (optical density) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Scratch wound healing assay

HaCaT and HSF cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well, with 2 mL of the corresponding complete culture medium added to each well. When the cell confluence reached approximately 90%, a straight scratch (“linear wound”) was created on the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. All scratches were performed by the same operator under identical conditions to minimize experimental variability. The culture medium was then removed, and the wells were gently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate detached cells and debris. For the experimental groups, serum-free medium containing 50 µg/mL D3-Exos, D7-Exos, or PL was added, whereas the control group received an equal volume of serum-free medium. Images were captured at 0, 12, and 24 h at predefined fields of view. Scratch areas were quantified using ImageJ software, and relative wound closure was calculated based on the initial scratch area (0 h) to evaluate cell migratory capacity.

Tube formation assay

The day before the experiment, Matrigel (Corning, USA) was thawed slowly at 4 °C, while 200 µL pipette tips and 96-well plates were pre-cooled at −20 °C. The Matrigel was then taken out and kept on ice. Using the pre-cooled pipette tips, 50 µL of Matrigel was carefully added vertically into each well of the 96-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow the gel to solidify. Subsequently, 15,000 cells were seeded into each well. The experimental groups were treated with 100 µL of complete medium containing 50 µg/mL of D3-Exos, D7-Exos, or PL, respectively, while the control group received an equal volume of complete medium without additives. After 6 h of incubation, images of tube formation were captured using a light microscope, and the extent of vascular structure formation was analyzed using ImageJ software.

ELISA measurement of Col I, Col III, and VEGF levels in HSF and HUVEC supernatants

HSF and HUVEC were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After cell attachment, the original culture medium was discarded, and cells were treated with D3-Exos, D7-Exos, or PL. After 24 h, the cell culture supernatants were gently collected and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min to remove debris. The concentrations of collagen type I (Col I) and collagen type III (Col III) in the HSF culture supernatants, as well as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the HUVEC culture supernatants, were quantitatively determined using commercial ELISA kits based on the sandwich immunoassay principle, following the manufacturers’ instructions. Recombinant human Col I (Cloud-Clone Corp, China), Col III (Cloud-Clone Corp, China), and VEGF (HUABIO, China) were used to generate standard curves. Briefly, 100 µL of standards or samples were added to 96-well plates pre-coated with monoclonal antibodies against human Col I, Col III, or VEGF. Following incubation, biotin-labeled detection antibodies were added to specifically bind to the captured growth factors. After washing to remove unbound substances, TMB substrate was added for color development. The reaction was terminated, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader. Concentrations of each factor were calculated using dedicated software.

Western blot analysis

HUVEC were lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected for total protein extraction. Protein samples were mixed thoroughly with 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. The denatured proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels, initially electrophoresed at 80 V for 30 min, followed by 120 V for 60 min to complete protein separation. Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes under a constant current of 200 mA in an ice-water bath for 1 h. After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST at room temperature for 1 h to prevent nonspecific binding. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted as described in Table 1. The following day, membranes were washed three times with TBST containing 0.1% Tween-20 (10 min each), then incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. After three additional washes, protein bands were detected using the Omni-ECL ultra-sensitive chemiluminescence detection kit (Epizyme Biomedical Technology, China) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (JP-K3300, Shanghai Jiapeng Technology).

Animals

Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats used in this study were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in the IVC-grade animal facility at Southwest Medical University with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest Medical University (Approval No.: 20230625-002). Before experimentation, the animals underwent at least two weeks of acclimatization to ensure experimental stability and animal welfare. All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Rat cutaneous wound model and treatment

Male Sprague–Dawley rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 50 mg/kg, with the injection volume adjusted according to body weight. After confirming adequate anesthesia, the dorsal hair was shaved, and the skin was disinfected with povidone-iodine solution. Two symmetrical full-thickness skin defects (15 mm in diameter) were then created on the dorsum of each rat using a 15 mm biopsy punch. A total of 12 rats were used to establish the wound model and were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 3 per group): control group (local injection of 200 µL PBS), D3-Exos group (200 µg D3-Exos in 200 µL), D7-Exos group (200 µg D7-Exos in 200 µL), and PL group (200 µg PL in 200 µL). All treatments were administered via multi-point subcutaneous injections around the wound margin. Sterile dressings were changed every 3 days postoperatively, and the rats’ activity status and dressing condition were monitored twice daily. A second local injection was performed on day 7 post-injury. Wound images were captured on days 0, 3, 7, and 14 to evaluate the healing process. All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and institutional regulations for animal care and use. On day 14, rats were euthanized by an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), and wound tissues together with surrounding skin were harvested for subsequent analyses.

Wound closure rate

Photographs of the wounds were taken on days 0, 3, 7, and 14 post-surgery to evaluate the wound healing process. The wound area was measured using ImageJ software, and the wound closure rate was calculated using the following formula: Wound closure rate (%) = [(initial wound area − current wound area)/initial wound area] × 100%.

Histological analysis

On day 14 post-surgery, the rats were euthanized, and the wound areas, along with surrounding healthy skin tissues, were harvested for subsequent histological analysis. The collected tissues were trimmed to approximately 2 cm × 2 cm using a sterile scalpel, followed by dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding. Paraffin-embedded blocks were sectioned continuously at a thickness of 3 μm using a microtome. The tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to observe and evaluate histological changes in the wound tissues under a light microscope. To further assess collagen fiber deposition and maturation, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed to evaluate the structural organization of collagen within the wound area.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0, and GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 was used for data visualization. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Before statistical analysis, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between D3-Exos and D7-Exos derived from the same platelet donor, a paired t-test was applied. In in vitro experiments, comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). When the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met, Tukey’s post hoc test was used; otherwise, Dunnett’s T3 test was applied for multiple comparisons. In in vivo experiments, individual animals were treated as the statistical unit (n = 3 animals per group), with two wounds per animal considered as nested repeated measurements. Between-group comparisons were performed using nested one-way ANOVA, in which treatment was set as a fixed effect and individual animals as a random effect to account for within-animal correlations. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of exosomes

TEM revealed that exosomes derived from apheresis platelets stored for 3 days (D3-Exos) and 7 days (D7-Exos) exhibited typical cup-shaped, double-membrane vesicular morphology with well-defined membrane structures (Fig. 1A and B). NTA showed that the mean particle sizes of D3-Exos and D7-Exos were 112.2 nm and 105.3 nm, respectively, with no significant difference in particle size between the two groups (Fig. 1C). However, the concentration of D7-Exos was approximately 1.50 ± 0.27 times higher than that of D3-Exos. Western blot analysis (Fig. 1D) demonstrated high expression of exosome-specific markers TSG101, CD63, and CD9 in both D3-Exos and D7-Exos, while the negative marker Calnexin was only detected in the PL group. These findings confirm that the vesicles isolated in this study were exosomes.

AP-Exos are enriched in growth factors

As shown in Fig. 2, ELISA results revealed that all examined growth factors were more abundantly expressed in AP-Exos compared to PL, with PDGF-BB and TGF-β1 exhibiting significantly higher levels (P < 0.05). Specifically, the expression levels of TGF-β1 in D3-Exos and D7-Exos were 8.69-fold and 15.38-fold higher than in PL, respectively, while PDGF-BB levels were 2.54-fold and 4.42-fold higher. Furthermore, D7-Exos contained significantly higher concentrations of growth factors than D3-Exos (P < 0.05), with TGF-β1 and PDGF-BB levels increased by 1.70-fold and 1.19-fold, respectively.

Uptake of AP-Exos by HaCaT, HSF, and HUVEC

To investigate the cellular uptake of AP-Exos, exosomes were labeled with PKH67 and co-incubated with HaCaT, HSF, and HUVEC. Fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3) revealed that PKH67-labeled AP-Exos appeared as green punctate fluorescence, while cell nuclei were stained blue. The green fluorescence was predominantly localized around the perinuclear region, indicating that AP-Exos were successfully internalized by HaCaT, HSF, and HUVEC cells. These results suggest that AP-Exos may exert biological effects within recipient cells, potentially regulating key cellular functions such as proliferation, migration, and secretion.

AP-Exos promote HaCaT proliferation and migration

To evaluate whether AP-Exos can promote HaCaT proliferation and migration, we performed CCK-8 and scratch assays. The CCK-8 results (Fig. 4C) showed that both PL and AP-Exos groups (D3-Exos and D7-Exos) significantly enhanced HaCaT proliferation. The PL group demonstrated a significant proliferative effect after 48 h (P < 0.05), whereas the AP-Exos groups exhibited a noticeable promotion as early as 24 h (P < 0.05). At all time points, the proliferation effect of the D7-Exos group was superior to that of the PL group (P < 0.05) and also surpassed the D3-Exos group at 48 and 72 h (P < 0.05). At 72 h, the D3-Exos group also showed a higher proliferation rate than the PL group (P < 0.05), indicating that AP-Exos, particularly D7-Exos, have a more significant proliferative effect. The scratch assay results (Fig. 4A and B) showed that at 12 h, the AP-Exos group significantly promoted cell migration (P < 0.05). By 24 h, the wound healing rates in the PL and AP-Exos groups were all higher than those in the control group (P < 0.05), with the D7-Exos group exhibiting the most pronounced migration-promoting effect (P < 0.05). This effect was significantly better than both the D3-Exos and PL groups, between which no significant difference was observed (P > 0.05).

AP-Exos promote proliferation and migration of HSF and enhance their collagen secretion

To evaluate the biological effects of AP-Exos on HSF, we assessed their impact on cell proliferation, migration, and collagen secretion. CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 5C) demonstrated that both PL and AP-Exos significantly promoted HSF proliferation. The PL group exhibited a marked proliferative effect after 48 h (P < 0.05), whereas the AP-Exos groups showed significant promotion as early as 24 h (P < 0.05). At all time points, the D7-Exos group outperformed the PL group (P < 0.05), and at 48 and 72 h, the D3-Exos group also showed significantly greater proliferation than PL (P < 0.05). However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the D7-Exos and D3-Exos groups at any time point (P > 0.05). Scratch wound assays (Fig. 5A and B) revealed that after 12 h of incubation, the wound closure rates in all three treatment groups were significantly higher than in the control group (P < 0.05). Among them, the D7-Exos group showed significantly greater migration compared to both the D3-Exos and PL groups (P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was found between the D3-Exos and PL groups (P > 0.05). This trend persisted at 24 h, with the D7-Exos group exhibiting the strongest migration-promoting effect (P < 0.05) and the D3-Exos group showing significant superiority over the PL group (P < 0.05). ELISA analysis (Figs. 5D) showed that all three treatment groups significantly upregulated Col I secretion by HSF (Control: 1446 ± 154.7 pg/mL; D3-Exos: 3898 ± 187.5 pg/mL; D7-Exos: 6325 ± 557.6 pg/mL; PL: 2679 ± 169.2 pg/mL; P < 0.05). AP-Exos groups exhibited higher Col I levels than the PL group (P < 0.05), and the D7-Exos group showed significantly greater Col I secretion compared to the D3-Exos group (P < 0.05). Regarding Col III secretion (Fig. 5E), AP-Exos and PL groups all significantly exceeded the control group (P < 0.05), though no statistically significant differences were observed among these three groups (P > 0.05).

AP-Exos promote HUVEC proliferation, angiogenesis, and enhance VEGF secretion

To evaluate the biological effects of AP-Exos on HUVEC, their impacts on cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and VEGF secretion were assessed. The CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 6C) demonstrated that AP-Exos and PL significantly promoted HUVEC proliferation. The PL group showed a significant proliferative effect after 48 h of incubation (P < 0.05), whereas the AP-Exos groups exhibited a notable effect as early as 24 h (P < 0.05). At 48 and 72 h, proliferation levels in the AP-Exos groups were significantly higher than those in the PL group (P < 0.05), with the D7-Exos group showing superior effects compared to the D3-Exos group at 48 h (P < 0.05). The angiogenesis assay results (Fig. 6A and B) indicated that after 6 h of Matrigel incubation, tube length was significantly increased in the AP-Exos and PL groups (P < 0.05). Among these, the AP-Exos groups exhibited greater angiogenic capacity than the PL group (P < 0.05), with the D7-Exos group showing the most pronounced effect. ELISA results (Fig. 6D) revealed that both D3-Exos (168.3 ± 14.92 pg/mL) and D7-Exos (208.0 ± 11.67 pg/mL) significantly promoted VEGF secretion by HUVEC (P < 0.05), whereas the PL group (106.9 ± 13.27 pg/mL) showed no significant difference compared to the control group (81.47 ± 15.90 pg/mL) (P > 0.05). Furthermore, VEGF secretion in the AP-Exos groups was significantly higher than in the PL group, with the D7-Exos group demonstrating the most significant enhancement (P < 0.05).

AP-Exos promote VEGF secretion in HUVEC via the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway

As shown in Figs. 7A–C, the expression levels of phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt) and VEGF were significantly elevated in both the AP-Exos and PL groups compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Notably, the D7-Exos group exhibited higher levels of p-Akt and VEGF expression than both the D3-Exos and PL groups (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed between the D3-Exos and PL groups (P > 0.05). To further verify whether D7-Exos promote VEGF secretion through the PI3K/Akt pathway, the PI3K/Akt inhibitor LY294002 was employed, and four experimental groups were established: (1) Control group; (2) LY294002 group (10 µM); (3) D7-Exos group (50 µg/mL); and (4) D7-Exos + LY294002 group. As shown in Figs. 7D–F, LY294002 effectively inhibited the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, while D7-Exos partially reversed this inhibitory effect. These findings suggest that D7-Exos may promote VEGF secretion in HUVECs at least partly through PI3K/Akt activation, although confirmation with genetic approaches and identification of the responsible exosomal cargo are needed.

AP-Exos promote wound healing in rats

Wound images were captured on postoperative days 0, 3, 7, and 14, and wound closure rates were calculated to evaluate the healing process. As shown in Fig. 8A and B, wound closure rates were reanalyzed using nested one-way ANOVA, with individual animals treated as the primary biological replicate and wound-level measurements treated as nested observations. Overall, the trends among treatment groups remained consistent after reanalysis. Except on day 7, no significant difference was observed between the PL group and the control group (P > 0.05), whereas all treatment groups exhibited significantly higher wound closure rates than the control group at the remaining time points (P < 0.05). On day 14, both the D3-Exos and D7-Exos groups showed significantly improved wound closure compared with the control group (D3-Exos vs. Control: MD = − 8.530, 95% CI: −11.96 to − 5.104; D7-Exos vs. Control: MD = − 9.071, 95% CI: −12.50 to − 5.645), while no significant difference was observed between the two exosome-treated groups (MD = − 0.5407, 95% CI: −3.967 to 2.886, P > 0.05). These findings indicate that AP-Exos exerted a stronger wound-healing effect than PL, with D7-Exos showing a more pronounced promotive trend during the early stage of wound repair. On day 14, wound tissues were collected for H&E staining to evaluate the residual epithelial gap. As shown in Fig. 8C and D, nested one-way ANOVA was similarly applied for statistical analysis. Compared with the control group (0.66 ± 0.11 cm), the unhealed wound length was significantly reduced in the D3-Exos group (0.35 ± 0.08 cm), D7-Exos group (0.17 ± 0.10 cm), and PL group (0.44 ± 0.07 cm) (P < 0.05). Moreover, the D7-Exos group exhibited a significantly shorter unhealed wound length than the PL group (MD = − 0.2680, 95% CI: −0.4912 to − 0.04482, P < 0.05), suggesting a superior pro-repair effect. Masson’s trichrome staining was performed to evaluate collagen deposition (Fig. 9). In the control group, collagen fibers appeared sparse and disorganized, whereas all treatment groups exhibited increased collagen deposition and improved tissue architecture. Notably, the D7-Exos group showed denser and more regularly arranged collagen fibers, accompanied by a greater number of regenerated skin appendages, indicating a higher degree of tissue remodeling. Collectively, these results demonstrate that AP-Exos significantly promote wound healing in rats, with D7-Exos exhibiting superior pro-repair potential.

Discussion

Growth factors such as TGF-β1, PDGF-BB, and VEGF play critical regulatory roles in chronic wound healing. Among them, PDGF-BB has been demonstrated in multiple randomized controlled trials to significantly promote wound healing and has been approved for clinical use19. In contrast, although TGF-β1 and VEGF have shown pro-regenerative potential in basic research, animal studies, and early clinical investigations, high-quality randomized controlled evidence remains limited20,21. Nevertheless, due to the complex pathological microenvironment of chronic wounds, the application of a single growth factor often fails to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. In comparison, platelet-rich plasma (PRP), which contains a wide range of bioactive factors, has shown promising potential in skin repair22. A systematic review including 19 clinical studies further supported the efficacy of PRP in promoting diabetic wound healing23. However, its therapeutic effects may be influenced by donor variability and leukocyte-mediated inflammatory responses, resulting in inconsistent outcomes24–26. In recent years, exosomes have attracted increasing attention due to their potential in tissue regeneration27. Compared with exosomes derived from other sources, particularly stem cells, platelet-derived exosomes (P-Exos) offer several distinct advantages. Their membranes are enriched with integrin proteins, conferring enhanced tissue-targeting capability28. In addition, P-Exos can be directly isolated from platelet concentrates, thereby avoiding potential heterologous contamination associated with cell culture, simplifying production processes, and reducing regulatory burden26. Emerging clinical evidence also suggests that P-Exos are safe and effective in the treatment of chronic wounds.

With the growing clinical demand for platelet products, the efficient utilization of platelets has become an increasingly pressing issue29. Due to the risk of microbial contamination, the shelf life of platelets is limited to only 5 days, after which they are discarded, resulting in the wastage of approximately 10–20% of platelet resources30. To enhance utilization efficiency, several alternative strategies have been proposed, including: (1) cryopreservation of near-expiry platelets31; (2) preparation of growth factor extracts30; (3) activation or lysis of platelets via freeze–thaw cycles32; and (4) extraction of platelet-derived exosomes (P-Exos) as functional substitutes16. Studies have shown that with prolonged storage, platelets become increasingly activated and continuously release P-Exos32,33. P-Exos play a pivotal role in modulating platelet function, suggesting that expired platelets could serve as a valuable source of high-quality exosomes34,35. Efficient extraction and utilization of these exosomes could not only harness their biological potential but also effectively alleviate the problem of platelet resource wastage.

In this study, exosomes (AP-Exos) were successfully isolated from APs stored for 3 days (D3) and 7 days (D7). TEM revealed that the isolated exosomes exhibited a typical cup-shaped morphology and lipid bilayer vesicular structure. NTA showed that the average diameters of D3-Exos and D7-Exos were 112.2 ± 62.4 nm and 105.3 ± 57.2 nm, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). However, the particle concentration of D7-Exos (26.07 ± 3.66 × 10¹⁰ particles/mL) was significantly higher than that of D3-Exos (17.98 ± 4.98 × 10¹⁰ particles/mL), approximately 1.50 ± 0.27 times greater (P < 0.05), indicating continuous exosome release during platelet storage. According to the MISEV2023 guidelines, the presence of classical exosomal markers—CD9, CD63, and TSG101—was confirmed by Western blotting, verifying the successful isolation of exosomes. ELISA further demonstrated that AP-Exos were enriched in key growth factors, including TGF-β1, PDGF-BB, and VEGF. Notably, the levels of TGF-β1 and PDGF-BB in both D3-Exos and D7-Exos were significantly higher than those in the PL group (P < 0.05), whereas VEGF levels showed no significant difference.

Wound healing is a highly coordinated process encompassing four overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. This process relies on the synergistic interactions among various cell types, including HaCaT, HSF, and HUVEC, as well as diverse bioactive factors36,37. In the present study, AP-Exos were efficiently internalized by these cells and exhibited significant biological effects. Functional assays demonstrated that both AP-Exos and PL promoted the proliferation of HaCaT, HSF, and HUVEC; however, AP-Exos exerted a stronger overall effect, with D7-Exos outperforming D3-Exos and PL. Scratch wound assays revealed that AP-Exos markedly enhanced the migratory abilities of HaCaT and HSF, with D7-Exos showing the most pronounced effect. Furthermore, tube formation assays indicated that AP-Exos significantly increased the angiogenic capacity of HUVEC, with D7-Exos exhibiting superior efficacy. Additionally, D7-Exos notably upregulated the secretion of Col I and Col III by HSF, as well as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secretion by HUVEC. Mechanistic studies revealed that AP-Exos could partially reverse the inhibitory effect of LY294002 and activate the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, thereby promoting VEGF secretion by HUVEC. Collectively, D7-Exos demonstrated superior potential in enhancing cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, underscoring their promising application as a regenerative medicine strategy.

The animal experiment results demonstrated that both AP-Exos and PL significantly accelerated wound closure, with the D7-Exos group exhibiting a markedly higher wound healing rate during the early stages compared to other groups. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed that AP-Exos and PL promoted re-epithelialization of rat skin, with AP-Exos—particularly the D7-Exos group—showing more pronounced reparative effects. Masson’s trichrome staining showed sparse and loose collagen deposition with no significant improvement in the control group. In contrast, wounds treated with D3-Exos or PL alone exhibited denser collagen deposition and more organized fiber arrangement relative to controls, although skin appendages remained scarce. Notably, the D7-Exos group displayed thick, dense collagen fibers with a more orderly and oriented arrangement, accompanied by a greater presence of skin appendages, suggesting more advanced tissue remodeling, though longer-term follow-up would be needed to confirm functional maturity of the regenerated tissue.

To our knowledge, this study represents an early exploration of expired platelet-derived exosomes in skin wound repair, systematically evaluating their effects on wound healing–related cells both in vitro and in vivo, as well as their therapeutic potential in promoting wound healing in a rat model. The findings suggest that this approach may represent a promising strategy for wound treatment and provide a theoretical basis for the reutilization of expired apheresis platelets and the optimization of blood resource allocation. However, this study still has several limitations. First, the in vivo experiments involved a relatively small sample size (n = 3). Although a nested model was used to account for data dependency, the robustness of the conclusions still requires validation in larger cohorts. Second, the dosing strategy was normalized based on protein concentration; given that D7-Exos exhibits a higher particle concentration, its superior effects may partially reflect dose-related contributions. Future studies should adopt particle number–based normalization to further distinguish these effects. At the mechanistic level, only preliminary validation using a single pharmacological inhibitor was performed in HUVECs. Future work should incorporate genetic approaches (e.g., siRNA) and extend validation to HaCaT and HSF cells to strengthen the specificity of the findings. In addition, ELISA results showed no significant difference in VEGF content between exosomes and PL; however, D7-Exos still significantly induced VEGF secretion in HUVECs, suggesting that its pro-angiogenic effects may be mediated by other bioactive components. Finally, the lack of a platelet lysate control derived from D3 samples also limits a comprehensive assessment of the effects of storage duration. In summary, this study provides preliminary evidence supporting the clinical translation potential of expired platelet-derived exosomes; however, their deeper molecular mechanisms and safety profile require further systematic investigation.
