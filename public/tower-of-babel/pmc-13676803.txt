Use of Intralipid Therapy and Therapeutic Plasma Exchange in the Management of Cholecalciferol Rodenticide Exposure in a Dog: A Case Report
Authors: Tanvi A. Kamat, Kara D. Altman, Emma Donnelly, Neus Elias Santo‐Domingo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo describe the presentation and outcome of a case of suspected cholecalciferol rodenticide intoxication managed with both intralipid emulsion (ILE) and therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE).Case SummaryA 3‐year‐old, neutered female, crossbreed dog presented for acute‐onset vomiting and lethargy after suspected exposure to cholecalciferol rodenticide. Severe hypercalcemia was observed without other identifiable causes found upon investigation. Standard therapy for hypercalcemia was initiated, but rebound hypercalcemia led to consideration of alternative management options. Although ILE therapy initially showed promise, rebound hypercalcemia persisted. Ultimately, TPE was performed as a novel therapy and successfully reduced serum concentrations of vitamin D metabolites. Normocalcemia was eventually maintained, though the dog developed endocarditis that progressed to congestive heart failure and death. Blood cultures revealed the growth of Serratia marcescens.New or Unique Information ProvidedTo the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report describing the use of both ILE and TPE in the management of cholecalciferol rodenticide intoxication in a dog. It is also the second reported case of S. marcescens causing endocarditis in a dog.

Introduction

Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) is an active ingredient in certain rodenticides that can lead to life‐threatening hypercalcemia in animals after ingestion [1]. After systemic absorption, cholecalciferol undergoes hepatic conversion to 25‐hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH]D) and further activation in the kidneys to 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25[OH]2D). This metabolite promotes calcium absorption and decreases excretion, leading to hypercalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and soft tissue mineralization [1]. Hypercalcemia can lead to polyuria, dehydration, and altered acid–base status, which can further complicate treatment [1]. Due to its lipophilic nature, cholecalciferol and its metabolites persist in adipose tissue. In dogs and people, 25[OH]D shows a biphasic half‐life, with a faster decline in the first 3 months and slower elimination thereafter [2, 3]. Conventional treatments for cholecalciferol toxicosis include intravenous fluid therapy, diuretics, corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, and calcitonin to enhance calciuresis, reduce intestinal absorption, and inhibit bone resorption [1]. However, some cases remain refractory to standard therapy, and prolonged hypercalcemia and tissue mineralization are meaningful clinical challenges [3, 4].

Intravenous lipid emulsion (ILE) therapy has been used in cases in which a lipophilic toxicant is known or suspected. The exact mechanism of action is not yet fully understood but, theoretically, administration of ILE therapy creates a lipid phase in the bloodstream, either sequestering lipophilic toxins and thereby lowering their concentration at target sites or facilitating active redistribution to organs responsible for metabolism [5, 6]. ILE therapy may also improve cardiac metabolism and function and has been demonstrated to be effective for treatment of some toxins with low lipophilicity [5, 6]. In veterinary patients, ILE therapy has shown promise in reducing circulating vitamin D metabolites [7], but efficacy in reversing established hypercalcemia remains uncertain. To the authors’ knowledge, ILE therapy has not been demonstrated as a treatment option in people with vitamin D intoxication.

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) has been infrequently reported in people as a treatment modality for severe vitamin D toxicosis, effectively lowering circulating 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)2D concentrations [8]. By directly removing vitamin D metabolites bound to plasma proteins, TPE provides a novel treatment approach for persistent hypercalcemia in cases unresponsive to conventional therapies [9].

This report presents the first documented case of a presumed canine cholecalciferol rodenticide intoxication successfully treated with TPE after conventional therapy and ILE failed, demonstrating the potential role of extracorporeal therapy in veterinary medicine for managing severe, refractory vitamin D toxicosis.

Case Summary

A 3‐year‐old neutered female Cocker Spaniel–Poodle mix weighing 6.3 kg presented with a 48‐h history of vomiting and lethargy. As the dog was a known scavenger, the presence of empty, unsealed cholecalciferol containers that had been placed in the garden 1 week earlier strongly suggested ingestion. On presentation, the dog was bright, ambulatory, normothermic, and hemodynamically stable.

Initial diagnostic tests revealed PCV of 54% and total plasma protein concentration of 78 g/L (7.8 g/dL). Venous blood gas analysis1 revealed mild respiratory acidosis (PvCO2 45.8 mm Hg, reference interval [RI]: 32.0–43.0), mild hypokalemia (3.8 mmol/L, RI: 3.9–4.9), severe ionized hypercalcemia (2.05 mmol/L, RI: 1.10–1.40) (Figure 1), mild hypochloremia (chloride 105 mmol/L, RI: 109–120), and mild hyperlactatemia (2.6 mmol/L, RI: <2.5). Serum biochemistry panel2 revealed mildly elevated BUN (12.3 mmol/L, RI: 2.5–9.6 [34.4 mg/dL, RI: 7–26.9]), mild hyperphosphatemia (2.48 mmol/L, RI: 0.81–2.20 [7.68 mg/dL, RI: 2.51–6.81]), and severe total hypercalcemia (>4.0 mmol/L, RI: 1.98–3.00 [>16.0 mg/dL, RI: 7.94–12.02]) but was otherwise unremarkable. Thoracic radiography; pleural, lung, and cardiac point‐of‐care ultrasound; and abdominal ultrasound were also largely unremarkable aside from irregular renal medullary echogenicity thought to either be individual variation or acute inflammation.

The dog was hospitalized for 7 days and received intravenous fluid therapy consisting of 0.9% NaCl73 and additional treatment with furosemide4 (between 0.1 and 0.2 mg/kg/h, IV infusion), dexamethasone5 (0.1 mg/kg/day, IV), and zoledronate6 (0.1 mg/kg, IV, single dose administered on the second day of hospitalization). On Day 3 of the dog's hospitalization, discoloration of the tongue was observed that developed into distal tongue ulceration and then resolved. An attempt to perform thromboelastography (TEG)7 was unsuccessful because of a machine error. The dog also developed self‐limiting hematochezia. Ionized calcium initially remained increased (fluctuating between 1.5 and 2.2 mmol/L) but trended downward and normalized by Day 6 (1.38 mmol/L). The dog was discharged home with prednisolone8 (0.8 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h) and furosemide9 (0.8 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h).

Three days after discharge, the dog developed lethargy and tenesmus. Severe ionized hypercalcemia (1.75 mmol/L) was found on repeat blood analysis. Therefore, the dog was readmitted to the hospital, and diuresis was resumed with 0.9% NaCl, while prednisolone (0.8 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h) and furosemide (0.8 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h) were continued. A second dose of zoledronate (0.15 mg/kg, IV) was administered 13 days after the first dose.

Normocalcemia was transient, and serial assessment revealed continued hypercalcemia (between 1.5 and 2.1 mmol/L) (Figure 1). Sixteen days after the dog's initial presentation, rebound hypercalcemia prompted the initiation of ILE therapy10 (1.5 mL/kg, IV, followed by 0.04 mL/kg/min infusion over 6 h). Normocalcemia was again temporary, and two further infusions of ILE (0.02 mL/kg/min infusion over 2 h, q 12 h) were administered along with a third dose of zoledronate (0.15 mg/kg, IV), 21 days after initial presentation. Calcium remained within normal limits for 48 h after ILE therapy (1.1–1.25 mmol/L). After 11 days of hospitalization, 21 days after initial presentation, the dog was discharged with oral medications.

At recheck the next day, the dog developed vomiting and abdominal pain. Ionized calcium was normal, but canine‐specific pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity (cPLi)11 concentration was moderately increased (413 ng/mL, RI: <200), consistent with active pancreatic inflammation. The dog was managed as an outpatient with buprenorphine12 (0.02 mg/kg, SC), maropitant13 (1 mg/kg, IV, followed by 2 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h), ondansetron14 (0.5 mg/kg, PO, q 8 h), and paracetamol15([acetaminophen] 15 mg/kg, PO, q 8 h) alongside ongoing oral furosemide and prednisolone therapy. Outpatient management was continued for 7 days with intermittent rechecks, and the dog regained its appetite and remained comfortable, with ionized calcium concentration fluctuating between 1.25 and 1.60 mmol/L.

Twenty‐eight days after initial presentation, the dog was readmitted for a third time due to the development of an acute kidney injury (AKI). Creatinine was 178 µmol/L (2.01 mg/dL; International Renal Interest Society AKI Grade II/V). Repeat abdominal ultrasound revealed bilateral kidney changes suggestive of mineralization and mild pyelectasia, consistent with calcification secondary to hypercalcemia. A urine sample was obtained via ultrasound‐guided cystocentesis and submitted externally. Urinalysis was unremarkable, and culture yielded no bacterial growth.

The dog was administered an intravenous buffered isotonic crystalloid solution16, as well as continued administration of oral prednisolone and furosemide. Creatinine values progressively normalized over 48 h, but there were continued fluctuations in blood ionized calcium concentration and recurrence of marked ionized hypercalcemia (1.92 mmol/L). Thirty days since the dog's initial presentation, TPE was performed (Figure 1). Prior to TPE, the dog's serum 1,25(OH)2D and total 25(OH)D concentrations were confirmed to be severely increased (543 pmol/L [RI: 164–523] and 1745 nmol/L [RI: 112–366], respectively).

A 5.5‐Fr × 13‐cm tri‐lumen central venous catheter was inserted through the jugular vein and remained in place for 7 days. Using a cell saver machine17, 1.5 plasma volumes (approximately 0.5 L) were exchanged with exogenous canine plasma in the form of 1 unit of fresh frozen plasma and 1 unit of frozen plasma. The dog required a continuous infusion of calcium gluconate18 (0.5–1.0 mL/kg/h) during the procedure to treat ionized hypocalcemia associated with extracorporeal citrate anticoagulation. After the procedure, 244 mL of the dog's washed RBCs were autotransfused. Hemodynamic stability was maintained throughout the procedure aside from one episode of mild hypotension at the end that resolved as the autotransfusion was administered. The dog's PCV after autotransfusion was 32%. After TPE and confirmed normalization of calcium concentration, the prednisolone dose was reduced (0.6 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h), and furosemide therapy was tapered and discontinued over 24 h. Post‐TPE serum 1,25(OH)2D had normalized (464 pmol/L), and total 25(OH)D had markedly decreased but remained above the RI (790 nmol/L).

Twenty‐four hours after TPE, the dog developed pigmenturia and abdominal pain. Repeat hematology and biochemistry panels from a reference laboratory revealed a regenerative anemia (hemoglobin 8.7 g/dL, RI: 12.3–18.3; reticulocytes 4.58%, RI: 0–2; nucleated RBCs 1.03 × 109/L, RI: 0), left‐shifted neutrophilia (21.36 × 109/L, RI: 2.9–13.6; bands 0.77 × 109/L, RI: 0–0.6), moderately increased BUN (16.7 mmol/L [46.8 mg/dL]), a mild increase in alanine aminotransferase enzyme activity (152 U/L, RI: 10–125), mild hyperbilirubinemia (24 µmol/L, RI: 0–15 [1.4 mg/dL, RI: 0–0.9]), and marked increase in cPLi (>2000 ng/mL, RI: <200). Hemoglobinuria secondary to mechanical damage of autotransfused RBCs was suspected along with acute pancreatitis. Maropitant (1 mg/kg, IV, q 24 h), ondansetron19 (0.5 mg/kg, IV, q 12 h), and methadone20 (0.2 mg/kg, IV, q 4 h) were added to the dog's treatment regimen along with continuous infusions of metoclopramide 21(2 mg/kg/day) and magnesium sulfate22 (1 mEq/kg/day) for symptomatic management of clinical signs, after which both pigmenturia and abdominal pain resolved.

Rebound hypercalcemia (1.54 mmol/L) was observed 3 days after TPE was performed. Therefore, an additional dose of ILE and a fourth dose of zoledronate (0.2 mg/kg, IV) were administered, and furosemide therapy was reinstituted. A further course of TPE was discussed; however, due to financial considerations, this was kept in reserve for use if marked hypercalcemia recurred (e.g., if >1.7 mmol/L). Over the next 3 days, normalization of calcium concentration was seen, and the dog was discharged home 9 days after readmission. Subsequent rechecks revealed continued fluctuations in serum ionized calcium and creatinine concentrations, but both eventually normalized without additional therapy. Furosemide was reduced and then discontinued.

One week after discharge and 45 days after initial presentation, the dog re‐presented with dyspnea and abdominal pain, normal ionized calcium (1.19 mmol/L), and no azotemia (creatinine 68 µmol/L [0.77 mg/dL]). Arterial blood gas analysis1, thoracic radiography, and pleural and lung point‐of‐care ultrasound were unremarkable. TEG7 revealed a mildly hypercoagulable state secondary to an increase in coagulation factor activity (CK‐R 2.6 min, RI: 4.6–9.1) but was otherwise unremarkable. Repeat TEG during hospitalization revealed normalization of values without specific intervention over the subsequent 48 h.

Echocardiography revealed hyperechoic foci in the myocardium consistent with mineralization. Abdominal ultrasound revealed evidence of mineralization in the muscular layer of the body and fundus of the stomach, as well as worsening of mineralization in the kidneys. Moderate pancreatic changes were also noted, most consistent with chronic inflammation or scarring/fibrosis. Urinalysis, obtained by cystocentesis, revealed an active sediment with growth (>100,000 CFU/mL) of Staphylococcus pseudintermedius, sensitive to all tested antimicrobials.

The dog was treated with an intravenous buffered isotonic crystalloid solution16 and potassium23 supplementation. The dog was also prescribed amoxicillin–clavulanate24 (20 mg/kg, IV, q 6 h), maropitant (1 mg/kg, IV, q 24 h), ondansetron (0.5 mg/kg, IV, q 12 h), paracetamol25 ([acetaminophen] 15 mg/kg, IV, q 8 h), and a continuous infusion of metoclopramide (2 mg/kg/day). While the dog was inappetent, dexamethasone was administered (0.09 mg/kg/day, IV). Analgesia was escalated to ketamine26 and fentanyl27 continuous infusions due to ongoing pain elicited on repeat abdominal palpation. A nasogastric tube28 was inserted to facilitate provision of enteral nutrition, although the dog's appetite returned soon after placement. The cause for the initial presentation of dyspnea was not identified. Analgesia was progressively de‐escalated, and blood parameters remained stable. After 2 days of hospitalization, the dog was discharged home with amoxicillin–clavulanic acid29 (21 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h), paracetamol (15 mg/kg, PO, q 8 h), prednisolone (0.6 mg/kg, PO, q 24 h), and ondansetron (0.7 mg/kg, PO, q 12 h). Two days later, normocalcemia was maintained, and the prednisolone dosage was reduced (0.4 mg/kg, PO, q 24 h).

Five days later, 57 days after initial presentation, the dog was re‐presented for inappetence, tachypnea, and intermittent left forelimb lameness. In addition, the dog was tachycardic with a new‐onset grade V/VI, left‐sided, pansystolic murmur. Ionized calcium and creatinine concentrations were within normal RIs (1.38 mmol/L and 121 µmol/L [1.37 mg/dL], respectively). Echocardiography revealed nodular lesions on the atrial surface of the parietal mitral valve leaflet and tricuspid valve parietal leaflet, with severe mitral and mild tricuspid insufficiency noted. These findings were deemed to be consistent with mitral (and possibly tricuspid) endocarditis. Pimobendan30 was added to the therapeutic regimen (0.25 mg/kg, IV, q 12 h). Serum troponin‐I was markedly increased (34.7 ng/mL, RI: 0–0.15). Blood culture samples were collected from both jugular veins and revealed growth of Serratia marcescens from both sites that was resistant to ampicillin, amoxicillin–clavulanate, and cephalexin. Urine culture yielded no bacterial growth. Pending culture results, the dog was prescribed marbofloxacin31 (4 mg/kg, IV, q 24 h) and clindamycin32 (11 mg/kg, IV, q 12 h).

Two days later, the dog developed persistent tachypnea and hypoxemia. Thoracic radiography revealed pulmonary edema. Furosemide therapy was reinstituted (2 mg/kg, IV), and bilateral nasal cannulas were placed to provide oxygen therapy. Unfortunately, within 12 h, the dog suffered cardiac arrest. CPR was performed with successful return of spontaneous circulation, but given the grave prognosis, the dog's owners elected for humane euthanasia. The dog was euthanized 60 days after initial presentation.

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first report of TPE used in a dog with presumed cholecalciferol rodenticide intoxication. The dose of ingested toxin was unknown, preventing accurate pharmacokinetic assessment; however, measurement of vitamin D metabolites immediately before and after TPE confirmed substantial reduction in 25(OH)D and normalization of 1,25(OH)2D. Earlier measurement during the initial workup may have clarified other treatment efficacy, especially ILE.

In this case, rodenticide ingestion was not witnessed; therefore, initial diagnostic efforts focused on ruling out alternative causes of hypercalcemia. Assessment of parathyroid hormone and parathyroid hormone‐related protein levels was attempted but was unsuccessful. Further imaging of the parathyroid glands was not performed. Empiric therapy for cholecalciferol toxicosis was initiated due to the strong suspicion of exposure. Decontamination was not attempted because of the delayed presentation, and hyperphosphatemia resolved without phosphate binders. Salmon calcitonin has been used in canine hypercalcemia [1] but was not available in this case.

Zoledronate was administered at the lower end of reported treatment ranges (0.1–0.2 mg/kg) [10] due to concern that successive treatments could have overlapping activity and result in a higher cumulative effect. As zoledronate is primarily stored and released from bone tissue and is hydrophilic [11], it was likely not affected by interim ILE therapy. ILE has been described as a successful treatment for canine and feline cholecalciferol intoxication, with a maximum of two doses previously reported, although vitamin D metabolites were not measured during treatment [4, 7].

Given the dog's ongoing severe hypercalcemia, TPE was performed. In human medicine, TPE has been shown to reduce circulating vitamin D metabolites and is thought to contribute to hypocalcemia, potentially in conjunction with citrate toxicity [9]. Two reported cases of vitamin D toxicosis in people demonstrated reductions in vitamin D metabolites after treatment with TPE, although both required ongoing treatment with either repeated TPE or diuretic therapy [8]. Hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis have also been used in people to reduce both serum vitamin D metabolite and calcium concentrations effectively [8, 12].

In this dog's case, TPE was chosen as it was readily available at our center, unlike hemodialysis, and was considered to carry fewer risks than peritoneal dialysis. TPE produced a marked reduction in serum vitamin D metabolite concentrations; however, rebound hypercalcemia remained a challenge, requiring ongoing medical management. There is limited information in both the veterinary and human literature on the management of rebound hypercalcemia in such scenarios. Given the suspected large ingestion of cholecalciferol rodenticide, it is likely that substantial stores of metabolites were present in hepatic and adipose tissues, leading to continued release of plasma metabolite concentrations after being reduced by ILE and TPE. Based on known vitamin D metabolite kinetics, repeat TPE every 1–4 days could have been considered to address rebound hypercalcemia [2, 3, 8]. If available, hemodialysis could have been considered as an adjunctive therapy, as it has been shown to reduce serum calcium concentration more effectively than TPE [8]. However, the dog in this case responded to ongoing medical management after TPE, and repeating this treatment was not financially possible for the owners.

This dog developed multiple complications over the course of its care, including tongue discoloration with ulceration and subsequent dyspnea, which prompted concern for thromboembolic events, a suspicion later reinforced by the hypercoagulable state found on TEG. Subsequent abdominal ultrasound examination, however, did not reveal further evidence of thromboembolic disease. Reports have linked hypercalcemia to thromboembolism in both human and canine patients [13, 14].

The dog also experienced AKI on two separate occasions, the etiology of which was likely multifactorial, including hypercalcemia. Furosemide has been associated with kidney injury, although most cases are grade I and do not appear to impact survival [15]. Specifically, continuous infusions of furosemide, as used in this dog, have not been conclusively demonstrated to increase the risk of developing AKI in veterinary literature [15]. Discontinuation of furosemide therapy could have been considered. Given the difficulty in controlling the dog's hypercalcemia, however, furosemide was continued, and the AKI resolved despite this. Zoledronate administration has also been rarely associated with AKI, with one study reporting an incidence of less than 0.1%, which was not associated with cumulative dose [10].

Human studies have demonstrated a higher incidence of AKI in patients treated with 0.9% NaCl compared with buffered isotonic crystalloids, which is thought to be due to chloride‐induced renal vasoconstriction, reduced renal perfusion, and activation of tubuloglomerular feedback mechanisms [16]. During the second occurrence of AKI, a balanced crystalloid solution was preferentially used for management as the dog was normocalcemic at the time. The presence of hemoglobinemia and hemoglobinuria after TPE, hypothesized to be secondary to mechanical damage to the autotransfused RBCs, is another potential cause of AKI.

Acute pancreatitis was another complication suspected on two separate occasions in this dog due to clinical signs and increases in cPLi. Accurately diagnosing acute pancreatitis can be challenging; however, it is widely accepted that the best diagnostic approach involves combining clinical signs with imaging and clinicopathologic findings. cPLi concentration is considered the most specific clinicopathologic indicator [17]. Hypercalcemia is a recognized risk factor for pancreatitis in people [18], although the pathophysiology remains unclear, and other treatment interventions may have contributed in the current case. ILE therapy has been associated with acute pancreatitis in people [5], and gastrointestinal signs have been reported in dogs after ILE administration [6]. Hypotension observed at the end of the dog's second TPE session potentially contributed to pancreatitis via ischemic injury [19]. Hypercalcemia may have predisposed to thromboembolic events, which could have compromised pancreatic perfusion [13].

Ultimately, the dog developed endocarditis that progressed to congestive heart failure and cardiopulmonary arrest. Although rare, hypercalcemia has been proposed as a potential risk factor for infective endocarditis in people, either through direct effects on cardiac valves or as a contributing factor alongside catheter‐related infections [20]. Although evidence in veterinary medicine is limited, central venous catheter placement in people has been associated with increased risk of endocarditis [21]. The urinary tract infection documented in this dog could have also been a contributing factor; however, the organism isolated from blood cultures at the time endocarditis was identified was different from the species previously isolated from the dog's urine. Furthermore, long‐term corticosteroid therapy has been demonstrated to predispose dogs to infection [22]. Blood cultures revealed growth of S. marcescens, a multidrug‐resistant organism and known cause of nosocomial infections in veterinary hospitals [23]. Although uncommon, S. marcescens has been implicated in infective endocarditis in people and was previously reported in a single canine case [24, 25].

In conclusion, this case report describes the use of ILE and TPE in the management of presumed cholecalciferol intoxication in a dog. The findings demonstrate that TPE can effectively reduce serum concentrations of vitamin D metabolites; however, due to redistribution, multiple TPE procedures may be necessary in patients with severe intoxication. Further prospective studies are needed to evaluate the role of extracorporeal therapy in managing severe or refractory vitamin D intoxications, and this case provides information supporting its use in veterinary medicine. Furthermore, to the authors’ knowledge, this is only the second veterinary case with S. marcescens reported as a cause of infective endocarditis.
