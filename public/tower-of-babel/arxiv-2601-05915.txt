The Lyapunov spectrum for Schneider map on p ​ ℤ p p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
Matias Alvarado and Nicolás Arévalo-Hurtado

Abstract
. We study the thermodynamic formalism associated with the Schneider map on the p-adic integers p ​ ℤ p p\mathbb{Z}_{p} . By introducing a geometric potential that captures the expansion of cylinder sets generated by the map, we define a Lyapunov exponent adapted to this non-Archimedean setting. We investigate the corresponding Lyapunov spectrum and show that it is real analytic on its natural domain. Moreover, we obtain an explicit closed formula for the spectrum. As a consequence, we recover and refine known results on the Hausdorff dimension of sets defined by a prescribed asymptotic arithmetic mean of the continued fraction digits. Finally, we relate the Lyapunov exponent to the exponential rate of convergence of rational approximations arising from truncations of the Schneider continued fraction expansion. This provides a p-adic analogue of classical results from Diophantine approximation and yielding precise dimension formulas for the associated level sets.

1.
Introduction

A central problem in multifractal analysis concerns the fractal decomposition of level sets of dynamically defined functions, typically quantified via their Hausdorff dimension. Let
f:M\to M
be a
C^{1}
map on a compact manifold
M
. The Lyapunov exponent of
f
at a point
x\in M
is defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lambda(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow{\infty}}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\|Df^{n}(x)\|,

whenever this limit exists. This quantity measures the exponential rate at which nearby orbits diverge. For each real number
\alpha
, we define

J_{\alpha}=\{x\in M:\lambda(x)=\alpha\}.

By the chain rule,
\lambda(x)
can be expressed as the pointwise limit of Birkhoff averages of
\log\|Df\|
. Consequently, for any ergodic measure, the Lyapunov exponent is constant almost everywhere. In particular, a single ergodic measure cannot capture the geometric properties of different level sets
J_{\alpha_{1}}
and
J_{\alpha_{2}}
. This motivates the study of these sets through their Hausdorff dimension. The Lyapunov spectrum of
\alpha
is defined by

\displaystyle L(\alpha)=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\left(J_{\alpha}\right),

whenever
J_{\alpha}\neq\emptyset
, where
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\cdot)
denotes the Hausdorff dimension.

For conformal expanding maps on smooth manifolds with Hölder continuous derivative, this problem dates back to the work of Weiss
[
19
]
. Using tools such as Markov partitions and Gibbs measures, Weiss proved that
L
is defined on a bounded interval
\left[\alpha_{min},\alpha_{max}\right]
, is real analytic, and satisfies

(1.2)

\displaystyle L(\alpha)=\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf\left\{P\left(-t\log\|Df\|\right)+t\alpha:t\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

where
P(-t\log\|Df\|)
denotes the topological pressure associated with the potential
-t\log\|Df\|
.
For interval maps, Pollicott and Weiss
[
14
]
, and later Kesseböhmer and Stratmann
[
11
]
, extended this result to the Gauss map
G
. This map has been extensively studied due to its intimate connection with the continued fraction expansion of real numbers.

The Gauss map
G:(0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is defined by

G(x)=\dfrac{1}{x}-\left\lfloor\dfrac{1}{x}\right\rfloor,

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the integer part.
Every irrational
x\in(0,1)
admits a unique continued fraction expansion of the form

(1.3)

\displaystyle x=\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\cdots}}}=[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},...],

where each
a_{i}
is a positive integer and
a_{i}=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{G^{i-1}x}\right\rfloor
.
The
n
-th rational approximation of
x
is given by

\displaystyle\dfrac{P_{n}}{Q_{n}}=\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\dfrac{1}{\ddots+\frac{1}{a_{n}}}}}}=[a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{n}].

The definition of the Lyapunov exponent extends naturally to the Gauss map. For
x\in(0,1]
we define
\lambda(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log|(G^{n})^{\prime}(x)|
, whenever this limit exists. Moreover,
\lambda(x)
can be written as (see
[
14
, Page 160]
)

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lambda(x)=-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left|x-\dfrac{P_{n}}{Q_{n}}\right|.

By equation (
1.4
),
\lambda(x)
quantifies the exponential rate at which the rational approximations
P_{n}/Q_{n}
converge to

x
, linking Lyapunov exponents to classical Diophantine approximation theory. For Lebesgue-almost every
x\in(0,1)
, one has
\lambda(x)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6\log 2}
since
\lambda(x)
is a pointwise limit of Birkhoff averages. The domain of the Lyapunov spectrum
L
for the Gauss map is
\left(2\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\right),\infty\right)
. since
(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
is a non-compact space, this leads to a different situation from the one considered by Weiss. Nevertheless,
L
is a real analytic function, and for each
\alpha
in this domain, there exists a unique ergodic measure supported on
J_{\alpha}
.

In this paper, we extend the study of Lyapunov spectra and their Diophantine implications to the Schneider map on
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Let
p
be a prime number and let
v_{p}
denote the
p
-adic valuation on
\mathbb{Q}
, defined by the property that for each
x\in\mathbb{Q}
, there exists a unique integer

v_{p}(x)
such that
x=p^{v_{p}(x)}\frac{m}{n}
with
(p,mn)=1
. The
p
-adic absolute value on
\mathbb{Q}
is defined by
|0|_{p}=0
, and
|x|_{p}=p^{-v_{p}(x)}
if
x\neq 0
. The field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of
p
-adic numbers is the completion of
\mathbb{Q}
with respect to this norm. The set of all elements
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that
|x|_{p}\leq 1
is denoted by
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and the subset
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
consists of those with
|x|_{p}<1
. Explicitly, any element in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
can be expressed as
\sum_{n\geq n_{0}}c_{n}p^{n}
, where
n_{0}
is an integer depending only on
x
and
c_{n}\in\{0,1,...,p-1\}
. An element
x
in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
can be written as
\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}p^{n}
. Thus,
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
consists of all elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
c_{0}=0.

Since
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is locally compact, there exists a Haar measure
\mu_{p}
normalized by
\mu_{p}(p\mathbb{Z}_{p})=1
. A detailed exposition on Haar measures can be found in
[
5
]
.

The Schneider map
T_{p}:p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is defined by
T_{p}(0)=0
, and for
x\neq 0

\displaystyle T_{p}(x)=\dfrac{p^{a_{1}(x)}}{x}-b_{1}(x),

where
a_{1}(x)=v_{p}(x)
and
b_{1}(x)\in\{1,2,...,p-1\}
is uniquely determined by the congruence
b_{1}(x)\equiv p^{a_{1}(x)}/x
(mod
p
). Outside a countable set, every element of
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
admits a continued fraction expansion analogous to (
1.3
). More precisely, for
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}T_{p}^{-k}(0)
and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one can write

(1.5)

\displaystyle x=\cfrac{p^{a_{1}(x)}}{b_{1}(x)+\cfrac{p^{a_{2}(x)}}{b_{2}(x)+\cfrac{p^{a_{3}(x)}}{\ddots+\cfrac{p^{a_{n}(x)}}{b_{n}(x)+T_{p}^{n}(x)}}}}.

where
a_{i}(x)=a_{1}(T^{i-1}_{p}x)
and
b_{i}(x)=b_{1}(T^{i-1}_{p}x)
. Note that a point
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
has a finite continued fraction expansion if and only if
T^{n}_{p}(x)=0
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
F
the set of points in
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with finite continued fraction expansion. By the preceding discussion,
F=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}T_{p}^{-k}(0)
. This situation is analogous to the classical Gauss map, where the points with finite continued fraction expansion are
\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=0}G^{-n}(0)=\mathbb{Q}
. In contrast, if
x
is a rational number with a infinite Schneider continued fraction expansion, then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{n}(x)=1
and
b_{n}(x)=p-1
for all
n\geq N
(see
[
7
, Theorem 1]
).
The Schneider map is ergodic with respect to the Haar measure
\mu_{p}
on
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(see
[
7
, Lemma 2]
) and its measure theoretic entropy is
\frac{p}{p-1}\log p
(see
[
6
]
).

From a fractal geometric perspective, Hu, Yu and Zhao (
[
8
]
) studied multifractal decomposition determined by the asymptotic arithmetic mean of the digits appearing in the continued fraction expansion. More recently, Song, Wu, Yu and Zeng
[
17
]
investigated the Hausdorff dimension of related level sets. We continue this line of research by studying the fractal decomposition induced by rational approximations associated with the Schneider map.

For each pair of integers
a\geq 1
and
1\leq b\leq p-1
, we define the cylinder set

I_{(a,b)}:=\{x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}:a_{1}(x)=a\text{ and $b_{1}(x)=b$}\},

and introduce the potential function

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\dfrac{1}{\left|I_{(a_{1}(x),b_{1}(x))}\right|_{p}}=p^{a_{1}(x)}.

As will be shown, this potential plays a role analogous to
\log\|Df\|
in the classical setting, in the sense that
\log\psi(x)
measures the local expansion rate of
T_{p}
.

For
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F
, we define the Lyapunov exponent by

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow{\infty}}\dfrac{1}{n}S_{n}\log\psi(x),

whenever this limit exists, where
S_{n}\log\psi=\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}\log\psi\circ T^{k}_{p}
denotes the
n
-th Birkhoff sum of
\log\psi
with respect to
T_{p}
. It follows that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow{\infty}}\log p\cdot\dfrac{a_{1}(x)+a_{2}(x)+\cdots+a_{n}(x)}{n},

so that, up to a multiplicative constant, the Lyapunov exponent coincides with the asymptotic arithmetic mean of the digits in the continued fraction expansion. The associated level sets are

J_{p}(\alpha)=\{x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F:\lambda_{p}(x)=\alpha\},

and the Lyapunov spectrum is defined by

L_{p}(\alpha)=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\,J_{p}(\alpha),

where
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}
now denotes the Hausdorff dimension with respect to the
p
-adic norm. In the case of the Gauss map, the Lyapunov spectrum is computed via the topological pressure of the geometric potential
-t\log|G^{\prime}|
, which allows one to estimate the diameters of cylinder sets using the Mean Value Theorem. However, since the Gauss map acts on a non-compact space, the thermodynamic formalism in this setting requires additional assumptions, such as the Big Images Property and Hölder continuity of the potential. Our main difficulty in developing a thermodynamic formalism for
T_{p}
stems from two facts. First, the set of points in
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with infinite continued fraction expansions is non-compact. Second, continuous functions on
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
do not, in general, satisfy a Mean Value Theorem. These considerations motivate our definition of the Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{p}
, which directly captures the diameter of cylinder sets and links it to Hausdorff dimension.

Our main theorems extends to the
p
-adic setting the results of Weiss
[
19
]
and Pollicott-Weiss
[
14
]
(see also
[
9
,
10
,
1
]
).

Theorem 1.1
.

The Lyapunov spectrum
L_{p}
is real analytic on
[\log(p),\infty)
. For each
\alpha\geq\log p

(1.7)

\displaystyle L_{p}(\alpha)=\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf\left\{P(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha:t>0\right\},

where
P(-t\log\psi)
is the topological pressure of
-t\log\psi
with respect to
T_{p}
. The infimum is attained at a unique
t_{\alpha}>0
such that
\frac{d}{dt}P(-t\log\psi)|_{t=t_{\alpha}}=-\alpha
. Moreover, there exists a unique equilibrium state
\mu_{t_{\alpha}}
for
-t_{\alpha}\log\psi
such that
\mu_{t_{\alpha}}(J_{p}(\alpha))=1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For each
\alpha\geq\log p,

(1.8)

\displaystyle L_{p}(\alpha)=\dfrac{\log(p-1)+\log(\alpha-\log p)-\log\log p+\alpha\log_{p}\alpha-\alpha\log_{p}(\alpha-\log p)}{\alpha}.

This explicit formula for the Lyapunov spectrum is remarkable. Such closed expressions are seldom available outside the context of affine iterated function systems on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We conclude by highlighting some arithmetic consequences.
Setting
\hat{\alpha}=\alpha/\log p
we recover and refine previous results concerning digit means (see
[
8
, Theorem 2.2]
) : the set of points
x
such that the digits
\{a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),...\}
have asymptotic mean
\hat{\alpha}
has Hausdorff dimension

\dfrac{\widehat{\alpha}\log\widehat{\alpha}-(\widehat{\alpha}-1)\log(\widehat{\alpha}-1)+\log(p-1)}{\widehat{\alpha}\log p}.

We also establish a precise relationship between the Lyapunov exponent and the rate of convergence of the rational approximations arising from Schneider continued fraction, obtaining a
p
-adic analogue of classical Diophantine results for the Gauss map. This is

\lambda_{p}(x)=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}\right|_{p},

where

\displaystyle\dfrac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}=\cfrac{p^{a_{1}(x)}}{b_{1}(x)+\cfrac{p^{a_{2}(x)}}{b_{2}(x)+\cfrac{p^{a_{3}(x)}}{b_{3}(x)+\cdots\dfrac{p^{a_{n}(x)}}{b_{n}(x)}}}}.

As a consequence, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.3
.

For each
\alpha\geq\log p
, the set

\displaystyle\left\{x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F:-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}\right|_{p}=\alpha\right\}

has Hausdorff dimension

\displaystyle\dfrac{\log(p-1)+\log(\alpha-\log p)-\log\log p+\alpha\log_{p}\alpha-\log_{p}(\alpha-\log p)}{\alpha}.

The proof of our main theorems combine several key ingredients.
First, we employ a compact approximation scheme by analyzing truncated subsystems where points have bounded digits.
Second, we apply the thermodynamic formalism to establish the existence and uniqueness of equilibrium states for the relevant family of geometric potentials.
Finally, we use Gibbs measures and multifractal analysis to derive dimension formulas via pointwise dimensions and Legendre transforms, and then extend the results to the full system.

Overview of the article

In Section 2, we collect preliminaries from symbolic dynamics, the Schneider map, and thermodynamic formalism.
In section 3, we study compact approximations of
(T_{p},p\mathbb{Z}_{p})
and derive the multifractal spectra for these subsystems. Section 4 is devoted to the thermodynamic formalism of the Schneider map, where we establish the main results of the article. In Section 5, we relate the geometric potential to rational approximations and prove Corollary
1.3
. Finally, in order to keep the presentation clear and focused, the technical lemmas are deferred to section 6.

2.
Preliminaries

We briefly recall the definition of the Hausdorff dimension, for further details, see
[
4
, Section 2.2]
. Let
Q
be a subset of
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and Let
s\geq 0
and
\delta>0
. We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(Q):=\inf\left\{\sum_{i}|U_{i}|_{p}^{s}:\begin{array}[]{c}\text{ $\{U_{i}\}$ is a countable cover of $Q$ such that for every $i$}\\
\text{ $|U_{i}|_{p}<\delta$.}\end{array}\right\},

where
|U_{i}|_{p}
denotes the diameter of
U_{i}
with respect to the
p
-adic metric. We then define
\mathcal{H}^{s}(Q)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(Q)
and the Hausdorff dimension of
Q
by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}(Q):=\inf\{s:\mathcal{H}^{s}(Q)=0\}.

Now we define the symbolic framework of associated with the map
T_{p}
. Let
E=\mathbb{N}\times\{1,...,p-1\}
and
\Sigma=E^{\mathbb{N}}
. We consider
(\Sigma,\sigma)
the full-shift on
\Sigma
, where
\sigma\left((a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\right)=(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})_{i=1}^{\infty}
. This is the standard full-shift on countable symbols. The topology on
\Sigma
is generated by the cylinder sets of the form
[(a,b)]=\{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}:a_{1}=a\text{ and $b_{1}=b$}\}
, where
(a,b)\in E
. For a finite sequence
\{(a_{0},b_{0}),...,(a_{1},b_{1})\}
, we define the cylinder

\displaystyle[(a_{1},b_{1}),...,(a_{n},b_{n})]=\bigcap^{n-1}_{k=0}\sigma^{-k}[(a_{k+1},b_{k+1})].

For each
(a,b)\in E
, let
I_{(a,b)}
denote the set of points
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
a_{1}(x)=a
and
b_{1}(x)=b
. Similarly,

\displaystyle I_{(a_{i},b_{i})^{n}_{i=1}}=\bigcap_{k=0}^{n-1}T_{p}^{-k}\left(I_{(a_{k+1},b_{k+1})}\right).

Observe that for any
x,y\in I_{(a,b)}
, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left|T_{p}(x)-T_{p}(y)\right|_{p}=\left|p^{a}\left(\dfrac{1}{x}-\dfrac{1}{y}\right)\right|_{p}=p^{-a}\dfrac{|y-x|_{p}}{|xy|_{p}}.

Since
|x|_{p}=|y|_{p}=p^{-a}
, it follows that
\left|T_{p}(x)-T_{p}(y)\right|_{p}=p^{a}|x-y|_{p}
. Therefore, the Schneider map is locally expanding, with expansion factor
p^{a}
on each cylinder
I_{(a,b)}
. Consequently, the countable partition
\{I_{(a,b)}\}_{(a,b)\in E}
generates the topology on
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F
. Define the map
\pi:\Sigma\to p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F
given by

\displaystyle(a_{i},b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\bigcap_{i=0}^{\infty}T_{p}^{-i}\left(I_{(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})}\right).

Since
\pi\left([a,b]\right)=I_{(a,b)}
and each
I_{(a,b)}
is both open and closed in the
p
-adic topology,
\pi
defines a homeomorphism between
\Sigma
and
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F
. In particular,
(\Sigma,\sigma)
and
(p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F,T_{p})
are topologically conjugated (
[
18
]
).
Henceforth, for real-valued functions
f\colon p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
g\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
S_{n}f(x)
and
S_{n}g(x)
the Birkhoff sums
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f\circ T_{p}^{k}(x)
and
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g\circ\sigma^{k}(x)
respectively.

A function
\rho:p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
Hölder
continuous with exponent
0<\alpha\leq 1
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x,y\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,

\displaystyle|\rho(x)-\rho(y)|\leq C|x-y|^{\alpha}_{p}.

For any
n\geq 1
, we define the variation of
\rho
on cylinders of length
n
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\rho):=\sup\{|\rho(x)-\rho(y)|:a_{i}(x)=a_{i}(y)\text{ and $b_{i}(x)=b_{i}(y)$ for $0\leq i\leq n-1$}\}.

If
\rho:p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F\to\mathbb{R}
is Hölder continuous,
the
topological pressure of
\rho
is defined by

(2.3)

\displaystyle P(\rho):=\sup\left\{h_{\mu}+\int\rho\,d\mu:\mu\in\mathcal{M}(p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F,T_{p}),-\int\rho\,d\mu<\infty\right\},

where
h_{\mu}
denotes the measure-theoretic entropy of
\mu
, and
\mathcal{M}(p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F,T_{p})
denotes the set of
T_{p}
-invariant probability measures. Any measure achieving the supremum is called an
equilibrium state
for
\rho
. If
\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{Var}_{k}(\rho)<\infty
, then
P(\rho)
can be computed using periodic points (
[
2
, Cor. 1]
)

(2.4)

\displaystyle P(\rho)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left(\sum_{T_{p}^{n}x=x}e^{-S_{n}\rho(x)}\right).

3.
Restricting the Schneider map to bounded valuations

Let us restrict the Schneider map to the set of points
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for which the digits
a_{i}(x)
are bounded by a fixed integer
n\geq 1
.
We denote this subset by
p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}
, and write
T_{p,n}
for the restriction of
T_{p}
to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}
. Let
E_{n}=\{1,...,n\}\times\{1,...,p-1\}
and
\Sigma_{n}=E_{n}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Analogously to
(T_{p},p\mathbb{Z}_{p})
, the subsystem
(T_{p,n},p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n})
is topologically conjugated to the full-shift
(\Sigma_{n},\sigma)
via the map

\displaystyle\pi_{n}:\Sigma_{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}\setminus F

\displaystyle(a_{i},b_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\bigcap_{i=0}^{\infty}T_{p,n}^{-i}\left(I_{(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})}\right).

For simplicity, we denote by
P_{n}
the topological pressure, whether computed on
\Sigma_{n}
or on
p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}\setminus F
. For Hölder continuous potentials,
P_{n}
can be computed using periodic points as in (
2.4
). Our proof of Theorem
1.1
follows the spirit of
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
1
, Theorem 5.1]
. To that end, we first establish an analogous result for the Lyapunov spectrum of
T_{p,n}
. In fact, we obtain a complete multifractal description of these dynamical systems, summarized in Theorem
3.2
below. Let
\nu
be any
T_{p,n}
-invariant measure. For
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}
, the
upper
and
lower pointwise dimensions of
\nu
at
x
are defined by

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{\nu}(x)=\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{\log\nu(B(x,r))}{\log r},\text{ and }\underline{d}_{\nu}(x)=\liminf_{r\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{\log\nu(B(x,r))}{\log r}.

If these two limits coincide, their common value is denoted by
d_{\nu}(x)
and is called the
pointwise dimension of
\nu
at
x
. It quantifies how the measure
\nu
distributes upon different scales (see
[
4
, Chapter 17]
and
[
13
]
). The measure
\nu
is said to be exact dimensional if
d_{\nu}(x)
exists and is constant
\nu
-almost everywhere on
p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}
. Exact dimensional measures describe the fine-scale geometry (see
[
13
]
) through the fractal decomposition given by the level sets

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}=\{x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}:d_{\nu}(x)=\alpha\}.

The associated dimension spectrum is defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle f_{\nu}(\alpha)=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(K_{\alpha}).

Let
K_{\infty}
denote the set of points where the pointwise dimension does not exist. Then

\displaystyle p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}=K_{\infty}\cup\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}K_{\alpha}.

Given a Hölder continuous function
\zeta
on
(\Sigma_{n},\sigma)
. A
\sigma
-invariant probability measure
\mu
on
\Sigma_{n}
is called a
Gibbs measure
for
\zeta
if there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
w\in E_{n}^{m}
, and all
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in[w]

\displaystyle C^{-1}\leq\dfrac{\mu\left([w]\right)}{\exp\left(S_{m}\zeta(x_{i})-mP_{n}(\zeta)\right)}\leq C.

Let
\mu_{\zeta}
be the unique equilibrium state for
\zeta
(see
[
15
]
for the existence and uniqueness of
\mu_{\zeta}
).

Now let
\rho:p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F\to\mathbb{R}
be Hölder continuous, and define
\zeta=\rho\circ\pi_{n}
. Set
\nu_{\rho}=(\pi_{n})_{*}\mu_{\zeta}
the pushforward measure of
\mu_{\zeta}
. Then
\zeta
is Hölder continuous on
\Sigma_{n}
,
\mu_{\zeta}
is a Gibbs measure for
\zeta
, and consequently
\nu_{\rho}
is an equilibrium state for
\rho
. Define the Hölder continuous function
\vartheta
on
\Sigma_{n}
by
\log\vartheta=\zeta-P_{n}(\zeta)
. By construction,
P_{n}(\log\vartheta)=0
, and

is also the equilibrium state for

. Moreover, the map

is differentiable and strictly convex for all
q\in\mathbb{R}

[

. It is a classical result in thermodynamic formalism that for each

there exists a unique

such that the potential

. Each potential in the one-parameter family

is Hölder continuous and admits a unique Gibbs measure

.

Theorem

shows that the potential function

plays the same role as

For each

For each

(3.2)

Proof of main theorems

We begin this section by developing the thermodynamic formalism associated with the Schneider map. Recall that
\log\psi
is locally constant on cylinders of length 1. Hence, from equation (
2.4
) we obtain

(4.1)

\displaystyle P\left(-t\log\psi\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left(\sum_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in E^{n}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}(p^{a_{k}})^{-t}\right)=\log\left(\dfrac{p-1}{p^{t}-1}\right).

By Sarig’s results on Countable Markov shifts (see
[
16
]
)
P_{n}(-t\log\psi)\to P(-t\log\psi)
as
n\to\infty
. Recall
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}P(t)=\infty
, then for each
\alpha>\log p
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large there exists a unique
t_{\alpha,n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
-\alpha=\frac{d}{dt}P_{n}(-t_{\alpha,n}\log\psi)
. Let
\nu_{\alpha,n}
denote the equilibrium state of
-t_{\alpha,n}\log\psi
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then

\displaystyle\alpha=\int_{p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}}\log\psi\ d\nu_{\alpha,n}=\int_{p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}\setminus B(0,\varepsilon)}\log\psi\ d\nu_{\alpha,n}+\int_{B(0,\varepsilon)}\log\psi\ d\nu_{\alpha,n}.

Define
M_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{\log\psi(x):x\in B(0,\varepsilon)\}
. Since
a_{1}(x)\leq n
for
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}
we have
M_{\varepsilon}\leq n\log(p).
Hence

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\int_{p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}\setminus B(0,\varepsilon)}\log\psi\ d\nu_{\alpha,n}+M_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{\alpha,n}(B(0,\varepsilon)),

which implies

\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha}{M_{\varepsilon}}\geq\nu_{\alpha,n}(B(0,\varepsilon)).

Therefore, for every
\delta>0
and
n
sufficiently large, there exists
\varepsilon(\delta)>0
such that

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha,n}\left(p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}\setminus B(0,\varepsilon)\right)=1-\nu_{\alpha,n}(B(0,\varepsilon))\geq 1-\dfrac{\alpha}{M_{\varepsilon}}\geq 1-\dfrac{\alpha}{n\log(p)}\geq\delta.

In other words,
\{\nu_{\alpha,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a tight sequence of probability measures. By Prohorov’s theorem, it admits a weak-* convergent subsequence. If
\nu_{\alpha}
is an accumulation point, then
\nu_{\alpha}(p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F)=1
. Indeed,
\{t_{\alpha,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
also admits a convergent subsequence, since
\{\frac{d}{dt}P_{n}(-t\log\psi)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a decreasing sequence of functions and
\frac{d}{dt}P_{n}(-t\log\psi)\geq\frac{d}{dt}P(-t\log\psi)
for all
n
.
If
t_{\alpha}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{t_{\alpha,n}\right\}
, then
\nu_{\alpha}
is an equilibrium state for the potential
-t_{\alpha}\log\psi
(see
[
9
, section 6]
).
Combining equation (
2.4
) with the thermodynamic formalism for countable Markov shifts (see
[
16
]
), we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
t\in\mathbb{R}

(4.2)

\displaystyle P(-t\log\psi)=\begin{cases}\log\left(\dfrac{p-1}{p^{t}-1}\right)&\text{if $t>0$}\\
\infty&\text{if $t\leq 0$},\end{cases}

and for each
t>0
there exists a unique equilibrium state
\nu_{t}
of
-t\log\psi
. If
\alpha=-\frac{d}{dt}P(-t\log\psi)|_{t=t_{\alpha}}
, then
\nu_{t_{\alpha}}
is a weak-* accumulation point of the sequence
\{\nu_{\alpha,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Remark 4.2
.

In this setting, the equilibrium state for
-\log\psi
( when

) coincides with the Haar measure. Indeed,
P(-\log\psi)=0
and
\mu_{p}
attains the supremum in equation (

(4.3)

\displaystyle h_{\mu_{p}}-\int\log\psi\ d\mu_{p}=\frac{p}{p-1}\log p-\frac{p}{p-1}\log p=0,

where

(

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Theorem
3.4
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in[\log p,n\log p]

(4.4)

\displaystyle L_{p,n}(\alpha)=\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf\{P_{n}(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha\}.

Since
p\mathbb{Z}_{p,n}\setminus F\subset p\mathbb{Z}_{p_{,}n+1}\setminus F
for every
n\geq 1
, the sequence
\{L_{p,n}(\alpha)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotone increasing and bounded above by the Hausdorff dimension of
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, which equals one. From equation (
4.4
), for each
\alpha\geq\log p

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{P_{n}(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha\}\leq L_{p}(\alpha),

since
J_{p,n}(\alpha)\subset J_{p}(\alpha)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To show that the Legendre transform of
P
is bounded above by
L_{p}(\alpha)
, we employ the notion of infimally convergence. We say that the sequence
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

converges infimally
to
P
if, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 0}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\{P_{n}(-s\log\psi):|t-s|<\beta\}=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\inf\{P_{n}(-s\log\psi):|t-s|<\beta\}=P(-t\log\psi).

Recall that the map
t\mapsto P_{n}(-t\log\psi)
is non-increasing. Hence, for any
\beta>0
,

\inf\{P_{n}(-s\log\psi):|t-s|<\beta\}=P(t+\beta).

On the other hand, from
[
16
, Theorem 2]
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P_{n}(-t\log\psi)=P(-t\log\psi).

We obtain
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}
and
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}
in equation (
4.5
) coincide for every
\beta>0
. Therefore,
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges infimally to
P
.

By Wijsman’s work on infimally convergence (see
[
20
, Theorem 6.2]
), infimmally convergence of
\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to
P
is equivalent to infimmaly convergence of their Legendre transforms. In particular, the sequence of functions
\{\frac{1}{\alpha}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{P_{n}(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha\}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge infimally to
\frac{1}{\alpha}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{P(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha\}
as functions on
\alpha
. Moreover, the function obtained by pointwise convergence dominates that arising from infimally convergence (see
[
20
, section 5]
). This implies that

\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{P(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha\}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{P_{n}(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha\}.

Together with inequality (
4.5
) we get

(4.8)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{P(-t\log\psi)+t\alpha\}\leq L_{p}(\alpha).

Now we prove the reverse inequality. Fix
t>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Since the function
t\mapsto P(-t\log\psi)
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
, by definition of topological pressure we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\varepsilon}:=P(-(t+\varepsilon)\log\psi)-P(-t\log\psi)=P(-(t+\varepsilon)\log\psi)-P(-t\log\psi)<0.

Equation (
2.4
) implies there exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq M

\displaystyle\sum_{x:T^{k}_{p}x=x}e^{-(t+\varepsilon)S_{k}\log\psi-kP(-t\log\psi)}<e^{\frac{P_{\varepsilon}k}{2}}<1.

Let
\delta>0
such that
\frac{P_{\varepsilon}}{2}-\delta\frac{P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}<0
. On the other hand, for every
y\in J_{p}(\alpha)
there exists
N_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq N_{y}

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{S_{k}\log\psi}{k}-\alpha\right|<\delta.

Using this fact, we construct for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
a countable cover of cylinder sets with diameter at most
p^{-k}
. Notice that each interval
I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}
satisfies

\displaystyle N[(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}]:=\min\{N_{y}:y\in J_{p}(\alpha)\cap I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}\}<\infty.

We define

\displaystyle\overline{A_{k}}:=\{I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{k}}:N[(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}]\leq k\},

and for every
n>k
we set

\displaystyle A_{n}:=\{I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}:N[(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}]=n\}.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\overline{A}_{k}\cup\bigcup_{n>k}A_{n}
. The set
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is a countable cover of
J_{p}(\alpha)
since each
A_{n}
is a countable collection of cylinders. On the other hand, recall that
T_{p}
is distance expansive, then for any cylinder
I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
we have that
|I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}|_{p}\leq p^{-n}
.

Recall that
|I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}|_{p}=e^{-S_{n}\log\psi(y)}
for all
y\in I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}
. Thus, following the definition of Hausdorff dimension, for
l=t+\varepsilon+\frac{P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}
we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{l}_{p^{-k}}(J_{p}(\alpha))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}}e^{-lS_{n}\log\psi(x_{(a_{i},b_{i})})},

where
x_{(a_{i},b_{i})}
is the periodic point of
I_{(a_{i},b_{i})^{n}_{i=1}}
with period
n
. Since
N[I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}]\leq n
,

\displaystyle-lS_{n}\log\psi(x_{(a_{i},b_{i})})

\displaystyle=-\left(t+\varepsilon+\dfrac{P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}\right)S_{n}\log\psi(x_{(a_{i},b_{i})})

\displaystyle\leq-(t+\varepsilon)S_{n}\log\psi(x_{(a_{i},b_{i})}))-n\frac{P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}(\delta-\alpha).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{l}_{p^{-k}}(J_{p}(\alpha))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}}e^{-(t+\varepsilon)S_{n}\log\psi(x_{(a_{i},b_{i})})-n\frac{P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}(\delta-\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}e^{-\frac{n\delta P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}}\sum_{I_{(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}}e^{-(t+\varepsilon)S_{n}\log\psi(x_{(a_{i},b_{i})}))+nP(-t\log\psi)}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}e^{-\frac{n\delta P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}}\sum_{x:T_{p}^{n}x=x}e^{-(t+\varepsilon)S_{n}\log\psi(x)+nP(-t\log\psi)}.

Thus, for all
k\geq M
we have that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{l}_{p^{-k}}(J_{p}(\alpha))

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}e^{-\frac{n\delta P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha}+\frac{P_{\varepsilon}n}{2}}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}e^{n(\frac{P_{\varepsilon}}{2}-\frac{\delta P(-t\log\psi)}{\alpha})}.

Since
\dfrac{P_{\varepsilon}}{2}-\frac{\delta P(-t\log(\psi))}{\alpha}<0
, we obtain a geometric series on the right-hand side of inequality (
4.9
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{l}(J_{p}(\alpha))=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{l}_{p^{-k}}(J_{p}(\alpha))=0.

This implies that
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(J_{p}(\alpha))=L_{p}(\alpha)\leq t+\varepsilon+\frac{P(-t\log(\psi))}{\alpha}
for all
t>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Thus,

\displaystyle L_{p}(\alpha)\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\inf\{P(-t\log(\psi))+t\alpha:t\in\mathbb{R}\},

and together with inequality (
4.8
) equality follows. Finally, from construction, it is straightforward to note that the domain of
L_{p}
is
[\log(p),\infty)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}[\log(p),n\log(p)]
. The fact that the infimum is attained in a unique
t_{\alpha}>0
such that
\frac{d}{dt}P(-t\log(\psi))|_{t=t_{\alpha}}=-\alpha
is a consequence of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

5.
Rational approximation for continued fraction expansions

We now turn to the rational approximations obtained from the truncations in Schneider’s continued fraction expansion. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F
, we consider the limit

(5.1)

\displaystyle-\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}\right|_{p},

whenever it exists. Define
\psi_{2}:p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(x)=p^{-a_{1}(x)}\left|x-\dfrac{p^{a_{1}(x)}}{b_{1}(x)}\right|^{-1}_{p}.

Next, we introduce the real-valued function
\varphi:p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\varphi(x)=p^{a_{2}(x)}\psi_{2}(T_{p}(x))/\psi_{2}(x)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The limit in equation (
5.1
) coincides with the pointwise limit of Birkhoff’s means for
\log\varphi
. Indeed,

\displaystyle-\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}\right|_{p}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}\log\varphi\left(T_{p}^{k}x\right)

Proof.

Let
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F
. By definition, the second rational approximation satisfies

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(T_{p}(x))

\displaystyle=p^{-a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}\left|\dfrac{p^{a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}}{b_{1}(T_{p}(x))+T_{p}^{2}(x)}-\dfrac{p^{a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}}{b_{1}(T_{p}(x))}\right|_{p}^{-1}

\displaystyle=p^{-a_{2}(x)}\left|\dfrac{p^{a_{2}(x)}}{b_{2}(x)+T_{p}^{2}(x)}-\dfrac{p^{a_{2}(x)}}{b_{2}(x)}\right|_{p}^{-1},

and by equation (
2.2
) we have
\psi_{2}(T_{p}(x))=p^{-a_{2}(x)-a_{1}(x)}\left|x-\frac{p_{2}(x)}{q_{2}(x)}\right|_{p}
. Hence

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\dfrac{\psi_{2}(T_{p}(x))}{\psi_{2}(x)}=\dfrac{\left|x-\dfrac{p_{2}(x)}{q_{2}(x)}\right|_{p}^{-1}}{\left|x-\dfrac{p_{1}(x)}{q_{1}(x)}\right|_{p}^{-1}}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}\log\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=-\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{k+1}(x)}{q_{k+1}(x)}\right|_{p}-\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{k}(x)}{q_{k}(x)}\right|_{p}

\displaystyle=-\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{n+1}(x)}{q_{n+1}(x)}\right|_{p}+\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{1}(x)}{q_{1}(x)}\right|_{p}.

Dividing by
n
and taking limits yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}\log\varphi\left(T_{p}^{k}x\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{n+1}(x)}{q_{n+1}(x)}\right|_{p}.

∎

Proposition 5.2
.

The potential
\log\varphi
is locally constant on cylinder sets of length 3. Moreover, for each
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F,

\log\varphi(x)=v_{p}\left(T^{2}_{p}(x)\right)\log p=a_{3}(x)\log(p).

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\psi_{2}(T_{p}(x))}{\psi_{2}(x)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{p^{-a_{1}\left(T_{p}(x)\right)}\left|T_{p}(x)-\dfrac{p^{a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}}{b_{1}(T_{p}(x))}\right|_{p}^{-1}}{p^{-a_{1}(x)}\left|x-\dfrac{p^{a_{1}(x)}}{b_{1}(x)}\right|_{p}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=p^{a_{1}(x)-a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}\dfrac{\left|\dfrac{b_{1}(T_{p}(x))T_{p}(x)-p^{a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}}{b_{1}(T_{p}(x))}\right|^{-1}_{p}}{\left|\dfrac{xb_{1}(x)-p^{a_{1}(x)}}{b_{1}(x)}\right|^{-1}_{p}}

\displaystyle=p^{a_{1}(x)-a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}\left|\dfrac{b_{1}(T_{p}(x))T_{p}(x)-p^{a_{1}(T_{p}(x))}}{xb_{1}(x)-p^{a_{1}(x)}}\right|_{p}^{-1}.

Using the definition of
T_{p}
, one finds
\left|\frac{p^{a_{1}(x)}}{b_{1}(x)+T_{p}(x)}b_{1}(x)-p^{a_{1}(x)}\right|_{p}=|T_{p}(x)|_{p}|x|_{p}
, so that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\psi_{2}(T_{p}(x))}{\psi_{2}(x)}

\displaystyle=p^{a_{1}(x)-a_{2}(x)}\left|\dfrac{T_{p}(x)T_{p}^{2}(x)}{xT_{p}(x)}\right|_{p}^{-1}

\displaystyle=p^{a_{1}(x)-a_{2}(x)}\cdot p^{v_{p}(T_{p}^{2}(x))-v_{p}(x)}

\displaystyle=p^{v_{p}(T_{p}^{2}(x))-v_{p}(T_{p}(x))}.

Therefore, if
x
and
y
belong to the same cylinder of length 3 (that is,
a_{i}(x)=a_{i}(y)
and
b_{i}(x)=b_{i}(y)
for
1\leq i\leq 3
), then

\displaystyle\left|\log\varphi(x)-\log\varphi(y)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\log\left(p^{a_{2}(x)}\dfrac{\psi_{2}(T_{p}(x))}{\psi_{2}(x)}\right)-\log\left(p^{a_{2}(y)}\dfrac{\psi_{2}(T_{p}(y))}{\psi_{2}(y)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=|a_{2}(x)+v_{p}(T_{p}^{2}(x))-v_{p}(T_{p}(x))-a_{2}(y)-v_{p}(T_{p}^{2}(y))+v_{p}(T_{p}(x))|

\displaystyle=|v_{p}(T_{p}^{2}(x))-v_{p}(T_{p}^{2}(y))|

\displaystyle=|a_{3}(x)-a_{3}(y)|=0.

So
\log\varphi
is locally constant on such cylinders, and
\log\varphi(x)=a_{3}(x)\log p
for all
x\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus F
.
∎

Let
x\in J_{p}(\alpha)
. Since
J_{p}(\alpha)
is a
T_{p}
-invariant set, we have
\alpha=\lambda_{p}(x)=\lambda_{p}(T_{p}^{2}(x))
. Then, by Lemma
5.1
and Proposition

,

\displaystyle\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\log p\cdot\dfrac{a_{1}(T_{p}^{2}(x))+\cdots+a_{n}(T_{p}^{2}(x))}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\log p\cdot\dfrac{a_{3}(x)+\cdots+a_{3}(T_{p}^{n-1}(x))}{n}.

Hence

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}\log\varphi(T_{p}^{k}(x))=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\left|x-\dfrac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}\right|_{p}.

Thus, the Lyapunov exponent of

coincides with the mean exponential rate at which
p
-adic rational approximations converge to

. Consequently, Theorem
1.2
implies Corollary
1.3
. Indeed, Theorem
3.4
yields an analogous statement for each subsystem

. This allows us to characterize, in terms of dimension theory, sets of

-adic integers in

according to the rate at which their rational approximations converge.
