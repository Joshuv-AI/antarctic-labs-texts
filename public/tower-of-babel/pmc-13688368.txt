Deep Learning-Based Classification for Grading of Respiratory Distress Syndrome on Neonatal Chest Radiographs
Authors: Seung-hak Lee, Sumin Jung, Eun Hee Lee, Ju Sun Heo, Byung Min Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of this study was to develop and validate a deep learning-based multi-class classification model for automated grading of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) severity on neonatal chest radiographs.MethodsA total of 23,210 radiographs, including normal and RDS cases, manually annotated by trained neonatologists, were divided into training, validation, and external test sets using patient-level splitting. Lung regions were segmented using UNet++, and RDS severity was classified into five ordered grades using a ResNet-50-based model.ResultsThe model achieved a quadratic weighted kappa of 0.696, with 85.7% of predictions within one grade and five-class accuracy of 0.575. AUROCs were 0.966 for detecting RDS and 0.866 for clinically significant RDS (grade ≥3). Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping demonstrated attention to lung regions with reduced aeration and granular opacities in severe RDS.ConclusionsThis model may provide objective and interpretable radiographic assessment of RDS severity and support more consistent radiographic evaluation in neonatal intensive care units.Keywords: Deep learning, Respiratory distress syndrome, Chest radiographs

Introduction

Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) is diagnosed and its severity assessed based on the infant's clinical condition, including laboratory findings, oxygen requirements, and characteristic lung aeration patterns on chest radiography or lung ultrasound in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). While lung ultrasound is increasingly used in Europe, its clinical adoption varies and it has not yet been incorporated into standardized diagnostic protocols [1, 2].

Chest radiography remains the standard bedside imaging modality for assessing neonatal lungs [3, 4]. Although it is not a functional imaging modality and its interpretation varies among clinicians, potentially limiting accurate assessment of disease progression [5–7], chest radiography remains widely used because of its accessibility and established role in evaluating lung pathology and confirming respiratory support devices [8, 9]. Typical radiographic findings of RDS include diffuse hypo-aeration, bilateral fine granular opacities, and peripherally extending air bronchograms. RDS severity is conventionally graded on a four-level scale based on these radiographic findings, reflecting progressive deterioration of lung aeration and clinical severity [3, 4].

To address these challenges, we developed a deep learning-based framework combining UNet++ for lung segmentation with a ResNet-50 classifier for automated multi-class classification of ordered RDS severity grades on neonatal chest radiographs. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was used to visualize image regions contributing to model predictions. We evaluated whether this framework could provide objective and interpretable radiographic assessment of RDS severity and support more consistent image interpretation in NICUs.

Methods

Dataset

A total of 26,082 portable supine chest radiographs (10,140 normal and 15,942 RDS) were retrospectively collected from the NICUs of 11 university hospitals in Korea between October 2022 and February 2023. All images were fully deidentified before analysis by removing DICOM metadata and patient identifiers. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions (IRB No. 2022AS0056, Korea University Ansan Hospital), and informed consent was waived because of the retrospective use of anonymized data.

RDS was diagnosed in preterm infants with early moderate-to-severe respiratory distress requiring respiratory support for >72 h, together with characteristic radiographic findings, including diffuse granular opacities, air bronchograms, or bilateral white-out appearance. Infants with concomitant pneumothorax or pulmonary hemorrhage were excluded. Radiographic severity was graded from 0 (normal) to 4 (near-complete white-out) according to standardized radiolucency criteria [3, 4].

Ground-truth labels were manually annotated by 22 trained neonatologists across the 11 hospitals. Final labels were adjudicated by a senior neonatologist at each institution and subsequently cross-validated by neonatologists from other hospitals, with only clearly erroneous interpretations corrected to improve inter-institutional consistency.

Preprocessing and Data Partitioning

Images were converted to RGB, resized to 320 × 320 pixels, normalized, and processed using lung masks generated by UNet++ to remove non-pulmonary regions. Of the 26,082 candidate radiographs, 2,872 were excluded because of unsuccessful lung segmentation (n = 1,909) or failure to meet geometric quality-control criteria (n = 963), leaving 23,210 radiographs for analysis. During training, medium-intensity augmentation was applied, including random cropping, horizontal flipping, brightness/contrast adjustment, geometric transformation, blur/noise, and random erasing.

Using a leave-site-out design, three hospitals were reserved as an external test set (4,733 radiographs from 3,969 infants). Data from the remaining eight hospitals were split at the patient level into training (75%; 13,738 radiographs from 5,662 infants) and validation (25%; 4,739 radiographs from 1,888 infants) sets, with no patient overlap between datasets. Baseline characteristics by RDS grade are summarized in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/neo/adsag006).

Network Architecture and Training

UNet++ was used for lung segmentation, and a ResNet-50 backbone pretrained on ImageNet was used to classify RDS severity into 5 ordered grades. Image features were concatenated with gestational age and birth weight before classification. The model was trained using categorical cross-entropy loss in PyTorch 2.5.1.

Explainability and Visualization

Model interpretability was assessed using Grad-CAM [10]. Activation maps generated from the last convolutional block of ResNet-50 were overlaid on chest radiographs using a semi-transparent jet colormap to qualitatively evaluate whether model attention corresponded to clinically relevant pulmonary regions.

Model Evaluation (Statistical Test)

Model performance was evaluated using class-wise precision, recall, F1 score, five-class accuracy, quadratic weighted kappa, mean absolute error, within-±1-grade accuracy, and AUROCs for detecting ≥ grade 1 and ≥ grade 3 RDS. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were estimated using patient-level bootstrap resampling (2,000 replicates). All analyses were based on individual sample predictions, including confusion matrices and class probability outputs.

Results

In the external test set, the model achieved a quadratic weighted kappa of 0.696 (95% CI: 0.677–0.715), with 85.7% of predictions within one grade and a five-class accuracy of 0.575. Grade 0 (normal lungs) showed the highest recall (0.917), followed by grade 4 (0.683), indicating reliable identification of both normal and severely affected pulmonary fields. Binary AUROC was 0.966 (95% CI: 0.961–0.970) for any RDS and 0.866 (0.854–0.879) for clinically significant RDS (grade ≥3) (Table 1).

The confusion matrix revealed that the model performed more consistently at the 2 severity extremes (grades 0 and 4), while recall declined for intermediate grades. Most misclassifications occurred between adjacent categories: grade 1 was frequently predicted as grade 2, grade 2 as grades 1 or 3, and grade 3 as grades 2 or 4. This pattern reflects the gradual radiographic transition and expected overlap in appearance across mild to moderate RDS stages (Fig. 1).

Grad-CAM analysis provided additional insights into the model’s decision-making and interpretability (Fig. 1). For higher RDS grades, the model’s attention was focused on the bilateral lower and mid-lung zones, consistent with findings of decreased aeration, granular opacities, and air bronchograms. In contrast, grade 0 images showed minimal internal activations, indicative of normal lung radiolucency. Although occasional attention was noted in peripheral or non-parenchymal areas, these did not dominate the overall activation patterns.

Discussion

Recently, deep learning-based digital image analysis has become a vital application of artificial intelligence in medicine, particularly in developing classification methods using chest radiographs to diagnose respiratory diseases in both adults and children [11–13]. Our research team previously created a fully automated deep learning algorithm based on ResNet50, which classified five common neonatal respiratory diseases and normal lungs in chest radiographs from NICUs [14]. This algorithm showed high concordance with expert-annotated classifications of RDS and demonstrated improved diagnostic performance when non-imaging clinical data were included. Building on these advancements, our current study introduces the first multi-class classification model over ordered grades for the automated grading of RDS severity.

Our model proved clinically useful by accurately distinguishing between normal lungs and severe RDS, which is crucial for ruling out disease or initiating urgent management. While the sensitivity for grade 4 was acceptable, lower precision indicates that the model may occasionally overestimate severe disease, necessitating cautious interpretation in borderline cases. As anticipated, performance declined for intermediate grades, where RDS severity manifests as a radiographic continuum rather than distinct categories. These results underscore the inherent challenges in assessing graded RDS severity and suggest that incorporating additional clinical data or multimodal imaging may enhance the differentiation between mild and moderate disease.

Analysis of the confusion matrix reveals that the model effectively learned the graded progression of RDS severity. Strong discrimination at the extremes indicates reliability in identifying clearly normal or severely affected lungs. Conversely, the concentration of errors among neighboring intermediate grades reflects the known clinical and radiographic ambiguity at these stages. The limited occurrence of misclassifications between nonadjacent grades further supports stable grade-order consistency and reduces the risk of clinically significant misinterpretations.

Grad-CAM visualizations indicated that the model primarily focuses on physiologically and clinically relevant pulmonary regions associated with known radiographic manifestations of RDS. The minimal activation observed in normal lungs suggests appropriate feature learning, while the occasional peripheral attention likely reflects contextual structural cues rather than misclassification. These findings enhance the interpretability and physiological relevance of the model, highlighting its potential as an objective and transparent tool for automated RDS severity assessment.

Despite its promising performance, this study has several limitations. First, the model was developed using a single architecture (UNet++ with ResNet-50), and comparisons with alternative architectures were beyond the scope of this study. Second, formal inter-rater agreement could not be assessed because only the final adjudicated labels were available. Third, although leave-site-out external validation was performed, all participating hospitals followed a common annotation protocol within a single national network, warranting validation in independent international cohorts. Fourth, the reported performance is conditional on successful lung segmentation, and exclusion of failed cases may have introduced slight selection bias. Finally, the model relied solely on chest radiographs and gestational age/birth weight information without incorporating additional clinical parameters that may improve comprehensive assessment of RDS severity.

In conclusion, we developed a deep learning-based multi-class classification model for automated grading of ordered RDS severity on neonatal chest radiographs. The model effectively distinguished normal lungs from severe RDS and may support more consistent radiographic severity assessment across clinicians and institutions. Because discrimination of intermediate grades remained limited, treatment decisions should continue to rely on comprehensive clinical evaluation, with model outputs serving as complementary information rather than a substitute for clinical judgment.
