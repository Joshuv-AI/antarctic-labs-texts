Spatial transcriptomics reveals clonal relationships between intraductal carcinoma and adjacent invasive prostate cancer
Authors: Hridya Harikumar, Myrthe de Waard‐van Baardwijk, Marit de Haan, Gregory van Beek, Karishma Lila, Martin E van Royen, Thierry PP van den Bosch, Mathijs A Sanders, Eric Bindels, Andrew Stubbs, Geert JLH van Leenders
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe pathogenesis of intraductal carcinoma (IDC) is still controversial. Contrary to current opinion, where IDC represents retrograde spread of invasive prostate cancer (PCa), we recently presented an alternative, unifying hypothesis named ‘Repetitive Invasion, Precursor Progression’ (RIPP). Little is known about genomic alterations in high‐grade Prostatic Intraepithelial Neoplasia (HGPIN), IDC and adjacent invasive PCa. Our objective was to clarify the mutual clonal relationships among HGPIN, IDC, and adjacent PCa using spatial transcriptomics.Methods and resultsRegions of interest containing HGPIN, IDC and adjacent invasive PCa were selected from six Gleason score 3 + 4 = 7 radical prostatectomy specimens. Spatial transcriptomic profiling and library preparation were executed according to the Visium workflow. Pathologist‐guided manual annotations were utilized to delineate regions of interest for the integrated analysis of chromosomal copy number variants (CNV) and spatiotemporal trajectories. Adjacent HGPIN, IDC and invasive PCa shared common CNV signatures across all samples, with various subclonal events. Unsupervised clonal analysis revealed that across three samples, the adjacent invasive subclone had acquired additional genomic alterations. In two samples, HGPIN, IDC and adjacent invasive PCa had identical CNVs. Finally, in one sample, IDC had additional CNVs compared with HGPIN and invasive glands. Supervised trajectory analysis consistently placed adjacent invasive PCa as the final step in the trajectory, after HGPIN and/or IDC.ConclusionsSpatial transcriptomics revealed strong clonal relationships among adjacent HGPIN, IDC and invasive PCa. Supervised trajectory analysis did not support retrograde spread in this limited number of samples, while unsupervised analysis revealed a complex mutual relationship among HGPIN, IDC and adjacent PCa.Keywords: clonal analysis, CNV analysis, intraductal carcinoma, invasive‐like glands, prostate cancer, spatial transcriptomics

Introduction

High‐grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN) is the well‐accepted precursor of prostate cancer (PCa). HGPIN is characterized by a flat, tufting or micropapillary proliferation of atypical luminal epithelial cells, with nuclear enlargement and prominent nucleoli within pre‐existing ducts and acini. 1 Meanwhile, Intraductal carcinoma (IDC) has been recognized as an expansile lesion, consisting mostly of a dense cribriform or solid proliferation of atypical luminal cells within pre‐existing glands. Some lesions exceed the morphological complexity of HGPIN but do not fulfil the histopathological criteria for IDC and are therefore termed atypical intraductal proliferation (AIP). AIP typically presents as a loose cribriform proliferation in distended glands or an atypical cribriform lesion within non‐distended glands. 1 Although the clinical impact of these structures is increasingly being recognized, the mutual pathophysiological relationships among HGPIN, AIP, IDC and invasive PCa remain unresolved.

In recent years, IDC has been identified as an independent prognostic factor for PCa outcome. 2 The majority of IDC is found adjacent to invasive PCa; isolated IDC without invasive disease is rarely observed. Despite its clinical significance, the pathogenesis of IDC remains controversial. The prevailing consensus suggests that IDC is the result of invasive carcinoma penetrating and expanding pre‐existing glands, which is referred to as retrograde spread. 3 This is substantiated by the fact that IDC is rarely observed without invasive PCa and that IDC may have more genomic alterations than invasive carcinoma. 4 Alternatively, IDC may function as a precursor lesion in some cases, since it rarely occurs without invasive PCa.

In an attempt to localize and characterize transitions between IDC and invasive PCa, our group previously identified invasive adenocarcinoma‐like (IAC‐like) glands, which were spatially positioned between IDC on the one side and clearly invasive PCa glands on the other, accompanied by a gradual loss of basal cells. 5 Based on the transitional position of IAC‐like glands between intraglandular HGPIN, AIP and/or IDC lesions and clearly invasive PCa in radical prostatectomy (RP) specimens, we proposed a novel model, titled ‘Repetitive Invasion Precursor Progression (RIPP)’, in which precursor HGPIN, apart from repetitively invading into adjacent stroma, could simultaneously progress to IDC.

Hypotheses on the pathogenesis of IDC have mostly been driven by observational pathological studies, while knowledge of the molecular features and clonal relationships among adjacent HGPIN, AIP, IDC and invasive PCa remains scarce. To investigate these spatial relationships, we employed spatial transcriptomics, which enabled the acquisition of high‐resolution gene expression profiles while preserving tissue architecture. This methodology permitted the precise delineation of specific histological domains, enabling comparative molecular characterization of distinct morphological features. The objective of this study was to decipher the clonal relationships between adjacent HGPIN, IDC and invasive PCa using spatial transcriptomics.

Methods

Sample Collection

Six RP specimens containing Gleason score 3 + 4 = 7 PCa with IDC were collected from our archive (Table S1). One representative formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissue block, containing HGPIN, IDC, IAC‐like glands and clearly invasive PCa was selected per case, according to the latest consensus definitions. 1 IAC‐like glands were defined as histopathologically invasive PCa glands with a gradual loss of basal cells, as identified by 34BE12 basal cell keratin immunohistochemistry, situated in proximity to HGPIN, AIP or IDC. 5 The study was approved by the local Medical Ethics Committee (MEC‐2024‐0281).

Sample Processing

Sample processing and library construction were performed in accordance with the 10xGenomics CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression protocols. 6 Deparaffinization, haematoxylin and eosin staining, imaging, destaining and decrosslinking were performed according to protocol CG000520. The Visium Cytassist Spatial Gene Expression Reagent Kit was used to prepare the libraries according to the CG000495 protocol. Fragment size and concentration of the library for sequence pooling were determined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The genes tested were consistent with the standardized panel for the Visium human transcriptome probe set v2.0. The Dual Index TS set A adaptor set was used for multiplexing. The estimated coverage percentage for all samples was 100%. Libraries were sequenced on a Novaseq6000 platform using an S2 flow cell with a 28–10–10–90 cycles setting (Illumina).

Data Processing and Analyses

Gene expression alignment and quantification were performed using Space Ranger version 2.1.1 (10xGenomics). A quality control step was performed on the spots for each sample by visualizing UMI and gene counts in violin plots to select appropriate cut‐offs for the maximum and minimum values (Table S2). Mitochondrial genes were removed to avoid skewing distributions. The filtered data were subsequently normalized using SCTransform. Visium spots for each sample were manually annotated by a genito‐urinary pathologist (GvL) for HGPIN, IDC, IAC‐like glands, invasive PCa at distance, normal glands and normal stroma using the Loupe Browser 8.0.0. (Figure 1). Annotations were exported as a CSV file for use in downstream analysis. The Python Scanpy package was used to validate the manual annotations using the corresponding sample expression profiles. The relative expression of a set of markers for each cell type was calculated and compared with a random set of genes from our dataset using the score genes function in Scanpy (Table S3).

Copy Number Variants Analysis

Copy number variants (CNV) analysis was first performed on overall tumour tissue annotations using SPATA2 to obtain a CNV signature for each sample. 7 Subsequently, SPATA2 analysis was performed on manually annotated HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands in each sample. Since pre‐existing normal glands were not present in each sample, normal glands from sample C served as a reference. CNV heatmaps were generated for each analysis.

Unsupervised Clonal Analysis

Spatial InferCNV was used for unsupervised clonal analysis. 8 All spots annotated as normal glands or normal stroma across samples were combined and subjected to unsupervised InferCNV analysis (parameters: cut‐off = 0.1, cluster_by_groups = FALSE, denoise = TRUE). From the denoised output, we identified a subset of spots with minimal inferred CNV signal, which we used as a consensus normal tissue reference for downstream per‐sample analyses. For each sample, CNV profiles were generated for all spots annotated as HGPIN, IDC or IAC‐like glands.

InferCNV was run in unsupervised mode using the consensus reference set and the following parameters: cut‐off = 0.1, cluster_by_groups = FALSE, denoise = TRUE and HMM = FALSE. The resulting heatmaps and dendrograms were visually inspected to identify ancestor nodes corresponding to clusters of spots sharing CNV profiles. These nodes were assigned clone and subclone identities, with subclones containing fewer than 10 spots being excluded from further analysis. For each subclone, CNV signals were aggregated to generate a consensus CNV profile. To reduce noise, deviations smaller than 0.02 from normal were removed. Phylogenetic relationships among clones were reconstructed using the Neighbour‐Joining method, resulting in patient‐specific clonal trees. Consensus CNV profiles were visualized as heatmaps alongside their corresponding phylogenetic trees and spatial distributions.

Supervised Trajectory Analysis

A supervised trajectory analysis was performed using the Pseudo‐Time‐Space (PTS) algorithm implemented in the stLearn framework. 9 This method infers spatially informed trajectories by integrating gene expression profiles, spatial coordinates and histology. StLearn first subdivided regions annotated as HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands into transcriptionally homogeneous subclusters to capture intraregional heterogeneity and define distinct transcriptional states. Pairwise PTS distances between subclusters were then computed based on the transcriptional similarity and spatial proximity. These combined similarities were used to reconstruct spatial trajectories representing a plausible progression of transcriptional states across tissues. To assess alternative hypotheses of disease progression, the trajectory was iteratively rooted in HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands, allowing each histological category to be evaluated as a potential early or late disease state.

CNV Signatures

SPATA2 analysis was first performed on general tumour annotations for each sample (Figures S1–S6). A detailed description of common and subclonal CNV signatures is presented in Table 1. 8p loss was the most common alteration present in five out of six samples, followed by 8q gain (3/6). 10q loss was present in two of six samples. These CNVs are well‐established in prostate carcinogenesis and PCa progression, consistent with previously reported genomic alterations. For example, 8p loss (including NKX3.1 locus), 8q gain (including c‐MYC locus) and 10q loss (including PTEN locus) frequently occur in both primary and metastatic PCa, and are associated with tumour progression and worse prognoses. 10 , 11 , 12 Subclonal events with additional dominant CNV signatures were present in most samples.

Unsupervised Clonal Analysis

To identify clonal relationships among HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands, we used InferCNV, which groups spots based on similarities in CNV profiles. The analysis revealed that most clones present across all samples consisted of a mixture of HGPIN, IDC and/or IAC‐like glands and were very closely related to one another, consistent with the patterns visualized in the SPATA2 CNV heatmaps for each sample. Sample A (Figure 3) demonstrated concomitant 8p loss and 8q gain across all HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands, indicating common ancestry. Subclones of HGPIN (clone B1) and IDC (clones B2 and B3) had acquired an additional 1p loss, while IAC‐like glands (clone C) had gained several other aberrations. Loss of 8p, 12q gain and 13q loss were common CNVs among HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands in sample B (Figure 4). One part of IDC, HGPIN and IAC‐like glands seemed to have derived from an early common progenitor (clone A). The other part, mainly consisting of HGPIN and IAC‐like glands, was characterized by an additional 7p gain (clones B and C), while a subclone mainly comprised of IAC‐like glands, but not IDC, had acquired 3p gain (clone B2). In sample C (Figure 5), 5p loss, 6q loss, 8p loss, 8q gain, 12p loss and 16q loss were observed across HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands. IAC‐like glands (clone B1) and HGPIN (clone B2) were closely related, whereas IDC had acquired an additional 9q gain and 13q loss (clones A) as well as subclonal 3q gain (clone A3). In Sample D (Figure S7), clone A, which primarily consisted of IAC‐like glands, possessed several CNVs compared to the other clones. Sample E (Figure S8) was predominantly composed of IAC‐like glands showing some genomic heterogeneity, with HGPIN and IDC being clustered together with IAC‐like glands (clones B and C), with little subclonal variation. Sample F (Figure S9) mostly consisted of HGPIN and IDC with common 8p loss and 10q loss with various subclonal events, where subclones enriched for HGPIN (clones A and B) had fewer CNVs than those enriched for IDC (clones C and D).

Supervised Trajectory Analysis

To gain further insight into the hierarchical relation of HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands, we performed supervised PSTS trajectory analysis using stLearn, independently including HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands as a pseudoroot. 9 PSTS calculates the most likely hierarchical trajectory based on gene‐expression values and the spatial relationships among annotated spots, with initiating pseudoroots placed at the beginning or end of the trajectory. When HGPIN was set as the pseudoroot, trajectories from HGPIN to IDC or from HGPIN to IDC to IAC‐like glands consistently occurred across all samples (Figure 6; Figures S10–S12). When IDC was rooted, it preceded IAC‐like glands in all samples. If IAC‐like glands were included as pseudoroots, they were always preceded by HGPIN and/or IDC.

Discussion

IDC of the prostate has been well accepted as an independent prognostic pathological factor in biopsy and RP specimens. It has been associated with adverse pathological features, shorter biochemical recurrence‐, metastasis‐ and disease‐specific free survival. 13 , 14 , 15 Therefore, the International Society of Urological Pathology and Genitourinary Pathology Society both recommend routinely reporting IDC in PCa biopsies and RP specimens. 16 , 17

Despite its clinical and pathological relevance, the pathogenesis of IDC remains a matter of debate. Since IDC is almost always associated with ≥ Gleason score 3 + 4 = 7 invasive PCa, most believe it originates from invasive adenocarcinoma glands that penetrate the pre‐existing glandular system. The observation that IDC may show PTEN loss while PTEN is partially retained in invasive PCa is considered to support the ‘retrograde spread’ hypothesis. 3 Since IDC is rarely found without invasive PCa, some IDC are assumed to be precursor lesions. 18 Currently, both pathways are postulated to coexist and are considered prerequisites for explaining all histopathological manifestations of IDC in PCa samples.

Following 2D and 3D characterization of the spatial transitions between invasive PCa and IDC, our group recently proposed ‘Repetitive Invasion, Precursor Progression’ as an alternative, unifying hypothesis on intraductal carcinogenesis. 5 According to this hypothesis, HGPIN represents the primary precursor lesion of both invasive PCa and IDC. HGPIN not only possesses the ability to invade into the surrounding stroma but also to progress to IDC, with AIP lying intermediate between these two states (‘precursor progression’). Since the invading potential has already been established, atypical luminal epithelial cells may repeatedly invade the stroma, eventually resulting in the histopathologically heterogeneous disease PCa is known to be. The following observations drove our hypothesis: (a) IDC is almost always localized adjacent to and continuous with AIP and HGPIN, and (b) both IDC and HGPIN spatially show transitions to invasive PCa glands with gradual loss from intraglandular to clearly invasive lesions. Observations from comparative biomarker studies on IDC and invasive PCa, which have been used as supporting arguments for the retrograde spreading hypothesis, could as likely be explained by the RIPP model. 5 , 19

To gain further insight into the molecular and clonal relationships among HGPIN, IDC, IAC‐like and invasive PCa glands, we performed spatial transcriptomics on six Gleason score 3 + 4 = 7 RP specimens with IDC. We identified well‐known genomic alterations in PCa, such as 8p loss, 8q gain and 10q, in line with established findings. 10 , 11 , 12 , 20 , 21 CNV analysis revealed common signatures among HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands across all samples, indicating close clonal relationships among the respective pathological entities. This is consistent with the study by Haffner et al., who identified identical TMPRSS2::ERG breakpoints among HGPIN, IDC and invasive PCa. 3 The existence of close clonal relationships, however, hampered the ability to make robust inferences about mutual ancestries. In one sample (sample C), IDC exhibited more pronounced genomic alterations than HGPIN and IAC‐like glands, consistent with both the retrograde spread and precursor progression hypotheses. In contrast, clones that were primarily composed of IAC‐like glands in three samples had relatively more aberrations (samples A, B and D). In sample B, HGPIN and IAC‐like glands were more closely clonally related to each other than to IDC, suggesting the IAC‐like glands were hierarchically derived from HGPIN. In Sample A, HGPIN and IDC were closely related with slightly more pronounced alterations in IDC, while IAC‐like glands seemed to have branched from a common precursor earlier. In samples D and E, HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands clustered together based on subtle differences, suggesting that IDC and IAC‐like glands originated from distinct HGPIN clones.

Finally, supervised trajectory analysis indicated progression from HGPIN to IDC, or from HGPIN to IDC to IAC‐like glands, when HGPIN was rooted. Additionally, regardless of the group used for rooting, IAC‐like glands were consistently at the end of all predicted trajectories. Together, the findings on these limited numbers of samples indicate that (a) HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands have very close clonal relationships, (b) considerable genomic subclonal heterogeneity exists even in small (6.5 × 6.5 mm) tumour areas, (c) morphologically distinct entities like HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands have overlapping genomic signatures implying they represent a continuum of tumour evolution and (d) although IDC harboured more genomic alterations than HGPIN and IAC‐like glands in one sample (sample C), IAC‐like glands showed progressive alterations in three samples (samples A, B and D).

The limited number of samples, combined with substantial subclonal heterogeneity, limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding IDC pathogenesis. The presence of additional CNV in IAC‐like glands in three samples (samples A, B and D) contradicts the fact that IDC has more pronounced genomic alterations than invasive PCa. All observations are consistent with the RIPP hypothesis, which posits that IAC‐like glands may be derived from HGPIN, IDC or both and with the supervised trajectory analysis.

Unravelling ancestral relationships in small areas of PCa tissue using spatial transcriptomics is challenging. Although tumour architectures are morphologically distinct, close clonal relationships appear to exist, hampering strong inferences about ancestry. In our analysis, we focused on differences in CNVs between adjacent pathological entities. The fact that pathological entities were not clearly characterized by unique genomic alterations may reflect gradual transitions between lesions, with no strict pathological–molecular correlations. This was also noted by Haffner et al., who found identical molecular alterations in different morphological entities. 3 For instance, as an initial precursor lesion, HGPIN is assumed to have the fewest genomic alterations. However, in the context of an established disease, HGPIN may have accumulated additional aberrations as it progresses to IDC, or may represent the expanding border of an IDC proliferation, as suggested by the close genomic relationship between HGPIN and IDC in this study. In this scenario, luminal HGPIN cells may acquire genomic alterations such as PTEN loss, which is known to destabilize mitotic spindle formation. 22 This may lead to vertical displacement of cells after mitosis, eventually giving rise to IDC with AIP as an intermediate. At the same time, tumorous cells have the capacity to invade the adjacent stroma, as supported by CNV signatures shared among HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands within individual samples. Ultimately, these findings can be interpreted within the framework of our RIPP hypothesis, considering pathological entities as snapshots of a dynamic biological process rather than discrete entities. 5 , 23

Currently, only one other study has specifically addressed the relationship between IDC and adjacent invasive PCa using the same spatial technology. Takahara et al. performed CNV analysis in six ≥ Gleason score 4 + 3 = 7 RP specimens. 24 Similar to our findings, they also observed considerable subclonal CNV variation and no unique IDC signature. However, Takahara et al. found more variation between IDC‐enriched and non‐IDC regions, which could, on the one hand, be explained by their inclusion of more aggressive tumours. On the other hand, their study did not specifically select for IAC‐like glands. It is tempting to speculate that selecting IAC‐like glands, which are assumed to be directly continuous with the intraglandular lesions, would indeed result in less clonal variation.

To the best of our understanding, this is the first study to elucidate the relationship among HGPIN, IDC and invasive PCa using spatial transcriptomics in Gleason score 3 + 4 = 7 RP specimens. The study is unique in that it specifically includes IAC‐like glandular areas, which are intermediate between intraglandular and clearly invasive PCa. However, this study has several limitations. Since HGPIN, AIP and IDC were located in close proximity, it was unclear which intraglandular lesion the IAC‐like glands were connecting to. Furthermore, the spatial transcriptomics platform (10X Genomics Visium) captures 55 μm spot areas spanning 1 to 10 cells, thereby introducing cellular heterogeneity within each annotated spot. 25 Together with the lack of matching single‐cell transcriptomics, this affected the comprehensive interpretability of gene expression profiles. Manual annotations were therefore required to minimize contamination, thereby reducing the number of spots per group available for analysis. The results were based on inferred CNV signatures from transcriptomic data, which may be insensitive to minor or subtle genomic alterations and may overlook changes such as mutations and methylation. Future studies could investigate clonal relationships using techniques that integrate higher resolution spatial transcriptomics and genomics.

Conclusion

The results of our spatial transcriptomics study on six patient samples revealed close clonal relationships among HGPIN, IDC and IAC‐like glands. Despite the limited sample size, the supervised trajectory analysis did not support retrograde spread theory, as IAC‐like glands were consistently positioned at the end of the trajectory, regardless of which group was used as the pseudoroot. The unsupervised analysis revealed that, in three samples, the adjacent invasive subclone had acquired additional genomic alterations. In two samples, HGPIN, IDC and adjacent invasive PCa had identical CNVs. Finally, in one sample, IDC had additional CNVs compared with HGPIN and invasive glands. Our posited RIPP hypothesis can explain all findings.
