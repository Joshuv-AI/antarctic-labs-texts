Pathological mechanisms of Sjögren’s disease-related dry eye: from multiglandular dysfunction to ocular surface inflammation
Authors: Tingting Gao, Gang Ding, Jinjin Gong, Xu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sjögren’s disease–related dry eye (SjD‑DE) refers to a form of dry eye disease driven by autoimmune dysregulation in Sjögren’s disease (SjD). Compared with primary dry eye, patients with SjD-DE typically present with more pronounced symptoms and signs, along with persistent and refractory disease courses that markedly impair daily functioning and quality of life. Although the precise mechanisms underlying SjD-DE have not been fully elucidated, accumulating evidence indicates that SjD affects not only lacrimal secretion but also multiple components of the ocular surface. In this context, we review recent pathological studies focusing on glandular dysfunction, goblet cell loss, tear film hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation, and corneal nerve pathology in SjD-DE. These pathological alterations are highly interconnected and mutually reinforcing, ultimately driving the “vicious cycle” of ocular surface damage. This review aims to provide insights to support the development of novel non-invasive diagnostic criteria and future therapeutic strategies for SjD-DE.

Introduction

Sjögren’s disease (SjD) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that affects multiple organs and tissues throughout the body. It occurs predominantly in women and is characterized primarily by xerostomia and xerophthalmia [1]. Dry eye (DE) is a complex, multifactorial disorder of the ocular surface, characterized by a disruption of tear film homeostasis and associated ocular symptoms. Key pathogenic mechanisms include tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, inflammation, and damage of the ocular surface, as well as neurosensory dysfunction [2]. Compared with patients with primary DE, individuals with SjD-DE exhibit an earlier age of onset, markedly reduced tear breakup time (TBUT) and Schirmer I test values, more pronounced ocular surface inflammation, and significantly higher corneal staining scores. The proportion of patients with moderate-to-severe DE is also substantially increased in SjD-DE [3]. SjD-DE typically presents with a chronic and progressive disease course and responds less favorably to conventional therapy than non-autoimmune-related DE, leading to severe impairment in quality of life.

The innate and adaptive immune defenses of the ocular surface normally protect against pathogens and immune-mediated injury. However, in contrast to primary DE, the immunoregulatory balance in SjD-DE is profoundly disrupted, resulting in excessive activation of ocular surface immunity, autoimmune-mediated tissue damage, and persistent inflammation [4]. Consequently, the pathological mechanism of SjD-DE extends beyond simple reductions in tear secretion.

Most existing studies describe isolated structural or functional abnormalities in SjD-DE, but a unified theoretical framework that integrates these findings remains lacking. To address this gap, this review synthesizes current evidence and advances a central concept: global dysfunction of the lacrimal glands (LGs), meibomian glands, and conjunctival goblet cells (CGCs) constitutes the initiating event in the pathogenesis of SjD-DE. This dysfunction induces tear hyperosmolarity, which in turn amplifies ocular surface inflammation. Together, they damage the ocular surface and destabilize the tear film. The combined effects of ocular surface injury and impaired tear film homeostasis further exacerbate tear hyperosmolarity, perpetuating a vicious cycle of ocular surface damage. Ultimately, this cycle manifests clinically as ocular dryness, pain, and blurred vision.

In addition, this review highlights potential diagnostic biomarkers for SjD-DE, aiming to provide scientific guidance for establishing non-invasive diagnostic criteria and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Global dysfunction of the LGs, meibomian glands, and CGCs

In patients with SjD-DE, lymphocytic infiltration of secretory glands disrupts glandular homeostasis and impairs gland function. Such disruption is considered an early event in SjD, occurring prior to overt inflammatory flares [5]. SjD-DE affects not only the LGs but also the meibomian glands and CGCs, all of which become compromised by autoimmune inflammation, leading to cell loss, atrophy, and functional decline.

LG Damage and functional impairment

The LG is an exocrine gland responsible for producing the aqueous component of the tear film, which lubricates and stabilizes the ocular surface. The epithelial compartment of the LG consists of acinar cells, ductal cells, and surrounding myoepithelial cells (MECs). MECs contribute to tear secretion by generating contractile force [6]. A hallmark of SjD is chronic infiltration and destruction of the LGs, salivary glands, and mucosal tissues by lymphocytes, macrophages, and natural killer cells [7]. CD4+ T cells are key drivers of SjD-DE; within the LG, they stimulate the production of monocyte chemoattractant proteins and other pro-inflammatory chemokines, thereby recruiting macrophages [8]. Moreover, IL-27 expression is markedly upregulated in the LGs, where it may facilitate the activation and local accumulation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells through engagement with IL-27Rα. Concurrently, it may exert inhibitory effects on Treg function, thereby collectively contributing to the initiation and progression of LG inflammation [9].

Current research on adaptive immunity in SjD has predominantly focused on the salivary glands, with relatively few studies investigating the mechanisms underlying ocular surface damage, most of which remain at the stage of animal experimentation. Recent studies have identified lymphocytic infiltrates exhibiting features of ectopic lymphoid structures (ELS) in the LGs of SjD mice at early disease stages. Within these infiltrates, the ratio of B cells to T cells is approximately equivalent, and the formation of ELS precedes ocular surface damage, such as CGC loss. These infiltrates can further mature into germinal center-like structures, thereby promoting B cell activation as well as local IgG synthesis and accumulation. This evidence suggests that B cell–, autoantibody–, and ectopic germinal center–mediated adaptive immune responses may contribute to LG injury and the pathogenesis of SjD-DE [10]. Clinically, patients with SjD-DE exhibit markedly distorted architecture and shrunken lobules of the main LG [11], along with apoptosis of acinar and ductal epithelial cells, ultimately resulting in glandular dysfunction and reduced tear secretion.

Impaired contractile function of LG MECs

Inflammasomes are multiprotein complexes that mediate potent inflammatory responses [12]. Major inflammasome subtypes include NOD-like receptor protein 1 (NLRP1), NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3), NOD-like receptor C4 (NLRC4), and absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) [13]. In SjD-DE, injury to LG epithelial cells leads to the accumulation of self-derived genomic DNA (gDNA) in the cytoplasm of MECs. This gDNA binds to the HIN200 domain [hematopoietic, interferon (IFN)-inducible nuclear protein with 200–amino acid repeats] of AIM2, thereby activating the AIM2 pathway [14]. The pyrin domain (PYD) of AIM2 subsequently interacts with the adaptor protein ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase-recruitment domain) to form the AIM2–ASC complex. This complex recruits pro-caspase-1, forming the inflammasome and inducing its activation. Activated caspase-1 cleaves pro-interleukin-1β (pro–IL-1β) and pro-interleukin-18 (pro–IL-18) into their mature forms, IL-1β and IL-18, which are then secreted extracellularly [15, 16].

Furthermore, IL-1β binding to interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) has been shown to activate the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling pathway, thereby upregulating the expression of membrane-type 1 matrix metalloproteinase (MT1-MMP) and its regulatory partner tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 2 (TIMP2). The MT1-MMP–TIMP2 complex binds pro-matrix metalloproteinase-2 (pro-MMP-2) at the cell membrane, leading to its activation. Active MMP-2 subsequently degrades key contractile proteins, such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and calponin, in LG MECs. This degradation markedly weakens the ability of oxytocin (OXT) to stimulate MECs contraction, thereby impairing MECs contractile function and reducing tear secretion [17–19]. Although human LG epithelial cells retain a certain degree of proliferative and differentiation capacity[20], chronic inflammation can progressively impair or even arrest LG regenerative processes [21]. Abnormal expression and function of Aquaporin-5 (AQP5)

Aquaporins (AQPs) are transmembrane channel proteins that facilitate the rapid and efficient transport of water and small solutes across cellular membranes [22]. AQP5, which is highly expressed in both the LG and cornea, localizes to the basolateral and apical membranes of LG acinar cells, and is the only AQP present on the apical membrane of human lacrimal acinar cells [23–25]. In SjD-DE, inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and interleukin-17 (IL-17) downregulate AQP5 expression and induce its mislocalization from the apical membrane to the cytoplasm or basolateral membrane, thereby impairing its physiological function [25]. Consistent with this, SjD-related rabbit models exhibit markedly reduced AQP5 abundance in acinar cell membranes compared with healthy controls [26].

The NLRP3 inflammasome plays an essential role in innate and adaptive immunity and in maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Recent studies have demonstrated that AQP5 deficiency increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in LG epithelial cells, which in turn activates the NLRP3 inflammasome. Activated NLRP3 recruits and cleaves pro-caspase-1 to generate active caspase-1, which subsequently processes pro–IL-1β and pro–IL-18 into their mature, bioactive forms [27–29]. Caspase-1 also cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), releasing its N-terminal domain (GSDMD-NT), which forms membrane pores, triggers pyroptosis, and facilitates the release of IL-1β and IL-18 [30, 31]. In parallel, AQP5 deficiency induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in LG epithelial cells, manifested by a swollen and shortened ER with enhanced ribosomal attachment [32]. ER stress can further promote inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and aberrant lipid metabolism, leading to LG injury [33]. Clinically, AQP5 has been detected in the tear fluid of SjD-DE patients, and its concentration correlates strongly with the severity of LG inflammatory injury [34]. This suggests that tear-derived AQP5 likely originates from leakage due to inflammatory damage of the LG [35], making it a promising biomarker for evaluating LG injury in SjD-DE. Experimental studies indicate that oral administration of Dendrobium extract can ameliorate DE symptoms in rat models by upregulating AQP5 expression in the LG, enhancing tear secretion, and alleviating inflammation [36]. Although evidence supporting the aforementioned pathway is largely derived from relevant animal models and in vitro cellular experiments and remains at the experimental stage, the detection of AQP5 together with significantly elevated levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in the tear fluid of patients with SjD-DE indirectly supports LG AQP5 impairment and the potential involvement of this pathway. At present, restoring AQP5 function and proper relocalization remains a considerable challenge. Other mechanisms

Studies have demonstrated that chronic dacryoadenitis in patients with SjD-DE can further damage LG cells and compromise their function and regenerative capacity. This occurs through inflammation-induced metabolic reprogramming, including downregulation of lipid-related pathways (e.g., fatty acid metabolism) and mitochondrial metabolism, together with enhanced cholesterol accumulation in lacrimal epithelial cells. These alterations may sustain inflammasome activation and thereby contribute to the persistence and progression of chronic inflammation within the LG [37, 38]. Additionally, studies have shown that arginase 1, though not an inflammatory mediator, is associated with reduced tear secretion in the LGs of SjD mouse models [39]. Epidermal fatty acid–binding protein (E-FABP) has been implicated in lipid oxidative stress and in the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling–mediated inflammatory response within the LG. Recent clinical evidence has shown that E-FABP levels in the tear fluid of patients with SjD-DE are significantly reduced compared with healthy controls, and that these levels correlate with ocular surface epithelial damage and tear film instability, suggesting its potential as a novel diagnostic biomarker for SjD-DE [40]. In contrast, studies of LGs from SjD mouse models have revealed robust E-FABP expression in lacrimal acinar epithelial cells. This inverse expression pattern, decreased E-FABP in tears despite abundant expression within the LG, may reflect either an early compensatory response to reduced tear secretion or, alternatively, dysfunction or loss of the gland’s E-FABP secretory capacity [41]. However, these findings are primarily derived from animal models and clinical observations, and a definitive regulatory mechanism has not yet been established, warranting further in-depth investigation.

Meibomian gland loss and dysfunction

The tear film’s lipid layer is secreted by the meibomian glands, and any dysfunction or atrophy of these glands, referred to as meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) or gland dropout, can significantly impair tear film stability. David et al. have proposed that patients with SjD also exhibit MGD, and their clinical investigation has confirmed this hypothesis: individuals with SjD demonstrate orifice metaplasia, ductal obstruction, and markedly reduced secretion quality, similar to patients with classic MGD [42]. Clinical studies by Yeon Soo Kang et al. have further revealed that SjD-DE patients exhibit more severe meibomian gland obstruction and gland dropout than those with non-SjD-DE or healthy controls. These patients also present with significantly reduced lipid layer thickness (LLT), resulting in compromised tear film stability [43–46]. Moreover, the degree of gland atrophy and dropout increases in proportion to disease duration in adult female patients with SjD [47].

In vivo confocal microscopy studies have demonstrated that, compared with MGD, SjD patients exhibit more pronounced periglandular inflammation, higher meibomian gland density, and narrower gland diameter. Relative to healthy controls, SjD patients also exhibit stronger periglandular inflammatory responses and higher secretory reflexivity. Gland density correlates negatively with TBUT, whereas gland diameter shows a positive correlation with TBUT. Additionally, periglandular inflammation and secretion reflexivity are positively correlated with corneal fluorescein staining [48]. These findings suggest that the meibomian glands in patients with SjD may be influenced by immune-mediated factors and are closely associated with abnormal glandular morphology, impaired function, and reduced meibum quality. Collectively, these alterations may contribute to ocular surface damage and tear film instability. Recent studies further indicate that the dropout rate of upper eyelid meibomian glands in SjD, particularly in the temporal region, correlates positively with histopathological findings from labial salivary gland biopsies, underscoring its potential as a non-invasive surrogate marker for salivary gland biopsy assessment [49].

MGD does not appear to be present at the earliest stage of SjD-DE. Yi Wang et al. have reported that in SjD, DE within the first 3 years is primarily attributable to LG involvement, whereas after 3 years, both lacrimal dysfunction and MGD jointly contribute to disease progression. This suggests that the third year marks a critical transition point in the pathophysiology of SjD-DE [50].

CGC Loss and dysfunction

CGCs are specialized secretory cells within the conjunctival epithelium that produce mucins essential for tear film formation. In addition to their secretory function, they facilitate antigen transport from the conjunctival surface to the underlying stroma, thereby contributing to tear film stability and playing a key role in maintaining ocular surface immune tolerance [51]. Loss or dysfunction of CGCs represents another key pathological hallmark of SjD-DE [52]. SjD-DE patients show a significantly elevated proportion of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and mature dendritic cells (DCs) in the conjunctiva, and these immune cell increases correlate positively with clinical DE severity and negatively with CGC density [53]. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining in SjD mouse models has demonstrated marked reductions in CGCs and mucin secretion [54]. MUC5AC, a CGC-derived specific mucin, is indispensable for tear film stability. Recent studies report that both CGC area and tear MUC5AC concentrations are markedly reduced in SjD-DE compared with healthy and non-SjD controls, with a strong positive correlation between tear MUC5AC levels and CGC area [55].

CGCs promote the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and suppress the differentiation of IFN-γ–producing T helper 1 (Th1) cells [56]. Studies reveal that Th1-derived IFN-γ is significantly elevated in the conjunctiva of SjD-DE patients [57]. CGCs are highly sensitive to IFN-γ, which induces ER stress and triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR), ultimately leading to loss of CGCs and depletion of mucin secretion [58]. These observations suggest that CGC depletion may weaken the inhibitory control over IFN-γ production. The resulting increase in IFN-γ can, in turn, further exacerbate CGC loss through the aforementioned pathway, thereby establishing a self-sustaining vicious cycle of goblet cell depletion. In addition, CGC loss in patients with SjD-DE may be linked to elevated proportions of APCs and mature DCs within the conjunctiva, which can promote Th1 cell activation. Moreover, IFN-γ overexpression driven by activated Th1 cells may further enhance APC activation, potentially forming a parallel inflammatory loop in which immune activation continuously drives CGC loss [53]. Among the available evidence supporting CGC depletion and these associated inflammatory circuits, clinical studies have primarily established a correlation between goblet cell loss and ocular surface inflammation. In contrast, the underlying molecular mechanisms have largely been elucidated through animal models and in vitro experiments and have not yet been directly validated in humans. Nevertheless, the existing evidence provides an important basis for clinical translation and offers potential research directions and theoretical support for future strategies aimed at preserving goblet cells and restoring mucin layer secretion.

Tear hyperosmolarity: a central upstream trigger for ocular surface inflammation and tissue damage

Specific mechanisms by which tear hyperosmolarity mediates ocular surface inflammation and tissue damage

Reductions in aqueous tear secretion, excessive evaporative loss, and disruption of tear film homeostasis collectively contribute to increased tear osmolarity, which represents a key upstream trigger in the ocular surface inflammatory cascade. Patients with SjD-DE exhibit significantly higher tear osmolarity than healthy individuals, and this elevation is positively correlated with corneal–conjunctival staining scores and overall ocular staining scores, while showing a negative correlation with Schirmer test values and TBUT [59–61]. Under DE conditions, hyperosmotic stress may activate the JNK and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, thereby inducing limbal epithelial cells to produce and secrete proinflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and the chemokine IL-8. This process subsequently initiates and amplifies downstream inflammatory responses on the ocular surface [62]. Of note, increased TNF-α expression under hyperosmotic conditions can further stimulate the NF-κB signaling cascade, upregulating transcription and expression of MMPs (MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-9, and MMP-13). This MMP upregulation disrupts tight junction (TJ) proteins in human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs), thereby compromising epithelial barrier integrity [63].

Hyperosmotic stress may also activate transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels and upregulate AQP5 in HCECs, thereby inducing the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and caspase-1 via the MAPK/NF-κB and JNK1/2 MAPK signaling pathways, respectively [64, 65]. Nuclear factor of activated T cells 5 (NFAT5), is an osmosensitive transcription factor. Recent findings demonstrate that hyperosmotic stress can activate NFAT5 in corneal epithelial cells(CECs) to induce the expression of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-20, and upregulated IL-20 levels have been detected in tear fluid [66]. IL-20 promotes macrophage polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype, further intensifying ocular surface inflammation. Persistent hyperosmotic stress can also induce severe apoptosis, necrosis, and cell cycle arrest in limbal epithelial stem cells, resulting in impaired epithelial repair, effects exacerbated in the presence of chronic inflammation [67].

NLR family pyrin domain containing 6 (NLRP6), a recently identified NOD-like receptor, plays a critical anti-inflammatory role. Hyperosmotic stress induces excessive production of ROS in ocular surface epithelial cells, leading to heightened NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reduced NLRP6 activity. This imbalance results in robust caspase-1 activation and amplifies downstream inflammatory cascades [68]. Studies show that SjD-DE patients exhibit significantly increased tear levels of NLRP3 and downstream effectors, including caspase-1, IL-18, and IL-1β, compared with primary DE patients [69]. Hyperosmolarity can also directly activate caspase-8, which, despite being produced independently of the NLRP3 inflammasome, can promote its activation, thereby contributing to ocular surface inflammation and pyroptotic cell death [68].

Piezo proteins are mechanosensitive transmembrane cation channels comprising Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) and PIEZO2 subtypes. Recent evidence indicates that hyperosmotic stimulation enhances PIEZO1 expression, which in turn activates the NLRP3 inflammasome in HCECs. Inhibiting PIEZO1 significantly alleviates inflammation, facilitates epithelial repair, and restores ocular surface barrier function. Thus, PIEZO1 may represent a promising therapeutic target for alleviating ocular surface inflammation in DE disease [70]. Dual roles of hyperosmolarity-induced autophagy activation

Autophagy is a lysosome-mediated degradation process for cellular components, which plays an essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. In HCECs, autophagy activation represents a late protective response following hyperosmotic stress–induced inflammation, alleviating inflammatory damage by reducing proinflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 [71]. However, autophagy-related gene 5 (ATG5) also participates in upregulating lipophagy under hyperosmotic conditions, promoting increased intracellular free fatty acid accumulation, triggering ferroptosis in epithelial cells, and further inducing downstream ocular surface inflammation [72]. ATG5 and the ratio of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B-II/I (LC3B-II/I) are recognized autophagy markers, which may be secreted by either LGs or ocular surface epithelial cells. Compared with non-SjD-DE patients, tear fluid from SjD-DE individuals exhibits significantly elevated levels of these autophagy markers [73], which may be associated with the higher tear osmolarity in patients with SjD-DE. Notably, when the ATG5 and LC3-II/I ratio exceeds 4.0 ng/mL/μg, ATG5 demonstrates a sensitivity of 94.6% and a specificity of 93.6% for diagnosing aqueous-deficient DE in SjD, suggesting its superior diagnostic performance over traditional measures such as TBUT and Schirmer I test. These findings imply its potential as a novel and effective biomarker for SjD-DE [74]. Evidence for the regulatory mechanisms through which autophagy activation either promotes or suppresses ocular surface inflammation is primarily derived from in vitro experiments and animal models and has not yet been directly validated in humans. In contrast, measurements of autophagy-related markers in the tears of patients with SjD-DE, as well as their reported sensitivity and specificity, are directly obtained from clinical human studies. Collectively, these findings provide a theoretical basis for the development of novel diagnostic approaches and for future mechanistic investigations in SjD-DE.

Other mechanisms

Tear hyperosmolarity also induces excessive production of ROS, which triggers ER stress in CECs, leading to apoptosis and disruption of ocular surface barrier integrity [75]. The chemical chaperone tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) has been shown to mitigate ER stress, reduce apoptosis, and preserve epithelial homeostasis [76]. In vitro studies indicate that sustained hyperosmotic stimulation drives CGCs through four consecutive bioelectrical response phases (KATP/NSC/VGCC/P2X7), which are associated with mucin release and depletion as well as eventual CGC loss [77]. Additionally, reduced tear volume and elevated osmolarity enhance the excitability of ocular surface polymodal nociceptors responsible for burning and stinging sensations, as well as cold receptors mediating dryness perception, collectively heightening ocular surface sensitivity and discomfort [78].

Tear cytokines: a hyperosmolarity-driven downstream inflammatory microenvironment

Tear hyperosmolarity, a central upstream trigger, together with ocular surface dryness and mechanical stress, activates signaling pathways in both immune and epithelial cells. This coordinated activation drives the establishment of a downstream proinflammatory tear microenvironment, characterized by the increased release of multiple cytokines [79]. CD4+ T cells, also known as Th cells, differentiate into Th1, Th2, Th17, and induced Tregs in response to distinct antigenic cues and secrete cytokines that orchestrate ocular surface inflammation [80]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that the number of Th cells in the ocular surface of patients with moderate-to-severe DE correlates positively with corneal and conjunctival staining scores, suggesting their involvement in ocular surface damage–related inflammatory processes. Furthermore, Treg cells exhibit a significant positive association with the onset of SjD [81]. Elevated tear cytokines and hyperosmolarity exacerbate corneal epithelial barrier disruption and other ocular surface damage. This damage, in turn, accelerates premature tear film breakup, which further amplifies tear hyperosmolarity, establishing a self-perpetuating “vicious cycle” [82].

Elevated proinflammatory cytokines in tears impair the ocular surface barrier

Tear hyperosmolarity promotes activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and increases the levels of key proinflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-8. However, ocular surface inflammation involves a complex regulatory network, and hyperosmotic stress alone does not uniformly upregulate all cytokines; additional factors such as chronic inflammation and mechanical injury to the ocular surface may also contribute to cytokine elevation[83]. IL-1β, a potent proinflammatory cytokine secreted by immune cells, serves as a central mediator of inflammatory responses[84]. Elevated IL-1β levels in tears not only induce CECs to secrete secondary proinflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and IL-8[85], but may also promote pyroptosis and cell death in CECs. This process can further amplify IL-1β production, thereby establishing a self-perpetuating inflammatory vicious cycle [86]. In parallel, TNF-α has been shown to significantly induce the expression of cathepsin S (CTSS) in HCECs. CTSS may directly degrade the TJ protein ZO-1, thereby compromising corneal epithelial barrier integrity and further exacerbating ocular surface dysfunction [87].

Several meta-analyses have consistently reported significantly elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-17, and IL-1β, in the tears of patients with SjD-DE[88, 89]. In addition, the expression of related genes in the conjunctival epithelium is markedly higher than that observed in non-SjD-DE patients, and conjunctival staining scores show significant correlations with the expression levels of MMP-9, IL-17, IL-6, and IFN-γ [90, 91]. Among these mediators, IFN-γ not only reduces CGC density and tear secretion, but also diminishes the metabolic activity of CECs and stromal fibroblasts, while compromising corneal epithelial barrier integrity [56]. Tear IL-17 levels have also been associated with the clinical severity of SjD-DE [92]. In a similar manner to TNF-α, the proinflammatory cytokines IL-17 and IL-6 can disrupt corneal epithelial barrier function by impairing TJ integrity between CECs[93].

Increased tear proteases exacerbate ocular surface and LG inflammation

CTSS is a cysteine protease essential for major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) antigen presentation and is closely associated with the generation of autoreactive T cells [94]. Rab proteins, key regulators of exocytosis, play a critical role in CTSS secretion. In SjD-DE patients, both LGs and tear fluid exhibit markedly elevated IFN-γ levels. In vitro studies have demonstrated that IFN-γ treatment of lacrimal gland acinar cells (LGACs) downregulates Rab3D expression while stimulating CTSS secretion [95]. Reduced Rab3D expression may trigger compensatory Rab27-mediated exocytosis of CTSS, which may contribute to the markedly elevated levels of CTSS in both tears and LGs in SjD patients [96, 97].

Tear CTSS further amplifies ocular surface inflammation by inducing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-8), MMP-9, and CTSS itself [98]. In SjD-DE patients, reduced cystatin C (Cys C) in tears leads to enhanced CTSS activity relative to other autoimmune diseases. CTSS also degrades protective tear proteins such as lactoferrin (LF) and secretory IgA (sIgA), which may compromise tear film quality and thereby increase susceptibility to ocular surface inflammation. [99]. Pharmacologic inhibition of CTSS in SjD-DE mouse models reduces CD4+ T cell–mediated inflammatory infiltration in the LGs, stimulates tear secretion, and improves corneal sensitivity [100, 101]. Collectively, these findings indicate that CTSS contributes to both LG inflammation and tear dysfunction, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in SjD-DE.

Proteoglycan 4 (PRG4), a natural lubricant on the ocular surface, is highly susceptible to proteolytic degradation. In patients with SjD, significantly increased ocular surface CTSS activity and subsequent PRG4 degradation have been associated with reduced tear PRG4 concentration, decreased tear volume, and overall deterioration of tear quality compared with healthy controls [102]. PRG4 deficiency may impair ocular surface lubrication and increase frictional shear stress between the eyelid and the ocular surface, which may contribute to ocular surface epithelial damage [103, 104]. Recent studies have demonstrated that recombinant human PRG4 (rhPRG4) mitigates ocular surface inflammation in DE by downregulating TNF-α–induced signaling and inhibiting NF-κB pathway activation in HCECs [105].

Others

Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) can disrupt TJs between CECs, promote epithelial shedding with exposure of underlying wing cells, and induce the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β). Accordingly, MMP-9 has been recognized as a reliable tear biomarker of ocular surface inflammation [106–108]. Tear MMP-9 levels correlate with TBUT, Schirmer test results, corneal and conjunctival staining scores, and meibomian gland counts, and are significantly elevated in patients with SjD–dominant autoimmune disease [109]. Longitudinal monitoring of 67 DE patients with concordant MMP-9 results in both eyes has revealed that MMP-9–positive patients experience subsequent significant reductions in tear secretion over 6 months [110], suggesting a potential role of MMP-9 in LG damage and dysfunction.

Thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), an immune-modulating protein, exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting MMP-9, with TSP-1 levels inversely correlated with MMP-9. The TSP-1/MMP-9 ratio in tears effectively distinguishes SjD-DE from non-SjD-DE patients [111]. TSP-1 can also bind to CD36 on the surface of CGCs, stimulating the secretion and activation of TGF-β, which maintains DCs in an immature or tolerogenic state and regulates ocular immune homeostasis [112]. Moreover, TSP-derived peptides have been shown to ameliorate SjD-DE ocular surface inflammation by suppressing Th17-associated proinflammatory cytokine activity [113].

The altered expression of the aforementioned tear factors, proteases, and related biomarkers has been confirmed in clinical studies of patients with SjD-DE. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which these factors mediate ocular surface damage, as well as their interrelated interactions, are still largely inferred from in vitro experiments and animal models and have not yet been directly validated in humans. Nevertheless, these findings provide an important theoretical framework for understanding the establishment of the ocular surface inflammatory microenvironment and the disruption of the corneal epithelial barrier in SjD-DE, and they also suggest potential avenues for the development of targeted therapies against inflammatory cytokines and proteases. Corneal nerve abnormalities and neuropathic pain

The cornea is the most densely innervated and highly sensitive tissue in the human body. Corneal nerves are responsible for sensory signal transmission, trophic support of the cornea and ocular surface epithelial cells, and play a central role in maintaining ocular surface homeostasis. These nerve fibers are predominantly sensory afferent fibers, originating mainly from the ciliary nerves of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal ganglion, with free nerve endings terminating within the corneal epithelium [120, 121]. Human corneal sensory innervation is organized into four layers from the innermost to the outermost: the stromal nerve plexus, subepithelial nerve plexus, subbasal nerve plexus, and intraepithelial nerve endings [122]. Corneal nerve receptors consist of specialized sensory terminals, including mechanonociceptors, polymodal nociceptors, and cold thermoreceptors [123], which transduce external stimuli into nerve impulses and are predominantly distributed within the corneal epithelium.

In addition, a smaller proportion of corneal nerves are autonomic efferent fibers, which regulate the secretion of the LGs, meibomian glands, and CGCs via the trigeminal–parasympathetic reflex pathway [121]. This rich innervation enables the cornea to rapidly respond to noxious stimuli and maintain the integrity of the corneal epithelial barrier. Studies have further demonstrated that ocular surface inflammation and abnormal corneal innervation can mutually influence each other, ultimately contributing to reduced reflex tear secretion [124, 125].

Abnormalities of corneal nerve structure

In patients with SjD-DE, increased mechanical stress on ocular surface epithelial cells induced by blinking may damage corneal nerve terminal branches, and the alterations in corneal nerve architecture are closely associated with repeated cycles of injury and repair [126]. Compared with patients with non-SjD-DE and healthy controls, individuals with SjD-DE exhibit more severe abnormalities in DE diagnostic parameters, including reduced TBUT and Schirmer I test values. Corneal subbasal nerve density and number are significantly decreased and show a negative correlation with corneal staining scores [124, 127], indicating a strong association between reduced corneal innervation and epithelial damage. Consistent findings across multiple studies further demonstrate increased corneal nerve tortuosity, mean bead count, and corneal DC density in patients with SjD-DE [128–131]. In addition, corneal nerve density and length are markedly reduced in SjD-DE patients with hyperosmolar tears compared with those with normal tear osmolarity, suggesting that tear hyperosmolarity may contribute to the development of corneal nerve structural abnormalities in SjD-DE [132].

Corneal neuropathic pain

Some patients with SjD-DE experience ocular pain that is disproportionately severe relative to clinical signs, reflecting typical neuropathic pain rather than nociception driven solely by tissue injury. Corneal neuropathic pain arises from lesions or dysfunction of corneal nerves and other components of the somatosensory system. Importantly, pain is not strictly equivalent to nociception, and the two processes may occur independently [133]. Under neuropathic conditions, pain may be elicited by normally innocuous stimuli, exacerbated and prolonged by noxious inputs, and may even spread beyond the original site of injury. The corneal hypersensitivity observed in SjD-DE is considered a manifestation of peripheral sensitization, closely linked to structural abnormalities of corneal nerves and cytokine–nerve interactions.

Corneal sensory nerve fibers are predominantly polymodal nociceptors, with TRPV1 widely recognized as a molecular marker. TRPV1 can be activated by inflammatory mediators, hyperosmolar stress, and capsaicin, leading to sensations of ocular burning pain and discomfort [126]. In mouse models of severe chronic aqueous-deficient dry eye (ADDE), corneal nerve terminal density is significantly reduced at early stages, followed by gradual reinnervation and a marked increase in TRPV1-expressing corneal neurons [134]. Under inflammatory conditions, activation of polymodal nociceptors such as TRPV1 lowers the threshold for action potential generation in corneal sensory neurons, including ciliary nerve fibers, enhances neuronal excitability, and thereby contributes to corneal hypersensitivity and hyperalgesia [135–137]. Transient receptor potential melastatin 8 (TRPM8) is a cold- and menthol-sensitive cation channel that also responds to osmotic changes and plays an essential role in maintaining normal blink frequency. Tear hyperosmolarity can increase TRPM8 sensitivity, enabling channel activation even at physiological temperatures, which may result in ocular discomfort, increased blinking, and spontaneous pain under otherwise normal thermal conditions in patients with SjD-DE [138, 139].

Peripheral sensitization pain is essentially pain resulting from the activation and heightened excitability of nociceptors. Nerve impulses generated at nociceptor terminals are transmitted via the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve to the trigeminal ganglion, where they may facilitate glutamate release and its binding to N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors on second-order neurons in the trigeminal subnucleus caudalis (Vc). These signals are then relayed to the thalamus and, following modulation by subcortical and limbic structures, projected to the somatosensory cortex and other higher-order brain regions, culminating in pain perception [140]. Persistent nociceptive input arising from chronic ocular surface inflammation and peripheral sensitization serves as a key initiating factor for central sensitization, driving neuroplastic changes within the central nervous system. On one hand, excessive activation of NMDA receptors, together with glutamate accumulation mediated by glial cells, amplifies pain signal transmission. On the other hand, inhibitory control of ascending nociceptive pathways via γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-mediated descending inhibitory circuits is weakened, resulting in disinhibition and overactivation of ascending pathways. Collectively, these mechanisms contribute to the initiation and maintenance of spontaneous and persistent ocular pain [141].

Cytokine-nerve interactions

Abnormal corneal nerve structure is considered a prerequisite for initiating cytokine–nerve interactions, which represent a key mechanism underlying the progressive exacerbation of neuropathic pain and the symptom–sign discrepancy observed in patients with SjD-DE. Clinical studies have demonstrated that proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β are significantly elevated in the tears of DE patients whose symptoms are disproportionate to clinical signs. These cytokines can bind to their corresponding receptors on corneal sensory nerve endings, induce aberrant nerve firing, and thereby contribute to the initiation of pain signaling [142]. In rat models with altered corneal nerve architecture, expression levels of the ion channels TRPM8 and TRPV1, as well as inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, are significantly upregulated in the trigeminal ganglion [143]. This suggests that corneal nerve injury may, in turn, promote local inflammatory mediator release and ion channel dysregulation, thereby further amplifying peripheral sensitization. In parallel, such peripheral inflammatory signals can be transmitted to the central nervous system, where they induce glial cell activation and marked increases in IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α within the ipsilateral trigeminal brainstem complex (TBSC), ultimately triggering central neuroinflammation that may contribute to the onset and persistence of chronic ocular pain [144].

Although abnormalities in corneal nerve structure have been documented in clinical studies, the molecular mechanisms underlying corneal neuropathic pain and cytokine–nerve interactions are still largely derived from animal models and have not yet been directly validated in humans. Nevertheless, these findings provide a theoretical framework for explaining the clinical presentation of disproportionate ocular symptoms and severe pain in SjD-DE patients, and they also suggest potential directions for future therapeutic strategies.

Sex-related differences in immune regulation

The prevalence ratio of SjD between males and females is approximately 1:10.72, and most female patients develop the disease during the perimenopausal and postmenopausal periods. Estrogen plays a critical role in maintaining LG cell viability, and its deficiency can induce LG cell apoptosis, lymphocytic infiltration, and increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-10 [144, 145]. Treatment with physiological doses of estradiol or dihydrotestosterone has been shown to prevent the upregulation of these proinflammatory cytokines, suggesting that estrogen is closely associated with the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines in the LG [146].

Human meibomian gland epithelial cells express estrogen, androgen, and progesterone receptors. Estrogen suppresses the expression of genes involved in meibum synthesis [147, 148], whereas androgens antagonize estrogen activity and help preserve meibomian gland function [149]. In female patients with SjD, androgen deficiency may contribute to MGD, altered lipid composition of meibomian secretions, tear film instability, and evaporative DE [150]. Androgens also upregulate multiple key enzymes involved in lipid biosynthesis in the meibomian gland, promote lipid production and glandular maturation, and suppress the expression of various proinflammatory genes in meibomian gland epithelial cells [151–153]. Furthermore, progressive sex hormone imbalance after menopause may induce acinar cells to release microparticles carrying potential autoantigens, which may activate T- and B-cell–mediated autoimmune cascades and thereby contribute to the pathogenesis of SjD [154].

Conclusions and perspectives

In summary, this review systematically outlines recent advances in SjD-DE. In contrast to previous studies that largely focused on isolated pathological mechanisms, we integrate the available evidence into a more systematic and coherent core pathogenic framework: autoimmunity drives comprehensive dysfunction of the LGs, meibomian glands, and CGCs, leading to tear hyperosmolarity that initiates and amplifies ocular surface inflammation and ultimately compromises corneal epithelial barrier integrity. Sustained inflammation and ongoing tissue injury subsequently induce structural abnormalities in corneal nerves, while bidirectional interactions between damaged nerves and inflammatory mediators initiate and exacerbate spontaneous chronic neuropathic pain. Concurrently, ocular surface damage promotes premature tear film breakup, which further aggravates tear hyperosmolarity and establishes a self-perpetuating vicious cycle. This central framework not only integrates pathological changes from glandular dysfunction to ocular surface injury, but also provides a theoretical basis for emerging biomarkers such as tear E-FABP, ATG5, and meibomian gland dropout rate. Moreover, it offers a mechanistic explanation for the characteristic symptom–sign discrepancy observed in a subset of patients with SjD-DE, in whom ocular pain is disproportionately severe relative to clinical findings.

Currently, the clinical management of SjD-DE relies predominantly on symptomatic therapies, including artificial tear supplementation; topical corticosteroids to suppress ocular surface inflammation [155, 156]; cyclosporine ophthalmic solutions and gels to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, enhance goblet cell numbers, and promote mucin secretion [157]; diquafosol sodium to stimulate goblet cell mucin secretion and activate the conjunctival accessory lacrimal apparatus, thereby improving tear quality and quantity, restoring physiological tear film, and maintaining tear film stability [158]; intense pulsed light combined with meibomian gland massage to relieve obstruction [159]; and scleral lenses to reduce tear evaporation and potentially facilitate corneal healing [160–162]. However, these approaches do not reverse or alleviate underlying LG injury.

Compared with preservative-free artificial tears (PFAT), serum- or plasma-derived therapies, including autologous serum eye drops (ASED) and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) eye drops, have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential for the treatment of SjD-DE. Relative to PFAT, ASED more closely resembles the composition of natural tears, thereby improving corneal nutritional support and alleviating neurotrophic epithelial changes, which in turn leads to reductions in Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) scores and improvement in patient-reported symptoms [163]. Both ASED and PRP eye drops have been shown to reduce corneal–conjunctival fluorescein staining scores and improve TBUT in patients with SjD-DE, with comparable therapeutic efficacy between the two modalities [164]. However, elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, in ASED derived from patients with SjD associated with other autoimmune conditions may compromise treatment outcomes for DE [165]. Consistently, ocular discomfort attributable to high inflammatory mediator levels in ASED can be significantly alleviated after switching to allogeneic serum eye drops (alloSED) [166, 167]. Nevertheless, current studies are limited by relatively small sample sizes, and larger, well-designed clinical trials are required to further delineate the efficacy differences between ASED and alloSED.

Addressing chronic LG inflammation and dysfunction in SjD-DE, stem cell–based therapies have shown promising efficacy. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are immunomodulatory stem cells endowed with multipotent differentiation potential and regenerative capacity, isolable from bone marrow, umbilical cord, and other tissues. They exert predominant immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects in SjD-DE [168]. Subconjunctival or LG injection of MSCs can suppress autophagy, inflammatory cell infiltration, and proinflammatory cytokine expression in the LGs [169–172] and also promote M2 macrophage polarization via AKT pathway activation, thereby alleviating autoimmune-mediated LG inflammation and restoring secretory function [173]. Additionally, MSC-derived extracellular vesicles retain immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties [174], and recent studies report that human umbilical cord MSC–derived small extracellular vesicles (huc-msc-sevs) can promote M2 macrophage polarization via miR-100-5p, providing a promising approach to prevent and treat autoimmune LG inflammation [175]. Early clinical trials involving MSC injection into LGs of SjD-DE patients indicate that this therapy is safe, well-tolerated, and significantly improves tear secretion and tear film stability, with marked alleviation of DE symptoms and signs [172, 176]. However, existing studies remain largely at the early clinical stage and are limited by small sample sizes. The long-term safety profile and regulatory framework for stem cell–based therapies have yet to be fully established, and their sustained efficacy and safety require further validation through large-scale studies with extended follow-up.

Novel rapamycin formulations, including nanocarriers or micelles, administered systemically, topically, or via LG injection, have demonstrated potent suppression of autoimmune LG inflammation while stimulating tear secretion in animal models. These delivery innovations decouple therapeutic efficacy from systemic toxicity and extend drug activity, providing new insights for clinical translation. [177–180]. Currently, most relevant studies remain at the preclinical stage, primarily based on animal experiments with generally small sample sizes, which limits the robustness and generalizability of the findings. In addition, the preparation protocols for drugs administered via different delivery routes have not yet been standardized, and key parameters such as dosage and administration frequency require further optimization. Moreover, the potential long-term toxicities and adverse effects remain insufficiently characterized. Consequently, the clinical translational potential of these approaches requires further validation through systematic and well-designed future studies.

For addressing tear hyperosmolarity and subsequent ocular surface inflammation, small-molecule interventions such as edaravone, Polydatin, fibroblast growth factor-10 (FGF10), and astaxanthin have shown robust ocular surface protection in experimental models by inhibiting oxidative stress, apoptosis, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and proinflammatory cytokine expression, representing the next generation of symptom-targeted therapies [181–186].

Although this review provides a relatively comprehensive and systematic overview of the pathological mechanisms underlying SjD-DE, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the conclusions regarding pathogenic mechanisms are primarily derived from studies specifically focused on SjD-DE and are therefore largely applicable to this condition alone. At present, evidence addressing the pathological mechanisms of SjD-DE in the context of other systemic autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis, remains limited, and the distinct features of their associated signaling pathways have yet to be clearly delineated, warranting further investigation in both clinical and basic research settings [187]. Second, the conclusions presented in this review are largely based on basic experimental studies and small-sample clinical observational data. Many of the proposed mechanistic pathways rely on indirect evidence and therefore do not fully elucidate the precise regulatory processes operating in humans. Consequently, the reliability and clinical translational relevance of these findings require further validation through large-scale, prospective studies with long-term follow-up.

Looking forward, effective SjD-DE management must fundamentally reverse its pathological course. Future research should prioritize large-scale clinical validation of stem cell therapies, optimizing delivery strategies and dosing, while further exploring the therapeutic potential of their derivatives. Concurrently, investigations into SjD-DE pathophysiology and novel pharmacologic interventions should proceed. Efforts should also focus on translating tear biomarkers such as E-FABP, ATG5, and meibomian gland dropout rate into reliable clinical diagnostic tools, enabling precise early diagnosis prior to full manifestation of clinical symptoms, thereby providing a therapeutic advantage in the management of SjD-DE.
