Masticatory myalgia to headache-like secondary hypersensitivity induces gene plasticity at dura mater
Authors: Anahit H Hovhannisyan, Jessie Alfaro, Jessica Aldape, Jennifer M Mecklenburg, Yi Zou, Zhao Lai, Wei Guo, Jiale Yang, M Danilo Boada, Malin Ernberg, Ke Ren, RE-JOIN Consortium Investigators, Armen N Akopian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with myofascial pain in the head and neck region often report widespread, referred pain or secondary hypersensitivity, including headache-like. Secondary hypersensitivity originating from masticatory myalgia may result from myalgia-induced plasticity within the central nervous system, affecting referred site sensitization. The main aims of this study were to develop animal models that mimic secondary hypersensitivity and to investigate whether stimulated myalgia induces gene expression plasticity at referred sites, which may contribute to the secondary hypersensitivity phenomenon.MethodsA majority of experiments were conducted in male and female mice. Masticatory myalgia was assessed as mechanical hypersensitivity in the region over the masseter muscle (MM). Secondary hypersensitivity was evaluated by measuring mechanical hypersensitivity at sites anatomically distinct from the stimulated muscle: periorbital and MM areas. Stimulated myalgia was achieved by either a single high-dose collagenase type-II (10U; Col) or repeated low-dose Col (0.2–0.5U) injections into the MM or the temporal muscle (TM), repetitive gentle vibration applied over the MM, a single forceful mouth opening (FMO), or repeated FMO. Statistical analyses were one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests.ResultsStimulation of the MM, whether by single, repeated Col injections or FMO, produced inconsistent and short-lasting (1–2 days) referred pain at a periorbital area in both males and females. In contrast, stimulation of the TM using multiple paradigms reliably induced mechanical secondary hypersensitivity in two referred sites: MM and periorbital areas. MM stimulation did not exhibit sex-dependent mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM area. In contrast, TM-induced secondary hypersensitivity at both the MM and periorbital areas was sex-dependent. Secondary hypersensitivity in the MM and periorbital regions following TM stimulation was accompanied by significant gene expression plasticity in both tissues. Notably, transcriptional changes in the MM after Col injection into the TM closely resembled those observed following direct Col injection into the MM.ConclusionsThe presented data suggest that secondary hypersensitivity from masticatory myalgia can be effectively modeled in mice through stimulation of the TM. Importantly, TM stimulation-induced transcriptomic changes at MM and dura mater may generate nociceptive signaling from these sites, thereby contributing to an input network underlying secondary hypersensitivity.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02361-8.Keywords: Masseter muscle, Temporal muscle, Dura mater, Secondary hypersensitivity, Masticatory myalgia, Headache, Female, Male

Background

Myogenous temporomandibular disorders (TMD-M) are classified as chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions [1]. TMD-M have a multifactorial etiology and complex pathophysiology, which significantly impact both affected individuals and society [1, 2]. According to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD), TMD-M are subdivided into local myalgia (MYA) and myofascial pain with referral (MFP) [3–5]. MYA is characterized by localized muscle pain and is frequently the patient’s primary complaint. In contrast, patients with MFP report widespread and/or referred pain or secondary hypersensitivity [4–6]. Consequently, MFP is often associated with additional symptoms such as headache, and pain in the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and neck muscles [7].

The mechanisms underlying referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity are not fully understood, but several possibilities have been proposed. One explanation is the sensitization of second-order brainstem neurons that serve as convergence points for sensory afferents innervating different head and neck regions. Such sensitization may lead to an expansion of receptive fields [8, 9]. Referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity may also reflect central sensitization resulting from reduced inhibition within descending pain pathways [10–12]. In addition, local mechanisms at the primary (trigger) site may contribute to referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity [11]. Stronger, longer-lasting, and more painful stimulation of the primary site, such as muscle tissue, has been shown to produce greater referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity [11, 12]. Moreover, injection of local anesthetics into the primary site reduces referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity [11]. These findings highlight the essential contribution of the primary site to referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity and raise the question of whether this phenomenon is solely explained by enhanced plasticity in primary (trigger) sites and/or within central nervous system pain pathways, or whether regulatory plasticity at secondary (referred) sites induced by the primary sites also plays a role.

Referred pain is not defined as nociceptive hypersensitivity in remote areas. Traditionally, the term referred pain is arising from trigger points that, upon palpation, elicit pain at distant sites. In the DC/TMD classification, the diagnosis “myofascial pain with referred pain” requires that pain be reported outside the anatomical boundaries of the muscle during palpation; however, the term “trigger points” itself is not explicitly used. Thus, referred pain must be perceived (or felt) in a location different from its true source, and it may also occur spontaneously. This phenomenon cannot be adequately modeled in mice. In contrast, secondary hyperalgesia refers to increased pain sensitivity in surrounding or remote, uninjured tissues and is assessed experimentally by measuring this hypersensitivity. Accordingly, in mice, we will be modeling secondary hypersensitivity.

In this context, the overarching goals of this study were to develop animal models of secondary hypersensitivity by stimulating the masticatory muscles (masseter (MM) or temporalis (TM)), to determine whether secondary hypersensitivity is sex dependent, and to investigate whether primary-site stimulation leading to masticatory myalgia induces gene expression plasticity at secondary referred sites that may contribute to the secondary hypersensitivity phenomenon.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study adheres to the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [13]. All animal care and experimental procedures complied with the ethical guidelines of the United States Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care (PHS Policy), Use of Laboratory Animals, the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (the Guide), and the International Council for Laboratory Animal Science (ICLAS) has published ethical guidelines. Additionally, we followed guidelines from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Society for Neuroscience (SfN) to minimize the number of animals used and their suffering. All mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia followed immediately by decapitation. Isoflurane ensured that the mice were unconscious, while dislocation provided for expedient euthanasia. Decapitation assured death. This procedure minimized animal distress and was recommended by the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. All animal experiments conformed to protocols approved by the University Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio (UTHSCSA) and the University of Maryland Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The IACUC protocol numbers in UTHSCSA are 20190114AR, 20220064AR and 20220069AR.

Key reagents and mouse lines

Mice were housed under controlled conditions (≈22°C), relative humidity 40–60%, and a 12-h light-dark cycle with lights on at 6:00 AM. Food and water were available ad libitum in their home cages. Experiments were conducted on wild-type adult (2–4-month-old) C57Bl/6 male and female mice purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Crude collagenase type II (Col; >125 units per mg; Cat: LS004214) preparations were acquired from Worthington (Lakewood, NJ). Colibri Retractors (item No: 17000–03) were acquired from Fine Science Tools (Foster City, CA). The vibration stimulation of muscles was performed using a Point Relief massager (Fabrication Enterprises, Elmsford, NY).

Muscle stimulation paradigms and masticatory myalgia models

In this study, we have used the following muscle stimulation paradigms and masticatory myalgia models:

Col MM model [14]

Left side MM was injected with 10 ml Col solution. Injection was performed once or repetitively every 4th day. Col dosage was 0.2 U, 0.5 U, or 10 U. These injections were performed under isoflurane anesthesia. The area of injection was swabbed with 70% alcohol beforehand. The site for injection was identified by palpating the zygomatic arch and the body of the mandible. A 28-gauge needle was inserted into the point inferior to the posterior third of the zygomatic arch and midway between the zygomatic arch and the body of the mandible. The needle was advanced in an anterior direction in about 3 mm. After a gentle aspiration, indicating that the blood vessel was not damaged, solutions were injected. PBS served as the vehicle control.

Col MM with repetitive gentle MM stimulation model (adapted from [14])

Left side MM was injected with 10 ml Col. Dosages of Col were 0.2 U or 1 U. Gentle repetitive MM stimulations (2x90 sec) were applied every day by the Point Relief vibrator using a sharp rubber insert. Gentle repetitive MM stimulations were always applied after measurements of mechanical hypersensitivity. Col injections and vibration stimuli were performed under isoflurane anesthesia.

Col TM model (adapted from [14])

Left side TM was injected with 10 ml Col. The distance from the eye to the ear canal is measured and divided by 2 to determine the injection site (7.23/2 = 3.62). The needle is inserted at a 45-degree angle and advanced 1.5 mm into the temporalis muscle for injection (Figure S1A). Injection was performed once or repetitively every 4th day. Col dosage was 0.2 U, 0.5 U or 10 U. These injections were performed under isoflurane anesthesia. Injection was performed into the middle part of the TM. The syringe’s needle was directed slightly parallel to the head. A needle penetrated the muscle, and the injected solution did not generate “bubbles” or air pockets under the skin.

Forst mouth opening (FMO) model (adapted from [15])

Mice’s mouths were forcibly opened by placement of a 3 cm Colibri Retractor along the top and bottom incisors following anesthetization. Mouth stretching lasted 5-, 15-, or 45-min. FMO was performed once or repeated every 4th day. Mice were anesthetized by injecting 40 μl of a cocktail of 156 μl ketamine + 250 μl dexmedetomidine into one thigh muscle. Once animals were anesthetized, forceps were used to open the mouth and put the refractor inside the mouth, where the teeth hold it in place. Mice were woken up by an intramuscular injection of 40 µl of Revertidine.

Measurements of orofacial pain and headache-like behavior

Hypersensitivity, gene expression, and behavioral responses are strongly influenced by circadian rhythms and can fluctuate throughout the day. To minimize variability and ensure consistency, all habituation procedures and behavioral experiments were conducted during a defined circadian window, specifically Zeitgeber Time (ZT) 2–5, which corresponds to 9:00 AM to 12:00 PM for our light-dark cycle. In the present study, we measured ipsilateral pain responses. In our previous work, we assessed contralateral sensitivity following CFA and collagenase stimulation of the masseter muscle. Our findings indicated that this secondary hypersensitivity does not cross the midline and does not produce substantial changes in contralateral sensitivity compared to baseline [14, 16].

Measurement of mechanical thresholds with von Frey filaments

At UTHSCSA, the mechanical hypersensitivity of the orofacial region was assessed as previously described [14, 17, 18]. Briefly, mechanical hypersensitivity was assessed in several locations of an orofacial region, including the left side of the MM [14, 18], TMJ [14] and periorbital areas [17, 19] (see schematic on Fig. 1A). To avoid any anticipatory movement from mice during von Frey application, the mice were habituated to small cages for 3 days, followed by habituation to a designated area on a table and experimenter’s hand for an additional 3 days. During the last 3 days, mice were also habituated to von Frey filament (0.07–0.6 g) stimulations in different orofacial areas. Measurements with von Frey were conducted on the animals kept in hands without restraint. Habituated naive mice that did not respond to 0.6 g von Frey filaments, which is considered baseline, were selected for the experimental procedure [14, 17–19]. Experimental mice were probed with 0.008–0.6 g filaments to the orofacial areas using an up-down approach [14, 17–19]. Intervals between von Frey filament applications were varied but were always > 30 sec. Mechanical thresholds were assessed and calculated as previously described [14, 17–19].

Measurement of mechanical sensitivity

At the University of Maryland, the mechanical sensitivity test of the orofacial region was performed as previously described [20]. Briefly, mice were habituated by holding them in the experimenter’s hand wearing a leather work glove. A series of calibrated von Frey filaments were applied to the skin above the injured MM. An active withdrawal of the head from the probing filament was defined as a response. Each von Frey filament was applied 5 times at intervals of 5–10 seconds. The response frequencies [(the number of responses/number of stimuli) X100%] to a range of von Frey filament forces were determined, and a stimulus-response frequency (S-R) curve was plotted. After non-linear regression analysis, an EF50 value, defined as the effective von Frey filament force (g) that produces a 50% response frequency, was derived from the S-R curve (Prism, GraphPad) [20]. The injection of Col into the MM resulted in a leftward shift of the S-R curve and a reduction of EF50, suggesting the development of mechanical hypersensitivity.

Measurement of conditioned place avoidance behavior (CPA)

At the University of Maryland, for the CPA test [20, 21], animals were placed within a 42 (L) x 18 (W) x 18 (H) cm Plexiglass chamber. One half of the chamber was made with white plates (light area), and the other half was made with black plates (dark area). During the test, animals were allowed unrestricted movement throughout the test chambers for the 30-min test period. Mice normally prefer to stay in dark areas. Testing began immediately with suprathreshold mechanical stimulation (117 mN von Frey monofilament) applied to the facial skin at 15-sec intervals. The mechanical stimulus was applied to the skin above the injured muscle when the animal was within the preferred dark area and to the facial skin on the non-injured side when the animal was within the non-preferred light side. Based on the location of the animal at each 15-sec interval, the mean percentage of time spent in the black (dark) or white (light) chamber was calculated. Staying in the preferred dark area was associated with an aversive to painful stimulus after Col injection and the injured mouse tended to leave the dark side to avoid the aversive stimulus. An increase in the time spent in the light area suggests an increase in stimulus aversiveness.

RNA seq transcriptomic data generation, analyses, and statistics

To minimize variability and ensure consistency, all tissue collections were conducted during a defined circadian window, specifically ZT 2–5, which corresponds to 9:00 AM to 12:00 PM for our light-dark cycle. MM was dissected from left side. Dissected MM included deep, middle and superficial parts, and maximally excluded tendons attaching MM to zygomatic bone and mandible. Frozen and sliced MM as previously described [22] and freshly dissected entire dura matter were homogenized in Rn-easy solution (Qiagen) using a Bead Mill Homogenizer (Omni International, Kennesaw, GA). Extraction of RNA, RNA quality and integrity control, cDNA library preparation with oligo-dT primers followed the Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA sample preparation guide (Illumina, San Diego, CA), and sequencing procedure with Illumina HiSeq 3000 platform with 30–50x106 total reading depth with each read RNA having about 50 base pairs were previously described in detail [14, 18, 23]. Total RNA with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) between 7 and 9 were used in bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experiments. This is a critical point, since power analysis is based on these numbers for the quality of total RNA. Post-sequencing de-multiplexing with CASAVA, the combined raw reads were aligned to the mouse genome build UCSC/mm10 using TopHat2 default settings, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 after performing median normalization, also previously described in detail [14, 18, 23].

Quality control statistical analysis of outliers, intergroup variability, distribution levels, principal component analysis (PCA), and hierarchical clustering analysis were performed to statistically validate the experimental data. Multiple correction test was performed with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to generate adjusted p-value (Padj).

To minimize a risk of Type I error in bulk RNA-seq experiments, we implemented several rigorous quality control and filtering strategies. First, two separate control sets (n = 3–4) were compared to each other. These sets were considered acceptable if no more than 50 DEGs with Padj < 0.05 were revealed. Second, RNA sample with RIN < 8 was excluded from analysis to ensure high-quality input material. Third, samples were required to cluster closely within their respective control or experimental groups, confirming consistency and minimal batch effects. The reported here, n = 3–4, refers specifically to the samples that passed these three quality control criteria.

If not specified in the text, criteria for selecting DEGs were expression levels with Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (RPKM)>1, fold-change (FC)>1.5, and statistically significant DEGs with Padj < 0.05. Venn diagrams were generated using https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/. Genes were clustered according to biological processes using the PANTHER-db software (http://www.pantherdb.org/).

Statistical analyses

Sample size calculations were based on our previous experience in RNA-seq and behavioral experiments on linear and inbred C57-black mice [14, 17–19, 23, 24]. Accordingly, in behavioral experiments, we estimate that 6–8 mice will achieve > 80% statistical power to achieve p < 0.05 difference in the measurement of hypersensitivity. For bulk RNA-seq, a sample size of triple quality control passed 3–4 mice per group (see the previous paragraph), using a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05, will achieve > 80% statistical power to detect a 1.5-fold change in gene expression with an estimated standard deviation of 0.5. We also assume 1% of genes are differentially expressed in the system, or 100 DEGs among the typical 10,000 expressed genes in tissues profiled. Using Scotty [25], we estimate that 40 million reads per sample are needed.

GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Differences between groups were assessed by unpaired t-test, 1-way or 2-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. A difference is accepted as statistically significant when p < 0.05. When it is appropriate, interaction F ratios, and the associated p values are reported in the text.

Results

Induction and measurement of secondary hypersensitivity

To induce masticatory myalgia associated with secondary hypersensitivity, we employed multiple experimental paradigms involving stimulation of either the MM or the TM. In humans with local myalgia or myofascial pain, the affected muscle does not always exhibit clear tissue damage or overt inflammation. To better reflect this variability, we employed different stimulation paradigms in mice. Specifically, we used a high dose of collagenase to model myalgia associated with inflammation, and a low dose of collagenase to approximate conditions with minimal or no inflammation. In addition, we applied non-invasive and non-chemical stimulation approaches, including gentle vibration applied to the skin overlying the MM and FMO, to further mimic myalgia-like states without inducing significant tissue injury.

Mechanical hypersensitivity, which could be indicative of masticatory myalgia, was assessed by probing the orofacial region over the MM, adjacent to the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), using von Frey filaments (Fig. 1A; green arrow) [14, 18]. In humans, von Frey filaments primarily stimulate the skin due to their small tip size, and that an algometer is generally preferred for assessing deep, myogenic pain. However, von Frey situation differs in mice due to their smaller tissue dimensions. In mice, von Frey stimulation can effectively engage not only cutaneous receptors but also underlying muscle afferents and used for measuring muscle hypersensitivity [26, 27]. In support of this, activation of nerve fibers innervating the masseter muscle has been observed following von Frey stimulation in mice (unpublished data).

Secondary hypersensitivity was evaluated by measuring mechanical hypersensitivity at sites anatomically distinct from the stimulated muscle. Specifically, following MM stimulation, secondary hypersensitivity was assessed at the periorbital region. In contrast, following TM stimulation, secondary hypersensitivity was evaluated at both the MM and periorbital regions (Fig. 1A; red arrow). Other commonly used approaches for assessing orofacial pain, such as facial grimace scoring, chewing or bite-force tests, food consumption assays, conditioned place preference (CPP), and conditioned place avoidance (CPA), were not employed to examine secondary hypersensitivity, as these methods do not allow clear discrimination between hypersensitivity at primary versus secondary referred sites.

It is important to note that mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM region may reflect masticatory myalgia but could also involve arthrogenic TMJ pain. Currently, there are no established methods in rodents that can reliably distinguish between these pain sources. In this respect, we monitored that intra-masseter muscle injection did not contaminate TMJ, which is on substantial distance from injection site (Figure S2C, D). Mechanical hypersensitivity in the periorbital region, as assessed by von Frey probing, is commonly interpreted as headache-like behavior [17, 19, 28]. This interpretation is based on the premise that central sensitization develops in second-order nociceptive neurons innervating the periorbital area. Nevertheless, periorbital pain is characteristic of certain, but not all, headache subtypes, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Secondary hypersensitivity after masseter muscle stimulation

The experimental schematic for MM stimulation using a single high-dose (10 U) intramuscular injection of Col or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as a vehicle control is shown in Fig. 1B(upper panel). Following habituation and treatment with Col or vehicle, mechanical hypersensitivity over the MM and periorbital areas were monitored longitudinally in male mice (Fig. 1B). Robust mechanical hypersensitivity over the MM area persisted for up to 16 days, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 1C) [14]. In contrast, mechanical hypersensitivity over the periorbital area was observed in only 30% of the animals, was mild in intensity, and lasted approximately up to 2 days (Fig. 1D). These findings indicate that stimulation of the MM with a single high-dose Col injection reliably induces pronounced and long-lasting hypersensitivity at the primary site, whereas secondary hypersensitivity at a periorbital area is mild and inconsistent occurring in only a subset of male mice.

A high-dose (10 U) Col injection induced substantial inflammation [14], whereas clinical characterization of masticatory myalgia indicated that facial muscles typically exhibit no-to-little inflammatory responses [29, 30]. Because low-dose Col does not induce inflammation in the MM [14], we stimulated MM using repetitive intramuscular injections of low-dose Col (0.2–0.5 U; Fig. 1B, lower panel). This paradigm produced persistent hypersensitivity over the MM area for as long as Col was administered repeatedly (Fig. 1E). In contrast, hypersensitivity at a periorbital area was not detected at any time point during the experimental period (Fig. 1F shows days 1–7 and days 19–25 as representative examples). These results demonstrate that repetitive low-dose Col stimulation of the MM induces persistent myalgia without eliciting secondary hypersensitivity at any evaluated time point in all tested male mice.

There is general agreement that women are at least 2–4 times more likely than men to experience pain induced by masticatory activity [31–33]. In line with this observation, we conducted an independent study at the University of Maryland to determine whether intramuscular Col treatment produces sex-dependent differences in masticatory myalgia. Col induced mechanical hypersensitivity in a dose-dependent manner in both males (two-way ANOVA; F (21,192) = 6.54, p < 0.0001; n = 7; Fig. 2A) and females (two-way ANOVA; F (21,192) = 8.01, p < 0.0001; n = 7; Fig. 2B). At the lower dose of 0.2 U Col, mechanical hypersensitivity did not differ between females and males (two-way ANOVA; F (7,96) = 0.43, p = 0.88; n = 7; Fig. 2C). In contrast, increasing the Col dose to 10 U revealed sex differences, with myalgia persisting slightly longer in males than in females (two-way ANOVA; F (7,96) = 4.62, p = 0.0002; n = 7; Fig. 2D). Because patients with TMD-M frequently report spontaneous pain [34, 35], we assessed this pain component using the conditioned place avoidance (CPA) assay in both sexes [20]. Male mice receiving a 10 U Col injection into the MM exhibited robust CPA behavior at day 10, which remained significant at day 21 (one-way ANOVA; F (2,18) = 113.9, p < 0.0001; n = 7; Fig. 2E). Baseline responses in females were comparable to those observed in males (unpublished data [20]). Similarly, administration of 10 U Col into the MM of female mice induced CPA behavior at day 10 that was comparable to that seen in males (Fig. 2F vs. Fig. 2E). By day 21 post-injection, spontaneous pain was modestly reduced in females compared with males (28.75 ± 4.09 in males vs. 22.14 ± 2.91 in females; Fig. 2E vs. Fig. 2F). Collectively, these findings indicate that Col-induced masticatory myalgia following MM stimulation is slightly more pronounced and longer lasting in males than in females.

Masticatory myalgia is thought to develop as a result of muscle ischemia caused by sustained, mild, repetitive overloading of the facial and deeper masticatory muscles [36, 37]. To model this condition, we applied daily mild vibration to the MM area (2 × 180 s per day) at locations indicated by the blue and green arrows in Fig. 3A. Repetitive mild vibration alone did not induce hypersensitivity in the MM and periorbital areas in male mice. However, when daily vibration was combined with a single low-dose intramuscular injection of Col (1 U), hypersensitivity in the MM area was prolonged, and hypersensitivity in the periorbital area emerged in male mice (F(10,72) = 4.06; p = 0.0002; n = 5; Fig. 3B, C). Hypersensitivity in the periorbital area persisted for as long as vibration stimulation was maintained. Similar effects were observed in both male and female mice (Fig. 3D, E). Nevertheless, when the Col dose was further reduced to 0.2 U, daily vibration-induced hypersensitivity in the periorbital area reached statistical significance only in female mice (males: F(8,90) = 1.10, p = 0.37, n = 6; females: F(8,89) = 2.71, p = 0.01, n = 6; Fig. 3E, F).

Together, these findings indicate that induction of secondary hypersensitivity at a periorbital area following stimulation over MM area requires a combination of stimuli; specifically, a single low-dose Col injection together with repetitive mild vibration. Moreover, this paradigm reveals a degree of sex-dependent sensitivity in the development of secondary hypersensitivity.

Secondary hypersensitivity following temporalis muscle stimulation

Stimulation and functional overload of the TM have been implicated in the development of TMD-M [38–41]. Accordingly, we investigated whether TM stimulation induces referred pain. The TM was stimulated either by a single intramuscular injection of a high dose of Col (10 U; Fig. 4A) or by repetitive intramuscular injections of low-dose Col (0.2–0.5 U; Fig. 4B). Mechanical hypersensitivity was assessed at the ipsilateral MM region and at the periorbital area (Fig. 1A, B). A single 10 U Col injection into the TM induced mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM region that persisted for up to 12 days (two-way ANOVA; F(14,115) = 29.87, p < 0.0001; n = 6; Fig. 4C). In addition, this paradigm produced robust mechanical hypersensitivity in the periorbital area lasting up to 9 days in all tested male mice (two-way ANOVA; F (15,115) = 34.8, p < 0.0001; n = 6; Fig. 4D). Repetitive low-dose Col injections into the TM resulted in more prolonged mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM region compared with a single high-dose injection (Fig. 4C), although the duration was shorter than that observed following repetitive low-dose Col stimulation of the MM (Fig. 1E). This TM stimulation paradigm also induced chronic mechanical hypersensitivity in the periorbital area of male mice (Fig. 4D).

We next examined the effects of TM stimulation by Col on secondary hypersensitivity in female mice. Similar to the responses observed in males, both single and repetitive TM stimulation with high and low doses of Col induced significant mechanical hyperalgesia in the MM region (two-way ANOVA; F(13,123) = 33.97, p < 0.0001; n = 5–6; Fig. 4E). In addition, single and repetitive TM stimulation with high and low doses of Col produced long-lasting mechanical hypersensitivity at a periorbital region (two-way ANOVA; F(13,122) = 50.42, p < 0.0001; n = 5–6; Fig. 4F). Secondary hypersensitivity induced by TM stimulation with Col exhibited clear sex-dependent differences. A single high-dose Col injection into the TM produced significantly longer-lasting mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM region in females compared with males (two-way ANOVA; F(10,94) = 12.8, p < 0.0001; n = 5–6; Fig. 4G). This TM stimulation paradigm also resulted in more persistent hypersensitivity at a periorbital of females (two-way ANOVA; F (10,94) = 8.8, p < 0.0001; n = 5–6; Fig. 4H). In contrast, repetitive low-dose TM stimulation produced more pronounced mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM region in males than in females (two-way ANOVA; F(14,149) = 6.317, p < 0.0001; n = 6; Figure S1B). Similarly, repetitive TM stimulation with lower doses of Col elicited longer-lasting referred periorbital hypersensitivity in males compared to females (two-way ANOVA; F(15,158) = 6.762, p < 0.0001; n = 6; Figure S1C).

Collectively, these experiments demonstrate that stimulation of the TM with Col across multiple experimental paradigms induces robust and long-lasting mechanical hypersensitivity at anatomically distinct sites, including the MM region and the periorbital area. The presence of sustained hypersensitivity at distal sites suggests that TM stimulation is sufficient to evoke pronounced orofacial secondary hypersensitivity, which could encompass both masticatory myalgia and headache-like behaviors.

Secondary hypersensitivity following forced mouth opening (FMO)

Masticatory muscle overload is thought to contribute to the development of myalgia [42, 43]. One commonly used method to model such overload is FMO paradigm [44, 45]. It should be noted, however, that in addition to modeling TMD-M, prolonged mouth opening may also affect the TMJ [46, 47]. In this context, we assessed mechanical hypersensitivity at the MM region and at the periorbital area using von Frey filaments (Fig. 1A, green and red arrows). Distinguishing between myogenous and arthrogenic TMD in mice using von Frey testing over the MM region is inherently challenging, and probably is not possible.

Several FMO paradigms were evaluated (Fig. 5A). Mechanical hypersensitivity over the MM region was comparable following either a single 45-min mouth stretch or four consecutive days of 45-min stretches (Fig. 5B). Reducing the duration of a single stretch from 45 min to 10 min did not significantly shorten the duration of MM mechanical hypersensitivity (Fig. 5C). Based on these findings, we implemented a paradigm consisting of repetitive 10-min mouth stretches applied every 5 days.

The stress model was used as a positive control in this study. Stress is known to trigger a systemic pain condition that can be detected in both the periorbital region and the hind paw [48]. Accordingly, we included two experimental groups: FMO alone and FMO combined with stress (FMO + stress).

Because it is preferable to evaluate a positive control within the same cohort of mice, we first administered FMO and assessed whether it induced referred pain. After determining the response to FMO alone, the same mice were then subjected to stress in order to evaluate stress-induced secondary hypersensitivity within the same individuals. To achieve this, FMO treatment was followed by a 3-day repetitive restraint stress protocol beginning on day 11, as previously described [48, 49] (see Fig. 5A). This design allowed us to directly compare the effects of FMO alone and FMO combined with stress in the same animals, thereby reducing inter-animal variability and strengthening the validity of the positive control. This protocol produced persistent mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM region. In male mice, hypersensitivity over the MM region was similar with or without stress during the first 20 days (Fig. 5D). This FMO paradigm induced short-lasting (1-day) periorbital mechanical hypersensitivity in 66% of mice (8 of 12; Fig. 5E). Mice subjected to repetitive FMO alone did not exhibit periorbital hypersensitivity beyond the 1-day time point (Fig. 5E). In contrast, the addition of restraint stress as a positive control resulted in long-lasting periorbital mechanical hypersensitivity beginning on day 14 after the first FMO (Fig. 5E) [48, 49].

Taken together, the FMO model reliably produced sustained mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM region, indicative of masticatory myalgia, but elicited only mild and transient periorbital hypersensitivity in male mice unless combined with a positive control - stress.

Gene plasticity in masseter muscle and dura mater after stimulation of temporal muscle

It was suggested that secondary hypersensitivity could involve both central and peripheral mechanisms in affected tissues [11]. However, it remains unknown whether secondary hypersensitivity induces changes such as gene and/or cell plasticity at the secondary referred sites. Here, we investigated whether there is gene plasticity at the secondary referred sites. To do so, we examined whether stimulation of the TM induces bulk RNA sequencing defined transcriptomic changes in the MM and dura mater.

Based on the observed secondary hypersensitivity at periorbital sites following a single injection of 10 U Col into the TM in both male and female mice (Fig. 4D, F), dura mater tissues were collected 2 days post-TM injection (n = 3–6). Dura mater from male and female mice injected with saline into the TM served as controls (n = 3–4). Transcriptomic changes were assessed using bulk RNA sequencing [14]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined using the following criteria: fold change (FC) >1.5, RPKM > 1, and adjusted p-value (Padj) <0.05. In males, 439 genes were upregulated, and 445 were downregulated with FC > 2; using FC > 1.5, 1,013 genes were upregulated, and 1,019 were downregulated. In females, 880 genes were upregulated, and 549 were downregulated with FC > 2; using FC > 1.5, 1,522 genes were upregulated, and 1,321 were downregulated. Gene ontology (GO) clustering using PANTHER-db revealed that upregulated DEGs in both male and female dura mater were predominantly associated with biological processes related to tissue repair and morphogenesis, axonogenesis, gliogenesis, synaptogenesis, and neurogenesis (Fig. 6A, B). These up-regulated processes - tissue repair, axonogenesis and synaptogenesis - play essential roles in the regulation of the nociceptive pathways at the periphery [50–55]. In contrast, downregulated DEGs were primarily enriched in immune system-related processes in both sexes (Fig. 6C, D).

(C, D) biological processes for down-regulated DEGs in the dura mater at 2d post-Col (10U) into the TM of male (C) and female mice (D). The X-axis on A-D represents the number of DEGs. Periorbital hypersensitivity following TM stimulation exhibited sex-dependent trajectories in the development of hypersensitivity (Figs. 4H, S1B). Although overall GO profiles were broadly similar between males and females, several sex-specific differences were observed between GO data. Among upregulated DEGs, males showed enrichment in gliogenesis-related processes, whereas females demonstrated greater enrichment in synaptogenesis and neurotransmission-related pathways (Fig. 6A, B). In addition, morphogenesis-related processes differed between sexes: muscle morphogenesis was predominant in females, while bone remodeling-associated processes were additionally enriched in males (Fig. 6A, B). For downregulated DEGs, males uniquely exhibited enrichment of skin development-related processes, whereas females showed a more pronounced representation of immune system-associated pathways compared to males (Fig. 6C, D).

Despite the overall similarity in GO profiles for undergoing plasticity in the dura mater, the actual overlap of individual DEGs between sexes was relatively limited, with only 15.5% (calculated based on the total number of DEGs) of upregulated genes and 13.9% of downregulated genes being common for males and females (Fig. 6E, F). Direct comparison of gene expressions in female versus male dura mater showed that 88 DEGs were significantly higher expressed in female dura mater compared to male dura (Padj < 0.05; FC > 2), whereas 10 DEGs were predominantly detected in male dura mater. Several of these sex-selective DEGs contribute or could potentially contribute to the regulation of nociceptive pathways (Fig. 2SA). Hbegf and Bdkrb2 are upregulated in response to tissue injury and inflammation and are known to participate in peripheral sensitization [56, 57]. Rims3, Grm6, Gdf3, Clcn6, Fgf11, and Sufu could potentially be involved in synaptic plasticity, neuronal excitability, remodeling of the presynaptic active zone, and peripheral sensitization. In addition, Gdf3 may play a role in neuro-immune interaction [58].

Headache attacks often exhibit clear circadian patterns, and these rhythms can be disrupted by sleep-wake disturbances or stress, which are well-established triggers of headache [59–62]. Consistent with this, robust circadian regulation within the trigeminal ganglion has been demonstrated at the behavioral, transcriptomic, and pharmacogenetic levels in mouse models of headache [62]. In this context, our data show that secondary hypersensitivity originating from the MM and spreading to the periorbital region is associated with altered expression of circadian rhythm-related genes in the dura mater in both males and females (Fig. 6A, B). To further investigate this observation, we performed a bioinformatic “clock-check” analysis on the DEG dataset from the dura mater following masseter muscle stimulation (Figure S2B). This analysis revealed that genes involved in circadian regulation were significantly affected. Notably, key master regulators of the circadian clock, including Clock and Bmal1, exhibited sex-dependent patterns of regulation. These findings suggest that circadian mechanisms may play an important role in mediating transcriptional responses associated with secondary hypersensitivity and may contribute to sex-specific differences in headache-related pathophysiology.

Overall, these findings indicate that, although the types of biological processes undergoing plasticity during secondary hypersensitivity are broadly similar in male and female dura mater. In contrast, the specific genes driving these processes are largely sex-specific, suggesting differential regulation of pain signaling pathways between sexes.

Stimulation of the TM with repeated low-dose Col injections induces chronic mechanical hypersensitivity in the MM of male and female mice (Fig. 4C, E). We previously reported that the resolution phase of Col injection-induced mechanical hypersensitivity in MM is accompanied by a sharp up-regulation of immune system-related DEGs [14]. Based on this, we investigated whether repetitive stimulation of the TM with low doses of Col (see schematic in Fig. 4B) induces gene plasticity in MM from female mice at 27 days after the first Col injection into the TM, corresponding to the hypersensitivity resolution time points (Fig. 4E). This treatment resulted in significant gene expression changes in MM. Specifically, 234 DEGs were up-regulated, and 177 DEGs were down-regulated with an FC > 2, whereas 329 DEGs were up-regulated and 259 DEGs down-regulated with FC > 1.5. GO clustering using PANTHER-db revealed that the up-regulated biological processes included muscle and bone development, extracellular matrix remodeling, gliogenesis, neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and especially genes associated with immune system activity (Fig. 7A). In contrast, relatively few biological processes were down-regulated in MM (Fig. 7B). Key processes in MM such as extracellular matrix remodeling, synaptogenesis, neurogenesis, gliogenesis, and particularly inflammatory pathways were consistently present for both treatment paradigms: 6d post-10 U Col into MM (resolution time point) and 27d post-repetitive Col into TM (Fig. 7A, B versus data in [14]). The comparison of regulated DEGs in MM after above mentioned MM and TM treatments using a Venn diagram indicated that of the 329 up-regulated DEGs in MM following TM stimulation, 84 were also up-regulated after direct Col injection into MM (Fig. 7C). These shared genes are relevant to nociceptive signaling and are involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, synaptogenesis, and inflammatory processes (Figure S2E). Similarly, 58 down-regulated DEGs were common to both TM and MM Col treatment paradigms (Fig. 7D).

In summary, stimulation of the TM induces secondary sex-dependent mechanical hypersensitivity in the periorbital area and over the MM region. This secondary mechanical hypersensitivity is accompanied by gene plasticity in the dura mater and MM, highlighting overlapping molecular pathways involved in both primary and secondary hypersensitivity, and implying possible contributions of this gene plasticity to the secondary hypersensitivity phenomenon.

Discussion

A substantial proportion of patients with TMD-M experience widespread and referred pain or secondary hypersensitivity, including headache, widespread facial myalgia, TMJ pain, and neck pain [4, 6, 7]. Clinical evidence suggests that both peripheral and central mechanisms contribute to referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity in the head and neck region [8–12]. However, the specific peripheral mechanisms underlying referred pain and secondary hypersensitivity are not yet fully understood. Previous research has mainly focused on the role of the primary (initiating) site in peripheral sensitization, while the potential contribution of the referred (secondary) peripheral site has received limited attention.

As a first step, we have established a preclinical model for the secondary hypersensitivity in the head and neck area. TMD-M are believed to develop as a result of muscle ischemia caused by sustained and repetitive overloading of the masticatory muscles [36, 37]. Ischemia-induced muscle injury is associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) dysfunction [63–65] and collagen degradation [66, 67]. To replicate these clinical features of TMD-M, we attempted to induce secondary hypersensitivity using several approaches: single and repeated intramuscular injections of Col, administration of low and high doses of Col, vibration-based stimulation over the masticatory muscles, and FMO. Secondary hypersensitivity was operationally defined as mechanical hypersensitivity measured by von Frey filaments at sites distant from the area of stimulation. For example, after stimulating the MM or TM, hypersensitivity was assessed in the periorbital region; conversely, following TM stimulation, hypersensitivity was measured over the MM region.

Mechanical hypersensitivity assessed with von Frey filaments was selected for several reasons. First, von Frey testing is part of quantitative sensory testing (QST) commonly used in TMD clinical research and is frequently applied to masticatory muscles, the TMJ, referred pain areas (e.g., periorbital region, neck), and control sites (e.g., forearm) [68–70]. Second, QST with von Frey filaments enables measurement of both the Mechanical Detection Threshold (MDT) and the Mechanical Pain Threshold (MPT) [69, 70]. Third, commonly used pain assessment methods, such as grimace scales, bite force, food intake, chewing behavior, CPP or CPA, reflect overall evoked or spontaneous pain and are not suitable for identifying referred pain specifically [70]. Using multiple stimulation protocols and measuring von Frey thresholds at distant sites, we found that stimulation of the TM, but not the MM and FMO, produced reliable, robust, longer-lasting, and sex-dependent secondary mechanical hypersensitivity in the head and neck region.

We assessed mechanical hypersensitivity over the MM region and at the periorbital area. In both rodents and humans, mechanical hypersensitivity in the periorbital region is commonly interpreted as a headache-like response [17–19, 69]. This interpretation is based on the concept that central sensitization develops in second- or third-order nociceptive neurons, leading to hypersensitivity in areas beyond the primary site of injury. Similarly, a von Frey stimulus applied to the skin over the MM region is often considered indicative of masticatory myalgia in experimental models [14, 16, 18, 70]. However, both approaches have inherent limitations. Von Frey filaments directly stimulate the skin surface rather than directly targeting deeper tissues such as muscles, joints, or the dura mater. In addition, animals typically display more pronounced behavioral responses than humans, including attempts to remove the filament with the forepaw, exaggerated withdrawal reactions, and frequent facial grooming [71]. These behaviors may reflect heightened reactivity to cutaneous probing rather than pain originating exclusively from deeper structures. Despite these limitations, von Frey testing remains widely used in animal studies and QST on human patients. Importantly, von Frey-derived thresholds have been shown to correlate with other behavioral pain assessments, including chewing tests, grimace scales, CPA/CPP, bite force measurements, and light-avoidance tests [14, 17, 20, 72].

In clinical settings, expansion of receptive fields within a muscle region is often described as “referred pain,” a phenomenon frequently observed in the MM area [8, 9]. However, referred pain (unlike secondary hypersensitivity) extending from facial muscles to headache, or from the TM to the MM, involve perception (or feeling) component and likely recruits more complex neuronal circuitry. The MM is innervated by the masseteric nerve (MN), whereas the TM is innervated by the posterior deep temporal nerve (TN). Both the MN and TN branch from the inferior alveolar nerve, which originates from the mandibular division (V3) of the TG [73]. Some studies also suggest that the MN and TN may provide innervation to tissues surrounding the TMJ condyle [73]. In contrast, the dura mater and the skin of the periorbital region are innervated by the ophthalmic division (V1) of the trigeminal nerve. Secondary hypersensitivity is thought to arise from sensitization of second-order neurons in the brainstem, which serve as convergence points for primary afferent inputs from different regions of the head and neck [8, 9]. Given this anatomical organization, neural convergence between the TM, MM, and TMJ, structures primarily innervated by branches of V3, may facilitate secondary hypersensitivity among these regions. By comparison, secondary hypersensitivity from masticatory muscles to dura mater (involving V1-innervated territories) likely requires broader trigeminal convergence and more complex central integration.

However, alternative explanations should be considered for the observed secondary hypersensitivity in the periorbital area following TM stimulation. One possibility is that TM stimulation inadvertently affected adjacent structures, such as the periosteum overlying the skull, which may receive collateral innervation from fibers associated with the dura mater [74]. In addition, diffusion or leakage of the injected substance from the TM could potentially influence nearby cranial structures, including the skull bone marrow, which is connected to the dura mater through bony channels. It has also been reported that, in the parietal dura mater, some sensory fibers travel through the nervus spinosus into the maxillary (V2) and mandibular (V3) divisions of the trigeminal ganglion [74]. Extensive or repeated TM injections could therefore activate dural-related pathways and contribute to secondary hypersensitivity in regions such as the MM or a periorbital area. Clinically, the DC/TMD classification includes the diagnosis “Headache attributed to TMD,” suggesting that myalgia of the TM may be perceived as headache in patients [75]. Hence, another possibility is that TM stimulation spreads to the overlying skin and secondarily diffuses toward the periorbital region, thereby contributing to secondary hypersensitivity. To evaluate potential diffusion, we performed pilot experiments using methylene blue injections into the TM (Fig. 2SC). Injection of concentrated (10%) methylene blue produced only minimal staining in the overlying skin, minimal-to-no detectable staining on the skull and no diffusion in TMJ area (Fig. 2SC vs SD), suggesting limited spread to adjacent tissues. Finally, the current understanding of TG projections to brainstem nuclei remains incomplete. This limited knowledge makes it difficult to fully explain why TM, but not MM, stimulation triggers headache-like hypersensitivity. Further studies with fluorophore or viral vector tracings are needed to clarify the convergent neuronal circuits linking the temporalis (via the temporal nerve) and masseteric pathways with dural afferents at the level of brainstem nuclei.

The contribution of local (peripheral) mechanisms to secondary hypersensitivity is well documented [11, 12]. These mechanisms primarily involve sensitization of neurons innervating the primary site from which the pain originates. However, our data suggest that peripheral tissues at the secondary (referred) sites may also substantially contribute to secondary hypersensitivity in the head and neck region. Specifically, TM stimulation induced gene plasticity in both the MM and the dura mater, indicating active molecular changes at sites distant from the initial stimulus. Notably, TM-induced gene plasticity in the MM was sex-dependent and partially resembled transcriptomic changes previously observed after direct MM stimulation [14]. TM stimulation also triggered gene plasticity in the dura mater. Importantly, the transcriptomic profiles of the MM and dura mater were markedly different. In the MM, gene plasticity predominantly involved pathways related to neuronal processes (e.g., synaptogenesis, axonogenesis) and tissue development (including ossification, muscle development, and mesenchymal processes). In contrast, additional to these processes, the dura mater exhibited upregulation of immune-related genes. Genes annotated as related to muscle and skin development in the dura mater likely reflect extracellular matrix remodeling, which pathway analysis tools such as PANTHER-db may categorize under developmental processes in other tissue types. Similarly, enrichment of synaptogenesis- and axonogenesis-related genes may be attributable to signaling cues released by non-neuronal cells, as well as to axonally localized mRNAs. These transcriptomic changes may result from activation of nerves innervating the MM and dura mater following TM stimulation, and possibly from systemic responses associated with pain or stress. However, unlike generalized stress responses described previously [49] (see also Fig. 5), TM stimulation did not produce detectable hypersensitivity in the hind paw (F (5, 68) = 7.149; p < 0.0001; * p < 0.05; **** p < 0.0001; n = 5–10; Figure S2F), suggesting that the observed molecular alterations were region-specific rather than global.

Limitation of the study

In addition to the limitations discussed above regarding the assignment and interpretation of von Frey data, several other shortcomings should be acknowledged. First, myalgia in clinical settings commonly includes both spontaneous pain and movement-evoked pain. However, mechanical hypersensitivity measured by von Frey filaments does not necessarily reflect spontaneous pain. Therefore, this study does not directly assess spontaneous secondary hypersensitivity. Second, although changes in gene expressions were identified in muscle and dural tissues, these alterations were not attributed to specific cell types. In particular, we did not determine whether the observed expression changes originated from neuronal, immune, glial, or other cell populations. Third, we did not examine function of DEGs involved in secondary hypersensitivity. Fourth, central mechanisms were not directly investigated. While peripheral changes were examined, potential alterations within central nociceptive pathways (e.g., brainstem or supra-brainstem processing) were not assessed. Finally, the effects induced by single injection into MM were relatively short-lived. This transient response may be related to the absence of sustained secondary hypersensitivity.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that stimulation of the TM, but not the MM, reliably produces referred mechanical hypersensitivity linking myalgia-like responses with headache-like behavior. This secondary hypersensitivity is accompanied by pronounced gene plasticity at anatomically distinct secondary (referred) sites, including the dura mater and the MM. Importantly, these transcriptomic changes occur in tissues that were not directly stimulated, indicating that peripheral molecular adaptations extend beyond the primary site of injury. The distinct gene expression profiles observed in the MM and dura mater further suggest that secondary tissues do not merely reflect central sensitization, but instead undergo active, site-specific biological remodeling. Together, these findings support the concept that gene plasticity at secondary (referred) peripheral sites could contribute to secondary hypersensitivity in the head and neck region. We propose that TM stimulation initiates a distributed peripheral network of molecular and neuronal adaptations across interconnected craniofacial tissues. This network may amplify and sustain pain signaling, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for the spatial spread of hypersensitivity between myalgia and headache in TMD.
