Dissecting epigenetic heterogeneity in single-cell DNA methylomes with a unified framework
Authors: Songming Tang, Siyu Li, Guangxin Zhang, Aoran Lyu, Han Li, Shengquan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-cell DNA methylation (scDNAm) sequencing offers unprecedented opportunities for dissecting epigenetic heterogeneity and gene regulatory mechanisms. However, most existing analytical methods are adapted from conventional single-cell sequencing frameworks, such as single-cell RNA sequencing, and fail to account for the unique characteristics of scDNAm data, including the widespread presence of explicit missing values and their distinct distribution patterns. To enable systematic exploration of DNA methylation-derived epigenetic variation at single-cell resolution, we propose scMethCraft, a versatile analytical toolkit. scMethCraft integrates multi-perspective genomic sequence features and position information of methylated regions via a hybrid neural network, and iteratively quantifies the cell-to-cell associations, enabling accurate modeling of scDNAm data. scMethCraft facilitates accurate reconstruction of DNA methylation landscapes and supports a spectrum of downstream analyses, including cell embedding, multi-source data integration, cell type annotation, epigenetic signal enhancement, and identification of differentially methylated regions, thereby significantly facilitating the identification and characterization of scDNAm heterogeneity. The integration of scMethCraft with complementary analytical workflows, including biological function enrichment, tissue-specific expression analysis, and partitioned heritability assessment, enables the discovery of biological insights into specific cellular subpopulations. Furthermore, scMethCraft identified oligodendrocyte-associated genes that are not well characterized in existing databases, providing an epigenetic perspective for investigating underlying biological mechanisms.

Introduction

Single-cell DNA methylation (scDNAm) sequencing has opened up unprecedented opportunities for exploring cellular epigenetic heterogeneity at an unparalleled resolution1. Genome-wide methylation maps of single cells show immense potential for unraveling the epigenetic landscapes underlying cellular diversity, gene regulation, and disease pathogenesis2.

In mammalian genomes, DNA methylation occurs predominantly at CpG sites3. Although numerous existing methods analyze scDNAm data at single-CpG site resolution4–9, their effectiveness is constrained by the intrinsic sparsity and noise of the scDNAm data, as well as the rapidly increasing throughput of sequencing technologies. Compared to single-CpG site analysis, the simplest and most common initial step in analyzing scDNAm data involves aggregating signals from multiple methylation sites through genome binning10–12. Specifically, by dividing the genome into fixed-length tiles (for example, 100 kbp) and calculating the average CpG methylation level within each tile for individual cells, a cell-by-region DNA methylation matrix can be generated, thus reducing the data volume, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio, and improving interpretability11. The common approach to analyze the scDNAm matrix is based on conventional pipelines designed for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) or single-cell assays for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq) data10,11.

However, due to considerable differences in the data structure of various omics, such a procedure is suboptimal. First, the scDNAm matrix contains a large number of explicit missing values. If no reads at all are within a given genomic region, the average methylation level for that region will be initialized as a not available (NA) value11. In contrast, scRNA-seq or scATAC-seq data do not contain such explicit missing values, as unobserved counts are typically treated as zeros13. Second, the value distribution of the scDNAm matrix differs from that of other omics data. Since genome binning represents the average methylation level ranges continuously between 0 and 1, which differs substantially from the read count distribution observed in scRNA-seq or scATAC-seq data4,14. Finally, DNA methylation data exhibit fundamental differences in numerical scale and biological semantics compared with other single-cell modalities. In scRNA-seq or scATAC-seq data, higher read counts typically correspond to stronger biological signals, such as higher gene expression or chromatin accessibility, and the majority of entries are zero15,16. In contrast, scDNAm data are commonly represented as regional methylation ratios, where larger values do not inherently indicate stronger biological signals and often correspond to lower gene expression3,11,17,18. This mismatch between numerical scale and biological interpretation means that analytical pipelines originally designed for scRNA-seq or scATAC-seq data may not optimally capture the biological information encoded in scDNAm matrices. As a consequence, directly applying such methods without adapting to the specific characteristics of methylation ratio data can lead to suboptimal representations and reduced performance11.

Although scDNAm sequencing is demonstrating its significance and providing unique biological insights, the development of computational and statistical methods for scDNAm data analysis is lagging behind that of scRNA-seq or scATAC-seq data. Methods specifically tailored for scDNAm data analysis remain limited and are still under active development. To the best of our knowledge, there is currently no standalone, straightforward, and user-friendly method capable of comprehensively processing and analyzing scDNAm matrices.

In this work, we develop scMethCraft, a single-cell DNA methylation comprehensive analysis framework toolkit that provides a complete pipeline for analyzing scDNAm matrices. The framework directly operates on scDNAm matrices with explicit NA values and integrates DNA sequence and genomic positional information through a hybrid neural network architecture. By incorporating an iteratively updated intercellular similarity module, scMethCraft improves modeling accuracy and reduces prediction bias. scMethCraft demonstrates superior performance across multiple analytical tasks. It achieves effective dimensionality reduction and robust cell clustering for scDNAm data. It also facilitates the integration of scDNAm datasets from diverse sources, mitigating batch effects on downstream analyses while providing a reliable foundation for cross-dataset cell-type annotation. In addition, scMethCraft imputes NA values while denoising scDNAm matrices through the incorporation of intercellular similarity information. Furthermore, scMethCraft encourages the identification of differentially methylated regions (DMRs). Through cell-type-specific DMR patterns, we identify the corresponding gene regulatory programs, tissue-specific expression enrichment, brain-specific heritability of disease risk, and associated gene ontology biological processes for several human brain cell types. In oligodendrocytes, we identify hypomethylated regions linked to genes that are underrepresented in existing annotation databases, including CellMarker19 and GeneCards20. In summary, scMethCraft supports multiple analytical tasks for the scDNAm matrix, provides a unified framework for the scDNAm data analysis pipeline, and offers insights into specific cellular populations.

scMethCraft characterizes epigenetic heterogeneity among cell populations in latent space

The high-dimensional nature of single-cell omics data poses significant challenges for conducting analytical workflows in the original high-dimensional feature space. A critical step in single-cell omics data analysis involves projecting high-dimensional data into a low-dimensional cell embedding that preserves intercellular relationships, known as dimensionality reduction21. Dimensionality reduction encourages multiple downstream analyses, including clustering of cellular subpopulations, visualization of relationships among distinct cell populations, and high-confidence cell type annotation. Thus, we first investigated the dimensionality reduction capability of scMethCraft for genome-wide scDNAm data. We collected 11 publicly available datasets, featuring diverse protocols, donors, and varying cell numbers, with well-annotated major type and subtype classifications (Supplementary Tables 1–3). We included the pipeline developed by Liu et al.22 and MethSCAn11 as baseline methods, both of which were specifically designed for scDNAm data analysis. In addition, we employed the dimensionality reduction methods implemented by EpiScanpy10 and SnapATAC221 as baseline methods, which were originally developed for other single-cell omics data types, but have been successfully applied to scDNAm matrices (Methods).

We performed unsupervised clustering on the cell embeddings generated by scMethCraft and the baseline methods using the Louvain algorithm with a binary search strategy23 (Methods). Following the best practice23–25, we evaluated clustering performance using five complementary metrics (Methods), including adjusted Rand index (ARI), adjusted mutual information (AMI), normalized mutual information (NMI), homogeneity score (Homo), and Fowlkes-Mallows index (FMI).

Compared with baseline methods, scMethCraft exhibits significant performance improvements. Across 11 datasets, scMethCraft consistently achieved high AMI scores (Fig. 2a). The mean AMI score of scMethCraft reached 0.77, representing a 3.12% improvement over MethSCAn and 5.38% over SnapATAC2 (Fig. 2b). scMethCraft demonstrated overall state-of-the-art performance across all five evaluation metrics and 11 datasets (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, we illustrated the advantages of scMethCraft by visualizing the cellular distributions using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP)26. Compared to the baseline methods that performed relatively well in quantitative evaluations, including EpiScanpy and MethSCAn, scMethCraft demonstrated superior capability in separating distinct major cell types and detecting subtle variations between cellular populations in the ACC dataset. For instance, OPCs, ODCs, and MGCs, all of which are glial cells and therefore exhibit a high degree of similarity, were difficult to cluster accurately using embeddings derived from EpiScanpy (Fig. 2e) and MethSCAn (Fig. 2f). In contrast, scMethCraft effectively distinguished these three cell types (Fig. 2g). Similarly, caudal ganglionic eminence-derived inhibitory neurons at the LAMP5 locus, including Lamp5 and Lamp5-Lhx6 types, were not clearly differentiated by the baseline methods (Fig. 2e,f). However, scMethCraft successfully captured the nuanced differences between these types, faithfully reflecting them in the resulting cell embeddings (Fig. 2g). Across these five particularly challenging cell types, scMethCraft achieved up to a 10% improvement in AMI compared to baseline methods. Similar results were also observed in the M1C dataset. Among the four major cell types, including Vip, Sncg, Lamp5, and Lamp5-Lhx6, EpiScanpy failed to capture the cellular heterogeneity among all these types, resulting in poor clustering performance (Fig. 2h). MethSCAn was able to distinguish the Lamp5-Lhx6 cells, but showed limited discriminative power for Vip and Sncg populations (Fig. 2i). Notably, only scMethCraft accurately captured the underlying heterogeneity across these cell types, leading to precise and biologically meaningful clustering with an approximately 20% improvement in AMI (Fig. 2j, Supplementary Figs. 3-5). Although the overall clustering improvements across entire datasets may be partially masked by abundant and easily separable cell types, these results on highly confusable cell populations highlight scMethCraft’s strength in resolving subtle biological differences, demonstrating its advantage in challenging and biologically relevant scenarios.

In summary, scMethCraft, as a toolkit leveraging both sequence information and intercellular relationships, generates accurate and robust low-dimensional embeddings for scDNAm data. Notably, scMethCraft exhibits a strong capability to capture subtle differences among cell types, even when they share highly similar DNAm patterns, thereby enabling researchers to more precisely distinguish closely related cellular populations.

scMethCraft enables multi-source data integration and facilitates automatic cell type annotation

Similar to scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data, scDNAm datasets derived from different sources, tissues, donors, or batches often exhibit systematic discrepancies, known as batch effects27,28. Performing downstream analyses without distinguishing batch effects from true biological variation can lead to misleading results25. For instance, we applied the analysis pipeline of EpiScanpy on the combined MTG and Pro datasets, which were collected from different brain regions and donors (Supplementary Table 1). As shown in Fig. 3a, b, pronounced batch effects emerged between batches, leading to clustering outcomes that primarily reflect inter-batch differences rather than true biological variation. This issue is exemplified by the L2/3-IT cells (highlighted by the purple box in Fig. 3b), which represent the most abundant cell type in the dataset. As presented in Fig. 3c, L2/3-IT cells from the MTG dataset were assigned to clusters 3 and 9, whereas those from the Pro dataset were grouped into clusters 2, 8, and 10. On the combined MTG-Pro dataset, scMethCraft effectively integrated cells from different batches while preserving biological variance and avoiding overcorrection (Fig. 3d,e). We further examined the cell clustering outcomes for L2/3-IT cells, highlighted by the purple box in Fig. 3e. As shown in Fig. 3f, the integrated L2/3-IT cells were primarily grouped into four clusters. Among these, three clusters (clusters 2, 4, and 5) were composed of a large number of L2/3-IT cells from both batches, with each cluster corresponding to a distinct, non-overlapping set of subtypes, suggesting that scMethCraft preserves subtype-specific structure across batches (Fig. 3g). We also examined MGE-derived SST cells and CGE-derived VIP cells, both of which exhibited pronounced batch effects before integration. scMethCraft achieved robust batch integration for both SST and VIP cells (Supplementary Fig. 9). Moreover, we designed a partially overlapping batch integration scenario in which selected cell types were present in only one batch, thereby mimicking a challenging integration setting. Under this condition, scMethCraft successfully aligned shared cell types across batches while preserving batch-specific cell populations as distinct groups, demonstrating accurate integration (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Moreover, we paired six human scDNAm datasets to generate three integrated datasets: MTG-Pro, A46-ACC, and A5A7-M1C. These were used to comprehensively evaluate the batch integration performance of scMethCraft. Following prior studies29, we assessed both batch correction metrics and biological variance conservation metrics (Supplementary Note 1). Except for Graph iLISI30 (not relying on annotation labels), all metrics were evaluated using both major types (namely MT) and subtypes (namely ST) annotations. Figure 3h summarizes the average performance of scMethCraft on batch correction metrics across the three datasets, while Fig. 3i presents the average biological variance conservation scores. scMethCraft consistently achieved significant improvements across all batch integration metrics. To further evaluate its performance in a multi-batch setting, we combined all six data sets as a Mixed dataset. scMethCraft improved all batch integration metrics compared to EpiScanpy (Fig. 3j), and the visualizations demonstrate that batch effects were significantly mitigated (Fig. 3k,l, and Supplementary Fig. 11). These results demonstrate that scMethCraft remains accurate even in complex, multi-batch scenarios, thereby facilitating the joint analysis of scDNAm data from diverse sources, allowing researchers to focus on biologically meaningful methylation pattern differences rather than technical variation.

When working with multiple datasets, where some have been manually curated and annotated while others represent newly generated data, a straightforward cell typing strategy is to designate the annotated datasets as the training set and the unannotated datasets as the test set31. A classifier is then trained on the labeled data to automatically predict cell types in the new, unlabeled datasets. Nevertheless, such an approach tends to disregard the inherent distributional discrepancies across datasets, which can result in inaccurate classification of closely related subtypes32. scMethCraft provides a cell-type annotation strategy that is simple, training-free, and batch-effect-robust. Specifically, scMethCraft effectively leverages the learnt adaptive similarity matrix to identify the nearest neighbors of a given cell, infer the most likely cell type for each query cell, enabling accurate cross-batch annotation without requiring additional model training (Methods).

We systematically evaluated the cross-batch cell-type annotation performance from multiple perspectives using the six human brain scDNAm datasets. In each evaluation, one batch was designated as the reference, and the remaining five batches were used as query sets and quantitatively evaluated using the macro F1-score. Given the current lack of automated annotation methods specifically designed for scDNAm data, we established several classic classifiers which have been widely applied to cell-type annotation across other single-cell omics data as baseline methods33,34, including support vector machines (SVM) and k-nearest neighbors (KNN) with K = 5 and K = 10, trained on the embeddings generated by EpiScanpy.

For the annotation of major types, where inter-type differences are relatively pronounced, all methods achieved good performance (Supplementary Fig. 12). However, when using the A5-A7 dataset as the reference, the annotation of certain major cell types, such as MGC, is difficult due to their relatively low abundance in the reference. Even the best-performing baseline model among the alternatives, SVM, struggled to accurately annotate these cell types. As illustrated in Fig. 3m, SVM erroneously assigned most MGC to L2/3-IT or Pvalb cells, and misclassified abundant OPC cells as ODC. In contrast, scMethCraft, benefiting from its iteratively optimized similarity representation, achieved near-perfect annotation of OPCs and MGCs, leading to a significant improvement in annotation accuracy (Fig. 3n). In the more challenging subtype-level annotation tasks, baseline methods generally exhibit suboptimal annotation accuracy. scMethCraft exhibited substantial performance, achieving the highest accuracy across all six evaluations (Fig. 3o). In summary, scMethCraft achieved high annotation accuracy across batches at varying levels of annotation granularity, enabling robust identification of cellular identity from the perspective of DNA methylation.

scMethCraft effectively imputes and enhances the scDNAm signal

scDNAm matrices typically contain both noise and a substantial proportion of explicit NA values (Supplementary Fig. 13), which significantly hampers the characterization of DNA methylation patterns and implementation of downstream analysis35. scMethCraft leverages sequence information and cell-to-cell similarity to model scDNAm distribution, denoise the matrix, and impute NA entries, thereby achieving effective data enhancement.

We first evaluated the ability of scMethCraft to impute and denoise scDNAm matrices. Following previous studies36, for each major cell type, we constructed a meta-cell by averaging the methylation levels of cells belonging to the same cell type and then evaluated data reconstruction fidelity using the Spearman correlation between every single cell and its corresponding meta-cell (Supplementary Note 1). Taking the ACC dataset as an example (Fig. 4a,b), the raw data exhibited low correlation with the meta-cells across all cell types, highlighting the adverse impact of noise. In contrast, scMethCraft substantially improved the Spearman correlation between individual cells and their respective meta-cells, indicating a more faithful reconstruction of the underlying methylation distribution. Besides, to intuitively visualize that raw scDNAm matrix can be effectively imputed by scMethCraft, we generated heatmaps of the raw matrix (Fig. 4b) and the scMethCraft-enhanced matrix (Fig. 4c). Evidently, the raw data exhibited a substantial amount of NA values and technical noise (with NA values appearing as white in the heatmap), leading to blurred patterns across different cell types. After enhancement by scMethCraft, the NA values were effectively imputed, and distinct DNA methylation patterns of different cell types became clearly discernible.

Baseline methods were not specifically designed to recover cell-type-specific methylation patterns. Instead, their main purpose is to provide reasonable initial values for missing entries, thereby enabling downstream analyses such as PCA or clustering to proceed without failures caused by explicit missing values (Supplementary Note 2). In contrast, scMethCraft consistently outperformed these baseline approaches across multiple datasets, underscoring the robustness and generalizability of its data enhancement capability (Fig. 4d).

Moreover, we validated whether the data enhancement performed by scMethCraft aligns with known biological priors. Following Tian et al.3, we grouped all cells of the ACC dataset into three classes: non-neuronal cells, inhibitory/non-telencephalic neurons, and telencephalic excitatory neurons. We used these three classes to color the UMAPs generated from the raw data (Fig. 4e). STMN2 is labeled as a marker gene37 for excitatory neurons in the CellMarker19 database. Given that gene expression is often negatively correlated with methylation levels, we queried the average methylation levels of the overlapping regions with the STMN2 gene and colored the UMAPs based on methylation level. The DNA methylation pattern in the raw data was disordered (Fig. 4f). In contrast, the scMethCraft-enhanced data (Fig. 4g), STMN2 displayed a more distinct methylation pattern, characterized by hypomethylation in telencephalic excitatory neurons and hypermethylation in the other two classes. As illustrated in Fig. 4h,i, scMethCraft, without requiring prior knowledge such as cell-type annotations, effectively enhanced the cell-type-specific patterns of the STMN2 gene. A similar observation was further validated in the enhanced data of the MAP2 and SATB2 gene, another markers for neurons38,39 (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15). Similarly, the MBP gene is specifically highly expressed in ODCs40 (a non-neuronal cell type), and the GAD2 gene, a marker for inhibitory neurons, also demonstrated enhanced cell-type-specific DNA methylation patterns41 (Supplementary Fig. 15). However, these specific patterns were not clearly discernible in the raw data due to noise and missing values.

Finally, we conducted an additional masking experiment to assess whether scMethCraft can correctly reconstruct cell-type-specific regions under extreme missingness, while preserving their expected cell-type specificity. Even when methylation values in regions overlapping known marker genes were entirely masked in specific cell types, scMethCraft successfully reconstructed methylation patterns consistent with known cell-type identities, preserving relative differences between cell types and underlying cellular heterogeneity. (Supplementary Note 3). Meanwhile, by comparing the average methylation levels of each cell type before and after imputation, we observed that scMethCraft does not overemphasize specific methylation patterns in any particular cell type (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Overall, scMethCraft enhances the scDNAm matrix through high-quality denoising and imputation, thereby improving the detectability of cell population-specific DNA methylation patterns.

scMethCraft reveals cell-type-specific biological insights by identifying candidate differentially methylated regions

Identifying DMRs from scDNAm data provides a useful approach for characterizing epigenetic variation3,4. DMRs provide key insights into the epigenetic differences across individuals, tissues, and cell types, enabling a comprehensive characterization of the epigenomic landscape. Here, we leverage scMethCraft to perform DMR analysis to highlight candidate regions exhibiting cell-type-specific methylation patterns (Methods). These analyses are intended to provide exploratory insights into cell-type-specific methylation patterns.

Given the strong association between DNA methylation and gene expression, previous studies have primarily focused on the identification of hypo-DMRs3,11,22. We assessed the hypo-DMR identification performance of scMethCraft and EpiScanpy (Methods). Taking the ACC dataset and ODCs as an example, EpiScanpy identified 142 ODC-specific hypo-DMRs (Fig. 5a), whereas scMethCraft identified 771 such regions (Fig. 5b). To evaluate whether the hypo-DMRs identified by different methods capture cell-type-specific gene regulatory patterns, we retrieved genes whose transcriptional loci overlap with these hypo-DMRs using genome annotations provided by the UCSC Genome Browser42. To qualitatively assess the relevance of these genes to ODCs, we categorized them into three groups based on extensive searches in the CellMarker19 and GeneCards20 databases: (1) ODC marker genes, which are explicitly annotated as ODC markers in the CellMarker database; (2) ODC-relevant genes, which are not annotated as ODC markers but have been reported to be closely associated with neural cells, brain development, or brain-related diseases in the GeneCards database; and (3) Other genes, which have not been explicitly record to have any known association with ODCs. We generated a word cloud (Fig. 5c) for 35 genes associated with ODC-specific hypo-DMRs identified by scMethCraft that appeared at least three times. Among these genes, eight genes were confirmed as ODC marker genes, nineteen genes were categorized as ODC-relevant genes, and only eight genes lacked strong evidence of ODC association according to the consulted databases (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, among the eight unrelated genes, although the CHD7 gene is not annotated as a marker gene or associated with ODCs in the two databases consulted, existing studies have validated that CHD7 may be functionally required for central nervous system myelination in humans, and crucial for the maturation of ODCs43,44. Moreover, although its specific function remains unclear, the Human Protein Atlas database indicates that CUEDC1 exhibits high expression in ODCs45. Design principles of scMethCraft inspire single-cell omics data modeling

Current modeling approaches for single-cell omics data matrices are primarily cell-centric, often overlooking the rich information inherently encoded within the DNA sequence. scMethCraft models scDNAm data from both the sequence and cellular perspectives, thereby capturing the biological insight embedded in DNA sequences while also effectively characterizing intercellular relationships from the methylation matrix. As a hybrid neural network model, scMethCraft integrates multiple components whose individual contributions are critical not only for optimizing overall model performance but also for establishing methodological paradigms for single-cell computational analysis.

We investigated the effectiveness of modeling scDNAm data of the following four key modules: the one-hot feature extraction sub-network (namely one-hot module), the k-mer feature extraction sub-network (namely k-mer module), the position feature extraction sub-network (namely position module), and the similarity-weighting module (namely similarity module). We constructed four ablated variants of the model by removing each of these components in turn.

scMethCraft employs two optimizers to independently minimize the loss functions of the sequence feature extraction module and the similarity weighting module. Firstly, we systematically evaluated the impact of different components on model convergence. Each model was trained for 100 epochs, and we recorded the total loss at each epoch. We first evaluated the total loss of the sequence feature extraction module. scMethCraft w/o one-hot maintained a markedly higher total loss after 100 epochs, suggesting that one-hot encoding captures the most informative sequence features (Fig. 6a). This observation is consistent with prior findings from scBasset25, which demonstrated the effectiveness of one-hot encoded genomic sequences for learning regulatory features. However, in scMethCraft, the one-hot module is re-engineered to accommodate region-level binning, explicit missing values, and continuous methylation-level signals, which are unique to scDNAm data and fundamentally differ from the scATAC-seq setting in which scBasset was originally developed. Moreover, although one-hot encoding theoretically contains all the information required to derive k-mer representations, practical limitations such as convolutional kernel size restrict its power. Thus, explicitly constructed k-mer features still provide additional, non-redundant information that facilitates model convergence. We also assessed the total loss associated with the similarity module. Secondly, we also evaluated the clustering performance of scMethCraft and its four ablated variants, which indirectly reflects the model’s ability to capture cell heterogeneity signals. We first computed the cell clustering performance of each method on the three benchmark datasets individually (Fig. 6c). The variant without the one-hot module achieved the lowest overall score, and the variant without the similarity module yielded the second-lowest score and achieved an average score of 0.76. The variants lacking the k-mer and position modules ranked third and fourth, respectively. Figure 6d summarizes the relative contribution of each module (Methods). The one-hot module made the most substantial contribution, accounting for an average of 68% of the overall performance. The similarity module contributed 18%, underscoring its role in enhancing performance through cell-to-cell relationship modeling. Although the contributions of the k-mer and positional modules were relatively modest, they collectively accounted for 14% of the performance gain. The k-mer module facilitates faster convergence during early training phases, while the positional module, as previous studies reported, enhances the model’s ability to capture local dependencies between genomic architecture and methylation patterns in scDNAm data51.

Thirdly, we verified whether the similarity weighting module effectively captured intercellular similarity information. As shown in the cell-to-cell similarity matrix (Fig. 6e), the regions along the main diagonal exhibited noticeably higher values compared to other regions, indicating that cells of the same type tend to have higher similarity values within the matrix. To further confirm the ability of the similarity weighting module to capture fine-grained cell subtype information, we sorted the cells by their subtypes. Figure 6f demonstrated that even for types with subtle differences, the similarity-weighting module of scMethCraft was still able to capture the biological heterogeneity and similarity between these cell types. The intercellular similarity modeling strategy of scMethCraft similarity enables accurate characterization of cell-to-cell relationships across multiple levels of resolution, establishing a methodological paradigm that can inform future studies of related omics analyses.

In summary, these findings highlight that one-hot encoding, as previously demonstrated in scBasset, provides a powerful foundation for sequence-based representation learning in single-cell omics analyses. By systematically extending this paradigm to region-level scDNAm matrices and integrating additional modules for k-mer features, genomic position, and intercellular similarity, scMethCraft establishes a unified and task-adaptive framework tailored to the unique challenges of single-cell DNA methylation data.

Discussion

In this study, we present scMethCraft, a hybrid neural network-based framework for modeling scDNAm data. Unlike most existing cell-centric single-cell omics methods, scMethCraft adopts a sequence-informed modeling paradigm, in which genomic regions and their corresponding DNA sequences are treated as primary inputs. Through multiple complementary sequence-encoding strategies, scMethCraft learns how sequence-derived features interact with cell-specific methylation states, thereby capturing regulatory information embedded in the reference genome. Under this formulation, the sequence module learns low-dimensional representations of genomic regions that encode their differential methylation behavior across cell types, which in turn provides strong discriminative power for downstream tasks such as dimensionality reduction and clustering. Building on these sequence-informed predictions, scMethCraft further incorporates an explicit cell-to-cell similarity weighting module through a trainable and iterative mechanism. This module leverages intercellular correlations to reweight and smooth region-level methylation estimates.

By integrating sequence-based modeling with cell-to-cell similarity learning, scMethCraft provides a unified framework capable of supporting a wide range of downstream analyses, including dimensionality reduction, cell clustering, batch integration, cell type annotation, data enhancement, and differential methylation analysis. We demonstrated how scMethCraft identified two potential ODC-associated genes and facilitates the discovery of underlying biological insights through function enrichment analysis, tissue-specific expression enrichment, and partitioned heritability analysis. Given the limited availability of tailored computational tools for scDNAm data, scMethCraft offers a valuable analytic framework and reference benchmark for the scDNAm research community. Notably, while we identified candidate differentially methylated regions based on the enhanced data, the introduction of cell-to-cell dependencies during the enhancement process may lead to inflated statistical significance. Therefore, we recommend examining the results from multiple perspectives and performing cross-validation with the original data whenever possible to ensure robust and reliable biological conclusions.

Future improvements to scMethCraft could focus on the following aspects. Firstly, scMethCraft struggles to directly align data with different genome versions due to the inherent variations in their underlying DNA sequences. Integrating data from different reference genome versions or across different species is a latent improvement for scMethCraft. Secondly, as the number and quality of publicly accessible datasets increase, pre-training key components of the model on large-scale scDNAm data from corresponding species, organs, and tissues, followed by fine-tuning other parameters on user-specific datasets, could achieve more efficient and accurate model training. Finally, the advancement of multi-omics sequencing technologies enables the decoding of biological genetic information from multiple perspectives, including the transcriptome and epigenome. Extending scMethCraft to the analysis of single-cell multi-omics data represents a promising direction for future development.

Besides, the similarity weighting module quantifies cellular similarity to enhance the predictions. The similarity weighting module is essentially a bias-free, fully connected layer that transforms the n-dimensional predicted output (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document}n represents the number of cells) to another n-dimensional vector. The similarity matrix serves as its parameter weights, constrained to be non-negative, real symmetric, and having zero diagonal elements. The zero-diagonal constraint ensures that each cell’s value is represented by a weighted average of other cells. Processing for the training dataset

The input of scMethCraft is a single-cell DNA methylation cell-by-region matrix, in which each element represents the average methylation level across CpG sites within a genomic tile for each individual cell. To ensure the quality and usability of the training data, scMethCraft performs several pre-processing steps. First, scMethCraft filters out genomic regions with excessive proportions of NA values, removing any region with more than 80% not available elements (Supplementary Note 4). Second, the variance of each region across cells is calculated, and low-variance regions are removed based on the 5th percentile threshold, retaining only regions with higher variability. Finally, PCA is applied to derive a low-dimensional representation for each region, based on which cosine similarity is computed. For each region, its 100 upstream and 100 downstream neighbors are examined, and those with a cosine similarity exceeding 0.9 are excluded to eliminate adjacent regions exhibiting nearly identical methylation patterns across all cells.

The ground truth cell type labels of brain datasets used for evaluation were obtained directly from the original studies associated with each dataset. For the PBMC dataset, we leveraged the paired scRNA-seq data to perform cell-type annotation based on well-established marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Implementation details of downstream analyses

SNPsea46 is a computational tool designed to pinpoint cell types and biological pathways potentially affected by genetic risk loci. Building upon insights from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which have identified numerous genomic regions linked to disease susceptibility, SNPsea offers a streamlined approach for evaluating which cell types may be influenced by genes located within these loci. In our analysis, we applied SNPsea with default parameters and analyzed expression profiles of 17,581 genes across 79 human tissues, as cataloged in the Gene Atlas database52. Genome-wide SNP data were retrieved from the HapMap3 resource (https://zenodo.org/records/7768714). We utilized the GenomicRanges53 R/Bioconductor package to identify SNPs located within each group of cell-type-specific peak regions. These SNPs were then used as inputs for SNPsea. Additional required data, including phenotype annotations and analysis parameters, were incorporated following the official SNPsea tutorial. All supporting files were obtained from http://www.broadinstitute.org/mpg/snpsea. The resulting enrichments across 79 tissues were quantified, and the top 30 significantly enriched tissues are visualized in the corresponding Figures.

LDSC (linkage disequilibrium score regression)49 is a command-line framework used to estimate trait heritability and genetic correlation based on GWAS summary statistics. We employed partitioned LDSC with default settings to evaluate the enrichment of heritability for brain-related phenotypes across different cell types. The LDSC analysis was performed using HapMap3 SNPs, with European ancestry individuals from the 1000 Genomes Project serving as the LD reference panel. GWAS summary statistics, including SNP and phenotype information, were obtained from the Broad LD Hub (10.5281/zenodo.7768714). As with SNPsea, SNPs located within peak regions were identified using GenomicRanges. These SNPs were subsequently used to compute LD scores via the LDSC pipeline, as described in the tutorial at https://github.com/bulik/ldsc/wiki/LD-Score-Estimation-Tutorial. Finally, these LD scores were used to calculate SNP-based heritability and genetic correlations for selected phenotypes.

Identification of hypo-DMRs

For scMethCraft, based on the scMethCraft-enhanced scDNAm matrix, we computed the methylation level fold change for each genomic region between the target cell type and all other cell types and performed a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. We defined hypo-DMRs as regions with a log2 fold change less than −1 and a Bonferroni-adjusted P-value below 0.01. Bonferroni correction was applied to control the family-wise error rate across all tested regions. Given that EpiScanpy does not provide a dedicated workflow for the identification of hypo-DMRs, we applied the same statistical test and thresholds used in scMethCraft to the EpiScanpy-imputed matrix to ensure a fair comparison. We note that the Wilcoxon rank-sum test assumes independence between observations, whereas the enhanced data may introduce dependencies across cells. Therefore, this analysis is primarily intended to prioritize candidate regions exhibiting cell-type-specific methylation differences, and the resulting statistical significance should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Performance evaluation

For dimensionality reduction and data visualization, since baseline methods SnapATAC2 and EpiScanpy do not support NA values as input, we first pre-imputed the raw data using the median (the median CpG methylation level for each region across all cells) according to EpiScanpy’s pipeline and then performed dimensionality reduction following the default workflows provided by each method. We then applied the UMAP algorithm to further reduce the dimensionality of the data to two based on cell embedding. Single CpG site-level scDNAm data analysis methods were not included in the comparison (Supplementary Note 6).

For cell clustering, we employed the widely used Louvain algorithm and used a binary search strategy to match the number of clusters with the number of cell types23. In the binary search strategy, we set the minimum resolution to 0 and the maximum resolution to 3, terminating after a maximum of 20 iterations. We evaluated the clustering performance using five widely used metrics: adjusted Rand index (ARI), adjusted mutual information (AMI), normalized mutual information (NMI), homogeneity (Homo), and Fowlkes-Mallows index (FMI). The Rand index (RI) measures the similarity between cluster labels and cell type labels. ARI is adjusted based on RI and accounts for chance agreement. Mutual information (MI) quantifies the correlation between cluster labels and cell type labels, while AMI and NMI are standardized variants of MI. Homogeneity quantifies whether each cluster contains only members of a single class. FMI is defined as the geometric mean of pairwise precision and recall.

For batch integration performance evaluation, following previous work29, we used batch correction metrics and biological conservation metrics to evaluate batch integration performance. In the evaluation process, we separately used the major type label (MT) and subtype label (ST). Biological conservation can be evaluated by clustering metrics, while batch correction metrics include the k-nearest neighbor batch effect test (kBET), batch silhouette, and graph integration local inverse Simpson’s index (Graph iLISI). kBET is a wrapper function of the implementation by Büttner et al54., which measures the bias of a batch variable in the KNN graph. The silhouette score measures the similarity between a cell’s own cluster and other clusters, and the batch silhouette measures the silhouette of a given batch and is used to measure how well batches are mixed. Graph iLISI is adapted from Korsunsky et al30. via shortest path-based distance computation on single-cell KNN graphs, and is noteworthy in that it does not require specifying cell type annotation labels. All batch correction metrics are scaled between 0 and 1, with higher scores representing better batch correction performance.

For cell type annotation performance evaluation, we concatenated six datasets, including the ACC, M1C, MTG, A5-A7, Pro, and A46 datasets, and retained only the major type (or subtype) that appeared in all six datasets. Subsequently, we used one dataset as the training set and the remaining five datasets as the test set to evaluate cell type annotation performance. For baseline methods including SVM and KNN (K = 5 and K = 10), we used cell embeddings obtained by EpiScanpy as the input and implemented baseline methods using sklearn55 with default parameters. We used the macro F1 score to evaluate the annotation performance. The F1 score can be interpreted as the weighted average of precision and recall, with the best score of 1 and the worst score of 0. The relative contributions of precision and recall to the F1 score are equal. The macro F1 score calculates the F1 score for each cell type and finds its unweighted average to better evaluate datasets with an imbalanced number of cell types.

For data enhancement performance evaluation, according to previous studies36, we first averaged the CpG methylation levels across all regions for all single cells of the same type to obtain the corresponding metacells. Subsequently, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient with the metacell of the raw data (only considering the available regions in the raw data), the data imputed by EpiScanpy, and the data enhanced by scMethCraft (which respectively includes the calculation of all regions and the NA regions in the raw data only), referred to as correlations with metacell. In data enhancement performance evaluation, to mitigate the confounding influence of pre-existing NA values on downstream evaluation, we restricted our analysis to cell types comprising more than 3% of the total cell population and regions with no more than 80% missingness in the original data.
