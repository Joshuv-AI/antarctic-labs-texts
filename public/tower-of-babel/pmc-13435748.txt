Single-cell RNA-sequencing profiles reveal the developmental landscape of hawthorn leaves
Authors: Guigang Zhao, Xien Wu, Baozheng Wang, Xiaolu Li, Junjuan Zheng, Zhaoli Ding, Guodong Li, Ticao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hawthorn (Crataegus spp.) is an economically and medicinally important genus in Rosaceae. Its leaves accumulate valuable secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, and have long been used in traditional Chinese medicine. Despite available chromosome-level genome assemblies and bulk transcriptome data, the cellular composition of hawthorn leaves, their developmental trajectories, and the cell-type-specific distribution of biosynthetic gene expression remain uncharacterized. Here we report the first single-cell transcriptomic atlas of hawthorn leaves, generated by integrating protoplast-based scRNA-seq and nucleus-based snRNA-seq from two species (C. pinnatifida var. major N. E. Br. and C. scabrifolia (Franch.) Rehder). From four libraries, we profiled 32,292 high-quality cells, resolved sixteen transcriptional clusters, and annotated nine canonical cell types: mesophyll, pavement, guard, xylem, metaxylem, phloem parenchyma, companion, sieve element, and meristematic cells. Cell-type-resolved interspecific comparison identified 642 differentially expressed genes between species, with divergent enrichment of photosynthesis and volatile biosynthesis processes in C. pinnatifida var. major and hypoxia-response processes in C. scabrifolia. Pseudotime analysis reconstructed branched developmental trajectories converging on mesophyll dominance at late stages, consistent with classical dicotyledonous leaf organogenesis. Cell-type-resolved profiling demonstrated spatial partitioning of flavonoid biosynthetic genes across multiple cell populations, with upstream enzymes concentrated in epidermal and phloem-associated cells and downstream enzymes in mesophyll and xylem cells. This atlas provides a cellular framework for mechanistic study of tissue‑specific secondary metabolite and for future functional and breeding applications in hawthorn.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09127-z.

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) resolve the transcriptional states of individual cells within complex tissues, enable classification of distinct cell populations, identification of rare cell types, and reconstruction of differentiation trajectories at high resolution [1–6]. Although every cell in a multicellular organism carries the same genome, distinct gene expression programs underpin cell identity and function [3, 4]. Bulk RNA-seq averages these signals across heterogeneous populations and cannot assign expression to specific cell types. Since their extension to plants, single-cell methods have been applied to roots [7–9], leaves [10–12], and developing organs across Arabidopsis [13], strawberry [10], rice [14], maize [15]), tea [16], and cassava [17]. Application to non-model plants remains limited by the absence of validated cell-type markers, variable cell size, and the technical demands of protoplast isolation [16].

The genus Crataegus (hawthorn) comprises over 280 species of flowering shrubs and trees in the Rosaceae, distributed across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere [18]. Both cultivated varieties and wild species (such as Crataegus chungtienensis) possess considerable nutritional and therapeutic value [19]. Hawthorn has a long history in traditional Chinese medicine; its leaves, fruits, and seeds are rich in flavonoids, triterpenoids, organic acids, and procyanidins [20–23], which confer antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, cardiotonic, antimicrobial, and anticancer activities [24–26]. Leaves are the main photosynthetic organs in plants and leaf morphology varies greatly among species and within different developmental stages and growth conditions [27]. Hawthorn leaves, a major site for the biosynthesis and accumulation of valuable secondary metabolites, are widely used medicinally in China. Hawthorn leaf flavonoids (HLF) in particular show cardioprotective and vasoprotective effects [28], and the optimal harvest window for total flavonoid accumulation varies by season [29]. Leaf form and size are determined by intrinsic genetic programs and external environmental cues [30]. At the cellular level, leaf development is primarily governed by two key biological processes—cell proliferation and cell expansion—both of which are regulated by multiple gene-encoded factors [30]. Leaves are the primary organs responsible for converting energy, and their morphology directly affects crop adaptability, yield, and quality [31]. Cellular differentiation and developmental trajectories during leaf development drive dynamic changes in compound biosynthesis. Despite these pharmacological and agronomic properties, the cellular composition of hawthorn leaves, their developmental programs, and the cell-type origins of secondary metabolite biosynthesis have not been characterized at single-cell resolution.

We selected two species for comparative analysis: Crataegus pinnatifida var. major N. E. Br., a widely cultivated variety listed in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia since 1963 [32, 33]; and Crataegus scabrifolia (Franch.) Rehder, the second most widely cultivated hawthorn in China, inferred to represent an ancestral lineage within Crataegus based on morphological, palynological, and SNP-based phylogenetic data [34–36]. C. pinnatifida var. major and C. scabrifolia are closely related evolutionarily and have long been used in traditional Chinese medicine. Chromosome-level genome assemblies are available for both [20, 37], providing reference resources for single-cell data analysis. Meanwhile, transcriptomic studies on Crataegus species have provided important insights into the quality traits of hawthorn fruits [19, 38, 39]. Together, these two species allow us to distinguish features conserved across the genus from those that are species- or variety-specific, and to ask whether the cellular organization and biosynthetic gene expression patterns of hawthorn leaves reflect the phylogenetic distance between a widely cultivated commercial variety and an ancestral wild relative.

In this study, we apply scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq to construct the first single-cell atlas of hawthorn leaves. Specifically, we: (1) optimize protoplast and nucleus isolation protocols for both species; (2) annotate nine major leaf cell types using Arabidopsis homolog-based marker matching and GO enrichment; (3) identify novel hawthorn cell-type-specific marker candidates; (4) perform a cell-type-resolved interspecific comparison to identify species-divergent transcriptional programs; (5) reconstruct pseudotime developmental trajectories; and (6) map the cell-type-specific expression of flavonoid biosynthetic genes. These findings provide a cellular framework for mechanistic and applied studies of secondary metabolism in hawthorn.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

C. pinnatifida var. major and C. scabrifolia were grown at the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Two leaf samples were collected per species: Samples 1 and 3 were from C. pinnatifida var. major; Samples 2 and 4 were from C. scabrifolia. Fresh, tender leaves immediately cut into ~ 1 mm fragments in ice-cold dissociation buffer (20 mM KCl, 10 mM CaCl₂, 0.1% BSA, 20 mM MES, 0.6 M mannitol). Plant identity was confirmed by Ticao Zhang; voucher specimens (YUNCM20230189) are deposited at the Herbarium of Yunnan University of Chinese Medicine (YUNCM). All samples were collected following the necessary permissions obtained from the Kunming Institute of Botany to ensure compliance with local, national, and international regulations regarding plant research.

Protoplast isolation

Protoplasts were isolated from leaf material (Samples 1 and 2) by enzymatic digestion method [16] with modifications. Leaf fragments were incubated in 5 mL digestion solution (20 mM KCl, 10 mM CaCl₂, 0.1% BSA, 20 mM MES, 0.6 M mannitol, 1% cellulase R10, 0.5% snailase, 0.5% macerozyme R-10) at 25 °C and 40 rpm for 4–5 h. Protoplasts were filtered through a 40 μm sieve, centrifuged at 200 ×g for 3 min, and washed with 2 mL wash buffer (5 mM KCl, 125 mM CaCl₂, 154 mM NaCl, 0.1% BSA, 20 mM MES). Count and viability were determined by trypan blue staining on a LUNA-FL Cell Counter.

Nuclei isolation

Nuclei were isolated from Samples 3 and 4 following a published protocol [40] with minor modifications. Leaf fragments were chopped in 1 mL ice-cold NIB buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 250 mM sucrose, 1 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM KCl, 0.25% Triton X-100, 40% glycerol, 0.5 mM spermine, 0.5 mM spermidine, 0.1% 2-ME) for 5 min until pipetted. The homogenate was filtered through a 40 μm sieve, incubated on ice for 10 min, then centrifuged at 50 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove debris, followed by 1,100 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Nuclei were resuspended in 500 µL NIB buffer, quality and count were assessed on aCountstar Rigel S6 system with AOPI staining.

Library construction and sequencing

Protoplasts were captured on a BD Rhapsody HT system and libraries constructed with the WTA Amplification Kit (BD). Nuclei were captured on a 10X Genomics GemCode instrument using the Chromium Next GEM 3’ Reagent Kit (v3.1). In both workflows, individual cells or nuclei were encapsulated, mRNA was captured on magnetic beads, libraries were prepared with unique barcode indices. Quantification used a Qubit 3.0; sequencing was on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System in PE150 mode.

Data processing and clusting

Raw FASTQ files were processed with 10X Genomics Cell Ranger (v8.0.1) and BD Rhapsody CWL-runner (v2.2.1), producing gene-cell matrices aligned to the C. scabrifolia genome [37]. Matrices were imported into Seurat (v5.2.1). Cells were retained with nFeature_RNA between 500 and 6,000, mitochondrial gene content below 5%, and chloroplast gene content below 10%. Datasets were normalized, integrated Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA), and reduced by PCA; the top 20 principal components were used for t-SNE visualization and graph-based clustering (FindClusters, resolution = 0.4). Cluster marker genes were identified with FindAllMarkers (min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25).

Cell-type annotation

Cell populations were annotated using Arabidopsis tissue-specific marker genes matched to hawthorn homologs and cross-referenced against the PlantCellMarker and scPlantDB databases [41, 42]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of cluster-specific upregulated DEGs were used to validate functional assignments. Top candidate markers per hawthorn cell type were defined as genes with logFC ≥ 0.25 expressed in at least 25% of cells in the target cluster; the top 20 by avg_log2FC were selected.

Interspecific comparison

To compare transcriptomes between C. pinnatifida var. major and C. scabrifolia, we performed two complementary analyses. First, a global (unclustered) species-level DEG analysis was run using the same thresholds as the cluster marker analysis (logfc.threshold = 0.25, min.pct = 0.25), treating all cells per species as a single group. Second, for each of the nine annotated cell types, we performed within-cell-type DEG analysis between species (same thresholds), and GO enrichment was run on the resulting gene sets.

Pseudotime analysis

Pseudotime trajectories were reconstructed with Monocle (version 2.30.1). Genes expressed in at least 10 cells and showing significant differential expression across clusters (qval < 0.01) were used for trajectory construction (max_components = 2, DDRTree). Meristematic cells were set as the trajectory root. Branch-dependent expression was identified by BEAM and visualized with plot_genes_branched_heatmap.

Results

Single-cell libraries capture comparable, high-quality transcriptomes from two hawthorn species

We constructed four single-cell libraries from leaves of two Crataegus species using the BD Rhapsody HT(scRNA-seq, protoplasts) and the 10X Genomics GemCode (snRNA-seq, nuclei) platform (Fig. 1a). For C. pinnatifida var. major, scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq generated 574,493,457 and 544,520,935 reads, yielding 7,147 protoplasts (mean 914 genes/cell) and 10,674 nuclei (mean 716 genes/nucleus) after quality filtering. For C. scabrifolia, 597,928,880 and 562,537,244 reads yielded 4,463 protoplasts (mean 937 genes/cell) and 10,008 nuclei (mean 2,005 genes/nucleus).

Integration of the four filtered datasets by CCA and unsupervised clustering produced 32,292 high-quality cells across sixteen distinct clusters, visualized by t-SNE (Fig. 1b; Supplemental Figure. 1). Importantly, cell-type proportions were broadly comparable across libraries, methods, and species (Fig. 1c-d), confirming that the integrated atlas is not dominated by a single library or preparation method. The consistency of cluster composition across scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq preparations indicates that protoplast-induced transcriptional stress responses did not create artifactual clusters in the dataset.

Nine canonical cell types are resolved from sixteen transcriptional clusters

Differential expression analysis across sixteen clusters identified 6,024 DEGs (Supplemental Table S1). Using Arabidopsis homolog-based markers (Supplemental Table S2) and GO enrichment, we annotated nine major cell types in hawthorn leaves: mesophyll cells (MC, Clusters 0 and 4), pavement cells (PC, Clusters 2 and 7), guard cells (GC, Cluster 11), xylem (XY, Clusters 1, 8, 12), metaxylem (MXY, Clusters 9 and 14), phloem parenchyma (PP, Clusters 3 and 10), companion cells (CC, Cluster 13), sieve elements (SE, Cluster 15), and meristematic cells (MER, Clusters 5 and 6) (Fig. 2a, Supplemental Table S2).

Mesophyll clusters showed high expression of canonical photosynthesis markers RBCS and LHCB [10, 43–45]. Epidermal clusters were defined by cuticle and suberin biosynthesis markers FDH, HDG2, and PDF1 [11, 12, 46, 47]. Guard cells (Cluster 11) expressed the stomatal development regulators SCRM and FAMA [48, 49]) [43]. Vascular assignment used a combination of phloem markers (argonaute 10-like, glyoxylase I4, linoleate 9 S-lipoxygenase 6 [11, 43, 50, 51] and xylem markers (ZAT12-like, SBT4.14, expansin-A4-like, IRX10 [8, 9, 50, 52–56]). Meristematic clusters were identified by enrichment of cell cycle genes including kinesin-like protein KIN-12D and histone H2A, validated meristematic markers across multiple specie [10, 57–59]. The agreement between marker-based assignment, chloroplast gene content profiles (Figs. 2 and 3), and GO enrichment provides multiple independent lines of support for each annotation.

Critically, both C. pinnatifida var. major and C. scabrifolia displayed the same nine cell types in broadly similar proportions (Fig. 2c, Supplemental Table S3). This conservation across two species separated by considerable phylogenetic distance within Crataegus indicates that core leaf cellular architecture is robustly maintained at the genus level, consistent with atlases from Arabidopsis [7, 11], rice [14], strawberry [52], and tea [16].

Cell-type-specific transcriptional programs and novel hawthorn marker genes

We characterized population-specific transcriptional programs by GO enrichment of the 154–567 upregulated DEGs per cell type (Supplemental Table S4-5). Mesophyll cells were enriched for photosynthetic light reactions and electron transport. Pavement cells showed enrichment of lipid metabolism, cuticle development, and osmotic stress responses, while guard cells were enriched for potassium ion and proton transmembrane transport. Phloem parenchyma cells were enriched for secondary metabolite biosynthesis, stress responses, and water transport; sieve elements for phloem development, autophagy, and responses to cellular component size and toxic compounds. companion cells were enriched in processes related to osmotic and oxidative stress responses, xenobiotic metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and cytoplasmic translation. Xylem and metaxylem cells were enriched for steroid and fatty acid metabolism and cell wall biosynthesis. Meristematic cells showed strong upregulation of mitotic spindle organization, microtubule movement, and chromatin assembly genes. These profiles are consistent across both species and align with the known biology of each cell type.

The top 20 upregulated DEGs per cell type (by avg_log2FC) yielded a set of novel hawthorn marker candidates (Fig. 3). Representative examples include: reduced chloroplast coverage 2-like and NADH dehydrogenase subunit F (mesophyll); desiccation protectant protein and very-long-chain aldehyde decarbonylase (pavement); two phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylases (guard cells); TMV resistance protein N and disease resistance protein RGA3 (phloem parenchyma); SIEVE ELEMENT OCCLUSION B and callose synthase 7-like (sieve elements); PHLOEM PROTEIN 2-like and glycine-rich protein A3-like (companion cells); DOMON/cytochrome b561 domain-containing protein and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase 19-like (xylem); probable rhamnogalacturonate lyase B and rhamnogalacturonan I rhamnosyltransferase 1 (metaxylem); and histone H1.2-like and histone H1-like (meristematic cells). The functional relevance of each marker to its cognate cell type is supported by the GO enrichment profiles described above, providing an empirically grounded set of hawthorn-specific markers for future use.

Cell-type-resolved interspecific comparison reveals divergent transcriptional programs

To leverage the two-species design, we performed two levels of comparative analysis: a global species-level comparison and a within-cell-type comparison for each of the nine annotated populations. At the global level, a bulk-style DEG analysis identified 642 differentially expressed genes between the two species (logFC ≥ 0.25, expressed in ≥ 25% of cells per group). Of these, 246 were upregulated in C. pinnatifida var. major, with GO enrichment for photosynthesis, volatile organic compound biosynthesis, and acetyl-CoA metabolic processes. Genes upregulated in C. scabrifolia were enriched for hypoxia-response and low-oxygen adaptation processes. This global signal reflects overall transcriptomic differentiation between the cultivated variety and its ancestral relative, but it cannot localize these differences to specific cell populations.

Cell-type-resolved analysis revealed that the global signal is not uniformly distributed. The photosynthesis and volatile biosynthesis enrichment in C. pinnatifida var. major was most prominent in the mesophyll and meristematic clusters, while the hypoxia-response signal in C. scabrifolia was concentrated in phloem parenchyma and xylem cells. This cell-type resolution would not have been accessible by bulk comparison alone: without the single-cell atlas, the contrasting enrichments would appear as a single, mixed transcriptomic difference with no information about which cell populations drive it.

A species difference was also identified at the level of flavonoid pathway gene expression. 4CL (Crsca07aG0047300 and Crsca08aG0053500) showed consistent expression in metaxylem cells of both species, but additional expression in guard and pavement cells was detected in C. pinnatifida var. major and not in C. scabrifolia. This species-specific pattern in epidermal cell types is notable because C. pinnatifida var. major has been selected for elevated leaf flavonoid content; the observation that an early flavonoid pathway enzyme is expressed in additional epidermal cell types in this variety but not in the ancestral lineage is therefore of interest. Whether this broader cellular distribution of 4CL transcripts contributes to higher flavonoid yield, or reflects selection on a distinct trait that co-segregated during domestication, cannot be determined from transcript data alone. The question is, however, directly testable by comparative metabolite profiling in isolated cell populations or by spatial metabolomics applied to leaves of both species at matched developmental stages.

Pseudotime reconstruction reveals a conserved but hawthorn-specific developmental program

Pseudotime analysis using Monocle (meristematic cells set as root) identified five major branch points and 11 discrete developmental states across 4,475 trajectory-associated genes (Fig. 4a-c; Supplemental Table S6). The branched trajectory separates into three principal lineages: an epidermal branch (pavement and guard cells), a vascular branch (xylem, metaxylem, phloem parenchyma, companion cells, and sieve elements), and a photosynthetic branch (mesophyll cells).

The temporal ordering of cell types across pseudotime was consistent between C. pinnatifida var. major and C. scabrifolia (Fig. 4b and d), indicating a conserved developmental sequence at the genus level. Pavement cells and vascular progenitors were enriched at early pseudotime; guard cells, companion cells, and sieve elements appeared at intermediate stages; and mesophyll cells dominated late pseudotime (Fig. 4e). This ordering is consistent with classical models of dicotyledonous leaf organogenesis, in which meristematic proliferation precedes coordinated expansion and differentiation of vascular, epidermal, and photosynthetic lineages [17, 19, 27, 60–63].

Although the broad trajectory architecture is conserved, several features are noteworthy in the hawthorn context. First, meristematic clusters (the trajectory root) showed high expression of chloroplast-associated genes (Figs. 2 and 3), a pattern less prominent in Arabidopsis pseudotime reconstructions, where meristematic clusters typically show lower chloroplast gene abundance [19, 61–66]. Second, BEAM analysis identified a set of genes with peak expression at the meristematic-to-mesophyll branch point; GO enrichment of this set was significantly associated with secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and several flavonoid pathway enzymes were among the differentially expressed genes at this branch. This correspondence between a defined developmental transition and flavonoid pathway gene induction is a testable observation warranting further investigation. Third, among the 4,475 trajectory-associated genes, GO enrichment identified terms for phenylpropanoid pathway activation and terpene biosynthesis, suggesting that secondary metabolic programs are co-regulated with cell differentiation in hawthorn leaves, though the functional significance of this co-regulation requires experimental validation.

A heatmap of the top 50 most significantly trajectory-associated genes illustrates coordinated transcriptional waves accompanying successive differentiation states (Fig. 4f).

Flavonoid biosynthetic genes are distributed across multiple cell types in a spatially partitioned manner

We examined the expression of flavonoid biosynthesis genes across the nine annotated cell types. Of the pathway genes annotated in the C. scabrifolia genome, 14 showed detectable single-cell expression (Fig. 5a). The failure to detect additional genomic copies likely reflects developmental-stage-specific expression, low transcript abundance under the sampled conditions, or sensitivity thresholds in our dataset; absence in this atlas should be confirmed by in situ hybridization or higher-coverage spatial transcriptomics before functional conclusions are drawn.

Flavonoid biosynthetic gene expression across hawthorn leaf cell types. a Heatmap of 14 detected pathway genes across cell types in both species. b Flavonoid biosynthesis pathway from phenylalanine to specialized compounds

The 14 detected genes showed distinct, reproducible cell-type specificity in both species (Fig. 5). Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL, Crsca12aG0117800) was highly expressed in both the pavement (PC) and phloem parenchyma (PP) cells. 4-Coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL, Crsca07aG0047300 and Crsca08aG0053500) was enriched in the metaxylem (MXY) cells of both species, with the additional species-specific epidermal expression in C. pinnatifida var. major described in Sect. 3.4. Chalcone synthase (CHS, Crsca04aG0218800) was predominantly expressed in xylem (XY) cells. Chalcone isomerase (CHI) paralogs were partitioned: Crsca01aG0098200 and Crsca09aG0185300 were enriched in meristematic (MER) cells, while Crsca12aG0102700 was expressed in phloem parenchyma. Flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H, Crsca09aG0185700) was highly expressed in guard (GC) and phloem parenchyma cells. Dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR, Crsca10aG0223200) showed highest expression in mesophyll (MC) cells of both species. Isoflavone synthase (IFS) paralogs were the most divergently partitioned: Crsca03aG0100400 in sieve elements (SE), Crsca04aG0315000 and Crsca07aG0097100 in meristematic cells, and Crsca13aG0040000 in phloem parenchyma of C. pinnatifida var. major only. Anthocyanidin synthase (ANS, Crsca14aG0165700) was expressed in mesophyll and phloem parenchyma cells.

The spatial pattern is consistent across species: upstream enzymes (PAL, 4CL) are concentrated in epidermal and phloem-associated cells, while downstream enzymes (CHS, DFR, ANS) are enriched in xylem-associated and mesophyll cells. This distribution, together with the observation that different paralogs of the same enzyme are expressed in different cell types, demonstrates that flavonoid biosynthesis in hawthorn leaves is distributed across multiple cell populations rather than restricted to a single biosynthetic compartment.

Discussion

This study presents the first single-cell atlas of hawthorn (Crataegus) leaves, profiling 32,292 cells from two species. The atlas addresses three principal questions: which cell types compose the hawthorn leaf; what cell-type-specific differences distinguish the two species; and which cells are the primary sites of flavonoid biosynthesis. We discuss each in turn.

Conservation of leaf cellular architecture across Crataegus

Both C. pinnatifida var. major and C. scabrifolia display the same nine leaf cell types in comparable proportions. This finding extends the pattern of core leaf cell-type conservation observed in single-cell atlases of Arabidopsis [7, 11, 38, 67–70], rice [14], strawberry [52], and tea [16] to a non-model genus in the Rosaceae. It also implies that the cellular blueprint established during leaf development is maintained even across the substantial phenotypic differences between a highly selected commercial variety (C. pinnatifida var. major) and a wild ancestral lineage (C. scabrifolia). Accurate cell annotation remains a central challenge for single-cell studies in non‑model plants [16]. The annotation of nine cell types was supported by multiple independent lines of evidence: Arabidopsis homolog marker matching, chloroplast gene content profiling, and GO enrichment of cluster-specific DEGs. The convergence of these approaches reduces the ambiguity inherent in marker-transfer annotation for non-model species [16, 46, 53, 71].

Cell-type-resolved interspecific differences

The value of the two-species design is most clearly demonstrated by the cell-type-resolved comparative analysis. Global DEG analysis identified 642 differentially expressed genes between species, with enriched processes for photosynthesis, volatile biosynthesis, and acetyl-CoA metabolism in C. pinnatifida var. major and hypoxia adaptation in C. scabrifolia. Without the single-cell atlas, these differences would be interpretable only as a bulk tissue signal with no information about their cellular origin. Cell-type resolution reveals that the enhanced photosynthesis and biosynthesis signal in C. pinnatifida var. major is concentrated in mesophyll and meristematic cells, while the C. scabrifolia hypoxia-response signal is concentrated in phloem parenchyma and xylem. This localization to specific cell populations is a direct product of the two-species design and provides testable hypotheses about the cellular basis of known metabolic differences between these varieties.

The species-specific 4CL expression in epidermal cells of C. pinnatifida var. major extends this comparative insight into the flavonoid pathway. The cultivated variety has been selected for elevated leaf flavonoid content; the observation that 4CL transcripts are present in guard and pavement cells of this variety but not in the ancestral lineage is therefore noteworthy. We present this as an observation that warrants follow-up rather than a mechanism: whether the broader cell-type distribution of 4CL expression in the cultivated variety contributes to higher flavonoid accumulation, or is instead a correlated trait from domestication selection, cannot be inferred from transcript patterns alone. Comparative spatial metabolomics of leaves from both species at matched developmental stages is the most direct way to test this.

Hawthorn leaf developmental trajectories

The development of plant leaves to their final form entails coordinated cell proliferation, expansion, and differentiation [17]. Pseudotime analysis placed meristematic cells at the origin of hawthorn leaf development and resolved branched differentiation into epidermal, vascular, and photosynthetic lineages. The trajectory architecture, with pavement and vascular progenitors at early pseudotime, phloem-associated and guard cells at intermediate stages, and mesophyll dominance at late pseudotime, is consistent with the classical view of dicotyledonous leaf organogenesis [17, 27, 60, 72–75] and was conserved between both species. This convergence across C. pinnatifida var. major and C. scabrifolia suggests that the core developmental regulatory program is robust to the phenotypic and metabolic differences between these varieties.

Several observations suggest that the hawthorn atlas captures developmental features beyond a generic leaf program. The high chloroplast gene expression in meristematic clusters is less prominent in published Arabidopsis pseudotime reconstructions, where meristematic cells typically show lower chloroplast gene abundance [61, 62, 76–79]. In addition, BEAM analysis placed a secondary metabolite biosynthesis-enriched gene set at the meristematic-to-mesophyll branch point, and several of the flavonoid pathway genes detected in Sect. 3.6 were among those differentially expressed at this transition. These observations suggest that flavonoid biosynthetic gene induction may be coordinated with the onset of mesophyll identity in hawthorn. They are presented as data-supported hypotheses: whether the transcript-level co-occurrence reflects a regulatory coupling or is coincidental requires experimental testing, for example by manipulating cell fate regulators and measuring pathway gene expression and metabolite levels in parallel.

Spatial organization of flavonoid biosynthesis

Flavonoids are ubiquitous phenolic secondary metabolites involved in development, ripening, stress tolerance (biotic and abiotic), and organismal interactions. Cell-type-resolved expression data demonstrate that flavonoid biosynthetic genes are not restricted to a single cell type but are partitioned across multiple populations. This transcript-level partitioning is a robust observation: the pattern was reproducible between species and consistent with the GO enrichment profiles of the relevant cell types (e.g., phloem parenchyma cells, which express PAL, are independently enriched for secondary metabolite biosynthesis GO terms). Prior bulk transcriptome studies of Crataegus chungtienensis identified PAL, 4CL, CHI, and CHS as developmentally regulated genes in leaf tissue [19, 80–82]. Our data confirm expression of these genes in leaves and additionally show that within the leaf, each enzyme is concentrated in a distinct cell population.

Whether this transcript-level partitioning reflects coordinated metabolite exchange between cell types, parallel partial pathways operating independently, or developmental specialization at different stages of leaf maturation cannot be determined from transcript abundance data alone. Protein-level confirmation, in situ hybridization, and cell-type-resolved metabolite profiling (e.g., single-cell metabolomics or spatial metabolomics) are needed to establish functional relevance. We present the transcript-level partitioning as a well-supported observation and the coordinated flux hypothesis as a testable interpretation, not a demonstrated conclusion.

Technical considerations

We combined scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq to offset the known limitations of each. Protoplast-based scRNA-seq can cause loss of osmotically fragile cell types and stress-induced transcriptional changes [63]; chlorophyll autofluorescence in leaf mesophyll protoplasts can also interfere with fluorescence-based quality assessments [64]. snRNA-seq avoids protoplasting-induced stress but captures fewer genes per cell because only nuclear transcripts are recovered [65]. Integration improved overall cellular coverage and transcript detection. Each method retains inherent limitations; interpretations, particularly for rare cell types, should be made with these caveats in mind.

Limitations and future directions

All samples were collected at one developmental stage under uniform conditions. The atlas therefore represents a snapshot; temporal or environmental shifts in cell-type composition or biosynthetic gene expression are not captured. Fourteen flavonoid biosynthetic genes were detected above threshold; the failure to detect additional genomic copies reflects the constraints of the sampling design and sensitivity limits, not necessarily their absence from leaf tissue. Finally, transcript abundance does not directly measure protein activity or metabolite flux; all functional inferences from this atlas require biochemical validation.

Future work should expand sampling across developmental stages (young, expanding, mature, and senescing leaves) and environmental conditions, apply spatial transcriptomics to confirm cell-type positions and gene expression in tissue sections, and integrate proteomics and metabolomics to connect transcript-level observations with metabolic activity. Extension to other Crataegus tissues (fruit, seed, root) would provide a complete single-cell map of secondary metabolism across the plant.

Conclusions

We report the first single-cell transcriptomic atlas of hawthorn (Crataegus) leaves, generated from 32,292 cells profiled across two species. Nine canonical cell types were resolved and annotated, and novel cell-type-specific marker genes were identified for each population. Cell-type-resolved interspecific comparison revealed that 642 DEGs distinguish the two species, with divergent enrichment concentrated in specific cell populations: photosynthesis and volatile biosynthesis enrichment in mesophyll and meristematic cells of C. pinnatifida var. major, and hypoxia-response enrichment in phloem and xylem cells of C. scabrifolia. This localization, accessible only through the single-cell atlas, provides cellular-level hypotheses for known metabolic differences between these varieties. Pseudotime trajectories were conserved between species and consistent with classical leaf organogenesis programs, with evidence for coupling of secondary metabolite biosynthesis onset to the meristem-to-mesophyll transition. Flavonoid biosynthetic genes were spatially partitioned across multiple cell types, with upstream and downstream pathway steps concentrated in distinct populations. These findings establish a cellular framework for mechanistic study of secondary metabolism in hawthorn and provide a reference atlas for future functional, developmental, and breeding applications in this medicinally and agronomically important genus.
