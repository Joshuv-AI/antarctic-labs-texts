Viral Infection‐Induced Epilepsy and the Gut Microbiome Crosstalk
Authors: Xi Guo, Niomi R Kaiser, Susan L Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTViral infections of the central nervous system (CNS) pose a significant risk for seizures, and in some cases, long‐term epileptogenesis. Seizure development following viral infection involves diverse CNS mechanisms, with emerging evidence that peripheral factors, including gut microbiome composition and immune‐inflammatory responses, also contribute. The gut microbiome is a multifaceted ecosystem with important roles in CNS health, and advances in sequencing have enabled investigation of host and microbial genomic changes associated with seizure development. While both animal and human studies link gut dysbiosis to epilepsy, including infection‐induced epilepsy, the specific effects of viral‐induced seizures on the gut microbiome remain unclear. Moreover, the influence of virus‐triggered epileptogenesis is not well understood. This review summarizes current knowledge on viral infections linked to seizures and highlights the limited but growing evidence for gut microbial changes in this context. We focus on Zika virus (ZIKV), West Nile virus (WNV), and herpes simplex virus‐1 (HSV‐1), all implicated in human epileptogenesis, though direct microbiome–seizure connections are still limited. We also discuss severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus‐2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), which causes neurological symptoms without consistently inducing seizures in rodent models despite neurological involvement, and Theiler's murine encephalomyelitis virus (TMEV), a murine virus widely used as a model of infection‐induced seizures because it induces strain‐dependent seizures in mice. Current evidence suggests associations among viral infections, gut microbiome alterations, and seizure occurrence; however, further causative studies are needed to define their roles and elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Infection‐Induced Seizure and Epilepsy

CNS infection is a significant risk factor for seizures and epilepsy. The infection itself can cause neuronal injury and provoke acute seizures within the first 2 weeks post infection, which may later result in unprovoked seizures epilepsy manifesting months to years later (Beghi et al. 2010; DePaula‐Silva et al. 2021; Smeal et al. 2012; Stewart et al. 2010b; WHO 2024). Moreover, CNS infections can also precipitate status epilepticus, a severe medical condition associated with high mortality (Trinka et al. 2012). Importantly, despite the brain's protective barriers, many viruses can breach the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and the blood‐cerebrospinal fluid barrier resulting in CNS infections and subsequent diseases (Al‐Obaidi et al. 2018; Dahm et al. 2016; Daneman and Prat 2015). Viruses can enter through several routes, including infiltrating the brain via blood circulation, the nasal olfactory nerves, the choroid plexus pathway into the cerebral spinal fluid, or through peripheral nerves, resulting in inflammation and/or encephalitis (Kirkman et al. 2010; Nath and Johnson 2022). Given these diverse mechanisms of viral entry and CNS impact, the incidence of infection‐related seizures and epilepsy varies widely depending on the extent to which specific pathogens disrupt these pathways (Annegers et al. 1988; Zhang et al. 2020). CNS infections can promote seizures and epilepsy through several interconnected mechanisms, including neuronal injury, neuroinflammation, disruption of neurotransmitter signaling, and remodeling of neural networks. Together, these processes lower seizure threshold, increase neuronal excitability, and in some cases contribute to the development of chronic epilepsy (Löscher and Howe 2022).

Infections contribute not only to the onset of seizures and epilepsy but are also associated with a higher prevalence of drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE). For example, approximately 16% of patients with autoimmune encephalitis develop DRE within the first year, with electroencephalographic (EEG) features such as status epilepticus, temporal lobe focality, and periodic discharges serving as strong predictors of poor outcomes (Wesselingh et al. 2022). Similarly, 10%–20% of viral encephalitis cases progress to long‐term medically intractable seizures (Misra and Kalita 2009). Notably, patients with viral encephalitis are significantly more likely to develop refractory status epilepticus (31%) than non‐refractory cases (6.2%) (Vooturi et al. 2014). The ketogenic diet (KD) is a high‐fat, low‐carbohydrate dietary therapy that has been used for decades as an effective treatment for patients with DRE, particularly when conventional anti‐seizure medications fail (Mishra et al. 2024). More recently, alterations in the gut microbiota have been identified as an important contributor to its anti‐seizure effects (Olson et al. 2018). Given that patients with DRE respond to microbiome‐based therapies such as the KD, and because these patients are particularly vulnerable to viral infections (Costa and Vale 2024; Zaccara et al. 2017), investigating gut microbial influences in infection‐induced epilepsy models represents a compelling avenue for therapeutic discovery. Also, with increasing evidence that gut microbial composition influences seizure susceptibility, neuroinflammation and responses to therapies such as the KD (Flammer et al. 2023; Riva et al. 2025), understanding how infection‐induced alterations in the microbiome affect seizures represents an important emerging area of investigation.

Gut Microbiome and Immune Signaling

There is an intimate interplay between the gut microbiota and the host immune system. The immune system maintains a delicate balance by regulating pathogens while preserving healthy tissue through various mechanisms, including maintenance of intestinal barrier (Choden and Cohen 2022). Central to this process, goblet and epithelial cells secrete mucins, the key structural glycoproteins of the intestinal mucus layer, which form a physical barrier preventing foreign bacteria from entering the systemic circulation thereby regulating immune function (Zhang and Wu 2020). Consequently, disruptions in microbial composition can compromise intestinal homeostatis and contribute to broader physiological and immune dysfunction. In line with this, gut dysbiosis, which is an imbalance in the composition, diversity, or function of the gut microbial community, has been associated with multiple diseases including inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, neurological and neuropsychiatric conditions, autoimmune diseases, cardiovascular disease, and altered susceptibility to infection (Shen et al. 2025). Accordingly, some therapeutic strategies aim to restore immune function by modulating the microbiome (Han et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2020). For instance, sepsis induced in mice by pathogens from a patient with lethal sepsis is ameliorated by fecal microbiota transplantation, which restores systemic immunity and enhances pathogen clearance (Kim et al. 2020). Importantly, this process is often characterized by shifts in the relative abundance of beneficial versus opportunistic bacterial taxa. Beneficial bacteria that support immune regulation include Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium pseudolongum (Matson et al. 2018). In contrast, features of dysbiosis, such as reductions in Firmicutes and Bacteroides species accompanied by Proteobacteria overgrowth, are associated with immune dysfunction and inflammatory diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (Frank et al. 2007; Sokol et al. 2006). Thus, the gut microbiome modulates host immunity not only through structural components of microbial cells but also via microbial composition‐dependent signaling pathways.

The gut microbiome also plays a central role in shaping both innate and adaptive immune responses through microbial ligands, host receptors, and metabolites (Belkaid and Hand 2014; Hooper et al. 2012). Mechanistically, commensal‐derived molecules such as lipopolysaccharide, flagellin, and peptidoglycan are recognized by host pattern‐recognition receptors (e.g., toll‐like receptors), triggering downstream activation of nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells, mitogen‐activated protein kinase, and inflammasome pathways (Tallant et al. 2004). These signaling cascades regulate cytokine production, reinforce barrier integrity by strengthening tight junctions and mucosal defense, and mediate immunoglobulin A responses (Belkaid and Hand 2014; Hooper et al. 2012). Beyond these structural ligands, microbial metabolites serve as potent immunomodulators. For instance, short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (acetate, butyrate, propionate) are microbial metabolites with well‐established anti‐inflammatory properties that can also indirectly limit viral and bacterial infections by strengthening epithelial barrier function, modulating host immune responses, and altering the local microbial environment (Tan et al. 2014). This is mediated in part by the ability of these SCFAs to promote differentiation of regulatory T cells and effector T‐cell subsets, through G‐protein coupled receptor signaling and histone deacetylase inhibition (Park et al. 2015). In addition to metabolite‐driven effects, specific microbial taxa exert species‐specific immunomodulatory roles. For example, Bacteroides fragilis polysaccharide A induces IL‐10–producing regulatory T cells via Toll‐like receptor 2, whereas segmented filamentous bacteria promote T helper 17 cell differentiation in the intestine (Ivanov et al. 2009; Round and Mazmanian 2010). Together, these interactions form a dynamic, bidirectional network in which microbial composition and metabolic output fine‐tune host immune tone, barrier protection, and inflammatory defense.

Models of CNS Infection in Epilepsy

Several animal models have been established to investigate the intricate mechanisms linking viral infection and epilepsy. However, the development and refinement of infection‐induced models remain limited, in part due to the heterogeneous nature of epilepsy and high mortality rates often observed in rodent models following viral challenge (DePaula‐Silva et al. 2021; Savoca et al. 2024). Despite these challenges, growing evidence highlights the pivotal role of viral infections in initiating inflammatory responses that can culminate in seizure generation. Currently, various viral infection seizure models, including those for herpes simplex virus type‐1 (HSV‐1), West Nile virus (WNV), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus‐2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), and Zika virus (ZIKV), can be used to examine the interplay between viral neuroinvasion, inflammation, and seizure susceptibility (Beckman et al. 2022; Chamberlin et al. 2025; Hussain et al. 2024). However, each model presents distinct limitations. In HSV‐1 models, seizures are often inconsistently reported and are frequently secondary to severe encephalitic pathology, limiting their utility for studying seizure mechanisms independent of overt neurodegeneration. WNV models consistently reproduce neuroinflammation and neuronal injury; however, seizure phenotyping is typically limited, and long‐term epileptogenesis is rarely assessed, as most studies focus on acute encephalitic pathology, immune responses, or survival rather than chronic seizure outcomes (Chaillot et al. 2025; van Marle et al. 2007). For SARS‐CoV‐2 and ZIKV, most existing models emphasize systemic infection, neurodevelopmental defects, or immune responses, rather than seizure outcomes, and often lack standardized behavioral or electrophysiological seizure assessments (Ahn et al. 2026; Miftahof et al. 2025; Moura et al. 2021; Noguchi et al. 2020). Additional viruses, such as the influenza viruses, dengue viruses, and arboviruses, have also been implicated in neurological pathogenesis and seizure activity in humans (Ekstrand et al. 2010; Misra and Kalita 2009; Murthy 2010; Solomon and Vaughn 2002). While these pathogens clearly provoke systemic inflammation and can trigger seizures, dedicated and well‐characterized animal models for many of them are still lacking. Consequently, across these viral systems, direct mechanistic links between viral infection, microbiome alterations, and epileptogenesis remain to be fully established, underscoring the need for refined and integrative preclinical models. Among available viral models, Theiler's murine encephalomyelitis virus (TMEV) remains the most extensively characterized model of infection‐induced seizures and epileptogenesis (Stewart et al. 2010b). The following sections summarize the strengths, limitations, and microbiome‐related findings associated with HSV‐1, WNV, SARS‐CoV‐2, ZIKV, and TMEV models.

Herpes Simplex Virus‐1

Herpes Simplex Virus‐1 (HSV‐1) is a double‐stranded DNA virus that is transmitted through direct contact with lesions (cold sores) or infected bodily fluids, such as genital fluids during sexual intercourse, saliva, or fluid from nearby tissues (Arduino and Porter 2008). In adult patients, primary infection typically begins in peripheral epithelial cells, producing skin or mucosal lesions before the virus spreads to the CNS, where it shows a predilection for the temporal lobes and limbic system structures but is also found in other brain regions (Bradshaw and Venkatesan 2016; Marcocci et al. 2020). Once within the brain, viral replication involves neurons and glial cells triggering the production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines (Rybak‐Wolf et al. 2023). After the acute phase, HSV‐1 establishes latency in peripheral sensory neurons, with periodic reactivation (Sawtell and Thompson 1992). These reactions can provoke additional CNS inflammation, neuronal necrosis, disruption of the BBB, and elevated levels of inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), INF‐γ and IL‐6 (D'Alessandro et al. 2022) (Figure 1). Evidence from an intranasal HSV‐1 infection mouse study indicates that astrocyte and microglial activation further amplifies the inflammatory cascade (Uyar et al. 2020). HSV‐1 in vitro infection revealed that pattern‐recognition receptors in glial cells detect HSV‐1‐associated molecular structures and initiate downstream transcription of inflammatory mediators including type‐1 interferon (IFN), C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) 1, 2, interleukin (IL), 1β, 6, 12B, and TNF‐α (Villalba et al. 2012). In parallel, studies using the HSV‐1 corneal infection model demonstrate that the immune response includes the recruitment and persistence of T cells within sensory ganglia at sites of active and resolved lesions, resulting in interferon‐γ expression (Decman et al. 2005).

HSV‐1 stands as the most prevalent pathogen causing acute viral encephalitis, accounting for approximately 90% of herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE) cases (Steiner and Benninger 2013). Among individuals who develop HSE, the risk of epilepsy is substantial. Acute seizures are a common manifestation of HSE, with clinical reports estimating that 50%–75% of patients experience seizures during the acute phase of infection (Chen et al. 2006; Elbers et al. 2007; Misra et al. 2008; Piret and Boivin 2020; Yin et al. 2025). Beyond these early events, chronic epilepsy is a well‐recognized long‐term consequence, with postencephalitic epilepsy developing in 40%–65% of survivors, often presenting as drug‐resistant temporal‐lobe epilepsy (Marks et al. 1992; McGrath et al. 1997). If left untreated, HSE carries high mortality even with antiviral treatment, and survivors frequently experience long‐term neurological complications such as amnesia, cognitive impairments, behavioral changes, and epilepsy (Gnann and Whitley 2017; Misra et al. 2008). Mechanistically, in vitro and in vivo HSV‐1 infection models indicate that hippocampal pathology, including neuronal loss, mossy fiber reorganization, and reduced inhibitory neurotransmitter expression, contributes to hippocampal hyperexcitability and long‐term epileptogenesis (Chen et al. 2004; Solbrig et al. 2006; Wu et al. 2003). At the cellular level, HSV‐1 binding to rat primary cortical neurons induces membrane depolarization followed by intracellular Ca2+ signaling, suggesting a direct impact on neuronal excitability (Piacentini et al. 2011). Taken together, these clinical and experimental findings underscore that HSE is one of the most clinically relevant viral infections directly linked to the development of both acute symptomatic seizures and chronic epilepsy in humans.

The contribution of the gut microbiota to host responses during HSV‐1 infection has been demonstrated in the context of dietary‐ and antibiotic‐induced microbial alterations. Mice administered the KD in combination with antibiotic‐mediated depletion of the gut microbiota, prior to intranasal HSV‐1 infection, exhibited higher neurodegenerative symptom scores, reduced survival rates, and altered KD‐induced neuroinflammatory responses (Li et al. 2022). Although these findings suggest that gut microbes are essential for KD‐mediated neuroprotection, the specific microbial shifts underlying these effects were not evaluated. However, this finding indicates that diet‐induced modulation of the gut microbiome can mitigate certain HSE‐associated symptoms. Additional experimental studies in mice demonstrated that the KD limits HSE progression following HSV‐1 intranasal infection by reducing neuroinflammation, viral burden, and neurodegeneration, resulting in improved survival (Shan et al. 2023). Notably, these protective effects were partially dependent on the gut microbiota, as antibiotic‐mediated microbial depletion attenuated KD efficacy. Importantly, despite implicating microbiota‐dependent mechanisms, the study did not explicitly quantify seizure frequency or severity and therefore provided no direct evidence of reduced HSE‐associated seizures, instead showing improvements in overall HSE pathology.

At the microbial level, HSV‐1 infection is associated with distinct shifts in gut bacterial composition, reflecting infection‐induced dysbiosis. In an intranasal mouse model of HSV‐1 infection, the abundance of several opportunistic bacterial species, including Bacteroides sartorii, Bacteroides acidifaciens , and Helicobacter ganmani, was significantly increased, accompanied by a reduction in the relative abundance of the beneficial bacterium Lactobacillus gasseri at 15 days post infection (dpi) (Li et al. 2022). Notably, the authors further demonstrated that gut‐derived metabolites such as nicotinamide N‐oxide, produced by beneficial Lactobacillus gasseri and Lactobacillus reuteri , were shown to inhibit HSE progression, suggesting a protective role for microbial metabolites in modulating HSV‐1–induced disease. Similarly, in a murine model in which mice were cutaneously infected with HSV‐1, treatment with beneficial probiotic Lactobacillus plantarum strain 06CC2 for 7 days delayed HSV‐1‐induced skin lesion development and reduced viral loads in the brain, correlating with enhanced Th1 immune responses and increased natural killer cell activity (Matsusaki et al. 2016). More broadly, probiotics from the genera Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Clostridium, Enterococcus, and Streptococcus, specifically the beneficial and non‐pathogenic strains, support gut barrier integrity and immune regulation, reducing susceptibility to HSV‐1 infection (Lopez‐Santamarina et al. 2021). For a comprehensive review see Lopez‐Santamarina et al. 2021. Using an ocular model of HSV‐1 infection, exposure at 7 dpi elicits distinct, sex‐specific alterations in gut microbiota composition, including shifts in the generally beneficial Firmicutes at the phylum level, the SCFA‐producing genus Clostridium XIVa, and Akkermansia muciniphila at the species level (Ramakrishna et al. 2020) (Table 1). These taxa are key producers of SCFAs and possess both barrier‐enhancing and anti‐inflammatory properties. As resident immune cells, microglia are at the frontline of CNS response to HSV‐1 infection, where they serve as primary sensors of viral invasion and coordinators of antiviral immunity (Reinert et al. 2016). Specifically, an ocular HSV‐1 infection murine model demonstrated that at 6 dpi, microglia are the principal source of HSV‐induced IFN expression within the CNS (Reinert et al. 2016). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HSV‐1 not only directly drives hippocampal pathology underlying seizures and epilepsy, but also interacts with the gut microbiome in ways that may influence disease severity, host immune responses, and treatment outcomes. While direct causal links between microbiome alterations and HSV‐1–induced epileptogenesis remain to be fully established, emerging evidence suggests that microbiome‐targeted interventions may hold promise as adjunctive strategies for mitigating post‐encephalitic epilepsy.

Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus‐2 (SARS‐CoV‐2)

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus‐2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) is a pathogenic enveloped, positive‐sense, single‐stranded RNA virus belonging to the Coronaviridae family and is the causative agent of coronavirus disease‐19 (COVID‐19). Clinical manifestations range widely, from mild respiratory illness to severe pneumonia, multiple organ failure, and gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (Li et al. 2020). Neurological involvement is also increasingly being recognized, with approximately 30% of COVID‐19 patients exhibiting neurological abnormalities, including seizures (Mao et al. 2020). Transmission occurs primarily through respiratory droplets, fomites, and person‐to‐person contact although viral shedding in stool has also been reported, though its clinical significance remains less well defined (Wang et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2020). Viral entry is mediated via the angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, which is expressed in the lungs, heart, kidney and intestinal epithelium (Deffner et al. 2020). Entry also requires priming by transmembrane serine protease 2, a cell surface enzyme expressed in epithelia cells that facilitates viral fusion and internalization (Matsuyama et al. 2010). Potential routes of CNS invasion include hematogenous spread, retrograde or anterograde axonal transport, cytokine–induced BBB disruption, and direct entry via the olfactory bulb (Dubé et al. 2018; Meinhardt et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2022), as illustrated in Figure 2.

Viral entry can occur through ACE2‐expressing cells in the nasal cavity and olfactory epithelium (2), enabling direct access to the CNS. Once in the brain, SARS‐CoV‐2 infects neurons, microglia, and astrocytes (3), inducing production and release of IL‐1β, IL‐6, glutamine and glutamate (4). Studies using human samples demonstrate that SARS‐CoV‐2 infection affects multiple cell types, including astrocytes, microglia, neurons and immune cells (Andrews et al. 2022; Crunfli et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2024). Among these, astrocytes appear particularly susceptible to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and viral replication has been observed even in the absence of detectable infected cortical astrocytes (Andrews et al. 2022). Consistent with this vulnerability, studies using long‐term (up to 2 weeks post‐infection) human stem cell‐derived cortical organoids show that SARS‐CoV‐2 infects both astrocytes and neurons and induces pronounced astrogliosis, with transcriptional changes implicating disruptions in astrocyte‐mediated neurotransmitter homeostasis and neuroinflammatory signaling (Colinet et al. 2025). Notably, transcriptomic analyses from this same study revealed that SARS‐CoV‐2 infection increases the expression of pro‐inflammatory, pro‐survival, and astrogliosis‐associated gene programs in human cerebral organoids. Neuronal‐specific effects have also been reported. For instance, non‐human primate studies demonstrate neuronal injury and apoptosis in the cerebellum and brainstem, and analyses of human tissue reveal reduced neurogenesis following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (Bayat et al. 2022; Rutkai et al. 2022). Such neuronal damage and synaptic loss are known to lower seizure thresholds and promote network instability. In addition to these direct glial and neuronal effects, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection is associated with pronounced immune‐related changes in the brain, including elevated levels of IL‐1β and IL‐6 in human brain tissue and preclinical models, as well as widespread microgliosis, astrogliosis, and immune cell infiltration in post‐mortem studies (Crunfli et al. 2022; Soung et al. 2022). Together, this evidence suggests that SARS‐CoV‐2–associated seizures arise from convergent mechanisms, including direct CNS infection, astrocyte‐driven metabolic and neurotransmitter dysregulation, neuronal injury and synaptic loss, and robust neuroimmune activation marked by IL‐1β‐ and IL‐6‐mediated microglial and astrocytic reactivity.

Clinically, seizures have been reported across multiple cohorts, with population‐level analyses showing an increased risk for new epilepsy diagnosis up to 2 years post‐infection (Taquet et al. 2022). In individuals with pre‐existing epilepsy, seizure frequency increased following COVID‐19 infection compared to their pre‐infection baseline (Zhou et al. 2023). Reported seizure types range from generalized tonic–clonic seizures to status epilepticus in both adults and children (Galanopoulou et al. 2020; Varatharaj et al. 2020). Febrile seizures have also been observed, particularly in children, with incidence rising during the Omicron variant surge (Kim et al. 2023). Overall, seizures occur in approximately 15%–30% of COVID‐19 patients with neurological symptoms, whereas chronic epilepsy appears less common but may develop in at‐risk subsets, underscoring the need for long‐term monitoring (Sun et al. 2021). Together, human studies indicate that SARS‐CoV‐2–associated seizures may arise from direct CNS infection, astrocyte and microglial activation, BBB disruption, and gliovascular‐mediated synaptic injury and modulation of ACE2 and angiotensin II pathways (Fekete et al. 2025; Reina et al. 2023; Vohora et al. 2020).

GI symptoms are also frequently reported in COVID‐19 patients including vomiting, anorexia, diarrhea, nausea, and abdominal pain (Guan et al. 2020). Beyond those clinical symptoms, SARS‐CoV‐2 infection induces notable shifts in gut microbial composition. Reported compositional changes include, reduced microbial diversity in both severe and non‐severe COVID‐19 patients compared to healthy controls (Wu et al. 2021). Specifically, analyses of fecal samples from COVID‐19 patients reveal reductions in beneficial commensals such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii , Eubacterium rectale , and Bifidobacterium, alongside increased abundance of opportunistic taxa including Streptococcus, Veillonella, Rothia, and Actinomyces (Gu et al. 2020; Yeoh et al. 2021) (Table 1). Importantly, disease severity correlates with these shifts, as Bacteroides species which is often enriched in inflammatory or disease states, are positively associated with severe COVID‐19, whereas beneficial anti‐inflammatory taxa such as Faecalibacterium are negatively correlated (Kim et al. 2021; Venzon et al. 2021).

The therapeutic potential of gut microbiome‐derived SCFAs has been explored in the context of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. In a clinical study, a high‐fiber diet enriched in SCFA‐producing bacteria significantly alleviated post‐acute COVID‐19 syndrome symptoms (Wang et al. 2022). However, preclinical studies yielded mixed results. For example, in a hamster model of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, SCFA supplementation did not alter disease severity (Sencio et al. 2022), and studies using human intestinal biopsies and epithelial cell culture systems showed that SCFAs did not affect SARS‐CoV‐2 entry or viral replication (Pascoal et al. 2021). Together, these findings suggest that while SCFAs may confer benefits in the post‐acute clinical setting, their direct antiviral or disease‐modifying effects during acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection remain limited and context dependent.

Given the established role of ACE2 in mediating SARS‐CoV‐2 entry into host cells, studies have investigated the effects of modulating ACE2 expression or activity on viral infection and disease outcomes. Microbiome analyses using the K18‐hACE2 intranasal model reveal inverse correlations between viral dose and overall microbial diversity, along with distinct dose‐dependent shifts in microbial community composition (Seibert et al. 2021). Specifically, during peak infection (6 dpi), SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in K18‐hACE2 mice was associated with a reduced abundance of Firmicutes, including members of the families Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae, taxa generally regarded as beneficial due to their roles in maintaining gut homeostasis and short‐chain fatty acid production. In contrast, infection at this same time point was accompanied by an increased representation of Verrucomicrobia, driven primarily by expansion of the family Akkermansiaceae, which includes Akkermansia species that are generally beneficial, although their expansion may also reflect context‐dependent adaptation to host or disease‐associated changes (Seibert et al. 2021) (Table 1). Using the same model, gut microbiome analysis performed at 5–7 dpi also revealed pronounced dysbiosis characterized by depletion of beneficial commensal bacteria and expansion of opportunistic pathobionts (Bernard‐Raichon et al. 2022). Specifically, health‐associated Firmicutes, including members of the Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae families, were reduced, while opportunistic taxa such as Enterococcus, Streptococcus, and members of the Enterobacteriaceae family were enriched. Interestingly, these microbial shifts coincided with epithelial barrier disruption. In the same study, evaluation of antibiotic‐treated COVID‐19 patients revealed gut dysbiosis characterized by loss of beneficial SCFA‐producing genera, including Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Blautia, Bifidobacterium, and expansion of opportunistic pathogens including Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, and Enterobacteriaceae (Bernard‐Raichon et al. 2022) (Table 1). Together, these findings demonstrate that SARS‐CoV‐2 infection consistently alters the gut microbiome by enriching opportunistic taxa and depleting beneficial symbionts. However, the direct contribution of these microbial changes to seizure activity or epileptogenesis remains unknown and warrants further investigation.

Zika Virus

Zika virus (ZIKV), is a mosquito‐borne arbovirus of the Flaviviridae family, and is transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes (Agumadu and Ramphul 2018). Additional modes of ZIKV transmission include through sexual contact, vertical transmission (maternal‐fetal), and exposure to infected bodily fluids (Baud et al. 2017; Bonaldo et al. 2016; Prisant et al. 2016; Rozé et al. 2016). Notably, ZIKV's capacity to cross the placental barrier distinguishes it from many other neurotropic viruses, making maternal–fetal transmission a major route with profound implications for neurodevelopment. In pregnant women, ZIKV infects placental trophoblasts and Hofbauer cells, enabling viral entry into fetal circulation (Jurado et al. 2016; Noronha et al. 2016). Once in the fetus, the virus preferentially targets cortical neural progenitor cells, radial glial cells, neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, leading to apoptosis, pyroptosis, and impaired neurogenesis, as illustrated in Figure 3 (Aubry et al. 2021; He et al. 2020; Lum et al. 2017; Nowakowski et al. 2016; Wen et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2016).

Clinically, seizures are common in congenitally infected infants, and the risk of epilepsy increases substantially among those with severe microcephaly. In one cohort, 54% of affected children developed epilepsy and abnormal EEG activity was reported in 72% of cases (Nunes et al. 2021; Pessoa et al. 2018). Although seizures are a consistent feature of congenital ZIKV infection, the progression to chronic epilepsy is less frequent overall and varies across cohorts (van der Linden Jr et al. 2018).

Experimental models provide critical insight into how viral infection perturbs excitatory–inhibitory balance in the brain, highlighting glial dysfunction as an early contributor to hyperexcitability. Consistent with this, ZIKV infection of human U‐251 glioblastoma astrocytoma cells resulted in significant dysregulation of multiple astrocytic proteins involved in synaptic regulation, cell migration, and differentiation within 48 h post‐infection (Sher et al. 2019). Also, studies in human fetal astrocyte cultures showed that ZIKV infects and replicates within astrocytes, with viral accumulation in enlarged vesicular compartments that precipitated neuronal apoptosis (Veilleux and Eugenin 2023). Because astrocytes play a central role in regulating extracellular glutamate, ZIKV‐induced astrocyte dysfunction may contribute directly to neuronal hyperexcitability. Accordingly, in primary neuron cultures, ZIKV infection caused marked elevations in intracellular Ca2+, TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and glutamate levels up to 48 h post infection, creating a cytotoxic and pro‐excitatory environment (Olmo et al. 2017). ZIKV infection also disrupts ion channel function and enhances neuronal excitability by upregulating the transient receptor potential canonical 4 (TRPC4) channel. TRPC4 is a Ca2+ permeable non‐selective cation channel that facilitates calcium entry and depolarizes the plasma membrane, hence contributing to neuronal firing and hyperexcitability. In a recent study, increased TRPC4 expression was observed 72 h following ZIKV infection in baby hamster kidney cells, and this effect was recapitulated in an in vivo mouse model of ZIKV infection (Chen et al. 2024). Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of TRPC4 reduced ZIKV replication, improved survival in neonatal mice, and significantly attenuated seizure‐like behaviors. Complementing these findings, transcriptomic analysis of neural progenitor cells in a ZIKV‐infected zebra fish model revealed downregulation of the glutamate transporter vglut1, which loads glutamate into synaptic vesicles in presynaptic excitatory neurons, indicating further disruption of glutamate homeostasis (Sow et al. 2024). Together, these findings highlight convergent pathways, excitotoxicity, neuroinflammation, and astrocytic impairment that likely contribute to seizure susceptibility following ZIKV infection.

Evidence evaluating ZIKV‐induced gut microbiome alterations is sparse. However, one study has provided initial insight into how ZIKV infection may perturb microbial composition. In C57BL/6J mice, intravenous ZIKV infection resulted in significant alterations to gut microbial composition 14 dpi, including reductions in health‐associated phyla such as Actinobacteria and Firmicutes, alongside increases in Deferribacteres and Spirochaetes (Corrêa et al. 2021) (Table 1). These microbial shifts were associated with intestinal epithelial damage and enhanced leukocyte recruitment. Interestingly, some of these taxa resemble microbiome profiles observed in epilepsy patients with diarrhea (Liu et al. 2022), suggesting potential parallels between infection‐induced and epilepsy‐associated dysbiosis. However, direct evidence linking ZIKV‐induced microbial alterations to seizure generation or epileptogenesis remains lacking. Future studies should investigate whether ZIKV‐driven changes in peripheral immune signaling and gut microbial composition modulate CNS excitability and influence long‐term seizure risk.

West Nile Virus

West Nile virus (WNV) is a neurotropic member of the Flaviviridae family that infects a wide range of various species, including humans and rodents (Brinton 2002; Habarugira et al. 2020). Transmission occurs through hematophagic mosquitoes of the Culex genus, although other genera are also capable of viral transmission. Birds serve as the reservoir hosts, while humans and other vertebrates serve as dead‐end hosts, unable to contribute to further spread of the virus (Habarugira et al. 2020). Following an infectious mosquito bite, WNV undergoes local replication at the inoculation site, triggering innate immune responses characterized by leukocyte recruitment and cytokine signaling (Chaillot et al. 2025). The virus can subsequently invade the CNS through multiple routes, including endothelial cell infection, BBB disruption, and axonal transport via motor, sensory, or peripheral nerves (Alimonti et al. 2018; Clé et al. 2020; Oh et al. 2017).

Clinically, patients with WNV infection often exhibit CNS pathology such as gliosis, neuronal loss, and inflammation in brain regions including the brain stem and deep gray nuclei (van Marle et al. 2007). Older adults or immunocompromised individuals are particularly susceptible to neuroinvasive disease (Davis et al. 2006; Mbonde et al. 2024), which is associated with higher mortality rates (Jean et al. 2007). The most severe manifestation of WNV includes meningitis and encephalitis, conditions characterized by viral replication in neurons and glial cells, notably astrocytes and microglia (Cheeran et al. 2005; Gould et al. 2025; Stonedahl et al. 2020; Szretter et al. 2010). These pathological changes provoke robust innate immune activation and the release of inflammatory mediators such as TNF‐α, IL‐6, and interferon‐beta (INF‐β) (Habarugira et al. 2020; Quick et al. 2014), as illustrated in Figure 4.

Following a mosquito bite, local viral replication activates innate immune responses involving leukocytes and cytokine signaling. WNV reaches the CNS via endothelial cell infection, BBB disruption, or axonal transport along motor, sensory, or peripheral nerves (2). Within the CNS, infection stimulates innate and adaptive immune responses and induces production of inflammatory mediators including TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IFN‐β (4). Although seizures are uncommon in WNV neuroinvasive disease, they can occur in cases of severe CNS involvement, such as during encephalitis with extensive neuronal injury and neuroinflammation. Early studies reported EEG seizure‐like activity in approximately 30% of patients with WNV encephalitis (Pruzanski and Altman 1962), while more recent estimates suggest an incidence of up to 15% depending on the outbreak (Bagic et al. 2007; Gandelman‐Marton et al. 2003; Klein et al. 2002). Case reports also described generalized tonic–clonic seizures in individual patients (Habeb et al. 2025). However, while acute seizures may occur during infection, progression to chronic epilepsy following WNV infection is exceedingly rare and remains poorly characterized.

WNV targets multiple resident cell types within the CNS, which together contribute to neurotoxicity and seizure generation. Analysis of autopsied neural tissue from patients with WNV encephalomyelitis demonstrates viral infection in both neurons and glial cells, confirming direct CNS involvement (Diniz et al. 2006). However, the same study noted that WNV infection of astrocytes progresses more slowly and does not significantly compromise astrocyte viability. In an ex vivo WNV infection model, astrocytes exhibited a reactive, neurotoxic phenotype 25 dpi, characterized by the secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and soluble neurotoxic factors, including TNF‐α (Garber et al. 2018). These astrocyte‐derived mediators indirectly drive neuronal injury and contribute to sustained neurotoxicity. Notably, experimental evidence further supports a direct role for immune signaling in seizure susceptibility during WNV infection. In an intranasal WNV mouse model, viral infection was detected by 6 dpi in the hippocampus, a brain region highly susceptible to seizure generation (Getts et al. 2007). In this context, interferon‐γ–deficient mice exhibit marked resistance to WNV‐induced seizure activity, despite intact activation of other cytokine pathways, implicating IFN‐γ as a key mediator of infection‐associated seizure generation (Getts et al. 2007). In support of this, direct neuronal infection further disrupts synaptic integrity and amplifies network instability. WNV infection replicates in primary mouse neurons, leading to neuronal injury and cell death, and these pathological changes contribute to reduced seizure thresholds and promote network hyperexcitability (Samuel et al. 2007; Shrestha et al. 2003). Furthermore, in a murine model of neuroinvasive WNV disease via intracranial infection, viral invasion of adult hippocampal neurons was observed 7 dpi and led to complement‐mediated elimination of presynaptic terminals (Vasek et al. 2016). This aberrant synaptic pruning resulted in synapse loss and circuit destabilization within the hippocampus. Together, astrocyte‐mediated inflammatory and metabolic dysfunction and direct neuronal injury with associated synaptic loss likely converge to promote seizure susceptibility by destabilizing excitatory–inhibitory balance and extracellular glutamate homeostasis.

Beyond neurological symptoms, WNV infection frequently causes GI complications, ranging from anorexia and nausea to severe dehydration, vomiting, and intestinal dysmotility (Hughes et al. 2007; Morrey et al. 2013; Sejvar et al. 2008; Tsai et al. 1998). Although no studies have directly examined how the gut microbiome influences WNV‐associated seizures, several lines of evidence suggest the gut may modulate overall disease progression. For example, antibiotic‐induced depletion of gut microbiota increased susceptibility to severe WNV‐induced infection in mice following subcutaneous inoculation in the footpad, with heightened disease severity evident as early as 3 days after antibiotic treatment (Thackray et al. 2018). However, although dietary fiber is well established to modulate gut microbial composition and SCFA production and enhance immune responses, it did not significantly alter clinical outcomes or survival in mice infected with WNV via the intranasal route, with outcomes assessed up to 22 dpi (Ni et al. 2022). WNV also affects the enteric nervous system, causing accumulation of viral antigens leading to GI stasis and diarrhea in mice (Bingham et al. 2014), and concomitantly increasing intestinal permeability in enteroid models (Lin et al. 2023). More recently, WNV‐induced intestinal dysmotility following subcutaneous footpad injection has been linked to CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell‐mediated injury of ENS neurons and glia, with the pathological effects persisting for up to 65 dpi (Janova et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings suggest that gut microbiota and ENS integrity influence WNV pathogenesis. However, none of these alterations have yet been directly linked to seizure generation or epileptogenesis, underscoring the need for targeted mechanistic studies.

Theiler's Murine Encephalomyelitis Virus (TMEV)

Theiler's Murine Encephalomyelitis Virus (TMEV) is a naturally occurring, non‐enveloped, positive‐sense, single‐stranded RNA virus that belongs to the Cardiovirus genus of the Picornaviridae family that naturally infects mice (Lipton 1978; Smeal et al. 2012). TMEV causes enteric infection in mice and is grouped into two subcategories based on virulence. Theiler's original subgroup, which includes the Daniels (DA) and BeAn 8386 strains, is less neurovirulent and induces non‐fatal CNS disease and infection‐related epilepsy (Libbey et al. 2011). In experimental models, more virulent subgroup strains cause severe and often fatal encephalitis and associated seizures (Libbey et al. 2011, 2008; Lorch et al. 1981). Importantly, TMEV remains one of the most robust rodent models of infection‐induced epilepsy, as it produces low mortality and recapitulates key features of human temporal lobe epilepsy, including hippocampal sclerosis and neuronal loss (Libbey et al. 2008).

TMEV‐induced epilepsy model typically involves intracerebral injection of the DA or BeAn strains into adult C57BL/6 mice. Although many studies report using ~3 × 105 plaque‐forming units (PFU), the dose required to reliably induce seizures is viral titer dependent; therefore, optimal PFU must be empirically determined for each experimental setting. Following intracortical infection, mice develop acute symptomatic, afebrile seizures in approximately 65% of cases by 3 dpi, with peak seizure activity at around 6 dpi (Libbey et al. 2011). Seizure severity is commonly assessed using the Racine scale, an ordinal scale method to evaluate seizure severity, ranging from facial automatisms and head nodding (stages 1–2) to forelimb clonus (stage 3), rearing (stage 4), and generalized tonic–clonic seizures with loss of postural control (stage 5), with symptomatic mice typically scoring ≥ 3 (Barker‐Haliski et al. 2016; Libbey et al. 2011; Racine 1972; Stewart et al. 2010b). Infected mice exhibit acute encephalitis that resolves within 2–4 weeks, enabling study of both acute and chronic seizure phases. Approximately 50% to 75% of TMEV‐infected mice display behavioral and EEG seizures between 3 and 7 dpi, while chronic seizures develop in 25% to 40% of mice that exhibit acute seizures (Libbey et al. 2008; Stewart et al. 2010a, 2010b).

TMEV infection induces neuroinflammation through two major mechanisms. First, viral replication leads to neuronal lysis, releasing cellular debris that activates inflammatory signaling pathways (Lindsley and Rodriguez 1989; Tomatis et al. 2023). Second, innate and adaptive immune responses amplify neuroinflammation through microgliosis, astrogliosis, cytokine release, and infiltration of peripheral macrophages (DePaula‐Silva 2024; Lindsley and Rodriguez 1989) (Figure 5). These processes particularly affect hippocampal CA1 and CA2 pyramidal neurons, which are highly vulnerable to TMEV infection and play a central role in seizure generation (Patel et al. 2017). For a detailed review of TMEV‐induced immune responses, see DePaula‐Silva 2024.

Summary schematic of TMEV‐induced neuroinflammation and seizure‐related pathology. Following intracranial infection, TMEV replicates within neurons and triggers neuronal lysis (1). This cellular damage activates inflammatory signaling pathways and recruits microglia and astrocytes (2), initiating robust neuroinflammation. Peripheral immune cells, including macrophages and T cells, enter the CNS (3), amplifying cytokine production and immune activation. These responses collectively promote neuronal loss, astrocytosis, microgliosis, and increased susceptibility to seizures (4). Cytokine signaling contributes significantly to TMEV‐induced seizures and epileptogenesis. TNF‐α acting through its receptors tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1) and 2 (TNFR2) has been linked to neuronal apoptosis and increased seizure susceptibility (Patel et al. 2017; Yamamoto et al. 2006). IL‐6 also plays a key role in acute seizure induction following intracranial injection of the DA strain, where reduction or absence of IL‐6 leads to fewer acute seizures, reduced seizure severity, and lower seizure incidence (Cusick et al. 2017; Libbey et al. 2011). These cytokine‐driven mechanisms underscore the contribution of neuroinflammation to TMEV‐associated epileptogenesis. Notably, susceptibility varies by mouse strain. C57BL/6 mice develop seizures and chronic epilepsy, whereas strains such as SJL/J, FVB/N, and BALB/c do not (Stewart et al. 2010a; Tsunoda and Fujinami 1996). In SJL/J mice, infection instead triggers a chronic biphasic demyelinating disease known as TMEV‐induced demyelinating disease, which models progressive multiple sclerosis (DePaula‐Silva et al. 2017).

TMEV infection is associated with significant alterations in the gut microbiome, which vary with mouse strains and disease stages (Carrillo‐Salinas et al. 2017; Gallucci et al. 2021). However, some studies reported minimal microbial effects, likely reflecting differences in diet, experimental design, or sampling time points, which were not consistently reported (Omura et al. 2020). For instance, Omura et al. observed decreases in the generally beneficial, SCFA‐associated genus Dorea at 4 dpi, increases in the commensal genus Marvinbryantia at 7 and 35 dpi, and later enrichment in Coprococcus, a typically beneficial but context‐dependent commensal, at 35 dpi. These variations underscore the substantial influence of diet and methodology on microbiome outcomes. Importantly, antibiotic‐induced gut dysbiosis reduces acute seizure burden in TMEV‐infected mice but also abolishes the antiseizure efficacy of carbamazepine, indicating that the microbiome is required for pharmacological seizure control (Erickson et al. 2025).

In our previous study, we reported that C57BL/6J mice infected with TMEV displayed microbial changes as early as 5–7 dpi, with clear distinctions between communities infected and control groups (Gallucci et al. 2021). Notable changes included depletion of Prevotella and Bifidobacterium (order Bifidobacteriales) in TMEV‐infected mice, taxa that are commonly associated with favorable health outcomes in humans, Table 1 (Gallucci et al. 2021). For example, Prevotella abundance has been identified as a potential indicator for liver carcinoma risk (Huang et al. 2018), whereas members of Bifidobacteriales are widely recognized for their probiotic properties and roles in supporting host health (Biavati et al. 2000; Cronin et al. 2011). Additionally, loss of the genus Allobaculum was identified as a putative biomarker of TMEV infection, independent of seizure phenotype. Prior studies associating Allobaculum with serotonin and glutamate regulation in mouse models of depression and schizophrenia suggest that this genus may exert a broader influence in neurological function (Gubert et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2022, 2020). Also, other microbial biomarkers observed in seizure‐negative TMEV‐infected mice included generally commensal taxa within phylum Tenericutes and the class Bacteroidia, alongside pathobiont‐associated Mollicutes, as well as beneficial SCFA‐producing genera such as Roseburia and Anaeroplasma (Gallucci et al. 2021) (Table 1). Importantly, these microbiota changes appear to be influenced not only by infection status but also by dietary factors that shape gut microbial composition. For instance, diet sterilization and formulation significantly affect both acute seizure onset and chronic disease burden in TMEV‐infected C57BL/6J mice, accompanied by shifts in the gut microbial composition (Zierath et al. 2024). This work emphasized inter‐laboratory variability and dietary differences as major confounders in microbiome–epilepsy research. In their earlier work, Libbey et al. showed that irradiated diets were associated with more severe seizures compared with autoclaved diets, suggesting that diet‐induced shifts in the microbiome may influence seizure outcomes, even though microbial composition was not directly assessed (Libbey et al. 2016). Together, these observations indicate that diet‐related microbial changes are likely to contribute to disease progression in TMEV infection.

Interestingly, TMEV‐infected, seizure‐positive mice showed marked changes in gut microbiome composition compared to infected mice without seizures (Gallucci et al. 2021). Among the most striking changes was a depletion of beneficial S‐equol–producing bacteria, particularly Adlercreutzia equolifaciens (family Coriobacteriaceae), compared to infected mice without seizures. S‐equol is a microbial metabolite with demonstrated BBB permeability and neuroprotective properties (Johnson et al. 2020). Additionally, S‐equol and its precursors exhibit antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory effects in microglial and astrocytic cell lines, and provide neuroprotection against hypoxia‐induced injury (Subedi et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2016). Functional analysis using whole‐cell patch‐clamp recordings confirmed that S‐equol reduces neuronal hyperexcitability in cortical slices from TMEV‐infected seizure‐positive mice, directly linking microbially derived metabolites to seizure mechanisms (Gallucci et al. 2021). These findings suggest that seizure activity itself may drive a loss of protective microbial metabolites, creating a feed‐forward loop that promotes hyperexcitability.

At the synaptic level, TMEV infection disrupts the excitatory/inhibitory balance within hippocampal networks. Following intracortical infection, electrophysiological recordings have demonstrated increased spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) and reduced spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSCs) in hippocampal CA3 circuit during both the acute (4 to 7 dpi) and chronic (2 months post‐infection) stages of TMEV‐induced seizure (Smeal et al. 2015, 2012). Elevated IL‐6 expression may further contribute to hyperexcitability by reducing the surface expression of γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor, thereby diminishing inhibitory neurotransmission (Garcia‐Oscos et al. 2012). Because imbalances in GABA and glutamate signaling are central to seizure susceptibility, these cytokine‐driven alterations significantly shift synaptic homeostasis toward excitation. Additionally, increased TNF‐α expression following intracortical TMEV infection correlates with enhanced α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid receptor (AMPAR) expression in the CA3 region of the hippocampus, further promoting neuronal hyperexcitability (Patel et al. 2017). Collectively, these findings indicate that TMEV infection perturbs neurotransmitters homeostasis and lowers seizure thresholds, providing mechanistic insight into TMEV‐induced hyperexcitability and epileptogenesis.

Taken together, studies involving ZIKV, WNV, HSV‐1, and SARS‐CoV‐2 highlight both the clinical relevance and the limitations of current viral models for understanding seizure susceptibility and epileptogenesis. ZIKV and WNV underscore the vulnerability of developing and mature CNS tissues to virus‐induced inflammation, although direct mechanistic links to microbiome alterations and chronic epilepsy remain limited. HSV‐1 provides the strongest evidence for virus‐associated epileptogenesis in humans, with high rates of postencephalitic epilepsy and emerging findings implicating microbiome–immune interactions in modulating disease severity. SARS‐CoV‐2, while less consistently linked to epilepsy, demonstrates how systemic viral infection and widespread microbial dysbiosis may contribute to neurological complications and potentially increase long‐term epilepsy risk. Although direct comparisons across models are limited by differences in experimental design, host species, and sampling time points, several common microbiome alterations have been reported following viral infection. Despite differences among the viral models, decreases in Actinobacteria were observed in ZIKV and SARS‐CoV‐2 mouse models, whereas increases were reported in patients with COVID‐19 (Corrêa et al. 2021; Seibert et al. 2021). Reductions in Firmicutes were reported following ZIKV infection, HSV‐1 infection, and SARS‐CoV‐2 mouse models (Corrêa et al. 2021; Ramakrishna et al. 2020; Seibert et al. 2021). Additionally, Bifidobacterium abundance was reduced in both the TMEV model and patients with COVID‐19 (Gallucci et al. 2021; Yeoh et al. 2021). Although the biological consequences are likely context‐dependent, many members of these taxa are associated with maintenance of gut homeostasis production of anti‐inflammatory metabolites and regulation of host immune responses. Therefore, reductions in these microbial populations may contribute to microbial dysbiosis, heightened inflammation and altered gut‐brain signaling during viral infection. These recurring microbial alterations suggest that certain taxa may be broadly responsive to viral infection and could contribute to neuroimmune responses relevant to epileptogenesis. However, additional longitudinal studies with standardized sampling approaches will be required to determine whether these microbiome changes consistently contribute to seizure susceptibility and epilepsy across viral models and clinical populations. Nevertheless, evidence directly connecting viral neuropathogenesis, gut microbial alterations, and seizure outcomes remains limited. These gaps underscore the importance of well‐controlled experimental systems such as the TMEV model, which, despite being murine‐specific, offers reproducible seizures, robust immune activation, and tractable opportunities to dissect gut–brain interactions in virus‐induced epilepsy.

Summary and Future Studies

Historically, epilepsy has been conceptualized primarily as a disorder rooted in intrinsic abnormalities of brain structure or function. However, this brain‐centric view neglects the profound influence of systemic metabolic and physiological states on neuronal excitability. Over the past decade, research into the gut microbiome has revealed compelling associations between microbial composition and neurological outcomes, including seizure susceptibility. Although many studies have documented correlations between changes in gut microbiota composition and epilepsy, establishing direct causal mechanisms is to be determined. Integrating these findings with evidence that (i) seizures frequently arise following CNS viral infections, (ii) viruses can substantially reshape gut microbial communities, and (iii) gut microbial metabolites influence neuronal hyperexcitability, we propose that virus disruption of the gut microbiota may represent a key mechanistic pathway contributing to seizure development during infection. This framework repositions the microbiome as a potential mediator linking CNS viral pathology to epileptogenesis.

Moving forward, several lines of investigation are essential for defining these mechanistic relationships. First, studies must delineate how CNS viral infections modulate specific bacterial taxa and microbial metabolites, particularly those influencing neurotransmitter systems, immune signaling, and neuronal excitability. Elucidating how viral entry routes, neurotropism, and inflammatory responses correlate with seizure incidence and microbiome shifts will provide critical mechanistic insight. Longitudinal studies tracking the temporal sequence of viral infection, seizure onset, and microbial perturbations are especially needed to establish causality rather than correlation.

Future research should also incorporate experimental manipulation of the gut microbiome, including antibiotic depletion, targeted probiotic supplementation, dietary interventions, and fecal microbiota transplantation. These approaches will help determine whether restoring or modifying microbial communities can prevent or mitigate infection‐induced seizures. Ultimately, such studies may uncover novel therapeutic strategies for reducing seizure burden in individuals with post‐infectious or drug‐resistant epilepsy. Understanding how microbial balance (eubiosis) versus imbalance (dysbiosis) influences CNS viral outcomes will be particularly important for clarifying the bidirectional relationship between infection and brain excitability. Despite recent progress, the study of infection‐induced epilepsy faces significant challenges, particularly the limited availability of suitable animal models. Among currently available systems, the TMEV model stands out as a powerful tool for exploring gut–brain interactions during viral‐induced epileptogenesis. Its reproducible seizure phenotype, robust neuroimmune activation, and measurable microbial alterations offer a tractable platform to dissect the interplay among viral neuropathogenesis, gut dysbiosis, and seizure susceptibility with far greater resolution than other viral models.

Despite growing interest in the intersection of viral neuropathogenesis, gut microbiome alterations, and seizure susceptibility, the current evidence base remains fragmented. For several viruses, only limited data exist linking microbial shifts to immune activation or metabolite changes, making it challenging to define mechanistic pathways with confidence. Future work must focus on integrating virology, immunology, microbiome profiling, and neurophysiology within the same experimental frameworks. In particular, studies that simultaneously measure gut microbial composition, immune signaling, microbial metabolites, and neuronal excitability will be essential for transforming correlative observations into causal understanding. Clarifying these relationships will be critical for identifying microbiome‐dependent mechanisms of infection‐induced epileptogenesis and for guiding the development of targeted interventions.
