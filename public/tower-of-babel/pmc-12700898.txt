Real time sugarcane stem health detection with YOLOv8 tinier and RepNCSP ELAN
Authors: Pushpendra Kumar, Shraddha Arora, Shaveta Arora
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSugarcane is the largest cash crop globally in terms of production and serves as a primary source of sugar and biofuel. The buds naturally present on sugarcane stems are used for vegetative propagation, and determining the health of the sugarcane stems is a crucial preliminary step in automated bud extraction pipelines to achieve better seed quality and higher crop yield. However, automated sugarcane stem health detection is not addressed in prior research. Additionally, agricultural automation models must be extremely lightweight and efficient for edge and embedded deployment on resource-constrained devices, where both model size and inference latency are important considerations. In this paper, we present a new lightweight object detection model, RepNCSP-ELAN-YOLOv8n (Repeated Normalised Cross Stage Partial with Efficient Layer Aggregation Network-You Only Look Once version 8 nano), for real-time sugarcane stem health classification (Fit vs. Unfit). The proposed model achieves ultra-low computational complexity of only 7.2 GFLOPs, 2.2 million parameters, and 4.6 MB model size (in PyTorch)—significantly lower than prior YOLOv8n and all other nano-level YOLO versions—while still providing state-of-the-art performance of 90.1% mAP@50, 64.2% mAP@95, and 14.33 ms average inference time. The model is trained on a self-curated dataset of 3839 images collected under various natural farm conditions in multiple Indian regions. We conduct extensive benchmarking with major YOLO versions, demonstrating our proposed model’s superior efficiency and competitive accuracy. Furthermore, we empirically validate the model’s real-world deployability across multiple edge AI platforms (Jetson Nano, Raspberry Pi 4B) and inference formats (PyTorch, TorchScript, ONNX, TensorRT, MNN and NCNN). The proposed model offers a real-time framework for automated sugarcane stem health detection, providing a crucial step toward intelligent, edge-enabled agricultural automation solutions for better seed quality and higher crop productivity. Introduction

Sugarcane is one of the most critical crops, cultivated on over 26 million hectares in 2020 worldwide, according to1 (UN). In addition to its primary use in sugar production, it is a source of biofuel and industrial by-products. The productivity of this crop is therefore of economic, environmental, and social significance. A key factor in this respect is the quality of stem nodes (buds), which are collected from the field and replanted as seed material for a new crop. Therefore, it is critical to ensure stem health to guarantee high germination rates, field vigor, and overall productivity. However, a manual expert inspection of their condition is laborious, subjective, and error-prone, especially in large-scale operations. Precision agriculture based on emerging digital solutions offers an opportunity to automate this task with the help of computer vision and deep learning. In particular, any off-the-shelf object detection model could be fine-tuned to localize and classify sugarcane stems to remove the burden from human inspectors. However, the agricultural domain has its specifics, with images acquired under variable lighting, unconstrained weather conditions, and uncontrolled cluttered backgrounds. On top of that, sugarcane nodes are typically small and long, as well as occasionally occluded, which significantly increases the challenge of detection. Finally, real-world deployment in farms is commonly required to be lightweight to run on edge devices with limited resources (Jetson Nano, Raspberry Pi, and others). This highly restricts the range of applicable solutions and calls for a careful balance between accuracy, robustness, and complexity in choosing a model.

YOLO (You Only Look Once)2–6 family of object detectors has become one of the leading choices for real-time tasks due to a unified detection pipeline and a favorable speed-accuracy trade-off. Over different versions of the framework, new architectures with improved components have been released. They include cross-stage partial (CSP) connections, advanced activation functions, re-parameterization techniques, anchor-free design7 and many others. As a result, outstanding results have been achieved for general-purpose detection. Agricultural applications of YOLO (You Only Look Once) models8, however, are limited by several known problems. Recent work in precision agriculture demonstrates that nano-scale YOLO pipelines, integrated with IoT, can provide real-time, on-device diagnosis in the field, reinforcing the shift toward edge-first designs9,10. One of the main issues is their sensitivity to small objects. In particular, traditional backbones in YOLO are designed with the focus on the efficient information fusion across different scales and have poor representational capacity to retain sufficient fine-grained features to detect tiny objects such as buds or nodes. At the same time, it is coupled with the feature redundancy problem, where shallow spatial cues in deeper layers of the model are gradually lost, while being critical to the task of identifying the health status of sugarcane stems. The second problem is high computational complexity, which imposes substantial limits on resource constraints and hinders widespread adoption of many YOLO variants on low-power devices that are typically used for on-field deployment11.

These observations call for research on a detection framework that can better address the domain-specific challenges above and outperform existing YOLO-based solutions. To this end, we introduce a new lightweight YOLOv8-based model12 that integrates the RepNCSP-ELAN convolutional block as its backbone. The RepNCSP module uses re-parameterization to create a low-redundancy architecture that is less computationally intensive to train and can yield a simplified structure for inference13. The ELAN-style feature aggregation component in this block also ensures an effective fusion of shallow and deep features. This gives two main benefits of the suggested solution for the target application. On the one hand, it would help the network retain fine-grained spatial information, which is crucial to detecting small and complex patterns on sugarcane stems. On the other hand, it should decrease its complexity and thus allow for efficiently running on edge AI accelerators commonly used in smart agriculture.

We present the experimental results that verify that RepNCSP-ELAN, when integrated with YOLOv8, provides a detector that is both highly accurate and efficient and can be robustly applied to sugarcane stem health detection in real-world field settings.

Related work

Automatic recognition of seed quality or the health of a crop, one of the application fields of artificial intelligence in contemporary agriculture14,15, can contribute to higher productivity or losses due to weeds16, diseases or pests17,18. Weed detection or pest recognition can particularly profit from the use of intelligent computer vision systems. Thanks to its real-time accuracy, the YOLO family of object detection algorithms is among the most commonly used solutions in agricultural research. Many studies use its various versions for crop health monitoring, weed control and pest detection19,20.

YOLO for agricultural applications

There is a growing emphasis on lightweight YOLO variants that preserve accuracy while meeting the strict latency, memory, and energy constraints of embedded platforms in agriculture21,22. Various YOLO variants have been for seed quality prediction. For example, the YOLO-R model has demonstrated high accuracy (mAP@50 of 0.9539) and fast detection times (0.011 s per image) in evaluating rice seed germination rates, making it suitable for real-time application23. YOLOv5-nano variant was efficient for artificial seed-like object detection from UAV images with high accuracy (mAP@50 of 84.6%) and high inference rates under ideal conditions24. An improved YOLO-v5 network, which incorporates RGB-D datasets and advanced feature extraction modules, increased the precision of soybean pod detection to 88.14%, facilitating rapid and large-scale phenotyping of soybean populations25. The model provides high accuracy and efficiency in disease detection. YOLO-based models, such as YOLO V5-CAcT26 and YOLOv527, have demonstrated high accuracy in detecting crop diseases, achieving recognition accuracies of up to 94.24% and 98.3%, respectively, while maintaining efficient processing times28. YOLO provides real-time detection capabilities as a single-shot detection model. YOLO models process images at high speeds, with some implementations achieving real-time processing at 45 frames per second, making them suitable for timely disease detection and intervention29. YOLO is a lightweight and scalable model. Improved YOLO models, such as YOLO-Banana and MobileNet-CA-YOLO, have been optimized to be lightweight, reducing model sizes significantly while maintaining high detection accuracy. These models are suitable for deployment on low-power devices such as the Raspberry Pi30. YOLO models have been successfully applied in various agricultural settings, from banana orchards to rice fields, demonstrating their versatility in detecting diseases and pests across different crops and environments31. Models such as YOLO-pest proved high precision and recall values for detecting multiple pest species in the same frame32. YOLOv5x, based on YOLOv5 and enhanced with transfer learning and a visual attention mechanism, is used for detecting and localizing weeds and vegetables33. YOLO-based models can be applied to multiclass weed and pest detection to address the challenges of identifying pest and weed species with high visual similarity.

Object detection challenges in sugarcane and YOLO-based solutions

Object detectors have numerous intrinsic challenges when applied to sugarcane crops due to their unique characteristics and the environment in which they grow. Some of these challenges are related to scale, background complexity, and the environment. Detection at different scales is often required for sugarcane fields, like seedling or stem node detection34,35 and leaf disease detection36. Backgrounds in sugarcane fields are complex and confusing, and cause a decline in detection accuracy37. Methods like the employment of attention mechanisms and deformable convolution networks have been used to enhance feature extraction and preserve accuracy in such environments. Models for detection have to be resilient to conditions in the environment, like shadows and blurring of images. Improved bounding box regression loss functions are a typical instance of an improvement that has been demonstrated to boost model generalization and robustness in complex sugarcane field settings. Accurate detection models are required for sugarcane diseases like white leaf disease (WLD)38. Although there are challenges in achieving high precision and recall values for different models, UAV-acquired RGB imagery combined with deep learning models like YOLOv5 can be effective.

Several studies have explored the detection of sugarcane stem nodes via various YOLO models. For example, an improved YOLOv4-Tiny algorithm was introduced to increase the speed and accuracy of sugarcane stem node detection. This model achieved a mean accuracy precision (mAP@50) of 99.11% and a detection accuracy of 97.07%, significantly outperforming Faster-RCNN and the original YOLOv4 algorithm39. Another study utilized YOLOv4 in composite environments for sugarcane stem node detection effectively. This model achieved an average precision (mAP@50) of 95.17% and a speed of 69 frames per second (fps). This processing speed is well above the real-time requirement of 30 fps. Lightweight models have been developed to fulfil the requirements of embedded and small devices. The MobileNet v2-YOLO v5s40 model, for example, was designed to reduce complexity while maintaining high precision. This model achieved fast detection speed and demonstrated superior performance on embedded devices compared with the original YOLO v5s. Similarly, a novel lightweight architecture based on YOLOv5, named G-YOLOv5s-SS, was proposed to enhance recognition accuracy while significantly reducing model size and complexity. This model achieved an average precision of 97.6% and demonstrated excellent performance in terms of both accuracy and efficiency41.

The accuracy of models for detecting sugarcane stem nodes can be highly degraded under complicated and complex field conditions. To address this, an improved YOLOv7-based model was developed that incorporates the SimAM attention mechanism and deformable convolution network to enhance feature extraction and model robustness. This model achieved a mean average precision (mAP@50) of 94.53% and an F1 value of 92.41, outperforming the original YOLOv7 model35. Another study proposed a YOLOv3-CSE network for the detection of sugarcane buds, achieving a precision of 96.93% and an mAP@50 of 95.87%. This performance was significantly better than that of the original YOLOv3 network. Combining deep learning models with traditional computer vision methods can further improve detection accuracy. A study that combined YOLOv3 with traditional methods of computer vision achieved 99.68% precision and 100% recall for sugarcane stem node detection. Compared with YOLOv342 alone, this hybrid approach demonstrated superior performance. Sugarcane images captured in natural environments43 can be complex, and these images can contain several objects in the background. It is difficult to differentiate between sugarcane stems and objects such as leaves and weeds. These studies showcase a constant trend toward optimizing YOLO systems for real-time, resource-efficient deployment in agriculture.

To summarize, while YOLO-based models have shown promising results in various agricultural applications, their direct deployment in sugarcane stem health detection has been less explored due to challenges such as sensitivity to small objects, complex field backgrounds, and the need for lightweight architectures that can run efficiently on edge devices. Existing approaches either achieve high accuracy at the cost of computational complexity or prioritize speed but compromise robustness in natural field conditions. These limitations underscore the need for a novel, lightweight yet accurate backbone design, which motivates our proposed RepNCSP-ELAN block to bridge this gap.

The proposed model

The detection pipeline of the proposed method is the conventional YOLO detection pipeline, with the following backbone changes. The pre-processed input images are sent to the backbone, where the original C2f modules are substituted with the proposed RepNCSP-ELAN blocks. The RepNCSP-ELAN blocks reduce redundancy via CSP-style feature partitioning and re-parameterization, while preserving the strong feature aggregation ability of ELAN design. The resulting feature maps are compact yet highly expressive. The neck and head of YOLOv8 are left unchanged, which perform multi-scale feature fusion and final bounding box and class predictions, as normal.

The proposed model is smaller than the original YOLOv8-nano (shown in Fig. 1) due to the inclusion of RepNCSP-ELAN blocks in the backbone. A state-of-the-art YOLOv8n model is selected as the baseline model for this study. The modular construction of the RepNCSP-ELAN block can be integrated into any YOLO model, including YOLOv9 to YOLOv12. We have adopted YOLOv8 as the base model for this study. YOLOv8 has been widely accepted and is being actively developed by the community.

The YOLO model can be constructed using three key sections, namely the backbone, neck, and head. Input images first traverse the backbone section. The relevant and meaningful features are extracted by the backbone section from the input image. The neck serves as an intermediary section and plays a major role in collecting the extracted features from the backbone and performing further transformations to prepare them for the detection stage. The head section is the final component, which makes the final predictions by outputting bounding boxes, object classes, and confidence scores. GELAN: motivation for RepNCSP-ELAN backbone

YOLOv947 presented generalized efficient layer network (GELAN) design template for convolutional block design with features such as high parameter usage, modularity, adaptability, and the ability to overcome information bottlenecks. GELANs are convolutional block modules based on the combination of CSPNet45 and ELAN44. GELAN (Fig. 3) is designed to address the information bottleneck problem. The information bottleneck34 problem arises when data undergoes transformations in deep networks, leading to potential information loss. It refers to the reduction in mutual information between the input and output, suggesting that as network depth increases, more of the original input information may be lost. As represented by Eq. (1), any deep network transformation on data X can result in a loss of information during this transformation.

GELAN.

In this equation, mutual information is represented by I, and f and g represent the transformation functions. θ is the parameter of f, and φ is the parameter of g. f and g are two consecutive deep neural network transformations.

GELAN is also designed to deliver higher parameter usage than conventional depth-wise convolution blocks do. The objective of incorporating the GELAN is to retain more information during the data feedforward process, thus mitigating the effects of the information bottleneck by incorporating reversible functions. The architecture of the GELAN enables more reliable gradients to be obtained, which is crucial for effective model training and convergence, addressing issues highlighted by the information bottleneck principle. GELAN provides a lightweight design, making it suitable for various inference devices. It allows for better parameter utilization, a decreased number of parameters, and less computational complexity. GELAN maintains robust performance across various convolutional blocks and depth configurations. Computational blocks within the GELAN can be replaced according to specific requirements without sacrificing performance. This feature makes the GELAN extremely adaptive and flexible.

ELAN

An efficient large kernel attention network (ELAN) can aggregate both global and local information more effectively by utilizing large kernel sizes. Figure 4 shows the block architecture of the ELAN block. Efficient large kernel attention networks (ELAN) are designed to improve image super resolution (SR) via long-range attention mechanisms while maintaining computational efficiency. This approach addresses the limitations of traditional transformer-based methods, which often involve high computational costs and redundant operations. ELAN employs shift convolution (shift-conv) to extract local structural information efficiently while maintaining the same complexity as a 1 × 1 convolution48.

Efficient large kernel attention network (ELAN).

ELAN provides a groupwise multiscale self-attention (GMSA) module, which calculates self-attention on nonoverlapped groups of features using different window sizes to capture long-range dependencies. By stacking highly effective long-range attention blocks (ELABs) that employ shift-conv and GMSA modules further improved with a shared attention mechanism, the ELAN architecture delivers superior performance to traditional transformer-based super resolution models but with significantly reduced complexity. ELAN provides a mechanism with efficient nonlocal contrastive attention (ENLCA)49,50 for enhancing single-image super resolution (SISR) via nonlocal attention (NLA), which is helpful for capturing intrinsic feature correlations in natural images.

CSPNet and its role in lightweight feature learning

Cross-stage partial network (CSPNet)45 blocks are classic and widely adopted convolutional blocks introduced in YOLOv4. Figure 5 represents the generalized CSPNet block composition. The CSPNet architecture is designed to extend the learning capability of convolutional neural networks and reduce computational costs. CSPNet achieves high learning capability by integrating feature maps from different stages of the network, which is helpful in mitigating the problem of duplicate gradient information during network optimization. CSPNet, which is integrated with depth-wise separable convolutions, is a lightweight network. CSPNet, combined with GhostNet, spatial pyramid pooling (SPP), and spatial pyramid pooling fast (SPPF) in various YOLO versions2, is used to enhance the detection capabilities.

RepNCSP-ELAN block: an efficient variant of GELAN

The design of the RepNCSP-ELAN block is shown in the following subsection. RepNCSP-ELAN block is a particular and condensed convolution block derived from the generalized efficient large attention network (GELAN) template. The RepNCSP-ELAN block architecture is shown in Fig. 6. The block is designed with CSPNet, ELAN, and RepNBottleneck blocks.

RepNCSP-ELAN block.

Design overview of RepNCSP-ELAN block

The block is initiated with a starting convolution, and then the features are split into two RepNCSP (repeated normalized cross stage partial) branches in parallel, where each branch has three serial convolutions. The outputs of both branches are concatenated and pass through a final convolution layer to produce the output of the RepNCSP-ELAN block. The RepNCSP block (Fig. 7) is made up of Conv and RepNBottleneck (repeated normalized bottleneck) blocks (Fig. 8). The RepNBottleneck (Fig. 8) convolutional block is an enhanced version of the CSPNet block (Fig. 5). The bottleneck module used in the block is used to reduce the dimensionality of the feature map. This helps to reduce the complexity and increase the computational efficiency of the model. The split and merge technique inherited from CSPNet can be potentially used to learn local and global features more efficiently by RepNBotteneck. RepNBotteneck is built by sequentially adding RepConvN (Repetitive Convolutional Normalized block) (Fig. 9) and Conv block (2D convolutional block) (Fig. 10) with a shortcut connection.

RepNCSP block.

RepNBottleneck block.

RepConvN block.

Conv block.

The RepConvN block consists of 2 Conv blocks with a Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU) in between. The Conv module has a 2D convolutional block, a batch normalization block, and a SiLU activation function chained in series.

Figure 7 a close look at all of the blocks needed to make the RepNCSP-ELAN block. As mentioned before, the RepNCSP block makes use of the Conv block as a basic element in its structure. The speed and performance of the RepNCSP block are directly related to the potential of the Conv block. Every layer of a CNN learns to identify specific features. Starting layers usually identify basic features such as edges and textures, and deep layers can recognize more complex patterns and objects.

The RepNBottleneck (Fig. 8) block comprises a RepConvN and a parallel 2D convolution branch, whose outputs are fused through element-wise addition to enhance feature representation. This block also has a shortcut option. The benefits of adding a shortcut connection include improved gradient flow and mitigated gradient vanishing problems, eventually enabling deeper network training.

Figure 9 illustrates the architecture of a RepConvN block. This block has two parallel Conv blocks, and their results are added using element-wise summation, and a SiLU activation is applied for non-linearity. The bottleneck layer is to reduce the dimensionality of the input feature map, which is required to lower the computational cost. The normalization layer is used to provide a smooth training with good performance. The Conv block (Fig. 10) is a basic block which is a 2D convolution followed by batch normalization and a SiLU activation to promote feature learning and training stability.

There are several benefits of increasing the number of layers in convolutional neural networks such as feature hierarchy and increased accuracy. With additional layers, CNNs can build more abstract and high-level representations of the input data making them crucial for understanding complex images. Deeper networks can generalize better to new, unseen data by learning more robust and diverse features. However, increasing the number of layers might lead to problems such as overfitting, vanishing or exploding gradients, increased computational cost, and complexity in architecture design51. Decreasing the number of parameters has advantages such as reduced over-fitting, lower computational cost, faster training, and simpler models. The drawbacks of reducing parameters are limited capacity, under-fitting, loss of detail, and reduced flexibility. RepNCSP-ELAN backbone advantages for sugarcane stem health detection

We summarize the four reasons why our RepNCSP-ELAN backbone is suitable for sugarcane stem health detection. (1) The ELAN-style fine-grained feature preservation strategy is conducive to our model’s ability to capture fine-grained and subtle features. (2) The fewer parameters and lower computational complexity also make our model lightweight, which can be further deployed on some field devices with less computing resources to realize real-time intelligent agricultural detection. (3) Our model also shows good cross-domain generalizability and robustness on brain-tumor dataset, which indicates the lower risk of overfitting. (4) The gradient flow is more stable and redundancy is less generated due to the CSP-based split-and-merge strategy. Overall, these characteristics make our RepNCSP-ELAN an efficient and robust backbone for fine-grained agricultural detection.

Significance of nano models

The study is based on nano models of YOLO family models. The applications for PF (precision farming) and modern agriculture depend heavily on the size and the detection speed of the model. This is because small and edge devices have very limited memory and processing power. In short, models with low inference time, less memory footprint, and a high number of frames per second (FPS) are needed for better real-time deployment. The YOLO algorithm can be used to provide real-time or near-real-time detection capability needed for real-time decision making. Deep learning models with fewer parameters have many advantages over models with larger parameters52. They are computationally more efficient53, and require less time and resources for training and inference, which makes them more suitable for deployment in resource-constrained environments. In addition, smaller models can also be more easily deployed on devices with limited storage and processing capabilities. Furthermore, these models are also more interpretable, as they have a simpler architecture and are less likely to overfit the data. Models with fewer parameters can still achieve similar performance compared to the larger models with a greater number of parameters, which makes these more budget-friendly. The merits of smaller deep learning models with fewer parameters are their efficiency, interpretability, and ability to achieve performance comparable to models with more parameters.

Brain tumor dataset

The brain tumor dataset is a standard benchmark dataset of medical images from CT and MRI scans, publicly released by Ultralytics (https://docs.ultralytics.com/datasets/detect/brain-tumor/). It consists of a diverse range of images depicting various types of brain tumors, imaging conditions, and anatomical variations, making it a challenging dataset for detection and classification tasks. Each image in the dataset is annotated for binary classification into two classes, positive (tumor present) and negative (tumor absent).

This dataset is employed in the study for two specific reasons that align with the research objectives. First, due to its origin in the medical imaging domain, it offers a set of images that are visually and structurally different from those in agricultural contexts, providing a stringent test for cross-domain robustness. Second, the binary classification nature of the dataset is similar to the class structure of the sugarcane stem health dataset, which is also divided into positive (cercospora infection) and negative (healthy stem) groups. This similarity in class structure, along with visual dissimilarity, makes it an ideal dataset to test the adaptability of the proposed model to a new domain without a loss in accuracy.

The integration of this dataset into the evaluation pipeline of the study ensures that the model is not only tested within the domain of agricultural images but is also exposed to a standard benchmark for generalization capabilities. This step is crucial for lightweight object detection systems, which are often intended for real-world applications where image sources and problem domains can vary. Furthermore, by using a publicly available benchmark, we can ensure reproducibility and facilitate meaningful comparisons with future studies.

Experimental setup

All experiments are implemented by the Ultralytics library in the development mode on Google Colab (free version) with NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU. Development mode is used to allow direct editing of the backbone to add or remove modules for a fair comparison. The proposed model was tested against nano versions of the mainstream YOLO families (YOLOv3, YOLOv5, YOLOv6, YOLOv7, YOLOv8, YOLOv9, and YOLOv10), and each model is implemented to be consistent.

All models were trained from scratch (no pre-trained weights) to avoid external influence and keep a fair comparison of pure architectural improvements. The sugarcane stem health dataset is divided into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing. All models are trained for 100 epochs without early stopping, to keep all experiments uniform and provide an identical baseline for comparison. The final model checkpoints are selected based on the best validation mAP.

Significance and limitations

These results validate that our proposed RepNCSP-ELAN backbone strikes a favourable balance in terms of accuracy, efficiency, and deployment feasibility compared to other YOLO nano variants. Despite its lightweight architecture (553 K parameters, 7.2 GFLOPs, 4.6 MB PyTorch model size), it attains competitive accuracy (mAP@50 = 91.8%, mAP@95 = 64.1%) and, more importantly, outperforms the baselines in inference speed on both high-end GPUs and edge devices. Notably, the cross-domain validation using the brain-tumor dataset further underscores the model’s robustness and versatility, extending its applicability beyond agricultural imagery. The ablation study highlights the indispensable role of the RepNCSP-ELAN block in eliminating redundancy while maintaining accuracy.

However, several limitations and future work avenues are to be noted. First, while the model demonstrates strong performance on sugarcane stems and brain-tumors, its generalization to other agricultural or medical datasets under different imaging conditions needs further validation. Second, although we achieved real-time performance on Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi 4 B, it is imperative for future research to conduct ablation experiments directly on these edge devices to more closely correlate backbone modifications with hardware-specific outcomes. Lastly, similar to other deep learning models, our approach remains susceptible to extreme noise, occlusion, and highly imbalanced datasets, possibly necessitating additional strategies such as domain adaptation, active learning, or more sophisticated augmentation techniques.

Conclusion

The imperative for this study arises from the pressing demand for an automated and real-time solution that can detect healthy sugarcane stems to facilitate accurate bud localization and extraction in sugarcane. Our proposition is a lightweight nano-scale YOLOv8n with a custom backbone that replaces the standard C2f building blocks with RepNCSP-ELAN blocks, based on GELANs as introduced in YOLOv9. This custom architecture is meticulously optimized to achieve an enhanced accuracy–efficiency trade-off, marked by significantly reduced redundancy and computational overhead. Experiments conducted to validate our approach have yielded promising results. We applied our optimized model on the dataset of sugarcane stem health detection, where our nano network achieved an mAP@50 of 90.1% with an inference speed of 14.33 ms (69.79 FPS) on a single Tesla T4 GPU, surpassing the performance of other YOLO nano variants. Crucially, the model also demonstrated real-time inference on the edge with 46–48 FPS on Jetson Nano and 3.1–3.6 FPS on Raspberry Pi 4 B. This level of performance affirms its applicability in resource-constrained, on-site agricultural settings. Moreover, to further validate its generality across domains, the model was tested on the benchmark brain-tumor dataset, reinforcing the robustness and adaptability of our approach. Additionally, our ablation study has shown that the progressive increase of RepNCSP-ELAN blocks reduces the parameter (553 K) and GFLOPs (7.2) while sustaining or even increasing the accuracy in detection, in comparison with C2f-based backbones. Collectively, these results not only demonstrate that the RepNCSP-ELAN backbone successfully optimizes the YOLOv8n for sugarcane health monitoring but also pave the way for a novel lightweight backbone in general for real-time AI in agriculture.

Nonetheless, certain challenges still persist, including the complexity of natural environments, variations in lighting and occlusion, as well as the inherent limitations of compact model architectures. Although the demonstration of the deployment on Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi suggests the practical feasibility of the approach, additional ablation analyses on different edge devices could better associate backbone modifications with hardware-dependent performance. Therefore, future work can explore this direction by expanding the application of the RepNCSP-ELAN backbone to other YOLO variants (YOLOv9, YOLOv10, and the recently released YOLOv11 and YOLOv12), applying this backbone to other relevant agricultural tasks, such as fruit maturity assessment and disease detection in more complex and cluttered crop environments, and integrating advanced data augmentation or domain adaptation strategies to enhance the model’s resilience in challenging and variable field conditions.

In conclusion, the proposed RepNCSP-ELAN backbone is a compact, accurate, and efficient solution that bridges the gap between high-performance object detection and the real-world needs of agricultural deployment, thus further advancing practical and generalizable AI systems for agriculture.
