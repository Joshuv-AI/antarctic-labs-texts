Krylov Complexity Under Hamiltonian Deformations and Toda Flows
Kazutaka Takahashi, Pratik Nandy, Adolfo del Campo

Abstract
The quantum dynamics of a complex system can be efficiently described in Krylov space, the minimal subspace in which the dynamics unfolds. We apply the Krylov subspace method for Hamiltonian deformations, which provides a systematic way of constructing solvable models from known instances. In doing so, we relate the evolution of deformed and undeformed theories and investigate their complexity. For a certain class of deformations, the resulting Krylov subspace is unchanged, and we observe time evolutions with a reorganized basis. The tridiagonal form of the generator in the Krylov space is maintained, and we obtain generalized Toda equations as a function of the deformation parameters. The imaginary-time-like evolutions can be described by real-time unitary ones. As possible applications, we discuss coherent Gibbs states for thermodynamic systems, for which we analyze the survival probability, spread complexity, Krylov entropy, and associated time-averaged quantities. We further discuss the statistical properties of random matrices and supersymmetric systems for quadratic deformations.



I
Introduction

Nonperturbative methods are crucial for understanding physical phenomena that fall beyond the scope of perturbative expansions, which typically rely on small coupling constants or weak-field limits. Such methods become crucial in regimes where perturbation theory breaks down, e.g., due to divergences or strong coupling
Mariño 2015
. In two dimensions, exactly solvable deformations of integrable field theories have shed light on quantum field theory and holography
Zamolodchikov 2004
;
Cavaglià et al. 2016
;
Smirnov and Zamolodchikov 2017
;
Jiang 2021
. In this context, Hamiltonian deformations, mapping the Hamiltonian to a function of the Hamiltonian, have also been discussed
Gross et al. 2020a
;
Gross et al. 2020b
;
Kruthoff and Parrikar 2020
;
Rosso 2021
;
Jiang 2022
;
Ebert et al. 2022
.
Such deformations can be applied to quantum mechanics both in a Hermitian
Gross et al. 2020a
;
Gross et al. 2020b
and non-Hermitian Hamiltonian setting
Matsoukas-Roubeas et al. 2023a
.

One may wonder whether theories related by such deformations exhibit different complexity and generate fundamentally different kinds of nonequilibrium dynamics. To address this question, one can use quantum complexity measures that quantify the growth of an operator or quantum state complexity under unitary evolution. Among them, Krylov complexity provides an insightful characterization of nonequilibrium dynamics by tracking the spread of an initial operator or quantum state using an orthonormal basis constructed via the Lanczos algorithm
Viswanath and Müller 1994
;
Parker et al. 2019
. This basis, known as the Krylov basis, spans the minimal subspace in which the dynamics unfolds. The use of Krylov subspace methods for quantum dynamics is thus of great interest both to the foundations of physics and applications in quantum science and technology
Nandy et al. 2025a
;
Baiguera et al. 2025
;
Rabinovici et al. 2025
, ranging from quantum control to quantum optimization algorithms
Takahashi and del Campo 2024
;
Takahashi and del Campo 2025
;
Bhattacharjee 2023
;
Balducci et al. 2024
;
Visuri et al. 2025
;
Grabarits and del Campo 2025a
;
Grabarits and del Campo 2025b
.

In this study, we investigate how the Krylov complexity evolves under Hamiltonian deformations. For a given Hamiltonian and initial state, the associated Krylov subspace is uniquely defined, and the dynamics within this subspace reduces to a one-dimensional nearest-neighbor hopping model. Since the deformation preserves the Krylov subspace, the resulting effective tridiagonal Hamiltonian depends parametrically on the deformation. We thus observe a continuous flow of the tridiagonal Hamiltonian as the deformation is implemented. This framework is reminiscent of the method of flow equations, where a Hamiltonian evolves, typically in a double-bracket form, toward a diagonal form while preserving its spectrum
Brockett 1991
;
Głazek and Wilson 1993
;
Glazek and Wilson 1994
;
Wegner 1994
;
Kehrein 2006
.

Among the various choices of flow generators, the Toda chain flow is particularly relevant to our setting, as it preserves the tridiagonal structure of the Hamiltonian throughout the evolution, and has been shown to saturate the operator growth quantum speed limit
Hörnedal et al. 2023
.
The Toda chain is known as one of the earliest examples of a nonlinear integrable system
Toda 1967a
;
Toda 1967b
.
The relevance of the Toda flow in the context of quantum dynamics was also discussed
Okuyama and Takahashi 2016
.
Mathematically, the relevance of the Toda lattice in the Krylov method, or equivalently
in the orthogonal polynomials, was discussed in Ref.
Ismail 2005
.
Its application to the imaginary-time correlation function in quantum systems
was discussed in Refs.
Dymarsky and Gorsky 2020
;
Kundu et al. 2023
;
Angelinos et al. 2025
.

The formulation naturally suggests the Krylov subspace method,

.
Then, we find in Sec.

II
Hamiltonian deformations

For a given Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{\mu=1}^{D}E_{\mu}|E_{\mu}\rangle\langle E_{\mu}|

and an initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
defined in
a
D
-dimensional Hilbert space, we consider the unitary time evolution

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iHt}|\psi_{0}\rangle
.
A quantum state of broad interest in quantum chaos, blackhole physics
and the study of thermal averages is
the coherent Gibbs state
del Campo et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2021
;
Balasubramanian et al. 2022

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}(\beta)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(\beta)}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{D}e^{-\beta E_{\mu}/2}|E_{\mu}\rangle,

(1)

where
Z(\beta)
represents the partition function, the “one-copy” version
of the thermofield double state
Maldacena 2003
;
Papadodimas and Raju 2015
;
Chapman et al. 2019
.
This state can be associated with the choice of an initial quantum state, the coherent superposition of all energy eigenstates with equal weight

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{\mu}|E_{\mu}\rangle/\sqrt{D}
, subject to the transformation

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle\to\frac{e^{-\beta H/2}|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-\beta H}|\psi_{0}\rangle}}=|\psi_{0}(\beta)\rangle.

(2)

In the study of Hamiltonian deformations, we consider generalizing
this transformation as

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle\to|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle=\frac{e^{-f(H,\tau)/2}|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-f(H,\tau)}|\psi_{0}\rangle}}.

(3)

Here,
f(\cdot,\tau)
is a real function that involves a set of real parameters

\tau=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots)
.
Although the following discussions can essentially be applied without specifying
the explicit form of
f(H,\tau)
, we are interested in a particular choice

\displaystyle f(H,\tau)=\tau_{1}H+\tau_{2}H^{2},

(4)

which includes, e.g., the
T\overline{T}
-deformation
Gross et al. 2020b

at first order, as well as its generalizations to the non-Hermitian case,
which arise naturally in the context of energy dephasing
in the absence of quantum jumps
Matsoukas-Roubeas et al. 2023a
;
Matsoukas-Roubeas et al. 2023b
.

The generator of the real-time evolution is kept unchanged and
the time-evolved state is given by

\displaystyle|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle=e^{-iHt}|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle.

(5)

A natural measure to compare the time evolution between the deformed and
underformed theories is the survival (or Loschmidt echo) amplitude

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}(\tau)|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle=\frac{\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-iHt}e^{-f(H,\tau)}|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-f(H,\tau)}|\psi_{0}\rangle}.

(6)

The coherent Gibbs state is obtained when
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=(\beta,0)
,
for which the survival amplitude reduces to the complex partition function,

Z(\beta+it)/Z(\beta)
.
Note that its absolute square value is then the spectral form factor, used in the characterization of quantum chaos and the spectral characterization of complex many-body
systems
Haake 2001
;
Cotler et al. 2017
;
del Campo et al. 2017
;
Cornelius et al. 2022
;
Matsoukas-Roubeas et al. 2024
;
Martinez-Azcona et al. 2025
.

The survival amplitude can be written as the Fourier transform of
the local density of states,

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}(\tau)|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle=\int dE\,e^{-iEt}\rho(E,\tau),

(7)

defined as

\displaystyle\rho(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}(\tau)|\delta(E-H)|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-f(E,\tau)}\rho(E,0)}{\int dE\,e^{-f(E,\tau)}\rho(E,0)}.

Thus, in this setting, all other quantities are fully determined by
the local density of states.
Furthermore, the structure of the local density of states implies that
only the case of a real-valued function
f
is physically relevant,
since any imaginary contribution vanishes when evaluating the overlap
in Eq. (
6
).

The aim of the present work is to understand the relation between
the original time-evolved state
|\psi(t)\rangle
and
the deformed state
|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle
.
Since both states belong to the space spanned by

{|\psi_{0}\rangle,H|\psi_{0}\rangle,H^{2}|\psi_{0}\rangle,\dots}
,
the Krylov subspace method provides a natural and effective strategy.

III
Krylov subspace method

To describe the unitary time evolution in Hilbert space,
it is convenient to identify the minimal subspace in which the state evolves.
In the Krylov subspace method, the state space is spanned by the Krylov basis
Viswanath and Müller 1994
.
Starting with the initial basis
|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle=|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle
, one constructs an orthonormal basis set through the recurrence relation:

\displaystyle|K_{n+1}(\tau)\rangle b_{n+1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle-|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle a_{n}(\tau)

(9)

\displaystyle-|K_{n-1}(\tau)\rangle b_{n}(\tau).

The so-called Lanczos coefficients are determined by imposing the orthonormality relation

\displaystyle\langle K_{m}(\tau)|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle=\delta_{m,n},

(10)

and can be identified as

\displaystyle a_{n}(\tau)=\langle K_{n}(\tau)|H|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle,

(11)

\displaystyle b_{n}(\tau)=\langle K_{n-1}(\tau)|H|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle,

(12)

where
a_{n}(\tau)
and
b_{n}(\tau)
are real and, in addition,
b_{n}(\tau)
is nonnegative.
The recurrence relation is applied until the condition

H|K_{d-1}(\tau)\rangle-|K_{d-1}(\tau)\rangle a_{d-1}(\tau)-|K_{d-2}(\tau)\rangle b_{d-1}(\tau)=0

is satisfied.
The Krylov dimension
d
, representing the total number of basis elements,
is less than or equal to the dimension of the Hilbert space
D
.
In our setting, the initial state at
\tau=0
is written as

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{\mu=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}|E_{\mu}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}\sqrt{\frac{d_{n}}{D}}|\epsilon_{n}\rangle,

(13)

where

\displaystyle|\epsilon_{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{n}}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{D}|E_{\mu}\rangle\delta_{E_{\mu},\epsilon_{n}},

(14)

and the degeneracy
d_{n}
represents the number of the eigenstates
with
E_{\mu}=\epsilon_{n}
.
Since the state space of the equienergy
E_{\mu}=\epsilon_{n}
is
spanned by the single basis
|\epsilon_{n}\rangle
,
the Krylov dimension is given by the number of distinct eigenvalues
of the original Hamiltonian.

In the present formulation, since the initial state depends on
the parameter
\tau
, all other quantities naturally inherit
a dependence on
\tau
.
The main objective of this work is to characterize the
\tau
-evolution.
As mentioned at the end of the previous section,
the Krylov space spanned by
\{|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle\}_{n=0}^{d-1}

must remain independent of
\tau
.
Consequently, the basis vectors
|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle
vary continuously with
\tau
,
and the Krylov dimension
d
remains constant, independent of
\tau
.

An essential property of the Krylov subspace method is that the time evolution can be effectively described as a one-dimensional motion within the Krylov space.
Expanding the state as

\displaystyle|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}\varphi_{n}(t,\tau)|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle,

(15)

the wave function components
\varphi_{n}(t,\tau)
are given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t,\tau)=\langle n|e^{-iL(\tau)t}|0\rangle.

(16)

Here, we define the fixed orthonormal basis

\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{d-1}
to represent the Krylov subspace.
The time-evolution generator
L(\tau)
, expressed in this basis, takes a tridiagonal form:

\displaystyle L(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}a_{n}(\tau)|n\rangle\langle n|

(17)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}b_{n}(\tau)(|n-1\rangle\langle n|+|n\rangle\langle n-1|).

In the Krylov space representation, the state is initially set to
|0\rangle

and propagates along the finite or semi-infinite chain by the generator
L(\tau)
.

Generally, the Krylov basis
|K_{n}\rangle
and the Lanczos coefficients
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
can be identified by specifying the Hamiltonian and the initial state.
In other words, the local density of states
\rho(E,\tau)
defined
in Eq. (
8
) uniquely determines the Krylov expansion.
To see this, we represent the Krylov basis as

\displaystyle|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle=P_{n}(H,\tau)|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle.

(18)

P_{n}(\cdot,\tau)
is a real polynomial of
n
th degree.
The orthonormality of the Krylov basis in Eq. (
10
) is written as

\displaystyle\int dx\,\rho(x,\tau)P_{m}(x,\tau)P_{n}(x,\tau)=\delta_{m,n}.

(19)

This relation shows that the Krylov basis is closely related to orthogonal polynomials
Viswanath and Müller 1994
;
Parker et al. 2019
;
Mück and Yang 2022
.
The recurrence relation is written as

\displaystyle b_{n+1}(\tau)P_{n}(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle xP_{n}(x,\tau)-a_{n}(\tau)P_{n}(x,\tau)

(20)

\displaystyle-b_{n}(\tau)P_{n-1}(x,\tau).

We can also represent the local density of states as

\displaystyle\rho(x,\tau)=\langle 0|\delta(x-L(\tau))|0\rangle.

(21)

Using the transformation operator

\displaystyle F(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle\langle n|,

(22)

connecting vectors in the original Hilbert space to those in the Krylov space,
we can write
|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle=F(\tau)|0\rangle
and
F^{\dagger}(\tau)HF(\tau)=L(\tau)
,
which give Eq. (
21
)

As mentioned above, since the Krylov expansion depends not only on the Hamiltonian but also
on the choice of the initial state,
L(\tau)
depends on
\tau
.
The comparison between the original definition of
the local density of states in Eq. (
8
)
and the Krylov space representation in Eq. (
21
) implies that
any eigenvalue of
L(\tau)
is equal to one of the eigenvalues of
H
.
Equation (
13
) shows that the eigenvalues of
L(\tau)

are given by
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n=0}^{d-1}
.
We note
\epsilon_{m}\neq\epsilon_{n}
for
m\neq n
by construction.
Using a unitary matrix
V(\tau)
, we can write

L(\tau)=V(\tau)L(0)V^{\dagger}(\tau)
and the equation of motion

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(\tau)}{\partial\tau_{\mu}}=[M_{\mu}(\tau),L(\tau)],

(23)

where
M_{\mu}(\tau)
is a real antisymmetric matrix.
Since
iM_{\mu}(\tau)
is Hermitian, this describes a unitary time evolution.
We show this property in the next section.
It is interesting to note that we obtain a unitary time evolution starting
from the imaginary-time-like evolution in Eq. (
3
).

When we apply the equivalence of Eqs. (
8
) and (
21
)
to the coherent Gibbs state
|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle
with
\tau=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=(\beta,0)
,
the thermodynamic properties are reflected in the Lanczos coefficients.
Especially,
a_{0}(\tau)
represents the thermodynamic energy and

b_{1}(\tau)
represents the energy variance.
When the system has a thermal phase transition, the energy variance is divergent
and
b_{1}(\tau)
displays a singular behavior.
Since the effective Hamiltonian
L(\tau)
contains information about the state, in addition to that of the Hamiltonian, this behavior is therefore natural.

IV
Toda chain flows

In the following, we consider Eq. (
4
) for the deformed function
f(x,\tau)
.
To identify how the Krylov basis and the Lanczos coefficients
are dependent on
\tau
, we consider the derivative of the orthonormal relation in
Eq. (
19
)
Ismail 2005
.
After some calculations described in Appendix
A
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}a_{n}=-(b_{n+1}^{2}-b_{n}^{2}),

(24)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}b_{n}=-\frac{1}{2}b_{n}(a_{n}-a_{n-1}),

(25)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}a_{n}=-\left[b_{n+1}^{2}(a_{n+1}+a_{n})-b_{n}^{2}(a_{n}+a_{n-1})\right],

(26)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}b_{n}=-\frac{1}{2}b_{n}\left(b_{n+1}^{2}-b_{n-1}^{2}+a_{n}^{2}-a_{n-1}^{2}\right).

(27)

The first set of differential equations represents the Toda equations
Toda 1967a
;
Toda 1967b

and the second is a generalization
Moser 1975a
.
Correspondingly, Eq. (
23
) is known as the Lax form
Lax 1968
, which represents a key signature of integrability
Faddeev and Takhtajan 2007
.
The explicit forms of
M_{1}(\tau)
and
M_{2}(\tau)
in the present case are given by

\displaystyle M_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}b_{n}(\tau)(-|n-1\rangle\langle n|+|n\rangle\langle n-1|),

(28)

\displaystyle M_{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{d-1}(a_{n}(\tau)+a_{n-1}(\tau))b_{n}(\tau)

(29)

\displaystyle\times(-|n-1\rangle\langle n|+|n\rangle\langle n-1|)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{d-2}b_{n}(\tau)b_{n+1}(\tau)

\displaystyle\times(-|n-1\rangle\langle n+1|+|n+1\rangle\langle n-1|).

M_{1}(\tau)
has a tridiagonal form while
M_{2}(\tau)
has a pentadiagonal form.
Since the latter is obtained from the quadratic term of Eq. (
4
), it is not difficult to imagine generalizations to arbitrary-order polynomials.
There exist corresponding Toda equations
Moser 1975a
;
Kac and van Moerbeke 1975
;
Moser 1975b
and the resulting generator
M_{\mu}
takes a band matrix form.

The Hermitian matrices
iM_{1}(\tau)
and
iM_{2}(\tau)
represent
the generators for the unitary evolution
V(\tau)

satisfying
\partial_{\tau_{\mu}}V(\tau)=M_{\mu}(\tau)V(\tau)
.
We show in Appendix
A
that

\displaystyle F(\tau)=F(0)V^{\dagger}(\tau).

(30)

Since two generators are described by the same unitary matrix
V(\tau)
, they satisfy the zero-curvature condition

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}M_{2}(\tau)-\partial_{\tau_{2}}M_{1}(\tau)-[M_{1}(\tau),M_{2}(\tau)]=0.

(31)

This set of Hamiltonians is an example of
integrable time-dependent Hamiltonians
Sinitsyn et al. 2018
.
Arbitrary evolutions represented by the path-ordering operator as

\displaystyle|\phi(\tau^{f};\tau^{i})\rangle=\mathrm{P}_{\tau}\exp\left[\sum_{\mu}\int_{\tau^{i}}^{\tau^{f}}d\tau_{\mu}M_{\mu}(\tau)\right]|\phi\rangle,

(32)

are determined by specifying the two points and are independent of
the choice of the path in the parameter space
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
,
provided
M_{\mu}(\tau)
is a smooth function.

In this formulation, the
\tau
-evolution can be described
universally by the Toda equations.
System-specific properties such as the Hamiltonian and the initial state are
incorporated into the initial condition for the
\tau
-evolution.
This universal dynamics comes from the property that

L(\tau)
keeps its tridiagonal form for any
\tau
.

Consider the time-evolved state
|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle
,
given by Eq. (
15
) along with Eq. (
16
).
Since
|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle=|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle
,
the survival amplitude is simply written as

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}(\tau)|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle=\langle 0|e^{-iL(\tau)t}|0\rangle.

(33)

Equation (
15
) is written in the basis set
\{|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle\}_{n=0}^{d-1}
.
As mentioned in the previous section, the subspace is independent of
\tau

and it is possible to write the state with respect to
the basis before the deformation
\{|K_{n}\rangle=|K_{n}(0)\rangle\}_{n=0}^{d-1}
.
Using Eq. (
30
), we can write

\displaystyle|\psi(t,\tau)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}|K_{n}\rangle\langle n|e^{-iL(0)t}V^{\dagger}(\tau)|0\rangle.

(34)

Of particular interest is to consider
t=0
, when

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}|K_{n}\rangle\langle n|V^{\dagger}(\tau)|0\rangle.

(35)

This state is originally prepared as an initial state.
Now, the imaginary-time-like evolution is described as a unitary time evolution
in Krylov space.
Note that the anti-time ordering is used in the evolution described by
V^{\dagger}(\tau)
.

V
Toda equations and spread complexity

V.1
Exact solutions from the first Toda equations

To understand the general structure of Hamiltonian deformations in Krylov space,
it is instructive to analyze explicit solutions of the Toda equations.
In the context of the Krylov algorithm, analytical solutions
are known in systems with dynamical symmetries
Caputa et al. 2022
;
Hörnedal et al. 2022
.
Here, we show that the Hamiltonian deformations with
\tau=(\tau_{1},0)

preserve the symmetries, admitting analytical solutions.

We consider the first set of the Toda equations in Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
).
As a finite-dimensional system, we can consider SU(2) symmetry.
We choose as an ansatz

\displaystyle a_{n}(\tau_{1})=2\gamma(\tau_{1})\left(n-\frac{d-1}{2}\right)+\delta,

(36)

\displaystyle b_{n}(\tau_{1})=\alpha(\tau_{1})\sqrt{n(d-n)},

(37)

where the Krylov dimension
d
takes a finite integer value.
The corresponding
L
-matrix is obtained when we consider a single-spin Hamiltonian.
As the sum
\alpha^{2}(\tau_{1})+\gamma^{2}(\tau_{1})
takes a constant value given the Toda equations, we choose the parameterization

\gamma(\tau_{1})=\gamma_{0}\cos\theta(\tau_{1})
and
\alpha(\tau_{1})=\gamma_{0}\sin\theta(\tau_{1})
.
Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\theta(\tau_{1})=-\gamma_{0}\sin\theta(\tau_{1}).

(38)

The general solution is given by

\displaystyle\cos\theta(\tau_{1})=\frac{\cos\theta(0)+\tanh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}{1+\cos\theta(0)\tanh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}.

(39)

This function increases monotonically
from
\cos\theta(0)
to 1 as
\tau_{1}
increases from 0 to
\infty
.
As a result, we find the Toda flow,
a diagonalizing evolution of the
L
-matrix
Moser 1975a
.
Since the
\tau
-evolution keeps the structure of the Lanczos coefficients,
we can find the dynamical properties at arbitrary
\tau_{1}
, once we know those at
\tau_{1}=0
.
For example, consider the spread complexity, which is generally defined as
Parker et al. 2019
;
Balasubramanian et al. 2022

\displaystyle K(t,\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}n|\varphi_{n}(t,\tau)|^{2}.

(40)

For systems with SU(2) symmetry, it takes the form

\displaystyle K(t,\tau_{1})=(d-1)\left(\frac{\sin\theta(0)\sin\gamma_{0}t}{\cosh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}+\cos\theta(0)\sinh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}\right)^{2}.

The second example is the system with Heisenberg-Weyl symmetry.
The Krylov dimension is infinite, and we put

\displaystyle a_{n}(\tau_{1})=2\gamma_{0}n+\delta(\tau_{1}),

(42)

\displaystyle b_{n}(\tau_{1})=\alpha(\tau_{1})\sqrt{n}.

(43)

The corresponding
L
-matrix represents the one-dimensional particle
in a harmonic oscillator potential with translational motion, for example.
The Toda equations give the solution

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau_{1})=\alpha(0)e^{-\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}},

(44)

\displaystyle\delta(\tau_{1})=\delta(0)-\frac{\alpha^{2}(0)}{2\gamma_{0}}(1-e^{-2\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}),

(45)

which also gives a diagonalization flow.
The spread complexity is given by

\displaystyle K(t,\tau_{1})=\left(\frac{\alpha(0)}{\gamma_{0}}e^{-\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}\sin\gamma_{0}t\right)^{2}.

(46)

The third example is the system with SL(2,R) symmetry given by

\displaystyle a_{n}(\tau_{1})=2\gamma(\tau_{1})(n+h)+\delta,

(47)

\displaystyle b_{n}(\tau_{1})=\alpha(\tau_{1})\sqrt{n(n+2h-1)}.

(48)

The Toda equations give
\alpha^{2}(\tau_{1})-\gamma^{2}(\tau_{1})=\mathrm{const.}
,
and we can formally study stable, unstable, and critical solutions.
The stable solution is obtained by setting

\gamma(\tau_{1})=\gamma_{0}\cosh\theta(\tau_{1})
and

\alpha(\tau_{1})=\gamma_{0}\sinh\theta(\tau_{1})
.
The differential equation for
\theta(\tau_{1})

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\theta(\tau_{1})=-\gamma_{0}\sinh\theta(\tau_{1}),

(49)

is solved as

\displaystyle\cosh\theta(\tau_{1})=\frac{\cosh\theta(0)+\tanh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}{1+\cosh\theta(0)\tanh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}.

(50)

The spread complexity is given by

\displaystyle K(t,\tau_{1})=2h\left(\frac{\sinh\theta(0)\sin\gamma_{0}t}{\cosh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}+\cosh\theta(0)\sinh\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}\right)^{2}.

(51)

We observe again a diagonalizing flow of the
L
-matrix
and an exponential decay of the spread complexity.

The unstable solution is obtained when
\gamma(\tau_{1})<\alpha(\tau_{1})
.
We put
\gamma(\tau_{1})=\gamma_{0}\sinh\theta(\tau_{1})
and

\alpha(\tau_{1})=\gamma_{0}\cosh\theta(\tau_{1})
to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\theta(\tau_{1})=-\gamma_{0}\cosh\theta(\tau_{1}),

(52)

and

\displaystyle\sinh\theta(\tau_{1})=\frac{\sinh\theta(0)-\tan\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}{1+\sinh\theta(0)\tan\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}.

(53)

The spread complexity grows without bound as a function of time

\displaystyle K(t,\tau_{1})=2h\left(\frac{\cosh\theta(0)\sinh\gamma_{0}t}{\cos\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}+\sinh\theta(0)\sin\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}\right)^{2}.

(54)

This solution of the Toda equations was obtained in Ref.

and was discussed as an onset of chaos.
It is interesting to see that the spread complexity in Eq. (

is a nonmonotonic function of
\tau_{1}
when
\theta(0)\neq 0
.
It is a decreasing function for small

as in the other examples,
and turns into an increasing one as
\tau_{1}
becomes larger.
This is because

is decreasing in

for large
\tau_{1}
,
This scenario is reminiscent of systems with an unstable fixed point,
as the inverted harmonic oscillator.

We can also study the critical point
\gamma(\tau_{1})=\alpha(\tau_{1})

as the boundary between stable and unstable solutions.
The equation of
\gamma(\tau_{1})
,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\gamma(\tau_{1})=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}(\tau_{1}),

(55)

is easily solved as

\displaystyle\gamma(\tau_{1})=\frac{2\gamma_{0}}{2+\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}.

(56)

The spread complexity is given by

\displaystyle K(t,\tau_{1})=2h\left(\frac{\gamma_{0}t}{2+\gamma_{0}\tau_{1}}\right)^{2}.

(57)

V.2
Exact solutions from the second Toda equations

We next discuss the second Toda equations (
26
) and (
27
).
In contrast to the first equations, we can put
a_{n}(\tau_{2})=0
to find a simple but nontrivial solution.
The equation for
b_{n}
reads

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}b_{n}^{2}(\tau_{2})=-b_{n}^{2}(\tau_{2})[b_{n+1}^{2}(\tau_{2})-b_{n-1}^{2}(\tau_{2})].

(58)

This equation denotes that the time evolutions of the odd components are
determined from the even components, and vice versa.
We also see that this equation is solvable when

b_{n+1}^{2}(\tau_{2})-b_{n-1}^{2}(\tau_{2})
is independent of
n
.
We use the ansatz

\displaystyle b_{2n+1}^{2}(\tau_{2})=(2n+1)\gamma^{2}(\tau_{2}),

(59)

\displaystyle b_{2n}^{2}(\tau_{2})=2n\alpha^{2}(\tau_{2}).

(60)

Given that
\alpha^{2}(\tau_{2})-\gamma^{2}(\tau_{2})
remains constant as a function of
\tau_{2}
,
we choose the parameterizations

(\alpha,\gamma)=(\gamma_{0}\cosh\theta(\tau_{2}),\gamma_{0}\sinh\theta(\tau_{2}))
and

(\alpha,\gamma)=(\gamma_{0}\sinh\theta(\tau_{2}),\gamma_{0}\cosh\theta(\tau_{2}))
.
Both forms yield the same equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}\theta(\tau_{2})=-\gamma_{0}^{2}\sinh\theta(\tau_{2})\cosh\theta(\tau_{2}),

(61)

and we obtain

\displaystyle\cosh 2\theta(\tau_{2})=\frac{\cosh 2\theta(0)+\tanh\gamma_{0}^{2}\tau_{2}}{1+\cosh 2\theta(0)\tanh\gamma_{0}^{2}\tau_{2}}.

(62)

This solution shows that the
L
-matrix is block-diagonalized into many
2\times 2
matrices.

We can also consider the marginal solution with

\alpha(\tau_{2})=\gamma(\tau_{2})
.
Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}(\tau_{2})=\frac{\alpha^{2}(0)}{1+2\alpha^{2}(0)\tau_{2}}.

(63)

In this case, the
L
-matrix takes a simple, known form
with
b_{n}(\tau_{2})=\alpha(\tau_{2})\sqrt{n}
and the spread complexity is obtained as

\displaystyle K(t,\tau_{2})=\alpha^{2}(\tau_{2})t^{2}=\frac{\alpha^{2}(0)t^{2}}{1+2\alpha^{2}(0)\tau_{2}}.

(64)

V.3
Fixed-point solutions and saturation of complexity

The introduction of the parameter
\tau
gives
a Toda flow in the Lanczos-coefficients space

(a_{0},a_{1},\dots;b_{1},b_{2},\dots)
.
We are interested in the large-
\tau
regime
where the flow is influenced by the fixed point.

We first consider the first set of the Toda equations,
Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
), with
\tau=(\tau_{1},0)
.
Setting the left hand side of each equation to be zero,
we find
b_{n}=0
.
Since the offdiagonal components go to zero,
the
\tau
-evolution represents a diagonalization flow.
The fixed point is given by

(\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{d-1};0,\dots)

where
(\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{d-1})

represent distinct eigenvalues of the original Hamiltonian
H
.
To find the convergence
b_{n}\to 0
in Eq. (
25
),
we require
a_{n}>a_{n-1}
, which gives the unique order

\epsilon_{0}<\epsilon_{1}<\dots<\epsilon_{d-1}

Moser 1975a
;
Monthus 2016
.
At large
\tau_{1}
, we obtain exponential decay

\displaystyle b_{n}(\tau_{1})\sim\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\epsilon_{n}-\epsilon_{n-1})\tau_{1}\right].

(65)

Next, we consider the second set of the Toda equations,
Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
), with
\tau=(0,\tau_{2})
.
In that case, the fixed-point condition is written as

\displaystyle b_{n}^{2}(a_{n-1}+a_{n})=0\qquad(n=1,2,\dots,d-1),

(66)

\displaystyle b_{n-1}b_{n}=0\qquad(n=2,3,\dots,d-1).

(67)

Although these equations also imply
b_{n}=0
,
Eq. (
27
) indicates that

a_{n}^{2}>a_{n-1}^{2}
is required to find the convergence
b_{n}\to 0
.
If the condition
|\epsilon_{0}|<|\epsilon_{1}|<\dots<|\epsilon_{d-1}|

is satisfied, we can find the fixed point

(\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{d-1};0,\dots)
.
We obtain the exponential decay

\displaystyle b_{n}(\tau_{2})\sim\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\epsilon_{n}^{2}-\epsilon_{n-1}^{2})\tau_{2}\right].

(68)

When
\epsilon_{m}=-\epsilon_{n}
holds for any pair
(m,n)
,
we can find a different fixed point.
For example, when

\epsilon_{0}<\epsilon_{1}<0<\epsilon_{2}<\epsilon_{3}

with
|\epsilon_{1}|=\epsilon_{2}<|\epsilon_{0}|<\epsilon_{3}
,
the fixed point is given by

(a_{0},-a_{0},\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{3};b_{1},0,0)

with
\sqrt{a_{0}^{2}+b_{1}^{2}}=|\epsilon_{1}|
.
The generalization is straightforward
and we obtain many block structures
when there exist many energy levels with
\epsilon_{m}=-\epsilon_{n}
.

As an example, we consider the case where the energy levels are
given in a symmetric form as

(\pm\epsilon_{1},\pm\epsilon_{2},\dots,\pm\epsilon_{d/2})
.
Here, we assume that
d
is even and

0<\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}<\dots<\epsilon_{d/2}
.
In this case, the diagonal components
a_{n}
are shown to be zero and
the Toda equations are simplified to Eq. (
58
).
The equations have the fixed point

(0,\dots,0;\epsilon_{1},0,\epsilon_{2},0,\dots,0,\epsilon_{d/2})
,
and the convergence to this point takes an exponential form
as Eq. (
68
).

We discuss the implication of the Toda flow for
the spread complexity written as

\displaystyle K(t,\tau)=\langle 0|e^{iL(\tau)t}{\cal K}e^{-iL(\tau)t}|0\rangle,

(69)

where
{\cal K}=\mathrm{diag}\,(0,1,2,\dots,d-1)
.
It depends on both
t
and
\tau
, and exhibit a rich behavior.
In chaotic systems,
we are often interested in instances that saturate
the complexity and its growth.
It is convenient to introduce time-averaged quantities.
We define

\displaystyle\overline{K}(\tau)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,K(t,\tau).

(70)

When
K(t,\tau)
is periodic in
t
,
the time duration
T
is set to the period,
while we let
T\to\infty
in the general nonperiodic case.
In Appendix
B
, we show that
the quantity
\overline{K}(\tau)
can be written as

\displaystyle\overline{K}(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}\frac{e^{-f(\epsilon_{n},\tau)+S_{n}}}{Z(\tau)}K_{n}(\tau),

(71)

\displaystyle K_{n}(\tau)=\langle\phi_{n}(\tau)|{\cal K}|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle,

(72)

\displaystyle|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle=F^{\dagger}(\tau)|\epsilon_{n}\rangle,

(73)

where
S_{n}=\ln d_{n}
,
Z(\tau)=\sum_{n}e^{-f(\epsilon_{n},\tau)+S_{n}}
,
and
F(\tau)
is defined in Eq. (
22
).
The transformation operator
F(\tau)
connects the eigenstate

|\epsilon_{n}\rangle
in the original space to that in the Krylov space.
We see that
|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle

is an eigenstate of
L(\tau)
satisfying

\displaystyle L(\tau)|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle=\epsilon_{n}|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle.

(74)

Since
S_{n}
represents the entropy for a given energy
\epsilon_{n}
,
Eq. (
71
) is interpreted as the generalized canonical average
of
K_{n}(\tau)
.
Although
K_{n}(\tau)
itself depends on
\tau
,
it takes values between 0 and
d-1
.

When
\tau
is large enough
and the flow is governed by the diagonal fixed point discussed above,
the off-diagonal components of the
L
matrix take small values and the diagonal ones align in the described order.
In that case, we can estimate

\displaystyle K_{n}(\tau)\sim n.

(75)

The saturation value of the complexity is given by
the generalized canonical average of the excitation number
from the ground state or the zero-energy state.
The scale justifying this relation is obtained from
Eq. (
65
) or Eq. (
68
).
We have
\tau_{1}\gg 1/\min_{n}(\epsilon_{n}-\epsilon_{n-1})

and
\tau_{2}\gg 1/\min_{n}(\epsilon_{n}^{2}-\epsilon_{n-1}^{2})
.
For many-body Hamiltonians with very small energy gaps,
we require very large
\tau
to find Eq. (
75
).

VII
Random matrix quenches

Random matrix theory (RMT) provides an ideal framework for exploring the complexity of deformed Hamiltonians.
In particular, Ref.
Balasubramanian et al. 2022
has extensively analyzed the evolution of complexity in RMT with different Dyson symmetry classes, focusing on evolutions initiated from the standard reference
states to coherent Gibbs states. Building upon these results, this section considers
a broader generalization, where the properties of complexity evolution are re-examined using the deformed Hamiltonian. We especially focus on the long-time behavior of the spread complexity.

VII.1
Two-dimensional example

Building on Refs.
Caputa et al. 2024
;
Nandy et al. 2025b
,
we present a two-dimensional random matrix example,
where explicit computation of Krylov complexity is possible.
The complexity is calculated for a single realization of
2\times 2
matrix
and the average over the ensemble is carried out afterwards.

When the Hamiltonian has eigenvalues

\epsilon_{0}=E-\omega/2
and
\epsilon_{1}=E+\omega/2

with
\omega\geq 0
, the initial state is written as

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle=|0\rangle\sqrt{p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)}+|1\rangle\sqrt{p(\epsilon_{1},\tau)},

(82)

with
p(\epsilon,\tau)\propto e^{-f(\epsilon,\tau)}
and

p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)+p(\epsilon_{1},\tau)=1
.
It is a simple task to apply the Krylov algorithm.
We obtain

\displaystyle a_{0}(\tau)=\epsilon_{0}p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)+\epsilon_{1}p(\epsilon_{1},\tau),

(83)

\displaystyle a_{1}(\tau)=\epsilon_{1}p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)+\epsilon_{0}p(\epsilon_{1},\tau),

(84)

\displaystyle b_{1}(\tau)=\omega\sqrt{p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)p(\epsilon_{1},\tau)}.

(85)

It is also straightforward to solve the Schrödinger
equation in the Krylov space as

\displaystyle|\varphi(t,\tau)\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)e^{-i\epsilon_{0}t}+p(\epsilon_{1},\tau)e^{-i\epsilon_{1}t}\\
\sqrt{p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)p(\epsilon_{1},\tau)}(-e^{-i\epsilon_{0}t}+e^{-i\epsilon_{1}t})\end{array}\right).

Since the dimension of the system is minimal,
the time dependence of the spread complexity is determined irrespective
of the choice of the deform function
f
as

\displaystyle K(t,\tau)=4p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)p(\epsilon_{1},\tau)\sin^{2}\frac{\omega t}{2}.

(90)

This gives the time-averaged complexity

\displaystyle\overline{K}(\tau)=2p(\epsilon_{0},\tau)p(\epsilon_{1},\tau)=\frac{2e^{-(f(\epsilon_{1},\tau)-f(\epsilon_{0},\tau))}}{[1+e^{-(f(\epsilon_{1},\tau)-f(\epsilon_{0},\tau))}]^{2}}.

(91)

In the case of the coherent Gibbs state
\tau=(\beta,0)
,
Eq. (
91
) is dependent only on
\omega=\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{0}
,
and not on
E=(\epsilon_{0}+\epsilon_{1})/2
.
For the Gaussian RMT associated with the Dyson index
\beta_{\mathrm{D}}=1,2,4
,
the probability distribution of the level spacing is given by
Haake 1991

\displaystyle\rho_{\omega}(\omega)=A_{\beta_{\mathrm{D}}}\frac{\omega^{\beta_{\mathrm{D}}}}{\Delta^{\beta_{\mathrm{D}}+1}}\exp\left(-B_{\beta_{\mathrm{D}}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\right),

(92)

where the coefficients
A_{\beta_{\mathrm{D}}}
and
B_{\beta_{\mathrm{D}}}

are determined from the normalization
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\rho_{\omega}(\omega)=1
and
the definition of the mean level spacing
\Delta=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\rho_{\omega}(\omega)\omega
.
The average of Eq. (
91
) is written as

\displaystyle\langle\overline{K}(\beta)\rangle

\displaystyle=

VIII
Supersymmetry for quadratic deformations

VIII.1
Supersymmetric quantum mechanics

The quadratic deformation
f(H,\tau)=\tau_{2}H^{2}
fits well with
the squared form of the Hamiltonian.
In supersymmetric quantum mechanics
Witten 1981
;
Cooper and Freedman 1983
;
Cooper et al. 1995
,
we introduce a set of Hamiltonians
H_{+}=A^{\dagger}A
and
H_{-}=AA^{\dagger}

for any operator
A
.
These Hamiltonians can be written as

\displaystyle Q^{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H_{+}&0\\
0&H_{-}\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&A^{\dagger}\\
A&0\end{array}\right).

When
H_{+}
has an energy eigenstate
|n_{+}\rangle
with a positive eigenvalue
E_{n}>0
,

A|n_{+}\rangle
represent an energy eigenstate of
H_{-}
with the same eigenvalue
E_{n}
.
For the Hamiltonian
H_{+}
and an initial state
|\psi_{0+}\rangle
,
we consider the time evolution
|\psi_{+}(t)\rangle=e^{-iH_{+}t}|\psi_{0+}\rangle
.
Then, we introduce

\displaystyle|\psi_{0-}\rangle=\frac{A|\psi_{0+}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi_{0+}|H_{+}|\psi_{0+}\rangle}}.

(111)

The time evolution of this state with
H_{-}
,

|\psi_{-}(t)\rangle=e^{-iH_{-}t}|\psi_{0-}\rangle
, is given by

\displaystyle|\psi_{-}(t)\rangle=\frac{A|\psi_{+}(t)\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi_{0+}|H_{+}|\psi_{0+}\rangle}}.

(112)

The state
|\psi_{0-}\rangle
is taken as the first Krylov vector.
We prepare the initial state

\displaystyle|K_{0}\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}|\psi_{0+}\rangle\\
0\end{array}\right),

and construct the Krylov series

\displaystyle|K_{n+1}\rangle b_{n+1}=Q|K_{n}\rangle-|K_{n-1}\rangle b_{n}.

(116)

We note that
a_{n}=0
in this case.
The first Krylov basis represents the initial state for
H_{-}
, that is,

\displaystyle|K_{1}\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
|\psi_{0-}\rangle\end{array}\right).

For a given initial state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
, the time-evolved state is constructed as

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iQ^{2}t}|\Psi_{0}\rangle.

(120)

We have

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}|\psi_{+}(t)\rangle\\
0\end{array}\right)&\mbox{for}\quad|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|K_{0}\rangle,\\
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
|\psi_{-}(t)\rangle\end{array}\right)&\mbox{for}\quad|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|K_{1}\rangle.\end{array}\right.

We note that
|\psi_{+}(t)\rangle
is represented by the even-order vectors of the Krylov expansion
and
|\psi_{-}(t)\rangle
by the odd-order vectors.
We expand the time-evolved state by
F=\sum_{n}|K_{n}\rangle\langle n|
as

|\Psi(t)\rangle=F|\Phi(t)\rangle
to define the vector
|\Phi(t)\rangle
in the Krylov space.
We obtain

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}|\Phi(t)\rangle=L^{2}|\Phi(t)\rangle.

(128)

The initial condition is given by

|\Psi(0)\rangle=(1,0,0,\dots)^{\mathrm{T}}
or

|\Psi(0)\rangle=(0,1,0,\dots)^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Since the diagonal components of the tridiagonal
L
-matrix are all equal to zero,

L^{2}
is written as

\displaystyle L^{2}=P_{\mathrm{even}}X^{\dagger}XP_{\mathrm{even}}+P_{\mathrm{odd}}XX^{\dagger}P_{\mathrm{odd}},

(129)

where
P_{\mathrm{even}}
is the projection operator onto even-order space (
0,2,4,\dots
),

P_{\mathrm{odd}}
is the projection onto odd space (
1,3,5,\dots
), and

\displaystyle X=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}b_{1}&b_{2}&0&\\
0&b_{3}&b_{4}&\\
0&0&b_{5}&\\
&&&\ddots\end{array}\right).

This upper tridiagonal matrix
X
is nothing but the Krylov transformation of the operator
A

introduced as
AF=FX
.
Each time evolution of
\pm
systems is transformed to that in the Krylov space

\displaystyle|\varphi_{+}(t)\rangle=e^{-iX^{\dagger}Xt}|0\rangle,

(135)

\displaystyle|\varphi_{-}(t)\rangle=e^{-iXX^{\dagger}t}|0\rangle.

(136)

These states are related to each other as

\displaystyle|\varphi_{-}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{b_{1}}X|\varphi_{+}(t)\rangle.

(137)

We note that the Krylov dimension is determined by the number of eigenstates
included in the initial state.
The spectrum of
H_{+}
equals to that of
H_{-}
,
except for the zero-energy of the
H_{+}
system.
Since the zero-energy state is defined as
A|0\rangle=0
, it cannot be transformed to the
H_{-}
system.
Thus, when
|\psi_{0+}\rangle
involves
d_{+}
eigenstates with
E_{n}>0
,
the total Krylov dimension is given by
d=2d_{+}
.
When
|\psi_{0+}\rangle
involves
d_{+}+1
eigenstates including
|0\rangle
,
we obtain
d=2d_{+}+1
.
In the latter case,
X
is a rectangular matrix
d_{+}\times(d_{+}+1)
.

Since the matrix
X
does not commute with
the complexity operator
{\cal K}=\mathrm{diag}\,(0,1,\dots)
,
we see from Eq. (
137
) that the spread complexity of the
H_{+}
system
is different from that of the
H_{-}
system.
It is known that certain kind of systems have the property

\displaystyle H_{-}(\alpha)=H_{+}(\alpha_{1})+\epsilon(\alpha_{1}),

(138)

where
\alpha
represents a set of parameters,

\alpha_{1}=f(\alpha)
is a function of
\alpha
, and
\epsilon(\alpha_{1})
is a positive function.
This shape invariance allows us to obtain the complete spectrum of the system
Gendenshteïn 1983
;
Cooper et al. 1995
.
In the Krylov subspace method, the tridiagonal matrix
L
representing
the generator of the time evolution depends not only on the original Hamiltonian
but also on the initial state,
which indicates that we require additional conditions on the state.
To find the invariance, we impose the condition

\displaystyle|\psi_{0-}(\alpha)\rangle\propto|\psi_{0+}(\alpha_{1})\rangle.

(139)

Then, we obtain

\displaystyle X(\alpha)X^{\dagger}(\alpha)=X^{\dagger}(\alpha_{1})X(\alpha_{1})+\epsilon(\alpha_{1}),

(140)

which is also written as

\displaystyle b_{2n+2}^{2}(\alpha)+b_{2n+1}^{2}(\alpha)=b_{2n+1}^{2}(\alpha_{1})+b_{2n}^{2}(\alpha_{1})+\epsilon(\alpha_{1}),

(141)

\displaystyle b_{2n+1}(\alpha)b_{2n}(\alpha)=b_{2n}(\alpha_{1})b_{2n-1}(\alpha_{1}).

(142)

The time-evolved state in the Krylov subspace is written as

\displaystyle|\varphi_{-}(t,\alpha)\rangle=e^{-i\epsilon(\alpha_{1})t}|\varphi_{+}(t,\alpha_{1})\rangle.

(143)

This relation shows that the spread complexity of the
H_{-}
system with
\alpha

is equal to that of the
H_{+}
system with
\alpha_{1}
:

\displaystyle K_{-}(t,\alpha)=K_{+}(t,\alpha_{1}).

(144)

We can also show that
X
and
X^{\dagger}
represent lowering and raising operators.
Combining Eq. (
137
) with Eq. (
143
), we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b_{1}(\alpha)}X(\alpha)|\varphi_{+}(t,\alpha)\rangle=e^{-i\epsilon(\alpha_{1})t}|\varphi_{+}(t,\alpha_{1})\rangle.

(145)

For the eigenstates of
X^{\dagger}(\alpha)X(\alpha)
,
|\phi_{n}(\alpha)\rangle
, satisfying

X^{\dagger}(\alpha)X(\alpha)|\phi_{n}(\alpha)\rangle=|\phi_{n}(\alpha)\rangle\epsilon_{n}(\alpha)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle X(\alpha)|\phi_{n}(\alpha)\rangle=|\phi_{n-1}(\alpha_{1})\rangle\sqrt{\epsilon_{n}(\alpha)},

(146)

\displaystyle X^{\dagger}(\alpha)|\phi_{n}(\alpha_{1})\rangle=|\phi_{n+1}(\alpha)\rangle\sqrt{\epsilon_{n+1}(\alpha)}.

(147)

One of the examples possessing shape invariance is the harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian.
When choosing the initial state as the coherent state, we have Eq. (
139
).
However, in that case, we obtain
\alpha_{1}=\alpha
and
H_{+}-H_{-}=\mathrm{const.}
.
The Lanczos coefficients are given by Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
), and
we can calculate everything analytically.
Many nontrivial examples of shape invariant Hamiltonians are known
Cooper et al. 1995
.
It would be an interesting problem to find nontrivial examples
that give implementable forms of Eq. (
139
).

VIII.2
Deformations

We apply our deformation scheme to the supersymmetric system.
With the deformed initial state

\displaystyle|\psi_{0\pm}(\tau_{2})\rangle=\frac{e^{-H_{\pm}\tau_{2}/2}|\psi_{0\pm}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi_{0\pm}|e^{-H_{\pm}\tau_{2}}|\psi_{0\pm}\rangle}},

(148)

the time-evolved state is written as

|\psi_{\pm}(t,\tau_{2})\rangle=e^{-iH_{\pm}t}|\psi_{0\pm}(\tau_{2})\rangle
.
This is a quadratic deformation and we can find the second Toda equations,
Eq. (
27
), with
a_{n}=0
.
The Lax equation reads

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}(X^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})X(\tau_{2}))=[M_{+}(\tau_{2}),X^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})X(\tau_{2})],

(149)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}(X(\tau_{2})X^{\dagger}(\tau_{2}))=[M_{-}(\tau_{2}),X(\tau_{2})X^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})],

(150)

where

\displaystyle M_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&-b_{1}b_{2}&0&\\
b_{1}b_{2}&0&-b_{3}b_{4}&\\
0&b_{3}b_{4}&0&\\
&&&\ddots\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle M_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&-b_{2}b_{3}&0&\\
b_{2}b_{3}&0&-b_{4}b_{5}&\\
0&b_{4}b_{5}&0&\\
&&&\ddots\end{array}\right).

These relations are reduced to the equation for
X
as

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}X(\tau_{2})=M_{-}(\tau_{2})X(\tau_{2})-X(\tau_{2})M_{+}(\tau_{2}).

(161)

In the case of the shape invariant systems,
we obtain
M_{-}(\tau_{2},\alpha)=M_{+}(\tau_{2},\alpha_{1})
.

VIII.3
Closed complexity algebra and exact solutions

When we use Eqs. (
59
) and (
60
) for the Lanczos coefficients,
we can find closed complexity algebra and
the spread complexity for the
\pm
-systems can be calculated analytically.

For
{\cal K}_{\pm}=\mathrm{diag}\,(0,1,2,\dots)
, we obtain

\displaystyle[X^{\dagger}X,{\cal K}_{+}]=-2M_{+},

(162)

\displaystyle[XX^{\dagger},{\cal K}_{-}]=-2M_{-}.

(163)

Taking the commutators again, we obtain

\displaystyle[X^{\dagger}X,M_{+}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}+8\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}{\cal K}_{+}

(164)

\displaystyle+(\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2})P_{\mathrm{even}}(X^{\dagger}X)_{\mathrm{od}}P_{\mathrm{even}},

and

\displaystyle[XX^{\dagger},M_{-}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}+8\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}{\cal K}_{-}

(165)

\displaystyle+(\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2})P_{\mathrm{odd}}(XX^{\dagger})_{\mathrm{od}}P_{\mathrm{odd}}.

Here,
Z_{\mathrm{od}}
represents the matrix that takes the diagonal components of
Z

to be zero.
We further obtain

\displaystyle[X^{\dagger}X,[X^{\dagger}X,M_{+}]]=4(\alpha^{2}-\gamma^{2})^{2}M_{+},

(166)

\displaystyle[XX^{\dagger},[XX^{\dagger},M_{-}]]=4(\alpha^{2}-\gamma^{2})^{2}M_{-}.

(167)

Thus, taking the commutators twice,
M_{\pm}
go back to the original matrix.
By using this property, we can calculate the spread complexity analytically.
For
+
-system, we obtain

\displaystyle K_{+}(t,\tau_{2})=2\alpha^{2}(\tau_{2})\gamma^{2}(\tau_{2})\left[\frac{\sin(\alpha^{2}(\tau_{2})-\gamma^{2}(\tau_{2}))t}{\alpha^{2}(\tau_{2})-\gamma^{2}(\tau_{2})}\right]^{2},

and for
-
-system

\displaystyle K_{-}(t,\tau_{2})=3K_{+}(t,\tau_{2}).

(169)

The difference of the factor 3 comes from the relations

b_{1}^{2}b_{2}^{2}=2\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}
and
b_{2}^{2}b_{3}^{2}=6\alpha^{2}\gamma^{2}
.

IX
Summary

We have discussed dynamical evolutions of deformed initial states
with the use of the Krylov subspace method.
Our definition of the deformations keeps the eigenvalues of the tridiagonal generator
in the Krylov space unchanged and the deformations are always described
in a form of Eq. (
23
),
representing a Lax equation for classical nonlinear integrable systems.
When the deformations are specified as Eq. (
4
) with a power-law function
f
,
we find the generalized Toda equations.
It was shown in previous works that a certain kind of deformations leads to the Toda
equations
Dymarsky and Gorsky 2020
;
Kundu et al. 2023
;
Angelinos et al. 2025
for the Lanczos coefficients.
We have shown similar results in a different way from a different setting
so that explicit applications become possible.

In principle, if we can apply the Lanczos algorithm for the deformed state to find
the explicit solution, there is no need to use the Toda equations.
Our method is useful when we want to compare results with
different deformed states systematically.
Some properties can be understood from the fundamental properties of the Toda equations.

In fact, for the coherent Gibbs state, we can find thermodynamic
properties of the corresponding thermodynamic system from the Lanczos coefficients.
The saturating behavior of the spread complexity is shown to be closely related to
a thermodynamic quantity.

The results from the random matrix ensembles also show that
the behavior at large deformation parameter
\tau

is governed by the fixed point solution of the Toda equations.
It guarantees the order of the eigenvalues, which can be used to
find the relation in Eq. (
75
).
The Toda equations also determine scales characterizing
the
\tau
-evolutions.

A possible application of the quadratic deformation is
the Krylov expansion of the Heisenberg operators.
To define the expansion, we need to introduce the inner product of operators.
When the inner product is parametrized by a real variable,
we find a flow in the parameter space.
Since the diagonal components of the tridiagonal matrix
L
are zero,
the first Toda equations are irrelevant and we need to use the second equations
as the simplest choice.

A different interesting generalization is to treat non-Hermitian Hamiltonian.
Since the Krylov method is applicable to Hermitian generators,
and since some non-Hermitian deformations without the Krylov method was discussed
in Ref.
Matsoukas-Roubeas et al. 2023a
, it is an interesting problem to extend
the deformation in Eq. (
8
) to the non-Hermitian case.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Toda equations

We derive the Toda equations,
the first set in Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
),
and the second set in Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
).
We follow the method in Ref.
Ismail 2005
where the simplest case
f(H,\tau)=\tau H
is discussed.
The generalization to the quadratic form in Eq. (
4
) is a straightforward task.

We first introduce the monic polynomial

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{n}(x,\tau)=b_{1}(\tau)\cdots b_{n}(\tau)P_{n}(x,\tau).

(170)

Instead of losing the normalization, it keeps
the coefficient of the highest degree of
\tilde{P}_{n}(x,\tau)
to unity.
The recurrence relation is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{n+1}(x,\tau)=(x-a_{n}(\tau))\tilde{P}_{n}(x,\tau)-b_{n}^{2}(\tau)\tilde{P}_{n-1}(x,\tau),

with
\tilde{P}_{0}(x,\tau)=1
.
The orthonormal relation is changed to

\displaystyle\int dx\,\rho(x,\tau)\tilde{P}_{m}(x,\tau)\tilde{P}_{n}(x,\tau)=h_{n}(\tau)\delta_{m,n},

(172)

where
h_{n}(\tau)=b_{1}^{2}(\tau)b_{2}^{2}(\tau)\cdots b_{n}^{2}(\tau)
.

The Toda equations can be derived by differentiating Eq. (
172
) with respect to
\tau
.
Since it is enough to consider the case
m\leq n
, we put
m=n-k
with
k=0,1,\dots,n
.
We use the relations

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\rho(x,\tau)=-(x-a_{0}(\tau))\rho(x,\tau),

(173)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}\rho(x,\tau)=-[x^{2}-(b_{1}^{2}(\tau)+a_{0}^{2}(\tau))]\rho(x,\tau),

(174)

and the property that
\partial_{\tau}\tilde{P}_{n}(x,\tau)
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
.
Using the recurrence relation and the orthonormal relation, we obtain

\displaystyle\int dx\,\rho(x,\tau)\tilde{P}_{n-k}(x,\tau)\partial_{\tau_{1}}\tilde{P}_{n}(x,\tau)

(175)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{k,0}[\partial_{\tau_{1}}h_{n}+h_{n}(a_{n}-a_{0})]+\delta_{k,1}h_{n-1}b^{2}_{n},

\displaystyle\int dx\,\rho(x,\tau)\tilde{P}_{n-k}(x,\tau)\partial_{\tau_{2}}\tilde{P}_{n}(x,\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{k,0}[\partial_{\tau_{2}}h_{n}+h_{n}(b_{n+1}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}+a_{n}^{2}-b_{1}^{2}-a_{0}^{2})]

\displaystyle+\delta_{k,1}h_{n-1}b^{2}_{n}(a_{n}+a_{n-1})+\delta_{k,2}h_{n-2}b_{n}^{2}b_{n-1}^{2}.

(176)

Taking
k=0
for these relations gives

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}h_{n}+h_{n}(a_{n}-a_{0})=0,

(177)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}h_{n}+h_{n}(b_{n+1}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}+a_{n}^{2}-b_{1}^{2}-a_{0}^{2})=0.

(178)

The first equation gives Eq. (
25
) and the second Eq. (
27
).
Equations (
175
) and (
176
) also give

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}\tilde{P}_{n}=b^{2}_{n}\tilde{P}_{n-1},

(179)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}\tilde{P}_{n}=b_{n}^{2}(a_{n}+a_{n-1})\tilde{P}_{n-1}+b_{n}^{2}b_{n-1}^{2}\tilde{P}_{n-2}.

(180)

In a similar way, Eqs. (
24
) and (
26
)
can be derived from the derivative of the relation

\displaystyle a_{n}(\tau)h_{n}(\tau)=\int dx\,\rho(x,\tau)x\tilde{P}_{n}^{2}(x,\tau).

(181)

We can also find that the
\tau
-evolution of

F(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle\langle n|

is described by
V^{\dagger}(\tau)
.
To find the result, we examine the derivative of the Krylov basis.
At the zeroth order, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}(H-a_{0}(\tau))|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle,

(182)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}[H^{2}-(b_{1}^{2}(\tau)+a_{0}^{2}(\tau))]|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle.

We note that
|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle=|\psi_{0}(\tau)\rangle
.
The higher-order terms can be calculated from

|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle=\tilde{P}_{n}(H,\tau)|K_{0}(\tau)\rangle/\sqrt{h_{n}(\tau)}
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{1}}|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}b_{n+1}(\tau)|K_{n+1}(\tau)\rangle+\frac{1}{2}b_{n}(\tau)|K_{n-1}(\tau)\rangle,

(184)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{2}}|K_{n}(\tau)\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}(a_{n}(\tau)+a_{n+1}(\tau))b_{n+1}(\tau)|K_{n+1}(\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(a_{n-1}(\tau)+a_{n}(\tau))b_{n}(\tau)|K_{n-1}(\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}b_{n+1}(\tau)b_{n+2}(\tau)|K_{n+2}(\tau)\rangle+\frac{1}{2}b_{n-1}(\tau)b_{n}(\tau)|K_{n-2}(\tau)\rangle.

These relations give

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{\mu}}F(\tau)=-F(\tau)M_{\mu}(\tau),

(186)

and Eq. (
30
).

Appendix B
Time average of expectation values in Krylov space

In this section, we derive Eq. (
71
) for the coherent Gibbs state.
To broaden the applicability,
we discuss the derivation in a more general setting.

For an operator
{\cal X}
in Krylov space,
we consider the expectation value

\displaystyle X(t,\tau)=\langle\varphi(t,\tau)|{\cal X}|\varphi(t,\tau)\rangle,

(187)

and its time average

\displaystyle\overline{X}(\tau)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,X(t,\tau).

(188)

Noting
|\varphi(t,\tau)\rangle=e^{-iL(\tau)t}|0\rangle
,
we introduce the eigenstates of
L(\tau)
defined from Eq. (
74
).
The eigenvalue
\epsilon_{n}
is given by one of the eigenvalues of the original Hamiltonian
and is independent of
\tau
.
The Lax equation (
23
) indicates that we can write

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{\mu}}|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle=M_{\mu}(\tau)|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle.

(189)

We can confirm that
|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle
satisfying Eqs. (
74
) and (
189
) is given by Eq. (
73
).

Using the spectral decomposition, we have

\displaystyle X(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}e^{i(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})t}\langle 0|\phi_{m}(\tau)\rangle

(190)

\displaystyle\times\langle\phi_{m}(\tau)|{\cal X}|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle\langle\phi_{n}(\tau)|0\rangle.

Taking the average over
t
removes contributions with
m\neq n
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\overline{X}(\tau)=\sum_{n}|\langle 0|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle|^{2}\langle\phi_{n}(\tau)|{\cal X}|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle.

(191)

We consider the case that
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is given by
the uniform linear combination of the eigenstates of
H
.
By applying
\langle 0|
to Eq. (
73
) from the left
and using Eq. (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle 0|\phi_{n}(\tau)\rangle=\langle\psi_{0}(\tau)|\epsilon_{n}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{d_{n}e^{-f(\epsilon_{n},\tau)}}{\sum_{n}d_{n}e^{-f(\epsilon_{n},\tau)}}}.

(192)

Thus, we find Eq. (
71
).
