Source identification via path-wise gradient estimation.
Richard B. Lehoucq, Scott A. McKinley, Petr Plecháč

Abstract
. In the context of PDE-constrained optimization theory, source identification problems traditionally entail particles emerging from an unknown source distribution inside a domain, moving according to a prescribed stochastic process, e.g. Brownian motion, and then exiting through the boundary of a compact domain. Given information about the flux of particles through the boundary of the domain, the challenge is to infer as much as possible about the source. In the PDE setting, it is usually assumed that the flux can be observed without error and at all points on the boundary. Here we consider a different, more statistical presentation of the model, in which the data has the form of discrete counts of particles arriving at a set of disjoint detectors whose union is a strict subset of the boundary. In keeping with the primacy of the stochastic processes in the generation of the model, we present a gradient descent algorithm in which exit rates and parameter sensitivities are computed by simulations of particle paths. We present examples for both Itô diffusion and piecewise-deterministic Markov processes, noting that the form of the sensitivities depends only on the parameterization of the source distribution and is universal among a large class of Markov processes.



1.
Introduction

Source identification for elliptic PDEs is a classical inverse problem first considered in the context of gravimetry
(
Isakov 1990
;
Isakov 2006
)
, and later
the problem has re-emerged in the context of EEG readings
(
El Badia and Ha-Duong 2000
;
Baratchart et al. 2004
)
while more recently being treated in several other more general settings (see
Liu 2017
;
Liu and Li 2020
for example). The theme of these problems is that particles are emerging from a source in the interior of a compact domain, they evolve according
to a prescribed stochastic process, and they exit the system either through the boundary of the domain or through degradation. The particle system is assumed to be in a steady state, and the question is whether the location, size, and/or magnitude of the source distribution can be inferred from data taken exclusively at the boundary.

The problem is typically postulated in terms of a deterministic boundary value problem (BVP), formulated as follows. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is an elliptic partial differential operator and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open domain with sufficiently regular boundary
\partial\Omega
. Then the
direct problem
is to be given a source function
s:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and boundary value function
f:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and then solve the boundary value problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u

\displaystyle=-s,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega;

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=f,

\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega.

to find the boundary flux
\partial g\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, satisfying

(1.2)

g(x)\coloneqq\nabla u(x)\cdot\nu(x)

for all
x\in\partial\Omega
, where
\nu(x)
is the outward normal vector at
x
and
u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
.

The
inverse problem
is to be given the Cauchy data pair
(f,g)
, and from that, infer the source
s
. This is not always a well-posed problem
(
Isakov 1990
;
Liu 2017
)
. A typical theme is that if two proposed sources have the same center and the same
“volume”, then the boundary data can be identical
(
El Badia and Ha-Duong 2000
;
El Badia and Nara 2011
)
.

To deal with ill-posedness investigators commonly restrict the source function to be a member of a reduced class of functions: e.g., point sources and dipoles
(
El Badia and Ha-Duong 2000
)
,
circles
(
Shigeta and Hon 2003
)
, and star-shapes
(
Alves et al. 2017
;
Liu 2017
)
are recent examples.
When explicit solutions for
u
are available, this yields a specific set of inverse methods that are difficult to generalize.
For this reason, a host of computational methods have been introduced, each tuned to the specifics of the challenges that the authors have in mind. A recent comprehensive review of numerical approaches to the inverse source problem for the Helmholtz equation can be found in the work by
Liu and Li 2020
.

1.1.
Inverse problems for stochastic exit processes: particle counts and binned data

In this work, we take a different point of view on the source identification problems that is rooted in a stochastic processes perspective.
In particular, if we think of the first two terms of the operator

\mathcal{A}
as the adjoint of the generator of a continuous-time Markov process, then we can interpret the inverse problem
with Dirichlet data (
f=0
)
as attempting to learn about the location of a source distribution
given information about particle exits.

This shift in perspective also encourages a shift in assumptions about what we consider to be the “data” used to perform the source identification.
For example, for PDE inverse problems it is commonly assumed that the pair of functions
(f,g)
data are specified at every point on the boundary. In particular, this means being able to measure the boundary flux, which is natural in gravimetry.
However, from the particle perspective, the more natural observable is particle
counts
as they exit through a set of distinct regions

\{D_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
that we call “detectors”.
Experimentally this amounts to observing the outcomes of a continuous random
variable (the exit location distribution) while restricted to binned data counts.

We can formalize this in the simplest case as follows. Suppose that particles are “born” in the domain according to a Poisson process (with the rate
\lambda>0
) and their initial locations are independently and identically distributed according to a probability distribution
\phi(x\,;\,\theta)
.
Suppose that the birth times prior to the time
t=0
are
enumerated
0>T_{1}>T_{2}>\ldots
and the particle trajectories
are labeled
\{X_{n}(t)\}_{t\geq T_{n}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\tau_{n}
denote the exit time from the domain
\Omega

of the
n
th particle.
Upon arrival they undergo Markov process movement that is
governed by a common generator
\mathcal{L}
until they exit the domain
at some time
\tau_{n}
.

Then, for any given observation window
[0,T]
, the data we consider in this work have the form

N_{j}(T)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau_{n}\in[0,T]\}}\mathbb{1}_{\{X_{n}(\tau_{n})\in D_{j}\}},\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}.

For the dynamics we have described, the particles are non-interacting, so there is a significant simplification in capturing the probability distribution of experimental outcomes.
A Poisson birth process implies that the particle exits.
When the particle arrival process is a Poisson process,
the exits of particles through each of the detectors are thinned Poisson processes, and in
Section

3
we show explicitly for the Itó diffusion case that particles exit through detector
D_{j}
according to a Poisson process and the observations are independent with respective distributions

N_{j}\sim\text{Pois}\big(\lambda p_{j}(\theta)T\big)

where
p_{j}(\theta)
is the probability that any given particle exits through detector
D_{j}
.

If we assume we have perfect knowledge of the exit rates, then we are connected back to the PDE-constrained optimization problem articulated in the previous section, but assuming the data takes the form of rates of exit through the various detectors.
To this end, let
u
satisfy

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}u(x\,;\,\theta)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\phi(x\,;\,\theta),

\displaystyle x\in\Omega;

\displaystyle u(x\,;\,\theta)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega.

the rates of exit through the detectors are then

(1.4)

g_{j}(\theta)\coloneqq\int_{D_{j}}\nabla u(x\,;\,\theta)\cdot\nu(x)\,\mathrm{d}S

where
\mathrm{d}S
denotes an integral along the surface of the domain
\Omega
.

For the binned-data PDE-inverse problem, the data is expressed as a vector of rates
\hat{\mathbf{g}}=(\hat{g}_{1},\ldots,\hat{g}_{J})
. Assuming that other parameters are known (
\lambda
in particular), let
\Phi(\delta)
be a convex cost function. Then our estimator for the parameter
\theta
has the form

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\hat{\theta}_{g}\coloneqq\argmin_{\theta}\sum_{j}\Phi\big(g_{j}(\theta)-\hat{g}_{j}\big),\\
&\qquad\qquad\text{subject to }\eqref{eq:bvp-u}\text{ and }\eqref{eq:g_j}.\end{aligned}\right.

From the stochastic process perspective, when
\lambda
is known, our task is to fit the exit probabilities
p_{i}(\theta)
. To this end, define
\hat{p}_{j}\coloneqq N_{j}/\lambda T
. We define our estimator for
binned, particle count data
as a stochastic-process constrained optimization:

(1.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\hat{\theta}\coloneqq\argmin_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Phi\big(\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\big(\mathbb{1}_{\{X(\tau)\in D_{j}\}}\big)-\hat{p}_{j})\\
&\qquad\qquad\text{subject to }X_{n}(t)\text{ having generator }\mathcal{L},\\
&\qquad\qquad\text{with initial position distribution }\phi(\cdot\,;\,\theta).\end{aligned}\right.

Given these formulations, any number of search strategies can be postulated. In this work we pursue a gradient descent method, which features a technical challenge that provides a major impetus for the work presented in this paper. Namely, taking the gradient of this objective function results in the need to compute the sensitivity of the expected value with respect to the parameter of interest:

(1.7)

\nabla_{\theta}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Phi\big(\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\big(\mathbb{1}_{\{X(\tau)\in D_{j}\}}\big)-\hat{p}_{j})\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Phi^{\prime}\big(\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\big(\mathbb{1}_{\{X(\tau)\in D_{j}\}}\big)-\hat{p}_{j}\big)\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\big(1_{\{X(\tau)\in D_{j}\}}\big).

1.2.
Approach to gradient estimation

There is an extensive literature investigating sensitivities of expected values with respect to parameters, both for general random variables
(
Glynn 1987
)
and for stochastic processes
(
Glynn 1989
)
. The method used is usually determined by the overlap (or lack thereof) of the supports of the probability distributions used to generate the expected values.

For example, suppose we would like to find the optimal choice from a candidate family of probability distributions, indexed by
\theta
, that have continuous densities. The support of each distribution is the set of all
x
such that for any open ball
B
containing
x
, we have
\int_{B}f(x)\mathrm{d}x>0
. When all distributions in the candidate family have the same support, we can use
likelihood-ratio methods
that compute the Radon-Nikodým derivative between candidate probability measures to assess the relative likelihood of experimental outcomes.

In recent works, parameter dependence of steady-state expectations computed from the stationary distribution of a Markov chain was studied in
Glynn and Olvera-Cravioto 2019
, for continuous time Markov chains (CTMC)
in
Wang and Plecháč 2019
, and for diffusion processes
in
Plecháč et al. 2021
;
Plecháč et al. 2023
.
Sensitivity with respect to the initial conditions
(the financial “greek”,
\Delta
) was computed using likelihood ratio methods in
Broadie and Glasserman 1996
.

However, if the initial condition of the generating stochastic process is just a single point, the family of candidate distribution functions are a set of Dirac
\delta
-functions respectively located at
\theta
. In this case, all candidate distributions are mutually singular and the likelihood ratio methods are not well-defined. In this case, ideas from Malliavin calculus have proved useful, see
(
Fournié et al. 1999
;
Chen and Glasserman 2007
;
Plyasunov and Arkin 2007
;
Warren and Allen 2012
;
Warren and Allen 2013
)
.

Here, our model problem is similar to a common assumption made in the PDE inverse theory literature: namely the source is supported on a ball centered at zero and therefore has compact support. Since the initial condition is distributed over a contiuum of points the Malliavin derivative technique can become computationally intensive. Meanwhile, likelihood ratio approaches cannot be immediately implemented because any two candidate distributions contain mutually singular subsets. However, our method can be seen as being in line with the likelihood ratio perspective, with an extension that follows from the Reynolds transport formula. A similar “shape derivative” technique was implemented by
Liu 2017
and
Charkaoui et al. 2021
for source identification techniques in the context of the Helmholtz equation with a variety of source support shapes.

1.3.
Pathwise Monte Carlo (MC) approach to estimation

As has been widely noted in the literature, differentiation of an expected value with respect to a parameter yields a formula that can be represented by an expectation with respect to the same distribution. Roughly speaking, this means that for the gradient appearing in
Equation

1.7
, there exists a random variable
Q
such that

(1.8)

\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\big(\mathbb{1}_{X(\tau)\in D_{j}\}}\big)=\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\big(\mathbb{1}_{X(\tau)\in D_{j}\}}Q\big).

It follows that if we can compute
Q
from the same information that generates knowledge of the event
\{X(\tau)\in D_{j}\}
, then all terms in the gradient calculation
Equation

1.7
can be estimated by MC methods and the MC samples can be reused.

In fact, MC methods are well-established in the study of radiation transport and can present a number of advantages over deterministic methods in specific geometries
(
Pascucci et al. 2004
)
, the medium in anisotropic
(
Pinte et al. 2009
)
, or the data of interest involves particle counts or statistics concerning individual particle exit locations or exit directions
(
Code and Whitney 1995
)
. See also
Brunner 2002
,
Juvela 2005
and
Kuiper and Klessen 2013
for interesting discussions concerning comparisons of computational methods. Building in a capability to simultaneously compute expected path outcomes and parameter sensitivities adds significant value to the methods.

Therefore, while the problem we present here can be solved by deterministic methods, the framework should generalize to situations where MC methods have already been demonstrated to have intrinsic advantages.

Overview of work

In
Section

2
, we define two “baseline” Markov processes that can be readily simulated and for which source identification applications are natural to consider. We define the steady state
stochastic fountain
in which particles are born according to a Poisson arrival process and evolve independently until they exit a given compact domain or switch out of the active state. In
Section

2.1
we report one of our main theoretical results, implementing the Reynolds transport formula to compute sensitivity of exit probabilities, and emphasize that the form of the result does not depend on the underlying baseline Markov process.

In
Section

3
we provide a theoretical framework that explicitly ties the inverse problem articulated for stochastic fountains to the classical PDE source identification problem articulated by
Equations

1.3
and
1.4
. This is followed in
Section

4
where we demonstrate the efficacy of the algorithm in a simple setting for both Brownian motion and for our scattering model. We numerically explore two senses of statistical consistency. In
Section

4.2
we show convergence of the MC estimates for the gradient of the exit probability with respect to the source location parameter.

2.
Steady state stochastic fountains: definition and source sensitivity

In this work, we consider two types of Markov processes: Itô diffusions with constant diffusivity, and piecewise-deterministic Markov processes (PDMP) with constant-velocity segments. We define
baseline
versions of that are then replicated in two types of ensemble systems. The first, which is
stochastic fountain
system, is the type of system we assume generates the data measured by our fictional observer. The second, a
simultaneous release
system, is useful for the computational approximation of exit probabilities and and derivatives with respect to parameters for the algorithm we report at the end of this section.

Definition 2.1

(Baseline Markov Processes)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the generator of a continuous-time, time-homogeneous Markov process
\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
in the sense that for all smooth and bounded functions

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}f(x)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\big(\mathbb{E}^{x}[f(X_{t})]-x\big).

Assumption 1

(Baseline Itô Diffusion)
.

When

is an Itô diffusion, we assume that it has the form

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X(t)

\displaystyle=\mu(X(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\eta}\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad t>0;

\displaystyle X(0)

\displaystyle=\xi\sim\phi(\cdot\,;\,\theta,\beta).

where

is twice continuously differentiable. In this case, the generator for the baseline Markov process is

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}f(x)=\mu(x)\cdot\nabla f(x)+\eta\Delta f(x).

Assumption 2

(Baseline Scattering Process)

Here we consider

\mathcal{L}f(x,v)=\mu(x,v)\cdot\nabla f(x,v)-(\sigma_{a}(x,v)+\sigma_{s}(x,v))f(x,v)+\sigma_{s}(x,v)\int_{{\Omega_{v}}}K(v,v^{\prime},x)f(x,v^{\prime})\,dv^{\prime}\,,

More precisely, we define the deterministic flow

Typically for free particles the trajectory is governed by the velocity and

(ii)

jumps

(or

The process

(i)

\xi_{1}=J_{0}(x,y)

\begin{cases}X_{t}=\Phi^{{x,v}}_{{t}}\,,\;\;V_{t}=v\,&\text{for }t\in[0,\xi_{1})\\

(ii)

\begin{cases}X_{t}=\Phi^{{X_{\xi_{n-1}},V_{\xi_{n-1}}}}_{{t}}\,,\;\;V_{t}=V_{\xi_{n-1}}\,,&\text{ for }t\in[\xi_{n-1},J_{n})\\

The PDMP construction is directly implemented in Monte Carlo sampling of the process. However, a crucial point in the simulation is that the jump times are exponentially distributed with a non-constant rate. This fact thus requires that the inhomogeneous Poisson process procedure is applied for sampling the segment durations

In the particle transport problems where the velocity function

The jump times are thus directly linked to the free path that is determined by the cross-section function

(Domain)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

\tau\coloneqq\inf\{t>0\,:\,X(t)\notin\Omega\}

Definition 2.2

(Admissible source distributions)
.

A function

We say that a collection of source distributions form a

\phi(x\,;\,\theta,\beta)=\frac{1}{C\beta^{d}}\Psi\left(\frac{|x-\theta|}{\beta}\right)

\Psi(r)=\begin{cases}e^{-\psi(r)},&r\in[0,1);\\
\lim_{r^{\prime}\uparrow 1}e^{-\psi(r^{\prime})}&r=1\\
0&r>1.\end{cases}

The constant

We define

(2.10)

\mathcal{O}(\theta,\beta)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,|x-\theta|=\beta\}

to be the boundary of the support of the source distribution.

The two forms of the base distribution that we will keep in mind throughout this work have the following forms:

(2.11)

\displaystyle\text{Uniform distribution: }\psi(r)

\displaystyle=1_{[0,1]}(r);

\displaystyle\text{Bump function: }\psi(r)

Definition 2.3

(Boundary Detectors and Exit Notation)
.

For a given domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\{D_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
be a sequence of disjoint connected subsets of the boundary

. These represent detectors where we can observe particles exiting the domain.

For a given baseline Markov process

(2.12)

For each

Definition 2.4

(Steady-state stochastic fountain exit processes)
.

Let a generator
\mathcal{L}
be given for a baseline Markov process and let
\Omega
be an open domain with boundary detectors
\{D_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
. Let
\phi
be an admissible source distribution for particles within

Let

For each

, let

be Markov processes driven by generator

with initial condition

. For each

Then the exit process through detector

(2.14)

N_{j}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\tau_{n}\in[0,t]}\mathbb{1}_{X_{n}(\tau_{n})\in D_{j}}.

Our goal in this work is to provide a method for use the fountain exit processes to infer the source location (and size, when possible). As mentioned in the introduction though, we would like to take a pathwise approach to estimating exit probabilities. Since stochastic fountain are in steady-state, numerical simulations of these systems entail producing significant burn-in periods. In practice this means that a substantial number of paths are discarded when particles are born well before the observation window
[0,t]
and exit the domain before time zero.

In order to conduct more efficient estimation of exit probabilities, we introduce another particle system in which all trajectories are used directly empirical average. Here, a fixed number of particles
M
are simulated starting at initial time
t=0
and they are simulated until their exit time, regardless of how near or far it is down the road. Through the theory presented in
Section

3
, the empirical exit probabilities for the
simultaneous release
system can be used to compute the exit probabilities and gradients that we need.

Definition 2.5

(Simultaneous release exit probabilities)
.

Let a generator
\mathcal{L}
be given for a baseline Markov process and let
\Omega
be an open domain with boundary detectors
\{D_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
. Let
\phi
be an admissible source distribution for particles within
\Omega
.

Let

be a given number of particles and let
\{X_{m}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be Markov processes driven by

with initial condition

. For each

be the exit time for

Then the empirical exit probability through detector

(2.15)

2.1.
Exit probability sensitivity

All of our results run through the observation that, for the fountain system we have described here, gradients can be computed from simulated data and restiction of the admissible source distributions to a compactly supported location-and-scale family. Importantly, the sensitivity with respect to this information has a universal form, meaning that no details about the baseline Markov generator
\mathcal{L}
appear in the statement of the senstivity. The generator only appears through evaluations of the generated paths.

At this point the advantage of using a mollified indicator function (the bump function) for our source distribution becomes apparent. Because the limit of the source distribution from the interior of the support is what appears the boundary terms of

2.16

On the other hand, the bump function approaches zero at its support boundary and therefore there is no contribution from a boundary term when computing gradients. The trade-off is the care that must be taken to sample from the bump function. We take an importance sampling approach, where elements are drawn from the uniform distribution on the support of the source, and then outcomes are re-weighted by the relative likelihood of that sampled initial condition coming from the bump function distribution.

Proposition 3.1

(Exit probabilities)

Theorem 3.2

(Stochastic fountain exit rates)
.

Under the conditions given by

Proof.

Our approach is to first establish that for all

The expected number that exit through detector

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\phi}[\widetilde{N}_{j}(t)]

Proposition 3.3

(Relationship to BVP boundary flux)
.

Define
u
to be the solution to the BVP

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}u(x)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\phi(x\,;\,\theta,\beta),

\displaystyle x\in\Omega;

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega.

Then the boundary exit rates have the form

(3.6)

\lambda p_{j}(\phi)=\int_{D_{j}}\eta(\nabla u(x))\cdot\nu(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}S(x)

is the outward pointing normal vector at the location
x

Proof.

Noting that
a(x)
is a vector-valued function while

and

are scalars, we have the product rule
\nabla\cdot\big(w(x)a(x)u(x)\big)=(\nabla\cdot w(x))a(x)u(x)+w(x)\nabla\cdot\big(a(x)u(x)\big)

Using this identity in conjunction with Green’s second identity and the divergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\langle w_{j},\mathcal{L}^{*}u\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle w_{j},\eta\Delta u\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle w_{j},\nabla\cdot(au)\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\eta\Delta w,u\rangle_{\Omega}-\eta\int_{\partial\Omega}w(x)\big(\nabla u(x)\cdot\nu(x)\big)-\big(\nabla w(x)\cdot\nu(x)\big)u(x)\mathrm{d}S(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\big(a(x)w(x)u(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+\langle a\cdot\nabla w,u\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}w,u\rangle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\eta w(x)\big(\nabla u(x)\cdot\nu(x)\big)-\eta\big(\nabla w(x)\cdot\nu(x)\big)u(x)

\displaystyle-\,w(x)u(x)(a(x)\cdot\nu(x))\mathrm{d}S(x).

\langle w_{j},\mathcal{L}^{*}u\rangle=-\int_{D_{j}}\big(\eta\nabla u(x)\big)\cdot\nu(x)\mathrm{d}S(x)

5.
Discussion

We have developed a method for finding the source of stochastically evolving particles based solely on their arrival at a set of detectors on the boundary of a given domain. Operating our investigation under the assumption that MC methods have advantages over deterministic numerical techniques in certain settings, we have demonstrated that gradients with respect to source distribution parameters can be computed pathwise with the same information necessary to produce exit probability results.

We emphasized finding the source location and believe that more investigation is needed regarding estimations of the source distribution’s scale parameter
\beta
. In fact, for the Brownian motion example presented in
Section

4
the scale parameter seems to be unidentifiable (see
El Badia and Ha-Duong 2000
for related discussion). If particle degradation is introduced, then the scale parameter may become identifiable, but with a much higher variance than source location estimator. Quantifying this question would follow from computation of the Fisher information of the estimators, but unlike the parameter gradients, we do not see at present how to estimate the Fisher information from path data alone.

The most significant limitation of the method we have presented is the need for simulated particles to regularly hit the boundary detectors. There exist adjoint methods that can improve efficiency of simulation
(
Smith et al. 2025
)
, but in complex geometries or situations where the detectors and source are small relative to
\Omega
, substantial innovation may be required to improve performance. Nevertheless, the robustness of the method with respect to the underlying generating process is a promising note for this method.
