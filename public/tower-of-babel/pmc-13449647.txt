pAnno: a comprehensive, precise, and fast proteogenomic workflow for the discovery of novel coding regions
Authors: Kaifei Wang, Zhuohong Wei, Changrong Li, Yaping Peng, Jiale Zhao, Pengzhi Mao, Ching Tarn, Jinyang Li, Ranfei Chen, Jiaxiang Ding, Feng Ge, Mingkun Yang, Hao Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteogenomics is a transformative approach for deciphering novel coding regions through integration of genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data. Here, we present pAnno, an end-to-end workflow designed to uncover hidden protein-coding elements with high precision and efficiency. pAnno generates customized protein databases by integrating multi-omic data, employs a multi-stage iterative open search strategy, and incorporates an efficient peptide-to-coding sequence mapping algorithm. Despite a 50-fold increase in database size, pAnno maintains high sensitivity and accuracy in peptide identification and achieves genomic localization of novel events with only ∼∼3% additional processing time, delivering unprecedented resolution and speed in proteogenomic analysis. By detecting splicing, mutations, and novel protein isoforms, pAnno supports various downstream applications and reveals overlooked events, identifying 1.73×× more novel proteins in Pyrus and 34×× more non-canonical HLA peptides in lung cancer. These capabilities position pAnno as a gold-standard proteogenomic workflow, excelling in non-canonical coding discovery and large-scale database processing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04111-0.

Background

Proteogenomics, which integrates proteomics, transcriptomics, and genomics, has advanced rapidly in recent years [1]. While genomics provides the foundational genetic blueprint, it often suffers from incomplete or inaccurate annotations and cannot fully capture the precise protein products generated through transcription and translation. Traditional tools [2] can infer gene structures based on annotations from related species or transcriptome data, but such predictions are frequently uncertain. Proteogenomics addresses these limitations by leveraging mass spectrometry-based proteomic data to search customized multi-omic databases, enabling the discovery of novel peptides and coding sequences (CDSs), which serves as the gold standard for validating novel coding events. This approach significantly enhances gene annotation, confirms translation products, reveals the functional impact of mutations, revealing the functional impact of mutations, advancing personalized medicine, and elucidating complex biological processes [3]. Notably, proteogenomics has been successfully applied in genome reannotation [4, 5], evaluation of short open reading frames (sORFs, łe≤ 300 nucleotides) [6, 7], and identification of non-canonical HLA-bound peptides [8, 9].

Like traditional proteomics, the core step of proteogenomics remains mass spectrometry-based peptide and protein identification, with three key distinctions. First, proteogenomic analysis requires the generation of customized databases that not only include reference proteins but also comprehensively cover potential novel events, such as proteins originating from genomic regions previously annotated as non-coding [1]. Second, beyond searching against these large-scale customized databases, proteogenomic approaches also incorporate multidimensional information, including post-translational modifications, alternative splicing, and mutations. This significantly expands the search space, which, in turn, reduces both sensitivity and accuracy while increasing computational requirements [10, 11]. Third, the identified novel peptides are used to infer novel CDSs, thereby facilitating downstream biological and functional analyses [12, 13]. However, the vast database size increases the likelihood of peptides matching multiple genomic locations by chance, leading to ambiguous interpretations of gene expression [14, 15]. This issue is particularly pronounced in eukaryotes, where extensive splicing further complicates the inference of CDSs.

Several efforts have been made to address these challenges in proteogenomic data analysis. For customized database generation, gene prediction-based methods such as Augustus [16, 17], Balrog [18], and SpliceAI [19] are commonly employed to determine coding regions within DNA sequences. However, these methods systematically overlook sORFs and multi-frame translation events, which are precisely that proteogenomics aims to detect [20, 21]. An alternative strategy combines six-frame genomic translation [13] with RNA-seq-based three-frame translation [22, 23], maintaining genomic coverage while incorporating splicing and mutations. However, this hybrid approach primarily generates artificial sequences, resulting in large yet imprecise databases [1]. To mitigate performance declines in searching against customized databases, two iterative strategies are commonly used: Dynamic Database Optimization, which refines the search space based on initial results [24, 25], and Hierarchical Spectral Analysis, which resolves part of the spectra against a reference database and uses unresolved spectra to search against a customized database [26, 27]. Additionally, grouping novel peptides with reference peptides for false discovery rate (FDR) estimation helps reduce suspicious identifications [28, 29], and several tools have been developed to validate identified peptides [30–32]. Despite these advancements, achieving high-quality peptide identifications remains challenging [1, 14]. To improve the accuracy of peptide-to-CDS inference, tools such as JUMPg [33], PGTools [34], and NextSearch [35] enable peptide matching to both the genome and pre-constructed splice junctions. GAPE [36] treats peptides matching multiple locations as unreliable, while IPAW [37] reports novel spliced peptides only when both corresponding CDSs are supported by high-confidence peptides. Nevertheless, these enhancements remain limited and may overlook valuable discoveries. More importantly, systematic evaluations of CDS reporting quality are currently lacking [15], which presents a critical challenge for further improving proteogenomic analysis and ensuring the reliability of inferred gene expression.

To address these challenges mentioned above, we present pAnno, a proteogenomic workflow that provides an end-to-end solution encompassing customized database generation, database searching, and CDS inference. Specifically, pAnno integrates DNA six-frame and RNA three-frame translation to capture a broad range of potential protein-coding regions. It employs a multi-step iterative search strategy, introduces DBReducer [25] to minimize the size of customized databases, and enables mutation and splicing event detection for CDS inference. In addition, species-specific filters are incorporated to improve result specificity. Crucially, we introduce a systematic simulation method to evaluate CDS reporting accuracy. Extensive benchmarking on four annotated datasets demonstrates pAnno’s superior sensitivity and reliability across all modules. Additionally, it localizes novel events with minimal overhead, as stages beyond database searching account for only about 3% of total runtime. Furthermore, we applied pAnno in several scenarios including genome annotation, sORF identification, and non-canonical HLA-bound peptide discovery, consistently identifying more reliable novel events. For instance, in Pyrus genome annotation, pAnno uncovered 2,108 additional BLASTp-validated novel events, including 10,466 splice sites and 3,664 mutations. In two lung cancer samples, pAnno identified over 34 times more non-canonical HLA-binding peptides, more than 70% of which were validated by NetMHC [38, 39] to have binding affinity to the corresponding alleles. Overall, pAnno is a comprehensive and reliable workflow for proteogenomic analysis with the potential to replace traditional methods for deeper multi-omics investigations. pAnno is available for free download from https://github.com/Wang-kaifei/pAnno_pub.

Results

Overview of pAnno

The workflow of pAnno comprises three major steps. In the first step (Fig. 1A), a customized protein database—combining reference proteins with sequences translated from DNA and RNA, where the RNA input can originate from any assembly or transcript source. Next, a two-stage search is performed against this customized database. Stage 1 performs a conventional search to identify a subset of MS/MS spectra. In Stage 2, the unresolved spectra are used to refine the customized database via DBReducer, after which an open search is carried out using the full dataset against the reduced database. Novel and known peptides (those matching the reference database) are then grouped separately for more accurate FDR estimation. Throughout the database search procedure, FDR is estimated using the Target–Decoy Approach (TDA) [40] at the peptide level. As shown in Additional file 1: Table S3, adding reversed decoys resulted in only 1% overlap between target and decoy peptides in the customized database, meeting the requirements for TDA-FDR. To ensure comprehensive upstream coverage, all potential coding regions over 10 amino acids are retained in customized database. In the second step (Fig. 1B), pAnno rapidly maps novel peptides to the genome in three patterns—normal, splicing, and single amino acid variants (SAAVs)—and then bidirectionally extends the genomic window to identify candidate CDSs. For splicing, all potential junctions are enumerated, and candidate proteins are separately extracted from each flanking sequence to construct two tables, which are then compared in pairs to validate splice mappings. For SAAV, each variant position is masked in turn to retrieve proteins covering the remaining residues. By leveraging a tag-based indexing strategy, pAnno achieves O(1) retrieval time complexity, greatly accelerating the mapping process.

In the third step (Fig. 1C), pAnno designs a species-specific scheme to distinguish plausible and spurious CDSs. For species with minimal splicing (hereafter simple species), pAnno uses a deep learning–based scoring model to evaluate each candidate CDS. This model is trained on samples derived from Stage 1 of the database search: positive samples are CDSs inferred from target peptides matching existing annotations, while negative samples include unmatched CDSs and CDSs inferred from decoy peptides (FDR < 1%). Features are then extracted to train an MLP-based system to assess novel CDS reliability. For species with extensive splicing (hereafter complex species), we designed a multi-stage filtering strategy to infer CDS mapping groups, each comprising several CDSs predicted to encode the same protein. Two soft constraints are applied to cover most legal cases: A) all CDSs within a group must originate from the same DNA strand; B) the order of CDSs along the strand must match the order of their corresponding novel peptides in the mRNA-derived protein. Constraint A is enforced by dual-index tables to quickly retrieve candidate groups. Constraint B is formulated as a graph coloring problem and solved via an adapted depth-first search (DFS) algorithm. This strategy links candidate CDSs through transcripts to reconstruct complete coding regions while eliminating random matches. Detailed designs of the individual pAnno modules are provided in the Methods section and Additional file 1: Figs. S18 to S24.

Moreover, pAnno supports diverse downstream applications (Fig. 1D). For sORF identification, pAnno uncovered 25% additional results. These findings highlight pAnno’s ability to fully exploit multi-omic data and its broad applicability to any large-scale proteomic searches and non-canonical coding region discovery.

Benchmarking datasets construction and evaluation scheme

We constructed benchmark datasets to evaluate pAnno and other proteogenomic data analysis workflows (Fig. 2). The core idea behind constructing these datasets is to simulate incomplete genome annotations by selectively masking certain annotated protein sequences and their corresponding CDSs, thereby mimicking the scenario where some coding annotations are missing (Fig. 2A). To mitigate the confounding effects caused by varying coverage ranges of mass spectrometry acquisition (an external factor independent of software), we need to determine which annotated proteins are truly covered by the mass spectrometry dataset. Therefore, for each MS/MS dataset, we first perform a search against the corresponding complete reference database to obtain a set of high-confidence baseline peptides filtered by 1% FDR threshold and the proteins supported by these peptides are defined as baseline proteins. Next, we randomly masked 50% of the baseline proteins, hereafter referred to as masked proteins. The corresponding peptides and CDSs (which should be supported by baseline peptides) are referred to as masked peptides and masked CDSs, respectively. The remaining proteins, termed retained proteins, collectively constitute a simulated reference database with incomplete annotation. We evaluated each stage separately: in the customized database generation stage, we focused on the database completeness, particularly its coverage of baseline peptides. In the subsequent database search and CDS inference stages, we assessed the performance in identifying baseline peptides and masked CDSs, aiming to maximize recall without significantly increasing false positives due to the large multi-omics database. As we state, our objective is to establish a fair and controllable benchmarking framework for evaluating pAnno and comparable tools under identical conditions. We have collected proteogenomic datasets, including genomic, transcriptomic (for eukaryotic species), and proteomic data from four species for evaluation. These species include E. coli, S. cerevisiae, H. sapiens, and A. thaliana, all of which have exhaustively annotated genomes, making the likelihood of discovering novel CDSs extremely low. As a result, CDSs reported beyond reference annotations were classified as unconfirmed CDSs in this evaluation. Detailed descriptions of these datasets can be found in Additional file 1: S1. Importantly, although our benchmarking uses well-annotated species, this does not imply that pAnno is limited to such organisms. On the contrary, pAnno is specifically designed to handle species with incomplete or lower-quality genome annotations and to uncover additional potential coding regions in these challenging contexts.

Given the current lack of an all-in-one proteogenomic analysis tool, we systematically evaluated pAnno’s performance across all three modules with several corresponding tools. For customized database generation, pAnno was evaluated against gene prediction-based tools such as Augustus, Balrog, and SpliceAI. For database searching, pAnno was evaluated against several mainstream search engines, including MaxQuant [41], FragPipe [42], Comet [43], and MS-GF+ [44]. For CDS inference, we re-implemented three common strategies and compared them with pAnno. Specifically, Rule 1: mapping all novel peptides to the genome without filtering; Rule 2: building upon Rule 1 and retaining only CDSs supported by unique peptides; and Rule 3: mapping novel peptides to the genome using BLASTp [45], retaining results with e-values less than 1e–3. Finally, we selected the second-best performing approaches from the database search and CDS inference stages and combined them into a hybrid pipeline to compare its overall performance with pAnno in proteogenomic data analysis.

Performance evaluation on the four benchmarking datasets

Customized database generation

In this stage, standardized parameters were applied. For pAnno, species-specific length thresholds were defined to ensure coverage of over 95% of proteins in the reference database. DNA-derived sequences undergo six-frame translation, retaining sequences longer than 50 amino acids (AA) for simple species (E. coli and S. cerevisiae, assumed to represent full-length proteins), and those longer than 10 AA for complex species (H. sapiens and A. thaliana, assumed to represent CDSs for complete protein assembly via splicing). RNA-derived sequences undergo three-frame translation, having already undergone splicing, with a minimum length threshold of 50 AA to represent a complete protein. For Augustus, customized databases were generated using species-specific official models under default parameters. Balrog, a tool designed for prokaryotes, was employed to generate customized databases for the E. coli and S. cerevisiae datasets (both almost without splicing events). SpliceAI, which provides a pre-trained model based on H. sapiens, was utilized to predict splicing sites in H. sapiens DNA sequences, and these predictions were subsequently incorporated into a customized database.

Without loss of generality, we used the baseline peptides identified by Open-pFind for comparison. The customized databases generated by pAnno not only encompass nearly all baseline peptides recovered by other tools but also include a substantial number of unique sequences (Fig. 2B and Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Notably, their coverage of baseline peptides exceeds 99.9% across all evaluated datasets (Additional file 1: Table S4), indicating that pAnno’s upstream module preserves virtually all correct identifications, a key factor in maintaining the integrity of downstream analyses. However, these customized databases are 6.4 to 53.3 times larger in amino acid count than the full reference database (Additional file 1: Table S5), highlighting the necessity for pAnno to manage the challenge of searching such large and imprecise databases.

Database searching

We evaluated pAnno’s database searching module against several state-of-the-art search engines. For each engine, ground-truth benchmarking was established by performing the database search against reference databases, thereby constructing a unique set of baseline peptides for each engine. The customized database was then searched, and peptides identifications matching the baseline peptides set were classified as credible peptides. In contrast, identifications absent from the complete reference database were considered as suspect peptides. Two performance metrics were used: recall rate, calculated as the number of credible peptides divided by the total number of baseline peptides, and suspect rate, calculated as the number of suspect peptides divided by the total number of identifications. All results presented in this section were obtained with a FDR less than 1% at the peptide level. It should be noted that our objective is to evaluate the stability of peptide identification when expanding the search space (from reference databases to customized databases) using the same search engine under identical configurations. Consequently, a uniform post-search filtering strategy was systematically applied to all tools during both the benchmarking and testing phases.

As shown in Fig. 2C, pAnno outperformed other search engines, achieving a superior mean recall of 97.8% on average. Even with the customized databases expanding 53.3-fold (H. sapiens) and 11.6-fold (A. thaliana) over reference databases for complex species (Additional file 1: Table S5), pAnno maintained significantly higher recall rates of 98.5% and 96.0%, respectively, while the corresponding values for the other search engines are 74.3% and 66.3% on average. FragPipe achieves the lowest suspect rate (0.5%) by leveraging Philosopher, which integrates multi-level information for peptide filtering. In contrast, pAnno relies solely on peptide-level features at this stage, resulting in a slightly higher suspect rate (1.1%). Nonetheless, pAnno preserves more valuable results during the database search and effectively filters them in downstream. Notably, pAnno demonstrated consistent improvements over its Open-pFind kernel, particularly in complex species, achieving a 3.1-percentage-point increase in recall and a 0.6-point reduction in the suspect rate (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Moreover, benefiting from the open-search architecture, pAnno identified 77.0% more peptides on average than other restricted-search engines (Additional file 1: Fig. S3), providing richer evidence for downstream biological discovery. Additionally, when processing the customized databases of H. sapiens and A. thaliana, both MS-GF+ and MaxQuant failed to execute successfully using one-click operations. For MS-GF+, we addressed this by employing the official database-splitting scripts. For MaxQuant, we had to reduce the search space by increasing the minimum sequence length in the customized database from 10 to 30 amino acids (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Other engines, including pAnno, maintained stable operational capabilities when handling large-scale customized databases. Overall, pAnno provides a superior solution for searching large and imprecise customized databases, reliably delivering nearly lossless peptide identification information to downstream modules.

We further analyzed the score distributions of peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) from Open-pFind searches against the customized databases of H. sapiens within a FDR of 1% at the peptide level to validate the suspect rate estimation scheme. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S5, credible peptides exhibited significantly superior PSM scores compared to suspect peptides, and the latter performed even worse than decoy peptides–representing random matches. These findings confirm the validity of the suspect rate estimation scheme and, in conjunction with the previously observed lowest suspect rates for pAnno, further highlight the reliability of its results.

CDS inference

We evaluated pAnno’s CDS inference module against the three established rules outlined earlier in the evaluation scheme. To focus solely on the evaluation of CDS inference, we assumed no loss in the upstream process, meaning that all masked peptides were considered identified in the preceding database search and were subsequently used as input for the CDS inference step. A CDS was considered validated if and only if it belonged to the predefined masked CDSs set. The recall rate was calculated as the number of validated CDSs divided by the size of the masked CDSs set, and the suspect rate was calculated as the number of unconfirmed CDSs which were reported beyond reference annotations divided by the number of the total reported CDSs.

As shown in Fig. 2D, pAnno significantly reduces the suspect rate while maintaining high recall in simple species. Rule 1 retains nearly all inferred results but is largely unreliable, with an average suspect rate of 57.2%. In contrast, both Rule 2 and Rule 3 improve reliability, though at the cost of recall. Compared to these established rules, pAnno achieves both higher recall and lower suspect rates across all datasets. The superior performance of pAnno is attributed to its deep neural network-based scoring model, which achieved an AUC of 0.99 in benchmark tests (Additional file 1: Fig. S6), outperforming other filtering rules with an average F1-score improvement of 17.9 percentage points (Additional file 1: Table S7). In complex species, the established rules suffer from severe recall degradation (averaging 70.0%), while pAnno maintains recall above 95%, owing to its ability to capture splicing events (Additional file 1: Table S8). Additionally, the average suspect rate of pAnno was substantially lower (6.4%) than that of the other approaches (38.4%, 18.3%, and 17.0% for Rule 1, 2, and 3, respectively), demonstrating its unique ability to infer CDSs for complex species with both sensitivity and reliability. We further explored the underlying reason for pAnno’s substantial reduction in the suspect rate of CDS inference. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S7, for shared peptides that initially mapped to an average of 3.5 genomic loci, pAnno’s filtering reduced this number to 1.5, demonstrating its effectiveness in resolving ambiguities caused by repetitive genomic regions. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S8, 22.3% and 23.0% of the validated CDSs reported in the H. sapiens and A. thaliana datasets, respectively, are shorter than 30 amino acids (90 nucleotides). Moreover, 36.9% and 19.0% of the CDSs in these datasets are supported by a single peptide. pAnno’s adaptive framework accommodates this biological reality by allowing a minimum translated length of 10 amino acids for complex species, while not discarding all CDSs supported by single peptides. In summary, across all evaluation datasets, pAnno demonstrates significantly superior recall and suspect rate compared to other rules. By systematically identifying previously underexplored short CDSs, pAnno provides an effective new approach for comprehensive proteogenomic discovery in splicing-rich species.

Full process

We constructed a hybrid pipeline by combining the second-best performing tools from the hierarchical evaluation–using FragPipe for database searching and Rule 2 for CDS inference–while retaining pAnno’s customized database. This hybrid pipeline was then compared with pAnno’s full workflow. As shown in Fig. 2E, pAnno consistently outperforms the hybrid pipeline across all datasets and evaluation metrics. For simple species, pAnno achieved a higher average recall (98.2% vs. 97.6%) and a lower suspect rate (2.8% vs. 5.5%). The advantage was even more pronounced in complex species, where pAnno reached an average recall of 95.5%, 22.5 percentage points higher than the hybrid, and a significantly lower suspect rate (7.6% vs. 16.0%). Notably, stages of CDS inference accounted for an average of only approximately 3% of the total runtime in pAnno (Fig. 2F, Table S10), despite encompassing computationally intensive tasks such as multi-omic peptide remapping involving splicing and mutation events. These results underscore pAnno’s significant advantages in proteogenomic analysis of complex multi-omic datasets, delivering high sensitivity, accuracy, and efficiency with minimal computational overhead.

Whole-genome annotation analysis of the Pyrus dataset

Dataset and task

The Pyrus proteogenomic dataset encompass both proteomic and transcriptomic data from 24 major tissues, covering all major organs and developmental stages [5]. The proteomic data include 240 raw files (36,945,141 MS/MS spectra, Additional file 1: Table S1), and the transcriptomic data include 24 RNA-Seq files generated using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. In the original study, 4,294 novel proteins were identified, 2,007 of which remained after removing redundant sequences and those lacking unique peptide support. To assess pAnno’s ability to identify novel gene expression events in practical research scenarios, we reanalyzed the dataset using the same genomic (NCBI: GCF_000315295.1) and reference protein database (Taxonomic ID: 225117, 2015) files as in the original study. The RNA-Seq files were assembled de novo to generate RNA sequences. The customized database was then generated by integrating a six-frame translation of DNA sequences (minimum length: 10 amino acids) and a three-frame translation of RNA sequences (minimum length: 50 amino acids) with the reference database. Detailed parameters used in the database search procedure of pAnno are provided in Additional file 1: Table S11.

Novel peptides identification

To validate the reliability of peptides identified by pAnno, we employed pDeep2 [46], a deep learning-based framework for predicting the theoretical spectra of peptides. The similarity between the predicted and experimental spectra was assessed using the dot product (COS) algorithm, with PCC, SPC, and KDT similarity scores provided in Additional file: S17. Among the 132,680 novel peptides identified by pAnno, 2,696 were also matched to the novel proteins originally reported, exhibiting a high median COS score of 0.98. For the novel peptides uniquely identified by pAnno, the median COS score was 0.97, with over 97% of cases exceeding 0.9. For complex species, pAnno categorizes reported CDSs into five tiers based on the strength of multi-omic evidence. Part 1 includes CDSs supported by genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic evidence that can be assembled into complete protein-coding regions through a multi-stage filtering process. This tier is further subdivided into Part 1-1 and Part 1–2, depending on the presence or absence of a valid initiation codon. Part 2 contains CDSs with genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic support that do not pass the multi-stage filter. Part 3 includes CDSs inferred from peptides identified exclusively through the transcriptome-assembled database, as they cannot be mapped to the genome. Part 4 consists of CDSs derived from genome-mapped peptides without transcriptomic support.

Figure 3B shows the theoretical spectrum similarity of the peptides covered in each parts. Part 1-1 and 1–2 exhibit the highest similarity (median COS = 0.98), outperforming Part 2 (median COS = 0.93), supporting the effectiveness of the multi-stage filtering process in pAnno. Part 4 has a lower median similarity score (0.84), indicating lower confidence for results lacking transcriptomic evidence. Interestingly, Part 3 demonstrates unexpectedly high confidence (median COS = 0.98), likely due to advancements in sequencing depth and MS acquisition, which may allow detection of peptides absent in earlier DNA sequencing. Overall, pAnno demonstrates high reliability in identifying novel peptides in the Pyrus dataset while the tiered classification system effectively prioritizes high-confidence results, improving efficiency by allowing researchers to focus on the most reliable groups first.

Novel proteins identification

As shown in Fig. 3C, pAnno identified 6,923 novel proteins in total, of which 1,729 co-identified with the original study (86.1% coverage). Notably, 89.3% of these co-identified proteins exhibited higher peptide coverage in pAnno, owing to its enhanced database search module designed to extract more comprehensive information from mass spectrometry data (Additional file: S18). Among the 278 novel proteins uniquely identified in the original study, 93.9% were supported by only a single peptide. In contrast, the 5,194 novel proteins uniquely identified by pAnno showed greater reliability, with approximately 70% supported by at least two peptides.

We further examined the novel proteins uniquely identified by pAnno, focusing on 3,263 of them with validated start codons (ATG, GTG, CTG, TTG, AAT or ATA) and supported by at least two peptides. To assess their broader relevance, we performed BLASTp searches against the NCBI database, retaining only the top-scoring match under stringent criteria (e-value < 1e–5, and sequence coverage \ge≥ 70%). Among these, 2,108 proteins (64.6%) exhibited significant homology to known sequences. Taxonomic classification of these homologous matches (Fig. 3D; detailed in Additional file: S19) revealed that the largest proportion (42.8%) aligned with sequences from Pyrus x bretschneideri, specifically the Hongxiangsu cultivar. Notably, the dataset analyzed in this study originates from Dangshansuli, a distinct cultivar within the same species group as Hongxiangsu. The strong phylogenetic relationship among these species underscores the reliability of pAnno’s CDS inference module in inferring novel gene expression events from novel peptides. In summary, pAnno recapitulates 99.0% of the novel proteins supported by multiple peptides in the original study, while additionally identifying over 2,000 novel proteins with homologous evidence in closely related species. These findings highlight pAnno’s extensive discovery capability, enabling the systematic detection of novel gene expression events through multi-omics integration, many of which remain undetectable by conventional methods.

Splicing junctions and SAAVs

pAnno identified 10,466 splice junctions in the Pyrus dataset, of which 4,613 were directly supported by spectral evidence. These junctions were categorized into five groups based on supporting PSM counts, and the theoretical spectrum similarity distributions across all groups (Fig. 4D) exhibited median scores of \ge≥ 97%, strongly validating the reliability of the reported splicing events. Due to the limited depth of the MS data, it is not feasible to achieve full coverage of all splice sites. Nevertheless, pAnno enables confident identification of splice junctions even without direct MS evidence by integrating transcriptomic and genomic sequencing data, along with internal spectral support from non-junction peptides within the same CDS. A complete list of all splice junctions identified by pAnno is provided in Additional file: S20.

pAnno exhibited strong capability in detecting single-amino-acid variants (SAAVs), identifying 3,664 novel CDS candidates with SAAV supported by both proteomic and transcriptomic evidence (Additional file: S22). As illustrated by the novel protein Cb116 (chromosome NW_008988050.1; Fig. 4E), reported in both pAnno and the original study, a missense mutation (GAC→→CAC at 254843–254845 bp) resulted in an aspartate-to-histidine substitution (D→→H). This mutation was confirmed by two mutant-specific peptides supported by 38 PSMs identified. Figure 4F shows a representative spectrum validating this mutation, with fragment ions aligning precisely with the experimental data, achieving 98.8% similarity to the theoretical spectrum. This high-confidence identification highlights pAnno’s proficiency in resolving SAAVs, offering valuable insights for differential gene expression studies.

sORF and micro/missing protein identification

Dataset and task

sORFs often originate from non-coding regions and may encode biologically functional peptides, offering new insights into gene regulation. However, the lack of effective tools for handling large customized databases makes sORF discovery challenging, leaving many undetected. To further evaluate pAnno’s capacity to identify sORFs and address specific search scenarios, e.g., micro and missing protein identification, we reanalyzed the Flagelliforme dataset [47] (36 raw MS files, 3,179,378 MS/MS spectra) and the He-Yeast dataset [48] (24 raw MS files, 372,846 spectra). Discussion

In this study, we proposed pAnno, a comprehensive, end-to-end proteogenomic workflow that integrates customized database generation, database searching, and CDS inference, leveraging genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data to identify previously unannotated events such as novel splicing events and mutations. Its database generation module combines DNA six-frame translation with RNA three-frame translation to maximize coverage of potential protein-coding regions, while its multi-step iterative searching strategy effectively addresses performance degradation in large search spaces. With species-specific filters for CDS inference, pAnno minimizes random mappings in large genomic regions, particularly for complex species. Benchmarking against four large-scale datasets demonstrated pAnno’s superior performance. Compared to existing database generation tools, pAnno achieved 100% peptide coverage and outperformed state-of-the-art search engines and common inference rules in both peptide and CDS reporting, with higher recall rates and lower suspect rates. Additionally, pAnno’s ability to handle expanded search spaces was exemplified in multiple applications, such as Pyrus genome annotation, Flagelliforme sORF identification, and lung cancer noncHLAp discovery. In each case, it consistently identifies more high-confidence novel genes, sORFs, and noncHLAp than the original studies. These results underscore pAnno’s effectiveness in handling large-scale multi-omic data analysis, making it a powerful tool for in-depth biological discovery.

A key advantage of pAnno lies in its specialized design for proteogenomic analysis of complex species, as demonstrated by our reanalysis of the Pyrus dataset. pAnno seamlessly integrates multi-omic data, enabling the systematic identification of splicing and mutation events. This process revealed 4,613 splicing sites and 3,664 mutation sites, all substantiated by mass spectrometry evidence in the Pyrus dataset. Furthermore, pAnno utilizes transcriptomic data to infer CDS mapping groups, which are sets of CDSs predicted to translate into the same protein, thereby facilitating the reconstruction of comprehensive gene coding regions. This methodology led to the recovery of 99.0% of the originally reported proteins supported by multiple peptides, along with the discovery of 2,108 new coding genes, validated via BLASTp against orthologous species. Additionally, pAnno effectively minimizes random matches during CDS mapping group inference and organizes results based on multi-omic evidence, prioritizing high-confidence candidates. In the Pyrus dataset, only 4.9% of the initial results were classified as valid CDS mapping groups. pAnno requires assembled RNA sequences, which can be derived through either reference-guided or de novo transcriptome assembly from RNA reads; here, we used de novo assembly to maximize candidate recovery. These findings underscore the wealth of valuable information often missed by conventional methods in existing multi-omic datasets, and highlight pAnno’s ability to integrate and leverage this information to uncover novel biological insights. Each modules of pAnno can also operate independently. For PTM-rich datasets, users may first perform database searching with specialized tools such as MetaMorpheus [50] and then apply pAnno to infer novel CDS from the identified peptides, highlighting the flexibility of the framework.

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The scoring model for simple species inherits the inherent limitations of deep learning—it can only learn patterns present in the training data. Therefore, we recommend that users prioritize candidates based on the model’s ranking rather than applying a fixed score threshold to remove results. Although pAnno effectively integrates multi-omic data, the accuracy and completeness of novel event discovery are still dependent on the quality and depth of the input datasets. For sORF identification, enrichment is often required due to typically low expression levels, which can hinder successful detection. In terms of mutation detection, pAnno currently supports only mutations occurring during DNA-to-RNA transcription and is limited to single amino acid substitutions, excluding insertions or deletions. For noncHLAp identification, pAnno’s database search module is not optimized for non-specific enzymatic cleavage, potentially leading to occasional false negatives or positives. Consequently, while pAnno shows promising capabilities in multi-omic data analysis, further optimization is necessary to address these specific challenges.

pAnno offers a valuable solution for multi-omic data analysis, including genome annotation, sORF identification, micro and missing protein detection, and noncHLAp identification. It serves as a powerful alternative to conventional methods, enabling the extraction of more meaningful biological insights. Given that pAnno accepts RNA sequences from any source, the continued advancement of long-read sequencing technologies will enable it to report a greater number of valuable and high-confidence findings. Looking ahead, we plan to enhance pAnno by integrating deep learning techniques to better address challenges related to non-specific digestion in noncHLAp identification. We also aim to incorporate a de novo sequencing approach to improve its capacity for mutation detection. Together, these advances will consolidate pAnno’s role as a robust and versatile platform for integrative omics discovery.

Conclusion

Proteogenomics provides a direct experimental route to validate translation products and refine genome annotation by integrating mass spectrometry evidence with transcriptomic and genomic information. However, practical analyses are frequently constrained by rapidly expanded customized databases, which can compromise identification stability, and by the ambiguity that arises when novel peptides map to multiple genomic loci—particularly in splicing-rich eukaryotic genomes. These challenges motivate an end-to-end workflow that preserves sensitivity during large-scale database searching while maintaining reliable reporting of novel coding events during downstream CDS inference.

Here, we developed pAnno as an integrated proteogenomic workflow encompassing customized database construction, database searching, and CDS inference. pAnno combines DNA six-frame translation with RNA three-frame translation to maximize candidate coverage, and employs a two-stage search strategy in which unresolved spectra are used to refine the customized database via DBReducer before performing open search on the reduced space. For downstream localization, pAnno rapidly maps novel peptides to the genome under normal, splicing, and single-amino-acid variant (SAAV) patterns and extends genomic windows to infer candidate CDSs. It further applies species-specific filtering: a deep learning based scoring model for simple species and a multi-stage CDS mapping group strategy for complex species to reduce random multi-locus mappings without trivially discarding biologically plausible short CDSs or single-peptide-supported candidates.

Using a controllable benchmarking scheme that masks a subset of baseline proteins to simulate incomplete annotations, we systematically evaluated pAnno on four well-annotated species and observed consistently high recall with reduced suspect reporting relative to representative tools and rule-based strategies. In multiple reanalyses, pAnno also demonstrated practical utility across diverse tasks, including whole-genome annotation analysis in Pyrus (recovering original findings while reporting additional BLASTp-supported novel events together with splicing and SAAV evidence), sORF and micro/missing protein identification in Flagelliforme and yeast datasets, and non-canonical HLA-bound peptide (noncHLAp) discovery in lung cancer specimens with substantially expanded candidate sets supported by downstream affinity prediction and structural feasibility analyses. Collectively, these results support pAnno as a scalable and reliable workflow for extracting actionable novel coding signals from proteogenomic datasets across a range of biological settings.

Customized database generation

pAnno’s customized database integrates three redundancy-filtered components: six-frame DNA translations, three-frame RNA translations, and the reference database. Nucleotide sequences are translated into amino acid sequences using the standard genetic code table, with DNA sequences undergoing bidirectional six-frame translation (both strands) and RNA sequences subjected to unidirectional three-frame translation. RNA sequences from any assembly source, including de novo assemblies [52], genome-guided assemblies [53], and other transcript sources such as ESTs [54], lncRNAs [55], and ncRNAs [56], can be directly processed by pAnno. To avoid imposing overly strict upstream requirements, pAnno adopts a “funnel-shaped” strategy—starting from a broad pool of candidates and progressively narrowing down to high-confidence results. Therefore, we recommend tools like Trinity [57] or StringTie [58] for de novo assembly of RNA reads to obtain longer RNA sequences. Specifically, we translate nucleotide sequences between stop codons, adding the resulting amino acid sequences to the translation database. Ambiguous ‘N’nucleotides are treated as unknown residues (X), and sequences containing more than 30% X-content are excluded. Users can configure minimum protein lengths (50 aa for simple species and 10 aa for complex species by default). To reduce redundancy, we perform substring-level filtering by eliminating shorter proteins fully embedded within longer ones. For complex species, this reduces the database size by 14–37% (Additional file 1: Table S15). Importantly, pAnno’s open search architecture accounts for all peptide fragments generated after protein digestion, ensuring that peptide identification is unaffected by the removal of substrings, as all potential digestion products are still covered.

For redundancy reduction, pAnno implements a multi-batch algorithm leveraging a protein status table (Additional file 1: Fig. S18) to track three states: undetermined, retained, and discarded. The workflow begins by initializing all proteins as undetermined and proceeds through four phases: (1) Protein sequences are sorted by descending length, ensuring that subsequent entries can only be subsequences of previous ones; (2) Test protein sets are created in batches using sequential processing windows; (3) In each batch, substring analysis is performed where undetermined sequences are marked as discarded if they are fully embedded within the longer batchmates, otherwise they are marked as retained; (4) All downstream proteins marked as undetermined are compared to upstream retained proteins, and if they are subsequences, they are marked as discarded. The process iterates until all proteins are either marked as retained or discarded. To reduce the scope of substring test in step three, pAnno employs the tag indexing method. Specifically, for proteins marked as retained, pAnno constructs a tag index table T. Multi-step database search

pAnno implements a multi-step database search pipeline powered by the Open-pFind kernel (Fig. 1A). In Step 1, pAnno performs a reference-database search using Open-pFind to resolve a portion of the spectra. In Step 2, it addresses the challenges of searching customized databases that contain a large number of synthetic sequences, such as reduced peptide identification sensitivity and excessively long search times. To tackle these issues, the DBReducer is employed to construct a more refined database before the main search. Given the differences in scale and reliability between DNA- and RNA-translated databases, DBReducer is applied separately to each (Additional file 1: Fig. S19). Subsequently, the reduced databases are merged with the reference database to form a refined database for Step 2. Importantly, only the unresolved spectra from Step 1 are used for DBReducer processing, which accelerates the reduction step when handling large customized databases. Using only unresolved spectra rarely leads to the loss of potential novel proteins because (1) the resolved spectra have already been assigned high-confidence matches, and (2) if a peptide corresponding to any PSM is contained within a protein, that protein is retained in the reduced database. In contrast, the full MS/MS dataset is used during the main search in Step 2 to allow the search engine to capture sufficient dataset features and improve peptide identification sensitivity. By default, the FDR threshold is set to 1% at the peptide level for both Step 1 and Step 2, while DBReducer operates with a relaxed FDR threshold of 10% at the peptide level. Peptides identified in Step 2 which not covered by the reference database are classified as novel and are subsequently used in downstream analyses to infer novel gene expression events.

Peptide mapping based tag-indexing

pAnno infers potential regions that may express novel peptides by mapping them back to DNA or RNA sequences, categorizing them into three mutually exclusive scenarios: exact mapping, splice mapping, and mutation mapping (Fig. 1C). Exact mapping refers to novel peptides being complete substrings of DNA-translated proteins. Since RNA-seq data theoretically capture post-splicing information, pAnno identifies splicing events by analyzing peptides derived from RNA-translated proteins. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S20, novel peptides that fail exact mapping proceed to splice mapping, where the analysis is limited to peptides spanning a single splicing junction (one donor and one acceptor site per peptide). We assume that the upstream and downstream regions of the splice junction correspond to two distinct (or identical) DNA ORFs, defined as the DNA sequence between adjacent stop codons, which are theoretically translated into proteins included in the customized database. The algorithm systematically enumerates each potential splice-junction-spanning amino acid (test AA) within the novel peptide. The test AA divides the peptide into N-terminal and C-terminal subsequences, which are independently subjected to exact mapping to identify two corresponding DNA-translated protein groups. Protein pairs from these two groups are then evaluated to determine whether their corresponding DNA sequences can concatenate to form the test AA and whether they adhere to canonical splicing compatibility rules (GT-AG/GC-AG/AT-AC rules [59]). Valid pairs are retained as biologically plausible splicing candidates.

The remaining unmapped peptides are subjected to mutation mapping analysis, with a focus on single-amino-acid substitutions (Additional file 1: Fig. S21). pAnno employs a masking strategy that temporarily obscures candidate mutated amino acids, enabling the remaining sequence to meet exact mapping criteria. Specifically, each peptide undergoes a systematic evaluation of all potential mutation sites through iterative masking. The longest contiguous subsequence without mutations is then mapped to DNA-translated proteins, simultaneously extending matches for peptide segments and corresponding proteins while skipping the mutated amino acids. Valid candidates are retained based on this process. Peptides that cannot be mapped through exact, splice, or mutation strategies are annotated as RNA-specific events, potentially representing multi-junction splicing, complex mutations (such as indels or multiple substitutions), or errors in genome assembly.

The peptide-to-protein alignment protocol utilizes tag-indexing technology to minimize computational overhead, requiring proteins to contain peptide-specific 5-mer tags before proceeding to full-sequence matching. For exact mapping, tags are generated from the N-terminal five amino acids of the peptide. For mutated or spliced peptides, the sequence is split at each potential mutation or splice site, and 5-mer tags are generated separately for the N-terminal and C-terminal subsequences. To ensure valid tag extraction, mutated or spliced peptides are first aligned to RNA-translated proteins and contextually extended to include at least five additional amino acids upstream and downstream of the alignment boundaries.

CDS inference

pAnno designs different CDS inference strategies for species with varying levels of splicing complexity. For simple species with minimal splicing, we assume that a protein is encoded by a single ORF, extending from the start codon to the stop codon. Specifically, mapped peptides are treated as reliable evidence of translation within an ORF, and the search for a start codon is extended upstream from the peptide’s N-terminus. Start codons are prioritized in the following order: ATG > GTG > CTG > TTG > AAT > ATA. If no valid start codon is found, the ORF is annotated with “ZZZ” at the first non-stop codon from the 5’ end. In contrast, for complex species with extensive splicing, the complete protein-coding sequence is composed of multiple CDSs joined through splicing, collectively referred to as a CDS mapping group. In these cases, only the CDS covering the protein’s N-terminus includes the start codon. Thus, for complex species, we only perform start codon inference for the ORF covering the protein’s N-terminus after determining the CDS mapping group.

CDS filter for simple species: deep learning-based scoring model

pAnno designs a deep learning-based scoring model specifically for simple species to assess the confidence of inferred CDSs and filter out unreliable results (Fig. 1D). This model is a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that processes six biologically relevant features: (1) CDS length; (2) start codon type; (3) minimum peptide q-value (from database searching); (4) novel peptide count; (5) amino acid coverage; and (6) unique peptide presence. The network consists of an input layer (6 neurons), two hidden layers (32→→8 neurons) with ReLU activation and dropout regularization (p=0.2), and a sigmoid output neuron that generates probability scores (ranging from 0 to 1). Trained as a binary classifier using cross-entropy loss and Adam optimization (learning rate=0.001, batch size=32, 100 epochs).

To ensure robust scoring performance, the training samples were carefully constructed to simulate both biologically valid and erroneous CDSs. Specifically, the training samples were constructed from peptides identified in Step 1 of pAnno’s database search module, which against the reference database (Fig. 1D). Since published genome annotations are typically well-validated, we mapped the target peptides identified in Step 1 back to the genome. If the inferred CDSs aligned with the published genome annotations, they were classified as positive samples; otherwise, they were considered negative samples, simulating cases where correct peptides are mapped to incorrect genomic loci. Negative samples also include CDSs inferred from decoy peptides, representing cases of incorrect peptide identification. Feature vectors were normalized using z-score standardization, followed by downsampling to maintain a balanced positive-to-negative ratio (1:1). The study employed five-fold cross-validation, splitting the dataset into five equal parts for iterative training (using four subsets) and validation (using one subset). The final performance metrics were derived from the average results of all iterations. This dynamic, data-specific training protocol enhanced biological representativeness through adaptive sample construction. Given the model’s compact architecture (total parameters: 497), training was efficiently completed on consumer-grade hardware. Detailed configuration guidelines are available at: https://github.com/Wang-kaifei/pAnno_pub.

CDS filter for complex species: multi-stage CDS mapping group-based filtering

For complex species with extensive splicing, the scoring model used for simple species is not suitable, as it relies on features extracted from complete protein expression sequences. However, in complex species, a single CDS represents only a fragment of the full protein and lacks these features. To address this, pAnno introduces the concept of CDS mapping group, which refers to a set of CDSs from the same DNA strand that are inferred to collectively encode a full-length protein through splicing. Since splicing occurs during the conversion of pre-mRNA to mRNA, the full-length protein expression theoretically corresponds to the mRNA sequence. This means that the protein sequence derived from the three-frame translation of mRNA (referred to as the mRNA protein) contains all the splicing information. In theory, each mRNA protein corresponds to at least one CDS mapping group, allowing us to use mRNA information to infer CDS mapping groups and, consequently, filter the reported CDSs.

The CDS mapping group inference protocol consists of three stages (Additional file 1: Fig. S22A): (1) All novel peptides are mapped back to the DNA–translated protein database (including exact, splicing, and mutation mappings) to identify candidate CDSs; (2) All novel peptides are then mapped back to the RNA-translated protein database using the exact mapping rule. RNA-translated proteins that cover \ge≥2 mapped peptides, with \ge≥1 unique peptide, are considered credible mRNA proteins; (3) The peptides mapped to the credible mRNA proteins are treated as anchor peptides. Anchor peptide–guided filtering is then applied, where candidate CDSs from DNA that cover at least one anchor peptide are selected, and it is determined whether they form a credible CDS mapping group. A credible CDS mapping group must meet two conditions: Condition 1: All CDSs in the group must originate from the same DNA strand; Condition 2: The order of the CDSs on the DNA strand must align with the sequence of corresponding amino acids in the mRNA protein. Examples of these two conditions are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S22B, C.

Specifically, we designed a multi-stage CDS mapping group-based filter to ensure both conditions are met (Additional file 1: Fig. S22D). In the first stage, a default threshold of 60% is applied, meaning that to be classified as a credible CDS mapping group, it must cover at least 60% of the anchor peptides while also satisfying Conditions 1 and 2. If credible mRNA proteins fail to find a credible CDS mapping group in the first stage, they proceed to the second stage, where the threshold is relaxed to 5%. This adjustment accounts for the potential loss of stop codons in de novo RNA-seq assembly results. In such cases, long mRNA proteins may cover many anchor peptides, which might actually originate from different proteins. This may make it challenging to find CDS mapping group candidates that meet Condition 1 for these mRNA proteins, which led us to relax the threshold to address the issue.

Condition 1 was enforced through dual groups of indexing tables (Additional file 1: Fig. S23). The first group contains an index table of mRNA protein mapping results, where each entry consists of a key-value pair: the mRNA protein ID (key) and the Gödel-number-encoded peptides mapped to this mRNA protein (value). Condition 2 was computationally modeled as an NP-hard graph coloring problem and addressed using a depth-first search (DFS) approximation algorithm with heuristic optimizations (Additional file 1: Fig. S24). For mRNA proteins with X mapped peptides and Y Condition 1-compliant CDS candidates, a bipartite grid (X × YX×Y) was constructed. The rows correspond to the peptides mapped to the mRNA protein, arranged vertically from the N-terminal to the C-terminal positions, while the columns represent CDS candidates ordered horizontally along the DNA strand in the 5’-to-3’ orientation. If the i-th peptide can be mapped back to the j-th CDS candidate, the corresponding cell in the i-th row and j-th column is marked as hollow; otherwise, it is blocked. The goal is to find a coloring scheme where each row has at least one hollow cell colored, and the column number of the colored cell in the (i-1)(i-1)-th row is not greater than that in the i-th row. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S24, gray and green cells represent blocked and hollow cells, respectively, while cells marked with √√ and *∗ indicate two valid coloring schemes that satisfy Condition 2. In other words, by selecting the CDS corresponding to all columns marked with √√ or *∗, a Condition 2-compliant credible CDS mapping group can be formed. In the implementation, we allow a certain threshold of peptides not to be covered by the CDS mapping group, meaning that a number of rows can be skipped in the graph coloring task. Confidence-based reporting classification

pAnno classifies CDS inference results based on confidence levels to facilitate analytical prioritization. For simple species, CDSs with score >0.5>0.5 from the deep learning-based scoring model are classified as high-confidence, while others are flagged as suspicious. For complex species, four mutually exclusive categories are defined: Part 1 includes CDSs within validated DNA mapping groups, supported by tri-omic evidence (DNA-RNA-protein) and confirmed by sibling CDSs (evidence of forming complete protein-coding regions through splicing). Part 2 contains CDSs with tri-omic support but failing the multi-stage CDS mapping group inference filter. Part 3 consists of RNA-exclusive CDSs lacking DNA concordance, potentially due to multi-junction splicing or nucleotide insertions/deletions mutations not accounted for by pAnno. Part 4 includes CDSs with novel peptides but lacking RNA evidence, likely due to RNA sequencing data loss or undetected short-lived RNAs. It is important to note that results from higher parts are not without value, but pAnno aims to optimize downstream analysis convenience. Users are encouraged to prioritize higher-confidence results for validation.

Multi-level parallel computation acceleration

pAnno’s peptide mapping process is computationally intensive, and we have designed parallel acceleration strategies based on data characteristics. Tag-indexing technology strikes a balance between time and memory usage. Storing all possible tag indexes for every protein in memory is not feasible for large-scale six-frame translation databases of complex species (Additional file 1: Table S16), as it would lead to excessive memory consumption and inefficient CPU utilization. To mitigate this issue, we adopted a batch processing approach, constructing only the necessary tag indexes for the current analysis. For exact and mutation mapping, the basic unit of analysis is a single translated protein from an individual ORF. For splice mapping, however, amino acid sequences from two different ORFs must be considered simultaneously. Since splicing occurs during the conversion of pre-mRNA to mRNA, these two ORFs must be located on the same DNA strand. As a result, the smallest unit of analysis for splice mapping is a single DNA strand. Unlike in the customized database generation module, we temporarily translate each DNA strand in three frames and focus solely on these proteins for mapping.

The computational hotspots for short and long DNA chains differ: for short chains, the primary bottleneck is translation and index construction, while for long chains, peptide mapping becomes the primary bottleneck. To address these differences, we developed a multi-level parallelism strategy that efficiently allocates computational resources. For DNA chains shorter than 1,000 bases, we implement parallelism at the DNA chain level, performing translations and tag index constructions for multiple short chains simultaneously. However, for each individual chain, peptide mapping is executed sequentially, one at a time. For longer chains, pAnno parallelizes at the peptide level, conducting translations and tag index constructions serially while allowing multiple peptides to be mapped concurrently.

Database searching parameters

For the evaluation datasets, we used consistent parameters across all searches whenever possible. For all search engines, we employed Trypsin digestion, allowed up to two missed cleavage sites. We considered methionine oxidation, N-terminal glutamine-to-pyroglutamic acid conversion and protein N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications, with cysteine carbamidomethylation as fixed modifications. Both precursor ion and fragment ion tolerances were set to 20 ppm. A maximum of three modifications per peptide was allowed. And a 1% FDR threshold was applied at the peptide level. The peptide length range considered for searches was between 6 and 100 amino acids. For the evaluation, we pre-processed customized databases using Trypsin digestion to simulate peptide generation, and then quantified the number of peptides of varying lengths. We limited the peptide length to ensure that the probability of finding a peptide of that exact length in the customized database did not exceed 5%. Specifically, for E. coli and S. cerevisiae, peptides longer than 6 amino acids were considered; for H. sapiens, peptides longer than 8 amino acids were considered; and for A. thaliana, peptides longer than 7 amino acids were considered (Additional file 1: Table S2). For this study, the following versions were used for evaluation: Open-pFind 3.2.2, MaxQuant 2.4.9.0, MS-GF+ 20230112, Comet 2023.1.2, and FragPipe 21.0 (with MSFragger 4.0, philosopher 5.1 and MSBooster). For the application datasets, we maintained the search parameters outlined in the original studies, as detailed in Additional file 1: Tables S11, S12, S13 and S14.
