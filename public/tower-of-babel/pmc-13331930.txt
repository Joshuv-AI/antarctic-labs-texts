Analysis of total and free amino acid compositions of fishery byproducts from three commonly consumed fish species in South Korea
Authors: Jeonghoon Han, Ji-Yeon Hyeon, Yeon-Ju Lee, Han-Jun Kim, Eunseong Lee, Young-Ung Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Largehead hairtail (Trichiurus japonicus), kwangtung skate (Dipturus kwangtungensis), and mottled skate (Raja pulchra) are the most popular aquatic products in Korea. In the present study, total and free amino acid analyses were performed to confirm the usability of byproducts from largehead hairtail (head and fins) and skates (skin and liver). Amino acid contents were determined using specific methods and high-performance liquid chromatography. Notably, 18 types of total amino acids and 19 free amino acids were identified in the byproducts of largehead hairtail, kwangtung skate, and mottled skate. Among the amino acids, the total amino acids glycine and glutamate/glutamine and the free amino acids leucine, alanine, and glycine exhibited the highest contents in the byproducts of the three species, indicating that the byproducts contained high-quality proteins. Conclusively, these results suggest that the byproducts are potential materials for producing nutritious foods, protein supplements, cosmetics, functional materials, and nutraceuticals. Overall, these data indicate that fishery byproducts can be used as recyclable resources.

Introduction

Marine fishery and aquaculture play important roles in meeting the food requirements of the growing global population. In particular, aquaculture is growing rapidly and accounts for a significant portion of fishery consumption, contributing to food security and nutrition (Smith et al. 2010; Filipski and Belton 2018; Bennett et al. 2021; Golden et al. 2021). Notably, marine capture fisheries and aquaculture production reached approximately 223.2 million tons (Mt) in 2022, a 4.3% increase from the previous record in 2020 (FAO 2024). However, fishery waste accounts for approximately 35% of the world’s fishery production. Considering that Korea is one of the leading countries in the world with respect to aquaculture, with a consistent increase in fish consumption, the amount of waste generated has also increased (Nawaz et al. 2020; Ozogul et al. 2021; Shaw et al. 2022; Roy et al. 2023). Importantly, these byproducts are primarily generated by industrial-scale fishing and aquaculture production processes. Most fish muscle tissues are used as a direct food source for human consumption, leaving a large number of byproducts, including heads, viscera, swim bladders, scales, fins, and skin (Chandra and Shamasundar 2015; Al Khawli et al. 2020; FAO 2020).

In the past, byproducts were often used as secondary materials in animal feed, silage, fertilizer, and leather industries (Ozogul et al. 2021). Recently, fishery byproduct recycling has attracted attention for its environmental and economic benefits. Fishery byproducts are sources of bioactive compounds, such as amino acids, proteins, peptides, polysaccharides, vitamins, minerals, enzymes, and essential nutrients, and can be utilized in the food industry, pharmaceuticals, therapeutics, and cosmetics (Abhari and Khaneghah 2020; FAO 2020; Ashraf et al. 2020; Stevens et al. 2018; Nghia 2020; Venkatesan et al. 2017). Therefore, research has been conducted on more efficient methods to extract, process, and utilize bioactive materials from fishery byproducts (Mkadem and Kaanae 2024). For example, collagen and gelatin, with beneficial physicochemical properties, have been extracted and manufactured from yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) abdominal fish skin (Yoo et al. 2008). Additionally, studies have been conducted on the application of collagen derived from fish skin as a functional material for whitening and moisturizing human skin (Wood et al. 2008; Hayashi et al. 2011; Hou et al. 2011, 2012). Although these fishery byproducts possess potential applications in various fields and industries, there is limited information on their chemical composition, including their amino acid profiles.

The largehead hairtail (Trichiurus japonicus) is a commercially important marine fish belonging to the order Perciformes and family Trichiuridae, and its fishing grounds are mainly located in the East China Sea, the west and south coasts of South Korea, and the coastal waters of Jeju Island (Kim et al. 2011; Zhang 1996). Notably, the largehead hairtail is one of the most preferred and consumed aquatic products in South Korea (Nam and Cho 2018) and is a useful resource in various industries due to its high catch volume (approximately 1.28 million tons in 2016) globally (Luan et al. 2017; FAO 2018). Furthermore, the kwangtung skate (Dipturus kwangtungensis) and the mottled skate (Raja pulchra) are large fish belonging to the Rajidae family (Ebert and Compagno 2007) and are widely distributed in East China and South Korea (Jeong 1999; Ishiyama 1967; Hou 2002). These species are cartilaginous fish and are commonly consumed as favorite foods in the relevant regions (Kwangtung skate, southern coast; mottled skate, western coast) and have high industrial utilization. However, there is limited information on the functional components, especially the amino acid compositions, of the byproducts (heads, skins, fins, and liver) from these three consumed fish species in South Korea.

In this study, we investigated the amino acid compositions of byproducts (heads, skins, fins, and livers) from the three most commonly consumed fish species in South Korea to obtain basic data and facilitate their utilization.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Specimens of largehead hairtail were obtained from a local fish market in Busan, South Korea, on April 24, 2024. Specimens of two commercial skates (Dipturus kwangtungensis and Raja pulchra) were procured from local fish markets in Busan and Mokpo, South Korea, on May 24 and September 22, 2023, respectively. Additionally, samples were collected from largehead hairtail (head and fin) and the two skates (skin and liver) (Fig. 1). Thereafter, the samples from each part (weight = 500 g each) were blended separately and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Total amino acid analysis

Briefly, the total amino acid contents (excluding tryptophan and cystine) of the samples were determined using the AOAC (2010) method. Specifically, 0.1 g of each sample was weighed in a hydrolysis test tube and treated with 10 mL of 6 N hydrochloric acid (HCl) containing 0.05% 2-mercaptoethanol. After hydrolysis in a sealed test tube at 110 ± 1 °C for 24 h, HCl was removed under reduced pressure. Thereafter, the sample was dissolved in 0.2 N lithium citrate buffer (pH 2.2) and filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane filter for quantitative analysis using an amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi Global Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The contents of 16 amino acids (tyrosine, glycine, serine, alanine, glutamate/glutamine, lysine, leucine, methionine, valine, arginine, aspartic acid/asparagine, isoleucine, threonine, phenylalanine, proline, and histidine) were calculated based on the Thermo Scientifc™ Pierce™ Amino Acid Standard H (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, New Jersey, USA). Considering that tryptophan is destroyed during acid hydrolysis, it was analyzed using an alkaline hydrolysis method (Lourenço et al. 2004; MFDS 2018). For tryptophan analysis, 0.1 g of each sample was weighed into a hydrolysis test tube and treated with 100 mg of soluble starch and 3 mL of 4.2 N sodium hydroxide solution. After hydrolysis in a sealed test tube at 135 ± 1 ℃ for 22 h, each sample was neutralized with 6 N HCl, followed by the addition of 0.2 N lithium citrate buffer (pH 4.25). Finally, the tryptophan content was determined using an amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi Global Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The tryptophan content was calculated based on an L-tryptophan standard (CAS No. 73-22-3, Sigma-Aldrich).

Cystine content was determined using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system after o-phthalaldehyde (OPA)/9-fluorenylmethyl chloroformate (FMOC) precolumn derivatization. Briefly, 0.1 g of each sample was treated with 10 mL of formic acid and kept in a freezer at 0 ℃ for 25 h. Thereafter, the samples were weighed into a hydrolysis test tube and treated with 10 mL of 6 N HCl containing 0.05% 2-mercaptoethanol. After hydrolysis at 110 ± 1 °C for 18 h, the samples were filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane filter for cystine determination using HPLC. The cystine content was calculated based on the L-cysteine (CAS RN®: 56-89-3, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). The analytical conditions are listed in Table 2.

Eighteen amino acids (tyrosine, glycine, serine, alanine, glutamate/glutamine, lysine, leucine, methionine, valine, arginine, aspartic acid/asparagine, isoleucine, threonine, phenylalanine, proline, histidine, cystine, and tryptophan) were detected and expressed as mg/g dry weight. A calibration curve was constructed using standard amino acid solutions of varying concentrations.

Free amino acid analysis

For free amino acid analysis, 2 g of each sample was extracted with 20 mL of 95% ethanol at 30 °C for 1 h under constant rotation at 130 rpm. After removing the distilled water, the sample was reconstituted with 10 mL of lithium citrate buffer (0.12 N, pH 2.2), followed by the addition of 0.2 g of 5-sulfosalicylic acid. Thereafter, the sample was left to stand at 4 °C for 1 h, filtered through a 0.2-µm membrane filter, and diluted with lithium citrate buffer (0.12 N, pH 2.2). Finally, the filtered samples were quantitatively analyzed using an amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi Global Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). A calibration curve was constructed using standard amino acid solutions of varying concentrations.

Results

Total amino acid composition

In this study, we examined the total and free amino acid contents and compositions of byproducts from three commonly consumed fish species (largehead hairtail, kwangtung skate, and mottled skate) in South Korea (Tables 3 and 4). Specifically, we examined the levels of 18 amino acids, including 9 essential amino acids (histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine) and 9 non-essential amino acids (alanine, arginine, aspartic acid/asparagine, cystine, glutamate/glutamine, glycine, serine, tyrosine, and proline), in the byproducts of largehead hairtail, kwangtung skate, and mottled skate. Our findings showed that the total amino acid contents of the head and fin of the largehead hairtail were 145.19 mg/g and 94.67 mg/g, respectively. In addition, the total amino acid contents of the skin and liver of kwangtung skate and mottled skate were 130.29 mg/g and 70.53 mg/g, respectively, and 180.13 mg/g and 73.59 mg/g, respectively. Among the byproducts examined, the skin of mottled skate had the highest amino acid content. Glutamate/glutamine (20.08 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of amino acids in the head of largehead hairtail, followed by glycine (16.44 mg/g), aspartic acid/asparagine (13.74 mg/g), and arginine (10.36 mg/g). In addition, glycine (17.29 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of amino acids in the fin of largehead hairtail, followed by glutamate/glutamine (11.09 mg/g), alanine (8.63 mg/g), and aspartic acid/asparagine (7.57 mg/g). Among the amino acids detected in the skin of kwangtung skate, glycine (19.79 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion, followed by glutamate/glutamine (17.13 mg/g), aspartic acid/asparagine (10.27 mg/g), and arginine (10.27 mg/g). Additionally, glutamate/glutamine (9.63 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of amino acids in the liver, followed by aspartic acid/asparagine (7.12 mg/g), and leucine (6.45 mg/g), and lysine (5.67 mg/g). Moreover, glycine (34.85 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of amino acids in the skin of mottled skate, followed by glutamate/glutamine (21.68 mg/g), arginine (14.80 mg/g), and aspartic acid/asparagine (13.02 mg/g). Furthermore, glutamate/glutamine (9.69 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of major amino acids in the liver of mottled skate, followed by aspartic acid/asparagine (7.19 mg/g), leucine (6.77 mg/g), and lysine (5.94 mg/g).

Free amino acid composition

In the present study, the total free amino acid contents of the head and fin of largehead hairtail were 3.78 mg/g and 2.29 mg/g, respectively. In addition, the total free amino acid contents of the skin and liver of kwangtung skate and mottled skate were 1.09 mg/g and 9.74 mg/g, respectively, and 1.09 mg/g and 12.86 mg/g, respectively. Notably, leucine (0.52 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of free amino acids in the head of largehead hairtail head, followed by alanine (0.46 mg/g) and glutamate/glutamine (0.43 mg/g). In addition, alanine (0.30 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of amino acids in the fin, followed by leucine (0.24 mg/g) and glutamate/glutamine (0.20 mg/g). Moreover, alanine (0.19 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of free amino acids in the skin of kwangtung skate, followed by glycine (0.11 mg/g) and threonine (0.11 mg/g). Furthermore, glutamate/glutamine (1.38 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of free amino acids in the liver of kwangtung skate, followed by leucine (1.37 mg/g) and alanine (0.86 mg/g). Among the free amino acids in the skin of the mottled skate, alanine (0.41 mg/g) was the highest, followed by glycine (0.25 mg/g) and tryptophan (0.09 mg/g). Finally, glutamate/glutamine (1.82 mg/g) accounted for the highest proportion of free amino acids in the liver of mottled skate, followed by leucine (1.62 mg/g) and alanine (1.48 mg/g).

Discussion

Fish are an important source of dietary amino acids for humans to obtain and maintain adequate protein nutrition and health (Ghaly et al. 2013). Fish have a high protein content and a balanced ratio of all amino acids with respect to human protein requirements (Ozden and Erkan 2008; Kim et al. 2020). Therefore, byproducts from fish processing are considered potential sources of protein and amino acids for humans.

In this study, glycine and glutamate/glutamine were the predominant amino acids in the byproducts. In general, fish skin is mainly composed of high molecular weight elastic proteins and contains high concentrations of collagen, a connective tissue protein (Jayathilakan et al. 2012). In this context, glycine is a foundational amino acid for collagen, has a neurotransmitter function in the central nervous system, and plays an important role in regulating homeostasis in the body and improving immune function (Razak et al. 2017). In addition, glycine, combined with other amino acids such as proline, alanine, arginine, serine, isoleucine, and phenylalanine, is essential for tissue healing and regeneration, primarily through the formation of collagen and other structural proteins. These amino acids form peptides that function as messengers or structural building blocks to promote cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and wound closure (Witte et al. 2002). Recently, collagen has been used in various fields such as medicine, cosmetics, and food, and its applications have expanded into anti-skin aging, skin elasticity improvement, and arthritis prevention (Al-Atif 2022). For example, collagen was manufactured using yellowfin tuna abdominal skin (Yoo et al. 2008). In addition, UV-protective and whitening effects of squid skin collagen have been studied (Kwon et al. 2008). Furthermore, analyses of amino acid components of fish byproducts have been conducted, particularly in fish skin. A previous study reported that seabass skin had a relatively high glycine content of 11.4 g/100 g, followed by red sea bream (11.1 g/100 g), olive flounder (10.9 g/100 g), and black rockfish (10.4 g/100 g) (Cho et al. 2014). Glycine has been reported to be an abundant amino acid in collagen extracted from squid and Alaska pollack skin (Yang and Hong 2012). In addition, among the byproducts of sea bream, the skin had the highest glutamic acid (25.41 mg/g), followed by aspartic acid (16.84 mg/g) and glycine (15.44 mg/g) (Pateiro et al. 2020). Therefore, we suggest that the byproducts, including the skin, of three fish species used in this study can be used for collagen production. Additionally, in sea bream, byproducts had a high glutamic acid content (10.01–25.41 mg/g of tissue; around 14% of TAAs), with the fillets and skin, viscera and liver, and gills and head having average glutamic contents of approximately 2500 mg/100 g, 1000 mg/100 g, and 1500 mg/100 g, respectively (Pateiro et al. 2020). Glutamic acid is involved in transamination and is essential for the synthesis of key molecules such as glutathione, which is essential for the removal of highly toxic products (Mohanty et al. 2014). In addition to its important clinical role, glutamic acid also performs important sensory functions (Baryłko-Pikielna and Kostyra 2007). For example, glutamic acid is a brain neurotransmitter that plays an important role in cognitive functions such as memory and learning. Although the glycine and glutamic acid contents in the fish skins examined in this study were lower than those in previous studies, glycine and glutamic acid were found to be high throughout the fish skin, as well as in other byproducts. Therefore, glycine and glutamic acid, which were present at high levels in the byproducts of the three fish species examined, possess potential application as functional materials.

Our study showed that except for the essential amino acids lysine, leucine, and valine, which were present at high levels in the byproducts of the three fish species, most essential amino acids were present at relatively low levels. In a previous study, lysine was found to be the most abundant essential amino acid in grass carp, catfish, and pacu (Lise et al. 2021). Additionally, the lysine contents of the three species were higher than those recommended by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (Lise et al. 2021). Lysine is an essential amino acid in all mammals and is required for protein synthesis (Ball et al. 2007; Tomé and Bos 2007). In addition, lysine is a precursor for carnitine biosynthesis, plays an important role in lipid β-oxidation (Tomé and Bos 2007), and is involved in regulating mitochondrial energy metabolism and alleviating obesity and the early stages of diabetes (Fukushima and Lopaschuk 2016). Moreover, lysine is involved in collagen synthesis and prevents skin aging (Zhang et al. 2007). Considering that lysine is an essential amino acid that cannot be biosynthesized in the body, it can be used as a raw material for health-promoting functional foods. Generally, fish byproducts and their processed counterparts, such as fishmeal, are considered premium raw materials and contain the highest levels of lysine among animal-based protein sources. Compared with plant-based protein sources, they possess significant advantages in lysine content and quality. Indeed, typical fishmeal contains 60–72% crude protein and is very rich in lysine, providing high lysine availability, typically at levels of 5–7.5 g/100 g protein (Hua 2013; Hussain et al. 2024). Collectively, these results suggest that the byproducts of largehead hairtail, kwangtung skate, and mottled skate possess potential applications as functional materials for nutraceuticals due to their high lysine content.

For free amino acid composition, these results showed that the major free amino acids in the byproducts were leucine, alanine, and glycine. In particular, alanine is known to have a sweet taste and is commonly used as a natural food additive (Shallenberger 1993). Regarding essential amino acids, leucine and phenylalanine contents were high in the head of largehead hairtail, the liver of kwangtung skate, and the liver of mottled skate. However, the contents of most essential amino acids were low in the byproducts of the three fish species. In general, leucine is a critical essential branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) that acts as a potent nutrient signal to stimulate muscle protein synthesis (MPS) by activating the mTORC1 signaling pathway. It is widely used across the food, nutraceutical, and cosmetic industries because to its roles in protein synthesis, flavor enhancement, and skin conditioning (D’Hulst et al. 2022; Rehman et al. 2023). For phenylalanine, a previous study showed that its content was high in the byproducts (heads, shells, and tails) of two shrimp species (northern pink and spotted shrimp) (Heu et al. 2003). Phenylalanine is a precursor of tyrosine and is involved in the formation of the hormone thyroxine. Considering that tyrosine is a precursor of skin and hair pigments (Fitri 2012), these byproducts can be utilized as functional materials for hair and skin products. Our study showed that the byproducts of largehead hairtail, kwangtung skate, and mottled skate were high in alanine, leucine, and phenylalanine, suggesting that they can be utilized as food additives, nutraceuticals, and cosmetic materials. Additionally, a relatively high threonine content was observed in the livers of kwangtung skates and mottled skates. Overall, the high threonine content suggests that these material may be effective for treating central nervous system diseases (Mohanty et al. 2014) and as functional materials for nutraceuticals.

Conclusively, fishery byproducts, such as heads, shells, fins, and entrails, possess potential application as valuable materials for functional foods, nutraceuticals, and cosmetics. This study contributes to our knowledge of the amino acid profiles of fishery byproducts and provides useful information on their potential applications and economic value.
