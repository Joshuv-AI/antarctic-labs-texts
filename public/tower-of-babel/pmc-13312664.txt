Spiritual needs in palliative care inpatients: a four-year analysis of clinical data from a tertiary care center
Authors: Valentin Frei, Simon Peng-Keller, Caroline Hertler, Stefan Pelz, David Blum, Markus Schettle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpiritual needs are a core dimension of palliative care, yet their systematic documentation remains limited. This study explores the spiritual needs expressed by palliative care inpatients and examines how these needs vary in documentation based on gender, below and above the Swiss retirement age, religious affiliation, and specialty documenting them.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 747 electronic health records (EHRs) from the Acute Palliative Care Unit at the University Hospital Zurich (2019–2022). Documentation by psychological and pastoral care services was analyzed using qualitative content analysis along with chi-square test for explorative purposes. Identified needs were categorized according to an adapted four-domain framework (existential, psychological, social/relational, religious).ResultsA total of 598 patients were analyzed (median age 68 years (20–97), 52.8% (n = 316) male). We identified 22 distinct spiritual needs. Overall, 61.2% (n = 366) of patients expressed existential needs, 78.9% (n = 472) psychological needs, 66.2% 396) social/relational needs, and 17.4% (n = 104) religious needs. The most common spiritual needs were life reflection (55.5%, n = 332), relationship and family dynamics (43.6%, n = 337), and history of illness (26.8%, n = 160). Women reported significantly more existential fears (p < 0.001), relational themes (p = 0.005), and requests for prayer (p = 0.028). Patients below the retirement age exhibited more needs, including fears (p = 0.019), grief (p = 0.002), and hope (p < 0.001) compared to older patients. Religious affiliation was associated with prayer (p = 0.003) and anointing of the sick (p = 0.036). Psychological services documented spiritual needs in 86.1% of cases (mainly existential and psychological needs), whereas pastoral care (53%) focused on religious themes, including all mentions of sacramental rituals.ConclusionsThis is the first Swiss study to classify spiritual needs based on routine clinical documentation. The findings highlight the prevalence and multidimensionality of spiritual concerns, with psychological needs being most frequently documented, and significant differences observed by age, gender, and religious affiliation. Distinct documentation patterns emerged between psychological and pastoral care, underlining the need for structured assessment tools and interprofessional collaboration. Implementing standardized documentation and training could enhance the visibility and continuity of spiritual care and guide future research and clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02117-w.Keywords: Palliative care, Spiritual care, Spiritual needs, Holistic care

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines palliative care as an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families through the prevention and relief of suffering, including addressing spiritual aspects of care [1]. Multiple guidelines recommend that spiritual care be integrated into routine interprofessional palliative care practice, with trained professionals such as chaplains, psychologists, and other team members contributing to the assessment and response to spiritual needs [1–3].

In this study we use the definition of spirituality put forth by European Association for Palliative Care as adopted in the Swiss Spiritual Care Guidelines [2, 4]. The EAPC defines spirituality as “a dynamic dimension of human life that relates to the way humans experience, express and/or seek meaning, purpose and transcendence, and the way they connect to the moment, to self, to others, to nature, to the significant and/or the sacred” [4]. According to this definition religion and spirituality are linked but not interchangeable. Religion can be understood as a structured system of beliefs, practices, and rituals that connects individuals or communities to the transcendent or sacred, often within an institutional framework. Spirituality tends to be a broader concept, not necessarily bound by institutions and varies between individuals. As Europe, and Switzerland in particular, become increasingly secular, it is important to keep this in mind [5].

In this study, we understand spiritual needs in line with this broad EAPC framework as concerns related to meaning, purpose, identity, connectedness and transcendence, which may be expressed through existential, psychological, social/relational and religious themes. In routine documentation spiritual concerns are often recorded in psychological or psychosocial terms and are rarely labelled explicitly as “spiritual”. We therefore deliberately included documentation by both psychological and pastoral care services and operationalized spiritual needs as those documented concerns that touched on ultimate questions of meaning, personhood, relationships, or the transcendent. We acknowledge that not all psychological or social problems are spiritual in nature, however given the realities of routine documentation practices we recognized an unavoidable overlap between spiritual and psychosocial needs. We therefore regarded spiritual and psychological dimensions as closely intertwined. At the same time, we acknowledge that not all psychological problems (e.g. symptoms of anxiety or low mood without reference to meaning, identity or connectedness) necessarily represent spiritual needs. In Switzerland spiritual care is an interprofessional responsibility, however documentation of such needs is often undertaken by both chaplains and psychologists. These needs can be documented under different professional headings and lack a standardized documentation tool.

Spiritual distress may arise in patients with terminal illness and does not necessarily require to have a set of theistic beliefs as a basis [6–9]. Spiritual care is a fundamental dimension of palliative care. It contributes significantly to patients’ overall well-being, coping mechanisms, and quality of life during advanced illness [2, 6–11]. Being aware of and incorporating patients’ spiritual needs into physicians’ practice is an opportunity to deepen the patient doctor relationship and a possibility to establish goals of care. Many patients wish to discuss spiritual needs with their physicians [6, 8, 12–14]. However, it is not often explicitly stated but rather indirectly in the subtext of a conversation [15]. This highlights the importance of systematic spiritual assessment and documentation. Otherwise, this aspect of care is at risk to be neglected in the hectic work environment that is health care.

Despite international consensus on the importance of spiritual care, the implementation of structured spiritual assessment or documentation in clinical settings remains inconsistent [6, 8, 12, 16, 17]. Spiritual care is formally included in Swiss national palliative care strategies, yet its systematic implementation at the clinical level remains a work in progress. While chaplains and psychologists routinely engage with patients, there is a lack of standardized tools for documenting spiritual concerns, which limits the visibility and continuity of such care [17]. Addressing this is essential. Numerous studies have demonstrated that spiritual care interventions improve quality of life, increase patient satisfaction, lead to less aggressive treatment at the end of life and even reduce symptom burden in palliative settings [8, 11, 14, 18, 19]. This limited visibility in documentation is also observed in clinical practice in Switzerland. To develop a meaningful assessment tool, it is important to have an understanding of the spiritual needs patients face and which of these needs are documented. As a first step in developing such a tool, analyzing the spiritual needs of our patient population is essential.

To address this gap, this study explores the spiritual needs documented in palliative care inpatients and examines how documentation varies based on gender, diagnosis, retirement age, nationality, existing relatives and religious affiliation. Furthermore, it investigates differences in how psychological and pastoral care professionals document these needs, and what insights could be derived to develop a clinically meaningful documentation approach in future studies.

Methods

Study design

In this study we used a retrospective observational chart review with a directed content analysis approach [20]. The initial coding structure was developed with a prior exploratory literature review, which allowed to gain an understanding of spiritual needs in the literature. This allowed to deductively (from the literature) as well as inductively (from our patient population) identify spiritual needs. The study was guided by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [21]. The study design is described in three steps in the following sections.

Setting and study population

This study was conducted at the Acute Palliative Care Unit (APCU) of the University Hospital Zurich (USZ), a tertiary academic center in Switzerland. It is a specialized 8-bed unit with approximately 250 admissions annually. The APCU is embedded within the Department of Radiation Oncology and provides specialized interdisciplinary care for patients with advanced, life-limiting illnesses.

All adult patients admitted to the APCU between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2022, were screened for inclusion. When a patient had more than one APCU admission, all eligible documentation was summarized into one single patient record. The four-year interval was chosen because, from 2019 onwards, lists of patients with psychological and pastoral care documentation were systematically available in the electronic health record, ensuring complete case identification and consistent data quality over time.

The inclusion criterion was the presence of at least one documentation entry by either the hospital’s psychological services or pastoral care team. All patients admitted to the APCU receive routine visits from both services so that no referral bias was present in our patient population. From an initial total of 1,049 patients, 747 met the inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria were missing documentation or a missing general consent statement. During coding another 149 patients were excluded due to non-encodable content, such as third-party (e.g. proxy documentation) or non-clinical content (e.g., family meetings only or purely logistical notes), rendering thematic analysis unfeasible. Documentation of spiritual needs from family members were also excluded. Documentation of the final phase of life were not analyzed as neither chaplains nor psychological services documented these periods sufficiently to allow analyzation. In addition, purely psychiatric or non-spiritual psychological documentation (e.g. delirium management, suicide prevention assessments, or formal psychiatric diagnostic evaluations) was excluded, as such entries did not contain material that could be meaningfully interpreted as spiritual concerns. Neither the duration of hospitalization nor the volume of documentation influenced case eligibility. We did not set a minimum number of notes per patient, as documentation intensity varied between professionals and over time. Instead, all available psychological and pastoral care entries for each eligible patient were screened, and any note containing encodable spiritual content was included in the analysis.

Identification and categorization of spiritual needs

Step 1: Deductive identification of spiritual needs

All narrative documentation by psychological or pastoral care services was extracted from the electronic health record (EHR), beginning from the date of each patient’s initial diagnosis qualifying for palliative care. Before the main analysis, we conducted a short exploratory literature review to identify commonly reported spiritual needs in adult palliative care populations. We searched PubMed for articles published between January 2000 and February 2023 using combinations of terms such as “spiritual needs”, “spiritual distress”, “spiritual care”, and “palliative care”, including corresponding MeSH terms where available. We included peer-reviewed empirical and review articles focusing on palliative care settings and excluded studies dealing exclusively with pediatric populations or non-palliative contexts. Relevant studies were used to inform an initial list of spiritual aspects and to align our categories with existing conceptual frameworks (e.g. Selman et al.). A summary of the search strategy, key terms and inclusion/exclusion criteria is provided in Supplementary Table i.

Step 2: Inductive identification of spiritual needs

Next, we analyzed a random sample of 100 electronic health records (25 per year, 2019–2022) to assess the applicability of the spiritual aspects informed by the literature review and to inductively search for additional aspects emerging from the our EHR. New identified spiritual aspects in our patient population were added to the prelimary list. All spiritual aspects were identified in narrative free-text entries documented by psychological and pastoral care services. These notes were exported from the electronic health record and read in full by the first author. No specialized qualitative software was used; analyzation and data organization were performed manually. The sample size was chosen for pragmatic reasons, balancing feasibility with the aim of capturing a broad spectrum of themes. Recurring spiritual aspects documented across multiple records were recorded, resulting in a preliminary list of 44 distinct aspects.

Step 3: Refinement and categorization

In an interdisciplinary meeting with members of the pastoral care and psychological services teams, the list of spiritual aspects was refined to reflect clinical reality and to ensure consistent terminology and definitions, thereby providing triangulation of category content. Aspects considered redundant or closely related were grouped together, and those with an occurrence of < 4% were excluded. The remaining aspects (n = 22) were then mapped to a widely accepted framework from Selman et al., which conceptualizes spiritual needs across four overarching domains: existential, psychological, social and relational, and religious [16]. A detailed overview of the initial 44 aspects, their category content and the aggregation or exclusion steps leading to the final set of 22 is provided in Supplementary Table ii, iii and iv.

A single researcher (VF) conducted all analyzation to minimize inter-rater bias and strictly followed the category content for coding. Periodic sample checks and corrections were performed by another researcher (MS). Each patient record was screened for the presence or absence of these predefined needs based on narrative context.

Definition of variables

The following domains and subthemes were included in the final classification.

Existential needs: reflections on illness history, fears of death, preparation for death, temporal orientation to remaining life, perceived quality of life, non-suicidal desire to die, wish to consult EXIT, a Swiss right-to-die organization that supports physician-assisted suicide under defined legal conditions [22], and questions of meaning.

Existential needs: reflections on illness history, fears of death, preparation for death, temporal orientation to remaining life, perceived quality of life, non-suicidal desire to die, wish to consult EXIT, a Swiss right-to-die organization that supports physician-assisted suicide under defined legal conditions [22], and questions of meaning.

Psychological needs: biographical narratives, resources for coping, loss of independence, unresolved issues before death, grief, and hope.

Psychological needs: biographical narratives, resources for coping, loss of independence, unresolved issues before death, grief, and hope.

Social and relational needs: family relationships, illness/death of loved ones, concerns about their future, feelings of being a burden, relational closure, and alienation.

Social and relational needs: family relationships, illness/death of loved ones, concerns about their future, feelings of being a burden, relational closure, and alienation.

Religious needs: belief in a higher power, desire for prayer, and receipt of sacramental rituals such as the anointing of the sick.

Religious needs: belief in a higher power, desire for prayer, and receipt of sacramental rituals such as the anointing of the sick.

Each need was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no) if mentioned in at least one care note during the patient’s admission(s).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, Version 29.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Frequencies and distributions of spiritual needs were analyzed by descriptive statistics. The Chi-Square test was performed for explorative analysis for associations between spiritual needs and patient characteristics such as gender, nationality, existing relatives, diagnosis, religious affiliation as well as with above and below the Swiss retirement age (< 65 years vs. > 65 years of age). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. When cell frequency was less than five cases Fisher’s exact test was used.

Results

Study population

Between January 2019 and December 2022, a total of 1049 patients were admitted to the Acute Palliative Care Unit (APCU) of the University Hospital Zurich. After screening for the presence of relevant psychological or pastoral documentation, 747 patients were eligible for inclusion (see Fig. 1). Of these, 598 patients (80%) had at least one encodable chart entry by psychological or pastoral care services and were analyzed further.

The median age was 68 years (range 20–97 years), and 52.8% of patients were male. The majority were Swiss residents (79.4%) and primarily German-speaking (94.8%). Most patients had a cancer diagnosis (93.8%). With regard to religion, the majority of patients identified as either Catholic (33.6%) or Protestant (28.8%), while 24.2% reported no religious affiliation or had unknown religious status (see Table 1: Basic patient characteristics).

Discussion

Spiritual Care is a fundamental dimension of palliative care. However, a systematic interprofessional exchange regarding spiritual needs as well as interprofessional spiritual care is still work in progress [6, 12, 17, 23–25]. In Switzerland today, these needs are often addressed by chaplains and psychologists. It is important to highlight, that our findings reflect documentation practices in clinical reality in which spiritual and psychosocial needs aren’t clearly distinctly documented. This study therefore aimed to systematically investigate and classify spiritual needs based on routine clinical documentation in a tertiary palliative care unit.

Interpretation of findings

Our findings indicate that spiritual needs, defined to include existential, psychological, relational, and religious concerns, are highly prevalent among patients at the end of life. The high prevalence of documented existential (61.2%) and psychological (78.9%) needs aligns with international literature, which highlights themes such as fear of dying, loss of autonomy, and the search for meaning as central to patients facing life-limiting illness [6–8, 12, 16, 26–32]. This resonates with the concept of total pain introduced by Cicely Saunders, which emphasized that physical suffering is inseparable from emotional, social, and spiritual distress [33]. The overall dominance of non-religious spiritual concerns in this setting supports an inclusive definition of spirituality in palliative care [2]. Interestingly, religious needs were documented far less frequently—only in 6.0% of psychological and 25.2% of pastoral care encounters. This may suggest a secularized patient population or a reluctance to explicitly address religious topics in clinical conversations, especially outside of pastoral care. This finding is in line with a survey conducted in Switzerland which revealed that patients primarily seek psychosocial support in their interactions with pastoral care services [34]. Religious affiliation was associated only with the religious domain, especially for documentation regarding religious practices (see: Table 6). Existential, psychological as well as social and relational needs were not affected by religious affiliation.

Women as well as patients below the retirement age (< 65 years old) expressed more spiritual needs than their respective groups (see: Table 4 as well as 6). The relationship between gender, younger patients (20–65 years) and spiritual needs in a palliative care setting is an under-researched area. Women reporting more spiritual needs is consistent with previous research, which has shown similar findings [35]. Whether this is due to documentation practices, different expressions of needs in women compared to men or other unknown factors remains to be explored in further research. Of the 22 identified spiritual needs, 10 were associated with patients below the age of 65. Younger age was associated with higher odds for spiritual distress has been demonstrated in other studies [36]. The realization of a shorter life expectancy may exacerbate spiritual distress, which in turn could be reflected in an increased number of documented spiritual needs in our patient population. Further research is needed to confirm this.

Documentation practices

Both psychological and pastoral care services documented their encounters using free-text entries. Notably, differences in the structural composition of these notes were observed. Documentation from psychological care was generally more detailed, often providing an account of the topics discussed during the encounter. Frequently, psychological care documentation included subsections with bullet points that reported on specific patient needs relevant to both the patient and the interdisciplinary care team. Conversely, pastoral care documentation tended to be more general without detailed elaboration on patient needs and remained superficial. It is important to note that pastoral documentation is subject to pastoral confidentiality, which in turn shapes documentation practices. Pastoral confidentiality is regulated in the Swiss Criminal code and applies in relation to medical personnel. In addition, it is also regulated by the church, which currently imposes restrictions on documentation practices in Zurich [37].

Both specialties did not document with a standardized spiritual assessment tool. This condition raises important questions regarding interprofessional communication. Psychologists were more likely to record existential and emotional content, while religious language and rituals were primarily mentioned in pastoral care notes.

These findings highlight the need for stronger integration of chaplains into interdisciplinary workflows and the need for consistent interdisciplinary vocabulary regarding spiritual needs. Moreover, this has direct implications for education, clinical documentation standards, and ethical responsibilities within the care team. Structured training programs should empower nurses, physicians, and social workers to recognize, address, and competently document spiritual needs. This is particularly relevant when chaplaincy services are limited or unavailable.

Relevance to clinical practice

Our results reveal a significant gap between the high frequency of spiritual needs and the absence of a structured approach to assess and document them. The reality in clinical practice is that spiritual needs are often times intertwined with psychosocial needs and not clearly distinctly documented. Currently, at the University Hospital Zurich, no standardized spiritual assessment tool is embedded within the EHR. While “true” spiritual conversations may occur with different disciplines (e.g. nurses, physicians, chaplains), without systematic documentation, this critical aspect of care remains invisible in patients’ records or is convoluted with psychosocial aspects and is difficult to track for clinical continuity or research purposes.

Implementing structured spiritual history tools such as FICA [13] or HOPE [38], combined with appropriate interprofessional training, could enhance the identification and documentation of spiritual concerns. Spiritual needs should not be viewed as the exclusive responsibility of chaplains or psychologists. Interprofessional education and team-based reflection are needed to ensure that all providers—nurses, physicians, social workers—are prepared and feel confident to address these dimensions in a patient-centered and ethically responsible manner [3]. To improve the integration of documentation into daily clinical workflows, a feasible tool, meaning time-efficient and user-friendly, should be developed and implemented in collaboration with health IT teams and clinicians of all specialties [23]. This approach–although only for chaplain spiritual service-has been successfully realized in practice [39].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design relies on what health care professionals chose to document. Without a standardized documentation system, the dataset may be heterogeneous, with potential interobserver variability and reporting bias. Differences in documentation styles and the involvement of various psychologists and chaplains may have influenced the observed prevalence of spiritual or religious needs. Moreover, the legal framework guiding chaplains’ work may have shaped their documentation, possibly misrepresenting patients’ needs. It also remains unclear whether the relatively low documentation of religious needs reflects true patient preferences or documentation practices, which warrants further study. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting documented spiritual concerns in clinical records rather than the full spectrum of patients’ lived spiritual experiences.

Second, coding was performed by a single researcher. While this ensured consistency, it may have introduced interpretation bias and limited generalizability.

Third, we were unable to assess whether the documented needs were addressed, resolved, or how severe they were—only that they were noted.

Fourth, because of the use of multiple chi-square tests performed in this study, the risk of type 1 error is increased. Therefore, our findings should be seen as hypothesis generating.

Fifth, excluding patients without psychological or pastoral documentation may have biased the sample toward those with more complex or communicative profiles.

Sixth, due to the nature of routine documentation, we were unable to disentangle with certainty which documented concerns were primarily psychological or social and which represented spiritual needs, or both. We assume that spiritual aspects are often implicit and embedded in biographical, relational, and emotional narratives rather than explicitly labelled. Our classification of spiritual needs is based on interpretative criteria rather than on a standardized, validated spiritual assessment tool. This conceptual “blurriness” limits the precision of our findings but mirrors everyday clinical practice and, as we assume, also the elusive ‘spiritual dimension’ of health. This ambiguity is not only a methodological limitation but also illustrates how spiritual concerns are embedded within psychological and social experience in palliative care today. In this sense, the categorization used in this study represents an interpretative operationalization of themes documented in clinical encounters rather than a strictly separable classification of spiritual needs.

Finally, the findings may not be generalizable to non-tertiary or outpatient palliative care settings, where time constraints and access to spiritual care differ substantially.

Conclusion

Spiritual needs are common and varied among palliative care patients, with psychological and existential concerns being the most frequently documented. Gender and age differences were significant, with women and patients under 65 reporting higher levels of existential fears, grief, and hope. These findings highlight the need for more structured documentation and assessment tools to capture the full range of spiritual needs.

The integration of standardized spiritual assessment tools, such as FICA or HOPE, into routine clinical practice would improve the consistency and quality of spiritual care documentation. Additionally, fostering collaboration between pastoral and psychological care teams and health IT professionals is crucial for enhancing the visibility and continuity of spiritual care in palliative settings.
