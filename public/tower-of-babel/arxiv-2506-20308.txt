Deep random difference method for high dimensional quasilinear parabolic partial differential equations Journal: —
Wei Cai, Shuixin Fang, Tao Zhou

Abstract
Solving high-dimensional parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs) with deep learning methods is often computationally and memory intensive, primarily due to the need for automatic differentiation (AD) to compute large Hessian matrices in the PDE. In this work, we propose a deep random difference method (DRDM) that addresses these issues by approximating the convection-diffusion operator using only first-order differences and the solution by deep neural networks, thus, avoiding explicit Hessian computation. When incorporated into a Galerkin framework, the DRDM eliminates the need for pointwise evaluation of expectations, resulting in efficient implementation. We further extend the approach to Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equations. Notably, the DRDM recovers existing martingale deep learning methods for PDEs [ 1 ] , without using the tools of stochastic calculus. The proposed method offers two main advantages: it removes the dependence on AD for PDE derivatives and enables parallel computation of the loss function in both time and space. We provide rigorous error estimates for the DRDM in the linear case, which shows a first order accuracy in Δ ​ t \Delta t used in the sampling of the paths by the Euler-Maruyama scheme. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the method can efficiently and accurately solve quasilinear parabolic PDEs and HJB equations in dimensions up to 10 4 10^{4} and 10 5 10^{5} , respectively.

Introduction

High-dimensional partial differential equations (PDEs) arise in a wide range of applications, including finance, physics, and engineering.
Traditional numerical methods for PDEs often suffer from the curse of dimensionality (CoD), where the computational cost increases exponentially with the dimensionality.
Recent advances in deep learning have demonstrated significant potential for overcoming the CoD, yielding various deep learning-based numerical methods for high-dimensional PDEs.
Up to now, the most popular deep learning methods for solving PDEs can be broadly classified into two categories:

To view the pros and cons of the two approaches above, we consider the quasilinear parabolic PDE as follows

\mathcal{D}v(t,x)=f(t,x,v(t,x)),\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(1)

with a terminal condition
v(T,x)=g(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
where
v:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the unknown solution, and
\mathcal{D}
is the nonlinear second-order differential operator defined as

\mathcal{D}:=\partial_{t}+\mu^{\top}\left(t,x,v(t,x)\right)\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma\sigma^{\top}(t,x,v(t,x))\partial_{xx}^{2}\right]

(2)

with
\mu
and
\sigma
are given functions valued in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}

The PDE (

When solving the PDE (
1

L(v):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left|\mathcal{D}v(t_{i},x_{i})-f(t_{i},x_{i},v(t_{i},x_{i}))\right|^{2},

(3)

(t_{i},x_{i})

[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

A key advantage of (

However, these methods depend on automatic differentiation (AD) to evaluate the differential operator
\mathcal{D}v
.
The back-propagation method to compute the

For instance,
[
5

A series of recent works
[

4

28

address this challenge by introducing techniques such as stochastic dimension gradient descent (SDGD), Hutchinson trace estimation, and stochastic Taylor derivative estimators in the framework of PINNs.

SDE-based approaches are grounded in the nonlinear Feynman-Kac formula, i.e., the solution
v
to the PDE (
1

Y_{t}=v(t,X_{t}),\quad Z_{t}=\partial_{x}v(t,X_{t})\sigma(t,X_{t},Y_{t}),\quad t\in[0,T],

(4)

where
t\mapsto(X_{t},Y_{t},Z_{t})
are stochastic processes given by the forward-backward SDEs (FBSDEs):

\left\{\begin{aligned} X_{t}&=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(s,X_{s},Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s},Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},\\
Y_{t}&=g(X_{T})-\int_{t}^{T}f(s,X_{s},Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},\end{aligned}\right.\quad t\in[0,T],

(5)

where
\int_{0}^{t}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
denotes the Itô integral with respect to a standard Brownian motion

.
By reformulating the problem into the FBSDEs (

\mathcal{D}v

However, this advantage comes at the cost of reduced parallelism in time compared to direct approaches.

(i) For quasilinear parabolic PDEs (
1

[

Y_{t}=v(t,X_{t})

Consequently, these sample paths need to be updated iteratively during training, and such updates are inherently sequential in time, resulting in increased computational cost.
(ii) The methods in
[

To enhance the parallelizability of SDE-based approaches, the SOC-MartNet method

and its derivative-free variant

This reformulation enables path sampling to be conducted offline, thereby allowing for highly parallelized online training.
As a result, computational efficiency is significantly improved, making it possible to solve high-dimensional PDEs with up to

dimensions.
However, the martingale deep learning methods proposed in

employ the tools of the Itô calculus, which may create a challenge for those without a solid background in stochastic analysis. Thus, it is the intention of this paper to

Moreover, it will be seen that the new approach will also provide a theoretical justification for the effectiveness of the proposed method, thus the SOC-MartNet method as well.
Specifically, we introduce a random difference method (RDM) for approximating the operator

in (

The RDM only involves a first-order difference, thereby, avoiding explicit Hessian computation.
By integrating the RDM with the WAN approach

We further provide rigorous error estimates for the DRDM in the case where the differential operator

The DRDM has several advantages.

Second, it combines the strengths of both direct and SDE-based approaches for PDEs:
it avoids the use of AD for computing the derivatives in the PDEs and enables parallel computation of the loss function in both time and space.

2
Random difference method

In this section, we present the random difference approximation to the differential operator defined in (
2
).
This technique forms a central component of our RDM for solving PDEs.

2.1
Basic expansion

We begin by considering the Laplace operator
\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\partial_{z_{i}}^{2}
acting on a function
z\mapsto f(z)
, where
z\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
f
has bounded fourth-order derivatives.
Let
\xi=\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{q}\right)^{\top}
be a
\mathbb{R}^{q}
-valued random variable.
By the Taylor expansion of
f(z)
around
z=0
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\sqrt{h}\xi)\right]=f(0)+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{h^{k/2}}{k!}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\xi^{\top}\partial_{z}\big)^{k}f(0)\right]+\frac{h^{2}}{4!}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\xi^{\top}\partial_{z}\big)^{4}f(c\sqrt{h}\xi)\right],

(6)

where
\xi^{\top}\partial_{z}:=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\xi_{i}\partial_{z_{i}}
,
\left|\xi\right|^{2}:=\xi^{\top}\xi
, and
c\in[0,1]
depending on
\sqrt{h}\xi
.
Assume the components
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{q}
of
\xi
satisfy:

\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{i}\right]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{ij}\right]=\delta_{ij},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\right]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\xi_{l}\right|\right]<\infty

(7)

for
i,j,k,l=1,2,\cdots,q
,
where
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta.
Under these conditions, the first- and third-order derivative terms in (
6
) vanish, yielding

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\sqrt{h}\xi)\right]=f(0)+\frac{h}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\partial_{z_{i}}^{2}f(0)+O\left(h^{2}\right).

(8)

The above expansion serves as the basis for the RDM to be introduced in the next subsection.

Remark 1
.

Typical examples of
\xi
that satisfy (
7
) include:

1.

\xi\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{q})
, where
I_{q}
is the
q
-dimensional identity matrix;

2.

\xi
has mutually independent components, each satisfying

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{i}=c\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{i}=-c\right)=\frac{1}{2c^{2}},\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{i}=0\right)=1-\frac{1}{c^{2}}

(9)

for some constant
c\geq 1
.

2.2
RDM for convection-diffusion operator

Next, we consider the convection-diffusion operator
\mathcal{D}
as defined in (
2
).
For a fixed point
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the function

V(s,z):=v(t+s,x+\mu s+\sigma z),\quad(s,z)\in[T-t]\times\mathbb{R}^{q},

(10)

where, for clarity, we suppress the dependence of
V
,
\mu
, and
\sigma
on
(t,x,v)
.
Applying the expansion (
8
) with
f
replaced by
z\mapsto V(h,z)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[V(h,\sqrt{h}\xi)\right]=V(h,0)+\frac{h}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial z_{i}^{2}}(h,0)+O\left(h^{2}\right).

(11)

By the definition in (
10
), the Taylor expansion and the chain rule imply

\displaystyle V(h,0)

\displaystyle=V(0,0)+\left(\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\mu^{\top}\partial_{x}v(t,x)\right)h+O(h^{2}),

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial z_{i}^{2}}(h,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial z_{i}^{2}}(0,0)+O(h)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma\sigma^{\top}\partial_{xx}^{2}v(t,x)\right]+O(h).

(13)

Inserting (
12
) and (
13
) into (
11
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[V(h,\sqrt{h}\xi)\right]=V(0,0)+h\big(\partial_{t}+\mu^{\top}\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma\sigma^{\top}\partial_{xx}^{2}\right]\big)v(t,x)+O(h^{2}).

(14)

By substituting (
2
) and (
10
) into the above equation, we arrive at the random difference approximation for
\mathcal{D}
as

\mathcal{D}_{h}v(t,x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{v(t+h,x+\mu h+\sqrt{h}\sigma\xi)-v(t,x)}{h}\right]=\mathcal{D}v(t,x)+O(h).

(15)

A rigorous derivation of (
15
), along with an explicit upper bound for the remainder term
O(h)
, is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

A notable feature of (
15
) is that it enables the approximation of second-order derivatives using only first-order differences, thereby avoiding explicit computation of the Hessian.

Remark 2
.

The approximation (
15
) can also be derived from the Itô formula under the regularity condition
v\in C^{1,2}
; see, e.g.,
[
29
, Theorem 2.3.3]
. A similar approach using random difference was considered in
[
30
]
.

2.3
RDM for solving quasilinear parabolic PDEs

By approximating
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{D}_{h}
defined in (
15
), the PDE in (
1
) can be reformulated as

\mathbb{E}\left[R(t,x,\xi;v)\right]=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T-h]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(16)

where
R(t,x,\xi;v)
is the residual function defined by

\displaystyle R(t,x,\xi;v)

\displaystyle:=\frac{v(t+h,x+\xi_{h})-v(t,x)}{h}-f(t,x,v(t,x)),

(17)

\displaystyle\xi_{h}

\displaystyle:=\mu(t,x,v(t,x))h+\sqrt{h}\sigma(t,x,v(t,x))\xi.

(18)

The formulation (
16
) avoids explicit computation of spatial partial derivatives including the Hessian, however an expectation in (
16
) is needed for each
(t,x)
. This is the trade off between the PINN and the RDM approach, the former needs the computation of partial derivatives of the solution at each sampled point
(t,x)
, which is avoided by the RDM in lieu of an expectation over the random jumps
\xi_{h}
from
(t,x)
.
Approximating this expectation directly using Monte Carlo methods can be computationally intensive.
In the next section, we address this issue by reformulating (
16
) using a weak form.

We close this section with two remarks that clarify the connections between the formulation (
16
) and existing works.

Remark 3

(Connection to martingale methods)
.

The formulation (
16
) is equivalent to the martingale formulation proposed in
[
1
,
26
,
27
]
.
To see this connection, we recall the martingale condition for the function
v(t,x)
being a solution of the PDE (
15
)
[
27
, subsection 2.1]
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}_{t+h}-\mathcal{M}_{t}\big|X_{t}=x\right]=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T-h]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(19)

where

\mathcal{M}_{t}:=v(t,X_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}f(s,X_{s},v(s,X_{s}))\,\mathrm{d}s,

(20)

and
X_{s}
is given by the forward SDE in (
5
).
When
X_{t}
is approximated by the Euler-Maruyama scheme
[
31
, section 14.1]
applied to the forward SDE in (
5
),
the martingale condition (
19
) reduces to the expectation condition (
16
) with
\xi\sim\mathrm{N}(0,I_{q})
.

Remark 4

(Connection to FBSDE methods)
.

The formulation (
16
) can also be derived from a time discretization of the FBSDEs (
5
).
Specifically, taking the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{t}^{x}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\,\cdot\,|X_{t}=x\right]
on both sides of the backward SDE in (
5
) gives

\mathbb{E}_{t}^{x}\left[Y_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{t}^{x}\left[Y_{t+h}\right]+\int_{t}^{t+h}\mathbb{E}_{t}^{x}\left[f(s,X_{s},Y_{s})\right]\,\mathrm{d}s.

(21)

The condition (
16
) then follows by: (i) substituting
Y
using the Feynman-Kac formula (
4
) in (
21
);
(ii) approximating the integral in (
21
) with the left-rectangle rule;
(iii) approximating the conditional expectations
\mathbb{E}_{t}^{x}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]
by the Euler-Maruyama scheme
[
31
, section 14.1]
applied to the forward SDE in (
5
).
For more details on time discretization methods for FBSDEs (
5
) and their generalizations, see
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, etc.

3
Deep neural network with RDM (DRDM)

In this section, we introduce deep learning implementation to learn the solution
v
from the condition (
16
), and subsequently extend the method to HJB equations.

3.1
RDM with a Galerkin method

Similar to PINN methods
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, we enforce the condition (
16
) on a set of randonly sampled time-space points in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that for each
t\in[0,T]
, the spatial sampling points are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples of a random variable
X_{t}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The construction of
X_{t}
is specified in (
38
) in later subsections.
We then reformulate (
16
) into a weak formulation:

\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(t,x)\mathbb{E}\left[R(t,x,\xi;v)\right]P_{X_{t}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=0,\quad\forall\rho\in\mathcal{T},

(22)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the set of test functions, typically defined as:

\mathcal{T}:=\left\{\rho:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}\;\Big|\;\rho\text{ is smooth and bounded}\right\},

(23)

Here,
P_{X_{t}}(\cdot)
is the probability density function (PDF) of
X_{t}
, serving as a weight function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It is worth noting that the test function
\rho
is chosen to be vector-valued, which enhances the stability of the adversarial training process described in the next subsection.

For the convenience of sample generation, let
X_{t}
be independent of
\xi
, leading to

P_{(X_{t},\xi)}(x,z)=P_{X_{t}}(x)P_{\xi}(z),\quad(t,x,z)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{q},

(24)

where
P_{(X_{t},\xi)}
is the joint PDF of
(X_{t},\xi)
, and
P_{\xi}
is the marginal PDF of
\xi
.
Using (
24
), the left side of (
22
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(t,x)\mathbb{E}\left[R(t,x,\xi;v)\right]P_{X_{t}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\rho(t,x)R(t,x,z;v)P_{\xi}(z)P_{X_{t}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\rho(t,x)R(t,x,z;v)P_{(X_{t},\xi)}(x,z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-h}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(t,X_{t})R(t,X_{t},\xi;v)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t.

(25)

Substituting (
25
) into (
22
), we obtain the following minimax problem for solving
v
:

\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\max_{\rho\in\mathcal{T}}\left|L(v,\rho)\right|^{2},\quad L(v,\rho):=\int_{0}^{T-h}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(t,X_{t})R(t,X_{t},\xi;v)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t,

(26)

where
\mathcal{V}
represents the set of candidate functions
v
satisfying the terminal condition, i.e.,

\mathcal{V}:=\left\{v:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\;\;\big|\;v\in C_{b}^{2,4},\;v(T,x)=g(x),\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}

(27)

with the regularity condition ensuring the validity of the RDM method in (
15
).

Compared to the original problem (
22
), the minimax formulation is computationally more efficient,
as it involves only a single expectation in the loss function, which can be effectively approximated through joint Monte Carlo sampling of
(t,X_{t},\xi)
.

3.2
Spatial sampling rule based on error propagation

In this subsection, we analyze how the residual of the PDE (
1
) propagates and affects the error of the numerical solution.
This analysis provides guidance for designing the PDF
P_{X_{t}}(\cdot)
in (
22
).

3.2.1
Error equation

To obtain an explicit relationship between the residual and the error,
we focus on the case where the PDE (
1
) is linear, i.e.,

\mu(t,x,v)=\mu(t,x),\quad\sigma(t,x,v)=\sigma(t,x),\quad f(t,x,v)=f(t,x)

(28)

for
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Although the following analysis is derived for the linear case, the sampling method obtained in
section

3.2.3
is also applicable to the nonlinear case.

We decompose the operator
\mathcal{D}
in (
2
) as follows:

\mathcal{D}=\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L},\quad\mathcal{L}:=\mu^{\top}\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\sigma^{\top}\partial_{xx}^{2}].

(29)

Let
\hat{v}
be an approximation to the solution
v
of (
1
).
Under the condition (
28
),
the error
\epsilon(t,x):=\hat{v}(t,x)-v(t,x)
satisfies the following equation:

(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L})\epsilon(t,x)=\hat{r}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(30)

where
\hat{r}(t,x)
denotes the residual at
(t,x)
, defined by

\hat{r}(t,x):=(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L})\hat{v}(t,x)-f(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(31)

Let
p(t,x)
be a weight function defined on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Taking the inner product of
\epsilon
with
p(t,x)
over
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we obtain the integral form of the error equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\epsilon p\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\partial_{t}\epsilon)p\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\epsilon(\partial_{t}p)\,\mathrm{d}x

(32)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{r}p\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}\epsilon)p\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\epsilon\partial_{t}p\,\mathrm{d}x,

where the second line follows from (
30
).

3.2.2
Error propagation from residuals

Now, we shall simplify the right-hand side of (
32
) to obtain an ODE with an explicit solution of
\int\epsilon p\,\mathrm{d}x
.
Let
\mathcal{L}^{*}
denote the
L^{2}
-adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}\epsilon)p\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\epsilon(\mathcal{L}^{*}p)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(33)

Inserting (
33
) into (
32
) yields

\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\epsilon p\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{r}p\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\epsilon(\partial_{t}p-\mathcal{L}^{*}p)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(34)

In particular, let
p
satisfies the adjoint equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}^{*})p=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
&p(0,x)=\delta_{x_{0}}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{aligned}\right.

(35)

where
\delta_{x_{0}}
denotes the Dirac delta function centered at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By substituting (
35
) into the right-hand side of (
32
), the second integral term is eliminated.
Integrating both sides with respect to
t
over
[0,T]
, we obtain the error representation at
(0,x_{0})
:

\displaystyle\epsilon(0,x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\epsilon p)(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(36)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\epsilon(T,x)p(T,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{r}(s,x)p(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

The representation (
36
) shows that the error at
(0,x_{0})
is determined by the terminal error, weighted by
p(T,x)
, and the accumulation of the residual
\hat{r}
over time and space, weighted by
p(s,x)
.
The function
p(t,x)
therefore quantifies the influence of the time-space point
(t,x)
on the error at
(0,x_{0})
, motivating the use of
p
as a sampling weight during training.

3.2.3
Sampling method

The key issue is how to efficiently sample according to
p
without incurring the high computational cost of directly solving the adjoint problem (
35
).
In fact, the adjoint equation (
35
) coincides with the Fokker-Planck equation, whose solution
x\mapsto p(t,x)
represents the PDF of
X_{t}
governed by the forward SDE in (
5
)
[
37
, Proposition 3.3]
.
Therefore, spatial samples can be generated by simulating the forward SDE in (
5
) using the weak Euler-Maruyama scheme.

Specifically, we introduce a time partition on
[0,T]

t_{n}=nh,\quad h=T/N,\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N.

(37)

The spatial samples
\left\{X_{n}^{m}\right\}_{m=1}^{M}
at time
t_{n}
are generated by

X_{n+1}^{m}=X_{n}^{m}+\mu\left(t_{n},X_{n}^{m},\hat{v}(t_{n},X_{n}^{m})\right)\,h+\sigma\left(t_{n},X_{n}^{m},\hat{v}(t_{n},X_{n}^{m})\right)\,\sqrt{h}\,\xi_{n+1}^{m},

(38)

for
n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
, where
\hat{v}
denotes a pilot approximation of
v
obtained during neural network training,
and for any
n
and
m
, the random variables
\xi_{n}^{m}
are i.i.d. samples of
\xi
satisfying the conditions in (
7
); see
[
31
, section 14.1]
.
It is important to note that in (
38
), the subscript of
\xi_{n+1}^{m}
is
n+1
rather than
n
.
This ensures the independence between
X_{n}^{m}
and
\xi_{n}^{m}
, in accordance with the condition (
24
).

Remark 5

(Connection to SDE-based approaches)
.

The sampling method (
38
) is widely adopted in SDE-based approaches for exploring the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
see, e.g.,
[
13
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
Our analysis offers a theoretical justification for this sampling method from the perspective of error propagation.

3.3
Mini-batch estimation of the loss function

Let
(X_{n}^{m},\xi_{n}^{m})
for
n=0,1,\cdots,N
and
m=1,2,\cdots,M
be i.i.d. samples of
(X_{n},\xi)
generated by (
7
) and (
38
).
The loss function in (
26
) can be estimated in an unbiased manner using its mini-batch version given by

\displaystyle\left|L(v,\rho)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx L^{\top}(v,\rho;A_{1})L(v,\rho;A_{2}),

(39)

\displaystyle L(\rho,v;A_{i})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\left|A\right|}\sum_{(n,m)\in A}\rho(t_{n},X_{n}^{m})R(t_{n},X_{n}^{m},\xi_{n}^{m};v)h,\quad i=1,2,

(40)

where
\left|A_{i}\right|
denotes the number of elements in the index set
A_{i}
, and
h=T/N
is the time step size.
The index sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are constructed by

\displaystyle A_{i}=\mathrm{N}_{i}\times\mathrm{M}_{i},\quad\mathrm{N}_{i}\subset\{0,1,\cdots,N-1\},

(41)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{i}\subset\{1,2,\cdots,M\},\quad\mathrm{M}_{1}\cap\mathrm{M}_{2}=\emptyset

for
i=1,2
.
Here, inspired by
[
38
,
3
]
, the disjointness between
\mathrm{M}_{1}
and
\mathrm{M}_{2}
is used to ensure the mini-batch estimation in (
39
) is unbiased.
The sets
\mathrm{N}_{1}
and
\mathrm{N}_{2}
can be chosen randomly, wthout replacements, from
\{0,1,\cdots,N-1\}
, or simply set to
\{0,1,\cdots,N-1\}
if
N
is not too large.

Remark 6
.

The simulation of the sample paths
X_{n}^{m}
in (
38
) is sequential in time, which can be computationally expensive.
However, since these samples are only used as spatial sampling points, it is not necessary to update them at every iteration.
Instead, they can be generated offline and updated less frequently; for example, by updating the sample paths every
I_{0}
iterations, where
I_{0}
is a tunable hyperparameter.

3.5
Extension to HJB equations

We now consider the HJB equation, which is a typical high-dimensional PDE arising in dynamic programming for stochastic optimal control problems (SOCPs)
[
40
]
.
The HJB equation and its terminal conditions take the form

\inf_{\kappa\in U}\left\{\mathcal{D}^{\kappa}v(t,x)+c(t,x,\kappa)\right\}=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(44)

with a terminal condition
v(T,x)=g(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a given control set, and
\mathcal{D}^{\kappa}
denotes the second-order differential operator parameterized by
\kappa\in U
, defined as

\mathcal{D}^{\kappa}:=\partial_{t}+\mu^{\top}(t,x,\kappa)\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma(t,x,\kappa)\sigma(t,x,\kappa)^{\top}\partial_{xx}^{2}\right].

(45)

Here, the functions
\mu
,
\sigma
, and
c
are defined on
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U
and take values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
, and
\mathbb{R}
, respectively.

To facilitate the application of the DRDM, we adopt the idea of policy improvement algorithm
[
9
]
, and decompose the HJB equation (
44
) into two subproblems:

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\kappa\in U}\left\{\mathcal{D}^{\kappa}v(t,x)+c(t,x,\kappa)\right\},

(46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{u}v(t,x)+c(t,x,u(t,x))=0,

(47)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the shorthand
\mathcal{D}^{u}:=\mathcal{D}^{u(t,x)}
.
In the context of SOCPs, the function
u
denotes the optimal state-feedback control.

Applying the random difference approximation (
15
) to the operator
\mathcal{D}^{u}
in (
46
) and (
47
), and then reformulating the resulting equations into weak forms as described in
section

3.1
, we arrive at the following formulation for solving the HJB equation:

\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\;\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{T}}\left|L(u,v,\rho)\right|^{2},\quad\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}L(u,v,1),

(48)

where the sets
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{V}
are given in (
23
) and (
27
), respectively;

\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
denotes the set of admissible feedback controls defined by

\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}:=\left\{u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to U:u\text{ is Borel measurable}\right\};

(49)

the loss function
L
is then defined by

L(u,v,\rho):=\int_{0}^{T-h}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(t,X_{t})R(t,X_{t},\xi;u,v)\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

(50)

with
R
the residual function defined by

\displaystyle R\left(t,x,z;u,v\right):=

\displaystyle v\left(t+h,x+\mu(t,x,u(t,x))h+\sigma(t,x,u(t,x))\sqrt{h}z\right)

(51)

\displaystyle-v(t,x)+hc(t,x,u(t,x)).

The notation
1
in
L(u,v,1)
denotes a constant test function with fixed output
1
.

Similar to
section

3.3
, for practical implementation, the loss function
L
in (
50
) is approximated by its mini-batch version, defined as

L(u,v,\rho;A_{i}):=\frac{h}{\left|A_{i}\right|}\sum_{(n,m)\in A_{i}}\rho(t_{n},X_{n}^{m})R(t_{n},X_{n}^{m},\xi_{n}^{m};u,v),\quad i=1,2,

(52)

where
A_{i}
are still given by (
41
), and
\{X_{n}^{m}\}_{n=0}^{N}
are samples generated by

X_{n+1}^{m}=X_{n}^{m}+\mu\left(t_{n},X_{n}^{m},\hat{u}(t_{n},X_{n}^{m})\right)\,h+\sigma\left(t_{n},X_{n}^{m},\hat{u}(t_{n},X_{n}^{m})\right)\,\sqrt{h}\,\xi_{n+1}^{m}

(53)

for
n=0,\cdots,N-1
and
m=1,\cdots,M
, with each
\xi_{n}^{m}
drawn i.i.d. from
\mathrm{N}(0,I_{q})
and
\hat{u}
a pilot approximation of
u
derived during neural network training.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a deep random difference method (DRDM) for solving high-dimensional quasilinear parabolic PDEs and HJB equations.
The DRDM approximates the convection-diffusion operator using only first-order differences, thereby avoiding explicit computation of Hessians and reducing reliance on automatic differentiation. By integrating DRDM within a Galerkin framework, we eliminate the need for pointwise evaluation of expectations, which enables efficient and highly parallelizable implementations.
The method is also extended to HJB equations.
We have provided rigorous error estimates for the linear case and demonstrated through numerical experiments that DRDM can efficiently and accurately solve PDEs in dimensions up to
10^{5}
.

The DRDM recovers existing martingale deep learning methods
[
1
,
26
,
27
]
without relying on stochastic calculus.
Our method offers several distinctive advantages:

1.

The loss function does not require AD for any PDE derivatives, resulting in efficient computation and reduced memory usage.

2.

The loss function can be computed in parallel across both time and space, further enhancing efficiency.

3.

The sample distribution can be tailored to the dynamics of the convection-diffusion operator, enhancing the method’s ability to handle problems with complex solution structures.

4.

The truncation error of the DRDM achieves first-order accuracy in time, which is higher than the half-order accuracy of methods based on the pathwise properties of the Euler-Maruyama scheme.

Future work will explore the application of DRDM to more challenging problems, including convection-diffusion equations with complex boundaries, committor functions, and non-convex SOCPs.

(83)

To estimate

For the stability analysis, we introduce the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
