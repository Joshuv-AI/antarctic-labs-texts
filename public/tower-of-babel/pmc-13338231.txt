Single-neuron network topology governs neural computation and learning in primate cortex
Authors: Zhuangyi Jiang, Ziang Liu, Li Shi, Fang Fang, Shiming Tang, Yang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neural networks underlie complex brain information processing, yet the role of their single-neuron topology in governing computation and behavior remains unclear, particularly regarding how it shapes individual neuron function and activity evolution during learning. Using two-photon calcium imaging, we tracked functional connectivity in thousands of posterior parietal cortex neurons as monkeys learned sensorimotor associations across days. We identified small-world networks with densely connected hub neurons that dominated encoding key task variables, driving local dynamics and neural encoding evolution during the monkeys’ task performance and learning. Dynamic transitions in hub/non-hub status captured how inter-neuronal interactions shaped neuronal encoding evolution during association formation. Modular structures supported specialized neuron ensembles, enabling segregated representations and interactions within local networks. Importantly, small-world network properties predicted behavioral performance, with global information processing efficiency increasing as learning progressed. These findings reveal how single-neuron-resolution brain networks, through small-world organization, orchestrate both global and modular neural computations within local network to mediate behavior and shape learning.

Introduction

Neurons form complex networks in the brain that underlie various processes1–7. Understanding these structural and functional networks at multiple scales is essential for elucidating how neural circuits contribute to overall brain activity and behavior8–12. Previous studies using diverse research tools, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), electrophysiological recordings, and neuronal tracing, have provided rich insights into both structural and functional neural networks, particularly at macroscopic scale13–22. Quantitative analyses based on graph theory have illuminated the features of these complex brain networks1,8,23,24. However, much less is known about neural networks at single-neuron resolution in awake, behaving brain, primarily due to the technical challenges of mapping precise structural or functional connectivity among many neurons in behaving animals.

Although reconstructing the structural neuronal network in the behaving brain remains challenging, several approaches have been developed to infer functional connectivity. Among these, noise correlation—capturing shared trial-to-trial variability in neural activity—has become a widely used metric for estimating functional coupling25–27. This measure has generated important insights into circuit-level interactions and their relationship to neural encoding, information readout, and ultimately behavioral performance26–31. However, prior studies have reported conflicting findings regarding whether higher noise correlations facilitate or impair behavioral performance27,28,31–35. One potential reason is that earlier studies typically treated noise correlation as a covariance statistic between pairs or small subsets of neurons, overlooking the broader network topology that arises at the population level. This has left critical gaps in our understanding of the mesoscale functional architecture that supports neural computation and learning. More recently, researchers have proposed that the fine-grained structure of correlations—such as those within versus across neuronal ensembles—may account for these divergent results26,27,32,33,35, highlighting the need to study network topology as an integrated whole.

Network topology—defined by connectivity patterns and node positioning—systematically shapes the functional roles of its elements9,36. For instance, structural properties like degree distribution and modularity directly influence node dynamics36, centrality measures (e.g., betweenness) predict functional importance in information flow or resource allocation37, and highly connected “hub nodes” are often functionally critical38. For instance, in vitro studies have shown that hippocampal GABAergic interneurons, characterized by their extensive axonal arborization, serve as functional hubs that drive large-scale network dynamics and synchrony during the development of hippocampal circuits39. However, how the functioning of individual neurons depends on their topology position in the neuron network remains largely unexplored. Moreover, learning is a dynamic process that fundamentally alters the structure and function of neuron networks40–45. During learning, specific patterns of neuronal activity would lead to the remodeling of neural connections, enhancing the efficiency of information processing within these networks43,46. Conversely, whether and how the functional network topology shapes the evolution of neuronal activity during learning remains unclear.

Small-world network topology, characterized by highly connected hubs and modularity, has been widely observed in both structural and functional brain networks1,2,5,8,14,16,17,20,22,30,47–49. This topology is hypothesized to be an efficient solution for achieving both global integration and functional segregation1,5,8,50,51. Yet, the mechanisms by which the small-world topology of brain networks at single-neuron resolution optimizes neural processing to generate behavior and shape learning remain largely unknown, largely due to the lack of investigation into the topology of neuron networks during animals’ task performance and learning. We hypothesize that highly connected hub neurons and the modular topology in small-world neuron networks play critical roles in global and modular neural processing, respectively. Specifically, hub neurons may dominate neural computations, such as neural encoding of behavioral variables and driving population activity dynamics, while modular connectivity could facilitate the formation of functionally specialized groups for segregated computations. We further propose that network topology coevolves with learning behavior, constraining the evolution of neural encodings of individual neurons and their mutual influences during learning.

Results

PPC functional neuron network exhibited small-world topology

We directly tested these hypotheses by examining the functional network of hundreds of neurons simultaneously recorded from area 7a of the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) in behaving monkeys, a region previously identified as a key hub for saccade planning and decision-making52–62. We trained two monkeys to perform an image-saccade association learning task (Supplementary Fig. 1), where they had to choose one of two saccade targets based on a presented visual image. The monkeys learned the associations between specific visual images and corresponding saccade directions through trial outcomes—receiving a juice reward for correct choices and a short time-out punishment for incorrect ones (see “Methods”). Once the monkeys fully mastered two image-saccade associations (accuracy 0.89 ± 0.05, mean ± STD), they were required to learn a pair of novel associations in subsequent days. We employed two-photon calcium imaging to simultaneously record the activity of hundreds of neurons over multiple days. We tracked the activity of the same population of neurons across a whole learning course, starting from the first day when monkey was tested with a pair of familiar associations to the day they mastered a new pair of associations for each recorded field of view (FOV). We successively finished recording from 12 FOVs and identified a total of 8537 single neurons with a good signal-to-noise ratio in two monkeys. Additionally, our previous study demonstrate that primate area 7a is involved in associative learning and that functional connectivity among 7a neurons may constrain the evolution of neuronal encoding across learning days63. This dataset offers a unique opportunity to explore how network topology at single-neuron resolution optimizes neural processing to generate behavior and shape learning.

To construct the functional neuron network, we calculated the partial noise correlation between each pair of simultaneously recorded neurons within each recording session, using these correlation values as the connection weights between neurons. We then applied a threshold to these connection weights to create a connection (adjacency) matrix representing the neuron network (Fig. 1a, see Methods). Partial noise correlation has been demonstrated to be an effective measure of the functional connectivity in neuron networks at the scale of a few hundred neurons64. While it does not necessarily represent monosynaptic connection, it reflects the influence of one neuron on another, significantly reducing the impact of common input and task-related modulation. On average, these PPC neuron networks consist of approximately 144 neurons (144 ± 22), and the sparsity of their connection matrix was 0.048 ± 0.013. The global connectivity within each network remained consistent throughout the successive learning days (Supplementary Fig. 2a, r = 0.688 ± 0.030). Notably, connection weights within the PPC neuron networks were stronger between neurons that exhibited similar encoding selectivity during monkeys’ task performance, compared to neurons with opposite encoding preferences (Fig. 1b, c), which is consistent with the previous finding that neurons exhibited similar neural encoding were more correlated28,63. Additionally, the connection weights did not significantly correlate with the spatial distance between neurons within most FOVs (Supplementary Fig. 2b). We next applied graph theoretical analysis to the PPC neuron networks, revealing significant deviations from random network properties. All observed networks demonstrated substantially greater standard deviations in average connection degrees compared to matched random networks (Poisson and Binomial distributions with equivalent connection degrees; Fig. 1d). The distribution of connection degrees consistently showed heavy-tailed characteristics (Fig. 1e). Notably, all the PPC neuron networks exhibited small-world properties (Fig. 1f): clustering coefficients, quantifying the degree of local clustering, were approximately six-fold greater than those of random networks, while the characteristic short-path lengths were comparable to those of random networks. This combination resulted in small-worldness values several times greater than those of random networks.

Interestingly, within each network, we identified a subset of neurons which are densely connected with each other and with other neurons, while the remaining neurons were more sparsely connected (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). By maximizing the coreness statistic—ensuring maximal hub-to-hub and minimal non-hub-to-non-hub connections (see “Methods”)—we classified these two subsets as hub and non-hub neurons, which were present in roughly equal numbers within each network. Across all the PPC neuron network, these two subsets of neurons exhibited a comparable ratio of positive connections to the total connections of each neuron (Supplementary Fig. 3c). The hub and non-hub topology of network organization was further supported by calculating the Q(core) coefficient, which quantifies the goodness of fit for the core/periphery model to the network, for all (but one) the networks (Fig. 1h). Although hub and non-hub neurons were spatially overlapped in the cortex (Supplementary Fig. 4), hub neurons exhibited significantly greater connection strength and degree compared to non-hub neurons (Fig. 1i-j, P(strength) = 1.6e-53, t(58) = 59.1, P(degree) = 1.2e-46, t(58) = 44.7, paired t-test). Meanwhile, hub neurons were crucial for maintaining the small-world properties of the identified neuronal network. Progressively ablating hub neurons (the top 1–10% highest centrality) led to a gradual decrease in the clustering coefficient and a gradual increase in the short-path length of the network (Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, connections between hub neurons were more stable, gaining or losing connections less frequently across learning days compared to connections between hub and non-hub neurons or between non-hub neurons (Fig. 1k, l). Interestingly, hub neurons exhibited greater fluorescence luminance and larger size on average than non-hub neurons (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), potentially indicating physiological difference between the two subsets. However, these differences in luminance and size did not substantially contribute to the neurons’ hub and non-hub status (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d).

Furthermore, modularity analysis revealed significant modular organization in all PPC neuron networks (Fig. 1m, example network), with modularity coefficients substantially exceeding those of random networks (Fig. 1g, P = 5.0e-34, t(58) = 26.4, t-test). On average, within-module connections showed significantly stronger weights and higher density, along with shorter path lengths, compared to between-module connections (Fig. 1n-pP(weight) = 1.1e-46, t(58) = 44.8, P(density) = 1.6e-49, t(58) = 50.3, P(path length) = 3.0e-31, t(58) =−23.4, paired t-test). The modular organization of the identified networks remained stable over two successive learning days for all FOVs (Supplementary Fig. 7a, r = 0.629 ± 0.032). Moreover, spatial clustering of neurons within modules was also evident (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Together, these results demonstrate that the PPC neuronal network in behaving monkeys exhibits a classical small-world topology.

Hub neurons exhibited greater and more stable encodings on task variables

Considering the phenomenon where hub neurons exhibit higher connection degrees and strengths, we hypothesize that these neurons facilitate global information processing within local networks through two distinct mechanisms compared to non-hub neurons. First, hub neurons likely contribute more significantly to encoding information critical for mediating task performance. Second, they may play a dominant role in driving the activity dynamics within local network. To test these hypotheses, we initially examined how neurons encoded key task variables during the ISAL task, such as saccade direction and trial outcome, across each network. We found that the encoding strength for both task variables positively correlated with each neuron’s centrality (Supplementary Fig. 8), a fundamental measure of hubness, defined as the sum of the absolute values of its connections. Consequently, hub neurons exhibited significantly stronger encoding of both saccade direction and trial outcome across most networks (Fig. 2a-b: example neurons; Fig. 2c-d: example session; Fig. 2e-f: population). The functional importance of these hubs was further validated through network ablation: selectively removing the hub neurons (top 1%-10% highest centrality) triggered a systematic decline in the population’s overall encoding strength for both variables (Fig. 2g-h). Previous studies have demonstrated that the neural encoding in PPC evolved across different time periods during learning63. We therefore examined the stability of neuronal encodings at the population level across different time periods separately for both hub and non-hub neurons through two complementary approaches. First, we assessed correlations in the patterns of neuronal selectivity for both saccade direction and trial outcome across different time periods within and between recording sessions (see Methods). We observed greater correlations among hub neurons compared to non-hub neurons in most networks, whether across different time periods within a single recording session or across successive learning days (Fig. 2i-j). This indicates greater encoding stability in hub neurons during learning. Second, we evaluated the stability of population neural codes by measuring the alignment between neural subspaces of population activity across different time periods. Using an alignment index ranging from 0 (perfect orthogonality) to 1 (perfect alignment; see Methods), we found that hub neurons consistently showed significantly higher alignment indices than non-hub neurons, both within individual learning session and between successive learning days (Fig. 2k-l), further confirming more stable neuronal encoding in hub neurons. Together, the stronger and more stable neuronal encodings indicate that hub neurons might play a primary role in encoding key task variables to mediate the neural computations within local network.

Hub neurons contributed more to the activity dynamics within network

Next, we tested the hypothesis that hub neurons dominate the activity dynamics within network during the monkeys’ task performance and learning. Initially, we examined the effect of hub versus non-hub neurons on the population activity dynamics within network. We performed principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce the dimensionality of the population activity during monkeys’ task performance and projected it onto the top 10 PCs that explained the majority of the variance in population activity (91.4%). This revealed that hub neurons contributed significantly more to the dynamics of the population activity compared to non-hub neurons, as reflected by their stronger weighting on these PCs (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 9). To further decompose task-related components of activity, we conducted a de-mixed PCA analysis, which isolated saccade-direction encoding, outcome encoding, and time-dependent activity for each recording session. Consistently, hub neurons demonstrated markedly stronger contributions to both task-variable encodings and temporal dynamics than non-hub neurons (Fig. 3b-d). Together, these findings indicate that hub neurons exert a dominant influence over population-level activity dynamics during the monkeys’ task performance. We then measured how each neuron’s current activity influenced the future activity of other neurons within the local network using a causality index. This index—a variant of Granger causality65–67—is defined as the partial correlation between a neuron’s activity in the current time period and the activity of other neurons in future time periods within the same task trial, conditioned on the current activity of these neurons (see “Methods”). Like Granger causality, this index quantifies directional influence strength between time series, with higher absolute value indicating greater influence of a neuron’s activity on the activity dynamics of other neurons over time. We computed the causality index across successive time frames for both hub and non-hub neurons. Across networks, hub neurons exhibited significantly higher outgoing causality (influencing other neurons) but lower incoming causality (being influenced) compared to non-hub neurons. (Fig. 3e-f, Supplementary Fig. 10a-c). The observed causal lead from hub neuron to non-hub neurons was independently corroborated by classic Granger causality analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10d). These results suggest that, during the monkeys’ task performance, hub neurons exerted a stronger influence on the activity dynamics of other neurons, while non-hub neurons were more susceptible to influence by other neurons.

Furthermore, we calculated the causality index using data from two successive learning days to assess the influence of hub and non-hub neurons on changes in neuronal encoding during learning. To achieve this, the causality index was computed based on neuronal selectivity to different task variables (see “Methods”). Specifically, we quantified how the activity of one neuron influences the evolution of neural encoding in another neuron from one learning day to the next, which we termed the “exerted learning effect”. Conversely, we assessed how the evolution of neural encoding in one neuron, from the previous learning day to the current day, is influenced by the activity of another neuron from the previous day, referring to this as the “received learning effect”. We found that hub neurons exhibited greater exerted learning effects and greater received learning effects compared to non-hub neurons, as reflected by higher absolute values of the causality index (Fig. 3g, h, Supplementary Fig. 10e, f). Notably, the greater received learning effect in hub neurons was observed only during successive learning days when the monkeys were learning the same associations, and was absent when they transitioned from familiar associations to new ones (Fig. 3iP(learning) = 5.3e-05, t(34) = 4.6; P(transition) = 0.85, t(11) = 0.19, paired t-test). Additionally, hub neurons showed greater exerted learning effects when quantifying the influence of neuronal activity on activity evolution from the first to the second half of one learning day (Fig. 3jP = 2.8e-17, t(58) = 12.0, paired t-test). Together, these findings suggest that hub neurons play a dominant role in shaping the evolution of neuronal encoding within the local network during learning.

Transitions of neurons’ hub/non-hub status across successive learning days

Interestingly, the network’s hub/non-hub architecture showed partial consistency across consecutive learning days (Supplementary Fig. 11a, φ = 0.502 ± 0.029). The hub/non-hub status of the neurons exhibited dynamic reorganization during learning, with neurons transitioning between hub and non-hub status. Specifically, some neurons lost hub status (hub-losing neurons), while others gained it (hub-gaining neurons). Meanwhile, other subsets of neurons maintained their hub/non-hub status across successive learning days: stable hub neurons (consistently identified as hubs) and stable non-hub neurons (which retained their non-hub status). Consistent with these topological changes, hub-gaining neurons acquired more network connections than other neurons across successive learning days (Supplementary Fig. 11b), while hub-losing neurons lost network connections more frequently than both stable hub neurons and hub-gaining neurons (Supplementary Fig. 11c, P(hub-losing vs. stable hub) = 2.3e-25, t(46) = −21.2, P(hub-losing vs. hub-gaining) = 8.5e-23, t(46) = 18.4, paired t-test).

We then quantified topological differences between these neuron subsets using the diversity coefficient, which quantifies each neuron’s inter-module connections relative to its within-module connections (see “Methods”). Hub neurons exhibited significantly higher average diversity coefficients than non-hub neurons (Fig. 4a, P = 2.3e-30, t(46) = 27.7, paired t-test), indicating that non-hub neurons were more likely to remain confined within a module, whereas hub neurons formed broader inter-module connections. Specifically, the diversity coefficient was significantly higher in stable hub neurons compared to hub-losing neurons (Fig. 4b, P = 6.5e-6, t(46) = 5.1, paired t-test), as well as in hub-gaining neurons compared to stable non-hub neurons (P = 1.7e-6, t(46) = 5.5, paired t-test). Critically, hub-losing neurons exhibited reduced negative inter-module connections but maintained similar positive inter-module connections relative to stable hub neurons (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 11d, e, P(positive) = 0.61, t(46) = 0.52, P(negative) = 1.6e-10, t(46) = 8.2, paired t-test). This resulted in hub-losing neurons exhibiting the strongest dominance of positive over negative inter-module connections among all neuron subsets (Fig. 4d). Similarly, hub-gaining neurons showed a greater dominance of positive over negative inter-module connections compared to stable non-hub neurons (P = 0.0011, t(46) = 3.5, paired t-test). Consistent with the dynamics of the hub/non-hub status across learning days, hub-losing neurons exhibited significantly weaker encoding of the task variables after the transition compared to the preceding day (Fig. 4e, f), whereas hub-gaining neurons exhibited significantly stronger task-related encoding following the transition (Fig. 4g, h). Furthermore, stable hub neurons displayed stronger connection weights and greater neuronal encoding of key task variables compared to hub-losing neurons (Supplementary Fig. 11f-h). Similarly, hub-gaining neurons exhibited a trend toward stronger connection strengths and neural encoding than stable non-hub neurons (Supplementary Fig. 11i, k). Additionally, stable hub neurons demonstrated greater encoding stability than other subsets of neurons (Supplementary Fig. 11l, P = 4.2e-8, t(46) = 6.6, paired t-test).

Interestingly, neurons that transitioned between hub and non-hub statuses exhibited distinct patterns of influence on changes in neuronal encoding across successive learning days. Hub-losing neurons demonstrated greater exerted learning effects than other neurons, suggesting that those hub neurons that exert a stronger influence on others’ activity are more likely to lose their hub status during learning (Fig. 4i, P = 8.7e-7, t(140) = 5.1, paired t-test). Conversely, hub-gaining neurons exhibited greater received learning effects, indicating that non-hub neurons more influenced by others’ activity are more likely to assume a hub role after learning (Fig. 4j, P = 7.8e-8, t(140) = -5.7, paired t-test). Notably, hub-losing neurons exhibited greater exerted learning effects than stable hub neurons (P = 0.035, t(34) = 2.2, paired t-test) despite weaker neural encodings. Similarly, hub-gaining neurons received greater learning effects than stable non-hub neurons (P = 0.041, t(34) = -2.1, paired t-test), despite stronger neuronal encodings. This suggests that neurons that switched between hub and non-hub statuses during successive learning days were more likely to exert a stronger influence on, or receive a greater influence from, other neurons’ activity during learning.

Moreover, the greater exerted learning effect in hub-losing neurons and greater received learning effect in hub-gaining neurons was observed only during the learning days when the monkeys were learning the same associations, absent during the successive days when they transitioned from familiar to novel associations (Fig. 4k-l). Together, these results suggest that the changing dynamics of neurons’ hub/non-hub status across successive learning days were not random fluctuations, but were instead shaped by their inter-modular connectivity. Specifically, these status transitions closely linked to the neurons’ differential influence on the evolution of neuronal encodings of other neurons, particularly during the acquisition of novel associations.

Modular computation within the PPC neural network

We next investigated how the modular structure of the PPC neuron network facilitates modular neural computation. An intuitive expectation is that modular connectivity patterns form function-specified groups within local networks, which enable functional segregation during neural computation. To test it, we quantified the similarity of neuronal encodings among individual neurons both within and across modules. We observed significantly higher signal correlation and noise correlation between neurons within the same module compared to those across different modules in most networks (Fig. 5a, b, P(signal) = 2.0e-22, t(58) = 15.6, P(noise) = 1.6e-27, t(58) = 19.8, paired t-test). Neuronal representations of saccade direction and trial outcome were partially separated between different modules (Fig. 5c, P = 6.2e-10, t(58) = 7.4, paired t-test). Additionally, PPC neurons exhibited more consistent neuronal encodings on individual task variables within modules than across modules, with greater neural selectivity similarity observed for both saccade direction and trial outcome (Fig. 5d-e, P(saccade) = 4.9e-23, t(58) = 16.1, P(outcome) = 3.3e-21, t(58) = 14.7, paired t-test). Furthermore, changes in neuronal encodings were more similar between neurons within the same module compared to neurons from different modules, both within a single learning day and across successive learning days (Fig. 5f, g, P(within session) = 2.5e-21, t(58) = 14.8, P(across session) = 1.9e-15, t(46) = 11.7, paired t-test). Furthermore, we examined whether the modular connectivity constrained the activity dynamics of individual neurons during monkeys’ task performance and learning. We predicted stronger mutual influence between neurons from the same module (within-module pair) than between neurons from different modules (across-module pair). Specifically, neurons may drive the activity of other neurons within the same module to be more similar to their own activity compared to neurons from different modules. To test this, we calculated the causality index separately for within-module and across-module pairs using condition-averaged activity across successive time frames within a task trial. We found a higher causality index for the within-module pairs compared to the across-module pairs in most networks (Fig. 5h, P(+1f) = 2.3e-25, t(58) = 18.0, P(+2f) = 3.4e-23, t(58) = 16.2, P(+3f) = 3.7e-20, t(58) = 13.9, paired t-test). This heightened influence within modules was independently corroborated by Granger causality analysis (Supplementary Fig. 12). These results suggest that within-module pairs had a stronger mutual influence on their activity dynamics during monkeys’ task performance.

Additionally, we calculated causality index to quantify how neurons’ activity influenced the changes in other neurons’ encodings of task variables either from the first to the second halves of a single learning session or from the current to the next learning days. This also revealed greater causality indexes for the within-module pairs compared to the across-module pairs (Fig. 5i-j, P(within session) = 2.2e-18, t(58) = 12.7, P(across session) = 3.4e-11, t(46) = 8.6, paired t-test), indicating that within-module pairs had a greater mutual impact on the evolution of neuronal encoding during learning compared to across-module pairs. These results collectively demonstrate that neurons within the same module were functionally more similar and exerted a stronger mutual influence on activity dynamics and neural encodings than those across modules during the monkeys’ task performance and learning.

Network topology predicted the similarity of neuronal encodings and monkeys’ behavioral performance during learning

To further investigate how small-world network topology influences the functional roles of PPC neurons and shapes activity evolution during learning, we tested whether the topological position of neurons could predict the similarity of their neural encodings during learning. Specifically, we employed a linear model incorporating parameters of network topology to predict the signal correlations between all pairs of recorded neurons for each PPC network (see Methods). This model primarily considers four topology parameters for each pair of neurons: the hub and non-hub status, the modular organization, the relative connection weight between them, and their topological overlap.

Our model significantly predicted the signal correlation between neurons within the PPC neuron network. On average, these four parameters explained more than 19% of the variance in signal correlation between all neurons, which was significantly greater than the shuffled control (Fig. 6a). For most networks, all four parameters contributed significantly to the model’s performance (Supplementary Fig. 13). Additionally, these four parameters also significantly predicted the similarity of neuronal encodings on the following learning days. Finally, we examined the relationship between network topology and the monkeys’ daily behavioral performance during learning. Using a stepwise linear model with a set of PPC network topological parameters, we predicted the monkeys’ daily performance and applied the Bayesian Information Criterion to identify the most relevant variables (see Methods). This revealed that the small-world topology of the PPC neuron network significantly predicted the monkeys’ performance accuracy during associative learning, with the characteristic path length, modularity, and their interaction as the significant predictors (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 14a, R² = 0.33, P < 0.001, permutation test). Specifically, as the monkeys’ performance accuracy improved across learning days, the averaged short-path length of the network decreased, and network modularity became less pronounced (Supplementary Table 1). The observed decrease in network short path length with increasing behavioral accuracy was not driven by changes in connection density over the course of learning, as similar results were obtained after normalizing short path length by the average connection density of the entire network (Supplementary Table 2). This suggests that global information integration and communication within the local PPC neuron network became more efficient with learning.

To further investigate the relationship between PPC network topology and the monkeys’ choice strategies during associative learning, we employed the dynamic PsyTrack model to fit the monkeys’ daily choice accuracy68 (see “Methods”). This resulted in two weights, Wstimulus and Wbias, reflecting the influence of visual stimuli and internal bias on the monkeys’ saccade choices, respectively. We modeled these weights independently using linear models incorporating PPC network topological features. This revealed that connection density, short-path length, and modularity coefficient were significant predictors of the monkeys’ choice bias (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 14b, R² = 0.23, P = 0.004, permutation test). Specifically, the monkeys showed a greater saccade choice bias toward the contralateral target when connection density was higher, short-path length was smaller, and network modularity was more pronounced. In contrast, short-path length, modularity coefficient, and their interaction were strong predictors of correct choices based on stimuli (Supplementary Table 1, 2, Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 14c, R² = 0.37, P < 0.001, permutation test). Improved choice performance was associated with more efficient global integration (smaller short-path length) and less local computation (weaker modularity) within the local neuron network.

Discussion

Together, we constructed the functional neuron network in the monkey PPC during long-term learning using partial noise correlation. We found that PPC neuron networks exhibited small-world properties, with network topology significantly shaping the functional roles and activity dynamics of individual neurons during the monkeys’ task performance and learning. Specifically, the densely connected hub neurons played a primary role in representing key task variables and driving the activity dynamics to support global information processing, while the modular structure formed the basis of neuron ensembles, enabling modular information processing within the local network. Notably, the small-world network dynamics across learning days not only captured how inter-neuronal interactions shaped the evolution of neuronal encoding during novel association learning but also reflected the monkeys’ behavioral performance and learning strategies, with global neural computation within local networks becoming increasingly efficient as learning advanced. These results provide direct evidence in behaving animal showing how the topology organization of brain network at single-neuron resolution facilitates both global and modular information processing to mediate task performance and learning, establishing a mechanistic link between microscale network organization, neural computation, and behavior.

Functional connectivity within neuronal ensembles has been widely investigated using diverse statistical approaches3,10,30,33,42,47,64,69,70. Prior work commonly conceptualizes activity correlations as covariance-based measures that impact neural computation and behavioral performance25,26,28. Particularly, distinct patterns of noise correlations have been shown to shape population-level information encoding and readout26,27,31,34; positive correlations within neuron ensembles can facilitate task performance, whereas negative correlations may impair it by destabilizing ensemble dynamics32. Building on these foundational insights, our study introduces a conceptual shift by leveraging sophisticate correlation structure to define and interrogate an explicit mesoscale neural network architecture based on graph theoretical approaches. We systematically investigated how the network’s topological features such as hub status and modular organization, as well as their dynamics, relate to neuronal encoding, inter-neuron communication, long-term plasticity, and behavioral performance throughout learning. Recent studies have also begun to interpret functional connectivity through the lens of network topology. For example, an electrophysiological study in behaving monkeys demonstrated that functional networks in the frontoparietal cortex exhibit small-world organization and highlighted the pivotal role of rich-club hub neurons in driving oscillatory dynamics30. Together, these studies and our findings highlight the necessity of understanding sophisticated network architecture to fully characterize neural computation and its behavioral consequences.

Small-world topology, well-documented across different brain networks1,2,5,10,12–17,20–22,30,47,49,71–75, is now demonstrated at the neuronal level through two-photon calcium imaging in behaving monkeys, supporting its role as a fundamental principle of brain organization. While previous work has established the benefits of small-world architecture for efficient information transfer, synchronization, and robustness with minimal wiring costs in various neural processes5,50,51, our study reveals how this topology shapes both individual neuron functions and inter-neuron interactions. Specifically, we uncover mechanisms by which how hub-dominated global processing and module-specific segregation emerge as organizational principles that constrain neuronal encodings, regulate information flow, and govern local activity dynamics - providing a cellular-level explanation for how neural computations emerge to support behavior and learning.

Hub nodes represent fundamental network features that serve critical integrative functions. In macroscopic brain networks, hub areas form densely interconnected core circuit cross-linking all major functional subsystems12,13,74,75. At the cellular level, hub neurons - identified both in vitro and in the brain13,15,30,42,47,73,76,77 - play leading roles in neural synchrony and in brain state transition30,77. Particularly, GABAergic hub neurons in hippocampal-entorhinal circuits have been shown to drive network dynamics and spontaneously coordinate neuronal synchronization70,76. Similarly, rich-club neurons in primate frontoparietal cortex were shown to play a dominate role in neural oscillations30. Building on previous findings, we identified hub neurons based on the architecture of functional connectivity, which may include both excitatory and inhibitory subtypes, each exhibiting extensive connectivity within the network. We demonstrate that hub neurons in the PPC play a primary role in encoding key task variables that mediate an animal’s behavioral performance, as well as guiding the activity dynamics and the evolution of neuronal representations during animal’s behavior performance and learning. Together, these results suggest that hubs are crucial network structures that significantly contribute to overall brain activity and animal behavior.

Modular organization - a hallmark of small-world networks - enables parallel, specialized processing in the brain1,8,10,72. This architecture has been observed across scales, from macroscopic networks of functionally related brain regions to mesoscopic neuronal circuits15,16,30,41,71–73,78. At the macroscopic scale, network modules typically comprise anatomically and/or functionally related brain areas that may support specific behavioral functions, allowing for rapid and efficient information sharing among regions involved in a common set of tasks or responses1,5,8,10,72,79. Consistently, we demonstrate that microscale modules in the PPC exhibit similar principles: neurons within the same module showed greater functional similarity (evidenced by shared encoding patterns) and stronger mutual influence on both activity dynamics and neuronal encoding during the monkeys’ task performance and learning than those across modules. These results collectively establish modularity as a conserved organizational principle that facilitates functional segregation across different levels of neural computation.

The functional brain network is dynamic, evolving over time2,8,9,17,80. It has been shown to gradually develop during brain maturation and undergo significant changes across different behavioral and brain states8,41,81–83. A previous study has demonstrated that computing neuron hubs are dynamic, with a neuron’s hub status potentially changing across different brain substates or even swapping roles77. Consistent with, and extending previous research, we observed changes in the hub/non-hub status of PPC neurons across days, which were closely correlated with the evolution of neuronal encoding and information flow during long-term learning. Specifically, changes in neurons’ hub or non-hub status were associated with shifts in their functional roles during task performance, as the strength of their encoding of key task variables increased after gaining hub status or decreased after losing it. Furthermore, hub-losing neurons exerted a greater influence on the evolution of neuronal encoding of other neurons within the local network, while hub-gaining neurons received stronger influences from other neurons in shaping their neuronal encoding. Notably, these dynamic neurons exhibited a higher proportion of positive (over negative) inter-module connections than neurons with stable hub/non-hub status. This suggests that a neuron’s topological transitions—and corresponding changes in information encoding during learning—may be constrained by its involvement in inter-module communication.

Importantly, we found that the small-world topology of PPC neuron networks predicted the monkeys’ daily behavioral performance during sensorimotor associative learning. Global neural computation within these networks became increasingly efficient over time, potentially supporting more effective information processing and the emergence of proficient, task-adapted behaviors. We speculate that these learning-induced changes in network topology may partially revert after a long period without task training, while still exhibiting slow, spontaneous drift over time. Our results indicate a potential principle whereby topological evolution of local circuits drives the refinement of neuronal representations during long-term learning. Supporting this idea, previous fMRI studies have reported increased local efficiency—closely related to local clustering—in the frontal cortex following motor learning84 and in the superior temporal cortex after spoken language learning85. While our findings appear to diverge from previous reports, these discrepancies may reflect differences in spatial scale—our micro-scale measurements versus macroscopic network observations—as well as differences in brain regions and learning paradigms. Additionally, our recordings were restricted to superficial cortical layers, which may exhibit distinct plastic properties compared to deeper layers or whole-region averages. Furthermore, studies of motor learning have observed diverse topological changes, including increased clustering coefficients, greater connection density and strength, shorter communication paths, and altered centrality86,87. Future research should explore the specific synaptic rules governing these topological changes during learning. We propose that the small-world topology may arise from a synergy between local Hebbian reinforcement of functional clusters and homeostatic pruning of redundant long-range paths. Additionally, Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity could facilitate “rich-get-richer” dynamics—a form of preferential attachment where synchronized firing drives the emergence of hubs88,89. Conversely, hub status may be lost via homeostatic scaling or metabolic constraints to prevent runaway excitation and maintain network economy5,90. Bridging these micro-scale synaptic mechanisms with meso-scale and macro-scale network dynamics is essential for a comprehensive understanding of learning.

Together, our study demonstrates that small-world functional network topology in behaving animals actively shapes functional roles of single neuron, inter-neuron communication, and long-term plasticity that support task performance and learning. These findings establish a fundamental mechanistic link between mesoscale functional connectivity, neural computation, and behavior from an integrated network perspective, and introduce a new framework for tracking single-neuron plasticity at the functional graph level to uncover the mechanisms underlying learning. Future studies should investigate how network organizations differ across neuron types (e.g., excitatory vs. inhibitory), cortical layers, and brain regions. While virtual node ablation in network models can, in principle, be used to probe the theoretical contributions of specific topological elements to neural computation, in vivo neural networks may dynamically reorganize when subsets of neurons are perturbed or removed. Consequently, causal manipulations through targeted single-neuron interventions, combined with structural network mapping, are essential to uncover how network topology enables interactions among neural elements, providing mechanistic insights into the emergence of diverse forms of neural computation.

Methods

Animal preparation and behavioral training

The procedures are described in detail in our previous study63, but here we will only provide a brief summary. Two male Rhesus Macaque monkeys, aged 4–5 years and weighing 5–6 kg, were trained on the behavioral task. Prior to the training, three head-posts were implanted on the monkeys’ skulls in an inverted Y-shape configuration. A T-shaped steel frame was then attached to these head-posts to enhance head fixation stability. Our surgical and behavioral methods have been described in detail previously. The monkeys were individually housed in cages and kept under a 12-h light/dark cycle. Behavioral training and experimental recordings were conducted exclusively during the light phase. The monkeys were seated comfortably and head-fixed in a custom-made primate chair inside a dark experimental rig. Task stimuli were displayed on a 27-in. color LCD monitor (1920 × 1080 resolution, 75 Hz refresh rate, 45 cm viewing distance). A solenoid-operated reward system was used to deliver juice rewards to the monkeys. Eye positions were monitored using an optical eye tracker (ISCAN ETL-200) at a sampling rate of 240 Hz and stored for offline analysis. Stimulus presentation, task events, rewards, and behavioral data acquisition were controlled by an Intel-based PC running MonkeyLogic software in MATLAB (http://www.monkeylogic.net). All experimental and surgical procedures were approved by the Peking University Animal Care and Use Committee.

Behavioral tasks

Image saccade association learning task (ISAL)

The task sequence and stimuli are described in detail in our previous study63. Briefly, monkeys were trained to associate visual images with two saccade directions in each learning phase. On each trial, they made a saccade to one of two targets based on the presented image (Supplementary Fig. 1). Each trial began with a central fixation point, which the monkeys had to maintain until making their saccadic choice. After 1500 ms of fixation, a visual image appeared on the screen’s contralateral side (relative to the recorded hemisphere) for 500 ms. Following a 1-second delay, the fixation point disappeared, and two saccade targets appeared simultaneously on the left and right sides of the screen, positioned orthogonally to the image’s orientation. Monkeys had to saccade to the target associated with the image within 400 ms and maintain fixation for 1 second to receive a juice reward, delivered immediately after target disappearance. Although no explicit feedback was provided, the monkeys quickly learned trial outcomes (correct or erroneous). Once they mastered one pair of image-saccade associations, they proceeded to learn two novel associations in subsequent sessions.

Task training

The task training sequence is described in detail in our previous study63. During the course of neural recordings, two monkeys learned a total of 12 pairs of novel image-saccade associations (Monkey H: 3 pairs, Monkey X: 11 pairs). This allowed us to consistently track the same neurons across all 12 subregions of area 7a throughout the entire learning process. During each learning phase, the monkeys were initially tested with the ISAL task using previously learned (familiar) associations on the first day. On subsequent days, they were introduced to a new pair of associations until they achieved full proficiency. Importantly, within each recording session, the image-saccade associations remained constant.

Neural recording

Virus injection and chamber implanting

We implemented two-photon calcium imaging recording during monkeys performing the ISAL task, in order to track the neuronal activity across different days. Detailed procedures for the surgical and recording processes are elaborated upon in the previous studies63,91. In brief, we conducted two surgeries on each animal to inject the virus for transferring the calcium indicator into cortex and to implant the optical recording chamber, respectively. During the first surgery, a craniotomy was executed on the skull over area 7a in the right hemisphere of each monkey. We located area 7a according to the macaque brain atlas, where it is defined as the posterior portion of the inferior parietal lobule92. Anatomically, area 7a lies posterior to and inferior to the intraparietal sulcus, anterior to the parieto-occipital sulcus, and dorsal to the superior temporal sulcus. Our recordings targeted the exposed cortical surface of area 7a, which is readily accessible for two-photon calcium imaging. Following the dura mater’s exposure, we injected 50–100 nL AAV9-Syn-GCaMP6f-WPRE-SV40 into cortex at a depth of approximately 400 μm. Approximately 45 days after virus injection, we performed the second surgery to implant the recording chamber. A glass coverslip glued in a titanium ring with silicone adhesive was used to gently cover the cortex.

Two-photon calcium imaging recording

The procedures for neural imaging were described in detailed in our previous study. In brief, we conducted the two-photon calcium imaging recording experiment using a Thorlabs two-photon microscope and a Ti:Sapphire laser (Mai Tai eHP, Spectra Physics) approximately one week after the implantation of the recording chamber. In each recording session, a cortical area of 800 μm × 800 μm was imaged at 30 Hz using a 16× objective lens (0.8 N.A., Nikon), and then averaged each 4 frames resulted in 7.5 frames/s. The recording depth ranged from 200 μm to 300 μm beneath the arachnoid membrane. A standard slow galvo scan was used to obtain static images of neurons with high resolution (1024* 1024). Approximately every 5–10 min, we manually corrected slow drifts in the field of view (FOV) by comparing them to a reference image. In total, we consistently monitored the activities of the same neurons throughout a full learning course spanning a period of 3 to 7 days within 12 FOVs. This resulted in 59 recording sessions from two monkeys (2 FOVs and 7 recording sessions for Monkey H and 10 FOVs and 52 recording sessions for Monkey X). To ensure alignment with the same imaging plane on consecutive days, we initially performed coarse alignment based on the recording coordinates, blood vessels, pia mater, and arachnoid, followed by precise alignment to reference images.

Data analysis

Behavioral performance

We included only those recording sessions in which the monkeys completed a sufficient number of trials (n ≥ 800 for Monkey H and n ≥ 700 for Monkey X) in our subsequent analyses. The monkeys’ performance accuracy was calculated as the ratio of correct trials to the total number of completed trials, including both correct and erroneous trials. To determine whether the monkeys had fully acquired a pair of image-saccade associations, we applied two criteria: 1) the monkey’s average accuracy throughout the session exceeded 0.8, and 2) the monkey’s average accuracy reached 0.9 over a continuous period of at least 200 trials.

Dynamic model fitting

As described in our previous study, we fitted monkeys’ performance accuracy with a dynamic Bernoulli generalized linear model, characterized by a set of smoothly evolving psychophysical weights, in order to capture the dynamics of monkeys’ behavior strategies during learning. We used “PsyTrack” package, which was proven suitable for parametrizing the time-varying behavior in learning task (https://github.com/nicholas-roy/PsyTrack). This model describes choice behavior at the resolution of single trials, allowing us to quantify the evolution of animal’s decision strategies (represented by different weights) across the learning course. Specifically, we utilized the task condition (Cue identity) as input for the model, denoting each single trial as either −1 or 1, while the monkeys’ choice (Saccade direction) served as the model’s output, labeled as 0 or 1 for each single trial. We determined two behavioral weights, namely the bias weight and stimulus weight, through model fitting.

Neuronal data analysis

Identify single neurons within each session and across sessions during learning

The procedures for identifying single neurons were described in detail in our previous study63. We used an image processing software (suite2p), for motion correction, definition of putative cell bodies, and extraction of fluorescence traces. We extracted the single neuron activity within each FOV based on three primary steps. First, the raw data streaming from the microscope system was converted into ome.tif format using Fiji software, and then transferred to ‘suite2p’ for offline processing, which included motion correction, extraction of neuronal signals, and denoising. Second, we manually validated the cell bodies (ROIs) candidates generated from suite2p, and then employed a machine learning approach to optimize parameters tailored to the data recorded from different subregions of area 7a in each monkey. These refined parameters were subsequently applied to “suite2p” to reprocess the data again, generating new ROIs for each data session. Lastly, we manually inspected and selected ROIs once more to ensure that all identified ROIs exhibited satisfactory neuron morphology and met the quality criteria. We manually filtered the results by excluding ROIs that were either smaller than 5 pixels in diameter, smaller than 15 pixels² in area, or displayed clearly abnormal, non-cellular shapes.

Following the selection of ROIs for each recording session, we proceeded to align the neurons across all the recording sessions/days throughout the learning course for each tested 7a FOVs. This involved the following steps. 1) We utilized ‘suite2p’ to generate a cross-day classifier by leveraging data from all sessions/days within each learning course for each 7a subregion. 2) Subsequently, we imported the cross-day classifier into “suite2p” to generate ROIs for each individual day within the learning course. 3) We manually cross-referenced and matched the neurons (ROIs) among different recording sessions within each learning course, based on their spatial location in the reference plane and their morphology. If a neuron could not be confidently identified in one of the sessions within the learning course, it was excluded from consideration for all recording sessions within that learning course. As discussed in detail in our previous study63, we have confidence that the observed changes in functional neuron network for each FOV during the learning course are unlikely to be primarily attributable to fluctuations in the recording quality across different recording sessions.

As described in our previous study63, we implemented a high-pass filter on the fluorescence trace of each neuron, effectively eliminating very slow dynamics within each recording session (High pass filter, 0.005 Hz), in order to mitigate the potential noise resulted from factors such as slow drifts in the imaging plane’s focus and light bleaching.

Quantify the encodings of key task variables

Identify outcome selectivity

To identify neurons with significant encoding of trial outcome, we applied a Wilcoxon Ranksum test to assess activity between the correct and error trials in the task period following target offset (1–16 frames (0–2.2 s) after target offset). We further divided this period into four smaller windows, each comprising 4 frames. If statistically significant differential activity between correct and error trials was observed within at least one of these windows (P < 0.01, Bonferroni corrected), we classified the neuron as outcome-selective.

Identify saccade direction selectivity

To identify neurons exhibiting significant saccade direction encoding, we employed a Wilcoxon Ranksum test to compare neural activities around saccade onset between trials featuring different saccade directions. Specifically, we assessed saccade direction selectivity during both the pre-saccadic period (1-4 frames before saccade onset) and the post-saccadic phase (1-8 frames after saccade onset). While the pre-saccadic epoch was analyzed as a single time window, we divided the post-saccadic epoch into two smaller time windows (4 frames each). Neurons were classified as saccade selective if there was statistically significant differential activity observed between trials with distinct saccade directions in at least one time window (P < 0.01, Bonferroni corrected).

Similar to previous studies, we performed one-way ANOVA on the neuron’s activity within each time frame to quantify the amount of information that each neuron encoded about trial outcome and saccade direction. Construct the functional neuron network

Generating the connection matrix

We quantified the connectivity between simultaneously recorded neurons using partial noise correlation, following these steps: 1) Single-trial responses were estimated by averaging the activity within three time windows (1–4 frames, 5–8 frames, and 9–12 frames before cue onset) during the fixation periods of the ISAL task. 2) A partial correlation algorithm was then applied to the neuron-by-trial matrix to compute correlations (Pearson correlation) for each neuron pair, excluding the influence of all other neurons. 3) The resulting correlation matrix was thresholded based on the p-value of the partial correlations, retaining pairs with P < 0.001 and setting the rest to zero. 4) The diagonals of the matrix were set to zero to prevent self-connections. This undirected matrix was then used as the weighted adjacency matrix in the subsequent analyses of the non-directional graph.

We used the Brain Connectivity Toolbox93 to analyze the resulting functional neuron network and calculate different network properties, with specific details outlined below: The length matrix was constructed using the inverse of the absolute value of the connection weights. The positive connection weights were used to calculate the strength of the nodes and to perform the core-periphery analysis. For community detection, the original weight values (including negative connections) were considered.

Quantify the influence of single neuron on the activity dynamics within network

Signal correlation

To quantify the similarity in neuronal encodings of task variables between neurons identified within the same session or across different sessions, we calculated the signal correlation for each pair of neurons. Unlike classical methods, we incorporated both time and trial conditions into the calculation of signal correlation. Specifically, we first constructed the condition-averaged activity vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\bf{A}}}\in {v}^{{c}_{1}{c}_{2}t}$$\end{document}A∈vc1c2t, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{1}$$\end{document}c1 denotes two saccadic conditions (left and right), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{2}$$\end{document}c2 denotes two outcome conditions (correct and error), and t denotes time (1–12 imaging frame before cue onset, 12 imaging frame between cue onset and saccade onset, 8 imaging frame between saccade and target offset, and 1–16 imaging frame after the target offset). The signal correlation between each pair of neurons was defined as the Pearson correlation between their respective activity vectors. We computed signal correlations for outcome encoding and saccade encoding separately, using different time periods: for outcome encoding, we used the 1–16 imaging frames after target offset; for saccade encoding, we used the 1–8 imaging frames before saccade onset and the 1–8 imaging frames after saccade onset.

Noise correlation

Noise correlations between different neurons were calculated using the neural activity in fixation time period of the ISAL task as for partial noise correlations. Specifically: 1) Single-trial responses were estimated by averaging the activity within three time windows (1-4 frames, 5-8 frames, and 9-12 frames before cue onset) during the ISAL task. 2) Pearson correlations were computed by calculating the pairwise trial-by-trial activity for each neuron pair.

Correlation of changes in neural selectivity

Selectivity changes are defined as the difference in activity vectors between two successive recording sessions or between the first and second halves of a given session. The correlation of selectivity changes between two neurons was calculated by computing the Pearson correlation between their respective differences in activity vectors.

Quantify the relationship between network topology and the monkeys’ daily performance during learning

To capture the dynamics of the monkeys’ choice strategies during learning, we fitted their performance accuracy using a dynamic Bernoulli generalized linear model (“PsyTrack”), characterized by a set of smoothly evolving psychophysical weights. The goal of this model is to estimate weights that reflect the animals’ decision-making strategies on a trial-by-trial basis, using a linear combination of relevant task variables. This approach allows us to quantify how the monkeys’ decision strategies, represented by these weights, evolve across learning periods. Importantly, the magnitude of each weight indicates how strongly the animal relies on the corresponding task variable in its decision-making process. Consistent with our previous study, we used the cue identity as the input to the model and the monkeys’ saccade choice as the output. From this, we derived two behavioral weights: the bias weight and the stimulus weight. These weights represent the extent to which the monkeys’ saccade choices were influenced by internal bias (toward the ipsilateral target relative to the recorded hemisphere) and the visual stimulus, respectively. We then averaged the decision weights for each day to represent the monkeys’ behavioral performance on that day.

Next, we applied generalized linear regression models to fit the monkeys’ daily performance accuracy, as well as the daily averaged stimulus weight and bias weight, using network topology parameters as predictors. Stepwise regression guided by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) was applied to select the most informative variables. Six network topology measures were considered as candidate regressors: rightward skew of the edge weight distribution (modeled by the Generalized Extreme Value distribution), connection density, clustering coefficient, characteristic short-path length, coreness, and modularity. The initial models included an intercept and a linear term for each predictor, and terms were subsequently added or removed one at a time based on their impact on the BIC value.
