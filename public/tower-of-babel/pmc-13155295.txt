Targeting cGAS-STING signaling to overcome antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections: Challenges and opportunities
Authors: Yu Seong Ham, Minseo Jeong, Chul-Su Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increasing antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections pose a global public health challenge that demands therapeutic strategies beyond conventional antibiotics. The cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate synthase stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway is crucial to innate immune defense through cytosolic DNA detection and antimicrobial response initiation. Emerging evidence suggests that antibiotic-resistant bacteria can subvert or overactivate this pathway, leading to immune evasion and excessive inflammation. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, and multidrug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis exploit cGAS-STING signaling to suppress host immunity or trigger damaging hyperinflammatory responses. This highlights the dual nature of the cGAS-STING pathway in bacterial infections. STING agonists may enhance immune responses against persistent infections, and STING inhibitors can mitigate excessive inflammation caused by resistant pathogens. Targeting the cGAS-STING pathway represents a host-directed therapy that modulates host immunity rather than targeting pathogens. Understanding the interplay between cGAS-STING signaling and antibiotic resistance mechanisms is essential for developing next-generation immunotherapeutics to complement conventional antibacterial treatments.

ANTIBIOTIC RESISTANCE: A CRITICAL GLOBAL ISSUE, NECESSITATING THE DEVELOPMENT OF ALTERNATIVE SOLUTIONS

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria are directly responsible for approximately 1.27 million deaths and are associated with 5 million deaths annually worldwide, posing a major public health threat (1). The World Health Organization updated its 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, highlighting the identification of 15 bacterial groups and 24 resistant pathogens urgently requiring therapeutic interventions (2). These include multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria, third-generation cephalosporin-resistant strains, and carbapenem-resistant organisms. Although efforts have focused on finding new therapies, even recent antibiotics designed to counteract earlier resistance are facing emerging resistance (3–5). This resistance cycle highlights the limitations of traditional antibiotic development and the need for alternative strategies beyond targeting pathogens (6).

To combat antibiotic resistance, substantial efforts have been directed toward host-directed therapies (HDTs) (7). Unlike conventional antibiotics targeting pathogens directly, HDTs modulate the host immune response to suppress infections, either by enhancing defense mechanisms or by regulating excessive inflammation (8–10). These interventions aim to improve clinical outcomes, including reduced morbidity, mortality, organ damage, and long-term functional recovery, thereby offering therapeutic potential even when standard antibiotics fail (11). For example, tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α)–neutralizing biologics infliximab are widely used to ameliorate inflammatory pathology and improve outcomes in several immune-mediated diseases and infection-associated complications (12). Most HDT approaches have focused on strengthening fundamental host antimicrobial cellular pathways that control or eliminate infection, including modulation of host kinases, lipid biosynthesis, and epigenetic regulators such as histone deacetylases (13). In parallel, another strategy has emerged that targets early innate immune responses by engaging host pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and initiate antimicrobial defense programs (13). In this context, the cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (GMP-AMP) synthase stimulator of interferon (IFN) genes (cGAS-STING) pathway offers a distinct advantage due to its unique ability to deliver the secondary messenger cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) to adjacent noninfected cells via gap junctions, unlike Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which operate autonomously within individual cells (13). This intercellular signaling effectively inhibits pathogen spread by establishing a preemptive defense in surrounding tissues and enables cGAS-STING–targeted HDT to overcome pathogen evasion strategies, thereby converting immediate innate defense into long-term adaptive immunity (13, 14).

The cGAS-STING pathway is a critical regulator of host defense and plays a pivotal role in antiviral immunity, cancer, autoimmune diseases, and immune responses to bacterial infections (15, 16). cGAS is a cytosolic DNA sensor expressed across diverse immune and nonimmune cell types, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and pancreatic β cells, and the pathway is prominently studied in innate immune cells such as macrophages (16–18). It specifically recognizes double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) in a sequence-independent and length-dependent manner (19, 20). cGAS detects pathogen-derived dsDNA from viruses or bacteria (21–27), as well as mislocalized self-DNA such as DNA within micronuclei or mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) (28, 29). Upon binding to DNA, cGAS catalyzes the synthesis of the noncanonical cyclic dinucleotide (CDN) 2′3′-cGAMP from adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and guanosine triphosphate (17, 30). STING in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) recognizes this secondary messenger, undergoes conformational activation, and subsequently translocates from the ER to the Golgi apparatus (31). At the Golgi apparatus, STING recruits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) to phosphorylate its C-terminal tail (CTT) (32). Phosphorylated CTT serves as a docking site for IFN regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), enabling its activation and dimerization (32). Activated IRF3 translocates to the nucleus, inducing type I IFNs and IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) that restrict bacterial proliferation and pathogenicity (Fig. 1) (22, 30, 32, 33). In addition, STING activation drives nuclear factor κ light-chain enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling, promotes autophagy, modulates inflammation, and restricts pathogen replication, while also contributing to dendritic cell activation and subsequent T cell priming, thereby linking innate immune responses to adaptive immunity (34–36). However, the downstream consequences of targeting the cGAS-STING axis as a host-directed therapeutic are highly dependent on the infecting pathogen and the tissue microenvironment (37). In human myeloid cells, for example, cytosolic DNA sensing can initiate a STING-dependent cell death program characterized by lysosomal translocation and membrane permeabilization, which subsequently induces potassium efflux and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to interleukin-1β (IL-1β) release and inflammatory cell death (37). As a result, STING-driven signaling may promote host defense in certain infection contexts, whereas in others, it shifts toward excessive inflammation and tissue damage rather than controlled immune enhancement (38). In particular, antibiotic-resistant bacteria often acquire adaptations that subvert host immune recognition and killing mechanisms concurrently with resistance traits. For instance, antibiotic-resistant strains such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) evade immunity by down-regulating TLR2 and other key receptors, complicating treatment (39–41). Under such conditions, intracellular sensing pathways that do not rely on surface receptor engagement may become increasingly important. During infection, aberrant exposure of cytosolic DNA, disruption of nuclear or mtDNA homeostasis, bacterial CDN signaling, and the accumulation of intercellularly transferred cGAMP reflect intracellular perturbations capable of activating STING independently of surface PRRs (29, 42, 43). While the cGAS-STING axis is not impervious to pathogen-mediated immune evasion (44), its activation is not governed by a single ligand-dependent interaction but instead arises from the convergence of multiple infection-associated intracellular perturbations (42). Provided that inflammatory risks are carefully controlled, the distinctive features of the cGAS-STING pathway position it as a promising immunotherapeutic target for counteracting bacterial resistance and enhancing immune surveillance.

Accordingly, this review discusses the immunological roles of the cGAS-STING pathway and the factors that modulate its activity in various bacterial infections, including those caused by antibiotic-resistant strains. It further explores the potential and limitations of strategies targeting the cGAS-STING axis for treating resistant infections, thus proposing potential directions for developing nonantibiotic-based therapeutic approaches.

ROLE OF CGAS-STING IN BACTERIAL INFECTIONS

First identified in mammalian cells in 2013, cGAS has since been recognized as part of an evolutionarily ancient immune mechanism (17, 45–48). Recent discoveries reveal that cGAS-like enzymes function in bacterial antiphage immunity (45, 47), and cGAS-like receptors are now found across nearly all domains of life (46), underscoring the evolutionary conservation of the cGAS-STING axis. During bacterial infection, the cGAS-STING axis can be activated through two mechanistically distinct routes. One is initiated by cytosolic dsDNA, whereas the other is driven by bacterially derived CDNs (15, 49). These inputs can arise in both intracellular and extracellular infection settings, often following host uptake processes such as phagocytosis, and their relative contribution depends on the infecting pathogen and strain-specific traits (15, 50). Although crucial for defense, the cGAS-STING pathway can worsen the disease, depending on the infection type and microbial context (Table 1) (15, 24, 51). Notably, its role in antibiotic-resistant infections remains underexplored; however, recent studies have begun to fill this gap (Fig. 1).

MRSA is a leading cause of severe skin, soft tissue, and bloodstream infections, characterized by its ability to evade host immune defenses and its resistance to multiple antibiotics (52–55). It remains classified as a high-priority pathogen due to its substantial contribution to global disease burden and its capacity for multi-drug resistance, underscoring the urgent need for targeted research, prevention, and control strategies (1, 56). Recent studies indicate that inflammatory and type I IFN responses during S. aureus infection are mediated by RIG-I–dependent RNA sensing and cGAS-dependent DNA sensing (57). Although S. aureus is primarily considered an extracellular pathogen, increasing evidence demonstrates that it can transiently access and exploit intracellular niches within both professional and nonprofessional phagocytes. This ability enables S. aureus to manipulate the host’s innate immune pathways, including autophagy and apoptosis, thereby enhancing its persistence, immune evasion, and transmission (23, 58). During this intracellular phase, autolysis of phagocytosed S. aureus can release bacterial DNA into the host cytosol during intracellular processing, thereby providing a potential trigger for cGAS-STING activation (58). Murine models of MRSA pneumonia suggest that STING enhances early host defense by suppressing necroptotic cell death (59). STING-deficient mice show higher mortality, increased bacterial loads in lung tissues and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, severe lung damage, and heightened inflammation. Although its absence increased cytokine production at 24 hours postinfection, STING primarily suppressed bacterial proliferation in the early phase without significantly affecting lung cytokine levels (59). Another study reported modestly reduced levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-12 at early time points following infection in STING-deficient settings [Fig. 1 in (60)]. Although a difference in bacterial inoculum is apparent between studies [1 × 108 versus 2 × 108 colony-forming units (CFUs)], this variation alone is unlikely to account for the observed discrepancies in cytokine profiles (59, 60). A likely source of this discrepancy is the distinct experimental strategies used to disrupt STING signaling across studies. While one approach relied on systemic deletion of STING, the other selectively targeted STING within the myeloid lineage. Such differences in the cellular scope of STING disruption may differentially influence early inflammatory readouts in the lung. In this context, global STING deficiency may mask compartment-specific cytokine changes through compensatory or opposing immune responses arising from nonmyeloid cell populations. Given the established role of macrophage-intrinsic STING signaling in mediating innate immune activation and type I IFN responses downstream of bacterial cytosolic sensing (61), cell type–specific STING signaling dynamics likely underlie these divergent observations. Collectively, these findings support a model in which cGAS-STING sensing of bacterial invasion contributes to macrophage-associated cytokine responses that facilitate early bacterial control, with the magnitude and timing of this response depending on the cellular compartment in which STING signaling is disrupted. STING regulates necroptosis by modulating receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 3 (RIPK3) and mixed lineage kinase domain–like (MLKL) protein, thereby preventing excessive macrophage death (62–64). In MRSA-infected mice, MLKL knockout reduced necroptosis, limiting macrophage loss and bacterial proliferation (59). As MRSA toxin–induced necroptosis drives lung injury, STING likely modulates toxin-driven necroptosis, balances inflammation, and prevents immune evasion (59).

Although the cGAS-STING pathway confers protective effects against MRSA-induced pneumonia, it appears to exert detrimental effects in skin burn–associated MRSA infections (51). In a murine skin burn model, inhibition of cGAS-STING reduced IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-β levels while promoting faster skin tissue recovery, suggesting that the pathway exacerbates pathology in burn-associated MRSA infections (51). In general, STING activation in MRSA infections enhances antimicrobial immunity via type I IFN responses. However, excessive IFN signaling in burn injuries aggravates disease severity and contributes to tissue damage.

Similarly, the cGAS-STING pathway exhibits distinct functions in other antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections. While carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) is traditionally considered an extracellular pathogen, latest in vitro investigations have revealed its capacity to persist within host cells under specific conditions (65). A recent study using a murine nasal infection model of CRAB identified up-regulation of type I IFN signaling–related genes through RNA sequencing analysis (66). CRAB-induced mitochondrial dysfunction promotes the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm, which is primarily mediated by bacterial virulence factors and leads to activation of the cGAS-STING pathway (50, 66, 67). The mtDNA release is facilitated by outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), which form complexes that colocalize with host mitochondria (50). These OMV-mediated interactions between bacterial virulence factors and host mitochondria alter mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production while elevating reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, collectively contributing to mtDNA release (68). mtDNA-mediated pathway activation results in the phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, IRF3, and NF-κB p65, thus amplifying the inflammatory response (66). In contrast, cGAS inhibitor treatment or STING-deficient mice show markedly reduced activation of these signaling pathways (66). To evaluate the immunological role of cGAS-STING during CRAB infection, Yang et al. used epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a natural polyphenol identified as a direct cGAS inhibitor (69). EGCG treatment in both macrophage cultures and CRAB-infected mice similarly suppressed STING/TBK1/IRF3 phosphorylation, reduced proinflammatory cytokine production, and alleviated lung tissue damage, thereby confirming that mtDNA-induced cGAS-STING hyperactivation contributes to host pathology (66). Consistently, STING-deficient mice showed increased survival rates following CRAB infection, suggesting that excessive activation of the STING pathway may exacerbate host damage rather than confer protection (66). CRAB infection led to the translocation of STING to the Golgi apparatus in alveolar macrophages, whereas STING deficiency mitigated inflammation and reduced lung tissue damage (66). Thus, although the cGAS-STING axis is integral to the host immune defense against bacterial infections, its overactivation may contribute to pathological damage, underscoring the potential therapeutic advantage of modulating this pathway.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) is primarily an intracellular pathogen that survives and grows within macrophages. One prominent mechanism, which regulates the host cGAS-STING pathway, is through membrane damage mediated by the ESX-1 secretion system. Early studies demonstrated that ESX-1 and its secreted substrates, including EsxA (ESAT-6) and EsxB (CFP-10), are required for phagosomal membrane disruption and virulence (70–74). Although other type VII secretion system (T7SS) paralogs, including ESX-2 and ESX-4, have also been implicated in phagosomal permeabilization and toxin trafficking (75), their potential roles in cGAS-STING activation remain to be clarified and warrant further investigation. Recent live-cell imaging confirmed that ESAT-6 can drive fibril-mediated vesiculation that destabilizes phagosome-like compartments. This disruption increases membrane permeability in a concentration-dependent manner, thereby facilitating its escape (76, 77). Both bacterial DNA and host-derived mtDNA can serve as ligands for cGAS under these conditions. Cytosolic MTB DNA, revealed in an ESX-1–dependent manner, is directly sensed by cGAS to induce type I IFN and autophagy (21). In contrast, another study reported that cytosolic accumulation of host mtDNA correlates more strongly with IFN-β induction, and this effect varies depending on the infecting MTB strain (78). Meanwhile, infection with a ΔESX-1 mutant, which is defective in phagosomal membrane disruption, resulted in markedly reduced cytosolic DNA exposure and type I IFN responses. These findings demonstrate that ESX-1–mediated membrane damage can expose both bacterial and host-derived DNA to the cytosol, with their relative contributions varying depending on the infecting strain and cellular context. This duality underscores the complexity of cGAS-STING activation during MTB infection and raises important questions. Specifically, to what extent do bacterial versus host DNA drive cGAS-STING responses in vivo, and how do these mechanisms differ across clinical strains? Addressing these issues will likely require approaches that trace the origin of cytosolic DNA and allow for strain-specific comparisons.

Along with DNA-mediated cGAS activation, MTB can produce CDNs, such as cyclic di-AMP, which directly activate STING in a cGAS-independent manner (49, 79). MTB infection is thus a valuable model for examining the dual role of the cGAS-STING pathway in bacterial infection. cGAS activation promotes selective autophagy in macrophages, directing intracellular MTB toward lysosomal degradation (80). Further, cGAS deficiency increases intracellular replication of MTB, whereas the activation of STING and TBK1 facilitates ubiquitin-mediated autophagic targeting of both cytosolic DNA and the pathogen itself (21).

In contrast, several studies have reported that type I IFNs induced by cGAS-STING signaling can suppress protective immunity against MTB. Type I IFNs suppress protective T helper 1 (TH1) cell immunity by inhibiting the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 and inducing immunosuppressive cytokines, including IL-10. This ultimately leads to reduced IFN-γ production during mycobacterial infection (81). This shift may compromise macrophage survival, driving necrosis that not only supports pathogen persistence but also actively facilitates bacterial dissemination throughout the host (82). This shift may impair the bactericidal capacity of infected macrophages and inadvertently support pathogen persistence. More recently, cGAS-STING–driven type I IFN signaling has been proposed to mediate enhanced intracellular survival of MTB. MTB interferes with host DNA repair, thereby increasing micronuclei formation that serves as potent activators of the cGAS-STING pathway. This, in turn, augments type I IFN production, suppresses IL-1 signaling, and up-regulates scavenger receptor–A1 (SR-A1). Elevated SR-A1 expression promotes lipid droplet accumulation, providing a nutrient-rich environment that supports MTB survival within host cells (83).

The dual role of the cGAS-STING pathway is evident in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) infections, depending on the type of host-pathogen interaction. In a mouse model of acute lung infection, wherein P. aeruginosa predominantly causes extracellular infections, cGAS is essential for recognizing bacterial extracellular DNA and promoting host defense. cGAS deficiency in this context led to reduced transcription of ER stress–associated proteins, including BiP, ATF4, and spliced XBP1 (XBP1s) (24). Although P. aeruginosa is typically considered an extracellular pathogen, it occasionally causes intracellular infections. In such cases, the bacterial effector protein ExoS, a T3SS component, facilitates intracellular survival by inhibiting autophagy. The adenosine diphosphate–ribosyltransferase domain of ExoS disrupts autophagic initiation by suppressing the kinase activity of the Atg14L-Vps34 complex and blocking phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate generation (84, 85).

During intracellular P. aeruginosa infection, cGAS-STING–mediated type I IFN production exacerbates bacterial pathogenicity and enhances survival in the lung. P. aeruginosa activates inflammasome components, like NLRP3 and NLRC4, inducing AKT phosphorylation at Ser473, which suppresses cGAS activity (86). Mechanistically, AKT phosphorylates Ser291 residue within the catalytic domain of cGAS, thereby attenuating its enzymatic activity. Although the precise mechanism of type I IFN–driven probacterial effects remains unclear, type I IFNs may stimulate IL-27 production, suppressing the production of IL-17A, a key antimicrobial cytokine (86, 87). Intracellular P. aeruginosa strains exhibit antibiotic resistance. A zebrafish embryo wound model with cystic fibrosis isolates showed adaptive resistance to tobramycin, colistin, and ciprofloxacin but remained susceptible to membrane-permeable ofloxacin. These results highlight the need to better understand the interplay between infection type, antibiotic resistance, and cGAS-STING activation (88).

This dual role of the cGAS-STING pathway raises questions regarding its function in antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections. Variations in virulence factors such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria can trigger other inflammatory pathways. Gram-negative bacteria produce both endotoxins and exotoxins, including LPS, which can activate other inflammatory pathways (15). Cross-talk between cGAS-STING and these pathways may worsen inflammation and tissue damage. In addition, STING can be activated independently of cGAS by bacterial CDNs like c-di-AMP and c-di-GMP, which regulate virulence and biofilms (89–91). These CDNs directly activate STING, bypassing cGAS (15). Although structurally similar, c-di-AMP binds STING with lower affinity than 2′3′-cGAMP (Kd: 56 to 61 nM versus 4 to 10 nM) (92), suggesting that pathogen-specific CDN production may modulate immune responses. Meanwhile, short cytosolic DNA fragments facilitate the interaction between cGAS and Beclin-1 (93, 94), which is mediated independently of the enzymatic activity of cGAS through its nucleotidyltransferase domain, with residues Lys173 and Arg176 playing key roles, whereas Beclin-1 engages through its coiled-coil domain (94). The resulting cGAS-scDNA–Beclin-1 complex promotes the dissociation of Rubicon, a negative regulator of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase class III (PI3KC3), from the Beclin-1–PI3KC3 complex, thereby activating PI3KC3 and triggering autophagy. In turn, autophagy regulates cGAS-STING signaling, forming a feedback loop that may enhance defense in a strain-dependent manner.

Bacterial immune evasion proteins complicate the role of cGAS-STING. Expressed during early infection or proliferation, these proteins may suppress signaling to escape immune detection or manipulate signaling to promote bacterial survival. They influence autophagy and cell death or trigger immunosuppressive cytokines (76, 77, 95, 96). Although cGAS-STING is crucial for sensing and responding to antibiotic-resistant bacteria, its effects vary with context, highlighting the need for precise therapeutic modulation.

ANTIBIOTIC RESISTANCE–ASSOCIATED BACTERIAL ADAPTATIONS INFLUENCING CGAS-STING SIGNALING

The link between antibiotic resistance and the cGAS-STING pathway remains incompletely understood; however, emerging evidence suggests that resistance-associated bacterial adaptations are associated with changes in host cGAS-STING signaling. In particular, alterations in bacterial cell–envelope composition, virulence factor expression, and CDN metabolism that accompany resistance acquisition have emerged as recurrent determinants of aberrant STING activation. These observations suggest that antibiotic resistance is not merely a microbial survival trait but may also influence host innate immune sensing pathways (Fig. 2). Proteomic profiling of rifampicin-resistant (RR), MDR, and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) MTB strains revealed the down-regulation of EsxA and EsxB compared to drug-sensitive strains (97). Resistant clinical isolates were reported to show reduced expression of MmsA, a protein that inhibits cGAS-STING signaling (97, 98). Although the mechanistic significance of these observations remains unclear, they are consistent with the notion that resistance acquisition may coincide with broader remodeling of bacterial immune-modulatory programs (98).

Furthermore, a recent pilot study showed that isoniazid (INH)–resistant MTB strains preferentially activated the cGAS-STING/STAT1 axis and up-regulated ISGs, to a greater extent than RR or drug-sensitive strains (99). While RR strains triggered cGAS-STING activation, their response was weaker than that of the INH-resistant strains (99). Because rifampicin and INH act via inhibition of bacterial RNA polymerase and mycolic acid–dependent cell-wall synthesis, respectively, these differences may reflect resistance phenotype–associated bacterial remodeling rather than direct antibiotic effects during infection (99–102). Consistent with this notion, INH resistance commonly arises through alterations in the KatG/InhA–mycolic acid axis and is accompanied by changes in mycobacterial lipid metabolism/cell-envelope composition, which are known determinants of host-pathogen interactions and immune activation (102–105). Accordingly, mycobacterial envelope glycoconjugates and mycolate-containing lipids are established immunomodulators that reshape phagosomal trafficking and tune innate immune outputs (106–108). Resistance-linked perturbations of mycolic-acid/cell-envelope composition may therefore plausibly alter redox and mitochondrial stress states in infected macrophages, although direct causal evidence remains limited. Oxidative/mitochondrial stress can, in turn, promote cytosolic mtDNA release and accumulation, enabling mtDNA-dependent cGAS-STING signaling to amplify type I IFN/ISG outputs (109).

Bacterial proteins that modulate both antibiotic resistance and immune signaling have gained increasing attention. For instance, OmpA (also known as Omp38) from A. baumannii impedes the diffusion of negatively charged antibiotics like β-lactams, thereby reducing their penetration (110, 111). Two conserved residues (Asp271 and Arg286) in its C-terminal OmpA-like domain mediate noncovalent binding to diaminopimelate in peptidoglycans, thus reinforcing membrane stability and resistance (112). OmpA promotes OMV formation, aiding in antibiotic efflux and PAMP delivery to the host (50). Further, OmpA activates the guanosine triphosphatase dynamin–related protein 1, inducing mitochondrial fission and cytochrome c release, which leads to mtDNA leakage and cGAS-STING pathway activation, thereby worsening pathogenicity (50).

Recently, CDNs and their metabolic enzymes have emerged as potential key mediators linking antibiotic resistance and the cGAS-STING pathway. Long-term exposure to antifolate antibiotics such as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole can promote the emergence of thymidine-dependent small-colony variants (TD-SCVs) of S. aureus, characterized by mutations in thymidylate synthase (encoded by thyA) (113, 114). Thymidylate synthase catalyzes the de novo synthesis of thymidylate by converting deoxyuridine monophosphate to deoxythymidine monophosphate, which is subsequently phosphorylated to 3′-deoxythymidine 5′-triphosphate and incorporated into DNA. In clinically isolated TD-SCVs harboring thyA mutations, thymidylate synthase activity is abolished, resulting in an intrinsic deficiency in thymidine nucleotide biosynthesis and imposing metabolic stress on the bacteria. Although a direct mechanistic link between thymidine starvation and c-di-AMP biosynthesis has not yet been established, it is plausible that c-di-AMP signaling participates in compensatory stress-response pathways triggered by thymidine depletion (113, 115). In line with this notion, clinically isolated TD-SCVs exhibit 10- to 100-fold higher intracellular c-di-AMP levels compared to wild-type strains and induce markedly elevated IFN-β secretion in primary bone marrow–derived macrophages (pBMDMs), leading to excessive STING activation even in the absence of cGAS (113). This heightened STING signaling has been linked to increased airway inflammation. Furthermore, GdpP is a membrane-associated phosphodiesterase (PDE) that degrades c-di-AMP and thereby constrains intracellular c-di-AMP levels in S. aureus (116). Mutations in gdpP, commonly observed in methicillin-resistant isolates lacking mec genes (MRLM), lead to intracellular accumulation of c-di-AMP, resulting in cell wall thickening and enhanced β-lactam resistance (116). Notably, S. aureus biofilms can release substantial amounts of c-di-AMP into the extracellular space, where it elicits STING-dependent type I IFN responses in macrophages (117). In this context, it is plausible that MRLM isolates harboring gdpP mutations modulate the magnitude or temporal dynamics of biofilm-associated c-di-AMP release, thereby influencing cGAS-STING activation in infected hosts. However, these effects are likely to be strongly strain background dependent and have not been consistently demonstrated across clinical isolates (116). The genome of P. aeruginosa PAO1 encodes at least 41 predicted proteins involved in the synthesis and degradation of c-di-GMP, including diguanylate cyclases (DGCs) and PDEs (118). Environmental factors can stimulate DGC activity in P. aeruginosa, leading to elevated intracellular c-di-GMP levels (119). Increased c-di-GMP promotes biofilm formation and activates the MexAB-OprM efflux pump, thereby increasing broad-spectrum antibiotic resistance (118, 120). However, whether and how elevated intracellular c-di-GMP levels in P. aeruginosa directly modulate host cGAS-STING signaling remains unclear, and further studies are required (119). In summary, these examples highlight the role of CDNs as central regulators of bacterial physiology and antimicrobial resistance.

Streptococcus pneumoniae is an extracellular bacterium that exhibits c-di-AMP metabolic regulation, affecting both antibiotic resistance and cGAS-STING activation (121). In S. pneumoniae, deletion of the PDEs pde1 or pde2 disrupts c-di-AMP homeostasis (121). Pde1 hydrolyzes c-di-AMP to 5′-phosphoadenylyl-adenosine (pApA), whereas Pde2 degrades both c-di-AMP and pApA to AMP (121). Loss of Pde2 leads to c-di-AMP accumulation, provoking hyperactivation of the cGAS-STING pathway in macrophages, causing approximately 80% more cytotoxicity than that of wild-type strains (121). Similarly, deletion of pde1 elevates c-di-AMP levels, altering cell wall synthesis and metabolism and conferring high-level resistance to antibiotics like ampicillin (122). These findings demonstrate that bacterial control of CDN metabolism can enhance resistance mechanisms and modulate host immune responses, potentially increasing virulence.

Collectively, CDNs and their metabolic enzymes are central to the interface between antimicrobial resistance and innate immunity. Targeting CDN pathways alongside cGAS-STING may offer a promising dual strategy for managing both resistance and host inflammation, as resistance-driven CDN changes can intensify immune activation. However, direct evidence of CDN-mediated STING activation in antibiotic-resistant pathogens remains limited. Establishing how resistance-associated CDNs influence both antimicrobial resistance and host STING signaling will therefore be critical, and such studies are expected to provide valuable insights for the development of STING-targeted immunotherapies.

IMMUNE MODULATION VIA CGAS-STING BY BACTERIAL EFFECTOR PROTEINS

Several antibiotic-resistant bacteria secrete effector proteins that modulate host cGAS-STING signaling to evade immunity (Fig. 1). Effector accessibility depends on pathogen localization, particularly whether the bacteria are intracellular or extracellular (Table 1). Although the precise mechanisms underlying effector secretion remain incompletely defined, several of these effectors have been shown to modulate host immune signaling during infection. These effectors either suppress or enhance the pathway, fostering favorable conditions for bacterial persistence (Table 2). Prominent examples encoding both activators and inhibitors of the cGAS-STING pathway are described below (95, 96, 123).

Host cGAS-STING regulatory mechanism for antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

Peptidyl prolyl isomerase A (PPiA, Rv0009) directly interacts with STING via its PXXP motif (PDP), with residues Pro170, Asp172, and Pro173 crucial for binding (124). This interaction enhances STING-TBK1-IRF3 signaling and promotes IFN-β production, facilitating MTB survival. Mutations in these residues impair the pathway and reduce bacterial fitness (124). Although altered PPiA expression in antibiotic-resistant MTB strains remains unreported, its immunomodulatory function is evident.

Another effector, urease C (UreC, Rv1850), promotes cGAS-STING activation by disrupting host DNA repair (83). Mechanistically, UreC binds to the host protein RUVBL2, thereby destabilizing the RUVBL1-RUVBL2-RAD51 DNA repair complex and leading to accumulation of DNA damage and micronuclei formation (83). The presence of micronuclei provides cytosolic DNA that can be sensed by cGAS, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway (83). Inhibition of UreC by acetohydroxamic acid reduces the bacterial burden in RR Mycobacterium marinum infections (83), highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Conversely, certain MTB-secreted proteins inhibit cGAS-STING signaling to evade immune surveillance. CDN PDE CdnP (Rv2837c) degrades both bacterial c-di-AMP and host-derived cGAMP, thereby attenuating STING activation. Mutant strains lacking CdnP show sustained STING signaling, elevated IFN levels, and reduced virulence, underscoring its role in immune evasion (125, 126). ENPP1 is a mammalian ectoenzyme that hydrolyzes extracellular cGAMP, thereby restricting STING activation (127, 128). Recent computational and experimental studies have begun to identify inhibitors targeting CdnP. Structure-based virtual screening combined with in vitro validation revealed synthetic small-molecule inhibitors of CdnP that block its PDE activity (129). In addition, natural compound libraries screened through docking and molecular dynamics simulations have yielded promising candidate inhibitors with predicted stable binding to the CdnP active site (130). Although CdnP and ENPP1 do not share structural homology, their functional convergence in degrading CDNs underscores the therapeutic potential of bacterial CdnP, highlighting the importance of developing inhibitors that can preserve or restore host STING signaling.

MmsA (Rv0753c) is another MTB effector that suppresses STING by promoting its autophagic degradation (98). It binds to STING via its N-terminal domain and recruits p62 (SQSTM1) through its C-terminal region (98), facilitating autophagosome-mediated degradation and dampening of the STING-TBK1-IRF3 axis. A hypervirulent MTB strain harboring Arg138→Trp (R138W) mutation in MmsA enhances this degradative activity, potentially contributing to severe clinical manifestations (98, 131). MmsA overexpression in Mycobacterium smegmatis paradoxically reduces bacterial load during infection by suppressing STING activity but reveals potential antibacterial effects mediated by MTB-induced immune modulation (98). Together, these findings highlight MmsA as a context-dependent regulator of host immunity. Given that resistant clinical isolates also exhibit altered MmsA expression (97), understanding how changes in MmsA expression and function influence both antibiotic resistance and cGAS-STING signaling will shed light on the interplay between immune modulation and resistance.

Beyond MTB, effector proteins that manipulate the cGAS-STING pathway have also been reported in antibiotic-resistant pathogens, including MRSA and CRAB. In MRSA, EsxB is secreted via the T7SS and directly binds to STING (60, 132). It inhibits STING activation by blocking palmitoylation, a posttranslational modification essential for STING clustering in the Golgi apparatus (60, 132). It interferes with the interaction of STING with the palmitoyltransferase DHHC3, which contains a conserved Asp-His-His-Cys (DHHC) motif responsible for mediating STING palmitoylation. Furthermore, EsxB binding to STING prevents EsxB’s degradation by inhibiting K48-linked ubiquitination at Lys33 while simultaneously suppressing palmitoylation at STING Cys91 and K63-linked ubiquitination at Lys83 (60). These interactions collectively attenuate proinflammatory cytokine production and contribute to increased MRSA virulence by dampening host innate immune responses.

In CRAB, OmpA, as previously discussed, was initially identified for promoting apoptosis through interactions with death receptors and mitochondrial disruption. Moreover, it induces ~180–base pair DNA fragments capable of activating cGAS (20, 133). OmpA translocates to the host mitochondria via OMVs and promotes mtDNA release by activating the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, leading to cGAS-STING hyperactivation, which may contribute to the pathogenicity of CRAB (66). However, the mechanisms underlying OmpA-mediated mtDNA release and its association with antibiotic resistance remain unclear. Further studies may uncover therapeutic targets to counteract the immune evasion of CRAB through mtDNA-induced signaling activation.

Antibiotic-resistant pathogens often deploy effector proteins that manipulate cytosolic DNA and host immune pathways to promote pathogenicity and evade immune responses. Although it remains unclear whether the acquisition of antibiotic resistance directly increases the abundance or delivery of effector proteins that modulate cGAS-STING signaling, several bacterial proteins have been reported to influence this pathway, including PPiA, UreC, MmsA, EsxB, and OmpA. In clinically relevant resistant pathogens, these factors can alter host innate immune responses and may contribute to immune evasion in a context-dependent manner. Clarifying whether and how resistance acquisition reshapes the expression, delivery, or activity of these effectors will advance our understanding of the role of cGAS-STING in drug-resistant infections and support the development of targeted therapies.

PRECLINICAL APPROACHES TARGETING THE CGAS-STING PATHWAY AGAINST ANTIBIOTIC-RESISTANT BACTERIA

Therapeutic strategies targeting the cGAS-STING pathway are currently being investigated as potential treatments for infections caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Depending on the pathogen and context, these approaches aim to activate or inhibit the pathway.

In S. aureus, planktonic forms activate IRF3 and IFN-β production via cGAS-STING, whereas biofilm-forming strains suppress this pathway (134). MRSA strains exhibit higher biofilm formation rates than those of antibiotic-sensitive strains, potentially complicating immune responses (134–136). To counter this, a mesoporous polydopamine-based m-Mncp nanoplatform responsive to near-infrared light was developed, which generates ROS to disrupt biofilms, enhance immune cell infiltration, and promote M1 macrophage polarization and dendritic cell maturation (137). A benzothiazole-urea hybrid compound has shown promise in treating MRSA infections after skin burns by inhibiting the cGAS-STING/NF-κB/IRF axis. The compound inhibited MRSA DNA gyrase via stable hydrogen bonding, demonstrating a lower minimum inhibitory concentration and reduced resistance development than that for ciprofloxacin (138). These findings support the dual antimicrobial and immunomodulatory potential of this pathway, although further studies are needed to clarify its mechanism. Preclinical research supports vaccine-based strategies involving cGAS-STING. An x-ray–inactivated P. aeruginosa vaccine (XPa) activates dendritic cells via the cGAS-STING axis, promoting their maturation by up-regulating CD86, CD80, and major histocompatibility complex II. This leads to downstream immune responses, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, CD8+ T cell proliferation, and long-term immune memory. XPa provides significant protection in murine pneumonia models infected with both laboratory and MDR P. aeruginosa strains in a cGAS-STING–dependent manner. These results highlight the potential of XPa as a vaccine strategy for high-risk groups, including patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, warranting further clinical investigation (139).

CLINICAL LANDSCAPE OF CGAS-STING MODULATORS AND IMPLICATIONS FOR ANTIBIOTIC-RESISTANT BACTERIAL INFECTION THERAPY

Therapeutic agents targeting the cGAS-STING pathway are being developed as agonists to boost immune responses in cancer and viral infections, or as inhibitors to curb inflammation in autoimmune diseases. Despite active preclinical interest, clinical development has thus far been largely confined to STING agonists in oncology. Representative examples include SNX281, a non-CDN STING agonist in phase I trials for solid tumors (NCT04609579), which promotes type I IFN and cytokine production to enhance antitumor immunity (140). Similarly, MK-1454 (ulevostinag; synthetic CDN, NCT04220866) (141), TAK-676 (dazostinag; small molecule, NCT04420884) (142), TAK-500 (a CCR2-directed antibody–drug conjugate delivering a STING agonist payload, NCT05070247) (143), and E-7766 (macrocyclic agonist, NCT04144140) (144) are being tested, often in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Beyond oncology, STING agonists like CF501 are being developed as potent vaccine adjuvants, showing strong neutralizing antibody responses and durable protection in SARS-CoV-2 studies (145).

In contrast, cGAS inhibitors remain largely at the preclinical stage. Although compounds such as VENT-03 and IMSB301 have been noted in press releases as preparing for or entering first-in-human evaluation, peer-reviewed clinical data are lacking, and these candidates therefore remain outside the validated pipeline. Preclinical small-molecule inhibitors such as CU-32 and CU-76 have demonstrated selective, dose-dependent inhibition of human cGAS in cell-based assays, with median inhibitory concentration values of ~0.45 and ~0.24 μM, respectively (146). These compounds specifically inhibit the DNA-sensing arm of the cGAS-STING pathway without appreciable effects on RIG-I–MAVS or TLR-mediated signaling (146). Despite promising preclinical autoimmune model findings, translation into first-in-human studies has yet to be validated.

Preclinical investigations of cGAS-STING modulation in antibiotic-resistant bacteria and ongoing clinical development in cancer, viral infection, and autoimmune disease underscore their broader therapeutic potential. For instance, STING agonists, such as SNX281 and MK-1454, may promote M1 macrophage polarization and enhance autophagy-mediated antimicrobial responses, raising the possibility that they could help counter the impaired or suppressed immune responses observed in drug-resistant infections. Their potential to counteract immune dysregulation caused by STING-targeting proteins, such as PPiA and EsxB, suggests alternative therapeutic possibilities. Vaccine strategies such as XPa and adjuvants like CF501 demonstrate the potential of harnessing the cGAS-STING pathway to enhance immunity against both viruses and antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Conversely, cGAS-STING inhibitors like CU-32 and CU-76 can alleviate hyperinflammation caused by excessive DNA release, as seen with OmpA, thereby offering protection against acute infections.

Repurposing these modulators, informed by molecular mechanisms and clinical insights, could provide therapeutic opportunities against antibiotic-resistant infections. Aligning such interventions with the specific immune and pathological characteristics of each infection may foster more precise and effective HDTs.

CONCLUDING REMARKS AND REMAINING CHALLENGES

The cGAS-STING pathway is central to innate immune defense, but its contribution to antibiotic-resistant infections remains poorly understood. Differential activation between resistant and sensitive strains has been observed, yet the precise mechanisms by which resistance reshapes this pathway and modulates host immunity require further elucidation. Current studies indicate that resistance-associated determinants and bacterial effectors can exert divergent effects on cGAS-STING signaling, highlighting its dual nature in host-pathogen interactions.

Nevertheless, several critical questions remain. First, how do resistance determinants such as altered CDN metabolism and secreted effectors influence the availability of signaling inputs and thereby adjust the intensity and quality of cGAS-STING responses? For example, gdpP mutations in S. aureus, pde1 or pde2 deletions in S. pneumoniae, and effectors such as EsxB or OmpA have been shown to alter CDN levels or DNA release, plausibly shaping immune activation outcomes (60, 66, 116, 122).

Second, how do specific antibiotic exposures modify ligand availability and downstream responses of the cGAS-STING pathway? TD-SCVs induced by trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, for instance, exhibit excessive c-di-AMP accumulation that drives cGAS-independent STING hyperactivation and inflammation, illustrating that antibiotics can, in certain cases, directly influence host immunity beyond their antimicrobial action (113, 114).

Third, under what tissue or infection contexts does cGAS-STING signaling switch from protective to pathological, and what molecular switches govern this polarity (Table 1)? In S. aureus infections, the pathway is protective in pneumonia but contributes to tissue injury in burn wounds (51, 59). In CRAB, mtDNA drives excessive STING activation, while in P. aeruginosa, extracellular sensing promotes defense whereas intracellular effectors such as ExoS interfere with host immune signaling (24, 66, 85). In addition, AKT-mediated phosphorylation of cGAS at Ser291 has been shown to dampen activity, underscoring that both pathogen effectors and host kinases may act as context-specific modulators (86). Building on these observations, the dual effects of cGAS-STING signaling are likely to reflect context-dependent differences in signal magnitude, cellular compartmentalization, and temporal persistence, rather than intrinsic pathway ambiguity (Table 1). Moderate and transient activation, such as extracellular DNA sensing during acute pneumonia, appears to support antimicrobial defense by promoting controlled type I IFN production and autophagy (35, 59). In contrast, sustained or excessive activation driven by abundant endogenous ligands, including mtDNA released during tissue damage or metabolic stress, may overwhelm regulatory checkpoints and shift the pathway toward pathological inflammation (66, 68). At the molecular level, such functional polarity is likely governed by a balance between host-encoded regulatory mechanisms and pathogen-derived effectors that fine-tune cGAS-STING signaling (Table 1). Host kinases such as AKT, which phosphorylates cGAS at Ser291 to suppress its enzymatic activity, may function as negative feedback regulators that restrain excessive signaling during intracellular infection (86). Conversely, bacterial effectors such as ExoS, EsxB, or OmpA can perturb autophagy, STING palmitoylation, or mitochondrial integrity, thereby either dampening or amplifying STING activation and biasing infection outcomes toward immune evasion or tissue damage (50, 60, 66, 84, 85).

Fourth, during resistance acquisition, how do the expression and function of immunomodulatory bacterial proteins such as EsxB, MmsA, OmpA, and CdnP change, and with what consequences? MmsA promotes STING degradation via autophagy, EsxB inhibits STING palmitoylation, and CdnP degrades CDNs to blunt immune activation (60, 98, 125, 126). While these factors are established modulators, whether their expression dynamically shifts in resistant strains remains to be clarified; longitudinal mapping could reveal conserved therapeutic targets.

Unlike antibiotics, which drive resistance through selective pressure, targeting host immunity offers a sustainable therapeutic approach (147). Repurposing modulators under preclinical and clinical investigation represents a promising therapeutic avenue. Moving forward, clarifying how resistance mechanisms, such as effector proteins and CDN metabolism, intersect with cGAS-STING signaling and host immunity will be essential for advancing precision HDTs for antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections.
