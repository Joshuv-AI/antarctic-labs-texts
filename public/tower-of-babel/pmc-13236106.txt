A 4-month-old female infant presented to a pediatric hospital with a 6-day history of being unable to extend her left lower extremity. Her parents had noted resistance of the leg during diaper changes, but no infant distress; redness, warmth, or swelling of the limb; history of trauma; or appetite loss. Hip joint radiograph and ultrasound taken 5 days previously at a community pediatric urgent care clinic showed no abnormalities. The physician had recommended observation. Persistent symptoms prompted the parents to seek further care. The infant was born full-term with normal weight and development. She received routine immunizations at age 2 and 3 months (diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, and Haemophilus influenzae type b; pneumococcal conjugate vaccine; hepatitis B virus; rotavirus), most recently 8 days before symptom onset, as per the Japanese immunization schedule.

On presentation, the infant was afebrile (36.9°C), with normal vital signs and no irritability. Physical examination revealed no lower extremity warmth, erythema, bruises, marks, or skin lesions. The left lower extremity remained slightly flexed without voluntary movement. Passive movement showed resistance without restricted range of motion or crying. Palpation revealed no joint abnormalities or potential infection entry sites.

Blood tests showed elevated C-reactive protein (CRP; 14.5 mg/L, reference range < 3 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR; 52.0 mm/h, reference range 3 to 13 mm/h), raising concern for bacterial infection. Leukocyte counts and other laboratory values were normal (Table 1). A knee radiograph showed a lucent lesion with periosteal reaction at the proximal left tibia (Figure 1).

The emergency physician admitted the infant and referred her to our general pediatric team, who collaborated with orthopedic surgery in managing the patient. Our differential diagnosis included infectious processes (osteomyelitis, Brodie abscess), neoplastic conditions (leukemia, neuroblastoma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis), and traumatic injuries. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated left tibial T2 hyperintensity from the epiphysis to the metaphysis and within the ipsilateral joint, consistent with inflammatory changes (Figure 2) and suggesting an infectious cause.

Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance T2-weighted image of a 4-month-old female showing a hyperintense signal from the proximal epiphysis to the metaphyseal region of the left tibia (arrow) and hyperintense signal within the joint space (arrowhead).

Surgical débridement revealed an intraosseous abscess cavity surrounded by sclerotic bone containing purulent material and a sequestrum — a devitalized bone fragment that had undergone necrosis owing to compromised vascular supply — indicating advanced chronic infection. During arthrotomy, we aspirated cloudy, purulent fluid from the patient’s knee joint. Culture of the tibial abscess and sequestrum yielded Streptococcus pyogenes; joint fluid and blood cultures showed no growth. Histopathology revealed neutrophilic inflammatory infiltration with areas of infectious bone necrosis and reactive changes of the sclerotic bone surrounding the abscess cavity. Our diagnosis was extensive chronic bacterial osteomyelitis presenting as Brodie abscess with intra-articular extension of the infection.

We initiated intravenous cefazolin (150 mg/kg/d), changing to ampicillin (300 mg/kg/d) on day 8. On day 23, follow-up MRI demonstrated improvement and bloodwork showed decreasing inflammatory markers. We changed the antimicrobial to oral amoxicillin (90 mg/kg/d) on day 27. C-reactive protein and ESR were normal the following day (0.1 mg/L and 8 mm/h, respectively). The patient was discharged on day 36. After 3 months of antibiotic therapy, she had complete symptom resolution. Radiographs showed bone repair. Because of growth plate involvement, she requires observation for potential growth disturbance with clinical examinations and MRI studies to monitor bony bridge formation and complete bone remodelling every 6 months until physeal closure and completion of growth. The patient showed no complications or recurrence at 1 year after treatment completion.

Discussion

Pseudoparalysis — limb immobility despite intact neuromuscular function — in infants presents diagnostic challenges. Limb disuse may result from traumatic, infectious, neurologic, rheumatic, and neoplastic conditions (Box 1). Traumatic injuries represent a common cause and must be systematically excluded through history, physical examination, and radiographs. Once trauma is excluded, clinicians should consider infectious and neoplastic causes. Osteomyelitis occurs in approximately 8 per 100 000 children,1 and septic arthritis in 4 to 10 per 100 000.2 Leukemia and neuroblastoma occur in 4.1 and 1.12 per 100 000 infants, respectively.3,4 Depending on the age of the child, other important considerations in the differential diagnosis include juvenile idiopathic arthritis, neurologic disorders, and metabolic conditions.

Osteomyelitis results from direct bacterial invasion of bone, spread from adjacent tissues, or hematogenous dissemination. In pediatrics, hematogenous spread predominates, with bacteria originating from minor skin injuries or the respiratory tract.1 Staphylococcus aureus is the most common causative organism in pediatric osteomyelitis; however, other important pathogens include Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Kingella kingae.

Osteomyelitis is classified as acute (< 2 wk), subacute (2 wk to 3 mo), or chronic (> 3 mo).1 However, the precise onset of infection is often unclear. The presence of Brodie abscess suggests at least a subacute course, whereas sequestrum formation characterizes chronic osteomyelitis.5 A sclerotic wall containing the infection distinguishes indolent from symptomatic presentations, rather than bacterial virulence. This wall localizes infection within the bone marrow, minimizing systemic inflammatory response and clinical symptoms.5

Blood cultures are positive in only 40% of cases,1 necessitating empiric antimicrobial coverage. Cefazolin is an appropriate first-line agent; methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) coverage should be reserved for critically ill patients or resistant organisms. Clinicians typically administer antibiotics for 3 to 6 months, although some studies report equivalent outcomes with shorter treatment courses.6 In this case, we monitored clinical progress and inflammatory markers, completing antibiotic therapy after 3 months. Intraoperative cultures are the gold standard for bacteriologic confirmation.

Although osteomyelitis presents with limping, fever, localized tenderness, and sometimes visible erythema and swelling around long bones in ambulatory children,1 otherwise healthy infants may present with isolated decreased limb movement.7 Initial evaluation includes observation of limb movement, palpation, assessment of range of motion, and skin examination for potential bacterial entry sites, although disease-specific findings are often absent. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for bone or joint infection even with minimal symptoms.

Evaluating both CRP and ESR improves diagnostic accuracy.8 Elevation of ESR in this age group carries greater clinical significance for serious bacterial infection, whereas CRP elevation may occur with viral infections or minor inflammatory processes. In this case, elevated CRP and ESR supported a bacterial cause.

Radiography has 43% to 75% sensitivity for acute osteomyelitis, 9 inadequate alone but useful for confirming other diagnoses without sedation. Sensitivity improves in chronic osteomyelitis as pathological changes become more apparent. Ultrasonography has similar sensitivity (46% to 74%) and cannot exclude osteomyelitis, as demonstrated in this case where initial ultrasound examination was normal. Magnetic resonance imaging has superior sensitivity (82% to 100%) and specificity (75% to 99%),9 making contrast-enhanced MRI the preferred modality for detecting osteomyelitis. Magnetic resonance imaging identifies bone marrow lesions undetectable by radiography. In locations where clinical suspicion remains high despite normal radiographs and MRI is not readily available, clinicians should strongly consider transport. After blood culture collection in such cases, clinicians should initiate empiric antistaphylococcal antibiotics, particularly in ill-appearing infants or those with acute symptoms or systemic signs.

Given that localization of pain in infants is difficult, imaging the entire affected limb is prudent. Patients with knee or foot pathology may report or appear to have pain in the hip.10 The femur and tibia represent common sites for osteomyelitis.1 In our patient, the tibial pathology appeared to cause hip discomfort. Radiography excluded a fracture, and MRI and pathological examination excluded neoplasm.

Brodie abscess represents a form of subacute to chronic osteomyelitis7 in which sclerotic bone walls off the infection, resulting in a contained, indolent process with minimal systemic symptoms. Without such containment, chronic osteomyelitis presents with more prominent clinical findings. Brodie abscesses occur more frequently in the tibia (48.6%) than in the femur (31.1%),5 contrasting with pediatric osteomyelitis, where the femur is more commonly affected.1 The sclerotic wall contains the infection within the bone marrow, typically causing minimal systemic inflammatory response5 and insidious onset. Thus, symptom onset often does not correlate with infection onset in chronic osteomyelitis in infants. This is well demonstrated in our case, in which the infant had only 6 days of visible symptoms, despite prolonged infection confirmed by the formation of a sequestrum (> 3 mo).

Receipt of immunizations before symptom onset may raise suspicion of bacterial seeding. However, post-vaccination osteomyelitis is exceedingly rare, typically caused by S. aureus and occurring within days of immunization, with a median onset of 2 days (mean 4.3 d, interquartile range 1.0 to 5.7 d) and 86% of cases manifesting within 1 week.11 Infection associated with vaccination is highly unlikely in this patient given the subacute presentation, confirmed S. pyogenes infection, and sequestrum indicating that the infection predated immunizations.

Risk factors for longer term sequelae in acute osteomyelitis include delayed diagnosis (≥ 4 d from symptom onset), inadequate treatment, neonatal age, sickle cell disease, and infection with MRSA or Panton–Valentine leukocidin-positive strains. Delayed diagnosis is also a risk factor for later sequelae in indolent, chronic infections, despite the lack of correlation between infection and onset of symptoms. Late complications of pediatric osteomyelitis particularly affect patients in whom infection involves the growth plate,1 which is predisposed to bacterial seeding due to its rich vascularity. Physeal damage may result in limb-length discrepancy, angular deformity, or joint incongruity that can manifest months to years after treatment.1 Long-term monitoring is essential.

This case underscores several critical principles in managing infants with limb disuse. Clinicians should maintain high suspicion for osteomyelitis even in the absence of fever, and image the entire affected limb. When radiographs are normal, timely MRI enables earlier intervention. Lastly, infection involving the growth plate should prompt long-term monitoring for late complications.
