Comparison of microbial assemblages associated with the stalked barnacle Neolepas marisindica from two adjacent hydrothermal vent fields on the Central Indian Ridge
Authors: Jae Kyu Lim, Jeong-A Kim, Nayoung Lee, Chulhong Oh, Seonock Woo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Neolepas marisindica, a stalked barnacle endemic to the Central Indian Ridge (CIR), hosts microbial communities that remain poorly characterized. In this study, we performed 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing to characterize the microbiome associated with N. marisindica collected from two geographically adjacent hydrothermal vent fields along the Central Indian Ridge (CIR): Solitaire (Site A) and Onnuri (Site D). We further used PICRUSt2 to infer the functional potential of these microbial communities and to assess whether differences in in situ geochemical conditions between the two vents shape microbiome composition and metabolic capacity. Both sites harbored microbiota dominated by Proteobacteria, yet displayed distinct taxonomic and functional differences. Site D was enriched in methanotrophic genera (Methylobacter, Methylomicrobium), while Site A was dominated by sulfur-oxidizing taxa such as Thiotrichaceae. Although alpha diversity was higher at Site D, the difference was not statistically significant. Beta diversity analysis showed clear clustering by site. Functional prediction using PICRUSt2 revealed elevated pathways for oxidative phosphorylation, lipid metabolism, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis in Site D samples, suggesting adaptation to methane-rich conditions. LEfSe analysis supported these site-specific functional patterns. Overall, this study reveals both a shared microbial core and localized differences in microbiome structure and function in N. marisindica, underscoring the influence of geochemical variation on host-associated microbiota in deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystems.

Introduction

Hydrothermal vent fields are dynamic components of deep-sea ecosystems that support unique microbial communities and play key roles in global biogeochemical cycling. These environments are characterized by the production of geothermally heated, chemically reduced fluids rich in energy sources such as hydrogen sulfide (H2S), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), methane (CH4), and hydrogen (H2). When these fluids rapidly mix with cold, oxygenated seawater, they generate hydrothermal plumes that can rise hundreds of meters above the seafloor and disperse widely, contributing to nutrient transport and microbial dispersal1–4. This process is considered essential for maintaining deep-sea biological communities, as microorganisms form the foundation of chemosynthetic food webs and drive primary production in the absence of sunlight. Vent-associated microbes mediate a diverse range of ecological and geochemical processes, including carbon fixation, sulfur and methane oxidation, nitrogen transformation, and hydrogen utilization5–7. These microbial communities in hydrothermal vents are thought to be influenced by localized physicochemical factors, including temperature, fluid composition, and the availability of reduced compounds (e.g., CH\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}, H\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}S, H\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}) that shape metabolic potential8,9. Through these metabolic processes, vent microbiota directly couple geological energy fluxes with biological productivity, thereby sustaining deep-sea ecosystems10–12.

Although the ecological importance of microorganisms in hydrothermal vent systems is well recognized13–16, studies of host-associated microbiomes have been fewer compared to environmental microbial communities from vent plumes, fluids, and sediments, and are often focused on a limited number of well-studied taxa17. This disparity is largely due to the technical difficulties of accessing deep-sea habitats, which require specialized research vessels and remotely operated vehicles (ROVs)18. As a result, phylogenetic and ecological studies of vent-associated bacteria in relation to their host fauna remain limited, and the taxonomic structure, ecological roles, and evolutionary origins of many symbionts are still not fully resolved. Recent studies further underscore that vent microbiomes are simultaneously shaped by environmental heterogeneity and host identity. At the environmental scale, hydrothermal vent microbiomes are strongly influenced by local geochemical heterogeneity, with studies showing that chimney mineral composition and temporal changes in fluid chemistry can shape both taxonomic and functional community structures19,20. In parallel, host-associated factors also play a decisive role, as demonstrated by vent snails in the Indian Ocean whose species-specific microbiota and metabolic capacities were maintained even under shared environmental conditions21. Together, these findings indicate that vent animal microbiomes cannot be explained solely by surrounding geochemistry or by host factors alone, rather, they emerge from the interplay between both. Model species such as Riftia pachyptila, Bathymodiolus thermophilus, and Rimicaris exoculata have greatly advanced our understanding of animal–microbe symbioses at hydrothermal vents22–26. However, many other vent-associated taxa remain insufficiently studied, leaving substantial gaps in our knowledge of how animals and their microbial partners co-adapt to such extreme environments.

Among deep-sea animals, barnacles in the genus Neolepas represent a unique group of vent-endemic crustaceans14,27. The stalked barnacle Neolepas marisindica, endemic to the Central Indian Ridge (CIR), was recently taxonomically classified as Arthropoda; Crustacea; Hexanauplia; Scalpellomorpha; Eolepadidae; Neolepas, and is morphologically distinct from its Pacific relatives N. zevinae and N. rapanuii27. This discovery provided critical insights into the biogeography of vent fauna, challenging the “Indian Ocean dispersal corridor” hypothesis and suggesting a southward migration route of Neolepas through the Southern Ocean27. Beyond its evolutionary and biogeographic importance, N. marisindica is also one of the few vent-associated animals for which a reference transcriptome has been assembled, based on a specimen collected from the Onnuri Vent Field at 2,022 m depth28. This genomic resource provides a foundation for investigating host physiology, environmental adaptation, and potential host-symbiont interactions.

Recent studies on Neolepadidae revealed that epibiotic bacteria colonize the cirri, providing supplementary nutrition and highlighting their ecological reliance on microbial partners29. Investigations of intertidal barnacles further demonstrated that distinct microbial communities inhabit different anatomical niches, with environmentally influenced epibionts on the cirri and more stable, host-regulated microbiota in the gut30. Microbial colonization has also been observed in barnacle footprints, where cement residues serve as substrates for unique microbial assemblages31. These findings demonstrate that barnacle-associated microbiomes are distributed across multiple anatomical niches, establishing N. marisindica as an ideal model for investigating host–microbe interactions and microbiome variation in hydrothermal vent ecosystems. These structural features and microbial habitats are summarized in the anatomical schematic presented in Fig. 1b and 1c. Because detailed anatomical references for Neolepas are not currently available, we referred to the well-studied intertidal stalked barnacle Pollicipes to illustrate general anatomical structures and highlight potential microbiome-associated niches32.

The Solitaire Vent Field, first reported in the early 2000s, is located at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 19^\circ$$\end{document}S on the CIR at a depth of about 2,606 m and harbors diverse vent fauna including Neolepas, mussels (Bathymodiolus), and gastropods (Alviniconcha)27,33. In contrast, the Onnuri Vent Field, discovered during the 2017–2018 RV Isabu expedition, is located at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 11^\circ$$\end{document}S on the CIR at a depth of about 2,022 m and represents the northernmost hydrothermal site along the CIR. This discovery extends the known distribution of CIR hydrothermal activity northward, emphasizing the potential for biogeographic connectivity across Indian Ocean vent ecosystems. This geographic expansion is relevant, as CIR vent fields differ in hydrothermal chemistry and associated fauna. For example, the Kairei Field is dominated by vigorous black smoker activity, whereas the Solitaire Field is characterized mainly by diffuse clear fluids with relatively high pH33. Such differences have been linked to subtle morphological variation in N. marisindica populations despite their genetic similarity27. These findings suggest that localized geochemical regimes may influence both vent fauna and their associated microbiomes, highlighting the importance of examining microbial communities across distinct vent environments.

In this study, we investigated the microbial composition of the stalked barnacle N. marisindica from two hydrothermal vent fields along the CIR, Solitaire (Site A) and Onnuri (Site D) (Fig. 1a). These sites are geographically adjacent yet differ in their hydrothermal characteristics, providing a natural setting to test how environmental heterogeneity influences host-associated microbiomes. We selected this species for comparing the microbiome composition between the two geographically distant hydrothermal vents because N. marisindica was the only species found in both the Solitaire and Onnuri vent fields. Unlike mobile organisms that move throughout the vent area, N. marisindica is a sessile species that remains attached to the seafloor, making it a more reliable indicator of the local hydrothermal environment. Additionally, this barnacle species exhibited higher habitat density and a much broader distribution at the two study sites compared to other Indian Ocean hydrothermal vents, such as the Dodo and Edmond fields, based on observations made during our exploration of the Central Indian Ridge (Supplementary Fig. 1). Using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing combined with PICRUSt2-based metabolic inference, we compared both the bacterial community composition of the barnacle and the predicted functional potential across the two sites. This approach allows us to address the current gap in microbiome comparisons of N. marisindica across distinct CIR vent fields and offers new insights into the interplay between local geochemical regimes and host-specific factors in shaping barnacle-associated microbial communities.Fig. 1Sampling locations and putative anatomy of Neolepas marisindica. Results

Microbial community diversity of stalked barnacles from the two hydrothermal vents

We analyzed microbial community diversity in N. marisindica from two sites of the CIR, Solitaire (Site A) and Onnuri (Site D). At each site, three individual barnacles (A1–A3 and D1–D3) were collected, and DNA from each specimen was extracted and sequenced separately, providing three independent biological replicates per site. To investigate whether microbial communities differed between the two vent fields, we calculated alpha diversity indices (Shannon, Chao1, and Inverse Simpson) and compared overall community composition using weighted UniFrac distances and principal coordinates analysis (PCoA).

Alpha diversity metrics (Shannon, Chao1, and Simpson indices) were consistently higher in microbial communities associated with N. marisindica from Site D compared to Site A (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The Shannon index, which accounts for both richness and evenness, showed slightly higher values at Site D (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.4000$$\end{document}), suggesting a community structure characterized by even distribution among dominant taxa (Fig. 2a). The Simpson index, which emphasizes evenness, also was higher at Site D (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.1870$$\end{document}), supporting the interpretation of a less dominance-skewed community (Fig. 2c). The Chao1 index, which specifically reflects species richness, was marginally greater at Site D (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.0587$$\end{document}), indicating the presence of more rare or low-abundance taxa (Fig. 2b). Comparative analysis of stalked barnacle-associated microbiota

To evaluate taxonomic patterns beyond overall diversity, we compared the composition of N. marisindica-associated microbial communities between the two vent fields at different phylogenetic resolutions (phylum, family, and genus levels). This analysis aimed to identify conserved microbial lineages shared across sites as well as site-specific taxa potentially shaped by local geochemical conditions.

At the phylum level, Proteobacteria were the dominant phylum across all barnacle-associated microbiota, in both sampling sites, accounting for 70.46% to 79.48% of the total bacterial population. Other taxa present in both sites included Bacteroidetes (10.1–20.9%), followed by Actinobacteria (0.8–5.0%), Planctomycetes (0.9–2.0%), and a minor proportion of unclassified bacterial communities (0–2.0%) (Fig. 4a). This indicates a conserved phylum-level structure across sites. While the microbial assemblages between sites did not show any discrete patterns at a phylum level, we find clear site-specific distribution patterns at the family level. The family- and genus-level profiles revealed clear site-specific differences (Fig. 4b and Fig. 4c). At the family level, Site A specimens were enriched in Thiotrichaceae (27.7%, 36.9%, and 37.3%), a lineage of sulfur-oxidizing Gammaproteobacteria. In Site D, however, Methylococcaceae was the predominant family, accounting for 21.4–24.6% of the community across replicates. Statistical testing confirmed that these differences were significant, with Thiotrichaceae enriched in Site A and Methylococcaceae enriched in Site D (Wilcoxon test, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.0495$$\end{document} for both families). Genus-level analysis (Fig. 4c) revealed further taxonomic differentiation. In Site A, Cocleimonas (derived from Gammaproteobacteria) was the most abundant genus in A1 (15.9%), A2 (24.7%), and A3 (15.9%). Other notable genera included Thiothrix (10.5% in A1, 11.2% in A2, 10.5% in A3) and Sulfurovum (5.2% in A1, 20.3% in A2, 5.2% in A3). Conversely, microbial communities from Site D showed a different taxonomic structure. In D1, dominant genera included Thiothrix (10.5%), Methylobacter (11.0%), and Methylomicrobium (9.5%). D2 had high abundance of Sulfurovum (20.3%), along with Methylobacter (7.8%) and Methylomicrobium (6.8%). In D3, Sulfurovum (20.0%) remained dominant, accompanied by Methylobacter (12.2%) and Methylomicrobium (7.2%). Notably, Methylobacter and Methylomicrobium are methanotrophic bacteria that utilize methane as a primary carbon and energy source. These genera have been identified as dominant taxa among the symbiotic microbial communities associated with Bathymodiolus platifrons inhabiting deep-sea hydrothermal vents34. Samples from Site D consistently exhibited a dominance of methanotrophic bacteria, likely reflecting methane-rich conditions at this vent field and suggesting a possible link between local geochemistry and barnacle-associated microbiota. To further evaluate taxonomic overlap beyond relative abundance patterns, we clustered sequences into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. This OTU-based approach was chosen to ensure stable diversity estimates with our limited sample size and to avoid over-splitting of closely related sequences, while still capturing overall community patterns. Across individual specimens, the number of OTUs ranged from 376 to 486, with an average of 403 OTUs in Site A and 449 OTUs in Site D. Shared OTUs were then visualized using Venn diagrams (Fig. 5). A total of 888 OTUs were identified from the specimens collected at Site A and Site D, among which 292 (32.9%) OTUs were shared between the two sites, while 253 (28.5%) and 343 (38.6%) OTUs were exclusively detected in Site A and Site D, respectively (Fig. 5a). Taxonomic analysis revealed that the shared OTUs were primarily affiliated with the phyla Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria, indicating the presence of a core microbial community associated with deep-sea barnacles. In contrast, site-specific OTUs encompassed a wider range of bacterial lineages, with Site D in particular harboring additional minor phyla such as Aquificae and Chloroflexi, which were absent from Site A.

To determine how consistent the microbial communities were within each vent site, we visualized the overlap of detected taxa using Venn diagrams (Fig. 5b and Fig. 5c). In Site A, 62 (11.4%), 40 (7.3%), and 65 (11.9%) OTUs were uniquely found in samples A1, A2, and A3, respectively. Additionally, 19 (3.5%) OTUs were shared between A1 and A2, 42 (7.7%) between A1 and A3, and 30 (5.5%) between A2 and A3, while 287 (52.7%) OTUs were commonly detected across all three A samples. These shared OTUs predominantly belonged to the phyla Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Planctomycetes. Similarly, in Site D, 34 (5.4%), 79 (12.4%), and 87 (13.7%) OTUs were uniquely identified in D1, D2, and D3, respectively. Furthermore, 48 (7.6%), 28 (4.4%), and 81 (12.8%) OTUs were shared between D1–D2, D1–D3, and D2–D3, respectively. A total of 278 (43.8%) OTUs were common across all three Site D samples, mainly affiliated with Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria.

These findings suggest that barnacles from both hydrothermal vent sites harbor a common microbial core predominantly composed of the phyla Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Planctomycetes. Functional prediction of barnacle-associated microbial communities from the Indian Ocean vent field

To examine the functional potential of barnacle-associated microbiota, we performed PICRUSt2 analyses using the KEGG database to infer putative gene functions from 16S rRNA gene profiles. Functional profiles were first compared using Z-score-normalized relative abundances of KEGG pathways, which allow relative enrichment patterns between sites to be compared independently of absolute pathway abundance. We then subsequently tested for site-specific functional biomarkers using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe). This two-step approach allowed us to assess both overall differences in metabolic capacity and statistically significant functional specializations between sites.

PICRUSt2-based functional prediction indicated that barnacle-associated microbial communities from both hydrothermal vent sites harbored a diverse array of metabolic and cellular processes (Fig. 6a). Microbial communities from Site A (A1–A3) exhibited consistently higher Z-scores across most functional categories compared to those from Site D (D1–D3), including pathways involved in energy metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. In particular, Site A samples showed increased representation in pathways such as lipid metabolism, glycan biosynthesis, folding and degradation, and transcription and translation. By contrast, Site D samples showed lower representation in most categories, with some enrichment observed only in specific cellular processes such as cell motility and signal transduction.

LEfSe analysis further supported this differentiation by identifying site-enriched functional biomarkers based on LDA scores (Fig. 6b). Microbial communities from Site A were significantly enriched in nucleotide and amino acid metabolism, transcriptional machinery, mismatch repair, and inorganic ion transport functions. Conversely, Site D samples were characterized by significantly higher abundances of pathways associated with oxidative phosphorylation, cell motility, membrane transport, and signal transduction. LEfSe also highlighted the overrepresentation of photosynthesis-related pathways and xenobiotic degradation in Site D. Discussion

The microbial community composition and predicted metabolic potential of the stalked barnacle N. marisindica inhabiting two hydrothermal vent fields, Solitaire (Site A) and Onnuri (Site D) vent fields, along the CIR were characterized and compared in this study. Although the barnacle-associated microbiota at both sites were dominated by Proteobacteria, distinct taxonomic and functional profiles were evident between the two locations, suggesting the influence of site-specific environmental factors. Comparisons of alpha diversity indices showed that the Shannon index (reflecting both species richness and evenness) and the Simpson index (emphasizing evenness by reducing the influence of dominant taxa) were slightly higher at Site D, and the Chao1 index (estimating potential species richness) also tended to be greater at Site D (Fig. 2). These results suggest the possible presence of a broader rare biosphere at this site. Meanwhile, beta diversity analysis based on weighted UniFrac distances indicated a tendency for samples to cluster by location (Fig. 3). However, the PERMANOVA test (pseudo-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F = 5.67$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.11$$\end{document}) was not statistically significant. Thus, although the separation between sites was visually apparent, the limited sample size (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 3$$\end{document} per site) constrains the statistical support for this pattern and should be considered a limitation of the study. This limitation, combined with the inherent constraints of 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing35–37, indicates that our findings should be interpreted with caution and verified through expanded sampling and complementary approaches. Nevertheless, 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing continues to represent a practical and informative strategy for exploring host-associated microbial communities. While geochemical data corresponding to barnacle sampling at the Site D were collected and published in a separate study, comparable measurements for the Site A were unfortunately not available, as geochemical sampling was not conducted simultaneously with barnacle collection at this site. According to Kim et al. (2020), Site D plumes display high methane concentrations and low metal contents, consistent with serpentinization-related hydrothermal activity38. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta ^{34}$$\end{document}S values of benthic organisms ranged from 1.8 to 12.8‰, which is clearly different from photosynthetic organic matter (18.7‰)39. These isotopic values, obtained directly from animal tissues, provide indirect evidence for the incorporation of hydrothermal sulfur and active sulfur cycling in this habitat. In addition, methane concentration in the water column was approximately 52.5 nmol/L. Surface sediments contained more than 0.3% TOC, 0.3–2.0% TS, and 100–12,000 ng/g of total mercury (THg), showing features closely linked to hydrothermal activity38. This geochemical background suggests the potential development of microbial assemblages associated with methane and sulfur metabolism at Site D. Consistent with this expectation, a previous study at Site D reported methanotrophic lineages in the surrounding environments using metagenomic analysis. Namirimu et al. (2022) identified members of the order Methylococcales (e.g., Methylococcus) as important components of OVF sediments, supporting the role of methane oxidation as a key metabolic process in this vent system40. Together, geochemical and metagenomic observations are consistent with our finding that barnacle-associated communities at Site D were dominated by methanotrophs such as Methylobacter and Methylomicrobium. Taken together, these results strengthen the interpretation that methane-rich geochemical conditions at Site D select for methanotrophic assemblages both in free-living and host-associated microbial communities. In contrast, although sulfur compounds were not directly measured at Site A, a previous geochemical study reported H2S concentrations of 4.75 mM41, which provides indirect evidence supporting the observation that the barnacle-associated microbiota at Site A included abundant sulfur-oxidizing lineages such as Thiotrix and Cocleimonas. Despite these indirect geochemical data and the observed composition of barnacle-associated bacterial communities, the lack of directly comparable geochemical measurements between the two sites highlights the need for future investigations that integrate geochemical data collected concurrently with biological sampling. In this context, the necessity for further studies still remains. Research on symbiotic microorganisms inhabiting deep-sea barnacles is still scarce. However, symbioses with methane- and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria have been reported in several other hydrothermal vent invertebrates. For example, the deep-sea mussel Bathymodiolus azoricus is known to harbor both methanotrophic and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria in its gills, which are tightly linked to the host’s energy metabolism42,43, while in lucinid clams, sulfur-oxidizing bacteria have been recently reported to colonize not only the gills but also the digestive tract44. These cases demonstrate that symbioses with methane- and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria are a common phenomenon in vent fauna, and the dominant lineages observed in barnacle-associated communities in this study likewise strongly suggest the potential for symbiosis. In particular, the high methane concentrations and hydrothermal isotopic signatures detected at Site D are consistent with the dominance of methanotrophic bacteria in Site D barnacles. Thus, our findings raise the possibility that barnacle-associated microbiota represent not merely attached environmental microbes but potential symbiotic assemblages closely integrated with the host’s ecology.

The functional prediction based on PICRUSt2 and LEfSe analyses revealed distinct metabolic and cellular functional profiles in barnacle-associated microbial communities from two hydrothermal vent sites. While both sites shared a conserved repertoire of core metabolic functions, including amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism, samples from Site A exhibited notably higher Z-scores across most functional pathways. These included biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, glycan metabolism, and major metabolic routes such as energy and lipid metabolism, suggesting a more active or enriched functional capacity in these pathways at Site A. These functions have been frequently associated with sulfur-oxidizing symbionts in other vent invertebrates, such as mussels and clams, where sulfur metabolism provides a major energetic basis for the host-symbiont system45,46. In contrast, LEfSe analysis identified Site D as being functionally enriched in pathways associated with oxidative phosphorylation, cell motility, enzyme families, signal transduction, and cellular processes. Similar functional repertoires have been reported in methane-oxidizing symbionts of deep-sea mussels, which rely on efficient energy conversion and environmental responsiveness in methane-rich habitats42,47. Collectively, these patterns suggest that barnacle-associated microbiota, although less well characterized than those in mussels or clams, may similarly reflect site-specific geochemical conditions by harboring lineages specialized in sulfur or methane metabolism. The possibility of such symbiotic interactions is further supported by recent findings in lucinid clams, where sulfur-oxidizing bacteria colonize both gills and the digestive tract, highlighting the diverse functional roles symbionts can play within marine invertebrates44. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that these functional predictions are based on 16S rRNA gene profiles and thus represent putative inferences rather than direct evidence of metabolic activity. PICRUSt2 relies on the availability of reference genomes and cannot capture strain-level variation or environment-specific gene content, meaning that the observed functional differences should be interpreted cautiously and ideally validated by metagenomic or metatranscriptomic approaches.

This study contributes to the relatively sparse literature on symbiotic and epibiotic microbial communities in stalked barnacles from hydrothermal vents. While studies on deep-sea mussels and tube worms have elucidated well-defined endosymbiotic relationships34, microbial associations in crustaceans such as N. marisindica remain poorly characterized. The identification of a shared microbial core across sites, primarily composed of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria, alongside site-specific lineages like Aquificae and Chloroflexi, suggests both evolutionary conservation and environmental filtering within barnacle microbiomes. These site-specific differences in predicted functional profiles likely reflect ecological selection pressures that regulate the activity of broadly conserved metabolic repertoires, rather than entirely distinct sets of microbial functions. Similar observations have been reported in hydrothermal vent gastropods, where chemoautotrophic symbionts across geochemically distinct habitats displayed broadly similar gene contents, and functional divergence was mainly attributed to regulation and efficiency of metabolic pathways48. Compared to hydrothermal plume and sedimentary microbiomes, which are highly diverse and fluctuate strongly in response to short-term geochemical changes49,50, the barnacle-associated microbiota analyzed in this study exhibited a more stable and host-linked structure. Across individuals and sites, we consistently detected a core dominated by Proteobacteria, with site-specific enrichment of sulfur-oxidizing Thiothrix and Cocleimonas at Site A and methanotrophic Methylobacter and Methylomicrobium at Site D. This pattern contrasts with the broad metabolic versatility and taxonomic heterogeneity of environmental microbiomes, and instead suggests functional specialization toward host-relevant energy metabolisms. Such stable yet geochemically responsive assemblages point to a dual influence of evolutionary conservation and environmental filtering in shaping barnacle-associated microbiomes, distinguishing them from free-living vent microbial communities. In conclusion, our findings reveal both conserved and divergent features of barnacle-associated microbial communities across two geographically and geochemically distinct hydrothermal vent fields. These insights advance our understanding of microbiome biogeography in deep-sea crustaceans and underscore the importance of local environmental factors in structuring symbiotic interactions in extreme marine ecosystems.

OTU analysis

A total of 291,623 reads were obtained from six barnacle samples (three from Solitaire and three from Onnuri) using the Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw reads were demultiplexed, adapters were trimmed, and paired-end reads were merged using FLASH v1.2.11. Sequences shorter than 400 bp, containing ambiguous bases, of low quality (Phred score < Q20), or identified as chimeric were removed. After quality filtering, between 23,677 and 42,341 reads per sample were retained. OTU clustering was performed with CD-HIT-OTU at a 97% sequence identity threshold, and representative sequences were taxonomically assigned in QIIME v1.9 using the NCBI 16S rRNA database (release 2018.12.02) with BLAST. Rarefaction curves demonstrated a progressive saturation trend across samples, with curves plateauing and showing relatively small standard deviations, suggesting that the sequencing efforts were adequate to represent the majority of microbial diversity (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Alpha diversity indices including Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1 were calculated from the OTU table in QIIME. Group comparisons (Site A vs. Site D) were performed using two-sided Student’s t-tests assuming equal variance. Visualization of diversity indices was conducted in Python using the pandas, plotly, and scipy libraries. Community composition differences were assessed using weighted UniFrac distances in QIIME. The resulting distance matrix was subjected to PCoA, and group-specific clustering was visualized with covariance-based confidence ellipses (approximately 85% confidence, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 1.5$$\end{document} standard deviations) calculated using the Elliptic Envelope method in scikit-learn. Statistical significance of compositional differences was further tested with PERMANOVA (adonis, 999 permutations). Relative abundance of microbial taxa at the phylum, family, and genus levels was obtained from the OTU table. Stacked barplots were generated in Python (pandas, plotly).

Functional profiles were predicted using PICRUSt2, with pathway annotations assigned according to the KEGG database.
